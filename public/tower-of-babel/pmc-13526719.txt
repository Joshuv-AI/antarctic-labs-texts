Gelatin methacryloyl/hyaluronic acid hydrogel for localized ropivacaine delivery alleviates neuropathic pain and supports functional recovery after spinal cord injury
Authors: Xuqi Yu, Liwen Du, Qiong Hu, Dan Xia, Fen Wang, Binnan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropathic pain caused by spinal cord injury severely compromises patients’ quality of life. The clinical application of ropivacaine is limited by its short duration of action and the significant side effects associated with repeated administration. In this study, we developed a Gelatin methacryloyl/hyaluronic acid-based hydrogel (Ropi-GelMA/HA) to enable localized and controlled delivery of ropivacaine by photo-crosslinking. In a rat model of spinal cord contusion, Ropi-GelMA/HA was associated with lower Nav1.3 and TNF-α expression and higher NGF and BDNF expression, together with improved motor recovery in rats with SCI. In vitro studies further supported the hydrogel’s favorable biocompatibility and controlled release behavior during the early phase after administration. Under the tested dosing regimens, Ropi-GelMA/HA was associated with reduced hepatorenal toxicity and more durable analgesic efficacy compared with free ropivacaine, resulting in prolonged analgesic effects and improved functional outcomes under localized controlled delivery conditions. These findings highlight the potential clinical utility of Ropi-GelMA/HA in the treatment of neuropathic pain following spinal cord injury.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating condition of the central nervous system, commonly caused by traffic accidents or traumatic events [1]. It is characterized by high rates of morbidity and disability, often resulting in multisystem dysfunctions involving the autonomic, respiratory, circulatory, urinary, and digestive systems [2]. Among the many complications of SCI, neuropathic pain is particularly prevalent, severely hindering rehabilitation and substantially reducing patients’ quality of life [3]. However, current pharmacological interventions for SCI-associated neuropathic pain are often limited in efficacy and accompanied by undesirable side effects [4].

Ropivacaine (Ropi), a long-acting local anesthetic, has shown promise in the management of neuropathic pain due to its capacity to block sodium channels in nerve fibers [5]. By targeting pain pathways directly, Ropi not only reduces nociceptive signaling but also attenuates the inflammatory response, providing analgesic and neuroprotective effects [6]. Its rapid onset of action and dose flexibility make it an attractive option for multimodal pain management, particularly in reducing postoperative opioid dependence [7]. Nevertheless, the short duration of Ropi’s therapeutic effects limits its utility in chronic or neuropathic pain conditions [8, 9]. Therefore, the development of a locally retained and controlled-delivery Ropi formulation is of great importance to enhance its clinical applicability and effectiveness in SCI-related pain management.

In recent years, hydrogels based on gelatin (Gel) and hyaluronic acid (HA) have emerged as promising platforms for sustained drug delivery [10]. These hydrogels combine the excellent biocompatibility of gelatin with the unique properties of hyaluronic acid (HA) within a three-dimensional network structure. HA contributes to an extracellular-matrix-mimicking architecture, high hydrophilicity, and modulation of the local inflammation, thereby supporting a cell-compatible microenvironment and enabling controlled local drug release during the early post-administration period. This approach may extend the duration of drug action, reduce the required frequency of administration, and minimize systemic exposure [11]. Moreover, GelMA/HA composite hydrogels can be engineered to exhibit temperature- or light-sensitive properties, allowing sol-to-gel phase transitions under physiological conditions or external stimuli [12]. This feature facilitates local administration and in situ gel formation, further enhancing the local retention and controlled release of therapeutic agents.

GelMA/HA hydrogels have been successfully applied for the delivery of a variety of drugs. For example, a temperature-sensitive GelMA/HA hydrogel was developed for the delivery of doxorubicin, enabling continuous drug release within tumor tissues and effectively suppressing tumor growth [13]. Similarly, GelMA/HA hydrogels loaded with gemcitabine and lipid nanocapsules have demonstrated localized and sustained drug release, significantly reducing tumor recurrence [14]. These findings highlight the versatility and clinical potential of GelMA/HA-based hydrogels in drug delivery applications.

Gelatin-containing hydrogels have shown promise as biomaterial platforms for SCI repair. Although direct evidence for GelMA/HA in this context remains limited, related gelatin-containing scaffold systems have shown that incorporating bioactive gelatin components can improve cell adhesion and support axonal extension, underscoring the broader relevance of gelatin-containing hydrogels for SCI repair. Furthermore, a three-dimensional printed biomimetic spinal cord scaffold constructed from a gelatin matrix has been reported to promote axonal connectivity and functional recovery in central nervous system (CNS) injuries [15]. Additional studies have shown that gelatin sponge scaffolds and dual-enzyme cross-linked gelatin hydrogels can reduce inflammation, inhibit apoptosis, and facilitate nerve regeneration, particularly when combined with cell transplantation and growth factor delivery [16].

Recent biomaterial-based strategies for SCI have evolved beyond simple structural support toward modulation of the regenerative microenvironment, axonal guidance, and regenerative rehabilitation [17–19]. For instance, aligned fibrin nanofiber hydrogels have demonstrated preclinical efficacy in non-human primate models by directing axonal outgrowth [18]. Similarly, biomimetic and bionic scaffolds are being developed to replicate the complex architecture of spinal cord tissue, offering new avenues for neural repair [20, 21]. These evolving concepts underscore the therapeutic potential of multifunctional hydrogel systems that not only deliver drugs locally but also actively contribute to tissue repair and functional recovery. In line with this paradigm, the present study aimed to develop a ropivacaine-loaded GelMA/HA hydrogel capable of simultaneously alleviating neuropathic pain and supporting a pro-reparative microenvironment after SCI.

Despite these advances, the potential of ropivacaine-loaded GelMA/HA hydrogels for the treatment of SCI-induced neuropathic pain remains largely unexplored. In particular, the mechanisms by which such formulations might relieve pain and promote nerve regeneration have not been fully elucidated. Addressing these gaps could lead to the development of more effective localized controlled-delivery systems for ropivacaine and provide new strategies for both pain management and functional recovery in SCI patients.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to develop an injectable GelMA/HA hydrogel (Ropi-GelMA/HA) capable of achieving localized and controlled delivery of ropivacaine, and to evaluate its therapeutic potential in SCI. The release behavior of Ropi-GelMA/HA was characterized in vitro using dialysis-based release assays and formulation optimization across different GelMA/HA ratios. In a rat model of spinal cord contusion, we assessed its efficacy in relieving neuropathic pain and enhancing motor function. Our findings suggest that Ropi-GelMA/HA enables effective early local drug delivery, with subsequent analgesic benefits and a repair-supportive microenvironment after SCI.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The rat neural stem cell line (NSCs) was obtained from Zhejiang Ruyiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). To maintain stemness, cells were cultured in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, Beijing, China) supplemented with 2% B27 (Gibco, USA), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF, PeproTech, USA), 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, PeproTech, USA), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Medium was refreshed every 48 h, and cells were harvested at approximately 70–80% confluency for subsequent experiments.

Rat SCI model

Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (200–250 g) were obtained from Zhejiang Ruyiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 60%±10% relative humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to water and a standard rodent diet. After a one-week acclimation period, Rats were randomly allocated to the experimental groups using a random number table. Allocation concealment was achieved by sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes that were opened only immediately before the surgical procedure. The investigator performing the surgeries was aware of group allocation due to the nature of the interventions (local injection vs intraperitoneal injection), but behavioral assessments and histological analyses were conducted by investigators blinded to group allocation (see Sections 2.11 and 2.12). All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council) and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Ningbo University (Approval No. 14612).

Spinal cord injury (SCI) was induced using a standardized moderate contusion model at the T9 spinal level. Under 4% isoflurane anesthesia for induction and 1.5–2.5% for maintenance (delivered via a nose cone), a dorsal laminectomy was performed to expose the spinal cord. A 10 g metal rod was dropped from a height of 25 mm using a custom-made weight-drop device to create a reproducible contusion injury. For sham-operated animals, laminectomy was performed without contusion. Immediately after injury, 50 μL of the designated hydrogel formulation was injected locally at the lesion site using a microsyringe. In the Ropi-GelMA/HA group, the hydrogel contained Ropivacaine and was photocrosslinked in situ by exposing the site to an LED light source (405 nm, 3 W) for 10 s. Blank GelMA/HA (without Ropivacaine) and free Ropivacaine (dissolved in PBS) were administered to the corresponding control groups. The surgical incision was closed in layers with absorbable sutures. Rats were divided into the following five groups (n = 6 per group): Sham group: laminectomy without SCI; SCI group: contusion injury with daily PBS injection; Free-Ropi group: SCI + daily intraperitoneal injection of free Ropivacaine (0.5 mg/kg); GelMA/HA group: SCI + local injection of blank GelMA/HA hydrogel with photocrosslinking; Ropi-GelMA/HA group: SCI + local injection of Ropivacaine-loaded GelMA/HA hydrogel with photocrosslinking. Manual bladder expression was performed twice daily until spontaneous urination resumed. To avoid interference with pain-related behavioral assessments, no systemic analgesics were administered postoperatively.

The dose of free ropivacaine for intraperitoneal injection (0.5 mg/kg/day) was chosen based on previous reports demonstrating its analgesic efficacy in rodent neuropathic pain models without causing significant motor blockade or systemic toxicity [6]. For the Ropi-GelMA/HA group, the hydrogel formulation contained 5 mg ropivacaine in 50 μL, which was determined from in vitro release optimization (see Fig. 2A) and pilot in vivo tolerability studies as the maximum local dose that achieved sustained perilesional drug levels without inducing local tissue irritation or systemic adverse effects. This local dose is substantially higher than the daily systemic dose of free ropivacaine, reflecting the distinct pharmacokinetic goals: rapid systemic clearance for the intraperitoneal route versus sustained local retention for the hydrogel-based delivery.

Preparation of GelMA and HA solutions

Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA; EFL-GM-60, degree of methacryloyl substitution ~60%, Suzhou Yongqin Quan Intelligent Equipment Co., Ltd., China) and the photoinitiator lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP; EFL-PI-LAP, Suzhou Yongqin Quan) were used. To prepare the GelMA stock solution, 10% (w/v) GelMA was dissolved in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing 0.25% (w/v) LAP. The mixture was dissolved in a 60 °C water bath for 30 min under light-protected conditions, with intermittent vortexing to ensure complete dissolution. The solution was then sterilized by filtration through a 0.22μm syringe filter. Hyaluronic acid (HA, product No. 12201590, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was separately dissolved in sterile PBS at a concentration of 5% (w/v) following the same heating and filtration procedure. For hydrogel preparation, GelMA and HA solutions were mixed at a 1:1 volume ratio, yielding a final formulation of 5% GelMA / 2.5% HA / 0.125% LAP. Ropivacaine was incorporated into the mixture to achieve the target drug loading used for in vivo experiments (5 mg per 50 μL hydrogel).

In situ photocrosslinking was performed immediately after local injection of the pre-gel solution into the spinal cord lesion site. A 405 nm LED light source (3 W, Shenzhen Luyi Technology Co., Ltd., China) was positioned at a fixed distance of 3 cm from the spinal cord surface, delivering an irradiance of approximately 25 mW/cm² (measured using a PM100D optical power meter with S121C sensor, Thorlabs). The exposure time was 10 s, corresponding to an approximate energy density of 0.25 J/cm². This condition was chosen based on preliminary optimization (gelation within 5 s at >0.15 J/cm²) and to minimize potential phototoxicity to the exposed spinal cord tissue. For in vitro experiments, the same pre-gel solution was transferred to moulds and crosslinked under identical illumination parameters to obtain hydrogel discs (diameter 8 mm, thickness 2 mm) for release and cell studies.

SEM analysis of GelMA/HA hydrogel

The lyophilized GelMA/HA hydrogel samples were fractured in liquid nitrogen to obtain clean cross-sectional surfaces. The samples were then coated with a conductive layer using gold sputtering to enhance conductivity for scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Morphological features of the hydrogel structure were subsequently observed using a field emission SEM (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). It should be noted that the porous morphology observed in lyophilized samples may be influenced by ice crystal formation during freezing and does not necessarily represent the native hydrated pore architecture of the hydrogel.

In vitro drug release assay

Ropi-GelMA/HA hydrogel samples were placed into dialysis bags with a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 100 kDa and immersed in 150 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) in a dissolution beaker at 37 °C. At predetermined time points (20, 40, 60, and 80 h), 1 mL of release medium was collected and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain sink conditions. The cumulative release of Ropivacaine was quantified using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Techcomp, Beijing, China) at a wavelength of 210 nm (the maximum absorption peak of ropivacaine in PBS).

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates, with 3–6 replicate wells per group and cultured for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ to allow cell attachment. Sterilized hydrogel samples were then added to the wells, ensuring direct contact with the cell monolayer. After co-incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1–4 h. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm to evaluate cell viability.

In vitro analgesic and neurotrophic effects

Rat neural stem cells (NSCs) were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL and allowed to adhere overnight. The medium was then replaced with serum-free DMEM, and cells were cultured for an additional 24 h.

For Ropi-GelMA/HA treatment, the pre-gel solution (containing ropivacaine, GelMA, HA, and LAP as described in Section 2.3) was cast into sterile silicone molds (diameter 8 mm, thickness 2 mm) and crosslinked using a 405 nm LED light source (3 W, 3 cm distance, measured irradiance ~25 mW/cm²) for 10 s to form solid hydrogel discs. The discs were then washed three times with sterile PBS to remove any unreacted components and placed directly onto the NSC monolayer (one disc per well). For free Ropivacaine treatment, an equivalent amount of ropivacaine (calculated based on the drug loading in the hydrogel disc) was dissolved in culture medium and added to the wells. Cells were then incubated for 48 h.

Expression levels of pain- and neural repair-related genes and proteins (e.g., Nav1.3, TNF-α, NGF, BDNF) were analyzed using quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) and Western blotting.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from neural stem cells (NSCs) using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA), and integrity was checked by agarose gel electrophoresis. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan) with the following conditions: 37 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 5 s.

qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, USA). β-actin was used as the internal reference gene. The reaction conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s. Each sample was run in triplicate. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Primer sequences: Nav1.3: Forward: 5’-CTCAGAACAGGAAGCGGAGG-3’, Reverse: 5’-GAATTGCCTGATGGAGAGCCT-3’; TNF-α: Forward: 5’-ATGGGCTCCCTCTCATCAGT-3’, Reverse: 5’-GCTTGGTGGTTTGCTACGAC-3’; NGF: Forward: 5’-CGCATCGCTCTCCTTCACAGA-3’, Reverse: 5’-TACGCCGATCAAAAACGCTG-3’; BDNF: Forward: 5’-GTCGCACGGTCCCCATTG-3’, Reverse: 5’-ACCTGGTGGAACTCAGGGT-3’; β-actin: Forward: 5’-ACGGTCAGGTCATCACTATCG-3’, Reverse: 5’-GGCATAGAGGTCTTTACGGATG-3’.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

Ropivacaine concentration in the culture medium was quantified by HPLC using an Agilent 1260 system (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm, Waters, USA). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile: water (25:75, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid, with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and the detection wavelength was set at 210 nm. The injection volume was 20 μL. Standard curves were prepared using known concentrations of Ropivacaine, and sample concentrations were calculated based on the peak area.

Western blot analysis

NSCs were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Beyotime, China). Total proteins were extracted from spinal cord tissues using RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against Nav1.3 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), TNF-α (1:1000, Proteintech, China), NGF (1:1000, Proteintech, China), BDNF (1:1000, Proteintech, China), and β-actin (1:1000, HuaBio, China) overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized and quantified using a chemiluminescent imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software.

In vivo safety and biocompatibility evaluation

Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were anesthetized using 4% isoflurane for induction and maintained at 1.5–2.5% isoflurane via a nose cone throughout the procedure. Body temperature, respiratory rate, and reflexes were monitored to maintain an appropriate anesthetic depth. A dorsal midline incision was made to expose the spinal cord. GelMA/HA hydrogel (50 μL) was injected directly at the exposed spinal site, followed by surgical closure. In the control group, rats received daily intraperitoneal injections of an equal volume of PBS. Postoperatively, rats received subcutaneous meloxicam (2 mg/kg) once daily for 3 days for analgesia. On postoperative day 15, all animals were euthanized via CO₂ inhalation. Blood and organ samples were collected and stored at –80°C. Serum biochemical parameters, including hepatic enzyme activity and creatine kinase (CK) levels, were measured using commercial assay kits (Beyotime, China). Liver and kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological evaluation.

Histopathological examination

The liver and kidneys of each rat were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and processed for paraffin embedding. Tissue sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and subjected to immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking non-specific binding with BSA, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody Iba1 (1:500; catalog number ET1705-78, Huabio). Following PBS washes, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibody iFluorTM 488 (1:500; catalog number HA1121, Huabio) at room temperature for 1 h under light-protected conditions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the sections were finally mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium. Observation and image acquisition were performed using a fluorescence microscope. Negative controls (with PBS substituted for the primary antibody) were included for all sections, and all images were captured under identical acquisition parameters to ensure comparability of results. Histological evaluations of structural integrity, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibrotic changes in both liver and kidney tissues were performed under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan). For quantitative analysis of Iba1 immunostaining, five random high-power fields (HPF, 400×) per section and three sections per animal were captured. The percentage of Iba1-positive cells per HPF was counted by two independent observers blinded to group allocation, and the average was calculated for each animal.

Von Frey test

Prior to modeling, rats were pre-screened using Von Frey filaments to determine baseline paw withdrawal thresholds, and animals with comparable responses were selected for spinal cord injury (SCI) modeling. Mechanical allodynia testing was performed on days 1, 3, 14, and 28 post-treatment. All Von Frey assessments were conducted by an investigator who was blinded to group allocation and had not participated in the surgical procedures or drug administration. A filament was applied perpendicularly to the plantar surface of the rat’s hind paw until it bent into a “C” or “S” shape and held for 6–8 s. A positive response was recorded if the rat withdrew or licked the paw (“X”), and no response was marked as (“O”). If no response occurred, a filament with greater force was used; if a response occurred, a lower-force filament was applied. Each filament was tested five times at 15-second intervals, and the paw withdrawal threshold was determined.

BBB locomotor scoring

Hind limb motor function was evaluated using the Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) rating scale (0–21) at the following time points post-injury: days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28. On each testing day, rats were placed in an open field for 4 min following a 2-min habituation period. Two independent observers, blinded to group allocation, simultaneously scored the animals. The final score for each rat at each time point was the average of the two observers’ scores. Inter-observer reliability was assessed by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC > 0.90 for all sessions). Statistical analysis of longitudinal BBB data was performed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (group × time), followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests for inter-group comparisons at each time point.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1) and ImageJ software. For repeated measurements over time, including BBB scores, Von Frey thresholds, and body weight, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (group × time) was used, followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests for intergroup comparisons at each time point. For single-timepoint measurements, including qPCR, Western blot quantifications, and serum biochemical parameters, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01. All statistical analyses were performed using coded datasets, with group identities revealed only after the final analysis. The statistician was blinded to group allocation.

Ethical approval

All the procedures in this experiment complied with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for using and caring for laboratory animals and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo University (Approval No. 14612).

Results

Photocuring and microstructure of Ropi-GelMA/HA hydrogel

A mixture of 10% (w/v) gelatin methacryloyl and 5% (w/v) hyaluronic acid (HA) solutions in a 1:1 volume ratio remained in a clear liquid state at room temperature. Upon exposure to an LED light source (405 nm, 3 W) for 10 s, the solution underwent rapid photocrosslinking and subsequently transitioned into a solid hydrogel (Fig. 1A, B). To verify the formation of a stable crosslinked hydrogel network, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed on lyophilized Ropi-GelMA/HA samples. The resulting images revealed a porous microstructure in the lyophilized state, which is commonly observed in freeze-dried hydrogel scaffolds and reflects the distribution of ice crystals during the freezing process (Fig. 1C, D). These findings confirm the successful synthesis of a photosensitive Ropi-GelMA/HA hydrogel with rapid in situ gelation properties suitable for localized drug delivery applications.

In vitro release profile and degradation of Ropi-GelMA/HA

To optimize the controlled-release characteristic of the hydrogel, different volume ratios of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) to hyaluronic acid (HA) were tested in the photo-crosslinked Ropi-GelMA/HA formulation. GelMA and HA were mixed at ratios of 1:2, 1:1, and 2:1 (v/v), each loaded with equal amounts of Ropivacaine (Ropi), and subjected to in vitro release assays in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 37 °C. All formulations exhibited sustained release of Ropi, though at varying rates. After 80 h, the cumulative release reached approximately 80%, 60%, and 20% in the 1:2, 1:1, and 2:1 groups, respectively (Fig. 2A). Notably, the 1:2 formulation exhibited a rapid initial burst release within the first 20 h, whereas the 2:1 formulation showed a considerably slower release rate. Swelling analysis revealed swelling ratios of 15.7%, 12.0%, and 11.1% for the 1:2, 1:1, and 2:1 groups, respectively (Fig. 2B). All hydrogels exhibited gradual degradation in PBS over 14 days, with mass loss reaching 92.7%, 90.7%, and 78.7%, respectively, by Day 14 (Fig. 2C). Considering that the formulation exhibited a more balanced early-phase release profile and that the GelMA/HA composite should not degrade too rapidly or too slowly during treatment, the GelMA: HA ratio of 1:1 (v/v) was identified as the optimal formulation for subsequent in vivo and in vitro studies.

In vitro analgesic and neurotrophic-supporting effects of Ropi-GelMA/HA

The cytocompatibility of GelMA/HA hydrogels was first evaluated using a CCK-8 assay. The phenol-treated group (positive cytotoxic control) exhibited a marked reduction in cell viability, confirming the sensitivity of the assay. The results showed no significant difference in neural cell viability between the GelMA/HA-treated and control groups (Fig. 3A), indicating good biocompatibility of the hydrogel matrix. To further assess the analgesic and neurotrophic-modulatory potential of Ropivacaine (Ropi), we examined the expression levels of key pain- and nerve repair–related markers, including the voltage-gated sodium channel Nav1.3, pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α, and the neurotrophic factors NGF and BDNF. qPCR analysis revealed that Ropi treatment significantly downregulated the mRNA levels of Nav1.3 and TNF-α (Fig. 3B, C), while simultaneously upregulating NGF and BDNF expression (Fig. 3D, E). These effects were dose-dependent in the NSC assay, with higher concentrations of Ropi inducing more pronounced changes. Western blot analysis confirmed these trends at the protein level (Fig. 3F–J), suggesting that Ropi modulates both transcriptional and translational regulation of these targets. To evaluate the time-dependent therapeutic effects of Ropi-GelMA/HA, we compared the expression patterns of these markers in neural cells treated with free Ropi versus Ropi-GelMA/HA over 12, 24, and 72 h. While free Ropi transiently reduced Nav1.3 and TNF-α expression and increased NGF and BDNF levels at 12 h, these effects diminished significantly by 24 and 72 h (Fig. 3K–O).

In contrast, the Ropi-GelMA/HA group maintained a sustained downregulation of pain- and inflammation-related markers and continuous upregulation of neurotrophic factors over the entire time course. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of the culture medium revealed a rapid depletion of Ropi in the free-drug group, whereas the Ropi-Gel/HA group produced a gradual and sustained increase in extracellular Ropi concentration over time (Fig. 3P). These findings are consistent with the sustained protein expression patterns, supporting the conclusion that the hydrogel-mediated delivery system prolongs the biological effects of locally administered Ropivacaine compared with the free-drug condition.

Biocompatibility and systemic safety evaluation of GelMA/HA hydrogel

Prior to assessing in vivo therapeutic efficacy, the safety and biocompatibility of the GelMA/HA hydrogel were systematically evaluated. Healthy rats received local injections of GelMA/HA into the spinal region, while control animals underwent identical surgical exposure without hydrogel administration. Body weight showed an initial postoperative decrease followed by gradual recovery, with no significant difference between the GelMA/HA and sham groups (Fig. 4A), suggesting no observable systemic toxicity. To further assess potential adverse effects, serum biochemical parameters were measured. The levels of creatine kinase (CK), as well as liver function markers—including aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), and γ-glutamyltransferase (γ-GT)—showed no significant differences between groups (Fig. 4B–E), indicating the absence of muscle injury or hepatotoxicity. Histological analysis of liver and kidney tissues using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed no signs of structural damage, necrosis, or inflammatory cell infiltration in the GelMA/HA-treated group (Fig. 4F, G). Iba1 immunofluorescence showed no significant increase in Iba1‑positive cells around the injection site compared with the sham group (Fig. 4H). These findings collectively support the excellent biocompatibility of the hydrogel. In summary, the GelMA/HA hydrogel demonstrated no systemic or local toxicity in healthy rats, establishing a strong safety profile for its use in subsequent in vivo therapeutic evaluations.

Assessment of in vivo analgesic effects and neurological recovery

Spinal cord injury (SCI) frequently leads to neuropathic pain, which is promoted by increased neuronal excitability, facilitating pain signal transmission. This process is mediated through the activation of glial cells, inducing an inflammation cascade. To evaluate the analgesic and reparative effects of Ropi-GelMA/HA following SCI, we established a rat model of moderate contusion. Mechanical hypersensitivity in the rats was assessed using von Frey thresholds. Following surgery, Ropi-GelMA/HA (50 μL containing 5 mg ropivacaine) was locally administered at the injury site to achieve localized hydrogel-mediated delivery of ropivacaine. A control group (Free-Ropi) received daily intraperitoneal injections of free Ropivacaine (0.5 mg/kg/day; Fig. 5A). Mechanical allodynia tests were conducted on days 1, 3, 14, and 28 to assess pain thresholds. Both Ropi-GelMA/HA and Free-Ropi groups exhibited significantly elevated pain thresholds compared to the surgery-only group, indicating significant attenuation of mechanical hypersensitivity (Fig. 5B). Notably, the Ropi-GelMA/HA group demonstrated a more durable analgesic effect over the observation period, indicating that, under the experimental conditions employed, the hydrogel-mediated local delivery regimen provided a more prolonged therapeutic benefit than daily systemic administration of free ropivacaine. It should be noted that the two treatment groups differed in route and frequency of administration; therefore, the observed differences in efficacy reflect the combined influence of formulation, local retention, and dosing regimen, rather than sustained release alone. Longitudinal BBB assessment revealed progressive recovery of hind limb motor function in all SCI groups over the 28-day observation period (Fig. 5C). Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA showed a significant group × time interaction (p < 0.01). Ropi-GelMA/HA treatment improved BBB locomotor scores during the later recovery phase, with significant improvement at the endpoint as shown in Fig. 5C, indicating superior and sustained locomotor recovery under the tested regimen. Representative H&E staining of spinal cord sections suggested better tissue preservation and a smaller lesion cavity in the Ropi-GelMA/HA group (Fig. 5D), suggesting that hydrogel-based localized controlled delivery promotes structural sparing. To investigate the underlying mechanisms of Ropi-GelMA/HA’s analgesic and functional recovery-associated effects, we examined the expression of pain-related, inflammatory, and regenerative proteins in spinal cord tissue. Western blot analysis showed that SCI induced significant upregulation of Nav1.3 (a voltage-gated sodium channel) and TNF-α (a pro-inflammatory cytokine). At the endpoint, Ropi-GelMA/HA reduced Nav1.3 and TNF-α expression and increased NGF and BDNF expression compared with the SCI and Free-Ropi groups (Fig. 5E, F). In addition, no histopathological damage was observed in the liver or kidney tissues upon H&E staining (Fig. 5G, H); Similarly, Iba1 immunofluorescence did not reveal obvious local inflammatory cell accumulation in the examined tissue sections (Fig. 5I), indicating good biocompatibility. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Ropi-GelMA/HA provides prolonged modulation of pain and inflammatory pathways, while concurrently enhancing the expression of neurotrophic factors (NGF, BDNF). This dual mechanism likely contributes to durable analgesic and functional recovery benefits after SCI, offering a promising therapeutic approach for the long-term management of SCI-induced neuropathic pain.

Discussion

This study presents a novel ropivacaine-loaded GelMA/HA hydrogel (Ropi-GelMA/HA) designed to address neuropathic pain after spinal cord injury (SCI). By leveraging photosensitive crosslinking, the hydrogel enables localized retention and controlled early-phase delivery of ropivacaine at the lesion site, thereby improving local drug availability while reducing the need for repeated administration. Within the limits of the present dosing and administration protocols, this platform produced more durable analgesic and functional benefits than daily intraperitoneal free ropivacaine. These findings support the hypothesis that early local drug control may prolong therapeutic effects after SCI.

The Ropi-GelMA/HA hydrogel with a GelMA: HA ratio of 1:1 (v/v) exhibited a controlled in vitro release profile over the measured 80-h period, together with gradual hydrogel degradation over 14 days. In vivo, the hydrogel significantly alleviated neuropathic pain in a rat model of spinal cord contusion, as evidenced by sustained inhibition of the pain-related sodium channel Nav1.3 and the inflammatory cytokine TNF-α. In parallel, Ropi-GelMA/HA enhanced the expression of neurotrophic factors NGF and BDNF, thereby supporting a pro-reparative microenvironment and facilitating recovery of motor function. These findings support that the hydrogel provides prolonged analgesia while supporting neural tissue preservation. Thus, although the hydrogel’s drug release was directly measured only over 80 h, the early local suppression of Nav1.3 and TNF-α during the acute phase may attenuate central sensitization and establish a pro-reparative milieu that supports long-term functional recovery beyond the period of active drug release.

Safety and biocompatibility were further confirmed through both in vitro and in vivo analyses. CCK-8 assays showed no adverse effects on neuronal cell viability, while biochemical analysis and histopathological examination of liver and kidney tissues revealed no signs of hepatorenal toxicity in treated animals. Rats receiving Ropi-GelMA/HA showed normal weight and exhibited standard serum biochemical profiles, comparable to those of untreated controls. These results highlight the hydrogel’s favorable safety profile and support its translational potential for clinical application in neuropathic pain management. Regarding the photocrosslinking procedure, the selected parameters (405 nm LED, 0.25 J/cm²) were optimized to achieve rapid gelation while minimizing potential phototoxicity to the exposed spinal cord. No acute tissue damage or exacerbated inflammation was observed in the Blank Gel group compared with the SCI group (Figs. 4H, 5H), suggesting that the illumination conditions were well tolerated. Nevertheless, systematic evaluation of photobiological effects on neural tissue remains warranted for future translation.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that chemical modifications and photosensitive crosslinking can enhance hydrogel performance by improving drug release profiles and biocompatibility [22]. However, most existing research has focused on GelMA/HA-based systems for the delivery of antibiotics, anti-inflammatory agents, and anticancer drugs [23, 24], with limited attention given to local anesthetics, particularly Ropivacaine. To the best of our knowledge, reports on photo-crosslinked GelMA/HA hydrogels for localized ropivacaine delivery in SCI remain limited. We demonstrated that a GelMA: HA ratio of 1:1 (v/v) provided a balanced formulation with favorable release behavior and was therefore selected for subsequent efficacy studies. These results highlight the translational potential of this system and expand the application of GelMA/HA hydrogels into a new therapeutic domain.

Several recent studies have also utilized GelMA-based hydrogels for spinal cord injury repair. For example, Jing et al. developed a GelMA scaffold loaded with Ferrostatin-1 that promoted recovery by inhibiting apoptosis and ferroptosis [25]. Compared with such pro-regenerative scaffolds, our Ropi-GelMA/HA system prioritizes the management of neuropathic pain—a major clinical burden in SCI patients—while concurrently upregulating neurotrophic factors and downregulating inflammatory mediators. Thus, rather than claiming robust structural regeneration, we propose that the primary benefit of Ropi-GelMA/HA lies in sustained analgesia combined with the establishment of a reparative microenvironment. This strategy may complement existing pro-regenerative scaffolds or cell therapies, potentially serving as an adjunct in multimodal treatment protocols for SCI.

While this study demonstrated encouraging therapeutic outcomes, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were performed in a rat model of SCI, which may not fully replicate the complexity of human spinal cord pathology. Future studies involving large animal models or clinical samples will be essential to validate the translational relevance of our findings.

Second, although no short-term toxicity was observed, the long-term biocompatibility and potential immunological or metabolic side effects of Ropi-GelMA/HA require further investigation through chronic exposure and long-term follow-up studies. Third, the precise molecular mechanisms by which Ropi-GelMA/HA modulates repair-associated signaling remain to be elucidated. In particular, the downstream signaling pathways involved in NGF and BDNF regulation, as well as potential interactions with inflammatory and glial responses, warrant detailed mechanistic exploration. Fourth, and most importantly, the comparison between free ropivacaine and Ropi-GelMA/HA involved not only different formulations but also fundamentally different administration routes (intraperitoneal vs local injection), dosing frequencies (daily vs single), and local drug exposure profiles. Consequently, the superior efficacy observed with Ropi-GelMA/HA cannot be attributed solely to the sustained-release property of the hydrogel carrier. Differences in local drug exposure, tissue retention, and route-specific pharmacokinetics may also have contributed to the improved outcomes. Future studies should include a control group receiving an equivalent single local bolus of free ropivacaine (without hydrogel) to isolate the contribution of sustained release from other pharmacokinetic variables. An additional limitation of this study is that the in vitro release behavior of Ropivacaine was only characterized over an 80-h period. Therefore, although the hydrogel clearly improved long-term behavioral and molecular outcomes in vivo, the present data do not allow us to conclude that drug release persisted throughout the full 28-day observation period. Future studies incorporating longer-term release kinetics under physiologically relevant conditions will be necessary to better define the relationship between local release duration and prolonged therapeutic efficacy. Finally, the porous microstructure shown by SEM was observed in lyophilized samples. As the reviewer pointed out, freeze-drying can introduce artifacts due to ice crystal formation, and the observed pores may not fully reflect the hydrated pore structure of the GelMA/HA hydrogel. Future studies using cryo-SEM or confocal imaging of hydrated samples would be needed to characterize the true three-dimensional architecture of the hydrogel in its physiological state. Addressing these limitations will strengthen the clinical potential of this platform and guide its rational optimization.
