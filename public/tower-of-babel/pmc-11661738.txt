Comment on: “Navigating a Path Toward Routine Recording in the Operating Room”: How Should We Approach Patient Consent for Surgical Video?
Authors: August A. Culbert, Alejandro Bribriesco, Michael S. O’Connor, Eric Kodish
Published: 2024

In the article “Navigating a Path Toward Routine Recording in the Operating Room.” Langerman et al1 discuss the implications of surgical video recording through 3 primary lenses: ownership/access, liability, and privacy.1 After weighing each of these concerns, they offer a variety of pathways forward. While important discussion is centered on topics such as liability and prohibiting punitive uses of surgical video data by hospitals or medical boards, their paper misses a critical and, perhaps, even more important topic: patient consent.

Surgical video is not a novel concept. Indeed, the earliest known motion picture of surgery and anesthesia was recorded in 1899 in Argentina, where a patient underwent anesthesia using chloroform for a lung hydatid cyst extirpation.2 Since then, as the technology for recording has expanded, the prevalence and utility of surgical video have grown at an exponential rate. Currently, surgical video is utilized for 1 of 3 primary aims: (1) patient care and quality improvement, (2) medical training, and (3) documentation.3 As surgical video recording becomes more widespread, several authors have commented on the potential benefits and harms of recording for both patients and members of the surgical team.3–5

In this letter, we analyze Langerman et al.’s1 recent article titled “Navigating a Path Toward Routine Recording in the Operating Room” through the lens of patient consent and discuss this work in context with our current bioethical understanding of surgical video ethics.

In the Ownership and Access section, Langerman et al1 discuss arguments for and against including surgical video as part of a patient’s medical record. Should patients have access to their videos? Do patients “own” the videos taken of their bodies while under anesthesia? One argument made against recording in the operating room is that “the culture and logistics of the operating room were not designed for [patient] observation.” A first-person view of their operation might, therefore, create unnecessary anxiety and undermine trust in the surgeon-patient relationship.

Certainly, Langerman et al1 assume that patient consent is obtained for all the subsequent ethical scenarios discussed in the article. However, it is worth pausing for a moment to discuss how surgical consent is obtained by health care institutions before discussing the institutional-level policies and liability associated with surgical video recording.

Consent for a surgical operation should be a multilayered process between the physician and patient in which the risks and benefits of treatment are outlined and discussed in detail. The patient should have ample opportunity to ask questions, understand the procedure, and, importantly, withdraw consent at any time. Often, patient consent is obtained for the primary operation “and other indicated procedures” which may include transfusion of blood products or conversion of a laparoscopic case to an open procedure, for example.

The consent process for the surgical operation is robust: it is a practice that has been refined by institutions across the globe for decades.6,7 However, patients rarely, if ever, consent to other concomitant critical elements of an operation with the same level of specificity and thoroughness. At most institutions, once a patient is admitted or registers for a procedure, they sign a blanket consent form, which may include a number of specific items to which they consent. Some of these items include permitting trainee involvement in medical/surgical care or consent to utilize their information in quality improvement initiatives.

If a patient is undergoing a procedure that is to be recorded, when, precisely, will they consent to a recording? One option is for consent to take place in the initial blanket consent form. While patients may opt out, they are presumed to consent unless they specify otherwise. Another option is to ask patients individually using a tailored and revised stem question if they will consent to having their operation recorded for medical research, education, or other quality improvement purposes.

In addition to a robust process for patient consent, patient’s individual expectations of health care delivery are an important factor in determining patient satisfaction and trust in modern hospitals.8–10 For example, many important elements of modern hospital medicine are now expected as standard of care by patients, such as expecting residents and medical students to be involved in their care. Most patients are not disturbed when they are introduced as part of the care team. Imagine, however, if a patient had never encountered a doctor-in-training and discovered after the fact that a junior doctor with less than 1 year of experience had performed any aspect of their surgical operation, even under direct supervision. This scenario has the potential to arouse significant anger and distrust for patients.

How would patients feel if they found out later that surgical video of their operation was used to train future doctors? What if the video was shared at a national conference, albeit anonymized? What if it was posted on social media by the surgeon? Clearly, although there are clear potential benefits to the implementation of surgical video, there are a multitude of opportunities for events to adversely impact patient care and the patient-physician relationship.

Therefore, we suggest that greater attention should focus on the nuances of patient consent for surgical video. If patients feel their best interests are not being respected, or that recording/dissemination of such recording is not happening in line with their consent, the potential benefits of surgical video will be eventually negated and potentially constricted in a legal sense. It is imperative that we consider both patient consent and patient perspectives in this conversation before arriving at legal or institutional policy-level discussions.
