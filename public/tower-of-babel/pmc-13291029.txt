Indirect Treatment Comparisons of Lisocabtagene Maraleucel Versus Axicabtagene Ciloleucel and Tisagenlecleucel in Third-Line or Later Relapsed or Refractory Follicular Lymphoma
Authors: Alexander P Boardman, Juan Luis Reguera Ortega, Pearl Wang, Merav Bar, Jinender Kumar, Thalia Farazi, Alejandro Martín García-Sancho, Koji Izutsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNo head-to-head clinical trials have compared CD19-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies for treatment of follicular lymphoma (FL). We conducted matching-adjusted indirect comparisons (MAICs) between lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel), axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel), and tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel), to establish their relative efficacy and safety in patients with third-line or later (3L+) relapsed or refractory (R/R) FL.MethodsSources included individual patient data from TRANSCEND FL (NCT04245839) for liso-cel, summary-level data from ZUMA-5 (NCT03105336) for axi-cel, and summary-level data from ELARA (NCT03568461) for tisa-cel. For each MAIC, TRANSCEND FL patients who met inclusion/exclusion criteria for the comparator study were included. MAICs adjusted for clinically relevant prognostic factors identified a priori to minimize population differences between studies.ResultsAfter adjustment, liso-cel had a higher complete response (CR) rate than axi-cel (response ratio [RR] 1.24, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.07‒1.43) and tisa-cel (RR 1.36, 95% CI 1.11‒1.68) and a higher overall response rate (ORR) than tisa-cel (RR 1.12, 95% CI 1.01‒1.24). Estimates numerically favored liso-cel for progression-free survival, duration of response, overall survival, and time to next treatment, though were not statistically significant. Liso-cel demonstrated better safety than axi-cel, with significantly reduced odds ratios (ORs) of grade ≥ 3 cytokine release syndrome (CRS; OR 0.07, 95% CI 0.01‒0.58), tocilizumab use for CRS (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.09‒0.44), any-grade neurological events (NEs; OR 0.14, 95% CI 0.05‒0.38), and infections (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.19‒0.93). Liso-cel and tisa-cel had similar safety profiles overall, excluding any-grade NEs, which was significantly lower for liso-cel (OR 0.19, 95% CI 0.08‒0.45).ConclusionAfter adjustment for population differences, liso-cel demonstrated a higher CR rate and numerically favorable trends of survival compared to axi-cel and tisa-cel with improved safety compared to axi-cel, and similar safety compared to tisa-cel. These findings underscore liso-cel as a potentially preferred CAR T cell therapy for 3L+ R/R FL. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?There are no clinal trials directly comparing chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies for the treatment of patients with relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma (FL).Two approved CD19-directed CAR T cell therapies, axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel) and tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel), have previously been indirectly compared with each other, but they have not been indirectly compared to lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel), another approved CD19-directed CAR T cell therapy.This study employed matching-adjusted indirect comparisons to characterize differences in efficacy and safety between liso-cel and axi-cel and tisa-cel in patients with relapsed or refractory FL after at least two prior lines of therapy.What was learned from the study?After adjustment for baseline population differences, liso-cel demonstrated a higher complete response rate compared with axi-cel and tisa-cel and a higher overall response rate compared with tisa-cel. Introduction

Follicular lymphoma (FL) is the most commonly diagnosed indolent non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) [1], accounting for approximately 35% of all NHL cases and 70% of indolent lymphoma diagnoses in the USA and Western Europe [1–3]. Approximately 90% of patients present with advanced disease at diagnosis [1, 4]. FL remains incurable, and despite initial response to first-line (1L) and second-line (2L) treatments [1, 5, 6], disease progression is common with response duration and survival shortening after each relapse. Patients who have progression of disease ≤ 24 months (POD24) of 1L treatment have substantially worse outcomes than patients without early progression, with 5-year overall survival (OS) of approximately 50% [7].

Among patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) FL after at least two lines of therapy (LOTs), there is no established standard of care and prognosis is poor [8–11]. Treatment regimens and sequencing for third-line (3L) or later (3L+) therapy remain highly heterogenous. Second-line treatment options that have not been previously utilized could be considered [6, 12, 13]; these include conventional chemoimmunotherapy regimens, along with combination therapies such as lenalidomide with rituximab. More recently, approved treatment options for use in the 3L+ setting include chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies, CD20 × CD3 bispecific T cell-engaging monoclonal antibodies [14, 15], the Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor zanubrutinib with obinutuzumab [16], or the EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat [17]. Lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel) is an autologous, CD19-directed, 4-1BB CAR T cell product that has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency for the treatment of patients with 3L+ R/R FL; it is also approved in Japan for 2L high-risk and 3L+ patients with R/R FL [18–20]. The efficacy and safety of liso-cel were evaluated in the phase 2 TRANSCEND FL study (NCT04245839), a single-arm, open-label study in adults with R/R FL, achieving an overall response rate (ORR) of 97% and complete response (CR) rate in 94% of 3L+ patients with a manageable safety profile [21]. Axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel) and tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel) are also anti-CD19 CAR T cell therapies that have been approved for the treatment of 3L+ R/R FL in the USA [22, 23]; in the European Union, tisa-cel is approved in 3L+ R/R FL and axi-cel is approved in fourth-line or later (4L+) R/R FL [24, 25]. The ORR in the phase 2 study for axi-cel (ZUMA-5 [NCT03105336]) was 94%, with a CR rate of 79% [26]; corresponding results in the phase 2 tisa-cel study (ELARA [NCT03568461]) were numerically lower, with an ORR and CR rate of 86% and 69%, respectively [27, 28].

There have been no prior direct or indirect treatment comparisons between liso-cel and other anti-CD19 CAR T cell therapies for the treatment of patients with R/R FL after at least two prior LOTs, although axi-cel and tisa-cel have previously been compared indirectly [29]. To address this gap in evidence, we performed separate matching-adjusted indirect comparisons (MAICs) between liso-cel and axi-cel and between liso-cel and tisa-cel.

TRANSCEND FL was a global, phase 2, single-arm, multicohort, multicenter study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of liso-cel in adult patients with R/R FL or marginal zone lymphoma. Patients with FL enrolled in the study must have received at least one prior line of systemic therapy. Prespecified cohorts included 4L+ R/R FL, 3L R/R FL, and 2L R/R FL. The 4L+ R/R FL cohort was combined with the 3L R/R FL cohort to infer efficacy and safety of liso-cel in the 3L+ population. The primary endpoint was ORR as assessed by independent review committee (IRC) per Lugano criteria [30]; CR rate (secondary endpoint) was IRC-assessed per Lugano criteria [30]. A post hoc analysis was performed on time to next treatment (TTNT).

ZUMA-5 and ELARA were global, phase 2, multicenter, single-arm studies [26, 27]. The primary endpoint for ZUMA-5 was IRC-assessed ORR per Lugano criteria [30]. The primary endpoint for ELARA was IRC-assessed CR rate per Lugano criteria [30].

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

Studies used in this analysis were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Institutional review boards at participating institutions approved the study protocols and amendments, and all participating patients provided written informed consent.

Study Design

All three studies were multicenter, single-arm studies in which patients underwent leukapheresis, lymphodepleting chemotherapy, and CAR T cell therapy infusion. Eligibility criteria for the three studies were similar, with only some minor differences. TRANSCEND FL included patients with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥ 40%, while in ZUMA-5 and ELARA, LVEF thresholds were ≥ 50% and ≥ 45%, respectively. ZUMA-5 and ELARA excluded patients with secondary CNS involvement and all patients with a history of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). However, there were no patients with such characteristics in TRANSCEND FL. A higher proportion of patients in TRANSCEND FL received bridging therapy (41.1%) compared with ZUMA-5 (3.2%). The use of bridging therapy was similar between TRANSCEND FL and ELARA (45.0%) [31]. With regards to the reporting of adverse events (AEs), ZUMA-5 reported AEs at any time after infusion, but the window for treatment-emergent AEs was not clear. Though clearly defined for tisa-cel, ELARA had a shorter window than TRANSCEND FL (8 weeks vs 90 days).

Patient Characteristics

Some differences were identified between baseline patient characteristics in TRANSCEND FL compared with ZUMA-5 and ELARA, and these were aligned by redefining variables using TRANSCEND FL IPD to facilitate fair comparisons (Table S1). Of the baseline patient characteristics reported in both TRANSCEND FL and ZUMA-5, differences were noted in definitions of bulky disease status (according to Groupe d’Etude des Lymphomes Folliculaires criteria), number of prior LOTs, and refractory status to the last previous therapy [26, 32]. Of the baseline patient characteristics reported in both TRANSCEND FL and ELARA, important differences were identified in the definitions of relapse or refractory status (Table S1) [27, 32].

Definition of Outcome Measures

For comparisons with both ZUMA-5 and ELARA, six efficacy outcomes (ORR, CR rate, progression-free survival [PFS], duration of response [DOR], OS, and time to next treatment [TTNT]) were assessed. Definitions were similar for all efficacy outcomes between studies. In the initial ZUMA-5 publication, efficacy outcomes (ORR, CR rate, PFS, and DOR) were reported as assessed by an IRC, while the most recent, 3-year follow-up analysis reported efficacy outcomes per investigator assessment. Hence, for the comparison between TRANSCEND FL and ZUMA-5, both IRC- and investigator-assessed outcomes were compared. Only IRC-assessed efficacy outcomes were compared between TRANSCEND FL and ELARA, except for OS and TTNT [28].

Safety outcomes assessed included cytokine release syndrome (CRS), neurological events (NEs), any-grade and grade ≥ 3 infections, prolonged cytopenia (grade ≥ 3 cytopenias based on laboratory values at day 29), and the use of corticosteroids or tocilizumab for the management of CRS. These definitions were generally similar. However, NEs in TRANSCEND FL were defined as investigator-identified neurological AEs related to liso-cel and graded per the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0, whereas those in ZUMA-5 and ELARA were identified using search queries based on Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) high-level group terms. This limitation also applied between definitions of prolonged cytopenia in TRANSCEND FL and ZUMA-5. Prolonged cytopenia in TRANSCEND FL was defined as grade ≥ 3 cytopenias based on laboratory values at day 29, while ZUMA-5 used a KITE-specified MedDRA search query based on cytopenias present on or after day 30 [26]. These differences could not be reconciled by rederivation from TRANSCEND FL IPD. In ELARA, prolonged cytopenia was not reported as a class and therefore it was not possible to compare this AE between TRANSCEND FL and ELARA.

Rank Ordering of Prognostic Factors and Treatment-Effect Modifiers

Relevant clinical factors for matching and adjustment were identified by (1) a targeted review of clinical covariates adjusted for in recently published MAICs in 3L+ FL [33, 34], (2) evaluation of baseline characteristics reported in the TRANSCEND FL primary analysis and relevant comparator studies, and (3) input from external clinical experts.

As a result of this multistep process, 30 candidate prognostic factors and treatment-effect modifiers were identified by clinical experts and ranked according to their expected clinical relevance (i.e., prognostic strength or degree of treatment-effect modification for the outcomes of interest; Table S2). Separate ranking assessments were performed for efficacy and safety outcomes resulting in four distinct prognostic factor ranking sets. After considering data availability across studies, a final set of 15 clinical factors were selected for inclusion in the MAIC analysis.

Statistical Analysis

As no common comparator was available, unanchored MAICs were conducted to determine the relative efficacy and safety of liso-cel vs axi-cel and tisa-cel. Efficacy outcomes were compared between the treated efficacy set from TRANSCEND FL and evaluable patients who had been followed up for ≥ 18 months after infusion with axi-cel (updated analysis set; Table 1) [26]. For the comparison with longer-term axi-cel data, investigator-assessed efficacy endpoints were compared using intention-to-treat sets in TRANSCEND FL and the intention-to-treat set from the 3-year follow-up analysis of ZUMA-5 [31]. Safety endpoints were compared between all treated patients in TRANSCEND FL and all treated patients with FL from ZUMA-5. For the efficacy comparisons with tisa-cel, the treated efficacy set from TRANSCEND FL was compared with the efficacy analysis set from ELARA (Table 1) [28]. For safety endpoints, all infused patients from TRANSCEND FL and ELARA were compared [27]. In TRANSCEND FL, baseline measurements for bulky disease status were taken closest to the administration of liso-cel, after bridging therapy for patients who underwent bridging, and at screening for those who did not. The time frame for bulky disease measurements at baseline was unclear in ELARA, which reported a higher proportion of patients with bulky disease status compared with TRANSCEND FL (66% vs 21%). As a result of the unclear timing of measurement in ELARA and its significant impact on effective sample size (ESS), the primary analysis for efficacy and safety did not include bulky disease status. A sensitivity analysis was conducted, including bulky disease status as a covariate.

Patients from the TRANSCEND FL IPD were reweighted to comparator studies using the populations described above (Table 1). To perform the MAIC, separate analyses were carried out sequentially for a given set of ranked clinical prognostic factors and treatment-effect modifiers. Each analysis adjusted for one additional variable at a time, following the order of ranked importance. Differences in baseline characteristics between the study populations were assessed using the absolute value of the standardized mean difference (SMD) [35]. An SMD ≥ 0.1 was considered indicative of potentially important imbalances between study populations [35]. The performance of each MAIC model was assessed on (1) ESS after adjustment, (2) distribution of patient weights to avoid extreme patient weights, and (3) summary statistics to evaluate the balance between study populations. The primary MAIC analysis was chosen based on a balance between these criteria, while also considering the number of clinical factors included.

Generalized linear models for binary outcomes (i.e., ORR, CR rate) were used to estimate relative response ratios (RRs) of liso-cel vs axi-cel and liso-cel vs tisa-cel. For time-to-event outcomes (i.e., DOR, PFS, OS, and TTNT), Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) of liso-cel vs comparators. The validity of the proportional hazard assumption for time-to-event outcomes was evaluated using the Grambsch–Therneau test, where p < 0.05 was considered indicative of a violation of the assumption, along with visual inspection of the Kaplan–Meier curve and cumulative hazard plots.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 [36], based on code outlined in the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence Evidence Synthesis Technical Support Document 18 [37].

Results

Axi-cel Comparison

Population Adjustment

In the efficacy analysis between TRANSCEND FL and ZUMA-5, 10 factors were adjusted for, including FL International Prognostic Index (FLIPI) risk factor, bulky disease status at screening, number of prior LOTs, refractory status to last therapy, POD24, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) before lymphodepleting chemotherapy, prior autologous HSCT (auto-HSCT), lymphoma presence in the bone marrow, sex, and histological subtypes (Table 2). Other ranked factors that could not be adjusted for included previous receipt of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody and alkylating agent, which was an eligibility criterion for TRANSCEND FL, and C-reactive protein values, which were not available for the efficacy population set for ZUMA-5. Additionally, to avoid redundancies, age, disease stage, and number of nodal sites were not adjusted for, as these were already captured by the FLIPI risk factor. The sum of product diameter and size of largest nodal mass, which were captured by bulky disease status, were also not adjusted for directly. Moderate to large differences (SMDs ≥ 0.1) were observed in eight out of 10 baseline characteristics, except for bulky disease and POD24. Matching and adjusting produced substantial improvements in the balance of clinical factors, resulting in good balance between the studies with SMD < 0.1 for all 10 key baseline characteristics with an ESS of 39.3 (Table 2).

In the safety analysis, 12 factors were adjusted for; disease stage and age were included in addition to the 10 factors adjusted for in the efficacy analysis (Table S3). Despite the redundancy with the FLIPI risk factor for efficacy comparisons, disease stage and age were adjusted for in the safety analyses based on clinical expert input, because of their higher prognostic value for safety outcomes. Moderate to large differences (SMDs ≥ 0.1) were observed for eight out of 12 factors, with the exception of bulky disease status at screening, refractory status to last therapy, POD24, and sex. After adjustment, all factors were well balanced (SMDs < 0.1) with an ESS of 46.6.

Response Outcomes

The MAIC comparing liso-cel to axi-cel showed similar ORR between treatments and a higher CR rate for patients treated with liso-cel (Fig. 1). Before adjustment, 97.1% of patients who received liso-cel responded, compared with 94.2% of patients who received axi-cel (RR 1.03, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.97‒1.10). After adjustment, ORR for liso-cel increased to 98.0% (RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.97‒1.11). Before adjustment, 94.2% of patients who received liso-cel achieved CR, compared with 79.1% of patients who received axi-cel (RR 1.19, 95% CI 1.03‒1.37). After adjustment, the CR rate for patients who received liso-cel increased to 97.8% (RR 1.24, 95% CI 1.07‒1.43). Results remained consistent with investigator-assessed ORR and CR evaluated in the intention-to-treat populations of TRANSCEND FL and ZUMA-5 (Fig. S1).

Time-to-Event Outcomes

For unadjusted IRC-assessed time-to-event outcomes, similar PFS (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.45‒1.30), DOR (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.43‒1.31), and OS (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.29‒1.32) were observed, with results numerically favoring liso-cel over axi-cel (Fig. 2). Cumulative probabilities of PFS, DOR, and OS were numerically higher for liso-cel at 12, 24, and 30 months. After adjustment, the outcomes of PFS (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.35‒1.39), DOR (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.34‒1.44), and OS (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.15‒1.30) remained similar, numerically favoring liso-cel, and cumulative probabilities for all outcomes remained numerically higher for liso-cel at all time points. Results for investigator-assessed PFS, DOR, and OS in the intention‑to‑treat populations were similar to the results for IRC-assessed outcomes (Fig. S2). Discussion

In the absence of prior direct or indirect treatment comparisons, results of these two MAICs in the 3L+ R/R FL setting showed generally similar efficacy profiles with respect to IRC-assessed outcomes, excluding response rates where liso-cel demonstrated a statistically significant higher CR rate compared with axi-cel, and significantly higher ORR and CR rate compared with tisa-cel. Efficacy results from the primary analysis were mostly confirmed by the investigator-assessed results and sensitivity analysis for the respective MAICs. For safety, liso-cel demonstrated a more favorable safety profile overall compared with axi-cel and a similar safety profile compared with tisa-cel. Statistically significant findings in favor of liso-cel over axi-cel included results for grade ≥ 3 CRS, any-grade NE, any-grade infections, and corticosteroid or tocilizumab use for CRS management, with all remaining safety outcomes numerically favoring liso-cel. While physicians treating patients with FL may not observe clinically meaningful differences in NEs between liso-cel and tisa-cel, this MAIC showed that any-grade NEs were statistically significant in favor of liso-cel. The remaining safety outcomes did not show significant differences between liso-cel and tisa-cel. Collectively, these MAIC results provide insights into the efficacy and safety of liso-cel relative to other CAR T cell therapies and should be confirmed in real-world clinical settings.

This is the first MAIC to compare liso-cel therapy with other CAR T cell therapies in patients with 3L+ R/R FL. At least one other MAIC has been conducted in the 3L+ R/R FL population comparing tisa-cel vs axi-cel using IPD from ELARA for tisa-cel and summary-level data from ZUMA-5 for axi-cel. Results from that MAIC indicated highly similar outcomes between tisa-cel and axi-cel after adjusting for 10 key baseline characteristics, with no significant differences between treatments in any of the efficacy outcomes measured (ORR, CR rate, PFS, and OS) [29]. Additionally, tisa-cel was found to have a better safety profile compared with axi-cel for CRS, NE, and tocilizumab and corticosteroid use for CRS [29]. When considering the totality of data from indirect comparisons across CAR T cell therapies in patients with 3L+ R/R FL, current evidence suggests that liso-cel may be favored across available CAR T cell therapies in clinical practice, as it offers higher CR rates and similar or numerically favorable time to event outcomes compared with other CAR T cell therapies coupled with a more favorable safety profile than axi-cel and similar safety compared with tisa-cel.

A strength of the current study is that these analyses ensured evidence-based population adjustments by combining three separate approaches to the selection and ranking of relevant prognostic factors, including consideration of all those factors reported in TRANSCEND FL and comparator studies, a targeted literature review to identify other clinical factors reported in earlier MAICs in FL, and rank-ordering of all plausible identified factors by a panel of clinical experts to achieve consensus on the relative importance of each factor.

The lack of a common comparator between each of the single-arm studies included in these MAICs meant that only unanchored comparisons could be performed. Unanchored MAICs have several limitations, most notably that validity is dependent on adjustment for all clinically relevant prognostic factors, which is a difficult assumption to meet [37]. In the primary analyses, as many clinical factors as possible were included while maintaining a reasonable ESS. Although formal guidance on appropriate ESS for MAIC is lacking, sensitivity analysis conducted by adding or removing factors (tisa-cel) or by evaluating investigator-assessed outcomes (axi-cel)—and accordingly decreasing or increasing ESS—showed similar results for all MAIC models regardless of the number of factors adjusted for, further supporting the robustness of the primary results. A second limitation is that differences in some outcome definitions, though minor, could not be adjusted for and may have introduced bias. NEs were identified using search queries for axi-cel in ZUMA-5 and for tisa-cel in ELARA, but were determined by investigator assessment in TRANSCEND FL. In addition, although the TRANSCEND FL, ZUMA-5, and ELARA studies were conducted during different time periods, the totality of available clinical trial data and real-world evidence does not suggest that the observed safety profile differences can be explained by differences in study timing or evolving clinical experience with CAR T cell therapies. There were also differences in AE reporting windows. ZUMA-5 reported AEs at any time after infusion, but the window for events considered treatment emergent was not clear. Though clearly defined for tisa-cel, ELARA had a shorter window than TRANSCEND FL (8 weeks vs 90 days), potentially allowing for more AEs captured with liso-cel than tisa-cel in the MAIC safety analysis. Despite this conservative approach, safety outcomes were similar between liso-cel and tisa-cel, indicating no increased risk of assessed AEs with liso-cel over tisa-cel. Lastly, it is also possible that insufficient follow-up time for some time-to-event endpoints, such as OS, may have limited the ability to demonstrate any clinically meaningful differences between CAR T cell therapies.

Beyond the inherent limitations of unanchored MAICs and methodological considerations mentioned, several factors associated with CAR T cell therapy that could not be accounted for in this analysis also warrant recognition. These factors include differences in vein-to-vein time, variability in manufacturing success rates, and prior bendamustine exposure. Median time from leukapheresis to infusion was similar in TRANSCEND FL and ELARA (50 and 46 days, respectively [21, 27]) and shorter in ZUMA-5 (27 days [22]). These differences are unlikely to have meaningfully affected patient outcomes and may instead explain the greater use of bridging therapy to control disease in TRANSCEND FL and ELARA compared with ZUMA-5. Manufacturing success rates were high and comparable across trials, ranging from 96% for tisa-cel and liso-cel to 100% for axi-cel [21, 26, 27]. However, results related to vein-to-vein time and manufacturing success should be interpreted with caution, as manufacturing specifications, processes, and site-level procedures have evolved substantially since these trials were conducted. Although bendamustine exposure before leukapheresis has been shown to impact CAR T cell expansion and outcomes for patients with large B cell lymphoma (LBCL) [38, 39], product-level data for liso-cel on quality or expansion by bendamustine exposure are not available. Bendamustine use within 12 months of leukapheresis was uncommon in TRANSCEND FL and ZUMA-5; clinical outcomes were generally similar between studies, yet less favorable compared with patients exposed ≥ 12 months before leukapheresis [31, 40]. Collectively, these observations suggest that bendamustine exposure is unlikely to materially influence the MAIC results. While these clinically relevant and potentially confounding factors merit consideration, available evidence collectively suggests a low likelihood of any meaningful influence on the outcomes of these MAIC analyses.

Our analysis demonstrated improved CR rate, similar time-to-event outcomes, and a more favorable safety profile in the MAIC of liso-cel vs axi-cel, which is meaningful to patients and the health care system. The lower incidence of CRS reported herein combined with the shorter duration of CRS reported in clinical trials of patients with FL [21, 26] supports the use of outpatient monitoring for AEs after liso-cel infusion at a certified health care facility instead of as part of an inpatient admission; this can result in reduced health care resource use and costs [41]. Specifically, a retrospective study that compared postinfusion monitoring costs of liso-cel treatment in the inpatient vs outpatient setting for patients with R/R LBCL found that outpatients had lower overall HCRU compared with inpatients, resulting in a mean 6-month cost savings of $52,833 per patient [42]. Further, real-world evidence suggests that liso-cel may be more commonly administered in the outpatient setting relative to axi-cel. In a recent retrospective study using US claims data, it was found that 44% of patients treated with liso-cel vs 10% treated with axi-cel were infused in an outpatient setting [43]. Additionally, liso-cel had a 73% lower risk of inpatient procedures in the 8‒30 days after infusion compared with axi-cel [43].

Conclusions

In the absence of head-to-head clinical trials, real-world evidence assessing the safety and efficacy of liso-cel vs alternative CAR T cell therapies would offer important information to complement MAICs and further inform clinical decision-making for the treatment of patients with 3L+ R/R FL. MAICs were used to assess the comparative efficacy and safety of liso-cel versus axi-cel and tisa-cel, respectively, in patients with 3L+ R/R FL. After adjustment for population differences in clinical prognostic and treatment-effect modifiers between studies, liso-cel demonstrated higher CR rates and numerically favorable trends of survival compared to axi-cel and tisa-cel, with improved safety compared with axi-cel. Higher ORR and CR rate with similar safety were observed in comparison with tisa-cel. These favorable outcomes associated with liso-cel provide valuable comparative clinical insights and warrant further validation in real-world clinical settings. Overall, these results underscore liso-cel as a potentially preferred CAR T cell therapy for the treatment of patients with 3L+ R/R FL.
