Abstract
. This paper discusses the two classical Hardy operators ℋ 1 \mathcal{H}_{1} on L 2 ​ ( 0 , 1 ) L^{2}(0,1) and ℋ ∞ \mathcal{H}_{\infty} on L 2 ​ ( 0 , ∞ ) L^{2}(0,\infty) initially studied by Brown, Halmos and Shields. Particular emphasis is given to the construction of explicit cyclic and ∗ * -cyclic vectors in conjunction with a characterization of their invariant and reducing subspaces. We also provide a complete description of the frame vectors for I − ℋ 1 ∗ I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*} .

1.
Introduction

This paper explores the invariant subspaces as well as the cyclic and frame vectors for the two classical Hardy integral operators. The first, known as the

finite Hardy operator

\mathcal{H}_{1}
, is defined on the Lebesgue space
L^{2}(0,1)
by the formula

(\mathcal{H}_{1}f)(x)=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt,\quad 0<x<1,

while the second, known as the
infinite Hardy operator

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
, is defined on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
by

(\mathcal{H}_{\infty}g)(x)=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}g(t)dt,\quad 0<x<\infty.

Although the formulas for these two operators are the same and have similar looking adjoint formulas

(\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}f)(x)=\int_{x}^{1}\frac{f(t)}{t}dt,\quad 0<x<1,

(\mathcal{H}^{*}_{\infty}g)(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{g(t)}{t}dt,\quad 0<x<\infty,

and even have the same operator norm equal to
2
(stemming from Hardy’s integral inequality), they act boundedly on different Hilbert spaces and have distinct properties.

In 1965, Brown, Halmos, and Shields
[
4
]
proved that the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is the closed disk

\{z:|z-1|\leqslant 1\}

while the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
is its boundary

\{z:|z-1|=1\}.

A more salient difference between
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
, also from
[
4
]
, comes from their representations as shifts, namely
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
is unitarily equivalent to the unilateral shift
(Sf)(z)=zf(z)
on the classical Hardy space of the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}:=\{z:|z|<1\}
(and denoted by
H^{2}
), while
I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
is unitarily equivalent to the bilateral shift
(Mf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
on the Lebesgue space
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)
, where
\mathbb{T}:=\{z:|z|=1\}
is the unit circle and
dm=d\theta/(2\pi)
is normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
.
As a straightforward consequence,
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
is a subnormal operator, while
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
is normal.

Accordingly, the invariant subspace structure for the operators
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
is essentially understood since, in the first case, the invariant subspaces of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
correspond, via Beurling’s theorem on the invariant subspaces of the shift on
H^{2}
, to the model spaces
(\Theta H^{2})^{\perp}:=H^{2}\ominus\Theta H^{2}
(where
\Theta
is an inner function), while in the second, via results of Wiener and Helson on the invariant subspaces of the bilateral shift, the invariant subspaces of
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
correspond to orthogonal complements of spaces of the form
qH^{2}
(where
q
is a unimodular function on
\mathbb{T}
) or
\chi_{E}L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)
(where
E
is a measurable subset of
\mathbb{T}
). See
[
14
, Ch. II]
for the details on this.

More recently, Agler and McCarthy
[
1
]
have shown that the invariant subspaces of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
can also be expressed as limits of sequences of finite dimensional spaces spanned by eigenfunctions for
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, namely the monomial functions
x\mapsto x^{s}
for
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits s>-\frac{1}{2}
, as so–called “liminf spaces”.
This is in contrast to the related Volterra operator

f(x)\mapsto\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt

on
L^{2}(0,1)
, which has no finite dimensional invariant subspaces (see, for example,
[
12
, Chap. 7]
). Indeed, every invariant subspace of the Volterra operator takes the form
\chi_{[a,1]}L^{2}(0,1)
for some
0\leqslant a\leqslant 1
.

The above descriptions of the invariant subspaces of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
rely either on the unitary operators involved in identifying
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
with
S
and
I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
with
M
or, in the somewhat implicit approach of Agler and McCarthy, as limits of functions via “liminf spaces”. This paper starts by giving an
explicit
description of the invariant subspaces in terms of the functions in the spaces
L^{2}(0,1)
or
L^{2}(0,\infty)
and not their realizations on
H^{2}
or
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)
. The ideas of Brown, Halmos and Shields, along with an explicit inversion formula for the Mellin transform (Proposition
2.6
), allows us to characterize the reducing subspaces for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
(i.e., those invariant for both
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
) as

\left\{t\mapsto\int_{E}t^{-ix-\frac{1}{2}}f(ix)dx:f\in L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})\right\}

for some measurable subset
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
(see Theorem
4.2
). Note that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
lacks reducing subspaces.

This, in turn, leads us to a characterization in Theorem
4.3
of the
\ast
-cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
, i.e., those
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
not contained in any nontrivial reducing subspace. Specifically,
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
is
\ast
-cyclic for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
if and only if the function

\beta\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(e^{x})e^{(i\beta+\frac{1}{2})x}dx

does not vanish on any subset of
\mathbb{R}
with positive measure. Some particular classes (Proposition
4.7
) of
\ast
-cyclic vectors include all nontrivial
L^{2}(0,\infty)
functions supported on
[0,a]
or supported on
[a,\infty)
,
0<a<\infty
.

The study of the cyclic vectors (vectors not contained in any nontrivial invariant subspace) for
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
is a complex undertaking as the provided criteria are difficult to verify. As particular instances, we show in §
5
that the functions
t\mapsto t^{\alpha}
, where
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
, are cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
while the functions

\frac{1}{(t+1)^{n}},\;n=1,2,\ldots,\;\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}(1+(\log t)^{2})},\;\;\mbox{and}\;e^{-t}

are cyclic for both
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{\infty}
. In fact,
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
share the same cyclic vectors (Proposition
5.6
).

This paper will also make connections to semigroups of composition operators on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
, the Laguerre shift, and, using our Mellin transform analysis, the commutants of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
. These results will also help deminstrate the variety of interesting invariant subspaces for our two Hardy operators (see Examples
6.2
and
6.3
).

The final section of the paper follows the work by Cabrelli, Molter, Paternostro, and Philipp
[
5
]

and characterizes the frame vectors for a particular variation of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. Here, for a bounded linear operator
T
on separable Hilbert space
H
, a vector
{\bf v}\in H
is a
frame vector
for
T
if the sequence
(T^{n}{\bf v})_{n\geqslant 0}
is a frame in that there are positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
so that

c_{1}\|{\bf x}\|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\langle T^{n}{\bf v},{\bf x}\rangle|^{2}\leqslant c_{2}\|{\bf x}\|^{2}\;\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x}\in H$}.

In particular,

[
5
, Thm. 3.3]
asserts that for
T
to have frame vectors at all,
is it necessary that
T^{*}
is similar to a strongly stable contraction, in other words,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|T^{*n}{\bf y}\|=0\;\;\mbox{ for all ${\bf y}\in H$.}

By the uniform boundedness principle, it follows that such
T
must satisfy

\sup_{n\geqslant 0}\|T^{n}\|<\infty

and hence, in particular,
\sigma(T)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}:=\{z:|z|\leqslant 1\}
.
Thus, we cannot hope to find frame vectors for the Hardy operators
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
, since, as just mentioned, their spectra are not contained
in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. However, we will show in §
8
that
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
does possess frame vectors while neither
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}
nor
I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
do.
Moreover, frame vectors for
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
exist in abundance and we give plenty of specific examples as well as a general characterization (Corollary
8.8
). Along the way, we show there are no frame vectors for
I-\mathcal{C}
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the classical (discrete) Cesàro operator on
\ell^{2}
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect some preliminaries regarding the framework of this manuscript. In particular, we will prove Proposition
2.6
which will play a key role in our
explicit
description of the invariant subspaces of the Hardy operators
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
on
L^{2}(0,1)
and
L^{2}(0,\infty)
, respectively.

An important tool in our analysis will involve
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
, the
Hardy space of the right half plane

\mathbb{C}^{+}:=\{z:\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits z>0\}
. Here an analytic function
f
on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
belongs to
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
when it satisfies the bounded integral means condition

\sup_{x>0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x+iy)|^{2}dy<\infty.

The square root of the above quantity defines the norm of
f
and will be denoted by
\|f\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})}
. Well known theory about Hardy spaces
[
13
,
22
,
24
]
says that for each
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
, the “radial limit”

f(iy):=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x+iy)

exists for almost every
y\in\mathbb{R}
and this function belongs to
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
with

\|f\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})}^{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(iy)|^{2}dy.

We will use the notation
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
for the set of radial boundary functions for
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
(which will be a closed subspace of
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
). In a similar way, we define the
Hardy space of the left half plane

\mathbb{C}^{-}:=\{z:\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits z<0\}
consisting of all analytic functions
g
on
\mathbb{C}^{-}
with

\sup_{x<0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|g(x+iy)|^{2}dy<\infty

with analogously defined boundary functions (with the radial limit as
x\to 0^{-}
). We denote the corresponding space of radial boundary functions by
H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
. One can verify that the linear transformation
C:L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
defined by
(Cf)(ix)=f(-ix)
is a unitary operator (and its own adjoint) and satisfies

(2.1)

CH^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})=H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R}).

The Paley–Wiener theorem (see
[
23
, p. 146]
or
[
22
, p. 203]
) says that the (normalized)
Laplace transform

(2.2)

(\mathcal{L}f)(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-uz}f(u)du,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

is an isometric isomorphism from
L^{2}(0,\infty)
onto
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
. Moreover, the corresponding Laplace transform

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-uz}f(u)du,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{-},

is an isometric isomorphism from
L^{2}(-\infty,0)
onto
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{-})
. From here it follows that

(2.3)

L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})=H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})\oplus H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R}).

Also important for our analysis will be the (modified)
Mellin transform
. It is known (see
[
23
, p. 166]
or
[
22
, p. 204]
) that the linear transformation

f\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)x^{is-\frac{1}{2}}dx,\quad-\infty<s<\infty,

defines an isometric isomorphism between
L^{2}(0,\infty)
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus, the operator
\mathcal{Q}
defined by

(\mathcal{Q}f)(i\beta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)t^{i\beta-\frac{1}{2}}dt,\qquad-\infty<\beta<\infty,

is an isometric isomorphism between
L^{2}(0,\infty)
and
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
.

The Mellin transform
\mathcal{Q}
is often difficult to compute and even more difficult to invert. To help us along, we will make use of the following connection (implicitly discussed in
[
23
, p. 166]
) between
\mathcal{Q}
and the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Recall that
\mathcal{F}
is initially defined on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
by

(\mathcal{F}f)(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\alpha x}f(x)dx

and extended to be a unitary operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with

(\mathcal{F}^{-1}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ix\alpha}f(\alpha)d\alpha.

Here is an important tool to help invert
\mathcal{Q}
. We first introduce the isometric isomorphism
T:L^{2}(0,\infty)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

(2.4)

(Tf)(x)=f(e^{x})e^{\frac{x}{2}},\quad-\infty<x<\infty,

noting, by the change of variables
t=e^{x}
, that

\|Tf\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(e^{x})|^{2}e^{x}\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

One can then invert
T
with the formula

(2.5)

(T^{-1}g)(t)=\frac{g(\log t)}{\sqrt{t}},\quad 0<t<\infty.

This yields the following useful identity.

Proposition 2.6
.

For each
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
, we have

(\mathcal{Q}f)(i\beta)=(\mathcal{F}^{-1}Tf)(\beta)

for almost every
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

By the same change of variables as above,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{Q}f)(i\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(e^{x})e^{(i\beta-\frac{1}{2})x}e^{x}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\beta x}(Tf)(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{F}^{-1}Tf)(\beta),

which yields the desired formula.
∎

Thus, using (
2.5
),
\mathcal{Q}^{-1}:L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(0,\infty)
is given by

(2.7)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{Q}^{-1}g)(t)

\displaystyle=(T^{-1}\mathcal{F}g(i\cdot))(t)

\displaystyle=T^{-1}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ixt}g(ix)dx\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ix\log t}g(ix)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-ix}g(ix)dx.

In a similar way as above, the isometric isomorphism

(2.8)

W:L^{2}(0,1)\to L^{2}(0,\infty),\quad(Wf)(u)=f(e^{-u})e^{-\frac{u}{2}}

satisfies

(W^{-1}g)(t)=\frac{g(-\log t)}{\sqrt{t}}.

Thus, for
f\in L^{2}(0,1)
, a use of the Paley–Wiener theorem says that the function

z\mapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}(Wf)(u)e^{-uz}du

on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
belongs to
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
. Moreover,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(Wf)(u)e^{-uz}du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(e^{-u})e^{-\frac{u}{2}}e^{-uz}du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}f(t)t^{z-\frac{1}{2}}dt.

This shows that

(2.10)

\mathcal{Q}f=\mathcal{L}Wf\;\;\mbox{for all $f\in L^{2}(0,1)$},

and thus

\mathcal{Q}L^{2}(0,1)=H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R}).

Since
H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})=H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})^{\perp}
by (
2.3
) and
\mathcal{Q}
is an isometric isomorphism, we also see that

(2.11)

\mathcal{Q}L^{2}(1,\infty)=H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R}).

3.
The Hardy operators as multiplication operators

In this section, we realize
I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
as a multiplication operator on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
. This was implicitly done in the Brown, Halmos, Shields paper
[
4
]
but we make this realization explicit here since we need it to appear in a certain form. In order to do this, we recall the Laguerre polynomials.

3.1.
The Laguerre polynomials

The
Laguerre polynomials

(L_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
are a sequence of polynomials which serve as an orthonormal basis for the weighted
L^{2}
space
L^{2}((0,\infty),e^{-x}dx)
in that

\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{m}(x)L_{n}(x)e^{-x}dx=\delta_{mn}

and
(L_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is complete in
L^{2}((0,\infty),e^{-x}dx)
. See
[
27
, Ch. V]
for the details.
Letting

V=I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*},

one can show by a direct integral calculation that
V
is a unitary operator on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
. Here is a calculation which follows from properties of Laguerre polynomials
[
20
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\chi
denotes the characteristic function for
(0,1)
, we have the following

(a)

For each
n=0,1,2,\ldots,

(V^{n}\chi)(x)=L_{n}(-\log x)\chi(x)

and
(V^{n}\chi)_{n\geqslant 0}
forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(0,1)
.

(b)

For each
n=0,1,2,3\ldots,

(V^{*n}\chi)(x)=-\frac{L_{n}(\log x)}{x}(1-\chi(x))

and
(V^{*n}\chi)_{n\geqslant 0}
forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(1,\infty)
.

Now consider the function

(3.2)

\varphi(s)=\frac{s-\tfrac{1}{2}}{s+\tfrac{1}{2}}

and observe that
\varphi
maps the right half plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}
conformally onto
\mathbb{D}
and
|\varphi(iy)|=1
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
.
This induces an isometric isomorphism
J:L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)\to L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
defined by

(3.3)

(Jf)(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{s+\frac{1}{2}}f(\varphi(s)),\quad s\in i\mathbb{R},

which restricts to an isometric isomorphism from
H^{2}
(the Hardy space of the disk) onto
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
(the Hardy space of the right half plane). Observe that the multiplication operator

(3.4)

M_{\varphi}:L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(i\mathbb{R}),\quad M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f,

is unitary and, moreover,
M_{\varphi}H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})\subseteq H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
. A calculation will verify that

J^{-1}M_{\varphi}J

is the bilateral shift on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m)
, which allows us to
carry the Beurling–Lax and Wiener invariant subspace theorems over to

L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
and
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
.

Using the well-known fact that
(z^{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
forms an orthonormal basis for
H^{2}
, along with the fact that
J
is an isometric isomorphism, we see that the functions

(3.5)

u_{n}(i\beta):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big(\frac{i\beta-\frac{1}{2}}{i\beta+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{n}\frac{1}{i\beta+\frac{1}{2}},\quad n=0,1,2,\cdots,

form an orthonormal basis for
H_{+}^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
and that
M_{\varphi}u_{n}=u_{n+1}
.
The following connects
\mathcal{Q}
with the Laguerre polynomials and the orthonormal basis
(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
.

Since the Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}(x)
for
n=0,1,2,\ldots,
can be written as

(3.6)

L_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n!}\left(\frac{d}{dx}-1\right)^{n}x^{n},\

the Laplace transform formula yields that

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xs}x^{n}\,dx=\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}},\quad s\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

Thus, we conclude from (
3.6
) that

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xs}L_{n}(x)\,dx=\frac{(s-1)^{n}}{s^{n+1}}

and so

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xs}e^{-\frac{x}{2}}L_{n}(x)\,dx=\frac{(s-\tfrac{1}{2})^{n}}{(s+\tfrac{1}{2})^{n+1}}.

We may rewrite this as a Fourier transform, taking
s=i\omega
, and then

(3.7)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega xs}e^{-\frac{x}{2}}L_{n}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{(i\omega-\tfrac{1}{2})^{n}}{(i\omega+\tfrac{1}{2})^{n+1}}.

Proposition 3.8
.

For each
n=0,1,2,\ldots,
we have

(\mathcal{Q}L_{n}(-\log x)\chi)(i\beta)=u_{n}(i\beta).

Proof.

An integral substitution in
(\mathcal{Q}L_{n}(-\log x)\chi)(i\beta)
will yield

(\mathcal{Q}L_{n}(-\log x)\chi)(i\beta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-i\beta u}e^{-\frac{u}{2}}L_{n}(u)du.

Now use (
3.7
).
∎

Furthermore, as observed earlier in Lemma
3.1
, noting that

(3.9)

(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*})|_{L^{2}(0,1)}=I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*},

we see that

(I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})L_{n}(-\log x)\chi=L_{n+1}(-\log x)\chi,

and thus

(3.10)

\mathcal{Q}(I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})f=M_{\varphi}\mathcal{Q}f,\quad f\in L^{2}(0,1).

We summarize this with the following proposition.

Proposition 3.11
.

I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
on
L^{2}(0,1)
is unitarily equivalent to
M_{\varphi}
on
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
.

Using the substitution
z\mapsto-z
, which transforms
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
onto
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{-})
(recall (
2.1
)), we conclude that the functions

(3.12)

v_{n}(i\beta):=i\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\Big(\frac{2\beta-i}{2\beta+i}\Big)^{n}\frac{1}{2\beta+i},\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

form an orthonormal basis for
H_{-}^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
. Another connection that the Mellin transform
\mathcal{Q}
makes with the Laguerre polynomials is the following.

Proposition 3.13
.

For each
n=0,1,2,\ldots
we have

(\mathcal{Q}(-\frac{L_{n}(\log x)}{x}(1-\chi)))(i\beta)=v_{n}(i\beta).

Finally, notice that

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*})v_{n}(i\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*})(-\frac{L_{n}(\log x)}{x}(1-\chi))(i\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}(-\frac{L_{n-1}(\log x)}{x}(1-\chi))(i\beta)

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\Big(\frac{2\beta-i}{2\beta+i}\Big)^{n-1}\frac{1}{2\beta+i}

\displaystyle=M_{\varphi}i\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\Big(\frac{2\beta-i}{2\beta+i}\Big)^{n}\frac{1}{2\beta+i}

\displaystyle=M_{\varphi}v_{n}(i\beta).

Thus, since
(v_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is an orthonormal basis for
H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{Q}L^{2}(1,\infty)=H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
(see (
2.11
)), it follows that

\mathcal{Q}(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*})f=M_{\varphi}\mathcal{Q}f,\quad f\in L^{2}(1,\infty).

Combining this with (
3.9
) and (
3.12
), we have the following.

Proposition 3.14
.

I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
is unitarily equivalent to
M_{\varphi}
on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
.

The unitary operator
f(ix)\mapsto f(-ix)
on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
induces a unitary equivalence between
M_{\varphi}
and
M_{\overline{\varphi}}=M^{*}_{\varphi}
. This yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.15
.

I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
is unitarily equivalent to
M_{\varphi}
on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
.

3.2.
A connection to the Laguerre shift

The
Laguerre shift
was formulated by von Neumann
[
28
]
(see also
[
25
, p. 18]
) as the bounded operator
\mathfrak{L}
on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
defined by

(3.16)

(\mathfrak{L}f)(x)=f(x)-\int_{0}^{x}e^{\frac{t-x}{2}}f(t)dt.

Recalling our earlier discussion that the
Laguerre functions
defined by

\Phi_{n}(x)=e^{-\frac{x}{2}}L_{n}(x),\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

constitute an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(0,\infty)
,
von Neumann showed that

(3.17)

\mathfrak{L}\Phi_{n}=\Phi_{n+1}

and thus
\mathfrak{L}
is a shift of multiplicity one. General theory of such shifts shows that
\mathfrak{L}
is unitarily equivalent to the unilateral shift
(Sf)(z)=zf(z)
on
H^{2}
.

Recalling the fact that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{(i\omega-\frac{1}{2})^{n}}{(i\omega+\frac{1}{2})^{n+1}}=u_{n}(i\omega),\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots

is the orthonormal basis for
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
discussed in (
3.5
), the identity in (
3.7
) says that

\mathcal{L}\mathfrak{L}\Phi_{n}=M_{\varphi}\mathcal{L}\Phi_{n},

where
\mathcal{L}
is the (normalized) Laplace transform from (
2.2
). This provides a restatement of (
3.17
) in the following way.

Proposition 3.18
.

The Laguerre shift
\mathfrak{L}
on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
is unitarily equivalent to
M_{\varphi}
on
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
.

Recall that a vector
{\bf v}
in a separable Hilbert space
H
is
cyclic
for a bounded linear operator
T
on
H
if
{\bf x}
is not contained in any nontrivial invariant subspace for
T
, equivalently, the linear span of its orbit
(T^{n}{\bf v})_{n\geqslant 0}
is dense in
H
. One can straightforwardly use Proposition
3.18
to provide some cyclic vectors for the Laguerre shift. Namely, if
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
is outer and hence, by Beurling’s Theorem, a cyclic vector for
M_{\varphi}
, then
\mathcal{L}^{-1}f
will be cyclic for the Laguerre shift. Particular instances of cyclic vectors for
\mathfrak{L}
are the
L^{2}(0,\infty)
functions

x^{n}e^{-cx},\quad\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits c>0,\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots

since their Laplace transforms will be nonzero constants times
(z+c)^{-n-1}
, which are outer functions in
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
.
Examples of different nature will be provided in §
5
.

3.3.
A connection to the bilateral Laguerre shift

The previous discussion can be taken a bit further by considering negative indices as well. Here an orthonormal
basis for
L^{2}(-\infty,0)
is clearly given by

-e^{\frac{x}{2}}L_{m}(-x)\chi_{(-\infty,0)}(x),\quad m=0,1,2,\ldots,

and, similarly as before, these functions
Fourier transform to

\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\dfrac{(i\omega+\frac{1}{2})^{m}}{(i\omega-\frac{1}{2})^{m+1}},\quad m=0,1,2,\ldots.

From (
3.12
), this is an orthonormal basis for
H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
.
A good reference for the bilateral Laguerre shift is
[
17
]
.

The Laguerre shift in the transform domain is just multiplication by the function
\varphi(i\omega)
from (
3.2
).
We have an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

\Phi_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}-e^{\frac{x}{2}}L_{-n-1}(-x)\chi_{(-\infty,0)}(x),&n=-1,-2,-3,\ldots\\
e^{-\frac{x}{2}}L_{n}(x)\chi_{(0,\infty)}(x),&n=0,1,2,\ldots.\\
\end{cases}

The bilateral Laguerre shift again satisfies
\mathfrak{L}\Phi_{n}=\Phi_{n+1}
, only now for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
(and not just
\mathbb{N}_{0}
as before).
From these considerations we see that
\mathfrak{L}
is unitarily equivalent to
M_{\varphi}
on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover, the von Neumann formula (
3.16
) extends to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

by

(\mathfrak{L}f)(x)=f(x)-\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{\frac{t-x}{2}}f(t)dt,

as can be verified by the analysis above.

4.
Invariant subspaces for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}

From Proposition
3.14
, it follows that every invariant subspace for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
must take the form

(4.1)

\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\mathcal{M}=T^{-1}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{M},

where
\mathcal{M}
is an
M_{\varphi}
-invariant subspace of
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
. Recall the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}
from (
3.4
).

Of particular interest among the invariant subspaces of a linear bounded operator
T
acting on a Hilbert space
H
are the reducing ones since they encode the projections in the communtant of the operator. Recall that a non-trivial subspaces
M
is a
reducing subspace
if both
M
and its orthogonal complement
M^{\perp}
are invariant under
T
, or, equivalently, the orthogonal projection
P_{M}
onto
M
commutes with
T
.

4.1.
Reducing subspaces for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}

Well known results of Wiener (originally stated for
L^{2}
of the circle, see
[
14
, Ch. 2]
, but which can be stated for
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
via the unitary operator
J
from (
3.3
)), describe the reducing subspaces for
M_{\varphi}
on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
as

\chi_{iE}L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
for some (Lebesgue) measurable subset
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. This gives us the following.

Theorem 4.2
.

A subspace
\mathcal{N}
of
L^{2}(0,\infty)
is reducing for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
if and only if there is a measurable subset
E
of
\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{N}=\left\{t\mapsto\int_{E}t^{-ix-\frac{1}{2}}f(ix)dx:f\in L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})\right\}.

Proof.

From (
4.1
) we see that
\mathcal{N}
is reducing for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
if and only if
there is a measurable subset
E
of
\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\chi_{iE}L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})=T^{-1}\mathcal{F}\chi_{iE}L^{2}(i\mathbb{R}).

Then from (
2.7
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\chi_{iE}L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle=\left\{t\mapsto\int_{E}t^{-ix-\frac{1}{2}}f(ix)dx:f\in L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})\right\}.\qed

A vector
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
is
\ast
-cyclic
for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
if the only
reducing
subspace containing
f
is all of
L^{2}(0,\infty)
. Since
I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
is unitarily equivalent to
M_{\varphi}
on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
, via
\mathcal{Q}
, we see that the
\ast
-cyclic vectors of
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
correspond to the
\ast
-cyclic vectors of
M_{\varphi}
and, from Wiener’s theorem, these are the functions in
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
which do not vanish on any set of positive measure. We summarize this discussion with the following result.

Theorem 4.3
.

For
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
the following are equivalent.

(a)

f
is a
\ast
-cyclic vector for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.

(b)

There is an
h\in L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
that does not vanish on any set of positive measure such that

f(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-ix-\frac{1}{2}}h(ix)dx.

(c)

The function

\beta\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(e^{x})e^{(i\beta+\frac{1}{2})x}dx

does not vanish on any subset of
\mathbb{R}
of positive measure.

Some specific
\ast
-cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
can be derived as follows.

Example 4.4
.

Consider the function

iy\mapsto\frac{1}{1-iy}\in L^{2}(i\mathbb{R}).

This function does not vanish on
i\mathbb{R}
and thus is
\ast
-cyclic for
M_{\varphi}
.
To calculate
Q^{-1}=T^{-1}\mathcal{F}
(see Proposition
2.6
) applied to this function, we
observe that

(4.5)

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}e^{iyx}\,dx=\frac{1}{1-iy},

and thus

\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{1}{1-iy}\right)(x)=\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-x}\chi_{(0,\infty)}(x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Now use the formula
(T^{-1}g)(t)=g(\log t)/\sqrt{t}
from (
2.5
) to see that

Q^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{1-iy}\right)(t)=\sqrt{2\pi}\frac{1}{t^{\frac{3}{2}}}\chi_{(1,\infty)}(t).

Note the use of the identity
\chi_{(0,\infty)}(\log t)=\chi_{(1,\infty)}(t)
in the above calculation.
Hence, an example of a
\ast
-cyclic vector for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
is

\frac{1}{t^{\frac{3}{2}}}\chi_{(1,\infty)}(t).

For
n=1,2,\ldots,
by differentiating (
4.5
)
(n-1)
times with respect to
y
we
see that

\mathcal{F}\Big(\frac{1}{(1-iy)^{n}}\Big)(x)=c_{n}e^{-x}x^{n-1}\chi_{(0,\infty)}(x),

where
c_{n}
is a nonzero constant depending only on
n
. Thus,
we obtain the following family of
\ast
-cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
:

\frac{1}{t^{\frac{3}{2}}}(\log t)^{n-1}\chi_{(1,\infty)}(t),\quad n=1,2,\ldots

Example 4.6
.

One can also provide other
\ast
-cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
by considering functions in
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
defined by

G_{N}(iy)=e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}H_{4N}(y),\quad N=0,1,2,\ldots,

where
H_{K}
is the
K
-th Hermite polynomial. Since these functions only vanish at a finite number of points on
i\mathbb{R}
, they are
\ast
-cyclic vectors for
M_{\varphi}
on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, the Fourier transform of
G_{N}
is equal to itself (as these are eigenvectors for the Fourier transform corresponding to the eigenvalue
1

[
15
]
). Thus,
T^{-1}\mathcal{F}G_{N}
will be a
\ast
-cyclic vector for the Hardy operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.
Note that

g_{N}:=T^{-1}\mathcal{F}G_{N}

can be computed as

g_{N}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\log t)^{2}}H_{4N}(\log t).

For example, when
N=0
we get

g_{0}(t)=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}(t)}}{\sqrt{t}}

is a
\ast
-cyclic vector for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
. When
n=1
we get

g_{1}(t)=\frac{4e^{-\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}(t)}\left(4\log^{4}(t)-12\log^{2}(t)+3\right)}{\sqrt{t}}

is a
\ast
-cyclic vector for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.

A wider class of
\ast
-cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
comes from this next result.

Proposition 4.7
.

For each
a>0
all nonzero
L^{2}(0,\infty)
functions supported on
[0,a]
are
*
-cyclic for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.
Likewise, all nonzero functions supported on
[a,\infty)
are also
*
-cyclic.

Proof.

Take an
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)\setminus\{0\}
supported on
[0,a]
.
By Theorem.
4.3
we wish to show that the function

\beta\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(e^{x})e^{i\beta x+x/2}\,dx

is nonzero almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
. By our assumption on
f
, this function becomes

\beta\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{\log a}g(x)e^{i\beta x}\,dx

where
g
is the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
function defined by
g(x)=(Tf)(x)=f(e^{x})e^{x/2}
.
Now set
y=\log a-x
to see that the function in question is

\beta\mapsto e^{i\beta\log a}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(\log a-y)e^{-i\beta y}\,dy.

By the Paley–Wiener theorem, recall (
2.2
), the integral above belongs to
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
, and, via standard Hardy space theory, is nonzero almost everywhere.

For the second part, the integral becomes

\beta\mapsto\int_{\log a}^{\infty}g(x)e^{i\beta x}\,dx

and we write
y=x-\log a
to obtain

\beta\mapsto e^{i\beta\log a}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(y+\log a)e^{i\beta y}\,dy,

with the same conclusion, except that the function above belongs to
H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
.
∎

It follows immediately that all nontrivial functions supported on
a finite subinterval
[a,b]
of
[0,\infty)
are
\ast
-cyclic for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.

4.2.
Non-reducing subspaces for
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{\infty}

With Theorem
4.3
at hand, some remarks about the non-reducing subspaces of
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{\infty}
are in order. First, inverting
\mathcal{Q}
from (
2.7
), we see that for
g\in L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
we have

(\mathcal{Q}^{-1}f)(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-ix-\frac{1}{2}}g(ix)dx.

By Helson’s theorem
[
14
, Ch. II]
, the non-reducing
M_{\varphi}
-invariant subspaces are
q(ix)H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
, where
q
is unimodular, and these can be written as

\left\{x\mapsto q(ix)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ixw}h(w)dw:h\in L^{2}(0,\infty)\right\}.

Note the use of the Paley–Wiener theorem here. Putting this all together gives us the following.

Theorem 4.8
.

For a subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
L^{2}(0,\infty)
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathcal{M}
is a non-reducing invariant subspace for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
.

(b)

There is a unimodular function
q
on
i\mathbb{R}
such that

(4.9)

\mathcal{M}=\left\{t\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-ix-\frac{1}{2}}q(ix)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ixw}h(w)dw\right)dx:h\in L^{2}(0,\infty)\right\}.

Example 4.10
.

For each
a>0
, the subspace
\chi_{[a,\infty)}L^{2}(0,\infty)
is

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
-invariant and thus its orthogonal complement

\chi_{[0,a]}L^{2}(0,\infty)
is
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{\infty}
-invariant (and non-reducing). The identity

\int_{0}^{a}f(t)t^{i\beta-\frac{1}{2}}dt=a^{i\beta}\sqrt{a}\int_{0}^{1}f(au)u^{i\beta-\frac{1}{2}}du

shows that

\mathcal{Q}\chi_{[0,a]}L^{2}(0,\infty)=a^{i\beta}H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R}).

To classify the non-reducing invariant subspaces for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
, rather than
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
, we note, from the fact that
|q(ix)|=1
almost everywhere on
i\mathbb{R}
and that
H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})\perp H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
(recall (
2.3
)), that
the orthogonal complement of the subspace
q(ix)H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
is
q(ix)H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
, also
that
f
belongs to
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
if and only if
ix\mapsto f(-ix)
belongs to
H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
(recall (
2.1
)).
Therefore, the corresponding formula for the non-reducing invariant subspaces for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
is obtained
by replacing (
4.9
) with

(4.11)

\mathcal{M}=\left\{t\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-ix-\frac{1}{2}}q(ix)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{ixw}h(w)dw\right)dx:h\in L^{2}(0,\infty)\right\}.

Returning to Proposition
4.7
, evidently, a function defined on
[0,a]
cannot be cyclic for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
alone, as
its images under powers of
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{\infty}
are all supported on
[0,a]
.
Curiously, it need not be cyclic for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
either, as the following example shows.

Example 4.12
.

Recall that
\chi
denotes the characteristic function of
[0,1]
. A preliminary calculation
with Fourier transforms yields

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{ixy}e^{\frac{y}{2}}\,dy=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}+ix}

and hence (inverting the transform)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ixy}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}+ix}\,dx=e^{\frac{y}{2}}\chi_{[-\infty,0)}(y).

Writing
t=e^{y}
we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-ix-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}+ix}\,dx=\chi(t).

Comparing this with (
4.11
), we see that
\chi
lies in the
nontrivial
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
-invariant
subspace corresponding to the unimodular function

q(ix)=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-ix}{\frac{1}{2}+ix}

and in this case
we have
h(w)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{w}{2}}
in (
4.11
).

A remarkable theorem of Volberg and Kargaev
[
16
]
says that given any positive number
\ell
there exists a measurable set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
|E|<\infty
and
\mathcal{F}\chi_{E}
is zero on
(-\ell,\ell)
. In particular, this yields the following corollary:

Corollary 4.13
.

There exist nonzero functions in
L^{2}(0,\infty)
vanishing on an interval which are not
\ast
-cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.

5.
Cyclic vectors: examples

Beurling’s theorem says that the cyclic vectors for
M_{\varphi}
on
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
are the outer functions in
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})

[
13
]
.
From Proposition
3.11
, the cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
are those
f\in L^{2}(0,1)
such that the
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
function

(\mathcal{Q}f)(z)=\int_{0}^{1}f(t)t^{z-\frac{1}{2}}dt,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

is an outer function. Recall (
2.9
) which shows that
\mathcal{Q}f
is the Laplace transform of

(Wf)(u)=f(e^{-u})e^{-\frac{u}{2}}.

In general, this seems difficult to unpack. However, we can provide a few examples.

Example 5.1
.

A first class of examples of cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
is

f_{\alpha}(t)=t^{\alpha},\quad\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\alpha>-\tfrac{1}{2}

(a necessary condition for
f_{\alpha}
to belong to
L^{2}(0,1)
). Here

(Wf_{\alpha})(u)=e^{-\frac{u}{2}-\alpha u}

has Laplace transform equal to
(s+\alpha+\frac{1}{2})^{-1}
, which is outer.

In order to provide examples of cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
, we first note that an invariant subspace for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
has the form

\mathcal{M}=\left\{t\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-ix-\frac{1}{2}}f(ix)dx:f\in\mathcal{V}\right\}

where
\mathcal{V}
is either
\chi_{iE}L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
or
q(ix)H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
for some unimodular
q
.

Writing
t=e^{u}
, with
-\infty<u<\infty
, we have
g\in\mathcal{M}
if

g(e^{u})=e^{-\frac{u}{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ixu}f(x)\,dx.

Example 5.2
.

Here are two examples of cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
:

(a)

{\displaystyle g(t)=\frac{1}{1+t}}
;

(b)

g(t)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{t}(1+(\log t)^{2})}
.

To see this, first observe that

e^{\frac{u}{2}}g(e^{u})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{\frac{u}{2}}}{e^{u}+1}&\text{in case (a),}\\
\\
\dfrac{1}{1+u^{2}}&\text{in case (b)}.\end{cases}

In each case we can invert the Fourier transform explicitly to obtain

f(x)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi}{2}}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\pi x&\text{in case (a),}\\
\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi}{2}}e^{-|x|}&\text{in case (b)}.\end{cases}

Each of these functions
f
is nonzero everywhere, so
g
cannot lie in a
nontrivial reducing subspace for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
, and likewise the
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
condition

(5.3)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{|\log|f(iw)|}{1+w^{2}}dw>-\infty

(a well known necessary condition for
f
to be noncyclic for
M_{\varphi}
on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
– see for example
[
23
, Cor. A.6.4.2]
) is
not
satisfied, so
g
does not
lie in a (nontrivial) non-reducing invariant subspace. Therefore, both these
g
are cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
.

The above example can be generalized to the following.

Example 5.4
.

For each
n=1,2,\ldots,
the function

g_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{(t+1)^{n}}

is a cyclic vector for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
.
This can be proved by induction. We have already seen the case
n=1
from Example
5.2
. Let

F_{n}
denote the inverse Fourier transform
of

\frac{e^{\frac{u}{2}}}{(e^{u}+1)^{n}}

so that
F_{1}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\pi x
.
Evaluating the integral in

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iux}\frac{e^{\frac{u}{2}}}{(e^{u}+1)^{n}}\,du

by parts we have that

F_{n}(x)=-\frac{1}{ix}(-n(F_{n}(x)-F_{n+1}(x))+\tfrac{1}{2}F_{n}(x))

so that

F_{n+1}(x)=\frac{1}{n}(F_{n}(x)(-ix+n-\tfrac{1}{2})).

Thus, by induction,
F_{n+1}
is a polynomial of degree
n
in
x
multiplied by
\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\pi x
and so
it satisfies the non-vanishing and non-log-integrability condition (recall (
5.3
)) for
g_{n}(t)
to be a cyclic vector
for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
.

Example 5.5
.

One can also consider more rapidly decreasing functions such as

g(t)=e^{-t}

as candidates for cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
.
Here

e^{\frac{u}{2}}g(e^{u})=e^{\frac{u}{2}}e^{-e^{u}}

which has inverse Fourier transform equal to

\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-ix\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

(just write out the integral formula for the Gamma function to see this Fourier transform detail).
This function
g
does not vanish on any set of positive measure and so
g
is a
\ast
-cyclic vector for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
. To see that
g
is a
cyclic
vector for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
, we use the Euler reflection formula
[
2
, p. 199]

\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi z}

with
z=\frac{1}{2}-ix
to see that

\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2}-ix)\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2}+ix)=\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi(\tfrac{1}{2}-ix)}=\frac{\pi}{\cosh(\pi x)}.

The two factors on the left are complex conjugates of each other and so

|\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}-ix)|
decreases like
e^{-\frac{x}{2}}
, which eliminates the possibility of log integrability from (
5.3
).

Let’s us make the following observation about the cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
and those for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
.

Proposition 5.6
.

For
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
the following are equivalent.

(a)

f
is a cyclic vector for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.

(b)

f
is a cyclic vector for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
.

Proof.

From our discussion from Proposition
3.14
and (
2.1
), recall that if
C:L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
is defined by
(Cg)(ix)=g(-ix)
, then
C
is unitary (and is it own adjoint) and
CM_{\varphi}C=M_{\overline{\varphi}}
. Furthermore,
CH^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})=H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
.

Now observe that
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
is cyclic for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
if and only if
\mathcal{Q}f
is cyclic for
M_{\varphi}
– which is precisely the condition that
\mathcal{Q}f
is nonvanishing almost everywhere on
i\mathbb{R}
and that
\mathcal{Q}f\not\in qH^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
for any unimodular function
q
on
i\mathbb{R}
. The conditions for a function in
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})
to be cyclic for
M_{\overline{\varphi}}
are that it is nonvanishing almost everywhere on
i\mathbb{R}
and does not belong to any space
pH^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
, where
p
is unimodular on
i\mathbb{R}
.

Our earlier discussion said that
\mathcal{Q}f
is cyclic for
M_{\varphi}
if and only if
C\mathcal{Q}f
is cyclic for
M_{\overline{\varphi}}
. But
\mathcal{Q}f
vanishes almost everywhere if and only if
C\mathcal{Q}f
vanishes almost everywhere and
\mathcal{Q}f
does not belong to
qH^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
if and only if
C\mathcal{Q}f
does not belong to
(Cq)H^{2}_{-}(i\mathbb{R})
. Thus,
f
is cyclic for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
if and only if
f
is cyclic for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.
∎

We conclude that the cyclic vectors for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
from Examples
5.2
,
5.4
, and
5.5
are also cyclic for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.

6.
Invariant subspaces for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
and semigroups

Following the work of Arvanitidis and Siskakis
[
3
]
, we consider the weighted composition semigroup
\{C_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
defined by

(C_{t}f)(x)=e^{-\frac{t}{2}}f(e^{-t}x),\quad 0\leqslant x<\infty.

Then, using the substitution
y=e^{-t}x
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(C_{t}f)(x)e^{-\frac{t}{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}f(e^{-t}x)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}f(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{H}_{\infty}f)(x).

One can also verify that
\|C_{t}f\|=\|f\|
for all
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
and thus
\{C_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is a contractive semigroup (which will be important in a moment). The connection between
\{C_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
and the invariant subspaces for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
is the following.

Proposition 6.1
.

A subspace of
L^{2}(0,\infty)
is invariant for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
if and only if it is invariant under every
C_{t}
,
t\geqslant 0
.

Let us outline the proof of this using some well known results about semigroups. Recall that for a given
C_{0}
-semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t\geqslant 0}
of bounded linear operators on a Hilbert space
H
, there exists a closed and densely defined linear transformation
A
called the
generator
of
\{T(t)\}_{t\geqslant 0}
, that determines the semigroup. It is defined by

A{\bf x}:=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{T(t){\bf x}-{\bf x}}{t},

where the domain
\mathcal{D}(A)
of
A
consists of all
{\bf x}\in H
for which this limit exists (see
[
8
, Chapter II]
, for instance). It is well-known that the generator is, in general, an unbounded operator which determines the
C_{0}
-semigroup.

Likewise, if
1
belongs to the resolvent of
A
, namely, the set

\rho(A)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\;(A-\lambda I):\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq H\to H\mbox{ is bijective}\},

then, by the closed graph theorem,
(A-I)^{-1}
is a bounded operator on
H
and the
Cayley transform of
A
, defined by

V:=(A+I)(A-I)^{-1},

is a bounded operator on
H
since
V-I=2(A-I)^{-1}
. Note that
V
determines the semigroup uniquely, since
A
does. This operator is called the
cogenerator
of the
C_{0}
-semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t\geqslant 0}
. Observe that 1 is not an eigenvalue of
V
.

In our setting,
T(t)
is equal to
C_{t}
on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
and a straightforward computation yields that

Af(x)=-xf^{\prime}(x)-\tfrac{1}{2}f(x)

for those
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
for which
xf^{\prime}\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
.
Likewise, it holds that
1\in\rho(A)
and, having in mind that the resolvent can be expressed in terms of the Laplace transform of the semigroup (see
[
7
, Thm. 11, p. 622]
), we note that

((A-\tfrac{1}{2}I)^{-1}f)(x)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t}{2}}(C_{t}f)(x)\,dt=-(\mathcal{H}_{\infty}f)(x),\quad 0<x<\infty.

Accordingly, if we write
W=(2A+I)(2A-I)^{-1}
, the cogenerator of the semigroup
\{C_{2t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
we have that

W-I=(A-\tfrac{1}{2}I)^{-1}=-{\mathcal{H}}_{\infty},

and, using the fact that
\{C_{2t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is a contractive semigroup (discussed earlier), the common invariant subspaces for the semigroup
\{C_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
are precisely those for
{\mathcal{H}}_{\infty}

[
9
, p. 146]
.

This approach was used in
[
10
]
in the context of a different semigroup
to obtain information about the invariant subspaces of the discrete Cesàro operator on
H^{2}
(see also
[
11
]
for more on invariant subspaces of the Cesàro operator). Here
the fact that the semigroup
\{C_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
consists of dilations enables us
to identify
some new invariant subspaces for
{\mathcal{H}}_{\infty}
.

Example 6.2
.

Consider the subspace
\mathcal{M}
consisting of all functions belonging to
L^{2}(0,\infty)
that are constant almost everywhere on
[0,1]
. One can see that
\mathcal{M}
is the closed linear span of
\{C_{t}\chi:t\geqslant 0\}
, where
\chi
is the characteristic function on
[0,1]
. Clearly
C_{t}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for all
0\leqslant t<\infty
and one can also see that
\mathcal{M}
is also
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
-invariant.

Example 6.3
.

As a generalization of the previous example, take a fixed
A>0
along with a finite set of monomials

x^{a_{1}},\ldots,x^{a_{n}}
with
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits a_{k}>-\tfrac{1}{2}
for each
k
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be the subspace consisting of all
L^{2}(0,\infty)

functions whose restriction to
[0,A]
is a linear span of these monomials.
Note that
\mathcal{M}
is invariant for all
C_{t}
and for
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.

In (
2.4
) we introduced the unitary operator
T:L^{2}(0,\infty)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

(Tf)(x)=f(e^{x})e^{\frac{x}{2}},\quad-\infty<x<\infty,

with

(T^{-1}g)(y)=\frac{g(\log y)}{\sqrt{y}}.

Let us calculate
TC_{t}T^{-1}
an operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
For each
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
we have

\displaystyle(TC_{t}T^{-1}g)(x)

\displaystyle=(C_{t}T^{-1}g)(e^{x})e^{\frac{x}{2}}

\displaystyle=(T^{-1}g)(e^{-t}e^{x})e^{-\frac{t}{2}}e^{\frac{x}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g(x-t)}{e^{\frac{x}{2}}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}}e^{\frac{x}{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{t}{2}}g(x-t),

so that
C_{t}
transforms into a multiple of a standard right shift on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

By the Beurling–Lax theorem, the common invariant subspaces of
TC_{t}T^{-1}
are functions with Fourier transform

\chi_{E}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
or
qH^{2}(U)
, where
U
is the upper half-plane and
q
is unimodular on
\mathbb{R}
. So we come back to the same conclusions by a
different route, since in
L^{2}(0,\infty)
the invariant subspaces of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\infty}
are of the form
T^{-1}\mathcal{M}
.

7.
The commutants

In this section we provide explicit expressions for those bounded linear operators commuting with the Hardy operators
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.
Our description makes use of the identity

\mathcal{Q}(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*})\mathcal{Q}^{-1}=M_{\varphi},

where
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}T
is unitary, so that

\mathcal{Q}(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty})\mathcal{Q}^{-1}=M^{*}_{\varphi}=M_{\bar{\varphi}}.

Recall our discussion from §
3
. This, along with the well known fact
[
12
, p. 183]
that the commutant of
M_{\varphi}
on
L^{2}(i\mathbb{R})

consists of all multiplication operators
M_{g}
with
g\in L^{\infty}(i\mathbb{R})
, yields the following.

Proposition 7.1
.

A bounded linear operator
A
in
L^{2}(0,\infty)
commutes with
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
if and only if there exists a
g\in L^{\infty}(i\mathbb{R})
such that

(7.2)

(Af)(t)=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-iy-\frac{1}{2}}g(iy)\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u)u^{iy-\frac{1}{2}}du\Big)dy

for all
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

If
A:L^{2}(0,\infty)\to L^{2}(0,\infty)
commutes with
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
,
we have

A(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty})=(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty})A

and so

(\mathcal{Q}A\mathcal{Q}^{-1})(\mathcal{Q}(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty})\mathcal{Q}^{-1})=(\mathcal{Q}(I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty})\mathcal{Q}^{-1})(\mathcal{Q}A\mathcal{Q}^{-1}),

and hence we can write

A=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}M_{g}\mathcal{Q}=T^{-1}\mathcal{F}M_{g}\mathcal{F}^{-1}T.

We therefore have, for each
f\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle(Af)(t)

\displaystyle=t^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{F}M_{g}\mathcal{F}^{-1}Tf)(\log t)

\displaystyle=t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\infty}^{\infty}(M_{g}\mathcal{F}^{-1}Tf)(y)e^{-iy\log t}\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-iy-\frac{1}{2}}g(iy)(\mathcal{F}^{-1}Tf)(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-iy-\frac{1}{2}}g(iy)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iyx}(Tf)(x)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{-iy-\frac{1}{2}}g(iy)\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u)u^{iy-\frac{1}{2}}du\Big)dy.

The above argument can be reversed and thus any
A
from (
7.2
) belongs to the commutant of
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.
∎

Similar calculations can be made to find an expression for the commutant of
{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
. It is more convenient to work with the adjoint and use (
3.10
) in the form

I-{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{*}=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}M_{\varphi}\mathcal{Q}.

The commutant of
M_{\varphi}
on
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
consists of all multiplication operators
M_{g}
with

g\in H_{+}^{\infty}(i\mathbb{R})
(the boundary function of the bounded analytic functions on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
)
[
12
, p. 122]
, and thus, by (
2.10
), the commutant of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{*}
consists of
all operators

(7.3)

B=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}M_{g}\mathcal{Q}=W^{-1}\mathcal{L}^{-1}M_{g}\mathcal{L}W.

Hence, similar as before, but for
f\in L^{2}(0,1)
we have

\displaystyle(Bf)(t)

\displaystyle=t^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{L}^{-1}M_{g}\mathcal{L}Wf)(-\log t)

\displaystyle=t^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}(M_{g}\mathcal{L}Wf)(s)e^{-s\log t}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}t^{-s-\frac{1}{2}}g(s)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx}(Wf)(x)\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}t^{-s-\frac{1}{2}}g(s)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sx-\frac{x}{2}}f(e^{-x})\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}t^{-s-\frac{1}{2}}g(s)\int_{0}^{1}f(u)u^{s-\frac{1}{2}}\,du\,ds

where
\gamma>0
, as is usual in the definition of the inverse Laplace transform. We state the result as follows.

Proposition 7.4
.

A bounded linear operator
B
in
L^{2}(0,1)
commutes with
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
if and only if there exists a
g\in H_{+}^{\infty}(i\mathbb{R})
such that

(Bf)(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}t^{-s-\frac{1}{2}}g(s)\Big(\int_{0}^{1}f(u)u^{s-\frac{1}{2}}du\Big)ds

for all
f\in L^{2}(0,1)
,
where
\gamma>0
.

Observe that a similar expression can be derived for the commutant of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
by
taking adjoints,
although it now involves the conjugate Toeplitz operator
T_{\overline{g}}
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
.

8.
Frame vectors

8.1.
Basic notation

For a bounded linear operator
T
a separable Hilbert space
H
, a vector

{\bf v}\in H
is a
frame vector
for
T
if the sequence
(T^{n}{\bf v})_{n\geqslant 0}

forms a frame. In other words, if there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

(8.1)

c_{1}\|{\bf x}\|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\langle{\bf x},T^{n}{\bf v}\rangle|^{2}\leqslant c_{2}\|{\bf x}\|^{2}\;\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x}\in H$}.

The optimal
c_{1},c_{2}
above are called the
frame bounds
.
It is known (see
[
21
, Chap. 4]
) that an equivalent condition for a sequence

({\bf v}_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
to be a frame on a Hilbert space
H
is that the mapping from
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
to
H
defined by

(8.2)

(a_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}{\bf v}_{n}

is bounded and surjective.

8.2.
Frame vectors for
S
and
M_{\varphi}

Recently, Cabrelli, Molter, Paternostro, and Philipp studied frame vectors for operators in
[
5
]
. In particular,

[
5
, Thm. 3.3]
asserts that for
T
to possess a frame vector
it is necessary that
T^{*}
be similar to a strongly stable contraction, namely,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|T^{*n}{\bf y}\|=0\;\;\mbox{ for all ${\bf y}\in H$.}

By the uniform boundedness principle, it follows that such
T
must satisfy

\sup_{n\geqslant 0}\|T^{n}\|<\infty

and hence, by the spectral radius formula,
\sigma(T)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.
Thus, we cannot hope to find frame vectors for the Hardy operators
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
, since, as discussed in the introduction, their spectra are not contained
in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.
However, also discussed in the introduction, we know that
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
is unitarily equivalent to the
unilateral shift
S
on
H^{2}
and
I-\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{*}
is equivalent to the a bilateral shift on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
.
Now
S
on
H^{2}
does possess frame vectors (as we shall demonstrate below), but
S^{*}
does not, nor does the bilateral shift on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. These negative results follow from the strong stability
criterion above but can also be proved directly, simply by noting that for such operators
T
we cannot have
a uniform lower bound

c_{1}\|{\bf x}\|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\langle T^{*n}{\bf x},{\bf v}\rangle|^{2}

if we let
{\bf x}
range over functions
e_{k}
with
e_{k}(z)=z^{k}
.

The following result is a special case of
[
6
, Prop. 3.8]
. For the sake of completeness, we provide its short proof.

Proposition 8.3
.

For the unilateral shift
(Sf)(z)=zf(z)
on
H^{2}
the following are equivalent for
u\in H^{2}
:

(a)

u
is a frame vector for
S
;

(b)

u
and
1/u
belong to
H^{\infty}
, the bounded analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

Take
u\in H^{2}
, with
u(z)=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}u_{k}z^{k}
and consider the condition described in (
8.2
).
Then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}S^{n}u=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}S^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{k}z^{k}\Big)=f(z)u(z),

where
f(z)=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}a_{k}z^{k}\in H^{2}
. Since
(a_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}\in\ell^{2}
(the sequence of Taylor coefficients of an
H^{2}
function belong to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
),
the mapping

(a_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}\mapsto\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{n}S^{n}u

from
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
to
H^{2}

is bounded and surjective precisely when
uH^{2}=H^{2}
. By standard facts about multipliers of
H^{2}

[
12
, p. 121]
, this holds if and only if
u
and
1/u
belong to
H^{\infty}
, the bounded analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
.
∎

If the sequence
(S^{n}u)_{n\geqslant 0}
forms a frame, then it
even forms a Riesz basis, since with
u
as in Proposition
8.3

the vectors are the image of the standard orthonormal basis
(z^{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
under
the bounded isomorphism
f\mapsto uf
.

It remains for us to describe the frame vectors for
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
. From our discussion from §
3
we know that
M_{\varphi}\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}(I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})
, where

\varphi(\beta)=\frac{\beta-\frac{1}{2}}{\beta+\frac{1}{2}}

and
\mathcal{Q}:L^{2}(0,1)\to H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
is defined by

(\mathcal{Q}f)(z)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{z-\frac{1}{2}}f(t)dt.

Recall that we also have
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{L}W
as in (
2.8
).
We shall use the transform from
\mathbb{D}
onto the right half plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}
defined by

z=\frac{\beta-\frac{1}{2}}{\beta+\frac{1}{2}},\quad\beta=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1+z}{1-z},

for
z\in\mathbb{D}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
.
As we saw in (
3.3
), an
isomorphism
J:H^{2}\to H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
is given by

(Jf)(\beta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\beta+\frac{1}{2}}f\Big(\frac{\beta-\frac{1}{2}}{\beta+\frac{1}{2}}\Big),\quad f\in H^{2},\quad\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

with inverse

(J^{-1}F)(z)=\sqrt{2\pi}\frac{1}{1-z}F\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\Big)\Big),\quad F\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+}),\quad z\in\mathbb{D},

and we note that
J^{-1}M_{\varphi}J=M_{z}=S
, the unilateral shift on
H^{2}
.

This yields the following result.

Corollary 8.4
.

A function
F\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
is a frame vector for
M_{\varphi}
if and only if
u:=J^{-1}F

satisfies
u,1/u\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
.

8.3.
Frame vectors for
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}

With Corollary
8.4
in hand, we can provide explicit examples of frame vectors for
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
.

Example 8.5
.

Consider the functions
t\mapsto t^{\alpha}
which lie in
L^{2}(0,1)
when
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
. We claim
they are frame vectors for
1-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
.
As discussed earlier, they correspond via
\mathcal{Q}
to

F_{\alpha}(\beta)=\frac{1}{\beta+\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{+}).

Now

\displaystyle J^{-1}F_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-z}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}\frac{1+z}{1-z}+\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}(1+z)+(1-z)(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha+1-\alpha z},

which is an invertible function in
H^{\infty}
since its zero
(\alpha+1)/\alpha

lies outside
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
when
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
.
Particular examples are
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1
, giving

(J^{-1}F_{\alpha})(z)=1
and
(2-z)^{-1}
, respectively (which are both clearly invertible elements of
H^{\infty}
).

Example 8.6
.

As a special case of the previous example, consider the constant function
\chi\equiv 1
on
[0,1]
. This will correspond to the constant function
1
on
\mathbb{D}
(Example
8.5
). Thus, the sequence

((I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})^{n}\chi)_{n\geqslant 0}
is a frame for
L^{2}(0,1)
.
In fact

(8.7)

((I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})^{n}\chi)(x)=L_{n}(-\log x),\quad n\geqslant 0,\quad 0<x\leqslant 1,

where
L_{n}
is the
n
th Laguerre polynomial discussed earlier. Clearly this is true when
n=0
. Now, assuming the result for
n=k
we have

((I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})^{k+1}\chi)(x)=L_{k}(-\log x)-\int_{x}^{1}\frac{L_{k}(-\log x)}{x}\,dx

or, setting
w=-\log x
,

((I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})^{k+1}\chi)(e^{-w})=L_{k}(w)-\int_{0}^{w}L_{k}(w)\,dw=L_{k+1}(w)

by an identity equivalent to (
3.17
). Thus, we have (
8.7
) by induction, and
an integral substitution shows that the
functions
(L_{n}(-\log x))_{n\geqslant 0}
are an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(0,1)
.

8.4.
Lack of frame vectors for the discrete Cesàro operator

The discrete Cesàro operator is defined on
H^{2}
by

(\mathcal{C}f)(z)=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{f(\xi)}{1-\xi}\,d\xi,

or equivalently, via power series, by

\mathcal{C}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\Big)=a_{0}+\frac{a_{0}+a_{1}}{2}z+\frac{a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}}{3}z^{2}+\cdots.

For more information about the discrete Cesàro operator, see, for example
[
26
]
and the references therein.
Work of Kriete and Trutt

[
18
,
19
]
produced a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}
of analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
that contains the polynomials as a dense set and such that

I-\mathcal{C}
is unitarily equivalent to
M_{z}
(multiplication by
z
) on

\mathfrak{H}
.

The same argument used to classify the frame vectors for the shift
S
on
H^{2}
(Proposition
8.3
), along with the fact from
[
19
, Thm. 4]
that
H^{2}\subseteq\mathfrak{H}
with continuous inclusion, says that
u\in\mathfrak{H}
will be a frame vector for
M_{z}
on
\mathfrak{H}
if and only if
uH^{2}=\mathfrak{H}
. This will never happen as can be seen by the following argument: If
uH^{2}=\mathfrak{H}
, then, since the constant functions belong to
\mathfrak{H}
, it follows that the function
u
has no zeros in
\mathbb{D}
. This means that the zero sequences for
uH^{2}
functions (sequences
A\subseteq\mathbb{D}
for which there is an
f\in uH^{2}\setminus\{0\}
with
f|_{A}=0
) must also be zero sequences for
H^{2}
. Classical Hardy space theory says that the zero sequences for
H^{2}
are the so-called Blaschke sequences. However, from
[
19
, §4]
there are nonzero functions in
\mathfrak{H}
that vanish on non-Blaschke sequences. Since there are no frame vectors for
M_{z}
on
\mathfrak{H}
, there are no frame vectors for
I-\mathcal{C}
.

8.5.
Frame vectors and the commutant

As it turns out from
[
6
, Proposition 3.8]
, one can, in a way, obtain
all
frame vectors from a single one. The general result for a bounded Hilbert space operator
T
on
H
is the following: If
{\bf x}_{0}
is a particular frame vector for
T
, then any frame vector
{\bf x}
for
T
must be of the form
V{\bf x}_{0}
, where
V
is a bounded invertible operator on
H
that also belongs to the commutant of
T
. We thank Victor Bailey for pointing this paper out to us.

The commutant of the unilateral shift is
\psi(S)
, where
\psi\in H^{\infty}
(which can also be written as
T_{\psi}f=\psi f

[
12
, p. 122]
) and
\psi(S)
is invertible if and only if
1/\psi\in H^{\infty}
.
This means that the commutant of
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
is
\psi(I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})
, where
\psi\in H^{\infty}
. Since we know that
\chi\equiv 1
is a frame vector for
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
(Example
8.6
) we have the following corollary.

Corollary 8.8
.

An
f\in L^{2}(0,1)
is a frame vector for
I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
if and only if there is a
\psi\in H^{\infty}
with
1/\psi\in H^{\infty}
such that
f=\psi(I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})\chi
.

Note that an expression for
\psi(I-\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*})
can be obtained from
Proposition
3.11
and in particular
(
3.10
), which implies an explicit unitary equivalence with
M_{\psi\circ\varphi}

on
H^{2}_{+}(i\mathbb{R})
.
