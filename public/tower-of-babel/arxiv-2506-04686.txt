Characterization of Hilbertizable spaces via convex functions
Nicolas Borchard, Gerd Wachsmuth

Abstract
We show that the existence of a strongly convex function with a Lipschitz derivative on a Banach space already implies that the space is isomorphic to a Hilbert space. Similarly, if both a function and its convex conjugate are C 2 C^{2} then the underlying space is also isomorphic to a Hilbert space.

1
Introduction

Convex functions which are strongly convex and which have a Lipschitz continuous derivative
are very useful
for, e.g., analyzing the convergence of first-order optimization methods.
In particular, it allows for an estimate of the convergence rate of
the gradient method, see
Nesterov 2004
, Theorem 2.1.15
.
This theorem is posed in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
but can be generalized to Hilbert spaces.
One could pose the question
whether it can be further generalized to arbitrary Banach spaces.
However, we will show that
the existence of a strongly convex function with Lipschitz continuous derivative
already implies that the space is isomorphic to a Hilbert space,
see
Theorem

2.5
.
Conversely,
given a space which is not isomorphic to a Hilbert space
(like
\ell^{p}
or
L^{p}(0,1)
for
p\neq 2
),
it is not possible to construct a strongly convex function
which has a Lipschitz continuous derivative.

There have been other characterizations of Hilbertizable spaces,
i.e., spaces which are isomorphic to a Hilbert space.
In the context of infinite-dimensional optimization,
it was shown in
Harder 2018

that if a Legendre form exists on a reflexive Banach space, then the space is
isomorphic to a Hilbert space.

Properties of uniformly convex and uniformly smooth convex functions
(which generalize strongly convex functions and convex functions with Lipschitz derivatives)
can be found in

Aze & Penot 1995
and
Zalinescu 2002
, Section 3.5
.
In particular,
these results can be applied to strongly convex functions
and convex functions with Lipschitz derivatives.

We extend
Theorem

2.5

to monotone operators,
that is, the existence of a strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous operator
implies that the underlying space is Hilbertizable,
see
Theorem

2.6
.
Note that the existence of such an operator
on a Banach space
has been assumed in some works in the literature,
see, e.g.,

Izuchukwu et al. 2022
, Theorems 4.13, 4.14 under conditions (a)*, (b)

and

Mendy & Mendy 2023
, Theorem 3.1
.
Hence, our findings show that
these results are only applicable in Hilbertizable spaces.

Finally,
in
Theorem

3.1

we show
that the existence of a convex function
f

such that
f
and its convex conjugate
f^{*}

are both
C^{2}
,
implies Hilbertizablility.

2
Strong convexity and Lipschitz derivative

In the next assumption,
we fix the class of functions
which are locally strongly convex and have a Lipschitz derivative.

Assumption 2.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space,

f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be a function,

\bar{x}\in X
and

\varepsilon>0
.
On the open ball in
X
centered at
\bar{x}
with a radius of
\varepsilon
, i.e.
U_{\varepsilon}^{X}(\bar{x})
,
let
f
be of class
C^{1}
,
\mu

L>0
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)-\frac{\mu}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\lVert x-y\rVert_{X}^{2},

\displaystyle\lVert f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y)\rVert_{X^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq L\lVert x-y\rVert_{X}

holds for all
x,y\in U_{\varepsilon}^{X}(\bar{x})

and all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

It is well known that the strong convexity of

implies

\mu\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}\leq\langle f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(y),x-y\rangle_{X}\qquad\forall x,y\in U_{\varepsilon}^{X}(\bar{x}).

(2.1)

Indeed,
from the strong convexity we get

\frac{f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)-f(y)}{\lambda}\leq f(x)-f(y)-\frac{\mu}{2}(1-\lambda)\lVert x-y\rVert_{X}^{2}

and
\lambda\searrow 0
implies

\langle f^{\prime}(y),x-y\rangle_{X}\leq f(x)-f(y)-\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}.

and

First, we check on every finite-dimensional subspace of
X
,
we can define an equivalent norm
and the constants of equivalence are bounded by
\sqrt{\mu}
and
\sqrt{L}

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
2.1
be fulfilled.
For every finite-dimensional subspace
U
of
X
,
there exists an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{U}:U\times U\to\mathbb{R}

such that the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{U}
are equivalent.
In particular,

\sqrt{\mu}\lVert x\rVert_{X}\leq\lVert x\rVert_{U}\leq\sqrt{L}\lVert x\rVert_{X}\qquad\forall x\in U

and all finite-dimensional subspaces
U
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let
\bar{x}=0
.
We define the function
g:=f|_{U}:U\to\mathbb{R}
.
Obviously,
g
is strongly convex and has a Lipschitz derivative
in a neighborhood of
0
and it inherits the constants of
f
.
As
g^{\prime}
is a locally Lipschitz continuous function between finite-dimensional spaces,
we can employ the well-known Rademacher theorem and obtain
\bar{u}\in U
such that
g^{\prime}
is differentiable in
\bar{u}
.
We set
A:=g^{\prime\prime}(\bar{u}):U\times U\to\mathbb{R}
.
Note that
A
is symmetric due to
Cartan 1967
, Theorem 5.1.1
.
Since
g
is twice differentiable at
\bar{u}
,
we have

\frac{g^{\prime}(\bar{u}+th)-g^{\prime}(\bar{u})}{t}\to A[h,\cdot]\qquad\text{as }t\to 0

for each fixed
h\in U
.
From the Lipschitz continuity of
f^{\prime}
and

\Hy@safe@activesfalse

we get

\mu\lVert h\rVert_{X}^{2}\leq\left\langle\frac{g^{\prime}(\bar{u}+th)-g^{\prime}(\bar{u})}{t},h\right\rangle_{U}=\left\langle\frac{f^{\prime}(\bar{u}+th)-f^{\prime}(\bar{u})}{t},h\right\rangle_{X}\leq L\lVert h\rVert_{X}^{2}.

Consequently,

\mu\lVert h\rVert_{X}^{2}\leq A[h,h]\leq L\lVert h\rVert_{X}^{2}\qquad\forall h\in U.

In total,
A
is an inner product
as it is bilinear, symmetric, and positive-definite.
The norm generated by
A
is equivalent to the original norm
with constants
\sqrt{\mu}
and
\sqrt{L}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Ledoux & Talagrand 1991
, Section 9.2

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
E(n):=\{-1,1\}^{n}
.
A Banach space
X
is said to be of type
q\in[1,2]
if
there exists a constant
C>0

such that

\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\varepsilon\in E(n)}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}x_{k}\right\rVert_{X}^{q}\leq C^{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert x_{k}\rVert_{X}^{q}

holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
.
The infimum over all possible constants is denoted by
T_{q}(X)
.
Similarly, it is of cotype
q\in[2,\infty)
if
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\frac{1}{C^{q}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert x_{k}\rVert_{X}^{q}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\varepsilon\in E(n)}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}x_{k}\right\rVert_{X}^{q}

holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
.
The infimum over all possible constants is denoted by
C_{q}(X)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space.
Is is known from
Ledoux & Talagrand 1991
, p. 247
that

L^{p}(\mu)
for
p\in[1,2]

is of type
q
for all
q\in[p,2]

and of cotype
q
for all
q\in\{2\}
.
Similarly,

L^{p}(\mu)
for
p\in[2,\infty)

is of type
q
for all
q\in\{2\}

and of cotype
q
for all
q\in[p,\infty)
.
In the case that
L^{p}(\mu)
is infinite-dimensional,
it is of no other type or cotype.
This covers the special cases
L^{p}(\Omega)
(for
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the Lebesgue measure)
and
\ell^{p}
.

An easy calculation shows that
any Hilbert space
H
has type
2
and cotype
2
,
and we have
C_{2}(H)=T_{2}(H)=1

(unless
H=\{0\}
).
It is easy to check that the type and cotype of a Banach space
does not change if one uses an equivalent norm.
Hence,
any space which is isomorphic to a Hilbert space
also has type
2
and cotype
2
.
In particular,
the spaces
\ell^{p}
are not isomorphic to a Hilbert space
for
p\neq 2
.
The same applies to
L^{p}(\mu)
if it is infinite dimensional.

Moreover, the next result shows that the property of
having type
2
and cotype
2

already
characterizes Hilbertizable spaces.

Theorem 2.4
.

Kwapie\’n 1972
, Prop. 3.1

A space is isomorphic to a Hilbert space if and only if it is of type
2
and cotype
2
.

Note that in
Kwapie\’n 1972
, the names type and cotype were not yet established.
Kwapień used the notion of the so called two-sided Khinchine inequality.
We also refer to
Yamasaki 1984
for a simple proof of Kwapień’s theorem.
From this proof, one obtains that
for every space
X
with type
2
and cotype
2
,
there exists a Hilbert space
such that
the Banach–Mazur distance
between
X
and
H

is bounded by the product
T_{2}(X)C_{2}(X)
.

Now, we are in position to prove our main result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
2.1
be fulfilled.
Then,
X
is isomorphic to a Hilbert space.

Proof.

We use
Theorem

2.4
.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X

be arbitrary.
We set
U:=\lin(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
We show the second inequality first.
As
U
is finite-dimensional,

Lemma

2.2
can be applied
and yields a scalar product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{U}
on
U
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2^{n}}\sum_{\varepsilon\in E(n)}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}x_{k}\right\rVert_{X}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\varepsilon\in E(n)}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}x_{k}\right\rVert_{U}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\varepsilon\in E(n)}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert x_{k}\rVert_{U}^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l=1\\
k\neq l\end{subarray}}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}\varepsilon_{l}(x_{k},x_{l})_{U}\right)

The first sum inside the brackets does not depend on
\varepsilon
anymore, thus the same sum is added
2^{n}
times.
The second inner sum can be swapped with the outer sum over
\varepsilon

and, due to the definition of
E(n)
, we have

\sum_{\varepsilon\in E_{n}}\varepsilon_{k}\varepsilon_{l}=0
for
k\neq l
.
Consequently, the second inner sum disappears.
Thus we get

\frac{\mu}{2^{n}}\sum_{\varepsilon\in E(n)}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k}x_{k}\right\rVert_{X}^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert x_{k}\rVert_{U}^{2}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l=1\\
k\neq l\end{subarray}}^{n}(2^{n-1}-2^{n-1})(x_{k},x_{l})_{U}\right)\leq L\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert x_{k}\rVert_{X}^{2}.

Since the constants
\mu
and
L

do not depend on the subspace
U
,
this shows that
X
is of type
2
.
Analogously, one can reverse all these inequalities while swapping
\mu
and
L
.
Thus
X
is of cotype
2
.

Theorem

2.4
yields the claim.
∎

From the proof, we see that the Banach–Mazur distance of
X

to a Hilbert space can be bounded by
\sqrt{L/\mu}
.

Finally,
we check that these considerations
can be slightly generalized.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
X
be a Banach space.
Further, let an operator
T\colon X\to X^{*}
be given
such that
T
is Lipschitz continuous and strongly monotone
on
U_{\varepsilon}^{X}(\bar{x})
for some
\bar{x}\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
,
i.e.,
there exist
\mu,L>0

such that

\displaystyle\lVert T(x)-T(y)\rVert_{X^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq L\lVert x-y\rVert_{X},

\displaystyle\langle T(x)-T(y),x-y\rangle_{X}

\displaystyle\geq\mu\lVert x-y\rVert_{X}^{2}

holds for all
x,y\in U_{\varepsilon}^{X}(\bar{x})
.
Then
X
is isomorphic to a Hilbert space.

Proof.

We briefly check that the assertion of
Lemma

2.2

is valid in our situation.
In fact, we can repeat the proof of
Lemma

2.2

with some minor modifications.
For a finite-dimensional subspace
U
, we consider the operator

S\colon U\to U^{*}
,
U\ni u\mapsto T(u)|_{U}\in U^{*}
.
Due to Rademacher’s theorem,
S
is differentiable at some
\bar{u}\in U

and we set
B:=S^{\prime}(\bar{u})\in\mathcal{L}(U,U^{*})
.
The only difference to the situation in
Lemma

2.2

is that the bilinear form associated with
B
might not be symmetric,
but we can symmetrize
A:=(B+B^{*})/2
.
The remaining steps of the proof are identical.
∎

3
Function and its conjugate being
C^{2}

In this section we consider the case that a function and its convex conjugate
are twice continuously differentiable.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space,

f\in C^{2}(X)
is convex
and its convex conjugate satisfies
f^{*}\in C^{2}(X^{*})
.
Then,
X
is isomorphic to a Hilbert space.

In the case that
X
is additionally reflexive,
we give a simple and self-contained proof.

Proof (
X
reflexive).

Let
\bar{x}\in X
be arbitrary.
We define

A:=f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})\in\mathcal{L}(X,X^{*})
and
B:=(f^{*})^{\prime\prime}(f^{\prime}(\bar{x}))\in\mathcal{L}(X^{*},X)
.

It is well-known that
x^{*}\in\partial f(x)
and
x\in\partial f^{*}(x^{*})
are equivalent.
This and the differentiability imply

x=(f^{*})^{\prime}(f^{\prime}(x))
for all
x\in X

and

x^{*}=f^{\prime}((f^{*})^{\prime}(x^{*}))
for all
x^{*}\in X^{*}
.
We differentiate this and obtain

\id_{X}=(f^{*})^{\prime\prime}(f^{\prime}(\bar{x}))f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})=BA

and

\id_{X^{*}}=AB
.
In particular,
A
and
B
are invertible.
Note that this argument fails in case that
X
is not reflexive.

We define the function
g:X\to\mathbb{R}
via

g(x):=\frac{1}{2}A[x,x]:=\frac{1}{2}\langle Ax,x\rangle_{X}
.
The symmetry of
A[\cdot,\cdot]
implies

\langle A^{*}x,y\rangle_{X}=\langle Ay,x\rangle_{X}=A[y,x]=A[x,y]=\langle Ax,y\rangle_{X}

for all
x,y\in X

and thus self-adjointness.
Similarly,
B
is self-adjoint.
We calculate the convex conjugate of
g
by

\displaystyle g^{*}(x^{*})

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in X}\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X}-\frac{1}{2}A[x,x]=\sup_{x\in X}\frac{1}{2}B[x^{*},x^{*}]-\frac{1}{2}A[Bx^{*}-x,Bx^{*}-x]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}B[x^{*},x^{*}].

Here, we used the symmetry of
A
and
\id_{X^{*}}=AB
.
Further, the derivative of
g^{*}
is given by

(g^{*})^{\prime}(x^{*})=\frac{1}{2}Bx^{*}+\frac{1}{2}B^{*}x^{*}=Bx^{*}
which is a Lipschitz continuous function.
It is well-known that this implies strong convexity of
g
for some constant
\mu>0
,
see, e.g.,
Zalinescu 2002
, Proposition 3.5.3
.

Strong convexity of
g
and
g^{\prime}(0)=A(0)=0
yield

\frac{1}{2}A[x,x]=g(x)-g(0)-g^{\prime}(0)(x-0)\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert x-0\rVert_{X}^{2}.

Thus, we get

\mu\lVert x\rVert_{X}^{2}\leq A[x,x]\leq\lVert A\rVert\lVert x\rVert_{X}^{2}
.
This shows that
A[\cdot,\cdot]
is an inner product on
X

and the associated norm is equivalent to the original norm.
∎

Note that the function
g
constructed in the proof fulfills
2.1
,
but we do not employ
Theorem

2.5

explicitly.

In absence of the reflexivity of
X

we show that the assumptions of
Theorem

3.1

imply
2.1

and this yields a proof of
Theorem

3.1

in the general situation.

Proof (general case).

W.l.o.g., we assume
0=f^{\prime}(0)

(otherwise we subtract the linear function
f^{\prime}(0)\in X^{*}
from
f
).
As
f^{*}
is
C^{2}
, there exist constants
\varepsilon,L>0
with
\lVert(f^{*})^{\prime\prime}(x^{*})\rVert\leq L
for all
x^{*}\in B_{\varepsilon}^{X^{*}}(0)
.
Thus, for all
x^{*},y^{*}\in B_{\varepsilon}^{X^{*}}(0)
it holds

\lVert(f^{*})^{\prime}(x^{*})-(f^{*})^{\prime}(y^{*})\rVert_{X^{**}}\leq\sup_{\xi\in[x^{*},y^{*}]}\lVert(f^{*})^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\rVert\lVert x^{*}-y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}\leq L\lVert x^{*}-y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}},

where we used the mean-value inequality,
see
Cartan 1967
, Proposition 3.3.1
.
Thus,
(f^{*})^{\prime}
is locally Lipschitz at
0
.

We are going to prove a local descent lemma.
Let
\varphi(t):=f^{*}(x^{*}+t(y^{*}-x^{*}))
. Obviously,
\varphi
is
C^{1}
.
Thus, for
x^{*},y^{*}\in B_{\varepsilon}^{X^{*}}(0)

we have

\displaystyle f^{*}(y^{*})-f^{*}(x^{*})-\langle(f^{*})^{\prime}(x^{*}),y^{*}-x^{*}\rangle_{X^{*}}=\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)-\varphi^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi^{\prime}(t)-\varphi^{\prime}(0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}\langle(f^{*})^{\prime}(x^{*}+t(y^{*}-x^{*}))-(f^{*})^{\prime}(x^{*}),y^{*}-x^{*}\rangle_{X^{*}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

As
x^{*}+t(y^{*}-x^{*})
and
x^{*}
are both in
B_{\varepsilon}^{X^{*}}(0)
, we can use the local Lipschitz continuity and get

\displaystyle f^{*}(y^{*})-f^{*}(x^{*})-\langle(f^{*})^{\prime}(x^{*}),y^{*}-x^{*}\rangle_{X^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}Lt\lVert y^{*}-x^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\frac{L}{2}\lVert y^{*}-x^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}.

As
f^{\prime}
is continuous, we find
\delta>0
with

\lVert f^{\prime}(x)\rVert_{X^{*}}=\lVert f^{\prime}(x)-f^{\prime}(0)\rVert_{X^{*}}\leq\varepsilon/2

for all
x
with
\lVert x-0\rVert_{X}\leq\delta
.

Now, we can show local strong convexity.
Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
x,y\in B_{\min(\delta,\varepsilon L/4)}^{X}(0)
be given.
Further, let
x^{*},y^{*}\in B_{\varepsilon/2}^{X^{*}}(0)
be arbitrary.
For these we have

\lVert x^{*}-\lambda y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}},\lVert x^{*}+(1-\lambda)y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}\leq\lVert x^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}+\lVert y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}\leq\varepsilon

which allows us to use the above local descent lemma.
Together with the Fenchel–Young inequality, this yields

\displaystyle\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\left[\langle x^{*}+(1-\lambda)y^{*},x\rangle-f^{*}(x^{*}+(1-\lambda)y^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-\lambda)\left[\langle x^{*}-\lambda y^{*},y\rangle-f^{*}(x^{*}-\lambda y^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\left[\langle x^{*}+(1-\lambda)y^{*},x\rangle-f^{*}(x^{*})-\langle(f^{*})^{\prime}(x^{*}),(1-\lambda)y^{*}\rangle-\frac{L}{2}\lVert(1-\lambda)y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-\lambda)\left[\langle x^{*}-\lambda y^{*},y\rangle-f^{*}(x^{*})-\langle(f^{*})^{\prime}(x^{*}),-\lambda y^{*}\rangle-\frac{L}{2}\lVert-\lambda y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda\langle x^{*}+(1-\lambda)y^{*},x\rangle+(1-\lambda)\langle x^{*}-\lambda y^{*},y\rangle-f^{*}(x^{*})-\frac{L}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\lVert y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big[\langle x^{*},\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\rangle-f^{*}(x^{*})\big]+\lambda(1-\lambda)\left[\langle y^{*},x-y\rangle-\frac{L}{2}\lVert y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}\right]

for all
x^{*},y^{*}\in B_{\varepsilon/2}^{X^{*}}(0)
.
Due to

\lVert\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\rVert\leq\delta
,
we set

x^{*}=f^{\prime}(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\in B_{\varepsilon/2}^{X^{*}}(0)

and with this choice, the first bracketed term on the last line
equals
f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)
.

Similarly,
we select an arbitrary

y^{*}\in\partial(\frac{1}{2L}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X}^{2})(x-y)\neq\emptyset
.
Together with
(\frac{1}{2L}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X}^{2})^{*}=\frac{L}{2}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}
,
we get

\frac{L}{2}\lVert y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2L}\lVert x-y\rVert_{X}^{2}=\langle y^{*},x-y\rangle\leq\lVert y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}\lVert x-y\rVert_{X}

It is easy to check that this implies

\lVert y^{*}\rVert_{X^{*}}=\frac{1}{L}\lVert x-y\rVert_{X}\leq\frac{2}{L}\frac{\varepsilon L}{4}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

and, thus, we are allowed to insert this
y^{*}
above.
The second bracketed term becomes

\frac{1}{2L}\lVert x-y\rVert_{X}^{2}
.
Altogether, this shows

\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)\geq f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)+\frac{1}{2L}\lambda(1-\lambda)\lVert x-y\rVert_{X}^{2},

i.e., strong convexity on
B_{\min(\delta,\varepsilon L/4)}(0)
.
In total,
the function
f
is strongly convex with constant
1/L
on that ball and we can find a smaller ball where
f^{\prime}
is locally Lipschitz as
f
is
C^{2}
.
∎

From these proofs,
it is also clear that
the assumptions of
Theorem

3.1

can be localized.
Indeed, it is sufficient that
f
is
C^{2}
in a neighborhood of some
x\in X
,
while
f^{*}
is
C^{2}
in a neighborhood of
f^{\prime}(x)
.
