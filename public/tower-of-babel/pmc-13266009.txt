Protocol to interrogate the role of creeping fat and mechanical tension in Crohn’s disease using a surgical mouse model of intestinal fibrosis
Authors: Elijah J. Suh, Benjamin Pham, Khristian E. Bauer-Rowe, Alexia Kim, Norah E. Liang, Joseph Y. Park, Deshka S. Foster, Jeffrey A. Norton, Daniel Delitto, Michael T. Longaker, Jeong S. Hyun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol to interrogate the role of creeping fat (CF) and mechanical tension in Crohn’s disease (CD) using a surgical mouse model of intestinal fibrosis. We describe steps for preoperative preparation, microsurgical techniques for stricture formation and mechanical stretching, and model variations such as mesenteric grafts. We then detail procedures for postoperative harvest, tissue embedding, and sectioning procedures. This model induces localized CF formation and transmural fibrosis, features distinct from those observed in chemical colitis models.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Bauer-Rowe et al.1

Before you begin

This protocol describes the creation of a surgical model of intestinal fibrosis in mice designed to interrogate the role of creeping fat (CF) and mechanical tension in Crohn’s disease (CD). By creating a longitudinal colotomy closed under tension, this model induces localized CF formation and transmural fibrosis, features distinct from those observed in chemical colitis models. We detail the preoperative preparation, the specific microsurgical techniques for stricture formation and mechanical stretching, model variations, post-operative harvest, and tissue embedding and sectioning procedures. We describe the surgery in 8–12-weeks-old C57BL/6 mice.

Innovation

A major barrier to studying intestinal strictures is the inability of current mouse models to replicate this unique phenotype. Existing CD animal models typically use caustic agents to induce inflammation,2,3 which require long treatment regimens and likely replicate an acute injury rather than the transmural, mechanically-driven strictures seen in advanced CD. One model generated CF using repeated endoscopic punch biopsies but required specialized equipment.4 We developed a novel and accessible surgical model that relies on transmural injury and mechanical tension to induce a stricture-like phenotype. This model’s innovation lies in its incorporation of mechanical tension by stretching the bowel during the colotomy closure, mimicking high mechanical tension in fibrotic tissue, to consistently produce CF and intestinal fibrosis. Thus, the model facilitates the study of late-stage, mechanically-driven fibrosis.

Timing: 30–45 min

Expected outcomes

In the standard model, creeping fat is expected to localize exclusively to the colotomy sites, with initial wrapping evident by POD 7 and maximal involvement by POD 14 (Figure 3A). Sham-operated or uninvolved bowel segments should not demonstrate fat wrapping. At the colotomy site, the bowel wall should show transmural fibrosis and an expanded muscularis propria layer (Figure 3B). Under these conditions, approximately 70% of colotomies are anticipated to develop creeping fat, whereas the remaining 30% may instead form visceral adhesions. For histological measurements, we quantified bowel wall thickness, muscularis propria thickness, trichrome positive area, MAT circumference, and mesenteric angle (Figure 3C). Bowel wall thickness was defined as the distance between the top of the submucosa and the first MAT adipocytes. Approximately 15 different lengths were measured and averaged within each serial section from bowel covered by MAT unless otherwise specified. To measure muscularis propria expansion, approximately 15 different lengths were measured and averaged within each serial section from bowel covered by MAT unless otherwise specified. To measure trichrome positive area, blue trichrome area was quantified in the bowel wall adjacent to MAT between the top of the submucosa to the first MAT adipocyte using Threshold Color on ImageJ with the same settings applied across all images in the same experiment. To capture mesenteric fat wrapping, we measured the total circumference by quantifying the area of MAT-covered bowel was divided by total bowel circumference and mesenteric angle, where the leading edges of MAT were identified and the angle between the leading edges and a point halfway between them on the bowel was then measured. For each metric, we used 2-5 serial sections for each experimental condition and at least 3 biological replicates. The unstretched colotomies should display markedly reduced transmural fibrosis and substantially less CF wrapping compared with the stretched standard model. Limitations

The procedure requires precise microsurgical skills, particularly the application of mechanical tension. Inter-operator variability in the duration or intensity of stretching can impact the severity of fibrosis. Additionally, approximately 30% of surgeries may result in non-specific visceral adhesions (to adjacent bowel) rather than true creeping fat wrapping; these samples fail to recapitulate the specific CF-bowel interaction and must be subsequently excluded.

As noted in our study, this model relies on a discrete surgical event (colotomy followed by tension) to induce fibrosis. Unlike chemical colitis models (e.g., DSS, TNBS) or spontaneous genetic models (e.g., SAMP1/YitFc), this model lacks a chronic, relapsing-remitting inflammatory background. Consequently, while it effectively isolates the mechanical drivers of creeping fat and stricture formation, it may not capture the early immunological events or the spatiotemporal dynamics of fibroblast emergence seen in chronic inflammation.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

There is tearing of the bowel during the surgery in step 14 from applying tension too rapidly or using suture material that is too thick/thin.

Potential solution

Apply tension gradually over 30–60 seconds before locking the hemostats for the 15-min duration. Ensure 8-0 nylon is used; thinner sutures may cut through the tissue (cheese-wire effect), while thicker sutures may cause excessive trauma.

Problem 2

There is high mortality prior to post-operative day 7 prior to step 18, potentially caused by bowel obstructions from excessive luminal narrowing during closure, or bowel perforation and leakage from loose sutures or tissue tearing.

Potential solution

Ensure the incision is closed transversely (suturing top-to-bottom relative to the longitudinal cut) rather than longitudinally. Use the stay sutures to keep the lumen wide open during closure. Verify luminal patency intra-operatively by forcing a fecal pellet past the suture line before closing the abdomen. If a leak is found, place an additional interrupted 8-0 suture. Avoid grasping the bowel wall directly with toothed forceps during stretching; use the hemostats on the stay sutures instead.

Problem 3

There is a lack of creeping fat (mesenteric adipose tissue) formation due to visceral adhesions found in step 26.

Potential solution

Create the colotomy strictly at a 90° angle to the mesenteric plane to maximize proximity to the fat. Flush the abdominal cavity extensively with sterile PBS throughout the procedure. Ensure the mesentery is draped over the repair site before closing the laparotomy. Even with adequate precautions, up to 30% of mice may develop adhesions.

Problem 4

There is inconsistent fibrosis severity, potentially due to the mechanical stretch being insufficient or inconsistent between mice discovered in step 26.

Potential solution

Use a timer to ensure exactly 15 min of stretching. Use a spacer (e.g., forceps of a fixed width) between the hemostats to maintain “maximal tension” consistently across all animals. The stay sutures should be taut but not ripping through the tissue.
