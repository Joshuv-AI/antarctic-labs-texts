Relevance of autoantibodies to carbonic anhydrase 6 (CA6), parotid secretory protein (PSP) and salivary gland protein 1 (SP1) to dry eye disease (DED)
Authors: Shyamal Raolji, Gurnoor Gill, Pragnya R Donthineni., Chloe Shields, Zachary T Campbell, Sara S McCoy, Anat Galor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the relevance of autoantibodies to carbonic anhydrase 6 (CA6), parotid secretory protein (PSP) and salivary gland protein 1 (SP1) on dry eye disease (DED) symptoms and signs.Methods and analysisA retrospective study of 115 individuals diagnosed with DED and tested for CA6, PSP and SP1 antibodies at the Miami Veterans Eye Clinic. Antibody status (positive: ≥1 antibody ≥20 EU/mL vs negative: all antibodies <20 EU/mL) and levels were analysed against DED metrics at baseline and follow-up (67 individuals).ResultsOf 115 individuals, 73 were antibody positive, with CA6 IgG most frequently elevated. Antibody-positive individuals had more severe DED symptoms (Ocular Surface Disease Index 56.64±22.56 vs 43.89±20.36, p=0.002) and greater tear film instability (5.38±3.52 vs 6.83±4.01 s, p=0.046). These associations remained significant in forward regression analyses. Quantitative antibody levels also related to ocular signs at baseline: corneal staining to SP1 IgA (r=0.36, p<0.001) and PSP IgM (r=0.19, p=0.04) and Schirmer scores to CA6 IgG (r=−0.19, p=0.04). At follow-up, 43% (n=29) showed an increase in DED symptoms and 82% (n=55) in at least one sign, with PSP IgM being most predictive for worsening DED status.ConclusionsOur study suggests that CA6, PSP and SP1 antibody status and levels are relevant in DED. Antibody positivity correlated with more severe baseline symptoms and signs and individual antibody values predicted progression. Rather than markers of a systemic diagnosis, these autoantibodies may better reflect ocular disease severity and progression.

Introduction

Sjögren’s disease (SjD) is a systemic autoimmune condition with an estimated prevalence range of 0.01%–0.05%, predominantly affecting women at an approximate ratio of 14:1.1 It is closely linked to symptoms and signs of dry eye disease (DED), a heterogeneous condition that can be driven by factors such as tear film instability, ocular surface inflammation and neurosensory abnormalities, among other contributors.2 3 Individuals with SjD-associated DED often present with ocular surface inflammation, reduced tear production and epithelial disruption, the latter two of which are part of the 2016 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) criteria for SjD.4 However, many individuals with these DED features do not meet criteria for a SjD diagnosis based on negative anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antigen A (SSA) (Ro) antibodies. This is not surprising as anti-SSA and SSB (La) antibodies often appear in late stages of disease or are absent altogether.5 To enhance the diagnosis and treatment of SjD, it is crucial to detect the disease at earlier stages, ideally when interventions may still modify its progression.

Initially reported in mice, antibodies to carbonic anhydrase 6 (CA6), parotid secretory protein (PSP), and salivary gland protein 1 (SP1) (termed early markers of Sjögren) generated initial excitement, as these antibodies were found in SjD mouse models and patients with DED, potentially indicating their use as predictors of early SjD.6 7 One study evaluated these autoantibodies in patients with long-standing SjD with high levels of anti-Ro antibodies, as well as in patients with SjD secondary to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), systemic sclerosis, and mixed connective tissue disease (CTD). IgA anti-CA6 antibodies were detected in 38% of these patients, whereas anti-SP1, CA6 and PSP IgM or IgG antibodies were identified only in a minority. The study concluded that in secondary SjD, antibodies to SP1, CA6 and PSP were more sensitive and specific than anti-Ro.8 Enthusiasm diminished, however, when CA6, PSP and SP1 were found not to be specific for SjD. In a cross-sectional study of 152 individuals with SjD, 16 individuals with chronic sialadenitis, 46 with CTD (eg, SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, scleroderma), and 36 non-autoimmune controls, a similar proportion of antibody positivity was found across all 4 groups (SjD: 40%, n=61; chronic sialadenitis: 50%, n=8; CTD: 35%, n=16; controls: 44%, n=16), with a sensitivity of 40% and specificity of 56% for SjD.9 Similar findings were reported in other independent cohorts. For instance, one cross-sectional study examined 105 paediatric patients who underwent testing for antibodies against CA6, PSP and SP1, 27 of whom had a confirmed diagnosis of juvenile SjD. In this population, CA6, PSP and SP1 demonstrated low sensitivity (55.6%) and much lower specificity (26.9%) for SjD.10

Despite their limited diagnostic utility for SjD, the potential relevance of CA6, PSP and SP1 antibodies to DED remains incompletely understood. Rather than serving as markers of a systemic autoimmune diagnosis, these antibodies may reflect underlying glandular or ocular surface dysfunction that contributes to DED severity and progression. This distinction raises the possibility that their clinical value lies in phenotyping and risk stratification within DED, rather than in diagnosing SjD.

Building on this distinction, there are several reasons why antibodies to CA6, PSP and SP1 may still be relevant in DED. First, there is biological plausibility as these proteins have relevance in exocrine function. CA6 is an enzyme secreted primarily by the parotid and submandibular glands of mice and humans to buffer saliva in order to maintain pH homeostasis.11 12 CA6 has also been detected in the lacrimal gland of animals, including mice, rats and sheep.13 14 PSP, also secreted by the parotid glands of mice and humans and other salivary glands (eg, sublingual gland) of mice, has antimicrobial properties, modulates saliva viscosity and possibly has anti-inflammatory effects.15 PSP has also been identified in the lacrimal gland of animals, specifically mice.14 In mice, SP1 is predominantly expressed in the sublingual glands and secreted into saliva, where it is thought to contribute to oral microbial interactions and host defence.16 17 However, its presence and significance in humans remain unclear, underscoring the need for further investigation. Second, the presence of certain antibodies has been linked to DED severity. In a retrospective study of 136 individuals with a clinical diagnosis of DED, 41% (n=56) tested positive for at least one autoantibody.18 The presence of anti-CA6 was associated with more severe DED manifestations, with a higher risk of having, or developing, corneal (HR 1.5, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.97, p=0.009) and conjunctival (HR 1.4, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.76, p=0.02) staining.

Antibodies against CA6, PSP and SP1 may also have prognostic value in guiding DED treatment decisions.18 In a retrospective study, we examined 47 individuals with DED symptoms and ≥1 of the following features: dry mouth symptoms, low Schirmer score or abnormal corneal staining. 73% (n=32) tested positive for at least 1 antibody (CA6, PSP or SP1), with CA6 IgG elevation being most common. Individuals who tested positive for ≥1 antibody more often required treatment escalation beyond artificial tears compared with individuals without antibody positivity (cyclosporine: 100% vs 75%, p=0.02; lifitegrast 41% vs 8%, p=0.07).19 Together, these findings suggest that although not diagnostic for SjD, these autoantibodies may provide clinically relevant insight into DED severity and treatment considerations. Building on prior work, the present study examines a larger DED cohort to assess whether antibody positivity—and importantly, quantitative antibody levels—are associated with baseline disease severity and predict longitudinal disease progression. By evaluating antibody titers alongside clinical outcomes, this study positions CA6, PSP and SP1 antibodies as potential markers of DED burden and progression risk rather than diagnostic markers of systemic autoimmunity.

Methods

Study population

Retrospective study of 115 US veterans who presented to the Miami Veterans Affairs eye clinic with symptoms or signs of DED and underwent laboratory testing for antibodies against CA6, PSP and SP1 between January 2017 and December 2024. Of note, patients with systemic autoimmune conditions were included in this study, with the exception of those with a prior diagnosis of SjD20 or documented positivity for anti-SSA (Ro) or anti-SSB (La) antibodies. This exclusion criterion was applied to enrich the study population for patients in whom the utility and relevance of anti-CA6, anti-PSP and anti-SP1 could be evaluated. Subgroup stratification by specific autoimmune condition was not performed, given the exploratory nature of this analysis and the relatively small sample sizes within individual disease categories. Individuals were then divided into two groups: (1) an antibody positive group of individuals with ≥1 antibodies (IgA, IgG and IgM to CA6, PSP and SP1) at a level ≥20 EU/mL and (2) an antibody negative group of individuals where all 9 values were <20 EU/mL.

Chart review

Charts were reviewed to extract data on demographics, past medical history, medication use and DED metrics. Medications were categorised into relevant groups, including antihistamines, antihypertensives and antidepressants.

DED and ocular pain symptoms

As part of our clinical protocol, all individuals filled out validated symptom questionnaires regarding DED (Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI; range 0–100)21 and 5 Item Dry Eye Questionnaire (DEQ5; range 0–22)22) and ocular pain (Numerical Rating Scale (NRS; range 0–10), 4 questions from the Neuropathic Pain Symptom Inventory-modified for the Eye (range 0–100)23) (regarding burning and evoked pain to wind, light and temperature).

Ocular surface evaluation

All individuals underwent a standardised ocular surface evaluation that included: (1) cotton tip assessed corneal sensation graded as none, reduced, normal and increased (0–3); (2) InflammaDry testing (Quidel, San Diego) which qualitatively examined matrix metalloproteinase-9 presence, graded as none, mild, moderate and severe based on the intensity of the pink stripe24; (3) upper and lower eyelid laxity rated as prompt, slow return and lack of full return; (4) eyelid features including anterior blepharitis, eyelid vascularity and Meibomian gland plugging, all graded as none, mild, moderate or severe; (5) tear stability measured by tear break up time (TBUT) in seconds; (6) fluorescein assisted corneal staining graded to the National Eye Institute scale25; (7) pain intensity before and after topical anaesthetic placement (proparacaine 0.5%) (0–10); (8) Schirmer strip assessed anaesthetised tear production measured in mm of wetting at 5 min and (9) papillary conjunctiva features including papillae graded as none, mild, or severe and fibrosis rated as present or absent

In vivo confocal microscopy

Of the 115 individuals, 80 had in vivo confocal microscopy images within 1 year of the baseline visit. For each eligible patient, we manually examined the images and quantified the presence of activated dendritic cells (DCs). DCs were identified as bright, individual dendriform structures with hyperreflective cell bodies.26 DCs were classified as ‘activated’ (aDCs) if they exhibited a slender body with a minimum of three processes extending from the cell trunk that are the same length or longer than the cell body itself.27 After reviewing all available scans per patient, the maximum number of aDCs identified on any one scan was recorded as well as whether any scan had ≥2 aDCs present.28

Laboratory data

Laboratory data were recorded for all individuals. CA6, PSP, and SP1 were considered positive for values ≥20 EU/mL (based on the manufacturer-recommended threshold and prior publications,7 10 anti-SSA and B were considered positive for values ≥1.0 antibody index, and anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) were considered positive for values ≥1:320.

DED clinical course

67 individuals had at least one follow-up visit where DED metrics were re-evaluated. A worsening in the clinical course was considered if (1) DEQ-5 increased by ≥3 or OSDI increased by ≥10,29 (2) InflammaDry increased by ≥1, (3) corneal staining increased by ≥2, and/or (4) Schirmer score decreased by ≥3 mm, in either eye.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.29.0.1.0 (IBM). First, demographics, medical and ocular history, medications, and DED metrics were compared between the antibody+ and antibody– groups using independent-samples t-tests or χ² analyses, as appropriate. Linear and logistic regression analyses were conducted to reassess relationships while controlling for possible confounders. Correlational analyses were performed to explore the strength and direction of associations between individual antibody levels and DED metrics. Next, Cox proportional hazard analyses were conducted to examine whether antibody positivity portended a worse disease course, followed by multivariable modelling to examine the impact of confounders. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a p<0.05 considered statistically significant. In this study, we chose to report all variables being compared without applying p value corrections (eg, Bonferroni), given the known limitations associated with such adjustment methods.30

Results

Study population

A total of 115 patients were included in the study, the majority of which identified as male (n=66, 57%), White (n=79, 69%) and non-Hispanic (n=72, 63%). The antibody positive group, defined as patients with ≥1 of the 9 antibodies at levels ≥20 EU/mL, consisted of 73 individuals (63%). Demographics were well matched across groups. Most medication categories were similarly distributed, except for aspirin use, which was significantly higher in the antibody negative group (p=0.03) (table 1).

In vivo confocal microscopy

On confocal microscopy images, no differences in maximum count or frequency of having ≥2 aDC per image (33%, n=17 vs 48%, n=14, p=0.19) were noted across groups.

Multivariable models

Forward stepwise linear regression models were performed that included antibody status (positive vs negative) and quantitative values of each antibody, along with demographics (age, gender, race, ethnicity) and smoking status (current/former vs never) as independent variables. Dependent variables included baseline DED symptoms and ocular pain scores and DED signs. In these models, antibody status (positive vs negative) remained significantly associated with both DED symptoms (DEQ5, OSDI, dryness severity) and signs (TBUT), with antibody presence relating to more severe symptoms and signs. Interestingly, quantitative levels of antibodies were more closely related to ocular signs than antibody status, with higher levels of SP1 IgA and PSP IgM relating to more severe corneal staining and higher levels of CA6 IgG relating to lower Schirmer scores (table 3).

DED course

Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted on the 67 patients with at least one follow-up examination to assess whether antibody status or quantitative antibody levels predicted worsening in clinical parameters over time, considering both symptoms and signs. Overall, 29 individuals experienced a worsening in DED symptoms, defined as an increase in either DEQ-5 by ≥3 (n=17) or OSDI scores by ≥10 (n=24). Additionally, 55 individuals experienced a worsening in at least one DED sign, defined as an increase in InflammaDry score by ≥1 (n=25), an increase in corneal staining by ≥2 (n=24), or a decrease in Schirmer by ≥3 mm (n=27).

In all analyses, antibody status did not remain a significant predictor of worsening disease status across symptoms or signs. However, quantitative values of antibodies did stay in the model in some cases (table 5). Namely, higher baseline PSP IgM levels increased the risk of worsening symptoms (HR=1.01, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.03, p=0.03) and InflammaDry grade (HR=1.02; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.03; p=0.003) and higher CA6 IgG levels increased the risk of Schirmer reduction over time (HR=0.96, 95% CI 0.93 to 0.99, p=0.02).

Discussion

In this study, we found that the majority of individuals (63%) in our population tested positive for at least one of the nine antibodies against CA6, PSP and SP1, with CA6 IgG being most frequently elevated. Importantly, our results support the premise that these antibodies may have relevance in DED severity and progression. We found that antibody status (positive vs negative) was associated with more severe ocular symptoms at baseline, including higher DED and ocular pain questionnaire scores, relationships that remained significant in multivariable models. Beyond antibody status, quantitative antibody levels demonstrated stronger and more specific associations with ocular signs and longitudinal disease course. Higher PSP IgM levels predicted worsening symptoms and ocular surface inflammation over time, while higher CA6 IgG levels were associated with reduced tear production at baseline and a progressive decline on follow-up. Collectively, the novelty of our findings lies in reframing CA6, PSP, and SP1 antibodies—previously proposed as early markers of SjD—not as diagnostic indicators of a systemic autoimmune condition, but as biomarkers reflecting ocular surface disease severity and risk of progression in DED.

When comparing our studies to others, similarities and differences emerge. Consistent with our prior study, antibodies to CA6 were most commonly elevated in our cohort with IgG more frequently elevated than IgM.19 This distribution aligns with findings from other cohorts; for instance, a US study of 46 individuals with SjD, 14 with non-SjD related DED, and 25 controls found elevations in CA6 to be most common, noted in 52% of the SjD cohort, 43% of the DED cohort, and 8% of controls.31 A follow-up study by the same group noted that in 136 individuals diagnosed with DED, elevations in CA6 were again most common, present in 57% (30 of 53) antibody-positive individuals. However, the authors did not specify which immunoglobulin isotype (IgG, IgA, or IgM) was most common.18 Together, these findings reinforce the dominant presence of CA6 antibodies across different DED populations.

Mixed results have been noted with respect to relationships between CA6, PSP and SP1 antibodies and DED symptoms and signs. The presence of these antibodies has been associated with a higher frequency of DED symptoms in a group of 26 individuals with sensory neuropathy, with 100% (n=12) of antibody-positive individuals reporting sicca symptoms (yes/no) compared with 28.6% (n=4) of those without antibodies.32 As noted above, the presence of these antibodies, namely to CA6, was also associated with increased corneal and conjunctival staining.18 In contrast, our prior study of 44 individuals with DED did not note significant associations between antibody status and DED metrics at baseline. Our current study builds on previous literature by demonstrating an association between CA6 and both DED symptoms and signs, while PSP and SP1 antibodies were more closely related to signs (corneal staining and TBUT) at baseline. Despite inconsistencies, taken together, these findings suggest that antibodies to SP1, CA6, and/or PSP may independently reflect disease severity in DED, including both symptoms and signs.

The anatomical location and function of these proteins, specifically CA6 and PSP, give biological plausibility to their role in DED. CA plays a regulatory function for acid-base balance through maintenance of the pH gradient.11 CA6, specifically, is expressed in the acinar cells located within the parotids and submandibular glands of humans and mice, as well as the lacrimal glands in animal models such as mice and sheep, where it helps maintain tear fluid pH and plays a protective role against epithelial injury.1113 The physiological role of CA6 is particularly relevant given our findings that higher CA6 antibody levels were associated with worse tear stability (TBUT) and production (Schirmer scores) at baseline and predicted decreased tear production over time. PSP is expressed in the parotid and sublingual glands, as demonstrated in both human and animal studies.33 34 In addition, PSP is also present in the lacrimal glands of mice.35 Among other functions, PSP provides antimicrobial protection for oral health.33 SP1 is present in both the ductal epithelia and acinar cells of murine salivary glands, playing a role in oral health by modulating bacterial adhesion; however, the antibody’s role and target in humans remains to be defined prompting the need for further exploration.3638 Together, these findings suggest that these proteins help maintain oral and tear film health, and that immune responses against them may contribute to glandular dysfunction including reduced salivary and tear secretion, change in tear composition and quality, increased inflammation, leading to the development of sicca symptoms and DED signs (online supplemental figure 1).

As with all studies, our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations, including the retrospective design, which resulted in missing data. For example, we relied on available histories rather than a standardised checklist, and not all tests were performed in all patients (eg, in vivo confocal microscopy). This may have biased associations if the likelihood of having these measurements is related to disease severity or outcomes. Additionally, this study examined a heterogeneous, real-world dry eye population presenting to a Veterans hospital centre rather than a SjD-specific cohort, which should be considered when interpreting generalisability. Furthermore, unmeasured confounders (eg, diet, sleep) and coding errors may have influenced the results. Also, some aspects of DED, such as treatment escalation, could not be examined as most individuals were already on chronic anti-inflammatory eyedrop therapy at the start of the study. Lastly, because repeat antibody testing is not routinely performed, the proportion of initially negative individuals who may later seroconvert remains unknown.

Despite these limitations, our study demonstrates that the presence and quantity of CA6, PSP and SP1 antibodies may have relevance in DED, relating to DED severity at baseline and to disease progression over time. These findings suggest that antibody status and titre could inform clinical decision-making by flagging patients with more severe disease and a higher risk of progression. This, in turn, may guide treatment—identifying those who might benefit from long-term topical anti-inflammatory therapy even in the absence of a defined autoimmune diagnosis. Large, international multicentre cohorts are needed to fully assess the generalisability or potential region-specificity of these findings and to better define the diagnostic and prognostic value of CA6, PSP, and SP1 antibodies across DED populations worldwide, with the aim of refining treatment algorithms, improving DED symptoms and signs, and ultimately enhancing patients’ quality of life.
