Strong Grain Boundary Passivation Effect of Coevaporated Dopants Enhances the Photoemission of Lead Halide Perovskites
Authors: Isabella
A Kalluvila Justin, David O Tiede, Manuel Piot, Michele Forzatti, Cristina Roldán-Carmona, Juan F Galisteo-López, Hernán Míguez, Henk J Bolink
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerein, we demonstrate that coevaporated dopants provide a means to passivate buried interfacial defects occurring at perovskite grain boundaries in evaporated perovskite thin films, thus giving rise to an enhanced photoluminescence. By means of an extensive photophysical characterization, we provide experimental evidence that indicate that the codopant acts mainly at the grain boundaries. They passivate interfacial traps and prevent the formation of photoinduced deep traps. On the other hand, the presence of an excessive amount of organic dopant can lead to a barrier for carrier diffusion. Hence, the passivation process demands a proper balance between the two effects. Our analysis on the role of the dopant, performed under different excitation regimes, permits evaluation of the performance of the material under conditions more adapted to photovoltaic or light emitting applications. In this context, the approach taken herein provides a screening method to evaluate the suitability of a passivating strategy prior to its incorporation into a device.Keywords: passivation, grain-boundaries, recombination, perovskite, dopants, coevaporated, photoluminescence, traps

Introduction

Lead halide perovskites (LHP) have emerged over the past decade as a new family of semiconducting materials with great potential to be incorporated into highly efficient optoelectronic devices. Beyond having become the fastest growing photovoltaic technology,1 LHP are being explored as active components for light emitting devices,2 X-ray detectors,3 or memristors.4 As for any semiconductor, the presence of structural defects strongly determines the performance of this material in any device and thus compromises its future commercial use. While, due to the peculiar nature of LHP,5 structural defects are commonly considered as benign, a proper understanding of their nature and role in their photophysical properties is still under debate.6,7 Defects introducing localized states within the electronic bandgap act as nonradiative recombination centers that compromise the device efficiency. On the other hand, defects introducing shallow traps, such as halide vacancies, which commonly have a low formation energy,8 do not have a direct impact on radiative recombination efficiency and only affect the recombination rate.9 Instead, they can contribute to ionic migration and thus lower material stability, which is the main bottleneck for the commercialization of these materials.10

Up to date, most studies regarding the defect structure of LHP, and how to control it, have been performed on solution processed films. This is because it constitutes the most straightforward approach to fabricate these materials. While the extended use of this approach has contributed to the rapid development of the field of LHP, it presents some issues from the point of view of their mass production on an industrial scale. Beyond the limitations in producing large-scale devices with high efficiencies,11 these methods demand the use of organic solvents which suffer from strong restrictions in industry. In this respect, vacuum evaporation comprises a growth method that is already being used in mass-production processes of commercial devices and has been also employed for LHP.12 While the efficiency curve has not been as steep as that for solution processed materials, recent reports are approaching state-of-the-art values.13 Thus, understanding the defect landscape as well as possible avenues to act on it for evaporated LHP is of relevance.

The most straightforward approach to modify the defect structure of LHP is to incorporate passivating agents either during its synthesis or as a postprocessing step.14 Here, while the former approach can lead to passivation of defects lying at the grain boundary in the interior of the film as well as on the surface, the latter is usually restricted to surfaces and, in many cases, just the film interface on which the passivating agent is applied. For the case of evaporated LHP, there has been a growing interest in the development of passivating strategies, mostly employed with fully inorganic CsPbBr3 films of relevance for LEDs.15−23 Many of these approaches are based on the passivation of either one or both interfaces of the active perovskite layer.16−18,21,22 While all of them resulted in improved emission of the LHP film and performance of the device, most involved solution-processing steps16,18,21,22 that make the process less appealing from an industrial point of view. Alternatively, coevaporation of different precursors permits the incorporation of the passivating agent into the buried intergrain boundaries.15,19,20 In this direction, triphenylphosphine oxide (TPPO) has been recently cosublimed with CsPbBr3 and led to an increase in luminescence efficiency of the resulting perovskite film.23

In this work, we present a study on the role of a TPPO dimer (Bis[2-(diphenylphosphino)phenyl]ether oxide, DPEPO) Figure S1 that we selected as it has a higher sublimation temperature as buried defect passivator for methylammonium-based LHP films. We coevaporate the dopant together with the LHP precursors in order to achieve grain boundary defect passivation. To evaluate the passivating action of the added dopant, we carry out an in-depth photophysical study comprising static and fluence dependent time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements. We find evidence of (i) surface passivation of the grains comprising the LHP thin film, as revealed by the enhanced photoluminescence intensity observed, and (ii) a transition from electrically connected to isolated grains as the amount of dopant is increased. This affects the carrier recombination and prevents carrier diffusion across the grains. Further, optical studies are also employed to evaluate the enhanced stability of the films against photoinduced processes characteristic for these materials.

Results and Discussion

Halide perovskite films with the nominal composition CH3NH3Pb(I0.85Br0.15)3 were vacuum deposited on glass substrates partially covered with a hole transporting layer (HTL) (CS9/TaTm) to a thickness of 100 nm.24 The HTL layer was used in order to ensure that the growth of the perovskite is the same as when used in full device stacks (full details on sample fabrication can be found in the Methods Section). In this manner, the halide perovskite deposition is identical for both the glass substrate and that covered with the HTL and a direct comparison can be carried out. Additionally, the halide precursors were coevaporated with different amounts of DPEPO in order to explore its role as a passivating agent. This large bandgap material has been used in the past as a means to efficiently passivate the top and bottom surfaces of CsPbBr3 quantum dot (QD) films employed in QD-based LEDs.25

In order to explore the changes introduced by coevaporating the organic dopant, photoluminescence (PL) and absorption spectroscopy measurements were first carried out on films codeposited with different amounts of DPEPO (Figure 1). Figure 1a shows the absorption spectra for LHP films coevaporated on glass with different amounts of DPEPO. From these spectra, it can be observed that adding larger amounts of the organic dopant leads to a slight redshift of the absorption front, indicating an I-richer composition. Further, the absorption front becomes less sharp. In order to further understand the origin of these changes, we can turn to PL spectra for the same set of samples (Figure 1b), in which a red-shifted PL confirms that the perovskite has a smaller bandgap. As the evaporated dopant amount increases, the PL becomes gradually broader (fwhm = 44.1, 44.9, and 47.8 nm for 0, 2 and 5% DPEPO concentrations). These values are slightly larger than those expected for MAPbX3 samples at RT. This, together with the fact that the PL peak shows a certain asymmetry, could point to some inhomogeneity in sample composition, particularly in the halide component being the main factor determining the electronic bandgap, leading to an inhomogeneous broadening. The broader fwhm observed as the amount of dopant is increased, together with a less sharp absorption front, likely points to a small increase in the distribution of I–Br ratios. These changes are also reflected in the XRD data (see Figure S2) where the diffraction peaks are shifted to smaller angles and become broader, as expected for an I-richer and less homogeneous composition. We note that a peak at 12.6° appears for coevaporated DPEPO 5%, suggesting a small gradual segregation of PbI2 for larger dopant amounts. Beyond the latter, the sample morphology is also affected by the amount of dopant added during coevaporation. Figure 1c-e shows scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the samples for the different dopant concentrations. Here, it is evident that small amounts (2%) of DPEPO lead to a reduction of the grain size due to a slower crystal growth induced by the dopant, as also observed in TPPO-doped CsPbBr3 perovskites.23,26 However, in the case of the 5% DPEPO sample, the morphology becomes less homogeneous, showing a larger grain size distribution as well as the appearance of some pinholes, denoting a phase inhomogeneity.

We next turn to the samples deposited on the HTL under identical evaporation conditions. The absorption and PL spectroscopy measurements (Figure 1f,g) show a similar trend to that observed on the films deposited on glass, namely, a red-shifted and less sharp absorption front accompanied by a red-shifted and broader PL peak. Coevaporation with DPEPO also leads to strong morphological changes (Figure 1h-j). While in the absence of any dopant the LHP films grow with a smaller grain size on the HTL, adding DPEPO leads to identical morphology regardless of the substrate. This indicates that the dopant strongly affects film growth and eventually makes its structure independent of the substrate. This point is further reflected in the optical properties of the grown films where initial spectral differences between substrates in absorption and PL are minimized when the organic dopant is coevaporated with the LHP (see Figure S3).

To evaluate the role played by the substrate and dopant on the emission properties of the LHP films, we performed a systematic study of relative photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) vs. excitation fluence. Results are shown in Figure 2. First, in Figure 2a, a theoretically estimated characteristic bell curve of relative PLQY vs fluence, originated by the transition between different carrier recombination regimes that takes place as the density of photoinjected carriers increases,27 is plotted as a reference. In brief, for low fluences a reduced PLQY is expected as nonradiative trap-assisted recombination dominates. Increasing the carrier density leads to trap filling, which allows radiative band-to-band recombination to take over, as evidenced by the rise in the relative PLQY. Eventually, a sufficiently large carrier density increases the probability of nonradiative multiparticle Auger processes, which lower again the PLQY of the sample. Data for the LHP film evaporated on glass for the two extreme cases (0% and 5% DPEPO coevaporated) are shown in Figure 2b. In the low fluence regime, the film coevaporated with DPEPO presents a higher emission, which indicates a lower trap density as excitation conditions are identical and absorption barely changes in the spectral range where excitation takes place (see Figure S3a). While data for both doped/undoped samples evidence a rise in PLQY as fluence increases, the onset of Auger recombination takes place for lower fluences in the doped case causing a displacement of the maximum of the bell curve toward lower fluences. Next, we consider the data for films grown under identical conditions onto an HTL previously deposited on glass (Figure 2c). These can be directly compared to the ones corresponding to the glass substrate as optical measurement conditions were kept constant. It can be seen that bare (i.e., in the absence of DPEPO) perovskite films deposited on glass present a higher relative PLQY (black line and scatter graphs in Figure 2b,c) for all recombination regimes than those deposited on HTL. This can be accounted for considering the change in morphology induced by the substrate. As shown in the SEM images of Figure 1, evaporation on HTL leads to much smaller grains, which must lead to a larger population of interface defects at grain boundaries, hampering radiative recombination in the low fluence regime. As we enter the bimolecular regime (i.e., as traps are gradually filled), identified by an increase of the relative PLQY, the smaller grain size implies a higher spatial confinement of carriers, thus further favoring nonradiative recombination as a result of multiple-particle processes, which yields further lower emission than in the case of the larger grains present in bare (i.e., codopant free) films also for the high fluence regime.28

As for the films prepared by coevaporation of the LHP precursors and organic dopants on HTL, we see drastic changes in the evolution of the relative PLQY when compared to either that of the bare film on HTL (red and black line and scatter graphs in Figure 2c, respectively) or the film with codopants on glass (red line and scatter graph in Figure 2b). Now we observe an abrupt 10-fold increase in the PLQY in the low fluence regime and the onset of Auger recombination taking place for much lower (2 × 1017 vs 2 × 1018cm–3) carrier densities. The organic dopant seems to passivate both superficial and buried interfacial traps located at intergrain boundaries, so that the PLQYs measured at the mono- and bimolecular recombination regimes are much higher than in the rest of configurations. These results are relevant in view of applications in photovoltaic devices as the lowest end of fluences under consideration in our experiments are in the range of those attained in LHP-based PV devices under 1-sun illumination.29 On the contrary, as in the case of the codoped samples on glass, in the very high fluence regime, Auger recombination becomes even more likely as a result of the extra confinement provided by the dopant, reaching PLQY values lower than the bare films.

Here, the change in slope of the measured data corresponds to the point where two dashed lines cross each other and thus the onset when one recombination mechanism becomes dominant over another. For the bare sample, the monomolecular rate k1 is sufficiently large to be retrieved from our measurements in the fluence range under consideration and thus a transition from k1 to bimolecular recombination dominated by k2 is clearly observed (Figure 2e). As we add the dopant, a reduction in the density of traps as a consequence of the passivation of defects leads to a drop in the value of k1 to a point where its precise value cannot be extracted anymore. For the bimolecular rate k2, a variation is not expected since band structure and thus the density of states at the band-edge are not expected to change. Thus, the crossing point between both processes takes place for a smaller carrier density in the doped sample, out of our measurement range, and thus, only an upper bound to its value is provided in Table 1. Finally, the Auger rate measured for the doped sample is an order of magnitude larger than for the bare one, in agreement with the observation that the change from radiative bimolecular to nonradiative Auger processes takes place at a lower carrier density in the former, as the comparison between the red line-scatter curves in Figures 2b and 2c reveals.

The processes involved in the recombination of charge carriers within the different samples under analysis were further investigated studying TRPL vs fluence.31Figure 3a-c shows PL decays for samples coevaporated with different amounts of DPEPO on glass substrates. For the case of the bare LHP film without organic dopant, the PL decays follow the expected trend, becoming faster and deviating from single exponentials as the fluence is increased. This behavior corresponds to the appearance of additional recombination paths as the density of injected carriers is increased.32 As different amounts of DPEPO are coevaporated with the LHP precursors, we see that the shape of the PL decays changes and stops following the above-mentioned trend with fluence. These deviations indicate that the organic dopant acts on the LHP films beyond passivating surface traps.

(a–c) Normalized TRPL decays as a function of fluence for (a) bare perovskite films, and films coevaporated with (b) 2% and (c) 5% DPEPO. (d–f) Time derivative of the data shown in (a−c) vs PL. Fluences employed in these experiments were 1.8 × 10–14 – 1.8 × 10–16 cm–3.

According to the Shocklye–Read–Hall (SRH) model, in which charges are assumed to be homogeneously distributed across the semiconductor upon optical excitation, the representation of the time derivative of the PL vs the PL (Figure 3d-f) should give rise to an alignment and partial overlapping of the data for different fluences following a monotonously increasing curve.27,33 This is, in fact, what we obtain for the case of the bare LHP film (Figure 3d). In this context, deviations from this behavior may be considered as evidence of the breakdown of the assumptions made in the SRH model (as it happens, for instance, if slow diffusion of carriers takes place, as it would hinder their homogeneous distribution). It has recently been demonstrated that loss of connectivity between grains, which forces recombination to occur at isolated grains, makes dPL/dt vs PL curves become parallel, ceasing to converge, evidencing in this way the absence of global recombination phenomena.33 If we now consider the shape of dPL/dt vs PL curves for 2% and 5% DPEPO doped films, we can see deviations from the curve alignment observed in the bare film for small amounts of dopant in the former (Figure 3e) and clearly parallel curves for the latter (Figure 3f). Hence, increasing the amounts of dopant added during coevaporation gradually hampers carrier diffusion across grains and eventually leads to carrier isolation at individual grains. This effect is consistent with the assumption that DPEPO is mostly deposited at the buried grain boundary interfaces, leading simultaneously to trap passivation and poor connectivity among the grains. This picture accounts well for both the enhanced relative PLQY measured from doped samples and the observation of a faster Auger recombination occurring at lower carrier concentrations present in the samples with the highest amount of DPEPO, as carriers are now confined within individual grains.

As our studies indicate that DPEPO has a passivating effect on structural defects, and since these are known to contribute to material instability,34−38 we next explored if the addition of this codopant has any influence on the photostability of the LHP film. It is well-known that under external irradiation, LHP films undergo simultaneous photo activation/deactivation associated with the formation/passivation of structural defects.39 Whether PL activation or darkening dominates depends on the rate of defect formation/annihilation under that particular irradiation conditions.40−42 We observe that, upon continuous wave (CW) laser irradiation with λ=450 nm and an average power of 10 μW with an illumination spot of 280 μm, the LHP film grown in the absence of DPEPO undergoes a reversible PL darkening that is more pronounced when the film is deposited on the HTL (black curves in Figure 4a,b). If we now consider a different film coevaporated with DPEPO, we observe a drastically different behavior under identical irradiation conditions (red curves in Figure 4a,b), the samples presenting a photoinduced PL activation. So, the rate of defect formation is much faster in the absence of any additive. Also, the fact that PL darkening is faster and more pronounced in the case of the film deposited on HTL, where grains are much smaller (Figure 1), indicates that it is likely defects at the grain boundaries that are responsible for initiating the darkening.43 For the fluences and durations employed in these experiments, no degradation was observed, as the reversibility of the deactivation observed indicates. It should also be remarked that, since deep traps for PL comprise interstitial iodide Ii in the bulk44 and vacancies VI at the surface,45 a plausible scenario for the observed PL deactivation in bare films is that surface iodide ions can migrate toward the bulk as interstitials upon irradiation, leaving behind a harmful defect at the surface and creating another within the bulk. Upon addition of DPEPO, the surface is stabilized, and the previous mechanism is forbidden, halting PL quenching. In this situation, only ionic migration within the bulk is allowed, which has been reported in the past to allow for defect passivation in the form of Ii-VI Frenkel pair annihilation,46 further enhancing the PL.

In light of the results and analysis presented in this work, we can provide a picture of the mechanism by which the incorporation of DPEPO into LHP films during coevaporation gives rise to the observed effects (see Scheme 1). In this picture, in the absence of any passivating species, photogenerated carriers are either trapped at grain boundary interfaces or recombined with a particle of opposite sign at the same or nearby grains. Incorporation of the passivating codopant occurs mainly at grain boundaries and leads to the passivation of the interfacial defects present therein, so that carrier trapping is prevented and, overall, the PL efficiency is enhanced. But, if excessive DPEPO is deposited at the surface, across-grain charge diffusion comes to a halt and we enter into a local recombination scenario where both radiative recombination and Auger recombination are enhanced, the prevalence of one or the other depending on the carrier density, which is in turn controlled by the fluence of the excitation. Here, it must be noted that the isolating effect of excess DPEPO will not only affect charge transport among the crystalline grains comprising the film but also to/from CTLs thus severely affecting device performance. In addition, we have observed that excess of DPEPO promotes partial segregation of PbI2 within the film, which might have unclear effects in device stability, specially within the long term and under illumination.47−50 Indeed, wide discrepancies can be found in literature regarding PbI2 effect in device efficiency and stability, which suggest that the final outcome might depend on the amount of excess PbI2 and the processing conditions, resulting in either passivation of surface defects or hole injection, as recently pointed out.50,51 In our case, small residues of PbI2 are detected for the 5% additive.

Proposed Mechanism on the Effect on Carrier Recombination of DPEPO as a Defect Passivator

As mentioned above, luminescent perovskites are of interest for application in light emitting diodes (LEDs) and photovoltaic devices. Even though the absolute photoluminescence efficiency remains somewhat low, we did prepare some LEDs to verify the performance of these films. Bright deep red emission could be observed (Figure S4) although still with modest external quantum efficiencies. This work, therefore, is primary of interest in identifying the operation of passivating molecules in perovskites, an area of research where detailed information is rather scarce.

Conclusions

In summary, we have performed an extensive photophysical characterization of LHP films coevaporated with a compound, DPEPO, that acts as passivating agent for structural defects. Experimental evidence points to DPEPO concentrating mainly in between grain boundaries, which gives rise to both the passivation of interfacial traps and the prevention of further formation of deep traps as a consequence of photoinduced ionic migration initiating at the surface. On the other hand, the presence of an excessive amount of organic dopant can lead to a barrier for carrier diffusion and thus preclude the use of LHP in a device. Hence, a proper balance between the two effects demands judicious control of the growth process. Further, our photophysical study constitutes a method that allows discerning the role of the dopant under different excitation regimes, which permits evaluation of the performance of the material under conditions more adapted for photovoltaic or light emitting applications. In this context, the approach taken herein provides a screening method to evaluate the suitability of a passivating strategy prior to its incorporation into a device.

Methods

Perovskite Film Fabrication

The 3 cm × 3 cm glass substrates were first cleaned by undergoing a series of washing steps using different solution, namely, soap, deionized water, and isopropanol. For each step, the samples were dipped inside the corresponding solution and placed in an ultrasonic bath for 4 min. After drying the isopropanol, the substrates were placed under UV-ozone treatment for 15 min.

On part of the substrate, the hole transport layer was deposited by vacuum thermal evaporation. A thin layer (2.5 nm) of CS9 was deposited, followed by 10 nm of TaTm.

The MAPIBr perovskite films were deposited via vacuum cosublimation of methylammonium iodide (MAI) and a mixed lead halide Pb(I1-nBrn)2 precursor, as described in a previous work.52 For the DPEPO-doped films, an additional third source was used to coevaporate the dopant alongside the precursors. The rates were monitored by using dedicated quartz crystal microbalances (QCMs) located next to the sources and at the substrate level. The amount of DPEPO doping was increased by incrementing the crucible temperature until the desired rate was reached, i.e., 0.05 and 0.12 A/s for the 2% and 5% doping, respectively. This doping percentage is given in relation to the Pb(I1-nBrn)2 evaporation rate, which was fixed at 2.4 A/s.

Absorption and Photoluminescence Spectra

Absorption spectra were collected in a UV–vis spectrophotometer and absorptance values were calculated from the total transmittance and total reflectance measurements in the 400–900 nm range using a Cary 5000 spectrophotometer UV–visible–near-infrared(UV–vis–NIR) with a DRA-2500(PMT/PbS) detector.

PL spectra were collected with a fiber coupled UV–vis spectrometer (USB2000 from Ocean Optics) upon excitation with a CW laser with λ = 450 nm, average power of 10 μW and 300 μm spot.

Relative Photoluminescence Quantum Yield Measurements

Relative PLQY measurements were performed using a pumped light source (PHAROS from Light Conversion) coupled to an Optical Parametric Amplifier (ORPHEUS from Light Conversion) that allows tuning the excitation density from several orders of magnitude without degrading the sample. The excitation intensity was controlled with an automated filterwheel and continuously tracked with a powermeter positioned behind a beam sampler. The emission was collected with a fiber coupled UV–vis spectrometer (Flame T UV/vis from Ocean Optics), where the integration time was adapted in each measurement to adapt the dynamic range of the spectrometer to the emission intensity. The obtained emission spectrum was integrated, and the obtained intensity was corrected by the integration time and the excitation intensity.

Transient Absorption Spectroscopy Measurements

TAS measurements were carried out in pump and probe configuration. The samples were optically excited with 190 fs pulses having a λ = 450 nm delivered by an optical parametric amplifier (OPA) (ORPHEUS from Light Conversion) which was fed with a high-power Yb:KGW fs laser operating at 1 kHz and λ = 1040 nm (PHAROS from Light Conversion). The differential transmission from the sample was measured at different time delays after excitation using a white light pulse generated with a fraction of the 1040 nm beam at a sapphire crystal and a delay stage using a commercial spectrometer (HELIOS from Ultrafast Systems).

TAS spectra at different time intervals present a dip associated with the ground state bleach (GSB) as a consequence of the depopulation of the band edges upon optical excitation. Monitoring the GSB amplitude allows extraction of the time evolution of the photoinjected carrier density. TAS spectra were corrected for any background coming from photoluminescence (particularly at high excitation fluences). Further time zero and chirping corrections were also performed.

Time-Resolved Photoluminescence Measurements

TRPL measurements were performed with a time correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) card (SPC-130-EMN from Becker and Hickl). Optical excitation was carried out using a spectrally tunable laser (Fianium SuperK from NKT) delivering 900 ps pulses with λ = 532 nm operating at 200 kHz. The sample PL was collected with an avalanche photo diode (id100 from ID Quantique). A long focal length (f = 10 cm) achromatic lens is used for both optical excitation and collection.

Device Fabrication and Characterization

The substrates containing the perovskite layers were transferred to another vacuum chamber, where PO-T2T, Ba, and Ag were thermally deposited. The vacuum evaporation rates were 0.06 nm s–1 and 0.05 nm s–1 for the ETL and cathode, respectively, with the background pressure being around 3 × 106 bar. Shadow masks were used during metal evaporation to obtain a final active area of 0.06 cm2. Devices were not exposed to water vapor or O2 after cathode deposition and before the characterization. After full device fabrication, the samples were introduced into a custom setup for a current density and irradiance versus voltage scan. For this, we employed a Keithley 2400 Source-Meter and a calibrated Avantes AvaSpec-2048L spectrometer to measure the electroluminescence spectra. A LabVIEW program was used to control the Keithley and the spectrometer and to obtain the data.
