Subspace Approximations to the Focused Transport Equation of Energetic Particles I. The Standard Form
B. Klippenstein and A. Shalchi

Abstract
The Fokker-Planck equation describing the transport of energetic particles interacting with turbulence is difficult to solve analytically. Numerical solutions are of course possible but they are not always useful for applications. In the past a subspace approximation was proposed which allows to compute important quantities such as the characteristic function as well as certain expectation values. This previous approach was applied to solve the one-dimensional Fokker-Planck equation which contains only a pitch-angle scattering term. In the current paper we extend this approach in order to solve the Fokker-Planck equation with a focusing term. We employ two- and three-dimensional subspace approximations to achieve a pure analytical description of particle transport. Additionally, we show that with higher dimensions, the subspace method can be used as a hybrid analytical-numerical method which produces an accurate approximation. Although the latter approach does not lead to analytical results, it is much faster compared to pure numerical solutions of the considered transport equation.

1
Introduction

Energetic particles such as cosmic rays interact with large scale magnetic fields and turbulence. The latter interaction is complicated and leads to a diffusive motion
of particles described by diffusion coefficients (see, e.g., Shalchi (2009a) and Shalchi (2020) for reviews). However, non-diffusive transport has been discussed in
the literature (see, e.g., Zimbardo (2005), Zimbardo et al. (2006), Perri & Zimbardo (2007), Shalchi & Kourakis (2007), Perri & Zimbardo (2009a), Perri & Zimbardo (2009b),
Tautz & Shalchi (2010), Zimbardo et al. (2012), and Perri et al. (2015)).

For the mean magnetic field it is often assumed that it is constant and points into the
z
-direction. In this case the mean field has the form
\vec{B}_{0}=B_{0}\hat{z}

where
B_{0}
is a constant. However, real magnetic fields are not constant. Parker (1958), for instance, suggested that the solar magnetic field can be described by an

Archimedean spiral
. To include such a complicated mean magnetic field into analytical theories for energetic particle transport is very difficult and only numerical
test-particle simulations can be performed for this case (see Tautz et al. (2011)).

The standard approach to incorporate a varying mean magnetic field is via the focusing effect originally introduced into the field of particle transport by Roelof (1969).
This effect leads to an additional term in the transport equation used to describe the particle motion. As describe in Schlickeiser (2002) and Zank (2014), there is a certain
hierarchy of transport equations. At the most fundamental level one can use a relativistic version of the Vlasov equation (see Vlasov (1938)). The next level provides an equation
for the ensemble averaged distribution function in phase-space. In the context of cosmic rays this is often called the
Fokker-Planck equation
(see Schlickeiser (2002)).
The third level is obtained by pitch-angle averaging the Fokker-Planck equation leading to a diffusive transport equation (see, e.g., Parker (1965)).

The general Fokker-Planck equation of cosmic ray transport is lengthy and contains several terms such as stochastic acceleration and perpendicular diffusion
(see, e.g., Skilling (1975), Schlickeiser (2002), and Zank (2014)). In the current paper we consider the following transport equation

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+v\mu\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left[D_{\mu\mu}\big(\mu\big)\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu}\right]-\frac{v}{2L}\big(1-\mu^{2}\big)\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu}

(1)

where we have used the Fokker-Planck coefficient of pitch-angle scattering
D_{\mu\mu}
. The latter scattering parameter is a function of the pitch-angle cosine
\mu
.
The second term on the right-hand-side is the focusing term. The parameter
L
is assumed to be constant and is called the
focusing length
. It corresponds
to a characteristic length scale over which one observes a variation of the mean magnetic field. The focusing length is usually introduced by considering a mean
magnetic field where the
z
-component is given by

B_{0z}\left(z\right)=B_{0}e^{-z/L}

(2)

where we have used the constant
B_{0}
. It follows from the solenoidal constraint
\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{B}_{0}=0
and the assumption of an axi-symmetric magnetic field that

B_{0x}\left(x,z\right)=\frac{x}{2L}B_{0z}\left(z\right)\;\;\textnormal{and}\;\;B_{0y}\left(y,z\right)=\frac{y}{2L}B_{0z}\left(z\right).

(3)

Eq. (
1
) is also known as the
focused transport equation
. It was explored in several papers over the past few decades (see, e..g, Earl (1976), Kunstmann (1979), Ruffolo (1995),
Shalchi (2009b), Shalchi (2011b), Shalchi & Danos (2013), Danos et al. (2013), Wang & Qin (2019), and Wang & Qin (2020)). The equation investigated here neglects the effects of
a finite non-zero solar wind speed. The focused transport equation for relativistic flows was discussed by Webb (1985) as well as Webb (1987). A version of the focused transport equation for
relativistic background plasma flows was obtained Kirk et al. (1988) who discussed the effects of backward and forward moving Alfvén waves on the energization of the particles due to second
order Fermi acceleration.

It is the purpose of the current paper to solve Eq. (
1
) by using the
N
-dimensional subspace method described in Lasuik & Shalchi (2019) and Shalchi (2024).
In Section 2 we discuss the subspace method by including the focusing term. Thereafter we approximately solve the Fokker-Planck equation analytically for
N=2
in Section 3.
After finding the solution, several quantities such as expectation values are computed in Section 4. In Section 5 we discuss the accuracy and speed of the
N
-dimensional
subspace approximation method. Limitations of the proposed method are also discussed. In Section 7 we summarize and conclude.

2
Fourier Transform and Subspace Approximation

In the following we discuss the
N
-dimensional subspace method for the focused transport equation. In order to solve Eq. (
1
) we first rewrite it by using a Fourier
transform of the form

f\big(z,\mu,t\big)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;F_{k_{\parallel}}\big(\mu,t\big)e^{ik_{\parallel}z}.

(4)

The corresponding inverse transform is given by

F_{k_{\parallel}}\big(\mu,t\big)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;f\big(z,\mu,t\big)e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}.

(5)

Using this in Eq. (
1
) gives us

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{k_{\parallel}}}{\partial t}+iv\mu k_{\parallel}F_{k_{\parallel}}

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left[D_{\mu\mu}\frac{\partial F_{k_{\parallel}}}{\partial\mu}\right]-\frac{v}{2L}\big(1-\mu^{2}\big)\frac{\partial F_{k_{\parallel}}}{\partial\mu}.

The latter equation can be attempted to be solved via the expansion

F_{k_{\parallel}}(\mu,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}(t)P_{n}(\mu)

(7)

where we have used the
Legendre Polynomials

P_{n}(\mu)
. Furthermore, the yet unknown coefficients
C_{n}(t)
are functions of time.
The idea of expanding the solution of the pitch-angle scattering equation into eigenfunctions, in our case the Legendre polynomials, was used
before in the literature (see, e.g. Earl (1974)). In such previous work the intention was to include the effects of cosmic ray inertia, the role of coherent
particle pulse propagation, and the effects of dispersive waves on the particle transport.

Using the expansion given by Eq. (
7
) in Eq. (
6
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\dot{C}_{n}P_{n}+iv\mu k_{\parallel}\sum_{n}C_{n}P_{n}

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}C_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left[D_{\mu\mu}\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial\mu}\right]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{v}{2L}\big(1-\mu^{2}\big)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}C_{n}\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial\mu}

where
\dot{C}_{n}
denotes the time-derivative of the coefficient
C_{n}
. Eq. (
8
) cannot be solved analytically for the general case
(meaning arbitrary
D_{\mu\mu}
). Even for a specific form of the Fokker-Planck coefficient, a solution cannot be found without using approximations. In the
following we employ the isotropic form for the Fokker-Planck coefficient

D_{\mu\mu}=D\big(1-\mu^{2}\big).

(9)

This form was derived systematically in Shalchi et al. (2009) based on the
second-order quasi-linear theory
developed in Shalchi (2005). It was demonstrated that
the isotropic form should be valid for strong (
\delta B\gg B_{0}
) and intermediate strong (
\delta B\approx B_{0}
) turbulence as found in interplanetary and interstellar spaces.
For the isotropic form, Eq. (
8
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\dot{C}_{n}P_{n}+iv\mu k_{\parallel}\sum_{n}C_{n}P_{n}

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D\sum_{n}C_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left[\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial\mu}\right]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{v}{2L}\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)\sum_{n}C_{n}\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial\mu}.

A well-known relation for Legendre polynomials is given by (see, e.g., Abramowitz & Stegun (1968))

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left[\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial\mu}\right]=-n(n+1)P_{n}

(11)

where
n
is integer
n=0,1,2,\dots
. Using this in Eq. (
10
) leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\dot{C}_{n}P_{n}+iv\mu k_{\parallel}\sum_{n}C_{n}P_{n}

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D\sum_{n}C_{n}n(n+1)P_{n}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{v}{2L}\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)\sum_{n}C_{n}\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial\mu}.

To get rid of the remaining explicit
\mu
-dependences we can use the relations (see again Abramowitz & Stegun (1968))

\mu P_{n}=\frac{n+1}{2n+1}P_{n+1}+\frac{n}{2n+1}P_{n-1},

(13)

and

\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial\mu}=nP_{n-1}-n\mu P_{n}.

(14)

Combining the latter two equations yields

\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial\mu}=n\frac{n+1}{2n+1}\left(P_{n-1}-P_{n+1}\right).

(15)

Therewith Eq. (
12
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\dot{C}_{n}P_{n}

(16)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle ivk_{\parallel}\sum_{n}C_{n}\bigg[\frac{n+1}{2n+1}P_{n+1}+\frac{n}{2n+1}P_{n-1}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D\sum_{n}C_{n}n(n+1)P_{n}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{v}{2L}\sum_{n}C_{n}n\frac{n+1}{2n+1}\left(P_{n+1}-P_{n-1}\right).

We now multiply this equation by the Legendre polynomial
P_{m}
, integrate over all
\mu
, and use the orthogonality relation (see, e.g., Abramowitz & Stegun (1968))

\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\;P_{n}\left(\mu\right)P_{m}\left(\mu\right)=\frac{2}{2m+1}\delta_{nm}

(17)

to derive

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-Dm(m+1)C_{m}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle ivk_{\parallel}\Bigg[\frac{m}{2m-1}C_{m-1}+\frac{m+1}{2m+3}C_{m+1}\Bigg]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{v}{2L}\Bigg[\frac{m(m-1)}{2m-1}C_{m-1}-\frac{(m+1)(m+2)}{2m+3}C_{m+1}\Bigg].

This leads to an infinite number of coupled ordinary differential equations for the functions
C_{m}(t)
. We can write those differential equations as the
following linear system:

\dot{C}=\boldsymbol{M}C

(19)

where we have used

\displaystyle C(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}C_{0}(t)\\[5.69046pt]
C_{1}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad\dot{C}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{C}_{0}(t)\\[5.69046pt]
\dot{C}_{1}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\end{array}\right),

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&M_{01}&0&\dots\\[5.69046pt]
M_{10}&M_{11}&M_{12}&\dots\\[5.69046pt]
0&M_{21}&M_{22}&\dots\\[5.69046pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right).

The elements of the latter matrix are given as the coefficients of
C_{n}
and are obtained via Eq. (
). From this it follows that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&-\frac{1}{3}ivk_{\parallel}-\frac{v}{3L}&0&\dots\\[5.69046pt]
-ivk_{\parallel}&-2D&-\frac{2}{5}ivk_{\parallel}-\frac{3v}{5L}&\dots\\[5.69046pt]
0&-\frac{2}{3}ivk_{\parallel}+\frac{v}{3L}&-6D&\dots\\[5.69046pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right)

(37)

corresponding to an infinite matrix. Eq. (
19
) is a well-known linear system of differential equations with the formal solution

C(t)=e^{\boldsymbol{M}t}C(0)

(38)

where we have used the matrix exponential. The column vector
C(0)
is determined using a sharp initial condition of the form

f\big(z,\mu,t=0\big)=2\delta\big(\mu-\mu_{0}\big)\delta\big(z\big)

(39)

where
\mu_{0}
is the initial pitch-angle cosine. The two Dirac deltas ensure that the particle is initially located at
z=0
and has the pitch-angle cosine
\mu=\mu_{0}
.
The factor
2
was chosen so that

\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;f\big(z,\mu,t=0\big)=1

(40)

corresponding to a normalization condition. In order to find the initial conditions for the function
F_{k_{\parallel}}\big(\mu,t\big)
, we need to use the inverse Fourier
transform as given by Eq. (
5
). Combining this with Eq. (
39
) yields

\displaystyle F_{k_{\parallel}}\big(\mu,t=0\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;f\big(z,\mu,t=0\big)e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\delta\big(\mu-\mu_{0}\big)\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;\delta\big(z\big)e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\delta\big(\mu-\mu_{0}\big).

In combination with expansion (
7
) this gives us

\sum_{n}C_{n}(t=0)P_{n}\big(\mu\big)=\frac{1}{\pi}\delta\big(\mu-\mu_{0}\big).

(42)

Multiplying this equation by
P_{m}
and invoking Eq. (
17
) results in

C_{m}\big(t=0\big)=\frac{2m+1}{2\pi}P_{m}\big(\mu_{0}\big)

(43)

Orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials also allows us to derive the following expression for the
\mu
-integrated solution:

\displaystyle M(z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\;f(z,\mu,t)

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;e^{ik_{\parallel}z}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\;F_{k_{\parallel}}(\mu,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;C_{0}(k_{\parallel},t)e^{ik_{\parallel}z}.

Unfortunately, Eq. (
38
) cannot be solved analytically and exactly. Therefore, we need to truncate the expansion provided by Eq. (
7
). That is, set

C_{n}=0,\qquad n\geq N

(45)

for some
N
, which corresponds to what we call the
N
-dimensional subspace approximation
. Evidently, the larger we take
N
to be, the better our approximation is.
Shalchi (2024) concluded that when dealing with Eq. (
1
) without the focusing term,
N=10
corresponds to a fast and accurate approximation. In Sect. 5, we extend
this idea with focusing included.

Alternatively, we can solve Eq. (
19
) analytically if
N
is small enough. This can be done by determining the eigenvalues of the
N\times N
matrix
M
.
In Lasuik & Shalchi (2019), it was concluded that
N=2
results in an accurate but simple approximation. However, the latter work is based on the transport equation without
focusing term. Section 3 discusses a similar approach but with the focusing term included.

3
The Two-Dimensional Subspace Approximation

In the case of the two-dimensional subspace approximation, the expansion provided by Eq. (
7
) is simplified to

F_{k_{\parallel}}(\mu,t)=C_{0}(t)+\mu C_{1}(t).

(46)

Additionally, Eq. (
19
) becomes

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{C}_{0}\\[5.69046pt]
\dot{C}_{1}\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-\frac{1}{3}ivk_{\parallel}-\frac{v}{3L}\\[5.69046pt]
-ivk_{\parallel}&-2D\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}C_{0}\\[5.69046pt]
C_{1}\\
\end{array}\right).

(54)

The
ansatz

C_{0}\big(t\big)=b_{0}e^{\omega t}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad C_{1}\big(t\big)=b_{1}e^{\omega t}

(55)

leads to the matrix equation

\displaystyle\omega\left(\begin{array}[]{c}b_{0}\\[5.69046pt]
b_{1}\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-\frac{1}{3}ivk_{\parallel}-\frac{v}{3L}\\[5.69046pt]
-ivk_{\parallel}&-2D\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}b_{0}\\[5.69046pt]
b_{1}\\
\end{array}\right)

corresponding to a simple eigenvalue problem. Alternatively, this can be written as

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}-\omega&-\frac{1}{3}ivk_{\parallel}-\frac{v}{3L}\\[5.69046pt]
-ivk_{\parallel}&-2D-\omega\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}b_{0}\\[5.69046pt]
b_{1}\\
\end{array}\right)=0.

Non-trivial solutions of the latter equation are obtained by setting the determinant of this
2\times 2
matrix equal to zero. We find

\omega^{2}+2D\omega+\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}-i\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{3L}=0.

(68)

This quadratic equation has the two solutions

\omega_{\pm}=-D\pm\sqrt{D^{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{3L}}.

(69)

These two values for
\omega
can be used in the
ansatz
given by Eq. (
55
). The general solution for the function
C_{0}(t)
is, therefore, obtained via the superposition

C_{0}\big(t\big)=b_{+}e^{\omega_{+}t}+b_{-}e^{\omega_{-}t}.

(70)

By combining Eqs. (
54
) and (
70
), we also obtain

C_{1}\big(t\big)=\frac{3i}{vk_{\parallel}-iv/L}\left(\omega_{+}b_{+}e^{\omega_{+}t}+\omega_{-}b_{-}e^{\omega_{-}t}\right).

(71)

Eq. (
43
) states that

C_{0}(0)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad C_{1}(0)=\frac{3}{2\pi}\mu_{0}.

(72)

Together, Eqs. (
70
)-(
72
) imply the linear system for
b_{+}
and
b_{-}
:

b_{+}+b_{-}=\frac{1}{2\pi}

(73)

and

\frac{3i}{vk_{\parallel}-iv/L}\left(\omega_{+}b_{+}+\omega_{-}b_{-}\right)=\frac{3\mu_{0}}{2\pi}.

(74)

Eqs. (
73
) and (
74
) can be combined to determine the parameters we are looking for. After some straightforward algebra we find

\displaystyle b_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\omega_{-}+iv\mu_{0}k_{\parallel}+v\mu_{0}/L}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}},

\displaystyle b_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\omega_{+}+iv\mu_{0}k_{\parallel}+v\mu_{0}/L}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}}.

(75)

These formulas can be used in Eqs. (
70
) and (
71
) and our coefficients
C_{0}(t)
and
C_{1}(t)
are completely determined. Our
solution for the Fourier-transformed distribution function as provided by Eq. (
46
) can, therefore, be written as

\displaystyle F_{k_{\parallel}}\big(\mu,t\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{3i\mu\omega_{+}}{vk_{\parallel}-iv/L}\right]b_{+}e^{\omega_{+}t}

(76)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{3i\mu\omega_{-}}{vk_{\parallel}-iv/L}\right]b_{-}e^{\omega_{-}t}

where the coefficients
b_{\pm}
are given by Eq. (
75
) and the coefficients
\omega_{\pm}
are provided by Eq. (
69
). Of course, our solution
is based on the two-dimensional subspace approximation and is, therefore, not an exact solution of Eq. (
6
).

One must be cautious when the two-dimensional subspace approximation is used as there are values of
v/(DL)
which can cause either of the eigenvalues to have a positive
real component for large wave numbers. This would lead to an infinitely large
F_{k_{\parallel}}
. To demonstrate this, we consider Eq. (
69
) and explore the limit

k_{\parallel}\rightarrow\infty
. We assume that
L>0
and first notice that

\displaystyle\Re\left\{\sqrt{D^{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{3L}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(D^{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{v^{4}k_{\parallel}^{2}}{9L^{2}}\right]^{1/4}

\displaystyle\times\cos\left[\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{3L}}{D^{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\pi\right]

(77)

where
\Re
denotes the real component. One can easily see here that in the limit
k_{\parallel}\to\infty
, the indeterminate form
\infty\cdot 0
arises. Evaluating the limit is
then a matter of applying l’Hôpital’s rule. After a tedious yet straightforward calculation, we derive

\lim_{k_{\parallel}\to\infty}\Re\left\{\sqrt{D^{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{3L}}\right\}=\frac{v}{2\sqrt{3}L}

(78)

As a result, we can see

\lim_{k_{\parallel}\to\infty}\Re\left\{\omega_{+}\right\}=-D+\frac{v}{2\sqrt{3}L}

(79)

which is positive if

\frac{v}{DL}>2\sqrt{3}.

(80)

Similarly, if
L<0
, we have that
\omega_{-}
has a positive real component for large wave numbers if

-\frac{v}{DL}>2\sqrt{3}.

(81)

Therefore, there is a limit for the possible values of the parameter
v/(DL)
.

It was demonstrated in Shalchi (2024) that in the absence of focusing, the two-dimensional subspace approximation is equivalent to using a telegraph equation.
Using a telegraph equation description of energetic particle transport goes back to the early work of Fisk & Axford (1969) in which the authors focused on early times
in solar flare events, where the particle inertia needs to be accounted for before a diffusive equilibrium sets in.

4
Expectation Values

By using the results derived in the previous section, we can calculate several important quantities. First we need to define the meaning of the ensemble average in particle transport
theory. Since the particle distribution function derived above is a function of
\mu_{0}
,
\mu
, and
z
, we understand the ensemble average of the quantity
A(z,\mu,t)
as

\displaystyle\big<A\big>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\;\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu_{0}

(82)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;A\left(z,\mu,t\right)f\big(z,\mu,t\big).

Sometimes one can drop one of the integrals therein. This is in particular the case if a pitch-angle dependent solution is needed. In the following subsections we consider several
examples for the quantity
A
such as the characteristic function, the velocity correlation function, and moments of the form
\langle z^{n}\rangle
. To know the analytical form
of such quantities is essential to develop analytical theories for perpendicular transport (see, e.g., Matthaeus et al. (2003), Shalchi (2010), Shalchi (2020), and Shalchi (2021).

4.1
The Characteristic Function

As a first application, we compute the characteristic function defined via

\displaystyle\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu_{0}

(83)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;f\big(z,\mu,t\big)e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}.

Therein we can use Eq. (
5
) to find

\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>=\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu_{0}\;F_{k_{\parallel}}\big(\mu,t\big)

(84)

meaning that the desired characteristic function is nothing else than the
\mu_{0}
- and
\mu
-averaged function
F_{k_{\parallel}}
. If we average Eq. (
7
) over all
\mu
,
we find

\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\;F_{k_{\parallel}}\big(\mu,t\big)=C_{0}\big(t\big)

(85)

where we have used orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials as given by Eq. (
17
). Using the result provided by Eq. (
70
) therein yields

\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>=\pi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu_{0}\;\left[b_{+}e^{\omega_{+}t}+b_{-}e^{\omega_{-}t}\right].

(86)

After employing Eq. (
75
) we can solve the remaining integral so that

\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>=\frac{\omega_{+}}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}}e^{\omega_{-}t}-\frac{\omega_{-}}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}}e^{\omega_{+}t}

(87)

where the parameters
\omega_{+}
and
\omega_{-}
are given by Eq. (
69
). Taking the complex conjugate of Eq. (
87
) yields

\left<e^{+ik_{\parallel}z}\right>=\frac{\omega_{+}^{*}}{\omega_{+}^{*}-\omega_{-}^{*}}e^{\omega_{-}^{*}t}-\frac{\omega_{-}^{*}}{\omega_{+}^{*}-\omega_{-}^{*}}e^{\omega_{+}^{*}t}.

(88)

For the case that
\omega_{+}
and
\omega_{-}
are real, we can easily see that

\left<e^{+ik_{\parallel}z}\right>=\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>.

(89)

For complex
\omega_{\pm}
it follows from Eq. (
69
) that
\omega_{\pm}^{*}=\omega_{\mp}
. Using this in Eq. (
88
) gives us the
right-hand-side of Eq. (
87

The characteristic function discussed above is directly related to the
\mu_{0}
- and
\mu
-averaged distribution function

M\left(z,t\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;\langle e^{\pm ik_{\parallel}z}\rangle e^{ik_{\parallel}z}.

(90)

Therein we can use results for the characteristic function such as the one given by Eq. (

).

In the following we consider special cases with the aim to simplify Eq. (

4.1.1
Small Wave Numbers

It is convenient to use the short notation

\sqrt{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}\dots}:=\sqrt{D^{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{3L}}

(91)

in Eq. (
69
). First we assume that

\left|-v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{L}\right|\ll 3D^{2}.

(92)

After Taylor-expanding the square root given by Eq. (
91
), we find up to
k_{\parallel}^{2}
the approximation

\sqrt{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}\dots}\approx D+i\frac{v^{2}}{6DL}k_{\parallel}+\left(\frac{v^{4}}{72L^{2}D^{3}}-\frac{v^{2}}{6D}\right)k_{\parallel}^{2}.

(93)

For isotropic pitch-angle scattering and without adiabatic focusing, the parallel spatial diffusion coefficient is given by (see, e.g., Shalchi (2006))

\kappa_{\parallel}=\frac{v^{2}}{6D}.

(94)

Therewith, our result can be written as

\sqrt{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}\dots}\approx D+i\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}}{L}k_{\parallel}-\kappa_{\parallel}\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{12L^{2}D^{2}}\right)k_{\parallel}^{2}.

(95)

Using this expansion in Eq. (
69
) yields

\omega_{+}\approx i\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}}{L}k_{\parallel}-\kappa_{\parallel}\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{12L^{2}D^{2}}\right)k_{\parallel}^{2}

(96)

as well as

\omega_{-}\approx-\frac{v}{\lambda_{\parallel}}-i\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}}{L}k_{\parallel}+\kappa_{\parallel}\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{12L^{2}D^{2}}\right)k_{\parallel}^{2}

(97)

where we have used the parallel mean free path without focusing effect (see again Shalchi (2006))

\lambda_{\parallel}=\frac{v}{2D}.

(98)

In general, the parallel mean free path is related to the parallel spatial diffusion coefficient via
\lambda_{\parallel}=3\kappa_{\parallel}/v
. The above expansion is valid as long as
Eq. (
92
) holds. Therefore, we can further simplify the result given by Eq. (
97
) by only keeping the lowest order terms for both the real and imaginary
components to obtain

\omega_{-}\approx-\frac{v}{\lambda_{\parallel}}-i\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}}{L}k_{\parallel}.

(99)

Then, on the other hand, we can write Eq. (
96
) as

\omega_{+}\approx-\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}}{L}k_{\parallel}

(100)

where we have used the parallel diffusion coefficient with focusing

\displaystyle\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa_{\parallel}\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{12L^{2}D^{2}}\right)

(101)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa_{\parallel}\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\lambda_{\parallel}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right).

Note, this reduction of the parallel diffusion coefficient due to focusing was found before (see, e.g., Shalchi (2011b) and Shalchi & Danos (2013)). Quantitatively, the
result derived here is slightly different compared to previous results. One reason is that it depends on how a diffusion coefficient is defined. It was demonstrated in
Danos et al. (2013) that diffusion coefficients defined via mean square displacements and diffusion coefficients defined via a time-integral over the velocity correlation
functions are not the same if there is focusing. Furthermore, Eq. (
101
) is based on the two-dimensional subspace approximation and is, therefore, not
an exact result.

In the limit considered here the parameters
\omega_{-}
and
\omega_{+}
are given by Eqs. (
99
) and (
100
), respectively.
Using this in Eq. (
87
) gives us

\displaystyle\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}}{L}k_{\parallel}-\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}^{2}}{2\sqrt{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}\dots}}e^{-vt/\lambda_{\parallel}-i\kappa_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}t/L}

(102)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{v}{\lambda_{\parallel}}+i\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}}{L}k_{\parallel}}{2\sqrt{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}\dots}}e^{i\kappa_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}t/L-\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}^{2}t}

where we have also employed Eq. (
89
). Since we have used already the condition given by Eq. (
92
), we can further simplify our result by

\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>=e^{i\kappa_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}t/L-\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}^{2}t}.

(103)

In Sect. (
4.1.3
), a more detailed discussion of this result can be found.

4.1.2
Large Wave Numbers

In the previous paragraph we considered the case of small wave numbers. However, we can also approximate Eq. (
87
) by using the limit

v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}\gg\left|3D^{2}+i\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{L}\right|.

(104)

In this case Eq. (

\displaystyle\omega_{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D\pm\left(i\frac{v|k_{\parallel}|}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{v\text{sgn}(k_{\parallel})}{2\sqrt{3}L}\right.

(105)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.i\frac{\sqrt{3}D^{2}}{2v|k_{\parallel}|}+i\frac{v}{8\sqrt{3}L^{2}|k_{\parallel}|}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-D\pm\left(i\frac{v|k_{\parallel}|}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{v\text{sgn}(k_{\parallel})}{2\sqrt{3}L}\right)

where
\text{sgn}(x)=1
if
x\geq 0
and
\text{sgn}(x)=-1
if
x<0
. Furthermore, it follows from Eq. (

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\rule{0.0pt}{8.61108pt}\dots}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v\text{sgn}(k_{\parallel})}{\sqrt{3}L}+i\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}v|k_{\parallel}|-\frac{\sqrt{3}D^{2}}{v|k_{\parallel}|}\right.

(106)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\frac{v}{4\sqrt{3}L^{2}|k_{\parallel}|}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{v\text{sgn}(k_{\parallel})}{\sqrt{3}L}+i\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}v|k_{\parallel}|.

Using this in Eq. (
87
) yields

\displaystyle\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2k_{\parallel}L}{1+2ik_{\parallel}L}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\Bigg[\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\frac{D}{v|k_{\parallel}|}+\frac{1}{4k_{\parallel}L}+i\frac{1}{2}\right)e^{-\left(\frac{v\text{sgn}(k_{\parallel})}{2\sqrt{3}L}+i\frac{v|k_{\parallel}|}{\sqrt{3}}\right)t}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\frac{D}{v|k_{\parallel}|}+\frac{1}{4k_{\parallel}L}+i\frac{1}{2}\right)e^{\left(\frac{v\text{sgn}(k_{\parallel})}{2\sqrt{3}L}+i\frac{v|k_{\parallel}|}{\sqrt{3}}\right)t}\Bigg]e^{-Dt}.

If we additionally assume that
L,|k_{\parallel}|\to\infty
, we can further simplify this result to obtain

\displaystyle\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[e^{i\frac{vk_{\parallel}}{\sqrt{3}}t}+e^{-i\frac{vk_{\parallel}}{\sqrt{3}}t}\right]e^{-Dt}

(108)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{vk_{\parallel}}{\sqrt{3}}t\right)e^{-Dt}.

The latter result is easy to understand. It corresponds to the unperturbed orbit but damped due to pitch-angle scattering. Note, this result was also derived in Lasuik & Shalchi (2019).
In the considered order, Eq. (
108
) does not depend on the focusing effect.

4.1.3
Early and Late Times

In general the characteristic function is given by Eq. (
87
). Combining this formula with Eq. (
90
) yields

\displaystyle M\big(z,t\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;\bigg[\frac{\omega_{+}}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}}e^{\omega_{-}t}-\frac{\omega_{-}}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}}e^{\omega_{+}t}\bigg]e^{ik_{\parallel}z}.

For
t=0
this becomes

\displaystyle M\big(z,t=0\big)

(110)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;\bigg[\frac{\omega_{+}}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}}-\frac{\omega_{-}}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}}\bigg]e^{ik_{\parallel}z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;e^{ik_{\parallel}z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\big(z\big)

where in the last step we have used (see, e.g., Zwillinger (2012))

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;e^{i(k_{\parallel}^{\prime}-k_{\parallel})z}=2\pi\delta\big(k_{\parallel}^{\prime}-k_{\parallel}\big).

(111)

Eq. (
110
) corresponds to the pitch-angle averaged initial distribution.

For
t\rightarrow\infty
, on the other hand, the main contribution to the integral in Eq. (
) comes from the smallest possible values of
\omega_{\pm}

due to the exponential in the aforementioned equation. It follows from Eq. (
69
) that this happens for the smallest possible wave number
k_{\parallel}
.
Therefore, we find with the help of Eq. (
103
) that

\displaystyle M\big(z,t\rightarrow\infty\big)

(112)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;e^{i\kappa_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}t/L-\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}^{2}t}e^{ik_{\parallel}z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}t}}e^{-(z+\kappa_{\parallel}t/L)^{2}/(4\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}t)}

where the parameter
\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}
is given by Eq. (
101
). Eq. (
112
) corresponds to a Fourier-transformed Gaussian distribution
with non-vanishing mean. From Eq. (
112
), we can easily read off the mean position

\big<z\big>=-\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}t}{L}

(113)

and the mean square displacement

\big<z^{2}\big>-\big<z\big>^{2}=2\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}t.

(114)

Eq. (
113
) is a well-known result in the theory of adiabatic focusing (see Shalchi (2009b)). The above findings corresponds to the solution of a diffusion-convection equation.
The mean of the Gaussian distribution moves with the speed
v_{c}=-\kappa_{\parallel}/L
whereas the width is given by Eq. (
114
). The parallel diffusion coefficient therein is altered
by adiabatic focusing as given by Eq. (
101
).

4.2
The Velocity Correlation Function

We can also use the formulation discussed so far to determine the parallel velocity correlation function. This can be done via

\displaystyle V_{zz}\big(t\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{2}\Big<\mu_{0}\mu\Big>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}}{4}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu_{0}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\;\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;\mu_{0}\mu f\big(z,\mu,t\big).

Therein we can use

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;f\big(z,\mu,t\big)

(116)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;F_{k_{\parallel}}\big(\mu,t\big)\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\;e^{ik_{\parallel}z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi F_{0}\big(\mu,t\big)

where we have employed again Eq. (
111
). Therefore, the velocity correlation functions becomes

\displaystyle V_{zz}\big(t\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}v^{2}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu_{0}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\;\mu_{0}\mu F_{0}\big(\mu,t\big)

(117)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}v^{2}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu_{0}\;\mu_{0}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\;\mu^{2}C_{1}\big(k_{\parallel}=0,t\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\frac{v^{2}}{3}\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu_{0}\;\mu_{0}C_{1}\big(k_{\parallel}=0,t\big)

where in the second step, we have again used the orthogonal relation given by Eq. (
17
). Using therein Eq. (
71
) with (
75
) yields

V_{zz}\big(t\big)=\frac{v^{2}}{3}\bigg[\frac{\omega_{+}}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}}e^{\omega_{+}t}-\frac{\omega_{-}}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}}e^{\omega_{-}t}\bigg]_{k_{\parallel}=0}.

(118)

For
k_{\parallel}=0
we have

\omega_{+}(k_{\parallel}=0)=0\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\omega_{-}(k_{\parallel}=0)=-2D.

(119)

Therewith we finally find for the velocity correlation function

\left<V_{z}(t)V_{z}(0)\right>=V_{zz}\big(t\big)=\frac{v^{2}}{3}e^{-2Dt}=\frac{v^{2}}{3}e^{-vt/\lambda_{\parallel}}

(120)

where we have used the parallel mean free path without focusing effect as given by Eq. (
98
). As demonstrated, the velocity correlation function is an exponential
function for the isotropic pitch-angle Fokker-Planck coefficient given by Eq. (
9
). The characteristic length for the decay of the obtained exponential is the parallel mean
free paths. For other forms of the pitch-angle Fokker-Planck coefficient, different velocity correlation functions can be obtained (see Shalchi (2011a)). With velocity correlation functions
there is an associated diffusion coefficient which is computed via the
TGK (Taylor-Green-Kubo) formulation
(see Taylor (1922), Green (1951), and Kubo (1957))

\displaystyle\kappa_{\parallel}^{TGK}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;\left<V_{z}(t)V_{z}(0)\right>

(121)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}}{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;e^{-2Dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}}{6D}.

The latter result agrees with Eq. (
94
). We conclude that within the two-dimensional subspace approximation, adiabatic focusing has no effect on velocity
correlation functions and the associated parallel diffusion coefficient
\kappa_{\parallel}^{TGK}
. However, in Appendix
A
we show that it does have an effect
if we instead use the three-dimensional subspace approximation instead.

\displaystyle\pi\int_{-1}^{+1}d\mu\mu\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dk_{\parallel}\;F_{k_{\parallel}}(\mu,t)\delta(k_{\parallel})

For

5
The
N
-Dimensional Subspace Approximation

In the following we discuss the
N
-dimensional subspace approximation. This approach is in detail discussed in Shalchi (2024) for the Fokker-Planck equation without
adiabatic focusing. In the current section we extend this method to include the focusing effect. Furthermore, we shall demonstrate that the
10
-dimensional subspace
approximation provides a very accurate approximation in most cases. This approach has to be understood as a semi-analytical method because some steps have to be
performed with a computer.

Within the
N
-dimensional subspace approximation, we employ the expansion in Legendre polynomials as given by Eq. (
7
). The time-dependent functions

C_{n}(t)
therein are obtained via solving numerically the matrix equation provided by Eq. (
38
). The initial conditions
C_{n}(0)
in the latter equation are obtained
via Eq. (
43
). The matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
in our matrix equation is provided by Eq. (
37
) where all matrix elements are given
as the coefficient of
C_{n}
in Eq. (
). In principle
\boldsymbol{M}
is an infinite matrix. However, as an approximate, we replace this matrix by an

N\times N
matrix by cutting off the expansion in Eq. (
7
). We call this an
N
-dimensional subspace approximation. Pure analytical treatments for
N=2

and
N=3
are presented in the current paper but numerically we can consider any value for
N
. Motivated by Shalchi (2024) we consider the case
N=10
and refer
to it as the
10
-dimensional subspace approximation.

To test the validity of various subspace approximations, we also solve the focused transport equation numerically. This is done by employing an
implicit Euler method
.
The numerical methodology employed here aligns with the one used in Shalchi (2024). It is a straightforward process to incorporate the focusing term. First, we employ the
variable transformations

\tilde{t}=Dt\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\tilde{z}=\frac{Dz}{v}

(145)

which transform Eq. (

5.2
Limitations of the
N
-Dimensional Subspace Approximation

As we discussed at the end of Sect.
3
, one fact that hinders the use of the
2
-dimensional subspace approximation is the possibility of the eigenvalues having a positive
real component. However, in this section, we show that this problem does not only occur when using two dimensions. In fact, for any given
N
-dimensional subspace approximation,
if the parameter
\xi=v/(DL)
is sufficiently large, we encounter a similar issue. We illustrate this concept in Figure
25
. To understand this figure, we begin with observing
through numerical evidence that if
\{\omega_{n}(k)\}_{n=1}^{N}
is the set of eigenvalues of the matrix in Eq. (
2
) associated with the
N
-dimensional subspace approximation,
we have that for each
1\leq n\leq N
,

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\Re\left\{\omega_{n}(k)\right\}=\tilde{\omega}_{n}

(150)

where
\Re
denotes the real component. In other words, the real component of each eigenvalue converges to some finite value as
k
goes to infinity.

The flaw of the
N
-dimensional subspace approximation is that if we do not take
N
to be large enough, there are values of
n
such that
\tilde{\omega}_{n}
is positive.
Figure
25
shows the relation between the parameter
\xi
, and the smallest value of
N
, denoted by
\tilde{N}
, which does not give rise to this issue. It should be noted
that we do not claim that for a given
\xi
, the
\tilde{N}
-dimensional subspace method guarantees a good approximation. Instead, we claim that if one wants a good approximation,
they must take
N
to be at least
\tilde{N}
. For example, Figure
25
shows that for
\xi=5
and
N=3
one does not encounter this problem. However, as demonstrated
above, this does not give a good approximation.

Figure 25:
The relation between
\xi
and
\tilde{N}
, which is the minimum dimension one must use for the subspace method to produce a good approximation.

There is another inconvenience that arises from the fact that the real component of the eigenvalues does not decay to zero. That is the uncertainty of knowing when to truncate the integral
in Eq. (
4
). For example, by solving Eq. (
6
) numerically, it can be seen that the magnitude of
F_{k_{\parallel}}
decays exponentially as the wave number
increases. This in turn leads to naturally truncating the integral in Eq. (
4
) when
F_{k_{\parallel}}
is deemed to be sufficiently small. As for the subspace method however,
we do not achieve this decay, and thus deciding when to truncate the Fourier integral is not clear. Nevertheless, in our results for the
\mu
-integrated solution, we truncate the Fourier integral
for the subspace method at the same wave number as we did for the numerical solution. By judging the figures we produced above, this choice of truncation is seen to be sufficient.

6
Summary and Conclusion

The understanding of particle transport is a very fundamental problem in space physics and astrophysics. Furthermore, there are a variety of applications of transport theory results. Most important
examples are the theories of diffusive shock acceleration and solar modulation (see Zank et al. (2000a), Li et al. (2003), Zank et al. (2004), Li et al. (2005), Zank et al. (2006), Dosch & Shalchi (2010),
Li et al. (2012), Ferrand et al. (2014), Hu et al. (2017), Shen & Qin (2018), Engelbrecht & Wolmarans (2020), Moloto & Engelbrecht (2020), Engelbrecht & Moloto (2021), Shen et al. (2021),
and Ngobeni et al. (2022)).

Using the
N
-dimensional subspace approximation method has been employed in previous literature to solve the Fokker-Planck equation (see, e.g., Zank et al. (2000b), Lasuik & Shalchi (2019),
and Shalchi (2024)). In the current paper we extended the subspace method by including the effect of adiabatic focusing. We first used the
2
-dimensional subspace method to obtain approximate
results for the solution as well as several expectation values. Unexpectedly, we saw that some results within this method are independent of the focusing term, which shows that
2
-dimensions
are not sufficient here. Therefore, we developed a three-dimensional subspace approximation and showed that the corresponding results are more aligned with what is expected, as demonstrated
in Appendix
A
. On the other hand, we show that using
10
-dimensions provides an accurate, yet quick approximation. This semi-analytical approach provides an alternative
compared to pure numerical solutions which can be very time-consuming. However, Section
5.2
discusses that if the focusing parameter
\xi=v/(DL)
is sufficiently large, one must proceed
with caution and ensure that using
10
-dimensions is still sufficient.

A central question in the theory of focused transport is how the focusing effect influences the analytical form of the parallel spatial diffusion coefficient. There have been contradicting results but
it was demonstrated in Danos et al. (2013) that how the parallel diffusion coefficient looks like with focusing effect depends on how this diffusion coefficient is computed. Results which are obtained
by employing the TGK formula are different compared to the one found by using mean square displacments. This difference can also be seen in the current paper. Within the two-dimensional
subspace approximation we derived Eq. (
101
) corresponding to a diffusion coefficient obtained via mean square displacement. Eq. (
221
) provides the same result
but based on the three-dimensional subspace approximation. It is slightly more accurate. However, if one uses the TGK formula for determining the parallel spatial diffusion coefficient with focusing
one obtains Eqs. (
121
) and (
227
), respectively. Since one is interested in the pitch-angle averaged distribution function and the corresponding diffusion-convection equation,
it seems to be more appropriate to use the diffusion coefficient given by Eqs. (
101
) and (
221
). This also confirms the results originally obtained in Shalchi (2011b).

In the current paper we have used the standard approach to explore the parallel spatial diffusion coefficient with focusing effect. This means we determined this coefficient by using a given form
of the pitch-angle scattering coefficient
D_{\mu\mu}
. It should be noted, however, that there have been investigations of this scattering coefficient and how it is influenced by the focusing effect.
Tautz et al. (2014) as well as Florinski (2024) determined
D_{\mu\mu}
with focusing effect by employing quasi-linear theory and they found a significant change of this scattering coefficient.
However, for such more realistic forms of
D_{\mu\mu}
it is even more difficult to solve the focused transport equation.

In the current paper we have employed the subspace method to solve Eq. (
1
). The latter equation desribes a pitch-angle isotropization process like the same equation but without
focusing effect. However, Eq. (
1
) does not conserve the norm. An alternative focused transport equation can be derived by employing the transformation

\tilde{f}\left(t,z,\mu\right)=f\left(t,z,\mu\right)e^{z/L}

(151)

leading to a
modified focused transport equation
which does conserve the norm. In the sequel to the current paper we employ the subspace approximation to solve this modified
equation.

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)+\frac{v}{L}(\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)+\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0))\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{v^{2}}{L^{2}}\big(3\mu_{0}^{2}-1\big)+2\frac{Dv}{L}\mu_{0}}{2\pi\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)},

\displaystyle b_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{v}{L}\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{v^{2}}{L^{2}}\big(3\mu_{0}^{2}-1\big)+2\frac{Dv}{L}\mu_{0}}{2\pi\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)(\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)-\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0))},

\displaystyle b_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{v}{L}\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{v^{2}}{L^{2}}\big(3\mu_{0}^{2}-1\big)+2\frac{Dv}{L}\mu_{0}}{2\pi\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)(\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)-\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0))}.

(205)

As we shall see later, an important quantity is
C_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0,t)
. By Eqs. (
198
), (
204
), and (
205
), we have

\displaystyle C_{1}(k_{\parallel},t=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-3\Bigg[\frac{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)\mu_{0}+2D\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{v}{L}\big(3\mu_{0}^{2}-1\big)}{2\pi\left[\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)-\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)\right]}e^{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)t}

(206)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)\mu_{0}+2D\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{v}{L}\big(3\mu_{0}^{2}-1\big)}{2\pi\left[\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)-\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)\right]}e^{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)t}\Bigg].

This formula will be used in Section
A.4.3
to compute the expectation value
\left<\mu\right>
.

A.4
Expectation Values

In the main part of this paper we employed the two-dimensional subspace approximation to compute several expectation values. In the following we determine the same quantities
but employ the three-dimensional subspace approximation. This is particularly important because several expectation values are independent of the focusing effect when the
two-dimensional approach is employed. We shall see that one advantage of using the extra dimension is that each result contains the focusing length
L
.

A.4.1
The Characteristic Function

The first few steps for calculating the characteristic function are provided by Eqs. (
83
)-(
85
) implying that

\displaystyle\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu_{0}\;C_{0}(t)

(207)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu_{0}\left[b_{0}e^{\omega_{0}t}+b_{1}e^{\omega_{1}t}+b_{2}e^{\omega_{2}t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{1}{3}\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{L}}{(\omega_{0}-\omega_{1})(\omega_{0}-\omega_{2})}e^{\omega_{0}t}+\frac{\omega_{0}\omega_{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{1}{3}\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{L}}{(\omega_{1}-\omega_{0})(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})}e^{\omega_{1}t}+\frac{\omega_{0}\omega_{1}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{1}{3}\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{L}}{(\omega_{2}-\omega_{0})(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})}e^{\omega_{2}t}.

In what follows, we simplify this result for small wave numbers and late times. To do this, we write
\omega=a+bk_{\parallel}+ck_{\parallel}^{2}
with the constants
a,b,c
independent of
k_{\parallel}
,
and place that into the eigenvalue equation (
180
) to obtain

\displaystyle a^{3}+8Da^{2}

\displaystyle+12D^{2}a+\frac{v^{2}}{5L^{2}}a+\left(3a^{2}b+16Dab+12D^{2}b+\frac{1}{5}\frac{v^{2}}{L^{2}}b-i\frac{3}{5}\frac{v^{2}}{L}a-i2\frac{Dv^{2}}{L}\right)k_{\parallel}

(208)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(3ab^{2}+3a^{2}c+8Db^{2}+16Dac+12D^{2}c+\frac{3}{5}v^{2}a+2Dv^{2}+\frac{1}{5}\frac{v^{2}}{L^{2}}c-i\frac{3}{5}\frac{v^{2}}{L}b\right)k_{\parallel}^{2}=0

where we neglect terms higher order than quadratic in
k_{\parallel}
. The equation found here has to be satisfied for any value of
k_{\parallel}
. Therefore, the terms without
k_{\parallel}
must
be zero and we find

a^{3}+8Da^{2}+12D^{2}a+\frac{v^{2}}{5L^{2}}a=0.

(209)

From this condition we can derive the following three values for the constant
a
:

a=0\quad\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\quad a=-4D\pm\sqrt{4D^{2}-\frac{v^{2}}{5L^{2}}}.

(210)

Since we are interested in the late time limit, we can see that the term with the slowest decay in Eq. (
207
) corresponds to the eigenvalue with
a=0
. Henceforth, we set
a=0

in Eq. (
208
) to simplify it to

\left(12D^{2}b+\frac{1}{5}\frac{v^{2}}{L^{2}}b-i2\frac{Dv^{2}}{L}\right)k_{\parallel}+\left(8Db^{2}+12D^{2}c+2Dv^{2}+\frac{1}{5}\frac{v^{2}}{L^{2}}c-i\frac{3}{5}\frac{v^{2}}{L}b\right)k_{\parallel}^{2}=0.

(211)

Now, from the linear term, we easily obtain the expression

b=10i\frac{\frac{v^{2}}{DL}}{60+\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}.

(212)

If we place this into the quadratic term of Eq. (
211
), we obtain

c=-\frac{40\left(900\frac{v^{2}}{D}-25\frac{v^{4}}{D^{3}L^{2}}+\frac{v^{6}}{D^{5}L^{4}}\right)}{\left(60+\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}\right)^{3}}.

(213)

By combining Eqs. (
212
) and (
213
), we derive an expression for one eigenvalue, which we denote by
\omega_{0}
:

\omega_{0}=-\frac{40\left(900\frac{v^{2}}{D}-25\frac{v^{4}}{D^{3}L^{2}}+\frac{v^{6}}{D^{5}L^{4}}\right)}{\left(60+\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}\right)^{3}}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i10\frac{\frac{v^{2}}{DL}}{60+\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}k_{\parallel}.

(214)

Since the factor
e^{\omega_{0}t}
has significantly slower decay than the other exponential factors in Eq. (
207
), we can simplify it to

\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>\approx\frac{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{1}{3}\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{L}}{(\omega_{0}-\omega_{1})(\omega_{0}-\omega_{2})}e^{\omega_{0}t}.

(215)

Next, we use the fact that
\omega_{0}\to 0
as
k_{\parallel}\to 0
once more to simplify the factor

\frac{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}-\frac{1}{3}v^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}+i\frac{1}{3}\frac{v^{2}k_{\parallel}}{L}}{(\omega_{0}-\omega_{1})(\omega_{0}-\omega_{2})}\approx 1.

(216)

If we assume further that
\xi=v/(DL)
is small, we can additionally simplify Eqs. (
212
) and (
213
) to

\qquad b=i\frac{1}{6}\frac{v^{2}}{DL}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad c=-\frac{1}{6}\frac{v^{2}}{D}+\frac{7}{540}\frac{v^{4}}{D^{3}L^{2}},

(217)

resulting in

\left<e^{-ik_{\parallel}z}\right>\approx e^{-\kappa_{\parallel}k_{\parallel}^{2}t+\frac{7}{15}\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}t}{DL^{2}}+i\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}}{L}k_{\parallel}t}.

(218)

Here, we have used the parallel diffusion coefficient without focusing as given by Eq. (
94
). From Eq. (
218
) we can read off the parallel diffusion coefficient
with focusing effect and we find

\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}=\kappa_{\parallel}\left(1-\frac{7}{15}\frac{\kappa_{\parallel}}{DL^{2}}\right).

(219)

Therein we can use the diffusion coefficient without focusing and write

\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}=\kappa_{\parallel}\left(1-\frac{7}{90}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}\right).

(220)

Or, we use the parallel mean free path without focusing (see Eq. (
98
) of the current paper) to write this as

\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}=\kappa_{\parallel}\left(1-\frac{14}{45}\frac{\lambda_{\parallel}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right).

(221)

This result can be compared with Eq. (
101
) which is based on the two-dimensional subspace approximation. Due to
14/45\approx 1/3
the results for the parallel
diffusion coefficient do not change significantly when going from the two-dimensional method to the three-dimensional one.

A.4.2
The Velocity Correlation Function

By following the ideas used in Eqs. (
)-(
117
), we have for the velocity correlation function

V_{zz}(t)=\pi\frac{v^{2}}{3}\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu_{0}\;\mu_{0}C_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0,t).

(222)

With Eq. (
206
), this becomes

\displaystyle V_{zz}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{v^{2}}{3}\left[\frac{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)+2D}{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)-\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)}e^{\omega_{1}t}+\frac{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)+2D}{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)-\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)}e^{\omega_{2}t}\right]

(223)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}}{6}\Bigg[\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}}\right)e^{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)t}+\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}}\right)e^{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)t}\Bigg].

If we assume that the parameter
\xi
is small, we can simplify this result to

V_{zz}(t)=\frac{v^{2}}{6}\left[-\frac{1}{40}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}e^{\left(-6D+\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{DL^{2}}\right)t}+\left(2+\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}\right)e^{\left(-2D-\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{DL^{2}}\right)t}\right].

(224)

Integrating this over all times yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;V_{zz}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}}{6}\Bigg[-\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}}\right)\frac{1}{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)}-\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}}\right)\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)}\Bigg]

(225)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}}{6}\Bigg[-\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)}+\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}}\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)}-\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}}{6}\frac{1}{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)}\Bigg[-\left(\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)+\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}}\left(\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)-\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}}{6}\frac{1}{12D^{2}+\frac{1}{5}\frac{v^{2}}{L^{2}}}\Bigg[8D+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}}4D\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{20}\frac{v^{2}}{D^{2}L^{2}}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v^{2}}{6}\frac{12D}{12D^{2}+\frac{1}{5}\frac{v^{2}}{L^{2}}}.

In the weak focusing limit the corresponding parallel diffusion coefficient becomes

\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}^{TGK}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;V_{zz}(t)=\frac{v^{2}}{6D}\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{60D^{2}L^{2}}\right).

(226)

Note, with the parallel diffusion coefficient without focusing effect (see again Eq. (
94
)) this can be written as

\bar{\kappa}_{\parallel}^{TGK}=\kappa_{\parallel}\left(1-\frac{1}{15}\frac{\lambda_{\parallel}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right).

(227)

This result can be compared with the time-integrated velocity correlation function based on the two-dimensional subspace approximation (see Eq. (
121
) of the current paper).
While the two-dimensional approximation did not provide a result depending on the focusing effect, the formula based on the three-dimensional approximation predicts a
reduction of the parallel diffusion coefficient. Still, we can see a difference between diffusion coefficients based on mean square displacements (see Eq. (
221
))
and the one obtained be employing the TGK formula. This difference can always be observed and is a special feature of particle transport with focusing (see Danos et al. (2013)).

A.4.3
The Expectation Value
\left<\mu\right>

To determine the expectation value
\left<\mu\right>
, we employ Eq. (
124
). With Eq. (
206
), this becomes

\displaystyle\left<\mu\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Bigg[\frac{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)\mu_{0}+2D\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{v}{L}\big(3\mu_{0}^{2}-1\big)}{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)-\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)}e^{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)t}

(228)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)\mu_{0}+2D\mu_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{v}{L}\big(3\mu_{0}^{2}-1\big)}{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)-\omega_{1}(k_{\parallel}=0)}e^{\omega_{2}(k_{\parallel}=0)t}\Bigg].

Again, we assume that
\xi
is small, and thus we obtain the simplified expression

\displaystyle\left<\mu\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Bigg[\left(-\frac{1}{8}\frac{v}{DL}(3\mu_{0}^{2}-1)+\frac{v^{2}}{80D^{2}L^{2}}\mu_{0}\right)e^{(-6D+\frac{v^{2}}{20DL^{2}})t}

(229)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left(\frac{1}{8}\frac{v}{DL}(3\mu_{0}^{2}-1)-\mu_{0}-\frac{v^{2}}{80D^{2}L^{2}}\mu_{0}\right)e^{(-2D-\frac{v^{2}}{20DL^{2}})t}\Bigg].

Keeping in mind that the focusing effect is assumed to be weak here, we can easily see that

\left<\mu\right>(t=0)=\mu_{0}.

(230)

Moreover, we also have

\left<\mu\right>_{t\rightarrow\infty}\rightarrow 0

(231)

regardless of what the value of the initial pitch-angle cosine
\mu_{0}
is. This is what we have expected due to the pitch-angle isotropization process described by Eq. (
1
).
