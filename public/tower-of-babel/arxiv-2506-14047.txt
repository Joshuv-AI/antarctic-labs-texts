On Universal Inverse Monoids with the F F -Inverse Property
IGOR DOLINKA, GANNA KUDRYAVTSEVA

Abstract
. An inverse monoid M M is called F F -inverse if each σ \sigma -class of M M , where σ \sigma is the minimum group congruence of M M , has a maximum element with respect to the natural order of M M . Upon Kinyon’s observation made in 2018 that F F -inverse monoids form a variety in the enriched signature involving an additional unary operation mapping each element of M M to the maximum element of its σ \sigma -class, a separate theory of F F -inverse monoids is developing in recent years, greatly aided by a recent solution to the long-standing problem of Henckell and Rhodes on finite F F -inverse covers of finite inverse monoids. In particular, a description of the universal object F ⁡ ( G , X ) F(G,X) in the class of X X -generated F F -inverse monoids with a prescribed maximum group image was provided recently. In this paper we introduce yet another object M F ​ ( G , X ) M_{F}(G,X) , the one that is universal for inverse monoids (so, not in the enriched signature) that are X X -generated with maximum group image a quotient of G G and which happen to be F F -inverse. We obtain presentations and geometric models based on Cayley graphs of groups for such inverse monoids. As an application, we are able to characterise all one-relator special inverse monoids Inv ⟨ X | w = 1 ⟩ \Inv\langle{X}\,|\,{w=1}\rangle with a cyclically reduced relator word w w enjoying the F F -inverse property.

1.
Introduction

If
S
is a semigroup or a monoid and
a\in S
we say that
a^{\prime}\in S
is a
(von Neumann) inverse
of
a
if
aa^{\prime}a=a
and
a^{\prime}=a^{\prime}aa^{\prime}
. If every element
of a semigroup has an inverse such a semigroup is called
regular
; regular semigroups in which the inverse of every element is unique are called

inverse semigroups
. In such a case, this unique inverse is usually denoted by
a^{-1}
and the mapping
a\mapsto a^{-1}
readily becomes an additional
unary operation of the underlying semigroup. Indeed, it is very convenient to consider inverse semigroups/monoids in this enriched signature because it is
well known that the class of inverse semigroups in this signature forms a variety, defined by the identical laws
(xy)^{-1}=y^{-1}x^{-1}
,
(x^{-1})^{-1}=x
,

xx^{-1}x=x
and
xx^{-1}yy^{-1}=yy^{-1}xx^{-1}
, with the latter effectively telling us that idempotent elements commute and thus form a semilattice.
Inverse semigroups have nowadays a rich and developed theory
[
15
,
23
]
, forming a bridge between groups and semigroups
[
20
]
, and making crucial
appearances in many mathematical areas such as differential geometry, topology, category theory, and even outside mathematics, e.g. in the formal structural
analysis in music theory
[
3
,
12
]
. Also, inverse monoids are today an indispensable tool in studying the
Adyan problem

[
22
]
, the question
whether all one-relator monoids have algorithmically soluble word problem, which amazingly still stands open even though the analogous problem for one-relator
groups was famously solved in the positive by Magnus
[
17
,
18
]
almost a century ago.

Every inverse semigroup carries a natural partial order defined by
x\leq y
if and only if
x=xx^{-1}y
(it can be shown that this is equivalent to
x=yx^{-1}x
).
This order is compatible both with multiplication and the inversion operations. On the other hand, it is easy to show that the intersection of any family of
congruences
\rho
of the inverse semigroup/monoid
S
with the property that
S/\rho
is a group again has the same property, and hence there is a
minimum
group congruence

\sigma
on
S
, so that
S/\sigma
is the maximum group homomorphic image of
S
. Of course, we may consider the restrictions of the order
\leq

to each
\sigma
-class of
S
. If it turns out that each such
\sigma
-class in an inverse monoid
S
has a maximum element with respect to the corresponding restriction
of
\leq
then we say the
S
is
F
-inverse
. An inverse monoid
S
is called
E
-unitary
if the
\sigma
-class of the identity element consists
precisely of the idempotents of
S
; clearly,
a\leq 1
implies that
a
is an idempotent, thus the
F
-inverse property of an inverse monoid implies that it
must be
E
-unitary. The
E
-unitary property is ubiquitous in inverse semigroup theory
[
15
,
23
]
, and, in a way, the
E
-unitary inverse monoids
are the ones that are considered the “well-behaved” ones.

In their influential paper
[
9
]
, Henckell and Rhodes posed, among others, the conjecture that every finite inverse monoid
M
has a finite
F
-inverse cover, that is,
there is an idempotent separating homomorphism from a finite
F
-inverse monoid to
M
, highlighting the importance of this property. This conjecture was confirmed only
recently by Auinger, Bitterlich and Otto
[
1
]
. On the other hand, in 2018 it was noted by M. Kinyon
[
13
]
that upon introducing an additional unary
operation
a\mapsto a^{\mathfrak{m}}
, which maps
a\in M
of an
F
-inverse monoid to the (unique) maximal element in the
\sigma
-class of
a
, the class of
F
-inverse
monoids becomes a variety in this enriched signature. This has immediately spurred a systematic study of
F
-inverse monoids as algebraic structures of type

\langle 2,1,1\rangle
resulting in the seminal paper
[
2
]
, where, given an
X
-generated group
G
, an
F
-inverse monoid
F(G,X)
was identified enjoying
the universal property for the class of all
X
-generated
F
-inverse monoids
M
such that their maximum group quotients
M/\sigma
are canonical morphic images of
G
.
This very much reminds to the
Margolis-Meakin expansion

M(G,X)
which has the analogous property with respect to the class of all
X
-generated
E
-unitary
inverse monoids
M
whose maximum group quotients
M/\sigma
are canonical morphic images of
G
; and indeed, the construction of
F(G,X)
is inspired by such expansions,
operating in the realm of pointed subgraphs of the Cayley graph
\Cay(G,X)
of the group
G
. In fact, in
[
14
]
it was later proved that one can obtain
(the inverse monoid reduct of)
F(G,X)
as a quotient of
M(G,Y)
, where
Y=X\cup\overline{G}
with
\overline{G}
being a bijective copy of
G
disjoint from
X
. Another
important recent paper in this vein is
[
26
]
, which provides a general connection between
E
-unitary /
F
-inverse monoids and closure operators on the
semilattice of (connected) subgraphs of the Cayley graph of
G
.

In this paper our aim is to introduce, in Section
3
, yet another inverse monoid
M_{F}(G,X)
with a universal property, starting from an
X
-generated group

G
. This inverse monoid will be universal for the class of all
X
-generated inverse monoids whose maximum group quotients are morphic images of
G
and which possess
the
F
-inverse property. This inverse monoid arises as a quotient of
M(G,X)
(reflecting the fact that any
F
-inverse monoid must be
E
-unitary); we identify the
corresponding congruence of the latter inverse monoid, leading us immediately to an inverse monoid presentation for
M_{F}(G,X)
. In Section 3 we also provide a geometric
model for
M_{F}(G,X)
based on suitable closure operators on subgraphs of
\Cay(G,X)
. In Section
4
this presentation is simplified in the case when
the group
G
admits a presentation in which every relator word defines a simple cycle in its Cayley graph (by
[
28
]
this includes all one-relator groups).
We then use this information to provide a characterisation of all one-relator special inverse monoids
\Inv\langle{X}\,|\,{w=1}\rangle
with a cyclically reduced relator word
w

that are
F
-inverse. Prior to all of this, in the next section we gather all the prerequisites necessary for stating and proving our results. For all undefined
notions we refer to
[
4
,
5
,
10
,
16
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Green’s relations

A basic tool in studying the structure of semigroups are five equivalence relations
\mathscr{R},\mathscr{L},\mathscr{J},\mathscr{H},\mathscr{D}
called
Green’s relations
. For a semigroup
S
we define:

a\,\mathscr{R}\,b\Leftrightarrow aS^{1}=bS^{1},\quad a\,\mathscr{L}\,b\Leftrightarrow S^{1}a=S^{1}b,\quad a\,\mathscr{J}\,b\Leftrightarrow S^{1}aS^{1}=S^{1}bS^{1},

where
S^{1}
denotes
S\cup\{1\}
unless
S
is already a monoid. Further,
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{R}\cap\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{D}=\mathscr{R}\vee\mathscr{L}
, the latter being just
\mathscr{R}\circ\mathscr{L}
as it may be shown that

\mathscr{R}
and
\mathscr{L}
commute. An element
a\in S
is
regular
if it has an inverse
a^{\prime}
(as defined in the introduction), and a semigroup is
regular
if such are all its
elements. It may be shown that every
\mathscr{D}
-class either consist entirely of regular elements (in which case each of its
\mathscr{R}
- and
\mathscr{L}
-classes contains at lest one idempotent)
or have no regular elements at all. The condition that every element has a unique inverse defines an important subclass of regular semigroups, the
inverse semigroups
,
which are best viewed as algebraic structures with the additional inversion operation
a\mapsto a^{-1}
. The uniqueness of the inverse for every element readily implies
that each
\mathscr{R}
-class and each
\mathscr{L}
-class contains precisely one idempotent (and in this sense each
\mathscr{D}
-class is “square”), namely, for all
a\in S
we have

d(a)=aa^{-1}\;\mathscr{R}\;a\;\mathscr{L}\;r(a)=a^{-1}a

(the reason for the notation
d(a),r(a)
will become clear shortly). An element
a
of an inverse monoid
S
is called
right invertible
(or a
right unit
)
if
d(a)=1
; similarly,
a\in S
is
left invertible
if
r(a)=1
. An element
a\in S
is
invertible
(or a
unit
) if it is both right and left invertible.
Invertible elements of an inverse monoid form its subgroup, the
group of units

U_{S}
of
S
.

2.2.
Alphabets, generation, free structures, presentations

If
X\neq\varnothing
is an alphabet, for the sake of representing and considering elements of groups, inverse monoids, and involution semigroups in general, it is useful to
introduce the “doubled” alphabet
\overline{X}=X\cup X^{-1}
, where
X^{-1}
is a copy of
X
(disjoint from
X
). Then
\overline{X}^{*}
is the free involution monoid, meaning that
every map
\iota:X\to S
where
S
is an involution monoid uniquely extends to a homomorphism of involution monoids
\overline{X}^{*}\to S
. If
\iota(X)
generates
S
we say that
(the involution monoid /  inverse monoid /  group)
S
is
X
-generated
(via
\iota
, which is in general not required to be injective). In such a case, every
element of
S
is represented by some word
w\in\overline{X}^{*}
; to distinguish between words as such and elements of
S
represented by them we write
[w]_{S}
for the element
of
S
represented by
w
, or just
[w]
if the structure in question (e.g. a group) is clear from the context. For two
X
-generated structures
S_{1},S_{2}
via
\iota_{1},\iota_{2}
, respectively, we say that a morphism
\psi:S_{1}\to S_{2}
is
canonical
if it respects the generators, that is if
\iota_{2}=\psi\iota_{1}
.

A word
w\in\overline{X}^{*}
is
reduced
if it does not contain a subword of the form
xx^{-1}
,
x^{-1}x
,
x\in X
. Otherwise, if a word
w
is not reduced, it is easy
to show that the process of successively removing subwords of the indicated form from
w
is confluent and thus terminates by the unique
reduced form

\red(w)
of
w
.
The collection of all reduced words over
\overline{X}
forms the
free group

FG(X)
on
X
under the operations
u\cdot v=\red(uv)
and
u^{-1}
obtained from
u
by
reversing the order of letters and then applying
-1
to each of them. A word
w\in\overline{X}^{*}
is
cyclically reduced
if it is reduced and the first and the last
letters of
w
are not mutually inverse.

Since inverse monoids form a variety,
free inverse monoids

FIM(X)
exist for all alphabets
X
. This is the quotient of
\overline{X}^{*}
by the
so-called
Wagner congruence
, generated by the pairs
(uu^{-1}u,u)
and
(uu^{-1}vv^{-1},vv^{-1}uu^{-1})
for all
u,v\in\overline{X}^{*}
. There is an elegant geometric
model for
FIM(X)
, due to Munn
[
21
]
and Scheiblich
[
24
]
, one which we will see a bit later, in Subsection
2.7
.

We say that an
X
-generated group
G
is defined by a
presentation

\Gp\langle{X}\,|\,{w_{i}=1\;(i\in I)}\rangle

if
G
is isomorphic to the quotient
FG(X)/N
where
N
is the normal subgroup of
FG(X)
generated (as a normal subgroup) by
w_{i}
,
i\in I
(here, the words
w_{i}
called

relators
are usually assumed to be reduced and even cyclically reduced). Group presentations constitute the main object of study of
combinatorial group theory

[
16
]
. Similarly, for an inverse monoid
M
we define that it is given by an inverse monoid presentation

\Inv\langle{X}\,|\,{u_{i}=v_{i}\;(i\in I)}\rangle

if
M
is isomorphic to the quotient of
FIM(X)
by its congruence generated by
\{(u_{i},v_{i}):\ i\in I\}
. Note that, unlike for groups, in the environment of inverse monoid
presentations we are neither allowed to cancel adjacent mutually inverse letters or words nor is the
defining relation

u_{i}=v_{i}
equivalent to
u_{i}v_{i}^{-1}=1
.
In the case when
M
admits a presentation by relations in which one of the words involved is the empty word
1
we say that
M
is
special
. A structure is

finitely presented
if it admits a presentation by finitely many generators and relations. We refer to
[
25
]
for the basic notions and techniques
related to the theory of inverse monoid presentations.

2.3.
Invertible pieces in special inverse monoids

Let

M=\Inv\langle{X}\,|\,{w_{i}=1\;(i\in I)}\rangle

be a special inverse monoid. Then each word
w_{i}
,
i\in I
, has the finest factorisation

w_{i}=w_{i,1}\cdots w_{i,k_{i}}

into subwords such that each
w_{i,j}
represents an invertible element of
M
; in other words, that the invertible factors are minimal in the sense that no
proper prefix or proper suffix of
w_{i,j}
represents an invertible element of
M
. Such minimal invertible factors are called the
pieces
of
M
.
It follows from
[
11
, Proposition 4.2]
that elements represented by the pieces of
M
generate
U_{M}
, the group of units of
M
. The recent paper
[
8
]

goes further by describing presentations for such groups of units. We also refer to the same paper for an extensive discussion of many contrasts regarding
the pieces and properties of groups of units with respect to ordinary monoid presentations and special monoids. In particular, while there is the
Adyan
overlap algorithm

[
6
,
8
,
22
]
for computing the pieces of finitely presented special monoids, it is not known if such an algorithm even exist for finitely
presented special inverse monoids.

2.4.
Symmetric inverse monoids

One of the earliest results of abstract group theory, the
Cayley theorem
, tells us that every group
G
is isomorphic to a group of permutations, as

G
embeds into
\mathbb{S}_{G}
, the symmetric group on the set
G
. In a very similar fashion, every monoid
M
is isomorphic to a monoid of transfomrations, as

M
embeds into
\mathcal{T}_{M}
, the full monoid of transformations on the set
M
. The role of the symmetric group and the full transformation monoid is in
inverse semigroup theory played by
\mathcal{I}_{A}
, the
symmetric inverse monoid
on
A
, which consists of all partial injective mappings on
A
with the
usual operations of composition (
\circ
) of partial mappings and of taking inverse (
-1
) of a partial injection. Unlike the morphisms in this paper
(which are composed from right to left and written on the left of their arguments), it is customary to compose mappings in
\mathcal{I}_{A}
(just as in
\mathcal{T}_{M}
)
from left to right. In particular, if
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{A}
we have that
d(\alpha)=\alpha\alpha^{-1}
is the identity mapping on the
domain
of
\alpha
,
and
r(\alpha)=\alpha^{-1}\alpha
is the identity mapping on the
range
of
\alpha
(thus explaining the notation for these idempotents). In general, the
idempotents of
\mathcal{I}_{A}
are exhausted by the identity mappings on subsets of
A
, and consequently
\alpha\,\mathscr{R}\,\beta
if and only if
\alpha,\beta

have the same domain, while
\alpha\,\mathscr{L}\,\beta
if and only if
\alpha,\beta
have the same range (image).

By the
Wagner-Preston theorem

[
5
,
10
,
15
,
23
]
, every inverse monoid/semigroup
S
is isomorphic to an inverse submonoid of
\mathcal{I}_{S}
.

2.5.
Graphs and Cayley graphs

In this paper all graphs considered are labelled digraphs
\Gamma=(V,E,\ell)
, where
V
is the set of
vertices
,
E
is the set of
edges
such that each
edge
e
has an initial vertex
\alpha(e)\in V
and a terminal vertex
\omega(e)\in V
, while
\ell:E\to Y
is a labelling function (the labels coming from a set
Y
).
A
path
is a non-empty sequence
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}
of consecutive edges (
\omega(e_{j})=\alpha(e_{j+1})
for all
1\leq j<m
) or just a vertex
v\in V
considered
as the empty path around
v
. A path is
simple
if all vertices involved in it, namely
\alpha(e_{1}),\dots,\alpha(e_{m}),\omega(e_{m})
, are distinct. A path
in which
\omega(e_{m})=\alpha(e_{1})
is called a cycle (
based
at
\alpha(e_{1})
); it is a
simple cycle
if all initial vertices of its edges are distinct.
By a
subgraph
of
\Gamma
we mean a subset of
V\cup E
closed under
\alpha
and
\omega
; the subgraph
spanned
by a path consists of all edges of the
path and all initial and terminal vertices of these edges. For a path
p=e_{1}\dots e_{m}
we define
\alpha(p)=\alpha(e_{1})
and
\omega(p)=\omega(e_{m})
. Two paths

p
and
q
can be composed if
\omega(p)=\alpha(q)
in which case the resulting path arises as the concatenation of two sequences of edges. Two paths
p,q
are
called
coterminal
if
\alpha(p)=\alpha(q)
and
\omega(p)=\omega(q)
. A (sub)graph is
strongly connected
if for any two vertices
u,v
involved
there is a (simple) path
p
such that
\alpha(p)=u
and
\omega(p)=v
.

A typical example of a labelled digraph in the sense just presented is the
(right) Cayley graph

\Cay(G,X)
of an
X
-generated group
G
. Here the vertices
are all the elements of the group
G
, the set of labels is
\overline{X}
, and for each
x\in\overline{X}
there is an edge from
g\in G
to
g[x]
. Notice that every such
edge
e
has an
inverse
edge
e^{-1}
so that
\alpha(e^{-1})=\omega(e)
and
\omega(e^{-1})=\alpha(e)
, as
g[x][x^{-1}]=g
must hold for all
g\in G
and

x\in\overline{X}
. In such graphs there is a path from
u
to
v
if and only if there is a path from
v
to
u
, and in that sense we may speak simply about the

connectedness
of graphs and subgraphs. Also, it is customary for the notion of the subgraph to require that it is also closed for the
-1
operation.
The
label
of a path
p=e_{1}\dots e_{m}
is the word
\ell(e_{1})\cdots\ell(e_{m})\in\overline{X}^{*}
or just the empty word for a empty path around a vertex.
An important remark is that
G
has a natural action by labelled graph automorphisms via left multiplication: indeed, for any
g_{1},g_{2},h\in G
there is an
edge labelled by
x\in\overline{X}
from
g_{1}
to
g_{2}
if and only if there is such an edge from
hg_{1}
to
hg_{2}
. In this sense, for a subgraph
\Delta\subseteq\Cay(G,X)
and
g\in G
we write
g\Delta
as the result of this left action on
\Delta
: all the vertices of
\Delta
are multiplied from the left by
g
.
In
\Cay(G,X)
whenever we spot a cycle based at
1
and labelled by
w\in\overline{X}^{*}
then we have
[w]_{G}=1
(for which we also say that “
w=1
holds in
G
”);
however, because of the previous fact, this is true for
any
cycle in
\Cay(G,X)
, regardless on the vertex
g
it is based at, because if
\Xi
is the
subgraph spanned by the cycle then
g^{-1}\Xi
is a subgraph spanned by a cycle based at
1
.

Cayley graphs are
deterministic
in the sense that every pathis uniquely determined by its initial vertex and its label. For a word
w\in\overline{X}^{*}

we denote by
\Gamma_{w}
the subgraph of
\Cay(G,X)
spanned by the path starting at
1
and labelled by the word
w
. Note that such a graph necessarily contains
the vertex
[w]
, as this is the terminal vertex of the path inducing
\Gamma_{w}
. In fact, for the sake of simplifying somewhat the terminology, we are going to
identify simple paths and subgraphs spanned by them whenever this does not cause confusion.

2.6.
Van Kampen diagrams

Van Kampen diagrams

[
16
]
are convenient topological/geometric tools invented to analyse the word problem of a group, that is the question whether
a word
w\in\overline{X}^{*}
satisfies
w=1
in the group. We assume that the underlying group is given by a presentation
G=\Gp\langle{X}\,|\,{w_{i}=1\;(i\in I)}\rangle
where all
words
w_{i}
are cyclically reduced. A van Kampen diagram
D
over
G
is a cell 2-complex along with its embedding into the real plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
the following properties hold:

•

there is a vertex (a 0-cell) called the
base vertex
;

•

the underlying 1-skeleton of
D
is a planar directed
\overline{X}
-labelled graph (so that the 1-cells are directed edges labelled by elements of
\overline{X}
)
closed under
-1
, meaning that for any edge
e
that belongs to
D
the edge
e^{-1}
also belongs to
D
;

•

D
is connected and simply connected;

•

for every
region
(2-cell)
R
of
D
, every vertex in the cycle formed by its boundary
\partial R
and every choice of the two possible directions,
the word labeling the cycle based at the chosen vertex is a cyclic conjugate of either
w_{i}
of
w_{i}^{-1}
for some
i\in I
.

Here, a word
w^{\prime}
is a
cyclic conjugate
of a word
w
if there is a factorisation
w^{\prime}=uv
such that
vu=w
. The
area
of
D
is the number of its regions.

Because of the connectedness and simple connectedness conditions, the boundary
\partial D
of this complex (which is also the boundary of the unbounded complement
of its planar embedding into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) induces a unique cycle (modulo direction) of its 1-skeleton. This cycle is called the
boundary cycle
of the
diagram; if the label of the boundary cycle (upon fixing a direction) is the word
w\in\overline{X}^{*}
then we say that we have a
van Kampen diagram for
w
over
G
.

A fundamental result for van Kampen diagrams is the
van Kampen Lemma

[
16
, Theorem V.1.1 & Lemma V.1.2]
which states (along with some extra information)
that
w=1
holds in
G
if and only if
w
has a van Kampen diagram over
G
.

2.7.
M(G,X)
and
F(G,X)

Let
G
be an
X
-generated group and let
M(G,X)
be the collection of all pairs
(\Gamma,g)
such that
\Gamma
is a finite connected subgraph of
\Cay(G,X)

containing the vertices
1
and
g
.
M(G,X)
turns into an inverse monoid with the operations defined by

(\Delta,g)(\Xi,h)=(\Delta\cup g\Xi,gh)

and

(\Delta,g)^{-1}=(g^{-1}\Delta,g^{-1}).

M(G,X)
is called the
Margolis-Meakin expansion
of the
X
-generated group
G

[
19
]
. This inverse monoid is

An application to one-relator special inverse monoids

The goal of this section is to apply the general results of the previous section, describing the universal inverse monoids with the
F
-inverse property,

This characetrisation will be given in terms of the factorisation of the relator word

We say that an

If a one-relator inverse monoid is a group, for example
\Inv\langle{a,b}\,|\,{aba=1}\rangle
, then it is obviously
F
-inverse, which happens
if and only if each of the minimal invertible pieces of
w
has precisely one letter. By way of an example, we now show that an
F
-inverse one-relator
inverse monoid is not necessarily a group.

Example 4.9
.

Let
M=\Inv\langle{a,b}\,|\,{(ab)^{n}=1}\rangle
.
We show that the minimal invertible pieces of
w=(ab)^{n}
with respect to
M
are precisely the factors
ab
. Since
w=1
, the word
ab
represents an invertible
element of
M
. We show that
a
and
b
are not invertible; for this it suffices to find a homomorphic image of
M
in which the images of
a
and
b
are not invertible.
Let
B=\Inv\langle{c}\,|\,{cc^{-1}=1}\rangle
be the bicyclic monoid
[
10
,
15
,
23
]
. In the inverse monoid
B\times C_{n}
, where
C_{n}=\langle d\rangle
is the cyclic group
of order
n
, define
\tilde{a}=(c,d)
and
\tilde{b}=(c^{-1},1)
. Then we have
(\tilde{a}\tilde{b})^{n}=(1,d)^{n}=(1,1)
. It follows that the assignment

a\mapsto\tilde{a}
,
b\mapsto\tilde{b}
extends to a homomorphism
M\to B\times C_{n}
. Since
\tilde{a}
is not invertible in
B\times C_{n}
,

a
is not invertible in
M
. Hence,
M
is not a group.

It would be interesting to tackle the following, more general problem, although it seems that it requires different techniques than those used in this paper.

Problem 4.10
.

Describe the one-relator special inverse monoids
M=\Inv\langle{X}\,|\,{w=1}\rangle
with the
F
-inverse property, regardless of whether
w
is cyclically reduced or not.

Remark 4.11
.

Certainly there are
F
-inverse one-relator special inverse monoids
M=\Inv\langle{X}\,|\,{w=1}\rangle
such that
w=1
not cyclically reduced. Namely, let
w\in\overline{X}^{*}
be a
non-reduced word such that
w^{\prime}=\red(w)
is cyclically reduced. By
[
7
, Corollary 3.2]
if a word
u
represents a right invertible element in an
inverse monoid then
u=\red(u)
holds in that inverse monoid, so
M
satisfies
w^{\prime}=1
. Consequently, there is a (canonical) homomorphism from
\Inv\langle{X}\,|\,{w^{\prime}=1}\rangle

to
M
preserving the
\sigma
relation, and thus if the former turns out to be
F
-inverse (i.e. if it satisfies the condition given in Theorem
4.8
) so is
M
.
If however
\Inv\langle{X}\,|\,{w^{\prime}=1}\rangle
is not
F
-inverse, the question of the
F
-inverse property for
M
remains open, just as in the case when
w
is reduced but
not cyclically reduced.
