Response to the Letter to the Editor Entitled “From Molecular Signal to Clinical Action in Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Disease 2”
Authors: William R Marshall, Thomas McDonnell, Darren Green, Smeeta Sinha, Philip A Kalra
Published: 2026

The author replies:

We thank Wu et al.1 for their response to our review2 and agree with the adoption of their 3-tier hierarchy for biomarker evidence, which maps roughly onto the established triad of analytic validity, clinical validity, and clinical utility.3 Our refinement is that each tier makes a claim about a different object—an assay, a comparison, and a care pathway.

Tier 1, analytic validity, is a claim about the assay. Aptamer and antibody measures of the same protein diverge across ancestry groups, attributed partly to protein-altering variant frequency4— the portability concern raised for polygenic risk scores recurs in measurement.

Tier 2, incremental value, belongs not to a biomarker but to a comparison—a named reference model and end point, and a metric, whether discrimination or net benefit. Their chronic kidney disease-versus-estimated glomerular filtration rate-decline contrast1 changes the end point, and the answer changes with it. Neither answer is a clinical utility claim.

Tier 3, clinical usage, is a claim about the care pathway. A biomarker-strategy trial cannot separate the biomarker from the threshold and action applied to it,5 so a positive result shows the pathway works, not which component made it work. A null result does not invalidate Tier 1 or Tier 2 and may say nothing about the pathway either. In GUIDE-IT, the arms barely diverged on NT-proBNP, so the strategy was never tested.6

We take Wu et al.’s point about metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. Our review used the diagnosis to modify cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic disease stage 1, letting presence alone carry a Tier-2 claim. Placement should follow the stepwise fibrosis pathway they cite; fibrosis determines how much weight the modifier carries, not whether it applies. Atherogenic lipids raise a separate Tier-2 question.

We agree that their 4 requirements are prerequisites, not refinements. An endotype is a classification, but its clinical claim, that it selects treatment, is a Tier 3 claim. Standardization and reporting are Tier 1-work; transportability, as they cite it, is Tier 2. The fourth, prospective utility testing, is that Tier 3 claim, which no cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic endotype has met. This list still names no incremental-value step: evidence that the endotype predicts outcome beyond cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic staging.

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic outcome trials already adjudicate endpoints under standardized follow-up. Serial biospecimen collection would settle Tier 2 against a named model and end point and identify the biomarker and threshold for a Tier-3 strategy trial.
