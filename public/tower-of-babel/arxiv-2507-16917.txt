Atomic hydrogen reservoirs in quiescent galaxies at z = 0.4 z=0.4
A. Bianchetti, G. Rodighiero, D. Donevski, F. Sinigaglia, E. Elson, M. Vaccari, A. Marasco, L. Bisigello, I. Prandoni, M. Baes, M. Glowacki, F. M. Maccagni, G. Lorenzon, I. Heywood

Abstract
Context. Based on Local Universe observations, quiescent galaxies (QGs) host lower to no H I compared to star-forming galaxies (SFGs), but no constraints have been derived so far at higher redshift ( z > 0.1 z>0.1 ). Understanding whether QGs can retain significant H I reservoirs at higher z is crucial to refine quenching and gas accretion models and to constrain overall star formation efficiency at different epochs. Aims. We aim to probe H I in candidate QGs at intermediate redshifts ( ⟨ z ⟩ ≈ 0.36 \langle z\rangle\approx 0.36 ) and to understand whether there exists a class of QGs retaining consistent H I reservoirs and which parameters (dust content, stellar mass, D n D_{n} 4000, morphology, environment) effectively capture H I -rich QGs. Methods. We perform 21-cm spectral line stacking on MIGHTEE-HI data at ⟨ \langle z ⟩ = 0.36 \rangle=0.36 , targeting two different samples of QGs, defined by means of a color-selection criterion and a spectroscopic criterion based on D n D_{n} 4000, respectively. We also perform stacking on subsamples of the spectroscopically-selected quiescent sample to investigate the correlation between the H I content and other galaxy properties. Results. We find that QGs with an IR counterpart (i.e., dusty galaxies) are found to host a substantial H I content, on average just 40 % 40\% lower than SFGs. In contrast, color-selected QGs still hold H I , but lower than SFGs by a factor ∼ \sim 3. Among dusty objects, we find morphology to have a mild impact on the atomic gas content, with spirals hosting approximately 15 − 30 % 15-30\% more H I than spheroids. Environmental effects are also present, with low-density regions hosting galaxies that are H I -richer than in high-density ones, by approximately 30 % 30\% for spirals and 60 % 60\% for spheroids. We suggest that, in general, H I content is driven by several factors, including quenching mechanisms and ISM enrichment processes. Also, quiescent galaxies — and especially dusty systems — seem to yield H I more consistently than in the Local Universe.



1
Introduction

Although not directly fueling star formation, neutral atomic hydrogen (H
I
) is a key ISM phase, especially at high redshift, and offers insight into quenching and gas retention and rejuvenation, especially becoming dominant at high z
(
Péroux & Howk 2020
)
.

While traditionally regarded as gas-poor, many recent works exhibit evidence of cold molecular gas in QGs
(
Combes et al. 2007
;
Davis et al. 2013
;
Gobat et al. 2018
)
. In particular, analysis of the ATLAS-3D sample
(
Cappellari et al. 2011
)
reports relevant amounts of atomic gas in ETGs
(
Serra et al. 2012
;
Maccagni et al. 2017
;
Yıldız et al. 2020
)
.
Young et al. 2014
further show that even these ETGs might have quenched from blue progenitors while retaining a fraction of their cold gas budget. Later work based on the SDSS
(
Abazajian et al. 2009
)
, the ALFALFA
(
Giovanelli et al. 2005
)
, the GASS
(
Catinella et al. 2010
)
), and the COLD GASS
(
Saintonge et al. 2011
)
surveys show evidence that the vast majority of massive quiescent disk galaxies yield abundant H
I
reservoirs
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
.

Conversely, other works have found a lack of H
I
in QGs. In the Local Universe,
Cortese et al. 2020
found no H
I
in quiescent spirals from the Extragalactic GASS survey (xGASS;
Catinella et al. 2018
), claiming that reduced star formation correlates with depletion of H
I
reservoirs.
Geréb et al. 2016
;
Geréb et al. 2018
reported only isolated cases of H
I
-rich QGs, terming them H
I
-excess galaxies. Building on this,
Saintonge & Catinella 2022
point out that the existence of galaxies below the Main Sequence (MS) with relevant H
I
reservoirs is not in contradiction with the broad trend of H
I
-poor QGs, as they yield low H
I
fractions. At slightly higher redshift (
\rm 0.04<z<0.09
), stacking color-selected QGs in the Deep Investigation of Neutral Gas Origins (DINGO;
Meyer 2009
) survey reveals no H
I

(
Rhee et al. 2023
)
. The existence of contradictory results makes the presence of atomic gas in QGs a debated issue.

The H
I
content of QGs beyond the Local Universe is still a rather unexplored topic and would unlock a better understanding of star formation efficiency throughout cosmic time.
In this letter, we stack samples of candidate QGs in the COSMOS field (from
Bianchetti et al. 2025
and
Donevski et al. 2023
, respectively
4
and
20
hereon) at a mean redshift
\rm z\approx 0.37
. We use the MIGHTEE survey (MeerKAT International Giga-Hertz Tiered Extragalactic Exploration,
Jarvis et al. 2016
;
Heywood et al. 2024
) data from the MeerKAT radio interferometer
(
Jonas & MeerKAT Team 2016
)
and study the presumed H
I
content of the two samples of quenched galaxies.

We assume a flat (
\Omega_{k}
=0) Lambda-CDM Cosmology, employing cosmological parameters from
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
, i.e.,
H_{0}=67.4\penalty\ {\rm km\penalty\ s}^{-1}\penalty\ {\rm Mpc^{-1}}
,
\Omega_{m}=0.315
, and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.685
, and an Initial Mass Function as in
Chabrier 2003
.

2
Data and sample

In this work, we stack spectra extracted from MIGHTEE-HI DR1
(
Heywood et al. 2024
)
, a survey made of 15 deep MeerKAT pointings (94.2 h total) within the COSMOS field. MIGHTEE-HI DR1 covers two separate frequency ranges (960-1150 MHz and 1290-1520 MHz), where the former is the one used in this paper, targeting the H
I
21 cm line in the redshift range
0.22<z<0.5
. We refer to
Heywood et al. 2024
for exhaustive details on data reduction and an accurate description of data products.

As input for the stacking pipeline, we use two different catalogs of candidate QGs, both covering the COSMOS field. The former defines quiescence with a double rest-frame color (
NUV-r
vs
r-J
) criterion and is the same catalog used in
4
. We refer to that paper for a full description.
The latter is inherited by
20
(to which we refer for details) and is originally made by 4362 galaxies at
0.01<z<0.7
with spectroscopic data from the hCOSMOS survey
(
Damjanov et al. 2018
)
. Here, the quiescent nature of the selected objects is identified by a combination of criteria: high 4000 Å-break strength (
D_{n}4000>1.5
), ensuring robust separation between star-forming and candidate QGs (i.e.,
Balogh et al. 1999
,
Damjanov et al. 2025
), and at least one additional criterion - either low specific star-formation rate (sSFR), placing objects more than five times below the
Speagle et al. 2014
main-sequence, or UVJ color criteria for QGs (
Muzzin et al. 2013
). This catalog is supplemented with IR counterparts from Herschel Extragalactic Legacy Project
(
Shirley et al. 2019
, HELP, )
to assess the dust properties of the sample.
20
defines a candidate QG as dusty if it is detected (S/N¿3) in at least four bands at
8\leq\lambda\leq 500\mu m
, with a reference 1
\sigma
sensitivity of
\rm\approx 1mJy
at
\rm\lambda=250\mu m
. Galaxies not meeting this criterion are considered ordinary, dust-poor QGs.

We display the properties of the investigated sample in Fig.
4
and discuss their quiescent nature in Appendix
A
. We also retrieve information about the environment (in particular, the overdensity parameter
\delta
=
\rho
/
\bar{\rho}
) by cross-matching our catalog with the density field constructed by
Darvish et al. 2017
.
Finally, we assign a morphology classification (Spiral, Spheroid, or Irregular) to each galaxy through visual inspection of F814W HST/ACS cutouts
(
Koekemoer et al. 2007
;
Massey et al. 2010
)
. These reveal a rich morphological diversity, including disks, bars, prominent bulges, spheroids, and irregular shapes.

3.1
A link between dust and H
I

We stack candidate QGs from the two catalogs and report the results in Fig.
1
, displaying the
M_{\rm HI}-M_{\star}
plane: as a reference, we also plot the
M_{\rm HI}
-
M_{\star}
scaling relation for SFGs from
4
. Besides the uncertainties on the H
I
masses (see App.
B
for more details), we also add uncertainties on the stellar mass domain, computed as the standard deviation of the bin.
For the QG color-selected catalog, we find that different stellar mass bins produce a statistically significant signal only in the high-mass end (i.e.
\log{(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})}
¿10.5, see bottom right panel of Fig.
5
). The amount of H
I
is a factor
\sim 3
less than what is retained by SFGs at the same stellar mass.
For the spectroscopically-quenched sample, stacking non-dusty QGs only leads to an upper limit on H
I
content (Fig.
5
, bottom left), computed as 3 times the RMS value obtained from the stack. In contrast, dusty QGs offer enough statistics to further divide the subsample into galaxies with
\log{(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})}>10.5
and galaxies with
8.0<\log{(M_{\star}/M_{\odot})}<10.5
(Fig.
5
, top panels), with both bins showing evidence for statistical detection of H
I
.

Fig.
1
suggests that QGs present diverse H
I
content, linked to diverse ISM properties: color-selected QGs are likely to encompass a very diverse dust mass range and to be mixing dust-rich and dust-poor objects, causing the H
I
stacked signal to be diluted. When studying the spectroscopically-selected QGs, instead, we observe a correlation between dust and H
I
content. Specifically, dust-rich galaxies are found to feature a significant H
I
amount, on average only
40\%
less abundant than MS galaxies. The existence of dusty candidate QGs is documented up to
z\sim 3
;
Gobat et al. 2018
,
Morishita et al. 2022
,
Rodighiero et al. 2023
,
Lee et al. 2024
,
Siegel et al. 2024
). Recent studies suggest that dust-rich QGs can retain cold dust and gas for extended periods (
>2
Gyr;
Michałowski et al. 2024
,
Lee et al. 2024
,
Lorenzon et al. 2025
). The diversity in the dust-cold gas mass ratio
(
Whitaker et al. 2021
;
Morishita et al. 2022
;
Donevski et al. 2023
)
hints at the fact that dust leftovers may not be exclusively relics of star formation. Grain growth on ISM metals is indicated as a dominant dust production channel to reproduce the observed
M_{\rm dust}/M_{\star}
distribution.
The presence of H
I
in dusty QGs, as reported in this letter, is likely to be connected to this scenario. In fact, H
I
reservoirs may be shielding dust from UV radiation and favoring its growth, thus allowing it to survive longer, as proposed by recent theoretical studies (
Chen & Oh 2024
).

3.2
Correlation with age, morphology and environment

We focus on the dusty, H
I
-rich QG sample and try to uncover any dependence on the environment or morphology.

First, we split the sample into three
D_{n}4000
bins (1.5¡
D_{n}
4000¡1.61, 1.61¡
D_{n}
4000¡1.8 and
D_{n}
4000¿1.8) and got a significant detection (S/N¿4) in all cases (Fig.

).
Fig.
2

I
fractions (where
f_{\rm HI}=M_{\rm HI}/M_{\star}
) as a function of the average
D_{n}
4000 value per each bin. A dashed blue line indicates the best-fit for a linear model. Uncertainties on the

This suggests that recently quenched systems with low
D_{n}
4000 have not yet consumed, expelled, or ionized their H
I
reservoirs, or have somehow reaccreted H
I
from the circumgalactic medium, via mechanisms such as tidal streams or mergers.

To investigate environmental properties, we use the overdensity parameter from
Darvish et al. 2017
and define two separate bins, one gathering galaxies lying in regions denser than average (
\delta
¿1) and one where density is lower than average (
\delta
¡1). Separately stacking the two groups provides a robust signal in both cases (S/N¿7, see Fig.
7
). Fig.
3
displays the logarithm of the ratio between the two H
I
mass estimates in the two environment bins and the H
I
mass expected at their average stellar mass for SFGs according to
4
(purple full squares). The higher-density stack yields an amount of H
I
just
\approx
0.15 dex below the lower-density case. Environmental factors are well-known to influence gas content, especially in the stellar mass range that we examine
(
Contini et al. 2020
)
.
Li et al. 2025a
argue that H
I
content is more strongly regulated by environment than morphology, highlighting that satellite galaxies in low-density regions often remain gas-rich. In contrast, high-density environments promote gas depletion through processes such as ram pressure stripping, tidal interactions, and starvation. Our observational frame fits with this interpretation.
Recent SIMBA-based results
(
Lorenzon et al. 2025
)
qualitatively support this picture. First, while dust- and gas-rich quiescent galaxies are found in both field and cluster environments, galaxies residing in less massive halos exhibit reduced dust destruction rates. If dust and H
I
share common removal timescales post-quenching, as observationally suggested
(
Michałowski et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, this aligns with our findings of slightly richer H
I
reservoirs in lower-density environments. Moreover, the same analysis indicates that satellites can be a secondary channel for enhancing dust and cold gas content primarily through mergers, rather than rejuvenation episodes.

Following our visual morphology classification, we stack spirals and irregulars together, while spheroids enter a separate bin.
We obtain a clear H
I
detection in both cases (S/N¿7, see Fig.
8
). A dotted crimson and dotted teal horizontal line in Fig.
3
marks the logarithm of the ratio between H
I
estimates for spirals and spheroids, respectively, over the H
I
mass for same-stellar mass MS galaxies, against the median
\delta
value for the two bins. A similar median
\delta
proves that the two morphology classes share similar environmental properties. Dusty quiescent spirals/irregulars have about 40% less H
I
mass than MS galaxies, while dusty spheroids are H
I
-poorer (
\approx
75% less than MS galaxies).
20
mirror these results, as they report that dusty spirals generally have slightly higher
M_{\rm dust}
/
M_{\star}
ratio than spheroids.

The presence of H
I
in quiescent spirals is not a novel result in the Local Universe.
(
Zhang et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
have shown that massive, quiescent disk galaxies present H
I
fractions ranging from 10 to 30%, comparable to those in SFGs. These studies attribute the cause of the quenching to a lack of molecular gas and dust, along with low star formation efficiency, while H
I
reservoirs survive instead. The proximity in H
I
content of our red spirals to MS galaxies suggests that these objects might be in some transitioning state — either off the MS or towards the green valley — i.e., experiencing a lull phase, or a post-starburst phase
(
Ellison et al. 2025
, also see)
. This also matches with the picture emerging from the SED fitting carried out by
20
, which suggests that the SFHs of most galaxies in the sample are consistent with quenching in the last 1 Gyr. Their rich H
I
and dust content hints to the potential for future starburst activity, placing them in a ”mini-quenching” phase
(
Dome et al. 2024
;
Looser et al. 2025
)
, pending the restoration of molecular hydrogen. Moreover, a growing number of observational studies link the occurrence of H
I
-rich QGs and post-starbursts to slow quenching mechanisms (e.g., morphological quenching), where H
I
reservoirs persist long after the final burst (
Michałowski et al. 2024
,
Li et al. 2025b
,
Ellison et al. 2025
). This conclusion is further supported by theoretical studies using SIMBA cosmological simulation with dust modeling, where slow quenching is found to be prevalent amongst dusty QGs with old stellar populations (
>5-10
Gyr)
(
Lorenzon et al. 2025
)
.

Instead, the fact that spheroids hold significant amounts of H
I
— while still H
I
-poorer than spirals — requires some other H
I
enrichment channel. HST cutouts (App.
D
) reveal that spheroids are often accompanied by streams and merger remnants, suggesting that environmental properties may be playing some role. While we have hypothesized that high density environments favor H
I
stripping, this does not mean that reaccretion is uncommon for quenched spheroids. These may result from past mergers or quenching events, but frequent interaction with external gas supplies might favor accretion
(
Dutta et al. 2019
)
.

To better disentangle the impact of environment and morphology on dusty QGs, we split each morphology class in
\delta
bins and get significant signal in all four cases (S/N
\geq
4, Fig.
9
): two empty crimson stars and two empty teal circles mark the split respectively for spirals/irregulars and spheroids (Fig.
3
). This splitting shows how galaxies with different morphologies respond differently to environmental properties. On one hand, spirals in high-density environments are
\approx
30% H
I
-richer than the low-density peers, while the depletion is stronger (
\approx
60%) for spheroids. On the other hand, a comparison to the square purple bins, which mark environment bins irrespective of morphology, shows that in lower-density environments spirals and spheroids have very comparable H
I
content (within 15%), while at higher densities spheroids are more strongly affected, retaining 30% less H
I
than spirals. Note that wide error bars indicate that these results must be taken with caution. This implies that while both morphologies react qualitatively similarly to environment (i.e., decreasing H
I
with increasing density), the effect is quantitatively stronger for spheroids. Overall, we interpret the environment to be a double-edged agent: galaxies interact with the surroundings and accrete gas from some external source, to either maintain or regrow their H
I
supplies. However, the densest environments, e.g. galaxy clusters, have opposite effects, promoting quenching via processes like ram pressure stripping, X-ray heating, tidal interactions, and harassment, and likely favor the removal of H
I

(
Cortese et al. 2011
;
Marasco et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. In such conditions, larger dust reservoirs may survive if grain growth is efficient, enriching the interstellar medium with larger, more resilient grains (e.g.,
Aoyama et al. 2018
,
Nanni et al. 2024
). This may also explain the presence of QGs from this work that are both dust- and H
I
-rich regardless of environment.

4
Conclusions

This work reports the highest-redshift information on H
I
in quiescent galaxies yet, albeit consisting of a stacking-derived result. While a direct quantitative comparison with Local Universe studies among quiescent galaxies is limited by sample selection differences and the wide spread in HI measurements, our results suggest that substantial HI reservoirs may be more prevalent at
\rm z\approx 0.36
, particularly in dusty systems — hinting at possible evolution in gas retention or quenching processes with redshift.
Finally, an independent ALMA Band 6 follow-up of 18 QGs, including several objects from this study, reveals that despite reduced molecular gas fractions, dusty QGs can retain high dust-to-gas ratios (
\gtrsim 1/100
), comparable to those of SFGs (Lorenzon et al., in prep.). They find that the total cold ISM content declines only mildly with stellar age, with quiescent spirals typically hosting slightly richer dust reservoirs than ellipticals.
Together with these results, our findings support a scenario in which a combination of internal ISM processes, morphology, and slow quenching mechanisms enables the persistence of cold dust and gas in QGs for extended periods after quenching (
>1-2
Gyr).
However, SKA-level sensitivities
(
Dutta et al. 2022
)
will strengthen this pioneering study by contributing with a significantly larger sample of H
I
QGs at this redshift, possibly with statistically significant populations of direct detections.

We hereby summarize in short the main steps of the stacking procedure outlined in
Bianchetti et al. 2025
, to which we refer for an exhaustive description. We extract spectra of galaxies by collapsing subcubes defined by an aperture equal to three times the beamsize in the sky plane. Instead of using the mean beamsize across the frequency range and adopting it as a fixed reference
(
4
)
, we now create apertures as large as three times the beamsize accounting for its variation as a function of frequency. This way, aperture adapts to the frequency channel. Subcubes extend roughly
\pm
2000
\rm kms^{-1}
in the spectral direction and we regrid them in frequency to get a homogeneous channel width of 100
\rm kms^{-1}
. Then, we assign galaxies a weight given by 1/
\sigma^{\gamma}
, where
\sigma
is the RMS noise computed on the wings of the individual spectrum and
\gamma
is a power index to be selected. We pick
\gamma
=1 following
Fabello et al. 2011
;
Sinigaglia et al. 2022
;
Sinigaglia et al. 2024
;
Bianchetti et al. 2025
. At odds with
4
, we do not weigh by primary beam, as the MIGHTEE-HI DR1 data are already primary beam-corrected. Also, since the noise profile is slightly different, the RFI mask that we use to flag channels with unusual noise peaks is slightly more relaxed than the one used for Early Science data.

H
I
mass is computed by integrating the final, stacked spectrum within a
\pm
500
\rm kms^{-1}
spectral range. This is slightly larger than what was done in
4
, since stacked spectra in this work are generally broader. The reason for that might be linked to an intrinsically higher accuracy of the spec-z of SFGs, whose spectrum is typically crowded with emission lines, which makes redshift determination more precise. Uncertainties on H
I
mass are given by the RMS computed on the wings of the spectrum, i.e. the channels lying outside the integration region.

Figures
5
-
9
show the stacks from which we estimate the H
I
masses used in the analysis. Note that
5
sustains the claim that non-dusty galaxies contain no H
I
. We have tested whether or not the non-detection of H
I
in non-dusty galaxies solely depends on the limited size of the sample: in fact, non-dusty galaxies in the sample are roughly as numerous as one-third of the total dusty galaxies. However, the stack of several realizations of a subset of the dusty QGs, as big as the sample of non-dusty, always yielded a detection, proving that this is a physically driven result. Thus, we can assume that no H
I
above the depicted upper limit (gray triangle in Fig.
1
) is contained in non-dusty galaxies.
