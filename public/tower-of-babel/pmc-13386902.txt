Artificial intelligence in population-based cancer screening: evidence from a national survey of Italian professionals
Authors: Giovanni Fattore, Mattia Benato Cortecci, Renato Fasoli, Tiziano Maggino, Paola Mantellini, Silvia Deandrea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly proposed to enhance population-based cancer screening. While several applications are under evaluation, there is limited evidence on professional and organisational perspectives regarding their value, risks, and implementation challenges.MethodsWe conducted a web-based survey targeting coordinators and reference professionals of Italian cancer screening programmes. The survey comprised 39 questions and explored prior experience with AI, perceived benefits and risks, organisational and legal barriers, and willingness to invest in AI applications. Recruitment combined a cascade procedure through regional and sub-regional coordinators with distribution via the mailing list of the Federation of Oncology Screening Associations (FASO). Descriptive analyses summarised responses, while multiple linear regression assessed determinants of willingness to invest in AI applications.ResultsA total of 305 professionals responded, representing all but one Italian region. About one-third reported AI experience, most frequently in colorectal polyp detection and breast imaging support. Overall, 74.8% expressed a strong interest in adopting AI applications, particularly for risk prediction and diagnostic support. The most valued benefits were improved diagnostic accuracy, workflow optimisation, and shorter reporting times. Concerns focused on legal liability (72.8%), lack of transparency and algorithmic bias (67.2%), and professional de-skilling (46.2%). Organisational barriers included inadequate IT infrastructure (77.0%), absence of guidelines (75.7%), and limited financial resources (77.4%). Regression confirmed that higher perceived benefits were positively associated with willingness to invest in AI applications, whereas risks were negatively associated.Conclusions Italian screening professionals generally support the adoption of AI but emphasise the need for robust evidence, clear regulatory frameworks, and sustained investment in digital infrastructure to ensure its safe and effective implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27717-2.

Background

In Europe, nearly two million people die from cancer each year, with lung, breast, and colorectal cancers representing the leading causes of cancer-related mortality [1]. For several malignancies, early detection significantly improves the likelihood of successful treatment and reduces mortality by identifying disease at a more manageable stage [2]. Population-based cancer screening programmes for breast, colorectal, and cervical cancers are well established across most European countries, while others are still under evaluation (e.g., prostate and lung cancer) or are endorsed by scientific societies but not yet systematically implemented. In a 2022 recommendation, the Council of the European Union encouraged Member States to pilot population-based screening programmes for prostate and lung cancer, while maintaining breast, cervical, and colorectal cancer as the established organised screenings [3].

Implementing population-based screening is a complex public health undertaking that involves multiple sequential steps and interconnections. These steps include establishing and maintaining target population databases, identifying and inviting eligible individuals, administering the screening test, evaluating results, communicating findings to participants, referring positive cases for further assessment and treatment, and updating population-based cancer registries. Depending on the institutional organisation of each country,or even within regional health systems,screening programmes require effective interfaces among patients, healthcare providers, public health authorities, and additional institutions (e.g., mortality registries, organisations promoting screening adherence). Ensuring high quality across all steps and interfaces is challenging and requires political commitment, strong governance, citizen engagement, adequate financial and human resources, robust quality assurance mechanisms, advanced digitalisation with interoperable information systems, efficient organisational routines, and systematic evaluation procedures [4–6].

The widespread digitalisation of screening processes has substantially lowered the barriers to their implementation while simultaneously creating the conditions necessary for the adoption of Artificial Intelligence (AI)-driven solutions. Applications range from large language models (LLMs) for medical reporting and patient communication to AI-based detection tools in imaging, risk prediction algorithms, and digital pathology systems.

Rigorous scientific evidence is essential for the adoption of such innovations, although the appropriate study designs vary depending on the type of AI application. For example, prospective randomised trials are critical for validating AI in triage, scoring, and cancer detection tasks [7]. In contrast, the evaluation of LLMs may not require randomised controlled trials but instead calls for alternative, tailored research approaches [8]. Most AI solutions are inherently disruptive, entailing substantial digital, professional, and organisational transformation, along with the need for patient acceptance and clear legal and regulatory frameworks.

Several conceptual frameworks have been developed to identify factors that facilitate or hinder the implementation of health and healthcare technologies [9, 10]. Our study was informed by Greenhalgh and colleagues’ NASSS framework (Non-adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread, and Sustainability). The NASSS framework was derived from a hermeneutic literature review and six in-depth case studies using interviews, ethnography, document analysis, and action research, and was subsequently validated in ten additional cases.

The framework emphasises that the adoption, diffusion, non-adoption, and abandonment of health technologies are driven by complex, dynamic, and emergent processes that cannot be fully anticipated [11]. It delineates seven domains critical to understanding past experiences and guiding implementation efforts: (1) the condition or disease targeted, (2) the technology itself (features, service model, expertise required), (3) the value proposition (expected benefits), (4) the adopter system (implications for patients and professionals), (5) organisational capacity and readiness, (6) the wider context (social, political, economic, professional), and (7) embedding and adaptation over time.

We developed a survey to address these domains, acknowledging that a comprehensive analysis requires a mixed-methods approach, given the heterogeneity of AI applications and the possibility that respondents may not be aware of all relevant issues. The NASSS framework nonetheless provided a robust theoretical lens. Within this perspective, our study examined the perceived value and implementation challenges of AI solutions through a survey of Italian professionals engaged in cancer screening, with a particular focus on breast, cervical, and colorectal programmes.

Methods

Participants

The target population of this web-based survey comprised the network of professionals serving as reference points for local cancer screening programmes in Italy, including the three population-based programmes (breast, cervical, and colorectal) as well as local pilot programmes for lung and prostate cancer. The recruitment of survey participants followed a two-channel approach. First, the survey was distributed to all regional coordinators belonging to the National Centre for Screening Monitoring (ONS), a network of cancer screening regional reference centres. Then, each centre was asked to invite its local referents to participate, using its own contact lists. Due to the regionalised structure of the Italian National Health Service (NHS), the organisation of screening programmes varies considerably across regions, as do the professional backgrounds of the local referents. Second, the survey was distributed through the national mailing list of the Federation of Oncology Screening Associations (FASO), which represents the scientific societies of cancer screening professionals. No a priori sample size calculation was performed, as the survey targeted the full population of reference professionals of Italian cancer screening programmes rather than a sample drawn from a larger population.

Survey development and content

The survey was developed specifically for this study by three of the authors (MBC, GF, SD), refined through discussions with experts from each of the three population-based programmes (TM, RF, PM), and has not been published elsewhere. A pilot test with five target participants led to minor revisions to the structure and wording. To ensure transparent and comprehensive reporting, the study is reported according to the CROSS checklist (Consensus-based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies) (See Additional File 1) [12].

The survey aimed to capture participants’ experiences and views on the potential and implementation of AI applications in cancer screening within their local context.

To ensure feasibility, the survey was kept concise and focused on selected domains of the NASSS framework: value proposition, adopter system, organisational capacity and readiness, and, to a lesser extent, the wider context. The main section explored: (a) prior experience with AI applications; (b) interest and motivation to adopt different AI solutions; (c) perceived benefits; (d) opinions on professional and patient resistance; (e) organisational and legal barriers and (f) perceived drawbacks. Participants were also asked to rate, on a 1–10 scale, the intensity of their interest in investing in AI solutions in their local context.

In total, the questionnaire comprised 39 questions organised into 11 blocks and took an average of 12 min to complete, plus two open-ended items. Originally developed and administered in Italian, the survey was distributed via Google Forms on February 1, 2025, with one reminder sent on February 15, and another on March 2, for a total data collection period of approximately six weeks. We did not explicitly guarantee anonymity or confidentiality, as responses to some questions could indirectly reveal participants’ identities. An English version of the questionnaire is provided in Additional File 2.

Data management and statistical analysis

We checked that no multiple entries were submitted. The platform prevented respondents from answering more than once using the same email address. In addition, we cross-checked individual characteristics to rule out potential duplicate responses, and no evidence of multiple submissions was found.

All participants completed the survey, although they could exit at any time. The questionnaire contained no missing data, as all items required a response before proceeding to the next section.

To present the frequency of reported AI solutions, we combined the closed-ended responses to question 12 with the 114 open-ended responses to the same question, after checking for duplicate entries, and visualised the results using a word cloud [13]. We do not have information on the specific software products used by respondents; furthermore, given the variety of professional categories surveyed, not all respondents were aware of which specific software their facility was using. The survey, therefore, focused on types of AI applications (e.g., computer-aided detection, risk prediction algorithms, generative AI for reporting) rather than on specific commercial products. For the open-ended responses, semantically equivalent terms were grouped under the appropriate category label to avoid artificial fragmentation of the visualisation. Perceptions of barriers to implementation were summarised using a heatmap [14].

Then we conducted a multiple regression analysis using participants’ willingness to invest in AI applications, measured on a 10-point scale, as the dependent variable. We estimated four progressive model specifications: model (1) includes only the type of screening programme; model (2) adds geographical area (north, centre, south/islands); model (3) further adds centre type (public, private, research/university centre); and model (4) incorporates three standardised composite scores capturing perceived benefits, risks, and barriers to AI implementation. For each domain, responses to relevant Likert-scale items were aggregated and standardised (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:z\:=\:\frac{x-mean}{SD}\:$$\end{document}). All models were estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) with heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC) robust standard errors of type HC3, which provide improved small-sample properties compared to the more commonly used HC1 estimator [15]. Statistical significance was assessed at the 5% level (p < 0.05). Data were collected via Google Forms and subsequently processed and analysed using Excel, R, and Python.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 305 experts participated in the survey. Because of the two-step recruitment process, the exact response rate could not be calculated. Nevertheless, by cross-referencing the participating centres with the national registry of the three population-based screening programmes, we estimated a response rate of 69.6% (80/115) for mammography screening, 68.7% (79/115) for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening, and 71.2% (79/111) for cervical cancer screening, corresponding to an overall response rate of 64.5% (220/341). The number of responses from professionals involved in lung, prostate, and HCV screening programmes was substantially lower.

The largest number of respondents came from Lombardy (n = 83; 27.2%) and included experts from research hospitals (n = 19; 6.2%) and university centres (n = 41; 13.4%) (Table 1). The sample covered all the main organisational units involved in population-based screenings: screening programme management units (n = 128; 28.8%), breast radiology units (n = 81; 18.2%), endoscopy units (n = 79; 17.8%), gynaecologic units (n = 59; 13.3%), pathology laboratories (n = 48; 10.8%), and cytology laboratories (n = 40; 9%). Regarding professional profiles, the most represented were screening organisation centre professionals (n = 75; 22.7%), endoscopists (n = 69; 20.8%), radiologists (n = 63; 19%), and gynaecologists (n = 49; 14.8%).

Use of AI

Approximately one-third of our respondents reported that their screening centres had some experience with AI applications in at least one of the three population-based programmes. For 114 centres, we were able to identify the specific type of application through the open-ended question, with some centres reporting up to three applications.

The most frequently cited application concerned real-time computer-aided colorectal detection, including polyp characterisation during the procedure (n = 28; 27.2%). AI-based breast imaging applications were also reported, particularly for malignancy scoring and visual identification of suspicious lesions (n = 17; 16.5%). AI use in cervical cancer screening was less frequently reported, with two applications mentioned: automated cytology reading and lesion detection (n = 10; 9.7%) (Fig. 1).

Interest and views on AI in screening programmes

Overall, 74.8% of respondents expressed interest in AI applications in their centres, with a further 16.1% reporting limited interest. However, only 17.8% reported substantial AI expertise at their centres, although a further 39.7% indicated some level of expertise. Fig. 2 presents the distribution of interest levels across the four AI application types. Overall, risk classification algorithms and offline diagnostic support elicited the highest levels of interest, with 69.5% and 62.8% of respondents reporting high interest, respectively, while in vivo diagnostic support and generative AI for text showed comparatively higher proportions of uncertainty or low interest.

These findings were consistent with the perceived benefits of AI: time optimisation of professionals, improved diagnostic accuracy, and reduction of waiting and reporting times were considered “very important” or “important” by 83.3%, 85.6%, and 79.7% of respondents, respectively. At the same time, respondents acknowledged relevant or highly relevant risks associated with AI in screening: legal liability in case of errors (72.8%), lack of transparency (67.2%), algorithmic and data biases (67.2%), and professional de-skilling (46.2%).

Regarding barriers to adoption, respondents most frequently identified uncertainty in the legal framework (71.8%), inadequate IT infrastructure (77.0%), absence of official guidelines (75.7%), and limited financial resources (77.4%) as “highly relevant” or “relevant” barriers. Resistance from patients and professionals was considered “relevant” or “very important” by 11.8% and 30.8% of respondents, respectively (Fig. 3).

Determinants of AI adoption

The multiple regression models showed no clear regional patterns and no significant differences across the three screening programmes (Table 2). Compared to the cervical cancer programme (baseline), centres delivering colorectal screening reported slightly higher willingness to invest (β = 0.25, 95% CI: −0.25 to 0.75, p = 0.324 in model 1), while those delivering breast screening reported slightly lower scores (β = −0.30, 95% CI: −0.80 to 0.20, p = 0.236 in model 1), though neither difference reached statistical significance across any of the four specifications. Similarly, no significant geographical differences emerged between the North (baseline), Centre, and South/Islands. Among structural characteristics, centre type was the only variable that reached statistical significance: IRCCS and university centres reported substantially higher willingness to invest compared to private centres (baseline), with a coefficient of approximately 0.77–0.79 across models (3) and (4) (95% CI: 0.28 to 1.26 in model 3; p = 0.002), suggesting that research-oriented institutions are more inclined towards AI adoption. Public centres did not differ significantly from private ones (β = 0.04–0.18, p = 0.916 in model 3; p = 0.666 in model 4).

The inclusion of the three standardised composite scores in model (4) substantially increased the explained variance, with adjusted R² rising from 0.011 in model (3) to 0.171. As anticipated, perceived benefits were positively and significantly associated with willingness to invest (β = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.47 to 1.00, p < 0.001), indicating that a one standard deviation increase in the benefit score was associated with an increase of approximately 0.73 points on the 10-point investment scale. Conversely, perceived risks were negatively and significantly associated (β = −0.44, 95% CI: −0.69 to − 0.19, p < 0.001), suggesting that greater risk perception substantially dampens investment intentions. Perceived barriers were also negatively associated with willingness to invest, but this effect did not reach statistical significance (β = −0.19, 95% CI: −0.44 to 0.07, p = 0.144). Notably, models (1) and (2) yielded negligible adjusted R² values (− 0.000 and − 0.004, respectively), indicating that screening programme type and geographical area alone explain virtually none of the variance in willingness to invest in AI applications.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first survey examining the use, interest, perceived benefits, risks, and barriers of AI in population-based screening programmes in Italy. Drawing on a large sample of coordinators from breast, cervical, and colorectal screening centres, our findings highlight scientific, professional, and organisational factors influencing AI adoption. Overall, respondents were generally positive towards AI and expressed a willingness to invest in its implementation, while acknowledging the important risks and barriers associated with it.

Approximately one-third of centres reported experience with AI, most notably in the detection and classification of colorectal lesions, where applications have been shown to improve adenoma detection rates and reduce missed colorectal cancers [16]. Breast cancer screening ranked second among the most frequently reported areas of use, with recent trials demonstrating greater accuracy and a marked reduction in the number of human readings required [17–19]. Overall, these initiatives seem to have developed in a bottom-up fashion, often within research-oriented institutions, and in the absence of strong national or regional coordination.

Drawing on the NASSS framework, we observe that AI applications are already being deployed across all three target conditions (breast, cervical, and colorectal cancer). In Italy, as in most European Union (EU) countries, population-based screenings invite millions of asymptomatic individuals each year. Any improvement on such a scale is valuable, and respondents appeared to recognise this potential. Technologies varied across the four categories (generative AI, offline diagnostics, in vivo support, and risk prediction), and interest was high across all domains. Nevertheless, each requires rigorous evaluation of clinical effectiveness, harms, cost-effectiveness, workflow impact, and patient acceptability. However, the type of evaluation varies across applications. For instance, AI in breast cancer detection demands robust clinical trials to assess accuracy and resource implications [7, 17–19], whereas applications such as voice recognition or automated text generation require different frameworks, such as the QUEST principles [8].

Respondents identified three main groups of benefits: improved patient outcomes, professional development, and workflow efficiency. Cost reduction was viewed as relevant, but less important than clinical and organisational gains, a finding consistent with the professional profile of the sample, predominantly clinicians whose primary focus is likely on patient outcomes rather than economic efficiency. However, given the financial constraints of the Italian NHS [20, 21], overlooking economic arguments may limit the adoption of AI applications. Those applications that demonstrate medium-term cost savings are more likely to gain institutional endorsement and support from healthcare organisations. Formal health technology assessment (HTA) methods, including cost-effectiveness and budget impact analyses, are sometimes used by national and regional agencies to evaluate whether new technologies should be adopted by the NHS. In this regard, the Italian NHS is decentralised, and HTA considerations often depend on national and regional institutional decision-making, which sometimes overlap [22]. However, it is worth noting that AI solutions are classified as medical devices and, once the appropriate CE (Conformité Européenne) mark is obtained, are subject to relatively soft regulation compared to pharmaceuticals.

Within the NASSS adopter system domain, most respondents did not anticipate significant patient resistance to AI in screening, a finding consistent with surveys from Italy [23], the US [24], and Norway [25]. Nevertheless, even minority resistance may affect participation, suggesting the need for parallel non-AI pathways in some contexts. Professional acceptance was also generally high, though about one-third anticipated resistance. Previous studies confirm that while most Italian radiologists view AI positively, a substantial minority expresses concern about its impact on their profession [26]. Acceptance among endoscopists appears higher, as is often observed in international settings [27], likely reflecting the substantial advancements AI has already brought to endoscopy, preceding its widespread adoption in many other clinical areas. Managing professional concerns is essential to ensure equitable and sustainable programme delivery.

Organisational readiness emerged as a critical barrier. Respondents widely recognised that AI requires mature digital infrastructures, which are often lacking. National efforts have prioritised performance monitoring systems, leaving clinical digitalisation to individual organisations and regions, resulting in heterogeneous solutions and poor interoperability [28]. The National Recovery and Resilience Plan, which allocates billions of euros to strengthen NHS digitalisation [29], may substantially shape the feasibility of large-scale AI adoption.

Regression analysis showed that willingness to invest in AI was not significantly associated with structural factors such as geographical area or screening programme type, suggesting a broadly shared openness to AI adoption across the country. The only structural characteristic associated with higher investment intentions was working in an IRCCS or university centre (β ≈ 0.78, p ≤ 0.002), which may reflect the bottom-up, research-driven pattern of AI adoption observed in the descriptive findings. Perceptions of benefits and risks appeared to be the strongest correlates of investment intentions, with benefit scores positively associated (β = 0.73, p < 0.001) and risk scores negatively associated (β = −0.44, p < 0.001), while perceived barriers did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that efforts to promote AI adoption should focus on building the evidence base and addressing professional concerns, rather than on geographical or programme-level targeting.

Finally, the broader institutional and social context remains pivotal. Alongside financial constraints and decentralisation, respondents emphasised the importance of scientific evidence, national guidelines, and a clear legal framework. Liability, privacy, transparency, and appropriate Health Technology Assessment methods remain unresolved challenges [30–32]. Moreover, economic risks, including overexploitation of demand, workforce displacement, and excessive profit concentration, should not be underestimated [33]. The need for guidelines, at least for breast cancer, may be met soon with the forthcoming publication of the Italian recommendation [34] for the use of AI in organised breast cancer screening.

Although the number of respondents and their representativeness of the local programmes seem satisfactory, a limitation of our study may be a self-selection of respondents and the fact that one third of answers came from Lombardy, which is the largest region in our country (comprising about one-sixth of the Italian population) and notably the only one that involves private providers at all levels of screening [35–37]. Additionally, confounding bias is a relevant limitation of the regression model. The explanatory variables were selected on theoretical grounds, but unmeasured factors, such as individual prior experience with AI, local digital infrastructure maturity, or institutional culture, may have confounded the observed associations.

The survey was administered to professionals serving as reference points for all Italian screening programmes, with the majority of respondents representing population-based programmes and a minority referring to opportunistic or pilot settings. This heterogeneity is acknowledged as a potential limitation; however, no statistically significant differences in attitudes toward AI were observed across screening programme types, suggesting that, at least in this population, programme context does not seem to substantially condition respondents’ perceptions. A possible explanation is that Italian screening referents, regardless of speciality, share a common public health orientation and face similar systemic challenges, including workforce pressures and the need for quality assurance, that may foster convergent views on the potential role of AI. Future studies with larger, programme-specific cohorts would be needed to more rigorously assess whether speciality or programme type modulates attitudes toward AI-assisted screening.

Conclusions

Most referents of Italian cancer screening programmes are open to adopting AI and aware of its risks and implementation challenges. While AI applications are already in use across all three screening areas, progress is fragmented and lacks strategic direction. The future of AI in screening will depend on initiatives that leverage local innovation while fostering national coordination, generating robust evidence, and establishing supportive institutional frameworks.
