Postoperative Sexual Quality of Life and Associated Factors After Loop Electrosurgical Excision Procedure Versus Cold Knife Conization in 185 Sexually Active Women With Preinvasive Cervical Lesions
Authors: Yesim Ozkaya Ucar, Taner Turan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExcisional treatment is widely used for preinvasive cervical lesions, but its effect on postoperative sexual quality of life remains uncertain. This study aimed to compare postoperative Sexual Quality of Life–Female (SQoL-F) scores and examine associated factors in women treated with cold knife conization or loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP).Material/MethodsThis study included 185 sexually active women treated with cold knife conization (n = 99) or LEEP (n = 86) for histologically confirmed preinvasive cervical lesions during 2022–2024 and assessed at least 6 months postoperatively. The primary outcome was the total score on the validated 18-item SQoL-F questionnaire, transformed to a 0–100 scale. Between-group comparisons and multivariable linear regression with heteroskedasticity-consistent type 3 robust standard errors were performed.ResultsMean SQoL-F scores were similar after conization and LEEP (48.74 ± 14.76 vs 49.70 ± 12.76; P = 0.640; Hedges’ g = −0.07). In multivariable analysis, surgical technique was not associated with SQoL-F scores (β = 4.03; 95% confidence interval, −1.13 to 9.20; P = 0.125). Age, marital status, weekly sexual frequency, family structure, preoperative sexual life perception, postoperative expectation, and body perception were associated with SQoL-F scores. The model explained 23.7% of the variance (R2 = 0.237), and internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.886).ConclusionsSQoL-F scores did not differ significantly between women who underwent cold knife conization and those who underwent LEEP. Several demographic, psychosocial, and interpersonal factors, rather than surgical technique, were associated with SQoL-F scores, although the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference.

Introduction

Preinvasive cervical lesions, particularly cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), are among the most commonly encountered gynecologic conditions in women of reproductive age [1]. Despite advances in prevention, cervical cancer remains a major global health burden, with substantial variation in incidence and mortality across regions [2]. Organized cervical screening programs using cytology and high-risk human papillomavirus testing facilitate the detection and treatment of precancerous lesions before progression to invasive disease [3,4]. As a result, many young and middle-aged women undergo diagnostic and therapeutic procedures for lesions that, although non-invasive, may carry important psychological and reproductive implications [5,6]. Current risk-based guidelines vary according to CIN grade (CIN1/2/3); they recommend treatment for histologic high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL), including CIN3 and most CIN2 lesions, while permitting observation of selected CIN2 cases when concerns about future pregnancy outcomes outweigh the potential risk of progression, and when close surveillance is feasible [4,7]. Observation is preferred for CIN1, although treatment is acceptable when CIN1 persists for at least 2 years [7].

American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology guidance favors excisional treatment over ablation for histologic HSIL, whereas World Health Organization guidance recognizes both ablative and excisional approaches for appropriately selected lesions within organized screening-and-treatment programs [4,7]. When excision is selected, loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP)/large loop excision of the transformation zone (LLETZ) and cold knife conization are established techniques, and the choice should be individualized according to lesion characteristics, endocervical extension, concern for occult invasion, reproductive priorities, and the need to obtain an interpretable histopathologic specimen [4,6,7]. The oncologic safety and effectiveness of these approaches are well established, and the reproductive and obstetric consequences of cervical excisional treatment, particularly the risk of preterm birth, have been extensively investigated [6]. In contrast, the psychosocial and sexual consequences of treatment have received comparatively less attention [8,9].

Sexual health is recognized by the World Health Organization as a key component of overall well-being and quality of life [10]. Female sexual well-being is inherently complex and multidimensional, encompassing not only physical comfort but also emotional security, relational satisfaction, self-image, and perceptions of femininity [10,11]. Even when the disease is non-invasive, receiving a diagnosis involving the cervix—an organ closely linked to fertility and sexual identity—can lead to anxiety, fear of disease progression, altered body image, and concerns about sexual attractiveness or partner acceptance [12,13]. These psychological responses may persist beyond the immediate postoperative period and influence sexual quality of life independently of physical recovery [12,13]. Previous studies examining sexual outcomes after excisional treatment for CIN have reported mixed results. Some prospective and retrospective studies have shown temporary declines in sexual desire, arousal, or satisfaction following conization or LEEP, with recovery occurring over time [9,14,15]. In contrast, other studies have not demonstrated clinically meaningful long-term impairment in sexual function, suggesting that anatomical changes do not necessarily result in sustained functional decline [9,15]. These inconsistencies may be explained by differences in study design, sample size, cultural context, measurement tools, and timing of postoperative assessment [9,15]. Importantly, many earlier studies relied on general sexual function scales rather than instruments specifically designed to assess sexual quality of life [9,16].

The Sexual Quality of Life–Female (SQoL-F) is an 18-item self-report instrument developed to assess the impact of female sexual dysfunction on sexual quality of life, including emotional well-being, sexual self-esteem, and relationship-related concerns [17]. The items are answered with reference to the preceding 4 weeks using a 6-point response scale, with higher total scores indicating better sexual quality of life. The Turkish version of the SQoL-F has demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency and construct validity [18]. More recent prospective studies have provided additional, although not definitive, evidence. Suchońska et al evaluated 40 women before and 3 months after LEEP/LLETZ using the Female Sexual Function Index and the Brief Index of Sexual Functioning for Women and found no significant deterioration in the overall scores [19]. Kim et al similarly reported no significant changes in Female Sexual Function Index or Female Sexual Distress Scale scores 6 to 12 months after LEEP [20]. Bartnik et al prospectively assessed 35 women undergoing LEEP and 44 undergoing surgical conization at baseline and at 3, 6, and 12 months. Although transient changes were observed in some sexual function domains, these changes largely resolved by 12 months, and the long-term outcomes of the 2 procedures were similar [14]. These studies primarily focused on changes in sexual function over time using instruments such as the Female Sexual Function Index rather than a measure specifically developed to assess sexual quality of life [14,19,20]. They also included relatively small cohorts and provided limited evaluation of relational and psychosocial factors that may be associated with postoperative sexual well-being [14,19,20]. Direct comparisons of postoperative sexual quality of life after cold knife conization and LEEP using the SQoL-F remain limited [9,15]. Examining whether surgical technique is associated with postoperative SQoL-F scores while accounting for demographic and psychosocial factors may provide clinically useful evidence. Such information could support preoperative counseling and help clinicians discuss sexual well-being alongside oncologic and reproductive considerations.

Therefore, this study aimed to compare postoperative SQoL-F scores between cold knife conization and LEEP and to evaluate demographic, reproductive, and psychosocial factors associated with these scores in 185 sexually active women treated for preinvasive cervical lesions. The primary hypothesis was that SQoL-F scores at or beyond the 6-month postoperative period would differ between the 2 surgical technique groups. Associations with demographic, reproductive, and psychosocial variables were examined as secondary exploratory analyses. Because these were exploratory analyses of cross-sectional data, the results were interpreted as associations.

Material and Methods

Ethical Statement

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ankara Bilkent City Hospital No. 2 Medical Research Scientific and Ethics Evaluation Committee (approval no. TABED 2-25-1663; approval date, November 26, 2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before questionnaire administration. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted at a tertiary gynecologic oncology outpatient clinic over a 3-month period following ethics approval obtained in November 2025. Women who had undergone cold knife conization or LEEP for histologically confirmed preinvasive cervical lesions between January 2022 and December 2024 were identified from institutional records and screened for eligibility during routine postoperative follow-up visits. Eligible women completed the SQoL-F questionnaire during the same visit. The final analytic cohort consisted of sexually active women aged ≥ 18 years who had a histologically confirmed preinvasive cervical lesion treated with cold knife conization or LEEP, attended routine postoperative follow-up during the study period, had undergone the excisional procedure at least 6 months before questionnaire assessment, reported sexual activity within the preceding 4 weeks, were able to complete the questionnaire independently, and had complete clinical and SQoL-F data. Both married and single/unmarried women were eligible for inclusion if they met these criteria. Women were excluded if they were pregnant at the time of evaluation, had a history of any malignancy, psychiatric disorder, cognitive impairment, communication difficulties, or incomplete clinical or questionnaire data. In total, 185 women met these criteria and were included in the analysis.

SQoL-F was administered once during postoperative follow-up, at least 6 months after treatment; therefore, the study assessed postoperative sexual quality of life cross-sectionally rather than as longitudinal change from baseline. Surgical technique had been selected at the time of treatment based on clinical indications and physician preference in routine practice.

Surgical Procedures and Selection of Comparison Groups

Although LEEP and LLETZ are frequently used as overlapping terms in the literature because of their technical similarities, they were recorded as separate procedures in our institutional records [19]. However, the number of women who underwent LLETZ during the study period was too small to permit a reliable separate statistical comparison. Therefore, the analysis was restricted to LEEP and cold knife conization, the 2 excisional treatment groups with sufficient patient numbers for meaningful comparison.

Diagnosis and Histopathological Classification

Histopathological diagnoses used in this study were obtained from the final pathology reports of the LEEP or cold knife conization specimens. Lesions were classified using the 2-tier Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology/World Health Organization system, in which the presence of low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions corresponds to CIN1, while the presence of HSIL corresponds to CIN2 and CIN3 [21,22]. Consistent with current recommendations, the CIN2 or CIN3 qualifier was retained when specified in the final pathology report. For the present analyses, CIN2 and CIN3 were combined as HSIL/CIN2-3, whereas low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions/CIN1 was categorized as non-high-grade lesions. Women with invasive cervical carcinoma were not included.

Data Collection and Clinical Variables

Data were collected through structured face-to-face interviews during outpatient visits and from institutional medical records. Clinical and pathological variables were abstracted from institutional medical records, whereas sexual life-related variables and SQoL-F responses were obtained directly from the participants during the follow-up visit. Participants completed the SQoL-F questionnaire individually. A researcher-prepared data collection form was used to obtain demographic, reproductive, clinical, pathological, and sexual-life-related variables, including age, body mass index, marital status, gravidity, parity, family structure, histopathological diagnosis, weekly sexual intercourse frequency, preoperative sexual life perception, postoperative sexual life expectation, and postoperative body perception. Marital status was recorded as married or single/unmarried according to the available clinical interview data; separate categories for widowed or divorced status were not available in the dataset. Histopathological diagnosis at the time of excisional treatment was recorded and categorized as high-grade lesion, defined as HSIL/CIN2/CIN3, or non-high-grade lesion for descriptive between-group comparisons. During the postoperative interview, participants categorized their recalled preoperative sexual life as good or poor, their expectation regarding sexual life after treatment as positive or negative, and their current postoperative body perception as positive or negative. These variables were recorded according to the response categories specified in the researcher-prepared data collection form.

Sexual Quality of Life–Female Questionnaire Assessment and Outcome Definition

Postoperative sexual quality of life was assessed using the SQoL-F questionnaire, originally developed by Symonds et al [17] and subsequently validated in Turkish by Tuğut and Gölbaşı [18]. The SQoL-F is an 18-item self-report questionnaire that assesses sexual quality of life during the preceding 4 weeks. Each item is rated on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (completely agree) to 6 (completely disagree). In accordance with the published scoring instructions, the positively worded items (items 1, 5, 9, 13, and 18) were reverse-scored. The item scores were then summed to obtain a raw total score ranging from 18 to 108. The raw total was converted to a standardized 0 to 100 score using the following formula: [(raw total score – 18)/90] × 100. Higher scores indicate better sexual quality of life.

The primary outcome was the standardized postoperative SQoL-F total score, and the primary comparison was between women treated with cold knife conization and those treated with LEEP. Associations between SQoL-F scores and the demographic, reproductive, clinical, and sexual life-related variables were examined as secondary exploratory analyses. Because the SQoL-F does not provide a validated categorical cutoff, the score was analyzed as a continuous outcome [17]. Return to a “normal sexual life” was not defined as a study endpoint, and participants were not classified as having returned or not returned to normal sexual life. Internal consistency of the SQoL-F in the present cohort was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All analyses were performed using complete cases (medical records with no missing data). Because complete clinical and SQoL-F data were required for inclusion, the analytic sample consisted of 185 women for all reported analyses, and no missing-data imputation was performed. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range according to their distribution, and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, together with visual inspection of histograms and quantile-quantile plots. Continuous variables with approximately normal distribution were compared using the independent-samples t test, whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Effect size for continuous between-group comparisons was calculated using Hedges’ g. The primary unadjusted analysis compared the mean standardized SQoL-F scores between the cold knife conization and LEEP groups using the independent-samples t test.

To examine the adjusted association between surgical technique and sexual quality of life, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed with SQoL-F score as the dependent variable. The model included surgical technique, age, body mass index, gravidity, parity, marital status, weekly sexual frequency, family structure, preoperative sexual life perception, postoperative sexual life expectation, and postoperative body perception. Cold knife conization was used as the reference category for surgical technique, married status for marital status, and nuclear family structure for family structure. The corresponding comparisons were LEEP vs cold knife conization, single/unmarried vs married, and extended vs nuclear family. Weekly sexual frequency was entered as an ordinal variable with increasing categories. Preoperative sexual life perception, postoperative sexual life expectation, and postoperative body perception were entered as categorical predictors according to the categories shown in Table 1. For consistency across the text, tables, and figures, these categorical predictors were reported as good vs poor preoperative sexual life perception, negative vs positive postoperative sexual life expectation, and positive vs negative postoperative body perception. Regression outputs included adjusted regression coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, P-values, overall model significance, and the coefficient of determination (R2).

Model diagnostics were assessed by examining residual plots, quantile-quantile plots of residuals, and collinearity statistics. Variance inflation factor values were reviewed to evaluate multicollinearity among predictors. The continuous SQoL-F score was retained as the dependent variable in the linear regression model because its distribution and residual pattern were considered acceptable for parametric modeling. Heteroskedasticity-consistent type 3 (HC3) robust standard errors were applied to improve the reliability of standard error estimates in the presence of potential heteroskedasticity. A 2-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Because this was a cross-sectional observational study based on eligible patients attending routine postoperative follow-up, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. However, with 99 women in the conization group and 86 women in the LEEP group, the available sample size provided approximately 80% power to detect a moderate between-group difference of about 0.42 standard deviations, corresponding to approximately 5.8 points on the SQoL-F scale, at a 2-sided alpha level of 0.05. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant difference between surgical techniques should be interpreted in the context that smaller differences may not have been detectable.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 185 women were included in the analysis, of whom 99 (53.5%) underwent cold knife conization and 86 (46.5%) underwent LEEP. The age range was 23 to 60 years in the overall cohort, 28 to 60 years in the conization group, and 23 to 58 years in the LEEP group. Demographic, clinical, pathological, and sexual-life-related characteristics according to surgical technique are presented in Table 2.

Women in the conization group were significantly older than those in the LEEP group (42.20 ± 8.49 vs 30.89 ± 5.68 years, P < 0.001). Gravidity (2.72 ± 1.25 vs 1.10 ± 1.18, P < 0.001) and parity (2.06 ± 0.88 vs 0.81 ± 0.93, P < 0.001) were also significantly higher in the conization group. Marital status differed significantly between groups (P < 0.001); married women constituted 86.9% of the conization group and 57.0% of the LEEP group, whereas single/unmarried women constituted 13.1% and 43.0%, respectively.

In contrast, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for body mass index (25.83 ± 3.92 vs 24.83 ± 4.41 kg/m2, P = 0.110), family structure (nuclear family: 80.8% vs 79.1%; extended family: 19.2% vs 20.9%; P = 0.770), high-grade lesion frequency (74.7% vs 70.9%, P = 0.560), weekly sexual intercourse frequency (P = 0.072), preoperative sexual life perception (P = 0.990), postoperative sexual life expectation (P = 0.940), or postoperative body perception (P = 0.920).

Sexual Activity-Related Characteristics

At the time of SQoL-F assessment, all 185 women included in the analysis had been sexually active during the preceding 4 weeks, as required by the eligibility criteria. In the overall cohort, weekly sexual intercourse frequency was once per week or less in 68 women (36.8%), 1 to 3 times per week in 91 women (49.2%), and 3 or more times per week in 26 women (14.1%). The corresponding distributions in the conization and LEEP groups were 43 (43.4%) vs 25 (29.1%); 46 (46.5%) vs 45 (52.3%); and 10 (10.1%) vs 16 (18.6%), respectively (P = 0.072).

Good preoperative sexual life perception was reported by 112 women (60.5%) in the overall cohort, including 60 women (60.6%) in the conization group and 52 women (60.5%) in the LEEP group (P = 0.990). Positive postoperative sexual life expectation was reported by 34 women (18.4%), including 18 women (18.2%) in the conization group and 16 women (18.6%) in the LEEP group (P = 0.940). Positive postoperative body perception was reported by 109 women (58.9%), including 58 women (58.6%) in the conization group and 51 women (59.3%) in the LEEP group. Conversely, negative postoperative body perception was reported by 41 women in the conization group (41.4%) and 35 women in the LEEP group (40.7%) (P = 0.920). These findings describe the sexual activity-related characteristics of the women assessed after treatment and should not be interpreted as rates of return to normal sexual life.

Sexual Quality of Life–Female Questionnaire Scores According to Surgical Technique

The internal consistency of the SQoL-F questionnaire was high in this cohort, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.886, indicating strong reliability. The mean SQoL-F score for the entire cohort was 49.19 ± 13.84 on the 0 to 100 scale. Normality assessment supported the use of parametric testing for SQoL-F scores; therefore, the main unadjusted comparison between surgical groups was performed using the independent-samples t test. In this analysis, there was no statistically significant difference in sexual quality of life between the conization and LEEP groups (48.74 ± 14.76 vs 49.70 ± 12.76, P = 0.640; Hedges’ g = −0.07). Figure 1 shows substantial overlap in the distribution of SQoL-F scores between the 2 surgical groups.

Multivariable Analysis of Factors Associated With Sexual Quality of Life–Female Questionnaire Scores

To evaluate whether surgical technique was associated with postoperative sexual quality of life after adjustment for potential confounding variables, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed in the full study cohort (n = 185). The model included age, body mass index, gravidity, parity, marital status, weekly sexual frequency, family structure, preoperative sexual life perception, postoperative sexual life expectation, and postoperative body perception. Weekly sexual frequency was entered as an ordinal variable. The overall model was statistically significant (P < 0.001) and explained approximately 23.7% of the variance in SQoL-F scores (R2 = 0.237). In the adjusted model (Table 1), surgical technique was not significantly associated with sexual quality of life (LEEP vs conization: β = 4.03; 95% confidence interval (CI): −1.13 to 9.20; P = 0.125).

Among the covariates included in Table 1, age was positively associated with SQoL-F scores (β = 0.26; 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.51; P = 0.039). Single/unmarried status was associated with lower SQoL-F scores compared with married status (β = −5.21; 95% CI: −10.23 to −0.18; P = 0.042), whereas higher weekly sexual frequency was associated with higher SQoL-F scores (β = 3.82; 95% CI: 1.00 to 6.64; P = 0.008). Extended family structure was associated with lower SQoL-F scores compared with nuclear family structure (β = −6.08; 95% CI: −11.39 to −0.78; P = 0.025). Good preoperative sexual life perception (β = 7.95; 95% CI: 4.42 to 11.47; P < 0.001) and positive postoperative body perception (β = 6.13; 95% CI: 2.92 to 9.34; P < 0.001) were associated with higher SQoL-F scores, whereas negative postoperative sexual life expectation was associated with lower SQoL-F scores (β = −5.36; 95% CI: −8.46 to −2.26; P = 0.001).

The adjusted regression coefficients and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals are visually summarized in Figure 2. The forest plot demonstrates that the CI for surgical technique crosses the null value, whereas several psychosocial and interpersonal variables show statistically significant adjusted associations with SQoL-F scores.

Discussion

The present study compared postoperative SQoL-F scores in 185 sexually active women who underwent cold knife conization or LEEP and were assessed at least 6 months after treatment. Mean SQoL-F scores were similar in the cold knife conization and LEEP groups (48.74 ± 14.76 vs 49.70 ± 12.76; P = 0.640), and surgical technique was not significantly associated with SQoL-F scores after multivariable adjustment (β = 4.03; 95% CI, −1.13 to 9.20; P = 0.125). In contrast, age, marital status, weekly sexual frequency, family structure, preoperative sexual life perception, postoperative sexual life expectation, and postoperative body perception showed significant adjusted associations with SQoL-F scores. The model explained 23.7% of the variance, indicating that postoperative sexual well-being reflects several interacting demographic, interpersonal, and psychosocial factors rather than surgical technique alone. Because the outcome was assessed at a single postoperative time point, these findings represent cross-sectional associations and should not be interpreted as evidence of causality or temporal direction.

Previous studies have reported heterogeneous sexual outcomes after excisional treatment for CIN, partly because of differences in measurement instruments and follow-up intervals. Suchońska et al prospectively evaluated women before and 3 months after LEEP/LLETZ and found no significant deterioration in overall Female Sexual Function Index or Brief Index of Sexual Functioning for Women scores [19]. Kim et al likewise reported no significant changes in Female Sexual Function Index or Female Sexual Distress Scale scores 6 to 12 months after LEEP [20]. Bartnik et al compared LEEP with surgical conization at baseline and at 3, 6, and 12 months. Mild early changes were observed in some sexual function domains, but these changes largely resolved by 12 months, and long-term outcomes were similar between the procedures [14]. Our findings are consistent with these reports, as SQoL-F scores at or beyond 6 months did not differ significantly between LEEP and cold knife conization, and the unadjusted effect size was very small. The present study adds a direct comparison using an instrument specifically focused on sexual quality of life and shows that surgical technique was not associated with SQoL-F scores after adjustment for demographic, reproductive, and psychosocial variables. The observed associations with postoperative expectations, body perception, marital status, and sexual frequency further suggest that postoperative sexual well-being reflects a broader psychosocial and interpersonal context. The absence of preoperative and early postoperative measurements meant that transient changes could not be evaluated.

An important observation was the strong association between preoperative perception of sexual life and postoperative outcomes. This suggests that sexual well-being after treatment may, at least in part, reflect continuity or adaptation of preexisting patterns rather than being explained solely by the intervention itself [11,20]. In a similar way, postoperative expectations and body perception were also significantly associated with SQoL-F scores. These findings highlight the potential role of illness perception, expectations, and self-image in shaping sexual experience after treatment [9,11,12,13]. These variables should therefore be viewed as correlates of postoperative sexual well-being rather than causal determinants. The relatively modest proportion of explained variance (R2 = 0.237) is consistent with the complex and multifactorial nature of female sexual health [11]. It also indicates that a considerable part of the variability in sexual quality of life remains unexplained and may be related to factors not captured in the present model. Strengths of this study include the direct comparison of 2 commonly used excisional techniques, use of the culturally adapted SQoL-F, and adjustment for several measured demographic and psychosocial variables. HC3 robust standard errors were used to reduce the influence of potential heteroskedasticity on the regression estimates.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design and the absence of a preoperative SQoL-F assessment precluded evaluation of within-person changes, early postoperative effects, recovery trajectories, and temporal or causal relationships. The preoperative sexual life variable was based on retrospectively reported perception rather than a contemporaneous baseline SQoL-F measurement, and may therefore have been affected by recall bias. Second, the surgical procedure was not randomly assigned, and the groups differed substantially in age, gravidity, parity, and marital status. Although these variables were included in the multivariable analysis, residual confounding and confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Third, this was a single-center cohort restricted to women who attended postoperative follow-up, had been sexually active during the preceding 4 weeks, and were assessed at least 6 months after treatment. Consequently, the findings may not be generalizable to women who had not resumed sexual activity or did not attend follow-up. Because recent sexual activity was an eligibility criterion and the SQoL-F has no validated threshold for return to normal sexual life, this study cannot estimate the proportion of women who resumed or returned to their preoperative sexual life. Fourth, the SQoL-F is a self-reported instrument and may be affected by response and social-desirability biases. Potentially relevant factors, including menopausal or hormonal status, detailed partner-related variables, anxiety or depression, postoperative pain, cone depth or volume, margin status, perioperative complications, and the exact interval between treatment and assessment, were not incorporated into the present analysis. Finally, the available sample size was sufficient to detect moderate between-group differences, but smaller effects may have remained undetected. Prospective longitudinal studies incorporating preoperative and serial postoperative SQoL-F assessments, together with detailed surgical and psychosocial variables, are needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

At least 6 months after treatment, SQoL-F scores did not differ significantly between women who underwent cold knife conization and those who underwent LEEP. Several demographic, psychosocial, and interpersonal factors, rather than surgical technique, were associated with SQoL-F scores, although the cross-sectional design precluded causal inference. Prospective longitudinal studies incorporating both preoperative and postoperative assessments are needed to confirm these findings.
