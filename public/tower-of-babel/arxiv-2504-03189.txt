Structure Learning via ADMM in Networks obeying Conservation Laws
Rohith Reddy Mada, Rajasekhar Anguluri

Abstract
Learning the edge connectivity structure of networked systems from limited data is a fundamental challenge in many critical infrastructure domains, including power, traffic, and finance. Such systems obey steady-state conservation laws: x = L ∗ ​ y x=L^{\ast}y , where x x and y ∈ ℝ p y\in\mathbb{R}^{p} represent injected flows (inputs) and potentials (outputs), respectively. The sparsity pattern of the p × p p\times p Laplacian L ∗ L^{\ast} encodes the underlying edge structure. In a stochastic setting, the goal is to infer this sparsity pattern from zero-mean i.i.d. samples of y y . Recent work by [ 1 ] has established statistical consistency results for this learning problem by considering an ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -regularized maximum likelihood estimator. However, their approach did not develop a scalable algorithm but relies on solving a convex program via the CVX package. To address this gap, we propose an alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), which is transparent and fast. A key contribution is to demonstrate the role of an algebraic matrix Riccati equation in the primal update step of ADMM. Numerical experiments on a host of synthetic and benchmark networks, including power and water systems, show the efficiency of our method.



I
Introduction

Critical infrastructure systems—such as power, water, and traffic networks—are modeled as graphs, where conservation laws enforce that nodal flows across the edges of the network are neither created nor lost. In steady state, these conservation laws reduce to linear relation
x=L^{*}y
, where
L^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a weighted symmetric Laplacian encoding the network’s edge structure through its off-diagonal entries
[
2
]
. The
p
-dimensional vectors
x
and
y
denote nodal injections and potentials, respectively.

In practice, the edge connectivity structure is unknown and must be learned from data
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. This entails recovering the sparsity pattern of the unknown (true)
L^{*}
using finite samples or summary statistics of the injection–potential pairs
\{x,y\}
. Assuming random injection
x
, and given the sample covariance matrix
S
of
y
,
[
1
]
proposed estimating the network structure by solving the optimization problem:

\displaystyle\hat{L}=\underset{L\in\mathcal{S}_{p}^{++}}{\text{arg\,min }}\Tr(SL^{2})-2\log\det(L)+\lambda\|L\|_{1,\text{off}},

(1)

where
\mathcal{S}_{p}^{++}
is the set of real-valued
p\times p
symmetric positive definite matrices and
\|L\|_{1,\text{off}}
is the
\ell_{1}
-norm applied to off-diagonals of
L
. The edge structure can then be inferred from the sparsity pattern of the estimate
\hat{L}
(see Section
II
).

Ref.
[
1
]
have established many properties of the problem in (
1
). Chief among them are that the problem is convex and that its statistical complexity depends on the maximum degree of the network. Further, (
1
) is related to many graph learning problems (see related work in
[
1
,
6
]
). Thus, developing simple and fast iterative algorithms for solving (
1
) is of great interest. This motivates our use of the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM).

We highlight that although (
1
) resembles the sparse inverse covariance estimation (ICE) problem in graphical models, a key difference lies in the trace
\operatorname{Tr(\cdot)}
. In (
1
),
\operatorname{Tr}(\cdot)
is quadratic in the variable
L
, whereas in ICE it is linear. This subtle yet significant distinction makes the development of an ADMM for (
1
) challenging and interesting. Our contributions are:

1.

For the unregularized case (
\lambda=0
), we show that the global minimizer
\hat{L}
of (
3
) is equal to the inverse of the positive semi-definite square root matrix of the sample covariance
S
, if inverse exists (see Prop.
II.1
).

2.

We propose a simple ADMM algorithm to numerically solve the structure learning problem in (
3
) for any
\lambda\geq 0
. We show that the dual and Lagrange multiplier update subproblems can be solved in closed form.

3.

The primal subproblem of our ADMM algorithm does not admit closed-form solution, as it involves solving an algebraic Riccati equation (NARE). Therefore, we resort to a Newton’s method to solve the NARE. In Remark
1
, we clarify why this is not an issue in the closely related sparse ICE problem in Gaussian graphical models.

Numerical results on many synthetic and real-world benchmark networks demonstrate the superior efficiency (in terms of iterations and execution time; see Table.
I
) of our ADMM method compared to the CVX-based approach in
[
1
]
. The limitations of the proposed approach and future work are discussed toward the end of the paper.

II
Preliminaries and Problem Setup

Consider an undirected, connected graph with
p+1
nodes, where the node set is
V=\{0,1,2,\ldots,p\}
and the edge set
E\subset V\times V
. Each edge
(i,j)\in E
is assigned a non-negative weight
a_{ij}\geq 0
. Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
denote the vectors of nodal injections and potentials, respectively. The conservation law is represented by
x=L_{\text{full}}^{*}y
, where
L_{\text{full}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}
is the full Laplacian matrix defined by

[L^{*}_{\text{full}}]_{ij}=\begin{cases}-a_{ij},&\text{if }i\neq j,\\
\sum_{k\neq i}a_{ik},&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

Thus,
(i,j)\in E
iff
[L^{*}_{\text{full}}]_{ij}\neq 0
. The applicability of linear conservation laws and their extensions is elegantly discussed in
[
2
]
. Fig.
1
illustrates the sparsity pattern of
L^{*}
.

Following
[
6
,
1
,
4
]
, we work with the reduced Laplacian matrix
L^{*}
, obtained by removing node
0
and its incident edges from the graph. In particular,
L^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is formed by deleting the row and column corresponding to node
0
from
L_{\text{full}}^{*}
. The reduced matrix is symmetric and positive definite, and therefore invertible, ensuring that
L^{*}
, or at least its sparsity pattern, can be recovered from data.

sparsity pattern of a Laplacian encoding edge structure
: (left) A network consisting of seven nodes labeled in a counterclockwise direction, with node 1 positioned to the left of node 7. Each node is assigned a pair
(x_{i},y_{i})
, where arrows represent the injection of flows
x_{i}
. (right) The indices of blue-colored cells in
L^{*}
indicate the presence of edges between nodes identified by these indices, while white-colored cells signify the absence of edges.

Structure learning problem
[
1
]
:
Estimate the sparsity pattern of the unknown true Laplacian matrix
L^{*}
using
N
i.i.d. samples of the potential vector
y
, assuming that the nodal injection vector
x
is a zero-mean Gaussian random vector with a known, inverse covariance matrix
\Theta_{x}\succ 0
.

The Gaussian random injection assumption is common in the literature on network structure learning and is motivated by studies in power systems, where the injections are random load fluctuations
[
3
,
5
]
. Thus, the available data consists of
(\Theta_{x},\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\})
, but not the actual flow injections
x
.

II-A

An
\ell_{1}
-regularized Maximum Likelihood Estimator

Ref.
[
1
]
framed the structure learning problem as an
\ell_{1}
-regularized maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), leading to the optimization problem given in (
1
). For completeness, we provide a high-level overview of the MLE derivation.

Let
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Theta_{x}^{-1})
. Because
L^{*}
is invertible, the conservation law can be written as
y=(L^{*})^{-1}x
. From Gaussianity of
x
, it follows that
y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Theta_{y}^{-1})
, where

\Theta_{y}=L^{*}\Theta_{x}L^{*}
. Consider
N
i.i.d samples
\{y_{,}\ldots,y_{N}\}
and define the
N\times N
sample covariance matrix
S=N^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N}yy^{\mathsf{T}}
.
The log-likelihood of the data
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}
is given by

\displaystyle g(L^{*})\propto 2\log\det(L^{*})-\operatorname{Tr}(SL^{*}\Theta_{x}L^{*}),

(2)

where
\propto
is the proportionality symbol. While
g(L^{*})
cannot be evaluated since
L^{*}
is unknown, we may instead consider
g(L)
for a generic positive definite matrix
L
. The MLE then seeks a matrix
L\succ 0
that maximizes
g(L)
.

We adopt the minimization framework to arrive at the
\ell_{1}
-norm regularized maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)
:

\displaystyle\hat{L}=\underset{L\in\mathcal{S}_{p}^{++}}{\text{arg\,min }}\Tr(SL\Theta_{x}L)-2\log\det(L)+\lambda\|L\|_{1,\text{off}}.

(3)

Here,
\mathcal{S}_{p}^{++}
denotes the set of
p\times p
real symmetric positive definite matrices;
\|L\|_{1,\text{off}}=\sum_{i\neq j}|L_{ij}|
is the
\ell_{1}
-norm applied to the off-diagonal entries of the optimization variable
L
; and
\lambda\geq 0
is a tuning parameter. The inclusion of the
\ell_{1}
-penalty promotes sparsity in the estimate
\hat{L}
. The formulation in (
3
) is general version of (
1
) where in
\Theta_{x}=I
.

Ref.
[
1
]
showed that the regularized MLE problem in (
3
) is convex, and that the solution
\hat{L}
is unique for all
\lambda>0
, even when
N<p
. Note that the optimization domain in (
3
) is
\mathcal{S}_{p}^{++}
, not the set of reduced Laplacian matrices, which form a strict subset of
\mathcal{S}_{p}^{++}
. Recent work
[
15
]
has addressed optimization over Laplacians directly, but that is beyond the scope of this paper.

Before presenting an ADMM-based algorithm to compute
\hat{L}
, we first state an interesting result for the unrestricted MLE
\lambda=0
, which is new.

Proposition II.1

Let
\lambda=0
and assume that
N>p
. Then with probability one, the unrestricted MLE (the unique global minimizer of (
3
)) is
\hat{L}=S^{-1/2}\left(S^{1/2}\Theta_{x}^{-1}S^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}S^{-1/2}
.

Proof:

The loss function in (
3
) (for
\lambda=0
) is convex and differentiable. The zero-gradient condition is
LSL=\Theta_{x}^{-1}
.
Because
N>p
with probability one,
S\succ 0

[
16
]
. Thus, there exists a unique positive definite square root
S^{1/2}
satisfying
S=S^{1/2}S^{1/2}
. It can be verified that the matrix
\hat{L}
, as stated in the proposition, satisfies the zero-gradient condition, and is the unique positive definite solution to the unrestricted MLE (see
[
17
, Exercise 1.2.13]
).
∎

Let
\Theta_{x}=I
. From Proposition
II.1
, the unrestricted MLE is
\hat{L}=S^{-1/2}
. This estimate is intuitive, as in the population case, we have
\Theta^{*}_{y}=(L^{*})^{2}
, which results in
L^{*}=(\Theta_{y})^{1/2}
. Therefore, in the sample setting, we expect that the estimate of
L^{*}
should equal the inverse square root of the sample covariance
S
, which is what MLE provides. Unfortunately, for
\lambda>0
, there is no simple closed-form expression for
\hat{L}
, unlike the result in Proposition
II.1
. This is because of the non-differentiability of the
\ell_{1}
-regularizer.

Problem
: Develop an efficient ADMM algorithm to numerically solve the maximum likelihood problem in (
3
) for any
\lambda\geq 0
, with either
N<p
or
N\geq P
.

III
An ADMM algorithm

The ADMM approach (e.g., see
[
7
]
) is based on rewriting the optimization in (
3
) as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{L,Z\,\in\mathcal{S}_{p}^{++}}{\text{minimize }}&\Tr(SL\Theta_{x}L)-2\log\det(L)+\lambda\|Z\|_{1,\text{off}}\\
\text{subject to }&L-Z=0.\end{array}

Define the augmented Lagrangian as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(L,Z,\Lambda)=f(L)+h(Z)+\langle\Lambda,L-Z\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|L-Z\|_{F}^{2},

where
\langle A,B\rangle\triangleq\Tr(AB^{\mathsf{T}})
and
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\top}
is the Lagrange multiplier. The parameter
\rho>0
is user-defined and affects only the convergence rate of the ADMM.
Further, define

\displaystyle f(L)

\displaystyle=\Tr(SL\Theta_{x}L)-2\log\det(L)

(4a)

\displaystyle h(Z)

\displaystyle=\lambda\|Z\|_{1,\text{off}}.

(4b)

We can find the ADMM iterates by sequentially minimizing the augmented Lagrangian with respect to
(L,Z,\Lambda)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &L^{k+1}=\underset{L\in\operatorname{S}_{p}^{++}}{\arg\min}\,f(L)+\langle\Lambda^{k},L-Z^{k}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|L-Z^{k}\right\|_{F}^{2}\\
&Z^{k+1}=\underset{Z\in\operatorname{S}_{p}^{++}}{\arg\min}\,h(Z)+\langle\Lambda^{k},L^{k+1}\!-\!Z\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|L^{k+1}\!-\!Z\right\|_{F}^{2}\end{aligned}

(5)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{k+1}=\Lambda^{k}+\rho\left(L^{k+1}-Z^{k+1}\right).

Note that to implement the ADMM iterates above we need to solve only the minimization problems associated with the primal (
L
) and dual variables (
Z
). The solution to the dual minimization is given by the soft-thresholding

\displaystyle Z^{k+1}=\frac{1}{\rho}\operatorname{soft}(\rho L^{k+1}+\Lambda^{k},\lambda),

(6)

where
\operatorname{soft}(\cdot,\cdot)
(applied element-wise in (
6
)) is

\operatorname{soft}(a,b)=\begin{cases}a-b,&\text{ if }a>b\\
0,&\text{ if }|a|\leq b\\
a+b,&\text{ if }a<-b.\end{cases}

To solve the primal minimization (associated with
L
) in (
5
) set the derivative of the loss to zero to get

\displaystyle\nabla_{L}(f(L)+\langle\Lambda^{k},L-Z^{k}\rangle+(\rho/2)\left\|L-Z^{k}\right\|_{F}^{2})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle 2\Theta_{x}LS-2L^{-1}+\Lambda^{k}+\rho(L-Z^{k})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle 2LSL-2\Theta_{x}^{-1}+\Theta_{x}^{-1}\Lambda^{k}L+\rho\Theta_{x}^{-1}(L-Z^{k})L

\displaystyle=0.

We obtain the last equality by pre-multiplying both sides of the second equation by
\Theta_{x}^{-1}
and then post-multiplying by
L
. Collecting the quadratic and linear terms of
L
finally gives us the quadratic matrix equation

\displaystyle(2LSL+\rho\Theta_{x}^{-1}L^{2})+\Theta^{-1}_{x}(\Lambda^{k}-Z^{k})L-2\Theta^{-1}_{x}

\displaystyle=0.

(7)

Without loss of generality, we may assume
\Theta_{x}=I
, since the data can always be whitened using the transformation
\Theta_{x}^{-1/2}
. As a result, the equality in (
7
) simplifies to the non-symmetric algebraic Riccati equation (NARE):

\displaystyle L(2S+\rho I)L+(\Lambda^{k}-\rho Z^{k})L-2I=0.

(8)

We discuss an interative method to solve the NARE in (
8
) using Newton’s method in Section
III-A
. For the moment, define the functional routines
\texttt{NARE}()
that solves for
L
in (
8
) and
\texttt{soft\_thresholding}()
that implements (
6
). Our ADMM algorithm is
summarized in Alg.
1
. To ensure that
L^{k+1}
in Alg.
1
remains positive definite, we symmetrize the result and apply a small positive diagonal perturbation.

III-A

Newton’s method for implementing
\texttt{NARE}(\cdot)

NARE of the form in (
8
) can be solved iteratively using Newton’s method. We briefly outline the approach, following the exposition in
[
18
]
. Consider the matrix-valued nonlinear equation
F(X)=0
, where
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. The recurrence relation forms the basis for the newton method to solve for the unknown matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

X_{t+1}=X_{t}-[F^{\prime}(X_{t})]^{-1}F(X_{t}),

where
F^{\prime}(X_{t})
is the Fréchet derivative. Rather than inverting
F^{\prime}(X_{t})
, one
solves
F^{\prime}(X_{t})H_{t}=-F(X_{t})
for the increment
H_{t}
, updating the solution as
X_{t+1}=X_{t}+H_{t}
.

Consider the function
F(L)=L\tilde{S}L+(\Lambda^{k}-\rho Z^{k})L-2I
for the NARE defined in (
8
), where
\tilde{S}=(2S+\rho I)
. Then, the Newton’s increment
H_{t}
needed to update
L_{t+1}=L_{t}+H_{t}
is obtained by solving the Sylvester equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}((\Lambda^{k}-\rho Z^{k})+L_{t}\tilde{S})H_{t}+H_{t}(L_{t}\tilde{S})=F(L_{t}).\end{split}

(9)

The Sylvester equations are linear in
H_{t}
, and standard methods exist for solving them efficiently. Putting the components together, the
NARE
(S,\Lambda^{k},Z^{k},\rho)
routine implements Newton’s method, with each increment computed by solving (
9
).

Remark 1

(ADMM for ICE and MLE)
The sparse ICE produces a sparse estimate of
\Theta_{y}
by applying the
\ell_{1}
-norm to
\Theta_{y}

[
19
]
. Instead the MLE in (
3
) estimates
L
in
\Theta_{y}=L\Theta_{x}L
for a known
\Theta_{x}
by applying the
\ell_{1}
-norm to
L
. Consequently, the ADMM for ICE and our MLE appear methodologically similar, except for a crucial difference in computing primal update (line 3) of Algorithm
1
. For ICE, we need to solve the matrix quadratic equation:

\rho\Theta_{y}^{2}+\Theta_{y}M=2I,

(10)

where
M=\rho(\Lambda^{k}-Z^{k})-S
. Fortunately, (
10
) is a rare case in which one can solve for
\Theta_{y}\succ 0
in a closed form (see
[
7
, Section 6.1]
and
[
20
]
for details).

On the other hand, constructing closed-form solutions for the NAREs in (
8
) and (
7
) is far from trivial, as the coefficient matrices must satisfy stringent conditions. Ref.
[
18
]
provides an up-to-date survey of such conditions for NAREs arising in transportation problems, under which one might construct closed-form solutions. Unfortunately, these conditions do not hold in our setting, necessitating new theoretical developments for our NARE, which we leave for future work.

IV
Simulations

We analyze the performance of the ADMM algorithm in Alg.
1
on synthetic networks and two real-world benchmarks: the IEEE 33-bus power system and Bellingham water distribution system. Additionally, we compare its performance with that of CVX
[
21
]
, as suggested by
[
1
]
.

We consider two metrics, averaged over 40 instances. The first metric is the F-score

\text{F-score}=\frac{2\text{TP}}{2\text{TP}+\text{FP}+\text{FN}}\in[0,1],

where
TP
(true positives) is the total number of correctly recovered edges;
FP
(false positives) is the total number of incorrectly recovered edges; and finally,
FN
(false negatives) is the number of actual edges that were not recovered. A higher F-score indicates better performance in recovering the true structure, with
\text{F-score}=1
signifying perfect structure recovery. The second metric is the worst-case error evaluated using
\|\hat{L}-L^{*}\|_{\infty}
, where
\|A\|_{\infty}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\max_{i,j}|a_{i,j}|
, and
\hat{L}
is the estimate returned by the ADMM or CVX.

We plot the above error metrics versus the rescaled sample size
\tau=N/(d^{2}\log(p))
, where
d=\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\sum_{j}e_{ij}
is the maximum degree of
L^{\ast}
with
e_{ij}=1
if
L^{*}_{ij}\neq 0
, and
e_{ij}=0
else. This scaling is justified in
[
1
]
which shows that the MLE estimator in (
3
) requires
N\approx O(d^{2}\log(p))
samples
1
1

1

We suppress constants in our big-O notation.
to accurately recover the sparsity of
L^{*}
with high probability.
Intuitively, this result means (for a fixed size
p
), the sample complexity is determined by the degree
d
. Therefore, plotting error metrics versus the rescaled sample teases out the effect of

while ignoring the absolute number of samples

.

In light of the foregoing discussion on the sample complexity, we expect that the performance of the MLE (

V
Conclusions

This paper addressed the structure learning problem in equilibrium networks, formulated as an
\ell_{1}
-regularized maximum likelihood estimation problem. As a modest theoretical contribution, we demonstrated that, in the unregularized case, the estimator admitted a pleasing analytical expression.

To solve the learning problem, the paper introduced an ADMM algorithm. While the dual and Lagrange multiplier updates admit a closed form, the primal step lacked such form and required solving a non-symmetric algebraic Riccati equation (NARE). This was handled iteratively using a Newton’s method. Our results showed that, for nearly all networks, the ADMM and CVX solvers achieve comparable performance; however, ADMM converges in fewer iterations and have lower time complexity.

Several directions remain open for future investigation. A key avenue is to establish theoretical convergence rates for the ADMM iterates. Another important challenge is to derive conditions that ensure the existence of a symmetric positive definite solution to the NARE, and to further explore both Newton-based iterative schemes and invariant subspace methods. Finally, incorporating explicit Laplacian constraints, as in
[
15
]
, remains an interesting extension.
