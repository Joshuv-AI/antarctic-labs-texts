Carotid atherosclerosis and aortic stiffness in masters athletes: Multimodality vascular phenotyping from the Master@Heart study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMiddle-aged male Masters athletes exhibit a higher burden of coronary atherosclerosis on coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) compared with healthy controls. Whether this is accompanied by differences in carotid atherosclerosis or aortic stiffness remains uncertain. We assessed carotid atherosclerosis and aortic stiffness across groups, association between carotid atherosclerosis and coronary disease, and the incremental value of carotid plaque beyond traditional risk factors.MethodsIn this cross-sectional analysis of the Master@Heart study, 549 low cardiovascular risk men (median age 55 [50–60] years), including lifelong athletes, late-onset athletes, and healthy controls, underwent vascular phenotyping. Carotid plaque was assessed by ultrasound, aortic stiffness by carotid–femoral pulse wave velocity (PWV), and coronary atherosclerosis by CCTA. Group differences were assessed using chi-square and Kruskal–Wallis tests. Association between carotid and coronary atherosclerosis was evaluated using regression models. Incremental discriminatory value was assessed using ROC curve analysis.ResultsPrevalence of ≥1 carotid plaque did not differ between lifelong athletes, late-onset athletes, and controls (29.5%vs. 33.2% vs. 27.9%; p = 0.54). Carotid stenosis ≥50% was rare (1.2%–2.7%) and similar across groups (p = 0.54). Median PWV did not differ between groups (p = 0.29). Carotid plaque was associated with coronary atherosclerosis on CCTA. However, adding carotid plaque to traditional risk factors did not improve discrimination of coronary atherosclerosis.ConclusionIn middle-aged male Masters athletes, excess subclinical coronary atherosclerosis is observed without corresponding differences in carotid plaque or aortic stiffness. Although carotid plaque is associated with coronary atherosclerosis, it does not meaningfully improve risk discrimination beyond traditional risk factors.

Introduction

Regular endurance exercise confers substantial cardiovascular benefits and is associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality [1,2]. However, recent imaging studies have shown a higher prevalence of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis on coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) in middle-aged male endurance athletes (Masters athletes), despite a generally favorable traditional cardiovascular risk profile [[3], [4], [5]]. The Master@Heart study previously extended these observations by demonstrating a higher prevalence of coronary plaques, including partially calcified and non-calcified plaques, in lifelong Masters athletes compared with age- and risk-factor matched controls [6].

However, it remains uncertain whether the previously observed excess coronary atherosclerosis in Masters athletes reflects a generalized vascular phenotype or is predominantly confined to the coronary circulation [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Previous studies evaluating carotid atherosclerosis and arterial stiffness in endurance athletes have yielded inconsistent findings [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. In addition, the coronary circulation is exposed to unique hemodynamic stresses during prolonged endurance exercise, making a coronary-specific vascular response biologically plausible [13]. We therefore hypothesized that carotid atherosclerosis and aortic stiffness would be comparable or only minimally different between Masters athletes and healthy controls.

To test this hypothesis, we performed multimodality vascular imaging within the Master@Heart cohort to compare carotid atherosclerosis and aortic stiffness between Masters athletes and healthy controls and to examine their relationship with the previously described coronary atherosclerotic burden on CCTA [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

Methods

Study design and population

The Master@Heart (Master Athlete’s Heart) study is a multicenter (Jessa Hospital Hasselt, University Hospital Leuven and Antwerp University Hospital) prospective cohort trial in Belgium and described in detail elsewhere, with participants enrolled between 2018 and 2021 [14]. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the local ethical committee (KU Leuven, S61336). The present manuscript is a secondary analysis of the Master@Heart study within a cross-sectional design [14].

In brief, male participants aged 45–70 years were recruited via the study website (www.masteratheart.be) and through media campaigns. Athletes were defined as engaging in cycling ≥8 h/week, running ≥6 h/week, or triathlon ≥8 h/week for ≥ 6 months. Lifelong athletes initiated endurance training before age 30, while late-onset athletes started after age 30. Controls exercised ≤3 h/week and had no history of regular endurance training. Participants with a history of cardiovascular disease, known cardiovascular risk factors (overweight with BMI > 27.2 kg/m2, smokers or former smokers, use of statins, use of antidiabetic or antihypertensive medications), or contraindications to contrast media were excluded. From all eligible candidates, 605 participants were randomly selected to minimize selection bias.

Carotid ultrasound

Carotid atherosclerosis was evaluated using B-mode, color Doppler, and pulsed-wave Doppler ultrasound (Vivid E9 with 9 L transducer, ProSound Aloka Alpha 6 with UST-5413 transducer, or Philips EPIQ 5 G with L12–3 transducer). Plaques were defined as focal regions with intima-media thickness (IMT) of ≥1.5 mm protruding into the lumen, or as focal wall thickening of ≥50% compared with adjacent segments. Plaque presence and distribution were recorded for each carotid segment to allow standardized assessment of carotid atherosclerosis. Mean posterior common carotid artery IMT was measured bilaterally using automated software and averaged across sides. Internal carotid artery stenosis was graded based on peak systolic velocity. Stenosis of <50% was defined as a peak systolic velocity <125 cm/s, stenosis 50–69% was defined as a peak systolic velocity of 125–230 cm/s, stenosis of >70% was defined as a peak systolic velocity of >230 cm/s, near occlusion was identified by markedly reduced or variable flow, while total occlusion was defined as the absence of detectable flow [15]. Carotid stenosis >50% was considered a clinically relevant threshold [16].

Applanation tonometry – aortic stiffness

Aortic stiffness was assessed using a SphygmoCor XCEL device (AtCor Medical, Sydney, Australia). Carotid-femoral PWV was determined by simultaneous recording of carotid and femoral pulses using tonometry and volumetric displacement. The transit time between the feet of the two waves was calculated, and the distance was measured from the suprasternal notch to the top of the thigh cuff, corresponding to the sites of carotid and femoral measurements. Central pulse pressure (CPP) and augmentation pressure (AP) were derived from the same waveforms as additional measures of aortic stiffness.

Coronary computed tomography angiography

CCTA was performed using either a 2 × 192-slice dual-source CT scanner (Siemens Somatom Force, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany), a 256-slice CT scanner (GE Revolution, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, USA), or a 320-slice CT scanner (Aquilion ONE ViSION, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan). Intravenous esmolol was administered when necessary to achieve target heart rates below 65 beats per minute. All participants received 0.4 mg of sublingual nitroglycerine two minutes prior to scanning. A non-contrast, ECG-gated scan was first acquired to quantify coronary artery calcification (CAC) using the Agatston scoring method. This was followed by contrast-enhanced CCTA, performed with an intravenous injection of iodinated contrast medium via a peripheral vein, followed by a saline flush.

Coronary plaques were analyzed using Syngo.Via (Siemens Healthineers) or GE Advanced Workstation (GE Healthcare) software. Interpreters were blinded to participants’ exercise history and other study assessments. Plaque evaluation followed the 2016 Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography guidelines, including assessment of location, stenosis severity, and composition. Plaques were classified as calcified if entirely composed of regions with a density >130 Hounsfield units, non-calcified if no calcification, and partially calcified if containing both calcified and non-calcified components [17,18].

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing

Peak oxygen uptake (VO₂peak) was determined during a maximal incremental cycling test using breath-by-breath gas analysis, as previously described [14].

Cardiovascular risk estimation

Ten-year cardiovascular risk was estimated using systematic coronary risk evaluation 2 (SCORE2), in accordance with the 2021 ESC guidelines [2], acknowledging that undiagnosed risk factors could not be entirely excluded. Participants were categorized into low-to-moderate, high, or very high risk groups based on age-specific thresholds: for those under 50 years, low-to-moderate risk was defined as <2.5%, high risk as 2.5–7.5%, and very high risk as ≥7.5%; for participants aged 50–69 years, the corresponding cut-offs were <5%, 5–10%, and ≥10%, respectively.

Endpoints and hypotheses

The primary endpoint was the prevalence of carotid atherosclerosis and aortic stiffness, assessed by carotid plaque presence, degree of carotid stenosis, and PWV, across lifelong endurance athletes, late-onset athletes, and healthy controls. Secondary endpoints included mean IMT, CPP, AP, and the association between carotid plaque and coronary atherosclerosis. The primary hypothesis was that measures of extracoronary vascular structure and function would not differ substantially between lifelong endurance athletes, late-onset athletes, and healthy controls.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation has been described previously [14]. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistics (version 29.0.2.0), and figures were generated with GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared across groups using one-way ANOVA, while non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Bonferroni correction was applied for post-hoc comparisons.

The presence of carotid plaque was compared across athlete groups (lifelong athletes, late-onset athletes, and healthy controls) using logistic regression. Univariable models evaluated the association between athlete group and the presence of carotid plaque, as well as with other clinically relevant cardiovascular risk factors. Multivariable models for carotid plaque presence were adjusted for clinically relevant confounders including age, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] >125 nmol/L, triglycerides, HbA1c, family history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (defined as myocardial infarction in a first-degree relative before age 50 in males or 60 in females, sudden death before age 50, or any stroke), and PWV. In univariable analyses, multiple related variables for cholesterol, Lp(a), blood pressure, and aortic stiffness were evaluated. To avoid multicollinearity, only one representative variable from each group was included in the multivariable models (Lp(a) >125 nmol/L, LDL cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, and PWV). Smokers and ex-smokers were excluded by study design. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported.

The association between carotid plaque and coronary atherosclerosis on CCTA was assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Subsequently, the incremental value of carotid plaque beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors for identifying coronary atherosclerosis was assessed using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Area Under the Curves (AUCs) were compared using the method of DeLong. Detailed ROC analyses are provided in the Supplementary Material.

A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were present for applanation tonometry (N = 9, 1.6%) and IMT measurements (N = 33, 6.0%). Analyses were conducted using available cases only.

Prevalence of coronary atherosclerosis

Consistent with the initial Master@Heart findings [6], lifelong athletes showed a higher prevalence of coronary atherosclerosis, independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, than controls, including ≥1 coronary plaque (63.7%vs. 50.0%; p = 0.005), ≥1 partially calcified plaque (30.0%vs. 22.1%; p = 0.024), and ≥1 non-calcified plaque (23.7%vs. 15.1%; p = 0.011). Lifelong athletes also more frequently had CAC >0 (57.4%vs. 45.9%; p = 0.027) and CAC >100 (23.2%vs. 15.1%; p = 0.045). The prevalence of obstructive plaques and severe coronary calcification (CAC >400) did not differ significantly between groups (Supplementary Table 1).

Determinants of carotid atherosclerosis

In univariable analyses, age and multiple cardiovascular risk factors, as well as aortic stiffness parameters, were associated with the presence of carotid plaque (Supplementary Table 2), while athlete status was not associated with carotid plaque. In multivariable analysis, only age, LDL cholesterol, and PWV remained independently associated with carotid plaque presence (Supplementary Table 3). A detailed comparison of baseline characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, SCORE2 risk category, and training characteristics according to carotid plaque status is provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Discussion

In this large, well-phenotyped cohort of middle-aged male Masters athletes and low-risk controls, neither lifelong nor late-onset endurance training was associated with increased carotid atherosclerosis or aortic stiffness, despite a higher burden of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis. Extracoronary structural and functional vascular measures were comparable across groups, and clinically relevant abnormalities were rare in all three study populations. Although the presence of carotid plaque was statistically associated with coronary atherosclerosis markers across the cohort, its addition to traditional cardiovascular risk factors did not meaningfully improve discrimination of coronary disease.

Carotid atherosclerosis

In the Master@Heart cohort, carotid plaque was observed in approximately one third of participants across all three groups, with prevalences of 29.5%, 33.2%, and 27.9% in lifelong athletes, late-onset athletes, and low-risk controls, respectively. While these proportions may appear substantial given the overall low cardiovascular risk profile of our participants, they are broadly consistent with population-based estimates of 26–34% for men aged 50–59 years [19]. Moreover, no dose-response relationship with exercise exposure was apparent, as plaque prevalence was similar across all three low-risk groups regardless of athletic history. Earlier studies reporting high prevalence of carotid or peripheral atherosclerosis in endurance athletes were largely confounded by lack of risk factor exclusion, high smoking rates, small sample size, and absence of a control group [8]. Although Burgstahler et al. reported a similar prevalence of carotid atherosclerosis (35%) in male marathon runners to that observed in our cohort, the absence of a non-athletic control group precluded conclusions regarding the effect of endurance exercise itself [7]. In addition, Taylor et al. demonstrated no difference in carotid IMT between marathon runners and non-running controls, despite a more favorable cardiovascular risk profile among runners [9].

Clinically significant carotid disease was rare across all groups, and while carotid plaque correlated with coronary atherosclerosis markers in the overall cohort, it did not distinguish athletes from controls nor improve coronary risk discrimination beyond established risk factors. Concerns have been raised regarding potential plaque destabilization in highly active individuals [20], but longitudinal outcome data linking carotid plaque to cerebrovascular events in Masters athletes are lacking. Future prospective studies are needed to determine whether carotid plaque in Masters athletes carries prognostic cerebrovascular significance, with objective training load quantification preferred over questionnaire-based approaches, which are known to underestimate vigorous-intensity exercise in this population [[20], [21], [22]].

Aortic stiffness

Aortic stiffness, assessed by carotid–femoral PWV, was preserved in both lifelong and late-onset athletes, with values comparable across groups, supporting the absence of adverse large-artery remodeling associated with long-term endurance exercise in this low-risk population.

These results are consistent with previous investigations reporting preserved central arterial compliance in lifelong exercisers and Masters athletes [12,23]. Some smaller studies have reported higher PWV values in intensive exercisers. However, absolute values in those reports remained within normal limits, suggesting limited clinical relevance [10]. Methodological heterogeneity, including the use of peripheral arterial stiffness assessment by radial tonometry rather than carotid–femoral PWV as the reference standard, may further account for discrepancies between studies [11].

Although PWV did not differ between athlete and control groups, higher PWV was independently associated with the presence of carotid plaque after adjustment for age and traditional cardiovascular risk factors. This finding is biologically plausible, as arterial stiffening and carotid atherosclerosis are closely related manifestations of arterial aging [24]. Although this association does not imply causality, it suggests that these structural and functional changes represent related processes rather than isolated abnormalities. However, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, no causal or temporal relationship can be inferred.

The relationship between carotid and coronary atherosclerosis

An elevated CAC score has been associated with myocardial infarction and coronary revascularization across a wide spectrum of physical activity levels, including highly active individuals [25,26], yet Masters athletes show lower all-cause mortality compared with less active individuals [25]. Whether the excess subclinical coronary atherosclerosis observed in this population carries prognostic significance therefore remains uncertain, and understanding which athletes harbor more advanced coronary disease may help clarify this question. Despite statistically significant associations between carotid plaque and coronary atherosclerosis markers in the present cohort, the addition of carotid plaque presence to traditional cardiovascular risk factors did not meaningfully improve discrimination for CCTA-defined coronary disease. These findings highlight the distinction between statistical association and clinical discrimination, and underscore that carotid ultrasound provides limited incremental value in explaining the mismatch between low estimated cardiovascular risk and prevalent coronary atherosclerosis in Masters athletes.

Implications and future directions

The absence of differences in carotid atherosclerosis and aortic stiffness despite the previously reported excess burden of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis in Masters athletes highlights a discordance between coronary and extracoronary vascular phenotypes. However, the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes conclusions regarding underlying mechanisms or causality.

Several hypotheses warrant further investigation. Coronary arteries are exposed to unique hemodynamic and biomechanical stresses that differ substantially from those encountered in extracoronary vascular beds and may be amplified during prolonged endurance exercise [13,[27], [28], [29]]. The development of coronary atherosclerosis in Masters athletes is likely multifactorial, potentially reflecting interactions between local vascular-bed–specific factors and non-traditional systemic determinants such as lipoprotein(a), genetic susceptibility, inflammatory responses, dietary factors, and cumulative training exposure. However, these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Future research should therefore focus on longitudinal evaluation of Masters athletes using detailed coronary plaque characterization, objective quantification of training load, biomarker profiling, and genetic analyses [13,22,30]. Such approaches may help clarify the determinants and clinical significance of the observed increased coronary atherosclerosis burden in Masters athletes, including the apparent discordance between coronary and peripheral vascular beds, and inform the development of risk prediction models tailored specifically to this population.

Limitations

The study population included only male, predominantly White, low-risk individuals, limiting generalizability to women, other ethnic groups, and higher-risk populations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and assessment of incident cardiovascular events. The low prevalence of significant carotid stenosis reduced statistical power for certain analyses. Whether the observed coronary atherosclerosis translates into differential long-term clinical outcomes remains to be established through longitudinal follow-up, which has been initiated within the ongoing Master@Heart II study.

Conclusion

In middle-aged male Masters athletes, excess subclinical coronary atherosclerosis occurs in the absence of increased carotid plaque burden or aortic stiffening. Although peripheral vascular imaging shows statistical associations with coronary atherosclerosis, it does not meaningfully improve risk discrimination beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors. The mechanisms underlying this vascular-bed discordance remain unclear.
