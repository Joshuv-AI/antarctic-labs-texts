Determinants of digital health information use among indigenous Taiwanese older adults: a cross-sectional study using the theory of planned behaviour and digital health literacy
Authors: Yu-Shuang Lee, Ying Wei Wang, Mei-Chuan Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesLack of familiarity and limited digital literacy frequently limit the ability and willingness of older adults to use digital health tools. This study aimed to investigate the factors influencing the intention to use digital health information and services (DHIAS) and their actual use among indigenous Taiwanese older adults, based on the theory of planned behaviour (TPB) and digital health literacy.DesignCross-sectional quantitative study using a structured questionnaire.SettingCommunity-based settings at the primary care level; 11 cultural health stations located in Eastern Taiwan.ParticipantsA total of 170 Indigenous elders aged ≥55 years were recruited. Inclusion criteria included: Indigenous descent, aged ≥55 years, cognitively intact and able to communicate in Mandarin. All participants completed the interview-based survey.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were the intention to use DHIAS and its actual use. Predictors included digital health literacy and TPB constructs. All variables were measured using validated or adapted items within the questionnaire.ResultsThe mean score for intention to use DHIAS was 1.50 (SD=0.90) and the mean score for actual behaviour was 1.68 (SD=1.14). Background

In the modern era, digital technology has become an essential component of daily life, particularly in the healthcare sector. This development has been further accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in a substantial increase in the use of telemedicine and digital health applications.1 The WHO defines electronic health (eHealth) as the cost-effective and secure use of information and communication technologies to support health and health-related fields.2 Various digital tools—such as online health education, electronic medical records, appointment systems, medical navigation platforms, teleconsultations and digital pharmacies—have significantly enhanced the accessibility, efficiency and responsiveness of healthcare delivery, expediting the dissemination of health information and services.3 4 Emerging evidence has indicated the benefits of digital health interventions, including improvements in medication adherence through interactive platforms,5 reduction in HbA1c levels and depressive symptoms among individuals with diabetes,6 and enhanced HIV care management, which contributes to improved outcomes and decreased transmission rates.7 Older adults in remote regions often encounter significant barriers in accessing adequate healthcare services. Given that older adults are frequent users of healthcare resources, digital health technologies offer a promising solution to bridge these service gaps.8

The effective use of digital health services requires sufficient digital health literacy (DHL). DHL refers to the ability of an individual to seek, locate, comprehend, evaluate and apply health information accessed through electronic platforms.9 Studies indicate that individuals with higher DHL are more likely to adopt health-promoting behaviours, implement preventive strategies during public health emergencies such as the COVID-19 pandemic, experience improved physical and mental health outcomes and use healthcare services more effectively.10 Older adults represent a high-risk group for low DHL, which exacerbates the digital divide and hinders their willingness to engage with digital health tools.11,13 According to a 2023 survey conducted by Taiwan’s Ministry of Health and Welfare, only 18.8% of adults aged 65 years and older reported frequently using the internet to obtain health information, while 14.6% reported occasional use. Furthermore, 16.1% identified the internet as their primary source of health information, with this proportion decreasing progressively with advancing age.14 These findings highlight that internet-based health information seeking remains relatively uncommon among older Taiwanese adults. This context underscores the importance of examining both DHL and behavioural determinants when studying health information use among older populations.

In research on behaviour change, it is therefore essential to adopt a theoretical framework to better understand the mechanisms underlying intention and action, providing a foundation for exploring the factors that influence older adults’ use of digital health information and services (DHIAS). A widely adopted framework for understanding and influencing human behaviour is the theory of planned behaviour (TPB), proposed by Ajzen in 1985. TPB posits that the behavioural intention of an individual is the most immediate determinant of actual behaviour. This intention is influenced by three core constructs: attitude towards the behaviour, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control.15 16 TPB has been extensively used to inform the design of behaviour change interventions, including those aimed at promoting physical activity among older adults,17 increasing fruit and vegetable consumption18 and predicting vaccination-related intentions and behaviours during the COVID-19 pandemic.19

The indigenous population of Taiwan constitutes approximately 2.52% of the total population, with the majority residing in remote regions in the eastern and southern parts of the country.20 These areas typically lack adequate healthcare infrastructure compared with urban centres, resulting in poorer health outcomes for residents. Although digital health services could potentially mitigate these disparities, they face significant challenges, including limited internet access and low digital proficiency among residents.21

To address these challenges, this study aimed to examine the factors influencing the intentions and behaviours of indigenous older adults regarding the use of DHIAS, using the TPB. In addition, we integrated DHL into the TPB framework as an extended predictor to assess its contribution to behavioural outcomes. Ultimately, we sought to inform the design of targeted interventions that enhance digital engagement and promote equitable healthcare access for older adults in regions with limited healthcare access.

Methods

This study was guided by the TPB,15 16 and the research framework was constructed to examine the intention and actual behaviour related to DHIAS use. In addition to TPB’s three core constructs—attitude, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control—the model was extended to include DHL as an additional predictor of intention and behaviour.

Study design

An analytical cross-sectional survey design was employed, with data collected at a single point in time using a structured questionnaire to investigate factors associated with the intention and behaviour of indigenous older adults in using DHIAS.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted among older indigenous individuals receiving services at cultural health stations in Hualien, Eastern Taiwan. These stations, established by the Taiwanese government, aim to address the needs of an ageing population by providing culturally tailored health and wellness services to indigenous older adults in tribal communities.22 At these stations, eligibility for older adult services begins at age 55—lower than the WHO’s threshold of 6023—because the average life expectancy of Taiwan’s indigenous population is shorter than that of non-indigenous groups.24 Consequently, many indigenous-specific elder services in Taiwan adopt 55 years as the starting point.25 Therefore, the inclusion criteria for this study are as follows: age ≥55 years, indigenous Taiwanese descent, no cognitive impairment and the ability to communicate in Mandarin. Exclusion criteria included severe cognitive impairment, inability to communicate in Mandarin and unwillingness to provide informed consent. Recruitment took place between 1 September 2023 and 31 March 2024. The study employed a cross-sectional design with no follow-up period.

Sample size and sampling method

The adequacy of the sample size was examined based on guidelines for multiple regression analysis. According to Green,26 the minimum sample size should satisfy the following criteria: N≥50+8 m for testing the overall multiple correlation, and N≥104+6 m for testing individual predictors, where m is the number of independent variables.26 27 In this study, six predictors were included (age, education, attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioural control and DHL). Thus, the minimum required sample sizes were 98 and 140, respectively. With 170 valid participants, the sample size exceeded both thresholds, indicating sufficient statistical power to conduct multiple regression analyses. These guidelines are based on the assumption of a medium effect size, a significance level of α=0.05 and statistical power of 0.80.

A cluster sampling method was adopted, with cultural health stations serving as the sampling units. In 2023, there were 106 cultural health stations in Hualien County.28 From these, 11 stations were selected through convenience sampling. Selection was based on the willingness of station directors to participate and on feasibility considerations related to available research personnel and funding resources. Within each selected station, all eligible older adults were included as the cluster sample, resulting in a total of 186 potential participants. Due to absence from the station on the survey day (eg, illness or travel), 16 individuals were unavailable, resulting in 170 completed and valid responses for analysis. The overall response rate was 91.4%.

Research tools

The structured questionnaire was developed by adapting validated instruments (eHEALS and TPB measurement guidelines) to fit the cultural context of indigenous older adults. Structured questionnaires are widely used in quantitative studies for their ability to standardise data collection and ensure comparability. It offers respondents predefined answer choices, which not only simplify completion but also facilitate the systematic aggregation and statistical analysis of data.29

DHL scale

The scale comprises two dimensions: (1) ease of using digital technologies (7 items) and (2) ease of accessing, understanding, appraising and applying health information and services in digital environments (11 items).

A 4-point Likert-type response scale—a self-assessment tool that measures perceived DHL—was used, including the options: very easy, easy, difficult and very difficult, along with an additional response option for ‘never used’.

Attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioural control, intention and behaviour scales for using DHIAS

Based on Ajzen’s TPB and established questionnaire design principles, the behavioural measurement scale was constructed to include the four key components, including action, target, context and time frame (within the past 3 months).30 The scale includes the following subscales:

Attitude: Measured using eight items that assess participants’ beliefs about the likely outcomes or characteristics of using digital health services, and their evaluations of these outcomes.

Subjective norms: Assessed using 10 items that evaluate the perceived social pressure from significant others regarding the behaviour, along with the participant’s motivation to comply with these expectations.

Perceived behavioural control: Measured using five items to capture individuals’ perceptions of their control over the behaviour, including available resources, opportunities and potential barriers.

Intention and behaviour: Each construct is measured using seven items that assess participants’ intention to use DHIAS and their actual behaviour within the past 3 months. Behavioural examples include searching for health information online, communicating with healthcare professionals or family members via digital platforms, and using health management applications. A 7-point Likert scale was used, with higher scores indicating greater agreement, likelihood or frequency.

Reliability and validity

Content validity was assessed by five experts in nursing, public health and health communication, who reviewed the draft instruments for clarity, relevance and conceptual alignment. Items with a Content Validity Index <0.80 were revised based on expert feedback to improve item appropriateness.

Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, calculated based on the responses of all 170 participants, with coefficients ranging from 0.74 to 0.97: DHL (α=0.97), attitude (α=0.94), subjective norms (α=0.93), perceived behavioural control (α=0.92), intention (α=0.82) and behaviour (α=0.74).

Pilot study and patient and public involvement

We conducted a pilot test with three indigenous older adults who met the inclusion criteria. In addition to evaluating the clarity and relevance of the questionnaire items, participants were invited to provide feedback on the overall interview experience. Based on their input, the research team made several key modifications, including enlarging font sizes and incorporating visual scales to enhance readability and comprehension. The average time required to complete the interview was approximately 30 min. These adjustments enhanced the accessibility and cultural appropriateness of the instrument, reflecting significant patient and public involvement in refining the study procedures.

Data collection

The researcher first contacted the cultural health stations to obtain approval for data collection. On receiving consent and cooperation from the station staff, trained interviewers conducted one-on-one, face-to-face interviews with participants during their regular scheduled sessions. Before data collection, all interviewers underwent standardised training to ensure interview technique consistency and minimise potential interviewer bias. Before data collection, interviewers explained the study’s purpose and procedures and obtained written informed consent from all participants.

To accommodate the needs of older adults, interviewers guided participants through the questionnaire using enlarged versions of the survey items to facilitate response selection.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics V.25.0, IBM. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise participants’ demographic characteristics. In addition, we performed hierarchical multiple regression analysis to assess the influence of independent variables on the dependent variables, such as intention and behaviour related to DHIAS use.

Results

Participants

A total of 170 indigenous older adults who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study and completed the survey. As this was a cross-sectional design, all recruited participants were included in the final analysis, and no follow-up was conducted.

Table 1 summarises the demographic characteristics of the 170 older participants. The mean age was 74.21 years (SD=8.62). The sample was predominantly female (72.4%), with males accounting for only 27.6%. In terms of marital status, the largest group was widowed (52.9%). Regarding education level, 62.9% had completed elementary school or less. Regarding internet use, 57.6% of participants reported having used the internet, whereas 42.4% had never used it. Mobile phones were the most commonly used digital device, reported by 58.2% of participants; however, 38.2% indicated that they did not own any digital devices or have access to digital resources.

Model 1 (demographic variables): Age and education level.

Model 2 (TPB constructs): Attitude, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control.

Model 3 (extended variable): DHL.

Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no significant issues, with variance inflation factor (VIF) values ranging from 1.101 to 2.647.31

Discussion

According to Taiwan’s 2023 Internet Usage Survey, the internet penetration rate among the general population was 84.67%. Furthermore, among individuals aged 60 years or older, usage rates ranged from 40.35% to 73.47%.32 In this study, despite participants having a mean age of 74 years, 57.6% reported having used the internet. This rate is comparable to national figures for the older population, highlighting considerable potential for developing digital health services tailored to older adults in Taiwan.

Although many DHIAS platforms have demonstrated effectiveness, older adults, who are often disproportionately affected by the digital divide, continue to face significant challenges in using these tools.33 Findings from this study indicate that although a portion of older participants had internet experience, their intention to use and actual engagement with DHIAS remained relatively low. This echoes the 2023 survey by Taiwan’s Ministry of Health and Welfare, which reported that fewer than 20% of adults aged ≥65 frequently use the internet to obtain health information.14 Together, these findings underscore that internet access or basic skills alone are insufficient to promote meaningful adoption of digital health services among older adults.

Our findings indicated that psychological factors are crucial in influencing behavioural intention. Specifically, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control significantly predicted the intention to use DHIAS, whereas attitude did not emerge as a significant predictor. This suggests that the willingness of older adults to engage in digital health activities is shaped more by social influence (such as approval from significant others) and their confidence in their ability to use these tools, rather than by their personal evaluations of digital health services.

This pattern is consistent with prior research. Morris and Venkatesh reported that older individuals’ decisions to adopt new technologies were more strongly influenced by subjective norms and perceived behavioural control than by attitude, unlike younger users.34 Compared with younger generations, older adults generally have less experience with digital technologies, tend to value ease of use and often rely on guidance from peers or family members. Our findings also echo those of van Houwelingen et al, who reported that children and grandchildren play a pivotal role in motivating older adults to adopt technology and in providing ongoing support for its use.35 Such intergenerational support reinforces the influence of subjective norms, but it may also contribute to older adults’ reliance on family assistance rather than fostering independent digital skills. These findings provide a plausible explanation for why attitude did not significantly predict intention in this study, while subjective norms and perceived control played a larger role.

Together, these results emphasise the importance of addressing psychological barriers and enhancing support systems to improve DHIAS use among older adults. Creating socially supportive environments, for example, through family encouragement, peer support and culturally grounded communication at health stations, may be more effective in promoting intention than focusing solely on shaping attitudes. In addition, strengthening perceived control by providing user-friendly digital platforms and ongoing technical assistance could further enhance intention to use DHIAS in this population.

In the final step of the regression analysis, the inclusion of DHL significantly enhanced the model’s explanatory power for intention, accounting for an additional 20.3% of the variance. Notably, this addition rendered the effect of perceived behavioural control non-significant. Although perceived behavioural control reflects individuals’ confidence in acting,36 DHL represents their capacity to navigate digital health platforms.9 The conceptual overlap between these constructs may explain why one diminishes in significance when both are included. Overall, DHL emerged as a stronger predictor of intention than the original TPB constructs, highlighting the importance of extending traditional behavioural frameworks with literacy-related variables.

Regarding behaviour, age and education were initially significant predictors. However, after adding intention and perceived behavioural control, the influence of education diminished, and the predictive effect of age increased. After DHL was included, the effect of age was further reduced, while both intention and DHL became the dominant predictors of actual behaviour. These results support the central premise of TPB, that behavioural intention is the most immediate determinant of behaviour,36 while also highlighting DHL as an independent and robust factor influencing digital health engagement.

Notably, although perceived behavioural control contributed to intention, its role in predicting behaviour was replaced by age until DHL was introduced, suggesting that DHL may mediate or moderate the influence of age-related barriers on digital behaviour. Future studies are warranted to explore this association further.

In summary, this study highlights DHL as a critical determinant of both intention and behaviour in the use of DHIAS among older adults, supported by evidence from other related studies.37,39 These findings underscore that improving DHL is not only about teaching basic technical skills but also about fostering the ability to evaluate and apply digital health information in daily life critically. From a practical perspective, strategies to enhance DHL should be embedded within culturally grounded interventions that leverage family and community support, with cultural health stations serving as key platforms for implementation. In addition, policies aimed at reducing the rural digital divide, such as improving internet infrastructure and promoting user-friendly, localised health applications, are essential for ensuring equitable access. Similar challenges have also been documented in other disadvantaged populations, where limited infrastructure and low digital literacy exacerbate health inequities. 40At the theoretical level, extending the TPB with DHL demonstrates that literacy-related capacities may function as stronger determinants than traditional constructs such as attitude, particularly in marginalised populations with limited digital exposure. This finding broadens the applicability of the TPB in the digital health domain and underscores the importance of integrating literacy frameworks when examining technology adoption in vulnerable groups.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the sample was limited to indigenous older adults residing in Hualien, Eastern Taiwan. Consequently, the findings may lack generalisability to populations in other regions or to older adults with different cultural, linguistic or socioeconomic characteristics. Future studies should consider including more geographically and demographically diverse samples to enhance the external validity of the findings.

Second, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce response bias. Participants might have overestimated or underestimated their digital competencies or actual engagement with digital health services. Incorporating objective assessments of DHL and actual usage behaviours—such as system log data or skill-based tasks—could improve the accuracy and robustness of future research.

Conclusions

This study found that DHL and subjective norms were the strongest predictors of intention to use DHIAS, while both DHL and intention significantly predicted actual behaviour among indigenous older adults. These results underscore the need to go beyond providing access and technical training by also strengthening DHL and fostering social support from family, peers and community health resources. Tailored interventions that consider cultural context and the specific challenges of indigenous populations are recommended. Future research should employ longitudinal or intervention designs to validate these findings and further explore strategies to reduce the digital divide in older adults. In the long term, such efforts may enhance older adults’ health management capabilities and ultimately contribute to better health outcomes and quality of life.
