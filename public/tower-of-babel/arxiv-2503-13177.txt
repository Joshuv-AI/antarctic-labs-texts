Simulation Of Infinite-Dimensional Diffusion Bridges
Thorben Pieper-Sethmacher, Frank van der Meulen, Aad van der Vaart

Abstract
Let X X be the mild solution to a semilinear stochastic partial differential equation. In this article, we develop methodology to sample from the infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge that arises from conditioning X X on a linear transformation L ​ X T LX_{T} of the final state X T X_{T} at some time T > 0 T>0 . This solves a problem that has so far not been attended to in the literature. Our main contribution is the derivation of a path measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to the path measure of the infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge. This lifts previously known results for stochastic ordinary differential equations to the setting of infinite-dimensional diffusions and stochastic partial differential equations. We demonstrate our findings through numerical experiments on stochastic reaction-diffusion equations.

Introduction

Consider a semilinear
stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE)
of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}&=\left[AX_{t}+F(t,X_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\geq 0,\\
X_{0}&=x_{0}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(1)

The operator
A
denotes the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on a Hilbert space
H
, whereas
F
denotes a nonlinear operator and
Q
is a symmetric, positive operator on
H
. The process
W
is a cylindrical Wiener process on
H
, defined on a stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
. We assume that the operators
A
,
F
and
Q
satisfy suitable conditions such that Equation (
1
) admits a unique mild solution
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for any
x_{0}\in H.

For some
k\geq 1
, let
L:H\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
denote a bounded, linear operator, termed the observation operator. Suppose we observe the linear transformation
LX_{T}
of
X_{T}
at some time
T>0
.
This includes the highly relevant cases that we observe an orthogonal projection of
X_{T}
onto a finite-dimensional subspace or that, in the case of
H=L^{2}(D)
for some
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we observe weighted integrals of
X_{T}
.

We are interested in sampling the process
X
on the interval
[0,T]
, conditioned on the event
\{LX_{T}=y\}
for some fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. This problems arises, for example, in parameter estimation for partially observed SPDEs.
Following the literature for diffusion processes with finite-dimensional state spaces, we refer to the conditioned process as an
infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge
and denote it by
X^{\star}
.
To the best of our knowledge, the problem of drawing samples of
X^{\star}
has not been considered before in the generality of our setup.

This stands in contrast to the corresponding problem for a stochastic
ordinary
differential equation, which has attracted considerable attention in the past two decades (see for instance
[
Delyon and Hu, 2006
]
,
[
Beskos et al., 2008
]
,
[
Papaspiliopoulos and Roberts, 2012
]
,
[
Schauer et al., 2017
]
,
[
Bierkens et al., 2020
]
,
[
Heng et al., 2021
]
).
By Doob’s
h
-transform (
[
Rogers and Williams, 2000
]
, Section IV.39), it can be shown that a
diffusion bridge

x^{\star}
associated with the strong solution
x
to the SODE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t}=f(x_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{t}

is characterized by yet another SODE given by

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\star}_{t}=[f(x^{\star}_{t})+\sigma\sigma^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h(t,x^{\star}_{t})]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{t}.

Here,
h
is a mapping that depends on both the transition densities
p
and the matrix representation of the map
L
.
In particular, the conditioned process
x^{\star}
solves an SODE itself, where the additional term
\sigma\sigma^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h(t,x^{\star}_{t})
is added to the drift coefficient to steer the process towards the point of conditioning.

Unfortunately, the transition density
p
, and consequently the function
h
, is not known in closed form except for a few special cases.
This has motivated several works in which the map
h
is replaced by a substitute
g
in the steering term (
[
Clark, 1990
]
,
[
Delyon and Hu, 2006
]
,
[
Schauer et al., 2017
]
,
[
Mider et al., 2021
]
). If
g
is chosen carefully, then indeed the laws of the conditioned process and the process that substitutes
g
for
h
are absolutely continuous on path space. In particular,
weighted samples
of the conditioned process can be obtained. This has important applications for example in Bayesian parameter estimation for discretely observed diffusion processes (see for instance
[
Roberts and Stramer, 2001
]
and
[
Papaspiliopoulos et al., 2013
]
).

The literature on the corresponding problem for infinite-dimensional diffusions is scarce. Notable exceptions are
[
Di Nunno et al., 2023
]
and
[
Yang et al., 2024
]
. The former considers the situation where
F\equiv 0
, in which case the diffusion bridge is given by a conditioned Gaussian process on
H
. Moreover, convergence rates are derived for the spatial discretisation of
X^{\star}
.
In
[
Yang et al., 2024
]
, the process
X
is conditioned on a set of positive measure and projected onto a finite-dimensional subspace of
H
after which the problem is considered in finite dimensions. Additionally, Equation (
1
) is assumed to admit a strong solution, an assumption that is generally not satisfied when working with SPDEs.

1.1
Contribution

In this paper, we lift earlier results obtained for SODEs in
[
Schauer et al., 2017
]
and
[
Bierkens et al., 2020
]
to SPDEs, resulting in computational methods for sampling infinite-dimensional diffusion bridges.
This extension is nontrivial as we need to deal with complications arising from working in infinite-dimensional state spaces.
We strongly depend on our earlier work in
[
Pieper-Sethmacher et al., 2024
]
on exponential change of measure for SPDEs. Here, it was shown that the infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge
X^{\star}
can be obtained by an
exponential change of measure

\mathbb{P}^{\star}
on the underlying filtered probability space.
Under additional assumptions, it was shown that
X^{\star}
is equivalent in law to the mild solution of the
bridge SPDE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{\star}(t)=\left[AX^{\star}(t)+F(t,X^{\star}(t))+Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h(t,X^{\star}(t))\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t),\quad t<T,

where
h(t,z):=\rho_{X}(t,z;T,y)
denotes the density of
LX_{T}\mid X_{t}=z
, evaluated at
y
.
As in the finite-dimensional case,
\rho_{X}
is in general intractable, rendering a direct simulation of
X^{\star}
infeasible. As a further complication, the formulation of the bridge SPDE relies on the existence of the gradient
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h
in the first place. However, to the best of our knowledge, no such regularity result for transition densities of SPDEs of the form (
1
) has been obtained in the literature so far.
On the other hand, the
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
process
Z
, which satisfies the linearized version of (
1
) with
F\equiv 0
, is a Gaussian process for which the the density of
LZ_{T}\mid Z_{t}=z
is tractable. Moreover, for this density, which we denote by
\rho_{Z}
, the required regularity assumptions can be validated.
This motivates substituting
\rho_{X}
by
\rho_{Z}
, by which we obtain the
guided process

X^{\circ}
as the mild solution to the SPDE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{\circ}(t)=\left[AX^{\circ}(t)+F(t,X^{\circ}(t))+Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(t,X^{\circ}(t))\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t),

where
g(t,x)=\rho(t,x;T,y)
.
In
[
Pieper-Sethmacher et al., 2024
]
, it was shown that
X^{\circ}
equals in law the process
X
under yet another exponential change of measure
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
.
Moreover, it was shown that the laws
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
of
X^{\star}
and
X^{\circ}
are absolutely continuous on the path space
C([0,t];H)
for any
t<T.

Our main result in this work, presented in Theorem
3.4
, shows that the absolute continuity persists in the limit
t\uparrow T
.
This facilitates
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
as a suitable proposal distribution for
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
, hence enabling a wide range of computational methods to sample from infinite-dimensional diffusion bridges. These include, for example, Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms, importance sampling or variational methods. Algorithm
1
shows how Theorem
3.4
can be used to define a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm for sampling diffusion bridges for SPDEs. We show numerical performance in stochastic reaction diffusion equations, including the Allen-Cahn equation.
A crucial step towards the main result concerns the convergence of the guided process
X^{\circ}(t)
towards the conditioning point
y
at an appropriate rate (Cf. Theorem
3.2
). While the main steps in the proof are similar to the proof of absolute continuity in
[
Bierkens et al., 2020
]
, complications arise from dealing with the infinite-dimensional setting. As it is unclear under which conditions transition densities exist, contrary to
[
Bierkens et al., 2020
]
, the proof of Theorem
3.4
avoids their existence. As we restrict to additive noise, we also obtain much “cleaner” conditions for absolute continuity to hold. Our examples show that Assumption
13
can easily be verified in the diagonalisable case (Cf. Section
5
). The other assumption, Assumption
3.3
, is shown to hold under the easily verified conditions of Lemma
3.6
.

2.2
Derivation of the diffusion bridge and guided process

For the reader’s convenience, we give an outline of the derivation of the infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge
X^{\star}
and the guided process
X^{\circ}
based on an exponential change of measure as introduced in
[
Pieper-Sethmacher et al., 2024
]
. The details of this subsection’s proofs can be found in Appendix
A
.

For this, let
(T_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the transition semigroup

\displaystyle(T_{t}\varphi)(s,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(s+t,X_{t+s})\mid X_{s}=x]

defined on the Banach space
C_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times H)
of continuous functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\|\varphi\|_{m}=\sup_{t,x}(1+\|x\|^{m})^{-1}|\varphi(t,x)|<\infty.

A sequence
(\varphi_{n})_{n}\subset C_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times H)
is said to converge to
\varphi\in C_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times H)
in the topology of
bounded-pointwise
convergence if
\lim_{n}\varphi_{n}(t,x)=\varphi(t,x)
for any
t\geq 0,x\in H
and
\sup_{n}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{m}<\infty.
We then write
\pi\text{-}\lim_{n}\varphi_{n}=\varphi.

In the topology of bounded-pointwise convergence, define the infinitesimal generator
(K,\dom_{m}(K))
of
(T_{t})_{t}
via

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{cases}\dom_{m}(K)&=\left\{\varphi\in C_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times H):\exists\psi\in C_{m}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times H)\text{ s.t. }\pi\text{-}\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\dfrac{T_{t}\varphi-\varphi}{t}=\psi\right\}\\
(K\varphi)(s,x)&=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\dfrac{(T_{t}\varphi)(s,x)-\varphi(s,x)}{t},\quad\varphi\in\dom_{m}(K),(s,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times H.\end{cases}\end{split}

(5)

For any positive function
h\in\dom_{m}(K)
, define the process

\displaystyle E^{h}_{t}=\dfrac{h(t,X_{t})}{h(0,x_{0})}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{Kh}{h}(s,X_{s})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right).

(6)

It can be shown that
E^{h}
is a continuous local
\mathbb{P}
-martingale whenever it exists, see for example Lemma 3.1 in
[
Palmowski and Rolski, 2002
]
.
If
E^{h}
is a true
\mathbb{P}
-martingale, it defines an
exponential change of measure

\mathbb{P}^{h}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
. Moreover, under additional assumptions on

, the change of measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
is of Girsanov-type, as was shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2

(Theorem 3.5,
[
Pieper-Sethmacher et al., 2024
]
)
.

Let
h:[0,T)\times H\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
satisfy the following for any
S<T
:

(i)

h\in C_{m}([0,S]\times H)
such that
Kh(t,x)
exists for any
[0,S]\times H
and
h^{-1}Kh\in C_{m}([0,S]\times H)
.

(ii)

h
is such that the process
(E^{h}_{t})_{t\in[0,S]}
is a
\mathbb{P}
-martingale.

Then
h
defines a unique change of measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T})
via

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{h}_{t}=\mathbb{E}^{h}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{t},\quad t<T.

Additionally, let
h
satisfy the following:

(iii)

h
is Fréchet differentiable in

such that
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}h\in C_{m}([0,S]\times H;H)
for any
S<T
.

Then
X
under
\mathbb{P}^{h}
is a mild solution to the SPDE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\left[AX_{t}+F(t,X_{t})+Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h(t,X_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{h}_{t},\quad t\in[0,T),

where

is a
\mathbb{P}^{h}
-cylindrical Wiener process.

The diffusion bridge
X^{\star}
and corresponding guided process
X^{\circ}
are defined through an application of Theorem
2.2
for different choices of the function

as follows.
Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and

be fixed. For any

and
t\in[0,T)
, denote by
\rho_{Z}(t,x;T,y)
the density of

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on

. As stated in Section

is Gaussian in

and covariance matrix

defined in (

Moreover, let
\rho_{X}(t,x;T,y)
denote the density of

, evaluated at

. The existence of
\rho_{X}(t,x;T,y)
is a consequence of the Girsanov theorem, from which the absolute continuity of

with respect to
\mathcal{L}(Z_{T})
, and hence of

On

\displaystyle\begin{split}h(t,x)&=\rho_{X}(t,x;T,y),\\

(7)

Remark 2.3
.

The function
h(t,x)=\rho_{X}(t,x;T,y)
is only defined
\mathcal{L}(LX_{T})
-almost surely in

, i.e. there exists a Borel set
\mathcal{A}_{0}
such that
\mathbb{P}(LX_{T}\in\mathcal{A}_{0})=1
and

is uniquely well-defined for all
t\in[0,T),x\in H
and

. The existence of such a set, independently of
(t,x)
, follows from the Girsanov theorem and the continuity of
(t,x)\mapsto LZ_{T}\mid Z_{t}=x
.
Throughout the remainder of this work, we assume without further notice that

.

The following two propositions verify that

satisfy the assumptions of Theorem

, and consequently, prove the existence of

and

.

Proposition 2.4

(Existence of the diffusion bridge)
.

The mapping

defined in (

and

of Theorem

with

. Moreover, the measure
\mathbb{P}^{\star}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{t}=\frac{h(t,X_{t})}{h(0,x_{0})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{t},\quad t<T,

is such that, for any bounded and measurable function

and

, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\star}[\varphi(X_{t_{1}},...,X_{t_{n}})]=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t_{1}},...,X_{t_{n}})\mid LX_{T}=y],\quad\mathcal{L}(LX_{T})-\text{a.s.}

We call the process

the infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge (of

).

Remark 2.5
.

If, in addition to Assumption (i) and (ii) of Theorem
2.2
,
h
satisfies Assumption (iii),
X
under
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
is a mild solution to the diffusion bridge equation

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{\star}_{t}=\left[AX^{\star}_{t}+F(t,X^{\star}_{t})+Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h(t,X^{\star}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{\star}_{t},\quad t\in[0,T),

(8)

for some
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
-cylindrical Wiener process
W^{\star}
. This generalizes the diffusion bridge equation known for conditioned diffusions in Euclidean spaces.

However, we do not impose this assumption on
h
for two reasons. Firstly, verifying the Fréchet differentiablity of
h
is a difficult problem in this infinite-dimensional setting. If the state space is Euclidean, this assumption can be verified based on regularity results for transition densities of diffusion processes. To the best of our knowledge, no such results are known for SPDEs.

Secondly, except for a few special cases, the term
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h
is intractable. This renders a direct simulation of the bridge process infeasible, even if it satisfies Equation (
8
). This motivates the construction of the guided process as a tractable substitute process in the next proposition.

Proposition 2.6

(Existence of the guided process)
.

The mapping
g
defined in (
7
) satisfies Assumptions
(i)
to
(iii)
of Theorem
2.2
with
Kg(t,x)=\langle F(t,x),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}g(t,x)\rangle.

In particular, there exists a unique measure
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\circ}_{t}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{g(t,X_{t})}{g(0,x_{0})}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\langle F(s,X_{s}),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(s,X_{s})\rangle\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{t},\quad t<T,

such that
X
under
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
is the unique mild solution to the SPDE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{\circ}_{t}=\left[AX^{\circ}_{t}+F(t,X^{\circ}_{t})+Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(t,X^{\circ}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{\circ}_{t},\quad t\in[0,T),

(9)

where
(W^{\circ}_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
is a
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
-cylindrical Wiener process. The process
X
under
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
is referred to as the guided process.

The proofs of Proposition
2.4
and Proposition
2.6
can be found in Appendix
A
.

Remark 2.7
.

To highlight the fact that the process
X
under
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
solves the SPDE (
9
), we write from now on, with slight abuse of notation,
X^{\circ}
instead of
X
whenever we assume it to be defined on the stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t},\mathbb{P}^{\circ})
.
Likewise, we write
X^{\star}
for the process
X
defined under the measure
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
.
Furthermore, we denote by
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
the law of
X
on
C([0,T];H)
under
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
respectively and by
\mathcal{L}^{\star}_{t}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}_{t}
their restrictions onto
C([0,t];H)
for any
t<T
.

Contrary to the function
h
, the function
g
is available in closed form with

\displaystyle g(t,x)=\rho_{Z}(t,x;T,y)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{k}\det(R_{T-t})}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}|R_{T-t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(y-L_{T-t}x)|^{2}\right).

Hence, the additional drift term
G(t,x)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(t,x)
appearing in Equation (
9
) is given by

\displaystyle G(t,x)=L^{*}_{T-t}R^{-1}_{T-t}(y-L_{T-t}x).

(10)

In particular, this enables us to draw samples directly from
X^{\circ}
by forward simulating the SPDE (
9
).

3
Main results

Throughout this section we fix some
T>0
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and let
X^{\star}
be the infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge and
X^{\circ}
be the guided process, respectively defined as the process
X
under the changes of measure
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
as introduced in Propositions
2.4
and
2.6
.

By construction, the measures
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
are absolutely continuous on
\mathcal{F}_{t},t<T,
with

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{t}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\circ}_{t}}(X^{\circ})=\frac{h(t,X^{\circ}_{t})}{g(t,X^{\circ}_{t})}\frac{g(0,x_{0})}{h(0,x_{0})}\Psi_{t}(X^{\circ})\quad\mathbb{P}^{\circ}\text{-a.s.}

(11)

where the process
\Psi_{t}(X^{\circ})
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(X^{\circ})=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl\langle F(s,X^{\circ}_{s}),G(s,X^{\circ}_{s})\Bigr\rangle\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right).

(12)

Our main results come in two parts. Firstly, we show that the guided process
X^{\circ}(t)
converges to the conditioning set
\{x\in H:Lx=y\}
. Besides the convergence in itself, it is crucial that this convergence occurs at an appropriate rate.

Secondly, we show that the absolute continuity in (
11
) persists as we take the limit
t\uparrow T
, i.e. that the measures
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
are equivalent on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
. As a consequence thereof, the laws
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
are equivalent on the path space
C([0,T];H)
.
This is critical for the guided process to serve as a valid proposal distribution for the infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge.

3.1
Limit behavior of the Guided Process towards the conditioning time

We first study the limit behavior of
X^{\circ}(t)
as
t\uparrow T.

Let the following hold.

Assumption 3.1
.

There exist positive constants
\underline{c},\bar{c}
such that for all
t\in(0,T]

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underline{c}~t^{-1}\leq\|R_{t}^{-1}\|&\leq\bar{c}~t^{-1}.\end{split}

(13)

We get the following convergence result for the guided process
X^{\circ}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
3.1
, there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\uparrow T}\frac{|y-L_{T-t}X^{\circ}_{t}|}{\sqrt{(T-t)\ln(1/(T-t))}}\leq M\quad\mathbb{P}^{\circ}\text{-a.s.}

(14)

3.2
Absolute continuity of
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}

In this section we establish sufficient conditions for the absolute continuity of
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
.
We require the following assumption on the functions
h
and
g
.

Assumption 3.3
.

There exist continuous, positive functions
\lambda_{1}(t)\leq\lambda_{2}(t)
satisfying
\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda_{1}(t)=\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda_{2}(t)=1
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(T-t)g(t,x)\leq h(t,x)\leq\lambda_{2}(T-t)g(t,x),\quad t<T,x\in H.

(15)

Theorem 3.4
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
,
3.1
and
3.3
, it holds that
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
are absolutely continuous on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
with

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\star}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\circ}}(X^{\circ})=\Phi_{T}(X^{\circ})\quad\mathbb{P}^{\circ}\text{-a.s.},

(16)

where
\Phi_{T}(X^{\circ})=\frac{g(0,x_{0})}{h(0,x_{0})}\Psi_{T}(X^{\circ})
with
\Psi_{t}(X^{\circ})
defined as in Equation (
12
).

We give the proof of Theorem
3.4
in Section
7
.

Remark 3.5
.

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
3.4
and a change of variables, it follows that the laws
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
are absolutely continuous on the path space
C([0,T];H)
.
This implies that we can obtain weighted samples of the intractable diffusion bridge
X^{\star}
by sampling from the tractable guided process
X^{\circ}
and evaluating the weights given by the Radon–Nikodym derivative (
16
).
The weights contain an intractable multiplicative term
h(0,x_{0})
. This term acts as a proportionality constant that cancels out in a renormalisation step of the weights.

3.3
Remarks on assumptions

Let us briefly comment on the assumptions underlying our main results.
The assumptions in
2.1
are standard, with the exception of the boundedness of
F
in Assumption
2.1
(iv). It is needed for our proof of Theorem
3.4
. However, our numerical illustrations in Example
5.6
indicate that this assumption is stronger than required for the absolute continuity to hold.

Assumption
3.1
essentially states that the covariance matrix
R^{-1}_{T-t}
present in the guiding term
G(t,x)
explodes as the guided process approaches the conditioning time
t\uparrow T
. Moreover, it is crucial that this divergence occurs at a linear rate. This aligns with the intuition gained from the simplest conditioned diffusion, the one-dimensional Brownian bridge, where the equivalent term in the Brownian bridge equation is given by
(T-t)^{-1}
.

In this infinite-dimensional setting, Assumption
3.1
depends through the operators
Q_{t}
on the interplay between the drift
A
and covariance operator
Q
of Equation (
1
), as well as on the observation operator
L
.
As illustrated in Section
5
, this assumption can typically be validated directly in applications of interest. It may fail though for hypo-elliptic diffusions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, such as an integrated diffusion. In that case, the temporal regularity of the sample paths is not the same for all components of the diffusion and therefore the conclusion of Theorem
3.2
won’t hold. We refer to
[
Bierkens et al., 2020
]
for a more detailed discussion.

Assumption
3.3
dictates the functions
h
and
g
to be asymptotically equivalent in the limit
t\uparrow T
. For finite dimensional state spaces, this assumption can be traced back to bounds on the transition densities of
X
and
Z
, using, for example, Aronson’s inequality (cf.
[
Aronson and Oser, 1967
]
). In our setting, such transition densities are not guaranteed to exist, making it necessary to work with
h
and
g
directly instead.

The following lemma gives a sufficient condition for Assumption
3.3
to be satisfied. It is shown in Section
7.2
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let the non-linearity
F
and covariance operator
Q
be such that
\tilde{F}(t,x)=Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}F(t,x)
is well defined with
|\tilde{F}(t,x)|\leq C_{\tilde{F}}
for all
t\in[0,T],x\in H
.
Then there exists a continuous function
\lambda(t)\geq 1
satisfying
\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)=1
such that

\displaystyle\frac{g(t,x)}{\lambda(T-t)}\leq h(t,x)\leq\lambda(T-t)g(t,x),\quad t<T,\>x\in H.

(17)

4
A Metropolis-Hastings algorithm for sampling infinite-dimensional diffusion bridges

We present a short description on how the theoretical result of Theorem
3.4
can be used to simulate the infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge
X^{\star}
.
This can, for example, be achieved through a simple Metropolis-Hastings sampler (MH sampler), using the law
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
of the guided process as a proposal distribution to draw samples
X^{\star}
of
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
by iterating between the following steps:

1.

Draw a sample from the proposal distribution
X^{\circ}\sim\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
.

2.

Accept/reject
X^{\circ}
with acceptance probability based on the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\Phi_{T}(X^{\circ})
.

In practical applications, one has to numerically approximate
X
and
X^{\star}
on some gridded domain
D\times[0,T],D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For such computational implementations, we write
\solve(A,F,Q,W)
for any
numerical SPDE solver
that approximates the mild solution
X
of an SPDE of the form (
1
) on the given grid.
We refer to
[
Lord et al., 2014
]
, Chapter 10, for an introduction to the solvers used in the field. Since we are dealing with diagonalisable operators
A
and
Q
, a natural choice for the SPDE solver is the
spectral Galerkin method
.

The proposals of
X^{\circ}
can be constructed to depend on the current value of the chain based on the
preconditioned Crank-Nicolson (pCN)
scheme (see
[
Neal, 1999
]
,
[
Beskos et al., 2008
]
,
[
Cotter et al., 2013
]
) as follows.
In step
i
, let
Z
denote the process such that for the current value of the chain
X^{\star}_{i}=\solve(A,F+QG^{\circ},Q,Z)
.
The proposal in the
i
-th sampling step then takes on the form:

(i)

Draw a Wiener process
W
, independent of
Z
;

(ii)

Set
Z^{\circ}=\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}Z+\beta W
;

(iii)

Compute
X^{\circ}=\solve(A,F+QG^{\circ},Q,Z^{\circ})
.

Here,
\beta\in(0,1]
denotes a hyperparameter that determines the size of the pCN step. For
\beta=1
, independent proposals of
X^{\circ}
are drawn.

We summarize the complete algorithm in the following.

Algorithm 1

MH Sampler of
X^{\star}

Input:

SPDE Parameters
A,~F,~Q
and
x_{0}
, conditioning point
y\in H
, gridded domain
D\times[0,T]
, iterations
N
, step size
\beta

Output:

Samples
(X^{\star}_{i})_{i=0}^{N}
of
X^{\star}
on
D\times[0,T]

Initialize:
Draw a Wiener process
Z
and set
X^{\star}_{0}=\solve(A,F+QG^{\circ},Q,Z)
;

for

i=0...~N-1

do

Proposal

(i) Draw a Wiener process
W
;

(ii) Set
Z^{\circ}=\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}Z+\beta W
;

(iii) Compute
X^{\circ}=\solve(A,F+QG^{\circ},Q,Z^{\circ})
;

Update

(i) Compute
M=\min\left(1,\dfrac{\Phi_{T}(X^{\circ})}{\Phi_{T}(X^{\star}_{i})}\right)
;

(ii) Draw
U\sim\text{Unif}(0,1)
;

if

U<M

then

Set
X^{\star}_{i+1}=X^{\circ}
and
Z=Z^{\circ}
;

else

Set
X^{\star}_{i+1}=X^{\star}_{i}
.

Remark 4.1
.

Since the results in Theorem
3.4
are infinite-dimensional, the validity of Algorithm
1
depends neither on the choice of the numerical solver nor on the discretisation of the domain
D\times[0,T]
.
In particular, for a practical implementation, both the solver and the mesh size can be freely chosen and the implementation will remain valid if the mesh size tends to zero.
With a trade-off of greater computational costs, a finer mesh size will not only improve the approximation quality of the numerical SPDE solver, but also the approximation of the likelihood ratios
\Phi_{T}(X^{\circ})
.

5
Examples

5.1
Diagonalisable equations

Consider the case that the linear operators
A
and
Q
share an eigenbasis of
H
. Specifically, assume the following.

Assumption 5.1
.

The operators
A
and
Q
are diagonalisable, i.e. there exists an orthonormal basis
(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of
H
and positive sequences
(a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(q_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
a_{j}\to\infty
,
\sup q_{j}<\infty
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}Ae_{j}&=-a_{j}e_{j}\\
Qe_{j}&=q_{j}e_{j}.\end{split}

(18)

Given Assumption
5.1
, Equation (
1
) is commonly referred to as a
diagonalisable SPDE
.
If
F
is a linear operator that commutes with
A
and
Q
, such an equation is referred to as
fully diagonalisable
. In that case, the mild solution
X
is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process that can be represented as an infinite series of decoupled SODEs in its eigenmodes. Note, however, that such a decomposition is no longer valid when
F
is a non-linear operator.

In the diagonalisable setting,
A
generates a strongly continuous semigroup
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
S_{t}e_{j}=\exp(-a_{j}t)e_{j}
and, in particular, satisfies Assumption
2.1
(i). Moreover, Assumption
2.1
(iii) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{q_{j}}{a_{j}}<\infty,

(19)

under which the operators
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are diagonalisable with eigenvalues

\displaystyle\begin{split}q_{j}(t)&=\frac{q_{j}}{2a_{j}}\left[1-\exp(-2a_{j}t)\right].\end{split}

(20)

In the following proposition, we show that, given a diagonalisable SPDE, Assumption
3.1
is satisfied for two common choices of the observation operator
L
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let Assumption
5.1
and Equation (
19
) be satisfied.
Then, Assumption
3.1
holds in either of the following cases:

•

L=P_{k}
is the projection onto the first
k
coordinates with respect to the eigenbasis
(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.

\displaystyle P_{k}x=\left(\langle x,e_{j}\rangle\right)_{j=1}^{k},\quad x\in H.

(21)

•

L=P_{w}
is the projection onto the coordinate of the subspace
\text{span}\{w\}
for some fixed
w\in H
, i.e.

\displaystyle P_{w}x=\langle x,w\rangle,\quad x\in H.

(22)

Proof.

Since
R_{t}=LQ_{t}L^{*}
is symmetric positive definite, to show (
13
) it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda_{\max}(R_{t})&\leq t~\underline{c}^{-1},\\
\lambda_{\min}(R_{t})&\geq t~\bar{c}^{-1},\quad t\in(0,T],\end{split}

(23)

for some constants
\bar{c}>0
and
\underline{c}>0
, with
\lambda_{\min}(R_{t})
and
\lambda_{\max}(R_{t})
denoting the smallest and largest eigenvalue of
R_{t}
.
We will use that, for any
j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{t\in(0,T]}t^{-1}(1-\exp(-2a_{j}t))&=2a_{j}\\
\inf_{t\in(0,T]}t^{-1}(1-\exp(-2a_{j}t))&=T^{-1}(1-\exp(-2a_{j}T)),\end{split}

(24)

which follows from a Taylor expansion at
t=0
.

Consider the first case of
L=P_{k}
. A simple computation shows that
R_{t}
is given by the diagonal
k\times k
-matrix with entries
q_{j}(t),j=1,...,k
. Hence, plugging in
q_{j}(t)
as in (
20
) and (
24
) we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\sup_{t\in(0,T]}t^{-1}q_{j}(t)&=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\sup_{t\in(0,T]}\frac{q_{j}}{2a_{j}}t^{-1}(1-\exp(-2a_{j}t))\\
&=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}q_{j}=:\underline{c}^{-1}\end{split}

as well as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{1\leq j\leq k}\inf_{t\in(0,T]}t^{-1}q_{j}(t)&=\min_{1\leq j\leq k}\inf_{t\in(0,T]}\frac{q_{j}}{2a_{j}}t^{-1}(1-\exp(-2a_{j}t))\\
&=\min_{1\leq j\leq k}\frac{q_{j}}{2a_{j}}T^{-1}(1-\exp(-2a_{j}T))=:\bar{c}^{-1}.\end{split}

This shows (
23
) in the first case. For the second case, note that
P_{w}^{*}
is the mapping
y\mapsto yw\in H
for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
. From this, it follows that

\displaystyle R_{t}y=(P_{w}Q_{t}P_{w}^{*})y=(LQ_{t})(yw)=\langle Q_{t}w,w\rangle y

and hence
R_{t}=\langle Q_{t}w,w\rangle
. Plugging in
q_{j}(t)
as given in (
20
), it holds

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T]}t^{-1}R_{t}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in(0,T]}t^{-1}\langle Q_{t}w,w\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{q_{j}}{2a_{j}}\left(\sup_{t\in(0,T]}t^{-1}\left(1-\exp(-2a_{j}t)\right)\right)|\langle w,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}q_{j}|\langle w,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w,Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\rangle=:\underline{c}^{-1}.

Here, we used (
24
) in the third step and the fact that
Q
is positive in the last step.
This shows the first inequality in (
23
). The second inequality follows from the fact that

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in(0,T]}t^{-1}R_{t}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{q_{j}}{2a_{j}}\left(\inf_{t\in(0,T]}t^{-1}\left(1-\exp(-2a_{j}t)\right)\right)|\langle w,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{q_{j}}{2a_{j}}T^{-1}\left(1-\exp(-2a_{j}T)\right)|\langle w,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}=:\bar{c}^{-1},

where we again used (
24
) in the second step and (
19
) in the last step to justify the convergence of the sum. This shows (
23
) and thus finishes the proof.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

Consider the special case that
H=L^{2}(D)
for some bounded domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In that case, the observation operator

\displaystyle P_{w}x=\langle x,w\rangle=\int_{D}x(\xi)w(\xi)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi

of Proposition
5.2
can be understood as observing a weighted average
P_{w}X_{T}
of
X_{T}
. Depending on the choice of
w\in L^{2}(D)
, this can, for example, correspond to observing a global or local average of
X_{T}
.
Moreover, it is clear that the second case in Proposition
5.2
extends to the case that one observes a set of weighted averages
\int_{D}x(\xi)w_{j}(\xi)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi
for functions
w_{1},...,w_{k}\in L^{2}(D)
.

Example 5.4

(Stochastic reaction-diffusion equations)
.

Consider a stochastic reaction-diffusion equation, formally written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}X(t,\xi)&=\partial^{2}_{\xi}X(t,\xi)+f(X(t,\xi))+\dot{w}(t,\xi),\quad t,\xi\in[0,T]\times[0,\pi],\end{split}

(25)

with initial condition
X(0,\xi)=x_{0}(\xi)
and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
X(t,0)=X(t,\pi)=0
for all
t,\xi\in[0,T]\times[0,\pi]
.
Here,
f
is a continuous, scalar-valued function of at most linear growth and
\dot{w}
is a Gaussian noise term that is white in time and possibly spatially dependent.

It is well known that (
25
) can be stated as a semilinear SPDE of the form (
1
), where
A
denotes the Dirichlet Laplace operator
A=\partial^{2}_{\xi}
on
H=L^{2}([0,\pi])
with domain
D(A)=H^{2}([0,\pi])\cap H^{1}_{0}([0,\pi])
. Here,
H^{2}([0,\pi])
is the Sobolev space
W^{2,2}([0,\pi])
and
H^{1}_{0}
denotes the closure of the smooth and compactly supported test functions in
W^{1,2}([0,\pi])
.
Furthermore,
F
is the nonlinearity given by the Nemytskii operator
F(X_{t})(\xi)=f(X(t,\xi))
generated by
f
and
Q
is a symmetric, positive definite operator on
H
.

The Dirichlet Laplace operator admits an eigenbasis
e_{j}(\xi)=\sin(j\xi)
with eigenvalues
-a_{j}=-j^{2},j\geq 1
on
H
.
Hence, Assumption
5.1
and Equation (
19
) are satisfied for any operator
Q
on
H
that is diagonalisable with respect to
(e_{j})_{j}
with eigenvalues
q_{j}\sim j^{-r}
for some
r\geq 0.

5.2
Stochastic Amari equation

Assume the stochastic Amari equation, formally given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}X(t,\xi)&=-X(t,\xi)+\int_{[0,\pi]}f(\xi,\xi^{\prime})s(X(t,\xi^{\prime})-\theta)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime}+\dot{w}(t,\xi),\quad t,\xi\in[0,T]\times[0,\pi],\end{split}

(26)

with initial condition
X(0,\xi)=x_{0}(\xi)
.
Here,
X(t,\xi)
models the activity of a neural field at time
t
and location
\xi
. The mapping
f
represents a spatial connectivity function on
[0,\pi]
, whereas
s
is an activation function with threshold
\theta>0
.
The term
-X(t,\xi)
is a linear damping term that models relaxation of neural activity.

For more details on the Amari equation and neural field models we refer to the recent survey in
[
Cook et al., 2022
]
and to
[
Faugeras and Inglis, 2015
]
for a mathematical view on the field.

The Amari equation can be formulated as a semilinear SPDE of the form (
1
) on
H=L^{2}([0,\pi])
with
AX=-X
and
F(X)(\xi)=\int_{[0,\pi]}f(\xi,\xi^{\prime})s(X(\xi^{\prime})-\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime}
. Typically,
f
is chosen to be continuous and
s
to be Lipschitz continuous, in which case
F
is itself a bounded and Lipschitz continuous mapping on
H
.
Since the semigroup generated by
A
is no longer smoothing, one requires the noise operator
Q
to be trace-class.
In that case, Assumption
2.1
is met. Moreover, it follows just as in the proof of Proposition
5.2
, that Assumption
3.1
is satisfied for the observation operators
L=P_{k}
and
L=P_{w}
.

We apply Lemma
3.6
to show that Assumption
3.3
is satisfied for common choices of the spatial connectivity function
f
.
One such typical choice is given by a difference of Gaussian kernels

\displaystyle f(\xi,\xi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=A_{1}\exp\left(-\dfrac{|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)-A_{2}\exp\left(-\dfrac{|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right)

for some positive parameters
A_{1},A_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
.
Consider the associated Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator
T_{f}
defined by

\displaystyle(T_{f}u)(\xi)=\int_{[0,\pi]}f(\xi,\xi^{\prime})u(\xi^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime},\quad u\in H.

By Mercer’s theorem, it follows that
T_{f}
admits an eigenbasis
(e_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of
H
and it is a well-known result that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
of
T_{f}
are of exponential decay
\lambda_{j}\sim\exp(-j^{2})
.
Hence, representing the non-linearity
F
as
F(X)=T_{f}s_{\theta}(X)
, where we write with slight abuse of notation
s_{\theta}
for the Nemytskii operator
s_{\theta}(X)(\xi)=s(X(\xi)-\theta)
, it follows that for any trace-class operator
Q
with eigenvalues
q_{j}\sim j^{-r},r>1
, we have that
Q^{-1/2}F=Q^{-1/2}T_{f}s_{\theta}
is well-defined and bounded.

Proof of Theorem
3.2

To ease readability of the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we write
\Delta_{t}=T-t
for any
t<T
.
The structure of the proof follows that of the proof of Proposition 2.2 in
[
Bierkens et al., 2020
]
.
It can be outlined as follows:

1.

Given
Y_{t}=y-L_{\Delta_{t}}X^{\circ}_{t}
, define the Lyapunov function
V(t,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\langle R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}Y_{t},Y_{t}\rangle.

2.

Apply Itô’s lemma on
V(t,Y)
.

3.

Bound the terms appearing in
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V(t,Y)
.

4.

Apply a Gronwall-type inequality.

Steps 1 and 2 are covered in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
Y_{t}=y-L_{\Delta_{t}}X^{\circ}_{t}
and let
V(t,Y_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\langle R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}Y_{t},Y_{t}\rangle.

Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V(t,Y_{t})&=-\langle L^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},F(t,X^{\circ}_{t})\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}L^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t}\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2}\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}L_{\Delta_{t}}^{*}R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},Q^{\frac{1}{2}}L_{\Delta_{t}}^{*}R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\dfrac{1}{4}\tr\left[R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}L_{\Delta_{t}}QL^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\quad\mathbb{P}^{\circ}\text{-a.s.},\end{split}

(35)

where
\tilde{W}
is the cylindrical Wiener process
\tilde{W}_{t}=-W^{\circ}_{t}
.

Proof.

Since
X^{\circ}
is a mild solution to the SPDE (
9
), it satisfies

\displaystyle X^{\circ}_{t}=S_{t}x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}S_{t-s}\left[F(s,X^{\circ}_{s})+QG(s,X^{\circ}_{s})\right]\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}S_{t-s}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{\circ}_{s},\quad\mathbb{P}^{\circ}\text{-a.s.}

Hence, using the semigroup property
S_{T-t}S_{t-s}=S_{T-s}
, it follows that

\displaystyle L_{\Delta_{t}}X^{\circ}_{t}

\displaystyle=LS_{T-t}X^{\circ}_{t}

\displaystyle=L\left(S_{T}x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}S_{T-s}\left[F(s,X^{\circ}_{s})+QG(s,X^{\circ}_{s})\right]\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}S_{T-s}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{\circ}_{s}\right).

Setting
\tilde{W}_{t}:=-W^{\circ}_{t}
, it thus holds

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=-L_{\Delta_{t}}\left[F(t,X^{\circ}_{t})+QG(t,X^{\circ}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+L_{\Delta_{t}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t}.

(36)

Next, by definition,
Q_{t}
satisfies
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q_{t}=S_{t}QS^{*}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
.
Hence, for
R_{t}=LQ_{t}L^{*}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R_{t}=L\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q_{t}L^{*}=LS_{t}QS^{*}_{t}L^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=L_{t}QL_{t}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

which gives that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}&=-R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}\left(\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}R_{\Delta_{t}}\right)R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}=R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}L_{\Delta_{t}}QL_{\Delta_{t}}^{*}R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}.\end{split}

(37)

Plugging in (
36
) and (
37
), we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\left(R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t}\right)&=\left(\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}\right)Y_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{t}\\
&=R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}L_{\Delta_{t}}QL_{\Delta_{t}}^{*}R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}Y_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad-R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}L_{\Delta_{t}}\left[F(t,X^{\circ}_{t})+QG(t,X^{\circ}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}L_{\Delta_{t}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t}\\
&=-R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}L_{\Delta_{t}}F(t,X^{\circ}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}L_{\Delta_{t}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t},\end{split}

(38)

where in the last step we use that
QG(t,X^{\circ}_{t})=QL^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}Y_{t}
.

Finally, it follows from Itô’s lemma in the second line, plugging in (
36
) and (
38
) in the third line and again using
QG(t,X^{\circ}_{t})=QL^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}Y_{t}
in the last equality that

\displaystyle\begin{split}2\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V(t,Y_{t})&=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\langle R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},Y_{t}\rangle\\
&=\langle R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{t}\rangle+\langle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\left(R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t}\right),Y_{t}\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\tr\left[R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}L_{\Delta_{t}}QL^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&=-\langle R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},L_{\Delta_{t}}\left[F(t,X^{\circ}_{t})+QG(t,X^{\circ}_{t})\right]\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\langle R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},L_{\Delta_{t}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t}\rangle\\
&\quad-\langle R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}L_{\Delta_{t}}F(t,X^{\circ}_{t}),Y_{t}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\langle R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}L_{\Delta_{t}}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t},Y_{t}\rangle\\
&\quad+\dfrac{1}{2}\tr\left[R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}L_{\Delta_{t}}QL^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&=-2\langle L^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},F(t,X^{\circ}_{t})\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+2\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}L^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{t}\rangle-\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}L_{\Delta_{t}}^{*}R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},Q^{\frac{1}{2}}L_{\Delta_{t}}^{*}R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\dfrac{1}{2}\tr\left[R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}L_{\Delta_{t}}QL^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(39)

∎

We proceed with the more technical steps 3 and 4.

Proof.

(of Theorem
3.2
)
We aim to bound all terms appearing on the right hand side of (
35
), starting with the stochastic integral term.
For this, fix some
t_{0}\in[0,T)
and define

\displaystyle M_{t}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}L^{*}_{\Delta_{s}}R_{\Delta_{s}}^{-1}Y_{s},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{s}\rangle,\quad t\in[t_{0},T).

Note that
M_{t}
is a square-integrable martingale with quadratic variation

\displaystyle\left[M\right]_{t}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}L_{\Delta_{s}}^{*}R_{\Delta_{s}}^{-1}Y_{s},Q^{\frac{1}{2}}L_{\Delta_{s}}^{*}R_{\Delta_{s}}^{-1}Y_{s}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

and in particular
M_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\left[M\right]_{t}
is a martingale that we can bound by an exponential martingale inequality as follows;

For any sequence
(\gamma_{k})_{k}
of positive numbers and
t_{k}=T-1/k,~k\in\mathbb{N}
, define the events

\displaystyle E_{k}=\left\{\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{k+1}}\left(M_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\left[M\right]_{t}\right)>\gamma_{k}\right\}.

An application of
[
Mao, 2007
]
, Theorem 1.7.4, then shows that
\mathbb{P}(E_{k})\leq\exp(-\gamma_{k})
.

Now, let
(\gamma_{k})_{k}
be an arbitrary but fixed sequence that satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp(-\gamma_{k})<\infty.

(40)

It then follows that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(E_{k})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp(-\gamma_{k})<\infty
, and thus, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, that
\mathbb{P}(\limsup_{k}E_{k})=0
.
Hence, for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
\omega
there exists some
k_{0}(\omega)
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}(\omega)

\displaystyle\sup_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{k+1}}\left(M_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\left[M\right]_{t}\right)\leq\gamma_{k}.

(41)

The remaining terms in (
35
) are easily bounded as follows.
From Assumption
2.1
, it follows that there exists some
c>0
such that
\sup_{x\in H,t\in[0,T]}|L_{\Delta_{t}}F(t,x)|\leq c
.
Hence, with
\|R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}\|\leq\bar{c}\Delta_{t}^{-1}
as given in Assumption
3.1
, it holds on
[0,T)
that

\displaystyle\left|\langle L^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},F(t,X^{\circ}_{t})\rangle\right|\leq c~\bar{c}~\Delta_{t}^{-1}|Y_{t}|.

Moreover, by Lemma
6.2
, we have
\tr[L_{t}QL_{t}^{*}]\leq\bar{c}^{\prime}
for any
t\in[0,T]
and thus

\displaystyle\left|\tr\left[R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}L_{\Delta_{t}}QL^{*}_{\Delta_{t}}\right]\right|\leq\bar{c}\bar{c}^{\prime}\Delta_{t}^{-1}.

In total, we get for any
t\in[t_{0},t_{k+1}]
that

\displaystyle\begin{split}V(t,Y_{t})&=V(t_{0},Y_{t_{0}})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V(s,Y_{s})\\
&\leq V(t_{0},Y_{t_{0}})+\gamma_{k}+c~\bar{c}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Delta_{s}^{-1}\left|Y_{s}\right|\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad+\frac{\bar{c}\bar{c}^{\prime}}{4}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Delta_{s}^{-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

(42)

Now define
\xi_{t}=\Delta_{t}^{-1}|Y_{t}|^{2}
. With
\|R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}\|\geq\underline{c}~\Delta_{t}^{-1}
as given in Assumption
3.1
and the symmetry of
R^{-1}_{\Delta_{t}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle V(t,Y_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\langle R_{\Delta_{t}}^{-1}Y_{t},Y_{t}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\underline{c}~\Delta_{t}^{-1}|Y_{t}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\underline{c}~\xi_{t}.

Hence, the inequality in (
42
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\leq M_{0}+\frac{2}{\underline{c}}\gamma_{k}+M_{1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{\xi_{s}}\Delta_{s}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+M_{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Delta_{s}^{-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

8
Discussion and future work

In this article, we introduced novel methodology to sample from the infinite-dimensional diffusion bridge
X^{\star}
, defined as the mild solution to a stochastic partial differential equation conditioned on a finite-dimensional, linear transformation
y=LX_{T}
of the state
X_{T}
for some
T>0
.

For this, we introduced the guided process
X^{\circ}
that mimics the dynamics of the original process while being forced to the conditioning state
y
. The key aspect of our results is the absolute continuity of its law
\mathcal{L}^{\circ}
with respect to the intractable law
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
of the diffusion bridge. This enabled the definition of Algorithm
1
, a Metropolis-Hastings sampler targeting
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
whose performance was illustrated in numerical examples for stochastic reaction-diffusion equations.

Fully observed states

One problem that we have not addressed in this article is the case that
L
equals the identity operator, i.e. when one ‘fully’ observes the state
y=X_{T}
instead of a finite-dimensional transformation thereof.
Several challenges arise in this scenario.
Firstly, the natural candidate for the
h
-function is given by
h(t,x)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log p(t,x;T,y)
, where
p
is the transition density of
X
with respect to some suitable reference measure
\nu
on
(H,\mathcal{B}(H))
. The existence of such transition densities is not necessarily given when
H
is infinite-dimensional. In some cases, a suitable Gaussian reference measure
\nu
can be constructed as the invariant Ornstein-Uhlenbeck measure. However, this imposes additional assumptions on
A
and
Q
.

The more difficult challenge is to show that the guided process
X^{\circ}
converges to the conditioning state as
t\uparrow T
, similar to our result of Theorem
3.2
. Heuristically speaking, this proves difficult to show as the convergence must occur at the same rate, simultaneously in all infinite coordinates of
X^{\circ}
.
In Theorem
3.2
, it is the observation operator
L
that maps
y
and
X^{\circ}
to a finite dimensional subspace, therefore decreasing the degrees of freedom in the components of
LX^{\circ}
, that allows us to prove convergence at a suitable rate.

While the case of observing a full state
X_{T}
is certainly of mathematical interest, let us remark that the assumption of observing finite dimensional transformations thereof certainly appears to be of more practical relevance.

Multiple observations

A natural extension of our work is to consider partial observations
L_{i}X_{t_{i}}
of
X
at multiple observation times
t_{i}
, possibly corrupted by some observation noise
\eta_{i}
.
The task of state estimation then branches into the two well-known problems of
filtering
and
smoothing
.
For finite-dimensional state space models, many state-of-the-art solutions to the filtering and smoothing problems are
particle-based
solutions, in which states of the latent process
X
are estimated by a set of weighted samples. Without going into too much detail, we refer to
[
Chopin et al., 2020
]
for an introduction into the rich literature in this field.

A common challenge in particle-based inference is to construct importance sampling distributions for the unobserved latent path that are tractable, mimic the behavior of the dynamical system and are informed by the data.
We hypothesize that the path measure of the guided process derived in this paper serves as a good starting point into the construction of such distributions for partially observed stochastic PDEs. We will investigate this further in future research.

.
For this, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}^{LX_{T}}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\right]\mathbbm{1}_{\{LX_{T}\in\mathcal{A}\}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\{LX_{T}\in\mathcal{A}\}}\right]

(57)

for any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Indeed we have for any
t\in[0,T)
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}^{LX_{T}}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\right]\mathbbm{1}_{\{LX_{T}\in\mathcal{A}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}^{y}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\right]\rho_{X}(0,x_{0};T,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\frac{\rho_{X}(t,X_{t};T,y)}{\rho_{X}(0,x_{0};T,y)}\right]\rho_{X}(0,x_{0};T,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\int_{\mathcal{A}}\rho_{X}(t,X_{t};T,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{LX_{T}\in\mathcal{A}\}}\mid X_{t}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\{LX_{T}\in\mathcal{A}\}}\right].

Here we use the definition of
\rho_{X}(0,x_{0};T,y)
in the first line, the definition of
\mathbb{E}^{y}\left[\varphi(X_{t})\right]
in the second line, Fubini’s theorem in the third step and the law of total expectation in the last step.
The claim now follows from a standard cylindrical argument, see e.g.
[
Ethier and Kurtz, 2009
]
, Proposition 4.1.6.
∎

Proposition A.2

(Proposition
2.6
above.)
.

The mapping
g
defined in (
7
) satisfies Assumptions
(i)
to
(iii)
of Theorem
2.2
with
Kg(t,x)=\langle F(t,x),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}g(t,x)\rangle.

In particular, there exists a unique measure
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\circ}_{t}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{g(t,X_{t})}{g(0,x_{0})}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\langle F(s,X_{s}),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(s,X_{s})\rangle\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{t},\quad t<T,

such that

under

is the unique mild solution to the SPDE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{\circ}_{t}=\left[AX^{\circ}_{t}+F(t,X^{\circ}_{t})+Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(t,X^{\circ}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{\circ}_{t},\quad t\in[0,T),

(58)

where
(W^{\circ}_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
is a
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
-cylindrical Wiener process.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
g
satisfies Assumption (iii) of Theorem
2.2
.
By definition of
g
we have

\displaystyle g(t,x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{k}\det(R_{T-t})}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}|R_{T-t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(y-L_{T-t}x)|^{2}\right),

from which it follows that
g
is continuous and bounded on
[0,S]
for any
S<T
.
Moreover,
g
is Fréchet differentiable in
x
with bounded derivative

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}g(t,x)=g(t,x)\left(L^{*}_{T-t}R^{-1}_{T-t}(y-L_{T-t}x)\right).

This proves Assumption (iii). To show the first assumption, denote by
\tilde{K}
the infinitesimal generator of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, defined as in (
5
) by substituting
T
with the transition semigroup of
Z
.
By the Fréchet differentiability of

, it follows from
[

]
, Theorem 4.1, that

\displaystyle(Kg)(t,x)=\tilde{K}g(t,x)+\bigl\langle F(t,x),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}g(t,x)\bigr\rangle.

Reiterating the arguments in the proof of Proposition
2.4
gives that
\tilde{K}g=0
. Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle(g^{-1}Kg)(t,x)=\bigl\langle F(t,x),\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(t,x)\bigr\rangle,

which shows Assumption (i) and the existence of the continuous local martingale

Appendix C

We provide additional numerical evidence that the results in Theorem
3.4
hold true in the setting of Example
5.6
, even when Assumption
2.1
(iv) and the condition of Lemma
3.6
are not met.
Assume that Equation (
16
) holds true. It then follows that

\displaystyle\rho_{X}(0,x_{0};T,y)=\rho_{Z}(0,x_{0};T,y)\mathbb{E}^{\circ}[\Psi_{T}(X^{\circ,y})].

(59)

Here, to underline the dependence on
y
, we add a superscript
y
to the guided process
X^{\circ}
with end state
LX^{\circ}_{T}=y
.
Furthermore, we denote the corresponding guiding term
G^{\circ}
of
X^{\circ,y}
by
G^{y}
.

Fix
x_{0},T
and denote by
\pi(y)=\rho_{X}(0,x_{0};T,y)
the density of
LX_{T}\mid X_{0}=x_{0}
.
It then follows from (
59
) that, for samples
\mathbb{X}^{\circ,y}=\{X_{i}^{\circ,y},i=1,...,n\}
, the estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(y,\mathbb{X}^{\circ,y})=\rho_{Z}(0,x_{0};T,y)\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{T}(X^{\circ,y}_{i})

(60)

is an unbiased estimator of the otherwise intractable density
\pi(y)
. This allows us to numerically sample from the distribution of
LX_{T}
in two ways:

1.

By approximating the solution to the Allen-Cahn equation (
33
) by a numerical SPDE solver and evaluating
LX_{T}
.

2.

By sampling from
\mathcal{L}(LX_{T})
via an MCMC scheme based on the unbiased estimator
\hat{\pi}(y,\mathbb{X}^{\circ})
.

For the numerical solver we use a spectral Galerkin approximation and a semi-implicit Euler-Maruyama scheme to approximate the resulting SODE.
For the second option we implement the correlated pseudomarginal (CPM) sampler as introduced in
[
Deligiannidis et al., 2018
]
. The proposals in
y
are simple random walk proposals. For the proposals in the latent variable
X^{\circ}
we use the same pCN scheme as introduced in Algorithm
1
.
The complete algorithm is outlined in Algorithm
2
.
