Effects of ambient PM2.5 and its components on the prognosis of colorectal cancer survivors: disease progression and potential mediation pathways
Authors: Qing Qin, Wenjing Wu, Chenxi Yin, Ziyue Li, Xiaozhou Yu, Huanle Cai, Xin Xu, Xian Lin, Xiaoru Wu, Sizhe Li, Zhupei Yuan, Qiao Hu, Ying Wang, Liangping Xia, Wangjian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence continues to support the role of air pollution as a contributor to adverse outcomes in colorectal cancer (CRC); however, its effects on the mortality and disease progression transitions in CRC survivors, and the mediation pathways, remain uncertain.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study enrolled 3,357 patients diagnosed with non-metastatic colorectal cancer who underwent curative surgery at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center between 2012 and 2017. The annual average concentrations of PM2.5 and its components were derived from the ChinaHighAirPollutants (CHAP) dataset and matched to participants based on their residential addresses. The associations between PM2.5 and its components with CRC mortality were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models. To delineate risks at different disease progression transitions, we employed multi-state models. Mediation analysis was conducted to elucidate the role of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress biomarkers in the exposure-outcome pathway.ResultsAn increase in PM2.5 exposure per IQR was linked to a 35% elevated risk of CRC mortality (HR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.18–1.54). Among the specific components analyzed, ammonium (NH₄⁺; HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.13–2.01) and sulfate (SO₄²⁻; HR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.20–2.34) demonstrated significant positive associations. Stratified analyses suggested stronger associations among older patients, males, smokers, those with later-stage disease, and patients with a family history of malignancy. In multi-state models, PM2.5 exposure was consistently related to all three disease progression transitions, with HRs of 1.14 (95% CI: 1.04–1.26) for diagnosis → DFS event, 1.99 (95% CI: 1.71–2.32) for DFS event → CRC death, and 1.58 (95% CI: 1.30–1.92) for diagnosis → CRC death. Mediation analysis identified multiple inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers with significant indirect effects, with proportions mediated ranging from 1.7% to 8.9%. The strongest mediators were MLR (8.9%), HDL (6.6%), and PLR (3.0%).ConclusionsLong-term exposure to PM2.5 was associated with poorer CRC prognosis and more rapid disease progression, partly via systemic inflammation and oxidative stress. These findings support precise air quality policies and integrated survivorship strategies targeting pollution-related mortality in cancer survivors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04939-0.Keywords: PM2.5, Chemical components, Colorectal cancer, Survival, Multi-state model

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) stands as a major global health burden. It was the third most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide in 2022, accounting for approximately 1.9 million new cases and 904,000 fatalities [1]. In China, the disease posed a similarly severe threat, ranking second in incidence (517,000 new cases) and fourth in mortality (240,000 deaths) [2]. For non-metastatic CRC patients, curative-intent surgery offers the primary chance of cure; nonetheless, recurrence or metastasis—defined as a disease-free survival (DFS) event—occurs in a substantial number of patients and often culminates in death.

In addition to established risk factors (e.g., advanced age, male gender, unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, poor diet, and genetic predisposition) [3–6], environmental pollution, particularly air pollution, has been increasingly linked to the burden of colorectal cancer (CRC) [7, 8]. PM2.5 (fine particulate with a diameter ≤ 2.5 μm) is a pervasive air pollutant. Its classification as a Group 1 human carcinogen by the IARC in 2013 solidifies the significance of air quality as a key factor in cancer etiology and outcomes [9]. Existing studies suggest that PM2.5 may reach the gut via systemic circulation of ultrafine particles or through mucociliary clearance and the swallowing of larger particles [10, 11], where it can promote carcinogenesis by inducing inflammation, oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and epigenetic alterations [12–20]. These mechanisms may also contribute to tumor recurrence and progression, thereby adversely influencing the prognosis of patients diagnosed with cancer. Indeed, emerging evidence has linked air pollution to poorer survival in patients with cancers other than CRC [21, 22]. However, in the context of colorectal cancer (CRC), epidemiological investigations have predominantly focused on risk in general populations [8, 23–25], while studies examining its impact on survival among existing patients remain scarce. To date, only a limited number of studies have directly examined this relationship. Jiang et al., using UK Biobank data, reported that higher air pollutant exposure was associated with poorer survival among patients with established CRC (HR = 1.13; 95% CI: 1.03–1.23) [20], and a recent multicenter cohort study further demonstrated that elevated PM2.5 exposures were significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality in CRC patients, with stronger associations observed in early and stage III disease [26].

Beyond this scarcity of prognostic evidence, additional knowledge gaps persist. First, prior work has largely centered on total PM2.5 mass, leaving the contributions of specific chemical components uncertain. Yet, individual components may have distinct biological implications and toxicities through heterogeneous mechanisms. Understanding this variation is crucial for elucidating the underlying pathways. Second, the clinical course of non-metastatic CRC involves distinct disease transitions, including recurrence or metastasis after curative surgery and eventual death; whether PM2.5 affects these transition-specific risks differentially remains unknown. Finally, experimental studies have implicated mechanisms like systemic inflammation and oxidative stress; however, direct evidence from epidemiological studies validating these pathways in human populations is still lacking.

The increasing burden of CRC motivates the need to clarify the role of long-term PM2.5 exposure on patient survival. Our study was therefore designed to examine the association between PM2.5 exposure and survival in non-metastatic CRC, pinpoint vulnerable subgroups, characterize risk patterns across disease progression transitions, and elucidate underlying biological mediators.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, one of the largest cancer hospitals in China, with a high patient volume and recognized clinical expertise. We initially identified 8,744 patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer between 2012 and 2017. Patients were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) non-Chinese nationality (n = 10) (2) distant metastasis at initial diagnosis (n = 2,981), given its substantially different prognosis and treatment approach; (3) absence of surgical resection record (n = 2,339), as surgery is the cornerstone of treatment for non-metastatic CRC. For all patients included in our cohort, the interval between pathological diagnosis and curative surgical resection was typically short, generally less than one month. Patients who died within one year after diagnosis (n = 57) were also excluded, as the exposure window would be too short to meaningfully contribute to long-term survival outcomes. After applying these exclusion criteria, a total of 3,357 eligible patients were included in the final analysis. A detailed flow diagram of patient selection is provided in Additional file 1: Figure S1. Baseline characteristics of included and excluded patients are presented in Additional file 1: Table S1. Baseline data were systematically collected and retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system upon initial diagnosis. Demographic information (gender, age at diagnosis, ethnicity) was extracted from patients’ identification documents during admission. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from routine physical examinations by trained nurses. Information on comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, diabetes), lifestyle factors (smoking status and alcohol consumption, dichotomized as yes/no), and family history of malignancy was self-reported through standardized patient interviews conducted by specialist physicians. Disease-related characteristics—including histological type, histological grade, TNM stage (according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer [AJCC] 8th edition), Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score, treatments (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy), and gene mutation status—were documented by specialist physicians based on a comprehensive review of medical records, pathological reports, and treatment records. All data underwent final review and validation by the specialist physicians to ensure completeness and accuracy. Patient follow-up extended from the date of pathological diagnosis until either a CRC-caused death or the study cutoff date (December 31, 2024), with approval granted by the Institutional Review Board of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the study.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was CRC-caused death, ascertained through hospital electronic medical records and classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10: C18–C20). Vital status was further confirmed by telephone follow-up when necessary. In addition, we defined disease-free survival (DFS) as the duration from initial diagnosis to recurrence or distant metastasis, whichever occurred first, based on review of electronic medical records.

Evaluation of air pollution and relevant environmental exposures

The exposures of interest were total PM2.5 mass and its chemical components, including chloride (Cl⁻), ammonium (NH₄⁺), nitrate (NO₃⁻), and sulfate (SO₄²⁻). All exposures were derived from the ChinaHighAirPollutants (CHAP) database, which provides nationwide, gap-free estimates at a 1 km spatial resolution using an artificial intelligence–driven modeling framework that integrates gap-filled satellite Multi-Angle Implementation of Atmospheric Correction (MAIAC) Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) retrievals, ground monitoring networks, atmospheric reanalysis, and emission inventories. Independent validation demonstrated high predictive accuracy for PM2.5 (cross-validation R² = 0.92, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) = 10.76 µg/m³), which was further confirmed for its major components (R²: 0.66–0.75; RMSE: 2.3–6.6 µg/m³) [27], collectively supporting the reliability of the CHAP model for environmental health research [28, 29].

For each participant, exposures were defined as the 3-year average concentrations of PM2.5 preceding CRC-caused death or end of follow-up, which is the most commonly adopted exposure window in previous studies [30, 31], estimated based on geocoded baseline residential addresses, and standardized by their interquartile range (IQR) to facilitate comparability. To control for potential co-pollutant confounding, we obtained data on additional air pollutants, such as ozone (O₃), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), and carbon monoxide (CO), from the CHAP program. Environmental covariates, including the annual mean temperature and relative humidity, were acquired from the National Earth System Science Data Center (http://www.geodata.cn).

Measurement of mediator biomarkers

A panel of ten biomarkers, comprising indicators of oxidative stress (gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT], uric acid [UA], high-density lipoprotein [HDL], and their ratio uric acid to HDL [UHR]) and systemic inflammation (neutrophil-to-lymphocyte [NLR], monocyte-to-lymphocyte [MLR], neutrophil–monocyte-to-lymphocyte [NMLR], platelet-to-lymphocyte [PLR], systemic inflammation response index [SIRI], and systemic immune-inflammation index [SII]), was investigated as candidate mediators, selected for their established roles in reflecting oxidative stress and systemic inflammation [32–36]. SIRI was calculated as (neutrophils × monocytes) / lymphocytes, and SII as (platelets × neutrophils) / lymphocytes. All biomarker data were retrospectively extracted from routine clinical laboratory tests performed in the hospital. Blood samples were collected from patients in a fasting state (typically 7–9 AM) via venipuncture. Whole blood was used for routine blood counts (neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, platelets) measured by Sysmex XN-9000 (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). For biochemical markers (GGT, UA, HDL), serum was obtained after centrifugation and analyzed using Roche Cobas 8000 (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Samples were typically stored at 4 °C in the hospital’s certified clinical laboratory, which maintains stringent internal and external quality control programs. For this study, biomarker values were represented by the average of each patient’s three most recent measurements. Given the routine three-month follow-up schedule, these measurements were all performed within the final year prior to the outcome event (CRC-caused death or end of follow-up).

Statistical analysis

Cox proportional hazards model for air pollution and CRC mortality

We used Cox proportional hazards models to investigate the relationships between long-term exposure to PM2.5 and, separately, its chemical components with CRC mortality. Guided by previous literature on the health effects of air pollution [21, 22, 25, 26, 31] and clinical considerations specific to CRC prognosis, we fitted three sequential models to progressively account for potential confounders. Model 1 (minimally adjusted) adjusted for basic demographic characteristics, including gender, age, and ethnicity. Model 2 (partially adjusted) further incorporated lifestyle and clinical factors, namely body mass index, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, smoking status, alcohol consumption, TNM stage, and family history of malignancy. Model 3 (fully adjusted) additionally accounted for meteorological conditions (annual mean temperature and relative humidity) and co-pollutants (O₃, SO₂, NO₂, and CO). As for each specific component, we additionally included the residual mass (total PM2.5 minus the component of interest) to isolate the effect of individual components and control for confounding by the remaining mixture. We calculated the variance inflation factors (VIFs) for each pollutant, and all VIF values ranged from 1.73 to 3.85, indicating no significant multicollinearity.

Stratified analysis

Stratified analyses were conducted to investigate whether the associations of PM2.5 and its components with CRC mortality were modified by pre-specified factors. The selection of these potential effect modifiers was based on prior epidemiologic studies [21, 22, 25, 26, 31], as well as established prognostic factors for colorectal cancer. Accordingly, we examined gender, age group, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI category, family history of malignancy, and disease stage (TNM I–II vs. III). These analyses were exploratory in nature, aimed at generating hypotheses rather than drawing definitive conclusions. To assess potential effect modification, we applied likelihood ratio tests by comparing models with and without the interaction terms and reported the corresponding P-values for interaction. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons.

Multi-State survival model for risks across disease progression transitions

To capture the differential associations of PM2.5 and its components on distinct transitions during CRC progression, we employed multi-state Cox models under the Markov assumption using the mstate package [37, 38]. Unlike conventional Cox models that estimate a single hazard for the terminal event, the multi-state framework simultaneously estimates transition-specific risks, offering a more comprehensive understanding of how exposures influence different stages of disease progression. This approach is particularly advantageous for disentangling direct mortality risks from intermediate events while accommodating competing progression pathways.

In our analysis, three transitions were defined: (1) from diagnosis to a disease-free survival (DFS) event, (2) from DFS event to CRC-caused death, and (3) from diagnosis directly to CRC-caused death (Fig. 1). We constructed a multi-state Cox model with adjustment for covariates consistent with those used in the fully-adjusted Cox analyses. The proportional hazards assumption was verified for all covariates across all primary Cox models and for each of the three transitions in the multi-state analysis separately using Schoenfeld residual tests, and no violations were detected (all P > 0.05).

Mediation analysis

Because significant positive associations with CRC mortality were observed only for total PM2.5, ammonium (NH₄⁺), and sulfate (SO₄²⁻) in the main analyses, mediation analyses were conducted for these three exposures. Ten a priori–defined biomarkers representing oxidative stress and systemic inflammation were considered as candidate mediators; all biomarkers were analyzed as continuous variables.

Mediation analysis was implemented using the CMAverse package in R [39], which estimates natural direct and indirect effects under a counterfactual framework and is suitable for time‑to‑event outcomes. For each exposure–mediator pair, we specified a linear regression model for the mediator and a Cox proportional hazards model for CRC mortality as the outcome. Both models adjusted for the same set of covariates as in the fully adjusted Cox models. Exposures were modeled as a contrast between the 25th and 75th percentiles, and mediators were fixed at their median values when estimating natural direct effects.

For each exposure–mediator pair, CMAverse provided hazard ratios (HRs) for the natural direct effect (NDE), natural indirect effect (NIE), and total effect (TE), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated using bootstrapping. The proportion mediated (PE) was calculated as PE = NIE / TE × 100%. Mediation was considered statistically significant if the 95% CI for the NIE excluded the null value.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of our primary findings: (1) Further adjusting the fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards model for additional clinical covariates, including histological type, histological grade, treatment regimens (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy), and genetic mutation status; (2) Repeating the multi-state analysis using the full follow-up period average exposure; (3) Redefining the exposure window for mediation analysis as the average concentration from years 4 to 2 prior to death or the end of follow-up (i.e., excluding the final year) to ensure the correct temporal sequence between exposure, mediator, and outcome.

Results are expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each interquartile range (IQR) increase in PM2.5 and components. A two-sided P-value below 0.05 was used as the threshold for statistical significance. All statistical computations were performed using R software, version 4.5.0.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

The characteristics of the study patients are presented in Table 1. The analysis included 3,357 patients with non-metastatic CRC, who had a median age of 57.0 years (IQR: 48.0–65.0) at diagnosis, and males constituted 61.8% of them. Over a median follow-up of 7.59 years (IQR: 5.33–9.20), totaling 24,474 person-years, we documented 1,093 disease-free survival (DFS) events and 796 deaths. Among the deaths, 501 occurred after a DFS event, while 295 occurred without a prior recorded DFS event (Fig. 1). At the end of the study period, 2,029 patients remained alive, while 532 patients were lost to follow-up. The median long-term exposure levels were 23.37 µg/m³ (IQR: 21.08–27.97) for PM2.5, 0.92 µg/m³ (IQR: 0.75–1.15) for Cl⁻, 3.43 µg/m³ (IQR: 2.96–4.09) for NH₄⁺, 4.44 µg/m³ (IQR: 3.74–5.52) for NO₃⁻, and 6.78 µg/m³ (IQR: 6.02–7.75) for SO₄²⁻. Compared with survivors, patients who died from CRC were generally older, more often male, had higher ambient PM2.5 and component exposures, and showed an unfavorable profile of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress biomarkers (higher GGT, NLR, MLR, NMLR, PLR, SIRI, and SII, and lower HDL), whereas UHR did not differ significantly between groups.

Relationship between air pollution and CRC mortality

Figure 2 and Additional file 1: Table S2 depict the hazard ratios for CRC mortality associated with each IQR increase in PM2.5 and its chemical components. In the fully adjusted model, PM2.5 exposure was associated with a 35% elevation in mortality risk (HR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.18–1.54). Specifically, the ammonium (NH₄⁺; HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.13–2.01) and sulfate (SO₄²⁻; HR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.20–2.34) components demonstrated significant positive associations, whereas no such links were observed for chloride (Cl⁻) or nitrate (NO₃⁻).

Results of stratified analyses are presented in Fig. 3 and Additional file 1: Table S3. For PM2.5 total mass, the association was more pronounced in men (HR = 1.50; 95% CI: 1.26–1.78) compared with women (HR = 1.23; 95% CI: 0.93–1.63), with a significant interaction by gender (P for interaction = 0.006). Risk also increased with age, reaching its highest in patients ≥ 65 years (HR = 1.92; 95% CI: 1.52–2.43), and effect modification by age was evident (P for interaction = 0.028). No significant modification was found for smoking, alcohol, BMI, family history of malignancy, or TNM stage.

Association between PM2.5 and components with CRC mortality, stratified by potential modifiers. Results were presented as HR (95% CI) per IQR increase with PM2.5 and components. Abbreviations: HR, Hazard Ratio; CI, Confidence Interval; IQR, Interquartile Range; CRC, colorectal cancer

For PM2.5 components, heterogeneous patterns were observed. NH₄⁺ showed stronger associations in women (HR = 2.10; 95% CI: 1.18–3.75) than in men (HR = 1.33; 95% CI: 0.93–1.92), with the greatest risks in patients ≥ 65 years (HR = 3.37; 95% CI: 1.80–6.30). Elevated risks were also evident among alcohol consumers (HR = 3.07; 95% CI: 1.71–5.48) compared with non-consumers (HR = 1.41; 95% CI: 0.94–2.11), and in stage III patients (HR = 2.21; 95% CI: 1.48–3.30) versus stage I–II (HR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.05–1.65). For SO₄²⁻, we observed a statistically significant interaction by gender (P for interaction = 0.024). The association reached statistical significance in men (HR = 1.52; 95% CI: 1.00–2.32), whereas in women the point estimate was even higher (HR = 1.77; 95% CI: 0.94–3.33) but did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting the smaller sample size and reduced statistical power in the female stratum. Risks were particularly high in patients ≥ 65 years (HR = 4.08; 95% CI: 2.18–7.61) and were elevated among smokers, drinkers, and those with stage III disease. In contrast, Cl⁻ and NO₃⁻ showed no significant associations across subgroups.

Risks of air pollution across CRC disease progression transitions

As presented in Table 2, multi-state models indicated that higher PM2.5 exposure was consistently associated with elevated risks across all three transitions. Per interquartile range (IQR) increase, fully adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) were 1.14 (95% CI: 1.04–1.26) for transition 1 (diagnosis → DFS event), 1.99 (95% CI: 1.71–2.32) for transition 2 (DFS event → CRC death), and 1.58 (95% CI: 1.30–1.92) for transition 3 (diagnosis → CRC death).

Sensitivity analysis

The robustness of our primary findings was evaluated through several sensitivity analyses. First, after additional adjustment for histological type, grade, treatment regimens (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy), and genetic mutation status, the associations of total PM₂.₅ (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.23–1.63), NH₄⁺ (HR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.17–2.11), and SO₄²⁻ (HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.08–2.12) with CRC mortality remained statistically significant, whereas Cl⁻ and NO₃⁻ remained non-significant (Additional file 1: Table S4). Second, repeating the multi-state analysis using the full follow-up period average exposure yielded results similar to those of the primary multi-state models (Additional file 1: Table S5). Finally, a mediation sensitivity analysis redefining exposure as the average concentration from years 4 to 2 prior to death or the end of follow-up (i.e., excluding the final year) yielded estimates consistent with the primary mediation findings (Additional file 1: Table S6).

Discussion

The present investigation into a large Chinese cohort of non-metastatic CRC patients yielded several principal findings. First, increased exposure to PM2.5 was significantly linked to poorer overall survival. Second, this association was substantially stronger in several vulnerable subgroups, including males, the elderly, smokers, those with a family cancer history, and patients with more advanced TNM staging. Furthermore, this association was consistently noted across multiple phases of disease progression—from diagnosis to disease-free survival (DFS) events, from DFS events to CRC death, and directly from diagnosis to CRC death. Importantly, systemic inflammation and oxidative stress were suggested as potential mediators, offering a plausible biological link between ambient air pollution and CRC mortality.

Air pollution and CRC mortality

To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to investigate the associations of PM2.5 and its chemical components with survival outcomes among patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer (CRC). Two prior studies based on the U.S. Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database explored the impact of air pollution exposure on survival among cancer survivors without distinguishing across cancer types. Both reported higher PM2.5 exposure to be associated with increased cardiopulmonary mortality, but not with cancer-specific mortality [40, 41]. More specifically relevant to our work is a recent study by Wang et al., which analyzed a multicenter CRC cohort and found that higher PM2.5 exposure was associated with increased post‑diagnosis mortality, with stronger effects in certain clinical stages [26]. Corroborating this, our findings were consistent with adverse prognostic associations of PM2.5 in an independent Chinese cohort. Extending their findings, we identified ammonium and sulfate as key components associated with this pattern and, using multi-state models, mapped their associations across specific disease progression transitions. This finding is highly consistent with recent evidence from other cancer patient cohorts. For instance, Li et al., in a cohort study of oral cancer patients in Fujian, reported that ammonium and sulfate were strongly associated with cancer-specific mortality, with hazard ratios of 1.28 and 1.27 per SD increase, respectively—effect estimates remarkably similar to those observed in our study [21]. Moreover, a study of breast cancer patients in Inner Mongolia also identified ammonium as the component with the strongest effect (OR = 1.545 per IQR increase), with sulfate showing a significant positive association (OR = 1.404) [22]. The consistency of these findings across different cancer types—oral, breast, and now colorectal—tentatively suggests that the adverse effects of ammonium and sulfate on cancer patient survival may not be limited to a particular tumor type, though this warrants confirmation in future studies. Mechanistically, ammonium has been linked to neuroendocrine stress activation via the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and elevated levels of stress hormones such as cortisol [42]. Beyond this neuroendocrine pathway, population-based studies have shown that ammonium is also robustly associated with both systemic inflammation and coagulation [43], suggesting that its adverse associations with cancer prognosis may operate through multiple interconnected pathways. Sulfate contributes to the formation of highly acidic fine particles (pH < 2) that dissolve insoluble transition metals; the solubilized metals then participate in redox cycling, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inducing oxidative stress—a proposed mechanism through which fine particulate matter may contribute to DNA damage, genomic instability, and cancer progression [44, 45].

Multi-state models further revealed adverse associations across different stages of CRC progression, from diagnosis to recurrence or metastasis, and ultimately to death. Notably, the risks were greater in transitions from recurrence or metastasis to death, underscoring the importance of reducing air pollution exposure in patients who have already experienced a DFS event. Interestingly, the associations of SO₄²⁻ with CRC outcomes differed by modeling framework. In Cox models, higher SO₄²⁻ concentrations were associated with increased CRC mortality risk, whereas in multi-state models, SO₄²⁻ appeared protective in transitions 2 and 3, but showed no significant effect in the initial transition. This discrepancy is understandable given the distinct research questions each approach addresses: Cox models capture the overall mortality risk from diagnosis to death, while multi-state models partition the disease process into stage-specific transitions. The seemingly protective patterns in the multi-state framework may reflect the complex interplay of SO₄²⁻ with co-occurring pollutants or mixture components [44], which could modify risks differently at various stages of disease progression. These findings underscore the value of applying multiple analytic frameworks and accounting for pollutant mixtures to obtain a more nuanced understanding of the role of PM2.5 components in cancer progression.

Effect modification by age and gender

We observed significant effect modification by both gender and age in the associations between PM2.5 exposure and CRC mortality. Stronger effects were observed among men compared with women. This pattern is consistent with the well-recognized gender disparity in CRC incidence risk, as men face a significantly higher risk of developing CRC relative to women [46]. Expert groups have even proposed gender-specific screening recommendations, with earlier initiation in men [47, 48]. The heightened vulnerability of men to both CRC incidence and PM2.5-related mortality may reflect biological factors such as sex hormones, differences in inflammatory responses or pollutant metabolism, as well as lifestyle-related exposure patterns. Notably, gender modification was also evident for sulfate (SO₄²⁻), where stronger adverse effects were observed among men, suggesting that specific components of PM2.5 may disproportionately contribute to the observed sex-related disparities.

Older age (≥ 65 years) also amplified the adverse effects of PM2.5. This finding aligns with evidence that aging is accompanied by diminished DNA repair capacity, immune senescence, and the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations, all of which may increase susceptibility to environmental carcinogens [49]. In addition, older adults generally experience greater cumulative exposure to ambient pollution over the life course, further contributing to their heightened risk. However, some stratum-specific estimates, particularly for NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻ in older patients, were associated with wide confidence intervals, and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of considering both gender and age when evaluating environmental risk factors for CRC progression, and further highlight the need to disentangle component-specific effects that may underlie these vulnerabilities.

Mediation by inflammation

Our mediation analyses suggested systemic inflammation and oxidative stress as biological pathways linking total PM2.5 exposure to CRC mortality, with MLR, HDL, and PLR emerging as the strongest mediators. In contrast, no significant mediation was observed for ammonium or sulfate, despite their strong associations with CRC outcomes. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by two factors. First, the biological mechanisms of specific components are complex—they may share some pathways while also having distinct ones—and findings from existing studies remain inconclusive and sometimes inconsistent. Second, components do not exist in isolation—their effects are intertwined with those of co-pollutants, and such complex interactions may further obscure component-specific mediation signals [43, 44, 50]. Thus, the null mediation findings for ammonium and sulfate are not inconsistent with their direct effects, but rather reflect their incompletely understood mechanisms, and the inherent complexity of multipollutant mixtures. These findings highlight the inherent complexity of component-specific mediation pathways, and suggest that such results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study lie in its large, well-characterized cohort of CRC patients with extended follow-up, which provides high statistical power and robust evidence. We conducted a comprehensive analysis for both PM2.5 and its chemical components, and employed multi-state models to capture detailed disease trajectories. Furthermore, the incorporation of mediation analyses enabled us to probe potential biological pathways linking PM2.5 exposure to CRC outcomes, providing mechanistic insights that complement the observed associations.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, exposure in this study was assessed by linking participants’ baseline residential addresses with satellite-based model estimates of ambient PM2.5 and its components, rather than using personal monitoring. This approach may introduce exposure misclassification from two sources. One is inherent to the exposure model itself, as ambient concentrations serve as a proxy for personal exposure. The other relates to residential mobility during follow-up, which could lead to changes in actual exposure levels. Some participants may have moved during the study period, but we did not collect this information, which could lead to some degree of exposure misclassification. However, since residential relocation is unlikely to be related to CRC-specific mortality, this misclassification is likely non-differential, which would be expected to bias estimates toward the null and produce more conservative conclusions [51, 52]. Second, in our multi-state analysis, exposure was defined as the average concentration preceding death or end of follow-up, which may introduce temporal overlap with disease progression—a known challenge in modeling time-varying environmental exposures. While restricting exposure to the period prior to the first stage would preserve the temporal sequence, it would fail to capture relevant exposures occurring between the first and final stages. To address this concern and following the idea of existing studies [53, 54], we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the average concentration over the entire follow-up period as an alternative exposure metric. The consistent findings from this analysis support the robustness of our results. Third, although we extensively adjusted for a wide range of clinical, lifestyle, and crucial CRC-specific covariates (including disease stage, treatment modalities), and our sensitivity analyses yielded robust and consistent findings, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be entirely excluded. Specifically, individual-level socioeconomic status (SES) data (e.g., education, income, occupation, dietary habits) were not routinely available, a common limitation in retrospective hospital-based studies. To assess the potential impact of such unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for our main associations, which ranged from 2.03 to 2.74, meaning an unmeasured confounder would need risk ratios of this magnitude to fully explain away our findings [55]. Fourth, as a single-center tertiary referral center study, the included patients may represent a selected subgroup of CRC patients. Notably, the largest imbalances between included and excluded patients were observed in BMI (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 0.45) and CVD history (SMD = 0.71), which likely reflect the selection of non-metastatic patients who underwent curative surgical resection, but do not fully rule out selection bias. Generalizability of our findings to broader CRC populations should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, as the cohort was based in Guangzhou, generalizability to populations with different environmental and clinical contexts may be limited. Nevertheless, the study was conducted at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, a major comprehensive cancer hospital in China that draws patients from various regions. This broader catchment area, together with the distinct clinical characteristics of this cohort, provides valuable insights that may inform studies in other settings.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that lower ambient PM2.5 exposure was associated with better survival outcomes for non-metastatic CRC patients, especially in identified vulnerable subgroups. The consistent adverse associations observed throughout the disease course, with inflammation and oxidative stress as potential mediating pathways, reinforce the public health imperative to address air pollution as a modifiable risk factor in cancer survivorship, advocating for stronger environmental controls and targeted patient protections.
