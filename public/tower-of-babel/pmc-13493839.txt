Structure-aware anomaly detection via semantic prototype reconstruction in hyperbolic space
Authors: Yanjun Feng, Jun Liu, Yonggang Gai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnomaly detection plays a crucial role in various applications such as industrial defect inspection and safety perception. Existing mainstream approaches typically rely on modeling the distribution of normal samples to build anomaly discrimination models. However, these methods often face challenges in practical scenarios due to ambiguous decision boundaries and limited capability to capture complex semantic and structural variations in defects. To overcome these limitations, we propose a novel industrial defect detection framework based on hyperbolic space. This framework exploits the negative curvature property of hyperbolic geometry to dynamically extract semantic prototypes and embed them into the hyperbolic space, enhancing the model’s ability to represent intricate semantic and structural changes. Furthermore, a semantic prototype-guided attention mechanism is integrated to assist the reconstruction of images, enabling accurate localization of anomalies through reconstruction error. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method achieves state-of-the-art results on multiple industrial anomaly detection datasets. Notably, on the MVTec AD benchmark, our approach attains 99.8% and 99.1% AUROC scores for image-level and pixel-level tasks, respectively, significantly surpassing current leading methods.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Anomaly detection has been widely adopted in industrial manufacturing due to its efficiency and reliability1–3. Its focus is on identifying small but critical defects within large image datasets. Examples of such defects include scratches, cracks, and missing parts. Anomaly detection addresses diverse defect types with complex features.Slight color changes, shape distortions, and missing components all fall within its scope4,5. Detecting different anomaly types accurately requires high sensitivity. Robustness to environmental changes and noise remains essential for industrial applications.

Traditional methods typically assume that defects follow stable and manually modelable patterns. Such assumptions limit their effectiveness in handling highly variable and unstructured defect forms found in real-world scenarios6. In recent years, deep neural network-based anomaly detection methods have achieved significant progress in industrial vision tasks. Anomaly detection and localization remain particularly challenging in industrial settings due to the scarcity of anomalous samples. Mainstream approaches usually rely on learning the feature distribution of large-scale normal samples during training, and then detect regions that deviate from this distribution during inference7,8. Some methods generate synthetic anomalies on anomaly-free images by applying random occlusions, noise perturbations, or simulated defects to train models for estimating the decision boundary between normal and abnormal regions9,10. Others detect anomalies by comparing input features against learned distributions or memory-encoded features11,12. These approaches generally assume high consistency among training samples and that the feature space can compactly and accurately represent the boundary of normal data distribution. In practical industrial environments, image data often exhibit high feature distribution variability and structural heterogeneity due to sensor noise, imaging angles, illumination changes, and intrinsic part diversity. These factors violate the above assumptions and pose significant challenges for anomaly detection methods.

Recent studies have adopted prototype-based modeling strategies by extracting representative prototype vectors from normal images to characterize the semantic properties of normal regions13,14. However, most existing prototype-based methods still organize features and prototypes in Euclidean space, which limits their ability to handle industrial anomaly images where anomalous regions are subtle, incomplete, or structurally similar to normal ones. In such cases, the semantic boundary between normal and anomalous features tends to become ambiguous, resulting in reduced discriminative power. To address this issue, we introduce hyperbolic space as a structural prior for prototype-based anomaly detection. As illustrated in Figure 1(a), hyperbolic space is a negatively curved non-Euclidean embedding space that is more effective at representing hierarchical structures and complex semantic relationships in high-dimensional data. By projecting semantic features and prototypes into hyperbolic space, our method can better separate different normal semantic modes while more effectively amplifying subtle deviations from normal prototypes.

In this paper, we propose a hyperbolic space-based framework for industrial defect detection. Unlike existing methods that perform shallow alignment directly on categories, we embed both prototypes and sample features into hyperbolic space. The negative curvature of hyperbolic space allows for a more natural modeling of hierarchical structures and long-tailed distributions. Specifically, we dynamically extract semantic prototypes from input images and structurally model them in hyperbolic space. Additionally, we employ a semantic prototype-guided attention mechanism to assist in reconstructing normal regions of the image, while leveraging reconstruction errors to detect the presence of anomalous regions. We validated our approach on three datasets, and the results demonstrate that our model outperforms the previously best-performing method.

Related work

Anomaly detection

Anomaly detection aims to identify unknown or inconsistent samples that deviate from the distribution of normal data15–17. It plays an important role in a wide range of critical applications, including industrial surface defect inspection, medical image analysis, and intelligent video surveillance18,19. In industrial visual inspection, anomalies usually manifest as surface defects on products, such as scratches, cracks, stains, and missing components20,21. Among existing methods, reconstruction-based methods and prototype-/memory-based methods have become two important research directions in industrial anomaly detection in recent years22–24.

Reconstruction-based methods detect anomalies by learning to recover normal samples and measuring the discrepancy between the input image and its reconstructed counterpart. Typical models, such as Autoencoders (AEs), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)25–28, are trained only on normal data under the assumption that anomalous regions cannot be faithfully reconstructed. To improve robustness, some studies introduce sparse constraints, feature compression, or masking and inpainting strategies to encourage the model to better capture normal semantics and highlight anomalous regions21,29,30. Although these methods have achieved promising results, they may still over-reconstruct anomalous regions when defects are subtle, structurally incomplete, or semantically similar to normal patterns.

Prototype-/memory-based methods represent normal data using prototype vectors, memory banks, or representative feature dictionaries15–17. During inference, anomaly scores are computed based on the discrepancy between input features and the stored normal representations. Compared with single-center distribution modeling, these methods are better suited to capturing multiple normal modes and handling intra-class variations18,19. Recent studies have further improved compactness and robustness by aggregating region-level semantic prototypes from normal samples22–24. However, most existing methods still organize these representations in Euclidean space and usually treat them as relatively static anchors, which limits their ability to model hierarchical semantic relations and subtle structural deviations in complex industrial images. These limitations motivate us to combine dynamic semantic prototypes with hyperbolic structural modeling for anomaly detection.

Hyperbolic space

Hyperbolic space is a non-Euclidean manifold with constant negative curvature, where the volume grows exponentially with radius. This geometric property gives hyperbolic space a strong advantage in modeling data with hierarchical or tree-like structures. Such structure-aware representations are particularly relevant for human-centered perception tasks, where relational and topological dependencies play a key role31,32. Compared to Euclidean space of the same dimension, hyperbolic space has higher representational capacity. It can represent complex structured relationships more compactly and accurately using the same number of embedding parameters33,34. For this reason, hyperbolic space has been widely used in natural language processing, especially in tasks such as hierarchical semantic modeling, knowledge graph embedding, and word meaning evolution, where it has shown clear performance gains35–37. In computer vision, hyperbolic embeddings have also achieved notable progress. Researchers have applied hyperbolic geometry to face recognition, image classification, action recognition, few-shot learning, and object segmentation. For example, in few-shot learning, hyperbolic space can amplify small geometric differences between samples, improving class separability38. Recent studies have shown that hyperbolic geometry can effectively model hierarchical semantic relations and structured feature dependencies, improving representation capacity in complex visual learning tasks39. In addition, hyperbolic geometry has been incorporated into various deep neural architectures, enabling more effective modeling of hierarchical and structured representations in non-Euclidean spaces40.

Although research on hyperbolic space in anomaly detection is still in its early stages, existing studies have preliminarily demonstrated its unique modeling potential. Traditional anomaly detection methods typically rely on Euclidean space for statistical computations, similarity measurements, or feature clustering. However, these approaches often struggle to effectively model complex fine-grained structures and multi-scale semantic information in high-dimensional images. In contrast, hyperbolic space naturally adapts to nested structures and geometric hierarchies, enabling it to capture structural shifts between normal and anomalous samples more effectively in the feature space. By amplifying subtle local differences, hyperbolic embeddings show particular promise in detecting micro-defects in industrial images.

Methods

Overall architecture

To improve the accuracy and generalization of industrial anomaly detection, we propose a dynamic anomaly detection framework based on hyperbolic space, as shown in Fig. 2. Our method exploits the hierarchical nature of hyperbolic space as a structural prior for organizing semantic features and prototypes. Such a design allows the model to better capture heterogeneous normal semantic patterns and to amplify subtle deviations that are otherwise difficult to separate in Euclidean space. Specifically, image features are first mapped into hyperbolic space to provide a geometry better suited to modeling hierarchical semantic structure. Based on the resulting hyperbolic representations, the INP Extractor dynamically derives semantic prototypes from normal patterns. These prototypes are then used by the SP-Guided Decoder to guide the reconstruction of normal regions, thereby improving the separation between normal and anomalous responses in the reconstruction space. Finally, reconstruction errors are used to localize anomalous regions. In the following sections, we provide a formal description and detailed analysis of each core module.

The overall architecture of our method. The input image first passes through an encoder to extract Euclidean features. These features are then mapped into hyperbolic space to obtain hierarchical hyperbolic features. A semantic prototype extractor and an MLP process these features. The decoder, guided by semantic prototypes, generates reconstructed features. These reconstructed features are compared with the original features. The model is optimized using Dynamic Focal Loss and Coherence Loss. Finally, the network outputs an anomaly heatmap.

Experiments

Datasets

MVTec-AD dataset41 comprises 15 categories, including 5 object types and 10 texture materials. Each category provides a collection of defect-free (normal) images along with multiple types of real-world anomalous images, accompanied by pixel-level annotations of defect regions, enabling its use in segmentation tasks. Specifically, the dataset contains 3,629 normal images for training, and 1,982 anomalous images together with 498 normal images for testing. The dataset exhibits significant variability in defect types and substantial appearance differences across categories. Moreover, the anomalous regions vary in size and shape, often exhibiting complex structures. These characteristics make MVTec-AD particularly suitable for evaluating the robustness and generalization ability of models across diverse real-world scenarios.

VisA dataset42 is designed for industrial visual inspection tasks and covers 12 representative categories, including electronic components, tool parts, and natural objects. The dataset consists of high-quality images, comprising 8,659 normal images for training, 962 additional normal images, and 1,200 anomalous images for testing. Region-level anomaly masks are provided, enabling its use in classification, detection, and segmentation tasks. VisA offers images that closely resemble real-world industrial environments, characterized by complex background textures, subtle anomalies, multi-scale structures, and high intra-class variability, thus presenting new challenges for model robustness and generalization.

Real-IAD dataset43 is collected from real-world industrial production lines and comprises six categories of industrial components. The data were acquired from multiple production batches and diverse industrial equipment, encompassing both normal samples and various types of anomalies. A subset of the images is annotated with segmentation masks. Real-IAD preserves the background context and noise inherent to in-situ environments. The dataset includes 30 distinct objects, with 36,645 normal images for training, 63,256 additional normal images, and 51,329 anomalous images for testing. In contrast to synthetic or standardized datasets, Real-IAD exhibits significantly higher real-world complexity, including variations in lighting, occlusion, background clutter, and ambiguity in anomaly definitions.

Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. The software environment was based on Python 3.9 with CUDA 11.2 configured to support GPU acceleration. All images were resized to a uniform resolution of 256×256 pixels. During training, models were optimized with a batch size of 8 for a total of 200 epochs. We employed the StableAdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, using a stepwise decay schedule for dynamic adjustment. The datasets were partitioned into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 8:1:1 to ensure scientific rigor and stable model evaluation.

In anomaly detection research, evaluation metrics must account for both anomaly identification and localization performance. We adopted the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), Average Precision (AP), and F1-score-max(F1 max) as metrics for anomaly detection accuracy. For anomaly localization, we employed the Area Under the Per-Region Overlap Curve (AUPRO) to assess the model’s performance at the region level.In all experiments, the balancing coefficients , , and are empirically set to 0.2, 0.4, and 0.4, respectively.

Ablation studies

Overall ablation

As shown in Table 3, we conducted a systematic ablation study on the VisA dataset to validate the effectiveness of each proposed component. including the hyperbolic representation module which implicitly reflects the effect of the Hyperbolic Alignment Loss. Influence of the number of SPs

As shown in Table 4, we conducted an ablation study on the number of semantic prototypes M. The experimental results indicate that the model performance stabilizes when M> 4, while excessively large M leads to mild degradation. This is because M determines the semantic granularity of normal modeling. A small M may compress different normal regions into coarse prototypes, while a large M can over-fragment features and introduce noisier assignments, making the model slightly less robust. In this study, we set M = 6 to balance model effectiveness and robustness.

Effect of curvature parameter c

As shown in Table 5, the model achieved peak performance at c = 0.01, attaining 99.8% I-AUROC and 99.1% P-AUROC, which significantly outperforms the Euclidean baseline (c = 0) that yielded 99.1% I-AUROC and 97.4% P-AUROC. Notably, the improvement in P-AUROC is particularly substantial, with hyperbolic space providing an 1.7 percentage point gain over Euclidean space. The results indicate that a moderate hyperbolic curvature (c = 0.01–0.1) maintains consistently high performance, with I-AUROC exceeding 98% and P-AUROC remaining above 97%. However, when curvature becomes excessively large (c \ge 0.3), performance begins to degrade markedly, underscoring the critical importance of proper curvature selection. Discussion

Our experimental results across multiple datasets consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of modeling semantic and structural information in hyperbolic space for anomaly detection. By leveraging the negative curvature of hyperbolic geometry, our method captures subtle semantic differences and hierarchical relationships more effectively than conventional Euclidean approaches. This leads to substantial improvements in both image-level and pixel-level AUROC scores, especially in complex cases with fine-grained or ambiguous anomalies. The adaptive semantic prototype mechanism enhances the model’s robustness against domain shifts and category variance, while the coherence and Dynamic Focal Losses further boost feature discrimination and localization precision. These results underscore the synergistic benefit of jointly modeling semantic structure and geometry, offering new insights into designing high-capacity representations for industrial visual inspection.

Despite its strong performance, the proposed method also presents certain limitations that open avenues for future exploration. First, the increased computational complexity introduced by hyperbolic embeddings and attention-guided reconstruction may hinder deployment in real-time or resource-constrained environments. Second, the sensitivity of the model to the curvature parameter c indicates the need for more adaptive or learnable curvature designs. In practice, a smaller curvature is typically a stable choice when a dataset exhibits modest semantic variability and relatively homogeneous normal patterns, whereas a moderately larger curvature can better separate richer part-to-whole structures and long-tail variations across more diverse categories. Furthermore, while dynamic prototype learning alleviates semantic drift to some extent, its effectiveness remains influenced by training data distribution and prototype size.Another challenge arises when detecting extremely low-contrast defects in reconstruction-based anomaly detection methods. When anomalous regions share similar local appearance with normal patterns, reconstruction models may partially restore these regions, which reduces anomaly discrimination capability. Future work may consider integrating lightweight geometric modules, multi-modal cues (e.g., text, audio), and self-supervised pretraining to further improve model generalizability and interpretability. Additionally, extending hyperbolic representation learning to other domains such as video anomaly detection or temporal event modeling may broaden its application scope.

Conclusions

In this work, we propose a novel structure-aware anomaly detection framework that leverages the geometric properties of hyperbolic space to enhance both the semantic representation and localization of anomalies in industrial inspection tasks. By dynamically extracting semantic prototypes and embedding them into a negatively curved space, our model effectively captures hierarchical semantic variations and local structural differences. Extensive experiments on three challenging benchmarks demonstrate that our method consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance at both image and pixel levels. Incorporating hyperbolic geometry into deep anomaly detection provides a promising direction for improving detection accuracy, especially in complex, real-world scenarios. Future work will explore lightweight hyperbolic modules, adaptive curvature learning, and multi-modal extensions to further enhance the scalability and applicability of the proposed framework.
