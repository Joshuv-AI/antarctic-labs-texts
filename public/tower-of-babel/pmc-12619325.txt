Construction and application of a risk prediction model for sputum viscosity in patients with laryngeal malignant tumors after operation
Authors: Qing Wang, Hongli Ma, Min Li, Yanan Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of sputum viscosity on postoperative recovery among patients with laryngeal malignancies. Additionally, it sought to identify risk factors associated with postoperative sputum viscosity and to develop a risk prediction model for its occurrence.MethodsConvenience sampling was employed to enroll 130 laryngeal malignancy patients hospitalized at a Grade III-A hospital in Shanxi Province between January 2022 and January 2024. Patients were divided into two groups based on postoperative sputum viscosity: the viscous group (n = 52) and non-viscous group (n = 78). General patient data were collected through an electronic medical record system. A logistic regression model was constructed using a 50% cross-validation method, with risk prediction visualization achieved via line graphs. The model’s fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and its predictive performance was assessed through receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Additionally, 49 surgical patients with laryngeal malignancies treated at another Grade III-A hospital in Shanxi Province between June 2024 and June 2025 were selected for external validation.ResultsData collected between 2022 and 2024 demonstrated a 40% incidence of postoperative sputum viscosity. Significant risk factors identified included age, smoking, placement of a drainage tube, and Ca²⁺ levels. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated a good model fit (x² = 14.617, P = 0.067). The area under the ROC curve was 0.914 (95% CI: 0.749–0.991) with a sensitivity of 0.90, specificity of 0.938, and overall accuracy of 0.891. The clinical application ROC curve results showed that the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.955 (95% confidence interval: 0.906-1.000), accuracy: 0.735, sensitivity: 0.350, and specificity: 1.000.ConclusionThe developed predictive model demonstrates robust performance in identifying individuals at high risk of postoperative sputum viscosity following laryngeal tumor surgery. This model holds potential as a valuable tool for clinical teams to facilitate timely evaluation and management of postoperative sputum viscosity, thereby improving patient outcomes.Keywords: Malignant laryngeal tumor, Prediction model, Sputum, Sputum viscosity, Tracheotomy

Introduction

Head and neck tumors primarily occur in the mouth, pharynx, larynx, sinuses, nasal cavity, and salivary glands [1]. These tumors currently rank among the 10 most common malignancies worldwide. Malignant laryngeal tumors constitute a subset of head and neck cancers. According to global data from 2020, malignant laryngeal tumors ranked 20th in incidence and 18th in cancer-related mortality [2]. In 2021, the United States reported 12,620 new cases of malignant laryngeal tumors, resulting in 3,770 deaths, with a 5-year survival rate of 60.7% [3]. In China, approximately 47,000 new cases and 21,000 mortalities from malignant laryngeal tumors occurred in 2020, representing 1% to 5% of all cancer cases [4]. The morbidity and mortality rates were notably higher in males compared to females. The high incidence and mortality of these tumors have placed significant strain on the healthcare system, posing a serious threat to public health.

The most common symptoms of laryngeal malignancies include sore throat, hoarseness, dysphagia, throat mass, and dyspnea [2]. The exact cause of laryngeal malignancies remains unknown, with a range of possible pathogenic factors including human papillomavirus (HPV), sex hormone imbalances, and environmental pollution [5]. HPV infection has been detected in 20% to 30% of patients with laryngeal malignancies. Studies suggest that smokers are 10 to 15 times more likely to develop laryngeal malignancies than non-smokers, and alcohol consumption also increases the risk [3]. Therefore, individuals with a history of tobacco and alcohol use face a significantly higher risk of developing laryngeal tumors.

Advances in medical technology and increased awareness of health have improved the diagnosis and treatment of laryngeal malignancies. Current treatment modalities include surgical intervention, precision radiotherapy, and targeted therapies. Surgery remains the primary treatment for laryngeal malignancies, and most surgical procedures require tracheotomy or tracheostomy to maintain airway function [6]. After tracheotomy, the loss of the upper respiratory tract’s ability to warm and humidify inhaled air leads to dryness of the respiratory tract, resulting in viscous sputum and an increased risk of pulmonary infections [7]. Increased sputum viscosity also raises the risk of postoperative airway complications, surgical site infections, and delayed wound healing, which negatively impacts recovery and the quality of life for patients while increasing the workload for medical staff.

Currently, sputum viscosity is managed using wet chemotherapy to aid in the drainage of airway secretions. Clinical research has focused on humidification fluids, temperature, and methods to improve sputum viscosity, but there has been limited investigation into the systematic factors contributing to this condition [8–10]. To date, no studies have reported predictive models of postoperative sputum viscosity in laryngeal malignancies. Most validation methods only conduct internal verification, with insufficient exploration of external bias risks. This study established model development and validation cohorts based on two medical centers. The development cohort investigated factors influencing postoperative sputum viscosity in laryngeal cancer patients, while the validation cohort assessed external predictive efficacy. These findings provide scientific evidence for early evaluation and targeted prevention strategies, aiming to accelerate postoperative recovery, improve disease prognosis, and enhance patients’ quality of life.

Methods

Study participants

A convenience sampling method was employed to select patients with malignant laryngeal tumors who underwent tracheotomy or tracheostomy at a tertiary care hospital between January 1, 2022, and January 1, 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who underwent elective surgery; (2) patients aged over 18 years; and (3) absence of lung infections, as confirmed by preoperative chest CT examination. The exclusion criteria included: (1) preoperative body temperature exceeding 37.5 °C for 3 days, and (2) patients with incomplete data. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the study. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the hospital’s ethics review committee (Approval No.: YXLL-2023–033).

The required sample size was determined based on the guideline that the sample should be 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables. Therefore, the minimum sample size for this study was 75 patients (15 × 5). To account for incomplete data and participant withdrawal, the sample size was increased by 20%. Thus, the final sample size required for the study was approximately 94 patients (15 × 5 ÷ 0.8).

Sputum collection and analysis of sputum viscosity

Special hospital staff were assigned to collect sputum specimens. The environmental conditions in all wards were maintained consistently, with room temperatures set between 18 °C and 22 °C humidity levels controlled at 50% to 60%. Postoperatively, patients received continuous airway humidification using 0.45% physiological saline upon returning to their wards. Beginning from the morning of the first postoperative day, sputum specimens were collected using a standardized technique [7, 8]. The patients were positioned in semi-recumbent posture, and nursing staff disinfected the tracheal cannula with iodine prior to specimen collection. The tracheal cannula was connected to the negative pressure tube of a disposable sterile sputum collector, which was attached to a suction device. At zero negative pressure, the sputum suction tube was inserted into the distal end of the airway via the tracheotomy cannula. Upon reaching the bottom of the trachea, the suction tube was withdrawn by 1 cm to 2 cm. Negative pressure was generated by sealing the side hole of the suction tube, which was then rotated slightly and gently lifted. When no negative pressure was encountered, the suction tube was retracted, and the sputum storage device was removed and sealed.

Specimens were independently evaluated for viscosity by two trained staff members. All samples were transported to the laboratory for further analysis within two hours of collection.

Sputum viscosity was classified as follows: grade I (thin sputum), characterized by a foam-like or rice soup-like consistency, was assigned 1 point; grade II (medium viscous sputum), with a sticky appearance and a small amount of sputum adhering to the inner wall of the collection bag that can be easily washed away by water, was assigned 2 points; grade III (severe viscous sputum), characterized by very sticky and yellow sputum, with a large amount adhering to the inner wall of the collection bag that cannot be easily washed away by water, was assigned 3 points. Sputum samples with an average stickiness score of ≥ 2.0 were considered positive, while those with an average score of < 2.0 were considered negative.

Data collection

Relevant data were systematically retrieved from databases such as CNKI, Wanfan, Weipu, Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Springerlink, and others. Consultation from experts in ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgery, head and neck surgery, respiratory care, inspection departments, and clinical care was sought to identify variables that may affect postoperative sputum viscosity. A self-designed questionnaire was employed to collect demographic data specific to patients with malignant laryngeal tumors.

Patient data were retrospectively collected from the electronic medical record system. General information collected included gender, age, body mass index (BMI), number of hospitalization days, smoking history, and presence of chronic diseases. Laboratory parameters included sputum pH and procalcitonin (PCT) levels. A pH meter was used to measure the sputum sample’s pH, while a fully automated biochemical analyzer was used to measure Ca2+ and PCT levels in the sputum samples.

The SF-12 scale was employed to evaluate quality of life in patients with laryngeal malignancies. This comprehensive assessment tool comprises 12 subscales across eight dimensions: General Health (GH), Physical Functioning (PF), Physical Restriction (RP), Bodily Pain (BP), Mental Health (MH), Vitality (VT), Social Functioning (SF), and Emotional Restriction (RE). These eight dimensions are synthesized into two composite indicators: Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Health Composite (MCS). The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this instrument reached 0.844, with scores ranging from 0 to 100 – where 0 indicates the worst health status and 100 represents optimal well-being. A score ≥ 50 is considered normal.

The collected data were reviewed by two uniformly trained researchers. Issues such as abnormalities, missing values, and duplicate entries were promptly addressed, and all data were verified to maintain accuracy and integrity.

Statistical analysis

Epidata 3.0 software was used for data entry, while SPSS 26.0 and R 4.2.1 software were used for statistical analyses. Count data are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The chi-squared test was applied for group comparisons. For normally distributed measurement data, results are expressed as mean ± SD (x ± s), and the t-test was used for group comparisons. For non-normally distributed measurement data, results are expressed as M (P25, P75), and the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test was used for group comparisons. Logistic regression was employed to construct the prediction model. The model’s goodness of fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. The model’s prediction ability was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Sensitivity and specificity were analyzed to determine the practical efficiency of the model. A 50-fold cross-validation test was used to assess accuracy, and calibration was assessed using a calibration chart. The column line model was externally validated with the external validation set data. Statistical significance was set at P = 0.05.

Results

Incidence of postoperative viscous sputum in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors

This study excluded 8 patients with incomplete data and 7 patients who discontinued treatment midway, resulting in a final model development cohort of 130 patients and a validation cohort of 49 patients. The average age of patients in the viscous sputum group and non-viscous sputum group within the model development cohort was 64.1 ± 6.4 years and 54.2 ± 5.3 years, respectively. Among all patients, 126 were male (96%) and 4 were female (4%). Viscous sputum occurred in 52 patients, representing an incidence rate of 40%.

Prediction model for postoperative sputum viscosity in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors

A predictive model was developed based on the results of the multivariate analysis using the following equation: Logit P = − 69.29 + 0.723 × Age + 3.048 × Smoking + 2.428 × Drainage tube placement + 7.446 × Ca2+ level. Evaluation and verification of the prediction model for patients with malignant laryngeal tumors

The relationship between the predicted P value and postoperative sputum viscosity was assessed using the ROC curve (Fig. 2). The area under the ROC curve was 0.914 (95% CI = 0.749–0.991), indicating that the prediction model demonstrated high diagnostic value for assessing viscous sputum in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors. The prediction model showed sensitivity and specificity of 0.90 and 0.938, respectively. The results of the Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed no significant difference between the predicted and observed values (χ² =, P = 0.067), confirming a good fit for the observed data. The five-fold cross-validation method was applied to generate the calibration curve for internal model verification. The accuracy rate was 0.838, with a 0.891 accuracy rate during cross-validation. The calibration curve closely aligned with the standard curve (Fig. 3), indicating that the model demonstrated good consistency with the actual risk. External validation results demonstrated that the linear model predicted a 95% CI (0.906-1.000 AUC for postoperative sputum viscosity in laryngeal malignancies (Fig. 4). Hosmer-Lemeshow fit adequacy test and calibration curve analysis confirmed good model performance in the training set (Fig. 5).

Discussion

High incidence of postoperative viscous sputum in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors

The incidence of postoperative viscous sputum in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors was found to be high, at 40%. Under normal physiological conditions, the airway epithelium is covered by airway surface liquid (ASL), which is approximately 10 μm to 20 μm thick. ASL plays a critical role in airway heating and humidification, as well as in forming a mucus barrier [9–12]. During surgery, the airways of patients with malignant laryngeal tumors are compromised, leading to insufficient humidification and heating of the inhaled air. This disrupts the ciliary function of the respiratory mucosa and impairs the ASL, resulting in a decreased ability to produce normal sputum and an increase in sputum viscosity. According to Wang et al., sputum viscosity is a key factor in the effectiveness of respiratory tract cleaning [13]. Active respiratory secretions can increase the risk of lung infections, worsening lung conditions.

In the current cohort, patients with viscous sputum stayed 4.3 days longer on average (12.7 ± 3.2 vs. 8.4 ± 2.1 days), and a higher incidence of pulmonary complications was observed (Analysis of single factors responsible for postoperative viscous sputum in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors section). These findings align with our Discussion (High incidence of postoperative viscous sputum in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors section) showing that viscous sputum predisposes to atelectasis and pneumonia, ultimately prolonging LOS and increasing medical costs.

Therefore, this study emphasizes the importance of effective post-operative respiratory management, including monitoring sputum viscosity, identifying risk factors for viscous sputum, and implementing preventive measures before surgery to reduce the risk of postoperative viscous sputum in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors.

Age affects postoperative sputum viscosity in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors

The average age of the patients in the viscous sputum group was 64.1 ± 6.4 years. As individuals age, there is a gradual decline in the internal structure of the lungs, including a reduction in surface area and supporting structures, along with a reduction in alveolar surfactant content. Additionally, aging impacts the capacity of the musculoskeletal pump, leading to diminished respiratory muscle strength and chest elasticity, resulting in a decreased ability to expel sputum and increases sputum viscosity. Cao reported that the increase in sputum neutrophils in older adults is associated with more frequent bronchospasms, increased airway resistance, and hindrance in sputum discharge, all of which affect sputum viscosity in these patients [14]. Gerday et al. demonstrated that in individuals over 50 years old, an increase in eosinophil count in sputum is associated with airway inflammation, leading to airway obstruction, expansion of the invalid cavity, and an increased burden on sputum expulsion [15]. These findings suggest that medical staff should pay particular attention to perioperative airway management in older adults. Prior to surgery, it is crucial to assess patients for risk factors such as airway obstruction and airflow restriction using lung function tests, the 6-minute walking test, and blood oxygen saturation levels. Furthermore, older adults should be encouraged to undergo preoperative cough training and breathing exercises. Timely postoperative supplementation of water loss resulting from tracheotomy is crucial. Yang et al. proposed a systematic humidification management method that provides prolonged and effective airway humidification, which has been shown to improve sputum viscosity in older adults [16].

Smoking affects postoperative sputum viscosity in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors

In the present study, 71.2% of the patients in the viscous sputum group were smokers. Several studies have demonstrated that smoking can lead to persistent chronic inflammation in the airway and lung parenchyma, causing an imbalance in the airway and pulmonary plasma protease-antiprotease systems and other airway components, which further impairs the patient’s lung function capacity and flow rate indices [17, 18]. As individuals age, smoking accelerates lung damage, limiting the ability to clear the airways and affecting sputum viscosity. Perez et al. analyzed sputum in smokers and found that nonsmokers had considerably less sputum than smokers [19]. Moreover, smokers exhibited higher protein content and more viscous sputum compared to nonsmokers, which aligns with the results of the present study. Therefore, medical staff should encourage patients to quit smoking during hospitalization, explain the harmful effects of smoking on lung function, sputum viscosity, and sputum expulsion, highlight the benefits of smoking cessation, emphasize its impact on treatment outcomes, and implement feasible smoking cessation strategies.

Drainage tube placement affects postoperative sputum viscosity of patients with malignant laryngeal tumors

The sputum of patients who had drainage tubes placed postoperatively was thicker compared to those without drainage tubes. This may be attributed to the postoperative incision pain and the fear of displacing the drainage tube, which may cause patients to avoid coughing up the sputum. Patients undergoing surgery for malignant laryngeal tumors undergo different surgical procedures based on their pathological condition and the stage of tumor invasion. Following the surgery, various drainage tubes are inserted to facilitate the removal blood and effusion [20]. Additionally, tracheotomy and tracheal cannulas are placed, which can damage the airway epithelial cells. The placement of catheters stimulates the vagus nerve, leading to increased mucus secretion [21]. Furthermore, the reluctance or inability to cough effectively results in inadequate airway clearance, causing sputum to accumulate and increasing its viscosity. To address this, medical staff should implement appropriate postoperative analgesic strategies and choose a comfortable and stable drainage tube fixation technique. Patients should also be instructed on effective coughing techniques to facilitate airway clearance and reduce sputum viscosity.

Ca2+ content affects postoperative sputum viscosity in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors

Ca²⁺ content was identified as a factor influencing sputum viscosity. The DNA of bacteria in the sputum forms a gel network by crosslinking with Ca²⁺ ions and glycoproteins, resulting in increased sputum thickness. Higher Ca²⁺ levels lead to a greater formation of these gel networks, thereby thickening the sputum. Although some studies have shown a correlation between sputum pH and PCT content with sputum viscosity, the present study did not observe such a correlation (P > 0.05), indicating that further research is needed to validate these aspects [8, 22–24].

Prediction model of postoperative sputum viscosity in patients with malignant laryngeal tumors exhibited a high diagnostic value

The prediction model demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy, as evidenced by the area under the ROC curve and the P value from the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit test. The agreement between predicted and observed values of postoperative sputum viscosity indicated the model’s reliability. The inclusion of Ca2+ content and other laboratory-related parameters, such as PCT and sputum pH, further enhanced the model’s accuracy. These findings suggest that this prediction model can be utilized to identify the risk factors for postoperative sputum viscosity in the early stages of hospitalization for patients with malignant laryngeal tumors, enabling effective prevention and precise interventions.

Conclusion

This study developed a predictive model to assess postoperative sputum viscosity risks in laryngeal malignancy patients. The model demonstrated excellent performance with high sensitivity, specificity, and good fit, providing reliable predictive accuracy. It serves as a valuable tool for early risk assessment of postoperative viscous sputum in these patients. In conclusion, using this predictive model enables early identification of sputum viscosity risk factors, which can reduce postoperative complications in elderly patients caused by thick sputum, thereby shortening hospital stays and further decreasing medical expenditures. However, some limitations exist in this research. The sample was predominantly male, resulting in gender imbalance. We recommend increasing the sample size and expanding the study scope to address this issue.
