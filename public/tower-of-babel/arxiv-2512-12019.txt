Robust series linearization of nonlinear advection-diffusion equations
T. Forrest Kieffer, Jakob Cupp, John S. Van Dyke, Paraj Titum, Michael L. Wall

Abstract
We consider nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) for advection-diffusion processes which are augmented by an auxiliary parameter δ \delta such that δ = 0 \delta=0 corresponds to linear advection-diffusion. We derive potentially non-perturbative series expansions in δ \delta that provide a process to obtain the solution of the nonlinear PDE through solving a hierarchical system of linear, forced PDEs with the forcing terms dependent on solutions at lower orders in the hierarchy. We rigorously detail our approach for a particular deformation that interpolates between linear advection-diffusion and the canonical Burgers’ equation modeling nonlinear advection. In this case, we prove that the series has infinite radius of convergence for arbitrary integrable initial data, analyze the cases of a Dirac-delta initial condition (IC) (i.e., the fundamental solution) in an infinite domain and arbitrary IC in a periodic domain, and demonstrate the approach to turbulent behavior in a scenario with periodic forcing. We then treat models of nonlinear diffusion involving the p p -Laplacian operator, including generalizations of the Poisson equation in 1 1 and 2 2 dimensions, and the heat equation in 1 + 1 1+1 dimensions. We detail series expansions for two different deformations of these equations about their linear (ordinary Laplacian) counterparts, providing numerical evidence for the convergence of the series outside of a perturbative regime and demonstrating that the rate and radius of convergence are affected by choice of deformation. Our results provide a rigorous foundation for using series expansion techniques to study nonlinear advection-diffusion PDEs, opening new pathways for analysis and potential applications for quantum-assisted computational fluid dynamics.



I
Introduction

Advection-diffusion equations, in which the concentration of a conserved quantity (e.g., particles or energy) is moved throughout a domain through transport (advection) and diffusion, occur ubiquitously in the physical sciences and contain many paradigmatic examples of parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs)
15
. In a general sense, we can parameterize advection-diffusion equations in
d
dimensions as the following equation for a density
u:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\!\left({\mathbf{j}(u,\nabla u)}\right)\,,

(1)

together with initial data
u(0,x)=g(x)
. We can interpret
\mathbf{j}
as a flux of the conserved quantity, and several well-known instances of advection-diffusion follow from this interpretation. For example, if we consider the total flux to be the sum of an advective flux
\mathbf{j}_{a}=\mathbf{v}u
with velocity
\mathbf{v}
and a diffusive flux given by Fick’s law
\mathbf{j}_{d}=-\nu\nabla u
with
\nu
the diffusivity (taken to be scalar for simplicity), we find the
linear advection-diffusion equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla u-\nu\Delta u\,,

(2)

where
\Delta=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}^{2}
denotes the Laplace operator on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The linearity of this equation as a PDE is inherited from the total flux
\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{j}_{a}+\mathbf{j}_{d}
being a linear function of
u
and its gradient, but nonlinear generalizations are possible. Specializing to
1
dimension and considering the case in which the advective velocity is proportional to the concentration,
{j}_{a}=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}
, we obtain the canonical
Burgers’ equation
for nonlinear advection

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=u\nabla u-\nu\Delta u\,.

(3)

In a similar vein, if we consider a case in which the diffusivity depends on the concentration gradient to some power, we obtain the
p
-Laplacian evolution equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{p}(u)

(4)

in which

\displaystyle\Delta_{p}(u)=\mathrm{div}\!\left({|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u}\right)

(5)

is the
p
-Laplacian operator, defined for
p\in\left(1,\infty\right)
, and
|\cdot|
denotes the standard Euclidean norm of a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When
p=2
, the
p
-Laplacian is just the regular Laplace operator and (
4
) reduces to the heat equation for linear diffusion.

Nonlinear advection occurs even for simple, Newtonian, incompressible fluids through the celebrated Navier-Stokes (NS) equations. Burgers’ equation Eq. (
3
) arises as a simplification of NS through ignoring the pressure term, which additionally enables the equation to be consistently studied in one spatial dimension, in contrast to full NS. Nonlinear diffusion also arises in fluid dynamics through large-eddy simulation (LES) models
19
, in which equations of fluid mechanics (e.g., NS) are filtered through a linear operator
u\to\bar{u}
which commutes with constant-coefficient differential operators. Qualitatively, the action of the filtering operator is to separate out the large-distance and small-distance scales, and so should produce a set of equations for the behavior at large scales with a source term feeding in the effects of smaller scales, perhaps phenomenologically. Applying the filtering operator to the NS equations produces a system of equations that looks again like NS in
\bar{u}
, but with an additional diffusion term represented by the divergence of the the so-called subgrid-scale tensor
T\left(\nabla u\right)=\overline{\nabla u\otimes\nabla u}-\overline{\nabla u}\otimes\overline{\nabla u}
. Ideally, the subgrid-scale tensor should be expressible as a function of
\bar{u}
alone (i.e., make no reference to
u
) in order to simplify the problem, but such an
exact closure
does not actually reduce the number of degrees of freedom, a situation called the
closure paradox

19
. Hence, practical LES approaches look for accurate models for
T\left(\overline{\nabla u}\right)
that are not expected to be exact but retain nice properties (e.g., that the filtered equations are provably well-posed). The LES model of Smagorinsky
38
amounts to taking

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}\left(\nabla u\right)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{2}\left|D\right|D\,,

(6)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\left[\nabla u+\left(\nabla u\right)^{T}\right]/2\,,

(7)

with
\varepsilon>0
chosen to reproduce the physics of the underlying microscopic equations, such as the
k^{-5/3}
scaling of energy-versus-wavenumber
k
in NS. The Smagorinsky model falls into the more general class of Ladyzenskaja models whose subgrid-scale diffusion tensor takes the form
T\left(\nabla u\right)=\beta\left(\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}\right)\nabla u
where
\beta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is monotone increasing and
c_{1}x^{\mu}\leq\beta(x)\leq c_{2}x^{\mu}
for
\mu\geq 1/4
. Ladyzenskaja models enjoy the property that they produce a well-posed system of equations that converge to weak solutions of NS in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. We also see that Ladyzenskaja models with
\beta
a pure power law produce the
p
-Laplacian as a diffusion operator with
p\geq 2.5
(Smagorinsky is the case
p=3
). While these two examples highlight important cases where nonlinear advection-diffusion arises in fluid dynamics, they are by no means exhaustive; Burgers’ equation appears in many other contexts to include condensed matter, nonlinear acoustics, and cosmology
17
, and the
p
-Laplacian appears in contexts of image processing, optimal transport, sandpile growth, and others
14
.

Given the ubiquity of nonlinear advection-diffusion phenomena, there is considerable interest in solving nonlinear PDEs of the form Eq. (
1
) and a range of methods have been developed. Perhaps the simplest is direct numerical simulation (DNS), in which the solution is obtained in high spatial and temporal resolution without relying on additional models or uncontrolled approximations. Finite difference or finite element methods on fine grids applied directly to the PDE are examples of DNS approaches. However, as is well-known, phenomena such as turbulence introduce couplings between a very large range of scales, challenging the limits and the scalability of DNS applied to such problems. Even in the case of effective nonlinear models which arise from treating the large-scale dynamics at high resolution and phenomenologically treating the fine-scale dynamics (e.g., the LES models described above), the scales involved can be stressing for DNS, and care must be taken that the numerical scheme captures the properties of the solution. A relevant example arises for the finite-difference approximation to the
p
-Laplacian; the only known finite-difference approximation to the
p
-Laplacian which is monotone
12
, i.e. converges to the proper viscosity solution, involves an operator whose support
r
scales non-trivially with
p
and with the grid spacing
h
, requiring large stencils. This can be contrasted with the ordinary Laplacian, where low-order stencils with
h\sim\mathcal{O}(r)
are accurate and monotone.

Given the difficulties of DNS applied to PDEs with conventional computing, there has been significant interest in applying alternative computing paradigms such as quantum computing to the solution of PDEs, see Refs.
11
;
36
;
24
;
7
;
26
for some recent reviews. A significant consideration here is that quantum computations are expressed in terms of unitary operations and so inherently linear. A linear PDE can be related to a linear system of equations through discretization as in DNS approaches, and then known quantum algorithms for linear systems such as the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) algorithm
21
;
29
can be applied. Direct application of similar ideas to nonlinear PDEs requires multiple copies of a known fiducial state and incurs an exponential overhead
31
, though this overhead can be reduced for certain types of polynomial nonlinearites together with variational means of representing quantum states
24
. Hence, significant effort has been devoted to means of linearizing the PDE in a means amenable to a quantum solution, with Carleman
35
and Koopman-von Neumann
27
linearizations being prominent approaches.

In this work, we take an approach in which the nonlinear PDE is converted into an infinite hierarchy of linear PDEs by introducing a deformation of the equation controlled by a parameter
\delta
such that
\delta=0
is a linear equation and the nonlinear equation of interest is obtained at some value
\delta_{\star}
, expanding the solution of our equation as a Taylor series in
\delta
about
\delta=0
, and then matching coefficients in the series expansion through the PDE order-by-order. As shown below, the resulting PDEs are all
linear
, and form a hierarchy in which functionals of the solutions from lower order appear as forcing terms in the equations for higher-order terms. This enables the collection of tools developed for linear PDEs to be brought to bear, including analytic techniques, local yet monotone finite difference schemes, and more straightforward adaptation for quantum computing.

The idea of using series expansions applied to nonlinear PDEs is not new; Bender
et al.
considered series solutions for a particular deformation of Burgers’ equation in Ref.
6
and the related homotopy analysis method
32
has a similar philosophy of using a homotopy to interpolate between a linear PDE and a nonlinear one. Our work expands significantly beyond this body of research, demonstrating that the series approach does not have to be interpreted perturbatively through the explicit identification of a deformation for the viscous Burgers’ equation proven to have infinite radius of convergence, and exploring the convergence behavior of nonlinear models of diffusion involving the
p
-Laplacian through nonlinear deformations centered around the Laplace operator. These results provide a rigorous foundation for future studies and applications of series methods across the myriad application areas of nonlinear advection-diffusion equations.

Our work is organized as follows: in Sec.
II
we consider series methods applied to deformations of Burgers’ equation, which features linear diffusion but nonlinear advection. After presenting a methodology to obtain the hierarchy for a general homotopy connecting Burgers’ to linear advection-diffusion, we study a particular deformation in detail, and show that the associated series has infinite radius of convergence given mild constraints on the IC. We analyze the case of delta function IC to discuss practical matters of series convergence, and present an optimization we call
refeeding
– wherein a truncated series expansion is summed and used as a new IC for the series expansion technique – that improves the convergence behavior. We use these methodologies in a simulation of Burgers’ equation with forcing on a periodic domain, and see hallmark characteristics of Burgers’ turbulence
25
;
5
. In Sec.
III
we apply similar techniques to nonlinear diffusive models involving the
p
-Laplacian, studying both an evolution equation that generalizes the heat equation as well as a Dirichlet problem that generalizes Poisson’s equation. We again present a general method to derive a hierarchy of linear PDEs from a parameterized homotopy before studying two different homotopies – which we call the ordinary and the dual– in detail. Using exactly known solutions for the
p
-Laplacian Dirichlet problem in 1D and 2D and the evolution equation in 1D, we study the convergence of these two series expansions for a range of
p
and show validity outside of a naive perturbative range
p=2+\delta
,
\left|\delta\right|<1
. Finally, in Sec.
IV
, we provide a discussion and outlook. Some technical points of our exposition are presented as Appendices.

II
Nonlinear advection: deformations of Burgers’ equation

We begin with a statement of the most general form of nonlinear advection we consider, referred to as the
homotopy Burgers’ equation
(HPE). On a possibly unbounded spatial interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, the HPE refers to the semilinear Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}h(u,\delta)-\nu\partial^{2}_{x}u=f\\[5.0pt]
u(0,x)=g(x),\end{array}\right.

where
u:(0,\infty)\times I\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the unknown field considered as a function of
(t,x,\delta)
,
\nu>0
is the viscosity,
h:\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function that satisfies
h(u,0)=vu
, with
v\in\mathbb{R}
a constant, and
h(u,1)=u^{2}/2
,
f:(0,\infty)\times I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an external force, and
g:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the given IC. If
I
is bounded, then
u
may have Dirichlet or periodic boundary conditions (BCs), while if
I=\mathbb{R}
, then we demand
u(t,x;\delta)\rightarrow 0
as
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
for any
(t,\delta)\in(0,\infty)\times[0,1]
(and likewise for the initial data
g
).

The conditions imposed on
h
ensure that, as
\delta
varies, Eq. (
II
) defines a family of nonlinear advection-diffusion equations which "deforms" a linear advection-diffusion equation (
\delta=0
) into the classical Burgers’ equation, Eq. (
3
), (
\delta=1
). There are infinitely many such deformations; as an example, Ref.
6
considers
h(u,\delta)=u^{1+\delta}/(1+\delta)
with
v=1
. The special case
h(u,\delta)=(1-\delta)vu+\delta u^{2}/2
, which we will call the
linear homotopy
, will be considered in detail momentarily. It is possible (and perhaps desired) to also have
h
depend explicitly on the independent variables
(t,x)
. This does not appreciably change the following exposition and so we ignore this generalization.

Let
u(t,x;\delta)
denote the solution to (
II
). Suppose
\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)
is analytic at
\delta=0
for almost every
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times I
. Then, there exists a sequence of functions
u_{n}:(0,\infty)\times I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that, for almost every
(t,x)
,
\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)
may be expanded as a convergent series with some radius of convergence
r=r(t,x)>0
:

\displaystyle u(t,x;\delta)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\delta^{n}u_{n}(t,x).

(10)

The functions
u_{n}
are then computed via

\displaystyle u_{n}(t,x):=\frac{1}{n!}\partial_{\delta}^{n}u(t,x;\delta)\Big|_{\delta=0}.

(11)

By expanding
h
in a Taylor series about the point
(u_{0},0)
, plugging this and (
10
) into (
II
), and matching powers of
\delta
, we may derive a hierarchy of PDE each coefficient function
u_{n}
must satisfy. The first few orders are derived here, with the general case treated in Appendix
A
.

We will suppress the dependence of
u
on
x
and
t
to keep the notation uncluttered and write
u(\delta)
for
u(t,x;\delta)
. First observe that
h(u(\delta),\delta)|_{\delta=0}=h(u_{0},0)=vu_{0}
, which follows from the homotopy property of
h
. The chain rule gives that

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\delta}h(u(\delta),\delta)\right|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=h^{(1,0)}_{0}u_{1}+h^{(0,1)}_{0},

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\delta^{2}}h(u(\delta),\delta)\right|_{\delta=0}

\displaystyle=h^{(2,0)}_{0}u_{1}^{2}+2h^{(1,1)}_{0}u_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+h^{(0,2)}_{0}+2h^{(1,0)}_{0}u_{2},

where
h^{(n,m)}_{0}:=(\partial_{1}^{n}\partial_{2}^{m}h)(u_{0},0)
for integers
n,m\geq 0
. (Here,
\partial_{1}
and
\partial_{2}
indicate the partial derivatives with respect to the first and second arguments of the function, respectively.) Therefore, the first few equations in the hierarchy of PDE read

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u_{0}+v\partial_{x}u_{0}-\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0}=f\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{t}u_{1}+\partial_{x}\left(h^{(1,0)}_{0}u_{1}\right)-\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u_{1}=-\partial_{x}h^{(0,1)}_{0}\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{t}u_{2}+\partial_{x}\left(h^{(1,0)}_{0}u_{2}\right)-\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u_{2}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(h^{(2,0)}_{0}u_{1}^{2}+2h^{(1,1)}_{0}u_{1}+h^{(0,2)}_{0}\right)\\
\hskip 85.35826pt\vdots\end{array}\right.

These equations are supplemented with the ICs
u_{0}(0,x)=g(x)
and
u_{n}(0,x)=0
for
n\geq 1
. Any BCs that come along with (
II
) may, in general, be handled in an analogous way as the ICs.

An essential point is that each equation in (
II
) is
linear
for the current order but with coefficients and forcing determined by
h
and the lower orders. This pattern continues for each order, as shown explicitly in Appendix
A
. As examples, the choice
h(u,\delta)=u^{1+\delta}/(1+\delta)
yields the first-order equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{1}+\partial_{x}u_{1}-\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u_{1}=-\ln{(u_{0})}\partial_{x}u_{0},

in accordance with Ref.
6
, and the linear homotopy
h(u,\delta)=(1-\delta)vu+\delta u^{2}/2
has first-order equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{1}+v\partial_{x}u_{1}-\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u_{1}=(v-u_{0})\partial_{x}u_{0}.

Clearly, the complexity of the advection and forcing term is dependent on the choice of homotopy.

As with any nonlinear PDE, well-posedness should not be taken for granted, and conditions should be imposed on
h
to ensure that, for each
\delta\in(0,1)
, (
II
) always possesses a unique time-global solution whenever
g
and
f
are appropriately regular. To appreciate this point, we note the classical result that the nonlinear heat equation
\partial_{t}u-\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u=u^{\delta}
equipped with an arbitrary non-negative, smooth, compactly supported initial data, does not possess a time-global, non-negative and integrable solution whenever
\delta\in(1,3)

18
. The remainder of this section will only be concerned with the simple choice of linear homotopy for
h
, for which time-global well-posedness is readily addressed through an explicit representation of the solution (c.f. Eq. (
29
)). Hence, it is outside the scope of this work to determine conditions on
h
which ensure time-global well-posedness of Eq. (
II
). We refer the interested reader to
20
;
8
for global existence and blow-up results for equations of the form (
II
), and
16
;
3
;
2
for more information on well-posedness of semilinear diffusion equations in general.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{N}
denote the partial sum of the series expansion (
10
) up order
N
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{N}(t,x;\delta)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\delta^{n}u_{n}(t,x),

(16)

where each

is obtained by solving each equation in (
II
). Assume the solution of (

) is analytic in

\delta=0

\delta=1
. Then,
\mathcal{S}_{N}(t,x;1)

. A key question of interest is then: what is the rate-of-convergence of
\mathcal{S}_{N}(t,x;1)
as
N\rightarrow\infty
as a function of
(t,x)
and other problem parameters?

The remainder of this section is devoted to tackling this and closely related questions for the choice of linear homotopy
h
under different BCs, initial data, and forcing. Section
II.1
formally introduces the linear homotopy Burgers’ equation and discusses its key properties. In particular, the Cole-Hopf transform
10
;
22
is used to show that
\mathbb{C}\ni\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)\in\mathbb{C}
is a holomorphic function for every
t>0
and
almost every

x\in\mathbb{R}
. One important practical implication of this result is that the radius of convergence of the Taylor series expansion about
\delta=0
(
10
) is infinite for every
t>0
and almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, allowing us to take
\delta=1
in (
10
). Section
II.1.1
will then look at the explicit solution of (
II
) evolved from a Dirac delta-function IC. Here, we will study the rate-of-convergence of the series (
10
) as a function of the variables
t
,
x
, and
v
using the exact Taylor expansion coefficient functions
u_{n}(t,x)
.

Section
II.2
turns to (
II
) on a compact spatial domain under periodic BCs. Here, we will use spectral methods to numerically solve each equation in (
II
) up to a desired order, say
N
, in order to approximate the solution of Burgers’ through the partial sum
\mathcal{S}_{N}(t,x;1)
. The solver will be validated using a closed-form solution to Burgers’ corresponding to a cosine-squared IC and zero forcing. Then, we will introduce the important concept of refeeding, in which the truncated series expansion
\mathcal{S}_{N}(t_{f},x;1)
is obtained by integrating (

and reused as an IC to the first equation in (

This section concludes with a study of Burgers’ turbulence through the series expansion. In particular, we show that the turbulent energy-versus-wavenumber scaling characteristics of the forced Burgers’ equation

;

II.1
The linear deformation, series expansion, and proof of analyticity

For
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
, consider the
linear homotopy Burgers’ equation
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u+(1-\delta)v\partial_{x}u+\delta u\partial_{x}u-\nu\partial^{2}_{x}u=f\\[5.0pt]
u(0,x)=g(x),\end{array}\right.

where, again,
v\in\mathbb{R}
is an arbitrary constant dictating the velocity of the linear advection term. The choice of
v
will impact the convergence rate of the series expansion and, using dimensional analysis, one may identify two "natural" choices for
v
. Let
\ell_{0}
,
u_{0}
, and
t_{0}=\ell_{0}/u_{0}
be nominal length, velocity, and time scales, respectively. Define the dimensionless field
\tilde{u}(s,y)
that is a function of dimensionless variables
(s,y)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
via
\tilde{u}(s,y)=u(t_{0}s,\ell_{0}y)/u_{0}
. The equation for
\tilde{u}
then reads

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{s}\tilde{u}+(1-\delta)\dfrac{v}{u_{0}}\partial_{y}\tilde{u}+\delta\tilde{u}\partial_{y}\tilde{u}-\dfrac{1}{R_{e}}\partial^{2}_{y}\tilde{u}=\tilde{f}\\[5.0pt]
\tilde{u}(0,y)=\tilde{g}(y).\end{array}\right.

where
\tilde{f}(s,y)=f(t_{0}s,\ell_{0}y)/u_{0}
,
\tilde{g}(y)=g(\ell_{0}y)/u_{0}
, and
R_{e}=u_{0}\ell_{0}/\nu
is the
Reynolds number
. The two "natural" choices for
v
are then
v=u_{0}
or
v=\nu/\ell_{0}=u_{0}/R_{e}
. The latter choice will be shown to yield improved convergence properties of the expansion (
10
) relative to the former choice. In any case, for the remainder of this manuscript, the linear homotopy Burgers’ equation will refer to the dimensionless equation:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u+(1-\delta)v\partial_{x}u+\delta u\partial_{x}u-\dfrac{1}{R_{e}}\partial^{2}_{x}u=f\\[5.0pt]
u(0,x)=g(x).\end{array}\right.

The main problem we are concerned with here is demonstrating the analyticity of the solution
u
to (
II.1
) as a function of the parameter
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
in order to rigorously justify the use of the series expansion (
10
). To tackle this problem we specialize to the case of
I=\mathbb{R}
,
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
real-valued, and
f=0
, and argue that
\mathbb{C}\ni\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)\in\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic at
\delta=0
through the Cole-Hopf transform. Then, we consider the radius of convergence
0<r(t,x)\leq\infty
of the Taylor expansion of
\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)
about
\delta=0
and argue that
r(t,x)=\infty
for every
t>0
and
almost every

x\in\mathbb{R}
. The important implication of this result is that the Taylor series expansion of
\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)
about
\delta=0
, i.e. (
10
), is convergent for every
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
and, in particular, at
\delta=1
. Equivalently, the functions
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
that satisfy (
II
) with the linear homotopy (see Eq. (
A
) for an explicit expression) yield a convergent series
\sum_{n\geq 0}\delta^{n}u_{n}(t,x)
for every
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
and which solves the original
\delta
-dependent nonlinear PDE. This result provides rigorous justification for using the series expansion to solve Burgers’ equation (i.e., taking
\delta=1
), at least in the special case when
f=0
.

The Cole-Hopf transform refers to a function
w:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
that is related to the solution
u
of Eq. (
II.1
) via

\displaystyle u=-\frac{2}{\delta R_{e}}\partial_{x}\ln{w}.

(21)

Note that
w
is only uniquely defined up to an arbitrary time-dependent multiplicative factor. It may be readily verified that
w
satisfies the following linear PDE:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}w+(1-\delta)v\partial_{x}w-\dfrac{1}{R_{e}}\partial_{x}^{2}w=-\dfrac{\delta R_{e}}{2}(\mathcal{T}\!{f})w\\[5.0pt]
w(0,x)=\exp{\left(-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}(\mathcal{T}\!{g})(x)\right)},\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{T}:L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow C(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is the linear integral operator defined by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}\!{g})(x):=\int_{-\infty}^{x}g(y)\mathrm{d}y.

(24)

Let’s introduce the linear differential operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\delta}:=(\delta-1)v\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{R_{e}}\partial_{x}^{2}

(25)

and let
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}
denote the associated evolution semigroup for
t>0
. The action of
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}
on a function
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is the convolution of a Gaussian integral kernel against
f
:

\displaystyle(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}f)(x)=\sqrt{\frac{R_{e}}{4\pi t}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{R_{e}(x-y-(1-\delta)vt)^{2}}{4t}}f(y)\mathrm{d}y.

(26)

In this notation, the unique solution to (
II.1
) when
f=0
is provided by the formula

\displaystyle w(t,x;\delta)=(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x).

(27)

Note that Eq. (
27
) makes sense for any
\delta=\delta_{1}+i\delta_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
because, for
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, which implies
w(t,\cdot;\delta)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

With (
27
), we may transform back into a solution for
u
using (
21
). The resulting general solution of (
II.1
) with
f=0
reads

\displaystyle u(t,x;\delta)=-\frac{2}{\delta R_{e}}\partial_{x}\ln{(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)}.

(28)

Expression (
28
) is valid for any
\delta\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
since the argument in the logarithm is positive for any
\delta\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. In particular, (
28
) gives the correct solution to Burgers’ when evaluated at
\delta=1
and to linear advection-diffusion when
\delta\to 0
following an application of L’H
\hat{\mathrm{o}}
pital’s rule. These observations justify carrying out the differentiation in (
28
) and using
[\partial_{x},\mathcal{L}_{\delta}]=0
to write

\displaystyle u(t,x;\delta)=\frac{(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}ge^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)}{(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)}.

(29)

The numerator and denominator in (
29
) are separately seen to be holomorphic functions of
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
because they each involve the composition, product, and integrals involving holomorphic functions of
\delta
. Hence, the ratio in (
29
) defines a meromorphic function of
\delta
. In particular, the function
\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)
is holomorphic at
\delta=0
since the denominator is
1
there. The radius of convergence of the series expansion of
\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)
about
\delta=0
is determined by the distance to the nearest non-removable singularity. Singularities, if any, will arise as zeros of the denominator in (
29
). Hence, a desirable assertion would be that
(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)\neq 0
for any
(t,x,\delta)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
whenever
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

It is straightforward to verify that the denominator in (
29
) never vanishes for
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
because
(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)>0
in this case. Hence, the challenge is ruling out the existence of a
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
with non-zero imaginary part for which there is a
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
such that
(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)=0
. Now, note that
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\ni(x,\delta)\mapsto e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}(\mathcal{T}\!{g})(x)}\in\mathbb{C}
is never zero because
\mathcal{T}\!{g}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. A preliminary question is then whether
|(e^{\frac{t}{R_{e}}\partial_{x}^{2}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)|_{\mathbb{C}}>0
for all
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
|\cdot|_{\mathbb{C}}
indicates the complex modulus.

We have not succeeded in demonstrating this strict inequality
for all

x\in\mathbb{R}
given our stated assumptions on
g
. In fact, it is plausible that evolution of
e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}}
under the heat kernel
e^{\frac{t}{R_{e}}\partial_{x}^{2}}
produces a zero at some
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\in\mathbb{R}
, even though
|e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}(x)}|_{\mathbb{C}}>0

1
1

1

The more general claim that, given
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
|f|_{\mathbb{C}}>0
implies
|e^{t\partial_{x}^{2}}f|_{\mathbb{C}}>0
is
false
. A counterexample we know of is a superposition of the form
f(x)=(c_{1}e^{i\xi_{1}x}+c_{2}e^{i\xi_{2}x})e^{-x^{2}/2}
for appropriately chosen
c_{1},c_{2},\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. However, this counterexample is not of the form
e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}(x)}
, and so doesn’t immediately imply that the assertion
|(e^{\frac{t}{R_{e}}\partial_{x}^{2}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)|_{\mathbb{C}}>0
is false.
. However,
\mathbb{C}\ni x\mapsto(e^{\frac{t}{R_{e}}\partial_{x}^{2}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)\in\mathbb{C}
is readily seen to be an entire function, and entire functions cannot vanish on a subset of
\mathbb{C}
that contain a limit point as a consequence of the identity theorem for holomorphic functions. Consequently, the set of
x\in\mathbb{R}
for which
(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\delta}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)=(e^{\frac{t}{R_{e}}\partial_{x}^{2}}e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x-(1-\delta)vt)
vanishes must have zero measure. This, in turn, implies the radius of convergence of the Taylor expansion of
\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)
about
\delta=0
is infinite for every
t>0
and almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

The above outlined proof of analyticity demonstrates that the series expansion technique exemplified in Eq. (
II
) is not necessarily perturbative; i.e.
|\delta|
is not restricted to small values in order that the process converge. One caveat here is that we restricted ourselves to specific BCs and
f=0
. Rigorous analysis in the case of
f\neq 0
appears far more delicate. For instance, it is not even immediately clear what assumptions must be made on
f
in order to ensure the solution of Eq. (
II.1
) is holomorphic at
\delta=0
, let alone what additional restrictions must be placed on
f
for the radius of convergence of the Taylor expansion of
\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)
about
\delta=0
to include
\delta=1
. Despite the lack of rigorous results for
f\neq 0
, numerical evidence in the following subsections strongly suggest analyticity at
\delta=0
and convergence of (
10
) at
\delta=1
in situations with
f\neq 0
. Questions regarding analyticity with respect to parameters for fundamental solutions of constant coefficient
linear
PDE goes back to at least Hörmander
23
, but the mathematical literature for similar questions regarding nonlinear PDE (or even non-constant coefficient linear PDE) appears sparse.

II.1.1
Case study: The fundamental solution

As an application of our techniques, consider the case of a Dirac-delta IC supplied to (

) with
f=0
. We refer to the corresponding time-evolved field as the

. From (

), this fundamental solution reads

\displaystyle u(t,x;\delta)=-\frac{2}{\delta R_{e}}\partial_{x}\ln{\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{R_{e}(x-y-(1-\delta)t)^{2}}{4t}-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(y)}\mathrm{d}y},

where
\mathbf{1}_{A}
denotes the indicator function for the set

\displaystyle u(t,x;\delta)=\frac{2(e^{\delta R_{e}}-1)e^{-\frac{(x-(1-\delta)vt)^{2}}{4t/R_{e}}}}{\delta\sqrt{\pi R_{e}t}\left(2+(e^{\delta R_{e}}-1)\erfc{\left(\frac{x-(1-\delta)vt}{2\sqrt{t/R_{e}}}\right)}\right)}.

(31)

in the Taylor expansion of (

\displaystyle u_{1}(t,x)=\sqrt{\frac{R_{e}^{3}}{4\pi t}}e^{-\frac{R_{e}(x-vt)^{2}}{4t}}\left(\erf{\left(\frac{x-vt}{2\sqrt{t/R_{e}}}\right)}-v(x-vt)\right)

Together

and

.

With the aid of computer algebra software (e.g., Mathematica), it is possible to exactly compute

to arbitrary order using (

(

II.2
Burgers’ turbulence through the series expansion

This section will consider (
II.1
) on the spatial domain
[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
with periodic BCs. In other words, we seek the solution
u:(0,\infty)\times[0,1]\times\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
to (
II.1
) which satisfies
u(t,0;\delta)=u(t,1;\delta)
for all
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
and
t>0
. In particular, the initial data
g
must satisfy
g(0)=g(1)
. The Cole-Hopf transform (
21
) may again be employed to transform the periodic version of (
II.1
) into (
II.1
), but with the definition of
\mathcal{T}
now modified to read

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}\!{g})(x):=\int_{0}^{x}g(y)\mathrm{d}y.

(32)

The aim is to leverage the (circular) Fourier transform to solve the periodic version of (
II.1
). However, the IC in (
II.1
), i.e.
\exp{\left(-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}(\mathcal{T}\!{g})(x)\right)}
, will not be periodic unless
(\mathcal{T}\!{g})(1)=\int_{0}^{1}g=0
. This condition can always be ensured by shifting
g\mapsto g-\int_{0}^{1}g
and so we consider this condition fulfilled in the following discussion.

The (periodic) Fourier transform of a function
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
on the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is denoted by

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(k)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi ikx}h(x)\mathrm{d}x.

(33)

Note
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
ensures
\widehat{h}\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})
. Fourier inversion in this case gives the classical Fourier series

\displaystyle h(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{h}(k)e^{2\pi ikx}.

(34)

Recalling that
\widehat{\partial_{x}h}(k)=2\pi ik\widehat{h}(k)
for
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, the Fourier transform of (
II.1
) when
f=0
reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widehat{w}(k)+2\pi ik(1-\delta)v\widehat{w}(k)+\frac{4\pi^{2}k^{2}}{R_{e}}\widehat{w}(k)=0,

which is readily solved to find

\displaystyle\widehat{w}(t,k)=e^{-(2\pi ik(1-\delta)v+4\pi^{2}k^{2}/R_{e})t}\widehat{w}(0,k).

Inverting with (
34
) then yields

\displaystyle w(t,x)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{2\pi ik(x-(1-\delta)vt-y)-\frac{4\pi^{2}k^{2}t}{R_{e}}}\right)w(0,y)\mathrm{d}y.

(35)

Using the Poisson summation formula, the inner series in (
35
) defines a Gaussian distribution that has been wrapped to the circle, i.e.

\displaystyle\Phi_{2+\delta}^{t}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{R_{e}}{4\pi t}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp{\left(-\frac{R_{e}(x-(1-\delta)vt-k)^{2}}{4t}\right)}.

(36)

In terms of (
36
), we can write the solution to our periodic linear advection-diffusion equation (
II.1
) as the convolution

\displaystyle w(t,x)=(\Phi^{t}_{\delta}\ast w(0,\cdot))(x):=\int_{0}^{1}\Phi_{2+\delta}^{t}(x-y)w(0,y)\mathrm{d}y.

Returning to the Cole-Hopf transform (
21
), the solution to the periodic linear homotopy Burgers’ equation (
II.1
) reads

\displaystyle u(t,x;\delta)=-\frac{2}{\delta R_{e}}\partial_{x}\ln{\left(\Phi_{2+\delta}^{t}\ast e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}}\right)(x)}\,,

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle u(t,x;\delta)=\frac{(\Phi_{2+\delta}^{t}\ast ge^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)}{(\Phi_{2+\delta}^{t}\ast e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}\mathcal{T}\!{g}})(x)}.

(37)

Notice the similarity between (
37
) and (
29
).

As an application of the preceding analysis, and to develop a closed-form solution for benchmarking, consider the initial data
g(x)=\cos^{2}{(2\pi x)}-1/2
. To develop a closed-form solution for this IC, we rely on the Jacobi-Anger expansion:

\displaystyle e^{iz\sin{\theta}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}J_{k}(z)e^{ik\theta},

valid for
\theta\in\mathbb{C}
, where
J_{k}:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the Bessel function of the first kind of order
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
(\mathcal{T}\!{g})(x)=\sin{(4\pi x)}/8\pi
, we may apply the Jacobi-Anger expansion to arrive at the identity

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\delta R_{e}}{2}(\mathcal{T}\!{g})(x)}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{k}\left(\frac{\delta R_{e}}{16\pi}\right)e^{2\pi ik\left(2x+\frac{1}{4}\right)},

(38)

where
I_{k}:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Identity (
38
) together with formula (
37
) yields

\displaystyle u(t,x;\delta)=\frac{16\pi}{\delta R_{e}}\partial_{x}\ln{\left(I_{0}\left(\frac{\delta R_{e}}{16\pi}\right)+2\sum_{k\geq 1}I_{k}\left(\frac{\delta R_{e}}{16\pi}\right)\cos{\left(4\pi k\left(x-(1-\delta)vt+\frac{1}{8}\right)\right)}e^{-16\pi^{2}k^{2}t/R_{e}}\right)}.

(39)

As with the Dirac-delta IC from Section
II.1.1
, one may use (
39
) to compute, for example,
u_{0}(t,x)=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}u(t,x;\delta)
and
u_{1}(t,x)=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\partial_{\delta}u(t,x;\delta)
, and verify that these functions satisfy
\partial_{t}u_{0}+v\partial_{x}u_{0}-\frac{1}{R_{e}}\partial_{x}^{2}u_{0}=0
with

and
\partial_{t}u_{1}+v\partial_{x}u_{1}-\frac{1}{R_{e}}\partial_{x}^{2}u_{1}=(v-u_{0})\partial_{x}u_{0}
with
u_{1}(0,x)=0
.

In general, the equations for
u_{n}
,

, satisfy (see Eq. (
A

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\widehat{u}_{n}(t,k)+\left(2\pi ikv+\frac{4\pi^{2}k^{2}}{R_{e}}t\right)\widehat{u}_{n}(t,k)=-2\pi ik\widehat{F}_{n}(t,k)\\[5.0pt]
\widehat{u}_{n}(0,k)=0,\end{array}\right.

where

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{n}(t,k):=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi ikx}F_{n}(u_{0}(t,x),\cdots,u_{n-1}(t,x))\mathrm{d}x,

(40)

and

is given by (

) in the appendix. Using Duhamel’s formula and Fourier inversion, we arrive at a convenient representation formula for

\displaystyle u_{n}(t,x)=-2\pi i\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}ke^{2\pi ikx}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\left(2\pi ikv+\frac{4\pi^{2}k^{2}}{R_{e}}\right)(t-\tau)}\widehat{F}_{n}(\tau,k)\mathrm{d}\tau.

(41)

Formula (

II.2.1
Refeeding

Consider solving each equation in (

II.2.2
Turbulence

As an example application of the series approach to solving Burgers’ equation using the linear homotopy, we consider the periodic version of (
II.1
) on the spatial domain
[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
with forcing given by

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{k=k_{\mathrm{min}}}^{k_{\mathrm{max}}}A_{k}\sin{(2\pi kx+\phi_{k})},

(43)

where
A_{k}\in[-1,1]
and
\phi_{k}\in[0,2\pi)
are a set of amplitudes and phases, respectively, selected from uniform distributions over their respective domains. An exact, closed-form solution of (
II.1
) when
\delta=1
corresponding to this choice of forcing appears to be unknown. (See
37
for a discussion of steady-state solutions when
k_{\mathrm{min}}=k_{\mathrm{\max}}=1
. Indeed, after applying Cole-Hopf, Hill’s equation results and some progress can be made by applying Floquet theory.) Our goal here is to use the series approach to numerically approximate the resulting turbulent solution of Burgers’ through
\mathcal{S}_{N}(t,x;1)
. We will demonstrate that
\mathcal{S}_{N}(t,x;1)
possesses the correct turbulent energy-versus-wavenumber scaling in the steady-state.

Consider the time-dependent
kinetic energy functional
associated with the (periodic) Burgers’ equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}|u(t,x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

(44)

Following an application of Parseval’s identity, one may write
\mathcal{E}(t)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{u}(t,k)|_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}.

(45)

Since
u
is real-valued, the instantaneous kinetic energy
E
associated with wavenumber
k\geq 0
is taken to be

\displaystyle E(t,k)=|\widehat{u}(t,k)|_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}.

(46)

It has long been known
25
that a turbulent steady-state solution of Burgers’ satisfies the scaling
E(t,k)\sim k^{-2}
in the inertial range (this is analogous to Kolmogorov’s famous
k^{-5/3}
scaling law for the Naiver-Stokes equations). Here, the lower and upper limit of the inertial range is determined by
k_{\mathrm{min}}
and
k_{\mathrm{max}}
in (
43
), respectively.

4^{\mathrm{th}}

\mathcal{S}_{4}(5,x;1)
. The solution was evolved to

to ensure it has reached a steady-state. As expected, Figure

shows that the scaled energy spectrum

is roughly flat for

, after which viscous dissipation determines the shape of the energy spectrum.

III
Nonlinear diffusion: deformations involving the
p
-Laplacian

We now turn our attention to nonlinear diffusion processes centered around the
p
-Laplacian operator, Eq. (
5
). Similar to how the homotopy Burgers’ equation, Eq. (
II
), interpolates between linear and nonlinear advection-diffusion through a choice of deformation
H(u,\delta)
, we define the
homotopy
p
-Laplacian evolution equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u=\mathrm{div}\!\left({|\nabla u|^{h(\delta)}\nabla u}\right)\\[5.0pt]
u(0,x)=g(x),\end{array}\right.

where
u:(0,\infty)\times\Omega\times[0,\delta_{p}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the unknown field defined on a (possibly unbounded) spatial domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
considered as a function of
(t,x,\delta)
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the IC, and
h:[0,\delta_{p}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth homotopy that satisfies
h(0)=0
and
h(\delta_{p})=p-2
. With this choice, the right-hand side of Eq. (
III
) becomes the ordinary
2
-Laplacian acting on
u
at
\delta=0
and becomes the
p
-Laplacian at
\delta=\delta_{p}
.

As we did in Eqs. (
10
)-(
11
), suppose the map
\delta\mapsto u(t,x;\delta)
, where
u
is the solution to (
III
), is analytic at
\delta=0
for almost every
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\Omega
. By considering the Taylor expansion of
\delta\mapsto\partial_{t}u(\delta)-\mathrm{div}\!\left({|\nabla u(\delta)|^{h(\delta)}\nabla u(\delta)}\right)
about
\delta=0
and matching powers of
\delta
in Eq. (
III
), we can find a hierarchy of PDEs each
u_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\partial_{\delta}^{n}u(\delta)
must satisfy. The general case is treated in Appendix
B
; here we only list the first three orders:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u_{0}=0\,,\\[5.0pt]
(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u_{1}=h^{\prime}(0)\mathrm{div}\!\left({\nabla u_{0}\ln\left|\nabla u_{0}\right|}\right)\,,\\[5.0pt]
(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u_{2}=\mathrm{div}\!\left({h^{\prime}(0)\nabla u_{1}\ln\left|\nabla u_{0}\right|+\dfrac{1}{2}\nabla u_{0}\left(h^{\prime}(0)\ln^{2}\left|\nabla u_{0}\right|+2h^{\prime}(0)\frac{\langle\nabla u_{0},\nabla u_{1}\rangle}{\left|\nabla u_{0}\right|^{2}}+h^{\prime\prime}(0)\ln\left|\nabla u_{0}\right|\right)}\right)\,,\\[5.0pt]
\hskip 142.26378pt\vdots\end{array}\right.

with corresponding ICs
u_{0}(0,x)=g(x)
and
u_{n}(0,x)=0
for
n\geq 1
. Similar to what we saw in (
II
), each equation in (
III
) is a linear, inhomogeneous heat equation in the unknown function
u_{n}
:

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u_{n}=\mathrm{div}\!\left({F_{n}(\nabla u_{0},\cdots,\nabla u_{n-1})}\right),

(53)

with a forcing term
F_{n}
dependent only on lower order functions (see Appendix
B
for an explicit expression). In terms of these inhomogeneities we can provide explicit solutions to the equations. For
n=0
, we simply have

\displaystyle u_{0}(t)=e^{t\Delta}g,

(54)

where
e^{t\Delta}
denotes the heat semigroup,

\displaystyle(e^{t\Delta}g)(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H_{2}(t,x-y)g(y)\mathrm{d}y\,,

(55)

H_{2}(t,x)
is the Gaussian heat kernel

\displaystyle H_{2}(t,x)=\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}/4t}}{(4\pi t)^{d/2}}\,,

(56)

and
g
is the IC from (
III
). For
n\geq 1
, the solution is given using Duhamel’s principle as (c.f. Eq. (
42
))

\displaystyle u_{n}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\mathrm{div}\!\left({F_{n}(\nabla u_{0}(s),\cdots,\nabla u_{n-1}(s))}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

(57)

For example, the solution to the equation for
u_{1}
in (
III
) reads

\displaystyle u_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\mathrm{div}\!\left({\ln{|\nabla e^{s\Delta}g|}\nabla e^{s\Delta}g}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

(58)

In addition to the evolution equation Eq. (
4
), we will also consider the static
p
-Laplacian Dirichlet problem on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta_{p}(u)=f&\text{in }\Omega\\[5.0pt]
u=g&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
f,g:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are given a priori. Eq. (
III
) generalizes the Dirichlet problem for Poisson’s equation, which it reduces to when
p=2
. Analogously to the homotopy
p
-Laplacian evolution equation, we can define the homotopy
p
-Laplacian Dirichlet problem as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{div}\!\left({|\nabla u|^{h(\delta)}\nabla u}\right)=f&\text{in }\Omega\,,\\[5.0pt]
u=g&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
h
was defined in Eq. (
III
). We emphasize that the restriction to Dirichlet BCs is not a necessity and our ideas may be readily adapted to other types of BCs. An expansion very similar to Eq. (
III
) is available for the Dirichlet problem Eq. (
III
).

PDE involving the
p
-Laplace operator must generally be interpreted in a weak sense because of the degenerate and singular nature of the operator for
p>2
and
1<p<2
, respectively. For example, the Dirichlet problem (
III
) may be interpreted as the Euler-Lagrange equation for the functional
I_{p}:C(\Omega)\cap W^{1,p}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle I_{p}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(f(x)u(x)-\frac{1}{p}|\nabla u(x)|^{p}\right)\mathrm{d}x

with boundary data
g\in C(\overline{\Omega})
,
u-g\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
, and
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. A unique minimizer of this variational problem exists, and is actually
C^{1,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
, but, unless
p=2
, the solution is generally not
C^{2}
at points where the gradient vanishes
34
, implying (
III
) cannot be interpreted pointwise. Likewise, there are a number of results concerning the
p
-Laplacian evolution equation. Perhaps the most relevant to our exposition is the existence of a unique
u\in C((0,\infty),L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
whenever
p>2
,
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
u
is stipulated to satisfy a growth condition at infinity
13
. Again,
C^{2}
-regularity of solutions is absent.

An issue related to well-posedness is how solutions to PDEs involving the
p
-Laplacian depend on
p
. Under what conditions do we have (real) analyticity with respect to
p
? Moreover, what the is nature of the radius of convergence for the expansion of the solutions about
p=2
? Unlike our results regarding the analyticity of solutions to homotopy Burgers’ equation (
II
) as a function of homotopy parameter
\delta
, this analogous question for PDEs involving the
p
-Laplacian appears far more challenging. Here, it is important to clarify what is meant by analyticity here. We do not necessarily mean that the solution
u(x;p)
to, say, the
p
-Laplacian Dirichlet problem (
III
) satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N}(p-2)^{n}u_{n}(x)=u(x;p)

pointwise for almost every
x\in\Omega
, as what ended up being the case for the similar series expansion for the homotopy Burgers’ equation (
II
). Instead, it is more plausible that the partial sums
\sum_{n=0}^{N}(p-2)^{n}u_{n}(x)
converge to the minimizer of
I_{p}(u)
in, say,
L^{p}(\Omega)
. We will abuse the language, however, and not distinguish between these two situations when using the terminology "analytic with respect to
p
".

For the Dirichlet problem, this question of
p
-analyticity is more naturally phrased as whether minimizers of the functional
I_{p}
are (real) analytic in
p
. In
d=1
, this is readily answered in the positive by applying well-established results on the parameter dependence of solutions to nonlinear ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
9
. In fact, the Dirichlet problem (
III
) with
d=1
is simply a boundary value problem for a nonlinear ODE:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime})^{\prime}=f&\text{in }(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}\\[5.0pt]
u(a),u(b)&\text{given}.\end{array}\right.

Let
J_{p}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote the bijective function
J_{p}(x)=|x|^{p-2}x
. Then we may write the ODE (
III
) as

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(x)=J_{p}^{-1}\left(|u^{\prime}(a)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(a)+\int_{a}^{x}f(y)\mathrm{d}y\right).

(65)

Upon another integration, we have the solution to the ODE in (
III
) in terms of the data
u(a)
and
u^{\prime}(a)
. This can of course be transformed into a solution of the boundary value problem (
III
) by solving an equation for
u^{\prime}(a)
in terms of
u(b)
. In any case, one can deduce from the explicit representation of
u
in terms of
f
and the boundary data that this solution is analytic for
p\in(1,\infty)
. This argument may be extended to radial solutions of (
III
) when the domain is a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the source
f
possesses radial symmetry. As a final remark, it is worth mentioning that when
d=2
and
f=0
, the hodograph transform turns the Dirichlet problem (
III
) into a linear PDE with non-constant coefficients (see
1
for details). We suspect analyticity may be deduced from this observation, but it is not pursued further here.

In general, only much weaker results are known rigorously. For example, the author in
33
considers the stability of the Dirichlet problem (
III
) with respect to
p
by showing that
\lim_{p\rightarrow q}\|\nabla u(\cdot;p)-\nabla u(\cdot;q)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}=0
(under some suitable assumptions). Similar results available for the
p
-Laplacian evolution equation (see, e.g.,
28
). The challenge here is due, in part, to the lack of an explicit representation of solutions whose
p
-dependence may be directly analyzed. Indeed, unlike how Cole-Hopf transforms Burgers’ equation into a linear PDE, there is no (known) transform which turns the
p
-Laplacian evolution equation or Dirichlet problem into a linear PDE. (As already mentioned, the exception is hodograph transform applied to the
p
-harmonic equation in
d=2
.) This prevents us from obtaining representations of solutions with explicit
p
-dependence for general initial data in the evolution case and general sources, domains, and boundary data in the Dirichlet case. However, there are a few special cases for which closed-form solutions with explicit
p
-dependence are known, and these solutions are readily seen to be analytic with respect to
p
. We will employ some of these closed-form solutions in the sequel in order to gain insight into the properties and convergence behavior of the series expansions.

The remainder of this section will be concerned with numerically demonstrating the convergence of the series expansion and studying its convergence properties for both
p
-Laplacian evolution and Dirichlet problems. Section
III.1
begins with a discussion of the two primary choices of homotopy
h
used to generate series expansions. Section
III.2
focuses on the
p
-Laplace Dirichlet problem in
d=1
and
d=2
. Special attention is given to a known closed-form solution on the ball and our ability to approximate this function with the partial sum
\sum_{n=0}^{N}(p-2)^{n}u_{n}
, where
u_{n}
is obtained by numerically solving its corresponding linear Poisson equation in the hierarchy. Section
III.3
carries out a similar analysis for the
p
-Laplacian evolution equation.

III.1
The ordinary and dual deformations of the
p
-Laplacian and their series expansions

In this section some explicit forms for the homotopy function
h(\delta)
are considered. Perhaps the simplest is the homotopy
h(\delta)=\delta
, in which case
\delta_{p}=p-2
. In other words,
p-2
is treated as a "small parameter" in problem (
III
) and the solution is expanded in a power series about
p=2
. The first three orders in the series expansion of Eq. (
III
) with this deformation follow immediately from (
III
) with
h^{\prime}(0)=1
and
h^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
. Similarly, the same homotopy applied to the static problem Eq. (
III
) yields a hierarchy of linear Dirichlet problems with sources at each order given by similar expressions as the inhomogeneities in (
III
). In this case, we supply
u_{0}
with the same BC as in (
III
), while all other
u_{n}
are zero on the boundary. We will refer to this homotopy and the resulting series expansions as
ordinary
.

Naively, we can expect the ordinary series to converge when
|\delta|<1
, which in turn implies convergence for
1<p<3
. As noted in the introduction, the
p
-Laplacian as an operator only makes sense (as written) for
p\in[1,\infty)
, and so we may further expect that
|\delta|<1
may be required for convergence on the grounds that the operator makes sense. However, we may be interested in values of
p\geq 3
for which convergence is less clear
a priori
. A similar ordinary expansion could be performed for (
III
) with
h(\delta)
there replaced by, say,
h(\delta)+1
and the domain of convergence overlap with our desired value of
p
. However, we lose the benefit of the resulting equations in the hierarchy being linear.

These considerations motivate an alternate homotopy, which we will refer to as the
dual
. We will arrive at the homotopy by considering the notion of the Hölder conjugate
p^{\prime}
of
1\leq p\leq\infty
, defined by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1,

(66)

or, equivalently,
p=\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}
and
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}
. Note that the Hölder conjugate operation maps
[1,\infty]
to itself, and
p\geq 2
if and only if
p^{\prime}\leq 2
, with
p=p^{\prime}=2
being the fixed point. In particular, if
p\geq 3
, then
p^{\prime}\leq 3/2
. One significant aspect of
p^{\prime}
comes from the fact that the dual of the Lebesgue space
L^{p}(\Omega)
is
L^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega)
for
1<p<\infty
.
L^{2}
is self-dual, a key feature of the Hilbert space aspect of
L^{2}
.

Suppose
p^{\prime}=2+\delta
, where
|\delta|<1
is a small parameter. Then,
p-2=(2-p^{\prime})/(p^{\prime}-1)=-\delta/(1+\delta)
. Notice how
3/2<p<3
if and only if
3/2<p^{\prime}<3
. This implies that both
p^{\prime}
and
p
can be written as
2+\delta
for
|\delta|<1
when
p,p^{\prime}\in(3/2,3)
. However, if
p\geq 3
, then only
p^{\prime}=2+\delta
for
|\delta|<1
. For example, if
p=4
, then
p^{\prime}=4/3=2-2/3
and
\delta=-2/3
. It is this property of being able to explore large values of
p
while keeping
\delta
“small" (i.e., keeping the
p
-Laplace operator close to the linear Laplacian) that motivates the choice of dual homotopy

\displaystyle h(\delta)=-\frac{\delta}{1+\delta},

(67)

with
\delta_{p}=(2-p)/(p-1)
, by analogy with the Hölder dual to the ordinary homotopy. The first few orders in the dual series are obtained from Eq. (
III
) with
h^{\prime}(0)=-1
and
h^{\prime\prime}(0)=2
. A similar dual expansion can also be obtained for the Dirichlet problem.

III.2
Convergence analysis - Dirichlet problem

Figure 6:

p
-Laplacian Dirichlet problem Eq. (

).
The exact solution to the
p

), is shown for a range of

. Note that the solution is only smooth for

; values of

Consider the Dirichlet problem (
III
) on the unit ball
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
g=0
and
f=-1
. The exact (weak) solution is known and given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{p}(x)=\frac{p-1}{pd^{1/(p-1)}}(1-|x|^{p/(p-1)}).

(68)

This solution for
d=1
is plotted for a range of
p
in Fig.
6
. As
p
increases from
p=2
, the cusp at the origin becomes sharper, highlighting that
\Phi_{p}(x)
is necessarily a weak solution of (
III
) for
p>2
. (For
p\leq 2
,
\Phi_{p}\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
.) Note that we may write (
68
) in terms of
\delta
, where
p=2+\delta
(i.e., the ordinary series), as

\displaystyle\Phi_{2+\delta}(x)=\frac{1+\delta}{(2+\delta)d^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}}(1-|x|^{\frac{2+\delta}{1+\delta}}).

The map
\delta\mapsto\Phi_{2+\delta}(x)
is real analytic on
(-1,\infty)
for any
x\in B_{1}(0)
. The radius of convergence about
\delta=0
with
d=1
is
|\delta|<1
(
1<p<3
). Letting
u_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\partial_{\delta}^{n}\Phi_{2+\delta}|_{\delta=0}
, the first two terms in the Taylor series about
\delta=0
are
u_{0}(x)=(1-|x|^{2})/2d
and

\displaystyle u_{1}(x)=\dfrac{1}{4d}\left((1+\ln{d^{2}})(1-|x|^{2})+|x|^{2}\ln{|x|^{2}}\right).

(69)

Clearly,
u_{0}(x)=(1-|x|^{2})/2d
satisfies
\Delta u_{0}=-1
and
u_{0}|_{\partial B_{1}}=0
. A straightforward calculation also demonstrates that
\Delta u_{1}=-\mathrm{div}\!\left({\ln\left|\nabla u_{0}\right|\nabla u_{0}}\right)
with
u_{1}|_{\partial B_{1}}=0
(which has to be interpreted in a weak sense because
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni x\mapsto|x|^{2}\ln{|x|^{2}}\in[0,\infty)
is not
C^{2}
at
x=0
).

Reparameterizing (
68
) in terms of
p^{\prime}
reads

\displaystyle\Phi_{p(p^{\prime})}(x)=\frac{1}{p^{\prime}d^{p^{\prime}-1}}(1-|x|^{p^{\prime}}).

(70)

Note the simpler expression (
70
) takes compared to (
68
). For this dual parameterization, the first couple of expansion coefficient functions
u_{n}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{n!}\partial_{\delta}^{n}\Phi_{p(2+\delta)}|_{\delta=0}
read
u_{0}^{\prime}(x)=(1-|x|^{2})/2d
and

\displaystyle u_{1}^{\prime}(x)=-\dfrac{1}{4d}\left((1+\ln{d^{2}})(1-|x|^{2})+|x|^{2}\ln{|x|^{2}}\right).

(71)

Again, we may readily verify that
u_{0}^{\prime}
and
u_{1}^{\prime}
satisfy their respective PDEs from the hierarchy.

It is possible to compute the Taylor series coefficient functions
u_{n}
and
u_{n}^{\prime}
corresponding to (
68
) and (
70
), respectively, exactly by using well-known relationships between power series coefficients of function compositions, products, etc. and their constituents (see, e.g., Faà di Bruno’s formula (
A
)). The formula for
u_{n}
is a bit cumbersome to write out explicitly for (
68
). However, for (
70
), we have for
d=1
and
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle u_{n}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}(1-|x|^{2})}{2^{1+n}}-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{n-k}\ln^{k}(|x|)}{2^{n-k}k!}.

(72)

The expansion
\sum_{n\geq 0}(p^{\prime}-2)^{n}u_{n}^{\prime}(x)
converges for any
p^{\prime}\in(0,4)
for any
d\geq 1
. This, in particular, implies that
\Phi_{p}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(p^{\prime}(p)-2\right)^{n}u_{n}^{\prime}(x)
is valid for any
4/3<p<\infty
and any
x\in B_{1}(0)
.

The zeroth-order functions
u_{0}
are the same between ordinary and dual series, as expected, because both series are expanded about the same point
p=p^{\prime}=2
. The fact that the first order functions (
69
) and (
71
) differ by a sign can be understood by this observation and comparing Eqs. (
III
) for
h(\delta)=\delta
and
h(\delta)=-\delta/(1+\delta)

III.3
Convergence analysis - evolution equation

We now turn to the
p
-Laplacian evolution equation Eq. (
4
). Here, the case of Dirac-delta IC (i.e. the fundamental solution, also known in this context as the
Barenblatt solution

4
) is known in closed-form and reads

\displaystyle H_{p}(t,x)=t^{-k_{p}}\left(c_{p}-q_{p}|t^{-k_{p}/d}x|^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)_{+}^{\frac{p-1}{p-2}},

(76)

where

\displaystyle k_{p}=\left(p-2+\frac{p}{d}\right)^{-1}\,,

(77)

\displaystyle q_{p}=\frac{p-2}{p}\left(\frac{k_{p}}{d}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\,,

(78)

and the constant
c_{p}
is determined by the condition

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H_{p}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x=1.

(79)

In (
76
), we make the assumption that
2d/(1+d)<p<\infty
. The lower bound here comes from the singular behavior of
k_{p}
at
p=2d/(1+d)
. Note that
1\leq 2d/(1+d)<2
for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
. The subscript "
+
" in (
76
) indicates we are taking the positive part of the function in parentheses, i.e.
f_{+}(x):=\max{\{0,f(x)\}}
. The constant
c_{p}
does have a closed-form in terms of the Euler Beta function. The explicit form depends on whether
p>2
and
p<2
. For
p>2
,

\displaystyle c_{p}^{+}=q_{p}^{(p-2)k_{p}}\left(\frac{p^{\prime}(p)}{|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|\mathrm{B}\!\left(\frac{d}{p^{\prime}(p)},1+\frac{1}{2-p^{\prime}(p)}\right)}\right)^{\lambda_{p}},

(80)

and, for
p<2
,

\displaystyle c_{p}^{-}=|q_{p}|^{(p-2)k_{p}}\left(\frac{p^{\prime}(p)}{|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|\mathrm{B}\!\left(\frac{d}{p^{\prime}(p)},\frac{1}{p^{\prime}(p)-2}-\frac{d}{p^{\prime}(p)}\right)}\right)^{\lambda_{p}},

(81)

where
|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)
is the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lambda_{p}=p(p-2)k_{p}/d(p-1)
.

The fundamental solution (
76

30
. For one, it is a “similarity solution" scaling as a universal function of
x
and
t
multiplied by another function of
t
, which comes from the scaling properties of the original equation. The presence of the norm
|\cdot|
highlights the general non-

nature of the weak solution when
p>2
. Additionally, for
p>2

q_{p}>0
and Eq. (
76

. Contrariwise, when
p<2
,
q_{p}<0
and the fundamental solution is non-compactly supported. A straightforward, if tedious, application of L’H
\hat{\mathrm{o}}
pital’s rule on Eq. (

and
p\rightarrow 2^{-}

, where

is the classical heat kernel (
56

1

\displaystyle u_{1}(t,x)=\lim_{p\rightarrow 2^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}H_{p}(t,x)=\left(\ln{\sqrt{t}}-\frac{x^{4}+4x^{2}t\left(\ln{\left(\frac{16\pi t^{3}}{x^{2}}\right)}-1\right)-4t^{2}\left(\ln{(256\pi^{2})}+2\gamma-3\right)}{32t^{2}}\right)\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}},

(82)

where
\gamma
denotes Euler’s constant, with numerical value
\gamma\simeq 0.577216
. One may likewise verify that the same limit is obtained from below, i.e.
u_{1}(t,x)=\lim_{p\rightarrow 0^{-}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}H_{p}(t,x)

\partial_{t}u_{1}-\partial_{x}^{2}u_{1}=\partial_{x}(\ln{|\partial_{x}u_{0}|}\partial_{x}u_{0})
with

, the heat kernel (
56

is non-compactly supported, reflecting the fact that every equation in (
III

Solutions to the
p
-Laplacian evolution equation for
p>2
with compactly supported initial data remain compactly supported, a property known as finite speed of propagation. This can be starkly contrasted with the standard heat equation (i.e.,
p=2

-Laplacian evolution equation with

III

This implies that sequential contributions to the series outside the compact region of support must exhibit some cancellation behavior. We will see this behavior arise numerically in the analysis that follows.

To numerically solve the ordinary and dual series hierarchies for the
p

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\Delta u(t,x)+\mathrm{div}\!\left({F(t,x)}\right)\,,

\displaystyle A\dot{\mathbf{a}}

\displaystyle F(t,x)

\displaystyle\left[A-\Delta tB\right]\mathbf{a}(t+\Delta t)

IV
Discussion and outlook

We considered nonlinear advection-diffusion PDEs which are augmented by an additional parameter
\delta
such that
\delta=0
produces linear advection-diffusion, and showed how a series expansion in
\delta
matched order-by-order obtains the solution of the nonlinear PDE through solving a hierarchy of linear, forced PDEs and summing their solutions. We studied the case of nonlinear advection through Burgers’ equation in detail, and proved that a novel linear deformation encompassing linear advection-diffusion and Burgers’ equation is analytic in
\delta
and has infinite radius of convergence. We further showed how the hierarchy of linear equations in this case can be solved efficiently using spectral techniques and long times can be accessed by iteratively restarting the series expansion in a time-stepping approach we call refeeding. Our approach was shown to reproduce known exact results as well as behavior characteristic of Burgers’ turbulence in a forced scenario with no known closed-form solution. We then considered nonlinear diffusion generated by the
p
-Laplacian operator in both static and dynamic contexts. Here, we analyzed two different deformations of the nonlinear equation connecting it to linear diffusion that we called the ordinary and dual, and analyzed performance against known exact solutions for statics in
1
and
2
dimensions and dynamics in
1+1
dimensions. The dual case involves a nonlinear function of the deformation parameter
\delta
, and enables efficient simulations outside of a naive perturbative regime.

Our work sets a rigorous foundation for using series methods to study nonlinear PDEs, and opens many further avenues for research. A clear next step is to apply series methods to higher-dimensional PDEs that include both nonlinear advection and nonlinear diffusion, such as those arising from LES models, or to apply the method to directly treat the nonlinear advection in NS. While the focus of the present work was on problems with known exact solutions for benchmarking purposes, it will be interesting to see how series methods perform against DNS in challenging regimes of fluid flow. Given that our methods reduce the solution of a nonlinear PDE to a system of linear PDEs, future work will also explore means of leveraging the linear representations for quantum-enhanced computations.

While our focus has been on advection-diffusion PDEs due to their central role in pure and applied science, we note that other PDEs can be treated in a similar fashion, and we hope our work provides motivation for researchers in other fields to apply similar series approaches. Further, we note that our methods can also be applied to the solution of systems of nonlinear ODEs, for which analytic dependence of solutions on parameters of the equation is well established
9
. This contrasts with the corresponding theory for PDEs, for which results are available in the linear case
23
, but the nonlinear case appears much less developed.
