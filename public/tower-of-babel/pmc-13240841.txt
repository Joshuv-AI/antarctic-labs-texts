Defibrillation outcomes by pad placement for initially non-shockable out-of-hospital cardiac arrest
Authors: Amandalin Rock, Matthew R. Neth, Craig D. Newgard, David Dennis, Jack Nuttall, Ritu Sahni, Jonathan Jui, Mohamud R. Daya, Joshua R. Lupton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study objectiveOur objective was to determine the association between anterior-posterior (AP) and anterior-lateral (AL) pad placement and outcomes for patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) with an initial non-shockable rhythm requiring subsequent defibrillation.MethodsThis observational cohort study included patients with OHCA treated by an emergency medical services (EMS) agency from July 2019 through June 2023. We included patients with initial non-shockable rhythms who subsequently developed a shockable rhythm and received EMS defibrillation. We compared outcomes between AP and AL placement. The primary outcome was the cumulative incidence in return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) after defibrillation among those at-risk using competing risks regression, adjusting for confounding variables. We assessed secondary outcomes through multivariable logistic regression, including ROSC, ROSC at emergency department (ED) arrival, survival to admission, survival to discharge, and survival with cerebral performance category score ≤2.ResultsWe included 109 patients, 69 with AP and 40 with AL. AP, compared to AL, had significantly higher cumulative incidence of subsequent ROSC (subdistribution hazard ratio [95% CI]: 2.66 [1.04–6.84]). AP did not achieve statistically significant differences in odds (adjusted odds ratio [95% CI]) of ROSC at any time (2.18 [0.65–7.38]), ROSC at ED arrival (1.11 [0.26–4.72]), or survival to admission (2.52 [0.61–10.31]). No patients with AL (0/40) survived to discharge (versus AP 3/69 [4.3%]), precluding multivariable analysis.ConclusionLarger studies on initial pad placement for patients suffering OHCA with initial non-shockable rhythms subsequently receiving defibrillation are necessary to determine whether AP or AL is the most optimal initial strategy.

Introduction

One of the leading causes of mortality continues to be out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA).1, 2, 3 Rapid defibrillation of shockable rhythms, specifically ventricular fibrillation (VF) and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT) has been shown to improve outcomes,4 but the optimal initial pad position (i.e., anterior-posterior [AP] or anterior-lateral [AL]) to improve outcomes remains unknown. The majority of OHCA patients present with a non-shockable initial rhythm, such as asystole or pulseless electrical activity (PEA).5 A subset of these non-shockable cases will go on to develop a shockable rhythm, necessitating defibrillation, which is associated with greater odds of survival.6, 7, 8, 9 It is estimated that this occurs in about 15–26% of patients who experience OHCA and present with initially non-shockable rhythms.6 There are no studies that specifically examine differences in initial defibrillator vector and outcomes in OHCA with initial non-shockable rhythms which subsequently become shockable.

Current American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines note that either an AL or AP positioning is acceptable for defibrillation.10 This recommendation is based on research evaluating the most effective placement of pads in the cardioversion of atrial arrhythmias and not ventricular tachyarrhythmias during cardiac arrest.5 The European Resuscitation Council (ERC) also noted that there is insufficient evidence to change their current recommendation of initial AL pad placement in cardiac arrest.11 The recently completed DOSE-VF trial,12 a cluster randomized study for refractory VF/pVT cardiac arrest where subjects received either continued AL defibrillation, vector change to AP defibrillation, or double sequential external defibrillation after three failed defibrillations, noted that the AP change group had higher ROSC, survival to discharge, and termination of VF/pVT than the continued AL position group.13 It is still unclear whether the improved outcomes observed in the DOSE-VF trial were driven by the vector change itself or if the AP vector is simply superior to the AL vector. A more recent study looked specifically at the differences between AP pad placement versus AL pad placement and found that AP placement had 2.64-fold significantly higher odds of achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and cumulative incidence of ROSC among patients presenting with VF/pVT.14

Even more limited than the evidence of pad placement for cases with initial VF/pVT is the evidence regarding pad placement for cases with non-shockable initial rhythms who subsequently require defibrillation. OHCA with non-VF/pVT initial rhythms have significantly worse outcomes compared to those with initial VF/pVT.1, 2, 3 Moreover, non-VF/pVT OHCA subsequently requiring defibrillation have lower prevalence of progression to shock-refractory VF/pVT.15 Although OHCA with non-VF/pVT initial rhythms may have the same associations between pad placement and outcomes as those noted for our separately published cohort of OHCA cases with initial VF/pVT,14 this should not be assumed. Thus, our objective was to evaluate patients with non-shockable initial rhythms in OHCA who subsequently require a defibrillation to determine if their outcomes are impacted by pad positioning (AP vs. AL).

Methods

Study design

This study was a registry-based cohort study using the Portland Cardiac Arrest Epidemiologic Registry (PDX Epistry) framework and under the approval from the institutional review board of Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU).14 The methods of data collection and description of the EMS system have been previously described, and we followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.14 We included cases treated by a single emergency medical services (EMS) agency in Oregon from July 2019 until June 30, 2023.

Study setting

We included patients treated by the participating EMS agency (Tualatin Valley Fire & Rescue) in the Portland, Oregon metropolitan region. The agency is a single large suburban fire-based EMS agency covering a population of over 550,000 residents. Throughout the study period, patients were served by a single-tiered dual-advanced life support (ALS) response, which includes both an ALS fire first response and either an ALS private ambulance or ALS fire ambulance transport unit. Starting in June 2019, the EMS agency protocol and training recommended initial pad placement of AP if possible, followed by a vector change to the alternative placement (AP or AL) if there was no change after 3 consecutive failed defibrillations. Treatment protocols called for the maximum energy allowed by the device manufacturers to be used regardless of device manufacturer recommendations on initial energy choice. Double sequential external defibrillation was not permitted during the study period.

Selection of participants

This study includes patients with an initial EMS-assessed rhythm of asystole or PEA, who subsequently developed a shockable rhythm (VF or pVT) that was defibrillated as documented by the treating EMS clinicians in their patient care report (PCR). Patients with initial shockable rhythms (VF/pVT) on initial EMS rhythm assessment or an automated external defibrillator rhythm of shockable (unknown if VF/pVT) were excluded. We further excluded cardiac arrests of obvious traumatic etiology, pediatric patients (aged younger than 18-years), interfacility transfers, and patients with preexisting do-not-resuscitate (DNR) status.

Variables and measurements

The primary exposure variable was the initial pad positioning, defined as either AP or AL, as noted by the EMS clinician in the PCR and recorded for each individual defibrillation. Additional variables collected and examined included age, sex, arrest location, witness status of arrest, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation, time from call to EMS arrival, initial rhythm (PEA or asystole), time from scene-arrival or arrest (if EMS witnessed) to initial defibrillation, time from scene-arrival or arrest (if EMS witnessed) and initial epinephrine, total epinephrine doses prior to initial shock, minutes of ROSC before initial shock, and year of arrest in the cohort (year 1–4). CPR quality metrics (e.g., fraction, rate) were not available for all cases and were not used in the analysis, though use of mechanical CPR was added as an adjusting variable as part of a sensitivity analysis. Minutes of ROSC before the initial shock was coded as 0 min for patients who had no periods of ROSC between their initial arrest and initial shock whereas for patients with brief ROSC who then rearrested we used the rounded minutes not in arrest prior to the initial shock (e.g. for a patient in arrest from minutes 1–10 with ROSC from minutes 11–15 then a VF rearrest receiving a shock at minute 16, they would have 4 min of pre-shock ROSC in the model).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was obtaining ROSC at any point in time after patient received a defibrillation for a shockable rhythm. Secondary outcomes included ROSC at emergency department (ED) arrival; survival to hospital admission; survival to hospital discharge; and survival to hospital discharge with a good neurologic outcome defined as a Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score of 2 or less. Process outcomes investigated included the total number of EMS defibrillations, the overall time to ROSC (regardless of duration) relative to time of initial defibrillation, and the need to subsequently change pad positioning (vector change).

Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to evaluate the sample and unadjusted comparisons, including t-tests (after confirming normality) and Fisher’s exact tests. Our primary model, to most optimally evaluate for differences in the cumulative incidence of ROSC for patients by the primary exposure (AP or AL pad position), was a competing risks regression model with adjustment for covariates. This analysis was chosen as it allowed us to account for a change in pad position (vector change) midway through a resuscitation where thereafter it was no longer possible to achieve ROSC as a result of the initial pad position; differences in the time of termination of resuscitation across resuscitations; and differences in time to arrival at the hospital (shorter EMS resuscitation time would reduce the opportunity to achieve ROSC). We coded termination of resuscitation or change in pad position as competing risks and arrival to the hospital as a censoring event. We adjusted for age, sex, arrest location (non-public location), witness status (none, bystander, EMS), bystander CPR, initial EMS rhythm (asystole or PEA), year of enrollment in the cohort, time from 9-1-1 call to EMS arrival, time from EMS arrival (or EMS witnessed arrest) to initial epinephrine dose, total epinephrine doses before the initial defibrillation, and minutes of ROSC prior to the first defibrillation.

In addition to our primary analysis, we performed sensitivity analyses adding in a variable for advanced airway placed at the time of the initial defibrillation and also a variable for mechanical CPR use. We also present as a supplement the unadjusted Kaplan-Meier curves for ROSC over time by pad position (AP or AL) accounting for pad position change and termination of resuscitation as censoring events along with hospital arrival. Finally, we performed multivariable logistic regressions, adjusting for the same above covariates to evaluate our primary and secondary outcomes. To evaluate for any interaction between AP and AL placement and EMS-estimated patient weight, we added in interaction term to our logistic regression models. We report statistical significance based on an alpha of 0.05.

Results

Out of 2093 EMS-assessed OHCA during the study period, 758 received EMS resuscitation efforts, were non-traumatic, did not have preexisting limits to their care, and were found with initial non-shockable rhythms. Of these cases, 113 subsequently required an EMS defibrillation (14.9%) and after excluding those with missing data, a total of 109 patients (median [IQR] age, 65 [53–76] years; 41 females [37.6%]) were included (Fig. 1), with initial pad positioning documented as either AP (n = 69: median [IQR] age, 65 [53–75] years; 27 females [39.1%]) or AL (n = 40: median [IQR] age, 63 [53–77] years; 14 females [35.0%]). Four eligible patients were excluded due to missing defibrillator pad position documentation. No patients were excluded for missing outcomes or covariates. Patients with initial AL pad placement at the time of defibrillation had more bystander CPR, less asystole, and were more often treated in earlier years compared to those with AP placement (Table 1). Patients with AP placement had non-statistically significant higher unadjusted ROSC (37.7% vs. 25.0%, p = 0.208), pulses at ED arrival (17.4% vs. 15.0%, p = 0.796), survival to hospital admission Discussion

Our study presents one of the first evaluations of the association between pad placement vector and patient outcomes for patients later requiring defibrillation after an initial non-shockable OHCA. Our multivariable competing risks analysis, which affords an accounting of changes to pad placement or varied times of EMS resuscitation in the analysis, found AP pad positioning was associated with a higher instantaneous hazard for ROSC at any given time and a significantly greater cumulative incidence of ROSC after defibrillation compared to AL. Although we did not find statistically significant differences in our logistic regression models of post-defibrillation ROSC, ROSC at hospital arrival, or hospital-based outcomes, this may have been driven by the small sample size of our study.

Our team’s prior study in a separate cohort of patients with initial VF/pVT OHCA from this same EMS agency and timeframe found that AP placement had significantly greater adjusted odds of ROSC, ROSC after propensity score matching, and cumulative incidence of ROSC compared to AL placement.14 The adjusted effect sizes and direction of effect across outcomes in the current study (of cases without initial VF/pVT) were very similar to this VF/pVT cohort of 255 patients, including similar subdistribution hazard ratios for ROSC (VF/pVT cohort: 1.81 [95% CI, 1.23–2.67] vs. this non-VF/pVT cohort: 2.66 [1.04–6.84]) and similar adjusted odds ratios for post-defibrillation ROSC (VF/pVT cohort: 2.64 [1.50–4.65] vs this non-VF/pVT cohort: 2.18 [0.65–7.38]).14 In this current study, numerically there were more patient with AP placement that met our secondary outcomes, including presence of ROSC at ED arrival, survival to admission to hospital, survival at hospital discharge, and functional survival at hospital discharge. However, these outcomes did not reach statistical significance. The exploratory nature of our study and lower sample size means these null results should be interpreted with caution due to the risk of underpowering and type II error and emphasize the importance or further research into pad placement given the consistent direction of effect and potential clinical significance.

It is well established that patients who present with a shockable rhythm and receive early defibrillation have an overall greater survival.4, 9 It has also been found that patients suffering OHCA who convert from an initial non-shockable rhythm to a shockable rhythm have better outcomes.4, 6, 7, 8, 9 Thus, establishing the most effective techniques for defibrillation, whether initially shockable or later shockable, is likely to be beneficial for all cardiac arrest patients. One could infer that if AP pad placement is found to be the superior positioning for defibrillation of shockable rhythms, that it would follow that it would also be effective in patients that convert into these shockable rhythms during their resuscitation course. However, the trend towards worse outcomes in non-shockable initial OHCA would require larger sample sizes to detect statistically significant differences in ROSC by pad placement, and our study was likely underpowered to detect this survival difference.

There are several mechanisms by which AP placement may have a greater probability for defibrillation success compared to AL placement. First, AP could be a better vector allowing for a pathway that delivers a greater current to a greater proportion of the ventricular mass.16, 17 Second, AP and AL could have the same efficacy at optimal positions, but AP may allow be less error-prone a placement strategy compared to AL (e.g., the lateral pad in AL placement being placed too anteriorly).18, 19 Third, during resuscitation there may be less distortion of the pads during chest compressions in the AP positioning compared to AL, as well as physical pressure on the posterior pad by the patient’s body mass (pressure augmentation), resulting in improved chest wall adhesion for AP pads.20 Importantly, the success of defibrillation is likely multifactorial and not only associated with pad positioning. Other considerations include equipment waveform (type of biphasic waveform and nature of impedance compensation), defibrillation energy setting selection, electrode type, the timing of a shock relative to ongoing CPR or ventilations, and patient impedance as these can all impact the current that is delivered through the myocardium during a defibrillation attempt.21 Maximizing the probability of defibrillation success by refining each of these components so that the initial defibrillation has the greatest chance of success may have a significant effect on overall OHCA survivability through reducing the pulseless period.

Our current study also found that, as weight increased, the odds favoring AP over AL placement for ROSC increased, but this interaction term only reached statistical significance for ROSC at ED arrival in our small cohort. Given that our results generally align with our prior findings in VF/pVT patients, as well as evidence from in-hospital cardiac arrests due to VF/pVF that obesity was associated with a greater number of defibrillations required to achieve ROSC,22 future studies on pad placement should carefully evaluate the interaction with between patient weight and outcomes. The theoretical mechanism for this could include modification to any of the above reasons AP could, in theory, be superior to AL (e.g., more difficulty properly positioning the lateral pad in an obese patient where their chest wall depth is significantly larger).

Limitations

This study has several limitations, most notably the small sample size of this cohort at only 109 patients. Thus, the biggest limitation is the risk of a type II error where our differences, which could have great clinical significance, were not statistically significant due to sample size. Our study involves cases treated by a single fire-based EMS agency, reducing generalizability to other EMS systems. Our study could also not confirm documented pad placement from the care record, and pad placement decisions were ultimately the choice of the treating EMS clinicians. Due to the observational design of our study, there are also likely unmeasured confounders that could bias our results despite our best efforts to adjust for any known confounders, including accounting for resuscitation duration as well as the presence and duration of brief periods of ROSC prior to the initial defibrillation. We were unable to adjust for pre-shock and post-shock CPR quality metrics as these data were not available uniformly for all cases. Notably, some baseline values differed between groups that may suggestion selection bias and further unmeasured confounding (e.g. 48% vs. 65% bystander CPR and 12% vs. 5% EMS-witnessed arrests in the AP and AL groups, respectively). We did attempt to adjust for temporal trends using year (1–4) but there may remain temporal confounding we were unable to account for. Most importantly, the survival outcomes reported should be interpreted with great caution given the small numbers and use of CPC scores as a correlate to favorable outcome.

Conclusion

Patients suffering OHCA with initial non-shockable rhythms receiving a subsequent defibrillation had a higher cumulative incidence of post-defibrillation ROSC when AP, compared to AL pad placement was used among those at-risk. Importantly, in this small, single-agency cohort, there were no statistically significant differences in survival outcomes. Larger observational studies and likely randomized trials are needed to evaluate the optimal initial pad placement position for all OHCA patients.
