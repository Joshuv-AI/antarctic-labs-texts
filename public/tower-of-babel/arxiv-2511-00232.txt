Sharp inequalities between Zolotarev and Wasserstein distances in 𝒫 2 ​ ( ℝ d ) \mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Abstract
. Based on a new Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality principle for the Hessian that was recently established by the two authors, we extend the Rio inequality to any dimension d ≥ 1 d\geq\nolinebreak 1 with an optimal constant. 1.
introduction

A considerable amount of literature is devoted to constructing metric structures on sets of measures.
It begins with total variation (TVD) and then, based on the needs of statistics and information theory, moves on to the pioneering works of Kullback-Leibler divergence (KL divergence) and Hellinger-Bhattacharyya distance.
More recently, Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport theory gave rise to transportation metrics, such as the Prokhorov distance (see for instance
[
9
]
) and the Wasserstein distances
[
21
,
18
]
. In this vein, V.M. Zolotarev introduced in the 1970s the vast class of so-called ideal metrics
[
22
]
. This class includes TVD and the first Wasserstein metric.

This topic remains active today, providing a fundamental framework for several areas of research. Notably, it is used in mathematical physics to treat random measures as random variables in a Polish metric space. In statistics, it is used to evaluate rates of convergence in the Central Limit Theorem and to establish a large deviation principle for interacting particle models. It is also used in the promising field of machine learning.

In
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}

whose first and second marginals are

With his seminal paper

Preliminary results

In this section, we provide an overview of the duality result in

Let us recall that the definition of the second-order Zolotarev distance between two elements

Remark 2.1

Given a pair

\nu\succeq_{c}\mu
, the unique minimizer for
{\mathcal{V}}(\mu,\nu)
is trivially
\overline{\rho}=\nu
, while a solution to (
2.1
) is given by
\overline{u}=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
. In particular, we have
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\big({\rm{var}}(\nu)-{\rm{var}}(\mu)\big)
in this case.

2.3.
An auxiliary identity

We will now derive an alternative expression for the Zolotarev-2 distance
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)
which was not presented in the paper
[
4
]
. This expression is crucial for establishing the optimal upper bound
(
1.3
) announced in the introduction.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\overline{u}
be a solution of the problem
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)
, see (
2.1
). Then, the following formula holds true,

(2.13)

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle x,\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rangle}\,(\nu-\mu)(dx).

Proof.

Assume a solution
\overline{\pi}\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
. Owing to Theorem
2.3
, it is of the form
\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)=\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)\otimes\delta_{\overline{z}(x,y)}(dz)
, where
\overline{\gamma}\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
and
\overline{z}(x,y)=\frac{x+y}{2}+\frac{\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)}{2}
. We get,

(2.14)

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\iiint\frac{1}{2}\Bigl({\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert}^{2}\Bigr)\,\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)

(2.15)

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{1}{2}\Bigl({\left\lvert\overline{z}(x,y)-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert\overline{z}(x,y)-y\right\rvert}^{2}\Bigr)\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy).

Thanks to the martingale conditions defining the set
\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
, for any Borel function
\Phi:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
of linear growth it holds that,

(2.16)

0=\iiint{\left\langle z-x,\Phi(x)\right\rangle}\,\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)=\iint{\left\langle\overline{z}(x,y)-x,\Phi(x)\right\rangle}\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy),

and similarly we get,

(2.17)

\iint{\left\langle\overline{z}(x,y)-y,\Psi(y)\right\rangle}\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)=0.

We then deduce the following chain of equalities,

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{1}{2}\Bigl({\left\langle\overline{z}(x,y)-x,\overline{z}(x,y)-x\right\rangle}+{\left\langle\overline{z}(x,y)-y,\overline{z}(x,y)-y\right\rangle}\Bigr)\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{1}{2}\Bigl({\left\langle\overline{z}(x,y)-x,\overline{z}(x,y)\right\rangle}+{\left\langle\overline{z}(x,y)-y,\overline{z}(x,y)\right\rangle}\Bigr)\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)

\displaystyle=\iint\Big\langle\overline{z}(x,y)-\frac{x+y}{2},\overline{z}(x,y)\Big\rangle\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)=\iint\Big\langle\frac{\nabla u(y)-\nabla u(x)}{2},\overline{z}(x,y)\Big\rangle\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint\big\langle\nabla u(y),\overline{z}(x,y)\big\rangle\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)-\frac{1}{2}\iint\big\langle\nabla u(x),\overline{z}(x,y)\big\rangle\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint\big\langle\nabla u(y),x\big\rangle\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)-\frac{1}{2}\iint\big\langle\nabla u(x),y\big\rangle\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy),

where:

-

to pass from the second to the third line, we used
(
2.16
), (
2.17
) with
\Phi(x)=x
,
\Psi(y)=y
;

-

to pass to the last line, we used (
2.16
), (
2.17
) with
\Phi(x)=\nabla u(x)
and
\Psi(y)=\nabla u(y)
.

The asserted equality (
2.13
) now follows since
\overline{\gamma}\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
.
∎

3.
The lower bound inequality

We now present a sharp extension of Rio inequality to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This inequality involves the ratio
\displaystyle\tfrac{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)}{W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)}
,
which is dilation-invariant since the Wassertein distance enters with a square.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be two probability measures in
\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. Then,

(3.1)

\frac{1}{4}W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq Z_{2}(\mu,\nu),

and the constant
\frac{1}{4}
cannot be improved. Moreover, the inequality is an equality if and only if
\mu=\nu
.

Let us remark that the inequality (
3.1
) is trivially true if
[\mu]\not=[\nu]
. Indeed, in this case the supremum in
(
2.1
) is infinite and, by convention,
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=+\infty.

Proof of the lower bound.

It is enough to observe that for any triple
(x,y,z)\in({\mathbb{R}^{d}})^{3}
we have,

(3.2)

\frac{1}{2}\Bigl({\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert}^{2}\Bigr)\geq\frac{1}{4}{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{2},

while the equality holds if and only if
z=\frac{x+y}{2}
. Let
\overline{\pi}
be a solution of the three-point optimal transport formulation (
2.9
) and denote
\overline{\gamma}:=\Pi_{1,2}^{\#}\overline{\pi}
. Then, by the assertion (i) of Theorem
2.3
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\iiint\frac{1}{2}\Bigl({\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert}^{2}\Bigr)\,\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\iiint{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{2}\,\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\iint{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{2}\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\,W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu).

To obtain the last inequality, we used the admissibility condition
\overline{\pi}\in\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
which entails that
\overline{\gamma}\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
.
The optimality of the constant
\frac{1}{4}
follows from the Example
3.2
below.

Let us now pass to examining the criteria for (
3.1
) being an equality. Clearly the condition
\mu=\nu
is sufficient. To show the necessity, we observe that the equality implies that the first inequality in (
3.3
) is an equality. In turn, this means that,

(3.4)

\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)=\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)\otimes\delta_{\frac{x+y}{2}}(dz).

The two martingale conditions (
2.2
) defining the set
\Sigma(\mu,\nu)
furnish the relations,

\displaystyle 0=\iiint{\left\langle\Phi(x),z-x\right\rangle}\,\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)=\frac{1}{2}\iint{\left\langle\Phi(x),y-x\right\rangle}\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy),

\displaystyle 0=\iiint{\left\langle\Psi(y),z-y\right\rangle}\,\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)=\frac{1}{2}\iint{\left\langle\Psi(y),x-y\right\rangle}\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy),

where
\Phi,\Psi:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
are any Borel maps of linear growth.
By adding the two lines above, after taking
\Phi(x)=x
and
\Psi(y)=y
, we infer that
\iint|y-x|^{2}\,\overline{\gamma}(dxdy)=0
.
Therefore
\overline{\gamma}
must be supported on the diagonal
\Delta=\{(x,x):x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\}
. It is thus straightforward that its marginals
\mu
and
\nu
must coincide.
The proof is complete.
∎

Example 3.2
.

For
b>a>0
consider the two centred probabilities on the real line:

\mu_{a,b}=\frac{b}{a+b}\,\delta_{-a}+\frac{a}{a+b}\,\delta_{b},\qquad\nu_{a,b}=\frac{a}{a+b}\,\delta_{-b}+\frac{b}{a+b}\,\delta_{a}.

The unique transport plan with a monotone support reads,

\gamma_{\mathrm{mon}}=\frac{a}{a+b}\,\delta_{(-a,-b)}+\frac{b-a}{a+b}\,\delta_{(-a,a)}+\frac{a}{a+b}\,\delta_{(b,a)}.

It is straightforward to check that
\Pi_{1}^{\#}\gamma_{\mathrm{mon}}=\mu_{a,b}
and
\Pi_{2}^{\#}\gamma_{\mathrm{mon}}=\nu_{a,b}
.
Thus, the square of the Wasserstein distance equals,

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{a,b},\nu_{a,b})

\displaystyle=\iint{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}^{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{mon}}(dxdy)

\displaystyle=(b-a)^{2}\frac{a}{a+b}+(2a)^{2}\frac{b-a}{a+b}+(b-a)^{2}\frac{a}{a+b}=2a(b-a).

In turn, let us propose an admissible 3-plan
\overline{\pi}\in\Sigma(\mu_{a,b},\nu_{a,b})
,

\overline{\pi}=\frac{a}{a+b}\,\delta_{(-a,-b,-b)}+\frac{b-a}{a+b}\,\delta_{(-a,a,0)}+\frac{a}{a+b}\,\delta_{(b,a,b)}.

Since
\Pi_{1,2}^{\#}\overline{\pi}=\gamma_{\mathrm{mon}}
, to show the admissibility of
\overline{\pi}
it remains to check the martingale conditions. We compute,

\displaystyle\Pi_{1,3}^{\#}\overline{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{a+b}\,\delta_{(-a,-b)}+\frac{b-a}{a+b}\,\delta_{(-a,0)}+\frac{a}{a+b}\,\delta_{(b,b)}

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{a+b}\,\delta_{-a}\otimes\bigg(\frac{a}{b}\,\delta_{-b}+\frac{b-a}{b}\,\delta_{0}\bigg)+\frac{a}{a+b}\,\delta_{b}\otimes\delta_{b}.

Since the probability
\frac{a}{b}\,\delta_{-b}+\frac{b-a}{b}\,\delta_{0}
has
-a
as its barycentre, it is easy to see that the above plan is a martingale. Similarly, one shows that
\Pi_{2,3}^{\#}\overline{\pi}
is a martingale as well. We can readily compute an upper bound for the Zolotarev distance,

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\mu_{a,b},\nu_{a,b})

\displaystyle\leq\iiint\frac{1}{2}\Bigl({\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}^{2}+{\left\lvert z-y\right\rvert}^{2}\Bigr)\,\overline{\pi}(dxdydz)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(-b+a)^{2}\frac{a}{a+b}+\frac{1}{2}\big(a^{2}+a^{2}\big)\,\frac{b-a}{a+b}+\frac{1}{2}(b-a)^{2}\frac{a}{a+b}=\frac{ab}{a+b}\,(b-a).

With
a>0
fixed, we look at the asymptotic quotient when
b
converges to
a
from above,

\limsup_{b\,\searrow\,a}\frac{Z_{2}(\mu_{a,b},\nu_{a,b})}{W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{a,b},\nu_{a,b})}\leq\lim_{b\,\searrow\,a}\frac{b}{2(a+b)}=\frac{1}{4}.

This establishes the optimality of the constant
\frac{1}{4}
in the lower bound in Theorem
3.1
in dimension
d=1
. Clearly this example can be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

by taking pairs of symmetric points on a straight line. Hence it holds for every
d\geq 1
that,

\inf\left\{\frac{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)}{W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)}\,:\,\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ ,\ \mu\not=\nu\right\}=\frac{1}{4}.

Remark 3.3
.

There does not exist a reverse inequality of the kind
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq C\,W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)
holding for pairs
(\mu,\nu)
such that

[\mu]=[\nu]
. This can be checked by showing that the ratio
\frac{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu_{n})}{W^{2}_{2}(\mu,\nu_{n})}
blows up when
\mu:=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{-1})
and
\nu_{n}:=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{1+1/n}+\delta_{-1-1/n})
.
Indeed, in that case, we have
W^{2}_{2}(\mu,\nu_{n})=1/n^{2}
, while, in view of Remark
2.4
, it holds that
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu_{n})=\frac{1}{2}\big({\rm{var}}(\nu_{n})-{\rm{var}}(\mu)\big)=\frac{1}{2}\big((1+1/n)^{2}-1\big)
, which goes to zero at the rate
1/n
. This shows that a reverse inequality
cannot be true even if we confine ourselves to probability measures with supports contained in a fixed compact set
K\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Another counter-example, but with non-compact supports, is given below for the Gaussian laws.

Example 3.4
.

For two positive numbers
\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}
consider two centred Gaussians on the real line with standard deviations equal to
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, respectively, that is
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{1})
,
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{2})
.
The Wasserstein-2 distance between two Gaussian distributions admits a closed form in any dimension
d
, see e.g.
[
8
]
. In the simple case of two 1D centered Gaussians, the formula reduces to,

(3.5)

W_{2}\Big(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{1})\,,\,\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{2})\Big)={\left\lvert\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right\rvert}=\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}.

It is also well known that every pair of centred Gaussians in 1D is in convex order. More precisely, in case when
\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}
, we have,

\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{1})\,\preceq_{c}\,\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{2}).

Meanwhile, for any measures in convex order
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
, the Zolotarev distance equals
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\big({\rm{var}}(\nu)-{\rm{var}}(\mu)\big)
, cf.
[
4
, Section 4.1]
. Accordingly, for the Gaussian measures we have,

(3.6)

Z_{2}\Big(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{1})\,,\,\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{2})\Big)=\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2}}{2}.

By considering
\mu:=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\nu_{n}:=\mathcal{N}(0,1+\frac{1}{n})
, we arrive at:

\frac{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu_{n})}{W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{n})}\,=\,\frac{2n+1}{2}\,\to\,+\infty.

4.
Upper bound inequalities

From now on, we consider pairs
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
such that
[\mu]=[\nu]
. Our goal is to derive an upper bound for the ratio
\frac{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)}{W_{2}(\mu,\nu)}

as a function of the variances of
\mu
and
\nu
. More precisely, we want to establish an explicit formula for the function
h(a,b):\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by,

(4.1)

h(a,b):=\sup\left\{\frac{Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)}{W_{2}(\mu,\nu)}\,:\,\sigma_{\mu}\leq a,\ \sigma_{\nu}\leq b,\ \mu\neq\nu,\ [\mu]=[\nu]=0\right\},

where
\sigma_{\mu}=\sqrt{{\rm{var}}(\mu)}
,
\sigma_{\nu}=\sqrt{{\rm{var}}(\nu)}
are the standard deviations.
By acknowledging how the two distances scale under dilations
T_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda x
, it is straightforward to check the homogeneity property
h(\lambda a,\lambda b)=\lambda\,h(a,b)
holding for every
\lambda>0
.
On the other hand, if
a=0
(thus
\mu=\delta_{0}
), we get
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\nu}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}b^{2}
, while
W_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\sigma_{\nu}=b
. Hence
h(0,b)=h(b,0)=\frac{b}{2}
.
We are going to show that, in fact,
h
is linear, i.e.
h(a,b)=\frac{1}{2}(a+b)
. This will give the optimal upper bound stated below.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that the two probability distributions
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
share the barycentre, i.e.
[\mu]=[\nu]
. Then, we have,

(4.2)

Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{\mu}+\sigma_{\nu})\,W_{2}(\mu,\nu),

and this upper bound is optimal in the sense that
h(a,b)=\frac{1}{2}(a+b)
in (
4.1
).

Furthermore, the inequality becomes an equality if and only if the probability measures coincide up to a dilation centred at their common barycentre.

Before giving the proof which is postponed to the end of this section, we present a straightforward variant of the inequality (
4.2
)
which, in constrast, is strict whenever
\mu\not=\nu
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
, we have the following upper bound,

(4.3)

Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)\,\leq\ \sqrt{\frac{{\rm{var}}\,\mu+{\rm{var}}\,\nu}{2}}\ W_{2}(\mu,\nu),

where the inequality is an equality if and only if
\mu=\nu
.

Proof.

The inequality (
4.3
) follows from (
4.2
) and from the inequality
\frac{a+b}{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}}
.
Noticing that the latter inequality is strict if
a\not=b
, we infer that an equality in (
4.3
) implies the equality in
(
4.2
) along with
\sigma_{\mu}=\sigma_{\nu}
. Owing to the last statement of Theorem
4.1
, we conclude that
\mu=\nu
since the only possible dilation sending
\mu
to
\nu
is the identity.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

From Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
4.1
, we infer that on the subset of centered probabilities with finite second order moment,

\mathcal{P}_{2}^{(0)}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):=\Big\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\,:\,[\mu]=0\Big\},

the topologies induced by the
W_{2}
and the
Z_{2}
distances are equivalent. We thus recover the result of
[
2
]
. Clearly, by the invariance with respect to translations, the same statement holds true if we enforce any fixed barycentre
a\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, not necessarily the origin.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Without any loss of generality, we can assume that the two probabilities are centered, i.e.
[\mu]=[\nu]=0
. Let
\overline{u}
be a solution of the problem (
2.1
). As
\mu-\nu
vanishes on affine functions, it is not restrictive to assume that
\overline{u}(0)=0
and
\nabla\overline{u}(0)=0
. Since
\mathrm{lip}(\nabla\overline{u})\leq 1
, this guarantees that
{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rvert}\leq{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}
for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.

Step 1

[
proving the upper bound and the sufficient condition for equality
]
Let us take an optimal plan
\hat{\gamma}\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
realizing the distance
W_{2}(\mu,\nu)
. Thanks to Lemma
2.5
, we obtain,

(4.4)

\displaystyle 2Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\iint\Big({\left\langle y,\nabla\overline{u}(y)\right\rangle}-{\left\langle x,\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rangle}\Big)\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)

\displaystyle=\iint\Big(\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle y-x,\nabla\overline{u}(x)+\nabla\overline{u}(y)\right\rangle}+\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle x+y,\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rangle}\Big)\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint{\left\langle\nabla\overline{u}(x),y-x\right\rangle}\,\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)+\frac{1}{2}\iint{\left\langle\nabla\overline{u}(x),y-x\right\rangle}\,\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\iint{\left\langle x,\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rangle}\,\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)+\frac{1}{2}\iint{\left\langle y,\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rangle}\,\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\iint{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iint{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\left(\iint{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(y)\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iint{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\left(\iint{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iint{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\left(\iint{\left\lvert y\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iint{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{2}\mu(dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iint{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\int{\left\lvert y\right\rvert}^{2}\nu(dy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\iint{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{\gamma}(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mu}W_{2}(\mu,\nu)+\sigma_{\nu}W_{2}(\mu,\nu).

Above, the first inequality (
4.5
) uses Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in
L^{2}_{\hat{\gamma}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
four times. To obtain the second inequality (
4.6
) we used the fact that
{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rvert}\leq{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}
and
{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rvert}\leq{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}
. Eventually we pass to the last line by using the optimality of
\hat{\gamma}
and the fact that
\mu,\nu
are assumed to be centred. This proves the upper bound inequality (
4.2
) and the fact that
h
given by (
4.1
) satisfies
h(a,b)\leq\frac{1}{2}(a+b)
.

Next, in order to show that (
4.2
) is optimal in the sense that
h(a,b)=\frac{1}{2}(a+b)
, we check that the equality is saturated by all centred pairs
(\mu,\nu)

such that
\mu
and
\nu
coincide up to a dilation. Without any loss of generality, we can assume that
\nu=T_{\lambda}^{\#}\mu=(\lambda x)^{\#}\mu
with
\lambda\geq 1
. Then, it is easy to see that
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu

4
4

4

by Strassen theorem, the probability kernel
\gamma^{x}=\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}\nu+\frac{1}{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda x}
renders
\gamma=\mu\otimes\gamma^{x}
an element of
\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}(\mu,\nu)
. Accordingly, as pointed out in Remark
2.4
, we have
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma_{\nu}^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{\mu}\big)=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda^{2}-1)\,\sigma^{2}_{\mu}
. On the other hand, the transport map
T_{\lambda}=\nabla\big(\frac{\lambda}{2}{\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert}^{2}\big)
is a gradient of a convex function. Therefore, by Brenier theorem
[

]
,
W_{2}(\mu,\nu)=(\int{\left\lvert T_{\lambda}(x)-x\right\rvert}^{2}\mu(dx))^{1/2}=(\lambda-1)\,\sigma_{\mu}
.
It follows that
Z_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda+1)\,\sigma_{\mu}\,W_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{\mu}+\sigma_{\nu})\,W_{2}(\mu,\nu).

Step 2

[
necessary condition for equality
]
Now we start with the assumption that (
4.2
) is an equality for some pair
(\mu,\nu)
. We may assume without any loss of generality that
\sigma_{\nu}\geq\sigma_{\mu}
. Furthermore, we can exclude the cases when either
\mu=\delta_{x_{0}}
or
\mu=\nu
since both trivially satisfy the dilation condition. Accordingly, we can assume that both
\sigma_{\mu}
and
\sigma_{\nu}
are strictly positive, and we want to show that
\nu=T_{\lambda}^{\#}\mu
for a suitable
\lambda\in(1,+\infty)
.

Going back to the chain of relations starting from (
4.4
), we see that the equality in (
4.2
) implies that the inequalities in (
4.5
) and in (
4.6
) are actually equalities. From the equality in (
4.6
), we infer that, for
\hat{\gamma}
-a.e.
(x,y)
, we have,

(4.7)

{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rvert}={\left\lvert x\right\rvert},\qquad{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(y)\right\rvert}={\left\lvert y\right\rvert},\qquad{\left\lvert\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)\right\rvert}={\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert},

By the continuity of
\nabla\overline{u}
, the equalities above hold true for every

. Since
\mu\not=\nu
,
x-y
is not zero as an element of

L^{2}_{\hat{\gamma}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, and the same can be deduced for the function

\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)
which shares the same norm by vitue of the third equality in (
4.7
).
With that information at our disposal, we may identify the conditions under which the inequality (
4.5
) is an equality. The four Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities in the vector valued space
L^{2}_{\hat{\gamma}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{d}})
must be equalities, which means that for any
(x,y)\in\spt\hat{\gamma}
,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\nabla\overline{u}(x)=a\,(y-x),\qquad

\displaystyle\nabla\overline{u}(y)=b\,(y-x),

(4.9)

\displaystyle x=c\,\big(\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)\big),\qquad

\displaystyle y=e\,\big(\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)\big),

for suitable constants
a,b,c,e\geq 0
which
do not depend
on
x,y
. The condition
\sigma_{\mu}>0
implies that the projection of
\spt\hat{\gamma}
on the first component does no reduce to
\{0\}
. Hence, from the first equality in (
4.9
), we infer that
c
must be strictly positive.
The equalities in the sequel of the proof will hold true for each pair
(x,y)\in\spt\hat{\gamma}
.
From (
4.8
), we get
\nabla\overline{u}(y)-\nabla\overline{u}(x)=(b-a)(y-x)
. Going back to (
4.7
), this means that
\varepsilon:=b-a\in\{-1,1\}
. In turn, from (
4.9
), we get
x=c\varepsilon\,(y-x)
, which we can rewrite as,

(4.10)

y=\lambda x,\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda:=1+\frac{1}{c\varepsilon}.

Therefore, for
\lambda
as above, it holds that
\hat{\gamma}=(\mathrm{id},T_{\lambda})^{\#}\mu
, hence
\nu=T_{\lambda}^{\#}\mu
.
Moreover, our assumptions
\sigma_{\nu}\geq\sigma_{\mu}
,
\mu\neq\nu
imply that
\lambda>1
, which gives
\epsilon=1
and
\mu\preceq_{c}\nu
(so that an optimal potential
\overline{u}
is given by
\overline{u}=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
).
This ends the proof.
