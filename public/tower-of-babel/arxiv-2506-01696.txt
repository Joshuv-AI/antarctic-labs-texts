Missing Data in Signal Processing & Machine Learning: Models, Methods & Modern Approaches

Abstract




Introduction

Missing data appears when parts of the data are not available for a given variable or a given observation. It is an ubiquitous problem in a wide range of scientific disciplines, including sensor networks, geophysical data analysis, radar and image processing, remote sensing, ecological statistics and biomedical studies, just to name a few
[

,

A quick search with the keyword “missing data” on IEEE Xplore, GeoRef and BioMed Central databases accumulates, respectively, 37838, 21512 and 181590 results concerning research articles, as by May, 2025. Signal processing is no exception to the rule, where missing data mainly come from sensor malfunction, hidden or impossible measurements, human errors and natural hazards, all of which can hinder a thorough understanding, analysis, and interpretation of the signal.

One of the earliest work on missing data was published in 1932 by Wilks, who mentioned the need to extract as much information as possible from fragmentary answers of questionnaires in social sciences and government statistics. Therefore, it is not surprising that the first discipline to witness this issue was mathematical statistics. This led Wilks to derive efficient estimators for the parameters of a normal bivariate distribution when the data contain missing values
[
6
]
. This work was extended to the multivariate case by Lord in 1955
[
7
]
. Since the early 1970’s, the literature in missing data has flourished with the development of computational capacity, leading to major developments in signal processing and its related fields, such as statistical inference
[
2
]
, data analysis
[
8
]
and machine learning
[
9
]
. In particular, the formulation of a missing-data theory framework by Rubin in
[
10
]
, which describes the relation between missingness and data values in the so-called missing-data mechanisms, has allowed tremendous advancements in statistical analysis.

As a consequence, in the past 30 years, a vast body of literature in signal processing (SP) & machine learning (ML) has flourished, not only with the adaptation of classical approaches to the case of incomplete signals (vectors, images, tensors, time series), but also with the development of new methodologies and algorithms in core SP & ML subfields, such as compressive sensing, parameter estimation, graph signal processing, subspace tracking, filtering, regression and classification, sparse signal recovery or time series analysis. Therefore, a tutorial paper aiming to summarize the existing and novel strategies in the SP & ML literature addressing various problems related to missing data, such as parameter estimation, matrix completion, missing data imputation and learning with missing values, as well as showing their potential applications, is an urgent
desideratum
.

This tutorial aims to provide practitioners with vital tools, in an accessible way, to answer the question:
How to deal with missing data?
There are many strategies to handle incomplete signals. In this paper, we propose to group these strategies based on three tasks commonly considered by SP & ML practitioners:

As outlined above, the objective of this tutorial is to highlight and address several key advances that have emerged from modern engineering problems faced by the SP & ML communities in real-life applications. In particular, this work focuses on three critical issues that are increasingly encountered in practice. First,
handling missing values with irregular data
: this requires taking into account the complex structure of SP & ML data settings, i.e., due to heterogeneity, outliers or model mismatch. Ignoring these factors can introduce substantial biases, distort estimates, and undermine predictive performance. A second challenge is
low-rank modeling with missing values
, which is closely related to subspace learning, principal component analysis, and matrix completion. This approach has gained prominence as an effective strategy for handling incomplete data in SP & ML problems. Based on the assumption that data matrices exhibit low-dimensional structures, these techniques enable not only the recovery of missing entries but also provide computationally and memory-efficient solutions in high-dimensional contexts.
Finally, a particularly critical but often overlooked issue is the case of
informative missingness
, where the absence of data conveys information about the underlying signal. For example, a sensor might fail to report values above a given threshold, making the missingness informative. If unaccounted for, such mechanisms can severely bias the results.

Structure of the tutorial
: Rather than organizing the manuscript strictly around these aforementioned SP & ML challenges, we have chosen to structure the tutorial based on the strategies and methodologies recommended for handling incomplete data. This approach improves clarity by presenting general methods first, before illustrating their application to specific SP & ML problems. It also highlights methodological connections between seemingly distinct challenges. In addition, it facilitates the integration of recent developments in robust, low-rank, and MNAR-aware techniques. This structure allows the tutorial to remain methodologically focused while addressing the key SP & ML challenges within each methodological category enhancing accessibility for a broader readership.

The missing-data pattern can be classified into six categories:
a)
univariate
missing data, which is widely encountered in environmental monitoring applications when a single sensor (e.g., humidity sensor) fails to report its readings over time; b) the
multivariate
missing data is experienced in wearable health devices sensing multiple physiological signals (e.g., heart rate, skin temperature, blood pressure), which may be missing simultaneously due to a transmission loss;
c) a common example of the
monotone
missing data scenario is large sensors sensing, when multiple antenna elements receive signals from one or several sources, data may be missing due to progressive sensor failures or communication dropout; d) The
file-matching
pattern can occur in multimodal contexts where at least two sets of variables are never jointly observed. This is often seen in situations where two sensing systems observe the same phenomenon but operate at different sampling frequencies; e)
general
pattern is a typical pattern that is present in imaging when some pixels or regions are missing due to occlusion across multiple images samples and regions in a non-structured way; f) finally, the
random
pattern can be any mix of the aforementioned patterns.

Missing data imputation

Removing samples that contain at least one missing value is a widespread practice in data analysis (commonly named as
deletion
or
complete-case analysis
). However, this approach is only justifiable when the
remaining data is representative of the original data and when the proportion of missing values is small. For example, if a filter deletes all transient spikes of an electroencephalogram (EEG), critical event-related information may be lost, altering the interpretation of neural activity.

A common approach when dealing with incomplete signals is to impute the missing values. This strategy is often used to obtain a complete dataset, but it can also serve as a preliminary or initialization step before applying standard techniques that require fully observed data, such as parameter estimation.

In this section, we examine various imputation strategies, whether incomplete data is represented by a matrix
\bm{X}
or by a graph
{\cal G}
. We also consider the well-known matrix completion problem, i.e., the case of recovering
\bm{X}
when it is low-rank.

A small tour of imputation methods

Naive imputation methods

Single imputation methods are techniques that predict each missing value individually. The most common approach is mean imputation: for each variable in data
\bm{X}
, the missing entries are replaced with the mean of the observed values of that variable. This method is very simple to implement, but it does not account for relationships between variables. To better preserve the empirical distribution of the data, one can instead predict missing values using the conditional mean given the observed values, with a random residual added to account for the noise in the data.

Other simple imputation methods predict missing values using observed values from similar individuals in the dataset. A method commonly used in data science is the
k
-nearest neighbors (
k
-NN) algorithm
[
19
]
. For each individual, missing values are predicted by averaging the values of their nearest neighbors. This requires defining a distance metric between individuals, computed over the variables they have in common. For example, the distance can be defined as the scaled Euclidean distance:

d(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}_{l})=\sqrt{\frac{p}{|S_{jl}|}\sum_{i\in S_{jl}}(X_{i,j}-X_{i,l})^{2}},

where
S_{jl}
denotes the set of variables for which both observations
j
and
l
are observed.
It also requires selecting a value for
k
, the number of neighbors to consider. When many variables are missing, the Euclidean distance in the
k
-NN algorithm is computed over a limited subset of features, which may significantly reduce its effectiveness.

While the simple methods presented above have clear limitations, they can serve as useful reference baselines, or even as practical imputation strategies when the proportion of missing data is very low.

Model-based imputation methods

Model-based imputation methods can generally be divided into two categories
[
8
, Sections 4.4–4.6]
. The first category referred to as iterative methods, aims to directly model the conditional distribution
f(\bm{x}_{\sf m}\mid\bm{x}_{\sf o})
without requiring a full specification of the joint distribution
f(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x}_{\sf o},\bm{x}_{\sf m})
(with
\bm{x}_{\sf o}
and
\bm{x}_{\sf m}
, the observed and missing part of the data, respectively).
The second category,
assumes a distribution for the complete data matrix
f(\bm{x})

and imputes the missing values for the
j
-th observation,
{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}
, by sampling from the conditional distribution given the observed data, i.e.,
{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}\sim f(\bm{x}_{\sf m}\mid\bm{x}_{\sf o})
.

In iterative methods, the process begins with a naive initialization of the missing values. Then, until convergence, the method alternates the following two steps across the variables: (i) learn a model to predict
[\bm{X}]_{i,:}
using the other variables, denoted as
[\bm{X}]_{-i,:}
, as predictors, based only on the samples where
[\bm{X}]_{i,:}
was initially observed; and (ii) use this model to impute the missing values in
[\bm{X}]_{i,:}
. The prediction model can be, for instance, a random forest
[
20
]
or a stochastic regression model, as in the multivariate imputation by
chained equation (MICE)
[
8
]
. These methods are highly flexible, adapting to various data types, but can be computationally intensive, precisely because a separate model must be trained for each variable with missing data, i.e., for each conditional distribution
f([\bm{X}]_{i,:}\mid[\bm{X}]_{-i,:})
.

In approaches that rely on a full generative model, the complete data
\bm{X}
may, for example, be assumed to follow a multivariate Gaussian distribution,
\bm{x}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
,
j=1,\dots,n
, with parameters estimated via the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm
[
21
]
(see
Estimation
section). Another approach, known as matrix completion, assumes that
\bm{X}
has a low-rank structure; this is discussed in detail in the next section. More recently, imputation methods based on deep learning have emerged, leveraging generative adversarial networks, autoencoders or diffusion models. In the
Learning
section we provide a tutorial on these methods.

Multiple imputation methods

Multiple imputation (MI) consists of generating several plausible values for each missing value, leading to several complete datasets. The analysis is then performed on each imputed dataset and the results are then combined. It is preferable because it takes into account the variability induced by imputation, unlike simple imputation methods. An example on MI of financial time series is illustrated at the end of this section.

The matrix completion problem

Matrix completion is an active area of research, with numerous applications, particularly in the recommendation systems community, and shares clear connections with missing-data imputation
[
15
]
.
Indeed, matrix completion addresses the problem of recovering a matrix
\bm{X}
from partial observations of its entries as
\bm{Y}=\bm{M}\odot\bm{X}
, by assuming that it has low-rank.
This holds, for example, in sensor array processing, where multiple antennas record signals from a small number of sources, and some measurements may be lost during transmission. By arranging the recorded signals from all sensors over time into a matrix, one typically observes a low-rank structure, as the number of underlying sources is much smaller than the number of sensors. In such cases, low-rank matrix completion techniques can effectively recover the missing sensor readings, enhancing tasks like source localization and beamforming even in the presence of incomplete data.

More specifically, exact matrix completion aims to solve the following optimization problem

\mathop{\mathsf{min}}_{\bm{X}}\mathrm{rank}(\bm{X})\quad\mathsf{s.\,t.}\quad\bm{M}\odot(\bm{X}-\bm{Y})=\bm{0}

(2)

i.e., to find a matrix
\bm{X}
with the minimal rank such that

\bm{M}\odot\bm{X}=\bm{Y}

is
satisfied.
This non-convex problem is intractable due to the rank constraint, which makes it in general NP-hard. Instead of enforcing a rank constraint, problem (
2
) can be relaxed by minimizing the nuclear norm, which yields a tractable formulation, as proposed in
[
22
]
:

\mathop{\mathsf{min}}_{\bm{X}}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{M}\odot(\bm{X}-\bm{Y})\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{X}\|_{*},

(3)

where
\|\bm{X}\|_{*}
is the nuclear norm of
\bm{X}
and
\lambda>0
controls the regularization level. By noticing that
\|\bm{X}\|_{*}=\mathop{\mathsf{min}}\limits_{\bm{U},\bm{V}:\bm{X}=\bm{U}\bm{V}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{U}\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{V}\|^{2}_{F}
, where
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
,
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
r
is the rank of
\bm{X}
, problem (
3
) can be rewritten as:

\mathop{\mathsf{min}}_{\bm{U},\bm{V}}\|\bm{M}\odot(\bm{U}\bm{V}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}-\bm{Y})\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda(\|\bm{U}\|^{2}_{F}+\|\bm{V}\|^{2}_{F}).

This new formulation is very close to the problem of retrieving an underlying subspace
\bm{U}
from the data
\bm{Y}
in the
batch
setting, i.e., when
\bm{Y}
has a fixed number of observations (columns). Chi
et al.

[
23
]
extended this problem to
online
subspace estimation with missing data, also known as subspace tracking (ST), i.e., when the original data
is gradually constructed from a data stream.

To solve the optimization problem (
3
), classical methods rely on proximal iterative algorithms based on Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), where the solutions are usually computed using a grid-search on the regularization parameter
\lambda
. More specifically, commonly used methods perform the following two steps iteratively, starting from an initial imputed data matrix
\hat{\bm{X}}^{(0)}
(for example, using the mean imputation) and a predefined rank
r
for the low-rank matrix:

(i)

Compute the rank
r
truncated SVD of
\hat{\bm{X}}^{(k)}
, i.e. compute the SVD and retain the top
r
singular values
\hat{\bm{X}}^{(k)}\overset{\text{SVD}_{r}}{=}\bm{U}_{r}^{(k)}\bm{S}_{r}^{(k)}\bm{V}_{r}^{(k){\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}}
.

(ii)

Update the imputed data matrix (only on the missing values) with the quantity computed in Step
(i)

[\hat{\bm{X}}^{(k+1)}]_{i,j}=\underbrace{\left[\left(\bm{U}_{r}^{(k)}\bm{S}_{r}^{(k)}\bm{V}_{r}^{(k){\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}}\right)\right]_{i,j}}_{\text{on missing values}}(1-M_{i,j})+\underbrace{Y_{i,j}}_{\text{on observed values}}M_{i,j}
.

Two widely used algorithms, called softImpute
[
24
]
and missMDA
[
25
]
, extend this framework by applying regularization to the singular values in Step
(i)
to better account for noisy data.

Graph Signal Recovery

Graph signal processing (GSP) is a research area focused on analyzing signals that are structured on graphs. These signals, known as graph signals, appear in various applications such as sensor networks (e.g., temperature measurements), social networks (e.g., user interactions), transportation systems (e.g., traffic patterns), biological systems (e.g., gene expression data), and financial markets (e.g., stock price data).

Consider a weighted graph with
p
nodes represented by
{\cal G}=\{{\cal E},{\cal V},\bm{W}\}
, where

{\cal V}=\{1,\ldots,p\}
is the vertex set,

{\cal E}\subseteq{\cal V}\times{\cal V}
is the edge set, and
\bm{W}
is the (weighted) adjacency matrix.
For a simple undirected graph with positively weighted edges,
\bm{W}
is a symmetric hollow matrix with non-negative entries. In that case, it is also common to represent the graph with the Laplacian matrix (or more precisely the combinatorial graph Laplacian) defined as
\bm{L}=\bm{D}-\bm{W}
, where
\bm{D}=\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{W}\bm{1})
is the diagonal degree matrix. The Laplacian matrix
of a simple undirected graph belongs to the feasible set
\Omega_{\bm{L}}
specified as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\bm{L}}=\{\bm{L}\succeq\bm{0}\mid L_{i,j}=L_{j,i}\leq 0\,\,(i\neq j),\,\,L_{i,i}\geq 0,\,\,\bm{L}\bm{1}=\bm{0}\}.

(4)

Given the underlying graph model, the
graph signal is defined as a (real/complex) function that assigns a (real/complex) value to each vertex of the graph.
Let
X_{i,j}
be the real scalar value assigned to vertex
i
at time instant (or experiment index)
j
. Then, the graph signal can be represented by
\bm{x}_{j}=[X_{1,j},\ldots,X_{p,j}]^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Given
n
(discrete) temporal samples or experimental realizations of the graph signal, we construct the data matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
through column-wise concatenation of the
\bm{x}_{j}
vectors.
In practical graph signal processing applications
, the observed data matrix often contains missing entries due to various factors. For instance, sensor networks may have absent nodal measurements caused by device malfunctions, while social networks may display partial user attributes due to privacy concerns. The imputation of missing values in such networked data, while accounting for the underlying graph topology, is known as
graph signal recovery
.
In this framework, the observations
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
can be modeled as
\bm{Y}=\bm{M}\odot\bm{X}
, exactly as for matrix completion.

Graph signal recovery can be approached in multiple ways.
One class of methods formulates signal recovery as an optimization problem, incorporating spatial-domain graph signal properties for imputation. This problem can generally be expressed as:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{X}}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{\bm{X}}d(\bm{X},\bm{Y};\bm{M})+\alpha\,g(\bm{X},\bm{W})+\beta\,h(\bm{X}),

(5)

where
\bm{W}
represents the graph adjacency matrix and

\alpha
and
\beta
are non-negative weighting parameters. The terms in this objective function are described as follows:

\circ

Fidelity term
d(\bm{X},\bm{Y};\bm{M})
: Accounts for the similarity of the reconstructed data to the available observations.
It is defined as a barrier function
d(\bm{X},\bm{Y};\bm{M})=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}{\cal I}_{B}(M_{i,j}(Y_{i,j}-X_{i,j}))
where

{\cal I}_{B}(0)=0
and, for
\forall x\neq 0
,
{\cal I}_{B}(x)=+\infty
.
In cases where the measurement may contain noise in addition to missing values, the observations could be modeled as
\bm{Y}=\bm{M}\odot(\bm{X}+\bm{N})
where
\bm{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
denotes the additive noise matrix. In such cases, the common choice for the fidelity function
is the norm of the residual as
d(\bm{X},\bm{Y};\bm{M})=\left\lVert\bm{M}\odot(\bm{Y}-\bm{X})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
or one could also use the Huber-loss function to allow for outliers, i.e.,
d(\bm{X},\bm{Y};\bm{M})=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}H_{\delta}(M_{i,j}(Y_{i,j}-X_{i,j}))
where
H_{\delta}(\cdot)
denotes the Huber-loss function with the slope parameter
\delta
defined as

H_{\delta}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|\leq\delta}+\delta\left(|x|-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>\delta}.

\circ

Smoothness term
g(\bm{X},\bm{W})
: Quantifies how well the data matrix
\bm{X}
conforms to the graph structure encoded in
\bm{W}
. For an undirected graph with non-negative edge weights, a common choice for
g(\bm{X},\bm{W})
is the graph-based (spatial) smoothness criterion, also known as the
graph Tikhonov regularization defined as follows
[
26
]
:

g(\bm{X},\bm{W})=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\bm{X}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}(\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{W}\bm{1})-\bm{W})\bm{X}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}W_{i,j}\left\lVert[\bm{X}]_{i,:}-[\bm{X}]_{j,:}\right\rVert^{2}.

Here
W_{i,j}\geq 0
denotes the edge weight between nodes
i
and
j
.
Based on this notion, a graph signal is considered smooth when the signal values at connected nodes are similar, with the degree of similarity proportional to the weight of connection between the two nodes. In other words, the larger
W_{i,j}
, the smaller the distance between
i
-th and
j
-th signal elements should be.

Another choice for
g(\bm{X},\bm{W})
is the spatio-temporal smoothness criterion, which applies to graph signals with temporal dimension (where the columns of the data matrix represent the time samples). This criterion generalizes spatial graph-based smoothness by additionally enforcing temporal smoothness in the signal’s evolution
[
27
]
. It is formulated by

\displaystyle g(\bm{X},\bm{W})=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\bm{\Delta}(\bm{X})^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}(\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{W}\bm{1})-\bm{W})\bm{\Delta}(\bm{X})\right),

where
\Delta(\bm{X})
is the temporal difference matrix defined as
\Delta(\bm{X})=[\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}-\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}-\bm{x}_{n-1}]
.

Several works also employ an
L_{1}
-norm variant of graph-based smoothness, suitable for edge-preserving denoising, called graph total variation, defined as

\displaystyle g(\bm{X},\bm{W})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}W_{i,j}\left\lVert[\bm{X}]_{i,:}-[\bm{X}]_{j,:}\right\rVert_{1}.

The above formulations apply to undirected graphs with symmetric and non-negative
\bm{W}
. For directed graphs, however, a slightly different notion of smoothness can be used by measuring the variations of a signal across the directed edges of the graph, defined as
[
28
]

\displaystyle g(\bm{X},\bm{W})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\lVert\bm{x}_{j}-\frac{1}{\left\lVert\bm{W}\right\rVert}\bm{W}\bm{x}_{j}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}.

This formulation, with
p=1
and
p=2
, gives alternative formulations for graph total variation and Tikhonov regularization for directed graphs, respectively.

\circ

Regularization term
h(\bm{X})
: Serves as a signal regularization criterion. It
is used to promote various properties of the data matrix, including bounded energy via Frobenius norm (as
h(\bm{X})=\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
) or low-rank structure via rank or nuclear norm function (as
h(\bm{X})=\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{*}
)
[
27
]
.

missing values using different smoothness criteria.

Multiple Imputation of Financial Time Series

Financial data of traded assets often contain missing values due to a variety of reasons such as non-trading periods, lack of liquidity, data collection gaps, data handling errors, etc. Traditional interpolation methods for imputation include the last observation carried forward (LOCF), which simply repeats the last observed price, or linear interpolation of prices (equivalent to mean interpolation of returns).

Generally speaking, imputation methods rely on some additional structural knowledge on the data. Examples include the low-rank property in recommender systems and the local geometry similarity in images for inpainting. In the case of a financial time series, we can rely on the temporal structure. This can be done, for example, with the
MICE method
[

]

by properly reengineering lagged time series in the input matrix. Alternatively, the temporal structure can be directly exploited based on a specific time series model, such as the random walk or autoregressive models. Assuming that the model has been fitted to the available observations, it can then be used in a Bayesian approach to model statistically the missing values, which can then be imputed in a very realistic way
[

]
. Fig.

It is clear from the figures that the model-based statistical imputation of the missing values performs very well. We now delineate the way the method works.
To capture the temporal structure as well as the non-Gaussian nature of financial data, the following autoregressive (AR) model is considered:

\displaystyle x_{t}=\mu+ax_{t-1}+\epsilon_{t},

(6)

where
x_{t}
denotes the value of the time series at time

,
\mu
is the so-called drift parameter,

is the AR coefficient, and

\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathrm{t}(0,\sigma^{2},\nu)

and degrees of freedom

(which controls the thickness of the tails).
The parameters of this model are then represented by
\bm{\theta}=(a,\mu,\sigma,\nu)
.

The crux of this approach lies in the estimation of
\bm{\theta}
. In the absence of missing values and with a Gaussian distributed innovation, this would be simply a least squares problem, whereas under the Student’s
t
-distribution, the estimation would become a bit more involved but still manageable.
However, once we allow missing values, the maximum likelihood estimator formulation is

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{MLE}}=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}}\,\,\log f(\bm{x}_{\sf o}|\bm{\theta})=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}}\,\,\log\left(\int\prod_{t=2}^{n}f(x_{t}|x_{t-1},\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{x}_{\sf m}\right),

(7)

where the whole time series vector

This problem is very difficult to manage because the integral does not admit a closed-form expression due to the Student’s

Estimation with Missing Data under a Parametric Model Framework

In this section, we address estimation and inference in the presence of missing data, operating under a parametric model that reflects the underlying data-generating process. The primary methodological approach involves constructing a cost function derived from either maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation, depending on the nature of the unknown parameter of interest. The latter approach can be further regularized to incorporate structural assumptions or prior knowledge available to the practitioner (such as low-rank constraints, parameter restrictions, or other domain-specific information.)

Probabilistic missing-data mechanism

The way data is missing and the cause of missingness greatly influences the choice of appropriate statistical inference techniques. The aim of this subsection is to define fundamental concepts for handling missing data that are needed for statistical inference,
i.e.
, missing-data mechanism and ignorability concept.
Before the seminal work of
[
10
]
, the missing-data pattern
\bm{M}
was considered as a deterministic parameter and was ignored in the statistical analysis.
As it will be shown in the following, considering the missing pattern as a random variable fundamentally changed the theory and practice of working with incomplete data. It allows for more flexibility and provides a framework for dealing with uncertainty in the missingness itself. However, the missing-data pattern does not describe the relation between missing and observed values or, in other words, the cause of missingness which is characterized by the so-called missing-data mechanism.

Definition 1: Missing-data mechanism
Assume that the missing-data pattern
\bm{M}\sim f(\bm{M}|\bm{\phi})
, where
f(\cdot)
characterizes the probability mass function of
\bm{M}
with
\bm{\phi}\in\Omega_{\bm{\phi}}
. Three scenarios can be considered:

•

Missing completely at random
(MCAR), which happens when the probability of a value being missing is
independent of the data values, i.e.,

f(\bm{M}|\bm{X},\bm{\phi})=f(\bm{M}|\bm{\phi})
.

•

Missing at random
(MAR) when the occurrence of missing values
depends only on the observed values, i.e.,

f(\bm{M}|\bm{X},\bm{\phi})=f(\bm{M}|\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{\phi})
.

•

Missing not at random
(MNAR) when the probability of being missing depends on the values of missing variables, and possibly on the observed ones.

The typology of the missing-data mechanisms is important, as it partly determines the choice of methods to be used. However, in most cases, the parameter
\bm{\phi}
of the missing-data mechanism is not the primary target of estimation: statistical inference typically aims to estimate the parameter
of the data distribution
\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}
. This leads us to the notion of ignorability, a key concept in missing data analysis that determines whether the missing-data mechanism must be explicitly modeled in order to perform valid statistical inference. Specifically, when the missing-data mechanism is ignorable, likelihood-based can proceed without explicitly modeling it, thereby simplifying the analysis.

Definition 2: Ignorable missing-data mechanism for likelihood inference

A missing-data mechanism is ignorable for likelihood inference if the estimates of

\bm{\theta}
obtained as the MLE from
f(\bm{X}_{\sf o}|\bm{\theta})
and
f(\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{M}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
are the same.

It is worth-mentioning that the maximum likelihood in the case of MCAR or MAR simplifies as follow

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{\rm MLE},\bm{\phi}_{\rm MLE}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}}}\log f(\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{M}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}}}\log\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\sf m}}f(\bm{X}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})f(\bm{M}|\bm{X},\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})d\bm{X}_{\sf m}

\displaystyle\overset{\rm MCAR/MAR}{=}\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}}}\log\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\sf m}}f(\bm{X}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})d\bm{X}_{\sf m}f(\bm{M}|\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})

\displaystyle\overset{(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}){\rm\ are\ {distinct}}}{=}\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}}\log f(\bm{X}_{\sf o}|\theta)+\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\phi}\in\Omega_{\bm{\phi}}}\log f(\bm{M}|\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{\phi}),

(8)

with
f(\bm{X}_{\sf o}|\bm{\theta})=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\sf m}}f(\bm{X}|\bm{\theta})d\bm{X}_{\sf m}
denotes the observed likelihood.
Consequently, from (
8
), one can state the following Lemma.

Lemma 1: Ignorability in the context of likelihood inference

The missing-data mechanism is said to be ignorable for likelihood inference if

•

The missing-data mechanism is MCAR or MAR

•

The parameters
(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
are distinct, i.e.,

\Omega_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}}=\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}\times\Omega_{\bm{\phi}}

The rest of this section presents methods in the MCAR and MAR cases, unless explicitly mentioned.

Mechanisms Governing Missing Data

Let us consider a health monitoring system that collects two types of data over time: heart rate (which is measured using a wearable device) and blood pressure (which can be measured using a cuff-based monitor).
The goal is to record over time these data for continuous health tracking. Nevertheless, the data could be missing for a variety of reasons. In the following we will explore the three mechanisms governing missing data. i) The first scenario that can lead to missing data is the fact that the wearable device occasionally experiences technical failures, such as a battery depletion or an unexpected signal dropout. These failures lead to missing measurements without any relationship to the actual values being recorded. In this case, the missingness is MCAR and the remaining data are representative of the whole data, as in Fig.
Probabilistic missing-data mechanism
.
Ignoring the missing entries or performing straightforward imputation, such as replacing missing values with the mean or median of the observed data can be a valuable solution.
ii) The second scenario occurs when missing data depends on an observed variable, such as the age (e.g.,
older patients may have health issues that make them less likely to complete the blood pressure measurement regularly). In this case, the missingness is MAR and is an ignorable mechanism, c.f., Fig.
4
. More sophisticated
algorithms can be used to estimate the missing values based on the relationship between the observed and missing data.
iii) Finally, consider a situation where patients may avoid checking their blood pressure when they know it is very high, possibly due to anxiety or a sense of denial about their health. This creates a situation where blood pressure values are more likely to be missing when the actual blood pressure is higher. From Fig.

Statistical estimation

Let us assume that the parametric model associated with a data-generating process and the missing mechanism for the
j
-th sample reads
f(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{m}_{j}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
, in which
\bm{\theta}
represents the deterministic unknown parameter of interest (e.g., the mean
\bm{\mu}
belonging to the set of real or complex numbers or the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
belonging to
the semidefinite positive matrices) and
\bm{\phi}
is the set of parameters governing the missing mechanism.
Under
ignorable
missing-data mechanisms with multiple independent samples, the
MLE takes the form:

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MLE}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}}\sum_{j}\log\int f({\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|\bm{\theta})d{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}.

Solving the latter in closed form expression is generally infeasible.
An exception is the MEL of the mean and/or the covariance matrix of a multivariate normal distribution from incomplete data with a monotone pattern and under MCAR or MAR missing mechanism
[
2
]
.
In most cases,
maximum likelihood estimates from incomplete data can be obtained using the EM
algorithm or its variants. The EM algorithm is a natural choice for missing data problems because it considers missing values as latent variables. In the E-step, the EM computes their conditional expectation, while the M-step updates parameters by maximizing this expectation. This approach avoids ad-hoc imputation, preserves uncertainty in missing data, and guarantees convergence to a local MLE. Its versatility extends to exponential families and complex models, making it a foundational tool in statistical inference with missingness. Specifically, at step
k=0
, we initialize the unknown parameter using a naive imputation method one of the imputation approaches listed above, to obtain
\bm{\theta}^{(0)}
. Then, the EM iterates between the following E- and M-steps until convergence:

•

E-step (Expectation)
: It consists of computing a surrogate function denoted by
Q(.)
, which
is the conditional expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood conditioned to
the observed data and the previous estimate of the parameter
\bm{\theta}^{(k)}
. The complete-data are in general the collection of observed data and latent data, in our case
\bm{X}_{\sf m},\bm{X}_{\sf o}
, i.e.,

Q(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(k)})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}_{\sf m}\sim f(\bm{X}_{\sf m}|\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{\theta}^{(k)})}[\log f(\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{X}_{\sf m}|\bm{\theta})]

(9)

•

M-step (Maximization)
: Updates the unknown parameter by maximizing the surrogate function

\bm{\theta}^{(k+1)}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}}Q(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(k)}).

(10)

The EM algorithm offers several advantages, including ease of implementation, a clear statistical interpretation of each step, and a general guarantee of convergence. However, a significant drawback is that a high proportion of missing information can result in a slow convergence rate. In the following, we briefly discuss different strategies to compute the EM in several scenarios.

The Gaussian distribution case

Let us assume that the samples
{\bm{x}}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,n
, are independent and that
{\bm{x}}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
. Under MAR/MCAR, the surrogate function of the EM algorithm reads

Q(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(k)})\propto n\log\det(\bm{\Sigma})-\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}\sim f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\theta}^{(k)})}[(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{\mu})^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{\mu})].

First, since
\bm{x}_{j}
contains the observed values
{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j}
and the missing ones
{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}
, the expectation is done only w.r.t. the missing values. Second, the term under the expectation contains the sufficient statistics
{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}
and

{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}
: consequently, being able to compute
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}\sim f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\theta}^{(k)})}[{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}]
and

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}\sim f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\theta}^{(k)})}[{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}]
gives us a closed form expression of
Q(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(k)})
. In the Gaussian case, the aforementioned expectation can be easily derived by noticing that

{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\sf m|o},\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf m|o})
. Here, the rectified mean and the
covariance matrix read
\bm{\mu}_{\sf m|o}=\bm{\mu}_{\sf m,m}-\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf m,o}\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf o,o}^{-1}({\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,i}-\bm{\mu}_{\sf o,o})
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf m|o}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf m,m}-\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf m,o}\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf o,o}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf o,m}
in which

\bm{\mu}_{\sf m,m}
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf m,m}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\sf m,o}
denote respectively, the mean of the missing values, the covariance matrix of the missing values and the covariance between
{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}
and
{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}

[
2

.

for unknown

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}\sim f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|\tau_{j},{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\theta}^{(k)})}[\log f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j},{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j}|\tau_{j},\bm{\theta})]

Namely, instead of computing the full expectation, it approximates it using random samples, often via Monte Carlo methods. This allows the algorithm to proceed even when the exact E-step is infeasible (e.g., due to a complex density generator function), enabling efficient parameter estimation while maintaining convergence properties under certain conditions. Several stochastic versions of the EM algorithm have been developed to address such kind of situation. To cite a few, stochastic EM (SEM) replaces the full E-step with a sample-based approximation, often using a single or a few random draws from the conditional distribution of the latent variables. This allows the algorithm to handle large or complex datasets when computing the exact expectation is impractical.
Monte Carlo EM (MCEM) improves this by using Monte Carlo sampling to approximate the E-step with multiple samples, providing a more accurate estimate of the expectation. It is especially useful when analytical solutions are unavailable but sampling is feasible.

MAP with missing values

The case of the MAP could be viewed as an extension of the MLE. Specifically, the MAP reads

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MAP}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}}\sum_{j}\log\int f({\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|\bm{\theta})d{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}+\log f(\bm{\theta})

in which
f(\bm{\theta})
denotes the prior information. In this case, we can notice that the E-step of the EM algorithm remains the same (the expectation is w.r.t. the latent variable only), whereas the M-step becomes

{\bm{\theta}}_{\rm MAP}^{(k+1)}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}}Q(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(k)})+\log f(\bm{\theta})

Due to the presence of the
a priori
, the M-step might be difficult to compute. We briefly review some variants of the EM algorithm, c.f., Table.

. The key difference among these lies in how the M-step is carried out. In classical EM, the M-step involves a full joint maximization over all parameters, which can become computationally expensive or even intractable for complex models. The ECM (Expectation Conditional Maximization) algorithm addresses this by decomposing the M-step into a series of simpler conditional maximizations over parameter blocks. ECME (Expectation Conditional Maximization Either) extends this idea by replacing some of these conditional steps with direct maximization of the observed-data log-likelihood, often resulting in faster convergence. Lastly, the GEM (Generalized EM) algorithm relaxes the requirement of full maximization entirely, requiring only that the surrogate function increases at each iteration. This flexibility allows for the use of partial updates or gradient-based methods when exact optimization is not feasible. These variants are particularly valuable in high-dimensional, constrained, or non-convex problems where the classical EM algorithm may not be practical
[
2
]
.

Statistical inference with non-ignorable mechanism

As discussed above, in the case of MNAR, considering only the observed likelihood can dramatically bias the estimation, since the observations are not representative of the complete data. In this situation, it is necessary to consider the joint distribution
f(\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{M}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
. Two important distinctions arise, depending on whether the missingness mechanism is non-ignorable but known (as in censored data with a known threshold, e.g., due to a sensor’s operating range) or non-ignorable unknown, in which case
\bm{\phi}
must be estimated jointly with
\bm{\theta}
.
In the following, we focus on the latter scenario, under the assumption of independent observations, i.e.,
f(\bm{X},\bm{M}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\prod_{i}\prod_{j}f(X_{i,j},M_{i,j}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
.
Several models can be used to infer under non-ignorable mechanism depending on the choice of target parameters. In the following, we consider the so-called
selection
model since it is more appropriate in the SP context since the marginal distribution of the data is of interest. Specifically, the
selection
model reads

f(X_{i,j},M_{i,j}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=f(X_{i,j}|\bm{\theta})f(M_{i,j}|X_{i,j},\bm{\phi}).

(12)

This requires external knowledge of the data model (e.g., a regression model for observed and missing outcomes) and a missingness mechanism (e.g., a logistic or probit model for the missingness probabilities). This formulation is often considered the most natural approach. Under parametric assumptions, modeling the data distribution also tends to be straightforward.

The MLE in the MNAR context (namely, considering the
joint distribution
f(\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{M}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
)
is unfeasible. Then, we use the EM algorithm which reads

•

Under independence, the surrogate function becomes

\displaystyle Q(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta}^{(k)},\bm{\phi}^{(k)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}\sim f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,i},\bm{m}_{j},\bm{\theta}^{(k)},\bm{\phi}^{(k)})}[\log f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j},{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{m}_{j}|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{i}M_{i,j}(\log f(X_{i,j}|\bm{\theta})+\log f(M_{i,j}|X_{i,j},\bm{\phi}))

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ +(1-M_{i,j})\,\mathbb{E}_{X_{i,j}\sim f(X_{i,j}|M_{i,j},\bm{\theta}^{(k)},\bm{\phi}^{(k)})}[\log f(X_{i,j}|\bm{\theta})+\log f(M_{i,j}|X_{i,j},\bm{\phi})]

Obtaining closed form expression of
Q(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta}^{(k)},\bm{\phi}^{(k)})
is rarely possible and stochastic variants of the EM (see Table.
III
) are commonly employed in this scenario. As an example, the SEM reads

\displaystyle Q^{\rm SEM}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}|\bm{\theta}^{(k)},\bm{\phi}^{(k)})=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}

\displaystyle M_{i,j}(\log f(X_{i,j}|\bm{\theta})+\log f(M_{i,j}|X_{i,j},\bm{\phi}))

\displaystyle+(1-M_{i,j})(\log f(X_{i,j}^{(k)}|\bm{\theta})+\log f(M_{i,j}|X_{i,j}^{(k)},\bm{\phi}))

with
X_{i,j}^{(k)}\sim f(X_{i,j}|M_{i,j},\bm{\theta}^{(k)},\bm{\phi}^{(k)}).

A natural choice of the conditional distribution of mask is

f(M_{i,j}|\bm{X},\bm{\phi})=(h(X_{i,j},\bm{\phi}))^{M_{i,j}}(1-h(X_{i,j},\bm{\phi}))^{1-M_{i,j}}

with
h(.)
any specific function.

On the other hand, the M-step for the SEM

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{(k+1)}

Estimation with prior information on the parameter structures

Many signal processing applications can benefit from an available
prior knowledge
on the unknown signal or statistical parameter to be estimated. This knowledge can be mainly obtained from
i)
physical considerations on the observed phenomenon,
ii)
previous estimates of the signal of interest or
iii)
by taking advantage of the fact that dependencies between variables can be established, which is often the case with large-scale, high-dimensional data.
In this section, we present recent developments in both parameter and signal estimation with prior structures in the context of incomplete data. This includes, but not limited to, specific structures on the parameter (e.g., spectral, linear or group symmetric structures on the covariance matrix
[
3
]
) and constraints on the parameter space (e.g., the data lies in a subspace of dimension
r\ll p
).
These signals are included in numerous modern science and engineering applications, such as climate data analysis, medical imaging, neurological signal processing, computer vision, remote sensing, and clutter tracking in radar and sonar processing, to name a few. Some examples include:

•

Signals from uniform linear arrays, which exhibit a Toeplitz-structured covariance matrix
[
32
]
;

•

The output of a sensor network can often be highly correlated and approximated using a low-rank matrix
[
33
]
(see also, the many examples of low-rank signals provided in
[
15
]
);

•

Enforcing special structures (e.g., spectral constraints on the adjacency or Laplacian matrices) on certain graph estimates is known to help in interpreting and identifying structures in the data
[
34
]
.

In the context of incomplete data, classical estimation approaches that leverage these priors are inefficient because they assume the data to be complete. In this section, we will discuss recent developments that extend structured parameter and signal estimation to the case of incomplete data
[
15
,
16
]
.

Estimation of structured covariance matrix

The covariance matrix plays a major role in numerous SP & ML applications. From a statistical standpoint, prior structural knowledge is often expressed as a constraint on the covariance matrix. In this setting, the covariance matrix can be naturally estimated using the EM algorithm, where E and M steps are given in equations (
9
) and (
10
). While the E-step remains unchanged, the M-step becomes the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bm{\theta}^{(k+1)}=&\underset{\bm{\theta}}{\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}}&Q(\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}^{(k)})\\
&\mathsf{s.\,t.}&\bm{\theta}\in{\cal S}\end{array}

where
{\cal S}
is any structure on the covariance matrix.
Several structures can be considered, e.g., spectral structures referring to constraints on the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix, linear structures of the form
{\cal S}=\{\bm{\Sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\bm{B}_{i},\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
\{\bm{B}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
is a known basis of the considered set, or group symmetric structures when the covariance matrix belongs to a set of the form
{\cal S}=\{\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{H}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{H}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}},\forall\bm{H}\in{\cal H}\}
, with
{\cal H}=\{\bm{H}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
being a multiplicative group of orthogonal matrices. One popular constraint in SP & ML assumes that the covariance matrix is the sum of a low-rank component plus an identity matrix, which can be modeled by the well-known factor model
[
35
]
:

{\cal S}_{(1)}=\Big\{\bm{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{p}+\bm{U}\bm{S}\bm{U}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}},\ \ \bm{U}\bm{S}\bm{U}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}\succeq\bm{0},\ \ \mathrm{rank}(\bm{U}\bm{S}\bm{U}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}})\leq r,\ \ \sigma^{2}>0\Big\}

where,
\bm{I}_{p}
denotes the
p
-dimensional identity matrix,
\bm{U}
is a
p\times r
matrix,
\bm{S}
is a
r\times r
diagonal matrix and
\sigma
is a strictly positive scalar. This model was originally used in
[
36
]
to estimate the disturbance covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
and noise level
\sigma
in the context of space-time adaptive processing. At step
k+1
of the EM, the solution to problem (
Estimation of structured covariance matrix
) constrained with
{\cal S}_{(1)}
is given by
[
37
]

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{(k+1)}=\hat{\sigma}^{2(k)}\bm{I}_{p}+\bm{U}^{(k)}\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(k)}\bm{U}^{{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}(k)}\quad\textrm{with}\quad\begin{cases}\hat{\sigma}^{2(k)}=\frac{1}{p-r}\sum_{i=r+1}^{p}\lambda_{i}^{(k)}\\
\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(k)}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}^{(k)}-\hat{\sigma}^{2(k)},\dots,\lambda_{r}^{(k)}-\hat{\sigma}^{2(k)}),\end{cases}

in which
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{(k)}\overset{\text{EVD}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}^{(k)}\bm{u}_{i}^{(k)}\bm{u}_{i}^{{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}(k)}
,
\;\lambda_{i}^{(k)}<\dots<\lambda_{p}^{(k)}
are the eigenvalues of
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{(k)}
and
\bm{u}_{1}^{(k)},\dots,\bm{u}_{p}^{(k)}
are the corresponding eigenvectors contained in
\bm{U}^{(k)}
.

Structure
{\cal S}_{(1)}
and the associated optimization problem have recently been adapted to the case of incomplete data in two applications, e.g.,
[
3
]
used it for detecting the optimal number of uncorrelated narrow-band sources
\hat{r}\in[0,p-1]
hitting an array from multiple directions. In this context,
\bm{S}
represents the
r\times r
diagonal source covariance matrix to be estimated,
\bm{U}
is a
p\times r
array manifold matrix and
\sigma^{2}
is the power of the white noise (unknown) perturbing the disturbance covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
.

Another model assumes that the covariance matrix has a lower bound on the
white disturbance power level:

{\cal S}_{(2)}=\Big\{\bm{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{p}+\bm{R},\ \ \bm{R}\succeq\bm{0},\ \ \sigma^{2}>\sigma_{\textsf{known}}\Big\}

where
\bm{R}
typically accounts for colored interference and clutter,
\sigma^{2}
is the power of the white disturbance term and
\sigma_{\textsf{known}}
is a lower bound on this power. In this case, the fast maximum likelihood procedure
[
38
]
provides a solution to the M-step of problem (
Estimation of structured covariance matrix
) constrained with
{\cal S}_{(2)}
:

\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{(k+1)}=\bm{U}^{(k)}\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(k)}\bm{U}^{{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}(k)}\ \ \textrm{with}\ \ \hat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(k)}=\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\lambda}_{1}^{(k)},\dots,\hat{\lambda}_{p}^{(k)})\ \ \textrm{and}\ \ \hat{\lambda}_{i}^{(k)}=\max(\lambda_{i},\sigma^{2}),\;i=1,\dots,p

where
\bm{U}^{(k)}\bm{\Lambda}^{(k)}\bm{U}^{{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}(k)}
results from the EVD of
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{(k)}
, the estimated covariance from the previous EM step. This model has been used in
[
3
]
to perform adaptive beamforming with monotone missing values in
\bm{X}
, where the covariance matrix must be estimated from incoming data to construct an adaptive beamformer. The same authors also explore other useful covariance structures for radar processing applications, such as centro-Hermitian symmetry or constrained-covariance with an upper bound to its condition number.

Subspace tracking with missing values

Robust subspace tracking (RST) is a crucial challenge in SP, which adaptively estimates and updates the subspace of a signal as it evolves over time. It concerns a wide range of applications involving high-dimensional data and time-varying systems, such as online robust PCA, adaptive filtering, DOA estimation or blind source separation, to name a few. In the past fifteen years, many techniques have flourished to adapt RST to missing data (RST-miss).
Specifically, in scenarios where the data exhibit a certain level of redundancy – such as when complete high-dimensional data can be well approximated by their projection onto a lower-dimensional subspace – incomplete data become particularly valuable, as they may still contain sufficient information thanks to this redundancy
[
16
]
.

At time
t
, the incomplete streaming observations
\{\bm{y}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are generally modeled as:

\bm{y}_{t}=\bm{m}_{t}\odot\big(\bm{x}_{t}+\bm{n}_{t}+\bm{s}_{t}\big),\;\;t\geq 1,

(15)

where
\bm{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the true signal that lies in a low dimensional subspace
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
(
r\ll p
),
\bm{n}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
stands for the noise,
\bm{s}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the (sparse) outlier vector and

\bm{m}_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
is the sampling mask vector at time
t
.
It is common to represent the true signal by
\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{U}\bm{w}_{t}
, where
\bm{w}_{t}
is an unknown weight vector.
Many of the subspace tracking algorithms are geometric approaches in the sense that they seek to minimize certain loss functions with no assumptions on the probabilistic model on the data. In this framework, the goal is to optimize a recursively updated loss function, such as the squared projection loss onto the subspace:

F_{t}(\bm{U})=\alpha_{t}F_{t-1}(\bm{U})+\beta_{t}\big\|[\bm{m}_{t}\odot({\bm{y}}_{t}-\bm{U}\bm{w}_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2},

(16)

where
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
control the trade-off between convergence rate and tracking adaptability to subspace changes, and
\bm{U}
is typically updated using the previous estimate.
Note that the right-hand side function of expression (
16
) can be regularized to promote sparsity or norm constraints on
\bm{w}
(or
\bm{U}
) using
L_{1}
-norm and
L_{2}
-norm, respectively. The minimization framework associated to this problem for the estimation of
\bm{U}
is based on the following general objective

\bm{U}_{t}=\underset{\bm{\bm{U}}}{\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}}\;F_{t}(\bm{U}).

Many of the subspace tracking algorithms handling missing data inherit from the projection approximation subspace tracking (PAST) algorithm
[
39
]
, which was originally designed for full data.
Among these approaches,
the parallel estimation and tracking by recursive least squares (PETRELS) is a second-order recursive least-squares (RLS) algorithm
[
23
]
that employs second-order stochastic gradient descent on the cost function. At each time
t
, PETRELS alternatively estimates the weight vector
\bm{w}_{t}
and subspace
\bm{U}_{t}
. In particular,
\bm{w}_{t}
is estimated by minimizing the projection residual on the previous subspace estimate
\bm{U}_{t-1}
:

\bm{w}_{t}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{\bm{w}}\big\|\bm{m}_{t}\odot({\bm{y}}_{t}-\bm{U}_{t-1}\bm{w}_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2},

(17)

where
\bm{U}_{0}
is a random subspace initialization. The subspace
\bm{U}_{t}
is then updated by minimizing

\bm{U}_{t}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{\bm{U}}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\lambda^{t-j}\big\|[\bm{m}_{j}\odot({\bm{y}}_{j}-\bm{U}\bm{w}_{j})\big\|_{2}^{2},

where
\{\bm{w}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t}
are estimates from (
17
).
For further reading, we refer to the study of Balzano et al.
[
16
]
, where authors provide an extensive overview of subspace tracking algorithms via geometric approaches, including theoretical discussions on performance analyses and a benchmark of subspace estimation errors.
Another class of algorithms that fall into the streaming PCA literature adopt a probabilistic approach to model (
15
). A recent method is the Streaming HeteroscedASTic Algorithm for PCA (SHASTA-PCA)
[
4
]
.
SHASTA-PCA employs a stochastic alternating EM approach to estimate the subspace of incomplete streaming data contaminated by heteroscedastic noise across samples. This method adopts a probabilistic view of model (
15
), where the data is contaminated with noise from
L
group levels and follows these assumptions:
\bm{w}_{t}\sim{\cal N}(\bm{0}_{r},\bm{I}_{r})
and
\bm{n}_{t}\sim{\cal N}(\bm{0}_{p},v_{g_{t}}\bm{I}_{p})
are i.i.d.,
g_{t}\in\{1,\dots,L\}
is the noise level group to which the
t
th sample belongs, and
v_{1},\dots,v_{L}
are deterministic noise variance to estimate. Note also that in this model,
\bm{s}_{t}=\bm{0}
. The factors and the variances are estimated at time
t
by maximizing the observed log-likelihood
f_{t}
:

\{\bm{U}_{t},\bm{w}_{t}\}=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{U},\bm{w}}f_{t}(\bm{y}_{\sf o}|\bm{U},\bm{w})

(18)

with
f_{t}(\bm{y}_{\sf o}|\bm{U},\bm{w})
defined as

f_{t}(\bm{y}_{\sf o}|\bm{U},\bm{w})=-\frac{|O_{t}|}{2}\log v_{g_{t}}-\frac{\|\bm{m}_{t}\odot({\bm{y}}_{t}-\bm{U}\bm{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}{2v_{g_{t}}}-\frac{\|\bm{w}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}{2},

(19)

where
O_{t}
is the set of indices corresponding to observed values in
\bm{y}_{t}
.
Weights and subspace are alternatively updated as:

\bm{w}_{t}=(1-c_{w})\bm{w}_{t-1}+c_{w}\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{w}}\Psi^{(w)}_{t}(\bm{w})\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \bm{U}_{t}=(1-c_{F})\bm{U}_{t-1}+c_{U}\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{U}}\Psi^{(U)}_{t}(\bm{U}),

where
c_{w}
and
c_{U}
are positive scalars,
\Psi^{(w)}_{t}(\bm{w})
and
\Psi^{(U)}_{t}(\bm{U})
are two approximations of the minorizer for (
19
), parameterized by
\bm{w}
and
\bm{U}
, respectively (see
[
4
]
for the full expressions of
\Psi^{(w)}_{t}(\bm{w})
and
\Psi^{(U)}_{t}(\bm{U})
). Note that the objective function of PETRELS is, in fact, the maximization of the complete log-likelihood in the homoscedastic setting with respect to the factors
\bm{U}
and latent variables
\bm{w}
without the
L_{2}
penalty on
\bm{w}
. In PETRELS-ADMM
[
40
]
, however, this objective is regularized on the subspace
\bm{U}
with the
L_{2,\infty}
-norm, which aims to control the maximum
L_{2}
-norm of the rows in
\bm{U}
to avoid performances loss when the fraction of missing data is too large.

Robust subspace tracking with the PETRELS-ADMM algorithm

Let us consider an observed signal
\bm{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
at each timestamp
t
, following model (
15
). We focus here on the PETRELS-ADMM algorithm
[
40
]
, a recent technique which investigates the robust subspace tracking with missing data (RST-miss) problem with corrupted data. This algorithm belongs to the class of PETRELS algorithms, which employ second-order stochastic gradient descent on the cost function
[
23
]
. PETRELS-ADMM comprises two stages that are alternatively executed: (1) the weight vector
\bm{w}_{t}
and outliers
\bm{s}_{t}
are first estimated using an ADMM solver; (2) then, the subspace
\bm{U}_{t}
is updated and tracked. This algorithm has interesting advantages and properties: it can handle large fraction of missing data and displays an enhanced convergence rate thanks to adaptive step size. It is also provable in the sense that it provides guarantees of both the existence of optimal tracking solutions and asymptotic convergence to a stationary point of the expected loss function
f(\bm{U})
under mild conditions
[
40
]
. Here is a description of the two stages of the PETRELS-ADMM algorithm.

Stage 1 – outliers and weights estimation
: outliers are detected in
\bm{s}_{t}
by projecting the new observation
\bm{y}_{t}
into the space spanned by the previous estimated subspace
\bm{U}_{t-1}
. This translates into the following minimization problem:

\{\bm{s}_{t},\bm{w}_{t}\}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny d}}}{=}}\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{\bm{s},\bm{w}}\big\{\big\|\bm{m}_{t}\odot(\bm{U}_{t-1}\bm{w}+\bm{s}-\bm{y}_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2}+\rho\|\bm{s}\|_{1}\big\}

(20)

where
L_{1}
regularization is used to promote entry-wise sparsity on the outlier vector and
\rho>0
is a regularization parameter that controls the sparsity level.

Stage 2 – subspace estimation
: the subspace
\bm{U}_{t}
is estimated by minimizing the
L_{2,\infty}
-regularized sum of the residuals on the subspace
\bm{U}

\bm{U}_{t}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny d}}}{=}}\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{\bm{U}}\Big\{\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\lambda^{t-j}\frac{\tilde{\bm{m}}_{j}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}\bm{1}}{p}\big\|\tilde{\bm{m}}_{j}\odot(\bm{y}_{j}-\bm{U}\bm{w}_{j})\big\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2t}\|\bm{U}\|_{2,\infty}^{2}\Big\}

(21)

where the regularization
\frac{\alpha}{2t}\|\bm{U}\|_{2,\infty}^{2}
scales the vectors in
\bm{U}
whilst the outliers
\bm{s}_{t}
have been disregarded, and the new missing-data pattern

\tilde{\bm{m}}_{j}

is
defined as
\tilde{\bm{m}}_{j}=\bm{m}_{j}\odot\mathbbm{1}(\bm{s}_{j}=\bm{0})
.

Graph Learning with Missing Data

We earlier discussed graph signals and their numerous applications.
The primary step in graph signal processing is to identify a graphical model that best captures the similarities or dependencies in the signal.
This process is referred to as
graph learning
,
for which there are various approaches,
broadly classified into
undirected
and
directed
methods.
An undirected graph models bilateral relationships, while a directed graph is used to represent unilateral dependencies.
Let

\bm{X}

denote the graph signal matrix and let
\bm{\theta}
be the parameters of the underlying graph model.
Then the MAP formulation for estimation of
\bm{\theta}
given the data yields:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{MAP}}&=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}}\,\,\log f(\bm{\theta}|\bm{X})=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}}}\,\,-\log f(\bm{X}|\bm{\theta})-\log f(\bm{\theta}).\end{split}

(22)

This formulation is specified in Table
V
for two standard graph learning frameworks: the Gaussian Markov random field (GMRF) model
[
41
]
for undirected graphs and the vector autoregressive (VAR) model
[
42
]
for directed graphs.

The graph learning framework based on (
22
) assumes complete data statistics, while in practice, observations often contain missing values due to various factors such as sensor failures or measurement errors.
One may consider
the missing samples as latent variables and use EM
for the estimation of the graph.
This approach, however, only infers the graph structure, requiring additional processing for imputation of missing values.

An alternative approach is to simultaneously estimate the missing values and the graph parameters,
enabling joint graph learning and signal imputation,
while reducing computational complexity.
Let
\bm{X}=[\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
represent the complete (ground-truth) data matrix.
Consider the case where the observed data contains missing values and noise. Replacing the missing values with
0
, the matrix of the observations could then be modeled as
\bm{Y}=\bm{M}\odot(\bm{X}+\bm{N})
where the missing mask
\bm{M}\in\{1,0\}^{p\times n}
specifies the missing pattern and
\bm{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
represents the measurement noise.
Assuming the missing data mechanism is ignorable for Bayesian inference,
the MAP estimation of the graph parameters
\bm{\theta}
and the complete data matrix
\bm{X}
given
\bm{Y}
and
\bm{M}
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{MAP}},\hat{\bm{X}}_{\text{MAP}}&=\mathop{\mathsf{argmax}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}},\,\bm{X}}\,\log f(\bm{\theta},\bm{X}|\bm{M},\bm{Y}),\\
&=\mathop{\mathsf{argmin}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\Omega_{\bm{\theta}},\,\bm{X}}\,-\log f(\bm{Y}|\bm{X},\bm{M})-\log f(\bm{X}|\bm{\theta})-\log f(\bm{\theta}).\end{split}

(23)

Classical methods for graph learning, however, only capture either spatial correlations through an undirected graph structure (GMRF model), or temporal dependencies via a directed graph topology (VAR model).
For spatio-temporal signals (i.e., networked time series data), both spatial and temporal dependencies are crucial. Recent approaches like
[
43
]
have therefore introduced a multi-relational graph model comprising both undirected and directed structures to simultaneously represent spatial and temporal dependencies.
This model is described by:

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{A}\bm{x}_{t-1}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t},\qquad f(\bm{\epsilon}_{t}|\bm{L})\propto\sqrt{\mathrm{det}^{*}\bm{L}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\epsilon}_{t}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}\bm{L}\,\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\right).

(24)

Here,
\bm{x}_{t}
and
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
are the signal and the innovations at time
t
, respectively;
\bm{A}
represents the adjacency matrix of a directed graph capturing temporal dependencies, and
\bm{L}
denotes the Laplacian matrix of an undirected graph encoding spatial correlations.
The parameters of this model are
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{L})
.

The spatio-temporal signal recovery and graph leaning (STSRGL) method in
[
43
]
(code available at
https://github.com/javaheriamirhossein/STSRGL
) solves problem (
23
) for joint inference of this spatio-temporal model.

In Figures
8
and
9
,
we compare the STSRGL method with baseline algorithms for

•

spatial graph learning: combinatorial graph learning (CGL)
[
41
]

Learning with missing data

This section focuses on the integration of learning strategies in the presence of missing data, encompassing tasks such as prediction, imputation, and inference on the missing-data mechanism.
We begin by formally defining the specific learning objectives associated with incomplete data scenarios. Subsequently, we present a taxonomy of learning methodologies, categorizing existing approaches designed to address, or remain robust to, the presence of missing values.

What to model?

An important question that arises in learning tasks concerns the target application, which guides the choice of a specific modeling approach. In this section, we classify these applications into two categories: imputation and learning tasks.

When imputation or inference is the main objective

When the goal is imputation, that is obtaining a complete dataset or estimating parameters in the presence of missing values, we saw in the previous sections that we model the joint distribution
f(\bm{X},\bm{M})
in the general case, and the distribution
f(\bm{X})
if the mechanism is MCAR or MAR (see Definition 2). However, since these distributions are not directly accessible, we instead consider the observed likelihoods, which are integrated over the missing components, i.e.,
f(\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{M}|\bm{\theta})=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{\sf m}}f(\bm{X}|\bm{\theta})f(\bm{M}|\bm{X},\bm{\phi})d\bm{X}_{\sf m}
in the general case (see Equation (
12
)).
A natural modeling choice might be to use the marginal distribution of the observed data (i.e., treating
\bm{\theta}
as the parameter of
f(\bm{X}_{\sf o})
), since this is the part of the data we actually observe. However, by construction, this approach does not allow us to estimate the distribution over the entire population and therefore cannot be used for imputation.

When prediction is the main objective

When the objective is to predict a target value
{y}_{\textrm{new}}
(e.g., a feature label) from a vector of new observations
\bm{x}_{\textrm{new}}
, several scenarios must be considered, depending on whether missing values appear in the training set
\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}
, in the new observations
\bm{x}_{\textrm{new}}
or in both. The case where
\bm{y}_{\textrm{train}}
is missing partially or completely can be cast as classical semi-supervised or unsupervised learning.

•

Only
\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}
contains missing values
: There may be missing data only in the training set; for example, consider the case of historical data from a sensor network recording various seismic features. In this case, some readings in the training set (
\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}
) may be missing due to sensor downtime or data corruption. In contrast, the test data (
\bm{x}_{\textrm{new}}
) is clean thanks to modernized and redundant monitoring sensors. In such cases, the objective is to learn
f(\bm{y}|\bm{X})
. Two strategies are available: one can either impute the training data
\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}
(by learning
f(\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}})
), or adapt the learning algorithm to handle missing values by applying naive imputation and accounting for the bias it may introduce;

•

Both
\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}
and
\bm{x}_{\textrm{new}}
contain missing values
:
When missing values are present in both the training set and the new observations, the key is to be able to make predictions despite incomplete data. The objective becomes estimating
f(\bm{y}|\bm{X}_{\sf o})
, and it is not necessary to model the full distribution
f(\bm{X})
. In particular, simple imputation methods (e.g., constant imputation) may suffice.

These different strategies naturally lead to two main modeling paradigms:
generative
and
discriminative
approaches. In the next sections, we first discuss generative methods, which aim to model the complete data distribution
f(\bm{X})
. Then, we turn to discriminative methods, which directly target the conditional distribution
f(\bm{y}|\bm{X})
, thereby bypassing the need to model
f(\bm{X})
.

Generative approaches

This section categorizes recent imputation methods, including those leveraging advances in deep learning, with respect to their methodological aspects. These approaches are designed either for imputing missing data or for making predictions on fully observed new observations.

Deep latent variable models

Several methods use deep latent variable models to estimate the distribution
f(\bm{X})
through a variational bound on the observed likelihood, which is typically intractable. The Missing data Importance-Weighted AutoEncoder (MIWAE) method
[
44
]
proposes training deep latent variable models to impute MCAR or MAR values. The generative model is described by a stochastic mapping, parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
, from a latent variable
\bm{z}_{j}
to each row
\bm{x}_{j}
of the data matrix
\bm{X}
, as follows

\bm{z}_{j}\sim f(\bm{z}_{j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\gamma}){\,=\,}\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j}),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j})\right)\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \bm{x}_{j}\sim f(\bm{x}_{j}|\bm{z}_{j},\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{j}),\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z}_{j})\right).

(25)

In practice, the conditional distribution
f(\bm{x}_{j}|\bm{z}_{j},\bm{\theta})
is typically modeled as a Gaussian (for continuous data), with parameters

\bm{\theta}
.
These parameters are learned by a deep neural network, known as the
decoder
. MIWAE introduces a conditional distribution
f(\bm{z}_{j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\gamma})
over the latent variables given the observed data, to compute a stochastic lower bound of the observed likelihood. This distribution is also assumed to be Gaussian, with parameters

\bm{\gamma}
,
learned by a deep neural network, called
encoder
.
More precisely, the lower bound reads

\ell_{K}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\gamma}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}_{j}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}_{j}^{(K)}\sim f(\bm{z}_{j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\gamma})}\left[\log\frac{1}{K}\sum_{c=1}^{K}\frac{f({\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j}|\bm{z}_{j}^{(c)},\bm{\theta})f(\bm{z}_{j}^{(c)})}{f(\bm{z}_{j}^{(c)}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\gamma})}\right],

(26)

with
K\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
being the number of draws, and we have
\ell_{K}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\gamma}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j})\leq\ell(\bm{\theta}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j})
, with
\ell(\bm{\theta}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j})=\log f({\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j}|\bm{\theta})
the observed log-likelihood.
The lower bound is then optimized using stochastic gradient-based algorithms.
Finally, the imputation step is performed either by sampling from the conditional distribution of the missing variables given the observed ones, or by computing the conditional expectation for a single imputation. We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\theta}]

\displaystyle=\int{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\theta})d{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}=\int\int{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{z}_{j},\bm{\theta})f(\bm{z}_{j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\theta})d\bm{z}_{j}{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j},

\displaystyle=\int\int{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}{\frac{f(\bm{z}_{j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\theta})}{{f(\bm{z}_{j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\gamma})}}}{f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{z}_{j},\bm{\theta})f(\bm{z}_{j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\gamma})}d\bm{z}_{j}\,d{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}.

The encoder
f(\bm{z}_{j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\gamma)
plays the role of a variational distribution and

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\theta}]

can thus be approximated using self-normalized importance sampling
[
45
, Section 9.2]
as
\sum_{c=1}^{K}w_{c}{\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}^{(c)}
where

\>\text{ $w_{c}=\frac{r_{c}}{\sum_{c=1}^{K}r_{c}}$, $r_{c}=\frac{f({\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j}|\bm{z}_{j}^{(c)},\bm{\theta})f(\bm{z}_{j}^{(c)})}{{f(\bm{z}_{j}^{(c)}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\gamma})}}$, $({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}^{(c)},\bm{z}_{j}^{(c)})\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}{f({\bm{x}_{\sf m}}_{,j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{z}_{j},\bm{\theta})f(\bm{z}_{j}|{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{\gamma})}$}.

A dedicated box presents an extension of this approach to handling MNAR values
[
46
]
, which involves learning the missing-data mechanism using a deep neural network.

Generative adversarial networks

Several studies adapt the Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) framework to the problem of missing data imputation. As in conventional GANs, two operators are used: the
generator
, which imputes missing values, and the
discriminator
, which predicts the missing-data pattern from the imputation.

We provide details for one of the most widely used GAN-based imputation methods, called Generative Adversarial Imputation Nets (GAIN)
[
47
]
. The generator
G
takes as input the observed components
{\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j}
, a noise vector
\bm{n}_{j}
, and the mask
\bm{m}_{j}
, and outputs a completed data vector
{\hat{\bm{x}}}_{j}=G({\bm{x}_{\sf o}}_{,j},\bm{n}_{j},\bm{m}_{j})
. The output
\hat{\bm{x}}
combines the observed values (which are left unchanged) and the imputed values generated by
G
. The purpose of the discriminator
D
is to output a probability vector
\hat{\bm{m}}_{j}
, to distinguish which values are real (i.e. observed) or fake (i.e. imputed). The study demonstrates that the optimization problem is intractable unless the discriminator
D
receives partial information about the missing data pattern
\bm{m}_{j}
, necessitating a third operator, the
hint mechanism

\bm{h}_{j}
, designed to incorporate information from
\bm{m}_{j}
. The generator and discriminator are trained simultaneously in an adversarial process with a minimax optimization problem, as follows:

\mathop{\mathsf{max}}_{G}\mathop{\mathsf{min}}_{D}\>-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{m}_{j}\log\hat{\bm{m}}_{j}+(1-\bm{m}_{j})\log(1-\hat{\bm{m}}_{j})\right],

with
\hat{m}_{j}=D({\hat{\bm{x}}}_{j},\bm{h}_{j})
.
The discriminator aims to accurately recover the missing-data mask (by minimizing the cross-entropy loss), while the generator seeks to maximize this loss by producing imputations that are indistinguishable from observed values, thereby deceiving the discriminator.
Once training is complete, the generator
G
is used to impute missing values by applying it to incomplete data corrupted with random noise.

Optimal transport

Optimal transport provides geometric distances for comparing discrete distributions, such as the empirical distributions of data. An imputation method based on minimizing the so-called Sinkhorn loss was first proposed for the missing data setting by
[
48
]
. This loss compares two empirical distributions,
\alpha=(1/n)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{x}_{j}}
and
\beta=(1/n)\sum_{l=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{x}^{\prime}_{l}}
, as follows:

S_{\epsilon}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathop{\mathsf{min}}_{P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\sum_{j,l}P_{j,l}\|\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}^{\prime}_{l}\|^{2}+R(\epsilon,\alpha,\beta,P)

The core intuition is that the quantity
P
minimizing the Wasserstein distance represents the optimal transport plan: it captures the effort required to shift mass from distribution
\alpha
to
\beta
, thereby quantifying their similarity. The Sinkhorn loss adds regularization terms, denoted as
R(\epsilon,\alpha,\beta,P)
, yielding a differentiable distance thanks to the entropy term. For missing data imputation, the algorithm proposed in
[
48
]
consists of the following steps: (i) perform an initial naive imputation, (ii) compute the Sinkhorn loss
S_{\epsilon}(\mu_{p}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{J}),\mu_{p}(\hat{\bm{x}}_{L}))
to compare the empirical distributions
\mu_{p}
of two imputed subsets
\hat{\bm{x}}_{J}
and
\hat{\bm{x}}_{L}
, selected via random index sets
J,L\subset{1,\dots,n}
such that
J\cap L=\varnothing
and
|J|=|L|<n
, and (iii) minimize this quantity to update the imputed values. The underlying assumption is that the subsets of the dataset, whatever their indices, have similar distributions.

Discriminative approaches

Discriminative approaches for missing data focus on directly modeling the relationship between inputs and outputs without explicitly modeling the data distribution.

Predict when
\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}
and
\bm{x}_{\textrm{new}}
are missing

When there are missing values in both the training data and the new observations, there are two different strategies
[
49
]
: (i) the first one is to use a learning algorithm that directly handles missing data, and (ii) the second strategy is two-step: first impute to obtain a complete dataset, then train a learning algorithm on the imputed data.

One-step strategy

One-step often refers to the Missing Incorporated in Attributes (MIA) method to handle missing values in
tree-based methods
[
50
]
. In a decision tree without missing data, at each node, the algorithm chooses the splitting variable and the threshold that minimizes the error. When a feature contains missing values, MIA considers missingness itself as informative: for each candidate split, it evaluates not only the threshold but also the optimal direction (left or right) to send missing values. This allows the model to learn during training the best routing decision for missing data, rather than imputing or discarding them. As a result, MIA integrates missingness directly into the tree structure and can capture patterns where the presence or absence of a value carries the signal. This makes MIA particularly well suited to MNAR scenarios. From an empirical perspective, a recent comparative study of prediction techniques shows that boosted trees using MIA to handle missing values achieve the best predictive performance in four healthcare data sets
[
51
]
.

A natural strategy for learning
f(\bm{y}|\bm{X}_{\sf o})
is to fit a separate conditional model for each missing-data pattern
\bm{M}
, in order to estimate
\mathbb{E}[\bm{y}|\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{M}]
. However, even in the linear case where
\bm{y}=\bm{X}^{\mkern-2.0mu\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}+\bm{\epsilon}
, this approach requires fitting one ordinary least squares (OLS) model per pattern, that is, up to
2^{p}
models and
p2^{p}
parameters in total. As a result, this approach becomes impractical due to the large sample size it requires. To address this issue, a recent work
[
52
]
proposes a more efficient approach under specific parametric assumptions, still in the linear case: Gaussian covariates
\bm{X}
, Gaussian noise, and a MAR mechanism (or certain MNAR settings). In this context, the authors show that only
p+p^{2}
parameters are sufficient to estimate
\mathbb{E}[\bm{y}|\bm{X}_{\sf o},\bm{M}]
. They further introduce a dedicated neural network architecture to estimate these parameters efficiently.

Two-step strategy

The two-step strategy, also known as the
impute-then-regress
approach, involves two distinct phases: imputation followed by prediction, as follows:

(i)

The training set
\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}
is imputed using an imputation method
\mathcal{I}(.)
.

(ii)

A predictive model
\hat{d}
is trained on the imputed dataset
(\mathcal{I}(\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}),\bm{y}_{\textrm{train}})
.

(iii)

The new observations
\bm{x}_{\textrm{new}}
is predicted using the model from step
(ii)
, i.e.,
{y}_{\textrm{new}}=\hat{d}(\mathcal{I}(\bm{x}_{\textrm{new}}))
.

It has first been emphasized that the same imputation model should be used for both the training data and the new observations
[
49
]
. For example, if
\mathcal{I}(.)
corresponds to mean imputation, the mean of each variable
i
is computed from the training set
{[\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}]}_{i,:}
and used to impute missing values in both
{[\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}]}_{i,:}
and
{[\bm{x}_{\textrm{new}}]}_{i}
. This requirement highlights the need for an out-of-sample imputation method. It has been shown that simple methods, such as mean imputation, combined with a powerful learner (typically random forests) at step
(ii)
can be sufficient. More precisely, if the learning algorithm
\hat{d}
is universally consistent when trained on complete data, it remains consistent within the impute-then-regress framework
[
49
]
. Some studies also focus on the finite-sample regime: zero imputation is recommended for linear models in high-dimensional settings
[
53
]
, and simple constant imputations are often sufficient in low-dimensional settings
[
54
]
. One study extends the generative deep imputation method MIWAE
[
44
]
to the supervised context, showing that complex imputation methods can be beneficial when the predictive model is not very flexible
[
55
]
.

It is also commonly recommended to include the missing-data pattern
\bm{M}
(
1
) as additional input features when training the predictive model
\hat{d}
, i.e., by concatenating the observed data matrix with the corresponding missing-data pattern. Intuitively, this corresponds to the case where the missing value itself carries predictive information. For example, if sensor measurements are frequently missing during extreme weather events (e.g., sensor dropout in storms) when the goal is to forecast weather anomalies, this information can be leveraged
[
51
,
49
]
.

Overall, the choice of the imputation method becomes less critical when the predictor is sufficiently flexible, the missing-data pattern is included, and the relationship between features and response is not linear
[
9
]
.

Predict when only
\bm{X}_{\textrm{train}}
is missing

When the main objective is to estimate parameters of an underlying model or to make predictions on fully observed new observations, a strategy involves first performing a naive imputation of the missing values, followed by an adaptation of the subsequent algorithm to account for the imputation error. More specifically, if the target algorithm
A
is designed for complete data, the first step consists in applying a simple imputation method
\mathcal{I}(.)
, such as constant imputation, to obtain a completed dataset
\mathcal{I}(\bm{X})
. In the second step, algorithm
A
is modified to correct for the bias introduced by the imputation. This debiased version of
A
is then applied to the imputed data
\mathcal{I}(\bm{X})
.

This strategy has been mostly studied in the linear regression setting when covariates are missing, i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{y}|\bm{X}\right]=h(\bm{X})
, with
h(.)
a linear function. As an example, in the context of sparse regression, adaptations of the Dantzig selector and LASSO have been proposed through debiasing the resulting covariance matrix
[
56
]
. In the context of ridge regression, debiased gradients have also been used to apply the averaged stochastic gradient descent algorithm
[
57
]
.

The underlying intuition is that some auxiliary information is necessary for reliable imputation. Second, allowing the missing-data mechanism to be too flexible can lead to an imbalance during model training. Specifically, the model may allocate too much capacity to estimating the nuisance parameter

Conclusion and perspectives

In this article, we provide an overview of existing approaches to handle missing data in signal processing and machine learning, which is a pervasive issue in numerous applications. For convenience, we have arranged them into three main categories:
imputation
,
estimation
and
learning
. Indeed, these frames can be intertwined in many ways. For example, a feature prediction or statistical estimation task on incomplete data might require imputing the data, first as a preprocessing step or in an iterative fashion. Inversely, we have seen that imputing the data might involve learning the underlying missing-data mechanism.

Although many classical problems have been successfully extended to the missing data case, achieving state-of-the-art results on real-world applications, this research topic is still evolving. We conclude this article with open research questions related to missing data and suggest possible directions for future research.

Structure-aware imputation, estimation and learning
– A challenge in SP and ML with missing data is to efficiently exploit what Mitra et al. recently called
structured missingness

[
59
]
, i.e., the combinations of missing-data mechanisms and patterns in the data. Some examples could include: 1) explicitly model structured missingness using informative statistical priors on the mechanisms and pattern parameters; 2) design doubly robust algorithms to both data distribution and missing-data mechanism for imputation, estimation and learning; 3) model and incorporate MNAR relationships in the signal grounded on physical assumptions.

Handling missing data in and through graphs
– Missing data is a relatively new topic in graph signal processing and graph learning. Promising directions of research include: 1) the design of a unified graph learning framework for data under the MNAR mechanism; 2) learning continuous-time graph-structured models with missing data (learning from irregularly sampled data); 3) developing imputation algorithms for data defined on large-scale graphs (networks) for online and streaming-data applications; 4) learning heterogeneous graphs (i.e., with mixed node/edge types) from data with missing information.

Towards a global accounting of uncertainty
–
Fully capturing uncertainty in the presence of missing data remains a central challenge, with several promising directions for future work: 1) leveraging labeled and abundant unlabeled data jointly is crucial, especially in real-world settings, yet remains out of scope in the present work; 2) modeling input uncertainty, for instance when multiple sensors provide slightly different measurements, by explicitly incorporating this variability into the model’s inputs; 3) quantifying output uncertainty, by constructing confidence intervals with strong theoretical guarantees, such as those offered by recent conformal prediction techniques adapted to missing data

60
]
