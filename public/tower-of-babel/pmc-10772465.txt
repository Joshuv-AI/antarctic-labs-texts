iPSC-derived hypoimmunogenic tissue resident memory T cells mediate robust anti-tumor activity against cervical cancer
Authors: Yoshiki Furukawa, Midori Ishii, Jun Ando, Kazuya Ikeda, Kyomi J Igarashi, Shintaro Kinoshita, Yoko Azusawa, Tokuko Toyota, Tadahiro Honda, Mahito Nakanishi, Koichi Ohshima, Ayako Masuda, Emiko Yoshida, Mari Kitade, Matthew Porteus, Yasuhisa Terao, Hiromitsu Nakauchi, Miki Ando
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryFunctionally rejuvenated human papilloma virus-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (HPV-rejTs) generated from induced pluripotent stem cells robustly suppress cervical cancer. However, autologous rejT generation is time consuming, leading to difficulty in treating patients with advanced cancer. Although use of allogeneic HPV-rejTs can obviate this, the major obstacle is rejection by the patient immune system. To overcome this, we develop HLA-A24&-E dual integrated HPV-rejTs after erasing HLA class I antigens. These rejTs effectively suppress recipient immune rejection while maintaining more robust cytotoxicity than original cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Single-cell RNA sequencing performed to gain deeper insights reveal that HPV-rejTs are highly enriched with tissue resident memory T cells, which enhance cytotoxicity against cervical cancer through TGFβR signaling, with increased CD103 expression. Genes associated with the immunological synapse also are upregulated, suggesting that these features promote stronger activation of T cell receptor (TCR) and increased TCR-mediated target cell death. We believe that our work will contribute to feasible “off-the-shelf” T cell therapy with robust anti-cervical cancer effects.Keywords: cervical cancer, CRISPR-Cas9 scarless gene editing, HLA class I-edited iPSCs, human papilloma virus-specific CTLs, hypoimmunogenic iPSC-derived CTLs, “off-the-shelf” CTL therapy, rejuvenated CTLs, single-cell RNA sequencing, tissue resident memory T cells, two-step gene editing

Introduction

Cervical cancer is one of the most common malignancies in women worldwide. In 2020, ∼604,000 women were diagnosed with cervical cancer globally; more than 341,000 died from cervical cancer.1 Cervical cancer harbors high-risk human papilloma virus (HPV) in 99% of cases, with HPV16 accounting for 50%–60% and HPV18 accounting for ∼20%.2,3,4,5,6,7 Although prophylactic HPV vaccines can prevent initial infection by high-risk HPV types, these vaccines are not effective against established cancers.2,8,9 In early-stage disease, total hysterectomy is indicated, which abolishes fertility and increases long-term risks for cardiovascular disease even with ovarian conservation.10 Metastatic and relapsed cervical cancer is generally incurable. It also is highly refractory to chemotherapy, especially in young women.11,12,13,14 Development of effective cervical cancer treatment thus remains urgent. As cervical cancer cells constitutively express E6 and E7 oncoproteins (contributors to tumorigenesis and cancer cell survival), E6 and E7 are attractive treatment targets.2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9,11,12,13,14

We demonstrated that induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived HPV16 E6-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) robustly suppress cervical cancer.15 Although antigen-specific CTL therapy can induce durable remission in selected tumors such as melanoma,16 CTLs are chronically exposed to tumor antigens, resulting in exhaustion.17,18 Functionally rejuvenated CTLs generated from iPSCs (rejuvenated CTLs [rejTs]) include stem cell memory and central memory phenotypes and persist long term in vivo, resulting in continuous tumor elimination by an invigorated immune system.15,19,20,21 The greatest advantage of rejT therapy is that once iPSCs are established from an antigen-specific CTL clone, unlimited numbers of therapeutic T cells can be generated.

However, generating rejTs from individual patients is time consuming and unrealistically expensive. Allogeneic T cell therapy banks can solve both those problems but do not overcome the two major obstacles to allogeneic T cell therapy: graft-versus-host disease and host-versus-graft disease. In immunocompetent cervical cancer patients, the dominant problem is rejection of foreign T cells by alloreactive recipient CD8+ T cells (host-versus-graft disease). Although β2-microglobulin (B2M) disruption can erase human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I expression to evade immunorejection by CD8+ T cells,22 recipient natural killer (NK) cells attack the HLA class I-negative cells. CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing approaches to reduce NK cell attack include overexpression of HLA-E, which binds to NKG2A23; expression of CD47, a “don’t eat-me” signal24; expression of HLA-G, an NK cell inhibitory ligand25; and HLA-C retention for binding to the inhibitory receptor killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptor.26 However, since work using these methods has not demonstrated evasion of recipient immune reaction against iPSC-derived T cells, whether they apply in allogeneic T cell therapy is uncertain. Use of iPSC-derived chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CARTs) with forced expression of HLA-E after triple knockout (HLA class I, class II, PVR) yielded alloimmune suppression. Unfortunately, T cell function and killing function of gene-edited CARTs were not thoroughly evaluated.27 Our study addressed these issues by using iPSC-derived HPV16 E6-specific CTLs targeting cervical cancer.

While CTLs, unlike CARTs, can recognize intracellular antigens, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) restriction on the T cell receptor (TCR) limits CTL deployment: it precludes generation of universal CTLs that could be used for all patients. However, gene editing to suppress recipient allogeneic immune reaction will permit treatment of many patients with CTLs generated from a single iPSC line. For example, if MHC binding of rejTs is mediated by HLA-A24, then only patients expressing HLA-A24 are eligible for treatment using rejTs that express HLA-A24 and thereby will be protected from attack by host T cells or NK cells. HLA-A24 is present in ∼60% of South American and Eastern Asian populations and in ∼30% of North American and European populations. With rejTs restricted by HLA-A24 and HLA-A02, achieved coverage in South America and Eastern Asia should be ∼80% and in North America and Europe ∼70%. This report thus aims to establish a proof of concept for “off-the-shelf” T cell therapy limited to specific HLA types, i.e., “limited off-the-shelf” T cell therapy.

We prioritized expressing HLA-A24 on HPV-rejTs both because we hoped to suppress NK cells via binding of the Bw4 ligand to KIR3DL1 and because HLA-A24 restriction already exists on HPV-rejTs. We also integrated a B2M-HLA-E fusion construct, expecting to inhibit NKG2A+ NK cells by HLA-E/NKG2A binding. Using CRISPR-Cas9 technology, we successfully generated HLA class I-edited HPV-rejTs with dual integration of HLA-A24 and HLA-E and lacking expression of other class I molecules. These were not rejected by recipient CD8+ T cells and could evade NK cell attack. Furthermore, these rejTs, which were enriched with tissue resident memory T cells (TRM), could maintain robust suppression of cervical cancer even without peptide-pulse treatment; those of the original CTL clone could not. We accordingly propose that HLA-engineered HPV-rejTs using this method provide a sustainable and promising approach to successful “off-the-shelf” T cell therapy in overcoming cervical cancer.

Results

CRISPR-Cas9 “scarless” technology successfully edited HLA class I antigen expression by iPSCs

To edit HLA class I antigens efficiently and safely, we used CRISPR-Cas9 two-step “scarless” gene editing, a combination of two-step homologous recombination and positive-negative selection with magnetic beads.28 As this technique does not leave selection markers, alleles can be edited without creating INDELs.28 Our goals were erasure of HLA class I antigens in iPSCs established from an HLA-A2402-restricted HPV16 E6-CTL clone15 and limited expression of HLA class I molecules, thereby evading recipient NK cell attack.

The donor plasmid used in the initial editing (“first edit”) contains a bicistronic cassette that expresses enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) and truncated CD8. EGFP expression permits distinction between left and right homology arms under control of the promoter of UBC, encoding ubiquitin C (Figure 1A). A short hairpin RNA (shRNA) was also introduced to permit negative selection for random integration on the first edit: Expression of shRNA (episomal or randomly integrated) marks cells as negative.28 Table S1 lists primers used for genotyping PCR. They are designed exterior to the left (5′) and right (3′) of each homology arm. Using electroporation we also transfected iPSCs with both the Cas9/guide RNA (gRNA) plasmid and the first-edit donor plasmid. After magnetic-bead selection for CD8 expression, EGFP-expressing cells were identified among the biallelic clones via fluorescent imaging on day 10 (Figure 1B). Biallelic iPSC clones with bright EGFP expression were selected, with integration confirmed using PCR (Figure 1C). For the second edit, a different (“second-edit”) donor plasmid and Cas9/gRNA plasmid were similarly transfected into biallelic iPSCs (Figure 1A). Since the HPV16 E6-rejTs used were HLA-A2402 restricted, we first generated HLA-A2402 single integrated iPSCs (KI-A24-iPSCs). The second-edit donor plasmid was designed to integrate HLA-A2402 cDNA with linker (GGGS×4) on the 5′ side of the B2M stop codon, thereby creating a biallelically integrated B2M-HLA-A24 fusion protein (Figure S1A). After magnetic-bead selection for cells that did not express CD8 and cloning, genotyping confirmed intact integration of HLA-A2402. The non-edited wild-type (WT) PCR product was 7.7 kb long, the first-edit PCR product (biallelic clone) was 10.6 kb long, and the HLA-A2402 integrated PCR product was 8.8 kb long (Figure S1A). To generate HLA-E0103 single integrated iPSCs (KI-E-iPSCs), we used another donor plasmid designed to replace the endogenous B2M locus with an exogenous B2M-HLA-E fusion construct, thereby creating a biallelically integrated B2M-HLA-E trimer fusion protein (Figure S1B). For the second edit, a different (“second-edit”) donor plasmid (for HLA-E integration) and the Cas9/gRNA plasmid were similarly transfected into biallelic iPSCs. After magnetic-bead selection for cells that did not express CD8-negative selection and cloning, PCR genotyping showed that the WT PCR product was 7.7 kb long, while the HLA-E0103 integrated PCR product was 4.4 kb long (Figure S1B). Finally, to yield a dual integrated HLA-A24 and B2M-HLA-E fusion construct in each allele, we transfected these two donor plasmids (for HLA-A2402 and HLA-E0103 integration) simultaneously, thereby generating HLA-A2402 and HLA-E0103 dual integrated iPSCs (KI-A24&E-iPSCs) in each allele (Figure S1C). After magnetic-bead selection for cells that did not express CD8 and cloning, PCR genotyping yielded two PCR products, one for HLA-E0103 (4.4 kb long) and one for HLA-A2402 (8.8 kb long). We selected clone #1, in which on gel electrophoresis the presence of both was confirmed (Figure 1D). Sanger sequencing detected no INDELs between homology arms and the integrated sequence in HLA class I-edited iPSCs (Figure S1D).

HLA class I-edited iPSCs differentiated efficiently into HPV16 E6-rejTs

To verify HLA expression by HLA class I-edited iPSCs, WT-, KI-A24-, KI-E-, and KI-A24&E-iPSCs were cultured with interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and levels of cell-surface expression of HLA class I molecules were determined by flow cytometry. WT-iPSCs clearly expressed HLA class I molecules, whereas KI-A24-iPSCs clearly expressed HLA-A24 molecules but lacked expression of HLA-B, -C, and -E (Figure 1E). KI-E-iPSCs lacked expression of HLA class I molecules but expressed HLA-E (Figure 1E). KI-A24&E-iPSCs expressed HLA-A24 and -E but did not express HLA-B or -C (Figure 1E). To assess whether gene editing influenced T cell differentiation, we next derived HPV16 E6-rejTs from WT-iPSCs, KI-A24-iPSCs, KI-E-iPSCs, and KI-A24&E-iPSCs (Figure 1F). WT-iPSCs and these HLA-edited iPSCs differentiated into HPV16 E6-rejTs, maintaining the same antigen specificity as the original HPV-CTL clone (Figure 1G). All rejTs securely expanded from 100-fold to 1,000-fold within 2 weeks. Using flow cytometry, we also verified HLA class I antigen expression by HLA-edited HPV-rejTs. KI-A24-rejTs clearly expressed HLA-A24 but lacked surface expression of HLA-B, -C, and -E (Figure 1H). KI-E-rejTs lacked surface expression of HLA class I molecules but expressed HLA-E (Figure 1H). KI-A24&E-rejTs expressed HLA-A24 and -E but did not express HLA-B and -C (Figure 1H).

HLA class I molecules of iPSCs were, in sum, accurately edited, and all HLA class I-edited iPSCs efficiently differentiated into limited HLA class I-expressing HPV16 E6-rejTs with almost 100% antigen specificity. These observations suggest that gene editing did not affect differentiation efficiency in HPV-rejTs.

HLA class I-edited rejTs reduced T cell response

We next assessed T cell proliferation levels to determine whether HLA class I-edited rejTs can evade T cell-mediated immune responses. Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-labeled allogeneic CD3+ T cells from an HLA-A24+ healthy donor were cocultured with WT-rejTs, KI-A24-rejTs, KI-E-rejTs, or KI-A24&E-rejTs for 5 days. Allogeneic T cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry for dilution of CFSE signal in the CD8+/CD4+ T cell subpopulations to investigate whether these T cells proliferated due to the presence of rejTs. For donor A, CD8+ T cells cocultured with WT-rejTs proliferated very strongly (proliferating CD8+ T cells, 68.6%), whereas proliferation of donor A CD8+ T cells cocultured with KI-A24-rejTs, KI-E-rejTs, and KI-A24&E-rejTs was clearly reduced (5.78%, 10.9%, and 15.4%, respectively) (Figure 1I). Proliferation of CD4+ T cells cocultured with WT-rejTs was 20.0%, and the percentages of proliferating CD4+ T cells were 11.5% (KI-A24-rejTs), 3.69% (KI-E-rejTs), and 2.61% (KI-A24&E-rejTs) when cocultured with HLA class I-edited rejTs (Figure 1I). For donors B and C, proliferation of CD8+ T cells increased slightly when cocultured with KI-A24-rejTs but decreased when cocultured with HLA-A24&E-rejTs (Figures 1J and 1K). Alloreactivity in the proliferation of CD4+ T cells was not clearly demonstrable (Figures 1J and 1K). As we knocked out only B2M in this study, WT- and HLA class I-edited HPV-rejTs expressed HLA class II antigens at levels ranging from 27% to 94% (Figure S1E). However, the mean percentage of proliferating CD4+ T cells from three donors in WT-, KI-A24-, KI-E-, and KI-A24&E-rejTs was approximately 10% (12.0%, 11.3%, 7.4%, and 7.2%, respectively) (Figure S1F).

In summary, although results varied depending on the donor, levels of allogeneic CD8+ T cell immune response were lower in cocultures with KI-A24&E-rejTs than in cocultures with WT-rejTs.

HLA-A24 and -E dual expressed rejTs efficiently evaded NK cell attack in vitro

Next, to evaluate the efficiency of HLA class I-edited rejTs in evading NK cell attack, allogeneic NK cells from HLA-A24+ healthy donors were cocultured for 5 h with WT-rejTs, KI-A24-rejTs, KI-E-rejTs, or KI-A24&E-rejTs. NK cell cytotoxicity against rejTs was then evaluated by chromium-51 (51Cr) release assays. We also tried to generate HLA class I knockout rejTs, but it was difficult to use them as a control because this editing interfered with T cell differentiation (Figure S2A). Therefore, we used post-first-edit rejTs, in which HLA class I expression is clearly reduced, and K562 cells, which do not express HLA class I antigen, as positive controls after confirming that cytotoxicity levels were similar (Figures S2B and S2C). NK cell cytotoxicity was evaluated at an effector/target (E/T) ratio of 9:1. Donor A-derived NK cell cytotoxicities against KI-A24-rejTs and KI-E-rejTs were 34.1% and 29.1%, respectively, while NK cell cytotoxicity against KI-A24&E-rejTs was 18.7%, showing stronger suppression of NK cell activity than that achieved by either KI-A24-rejTs or KI-E-rejTs (KI-A24-rejTs vs. KI-A24&E-rejTs, p = 0.0007; KI-E-rejTs vs. KI-A24&E-rejTs, p = 0.0054) (Figure 2A). Donor B-derived NK cells were cocultured with rejTs similarly. At an E/T ratio of 9:1, NK cell cytotoxicities against KI-A24-rejTs and KI-E-rejTs were 81.0% and 79.7%, while NK cell cytotoxicity against KI-A24&E-rejTs, at 61.6%, showed the most effective evasion of NK cell attack (KI-A24-rejTs vs. KI-A24&E-rejTs, p < 0.0001; KI-E-rejTs vs. KI-A24&E-rejTs, p < 0.0001) (Figure 2A). At an E/T ratio of 9:1, donor C-derived NK cell cytotoxicities against KI-A24-rejTs and KI-E-rejTs were 43.4% and 60.5%, while NK cell cytotoxicity against KI-A24&E-rejTs was 38.8%, which had the most effective evasion of NK cell attack although no significant difference was observed between KI-A24- and KI-A24&E-rejTs (KI-A24-rejTs vs. KI-A24&E-rejTs, p = 0.3821; KI-E-rejTs vs. KI-A24&E-rejTs, p = 0.0016) (Figure 2A). Seeking further information on whether HLA class I-edited rejTs could evade NK cell immune responses, we used CD107 assays to evaluate NK cell cytotoxicity in the presence of HPV-rejTs. NK cells were cocultured with HPV-rejTs at an E/T ratio of 1:3 for 20 h, and CD56+ NK cells were analyzed by flow cytometry for surface expression of the degranulation marker CD107a. WT-rejTs expressed all HLA-class I molecules, so they were used as one of the negative controls. The percentages of CD107a expression among autologous NK cells cocultured with KI-A24-rejTs, KI-E-rejTs, and KI-A24&E-rejTs were 20.33% ± 4.42%, 19.2% ± 3.4%, and 14.2% ± 5.29%, respectively, showing that all HLA class I-edited cells suppressed NK cell activity significantly, with HLA-A24&E-rejTs showing the most efficient suppression (KI-A24 vs. KI-A24&E, p = 0.0088; KI-E vs. KI-A24&E, p = 0.0389) (Figure 2B). Similarly, the percentages of CD107a expression among donor A-derived allogeneic NK cells cocultured with KI-A24-rejTs, KI-E-rejTs, and KI-A24&E-rejTs were 18.67% ± 5.93%, 20.31% ± 5.08%, and 8.17% ± 1.11%, respectively, indicating that dual integrated rejTs assisted cells from this donor in evading NK cell attack (KI-A24 vs. KI-A24&E, p = 0.0273; KI-E vs. KI-A24&E, p = 0.0061) (Figure 2B). The percentages of CD107a expression among donor B-derived NK cells cocultured with KI-A24-rejTs, KI-E-rejTs, and KI-A24&E-rejTs were 20.3% ± 5.89%, 22.4% ± 3.84%, and 10.42% ± 0.78%, respectively. KI-A24&E-rejTs similarly suppressed NK cell activity most efficiently among HLA class I-edited rejTs (KI-A24 vs. KI-A24&E, p = 0.0257; KI-E vs. KI-A24&E, p = 0.0009) (Figure 2B). Moreover, the percentages of CD107a expression among donor C-derived NK cells cocultured with KI-A24-rejTs, KI-E-rejTs, and KI-A24&E-rejTs were 9.55% ± 2.98%, 12.93% ± 0.5%, and 11.42% ± 1.05%, respectively (KI-A24 vs. KI-A24&E, p = 0.1203; KI-E vs. KI-A24&E, p = 0.042), indicating that KI-A24&E-rejTs suppressed CD107a expression more strongly than did KI-E-rejTs alone (Figure 2B). To confirm our hypothesis that KI-A24&E-rejTs could suppress a larger population of NK cells than could KI-A24-rejTs or KI-E-rejTs, we used flow cytometry to assess NKG2A and KIR3DL1 expression in the (CD3−, CD56+) subset of donor NK cells in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Proportions of NKG2A+/KIR3DL1− NK cells varied among the three donors (donor A, 54.97% ± 2.06%; donor B, 42.33% ± 0.21%; donor C, 28.43% ± 2.06%) (Figure 2C). Bw4/KIR3DL1 binding inhibited NK cell activity in KIR3DL1+ NK cells (NKG2A−/KIR3DL1+: donor A, 8.11% ± 0.13%, donor B, 9.71% ± 0.16%, donor C, 10.73% ± 0.82%; NKG2A+/KIR3DL1+: donor A, 3.17% ± 0.07%; donor B, 4.73% ± 0.11%; donor C, 1.93% ± 0.18%), suggesting that KI-A24 in conjunction with KI-E made it possible to suppress a larger population of NK cells (donor A, 66.24% ± 1.84%; donor B, 56.77% ± 0.91%; donor C, 41.09% ± 0.01%) (Figure 2D) than either single integrated KI-A24-rejTs or KI-E-rejTs could suppress. These data tracked those acquired in 51Cr release and CD107a assays (Table 1).

KI-A24&E-rejTs evaded NK cell attack and survived in vivo

To examine whether KI-A24&E-rejTs could evade NK cell attack in vivo, KI-A24&E-rejTs were transduced with a retrovirus encoding both firefly luciferase and EGFP (FFluc/EGFP). These cells were then intraperitoneally injected into 6-week-old female non-obese diabetic/Shi-severe combined immunodeficiency interleukin (IL) 2Rγnull (NOG) mice that had been intraperitoneally engrafted with HPV16+ cervical cancer SiHa cells 4 days earlier. Three hours after KI-A24&E-rejT injection, mice were divided into three groups. Allogeneic HLA-A24+ or HLA-A24− NK cells were intraperitoneally injected into members of two of the three groups, and no NK cells were injected into the third control group. Luciferase-labeled KI-A24&E-rejTs in all mice tended to activate and to expand on day 1 (Figures 2E and S2D). This result was ascribed to antigen stimulation by SiHa cells and alloimmune response against NK cells. On day 7, KI-A24&E-rejTs in mice that received no NK cell injections persisted (Figure 2E, right), as did KI-A24&E-rejTs in mice that had received HLA-A24+ NK cells, demonstrating that KI-A24&E-rejTs were not attacked and eliminated by HLA-A24+ NK cells (Figure 2E, left). In contrast, KI-A24&E-rejTs in mice with HLA-A24− NK cells were not detected on day 7, indicating that A24− NK cells had eliminated rejTs (Figure 2E, middle). We also compared bioluminescence signals of KI-A24&E-rejTs on day 7 among the three groups. In mice receiving HLA-A24+ NK cells, mean bioluminescence intensity was 60,205 (44,540–79,840) (n = 4). Mice receiving no additional NK cell injection had a mean bioluminescence intensity of 78,287.5 (48,250–99,430) (n = 4), showing no significant difference (p = 0.2607). However, in mice injected with HLA-A24− NK cells, mean bioluminescence intensity was 33,378.3 (28,440–36,970) (n = 6), which was significantly different from that in mice receiving no additional NK cell injection (p = 0.0023) as well as from that in mice receiving HLA-A24+ NK cells (p = 0.0494). This demonstrates that KI-A24&E-rejTs were not eliminated by HLA-A24+ NK cells and survived in vivo on day 7 (Figure 2F). The bioluminescence signal on day 35 was significantly higher in mice receiving HLA-A24+ NK cells (n = 4) than in mice receiving HLA-A24− NK cells (n = 5) (p = 0.0069, Figure S2D).

To examine the long-term effect of allogeneic NK cell attack, we compared mouse survival in these groups. In mice receiving allogeneic HLA-A24− NK cells, KI-A24&E-rejTs did not persist and proliferation of cervical cancer cells was not suppressed, resulting in no significant prolongation of survival beyond that in the untreated group (p = 0.0906). On the other hand, mice receiving allogeneic HLA-A24+ NK cells survived dramatically longer than did mice receiving no treatment and mice receiving HLA-A24− NK cells (p = 0.0101 and p = 0.0405, respectively). Mice receiving KI-A24&E-rejTs but with no additional NK cell injection also lived significantly longer than did mice in the untreated group and mice receiving HLA-A24− NK cells (p = 0.0101 and p = 0.0405, respectively); the results resembled those in mice receiving HLA-A24+ NK cells (Figure 2G).

To examine further the rejection of allogeneic CD8+ T cells and NK cells from an HLA-A24+ donor in vivo, we intraperitoneally injected KI-A24&E-rejTs labeled with FFluc/EGFP into 6-week-old female NOG mice that had been intraperitoneally engrafted with SiHa cells 4 days earlier. Allogeneic HLA-A24+ NK cells and CD8+ T cells were simultaneously injected 3 h later (day 0; repeated on days 7 and 14) to create four groups: recipients of KI-A24&E-rejTs + NK cells + CD8+ T cells (n = 3), KI-A24&E-rejTs + NK cells (n = 3), KI-A24&E-rejTs + CD8+ T cells (n = 3), and KI-A24&E-rejTs (n = 3). Bioluminescence signal persisted on day 35 in all mice injected with KI-A24&E-rejTs (Figure S2F). We also compared bioluminescence signals of HLA-A24&E-rejTs on day 14 and day 35 among the four groups. On day 14, although the median bioluminescence intensity in mice given KI-A24&E-rejTs + NK cells + CD8+ T cells was lowest, it did not differ significantly from those in the other three groups (NK + T vs. rejTs + NK cells, p = 0.8221; NK + T vs. rejTs + CD8+ T cells, p = 0.3487; NK + T vs. rejTs, p = 0.1981) (Figure S2G). Furthermore, on day 35, no significant differences in bioluminescence were observed among the four groups, indicating that KI-A24&E-rejTs were not eradicated by NK cells, T cells, or NK + T cells (NK + T vs. rejTs + NK cells, p = 0.7966; NK + T vs. rejTs + CD8+ T cells, p = 0.8089; NK + T vs. rejTs, p = 0.6597) (Figure S2G).

Overall, robust suppression of cervical cancer progression and clear survival advantage was demonstrated in vivo. This proved that KI-A24&E-rejTs persisted in vivo without rejection regardless of the presence of allogeneic HLA-A24+ NK cells and/or CD8+ T cells.

Robust anti-cervical cancer effect of KI-A24&E-rejTs

To compare the anti-tumor activity of pre-HLA class I-edited rejTs and post (WT HPV-rejTs and HLA class I-edited HPV-rejTs) in vitro, we used real-time cell analysis. The HPV16+ cervical cancer cell line SKGIIIa was cocultured with the original CTL clone, WT-rejTs, KI-A24-rejTs, KI-E-rejTs, or KI-A24&E-rejTs. Proliferation of SKGIIIa was suppressed immediately after coculture with WT-rejTs, KI-A24-rejTs, KI-E-rejTs, and KI-A24&E-rejTs began, and suppression of tumor proliferation continued for >70 h (Figure 3A). At 20 h, cytotoxicity of WT-HPV-rejTs against SKGIIIa cells was 88.3% ± 2.2%, and the cytotoxicities of various rejTs were similar (KI-A24-rejTs, 92.6% ± 2.8%; KI-E-rejTs, 92.4% ± 4.6％; KI-A24&E-rejTs, 88.7% ± 2.2%), indicating that HLA editing did not impair cytotoxicity. Moreover, HPV-rejTs exhibited stronger anti-tumor activity than that of the original HPV-CTL clone (48.8% ± 2.0%; WT-rejTs, p < 0.0001; KI-A24-rejTs, p < 0.0001; KI-E-rejTs, p < 0.0001; KI-A24&E-rejTs, p < 0.0001) (Figure 3A). We also evaluated anti-tumor activity of KI-A24&E-rejTs against SiHa cells using real-time cell analysis. SiHa cells were cocultured with the original CTL clone, WT-rejTs, or KI-A24&E-rejTs. At 20 h, anti-tumor activity against SiHa cells of the original CTL clone was 30.3% ± 2.0%, while anti-tumor activities of WT-rejTs and KI-A24&E-rejTs reached 84.3% ± 1.9% and 85.2% ± 0.8%, respectively, showing anti-tumor activity in HPV-rejTs significantly higher than that of the original HPV-CTL clone (p < 0.0001 for both) (Figure 3B). Both WT-rejTs and KI-A24&E-rejTs showed very strong anti-tumor activity against SiHa cells. This persisted for 70 h, indicating that HLA editing did not alter anti-tumor activity of HPV-rejTs (Figure 3B).

We next evaluated whether HLA editing affects anti-cervical cancer activity and compared the cytotoxicity of KI-A24&E-rejTs in vivo with that of the original HPV-CTL clone. We intraperitoneally injected SiHa cells transduced with FFluc/EGFP into NOG mice (1 × 105/mouse) and monitored tumor growth by in vivo imaging. Once bioluminescence could be detected (4 days after injection), we divided the mice into four groups composed of one no-treatment group (control group, n = 5) and three treatment groups, treated with either the original CTL clone (n = 5), WT-rejTs (n = 4), or KI-A24&E-rejTs (n = 4) (2.5 × 106 per dose, 3 doses). Bioluminescence rapidly increased in mice without treatment (Figure 3C). Although the original HPV-CTL clone seemed to suppress cervical cancer progression (Figure 3C), no significant difference in tumor signal between original CTL-clone-treated mice and untreated mice was observed 21 days after the first treatment (p = 0.1699) (Figure 3D). In contrast, tumor suppression was evident in mice receiving WT-rejTs or KI-A24&E-rejTs (Figure 3C); signals were significantly fewer than in mice without treatment (p = 0.0046 and p = 0.0025, respectively) (Figure 3D). Long-term observation (100 days) revealed that mice treated with WT-rejTs did not meet the criteria for euthanasia and survived longer than both mice receiving no treatment (p = 0.0049) and mice receiving the original HPV-CTL clone (p = 0.0045). On the other hand, the original CTL clone did not prolong survival beyond that of the untreated mice (p = 0.153) (Figure 3E). Mice treated with KI-A24&E-rejTs had similar results, prolonging survival beyond that of mice receiving no treatment (p = 0.0049) and that of mice receiving the original HPV-CTL clone (p = 0.0045) (Figure 3E).

To confirm persistence of the anti-tumor effect in vivo, we sacrificed and dissected the surviving mice 6 months after initial treatment and examined tissue samples by light microscopy. Residual cervical cancer cells were not observed macroscopically or microscopically in the uterus, small intestine, lungs, spleen, and liver (Figure 3F).

These findings indicate that WT-rejTs (before HLA editing) and KI-A24&E-rejTs (after HLA editing) both acted robustly, and to similar extents, against cervical cancer in vitro and in vivo, and that tumor remission was sustained long term. Furthermore, both WT-rejTs and KI-A24&E-rejTs demonstrated stronger anti-tumor activity and conferred longer survival than did the original HPV-CTL clone.

To further evaluate migration and tumor-infiltration abilities of HPV-rejTs, we deployed another in vivo model. NOG mice were subcutaneously engrafted with SKGIIIa cells (5 × 105/mouse). Seven days after tumor injection, 5 × 106 original CTL clone or HPV-rejTs were intravenously administered once a week for 5 weeks. Tumor size was measured using a digital caliper. Although the original CTL clone showed a tumor-suppressive effect during the treatment, cervical cancer started growing rapidly after day 30 in mice treated with that clone. On day 50, no tumor-suppressive effect significantly greater than that in the untreated mice group was observed (p = 0.0818, one-way ANOVA). On the other hand, on day 50, HPV-rejTs had suppressed tumor growth significantly more than did the original CTL clone (p = 0.0498, one-way ANOVA) (Figures S3A and S3B).

These results demonstrated that HPV-rejTs efficiently migrated to cervical cancer cells and exhibited robust anti-tumor activity even when delivered intravenously.

HLA class I-edited rejTs are more potent than the original CTL clone

To define the phenotype of KI-A24&E-rejTs, we used flow cytometry and evaluated the expression of cell-surface markers and of the intracellular markers granzyme B and perforin. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of granzyme B was 4,962.3 ± 2,358.8 in KI-A24&E-rejTs, significantly higher than that in the original HPV CTL clone (278.8 ± 92.8, p = 0.0046). The MFI of perforin was 1,732.3 ± 486.6 in the KI-A24&E-rejTs, significantly higher than that in the original HPV CTL clone (578.5 ± 67.3, p = 0.0017) (Figure 4A). We also defined memory phenotype subsets based on expression of the cell-surface markers CD45RA, CD62, CD95, CD27, and CD28. The percentage of stem cell memory T cells (CD45RA+ CD62+ CD95+ CD27+ CD28+) was 3.5% ± 0.8% in KI-A24&E-rejTs, significantly higher than the 0.09% ± 0.06% observed in the original HPV CTL clone (p = 0.002) (Figure 4B). The observed difference in the stem cell memory T cell population between the original HPV CTL clone and HPV-rejTs correlated with their in vivo persistence, resulting in sustained long-term tumor remission in mice treated with HPV-rejTs. We used 51Cr assays to compare the cytotoxicity in vitro of KI-A24&E-rejTs with that of the original HPV CTL clone. The two cervical cancer cell lines SiHa and SKGIIIa were targets (T), with KI-A24&E-rejTs and the original HPV CTL clone as effectors (E). Neither KI-A24&E-rejTs nor the original HPV CTL clone showed cytotoxicity when cocultured with HLA-mismatched lymphoblastoid cells; i.e., non-specific killing was not observed (Figures 4C and 4D). Although KI-A24&E-rejTs exhibited robust cytotoxicity against SiHa cells (E/T ratios: 20:1, 88.4%; 10:1, 69.9%), none was observed in the original HPV CTL clone without peptide pulse (E/T ratios: 20:1, −0.1%, p < 0.0001; 10:1, 0.4%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 4C, left), although cytotoxicity increased in the presence of the target peptide (E/T ratios: 20:1, 38.4%; 10:1, 24.1%) (Figure 4C, right). KI-A24&E-rejTs also exhibited robust cytotoxicity against SiHa cells in the presence of the target peptide (E/T ratios: 20:1, 86.6%; 10:1, 70%) (Figure 4C, right). KI-A24&E-rejTs similarly exhibited more robust cytotoxicity against SKGIIIa cells even without the target peptide (E/T ratios: 10:1; 63.1%; 5:1, 57.9%), whereas the cytotoxicity of the original CTL clone against SKGIIIa cells was <10% (E/T ratios: 10:1, 8.9%, p < 0.0001; 5:1, 6.3%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 4D, left). The cytotoxicity of the original CTL clone against SKGIIIa cells differed with and without target peptide (E/T ratio 10:1: peptide pulse 71.0%, no peptide pulse 11.7%) (Figure 4D). Enhanced cytotoxicity of KI-A24&E-rejTs was due not to genetic modification but to rejuvenation, as WT-rejTs also displayed strong cytotoxicity against SiHa and SKGIIIa cells with and without the target peptide (Figures S4A and S4B).

In summary, KI-A24&E-rejTs appear more potent in therapy than conventional CTL, since by comparison with the original CTL clone KI-A24&E-rejTs secreted higher levels of cytotoxic molecules, included a larger population of stem cell memory phenotype, and exhibited stronger cytotoxicity against cervical cancer even without peptide pulse.

HPV-rejTs highly expressed genes associated with tissue resident memory T cells

To investigate further the difference in peptide pulse dependency between the original CTL clone and rejTs, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of both the original HPV-CTL clone and HLA-A24&-E dual integrated HPV-rejTs. RNA-based clustering of the scRNA-seq dataset yielded nine clusters (Figure 5A). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis generated a heatmap showing gene expression for cell-type-specific genes (Figures 5B–5F). HPV-rejTs expressed genes associated with cytotoxicity (IFNG, PRF1, FASLG, GZMA, GZMB, LTA, and LTB) more strongly than did original CTLs. HPV-rejTs also showed high expression of PTPRC, ITGAM, and PECAM1, CD58, and CD44, with low expression of CD2, genes associated with the immunological synapse (Figures 5B and 5F), suggesting that TCR binding is increased in HPV-rejTs. The original CTL clone expressed TRAC, TRBC, CD8A, and CD8B (TCRαβ+, CD8αβ+), whereas HPV-rejTs expressed TRAC, TRBC, and CD8A (CD8αα+) with several NK cell receptors including KLRK1 (NKG2D) and CD244 (Figures 5D and 5F), consistent with the phenotype of innate-like intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs).29 However, innate-like IELs are activated by innate ligand-receptor axes,29,30 whereas HPV-rejTs are activated in a TCR/CD3-dependent manner15 (Figures 4C and 4D). HPV-rejTs (notably clusters 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6) showed high expression of genes associated with TRM (CD69, ITGAE, ITGA1, TGFBR1), whereas these genes were expressed at low levels in the original CTLs (Figure 5E). Although TRM express CCR7 and CD62L, required for tissue exit, at low levels,31 HPV-rejTs expression of CCR7 was higher than that in the original HPV-CTL clone (Figure 5F). Comparisons among gene expression patterns of individual clusters found that clusters 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 were highly enriched in genes associated with TRM, IEL, and cytotoxicity (Figures 5E and 5F). Transcription factors consistent with TRM,31 including ZNF683 and RUNX3, were highly expressed in HPV-rejTs. Exhaustion markers such as PDCD1, LAG3, and TIGIT were expressed at similar levels by the HPV-CTL clone and by HPV-rejTs, whereas CTLA4 expression by HPV-rejTs was lower than that by the HPV-CTL clone. We also confirmed memory phenotype subsets by flow cytometry where the percentage of TRM (CD3+ CD8+ CCR7− CD62L− CD69+ CD103+) in KI-A24&E-rejTs (72.38% ± 2.3%) was significantly higher than that in the original HPV CTL clone (33.5% ± 2.6%; p < 0.0001) (Figure 5G). Furthermore, this CD69+ CD103+ population strongly expressed CD49a and CD44,32,33 consistent with TRM characteristics (Figure 5H). To examine the advantage conferred by the TRM population among HPV-rejTs targeting cervical cancer, we analyzed expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin on cervical cancer cell lines by flow cytometry: CD103-expressing TRM bind to E-cadherin, resulting in enhancement of cytotoxic function against epithelial malignancies.31 SiHa, SKGIIIa, and Caski cells clearly expressed E-cadherin (Figure 5I).

TGFβR signaling is essential for robust killing in HPV-rejTs

We sorted TRM and effector memory T cells (TEM; CD3+ CD8+ CCR7− CD62L− CD69− CD103−)34 from the original CTL clone by flow cytometry (Figure 6A) and examined whether TRM from the original CTL clone showed cytotoxicity against E-cadherin-expressing cervical cancer cells even without target peptide-pulse treatment. Original CTLs specifically killed cervical cancer cells only with peptide-pulse treatment (Figures 4C and 4D). As expected, cytotoxicity of TRM in CTL clone (E) against SKGIIIa cells (T) increased by 32.0% ± 0.8% and 25.1% ± 2.6% (E/T ratios 20:1 and 10:1, respectively) without target peptide-pulse treatment, whereas cytotoxicity of TEM in CTL clone (E) against SKGIIIa cells (T) was only 0.8% ± 0.3% (E/T ratio 20:1) without such treatment (Figure 6B). With target peptide-pulse treatment the cytotoxicity of TRM was far higher than that of TEM, at 74.3% ± 0.8% and 37.5% ± 5.8%, respectively (E/T ratio 20:1).

Hence, we successfully demonstrated that the CD103+/CD69+ TRM population is important for robust killing in cervical cancer. To confirm further the role of CD103 and E-cadherin interaction in enhancing the cytotoxicity of HPV-rejTs, we attempted to block E-cadherin/CD103 binding by using specific antibodies. Using real-time analysis, we evaluated whether anti-tumor activity decreased on inhibiting CD103, E-cadherin, or transforming growth factor β receptor (TGFβR). Interestingly, inhibition of TGFβR significantly suppressed cytotoxicity of HPV-rejTs against SiHa cells, while blocking E-cadherin or CD103 did not reduce cytotoxicity at the concentrations added in the experiments (TGFβR inhibitor 76.5% ± 2.6%, anti-E-cadherin antibody 98% ± 1.2%, anti-CD103 antibody 97.7% ± 0.6%, no antibody/inhibitor 97.8% ± 2.1%, 70 h after starting coculture) (TGFβR inhibitor vs. no antibody/inhibitor, p = 0.0039; anti-CD103 antibody vs..no antibody/inhibitor, p > 0.9999; anti-E-cadherin antibody vs. no antibody/inhibitor, p > 0.9999). These results indicate that TGFβR is essential for maintaining robust anti-tumor activity in HPV-rejTs (Figure 6C).

To investigate the significance of TGFβR signaling in the anti-tumor activity of HPV-rejTs, we analyzed CD103 expression on HPV-rejTs under each condition at 70 h after starting coculture (Figure 6D). The expression of CD103 clearly increased when cocultured with SiHa cells (RejTs only, no SiHa vs..RejTs cocultured with SiHa, no antibody [Ab]/inhibitor, p = 0.0002) (Figures 6D and 6E). On the other hand, inhibition of TGFβR significantly decreased CD103 expression on HPV-rejTs cocultured with SiHa cells (no Ab/inhibitor vs. TGFβR inhibitor, p < 0.0001), suggesting that TGFβR enhances cytotoxicity by promoting CD103 expression on HPV-rejTs (Figures 6D and 6E). To verify the reproducibility of these results, we also performed coculture experiments. We analyzed CD103 expression on HPV-rejTs at 72 h after starting coculturing and obtained results similar to those in assays using real-time cell analysis. The expression of CD103 on HPV-rejTs significantly increased on coculture with SiHa cells (coculture− vs. coculture+, p = 0.0002), but it clearly decreased with the addition of TGFβR inhibitor (coculture+ vs. coculture+ and TGFβR inhibitor, p = 0.0003) (Figures S5A and S5B). To confirm that the robust killing observed in HPV-rejTs was not due to NK-dependent killing and to assess the extent of off-target cytotoxicity against E-cadherin-expressing normal cells or tissues, we performed additional cytotoxic assays. NK-dependent killing or off-target cytotoxicity against NK ligand CD155+ and/or E-cadherin+ cells was not clearly observed (Figures S5C–S5E).

Based on these results, we conclude that TGFβR plays a crucial role in facilitating robust antigen-specific killing in HPV-rejTs with enhanced CD103 expression. Hence, TGFβR signaling enhances TCR activation against cervical cancer in a manner independent of peptide pulse (Figure S5F).

Discussion

In this study, we used the CRISPR-Cas9 two-step “scarless” gene-editing system to generate hypoimmunogenic rejTs without creating INDELs. Scarless editing is ideal for clinical translation, since it does not leave remnants of the editing process, including selection markers or silent mutations.28 Leaving behind a selection marker in the genome can interfere with regional transcriptional regulation.28

Generating iPSCs that overexpress HLA-E reduces NK cell-mediated lysis of B2M-deficient cells23; however, this editing strategy does not affect NKG2A− NK cells.25 To study NK cell responses in detail, we used HPV-rejTs and overexpressed HLA-A24, which is found in several populations around the world. Overexpression of the HLA-A24 molecule effectively suppressed allogeneic NK cell attack (Figures 2A and 2B). The Bw4 ligand of HLA-A24 binds to KIR3DL1, one of the most important molecular components of the NK cell inhibitory receptor.35 KIR3DL1 is prevalently expressed on NK cells (Caucasian 90%,36 African American 99%,37 Japanese 95%–99%,38,39 and other populations); its expression levels are high. Our results show that HLA-A24 and HLA-E dual integrated HPV-rejTs reduced NK cell attack more effectively than did HLA-A24 or HLA-E single integrated rejTs (Figures 2A and 2B). This may be due to donor-to-donor variation in NK cell NKG2A expression levels (Figure 2C). However, ∼20% of NKG2A− NK cells expressed KIR3DL1 (Figures 2C and 2D). We thus believe that dual integration of HLA-A24 and HLA-E into rejTs holds promise in suppressing allogeneic NK cell immunogenicity. We applied this strategy focusing on HLA-A24 because of the MHC restriction of CTL, but future studies can use other HLA allele types. Other strategies such as overexpression of CD47 inhibit phagocytosis by macrophages24,25; however, whether CD47-overexpressing rejTs can suppress NK cell activity must yet be studied. Although HLA-G overexpression reduces NK cell activity,25 KIR2DL4, an HLA-G receptor, is considered activating; HLA-G overexpression thus might paradoxically enhance NK cell-mediated responses.40,41 Examination of allogeneic NK cell attack against rejTs that overexpress HLA-G is thus necessary. While HLA-C integration also effectively suppresses NK cell response,26 when iPSCs that retain HLA-C are used for allogeneic transplantation, an estimated 7–11 iPSC lines are required to cover >95% of persons in each ethnic deme—a laborious approach.

Alloreactivity of CD8+ T cells was less when cocultured with HLA-edited rejTs than when cocultured with WT-rejTs (Figure 1I). Furthermore, alloreactivity of CD4+ T cells against HLA-edited HPV-rejTs was not observed, although in this study we knocked out only B2M (Figures 1I–1K). With partially HLA-matched virus-specific CTL therapy derived from third-party donors following transplantation in clinical trials,42,43 the degree of HLA matching was not correlated with response; complete or partial remission was achieved in 63% of patients matched for 1–3 alleles.42 Therefore, we think that the presence of HLA class II with those of rejTs would not be an issue and that disrupting CIITA to eliminate class II antigen expression could further reduce rejection.

HLA-edited HPV-rejTs maintained antigen specificity after differentiation (Figure 1G), exhibiting robust antigen-specific cytotoxicity against cervical cancer (Figures 3A and 3B) and prolonged survival (Figures 2G and 3E). Cytotoxicity against cervical cancer was much stronger in HLA-edited HPV-rejTs than in the original CTL clone even without peptide-pulse administration to the target cells (Figures 4C and 4D). Interestingly, scRNA-seq results revealed that HPV-rejTs included more TRM than did the original HPV-CTL clone (Figures 5E–5G). TRM enhance cytotoxic function against epithelial malignancies when activated by binding to CD103 and E-cadherin.31,44 TCR interaction with specific peptide-MHC-I complexes in the presence of TGF-β induces expression of CD103 on the CD8+ T cell surface. Phosphorylation of the integrin-linked kinase by TGFβR1 and consequent binding to CD103 increases affinity for E-cadherin, promoting CD8+ T cell effector functions such as cytokine production and polarized release of cytotoxic granules, leading to TCR-mediated target cell death31 (Figure S5F). In our study, even exhausted CTLs exhibited stronger cytotoxicity when they were enriched with TRM (Figure 6B). Therefore, TRM were important and TRM-enriched HPV-rejTs enhanced robust antigen-specific cytotoxicity against cervical cancer through CD103 enhancement (Figures 6C–6E). TGFβR signaling plays an essential role in enhancement of CD103 expression, resulting in maintenance of the robust killing in HPV-rejTs (Figures 6C–6E, S5A, and S5B). However, this enhancement was not mediated through E-cadherin, contrary to our expectations. Blocking only E-cadherin did not reduce killing of HPV-rejTs, indicating that binding between CD103 and E-cadherin does not fully explain enhancement. Furthermore, despite the increase of CD103 expression the antibody to CD103 did not block the killing activity of HPV-rejTs, suggesting the existence of other mechanisms to enhance TCR-mediated killing.

Furthermore, these rejTs also showed an IEL-like phenotype (Figures 5D and 5F), although HPV-rejTs recognize antigens in a TCR/CD3-dependent manner. HPV-rejTs were generated from a CTL clone that was MHC restricted. However, because these rejTs did not physically pass through the thymus, these TRM-enriched IEL-like HPV-rejTs maintained MHC-restricted robust antigen-specific cytotoxicity. Further investigation will address factors required to generate TRM-enriched rejTs, examining differentiation and stimulants, including lectin phytohemagglutinin-L (PHA-L) stimulation.30 These results suggest that HPV-rejTs can recognize antigens at various expression levels, whereas the original HPV-CTL clone cannot recognize antigens at low expression levels. Therefore, although HPV16 E6 expression in patient cervical cancer cells varied among samples (Figure 4E), HPV-rejTs can be expected to show a strong anti-tumor effect even when HPV16 E6 antigen expression by a particular cervical cancer is low, which would be an advantage in clinical translation. We have completed in vivo general toxicity and tumorigenesis testing using KI-A24&E-rejTs without safety problems.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that HLA-A24 and HLA-E dual integrated rejTs can evade both allogeneic CD8+ T cell and NK cell attack, overcoming drawbacks of conventional T cell therapy by surviving in vivo and yielding a tumor-suppressive effect more sustained than that achieved by peripheral blood-derived original CTLs. Although this report demonstrates proof of concept for this method using HLA-A24, similarly engineered cells can be varied for HLA types such as HLA-A0245 and banked, so that when need arises, aliquots can be withdrawn, expanded, and used to supply unlimited numbers of therapeutic CTLs. Moreover, this strategy can also be applied to engineered TCR-T cell therapy. These preclinical data offer promise; clinical translation, with TRM-enriched potent “off-the-shelf” therapy targeting cervical cancer, awaits.

Limitations of the study

A potential limitation of our study includes the fact that only T cell proliferation was the readout for T cell-mediated alloreactivity by CFSE assay. Also, confirmation to detect allorejection by mixed-lymphocyte reaction assay would be ideal; however, donor relocation precludes further sampling of peripheral blood. Another limitation of our study is that because tumor cells and residual cervical cancer cells were not observed macroscopically or microscopically, we were not able to analyze tumor-infiltrating cells to learn whether their surface phenotypes were consistent with TRM. In several experiments the use of primary cervical cancer cells was desirable but impracticable, as fresh primary cervical cancer cells known to be HLA-A2402 are difficult to obtain. Although our study has some limitations, it provides a solid step for further exploring TRM-enriched HPV-rejTs as a promising therapy for cervical cancer.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and cell culture

Healthy donors bearing HLA-A24 were recruited and informed consent was obtained. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee at Juntendo University School of Medicine (protocols M16–0132 and M20-0226). The human iPSC line was established from an HLA-A2402-restricted HPV16 E6-specific CTL clone as described.15 The HLA-A24+ cervical cancer cell line SiHa was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA). The cervical cancer cell lines HLA-A24+ SKG-IIIa and HLA-A02+ Caski were purchased from RIKEN BioResource Research Center (RIKEN BRC; Ibaragi, Japan). Cervical cancer cell lines were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin-glutamine (PSG; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). K562 cells purchased from Japanese Collection of Research BioSources Cell Bank (Osaka, Japan) were maintained in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM; Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco Life Technologies) and 1% PSG (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Epstein-Barr virus–infected lymphoblastoid cells were derived from HLA-A24− healthy donor PBMCs and were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich) with 10% FBS (Gibco Life Technologies) and 1% PSG (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The non-small cell lung cancer cell line A549 was purchased from ATCC and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PSG. To monitor tumor volume in vivo, FFluc/EGFP-expressing SiHa cells were generated by transduction of SiHa cells with retrovirus vectors encoding both FFluc and EGFP as described.15

All cell lines were tested mycoplasma-free. Cultures with <10 passages were used for experiments (all cell lines). C3H10T1/2 feeder cells (RIKEN BRC) and DL1/DL4-expressing C3H10T1/2 feeder cells were used to differentiate iPSCs into HPV-rejTs. These feeder cells were maintained in minimum essential medium-α (MEM-α; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco Life Technologies) and 1% PSG (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The HEK 293T cell line (RIKEN BRC), used for retrovirus production, was maintained in DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco Life Technologies) and 1% PSG (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Mice

All in vivo experiments were approved by the Animal Research Committees of Juntendo University School of Medicine (#2023233). Female NOG mice aged 6weeks (In-Vivo Science, Tokyo, Japan) were used for all in vivo experiments. All mice were bred and housed at the animal facility of Juntendo University School of Medicine on a 12h light/dark cycle with constant temperature (23°C) and a humidity level (60%) with free access to food and water.

Methods details

CRISPR-Cas9 two-step “scarless” gene editing

Two million iPSCs were dissociated and subjected to electroporation using the P3 Primary Cell 4D-Nucleofector system (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (program: CA-137) with 5μg of px330 plasmid and total 5μg of donor vector. The gRNAs for first-step editing and second-step editing were used as reported.28 Table S1 lists gRNA sequences used for editing. After electroporation, iPSCs were seeded onto 6-well plates coated with iMatrix-511 (Nippi, Tokyo, Japan) and cultured with StemFit (Ajinomoto Healthy Supply, Tokyo, Japan). Ten days after transfection, iPSCs were harvested and conjugated with anti-human CD8 beads (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). MS columns (Miltenyi Biotec) were used for positive selection according to the manufacturer’s protocol. CD8+ cells were then serially diluted onto iMatrix-511-coated 6-well plates for single cell cloning. Fluorescence of EGFP in iPSCs was confirmed using fluorescence microscopy, and iPSC clones with bright EGFP expression were selected for study and propagation.

As with first-step editing, iPSCs were dissociated and subjected to electroporation as described above. To obtain KI-A24&E-iPSCs, donor vectors (2.5 μg each, for a total of 5.0 μg) were mixed. After electroporation, iPSCs were seeded onto iMatrix-511 (Nippi)-coated 6-well plates and cultured with StemFit (Ajinomoto Healthy Supply) as described above. Ten days after transfection, iPSCs were harvested, conjugated with anti-human CD8 beads (Miltenyi Biotec), and subjected to negative selection with LD columns (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. CD8− cells were then serially diluted onto iMatrix-511 (Nippi)-coated 6-well plates for single cell cloning. Lack of EGFP fluorescence in iPSCs was confirmed using fluorescence microscopy and iPSC clones without EGFP expression were selected for study and propagation.

Genotyping PCR and DNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from healthy donor PBMCs using QIAamp DNA Blood Midi Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, The Netherlands) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Genotyping of HLA-E was performed with PrimeSTAR GXL DNA polymerase (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan): extracted genomic DNA was amplified for 30 cycles (98°C for 10 s, 58°C for 10 s, and 68°C for 90 s). iPSC colonies were picked up and genomic DNA was extracted using QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (Lucigen, Middleton, WI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genotyping of B2M was performed with PrimeSTAR GXL DNA polymerase (Takara Bio). Extracted genomic DNA was amplified by 30 cycles of PCR (98°C for 10 s, 58°C for 10 s, and 68°C for 6 min). Using NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up (both Takara Bio), purified PCR products for sequencing were obtained from agarose gels. Sequencing analysis was done by FASMAC (Kanagawa, Japan) with BigDye Terminator v3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using an Applied Biosystems 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). HLA-A alleles were determined at SRL (Tokyo, Japan) by PCR-rSSO/Luminex methods. HLA-E alleles were determined using PCR and direct sequencing. Table S1 lists all primers used for genotyping PCR and sequencing.

Differentiation of rejTs

WT- and HLA class I-edited iPSCs were differentiated into WT- and HLA class I-edited rejTs as previously described.15,19,20,21,46,47,48,49,50 To differentiate iPSCs into hematopoietic stem cells, clumps of iPSCs (<100 cells) were transferred onto C3H10T1/2 cells. iPSCs and C3H10T1/2 cells were cocultured in IMDM (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 15% gamma-irradiated FBS (HyClone, GE Healthcare, South Logan, UT) and a cocktail of 10 mg/mL human insulin, 5.5 mg/mL human transferrin, 5 ng/mL sodium selenite, 2 mM L-glutamine (all Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.45 mM α-monothioglycerol (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical), and 50 mg/mL ascorbic acid (Takeda, Osaka, Japan) in the presence of 20 ng/mL vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF; Miltenyi Biotec). Hematopoietic progenitor cells collected from iPSC sacs were transferred onto DL1/4-expressing C3H10T1/2 feeder cells to induce T lineage differentiation. Hematopoietic progenitor cells and feeder cells were cocultured in MEM-α (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 20% FBS (HyClone) and 1% PSG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the presence of 20 ng/mL recombinant human stem cell factor, 10 ng/mL Fms-related tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt-3L), and 10 ng/mL IL7 (all Miltenyi Biotec). Floating T lineage cells were harvested 4weeks after coculture began, stimulated with 5 μg/ml PHA-L (Sigma-Aldrich), and mixed with irradiated PBMCs in NS-A2 CTL medium (Nissui, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with 1% PSG in the presence of 10 ng/ml IL7 and 10 ng/ml IL15 (Miltenyi Biotec). HPV-rejTs and the original CTL clone were stimulated with 5 μg/mL PHA-L and mixed with irradiated PBMCs in the presence of 10 ng/mL IL7 and 10 ng/mL IL15 for 2weeks to prepare activated T cells for experiments. Media such as C3H10T1/2, the DL1/4-expressing C3H10T1/2 feeder cell lines, and gamma-irradiated FBS (HyClone) have already been validated and are compatible with good manufacturing practice standards for clinical use. Master cell bank stocks have been made.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry used BD LSRFortessa equipment (BD Biosciences). The results were analyzed with FlowJo software 10.5.3 (Tree Star, Ashland, OR). Propidium iodide (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to discriminate dead cells. Fluorescence-minus-one controls were used in all antibody combinations to interpret flow cytometry data. To verify HLA expression, isotypes were used as controls. For intracellular staining, eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription Factor staining Buffer Set was used according to manufacturer protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

The cell suspensions were incubated with antibody cocktails at 4°C in the dark for 30 min. Afterward, the cells were washed once with 6 mL PBS. Live cells were gated using propidium iodide (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in all analyses. The fluorescence-minus-one method was employed to analyze flow cytometry data for all combinations of antibodies. MFI of granzyme B, perforin, and CD103 was analyzed using FlowJo software 10.5.3.

Generation of NK cells and T cells

PBMCs isolated from HLA-A24+ and HLA-A24− healthy donors were conjugated with human CD3 beads (Miltenyi Biotec). Negative selection was performed with LD columns (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. NK cells were generated based on previous reports with modifications.51 CD3-negative PBMCs were cultured in NS-A2 CTL medium (Nissui) supplemented with 1% PSG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a cocktail of 5μM zoledronic acid (Novartis International, Basel, Switzerland) and 0.01 kE/ml OK-432 (Chugai Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) in the presence of 700 IU/mL IL2 (Miltenyi Biotec) on anti-CD16 antibody (Beckman Coulter)-coated T25 flasks. Three days after culture, floating cells were harvested and transferred to a new T25 flask without coating. The cells were maintained in NS-A2 CTL medium (Nissui) supplemented with 1% PSG in the presence of 700 IU/mL IL2 (Miltenyi Biotec) for 2weeks to obtain activated NK cells for experiments. The remaining PBMCs were stimulated with TransAct (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer’s protocol and cultured in NS-A2 CTL medium (Nissui) supplemented with 1% PSG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the presence of 10 ng/mL IL7 and 10 ng/mL IL15 (both Miltenyi Biotec) for 2weeks to prepare activated T cells for experiments.

Acquisition of E-cadherin-positive cells

Bone marrow samples from healthy donors were thawed and suspended in RPMI (Sigma-Aldrich) with 10% human AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich) and seeded onto 6-well E-cadherin Fc plates (SOMAR, Tokyo, Japan). Medium was changed every 2-3days, and the floating cells were removed during each medium change. After 7days, spindle-shaped cells adhered to the plates were detached using trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and used for chromium release assay and flow cytometry.

CFSE-labeled T cell proliferation assay

HLA-A24+ allogeneic T cells (2x106 cells) were labeled with 5μM CellTrace CFSE (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20 min according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, WT- and HLA class I-edited rejTs (5x105 cells) were cocultured with CFSE-labeled T cells in RPMI supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated human AB serum and 1% PSG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the presence of 10 ng/mL IL7 and 10 ng/mL IL15 (both Miltenyi Biotec) for 5days. On day 5, cocultured cells were harvested and stained with anti-CD3, -CD4, and -CD8 antibodies (all BD Biosciences), anti-human HLA-A24 antibody, and HLA-A∗2402/E649-57 tetramer (both MBL International). After staining, CFSE intensity was analyzed by flow cytometry. T cells cultured for 5days without target cells were used as a negative control. T cells treated for 5days with TransAct (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer’s protocol were used as a positive control.25

51Cr release assay

The cytotoxicity of effector cells against target cells, as analyzed by standard 51Cr release assays, was determined at different E: T ratios.52 Target cells were labeled with 51Cr (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA) for 1 h and effector cells were cocultured with the target cells for 6h. Some target cells were also pulsed with HLA-A2402/HPV16 E649-57 custom-synthesized peptide (50 ng) (Mimotopes, Mulgrave, Australia) at 37°C for 30 min (Figure 4C right and 4D right). After 6h of incubation, supernatant was harvested and radioactivity was measured with a 1450 MicroBeta TriLux scintillation counter (PerkinElmer). Percentage of cytotoxicity was calculated as [(experimental release − spontaneous release)/(maximum release − spontaneous release)] × 100 (%).

CD107a NK cell degranulation assay

HLA-A24+ NK cells (1x105 cells) were cocultured with WT- or HLA class I-edited rejTs (3x105 cells) in 24-well plates at E: T ratios of 1:3. APC mouse anti-human CD107a (10μL; BioLegend) and Protein Transport Inhibitor Cocktail (1μL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were then added. NK cells and HPV-rejTs were spun down at 2,000 rpm for 5 min for sufficient effector and target contact. After 20h of incubation, cells were collected, stained with PE-conjugated mouse anti-human CD56 (BioLegend) and APC/Cy7-conjugated mouse anti human CD3, and analyzed by flow cytometry for cell-surface expression of CD107a. NK cells without target cells were used as negative controls, while NK cells treated with Cell Stimulation Cocktail (plus protein transport inhibitors) (500X) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s protocol, were used as positive controls.25

In vivo experiments

To examine in vivo immunoreactivity of allogeneic NK cells in KI-A24&E-rejT injected mice, KI-A24&E-rejTs transduced with γ-retroviral vector encoding the fusion protein EGFP/FFluc were sorted for EGFP expression by flow cytometry. Female NOG mice aged 6weeks (In-Vivo Science, Tokyo, Japan) intraperitoneally inoculated with SiHa cells (1.0 x 105 cells/mouse) 4days earlier were intraperitoneally injected with EGFP/FFluc KI-A24&E-rejTs (2.5 x 106 cells/mouse). After 3h mice were divided into 2 experimental groups (intraperitoneally injected with either HLA-A24+ or HLA-A24− NK cells, 2.5x 106 cells) and a control group (KI-A24&E-rejTs only); injections were given on days 0, 7, and 14. Bioluminescence signals were successively evaluated. Evasion from NK cell attack was monitored using the Caliper in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Caliper Life Sciences, Mountain View, CA), with firefly D-luciferin substrate (OZ Biosciences, Marseille, France) intraperitoneally injected 15min before monitoring.

Escape from allogeneic NK cells and CD8+ T cells in KI-A24&E-rejT-injected mice was also monitored. Female NOG mice aged 6weeks (In-Vivo Science) were intraperitoneally inoculated with SiHa cells (1.0 x 105 cells/mouse) 4days before intraperitoneal administration of EGFP/FFluc KI-A24&E-rejTs (2.5 x 106 cells/mouse). After 3h, mice were divided into 4 groups, with 3 experimental groups (intraperitoneally injected with KI-A24&E-rejTs+NK+CD8+ T cells, KI-A24&E-rejTs+NK cells, or KI-A24&E-rejTs+CD8+ T cells, each receiving 2.5 x 106 cells once weekly for 3weeks) and a control group (KI-A24&E-rejTs only). Persistence of KI-A24&E-rejTs in vivo was monitored by IVIS.

To verify that HLA-rejTs suppressed tumor, 6-week-old female NOG mice (In-Vivo Science) intraperitoneally engrafted with EGFP/FFluc SiHa (1.0 x 105 cells/mouse) were divided into 3 treatment groups (original CTL clone, WT-rejTs, or KI-A24&E-rejTs, 2.5x 106 cells, 3 doses once a week) and one no-treatment group. Tumor growth was monitored using IVIS (Caliper Life Sciences). Living Image software version 4.7.2 (PerkinElmer) was used for luminescence analyses. Intensity signal was measured as total photon/s/cm2/steradian (p/s/cm2/sr) as previously reported.19,53

To further verify antitumor efficacy of HPV-rejTs in vivo, we also deployed a subcutaneous cervical cancer model. Six-week-old female NOG mice (In-Vivo Science) subcutaneously engrafted with SKGIIIa (5 x 105 cells/mouse) were divided into 2 treatment groups (original CTL clone or HPV-rejTs, 5x 106 cells/mouse, once a week for 5weeks) and untreated group 7days after tumor injection. 1000 IU of IL-2 were additionally injected intraperitonially 3 times a week for 5weeks. Tumor growth was monitored by measuring tumor size in 3 dimensions with a digital caliper.

Real-time tumor killing assay based on impedance

Effector (E) and target (T) cells were cocultured to perform cytotoxicity assays using xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analyzer (RTCA) (ACEA Biosciences, San Diego, CA) as described.15,20,35,36,54 Target cells were seeded (1x104 SiHa or SKGIIIa cells per well) and the continuous increase in electrical impedance (cell index, CI) of target cells was monitored according to manufacturer instructions (ACEA Biosciences). After adhesion of target cells, effector T cells (E: T ratio, 1:1) were added. In both experiments, T cells not specific to HPV16 E6 antigen served as controls. We also evaluated cytotoxicity of KI-A24&E-rejTs with anti-CD103 Ab (clone 2G5, Beckman Coulter), anti-E-cadherin Ab (clone SHE78-7, Takara Bio), and/or TGFβR-inhibitor (SD-208, Selleck Biotech, Tokyo, Japan) against SiHa cells using RTCA. The anti-CD103 and anti-E-cadherin Abs were added at a final concentration of 25 μg/mL and 20 μg/mL on the starting coculture with the effector. TGFβR-inhibitor was added at a final concentration of 100 μM at the same time as the other 2 reagents.

CI of adherent cells in each well was automatically assessed and recorded by RTCA every 15min for up to 70h (the interval during which tumor cells in control wells became confluent. All data were analyzed using xCELLigence RTCA software (version 2.0), with results graphed as CI values (horizontal axis; time in coculture, vertical axis) normalized to 1 at the time of addition of effector T cells. Detergent (0.2% Triton X-100, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to some wells and value upon complete cell lysis (CImax) was determined. Percentages of cytotoxicity were calculated as ([CIno effector - CIeffector]/[CIno effector - CImax]) × 100 (%).12,55 All electrical impedance-based cytotoxicity assays were performed in triplicate with 3 independent assays to ensure reproducibility.

Coculture experiments

KI-A24&E-rejTs were cocultured with SiHa cells at an E: T ratio of 1:1 (1x105/1x105 cells per well) in RPMI (Sigma-Aldrich) with 10% AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich). TGFβR inhibitor (SD-208, Selleck Biotech) was added to achieve final concentrations of 100 μM and 200 μM, respectively. KI-A24&E-rejTs were collected at day 3 of coculture and stained with anti-CD3, anti-CD8, anti-CD62L, anti-CD69 and anti-CD103 antibodies. CD3+ CD8+ CD62L− CD69+ gated CD103 expression on KI-A24&E-rejTs was evaluated by flow cytometry.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and quantitative real-time PCR

Expression of HPV16 E6 was measured by quantitative real-time PCR in resected squamous cell carcinoma tissue from 7 cervical cancer patients (Figure 4E; HPV16 was positive in 5 of the 7, HPV16 was absent from 2). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee at Juntendo University School of Medicine (protocol M09-0558). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Total RNA was extracted from homogenized clinical tissue samples using TRIzol (Invitrogen) and cDNA was synthesized using a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) according to manufacturer protocol. Table S1 shows sequences of primers and probes used to assess expression of HPV16 E6 by quantitative real-time PCR. Quantitative real-time PCR assays were performed on the StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA).56 Individual PCR reactions were normalized against glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase levels.

scRNA-seq data processing and analysis

Approximately 2.0 x 106 cells (in vitro cultured original CTL clone and KI-A24&E rejTs, 13days after stimulation with 5 μg/ml PHA-L (Sigma-Aldrich), mixed with irradiated PBMCs in NS-A2 CTL medium (Nissui, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with 1% PSG in the presence of 10 ng/ml IL7 and 10 ng/ml IL15 (both Miltenyi Biotec)), viability >85%, were suspended in 50 μL of chilled phosphate-buffered saline +0.04% bovine serum albumin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and processed to yield scRNA-seq libraries using 10X Chromium Next Gem Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v 3.1 (Dual Index) (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) according to manufacturer instructions. Samples were sequenced (Macrogen, Seoul, South Korea) on the HiseqX (Illumina, San Diego, CA) platform (paired end; read 1: 28, i5 index: 10, i7 index: 10, read 2: 90) at a depth of 60,000 read pairs/sample. Data were demultiplexed, aligned to human reference genome (GRCh38-2020-A), and quantified using Cell Ranger (v7.0.1, 10X Genomics). Downstream analyses used Seurat (v4.0).57 Doublets were removed (Doublet Finder58) and samples were filtered for cells with <20% mitochondrial gene expression and >500 genes detected per cell. Samples were normalized using sctransform59,60 followed by integration of samples with clustering (resolution = 0.4) and UMAP analysis using the standard Seurat workflow. Cluster numbers were determined based on computational methods (clustree61) and the expression of marker genes (Figure 5). Clusters were manually annotated using published gene expression profiles of immune cell types29,31,62,63 and further annotated using the FindConservedMarkers and FindAllMarkers functions. Differential gene expression analysis between clusters or cell populations in samples used the FindMarkers function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± standard deviation. Results were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t test (two-tailed) or ordinary one-way or two-way analysis of variance as stated, with a p value < 0.05 indicating a significant difference. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log rank testing was used to compare survival curves. Excel Office 2019 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) and Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) software was used for all statistical analyses.
