Prevalence and determinants of general and abdominal obesity and overweight among older Iranian adults using 2016 and 2021 STEPS surveys
Authors: Shaghayegh Khanmohammadi, Keyvan Karimi, Mohadeseh Sadri, Mahbube Ebrahimpur, Moloud Payab, Sina Azadnajafabad, Samaneh Akbarpour, Farshad Sharifi, Nazila Rezaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity is a recognized risk factor for non-communicable diseases (NCDs). With Iran’s aging population and ongoing demographic shifts, monitoring obesity trends among older adults is essential for public health planning. This cross-sectional study used data from the 2016 and 2021 STEPwise Approach to NCD Risk Factor Surveillance (STEPS) national surveys to estimate the prevalence of overweight, general obesity, and abdominal obesity among Iranian adults aged ≥ 60 years at national and subnational levels. Stratified cluster sampling was applied to select participants from urban and rural areas. Standardized WHO protocols were used to collect demographic, lifestyle, and anthropometric data. A total of 10,555 older adults were included. The prevalence of general obesity increased from 27.4% in 2016 to 29.1% in 2021, while abdominal obesity rose from 53.6% to 59.1% (p < 0.001). Higher prevalence was observed among women, individuals with lower physical activity, and those with higher socioeconomic status (p < 0.001). Factors associated with abdominal obesity included female sex, younger age, hypertension, smoking, hypercholesterolemia, and diabetes. The findings indicate a rising trend in both general and abdominal obesity among older Iranian adults. Identifying population subgroups with greater risk may support the development of targeted, community-based prevention strategies.Keywords: Older adults, Obesity, Abdominal obesity, Risk factors, STEPS, IranSubject terms: Obesity, Epidemiology

Introduction

Obesity is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus (DM), musculoskeletal disorders, and cancers, all of which constitute a large proportion of non-communicable diseases (NCDs)1,2. Over the past two decades, the global prevalence of individuals with overweight and obesity has doubled, with nearly a third of the world’s population now considered to have obesity or overweight3,4.

A wide range of determinants influence obesity, including genetic, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors5. Age is another key factor influencing obesity, with studies showing that body weight often increases in middle-aged adults due to lifestyle and metabolic changes such as reduced resting metabolic rate and metabolic adaptation6. In older populations, obesity is associated with adverse health outcomes, such as an elevated risk of diabetes and heart failure7,8.

At the same time, global aging is emerging as one of the most pressing public health challenges. By 2050, one in five people worldwide will be 60 years or older, with projections reaching 2 billion older adults9. Many developing countries, including Iran, are experiencing rapid population aging and face significant challenges in providing adequate healthcare for their growing older adult populations10,11. Aging is accompanied by progressive physiological changes that impact functionality, autonomy, and nutritional status, often leading to decreased energy expenditure and shifts in body composition12. While there is still debate about the optimal weight in older age13, evidence from cross-sectional and longitudinal studies suggests that obesity and abdominal obesity are rising among older adults14,15. In Iran, a systematic review in 2019 reported that the prevalence of obesity in older adults was 21.4%16.

Given the increasing older adult population in Iran and the growing burden of high body mass index (BMI)17, there is a critical need for targeted research to better understand obesity trends and associated risk factors in this age group. While previous studies have examined obesity prevalence at the national level, limited research has explored subnational disparities in obesity rates among older adults, despite Iran’s well-documented regional variations in health and socioeconomic conditions. This study aims to fill this gap by using nationally representative data from the STEPwise Approach to NCD Risk Factor Surveillance (STEPS) survey18 to estimate the prevalence of individuals with overweight and with general and abdominal obesity among the Iranian older adult population at both national and subnational levels. Additionally, identifying the factors associated with overweight, general obesity, and abdominal obesity serves as a secondary objective of this study. The findings will contribute valuable insights to inform targeted public health strategies and policy interventions to address obesity in older adults.

Methods

Overview

The STEPs study is an ongoing sequential large-scale cross-sectional population-based surveillance of NCD risk factors18. The national STEPs surveys in 2016 and 2021 were conducted with representative samples from urban and rural areas of Iran. The details of the methods and procedures are discussed elsewhere19. Here, we only point to some essential requirements.

Sampling

Aimed at addressing the complexities of regional differences, heterogeneous and incommensurable population distributions, and financial considerations, we used the systematic cluster classification method combined with different probability sampling methods18. Concurrently, considering the representativeness of the samples at the national and sub-national levels, the sample size required for evaluating NCDs’ risk factors in each province was calculated using a proportion-to-size method18.

The study population was selected using a systematic, size-proportional cluster sample from urban and rural areas across all 30 provinces of Iran. In 2016, there were 30,541 participants, and in 2021, there were 27,874. Of these, 5,298 and 5,758 participants were aged 60 years or older, respectively. The missing data in 2016 and 2021 amounted to less than 5% of the total sample size and showed no particular pattern. Therefore, the missing data were deleted, and the complete case was used in all analyses. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the potential impact of missing data, and no significant bias was observed. After removing the missing data, the final sample size was 5,085 and 5,470 individuals in 2016 and 2021, respectively (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study population.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Based on the study protocol of the 2021 STEPs survey, participants were eligible if they were ≥ 60 years old and resided in Iran during the data collection period, with no BMI restrictions. Exclusion criteria included severe physical or mental conditions that impeded participation and the inability to provide laboratory specimens.

The Iran STEPS Survey 2021, as detailed in the study protocol18, was conducted in two main phases of design and implementation, including three steps of data collection: questionnaires, physical measurements, and laboratory assessments. In the first step, the questionnaires were designed based on the latest version of the WHO STEPS instrument (version 3.2)20, with trained experts responsible for sampling and conducting the examinations. Validated questionnaires were used to collect demographic and epidemiological data, as well as information on lifestyle factors such as nutritional habits, physical activity, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Additionally, the questionnaires addressed participants’ history of metabolic risk factors, treatments, injuries, healthcare utilization, and participation in screening programs. Physical activity was assessed using the WHO Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ)21. The total minutes of leisure and walking time (non-occupational) physical activity were converted to tertiles and classified into low, moderate, and high based on the lower to higher tertiles, with exact cutoffs reported in supplementary materials.

To ensure accuracy, specific interviewing guidelines were developed and provided to the interviewers.

In the second step, physical measurements were taken according to WHO criteria, using calibrated instruments by trained healthcare professionals. Participants’ height was measured without shoes while standing against a wall, ensuring that their heels, hips, and back of the head were aligned. Participants’ weight was measured to the nearest 100 g using a calibrated digital scale (Inofit), with the device recalibrated each time it was moved using a 5 kg reference scale. Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the hip, crossing the umbilicus, with an acceptable range of 30–200 cm. Hip circumference was measured at the widest part of the hip, within an acceptable range of 45–300 cm. Blood pressure and pulse rate were measured three times using Beurer sphygmomanometers, with a 15-minute interval between readings. The mean of the second and third readings was used as the final blood pressure.

The BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by squared height (m²). The WHO criteria were used in classifying BMI < 18.5; 18.5 ≤ BMI < 25; 25 ≤ BMI < 30; and BMI ≥ 30 as underweight, normal, overweight, and obesity, respectively22. Considering potential variations in obesity classification among the older adults, sensitivity analyses were performed to compare WHO cutoffs with region-specific thresholds. Abdominal obesity was defined as a waist circumference of 89 cm (35 inches) or more for women and 102 cm (40 inches) or more for men23.

In the third step, laboratory tests (FPG and cholesterol) were conducted at the survey headquarters using an auto-analyzer (Roche-Hitachi Cobas C311, High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), which was approved by the reference laboratory18. This three-step process ensured that data was collected comprehensively and using standardized methods.

Definitions of variables

Marital status was classified as single and married. Unmarried people included all older adults who were never married, widowed, or separated. Education was assessed based on the number of completed years of schooling in four subcategories [0 (illiterate), 1–6, 7–11, and ≥ 12 years]. The occupation was classified into three categories: not working/disabled, working, and retired.

The wealth index (WI) was calculated based on the factors assessed via the household assets questionnaire, which included 36 questions examining various dimensions of participants’ assets. The principal component analysis (PCA) method was utilized to summarize the multivariable collected data into a factor that explains the largest proportion of the total variance. Then, participants’ calculated wealth indices were categorized into five quintiles from the poorest (first quintile) to the richest (fifth quintile)19. Current smoking habits were categorized into never-smokers and current smokers. Alcohol consumption was defined as the use of alcoholic beverages within the last year. Hypercholesterolemia was defined either as self-reported related drug consumption or a cholesterol level of ≥ 200 mg/dL. Hypertension criteria were systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or self-reported related drug consumption. Diabetes based on fasting plasma glucose (FPG) was considered as FPG ≥ 126 mg/dl or self-reporting of oral hypoglycemic agent (OHA) use and/or insulin injection.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were carried out using STATA version 17. Data from the 2016 and 2021 surveys were weighted by age, sex, and residence based on the 2016 Iranian census24. The non-response rate was also considered in the weighting in order to minimize potential response bias. The weighted proportion was used to describe the baseline characteristics in the two surveys according to the levels of general and abdominal obesity. The Chi-square test was applied to evaluate the relationship between different categorical parameters and levels of general and abdominal obesity. A proportion test was used to determine the change in weight status (normal/overweight/obesity) from 2016 to 2021 in different subgroups of study parameters. This test is a specific multinomial test that was used to compare the distribution of qualitative variables. Stepwise logistic regression with forward variable selection was used to determine factors associated with abdominal obesity. The critical p-value for deleting variables was set at p > 0.20, based on previous studies, to ensure important predictors were not prematurely excluded. A multicollinearity assessment was conducted before including variables in the models. Multinomial logistic regression was also applied to identify factors associated with general obesity. The results of these models were presented as odds ratios (OR) with the corresponding confidence intervals. The subnational prevalence of obesity was plotted using the “ggplot2” package in the RStudio software. The confidence interval for all analyses was 95%, and p < 0.05 was interpreted as statistically significant20.

Ethical considerations

This study has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were fully informed about the study’s objectives and procedures. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrollment. To ensure confidentiality, the final dataset was de-identified before analysis, and access to the survey database was restricted to the primary investigator and the database manager. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the National Institute for Health Research (ID: IR.TUMS.EMRI.REC.1403.053).

Results

This study analyzed data from 10,555 older adult participants. In 2016, 5,085 individuals with a mean age of 69.2 ± 7.5 years (50.9% female) were included, while in 2021, the sample comprised 5,470 individuals with a mean age of 68.5 ± 7.1 years (51.6% female). Table 1 presents the prevalence of older adults with general obesity and overweight in Iran at both time points. The overall obesity rate showed a slight, non-significant increase from 27.4% in 2016 to 29.1% in 2021 (p = 0.062). However, significant differences were observed across subgroups. Obesity prevalence among adults over 80 years increased from 14.0% to 17.2% (p = 0.03), while males experienced a significant rise from 16.7% to 18.8% (p = 0.029). Furthermore, individuals with lower physical activity levels had a higher prevalence of general obesity and a significant rise in obesity from 2016 to 2021 in this group was recorded. Moreover, obesity prevalence was significantly higher among married individuals, those with 7–12 + years of education, and those in the lowest wealth quintile in 2021 compared to 2016 (Table 1).

The prevalence of abdominal obesity among older adults significantly increased from 53.6% in 2016 to 59.1% in 2021 (p < 0.001). Abdominal obesity prevalence increased significantly across most population subgroups, except for those with ≥ 12 years of education, individuals in the 2nd, 4th, and 5th wealth index quintiles, alcohol users, people without hypertension, and those with cardiovascular diseases. The most notable rises were observed in the 60–70 and 80 age groups, where prevalence increased from 56.7% to 61.6% and from 42.1% to 49.4%, respectively (p < 0.001 for both). The prevalence of abdominal obesity remained significantly higher in females than males, though both sexes experienced a significant increase. In males, prevalence rose from 29.0% to 35.3%, while in females, it increased from 77.1% to 81.1% (p < 0.001 for both). Additionally, individuals with lower physical activity levels and higher alcohol consumption had a greater prevalence of abdominal obesity (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of overweight and general and abdominal obesity and the distribution of BMI levels among the Iranian older adult population. The results highlight key demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and health-related factors associated with obesity among older adults. Age and sex significantly influenced obesity risk, with males and those aged > 80 years showing lower odds of both general and abdominal obesity. More affluent individuals and those with lower physical activity levels were at higher risk, emphasizing the role of lifestyle and socioeconomic disparities. Chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia were firmly associated with rising obesity prevalence.

BMI and WC are both widely used tools to assess health risks associated with body weight and fat distribution, but each has its advantages and disadvantages. BMI is a simple and cost-effective measure calculated from height and weight, making it easy to use in large populations and clinical settings. However, it does not differentiate between muscle mass and fat mass, potentially misclassifying muscular individuals as overweight or obese. On the other hand, WC directly measures abdominal fat, which is a stronger predictor of metabolic risks such as cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. Despite its relevance, WC requires precise measurement techniques and may vary based on ethnicity and body shape, limiting its universal applicability. While BMI provides a broad assessment of weight status, WC offers more specific insight into central obesity, making them complementary tools in health evaluations25.

Among adults aged 80 years and older, the prevalence of obesity increased significantly from 14.0% in 2016 to 17.2% in 2021. Moreover, obesity prevalence was significantly higher among married individuals, those with 7–12 + years of education, those with low physical activity, and those in the lowest wealth quintile in 2021 compared to 2016. Our results also indicate that abdominal obesity prevalence in older adults significantly increased from 53.6% in 2016 to 59.1% in 2021. This increase was statistically significant across both sexes, married individuals, different levels of physical activity, and chronic conditions. This finding aligns with global trends, which indicate that the prevalence of obesity in older adults has increased threefold worldwide in the last forty years, affecting both developed and developing nations26. This trend may be attributed to lifestyle changes, unhealthful dietary habits27, and aging-related physiological changes, such as decreased metabolic rate and hormonal shifts28.

A meta-analysis on the prevalence of obesity in Iran at the national and subnational level showed that, according to reports published between 2012 and 2021, the highest prevalence of overweight and obesity in Iran was observed in the provinces of Alborz, Kermanshah, Hormozgan, Bushehr, East Azerbaijan, Yazd, Hamedan, and Guilan, respectively. In contrast, the lowest rates of overweight and obesity were consistently reported in the provinces of Lorestan, Kordistan, Ilam, and Chahar-Mahal during the same period29. The prevalence values in our study may differ from those reported in this study at the subnational level in Iran due to variations in sample size, demographic characteristics, and data collection methods. Our findings revealed that the highest prevalence of obesity among provinces was observed in Qazvin in 2016 and in Qom in 2021. Additionally, we found that in both 2016 and 2021, the southern and southeastern provinces, such as Hormozgan, Kerman, and Sistan & Baluchestan, had lower rates of obesity. In contrast, northern provinces like Guilan and Mazandaran, as well as the northwestern Azerbaijani provinces, exhibited higher obesity rates. Higher prevalence of obesity in northern and northwestern provinces may be linked to higher incomes, sedentary lifestyles, unhealthy diet habits, cultural attitudes, and easier access to calorie-rich options30. Overall, there were modest increases in obesity prevalence across most provinces in 2021 compared to 2016. Furthermore, in both 2016 and 2021, central provinces, including Isfahan, Qom, and Markazi, had the highest abdominal obesity rates, while the eastern and southeastern regions reported the lowest. This trend may be attributed to sedentary urban lifestyles prevalent in the central area31. These findings emphasize the necessity for interventions that are specific to each region. In the northern and northwestern areas, nutrition education programs could focus on promoting a high-fiber, low-glycemic index diet and healthier food preparation behaviors. Highly urbanized central provinces might benefit from initiatives that promote physical activity. In the southeastern regions, economic initiatives are needed to improve access to healthy foods.

Regarding wealth and obesity, our results reveal that older adults in the middle quintile of wealth experienced a significant increase in abdominal obesity prevalence, from 57.1% in 2016 to 59.4% in 2021. This pattern is in line with previous research, such as the study by Wolfe et al., which explored the relationship between wealth and obesity among adults entering midlife and found an inverse relationship between net worth and obesity, except for Black men32. One possible explanation for this trend is the “poverty-obesity paradox,” described by Alexander Bentley, which suggests that in high-income countries, obesity disproportionately affects people with lower incomes. Lower-income households tend to have higher rates of obesity due to limited access to healthy foods and increased reliance on calorie-dense, processed foods. On the other hand, the “Insurance Hypothesis” proposes that individuals store more fat in response to perceived food insecurity, a condition more prevalent among economically disadvantaged populations33. Conversely, higher wealth can also lead to increased access to hypercaloric foods, sedentary leisure activities, and reduced physical labor, which contribute to obesity in some population groups34.

The multinomial regression analysis in our study showed significant associations between various factors and having overweight or obesity compared to having normal weight. Our results indicate that adults aged > 80 years were significantly less likely to have overweight or obesity compared to those aged 60–70. This finding aligns with data from the National Health Insurance Service database, which reported an obesity prevalence of 36.6% among adults aged 70–79 in 2015, compared to 27.5% among those aged ≥ 80 years35. Several factors may contribute to this decline in obesity with advanced age, including reduced appetite due to hormonal, physiological, or psychological factors, increased prevalence of chronic diseases, polypharmacy, and greater dependence on caregivers, all of which can lead to weight loss in older adults36.

According to CDC data from 2017 to 2018, obesity prevalence among adults aged 60 and older was slightly higher in women (43.3%) than in men (42.2%), though the difference was not statistically significant37. While some studies suggest similar obesity prevalence between older men and women38,39, others indicate that men are more likely to have overweight than women40,41. Our findings indicate that males were significantly less likely to have overweight or obesity than females. This aligns with previous studies2,10, which attribute the difference to variations in fat distribution, greater muscle mass in men, and a more active metabolism that may help prevent significant weight gain42. Additionally, age-related hormonal changes play a role; in women, declining estrogen levels during menopause contribute to fat accumulation, while in men, decreasing testosterone levels affect muscle mass and fat distribution43.

The relationship between smoking and body weight in older adults suggests that smoking may reduce the likelihood of having overweight or obesity. Research indicates that smokers generally have a lower BMI than non-smokers due to nicotine’s impact on basal metabolism and appetite suppression44,45. However, some studies, such as Driva et al., report that heavy smokers (≥ 15 or 25 cigarettes per day) have higher BMI and an increased risk of obesity compared to light smokers46. Our multinomial regression analysis found that smokers had up to 53% lower odds of obesity and up to 34% lower odds of having overweight. While smoking may contribute to lower overall body weight, it is associated with an increase in visceral fat rather than subcutaneous fat. Visceral fat produces inflammatory cytokines, leading to chronic inflammation and increasing the risk of metabolic disorders such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes47,48.

The logistic regression analysis in our study evaluated the association between abdominal obesity and related factors. Our findings indicate that alcohol consumption is associated with up to 50% higher odds of abdominal obesity. Various studies have linked excessive alcohol intake to an increase in visceral fat, which significantly contributes to metabolic syndrome and other health risks48,49. Kye-Yeung Park et al. reported that in normal-weight, middle-aged men, consuming more than seven drinks per occasion was associated with higher odds of abdominal obesity compared to those consuming fewer than two drinks50. Additionally, cross-sectional studies have shown a consistent association between alcohol intake and increased abdominal obesity risk51,52.

The 2008 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans recommend at least 150 min of moderate-intensity physical activity per week for significant health benefits53,54. Our study found that moderate duration of physical activity was not associated with lower odds of abdominal obesity, while high levels of physical activity were protective, reducing the odds by up to 22%. This finding suggests that exercises with longer duration may be more effective in reducing visceral fat accumulation around the abdomen. Some studies indicate that resistance training and high-intensity interval training (HIIT) may be more beneficial than moderate-intensity aerobic exercises in reducing abdominal obesity55,56. Future studies should further investigate the optimal exercise modalities for preventing and managing obesity in older adults.

Strengths and limitations

A significant strength of this research is its substantial sample size, which enhances the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Data were collected over multiple years from a broad range of provinces in Iran, ensuring diversity in the sample and reflecting the heterogeneity of the national population. Additionally, standardized data collection procedures using validated questionnaires administered by a trained and certified team bolster the reliability and consistency of the data.

Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causality, meaning that the directionality of associations remains uncertain. While obesity is linked to chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, it is unclear whether obesity predisposes individuals to these conditions or if the presence of these conditions contributes to obesity through reduced mobility, medication effects, or metabolic changes. Another limitation is the reliance on self-reported data, which may introduce recall and social desirability biases. Self-reported physical activity levels and dietary intake may not accurately reflect actual behaviors. Furthermore, unmeasured confounding variables may have influenced the observed associations. Factors such as genetic predisposition, medication use, dietary composition, and psychological conditions (e.g., depression or anxiety) were not assessed but could play a role in obesity among older adults. Also, the “single” category is heterogeneous in terms of social support, economic stability, and life circumstances. Therefore, the result regarding this group needs a more cautious interpretation.

Based on these findings, targeted exercise, nutritional, and educational intervention programs should be designed and implemented to improve obesity outcomes among older adults. Additionally, longitudinal studies are needed to better examine the cause-and-effect relationships underlying obesity in older adults and provide stronger evidence for effective intervention strategies.

Conclusion

This study highlights the increasing prevalence of general and abdominal obesity among the older adult population in Iran, with a significant rise observed from 2016 to 2021. Our findings underscore the multifaceted interplay of demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors contributing to obesity in older adults. Specifically, higher obesity rates were observed among women, individuals with lower physical activity levels, and those in higher wealth categories, emphasizing the role of lifestyle disparities and socioeconomic status in obesity risk.

While our results align with global trends indicating a rise in obesity among older adults, they also provide context-specific insights for Iran. Notably, high-intensity physical activity demonstrated a protective effect against abdominal obesity, whereas moderate-intensity activity did not yield the same benefits. Additionally, lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption were associated with distinct obesity patterns. Although smoking was linked to a lower likelihood of obesity, it presents significant health risks due to its contribution to visceral fat accumulation and associated metabolic disorders.

Given these findings, targeted public health interventions are essential to mitigate obesity-related risks among older individuals. Efforts should focus on promoting structured high-intensity physical activity programs, encouraging healthier dietary habits, and addressing lifestyle behaviors such as smoking and alcohol consumption.
