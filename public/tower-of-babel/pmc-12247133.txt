A novel mutation in SMARCB1 associated with adult Coffin-Siris syndrome and meningioma
Authors: Zhenglong Guo, Jie Bai, Yang Liu, Xianwei Zhang, Wenke Yang, Jinming Wang, Yuwei Zhang, Hai Xiao, Bingtao Hao, Shixiu Liao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract SMARCB1 encodes a core subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, which plays a crucial role in the regulation of gene expression. Germline mutations in the SMARCB1 gene have been linked to early childhood Coffin-Siris syndrome type 3 (CSS3), a rare congenital malformation syndrome characterized by severe developmental delay and intellectual disability. In this study, we report a family of two adult CSS3 patients with a novel missense SMARCB1 mutation (c.1091A>C, p.Lys364Thr) identified through whole-exome sequencing (WES). Both patients exhibit selective difficulties in verbal learning and experience language delays. Additionally, the development of meningioma is confirmed in one of the patients. Mechanistic studies suggest that this missense mutation may abnormally activate the MAPK signaling pathway, which is implicated in the pathogenesis of tumor progression and neurodevelopmental disorders. This is the first reported case of a germline mutation in the SMARCB1 gene associated with both CSS3 and meningioma, thereby expanding the phenotypic spectrum of SMARCB1-related disorders. Keywords: SMARCB1, SWI/SNF, neurodevelopmental disorder, meningioma

Introduction

The mammalian SWI/SNF (Switch/sucrose non-fermentable) complex is an evolutionarily conserved, ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complex that functions as an epigenetic modulator in the regulation of gene accessibility and expression [ 1– 3] . Germline variants in genes encoding the core subunits of the SWI/SNF complex, including SMARCB1, SMARCA4, SMARCA2, SMARCE1, ARID1A, and ARID1B, have been implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders [ 4– 8] . One such disorder is Coffin-Siris syndrome (CSS), a rare intellectual disability syndrome characterized by developmental delay, hypotonia, coarse facial features, feeding difficulties in infancy, and fifth digit anomalies [ 7, 9– 12] . Notably, mutations in SMARCB1 (also known as SNF5, BAF47, and INI1) are often associated with individuals exhibiting more severe intellectual disability and choroid plexus hyperplasia, a condition referred to as CSS3 (OMIM# 614608) [ 4, 13, 14] . In addition, genome-sequencing studies have revealed the extensive role of the SWI/SNF complex in cancer, with approximately 25% of all human tumors displaying aberrations in these genes [ 15– 17] . SMARCB1 is recognized as a tumor suppressor, and its mutations have been linked to malignant rhabdoid tumors, schwannomas, and meningiomas, either individually or concurrently [ 18, 19] . SMARCB1 consists of 385 amino acids, which include the N-terminal winged-helix DNA binding domain [20], two tandem repeat domains, and the highly conserved putative coiled-coil C-terminal domain (CTD) [14]. Notably, single-residue mutations closely associated with CSS and cancer are enriched in the CTD [ 14, 21] . However, it remains unclear whether individuals with CSS3 concurrently develop tumors due to the limited understanding of the adult phenotypes of these patients.

In this study, we identified a novel germline missense mutation in two members of a family with CSS3, one of whom developed meningioma. This report expands the phenotypic spectrum associated with SMARCB1-related disorders.

Materials and Methods

Patients

The patients in this study are a pair of siblings. According to previously described method [22], genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples and the whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on both siblings and their parents, comprising a total of four family members. Sanger sequencing was employed to assess the presence of candidate pathogenic variants in the family members, including the sister’s two children. Additionally, the father’s sperm DNA was analyzed to investigate potential germline mosaicism. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2020-75). The parents have signed informed consent and agreed to publish the data included in this article.

Cell culture

The IOMM-Lee cell line was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO 2 at 37°C.

Overexpression of wild-type and mutant SMARCB1

Wild-type and A1091C-mutated SMARCB1 overexpression plasmids were constructed by Gene Universal Technology (Hefei, China) through the cloning of the human SMARCB1 coding sequence (Gene ID: 6598) into the lentiviral vector, followed by lentivirus packaging and validation. IOMM-Lee cells were subsequently infected with wild-type or mutated lentivirus, followed by selection with 2 μg/mL puromycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) to obtain stably expressing cells. Bright field and fluorescent images were acquired using Olympus microscope.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as described previously [23]. The following antibodies were used: anti-SMARCB1 antibody (20654-1-AP, 1:1000 dilution; Proteintech, Rosemont, USA), anti-P38 MAPK antibody (#9212, 1:1000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA), anti-phospho-P38 MAPK antibody (AF4001, 1:1000 dilution; Affinity, Cincinnati, USA), anti-ERK1/2 antibody (AF0155, 1:1000 dilution; Affinity), anti-phospho-ERK1/2 antibody (AF1015, 1:1000 dilution; Affinity), anti-ACTB antibody (66009-1-Ig, 1:3000 dilution; Proteintech), and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody (SA00001-2, 1:10000 dilution; Proteintech). Image J was used to quantify relative protein expression.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from IOMM-Lee cells via TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) according to Illumina standard instruction (VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina®). An Agilent 4200 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) was used to evaluate the concentration and size distribution of the cDNA library, followed by sequencing with an Illumina Novaseq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Raw reads were filtered and mapped to human RefSeq using Hisat2 (version: 2.0.4). Significant differential expressed genes (DEGs) were identified as those with a False Discovery Rate (FDR)<0.05 and a fold change>2 via DESeq2 software. An online platform ( https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn) was used for data analysis and visualization. The clean RNA-seq data used in this study have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database of NCBI with the accession number GSE273963.

In-silico prediction

In silico analysis was performed to evaluate the pathogenicity of missense variants through online softwares including Polyphen-2 ( http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), MutationTaster ( https://www.mutationtaster.org/), VarSite ( https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/VarSite), and DynaMut2 ( https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/dynamut2). The three-dimensional structure of SMARCB1 was obtained from the PDB database ( https://www1.rcsb.org/), with the PDB ID: 7Y8R. The online tool AlphaFold v3 [24] ( https://golgi.sandbox.google.com/) was subsequently utilized to establish a structural model of the protein SMARCB1_K364T.

Cell proliferation and migration assays

To evaluate cell proliferation, growth curves for both wild-type and SMARCB1-mutant cells were generated via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Briefly, 10,000 cells were plated in 96-well plates and incubated in culture medium for 0, 24, 48, or 72 h. The optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader. To evaluate cell migration, wound healing assay was conducted as previously described [25], and the relative width of the scratch was measured using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, USA).

Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR)

Genomic DNA (50 ng and 100 ng) from the father’s sperm (I:1) was extracted to detect the germline mosaicism of the c.1091A>C variant via the QX200 AutoDG Droplet Digital PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA). A total of 50ng of genomic DNA extracted from whole blood at ratios of II:1 and III:1 was used as a positive and negative control, respectively. The wild-type sequence was labeled with the FAM probe, whereas the c.1091A>C variant was labeled with the VIC probe. The ratio of VIC copies to the sum of VIC copies and FAM copies was used to identify the level of mosaicism.

Immunohistochemistry

Following the surgical procedure, tumor tissue samples were dispatched to the pathology department for thorough pathological examination and immunohistochemical staining. The samples were fixed in 3.7% neutral formaldehyde, followed by dehydration using graded alcohol, embedding in paraffin, and sectioning to a thickness of 3 μm. Immunohistochemistry was performed using the EnVision two-step method, utilizing antibodies including S100, EMA, STAT6, and SSTR2A, all obtained from Maixin Biotechnology (Fuzhou, China). The entire process was carried out on a fully automatic immunohistochemistry instrument (Leica, Heidelberg, Germany), with subsequent analysis conducted after scanning via a fully automated scanner (Leica).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as the mean ± SD, and two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to analyze statistical differences between two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characterization and genetic analysis of the patients

The patients, siblings born to unrelated healthy Chinese parents following full-term delivery, consisted of a 36-year-old sister and a 33-year-old brother ( Figure 1A). The sister began walking and speaking at 14 months, whereas the brother achieved these milestones three months later. Neither sibling experienced feeding difficulties, significant growth retardation, or severe intellectual disability. However, both exhibit coarse facial features, including thick eyebrows, wide mouths, long eyelashes, and thick lips, along with musculoskeletal findings, particularly hypoplastic fifth toenails ( Figure 1B,C). Notably, they display mild selective neuropsychological deficits, such as poor learning ability, impaired memory, slow reactions, and language delay, which differ from the severe intellectual disability observed in previously reported cases. Additionally, the brother suffered from poor eyesight, strabismus, and abnormal hearing ( Figure 1D).

Trio-based whole exome sequencing (Trio-WES) identified a missense mutation (GenBank: NM_003073.5; c.1091A>C) within exon 8 of the SMARCB1 gene, which is associated with Coffin-Siris syndrome 3 (CSS3, OMIM#614608). This mutation is not listed in the ClinVar ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) or HGMD databases ( https://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php) and lacks allele frequency data in the gnomAD database ( https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/). In accordance with the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), it is classified as likely pathogenic ( Figure 1E,F). Sanger sequencing confirmed the heterozygous mutation in both patients but not in their parents, indicating a de novo pattern ( Figure 1G). Given the low probability of two siblings independently inheriting the same de novo mutation, we speculated germline mosaicism, which was confirmed through ddPCR testing of the father’s sperm, revealing a mutation ratio of approximately 12% ( Figure 1H).

Further examinations ruled out other CSS3-related developmental abnormalities in the heart, brain, renal and urinary systems. However, the sister’s brain magnetic resonance images (MRI) revealed space-occupying lesions adjacent to the cerebral falx. Subsequent surgical sampling and pathological examination revealed spindle-shaped tumor cells exhibiting increased collagen deposition. Moreover, the immunohistochemistry results indicated that STAT6 was negative, whereas EMA, S100, and SSTR2A were positive. On the basis of these findings, the tumor was classified as a World Health Organization (WHO) grade I fibrous meningioma [26], which was not detected in her brother ( Figure 1I,J). Genetic testing of the tumor revealed no positive results associated with meningioma. SMARCB1 mutations can increase the risk of meningiomas, suggesting a potential link between SMARCB1 mutations and the concurrent occurrence of CSS and meningiomas in this study.

Functional study of the c.1091A>C mutation in SMARCB1

To evaluate the impact of the c.1091A>C missense mutation on protein function, we first assessed its pathogenicity via various online in silico prediction tools, including Polyphen2, Varsite, Mutation Taster, DynaMut2, and AlphaFold. The results indicated that this mutation may be associated with disease ( Figure 2A). The substitution of lysine 364 with threonine (K364T) alters the protein’s hydrophobicity, increases the formation of hydrogen bonds with adjacent amino acids, and enhances the overall interactions within the 3D structure ( Figure 2B). Furthermore, lysine 364 is highly conserved across multiple mammalian species ( Figure 2C).

We established a stable IOMM-Lee cell line, a meningioma cell line expressing either wild-type or K364T-mutated SMARCB1, through lentivirus infection and subsequent puromycin selection ( Figure 2D). Transcriptome analysis revealed that the K364T mutation induced significant alterations in gene expression profiles, resulting in 22 DEGs, with |log2FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05 ( Figure 2E, F). KEGG enrichment analysis of the significantly up-regulated genes revealed that pathways associated with neurodegeneration and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, which are associated with MAPK14, DNAL1, and GPX1, are aberrantly activated ( Figure 2G). MAPK14, also known as P38 MAPK, is a critical protein in the MAPK signaling pathway and is involved in various biological processes [27]. Its abnormal activation has been implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders as well as tumor progression in cancers, including meningioma [ 28– 31] . SMARCB1 plays a crucial role in SWI/SNF complex-involved activation of enhancers and bivalent promoters [32]. We examined ChIP-seq data from the Cistome database ( http://cistrome.org/db) and found that SMARCB1 deficiency significantly reduced the binding affinity of the SWI/SNF complex to the promoter region of MAPK14 ( Figure 2H). Furthermore, western blot analysis results confirmed a prominent increase in the expression of P38 MAPK, ERK1/2, and phosphorylated ERK1/2 in SMARCB1-mutant cells ( Figure 2I). Consistently, growth curves revealed greater proliferative activity in SMARCB1-mutant cells than in control cells, and wound healing assays indicated that SMARCB1 mutation enhanced cell migration ( Figure 2J,K). These findings suggest that the c.1091A>C mutation in the SMARCB1 gene may affect the transcriptional regulation function of the SWI/SNF complex, thereby activating the MAPK signaling pathway and contributing to the development of CSS and meningiomas.

Virtual screening of small molecule-targeting drugs for mutated SMARCB1

Having preliminarily confirmed the role of the mutated SMARCB1 protein in the pathogenesis of CSS and meningioma within this family, we investigated whether pharmacological agents that can target the mutated SMARCB1 protein to ameliorate the disease phenotype in affected patients exist. Accordingly, we downloaded the structures of 2726 small molecules derived from the Clinical & FDA Approved Drug Library ( https://www.apexbt.com/) and conducted molecular docking analysis via MOE software (v2022.02). By scoring the binding conformations of small molecules with the mutated SMARCB1, a total of 183 small molecules exhibiting strong binding affinity were selected. Further comparison of their binding abilities with those of wild-type SMARCB1 led to the identification of three small molecules with anti-tumor activity: the MDM2 inhibitor RG7112 [ 33, 34] , the tubulin-targeting peptide dolastatin 10 [35], and the FGFR-targeting drug erdafitinib [36]. Finally, we employed PyMOL (v2.5.4) to analyze the amino acid residues involved in the hydrogen bond interactions between SMARCB1 and the small-molecule drugs, resulting in the creation of a 3D interaction diagram. The results indicate that RG7112 forms a hydrogen bond with lysine at position 364 of the wild-type SMARCB1 protein, whereas its interaction with threonine at position 364 of the mutant SMARCB1 protein is increased, leading to the formation of three additional hydrogen bonds with other amino acids ( Figure 3A). In contrast, dolastatin 10 and erdafitinib did not demonstrate significant binding specificity ( Figure 3B,C). These findings suggest that RG7112 can bind effectively to the mutant SMARCB1 protein and may serve as a targeted therapeutic option for patients with SMARCB1-mutant tumors or CSS.

Summary of reported SMARCB1 pathogenic mutations in CSS patients and meningioma patients

Through literature review, we identified 31 patients diagnosed with CSS, including those in our study, who harbored 15 reported germline mutations in SMARCB1 [ 7, 11– 13, 37– 45] ( Figure 4A and Supplementary Table S1). The mutations included 12 missense mutations (80%) and 3 deletion/duplication mutations. The most common variant was c.1091_1093del, which was found in 10 patients, accounting for 32.3% of all patients. This was followed by c.110G>A and c.1121G>A, with 4 and 3 cases, respectively. Moreover, 83.9% of the mutations were classified as de novo, although germline mosaicism could not be excluded. In our study, the ages at diagnosis for the patients were 33 and 36 years, whereas those of the other reported patients did not exceed 26 years. Among the documented cases, the male-to-female ratio was 5:12. Notably, most CSS patients exhibit moderate to severe intellectual disability. For example, patients with the c.110G>A variant demonstrate severe intellectual disability and choroid plexus hyperplasia [13]. Conversely, a recent study indicated that patients with the c.568C>T mutation exhibited normal intelligence, with only mild selective neuropsychological deficits [37]. Interestingly, 11 of the 15 mutations occurred in the C-terminal domain (CTD) of the SMARCB1 protein and were specifically concentrated at amino acids 352--377. These amino acids are crucial for the interaction of SMARCB1 with histones, including H2AC6 and H2BC4, facilitating the chromatin remodeling and gene expression mediated by the SWI/SNF complex ( Figure 4B). These findings suggest that mutations in the CTD of the SMARCB1 protein may impair the function of the SWI/SNF complex, contributing to the pathogenesis of CSS.

Additionally, we identified four germline mutations associated with meningiomas, primarily located in the N-terminal region of the SMARCB1 protein ( Figure 4A and Supplementary Table S1)[ 46– 48] . The average age at diagnosis among the 12 reported patients with meningiomas was approximately 35 years, and meningioma often occurs concurrently with schwannomatosis. To date, reports on the development of tumors in patients with CSS are limited. Notably, we are the first to report that CSS patients carrying the c.1091A>C mutation develop meningiomas simultaneously. Furthermore, among the three patients with the c.1121G>A mutation, one developed schwannomatosis at the age of 26 [39]. These findings suggest that variants in the SMARCB1 gene may not only cause CSS but also increase the risk of tumor development, potentially influenced by the age at which the patient’s phenotype is diagnosed. Currently, there is a lack of phenotypic studies focused on adult CSS patients. Extending the follow-up period may be beneficial for establishing correlations among SMARCB1 gene mutations, CSS, and tumor development.

Discussion

This study presents a novel missense mutation (c.1091A>C, p.Lys364Thr) in the SMARCB1 gene, which is associated with Coffin-Siris syndrome type 3 (CSS3) and meningioma in two adult siblings. Our findings broaden the phenotypic spectrum of SMARCB1-related disorders and suggest a potential relationship between SMARCB1 mutations and tumor development.

The pathogenicity of the c.1091A>C mutation was assessed via multiple in silico prediction tools, which suggested a likely association with disease. This mutation results in the substitution of lysine with threonine at position 364 (K364T), which alters the protein’s hydrophobicity and enhances its interactions within the three-dimensional structure. Lysine 364 is highly conserved across various mammalian species, underscoring its functional importance. Transcriptome analysis of a stable IOMM-Lee cell line expressing the K364T-mutant SMARCB1 revealed significant alterations in gene expression profiles, notably activating pathways associated with neurodegeneration and tumor progression, such as the P38-ERK pathway. The increased expressions of P38 MAPK, ERK1/2, and phosphorylated ERK1/2, confirmed by western blot analysis, highlights the potential mechanistic link between these mutations and the pathogenesis of both CSS and meningiomas.

Our study identified the c.1091A>C mutation in two adult patients with CSS3 who exhibited selective difficulties in verbal learning and language delay, in contrast to the severe intellectual disabilities typically reported in CSS3 patients. Additionally, one patient developed meningioma, a finding that has not been previously reported in patients with CSS3. This discovery suggests that SMARCB1 mutations may predispose individuals to tumor development, potentially influenced by the patient’s age at diagnosis and phenotype, which underscores the need for extended follow-up and phenotypic studies in adult CSS patients. Long-term monitoring of patients with SMARCB1 mutations could provide valuable insights into the progression of CSS and the risk of tumor development, ultimately improving patient management and outcomes.

Given that mutations in the SWI/SNF subunits contribute to the development of cancer and neurodevelopmental disorders, therapeutic manipulation has garnered significant attention. Furthermore, genome-wide perturbation screenings of tumor cells with SWI/SNF mutations have informed drug discovery and clinical trials [ 2, 16, 49] . Three strategies for directly targeting SWI/SNF complexes are summarized below: (a) Design small molecules that bind to bromodomains, thereby inhibiting histone post-translational modifications (PTMs) and ultimately blocking the chromatin localization of SWI/SNF complexes, such as BRD9 inhibitors [ 50, 51] . (b) Develop ATPase inhibitors to catalytically inactivate SWI/SNF complexes, such as BRM014 and FHD-286 (NCT04891757) [ 52, 53] . c) Utilize proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) for the targeted degradation of SWI/SNF subunits, including ACBI1/2 [ 54, 55] , JQ-ds-4 [56], PRT3789 (NCT05639751), CFT-8634 (NCT05355753) and FHD-609 (NCT04965753). Additionally, the activities of polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) [57], DNA damage repair pathways [58], and sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibition have been identified as therapeutic vulnerabilities [59]. In this study, MOE docking analysis identified RG7112 as a potential therapeutic drug with high binding affinity for the mutated SMARCB1 protein. This drug targets the MDM2‒P53 pathway and is currently undergoing clinical trials for the treatment of leukemia [33]. Moreover, we hypothesize that MAPK pathway inhibitors may also have potential therapeutic effects on SMARCB1-mutant meningiomas.

In conclusion, this study highlights the novel association between a SMARCB1 mutation and CSS3, as well as meningioma, thereby expanding our understanding of SMARCB1-related disorders. Further research is essential to elucidate the mechanisms that link SMARCB1 mutations to tumor development and to explore potential therapeutic interventions for affected individuals.
