Robotic subxiphoid-optical thymectomy for Good’s syndrome in a severely immunocompromised patient: a case report
Authors: Rintaro Kawanishi, Masanori Shimomura, Satoru Okada, Tatsuo Furuya, Atsuki Uchibori, Machiko Nishii, Taiyo Kawamura, Takumi Nakagawa, Tsunehiro Ii, Masayoshi Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGood’s syndrome is a rare adult-onset immunodeficiency characterized by thymoma and hypogammaglobulinemia. The optimal surgical approach for thymoma in patients with severe immunodeficiency remains undefined. In this report, we describe a case of Good’s syndrome in an elderly male patient with severe hypogammaglobulinemia, who underwent robotic subxiphoid-optical thymectomy as part of a perioperative strategy aimed at reducing surgical invasiveness.Case DescriptionAn 80-year-old man with Good’s syndrome presented with hypogammaglobulinemia (serum IgG, 60 mg/dL), complete absence of B cells, and a 5.5-cm anterior mediastinal tumor. Given this pronounced immunodeficiency, robotic subxiphoid-optical thymectomy was indicated to minimize surgical trauma while avoiding median sternotomy. The perioperative management strategy included preoperative intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) replacement therapy, extended antibiotic prophylaxis, careful patient selection, and close perioperative monitoring. Complete (R0) resection was achieved, and pathologic examination demonstrated type AB thymoma, Masaoka-Koga stage II. No perioperative infectious complications occurred, allowing timely initiation of cyclosporine when pure red cell aplasia developed eight weeks postoperatively, followed by partial hematologic recovery. Monthly IVIG replacement was continued, and no further infectious complications or thymoma recurrence were observed during 24 months of follow-up.ConclusionsThis case suggests that robotic subxiphoid-optical thymectomy could be an option in selected patients with Good’s syndrome and severe immunodeficiency.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Robotic subxiphoid-optical thymectomy (RST) was safely performed in a patient with Good’s syndrome and severe immunodeficiency without perioperative infectious complications.

• No perioperative infectious complications occurred in the setting of a multifactorial management strategy, including preoperative intravenous immunoglobulin replacement, extended antibiotic prophylaxis, careful patient selection, close monitoring, and a minimally invasive surgical approach.

• Early postoperative development of pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) was successfully managed with cyclosporine following an infection-free course.

What is known and what is new?

• Good’s syndrome is associated with severe hypogammaglobulinemia, B cell depletion, and a high risk of infection, which significantly influences prognosis. Surgical approach in these patients remains controversial, particularly regarding perioperative infectious risk.

• This case suggests that RST could be an option in selected patients with Good’s syndrome and severe immunodeficiency.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• In patients with severe immunodeficiency, surgical strategy should prioritize not only oncologic resection but also minimization of perioperative risk.

• Minimally invasive robotic approaches may represent a reasonable alternative to median sternotomy in carefully selected high-risk patients with Good’s syndrome.

• Avoiding postoperative infection may be important when early immunosuppressive treatment is required for associated autoimmune complications such as PRCA.

Introduction

Good’s syndrome is a rare adult-onset immunodeficiency characterized by thymoma and hypogammaglobulinemia. It is marked by reduced or absent B cells and impaired T cell-mediated immunity, resulting in recurrent bacterial and opportunistic infections that significantly affect prognosis (1). Although thymectomy is indicated for tumor control, the optimal surgical approach in patients with severe immunodeficiency remains undefined. In particular, the effect of minimally invasive techniques on perioperative infectious risk in severely immunocompromised patients remains unclear. Robotic subxiphoid-optical thymectomy (RST) provides a midline view of the anterior mediastinum and facilitates visualization of both phrenic nerves. We report a case of Good’s syndrome with severe hypogammaglobulinemia and complete absence of B cells managed with RST. This case highlights surgical decision-making in the context of profound immunodeficiency. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0640/rc).

Case presentation

An 80-year-old man experienced two episodes of pneumonia within one month and was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. Laboratory investigations demonstrated hypogammaglobulinemia. Chest computed tomography revealed a 5.5-cm anterior mediastinal tumor without evidence of invasion into adjacent structures (Figure 1A). 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography demonstrated mild uptake within the tumor, with a maximum standardized uptake value of 2.57 (Figure 1B). Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) measured 60 mg/dL (reference range, 870–1,700 mg/dL), consistent with severe hypogammaglobulinemia. Immunoglobulin M and A levels were also markedly reduced at 3 and 4 mg/dL, respectively. Flow cytometry was performed using antibodies against CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD5, CD19, CD20, CD56, and HLA-DR (Figure S1). This demonstrated a complete absence of circulating B cells and a relative predominance of T cells (Table 1). The CD4/CD8 ratio was inverted, although the absolute CD4+ T-cell count was preserved at 824 cells/µL, with a higher proportion of CD8+ than CD4+ T cells. A substantial CD5+/HLA-DR+ population was also observed, suggesting activation of circulating T cells. Overall, these findings indicated profound B-cell deficiency accompanied by T-cell subset imbalance and activation. These findings supported a diagnosis of Good’s syndrome. Monthly intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) replacement therapy was initiated preoperatively. IVIG was administered intravenously at a dose of 20 g once monthly, corresponding to approximately 0.32 g/kg/month based on a body weight of 63 kg. IVIG was administered on days −34 and −7 before surgery and subsequently on postoperative days 22, 61, 99, and 134. Thereafter, monthly IVIG replacement therapy was continued. After initiation of IVIG replacement therapy, serum IgG levels increased but remained below the reference range before surgery, whereas serum IgA and IgM levels showed no meaningful recovery and remained markedly reduced. Despite persistent hypogammaglobulinemia, no perioperative infectious complications were observed.

Given the underlying immunodeficiency, advanced age, and high susceptibility to mediastinal infection, RST was indicated to avoid median sternotomy (2). In brief, we used the Da Vinci XiTM surgical system (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) with 3 arms: 8-mm ports placed in the right 6th intercostal space on the midclavicular line (arm 1), subxiphoid (arm 2: camera, 30° oblique view), and left 6th intercostal space on the midclavicular line (arm 3). A 12-mm air SealTM port (Conmed, Milford, CT, USA) was placed on the anterior axillary line of the right 3rd intercostal space. Operative time was 206 minutes and estimated blood loss was 3 mL. The chest drain was removed on postoperative day 1. Given the patient’s severe immunodeficiency, sulbactam/ampicillin was administered perioperatively for seven days as extended prophylaxis. The postoperative course remained uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 9. Pathologic examination demonstrated type AB thymoma, Masaoka-Koga stage II (Figure 2). Complete resection was achieved, with microscopically negative surgical margins (R0 resection). The tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage was pT1bN0M0, stage I. Because a complete resection was achieved, postoperative observation without adjuvant therapy was selected.

Histopathologic findings (hematoxylin and eosin staining). The lesion comprises spindle-shaped epithelial cells with oval nuclei (type A component, left) admixed with lymphocyte-rich areas (type B component, right), consistent with type AB thymoma. Scale bar, 250 µm.

Preoperatively, pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) was not clinically evident; the hemoglobin level was 9.4 g/dL, and the reticulocyte percentage was 1.8%. Eight weeks postoperatively, the patient presented with dizziness. Laboratory evaluation revealed severe anemia, with a hemoglobin level of 4.5 g/dL, and reticulocytopenia of 0.2%. At the time of PRCA diagnosis, the white blood cell count was 4,900/µL and platelet count was 355,000/µL, indicating that pancytopenia was not present and supporting PRCA rather than aplastic anemia. Bone marrow examination demonstrated erythroid aplasia consistent with PRCA, further reflecting immune dysregulation associated with Good’s syndrome. Anemia persisted despite repeated transfusions. Cyclosporine was initiated on postoperative day 61 at 250 mg/day, targeting a trough level of 100–160 ng/mL. Trough levels were monitored regularly and were generally maintained within the target range. No clinically significant cyclosporine-related adverse events were observed. Hemoglobin levels subsequently increased to 7–10 g/dL, indicating partial hematologic recovery (Figure 3). At 24 months after surgery, the patient remains free of thymoma recurrence. Hypogammaglobulinemia persists; however, monthly IVIG replacement therapy continues, and no further infectious complications have occurred.

Discussion

Good’s syndrome is a thymoma-associated immunodeficiency characterized by hypogammaglobulinemia, absent or markedly reduced B cells, and variable T cell dysfunction. A systematic review of 162 patients reported infections in 92.6% of cases and an overall mortality of 15.4%, with infection accounting for 92.0% of deaths (1). These findings identify infectious complications as the principal determinant of prognosis.

Management of thymomas alone may not correct the underlying immunodeficiency, and IVIG replacement therapy is often required (1). Although no randomized trials have evaluated immunoglobulin replacement specifically in Good’s syndrome, meta-analytic data from primary immunodeficiency, which shares features of antibody deficiency with Good’s syndrome, demonstrate a significant inverse association between trough IgG levels and pneumonia incidence. Higher trough IgG levels correlate with lower infectious risk, rather than a protective effect at a fixed threshold (3).

Median sternotomy carries a risk of deep sternal wound infection, including mediastinitis. In immunocompromised hosts, such complications can have serious consequences. A previous report described bacterial mediastinitis following transsternal thymectomy in a patient with Good’s syndrome, followed by recurrent infectious episodes (4). These observations highlight the importance of minimizing surgical trauma in this population. Minimally invasive approaches such as robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery avoid median sternotomy, minimize chest wall disruption, and may reduce overall surgical trauma compared with a transsternal approach (5). In addition, robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery has achieved high R0 resection rates and acceptable oncological outcomes, even for thymomas measuring 5 cm or larger (6). The subxiphoid approach offers a midline view of the anterior mediastinum, allowing simultaneous visualization of both phrenic nerves and the upper poles of the thymus, which may facilitate precise dissection (2). Evidence specifically addressing postoperative infectious complications after subxiphoid thymectomy remains limited, because most comparative studies have reported only overall postoperative morbidity without separately evaluating pneumonia, surgical-site infection, or mediastinitis. Nevertheless, available comparative data suggest that the subxiphoid approach is not associated with increased postoperative morbidity and may result in fewer overall complications than conventional lateral thoracoscopic or open approaches (7,8). In addition, a recent propensity score-matched study of subxiphoid single-port robotic thymectomy demonstrated a numerically lower overall complication rate than subxiphoid VATS, although the difference was not statistically significant (9). We have also previously reported on the safety of RST and its low morbidity (10). Therefore, we considered this surgical procedure to be appropriate for this case, in which the risk of postoperative infection was a major concern.

Approximately 51% of patients with Good’s syndrome develop autoimmune complications, which may occur before or after thymectomy (1). In the present case, PRCA developed early in the postoperative period, consistent with the autoimmune manifestations characteristic of Good’s syndrome. Immunosuppressive therapy, particularly cyclosporine, represents an established treatment for acquired PRCA and has demonstrated hematologic responses in previous studies (11). However, immunosuppression increases susceptibility to infection, a critical concern in patients with underlying hypogammaglobulinemia.

The absence of postoperative infectious complications in this patient permitted timely initiation of cyclosporine therapy. Partial hematologic recovery followed, and repeated transfusions were avoided. This clinical course illustrates that an infection-free perioperative course, achieved in the setting of IVIG replacement, extended antibiotic prophylaxis, careful monitoring, and a minimally invasive approach, can be clinically important when early postoperative immunosuppressive treatment is required.

However, this report has several limitations. First, this is a single case report, and a direct causal relationship between the minimally invasive robotic approach and reduced postoperative infectious risk cannot be established. Second, no direct comparison with conventional transsternal thymectomy was available. Further accumulation of cases is needed to clarify the optimal surgical strategy for patients with Good’s syndrome.

Conclusions

In patients with severe immunodeficiency, surgical decision-making must account for perioperative infectious vulnerability alongside oncologic completeness. This case suggests that RST could be an option in selected patients with Good’s syndrome.
