Ghana’s Diagnosis Related Grouping (G-DRG) tariffs versus hospital charges: implications for inpatient care financing at a secondary hospital in Ghana
Authors: Peter Gyasi Darkwa, Genevieve Cecilia Aryeetey, Eric Nsiah-Boateng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGhana introduced the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) in 2003 as a means of achieving Universal Health Coverage. The Ghana-Diagnosis Related Groupings (G-DRG) is one of the payment mechanisms used to reimburse providers for services and procedures. However, evidence suggests that the amount reimbursed for services through the G-DRG tariffs may be lower than the market prices for same, creating a financial gap that concerns providers.ObjectiveThe study assessed the financial implications of the G-DRG payment system on service delivery for inpatient services between 2018 and 2022 at the Greater Accra Regional Hospital in Ghana.MethodsThe study adopted a descriptive cross-sectional study design approach to gather and analyze the data. The 2021 annual performance report of the hospital was used to identify the top hundred reported inpatient admissions. Institutional data on G-DRG tariffs issued by the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) and the approved hospital charges for managing sampled medical conditions were extracted. The top ten conditions from the sample were used to determine the price differentials between G-DRG tariff and hospital charges for same conditions on admission.ResultsThe study found that 3,323 (63.5%) of the top 10 conditions on admission in 2022 were related to children, with neonatal jaundice constituting the highest (1,177; 23%). Gastroenteritis, Septicaemia, hypertension and appendicitis were the common adult conditions on admission. While the G-DRG tariffs reported price increment every other year (16%), hospital price increment was unstable ranging from 20% to 58%. Nonetheless, the study found a cumulative financial gain to the facility of 23% on the top ten inpatient medical conditions over the five-year period.ConclusionWhile the G-DRG had positive price differentials over the five-year period, ensuring macroeconomic stability is important in maintaining the gains and supporting the financial viability of the G-DRG payment system. Future research should look investigate the relationship between price differentials and average length of stay in admissions.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Price differentials, Tariffs, Diagnosis Related Groupings, National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), Inpatient care financing, Hospital admissions, Ghana

Introduction

In 2004, Ghana began implementation of a National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) in response to the catastrophic cost of healthcare borne by the citizenry. The introduction of the NHIS policy was part of collaborated efforts at ensuring sustainable health care financing options that promote financial risk protection and renewed commitment for universal access to healthcare [1]. As a social insurance scheme, the NHIS is financed largely through taxation and annual contributions by members. Sources such as indirect tax (VAT levy of 2.5%) on selected goods and services, 2.5% points deductions from the social security contributions of formal sector workers, internally generated funds from activities such as investments and support from donors as well as development partners [2, 3].

The National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) employs multiple provider payment systems with periodic revisions to contain cost and ensure sustainability. From its inception, credentialed providers were paid for both services and medicines through the standardized itemized fees (or fee-for-service) system. However, the rising demand or utilization of healthcare services and the subsequent increasing cost of claims posed a threat to the sustainability of the Scheme. In response, the Ghana Diagnosis Related Grouping (G-DRG) payment system was introduced to pay for services and procedures while payment for medicines were made using standardized itemized fees (fee-for-service) based on approved medicines list [4]. Under the G-DRG clinical case classification system, patients are categorized into groups with similar clinical symptoms and resource consumption based on diagnosis, procedure, comorbidities, severity, among other factors with the assumption that patients tied to a DRG are deemed to be medically and economically similar [5]. This payment method was also aimed at incentivizing healthcare providers to deliver efficient healthcare services to the insured due to its inherent flexibility to minimize cost by ensuring prudent use of resources.

Concerning payment (reimbursement) of providers for services rendered under the G-DRG, healthcare providers receive a fixed amount (tariffs) for groups of conditions treated for outpatient and inpatient services rather than existing market fees for same group of conditions [6]. This approach of reimbursement, especially under fluctuating macroeconomic environment and the non-adherence to the timelines for tariff reviews, create the possibility of providers incurring high costs when treating for instance extraordinarily expensive patients [7]. Moreover, the all-inclusive nature of the tariff may adversely impact on the finances of facilities, ultimately affecting the satisfaction and population coverage of the scheme as some providers may transfer treatment costs to patients through unauthorized practices such as co-payment. This practice also lowers members’ trust and confidence in the scheme and ultimately affects re-enrolment and the quest to achieve UHC.

Studies on provider payment methods have focused on the incentives, implementation challenges and preferences for alternative forms of payment methods for services [4, 8]. For instance, a study by Mordi et al. (2015) indicated that the tariff rates assigned to services and procedures in case groupings per the G-DRG payment mechanism were low, not updated yearly as per policy, thereby increasing the difficulties for service providers to work with such tariffs especially under fluctuating economy [9]. In addition, Fodjour et al. (2015) indicated that the introduction of the G-DRG payment has escalated the cost of health care reimbursable by the NHIA [10]. This study thus sought to examine the financial implications of G-DRG payment system for inpatient care in a secondary level facility in Ghana. Specifically, the study compared price differentials between G-DRG tariffs and hospital-approved charges for the top ten medical conditions admitted to the hospital, determining whether the reimbursement rates from the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) are higher or lower than the hospital’s actual charges; and estimated the long-term financial gains or losses for the hospital by comparing cumulative utilisation costs under the G-DRG payment system and hospital charges for the top ten conditions over five years.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Greater Accra Regional Hospital (GARH) in Ghana. The hospital occupies about 15.65 acres with a catchment area population of over 4,671,363. This is an old facility established in 1928 by the Europeans and has gone through several upgrades from district to a regional hospital. Currently, the hospital has been successfully redeveloped and upgraded into an ultra-modern 420 bed capacity. Apart from the general cases at the Out-Patient Department, the facility renders specialized services such as neurosurgery, spine health, renal services, child health, ophthalmology, radiology, anesthesia and pain, maxillofacial, specialized dental services, accident and emergency, mental Health, and pharmaceutical services. The number of patient visits averages 4,287 per week, of which 11% constitutes admissions.

The facility has a well-developed record system for medical conditions and payment. It inputs data into the District Health Information Management System (DHIMS2), records and submits claims to the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) for registered clients on the scheme. The hospital also provides both outpatient and inpatient services to private health insurance clients and submits the claims to their respective insurers for reimbursement.

Data extraction approach

The study utilized secondary data to address its objectives. The data was extracted from three main sources: the facility’s electronic medical record system for capturing patients’ transactional database, DHIMS II database, and the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) tariff database. The following indicators were extracted from the facility’s system; number of conditions admitted in the past year, the 3-day average price (cost) for all conditions admitted, the number of days admitted per condition, the number of people admitted per condition. The top ten conditions from 2018 to 2022 was extracted from the DHIMS 2 database and the G-DRG approved tariff (payment) for inpatient admissions for all conditions from the NHIA database. The tariff codes provided by the NHIA for G-DRGs, and the specialties were maintained as they uniquely identify the services provided. The tariff for each G-DRG at the current reimbursable rate were extracted and recorded. As per the G-DRG classification, tariffs for adults were used for all disease conditions sampled except in child-specific diseases that the corresponding versions of paediatric tariffs were applied. Data extraction was done with Microsoft Excel 365 using customized workbooks for the selected G-DRGs and that of the approved hospital charges. We checked the extracted data for gaps and errors for consistency and validity. To reduce errors, two research assistants extracted and independently entered the data.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted using two reference points. First, we determined the top 100 disease conditions from the Hospital’s annual report using the frequency of diagnoses for a disease condition. Second, we determined the top 10 disease conditions reported over a five-year period, that is, between 2018 and 2022, from the District Health Information Management System (DHIMS2) using the number of times a disease condition is diagnosed and reported in the database. We estimated the price differential for the top 100 conditions for 2022 and the top 10 conditions for 2018 through to 2022 by subtracting the costs of hospital admission services for a three-day period from the G-DRG tariffs.

The second objective was to determine the financial implications of the G-DRG payment system. We compared the utilization costs between the two payment systems for the top 10-conditions for the 2018–2022 period. Thus, the yearly attendances for each condition were multiplied with the corresponding NHIS tariff and hospital approved charge to obtain yearly utilization costs for each payment system. The net gain/loss of utilization cost was then calculated by subtracting the total of the hospital utilization cost from that of the of NHIS utilization cost. Using the NHIS tariff as benchmark, we estimated the percentage gain or loss for each of the disease condition over the five-year period (2018–2022) by subtracting the hospital charges from the NHIS tariffs, dividing it by the NHIS tariff, and multiplying the resultant figure by 100.

Financial gains or losses for the hospital

Overall, the hospital recorded a gain of GHS261,047.95(US$ 16,315.50), representing 22.9% of the top 10 inpatient conditions used for the study (Table 3). The facility made the highest amount gained of about 61.7% on the treatment of Septicaemia conditions followed by Hypertension (51.9%), respiratory distress syndrome (41.1%) and neonatal jaundice (25%) over the five-year period (2018–2022) as shown in Table 3. The condition that recorded the least percentage gained was treatment of Bronchopneumonia (1.4%). However, the hospital recorded losses in the treatment of some of the reported medical conditions for the five years under review. For instance, a loss of 15.8% was recorded for the treatment of Gastroenteritis conditions followed by a loss of 4.9% on the treatment of Birth Asphyxia.

Discussion

This study sought to examine the price differentials between G-DRG tariffs and hospital-approved prices over the period, 2018–2022. The findings show that the G-DRG payment method by the NHIS produced a cumulative financial gain for the hospital over the five-year period in terms of utilization of services although independently some years recorded significant financial losses. The overall financial net gain of services utilization could be explained by the lower approved hospital fees and charges in comparison with the G-DRG tariffs and strict supervision to adherence of the approved charges by Parliament of Ghana. Under the healthcare system in Ghana, the government uses legislation to control determination of hospital charges and to manage extortion by healthcare providers. The Fees and Charges (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1080 [11] empowers the Finance Minister to determine charges for services rendered in public health facilities and provide for their annual adjustment in line with prevailing economic conditions. The portions of the Fees and Charges Act specific to health is operationalized through the Ministry of Health structures with supervision at various levels of the health delivery systems to keep hospital fees in check to ensure access to affordable and quality healthcare services.

The annual revision of the NHIS tariffs and medicines price lists, albeit delays occasionally, is also a contributory factor to the overall positive price differentials found in this study and other [12]. The NHIA tariff development and review processes is comprehensive. The process engages independent consultants and stakeholders, including key representatives from the credentialed healthcare facilities for inputs. This approach provides the platform for the development of realistic healthcare tariffs that could withstand fluctuations in medical inflation and other exogenous factors. According to the NHIA Tariff Operations Manual (2015), the review of the 2014 tariff was occasioned by the need to have tariff rates that are adequate and reflect medical price inflation [13]. The G-DRG also incentivizes providers to make good use of their resources in treating patients in order to breakeven and make some profits.

Another reason for the net gain in price differentials between the NHIS tariff and the Hospital approved charges is the stability of the macroeconomic indicators particularly inflation over the study period (2018–2022). Given that in four (2018–2021) out of the five years of the study period, Ghana recorded consistently a single digit inflation of 7.81%, 7.14%, 9.89% and 9.97% respectively. Stability of inflation is essential for ensuring provision and access to comprehensive healthcare [14]. Inflation affects the prices of goods and services including the cost of health-related materials. In many hospital budgets, healthcare supplies and commodities account for 20–30% of the total sum, making them the second highest healthcare expenditure behind human resources [15]. Since there is importation of over 80% of hospital commodities into the country, management of inflation is key to ensure that healthcare providers stay financially viable to continue to provide services to population. However, the sharp increase in inflation rate to 31.7% and 50.3% respectively in July and November 2022 [16, 17] could justify the overly negative price differentials recorded on almost all the tariffs for the medical conditions for 2022.

Moreover, the benefits of health insurance in providing long-term profits to health facilities as evident in this study have been well documented in other studies in upper middle income countries such as Indonesia [18] and Thailand [19]. Given that, the setting of this study is a lower middle-income country, the cumulative financial gain to healthcare providers from price stability might be supported by the implementation of sound monetary policies by the government to tackle inflation, the lesser spillover pressure from the strengthening of the United States dollar on international markets and higher production outturn for crude oil during the period [20]. Ultimately, macro-economic stability might be a contributory factor for healthcare providers to fully benefit from a G-DRG payment system.

Our findings imply that the positive price differentials between the NHIS tariff and the hospital charges would improve financial resources of the hospital to continue to provide care to members of the scheme and the public especially if the NHIS tariffs are renewed annually as stipulated in its operational manual.

Limitations

The following limitations are identified in the interpretation of our study findings. First, the study was undertaken in one secondary level health facility, making it a case study, which may limit the extent to which the findings can be generalized. Nonetheless the findings provide useful information to inform policy on the implications of the G-DRG payment system in the country. secondly, we conceptualized to determine the association between average length of stay in admissions and the price deferential but had to abandon it due to lack of readily available data on other predictor variables found in literature to influence the outcome variable, average length of stay. We acknowledge that average length of stay may not be influenced only by health costs but several factors such as patient-related (severity, comorbidities, age, frailty index), clinical-care related (availability of specialists, diagnostic services, complications, discharge planning, and adherence, etc.). nonetheless, our findings provide useful information for policymakers and healthcare providers, as well as further research.

Conclusion

The G-DRG payment system contributes to the financial resources of healthcare providers for commonly managed disease conditions in Ghana. The payment system had a cumulative net financial gain on services rendered to the insured. Policymakers need to review the tariff regularly as stipulated in the guidelines to address medical inflation and other economic factors. As a way forward, the NHIA needs to keep adjusting upward, a fixed percentage on tariff for all G-DRGs to cushion providers for the years for delays in tariffs review.

Further research on developing a G-DRG reimbursement formula that factor exchange rate at a defined period, strict regulation of mark-ups on approved hospital charges that would allow uniform pricing for healthcare services in healthcare facilities by the Health Ministry are all needed to improve the payment system. In addition, we recommend that future research look into the relationship between price differentials and average length of stay in admissions by incorporating broader determinants including, patient related factors and clinical care factors to provide understanding of predictors of length of stay in admissions.
