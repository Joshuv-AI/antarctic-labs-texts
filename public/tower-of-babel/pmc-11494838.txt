Intestinal obstruction following antituberculosis therapy in a patient with pancreatic carcinoma and pulmonary tuberculosis: a case report
Authors: Wei Hong, Lei Zhang, Zunshun Yu, Yanjun Wang, Youkun Qi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIntestinal obstruction is a common complication in patients with advanced malignancies, often attributed to the disease itself or as a side effect of opioid analgesics used for pain management. However, the occurrence of intestinal obstruction following antituberculosis therapy is rare.Case presentationWe report a unique case of a 58-year-old Asian male diagnosed with stage IV pancreatic carcinoma and pulmonary tuberculosis. The patient was initiated on a regimen of ethambutol hydrochloride, pyrazinamide, rifampicin, and isoniazid tablets (II) for tuberculosis, alongside morphine for the management of severe cancer-related pain. Subsequently, he developed symptoms indicative of intestinal obstruction. Despite discontinuation of morphine, the patient’s symptoms persisted until he autonomously ceased all medications, leading to a rapid improvement in his condition. This unexpected resolution highlighted the antituberculosis drugs as the probable cause of his intestinal obstruction.ConclusionsThis case underscores the importance of considering antituberculosis drugs as a potential cause of intestinal obstruction, especially in patients who do not respond to conventional management strategies for drug-induced gastrointestinal side effects. It also emphasizes the need for heightened vigilance and monitoring when prescribing these medications to patients with advanced malignancies, to promptly identify and address rare but significant side effects.

Introduction

The intersection of infectious diseases and oncology presents a complex therapeutic landscape, particularly when managing patients with both advanced malignancies and infectious conditions such as tuberculosis (TB). The treatment of TB in patients with cancer is compounded by the potential for drug interactions and side effects that can significantly impact patient outcomes and quality of life. Among the drugs against TB, the combination of ethambutol hydrochloride, pyrazinamide, rifampicin, and isoniazid represents a cornerstone of therapy [1]. However, the side effects of these drugs, while well-characterized in many aspects, can occasionally manifest in less common, yet severe adverse events [2].

Intestinal obstruction in patients with advanced malignancies is a clinical challenge, often attributed to the direct effects of the tumor burden or as a consequence of the analgesic regimens employed for pain management, notably opioids like morphine [3]. The occurrence of intestinal obstruction as a consequence of antituberculosis therapy, however, is a rarity in clinical practice and scarcely documented within the medical literature [4]. This gap underscores the necessity of reporting such cases to broaden our understanding and enhance the clinical management of similar future instances.

This case report aims to shed light on a rare instance of intestinal obstruction following the administration of antituberculosis therapy in a 58-year-old male patient concurrently diagnosed with pancreatic carcinoma and pulmonary tuberculosis. Through this report, we discuss the diagnostic challenges encountered, the process leading to the identification of antituberculosis drugs as the probable cause, and the implications for clinical practice.

Case presentation

The patient, a 58-year-old Asian male, presents with no significant past medical history or known allergies. Historically, he has been healthy, with no prior medication treatments and no history of contact with tuberculosis patients. He is employed in a non-healthcare setting. The patient initially sought medical attention due to severe neck pain, which was subsequently diagnosed as cervical bone metastases originating from stage IV pancreatic carcinoma. Notably, the patient does not smoke or consume alcohol. Upon admission, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: temperature at 36.6 °C, heart rate at 80 beats per minute, respiratory rate at 18 breaths per minute, and blood pressure at 127/76 mmHg. His pain level, assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), was initially 7. Physical examination upon admission revealed significant tenderness at cervical vertebrae C4 to C6 and in the upper abdomen. Upon admission, the patient’s laboratory findings revealed the following: white blood cell count was 6.48 × 109/L, with neutrophils at 4.23 × 109/L. Red blood cell count was 4.43 × 1012/L and hemoglobin level was 128 g/L. Platelet count was recorded at 211 × 109/L. C-reactive protein was measured at less than 0.5 mg/L, indicating no significant inflammatory response. Liver function tests showed an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) level of 9 U/L and an aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level of 20 U/L, both within normal limits. The creatinine level was 73 µmol/L, suggesting normal renal function. Tumor marker analysis indicated a carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level of 3.23 ng/mL and an alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) level of 2.39 ng/mL, both within normal ranges. However, the carbohydrate antigen (CA)-125 was elevated at 72.92 U/mL, while CA19-9 was 2.54 U/mL and CA72-4 was 3.62 U/mL, both within normal limits. An abdominal enhanced computerized tomography (CT) scan initially revealed pancreatic cancer with peripheral vascular infiltration and cystic lesions in the tail, alongside multiple low-density lesions in the liver suggestive of metastases and cysts, cholecystitis, and a left kidney cyst. Further insights from abdominal enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed the tail of the pancreas is swollen and thickened, with decreased T1 dual signal and iso/low signal on T2-weighted Fat Suppression Sequence (fs T2), measuring about 71 mm × 28 mm. The upstream pancreatic duct is slightly dilated. After contrast injection, no significant enhancement is seen in the arterial phase, appearing relatively low in signal, with mild enhancement in the portal venous and delayed phases, with a duct-like low signal structure inside. The tail of the pancreas shows low T1 dual signal and high fs T2 signal with a relatively clear border, and the cyst wall is mildly enhanced. No significant filling defects are seen in the portal vein and inferior vena cava, and multiple small lymph nodes are visible in the retroperitoneum (Fig. 1). Concurrently, a CT chest scan detailed multiple nodular, patchy, and striped high-density shadows with partial calcification in the upper lobes of both lungs, strips of high density in the lower lungs parallel to the pleura, unobstructed trachea and bronchus, small mediastinal lymph nodes, and a left third rib fracture with pleural thickening adjacent to it (Fig. 2). These findings led to a preliminary diagnosis of tuberculosis with partial induration and calcification. Then we carried out sputum cultures and T cell detection of tuberculosis infection (immune plaque). Sputum samples cultured positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, providing microbiological proof of tuberculosis [5, 6]. Given the severity of his pain, which was not alleviated by weak opioid analgesics such as tramadol, the patient was initiated on a morphine regimen for pain management [7]. Following administration of sustained-release morphine tablets at a dosage of 30 mg every 12 h, his pain level decreased to an NRS of 2. His oncologic treatment was primarily palliative, focusing on pain control and maintaining quality of life, considering the advanced stage of his carcinoma. Chemotherapy was initiated with the first cycle consisting of nab-paclitaxel at 200 mg on days 1 and 8, combined with S-1 (tegafur, gimeracil, and oteracil) at 60 mg in the morning and 40 mg in the evening from days 1 to 14, repeated every 3 weeks [8]. Additionally, ibandronate sodium 4 mg was included for anti-bone metastasis therapy, aiming to address the spread of cancer to the bones and further alleviate the patient’s pain [9]. This comprehensive treatment strategy was tailored to manage both the symptoms and progression of his advanced carcinoma.

Anti-tuberculosis therapy was commenced with a combination tablet containing rifampicin 150 mg, isoniazid 75 mg, pyrazinamide 400 mg, and ethambutol hydrochloride 275 mg per tablet, administered at one tablet three times daily. This choice of therapy aimed to provide comprehensive coverage against the tuberculosis infection while considering potential interactions with his ongoing cancer treatments. Following the initiation of this regimen, the patient began experiencing abdominal distension. The patient’s past medical history was otherwise unremarkable, with no prior episodes of gastrointestinal obstruction or significant comorbidities that could predispose him to such complications. Notably, before the introduction of antituberculosis medication, the patient did not have a bowel movement in the 3 days but was able to pass gas normally without any abdominal discomfort. However, after beginning antituberculosis therapy, the patient reported abdominal bloating gradually. Lactulose was prescribed at 10 mL three times daily; however, the symptoms did not improve. During the examination, the abdomen was noted to be distended, with tympanic sounds upon percussion. Bowel sounds were present at a frequency of once per minute, with no significant tenderness, rebound tenderness, or visible intestinal peristalsis or waves observed. On the upright abdominal radiograph, intestinal gas is seen, without obvious dilatation of the intestinal loops, and no distinct air-fluid levels are observed. No free gas is seen under the diaphragm (Fig. 3). As symptoms worsened, resulting in cessation of flatus and bowel movements. Initially, it was suspected that morphine might have contributed to this complication, a notion supported by clinical experience with the constipating effects of opioids. Consequently, morphine was discontinued, yet there was no improvement in the intestinal obstruction. Further investigations ruled out enterophthisis and tuberculous peritonitis, leading to the conclusion that the antituberculosis drugs were unlikely the cause [10].Fig. 3Abdominal plain X-ray: intestinal gas accumulation. In an effort to alleviate the intestinal obstruction, various methods were employed, including lactulose, Aole Paidu capsule, glycerine enema, and clysis [3], yet none led to improvement. The patient, frustrated by the lack of progress in treating both his pancreatic carcinoma, pulmonary tuberculosis, and intestinal obstruction, decided to stop all medications. Remarkably, within 2 days of discontinuing the drugs, his intestinal obstruction began to resolve; he started passing gas again and had a bowel movement the following day. This unexpected improvement highlighted the antituberculosis drugs as the likely culprits behind the intestinal obstruction, underscoring the challenges in predicting drug interactions and adverse effects, especially in a patient with multiple serious health conditions. Following the resolution of the intestinal obstruction, pain management with morphine was resumed. Subsequent systemic chemotherapy was conducted for next five additional cycles. During the subsequent treatment course, the patient did not experience any further episodes of intestinal obstruction.

Discussion

The simultaneous diagnosis and treatment of advanced pancreatic cancer and pulmonary tuberculosis is uncommon. The case highlights the complexities and challenges of managing these conditions concurrently, particularly in terms of drug interactions and treatment side effects. The development of intestinal obstruction following the initiation of anti-tuberculosis therapy, which resolved upon discontinuation of all medications, is a unique presentation. This suggests a potential adverse reaction or interaction not widely reported in the literature, emphasizing the need for vigilance when managing patients with multiple comorbidities. This case prompts us to explore potential underlying mechanisms that could explain the patient’s clinical presentation and outcomes.

Enterophthisis (intestinal tuberculosis) and tuberculous peritonitis are well-recognized complications of tuberculosis that can lead to intestinal obstruction [10]. These conditions typically result from the direct invasion of mycobacterium tuberculosis into the gastrointestinal tract or peritoneum, leading to inflammation, scarring, and ultimately obstruction. Despite thorough investigations ruling out these conditions in our patient, the possibility of subclinical or incipient forms of these complications cannot be entirely dismissed. The articles suggest that intestinal tuberculosis and tuberculous peritonitis can present subtly and may progress to more overt disease even after the initiation of antituberculosis therapy. Even in the absence of clear diagnostic evidence, the possibility of a mild or evolving form of these conditions contributing to obstruction should be considered.

Antituberculosis drugs, including ethambutol hydrochloride, pyrazinamide, rifampicin, and isoniazid, have well-documented side effects [11, 12]. These medications may affect the smooth muscle of the gastrointestinal tract or the enteric nervous system, altering motility and leading to conditions conducive to obstruction. A side effect of these medications could be gut mucosa irritation, leading to swelling and narrowing of the intestinal lumen. Beyond direct mucosal irritation, antituberculosis drugs could potentially induce or exacerbate an inflammatory response in a predisposed gastrointestinal tract, tipping the balance towards obstruction in patients. And there are maybe some drug interactions when combine with chemotherapy, ibandronate, and morphine. Several hypotheses could explain this occurrence:Morphine: morphine, a potent opioid analgesic, is widely recognized for its efficacy in pain management. Morphine’s activation of μ-opioid receptors in the gastrointestinal tract can lead to a decrease in intestinal motility, commonly referred to as opioid-induced bowel dysfunction (OIBD) [7, 13, 14]. This effect is characterized by a reduction in the propulsive peristaltic waves that are essential for the normal transit of intestinal contents. Moreover, morphine can increase the pressure within the sphincters of the gastrointestinal tract, such as the sphincter of Oddi, which can further impede the flow of digestive contents and bile.Albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-paclitaxel): this formulation of paclitaxel is designed to increase drug solubility and reduce solvent-related side effects. However, like other chemotherapeutic agents, it can cause gastrointestinal side effects, including nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea [15]. The impact on gut motility is less commonly discussed but, combined with other drugs affecting the gastrointestinal tract, could contribute to an increased risk of obstruction.Tegafur–gimeracil–oteracil potassium capsule (S-1): this oral fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy drug is known to cause gastrointestinal side effects such as diarrhea, which could potentially exacerbate conditions leading to obstruction, especially in a compromised gastrointestinal system [15]. The drug’s effect on the mucosa could also predispose to inflammation increasing obstruction risk.

The combined use of these chemotherapy drugs with antituberculosis medications and morphine introduces a complex interplay of drug effects and interactions. Morphine’s well-documented effect on reducing gastrointestinal motility could be compounded by the gastrointestinal side effects of chemotherapy and the potential direct or indirect effects of antituberculosis drugs on the gut. Some medications can cause direct irritation to the gastrointestinal (GI) tract mucosa, potentially leading to inflammation that might contribute to obstruction. The immune modulation by these drugs, especially in a patient with an underlying malignancy, could predispose to an exaggerated inflammatory response to minor triggers, including microbial translocation or even the malignancy itself.

Conclusions

Managing a patient with both advanced malignancy and TB poses a significant challenge due to the complex interplay of disease processes and treatment side effects. This case underscores the necessity of a multidisciplinary approach involving oncologists, infectious disease specialists, gastroenterologists, and pharmacists to carefully select and monitor antituberculosis therapy, considering potential side effects and interactions with cancer treatments. Further research into the mechanisms underlying these rare side effects and their management is crucial to improving outcomes for patients at the intersection of infectious diseases and oncology. Given the rarity of this complication, collaborative efforts to compile case reports and conduct retrospective analyses could be invaluable. Prospective studies or pharmacovigilance databases might also provide insights into the incidence, risk factors, and outcomes of intestinal obstruction in patients receiving antituberculosis therapy.
