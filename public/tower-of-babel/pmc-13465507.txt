Knowledge of mothers regarding prematurity and related problems in Abakaliki, South-East Nigeria

Introduction

Prematurity is associated with high perinatal morbidity and mortality. It poses a very high financial and psychological burden to both the families and health care providers [1]. Globally, about 15 million premature babies are delivered annually, and this gives a global preterm birth rate of 11% [2]. Preterm birth also accounts for 35% and 18% neonatal and under-5 mortalities, respectively [3]. Over 50% of neonatal mortality is due to prematurity, and about two-thirds of these deaths occur in Sub-Saharan Africa [1, 2]. Preterm deliveries are deliveries of babies before 37 weeks gestational age. This can be classified into extreme preterm (< 28 weeks gestation), very preterm (28 weeks to < 32 weeks gestation), moderate preterm (32 weeks to < 34 weeks gestation) and borderline preterm deliveries (34 weeks to < 37 weeks gestation) [1]. The earlier the preterm birth, the higher the risk of perinatal morbidity and mortality. Preterm deliveries are associated with maternal age, parity, unbooked status, previous history of preterm birth, multiple pregnancy, living in a rural area, preterm premature rupture of membranes, hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, genetic disorders, infertility treatment and infections such as malaria in pregnancy [2, 4–9].

Preterm delivery and care of the premature babies can pose a huge psychological and physical burden to the mothers. The fathers and mothers of very preterm infants in Portugal implicated placental complications, hypertensive disease in pregnancy, multiple gestation and intrauterine growth restriction as causes of preterm delivery while low socioeconomic status, cardiac complications and respiratory morbidity were the main complications of prematurity [10]. This study also showed that parents with higher socio-economic class had better knowledge of the causes and consequences of premature births than those of low socio-economic class [10]. A study among mothers in Singapore on parental knowledge of prematurity and related issues showed that the general knowledge on prematurity among the women was poor, hence the need to improve the knowledge of the women on prematurity and its risks [11].

The global rate of preterm births has remained largely unchanged for over two decades, and it is even increasing in some countries [1]. In Nigeria, prematurity is associated with high perinatal morbidity and mortality. Management of prematurity requires adequate facilities, manpower and funding which are not readily available in this environment. The knowledge of mothers on the possible causes and complications of prematurity may help in prevention of some of the risk factors for prematurity and policy formulation towards its management. Based on Medline search, there was a paucity of studies on the knowledge of the postnatal women on prematurity and its related problems, and majority of the few previous studies were qualitative studies. There was also no previous study on the subject matter in Abakaliki, South-East Nigeria. This study was therefore aimed at determining the knowledge of mothers on prematurity and its associated complications. The findings from this study would help bridge the knowledge gap in Ebonyi State and help in policy formulation towards improving the knowledge of reproductive age women on prematurity and its related complications.

Methodology

Study setting

Alex Ekwueme Federal University Teaching Hospital, Abakaliki (AEFUTHA) is a tertiary hospital in Ebonyi State. It gets referrals from Ebonyi State and neighbouring states of Enugu, Abia, Imo, Cross River and Benue.

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study among postnatal women who came for immunization of their children at the Institute of Child Health, AEFUTHA, from January 2 to June 30, 2022. Semi-structured questionnaires were used to collect information from the eligible participants.The questionnaires were designed by the authors and were interviewer-administered to the eligible participants. The questionnaires contained information on the socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge of the participants on preterm delivery, experience of the participants with preterm delivery, complications of preterm delivery and how the premature babies were managed.

Inclusion criteria

These comprised consenting postnatal women within 6 weeks of delivery of their babies.

Exclusion criteria

These comprised pregnant women, postnatal women beyond 6 weeks of delivery and those who, despite being within 6 weeks of delivery, declined consent to participate in the study.

Data analysis

The data was analysed with the Statistical Product for Service Solutions (IBM SPSS, Armonk, New York, USA) version 23.0 for Windows. Categorical variables were analysed with the chi-square test. P-value < 0.05 was accepted as statistically significant.

Human ethics and consent to participate declarations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Human Research and Ethics Committee of the Alex Ekwueme Federal University Teaching Hospital with reference number AEFUTHA/HREC/2021/11/1022 and dated 11th November 2021. Informed written consent was obtained from all the eligible participants in the study. The ethical principles of confidentiality, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice were observed while obtaining and handling information/data from the eligible participants. Data collection from the eligible participants was in compliance with the Helsinki declaration [13].

Results

Figure 1 shows Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Flow chart for the participants. A total of 491 eligible mothers participated in the study. Table 1 shows the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants. Majority of the participants were less than 30 years(49.5%), had secondary education(51.1%), were traders(48.3%), urban dwellers(83.1%), married (88.0%) and multiparous(61.1%). The knowledge and experience of the mothers on preterm delivery are stated in Table 2. The proportion of the participants who had correct knowledge of preterm delivery and had experienced preterm delivery were 40.3% and 17.3% respectively. Table 3 shows the knowledge of the participants on the risk factors for preterm delivery. Majority of the participants knew that smoking(88.2%), alcohol intake(86.8%), poor nutrition(83.1%), maternal infections(65.8%), premature rupture of membranes(80.8%), absent/poor antenatal care(74.5%), maternal trauma(80.7%) and stress(79.4%) were the risk factors for preterm delivery. About half (51.1%) of the participants knew that the use of herbal medication in pregnancy was also a risk factor for preterm delivery. However, majority of the participants did not know that hypertensive disease in pregnancy (53.6%), multiple pregnancy (63.5%) and previous preterm delivery(58.5%) were also risk factors for preterm delivery. Table 4 shows the knowledge of the participants on neonatal complications of preterm delivery. Approximately half(50.1%) of the participants knew that preterm delivery could cause birth asphyxia. Majority (75.6%) of the participants also knew that preterm delivery could cause neonatal infections. However, majority of the participants did not know that preterm delivery could cause fluid and electrolyte imbalance (70.9%) and abnormal glucose metabolism (63.5%). Table 5 shows the influence of the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants on knowledge of preterm delivery. Being less than 35 years old, having at least secondary education, urban residence, and previous history of preterm birth had a significant influence on the knowledge of preterm delivery (P-value = < 0.05). However, parity, occupation and history of previous miscarriage among the participants did not have a significant influence on their knowledge of preterm delivery (P-value = > 0.05). Discussion

This study showed that the proportion of the participants who had correct knowledge of preterm delivery and had experienced preterm delivery were 40.3% and 17.3% respectively. The participants had a moderate knowledge of the risk factors for preterm delivery. With the exception of neonatal infections and birth asphyxia, majority of the participants did not know that prematurity could cause fluid and electrolyte imbalance as well as abnormal glucose metabolism. Being less than 35 years old, having at least secondary education, urban residence, and previous history of preterm birth had a significant influence on the knowledge of preterm delivery.

The 40.3% proportion of the participants having correct knowledge of preterm delivery in this study is poor and can be a public health challenge. The 17.3% prevalence of preterm birth among the participants in this study is higher than 6.5% previously reported in Abakaliki, Nigeria, but lower than 23.7% reported in Sokoto, Nigeria [14, 15]. The possible reason for the higher prevalence of preterm birth in Sokoto may be due to the differences in the socio-demographic characteristics between women in southern and northern regions of Nigeria. The socio-demographic characteristics like teenage pregnancy and preeclampsia/eclampsia are more common in northern than southern Nigeria [16, 17]. Majority of the participants knowing that smoking, alcohol intake, poor nutrition, maternal infections, premature rupture of membranes, absent/poor antenatal care, maternal trauma and stress were the risk factors for preterm delivery, was in keeping with previous studies on the subject matter [9–11]. However, about half or major proportion of the participants who did not know that the use of herbal medication in pregnancy, hypertensive disease, multiple pregnancy and previous preterm delivery were also risk factors for preterm delivery in this study, is contrary to the previous reports [10, 11]. The poor knowledge of the participants on these risk factors for preterm delivery may potentially increase the risk of such patients having preterm delivery.

The poor knowledge of the mothers on the neonatal complications of preterm delivery in this study is contrary to the previous report in Ghana [18]. The possible reasons for the difference in knowledge on neonatal complications of preterm delivery between this study and that done in Ghana may be attributed to the difference in the study designs. While this was a quantitative study, the one by Adu-Bonsaffoh et al., was a qualitative study. Also, the study participants used by Adu-Bonsaffoh et al., were only mothers who had preterm deliveries, unlike this study, in which every mother who had delivery, irrespective of whether it was preterm or term delivery, was recruited. The poor knowledge of the participants on neonatal complications in this study underscores the need to incorporate sensitization of antenatal clinic attendees on preterm delivery, its risk factors and complications, and how the women can mitigate the effects of such complications. This study revealing the significantly better knowledge of preterm delivery among the participants that were less than 35 years old, having at least secondary education and previous preterm deliveries, as well as living in urban areas, underscores the need for massive sensitization of the pregnant women both at the antenatal clinics and communities on the risk factors to, and association between preterm deliveries and maternal and perinatal mortality.

Strengths and limitations

Though this study brought insight into the knowledge and experience of mothers on preterm deliveries, it is weakened by the cross-sectional design in which some information provided by the participants were prone to recall bias. There was no standard validation of the questionnaires.This was also a hospital-based study in which the results may not reflect the real situation in the community.

Conclusion and recommendation

There was a poor knowledge of preterm delivery and its associated perinatal complications among post-natal women in Abakaliki, South-East Nigeria. This underscores the need to sensitize pregnant women, both at the antenatal clinics and in the communities, on the risk factors and complications of preterm delivery.
