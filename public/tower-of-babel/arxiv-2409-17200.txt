A random measure approach to reinforcement learning in continuous time
Christian Bender 1, Nguyen Tran Thuan 1,2

Abstract
. We present a random measure approach for modeling exploration, i.e., the execution of measure-valued controls, in continuous-time reinforcement learning (RL) with controlled diffusion and jumps. First, we consider the case when sampling the randomized control in continuous time takes place on a discrete-time grid and reformulate the resulting stochastic differential equation (SDE) as an equation driven by suitable random measures. The construction of these random measures makes use of the Brownian motion and the Poisson random measure (which are the sources of noise in the original model dynamics) as well as the additional random variables, which are sampled on the grid for the control execution. Then, we prove a limit theorem for these random measures as the mesh-size of the sampling grid goes to zero, which leads to the grid-sampling limit SDE that is jointly driven by white noise random measures and a Poisson random measure. We also argue that the grid-sampling limit SDE can substitute the exploratory SDE and the sample SDE of the recent continuous-time RL literature, i.e., it can be applied for the theoretical analysis of exploratory control problems and for the derivation of learning algorithms. Keyworks. Exploratory control; Orthogonal martingale measures; Poisson random measures; Reinforcement learning; Weak convergence. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary: 60G57; Secondary: 28A33, 60H10, 93B52, 93E35.



1.
Introduction

Recent years have seen tremendous progress in the development of reinforcement learning (RL) for systems in continuous time and space, which are formulated in the language of stochastic differential equations (SDEs). The articles
[
28
,
29
]
constitute an important starting point for the modeling of exploration of the state space in such a framework. Roughly speaking, the exploration mechanism consists of first choosing a relaxed control (which is a policy with values in the set of probability distributions) and then executing the policy by drawing a sample from the chosen distribution.
Based on a heuristic argument using law of large numbers, Wang et al.
[
28
]
identify the drift and diffusion coefficient, when averaging over many independent executions of the relaxed control, leading to the
exploratory SDE
in a diffusion setting. Regularizing the cost function by adding a running reward for exploration (e.g., in terms of Shannon entropy as in
[
28
,
29
]
), they come up with a formulation of
exploratory control problems
.

The exploratory control approach of
[
28
]
has been generalized in many directions, including a mean-field setting
[
5
,
8
]
, regime-switching models
[
30
]
, and models with jumps
[
1
,
6
]
. A significant part of the literature focuses on exploratory versions of linear-quadratic problems (which are no longer linear-quadratic due to the presence of the regularization term) and on applications to mean-variance portfolio selection, see, e.g.,
[
1
,
3
,
8
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
. Moreover, alternatives to the Shannon entropy regularization term have been suggested, see
[
4
,
8
,
9
,
22
]
. More information about the recent progress in continuous-time RL can be found in the survey article by Zhou
[
34
]
.

While the exploratory SDE is tailor-made to adapt the classical dynamic programming approach and to tackle exploratory control by means of a suitable variant of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation (see
[
27
]
for a detailed study of the exploratory HJB equation), it cannot be interpreted as the response of the system to a randomized control (i.e, a sample drawn from a given relaxed control). This is due to the averaging effect in its derivation. Hence, trajectories of the exploratory SDE cannot be regarded as observable and, thus, learning algorithms cannot be formulated in terms of (time-discretized) trajectories of the exploratory control, see also the discussion in
[
14
, p.9]
.

As a way out, Jia and Zhou
[
14
,
15
]
introduce a
sample SDE
, which models the dynamics of the system along a randomized control in continuous time. Based on the sample SDE and martingale criteria for optimality in continuous time, they provide continuous-time versions of several learning algorithms (including temporal-difference learning and
Q
-learning), see also
[
25
]
for an overview on learning algorithms in the classical framework of Markov decision processes. The continuous-time algorithms of
[
14
,
15
]
are only discretized at the implementation stage, so they follow the “first-optimize-then-discretize” methodology.
However, no explicit construction of the randomization mechanism (for sampling from a given relaxed control) is provided in
[
14
,
15
]
. The latter reference mentions an uncountable family of independent uniform random variables
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
on the unit cube. So the construction of sample SDEs might need to deal with some measurability issues, for which we refer, e.g., to
[
24
, Proposition 2.1 and Corollary 4.3]
.
To circumvent such measurability problems, we adapt some ideas of
[
26
]
. We sample the independent uniform random variables on a finite time-grid only and extend the randomization scheme piecewise constantly to a left-continuous process (which, consequently, becomes predictable). This approach leads to a well-defined SDE (which we call
grid-sampling SDE
), which has a sound interpretation as response of the system to the grid-randomization of a relaxed control. Technically, this is an SDE with random coefficients.

We are mainly interested in the limit dynamics of this grid-sampling SDE, as the mesh-size of the grid tends to zero. To this end, we reformulate it as an SDE with deterministic coefficients driven by random measures which depend on the grid-sampling randomization process. In this way, the additional randomness for policy execution is moved from the integrand to the integrator. Our main result (
Theorem

2.7
below) implies vague convergence of these grid-dependent random measures, as the grid-size converges to zero. Replacing the grid-dependent random measures by their limit measures, we arrive at the
grid-sampling limit SDE
, which we consider as a natural SDE formulation for RL with state space exploration in continuous time.

Note that we work in a framework with controlled diffusion and controlled jumps in which the SDE under a classical control is driven by a multivariate Brownian motion and a Poisson random measure. In the “control randomization limit”, i.e. in our formulation of the grid-sampling limit SDE, the Brownian motion is replaced by a family of independent white noise martingale measures (in the sense of
[
31
,
17
]
) and the limit Poisson random measures is defined on an extended measurable space to account for the randomization.

Our weak convergence approach extends the derivation of the exploratory dynamics for mean-variance portfolio selection with jumps in
[
1
]
. Due to the linear dependence of the diffusion coefficient on the control, the white noise martingale measures do not show up there but are replaced by a high-dimensional Brownian motion (which features additional components to model the control randomization) in the context of
[
1
]
, see also
Example

2.13
. However, the limit Poisson random measure is essentially the same as in
[
1
]
in our more general situation.

We also mention that recently the framework of Zhou and coauthors
[
14
,
15
,
28
]
has been extended to the jump-diffusion case by Gao et al.
[
6
]
. They derive in
[
6
]
the infinitesimal generator of the averaged (over independent policy executions) dynamics heuristically by extending the law of large numbers argument from
[
28
]
in order to define an exploratory SDE with jumps. While the jump part features the same structure as in our grid-sampling limit SDE and as in
[
1
]
, the diffusion part of their exploratory SDE with jumps is driven by a Brownian motion (which can be lower-dimensional than the Brownian motion that drives the original SDE without control randomization). We also mention that the final form of the grid-sampling limit SDE resembles the classical formulation of relaxed control, see, e.g.,
[
21
]
for the case of diffusion control or Chapter 13 in
[
19
]
. We emphasize, however, that relaxed controls have been introduced as a technical tool for compactification of the control space in the framework of classical control, while the importance of the grid-sampling limit SDE is in its interpretation as limit to the response of the system to randomized controls.

Structure of this article

In
Section

2
we motivate and discuss the main result. After explaining the general setting and discussing several sampling schemes for randomization, we introduce the grid-sampling SDE at the end of
Subsection

2.1
. In
Subsection

2.2
, we construct some random measures related to grid sampling and reformulate the grid-sampling SDE as an SDE driven by these random measures. The main limit theorem is stated in
Subsection

2.3
, leading to the definition of the grid-sampling limit SDE.

In
Subsection

2.4
, we compare the exploratory SDE of
[
28
]
and the grid-sampling limit SDE in a simplified setting. It turns out that the solutions to both SDEs share the same probability law, although one is derived by averaging out the policy randomization a-priori, while the other one is obtained in a limit, when one adds more and more randomization noise. A main difference is that our limit theorem combined with stability results for SDEs driven by martingale measures (e.g., Chapter 13 in
[
19
]
) suggests a joint convergence of SDE and integrator for the grid-sampling limit SDE, while such a result cannot hold for the exploratory SDE. This difference plays a key role in
Subsection

2.5
, where we re-derive the temporal difference TD(0)-algorithm of
[
13
,
14
]
for policy evaluation in continuous time based on the grid-sampling limit SDE. In doing so, we avoid reference to any kind of idealized sampling that requires independent, identically distributed families of random variables indexed by continuous time for control randomization.

The proof of the main theorem (
Theorem

2.7
) will be given in
Section

3
and relies on a limit theorem for triangular arrays by Jacod and Shiryaev
[
12
]
. The key step of the proof is contained in
Proposition

3.4
, which implies convergence of the (modified) semimartingale characteristics of the grid-sampling random measures (integrated against a sufficiently large class of test integrands) to the semimartingale characteristics of the limit random measures.

Proofs of some technical results and background information on martingale measures are compiled in the appendices.

2.
Motivation and discussion of the main result

2.1.
Controlled SDEs with randomized policies

We think of the model dynamics as a system with input coefficients (
a,b,\gamma
below) that depend on a policy
h
in feedback form. The output of the system is influenced by the random noise generated by a multivariate Brownian motion
B
and an independent Poisson random measure
N
. Thus, for a classical (non-randomized) policy
h
, we end up with the dynamics, for
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{h}_{t}

\displaystyle=b(t,X^{h}_{t-},h(t,X^{h}_{t-}))\mathrm{d}t+a(t,X^{h}_{t-},h(t,X^{h}_{t-}))\mathrm{d}B_{t}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0<|z|\leq\mathfrak{r}}\gamma(t,X^{h}_{t-},h(t,X^{h}_{t-}),z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)+\int_{|z|>\mathfrak{r}}\gamma(t,X^{h}_{t-},h(t,X^{h}_{t-}),z)N(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z),

with initial condition
X^{h}_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The coefficients
b\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
a\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
\gamma\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the feedback policy
h\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are measurable and assumed to be sufficiently regular to guarantee existence of a unique strong solution. Moreover,
B=(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a standard
p
-dimensional Brownian motion,
N(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)
is a (possibly inhomogeneous) Poisson random measure independent of
B
with intensity
\nu(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)=\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t
where
\nu_{t}
is a Lévy measure on
\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}
(i.e.,
\nu_{t}
is a Borel measure with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}}(|z|^{2}\wedge 1)\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}z)<\infty
) for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Throughout this article, we assume that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}}(|z|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{0<|z|\leq\mathfrak{r}\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{|z|>\mathfrak{r}\}})\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t<\infty

for some fixed
\mathfrak{r}\in[0,\infty]
. We may think of
\mathfrak{r}
as the threshold to distinguish between small jumps and large jumps – and, as usual, the small jumps are integrated with respect to the compensated random measure
\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)=N(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)-\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t
. Here, the Brownian motion
B
and the Poisson random measure

are defined on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\bar{\mathbb{F}},\mathbb{P})
which satisfies the usual conditions. Note that the filtration
\bar{\mathbb{F}}
may be larger than the one generated by
(B,N)
.

Remark 2.1
.

(1)

One typically takes
\mathfrak{r}=1
which corresponds to the canonical truncation function
z\mathbbm{1}_{\{0<|z|\leq 1\}}
. However, since the random measures are handled differently between the “compensated jump part” and the “finite activity jump part”, we include here the case
\mathfrak{r}=0
, which means that the jump part
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}}zN(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)
of the driving inhomogeneous Lévy process is of finite activity, and the case
\mathfrak{r}=\infty
which means that the jump part
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}}z\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)
is a square integrable martingale.

(2)

Note that (
2.2
) holds for some
\mathfrak{r}\in(0,\infty)
if and only if (
2.2
) holds for all
\mathfrak{r}\in(0,\infty)
.

A
relaxed (or, measure-valued) control
in feedback form is a mapping
{h}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathcal{P}r(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, where
\mathcal{P}r(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
denotes the space of probability measures on the Borel field
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For the execution of a relaxed control, we consider an
\bar{\mathbb{F}}
-predictable stochastic process
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
independent of

, whose marginal distribution
\xi_{t}
is a uniform distribution on
[0,1]^{d}
for every

. Such a
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
will be called a

. We think of a measurable function

as a

in feedback form. The actual randomization is performed by plugging a randomization process in the last variable of
{\mathbf{h}}
. Adapting the terminology in
[

to our setting, we say that a randomized control
{\mathbf{h}}

has the distribution

for every

(for some, and then for any, randomization process

). For a given randomization process

Remark 2.2
.

(1)

We have only fixed the marginal distribution of the randomization process
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, but not the joint distribution. In particular,
\xi_{s}
and
\xi_{t}
are, for the moment, not supposed to be independent for

. Several constructions of the process

(2)

It is well known that for every distribution

\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there is a measurable function

such that

is

on
[0,1]^{d}
. This is one motivation to assume that the marginals of

are uniformly distributed. Note, however, that for any vector
(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d})
of independent standard Gaussian random variables, the vector
(\Phi(\eta_{1}),\ldots,\Phi(\eta_{d}))
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
. Here,

denotes the cumulative distribution function of a standard Gaussian. Hence, changing the marginal distribution of
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, e.g., to a multivariate Gaussian as in

does not make any essential difference in the constructions to come.

The crucial property of the randomization process
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is its predictability which necessarily implies the predictability of the random field
({\mathbf{h}}(t,x,\xi_{t}))_{t\in[0,T],\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}
. Hence, for a randomized control
{\mathbf{h}}
and a fixed randomization process

, it makes sense to consider the random coefficient SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t}

\displaystyle=b(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},\xi_{t}))\mathrm{d}t+a(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},\xi_{t}))\mathrm{d}B_{t}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0<|z|\leq\mathfrak{r}}\gamma(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},\xi_{t}),z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)

(2.3)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{|z|>\mathfrak{r}}\gamma(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},\xi_{t}),z)N(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z),

which describes the dynamics of the system along the
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
-randomized feedback policy
({\mathbf{h}}(t,x,\xi_{t}))_{t\in[0,T],\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}
.

We next discuss two approaches for
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
:

•

Idealized sampling:
In idealized sampling, the family
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of random variables is assumed to be independent. Note that there is no problem to construct the triplet
(B,N,\xi)
on an appropriate product space. It is, however, known that a family of non-constant independent identically distributed random variables
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
cannot be realized in a jointly measurable way with respect to the standard product
\sigma
-field, i.e., the map
\xi\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow[0,1]^{d}
cannot be
\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{B}([0,T])/\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d})
-measurable, see, e.g., Proposition 2.1 in
[
24
]
and the detailed discussion on the relevance of the results in
[
24
]
for policy execution in
[
26
]
. In particular, with idealized sampling, we can never obtain the crucial predictability property of
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, and, hence, it is not clear how to make any good sense of the SDE (
2.3
) for a sufficiently large class of
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
-randomized policies. While no explicit construction of the policy execution for the sample SDE in
[
15
]
is provided, the authors introduce an uncountable family of independent uniform random variables for performing the policy execution. Thus, their sample SDE could face the measurability issue detailed above.

•

Grid-sampling:
Let
\Pi
be a partition of
[0,T]
with grid points
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and mesh-size
|\Pi|:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|t_{i}-t_{i-1}|
. Assuming that the probability space carries an independent family
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
of uniforms on
[0,1]^{d}
independent of
(B,N)
, we consider
the randomization process
\xi^{\Pi}=(\xi^{\Pi}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
given by

\displaystyle\xi^{\Pi}_{t}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}{\bf 1}_{(t_{j-1},t_{j}]}(t),\quad t\in[0,T].

Writing
\mathbb{F}^{\Pi}=(\mathcal{F}^{\Pi}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
for the right-continuous, augmented version of the filtration generated by
(B,N,\xi^{\Pi})
, the process
\xi^{\Pi}
is left-continuous and adapted, hence
\mathbb{F}^{\Pi}
-predictable. Remark that
\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}=\xi_{i}
is
\mathcal{F}^{\Pi}_{t_{i-1}}
-measurable, but independent of
\mathcal{F}^{\Pi}_{(t_{i-1})-}
, and
B
and
N(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)
are still a Brownian motion and a Poisson random measure with intensity
\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t
with respect to
\mathbb{F}^{\Pi}
.

We emphasize that the authors in
[
26
]
and
[
7
]
have already applied this type of grid-sampling as a substitution for the infeasible idealized sampling when executing Gaussian relaxed policies in the context of linear-quadratic control.

By predictability of the grid-sampling process
\xi^{\Pi}
, we may consider the SDE (
2.3
) with
\xi=\xi^{\Pi}
and we call this SDE the
grid-sampling SDE
along the randomization process
\xi^{\Pi}
. Remark that it can be solved iteratively on the subintervals of the partition under standard Lipschitz and growth assumptions, i.e., for
t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
,

\displaystyle X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t}

\displaystyle=X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t_{i-1}}+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t}b(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}))\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t}a(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}))\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(t_{i-1},t]}\int_{0<|z|\leq\mathfrak{r}}\gamma(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}),z)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)

(2.4)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(t_{i-1},t]}\int_{|z|>\mathfrak{r}}\gamma(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}),z)N(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z),

see, e.g., Theorem IV.9.1 in
[
11
]
for the case of a homogeneous Poisson random measure.

Remark 2.3
.

Suppose that the randomized control
{\mathbf{h}}
is continuous and executes the relaxed control
h
and that the sampling grid
\Pi
is “sufficiently fine”. Then, we may consider

{\mathbf{h}}(t_{i-1},X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t_{i-1}},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})=\lim_{s\searrow t_{i-1}}{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})

as a “good” approximation to
{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})
for
s\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
. Note that
X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t_{i-1}}=X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{(t_{i-1})-}
a.s. Thus,
X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t_{i-1}}
is
\mathcal{F}^{\Pi}_{(t_{i-1})-}
-measurable and, consequently, independent of
\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}
. Therefore, we can interpret the approximation
{\mathbf{h}}(t_{i-1},X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t_{i-1}},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})
in the following way: The actor first chooses the distribution
h(t_{i-1},X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t_{i-1}})
and, then, the independent uniform random variable
\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}
is generated to sample from this distribution.

2.2.
Random measure interpretation of grid-sampling

We are interested in the limit dynamics of the grid-sampling SDE (
2.4
) as the mesh-size of the sampling partition
\Pi
tends to zero. Note that the limit (in finite-dimensional distributions) of grid-sampling scheme leads to idealized sampling. So it does not appear to be promising to pass to the limit on the level of the random coefficients of the grid-sampling SDE. Instead, we change the perspective and consider the SDE (
2.4
) as a system with deterministic input coefficients
(b,a,\gamma,\mathbf{h})
which is subjected to the noise given by
(B,N,\xi^{\Pi})
. This means that, in this subsection, we first identify suitable random measures depending on
(B,N,\xi^{\Pi})
such that (
2.4
) can be re-written in the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t}

\displaystyle=b(t,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},u))M_{D}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}u)+a(t,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},u))M_{B}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0<|z|\leq\mathfrak{r}}\gamma(t,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},u),z)\tilde{M}_{J}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

(2.5)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{|z|>\mathfrak{r}}\gamma(t,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{t-},u),z)M_{J}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u).

In the next subsection, we will then state our main result on the joint convergence of the random measures
(M_{D}^{\Pi},M_{B}^{\Pi},M_{J}^{\Pi})
as the mesh-size of
\Pi
tends to zero. The limit random measures can finally be used to define a meaningful limit SDE of the grid-sampling SDE.

For the drift part
: We consider

(2.6)

M_{D}^{\Pi}(\omega,\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}u):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{i-1},t_{i}]}(t)\delta_{\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}(\omega)}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t,

where
\delta_{y}
denotes the Dirac distribution on the point
y
. Then,
M^{\Pi}_{D}
is a random measure in the sense of
[
12
, Definition II.1.3]
. The following lemma, which links integration with respect to
M_{D}^{\Pi}
to the drift part of the grid-sampling SDE, is straightforward to prove.

Lemma 2.4
.

A measurable random field
Y\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\times[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is integrable with respect to
M_{D}^{\Pi}
, if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}|Y_{s}(\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})|\mathrm{d}s<\infty,\quad\mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.}

In this case, a.s.,

\int_{(0,T]\times[0,1]^{d}}Y_{s}(u)M^{\Pi}_{D}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}Y_{s}(\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})\mathrm{d}s.

For the Brownian part
: We define

(2.7)

M_{B^{(l)}}^{\Pi}(\omega,t,A):=\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{i-1},{t_{i}}]}(s)\mathbbm{1}_{A}(\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})\;\mathrm{d}B^{(l)}_{s}\bigg)(\omega),\quad A\in\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d}),\;t\in[0,T],\;l=1,\ldots,p.

Note that the integrand is a bounded
\mathbb{F}^{\Pi}
-predictable process, and, hence, the Itô integrals are well defined.

Lemma 2.5
.

For any
l=1,\ldots,p
,
M_{B^{(l)}}^{\Pi}
is an orthogonal martingale measure on
[0,T]\times\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d})
in the sense of
[
17
]
with intensity measure
M^{\Pi}_{D}
. Moreover, for every
\mathbb{F}^{\Pi}
-predictable (i.e.,
\mathcal{P}_{{\mathbb{F}^{\Pi}}}\otimes\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d})/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
-measurable) random field
Y\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\times[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}|Y_{s}(\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]<\infty,

Y
can be integrated against
M_{B^{(l)}}^{\Pi}
and, a.s.,

(2.8)

\int_{(0,T]\times[0,1]^{d}}Y_{s}(u)M_{B^{(l)}}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}Y_{s}(\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})\mathrm{d}B^{(l)}_{s}.

Background information on orthogonal martingale measures, including a review of the integration theory, and a proof of
Lemma

2.5
can be found in
Appendix

A
.

For the jump part
: We first consider the (inhomogeneous) purely non-Gaussian Lévy process

(2.9)

\displaystyle L_{t}=\int_{(0,t]}\int_{0<|z|\leq\mathfrak{r}}z\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)+\int_{(0,t]}\int_{|z|>\mathfrak{r}}zN(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z),\quad t\in[0,T],

and recall that
N
is the jump measure of
L
, i.e.,

N(\omega,\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)=\sum_{t\in(0,T]}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\Delta L_{t}(\omega)\neq 0\}}\delta_{(t,\Delta L_{t}(\omega))}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z).

We now introduce the new integer-valued random measure

(2.10)

M_{J}^{\Pi}(\omega,\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\Delta L_{t}(\omega)\neq 0\}}\delta_{(t,\Delta L_{t}(\omega),\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}(\omega))}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}
. It has the same jump times as
N
, but features an “extra jump size”
\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}
in the new variable
u
for the control randomization, if the jump takes place in the interval
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
. As stated in the following lemma, its predictable compensator measure is given by

\mu_{J}^{\Pi}(\omega,\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{i-1},t_{i}]}(t)\delta_{\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}(\omega)}(\mathrm{d}u)\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t.

Hence, stochastic integration with respect to the compensated random measure
\tilde{M}^{\Pi}_{J}=M^{\Pi}_{J}-\mu_{J}^{\Pi}
can be defined in the sense of
[
12
, Ch.II, §1d]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

(1)

The random measure
\mu_{J}^{\Pi}
is the
(\mathbb{F}^{\Pi},\mathbb{P})
-predictable compensator measure of the integer-valued random measure
M_{J}^{\Pi}
.

(2)

Suppose that
Y\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an
\mathbb{F}^{\Pi}
-predictable random field (i.e.
Y
is
\mathcal{P}_{{\mathbb{F}^{\Pi}}}\otimes\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}})\otimes\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d})/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
-measurable). If

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{(t_{i-1},{t_{i}}]\times{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}}}|Y_{s}(z,\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})|N(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)<\infty,\quad\mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.},

then
Y
is integrable with respect to
M_{J}^{\Pi}
and, a.s.,

(2.11)

\int_{(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}}Y_{s}(z,u)M_{J}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{(t_{i-1},{t_{i}}]\times{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}}}Y_{s}(z,\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})N(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z).

Moreover, if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}}|Y_{s}(z,\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})|^{2}\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\bigg]<\infty,

then
Y
is integrable with respect to
\tilde{M}_{J}^{\Pi}
and, a.s.,

(2.12)

\int_{(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}}Y_{s}(z,u)\tilde{M}_{J}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{(t_{i-1},{t_{i}}]\times{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}}}Y_{s}(z,\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}})\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z).

Again, the proof can be found in
Subsection

A.2
.

In view of
Lemmas

2.4
,
2.5
and
2.6
, we can, indeed, re-write the grid-sampling SDE (
2.4
) in the form (
2.5
), utilizing the random measures introduced in (
2.6
), (
2.7
), and (
2.10
). For instance, assuming that
a
is bounded and applying standard conventions on integration of matrix-valued integrands, the Brownian part in (
2.4
) becomes for
t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
,

\displaystyle\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t}a(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}))\mathrm{d}B_{s}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t}a^{(1,l)}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}))\mathrm{d}B^{(l)}_{s}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t}a^{(m,l)}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}))\mathrm{d}B^{(l)}_{s}\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{(t_{i-1},t]\times[0,1]^{d}}a^{(1,l)}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},u))M_{B^{(l)}}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{(t_{i-1},t]\times[0,1]^{d}}a^{(m,l)}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},u))M_{B^{(l)}}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{(t_{i-1},t]\times[0,1]^{d}}a(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}}_{s-},u))M_{B}^{\Pi}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u),

where
M^{\Pi}_{B}=(M^{\Pi}_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M^{\Pi}_{B^{(p)}})^{\mathsf{T}}
.

2.3.
Limit theorem and grid-sampling limit SDE

In this subsection, we establish a limit theorem for the random measures
(M_{D}^{\Pi},M_{B}^{\Pi},M_{J}^{\Pi})
defined in (
2.6
)–(
2.10
), which drive the grid-sampling SDE (
2.5
), as the mesh-size of the partition
\Pi
goes to zero. This limit theorem suggests a formulation for the
grid-sampling limit SDE
, which replaces
(M_{D}^{\Pi},M_{B}^{\Pi},M_{J}^{\Pi})
by the limit random measures
(M_{D},M_{B},M_{J})
in (
2.5
).

We define

M_{D}(A):=\bm{\lambda}_{[0,T]}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{[0,1]}^{\otimes d}(A),\quad A\in\mathcal{B}([0,T])\otimes\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d}),

where
\bm{\lambda}_{U}
stands for the restriction of the Lebesgue measure to a Borel set
U
. Moreover, we let
(M_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}})
denote
p
independent martingale measures with continuous paths and intensity measure
M_{D}
. Continuous martingale measures with deterministic intensities are also called white noise martingale measures, and we refer to
[
17
]
for a construction of such martingale measures and more background information.
Lemma

2.11
below provides some information on their relation to Brownian motion.

Finally,
M_{J}
denotes a Poisson random measure on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}
with intensity measure

\mu_{J}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u):=\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}t.

An explicit construction of
M_{J}
can be found in
Subsection

B.3
. As usual
\tilde{M}_{J}=M_{J}-\mu_{J}
stands for the compensated Poisson random measure.

We assume that the original filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\bar{\mathbb{F}},\mathbb{P})
has been chosen sufficiently large to carry
(M_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}})
and
M_{J}
. By
Subsection

B.4
,
(M_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}})
and
M_{J}
are automatically independent. We denote by
\mathbb{F}
the right-continuous, augmented version of the filtration generated by
(M_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}},M_{J})
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
(\Pi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of finite partitions of
[0,T]
with
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|\Pi_{n}|=0
. For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
R\in(0,\infty)\cup\{\mathfrak{r}\}
, and for any bounded measurable functions
f_{l}^{(k)}\colon[0,T]\times[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
(
l=0,\ldots,p
;
k=1,\ldots,m
),
f_{l}^{(k)}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
(
l=p+1,p+2
;
k=1,\ldots,m
),
consider the sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued processes
\mathcal{X}^{n}=(\mathcal{X}^{n,(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{X}^{n,(m)})
defined via

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{n,(k)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{0}^{(k)}(s,u)M_{D}^{\Pi_{n}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{l}^{(k)}(s,u)M_{B^{(l)}}^{\Pi_{n}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,t]\times\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{p+1}^{(k)}(s,z,u)|z|\tilde{M}_{J}^{\Pi_{n}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,t]\times\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{p+2}^{(k)}(s,z,u)M_{J}^{\Pi_{n}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u),\quad t\in[0,T],\;k=1,\ldots,m.

Then,
(\mathcal{X}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly in the Skorokhod topology on the space
\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued, càdlàg functions to
\mathcal{X}=(\mathcal{X}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{X}^{(m)})
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{(k)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{0}^{(k)}(s,u)M_{D}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{l}^{(k)}(s,u)M_{B^{(l)}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,t]\times\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{p+1}^{(k)}(s,z,u)|z|\tilde{M}_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,t]\times\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{p+2}^{(k)}(s,z,u)M_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u),\quad t\in[0,T],\;k=1,\ldots,m.

The proof will be provided in
Section

3
and
Subsection

C.1
contains some background information on weak convergence in the Skorokhod topology.

Remark 2.8
.

As a consequence of
Theorem

2.7
,
(M_{D}^{\Pi_{n}},M_{B^{(1)}}^{\Pi_{n}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}}^{\Pi_{n}},M_{J}^{\Pi_{n}})
vaguely converges to
(M_{D},M_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}},M_{J})
in the following sense: For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and for any continuous functions with compact support
f_{l}^{(k)}\colon[0,T]\times[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
(
l=0,\ldots,p
;
k=1,\ldots,m
),
f_{p+2}^{(k)}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
(
k=1,\ldots,m
),
the sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued processes
\mathcal{X}^{n}=(\mathcal{X}^{n,(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{X}^{n,(m)})
defined via

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{n,(k)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{0}^{(k)}(s,u)M_{D}^{\Pi_{n}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{l}^{(k)}(s,u)M_{B^{(l)}}^{\Pi_{n}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{p+2}^{(k)}(s,z,u)M_{J}^{\Pi_{n}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u),\quad t\in[0,T],\;k=1,\ldots,m,

weakly converges in the Skorokhod topology on
\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
to
\mathcal{X}=(\mathcal{X}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{X}^{(m)})
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{(k)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{0}^{(k)}(s,u)M_{D}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{l}^{(k)}(s,u)M_{B^{(l)}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{p+2}^{(k)}(s,z,u)M_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u),\quad t\in[0,T],\;k=1,\ldots,m.

Indeed, if the
f_{l}^{(k)}
’s
(l=p+1,p+2;\;k=1,\ldots,m)
in
Theorem

2.7
have compact support, then there is an
\varepsilon>0
(independent of
k,l,t,u
) such that
f_{l}^{(k)}=0
, if
0<|z|\leq\varepsilon
. Hence, we can apply
Theorem

2.7
with
R=\varepsilon
. We also refer to
[
16
]
for background information on the general theory of vague convergence of random measures and to
[
32
,
33
]
for the case of martingale measures.

In view of
Theorem

2.7
, the random measure formulation (
2.5
) of the grid-sampling SDE (
2.4
) in
Subsection

2.2
, and the definition of
M_{D}
, a natural limit formulation of the grid-sampling SDE for a given randomized policy
\mathbf{h}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\displaystyle X^{\mathbf{h}}_{t}=x

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}b(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},u))\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]^{d}}a^{(\cdot,l)}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},u))M_{B^{(l)}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle+\int_{(0,t]\times\{0<|z|\leq\mathfrak{r}\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\gamma(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},u),z)\tilde{M}_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

(2.13)

\displaystyle+\int_{(0,t]\times\{|z|>\mathfrak{r}\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\gamma(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},u),z)M_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u).

We call this SDE the
grid-sampling limit SDE
for policy
{\mathbf{h}}
.

Remark 2.9
.

We stress that the random measures
(M_{D},M_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}},M_{J})
appearing in the limit are independent, whereas the pre-limit random measures
(M_{D}^{\Pi},M_{B^{(1)}}^{\Pi},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}}^{\Pi},M_{J}^{\Pi})
are jointly constructed in terms of the randomization process
\xi^{\Pi}
and are, thus, dependent. In particular, a solution
X^{\mathbf{h}}
of the grid-sampling limit SDE (
2.13
) cannot be interpreted as the model dynamics evaluated along a
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
-randomized policy, i.e., it cannot be reformulated in the form (
2.3
) for some randomization process
\xi
in general. Nonetheless, we think that the limit SDE (
2.13
) is practically relevant for justifying learning algorithms derived by the first-optimize-then-discretize approach. This aspect will be briefly sketched in
Subsection

2.5
below.

Remark 2.10
.

Suppose that we are in the no-jump case, i.e,
\gamma\equiv 0
.

(1)

Pathwise existence and uniqueness of the grid-sampling limit SDE (
2.13
) follows from Proposition IV-1 in
[
17
]
, provided the coefficients

b_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x,u)=b(t,x,{\mathbf{h}}(t,x,u)),\quad a_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x,u)=a(t,x,{\mathbf{h}}(t,x,u))

are Lipschitz continuous and of linear growth in
x
uniformly in
(t,u)
. Moreover, under these conditions, the law of
X^{\mathbf{h}}
is the unique solution of the martingale problem for the operator

(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{h}}f)(t,x):=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(a_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x,u)a^{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x,u))^{(i,j)}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(b_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x,u))^{(i)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\bigg)\mathrm{d}u.

(2)

By combining
Theorem

2.7
with the stability results for SDEs driven by continuous orthogonal martingale measures in
[
19
, p.354]
, we observe that under at most technical assumptions the following limit theorem is valid: If
\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{K}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
are randomized policies, then one obtains the joint weak convergence

(X^{\Pi_{n},\mathbf{h}_{1}},\ldots,X^{\Pi_{n},\mathbf{h}_{K}},M_{D}^{\Pi_{n}},M_{B^{(1)}}^{\Pi_{n}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}}^{\Pi_{n}})\to(X^{\mathbf{h}_{1}},\ldots,X^{\mathbf{h}_{K}},M_{D},M_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}}).

This result serves as another justification for using the grid-sampling limit SDE (
2.13
).

We leave a detailed study of these aspects in the general case with jumps to future research.

We close this subsection by two examples in which the grid-sampling limit SDE (
2.13
) is simplified. They rely on the following elementary lemma, whose proof is given in
Subsection

A.2
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Suppose that
\eta\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\times[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an
\mathbb{F}
-predictable random field satisfying

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\eta_{t}(u)\eta_{t}(u)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{d}u=I_{m}\quad\mathbb{P}\otimes\bm{\lambda}_{[0,T]}\textrm{-a.e. }(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T].

Define

B^{\eta,(k,l)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\eta^{(k)}_{s}(u)\,M_{B^{(l)}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u),\quad t\in[0,T],\;l=1,\ldots,p,\,k=1,\ldots,m.

Then,
B^{\eta}=(B^{\eta,(k,l)}:l=1,\ldots,p,\,k=1,\ldots,m)
is an
mp
-dimensional Brownian motion.

Example 2.12
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{h}
is a classical, non-randomized control in feedback form, i.e.,
\mathbf{h}
does not depend on
u
. By
Lemma

2.11
(with
\eta
being the
\mathbb{R}
-valued function which is constant 1),

B_{t}^{\bf 1}=\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\,M_{B^{(1)}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u),\ldots,\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\,M_{B^{(p)}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)\bigg)^{\mathsf{T}}

is a
p
-dimensional Brownian motion. Moreover,

N^{\bf 1}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}M_{J}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

is a Poisson random measure independent of
B^{\eta}
with intensity
\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t
. Then, SDE (
2.13
) can be re-written as

\displaystyle X^{\mathbf{h}}_{t}=x

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-}))\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}a(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-}))\mathrm{d}B^{\bf 1}_{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{(0,t]\times\{0<|z|\leq\mathfrak{r}\}}\gamma(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-}),z)\tilde{N}^{\bf 1}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle+\int_{(0,t]\times\{|z|>\mathfrak{r}\}}\gamma(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-}),z)N^{\bf 1}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u),

i.e., we recover the dynamics (
2.1
), as it should be.

Example 2.13
.

We now assume the drift coefficient
b
and the diffusion coefficient
a
are affine-linear in the control, i.e.,

a(t,x,y)=a_{0}(t,x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}y^{(j)}a_{j}(t,x),\quad b(t,x,y)=b_{0}(t,x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}y^{(j)}b_{j}(t,x)

for measurable functions
a_{j}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
b_{j}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The randomized control is given in terms of the measurable function
\mathbf{h}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that the coefficients are sufficiently regular to guarantee that a solution
X^{\mathbf{h}}
to (
2.13
) exists. Supposing that
\mathbf{h}
is square integrable with respect to the uniform distribution in the
u
-variable, we then consider the mean vector and covariance matrix

\mu_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathbf{h}(t,x,u)\mathrm{d}u,\quad\Theta_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}(\mathbf{h}(t,x,u)-\mu_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x))(\mathbf{h}(t,x,u)-\mu_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x))^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{d}u

as a function of
(t,x)
. Assuming that
\Theta_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x)
is positive definite for every
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we write
\vartheta_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x)
for the positive definite matrix root of
\Theta_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x)
and define

\eta_{\mathbf{h}}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},(t,x,u)\mapsto\vartheta_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x)^{-1}(\mathbf{h}(t,x,u)-\mu_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x)).

Note that for every
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\eta_{\mathbf{h}}(t,x,u)\mathrm{d}u=0,\quad\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\eta_{\mathbf{h}}\eta_{\mathbf{h}}^{\mathsf{T}}(t,x,u)\mathrm{d}u=I_{d}.

Thus, the
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
-valued random field

\eta_{t}(u)=(\eta^{(1)}_{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{t-},u),\ldots,\eta^{(d)}_{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{t-},u),1)^{\mathsf{T}}

satisfies the assumptions of
Lemma

2.11
and we denote the corresponding Brownian motion by
B^{\eta}=(B^{\eta,(i,l)})_{i=1,\ldots,d+1,\;l=1,\ldots,p}
. Then, the white noise measures can be replaced by the
(d+1)p
-dimensional Brownian motion
B^{\eta}
and (
2.13
) becomes

\displaystyle X^{\mathbf{h}}_{t}=x

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(b_{0}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-})+\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-})\mu_{\mathbf{h}}^{(j)}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-})\bigg)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(a^{(\cdot,l)}_{0}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-})+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a^{(\cdot,l)}_{j}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-})\mu_{\mathbf{h}}^{(j)}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-})\bigg)\mathrm{d}B^{\eta,(d+1,l)}_{s}

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}a^{(\cdot,l)}_{j}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-})\vartheta_{\mathbf{h}}^{(j,i)}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-})\bigg)\mathrm{d}B^{\eta,(i,l)}_{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{(0,t]\times\{0<|z|\leq\mathfrak{r}\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\gamma(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},u),z)\tilde{M}_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle+\int_{(0,t]\times\{|z|>\mathfrak{r}\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\gamma(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s-},u),z)M_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u).

This example extends the analogous SDE formulation for entropy-regularized mean-variance portfolio optimization with jumps derived in
[
1
]
. Note, however, that the white noise measure approach clarifies that (and how exactly) the driving Brownian motion depends on the choice of the randomized control
{\mathbf{h}}
.

2.4.
Comparison to the exploratory SDE of
[
28
]

In this subsection, we briefly compare the grid-sampling limit SDE (
2.13
) to the exploratory SDE introduced in
[
28
]
. In order to keep the notation simple, we confine ourselves to the one-dimensional case (
m=p=d=1
) without jumps
\gamma=0
, compare
[
28
]
. We note, however, that the multivariate case of the exploratory SDE is covered in
[
14
]
and, recently, a setting with jumps has been developed in
[
6
]
. In any of these cases, the derivation of the exploratory SDE relies on a heuristic law of large number argument to extract the semimartingale characteristics when averaging over independent executions of a relaxed control.

Given a relaxed control
{h}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{P}r(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
with Lebesgue density
\dot{h}(t,x,\cdot)
, the exploratory SDE takes the form

\tilde{X}^{h}_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}b(s,\tilde{X}^{h}_{s},y)\dot{h}(s,\tilde{X}^{h}_{s},y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}}a(s,\tilde{X}^{h}_{s},y)^{2}\dot{h}(s,\tilde{X}^{h}_{s},y)\mathrm{d}y}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}

for some 1-dimensional Brownian motion
W
. Lemma 2 in
[
14
]
states sufficient conditions on
b
,
a
, and
\dot{h}
for existence and uniqueness of a strong solution. Note that the law of
\tilde{X}^{h}
then solves the martingale problem for the operator

(2.14)

(\mathcal{L}_{h}f)(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}a(t,x,y)^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)+b(t,x,y)f^{\prime}(x)\bigg)\dot{h}(t,x,y)\mathrm{d}y.

We now assume that
{\mathbf{h}}
is a randomized control, which executes
h
, and that the assumptions of
Remark

2.10
(1) are satisfied. By a change of variables, the law of the unique solution
X^{\mathbf{h}}
to the grid-sampling limit SDE solves the martingale problem for the same operator
\mathcal{L}_{h}
and, by uniqueness of the martingale problem under the Lipschitz assumptions,
\tilde{X}^{h}
and
X^{\mathbf{h}}
have the same probability law. Hence, in a stochastic control framework (e.g., to compute the expected cost of a given relaxed/randomized control pair
h
,
{\mathbf{h}}
or for the derivation of an HJB equation), the grid-sampling limit SDE
X^{\mathbf{h}}
and the exploratory SDE
\tilde{X}^{h}
will lead to the same result – and it is a matter of taste which one to use. In the first SDE the white noise martingale measure comes up, while, in the second SDE, one has to deal with the square-root in the diffusion coefficient, compare the Remarks in
[
19
, pp. 350–351]
.

However, if one considers several controls at the same time, the joint distribution of
(\tilde{X}^{h_{1}},\tilde{X}^{h_{2}})
and
(X^{\mathbf{h}_{1}},X^{\mathbf{h}_{2}})
may differ, as illustrated by the following simple example.

Example 2.14
.

Suppose
T=1
,
b=0
and
a(t,x,u)=u
. We apply the randomized controls
{\mathbf{h}}_{j}(t,x,u)=\mu_{j}+\sigma_{j}\Phi^{-1}(u)
, (
\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma_{j}>0
,
j=1,2
), which execute a Gaussian law
h_{j}(t,x)
with mean
\mu_{j}
and variance
\sigma_{j}^{2}
independent of the time and state of the system. For a fixed sampling partition
\Pi
, the predictable covariation of the model dynamics along the
\xi^{\Pi}
-randomized controls satisfies

\langle X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}_{1}},X^{\Pi,\mathbf{h}_{2}}\rangle_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(t_{i}-t_{i-1})(\mu_{1}+\sigma_{1}\Phi^{-1}(\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}))(\mu_{2}+\sigma_{2}\Phi^{-1}(\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{i}}))

If, e.g.,
\Pi_{n}
is the equidistant partition of the unit interval into
n
subintervals, then a straightforward application of the strong law of large numbers implies, a.s.,

\langle X^{\Pi_{n},\mathbf{h}_{1}},X^{\Pi_{n},\mathbf{h}_{2}}\rangle_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}\left[(\mu_{1}+\sigma_{1}\Phi^{-1}(\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{1}}))(\mu_{2}+\sigma_{2}\Phi^{-1}(\xi^{\Pi}_{t_{1}}))\right]=\mu_{1}\mu_{2}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}.

This limit coincides with the predictable covariation of the grid-sampling limit SDEs, because, by Proposition I-6(2) in
[
17
]
,

\displaystyle\langle X^{\mathbf{h}_{1}},X^{\mathbf{h}_{2}}\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\int_{(0,\cdot]\times[0,1]^{d}}(\mu_{1}+\sigma_{1}\Phi^{-1}(u))M_{B}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u),\int_{(0,\cdot]\times[0,1]^{d}}(\mu_{2}+\sigma_{2}\Phi^{-1}(u))M_{B}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)\bigg\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,1]\times[0,1]^{d}}(\mu_{1}+\sigma_{1}\Phi^{-1}(u))(\mu_{1}+\sigma_{1}\Phi^{-1}(u))\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}u=\mu_{1}\mu_{2}+\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}.

However, the predictable covariation of the corresponding exploratory SDE is

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{X}^{h_{1}},\tilde{X}^{h_{2}}\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\langle\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}}y^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{1}^{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-(y-\mu_{1})^{2}/(2\sigma_{1}^{2})}\mathrm{d}y}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s},\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}}y^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-(y-\mu_{2})^{2}/(2\sigma_{2}^{2})}\mathrm{d}y}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\Bigg\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\mu_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2})(\mu_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2})}.

Let us summarize: By the considerations at the beginning of this subsection
\tilde{X}^{h}
and
X^{\mathbf{h}}
have the same probability law, if
{\mathbf{h}}
executes
h
. The SDEs governing these two processes cannot be interpreted as dynamics of the system along a
\xi
-randomized control. One way to justify these SDEs is to view them as the limit dynamics of the grid-sampling SDE, which has a sound interpretation in terms of
\xi^{\Pi}
-randomized controls. By
Remark

2.10
(2), we observe that the law of
X^{\Pi_{n},\mathbf{h}}
converges to the law of
\tilde{X}^{h}
under at most technical conditions for one fixed control pair
h
,
{\mathbf{h}}
. However, as illustrated by
Example

2.14
, one cannot hope that the joint convergence result
to the grid-sampling limit SDEs indicated in
Remark

2.10
(2) carries over
to the exploratory SDE. We will illustrate in the next subsection that this difference can be essential for the justification of learning algorithms.

2.5.
Outlook: Towards learning

In this subsection, we exemplify how the algorithms of the first-optimize-then-discretize approach of
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
can be justified by applying the grid-sampling limit SDE (
2.13
) instead of the sample SDE of
[
14
,
15
]
. In this way we can ensure that the derivation bypasses any potential problems related to idealized sampling.

For sake of illustration, we will here only consider the problem of policy evaluation of a fixed randomized control
\mathbf{h}
and restrict ourselves to the no-jump case in dimension one (
m=d=p=1
). Assuming that the Lipschitz conditions in
Remark

2.10
are satisfied, the unique solution of the grid-sampling limit SDE takes the form

\displaystyle X^{\mathbf{h}}_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}b(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},u))\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s+\int_{(0,t]\times[0,1]}a(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},u))M_{B}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u).

We suppose that the law of
\mathbf{h}(t,x,\eta)
(where
\eta
is a uniform random variable on
[0,1]
) is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure with density
\dot{h}(t,x,\cdot)
for every
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
and that its Shannon entropy

-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{h}(t,x,y)\log\dot{h}(t,x,y)\mathrm{d}y

exists in
\mathbb{R}
and is measurable and bounded as a function in
(t,x)
. We consider the problem of evaluating the expected terminal cost with a running entropy-regularization term, which rewards exploration, as suggested in
[
28
]
. The corresponding cost process is given by

\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{h}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}\bigg[g(X^{\mathbf{h}}_{T})+\lambda\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\log\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s\,\bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\bigg]

for some fixed temperature parameter
\lambda>0
. We here assume, for the sake of simplicity, that the terminal cost function
g
is bounded, and, consequently, the process
\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{h}}
is bounded as well. We say that a measurable function
J^{\mathbf{h}}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a version of the
value function
of
{\mathbf{h}}
, if

J^{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{t})=\mathcal{J}^{\mathbf{h}}_{t}\quad\mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.,}\quad t\in[0,T].

The aim of policy evaluation is to learn the value function
J^{\mathbf{h}}
from observations of the system
X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}
, when feeding in the
\xi
-randomized policy
\mathbf{h}(t,x,\xi_{t})
for some randomization process
\xi
, without knowing the true model parameters
b,a
. Recall that in the simplified setting of this subsection

(2.15)

\mathrm{d}X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t}=b(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t},\xi_{t}))\mathrm{d}t+a(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t},{\mathbf{h}}(t,X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{t},\xi_{t}))\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad X^{\xi,\mathbf{h}}_{0}=x.

The algorithms for policy evaluation derived in
[
13
,
14
]
rely on the martingale characterization of the value function
J^{\mathbf{h}}
, which can be formulated for the grid-sampling limit SDE in the following way (see
Subsection

B.1
for the routine proof).

Proposition 2.15
.

(1)

Suppose that the following partial differential equation has a bounded solution
J\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
:

\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}(t,x)+(\mathcal{L}_{h}J(t,\cdot))(t,x)+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{h}(t,x,y)\log\dot{h}(t,x,y)\mathrm{d}y=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R},

with the terminal condition
J(T,\cdot)=g
(where the differential operator
\mathcal{L}_{h}
is defined in (
2.14
)). Then,
J
is a version of the value function of
{\mathbf{h}}
.

(2)

Assume that
\tilde{J}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is measurable with
\tilde{J}(T,\cdot)=g
. Then,
\tilde{J}
is a version of the value function of
{\mathbf{h}}
, if and only if

\tilde{J}(t,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{t})+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\log\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s,\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

is an
\mathbb{F}
-martingale.

We now provide an alternative derivation of the offline variant of the continuous-time TD(0)-algorithm in
[
13
,
14
]
: To this end, fix a parametric class of functions
\{J_{\vartheta}:\vartheta\in\Theta\}
for some open parameter set
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{L}
. We will implicitly assume that the function

{\bf J}_{\Theta}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad(t,x,\vartheta)\mapsto J_{\vartheta}(t,x)

satisfies sufficient smoothness and boundedness assumptions to justify the manipulations below. Moreover, we postulate that
J_{\vartheta}(T,\cdot)=g
for every
\vartheta\in\Theta
. We aim at finding a parameter
\vartheta^{*}\in\Theta
such that
J_{\vartheta^{*}}
is a good approximation to the value function
J^{\mathbf{h}}
of the randomized control
\mathbf{h}
. Since integrals of sufficiently good integrands with respect to a martingale have zero expectation, the martingale characterization of the value function in
Proposition

2.15
motivates to search for a parameter
\vartheta^{*}
such that

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{\vartheta}{\bf J}_{\Theta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},\vartheta^{*})\bigg(\mathrm{d}J_{\vartheta^{*}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},\vartheta^{*})+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\log\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\bigg]=0,

compare
[
13
]
.
Here,
\nabla_{\vartheta}
stands for the gradient in the
\vartheta
-variable. Then, stochastic approximation
[
23
]
suggests to consider the update step

(2.16)

\vartheta\leftarrow\alpha\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{\vartheta}{\bf J}_{\Theta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},\vartheta)\bigg(\mathrm{d}J_{\vartheta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s})+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\log\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

for some step-size
\alpha>0
.
Up to here, the derivation follows exactly the one in
[
13
,
14
]
with the grid-sampling limit SDE in place of the sample SDE of
[
14
]
. Note that, although the unknown coefficients
b
and
a
do not show up in (
2.16
), its implementation is infeasible, because
X^{\mathbf{h}}
is not observable (it is not the response of the system to a
\xi
-randomized control). We view (
2.16
) as an idealized continuous-limit update step, which will be discretized next. By Itô’s formula, recalling that
\mathcal{L}_{h}
in (
2.14
) is the infinitesimal generator of
X^{\mathbf{h}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{\vartheta}{\bf J}_{\Theta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},\vartheta)\bigg(\mathrm{d}J_{\vartheta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s})+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\log\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{\vartheta}{\bf J}_{\Theta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},\vartheta)\frac{\partial J_{\vartheta}}{\partial t}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{\vartheta}{\bf J}_{\Theta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},\vartheta)\bigg((\mathcal{L}_{h}J_{\vartheta}(s,\cdot))(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s})+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\log\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\mathrm{d}y\bigg)\mathrm{d}s

(2.17)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,T]\times[0,1]}\nabla_{\vartheta}{\bf J}_{\Theta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},\vartheta)a(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},u))\frac{\partial J_{\vartheta}}{\partial x}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s})M_{B}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u).

By change of variables and applying the notation introduced in
Remark

2.10
(1), the second integral on the right-hand side of (
2.17
) becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{\vartheta}{\bf J}_{\Theta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},\vartheta)

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{1}{2}a_{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},u)^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}J_{\vartheta}}{\partial x^{2}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s})+b_{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},u)\frac{\partial J_{\vartheta}}{\partial x}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s})

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\log\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},{\mathbf{h}}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},u))\bigg)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s,

which, in fact, is an integral with respect to the limit drift measure
M_{D}
. Thus, the joint convergence in
Remark

2.10
(2) suggests that

\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{\vartheta}{\bf J}_{\Theta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},\vartheta)\bigg(\mathrm{d}J_{\vartheta}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s})+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\log\dot{h}(s,X^{\mathbf{h}}_{s},y)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

can be approximated in law by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}^{\mathcal{X}}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\bigg[\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f_{0}(s,u)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\mathfrak{h}(f_{p+2}(s,z,u))\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt+\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}[\mathfrak{h}(f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|)-f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\bigg]\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle C^{\mathcal{X}}_{t}=\bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}(f^{(k)}_{l}f^{(k^{\prime})}_{l})(s,u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s\bigg)_{k,k^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

\displaystyle\nu^{\mathcal{X}}((s,t]\times A)=\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(f_{p+1}(r,z,u)|z|)\nu_{r}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\hskip 85.0pt+\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(f_{p+2}(r,z,u))\nu_{r}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}r

for
0\leq s<t\leq T
,
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m}_{0})
.

Remark 3.2
.

By a standard approximation argument, the measure
\nu^{\mathcal{X}}
in
Lemma

3.1
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}_{0}}g(y)\nu^{\mathcal{X}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}}[g(f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|)\mathbbm{1}_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}}+g(f_{p+2}(s,z,u))\mathbbm{1}_{\{|z|>R\}}]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s

for any measurable
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
which is non-negative or
g\mathbbm{1}_{[0,T]}
is
\nu^{\mathcal{X}}
-integrable. In particular, for
g(y)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|\geq\kappa\}}
with some
\kappa>0
we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{|y|\geq\kappa}\nu^{\mathcal{X}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{|f_{p+1}(s,z,u)||z|\geq\kappa\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{|f_{p+2}(s,z,u)|\geq\kappa\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|z|>R\}}]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|f_{p+1}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{V};\mathbb{R}^{m})}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{0<|z|\leq R}|z|^{2}\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\|f_{p+2}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{V};\mathbb{R}^{m})}}{\kappa}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{|z|>R}\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle<\infty,

where the finiteness can be derived from (
3.1
) and the inequalities

\mathbbm{1}_{\{|f_{p+1}(s,z,u)||z|\geq\kappa\}}\leq\kappa^{-2}\|f_{p+1}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{V};\mathbb{R}^{m})}^{2}|z|^{2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathbbm{1}_{\{|f_{p+2}(s,z,u)|\geq\kappa\}}\leq\kappa^{-1}\|f_{p+2}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{V};\mathbb{R}^{m})}.

We now turn to
\mathcal{X}^{n}_{\rho_{n}}
, whose modified semimartingale characteristics will be computed in relation to a new filtration, which we construct next. To this end,
we set

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(t):=\sup\{i:t^{n}_{i}\leq t\}\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\},\quad t\in[0,\infty).

Denote
\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n}:=\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t^{n}_{i}}-\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t^{n}_{i-1}}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{n}_{\rho_{n}(t)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(t)}\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n},\quad t\in[0,T].

For
n\geq 1
, we define the discrete-time filtration
(\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{n}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\{\emptyset,\Omega\},\quad\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i}:=\sigma\{\Delta^{n}_{j}\mathcal{X}^{n},j\leq i\},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Then
\{\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n},\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i}:1\leq i\leq n,n\geq 1\}
is an adapted triangular array.
Since
\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n}
is independent of
\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i-1}
, we get for any bounded measurable
g
and
t\in[0,\infty)
that, a.s.,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(t)}\mathbb{E}[g(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})|\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i-1}]=\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(t)}\mathbb{E}[g(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})].

Remark 3.3
.

(1)

By
[
12
, Ch.II, §3b]
, the modified semimartingale characteristics of
\mathcal{X}^{n}_{\rho_{n}}
with respect to the filtration
\mathbb{G}^{\sigma_{n}}=(\mathcal{G}^{n}_{\sigma_{n}(t)})_{t\geq 0}
is the triplet (drift part, modified diffusion part, jump part) which is respectively described by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{h}(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})],

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}}\big(\mathbb{E}[(\mathfrak{h}^{(k)}\mathfrak{h}^{(k^{\prime})})(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})]-\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{h}^{(k)}(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})]\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{h}^{(k^{\prime})}(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})]\big)\bigg)_{k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,m},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}}\mathbb{E}[g(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})],

where
g
runs through a sufficiently large class of test functions vanishing around zero.

(2)

A key difference between
\mathbb{G}^{\sigma_{n}}
and
\mathbb{F}^{\Pi_{n}}
is that information about the random variable
\xi^{\Pi_{n}}_{t_{i}^{n}}
, which is sampled for the randomization on the interval
(t^{n}_{i-1},t^{n}_{i}]
, is only revealed at time
t^{n}_{i}
in the filtration

\mathbb{G}^{\sigma_{n}}
, whereas it is already known at time
t^{n}_{i-1}
in the filtration
\mathbb{F}^{\Pi_{n}}
.

The following proposition plays the key role for deriving the convergence of the semimartingale characteristics.

Proposition 3.4
.

For any
g\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, one has

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg|\mathbb{E}[g(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})]-g(0)-\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\Psi_{f}(g)(s)\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,

where the function
\Psi_{f}(g)\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle\Psi_{f}(g)(s)

\displaystyle:=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\bigg(\nabla g(0)^{\mathsf{T}}f_{0}(s,u)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{m}\partial^{2}_{k,k^{\prime}}g(0)\sum_{l=1}^{p}(f^{(k)}_{l}f^{(k^{\prime})}_{l})(s,u)\bigg)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\big[g(f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|)-g(0)-|z|\nabla g(0)^{\mathsf{T}}f_{p+1}(s,z,u)\big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u

(3.13)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\big[g(f_{p+2}(s,z,u))-g(0)\big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u.

Consequently, for any
t\in[0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(t)}\mathbb{E}[g(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})]\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}g(0)+\int_{0}^{t\wedge T}\Psi_{f}(g)(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

Step 1.
It is obvious that
\Psi_{f}(g)
is measurable by Fubini’s theorem, and moreover, there exists a constant
c_{T,m}>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}|\Psi_{f}(g)(s)|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq c_{T,m}\bigg(\|f_{0}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{U};\mathbb{R}^{m})}|\nabla g(0)|+\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{m}|\partial^{2}_{k,k^{\prime}}g(0)|\sum_{l=1}^{p}\|f^{(k)}_{l}f^{(k^{\prime})}_{l}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{U})}

\displaystyle\quad+\|f_{p+1}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{V};\mathbb{R}^{m})}^{2}\|\nabla^{2}g\|_{B_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R}^{m\times m})}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}|z|^{2}\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+2\|g\|_{B_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

\displaystyle<\infty,

Next, for
n\geq 1,i=1,\ldots,n
, we define the càdlàg and
\mathbb{F}^{\Pi_{n}}
-adapted process
F^{n,i}=(F^{n,i}_{t})_{t\in[t^{n}_{i-1},t^{n}_{i}]}
null at
t^{n}_{i-1}
by setting, for
t\in(t^{n}_{i-1},t^{n}_{i}]
,

\displaystyle F^{n,i}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t}f_{0}(s,\xi^{n}_{i})\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t}f_{l}(s,\xi^{n}_{i})\mathrm{d}B^{(l)}_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t}\int_{0<|z|\leq R}f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})|z|\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)+\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t}\int_{|z|>R}f_{p+2}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})N(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z).

Let
s\in(0,T]
be now fixed. Then, for any
n\geq 1
, there exists uniquely
1\leq i(s,n)\leq n
such that

\displaystyle s\in(t^{n}_{i(s,n)-1},t^{n}_{i(s,n)}]\quad\textrm{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}t^{n}_{i(s,n)-1}=\lim_{n\to\infty}t^{n}_{i(s,n)}=s.

We claim that

\displaystyle F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{L}^{1}(\mathbb{P})}0\quad\textrm{as }n\to\infty.

It is straightforward to check when
n\to\infty
that, in the representation of
F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s}
, the Lebesgue integral part tends to
0
in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P})
as
f_{0}
is bounded, the martingale part converges to
0
in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P})
by applying Itô’s isometry and using the boundedness of
f_{l}
,
l=1,\ldots,p+1
. For the “large jump part”, since
\nu_{r}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}r
is the predictable compensator of
N(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z)
, together with (
3.1
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{t^{n}_{i(s,n)-1}}^{s}\int_{|z|>R}f_{p+2}(r,z,\xi^{n}_{i(s,n)})N(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z)\bigg|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{p+2}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{V};\mathbb{R}^{m})}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t^{n}_{i(s,n)-1}}^{s}\int_{|z|>R}N(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\|f_{p+2}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{V};\mathbb{R}^{m})}\int_{t^{n}_{i(s,n)-1}}^{s}\int_{|z|>R}\nu_{r}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}r\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,

which verifies the claim. Since
\mathbb{E}[N(\{s\}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0})]=\nu(\{s\}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0})=0
, it holds that
F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s}=F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s-}
a.s.,
and hence,

(3.14)

\displaystyle F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s-}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{L}^{1}(\mathbb{P})}0\quad\textrm{as }n\to\infty.

Step 2.
Using Itô’s formula for
F^{n,i}
and
g\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
(see, e.g.,
[
18
, Theorem 2.5]
) we get, a.s.,

\displaystyle g(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})=g\big(F^{n,i}_{t^{n}_{i}}\big)

\displaystyle=g(0)+\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\nabla g(F^{n,i}_{s-})^{\mathsf{T}}f_{0}(s,\xi^{n}_{i})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\nabla g(F^{n,i}_{s-})^{\mathsf{T}}f_{l}(s,\xi^{n}_{i})\mathrm{d}B^{(l)}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\partial^{2}_{k,k^{\prime}}g(F^{n,i}_{s-})(f^{(k)}_{l}f^{(k^{\prime})}_{l})(s,\xi^{n}_{i})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{0<|z|\leq R}\Big[g\big(F^{n,i}_{s-}+f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})|z|\big)-g(F^{n,i}_{s-})\Big]\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{0<|z|\leq R}\Big[g\big(F^{n,i}_{s-}+f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})|z|\big)-g(F^{n,i}_{s-})-|z|\nabla g(F^{n,i}_{s-})^{\mathsf{T}}f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})\Big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{|z|>R}\Big[g\big(F^{n,i}_{s-}+f_{p+2}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})\big)-g(F^{n,i}_{s-})\Big]N(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z).

Since
\nabla g
and
f_{l}
are bounded for any
l=1,\ldots,p+1
, the integrals with respect to the Brownian motions and the compensated random measure are square integrable martingales which vanish after taking the expectation
\mathbb{E}
. Let us now investigate the remaining parts.

\bullet

The “drift part”:
Using Fubini’s theorem and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\nabla g(F^{n,i}_{s-})^{\mathsf{T}}f_{0}(s,\xi^{n}_{i})\mathrm{d}s\bigg]-\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\nabla g(0)^{\mathsf{T}}f_{0}(s,u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s\bigg|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\nabla g(F^{n,i}_{s-})^{\mathsf{T}}f_{0}(s,\xi^{n}_{i})\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\nabla g(0)^{\mathsf{T}}f_{0}(s,\xi^{n}_{i})\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{0}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{U};\mathbb{R}^{m})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\mathbb{E}[|\nabla g(F^{n,i}_{s-})-\nabla g(0)|]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\|f_{0}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{U};\mathbb{R}^{m})}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\nabla g(F^{n,i}_{s-})-\nabla g(0)|\mathbbm{1}_{(t^{n}_{i-1},t^{n}_{i}]}(s)\bigg]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\|f_{0}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{U};\mathbb{R}^{m})}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\nabla g\big(F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s-}\big)-\nabla g(0)\big|\big]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,

where we apply the dominated convergence theorem using (
3.14
) together with the continuity and boundedness of
\nabla g
. Analogously, for
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,m
and
l=1,\ldots,p
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\partial^{2}_{k,k^{\prime}}g(F^{n,i}_{s-})(f^{(k)}_{l}f^{(k^{\prime})}_{l})(s,\xi^{n}_{i})\mathrm{d}s\bigg]-\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\partial^{2}_{k,k^{\prime}}g(0)(f^{(k)}_{l}f^{(k^{\prime})}_{l})(s,u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

\bullet

The “small jump part”:
For
i(n,s)
introduced in
Step 1
one has

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{0<|z|\leq R}\Big[g\big(F^{n,i}_{s-}+f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})|z|\big)-g(F^{n,i}_{s-})-|z|\nabla g(F^{n,i}_{s-})^{\mathsf{T}}f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})\Big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\Big[g(f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|)-g(0)-|z|\nabla g(0)^{\mathsf{T}}f_{p+1}(s,z,u)\Big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{0<|z|\leq R}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big|g\big(F^{n,i}_{s-}+f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})|z|\big)-g(F^{n,i}_{s-})-|z|\nabla g(F^{n,i}_{s-})^{\mathsf{T}}f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad-g(f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})|z|)+g(0)+|z|\nabla g(0)^{\mathsf{T}}f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})\Big|\mathbbm{1}_{(t^{n}_{i-1},t^{n}_{i}]}(s)\bigg]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{0<|z|\leq R}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|g\big(F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s-}+f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i(s,n)})|z|\big)-g\big(F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s-}\big)-|z|\nabla g\big(F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s-}\big)^{\mathsf{T}}f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i(s,n)})

\displaystyle\qquad-g(f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i(s,n)})|z|)+g(0)+|z|\nabla g(0)^{\mathsf{T}}f_{p+1}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i(s,n)})\Big|\Big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=:\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{0<|z|\leq R}\mathbb{E}[G^{\textrm{S}}_{n}(s,z)]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s.

Using Taylor’s expansion we obtain a constant
c_{m}>0
depending only on
m
such that

\displaystyle G^{\textrm{S}}_{n}(s,z)\leq c_{m}\|\nabla^{2}g\|_{B_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R}^{m\times m})}\|f_{p+1}\|_{B_{b}(\mathbf{V};\mathbb{R}^{m})}^{2}|z|^{2}.

Hence, it is easy to check using (
3.14
) and dominated convergence that
\mathbb{E}[G^{\textrm{S}}_{n}(s,z)]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for any
s,z
. Due to (
3.1
), dominated convergence also yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{0<|z|\leq R}\mathbb{E}[G^{\textrm{S}}_{n}(s,z)]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

\bullet

The “large jump part”:

Since
\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s
is the predictable compensator of
N(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)
, using Fubini’s theorem, again, for interchanging integrals we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{|z|>R}\Big[g\big(F^{n,i}_{s-}+f_{p+2}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})\big)-g(F^{n,i}_{s-})\Big]N(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\big[g(f_{p+2}(s,z,u))-g(0)\big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{|z|>R}\Big[g\big(F^{n,i}_{s-}+f_{p+2}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i})\big)-g(F^{n,i}_{s-})\Big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\int_{|z|>R}\big[g(f_{p+2}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i}))-g(0)\big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{|z|>R}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|g\big(F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s-}+f_{p+2}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i(s,n)})\big)-g\big(F^{n,i(s,n)}_{s-}\big)-g(f_{p+2}(s,z,\xi^{n}_{i(s,n)}))+g(0)\Big|\Big]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=:\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{|z|>R}\mathbb{E}[G^{\textrm{L}}_{n}(s,z)]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s.

It is obvious that
G^{\textrm{L}}_{n}
is uniformly bounded by
4\|g\|_{B_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}
. Moreover, using the Lipschitzian of
g
, the boundedness of
f_{p+2}
and (
3.14
) and (

), we may apply the dominated convergence theorem to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{|z|>R}\mathbb{E}[G^{\textrm{L}}_{n}(s,z)]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}s\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Combining the arguments above yields (
3.12
). The consequence follows from
\int_{\rho_{n}(t)}^{t}|\Psi_{f}(g)(s)|\mathrm{d}s\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

We apply

Lemma 3.5
.

For
\mathfrak{b}^{\mathcal{X}}
in

3.1
and any

,

(3.15)

\displaystyle I_{\eqref{eq:limit-drift}}:=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(s)}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{h}(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})]-\mathfrak{b}^{\mathcal{X}}_{s\wedge T}\bigg|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Proof.

It is sufficient to verify the convergence for any
k
-th coordinate,
k=1,\ldots,m
and
t\in[0,T]
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}^{\mathcal{X},(k)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{f}(\mathfrak{h}^{(k)})(s)\mathrm{d}s

for
\Psi_{f}(\mathfrak{h}^{(k)})
associated with
\mathfrak{h}^{(k)}
introduced in

3.4
.
Then we get

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(s)}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{h}^{(k)}(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})]-\mathfrak{b}^{\mathcal{X},(k)}_{s}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(s)}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{h}^{(k)}(\Delta^{n}_{i}\mathcal{X}^{n})]-\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(s)}\int_{t^{n}_{i-1}}^{t^{n}_{i}}\Psi_{f}(\mathfrak{h}^{(k)})(r)\mathrm{d}r\bigg|+\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\bigg|\int_{\rho_{n}(s)}^{s}\Psi_{f}(\mathfrak{h}^{(k)})(r)\mathrm{d}r\bigg|

Background on martingale measures and proofs for Subsection

Suppose

and let
\mathbb{I}=[0,T]
or
\mathbb{I}=[0,\infty)
. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions with
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{I}}
. Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{F}}
the predictable
\sigma
-field on
\Omega\times\mathbb{I}
associated with the filtration

.

A.1.
Background on martingale measures

Assume that
(E,d_{E})
is a complete and separable metric space equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}(E)
.

Definition A.1

(
[
31
,
17
]
)
.

Assume
M\colon\Omega\times\mathbb{I}\times\mathcal{B}(E)\to\mathbb{R}
.

(1)

M
is an
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
-martingale measure
on
\mathbb{I}\times\mathcal{B}(E)
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(a)

For
A\in\mathcal{B}(E)
,
(M(t,A))_{t\in\mathbb{I}}
is an
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-martingale adapted with
\mathbb{F}
and
M(0,A)=0
;

(b)

For
t\in\mathbb{I}
and disjoint
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(E)
, one has
M(t,A\cup B)=M(t,A)+M(t,B)
a.s.;

(c)

There exists a non-decreasing sequence
(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(E)
such that

(i)

\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{n}=E
;

(ii)

For any
t\in\mathbb{I}
,
\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}(E_{n})}\|M(t,A)\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P})}<\infty
;

(iii)

For any
t\in\mathbb{I}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, one has
\|M(t,A_{k})\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{P})}\to 0
for all decreasing sequence
(A_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(E_{n})
with
\cap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}A_{k}=\emptyset
.

(2)

An
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
-martingale measure
M
is said to be
orthogonal
if
M(\cdot,A)M(\cdot,B)
is an
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
-martingale whenever
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(E)
with
A\cap B=\emptyset
.

(3)

An
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
-martingale measure
M
is said to be
continuous
if
\mathbb{I}\ni t\mapsto M(t,A)
is continuous for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(E)
.

It is obvious that, for a given
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
, a martingale measure on
[0,\infty)\times\mathcal{B}(E)
is also a martingale measure on
[0,T]\times\mathcal{B}(E)
by the restriction on
[0,T]
. Conversely, if
M
is a martingale measure on
[0,T]\times\mathcal{B}(E)
, then
\hat{M}(t,\cdot):=M(t\wedge T,\cdot)
is an
(\hat{\mathbb{F}},\mathbb{P})
-martingale measure on
[0,\infty)\times\mathcal{B}(E)
, where
\hat{\mathbb{F}}=(\mathcal{F}_{t\wedge T})_{t\geq 0}
.

It is indicated by Walsh
[
31
]
(see also
[
17
, Theorem I-4]
) that if an
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
-martingale measure
M
is orthogonal, then there is a random positive finite measure
\mu_{M}
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{I}\times E)
, which is
\mathbb{F}
-predictable (i.e.
(\mu_{M}((0,t]\times A))_{t\in\mathbb{I}}
is
\mathbb{F}
-predictable for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(E)
), such that

\displaystyle\mu_{M}((0,t]\times A)=\left<M(\cdot,A)\right>_{t}\quad\mathbb{P}\textrm{-a.s.,}\quad\forall(t,A)\in\mathbb{I}\times\mathcal{B}(E).

The measure
\mu_{M}
is then called the
intensity measure
of
M
. Moreover, for
t\in\mathbb{I}
,
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(E)
,

\displaystyle\left<M(\cdot,A),M(\cdot,B)\right>_{t}=\left<M(\cdot,A\cap B)\right>_{t}=\mu_{M}((0,t]\times(A\cap B))\quad\mathbb{P}\textrm{-a.s.}

Let us briefly recall the construction of stochastic integrals driving by an orthogonal martingale measure
M
following the Itô’s approach (see
[
17
,
31
]
). Define

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{F},\mu_{M})

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{H:\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{F}}\otimes\mathcal{B}(E)/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\textrm{-measurable}\,\bigg|\,\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{I}\times E}H(t,x)^{2}\mu_{M}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}x)\bigg]<\infty\bigg\}.

For a simple function
H(\omega,t,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i-1}(\omega)\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{i-1},t_{i}]}(t)\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}(x)
where
A_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(E)
,

,

is bounded and

A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m}_{0})
by using the approximation sequence
(A\cap B_{m}(\frac{1}{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
For the predictable finite variation part
\mathfrak{b}^{\mathcal{X}}
, one has, a.s.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{X}_{t}-\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f_{l}(s,u)M_{B^{(l)}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)-\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}_{0}}(y-\mathfrak{h}(y))N_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f_{0}(s,u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|\tilde{M}_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{p+2}(s,z,u)M_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}[f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|-\mathfrak{h}(f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|)]M_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}[f_{p+2}(s,z,u)-\mathfrak{h}(f_{p+2}(s,z,u))]M_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f_{0}(s,u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\mathfrak{h}(f_{p+2}(s,z,u))\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}[f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|-\mathfrak{h}(f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|)]\nu_{s}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|\tilde{M}_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{|z|>R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}\mathfrak{h}(f_{p+2}(s,z,u))\tilde{M}_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\{0<|z|\leq R\}\times[0,1]^{d}}[f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|-\mathfrak{h}(f_{p+1}(s,z,u)|z|)]\tilde{M}_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u),

where in the last equality we use the fact that
\int F\tilde{M}_{J}=\int FM_{J}-\int F\mu_{J}
if
F
is predictable and
\mu_{J}
-integrable, see
[
12
, Proposition II.1.28]
. By identifying the predictable finite variation component of
\mathcal{Y}
, we obtain the desired expression of
\mathfrak{b}^{\mathcal{X}}
.

B.3.
On the Poisson random measure
M_{J}

Assume the Lévy process as defined in (
2.9
). Let
\{T^{n}_{j}\}_{n,j\geq 0}
be the of jump times of
L
given by

\displaystyle T^{0}_{0}:=0,\quad T^{0}_{j}:=\inf\{t>T^{0}_{j-1}:|\Delta L_{t}|>1\},\quad j\geq 1,

\displaystyle T^{n}_{0}:=0,\quad T^{n}_{j}:=\inf\{t>T^{n}_{j-1}:1/(n+1)<|\Delta L_{t}|\leq 1/n\},\quad j\geq 1,\quad n\geq 1.

Let
\{\xi^{n}_{j}\}_{n,j\geq 0}
be i.i.d. with uniform distribution on
[0,1]^{d}
. Assume that
\{\xi^{n}_{j}\}_{n,j\geq 0}
is independent of
L
. We define the Poisson random measure
M_{J}
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}
by

\displaystyle M_{J}(\omega,\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\delta_{(T^{n}_{j}(\omega),\Delta L_{T^{n}_{j}(\omega)}(\omega),\xi^{n}_{j}(\omega))}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u).

We note that, in general, there is no semimartingale which possesses
M_{J}
as the associated random jump measure because
\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{0<|z|^{2}+|u|^{2}\leq 1}(|z|^{2}+|u|^{2})\mu_{J}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)
might be infinite, except the case
\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}}\nu_{t}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{d}t<\infty
(i.e.
L
is of finite activities).

B.4.
On the independence of
(M_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}})
and
M_{J}

Assume that
(M_{B^{(1)}},\ldots,M_{B^{(p)}})
and
M_{J}
define on the same probability space, then

\bigg\{\int_{(0,T]\times[0,1]^{d}}g_{l}(s,u)M_{B^{(l)}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u)\,\bigg|\,g_{l}\colon[0,T]\times[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\textrm{ measurable and bounded},l=1,\ldots,p\bigg\}

is independent of

\bigg\{\int_{(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}}h(s,z,u)M_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u)\,\bigg|\,h\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{0}\times[0,1]^{d}\to[0,\infty)\textrm{ measurable}\bigg\}.

Indeed, it is sufficient to show that
G=(\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}g_{l}(s,u)M_{B^{(l)}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}u))_{t\in[0,T]}
is independent of
H=(\int_{(0,t]\times\{|z|>\kappa\}\times[0,1]^{d}}h(s,z,u)M_{J}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}u))_{t\in[0,T]}
for all (non-random) measurable and bounded
g_{l}
,
h\geq 0
and
\kappa>0
. It is clear that
H
is of finite variation and
G
is a continuous martingale (see
[
17
, Section II(3)]
), and both are processes with independent increments. Observe that
[G,H]_{t}=\sum_{0\leq s\leq t}\Delta G_{s}\Delta H_{s}=0
for
t\in[0,T]
a.s. It then follows from
[
10
, Theorem 11.43]
that
G
and
H
are independent.

Appendix C
Weak convergence in the Skorokhod topology and Jacod–Shiryaev’s limit theorem for triangular arrays

C.1.
Skorokhod spaces and weak convergence

Fix
T\in(0,\infty)
and let
\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
be the family of all càdlàg functions
f\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\Lambda_{T}
consists of all strictly increasing and continuous
\lambda\colon[0,T]\to[0,T]
with
\lambda(0)=0
,
\lambda(T)=T
. We equip
\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
with the Skorokhod metric

\displaystyle d^{m}_{T}(x,y):=\inf_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{T}}\max\bigg\{\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\bigg|\log\frac{\lambda(t)-\lambda(s)}{t-s}\bigg|,\;\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|x(t)-y(\lambda(t))|\bigg\}.

It is well-known that
(\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m}),d^{m}_{T})
is a complete and separable metric space (see
[
2
, Section 14]
), however, it is not a topological vector space. It is also convenient to work with the metric
\tilde{d}^{m}_{T}
, which defines the same topology as
d^{m}_{T}
does, given by

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{m}_{T}(x,y):=\inf_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{T}}\max\bigg\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\lambda(t)-t|,\;\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|x(t)-y(\lambda(t))|\bigg\}.

However,
(\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m}),\tilde{d}^{m}_{T})
is not complete.

An
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued càdlàg process
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
can be regarded as an
\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m}))
-measurable function
X\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m}))
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra induced by the Skorokhod metric
d^{m}_{T}
. A sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued càdlàg processes
(X^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
X^{n}
is defined on
(\Omega^{n},\mathcal{F}^{n},\mathbb{P}^{n})
, is said to be
weakly convergent
to a càdlàg process
X
defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})

if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{n}[f(X^{n})]\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[f(X)],\quad\forall f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{D}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{m})),

where
\mathbb{E}^{n}
and
\mathbb{E}
are the expectation under
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
\mathbb{P}
, respectively. We then write

X^{n}\xrightarrow{\mathscr{D}_{T}}X
.

C.2.
A limit theorem of Jacod–Shiryaev for triangular arrays

For the reader’s convenience, we recall (and adapt to our setting) a limit theorem establishing the weak convergence of triangular arrays which we use to prove the main result in this article.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space and suppose that
\{U^{n}_{i},\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i}:i\geq 0\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are adapted sequences of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variables. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider a change of time
\sigma_{n}\colon\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with respect to
(\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
, i.e.,

(1)

\sigma_{n}(\cdot,0)=0
;

(2)

For any
\omega
,
\sigma_{n}(\omega,\cdot)
is increasing, right-continuous, with jumps equal to 1;

(3)

For any
t\geq 0
,
\sigma_{n}(\cdot,t)
is a
(\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
-stopping time.

Theorem C.1

(
[
12
]
, Theorem VIII.2.29)
.

Assume a sequence of
d
-dimensional semimartingales
(X^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
where
X^{n}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(t)}U^{n}_{i}
,
t\geq 0
. Let
X
be a
d
-dimensional process with independent increments and without fixed time of discontinuity, having characteristics
(\mathfrak{b},C,\nu)
in relation to a truncation function
\mathfrak{h}
. Set

\widetilde{C}_{t}^{(k,l)}:=C_{t}^{(k,l)}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathfrak{h}^{(k)}\mathfrak{h}^{(l)})(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}y)
as in
[
12
, II.5.8]
. If there exists some dense subset
D
of
[0,\infty)
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(s)}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{h}(U^{n}_{i})|\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i-1}]-\mathfrak{b}_{s}\bigg|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0\quad\forall t\geq 0,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(t)}\Big(\mathbb{E}[(\mathfrak{h}^{(k)}\mathfrak{h}^{(l)})(U^{n}_{i})|\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i-1}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{h}^{(k)}(U^{n}_{i})|\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i-1}]\,\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{h}^{(l)}(U^{n}_{i})|\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i-1}]\Big)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\widetilde{C}^{(k,l)}_{t}\quad\forall t\in D,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\sigma_{n}(t)}\mathbb{E}[g(U^{n}_{i})|\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i-1}]\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)\nu(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}y)\quad\forall t\in D,g\in C_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

then

converges weakly to

in the Skorokhod topology on the space
\mathbb{D}_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of càdlàg functions

. Here,

[
12

.
