Preinfusion Glucocorticoid Reduction and Elimination in Patients With Uncontrolled Gout on Pegloticase and Methotrexate: A Case Series
Authors: John Albert, Zana Vranic, Brian LaMoreaux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Pegloticase, an infused pegylated uricase enzyme, rapidly lowers serum urate (SU) in patients with uncontrolled gout. Preinfusion intravenous glucocorticoids (GCs) are typically administered before pegloticase to reduce risks of infusion reactions (IRs); however, they may exacerbate comorbidities in this patient population, and thus minimizing GC exposure is desirable. This study examined preinfusion GC dose reduction and elimination in patients with uncontrolled gout receiving pegloticase and methotrexate (MTX) co-therapy.Methods:This retrospective case series of medical data (January 1, 2020, to April 1, 2023) from 1 rheumatology practice included patients receiving pegloticase (8 mg every 2 wk) with MTX co-therapy and undergoing preinfusion methylprednisolone dose reduction and elimination. The percentage of patients with SU <6 mg/dL (pegloticase responders) was assessed at 6 months and study completion; safety was assessed by frequency and severity of adverse events.Results:Twelve patients with multiple comorbidities were evaluated (male, 92%; White, 58%; mean [SD] age, 60.3 [11.0] years; mean [SD] baseline SU, 8.8 [1.9] mg/dL). Patients received a mean (SD) of 14.3 (4.2) pegloticase infusions and discontinued GCs after a mean (SD) of 7.6 (1.3) infusions. At 24 weeks, 10/12 (83%) patients were responders and 9/12 (75%) continued to respond following GC reduction and elimination; 3/12 (25%) had an SU rise and discontinued pegloticase. The most common adverse event was gout flare (10/12 [83%] patients); no patients experienced IRs.Conclusions:This study demonstrated that preinfusion GC reduction and elimination are possible in patients with uncontrolled gout receiving pegloticase and MTX co-therapy.

Gout is a systemic, inflammatory arthritis defined by the accumulation of monosodium urate crystal deposits in joints, leading to inflammation and pain.1 The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology guidelines for gout recommend that patients on urate-lowering therapies (ULTs) achieve and maintain a target serum urate (SU) level of <6 mg/dL to improve gout signs and symptoms and reduce the rate of acute gout flares.2–4 Patients with uncontrolled gout, including those refractory or intolerant to conventional oral ULTs, have a higher disease burden than patients whose gout is controlled and suffer from comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, and renal disease.5

Pegloticase is an infused pegylated uricase enzyme that rapidly lowers SU in patients with uncontrolled gout.6 Patients on pegloticase therapy experienced near-complete urate-deposit depletion and a marked reduction in gout flares during 1 year of treatment and resolution of ≥1 tophus during 6 months of treatment.7,8 Preinfusion prophylaxis, including intravenous (IV) glucocorticoids (GCs), is included in the pegloticase label and typically administered before each pegloticase infusion to reduce the likelihood of infusion reactions (IRs).6 However, the use of GCs in patients with uncontrolled gout is generally discouraged due to their potential to exacerbate specific preexisting comorbidities common in gout patients, such as hypertension, diabetes, and heart disease.9,10

Weekly methotrexate (MTX) co-administration is recommended for patients receiving pegloticase treatment for uncontrolled gout.6 As demonstrated in the MIRROR randomized, controlled trial (RCT), MTX co-administration with pegloticase decreases immunogenicity (antidrug antibody [ADA] development), thus increasing SU-lowering response rates and decreasing IR risks.11,12 These benefits of MTX co-administration raise the question of whether preinfusion GCs are still necessary to reduce the risk of IRs.

In this case series, we retrospectively examined the medical records of a community rheumatology practice implementing systematic preinfusion GC dose reduction and discontinuation in patients with uncontrolled gout receiving pegloticase and MTX co-therapy.

METHODS

Study Design and Patients

This was a retrospective chart review of 1 community rheumatology practice using medical record data of all patients with uncontrolled gout—defined by the 2020 ACR guidelines for gout management2 —receiving pegloticase co-administered with MTX between January 1, 2020, and April 1, 2023. Patients were included if they received pegloticase (8 mg once every 2 wk) with MTX co-therapy and underwent preinfusion GC dose reduction at any point during treatment. Patient demographics, baseline gout characteristics, baseline SU, and comorbidities were collected.

This retrospective, noninterventional, observational study was reviewed and approved by the Western Institutional Review Board Copernicus Group (WCG; Puyallup, WA; approval #1370161). As all data were existing and deidentified, this study did not meet the US definition of human subject research, as defined in the US Code of Federal Regulations (45 CFR 46 | HHS.gov) and per the US National Institutes of Health determination tool.13 As a result, the study was assigned exempt status, waiving the requirement for informed consent. The treating physician and patient discussed the risks and benefits of preinfusion GC reduction before initiation and jointly decided whether to proceed.

Treatments and Assessments

All patients on pegloticase therapy initially received standard IR prophylaxis of 125-mg IV methylprednisolone to reduce the risk of IRs; the GC dose was gradually reduced to 62.5 mg, 31.25 mg, and then 0 mg, beginning after the third or fourth pegloticase infusion, at the discretion of the treating physician. All patients received prophylactic oral fexofenadine 180 mg the night before or the day of each pegloticase infusion. All patients began receiving weekly, subcutaneous MTX before the first pegloticase infusion (10 mg, n=2; 15 mg, n=10; see Table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/RHU/A919, which provides information on the treatment and disposition of patients in the study). Patients discontinued pegloticase upon complete healing of the skin, resolution of subcutaneous tophi of the hands and feet, and maintenance of an SU level of 0 mg/dL and were reinstated on oral ULT if this treatment was tolerable. If the SU stopping rule (SU >6 mg/dL at 2 consecutive visits) was met, patients discontinued early from pegloticase treatment.

Pegloticase efficacy during GC dose reduction was assessed weekly by SU levels; the percentage of patients with SU <6 mg/dL (pegloticase response rate) after 6 months of GC dose reduction and at the final pegloticase infusion (study completion) is reported. Patients who received ≥12 pegloticase infusions with SU <6 mg/dL were categorized as completing treatment. Safety was assessed through the frequency and severity of adverse events (AEs), including IR rates. Gout flare severity was determined by clinical examination and patient-reported pain; flares were considered severe if joints were acutely swollen, red, and hot and patient pain was severe. All patients were monitored during the study through clinical examinations and laboratory testing. Data are presented as n (%) or mean (SD) as appropriate.

RESULTS

Patient Demographics and Disposition

A total of 12 patients were included in the study, a majority of whom were male (92%) and White (58%); mean (SD) age and baseline body mass index were 60.3 (11.0) years and 29.6 (7.5) kg/m2, respectively (Table 1). Previous oral ULTs included febuxostat (80 mg, n=2; 40 mg, n=1) and allopurinol (300 mg, n=5; 100 mg, n=1; dose not available, n=2; 1 patient did not receive prior oral ULT). Patients were prescribed pegloticase due to persistent gout flares and tophi causing pain and mobility issues, which were not managed by prior oral ULTs. At baseline, mean (SD) SU and gout duration were 8.8 (1.9) mg/dL and 13.9 (8.9) years, respectively, and a majority of patients (11/12, 92%) had visible tophi (Table 1). As reported in the patient medical records, the most common comorbidities were hypertension (58%), hyperlipidemia (33%), osteoarthritis (25%), and atrial fibrillation (25%; Table 1). The mean (SD) duration between the first MTX dose and first pegloticase infusion was 25.3 (6.5) days (3.6 [0.9] weeks). Patients received a mean (SD) of 14.3 (4.2) pegloticase infusions over a mean (SD) treatment duration of 198.3 (65.1) days (28.3 [9.3] weeks; see Table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/RHU/A919).

GC Dose Reduction and Pegloticase Efficacy With MTX Co-Therapy

Preinfusion GC dose reduction began at infusion 5 for 11/12 (92%) patients and infusion 6 for 1/12 (8%) patients; 8/12 patients discontinued GCs at infusion 7; 1 patient each discontinued GCs at infusions 8, 9, and 11; and 1 patient reduced but did not discontinue GCs during the study (Fig. 1A). At 6 months (24 wk, 12 pegloticase infusions), the response rate to pegloticase with GC reduction and elimination was 83% (10/12 patients completing therapy). By study completion, 9/12 (75%) patients continued to respond for an additional mean (SD) 11.7 (2.2) pegloticase infusions after initiating GC dose reduction (see Table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/RHU/A919; Fig. 1A). After discontinuing pegloticase, 10/12 patients initiated oral ULT (febuxostat 40 mg, n=2 [1 patient switched to 80 mg]; febuxostat 80 mg, n=1; allopurinol 100 mg, n=2; allopurinol 300 mg, n=5). A total of 3/12 (25%) patients (patients 01, 04, and 09) had an SU rise at some point following GC reduction and discontinued pegloticase (see Table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/RHU/A919; Fig. 1B). Patient 01 completed treatment and discontinued after 17 pegloticase infusions per the SU stopping rule; preinfusion GCs were reinitiated at the time of SU increase to reduce SU levels, but the patient did not achieve an SU-lowering response. Patient 04 voluntarily discontinued pegloticase after infusion 6 with 1 SU measurement > 6 mg/dL; patient 09 discontinued pegloticase prematurely after 7 infusions per the SU stopping rule. All 3 patients received 15-mg MTX weekly while on pegloticase therapy and initiated oral ULT following pegloticase discontinuation (patient 01, febuxostat 40 mg, increased to 80 mg; patient 04, allopurinol 300 mg; patient 09, febuxostat 80 mg). These patients did not develop new tophi and had their existing tophi resolve despite the SU rise and pegloticase discontinuation. In addition, all 3 patients had multiple comorbidities (Table 1).

Adverse Events

At least 1 AE was reported in 11/12 (92%) patients (Table 2). The most common AE was gout flare in 10/12 (83%) patients. A total of 57 flares were reported during the study: 42% were mild, 25% were moderate, and 33% were severe. Patients experienced gout flares both before and after GC elimination. One patient (patient 01) experienced a major cerebrovascular accident (CVA) and regained all function without any residual effects; notably, this patient had comorbidities of CVA and hypertension. A second patient (patient 12) experienced facial drooping, unrelated to treatment, which resolved with oral prednisone and did not prevent completion of pegloticase treatment. Among the 3 patients who discontinued pegloticase due to SU rise, patient 01 experienced the aforementioned CVA and 2 gout flares, patient 04 experienced no AEs, and patient 09 experienced a diaphoretic episode with nausea. No patient experienced an IR.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective chart review evaluated gradual dose reduction and subsequent elimination of GCs administered before pegloticase and MTX co-therapy in patients with uncontrolled gout. After a 6-month response rate of 83% with pegloticase and MTX treatment and GC reduction/elimination, 75% of patients in the study ultimately maintained SU lowering following preinfusion GC reduction and elimination. However, 3/12 patients had an SU rise >6 mg/dL that appeared to coincide with GC reduction. Notably, the 75% response rate we observed after GC reduction is similar to that observed in the MIRROR RCT, where preinfusion GC reduction was not incorporated (71% at month 6 and 60% at month 12 of treatment).11,12 Among the 12 patients in the current study, the most common AEs were gout flares, the majority of which were mild or moderate, and despite preinfusion GC decrease and withdrawal, no IRs occurred. An initial increase in gout flares is known to occur in patients on pegloticase because decreases in SU cause shifts of monosodium urate crystals from tissue deposits, leading to mobilization flares and inflammation.4,6 Overall, these results suggest that preinfusion GC reduction and elimination are possible in patients with uncontrolled gout receiving pegloticase and MTX, likely due to mitigation of ADA development and reduced risk of IRs through MTX co-administration.

Patients with gout suffer from a high burden of comorbidities, and those with uncontrolled gout are more likely than those with controlled gout to suffer from congestive heart failure, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and atrial fibrillation.10 These comorbidities not only add to the burden of disease experienced by uncontrolled gout patients but also make reducing preinfusion GCs particularly desirable, as GCs exacerbate many of the aforementioned comorbidities.9,10 Here, we suggest that GC elimination is possible in patients with uncontrolled gout treated with pegloticase and an immunomodulator (MTX), results that are largely consistent with case series of pegloticase and GC reduction in patients with uncontrolled gout.14,15 Together, these studies demonstrate that some patients can successfully eliminate GCs while being co-treated with immunomodulation; thus, immunomodulators such as MTX may be necessary to allow GC reduction and maintain the consistently demonstrated high efficacy of pegloticase therapy for uncontrolled gout.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include the real-world setting and inclusion of patients of a wide age range with various comorbidities, who are reflective of the general population with uncontrolled gout; however, some prominent comorbidities in gout patients, such as chronic kidney disease, are underrepresented in this patient population, likely due to the small number of patients (N=12) included in this case series. The small number of patients, all of whom received MTX while on pegloticase, also limits the generalizability of the results to patients treated with pegloticase monotherapy or another immunomodulating therapy. Furthermore, this case series did not investigate improvements in other clinical manifestations of gout, such as resolution of tophi after GC reduction. Finally, further study, with a larger population, of baseline disease characteristics, comorbidities, and other clinical features of patients who experienced an SU rise versus those who responded to treatment following GC reduction, is warranted.

CONCLUSIONS

This small case series suggests that preinfusion GC reduction and elimination are feasible in patients with uncontrolled gout treated with pegloticase and MTX co-administration, as most patients successfully continued pegloticase therapy after preinfusion GC reduction with no IRs observed. Thus, co-administering MTX with pegloticase and tapering preinfusion GCs after 2 months of therapy may serve to maximize the pegloticase response rate while minimizing ADAs, gout flares, and overall GC exposure.
