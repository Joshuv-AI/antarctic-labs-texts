Progressive unilobar cavitating lung disease despite medical therapy: Diagnostic limitations and definitive surgical management
Authors: Hafiz Gulzeb Kamil, Elizabeth Finn, Shilajit Ghosh, Christos Kosmidis, Badis Al Harbawi, Mohammed Haris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cavitating lung lesions are a common radiological finding with a broad differential diagnosis, including infectious, malignant and inflammatory causes. Management of infective lesion is often challenging, particularly when disease progresses despite appropriate medical therapy. We report a case of progressive, unilobar cavitating lung disease in a 59-year-old man related to Aspergillus colonization and Streptococcus constellatus infection, despite prolonged optimal antibacterial and antifungal treatment. Initial bronchoscopic investigations identified Aspergillus hyphae, but repeated conventional microbiological sampling failed to detect the underlying bacterial pathogen. Serial imaging demonstrated persistent progressive cavitation coalescing into large fluid-filled abscess confined to upper lobe, prompting surgical intervention. The bacterial pathogen was only identified after lobectomy, with cultures confirming S. constellatus. This case highlights the limitations of standard diagnostic techniques, the importance of recognising failure of medical therapy and the role of timely thoracic surgical resection in achieving definitive source control in anatomically localised infective cavitating lung disease.

Introduction

Cavitating lung lesions are a clinically important radiological finding associated with a broad range of underlying pathologies and variable morbidity and mortality, depending on the etiology. They represent a manifestation of disease rather than a diagnosis, arising from infectious, malignant and inflammatory processes [1,2]. Although identification of a cavity on imaging is usually straightforward, determining the underlying cause can be considerably more challenging and requires integration of clinical, radiological and microbiological findings [1].

Most infective cavitating lung lesions respond to appropriate antimicrobial therapy. However, a subset progresses despite optimal medical management, increasing the risk of complications such as abscess rupture, bronchopleural fistula and pleural contamination. In such cases, surgical intervention may be required for definitive source control. Guidance on the optimal timing of surgical referral in anatomically localised, progressive cavitating lung disease remains limited, often resulting in delayed intervention and increased morbidity. This case illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges encountered in managing unilobar progressive cavitating lung disease.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old man, never smoker, with well-controlled asthma (treated with fluticasone/salmeterol inhaler) and type 2 diabetes mellitus, underwent a CT chest during evaluation for abdominal pain and weight loss. His case was discussed in a lung multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, where malignancy was considered less likely; an infectious etiology was suspected. Serial imaging demonstrated persistent abnormalities. Flexible diagnostic bronchoscopy revealed thick purulent secretions in the LUL bronchi, which appeared oedematous and inflamed. Bronchial washings grew Aspergillus hyphae; Mycobacterium cultures were negative. Five days post-bronchoscopy, the patient developed pleuritic chest pain, productive cough with blood-streaked sputum and breathlessness. Inflammatory markers were elevated (CRP 194 mg/L, WBC 11.4 × 109/L) and empirical levofloxacin was commenced as the patient was allergic to penicillin. Blood cultures were negative.

In view of raised total IgE (>5000 IU/mL) and Aspergillus fumigatus-specific IgE level (82 kUA/L), prednisolone was commenced for suspected allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis. However, this was discontinued as progressive cavitation became evident. Follow-up CT imaging in June 2024 demonstrated radiological progression with enlarging cavitary lesions and bronchiectatic changes, prompting initiation of itraconazole therapy for presumed chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis. Despite sequential escalation of antimicrobial and antifungal therapy, serial imaging demonstrated ongoing anatomical destruction confined to the left upper lobe, with increasing cavity size and air–fluid levels (Fig. 1). Repeated sputum cultures failed to identify bacterial pathogens. Serum beta-D-glucan was negative, arguing against invasive aspergillosis.

Following multidisciplinary discussion with the National Aspergillosis Centre and concern for chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis, itraconazole was switched to voriconazole, which was subsequently discontinued due to hepatotoxicity. Liposomal amphotericin B was then commenced. Serial chest radiographs between May and October 2024 demonstrated ongoing progression of cavitation coalescing into a large fluid-filled abscess within the LUL (Fig. 2). Given radiological progression and clinical deterioration despite prolonged medical therapy, the patient underwent emergency thoracotomy with LUL lobectomy. Histopathological examination revealed extensive necrosis with fungal hyphae, and intraoperative cultures grew Streptococcus constellatus. Fungal cultures were still negative at this point. Post-operative recovery was uneventful, with resolution of symptoms.

Discussion

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with progressive focal cavitating lung disease. Although Aspergillus hyphae were identified on bronchoscopic sampling, the dominant bacterial pathogen remained undetected until surgical resection. S. constellatus is part of the Streptococcus anginosus or milleri group, which has traditionally been considered to have a propensity to cause empyema and purulent abscesses [3]. Zhu Y et al. reported 9 cases of pulmonary abscess caused by Streptococcus constellatus; one case was confirmed by traditional cultures, while metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) confirmed the other 8 cases [4].

Bronchoscopic sampling has inherent limitations in cavitating lung disease, particularly when organisms are sequestered within necrotic or poorly perfused cavities. In this patient, persistent radiological progression despite prolonged and appropriate antimicrobial and antifungal therapy was a key indicator of treatment failure. Serial imaging demonstrated increasing cavitation and parenchymal destruction confined to a single lobe, suggesting poor antimicrobial penetrance into devitalised tissue.

In our case, serum β-D-glucan was negative. However, β-D-glucan lacks specificity for Aspergillus species. As highlighted by Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines, serum and bronchoalveolar lavage galactomannan (GM) are recommended as more specific diagnostic markers for invasive aspergillosis [5]; however, GM testing was not performed in this case, representing a limitation of the diagnostic evaluation.

The role and timing of surgery in managing necrotising pneumonia is a highly debated topic. Numerous case studies have analysed surgical utility in specific cases [6]. Surgical resection for necrotizing lung infections is a reasonable option in patients with persistent sepsis who are failing medical therapy [7] and outcomes are generally poorer in patients with chronic pulmonary aspergillosis undergoing surgery [8]. This case supports early multidisciplinary discussion, broad diagnostic consideration and timely referral for thoracic surgical assessment in selected patients with anatomically localised, progressive cavitating lung disease despite optimal medical therapy.

Conclusion

Progressive unilobar cavitating lung lesions may not respond to medical therapy, likely reflecting poor antibiotic penetration into necrotic cavitary tissue and conventional diagnostics may fail to detect fastidious pathogens such as Streptococcus constellatus. Definitive diagnosis and cure may require surgical resection when disease is anatomically localised and progressive despite optimal medical therapy. Early recognition of treatment failure and timely thoracic surgical referral are essential to prevent complications and achieve source control.
