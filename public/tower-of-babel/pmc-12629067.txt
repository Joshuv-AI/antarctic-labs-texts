Application of the theory of planned behavior in self-care behaviors of knee osteoarthritis in the rural older people
Authors: Tayebeh Rakhshani, Sara Alamdar Bushehri, Hadi Daneshmandi, Seyyed Mansour Kashfi, Amirhossein Kamyab, Ali Khani Jeihooni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoarthritis is the most prevalent type of arthritis and the second cause of long-term disability among older people. Identifying the disease in time and improving the level of awareness and attitude of people can be effective in reducing the complications caused by the disease. The purpose of this study is to look at how education based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) affected a group of older people living in rural areas’ preventive behaviors related to knee osteoarthritis.MethodsThis clinical trial study was performed on 100 older people with knee osteoarthritis referred to rural health centers in Bushehr Province, Iran. Fifty individuals were randomly distributed to the experimental and control groups in the study using the multi-stage cluster sampling approach. The TPB questionnaire on osteoarthritis self-care habits was used to gather data; it was completed both before and three months after the intervention. The intervention comprised four sessions lasting 45–60 min each, held once a week for a month. Independent t and chi-square statistical tests were applied to assess the data after entering into SPSS 24.ResultsThe age distribution of the experimental group was 65.20 ± 5.07 years, yet the control group had an age distribution of 66.28 ± 5.88 years. The independent t-test results revealed no differences in attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, behavioral intention, or behavior between the study groups. But following the intervention, there was a noteworthy distinction between the two groups in the aforementioned characteristics (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe results showed that the use of TPB contributed to the improvement of self-care for knee osteoarthritis in older people. Due to the importance of health education in promoting self-care behaviors and preventing complications, longer interventions with the aim of preventing the complications of knee osteoarthritis in older people are suggested.Trial registrationThe registration number: IRCT20221206056722N1. Background

Disease does not directly cause aging, as it is a physiological process. However, as time passes and environmental influences and unhealthy lifestyles take effect, the prevalence of diseases, particularly chronic diseases, rises throughout this time [1]. 88.7% of the older people over the age of 65 have one chronic disease, 71.7% have two chronic diseases, and 51.3% have three or more chronic diseases [2]. One of the most prevalent diseases of old age is osteoarthritis, especially knee osteoarthritis [3]. This disease is one of the most common in developing countries [4]. In America, knee osteoarthritis is the most common type of arthritis and the second cause of long-term disability among adults [5].

The condition is currently becoming more common, and this tendency is anticipated to continue as life expectancy and the incidence of obesity rise [6]. Various countries have reported varying rates of osteoarthritis prevalence; for example, according to Dhakal et al., the prevalence of osteoarthritis in Indian women was 19.4% [7]. Liu et al., in their systematic review, reported the predominance of osteoporosis among women as 27.67% [8]. Another study by Chandran et al. reported a 10–30% prevalence of osteoporosis in women aged 40 years and older [9]. A study in Iran reported the predominance of femur and spine osteoarthritis in Iranian women to be about 20%, which is a significant statistic [10]. Another study in Iran reported a 41.5% prevalence of osteoarthritis among the elderly population [11].

Self-care behaviors in patients with knee osteoarthritis involve managing pain, protecting joints, maintaining physical activity, and adhering to medical and non-pharmacological treatments to enhance the quality of life and prevent further complications [12, 13]. The management of knee osteoarthritis requires a thorough understanding of both the complications and the self-care interventions necessary to mitigate or track the progression of these complications. Common complications include joint stiffness, reduced mobility, and chronic pain, which can lead to decreased quality of life and increased dependency on others for daily activities [14]. Effective self-care interventions, such as consistent physical activity, weight management, and joint protection strategies, play a critical role in preventing the worsening of symptoms and improving overall patient outcomes [12]. Regular monitoring and follow-up on these interventions are crucial, as they enable healthcare providers to adjust treatment plans based on the patient’s evolving condition and prevent further complications [15]. Reporting the outcomes of these follow-ups is essential for understanding the long-term effectiveness of self-care behaviors in managing osteoarthritis-related complications.

Preventing and treating osteoarthritis includes pharmaceutical and non-pharmacological interventions. Regarding non-pharmacological interventions, effective improvement in the behaviors related to the prevention of osteoarthritis in people requires understanding the basic factors that play a vital role in people’s decisions to improve their lifestyle. Only a theory that identifies mediating factors in the behaviors related to osteoporosis prevention can meet this need [16]. One of the most important non-pharmacological interventions involves implementing educational strategies such as promoting proper and continuous physical activity, maintaining a healthy lifestyle, and avoiding unnecessary movements [17]. Today’s approach to preventing this disease involves an educational approach and the application of health education [17].

Models for health education have been developed with the intention of providing useful assistance in creating programs that effectively alter the behaviors that lead to social problems. One of the most useful health education frameworks in the field of preventive medicine is the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [18]. This theory asserts that the three constructs of attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control significantly influence an individual’s intention, thereby shaping their conduct [19]. Self-care is a valuable step to increase awareness, help patients gain individual independence, facilitate their adaptation to their disease, and the subsequent increase in quality of life. Continuous follow-up can prevent acute and chronic complications, while also reducing costs [20].

The application of the TPB to guide self-care behaviors in patients with knee osteoarthritis is highly appropriate due to its comprehensive approach to influencing behavior through key psychological constructs—attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. These constructs help in shaping behavioral intentions, which are central to initiating and maintaining self-care practices, such as physical activity, pain management, and joint protection. Similar studies have demonstrated that TPB-based interventions are effective in promoting self-care behaviors among patients with chronic conditions, including osteoarthritis. For instance, research by Duarte et al. and Jormand et al. highlights the success of using TPB to improve health outcomes by targeting patients’ attitudes towards self-care, enhancing perceived social support, and building confidence in their ability to manage their condition independently [21, 22].

Given the high prevalence of knee osteoarthritis among older adults and its significant impact on quality of life, effective management of the disease through self-care behaviors is essential [23]. In some studies, the effectiveness of health interventions based on the TPB in the improvement of self-care behaviors of osteoarthritis in the older people has been pointed out [21], but in Iran, there are few studies on the prevention of osteoarthritis. The goal of this study is not to prevent osteoarthritis but to enhance the self-care practices of individuals already diagnosed with the condition. By utilizing the TPB, this study aims to assess whether an educational intervention can improve self-care behaviors, such as pain management, joint protection, and maintaining physical activity, among older adults with knee osteoarthritis. By targeting key constructs of TPB—attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention—this study seeks to promote positive behavior changes that could mitigate the complications and improve the overall quality of life for these patients.

Methods

Study design

This clinical trial study was conducted in 2022 on older people with knee osteoarthritis in Bushehr City, Iran. The criteria for entering the study were age over 60 years, patients diagnosed with knee osteoarthritis by a doctor, having at least 6 months history of the disease, appropriate visual and auditory function, and willingness to participate in the survey. The criterion for leaving the study was unwillingness to cooperate at any time during the study or not participating in at least two training sessions. This study has a clinical trial code (IRCT20221206056722N1) of the Iranian Clinical Trial Registration Center, registered on 2022/12/12, available at: “https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/67223” and an ethics code (IR.SUMS.SCHEANUT.REC.1401.081) of the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences.

Sampling

To determine the sample size, taking into account the average comparison formula in two communities and also according to a study by Jormand et al., the confidence level of 0.95%, the power of the test of 80%, and the attrition rate of 10% were calculated for 50 people for each group [22]. A multi-stage cluster sampling method was applied. In this way, out of all comprehensive health centers with active records of knee osteoarthritis patients in Bushehr Province, four were randomly selected as clusters (two serving as the experimental group and two for choosing the control group). Then, from each center, 25 people were selected randomly using a computer-generated number sequence.

Procedure

After selecting the samples, a trained interviewer had the participants fill out a questionnaire in person before the intervention. A rheumatologist and physiotherapist then contributed their opinions to the development of the educational program. The educational program was carried out in the form of four sessions (three sessions of 45 min and one session of one hour) designed to improve behavior (Table 1). Following the training, the study instructed the participants to utilize the educational materials provided at home. Following the conclusion of four training sessions and the passage of three months since the previous session, skilled interviewers revisited the questionnaires by knocking on the door.

The study’s intervention was carefully designed by health education experts to align with the constructs of the TPB, including attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intention. The intervention aimed to improve self-care behaviors in older individuals with knee osteoarthritis by directly targeting these model structures.

The intervention consisted of four structured educational sessions, each focusing on different TPB constructs:

Session 1 (Attitude): This session aimed at improving participants’ attitudes towards self-care behaviors by emphasizing the benefits of regular physical activity, proper nutrition, and the prevention of osteoarthritis complications. Participants were encouraged to view these behaviors positively, which would increase their motivation to adopt them.

The sessions were delivered in the form of lectures, group discussions, and Q&A sessions, providing participants with opportunities to engage with the content and clarify any doubts. Each session was designed to be interactive, ensuring that the participants could actively apply the concepts of the TPB model to their daily lives. The control group did not receive any educational intervention but was provided with the same educational materials after the study to ensure ethical standards.

To minimize bias, the personnel involved in the outcome assessment were blinded to the group allocation of the participants. The control group received no intervention and continued with routine care. However, to maintain ethical standards, the control group was provided with the same educational materials at the conclusion of the study.

Questionnaires

The demographic information questionnaire contained demographic data such as age, marital status, education, and Body Mass Index (BMI)

The TPB Questionnaire

This instrument has 5 sections, including attitude towards behavior with 12 questions, subjective norms with 11 questions, perceived behavioral control with 10 questions, behavioral intention with 9 questions, and behavior with 9 questions. Questions were on a five-choice Likert scale from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree). Mahmoodabad et al. conducted a study to test the validity and reliability of this questionnaire, with the aim of influencing teachers’ adoption of preventive behaviors for knee arthritis based on the TPB. The Cronbach’s alpha range for the subscales was 0.70 to 89.00 [24]. Additionally, Fahimfar et al. reported the prevalence of osteoarthritis among the elderly in Iran, emphasizing the need for reliable tools to measure preventive behaviors in this population​ [11].

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine the data’s normalcy before SPSS 24 was used to analyze it. The independent t-test and the chi-square test were taken into consideration (P < 0.05) in order to characterize the data, mean, and standard deviation, as well as to evaluate the data. Cohen’s d was calculated to assess the effect size of the intervention for each TPB construct. It was computed using the following formula:

Cohen’s d = (M₁ – M₂)/pooled standard deviation,

where M₁ and M₂ are the means of the two groups, and the pooled standard deviation is calculated from both groups’ standard deviations. According to standard interpretation, values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 represent small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively.

Results

The age of the experimental group was 65.20 ± 5.07 years, while the control group had an average age of 66.28 ± 5.88 years. The chi-square test revealed no significant differences between the groups in terms of demographic characteristics before the intervention (P > 0.05). The comparison of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) constructs before and after the intervention revealed significant differences between the experimental and control groups, demonstrating the impact of the educational intervention. Before the intervention, no significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of attitude (P = 0.66), subjective norms (P = 0.95), perceived behavioral control (P = 0.99), behavioral intention (P = 0.84), or behavior (P = 0.65). However, after the intervention, there were significant improvements in the experimental group across all constructs compared to the control group (P < 0.05).

For attitude, the experimental group’s mean score increased from 37.77 ± 1.30 before the intervention to 41.90 ± 1.45 after the intervention. In contrast, the control group experienced a slight decrease in attitude, with their mean score dropping from 37.45 ± 0.95 to 36.72 ± 1.02. The effect size before the intervention (Cohen’s d = 0.281) was small, indicating minimal differences between the groups. However, after the intervention, the effect size increased dramatically to 4.132, showing a very large impact of the intervention on improving the attitude toward self-care behavior in the experimental group.

In terms of subjective norms, the intervention led to a marked improvement in the experimental group, with scores rising from 38.79 ± 0.74 to 44.95 ± 1.01. Meanwhile, the control group remained relatively unchanged, with scores shifting only slightly from 37.69 ± 0.82 to 37.85 ± 0.74. The effect size before the intervention (Cohen’s d = 1.408) was already large, but it increased significantly post-intervention to 8.019, indicating a substantial impact of the intervention on subjective norms.

For perceived behavioral control, there was a significant increase in the experimental group after the intervention, with scores rising from 32.93 ± 0.45 to 35.01 ± 0.65, while the control group saw only a minor increase from 31.85 ± 0.35 to 32.65 ± 0.74. The effect size before the intervention (Cohen’s d = 2.679) was large, and it remained high after the intervention at 3.389, indicating the intervention’s strong impact on enhancing the elderly individuals’ belief in their ability to control their self-care behavior.

The intervention also significantly improved the behavioral intention of the experimental group, with mean scores rising from 34.90 ± 0.38 to 40.11 ± 0.39 after the intervention, while the control group showed only a marginal increase from 34.79 ± 0.65 to 35.01 ± 0.74. The effect size before the intervention (Cohen’s d = 0.207) was small, but after the intervention, it surged to 8.622, demonstrating a very large effect of the intervention on the elderly participants’ intentions to engage in self-care behaviors.

Finally, the self-care behaviors of the experimental group showed a significant improvement after the intervention, with scores increasing from 24.81 ± 0.70 to 27.81 ± 1.11, whereas the control group exhibited no significant change, with scores shifting slightly from 23.95 ± 0.84 to 24.08 ± 0.95. The effect size before the intervention (Cohen’s d = 1.112) was large, and it further increased post-intervention to 3.610, reflecting a strong impact of the intervention on promoting self-care behaviors among the elderly in the experimental group [Table 3].

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the potential impact of educational interventions based on the TPB on the self-care practices of older rural residents with knee osteoarthritis in 2022. The current study’s findings showed that the educational intervention based on the TPB improved the experimental group’s attitude toward self-care behavior compared to the control group. It should be stated that attitude about a behavior is a degree of a person’s favorable or unfavorable evaluation of that behavior, so that attitude refers to judging whether the desired behavior is desirable or undesirable, or whether the person decides to do it, so that the more positive a person’s attitude was, the more likely he was to perform the behavior. Research by Duarte et al., Esmat Abd-El Hafeez et al., Hamilton et al., and Hunter et al. [21, 25–27]. However, Sharma et al. presented contradictory results, suggesting that attitude alone may not be sufficient to drive behavior change in elderly populations. They found that despite positive attitudes toward self-care, elderly individuals may still face barriers like physical limitations and lack of motivation​. This indicates that while attitude is crucial, it must be supported by other factors, such as perceived control and external support, for sustained behavior change [28].

According to our findings, the intervention led to an improvement in the subjective norms toward self-care behavior in the older people in the experimental group compared to the controls. Subjective norms mean the understanding of social pressures to adopt or not adopt a behavior and the opinion of whether certain people approve or reject the desired behavior. In this study, the number of subjective norms increased considerably after the intervention. This result is in line with research findings by Arliansyah et al., Lorbeer et al., Nelligan et al., Gurjar et al.‘s study, and Thapa et al.‘s study [29–33]. However, a study by Lin et al. contradicts this, suggesting that in some cases, elderly individuals may feel pressured or overwhelmed by social expectations, which can lead to non-compliance with recommended behaviors. Thus, while subjective norms can be a driving force for positive behavior change, they must be balanced with the individual’s capacity to manage external pressures [14].

The results showed a significant increase in perceived behavioral control in the intervention group. This is a critical finding, as individuals are more likely to engage in self-care behaviors when they believe they have the ability and resources to do so. Research by Eslamimehr et al. and Krystia et al. supports this, indicating that enhancing confidence and providing practical skills can lead to better management of chronic conditions [34, 35]​. However, Goff et al. found that perceived behavioral control is often limited in older populations due to physical constraints or a lack of access to resources [36]. This suggests that while educational interventions can increase perceived control, practical barriers such as mobility issues or insufficient healthcare access must also be addressed for long-term success in self-care behaviors.

The study demonstrated that the educational intervention significantly increased the participants’ intention to engage in self-care behaviors. This aligns with the TPB model, which posits that intention is the strongest predictor of actual behavior. Studies by Chen et al. and Kooranian et al. support the role of intention in predicting self-care behaviors [37, 38]​. However, Wainwright et al. argue that while intention is necessary, it may not always translate into action, particularly in older adults, where intention-action gaps can arise due to unforeseen health challenges or lack of motivation [39]. This highlights the need for continuous support and follow-up to ensure that behavioral intentions are carried out in practice.

The intervention effectively improved the self-care behaviors of participants, including pain management, joint protection, and regular physical activity. These behaviors are essential for managing knee osteoarthritis and preventing further complications. The findings are consistent with previous studies by Lin et al. and Shah et al., which highlighted the effectiveness of educational interventions in promoting self-care​ [12, 14]. However, contradictory findings by Ishfaq et al. suggest that elderly individuals may struggle to maintain these behaviors in the long term, especially if they face physical or psychological barriers [15]. This underscores the importance of personalized follow-up strategies to support sustained behavior change.

Limitations

From the limitations of this study, we could mention the possible limited sample size of the participants, which could be compensated by future research conducted on a more generalized population. Besides the follow-up time of the participants, due to our restrictions in time and human resources, it could be more difficult to assess the long-term effects of education.

Conclusion

The results of the present study showed that the use of TPB led to an improvement in self-care for knee osteoarthritis in older people. Due to the importance of health education in promoting self-care behaviors and preventing complications, longer interventions with the aim of preventing the complications of knee osteoarthritis in older people are suggested. The application of this theory of behavior change to other diseases and other health problems is also recommended.
