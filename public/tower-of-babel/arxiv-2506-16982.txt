Language Bottleneck Models: A Framework for Interpretable Knowledge Tracing and Beyond
Antonin Berthon, Mihaela van der Schaar

Abstract
Accurately assessing student knowledge is critical for effective education, yet traditional Knowledge Tracing (KT) methods rely on opaque latent embeddings, limiting interpretability. Even LLM-based approaches generate direct predictions or summaries that may hallucinate without any accuracy guarantees. We recast KT as an inverse problem: learning the minimum natural-language summary that makes past answers explainable and future answers predictable. Our Language Bottleneck Model (LBM) consists of an encoder LLM that writes an interpretable knowledge summary and a frozen decoder LLM that must reconstruct and predict student responses using only that summary text. By constraining all predictive information to pass through a short natural-language bottleneck, LBMs ensure that the summary contains accurate information while remaining human-interpretable. Experiments on synthetic arithmetic benchmarks and the large-scale Eedi dataset show that LBMs rival the accuracy of state-of-the-art KT and direct LLM methods while requiring orders-of-magnitude fewer student trajectories. We demonstrate that training the encoder with group-relative policy optimization, using downstream decoding accuracy as a reward signal, effectively improves summary quality.

Introduction

The Challenge of Estimating a Student’s Knowledge State.

A fundamental objective in education is accurately assessing what a student knows, identifying misconceptions, and understanding how their knowledge evolves over time
posner1982accommodation
;
larkin2012misconceptions
;
chen2020impact
. Teachers intuitively achieve this through diagnostic reasoning: by observing students’ answers, they infer not merely correctness, but deeper patterns reflecting conceptual mastery or specific misunderstandings. For instance, a student consistently misapplying algebraic properties signals conceptual gaps requiring targeted intervention. Replicating this nuanced interpretability within AI would profoundly enhance personalized education at scale, augmenting human teaching by providing transparent, actionable insights into learners’ knowledge states
(
khosravi2022explainable
)
.

Building algorithms that can learn the knowledge state of students as they interact with educational material is an active field of study known as Knowledge Tracing (KT)
(
corbett_knowledge_1994
;
shen2024survey
)
(see Appendix A for a detailed introduction to KT).
However, accurately modeling a student’s knowledge state in a way that is both predictive and interpretable remains a significant open challenge. Traditional KT methods
(
piech_deep_2015
;
zhang2017dynamic
;
pandey2019self
;
ghosh2020context
)
rely primarily on statistical, embedding-based representations. Such models lack transparency, representing knowledge states as high-dimensional latent vectors that provide limited interpretability or actionable insights for teachers and learners (Figure
1
B
). A truly impactful AI assistant in education must go beyond opaque predictions, explicitly articulating what the student understands and what they do not, in an accurate and interpretable manner.

Limitations of Existing LLM-based approaches for KT.

Recent approaches leveraging large language models (LLMs) have shown that LLMs can produce sensible predictions of students’ future behavior when provided with relevant information
(
li2024explainable
;
kim2024beyond
)
, help mitigate the KT’s cold-start problem
(
lee2024language
)
and be finetuned to improve accuracy on KT tasks
(
wang2025llm
)
. Nonetheless, these LLM-based methods still lack rigorous interpretability, as they either treat the model as a black-box or rely on free-form explanations that are susceptible to hallucination
(
bender2021dangers
)
.
Without ensuring faithful and grounded summaries, LLM-generated explanations fall short as reliable educational tools, emphasizing the need for a structured and principled foundation for generating interpretable, reliable, and adaptive student knowledge representations.

Knowledge Tracing as an Inverse Problem: Towards Concise and Interpretable Summaries.

Existing Knowledge Tracing methods typically approach student modeling as a forward prediction task, using latent statistical embeddings to predict future responses. However, this approach provides limited insight into the underlying student knowledge. We reframe Knowledge Tracing as an inverse problem: viewing student responses as outcomes of an underlying knowledge state
\mathcal{S}
, manifested through the student’s cognitive process (Figure
1
A
). The goal is to recover an interpretable representation
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
of the student knowledge state. Crucially, answers to downstream questions are expected to arise from the same latent knowledge state—an assumption we discuss further in Section
2.2
. A useful representation must therefore reconstruct observed answers, enable prediction of future responses, and remain interpretable and steerable for teachers.

Introducing Language Bottleneck Models (LBMs).

Motivated by the inverse-problem framing described above, we introduce
Language Bottleneck Models (LBMs)
. As illustrated in Figure
1
C
, LBMs uses two Large Language Models (LLMs) as student knowledge state extractor (encoder) and interpreter (decoder), generating succinct textual summaries meant to capture the student knowledge state. Specifically, LBMs map sequences of student interactions
\mathcal{X}
to a concise, natural-language summary
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
. This summary then serves as the sole basis for reconstructing the observed responses
\mathcal{X}
and accurately predicting future responses
\mathcal{Y}
. By design, this ensures that the generated explanations encompass precisely the information necessary to interpret past behavior and reliably forecast future student performance.

2
Knowledge Tracing as an Inverse Problem

In this section, we formally introduce the knowledge tracing problem. We then reframe it as an inverse problem—inferring interpretable knowledge states from observed responses, outlining key desiderata for such representations and the conditions under which this formulation is appropriate.

2.1
Knowledge Tracing: Formal Problem Definition

Knowledge Tracing (KT) is fundamentally a sequence modeling problem where the objective is to infer a student’s latent knowledge state based on observed interactions. Formally, let
\mathcal{X}=\{(q_{1},a_{1}),(q_{2},a_{2}),\ldots,(q_{t},a_{t})\}
denote a sequence of question-answer pairs, where
q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}
represents a question from the question space
\mathcal{Q}
, and
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
represents the student’s answer from the answer space
\mathcal{A}
. The goal of KT is to learn a function that predicts the probability of correctly answering a future question
q_{t+1}
given the historical interaction sequence:

P(a_{t+1}|q_{t+1},\mathcal{X})
.

While this formulation captures the predictive aspect of KT, it fails to address a crucial dimension: the interpretability. An ideal KT system should not only predict accurately but also provide educators with an explicit, human-understandable representation of the student’s knowledge state—such as mastered concepts, knowledge gaps and misconceptions. The importance of interpretability for KT is further discussed in the Appendix.

2.2
Knowledge Tracing as an Inverse Problem: From Black–Box Predictions to Interpretable Summaries

The conventional KT formulation (§2.1) focuses on predictive accuracy but offers limited insight into the underlying knowledge that drives student behavior. We instead frame KT as an
inverse problem
: in many practical scenarios—such as a quiz completed in a single sitting—a student’s responses can largely be explained by a fixed underlying knowledge state, expressed through the student’s cognitive process of answering questions. Most observed behavior reflects this latent state, with occasional deviations due to noise—such as
slips
(careless mistakes) or
guesses
(lucky correct answers), as formalized in Bayesian KT
corbett_knowledge_1994
. The goal, then, is to recover an interpretable representation
\tilde{S}
of the knowledge state that accounts for the observed interactions and supports accurate future predictions.

Framing KT in this way highlights three desiderata. (1)
Self-consistency
: the inferred summary
\tilde{S}
should reconstruct the past by making the observed answers in
\mathbf{X}
likely under a decoder. (2)
Predictive sufficiency
:
\tilde{S}
must retain all information necessary to accurately predict future responses to unseen questions. (3)
Human interpretability and steerability
: the summary should be readable and editable by domain experts, enabling the diagnosis of student misconceptions and manual refinement or augmentation of
\tilde{S}
to guide future predictions.

When is the inverse formulation appropriate?
Although real learning is dynamic, many high–stakes diagnostic situations (end–of–unit quizzes, placement tests, or short tutoring sessions) span minutes rather than weeks. In such windows, it is reasonable to assume a quasi–static knowledge state. In this work, we explicitly focus on scenarios characterized by such quasi–static knowledge states. For longer horizons, the knowledge state might be better modeled as piecewise-static or smoothly evolving
S(t)
. We leave the extension of our framework to dynamically evolving knowledge states for future work.

2.3
Natural Language as the Interface

While a student’s knowledge state is inherently latent, natural language offers a flexible and interpretable medium for expressing it. Unlike fixed concept vocabularies, natural language accommodates open-ended reasoning patterns, misconceptions, and levels of mastery. In this work, we propose to use concise natural language summaries as proxies for student knowledge, designed to capture the latent cognitive state in a way that is both human-readable and predictive. The next section introduces a model family that operationalizes this idea.

3.1
Formal Definition

To tackle the inverse problem formulation of KT introduced in Section
2
, we propose
Language Bottleneck Models
(LBMs): a two-stage architecture for inferring interpretable and accurate knowledge states from a student’s past interactions.

Let
X_{s}\subseteq X
denote a subset of observed student responses, and let
Y_{s}\subseteq Y
denote a set of future questions whose answers the model aims to predict. An LBM is defined by:

•

An
encoder

f_{\theta}:X_{s}\rightarrow S
, which maps a history of interactions to a textual summary
S
of the student’s implicit knowledge state.

•

A
decoder

g:S\rightarrow\hat{Y}_{s}
, which uses only the textual summary to predict the student’s answers to new questions.

The explicit use of natural language as a bottleneck encourages the model to produce summaries that balance brevity with completeness. If the language summary omits essential details, predictions of future responses will suffer; conversely, if the summary is overly detailed or redundant, its utility for educators decreases. Thus, this design ensures the resulting textual summary is simultaneously concise, informative, and directly interpretable by humans.

The following sections motivate this approach and outline how LBMs can be trained effectively.

3.2
Motivating Observations

Before detailing our training procedure, we highlight two motivating observations that justify the LBM framework and its constraints.

Observation 1: Summarizing a knowledge state is challenging.

Figure
2
compares summaries produced by different LLMs when prompted to describe a student’s knowledge state from 100 question-answer pairs from a synthetic dataset (see Section
5
for details). While all models capture high-level skill mastery, only one correctly identifies a misconception (errors with negative numbers) out of the four existing ones, illustrating that standard summarization approaches often miss crucial latent patterns.

Figure 3:
Accuracy on synthetic dataset given ground truth knowledge state summaries.

Observation 2: Given a good summary, capable LLMs can decode with high fidelity.

Figure
3
evaluates the performance of different decoder models when given access to the ground truth summary of the student’s latent knowledge state (an example of a ground-truth summary is shown in Figure

). Stronger models like GPT-4o achieve nearly perfect accuracy (
98\%
), indicating that the bottleneck representation is indeed sufficient to drive effective downstream prediction—provided it encodes the right information.

Together, these observations motivate the inverse formulation: while generating summaries from raw interactions is hard, once obtained, these summaries are powerful inputs for reasoning and prediction. This validates our approach of learning encoders that optimize predictive utility via interpretable, compressed natural language representations.

3.3
Training Objective and Optimization

We train LBMs to produce informative and interpretable summaries of student knowledge states. As demonstrated above, strong decoders such as GPT-4o can achieve near-perfect accuracy when provided with accurate knowledge summaries. We therefore optimize the encoder
f_{\theta}
to produce such summaries, while freezing the decoder
g
to a strong LLM during training.

Summary generation and evaluation.

Given a student’s interaction history
X
, we sample a subset
X_{\text{enc}}\subseteq X
of
N
interactions to generate a summary
S=f_{\theta}(X_{\text{enc}})
. We evaluate this summary on two disjoint subsets: (i)
X_{s}\subseteq X_{\text{enc}}
: past questions for assessing reconstruction ability; and (ii)
Y_{s}\subseteq Y
: future questions for evaluating predictive accuracy. The summary
S
is passed to the frozen decoder
g
, which outputs predictions
\hat{X}_{s}=g(S;X_{s})
and
\hat{Y}_{s}=g(S;Y_{s})
.

Reward function.

The reward for a summary
S
is defined using a function
\Phi
that balances multiple criteria:

\mathcal{R}(S;g)=\Phi\left(\texttt{Acc}(\hat{X}_{s},X_{s}),\;\texttt{Acc}(\hat{Y}_{s},Y_{s}),\;|S|,\;\Omega(S)\right),

(1)

where
\texttt{Acc}(\cdot)
measures accuracy,
|S|
is the summary length, and
\Omega(S)
enforces structural constraints (e.g., inclusion of a
Misconceptions
section). The function
\Phi
balances these components using hyperparameters or indicator functions to enforce constraints as needed.

Optimization via GRPO.

To optimize
f_{\theta}
, we employ Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
shao2024deepseekmath
. For each input
X
, the encoder generates
G
candidate summaries
\{S_{1},\dots,S_{G}\}
, each evaluated using
\mathcal{R}(S_{i};g)
. We compute group-relative advantage and update parameters:

A(S_{i})=\frac{\mathcal{R}(S_{i};g)-\mu}{\sigma},\quad\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim f_{\theta}(X)}[\mathcal{R}(S;g)]\approx\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}A(S_{i})\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(S_{i}|X),

(2)

where
\mu
and
\sigma
are the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the group. This approach encourages the encoder to produce summaries that maximize the reward function defined in Eq.
1
.

3.4
Steerability of the Estimated Knowledge States

A key advantage of LBMs is the ability for humans to steer the model’s behavior. We identify three mechanisms for human-model interaction (detailed in Appendix):

•

Prompt engineering the encoder.
Directly shaping summary generation through input instructions.

•

Steering via reward signals.
Incorporating human preferences during encoder training through the
\Omega(S)
term in Eq.
1
.

•

Augmenting with student-specific information.
Educators can supplement the model with additional knowledge not present in observed data—either by augmenting encoder inputs or by editing the generated summary before decoding. This enables integration of recent classroom observations or specific misconceptions identified through in-person interactions.

4
Related Work

In this section, we review the literature related to our proposed approach, spanning traditional knowledge tracing, recent advances in LLM-based student modeling, and concept bottleneck models (CBMs), and text summarization models.

Traditional Knowledge Tracing

Traditional knowledge tracing (KT) methods have long been used to model student learning and predict future performance
shen2024survey
. Early works such as Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT)
corbett_knowledge_1994
model student mastery as a probabilistic process, while more recent methods like Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT)
piech_deep_2015
and Attentive Knowledge Tracing (AKT)
ghosh2020context
uses an attention-based architecture to learn contextual representation of questions and student knowledge states. Despite their strong predictive performance, these models represent a student’s knowledge state as an abstract, high-dimensional latent vector, which poses significant challenges in interpretability and actionable feedback for educators.

LLM-Based Knowledge Tracing

Recent studies have begun integrating Large Language Models (LLMs) into the KT framework. For example,
li2024explainable
demonstrated that LLMs are able to make sensible predictions about student responses when prompted with adequate information.
Other works
lee2024language
;
kim2024beyond
have studied how LLMs can help mitigate the cold-start problem compared to traditional KT approaches, while
wang2025llm
demonstrated state-of-the-art performances in KT by combining LLMs with sequence interaction models. However, these methods generally remain opaque: they either treat the LLM as a black-box, or rely on model-generated explanations that are susceptible to hallucination
(
bender2021dangers
)
.

Concept Bottleneck Models

Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs)
koh2020concept
improve interpretability through human-understandable concept activations as intermediates, with extensions exploring unsupervised concept learning
oikarinen2023label
, test-time interventions
shin2023closer
, and theoretical analyses of concept set design
luyten2024theoretical
. However, CBMs typically rely on finite predefined concept sets, limiting their applicability to complex tasks like knowledge tracing.
Recently,
yamaguchitoward
introduced Explanation Bottleneck Models (XBMs), which use textual rationales as intermediates for vision classification, justifying a single known label per input. While our Language Bottleneck Models (LBMs) adopt this language bottleneck concept, they differ fundamentally. Unlike XBMs’ instance-specific, task-specific rationales, LBM summaries aim to capture an
implicit
knowledge states—as emphasized by our inverse problem formulation—and generalize to future, unknown questions. These requirements necessitate holistic, adaptable summaries rather than XBMs’ task-specific rationales, leading us to introduce Language Bottleneck Models (LBMs) as a distinct framework with broader applicability.

Contrast with Text Summarization Models

Recent text summarization models such as BART
lewis2019bart
, T5
raffel2020exploring
, and PEGASUS
zhang2020pegasus
effectively produce concise summaries based on explicitly available textual content. However, these approaches differ fundamentally from knowledge tracing, where summaries must infer implicit student knowledge states not directly observable in the input. Standard summarization metrics (e.g., ROUGE, BLEU) rely on explicit reference summaries, making them unsuitable for evaluating latent knowledge inference tasks. In contrast, our Language Bottleneck Models generate textual summaries of the student’s implicit knowledge state, optimized for predictive accuracy on downstream questions rather than syntactic overlap with the observed input.

5.2.4
Steering LBM behavior

We demonstrate multiple steering strategies described in Section
3.4
(details in Appendix). We find that providing explicit misconception information during encoder training produces significantly stronger learning effects than adding the same information at the decoder stage. This suggests the encoder leverages this additional context to better interpret patterns in student responses. We also show the encoder can be steered during training to explicitly mention misconceptions through reward signals, and illustrate how supplementing the bottleneck with information not present in the input can significantly improve decoder accuracy.

6
Discussion

Core contributions

Framing Knowledge Tracing as an inverse problem shifts focus from fitting statistical regularities to understanding the underlying reasons for student responses. Language Bottleneck Models operationalize this view by compressing interaction histories into explicit natural-language summaries that maintain high predictive power while being both interpretable and steerable by domain experts.

Why can’t we just prompt GPT-4o directly?

The split encoder-decoder architecture of LBMs offers three key advantages over direct LLM prompting: it creates a global student model with a single latent summary shared across all predictions rather than isolated per-question reasoning; it ensures faithful summaries through a closed-loop decoding objective that penalizes non-predictive summaries; and it provides an explicit interface layer that teachers can read, steer and intervene on.

Wider applicability

LBMs extend beyond education to any task requiring compact, human-readable summaries with predictive power. The minimal ingredients needed are: (1) a sequence of observations about an entity, (2) a need to predict future behaviors of that entity, and (3) value in having interpretable representations. For example, clinical decision support could distill patient data into textual state descriptions that forecast outcomes while remaining auditable; preventive maintenance could compress sensor logs into explanations predicting machine failure; customer success teams could summarize interaction histories to forecast churn.

Limitations

LBMs face several constraints: context length limitations imposed by underlying LLMs, which could be addressed through iterative summarization; the requirement for textual question content rather than just identifiers, limiting applicability to some KT datasets; and the substantial computational cost of running LLMs. We discuss limitations extensively in the Appendix.

Future work

Several promising extensions include: implementing iterative encoding to process longer inputs through sequential chunking; developing active sensing capabilities to select questions that maximize information about students’ knowledge states; adapting LBMs for evolving knowledge states to capture learning and forgetting over time; expanding beyond question-answer inputs to leverage richer data sources; and combining with pedagogical-alignment techniques like LearnLM
(
team2024learnlm
)
. We discuss these points in more details in the Appendix. By bridging the gap between predictive power and human interpretability, Language Bottleneck Models represent a significant step toward AI systems that can genuinely augment human expertise in education and beyond.

Appendix A
Introduction to Knowledge Tracing

In this section, we provide a more detailed introduction to Knowledge Tracing. For recent comprehensive reviews of the field, see
[
1
,
25
]
.

Knowledge Tracing (KT) is the task of modeling students’ knowledge states and predicting their performance on future questions based on their historical interactions with educational content. The goal is to dynamically track the acquisition, retention, and application of knowledge as students engage with learning materials.

A.1
Formal Problem Definition

Knowledge Tracing (KT) is fundamentally a sequence modeling problem where the objective is to infer a student’s latent knowledge state based on observed interactions. Formally, let
\mathcal{X}=\{(q_{1},a_{1}),(q_{2},a_{2}),\ldots,(q_{t},a_{t})\}
denote a sequence of question-answer pairs, where
q_{i}\in\mathcal{Q}
represents a question from the question space
\mathcal{Q}
, and
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
represents the student’s answer from the answer space
\mathcal{A}
. The goal of KT is to learn a function that predicts the probability of correctly answering a future question
q_{t+1}
given the historical interaction sequence:

P(a_{t+1}|q_{t+1},\mathcal{X})
.

In practice, each interaction
(q_{i},a_{i})
in the dataset typically contains richer information. Questions
q_{i}
often include metadata about the underlying knowledge constructs they assess (e.g., specific skills, concepts, or learning objectives). For example, a mathematics question might be tagged with constructs like "quadratic equations" or "algebraic factoring." Similarly, observed answers
a_{i}
can vary in granularity—from binary correctness indicators to specific answer choices in multiple-choice questions, or complete textual/code responses for open-ended questions. Many KT datasets also capture additional contextual information such as response time or self-reported confidence scores, which can be leveraged by KT models.

The prediction target can also extend beyond binary correctness. Models may predict specific answer choices a student will select (see for example Task 2 of Eedi’s NeurIPS 2020 Education Challenge

[
30
]
), enabling more nuanced understanding of misconceptions. In domains like programming education, models might predict the exact code a student will write
[
14
]
, capturing finer-grained knowledge states.

A.2
Knowledge Tracing Paradigms

Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT).

Introduced by
[
5
]
, BKT models student knowledge as a set of binary latent variables corresponding to specific skills or concepts. For each skill, a student is either in a "known" or "unknown" state. BKT employs a Hidden Markov Model to update these probabilities based on observed student responses, accounting for learning, guessing, and slipping in its probabilistic framework. This approach provides interpretable outputs, with each concept having a probability of mastery, but is limited in modeling the complex interdependencies between skills.

Deep Learning Approaches.

Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT)
[
20
]
marked a paradigm shift by using recurrent neural networks to model student knowledge as continuous latent representations. Unlike BKT’s discrete skill probabilities, DKT captures complex patterns and skill relationships through high-dimensional embeddings, significantly improving prediction accuracy. Subsequent models have enhanced this approach, including: Dynamic Key-Value Memory Networks (DKVMN)
[
34
]
which incorporate memory components for better concept tracking; Self-Attentive Knowledge Tracing (SAKT)
[
19
]
and Attentive Knowledge Tracing (AKT)
[
6
]
which leverage attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies; and Graph-based Knowledge Tracing models
[
17
,
33
]
which incorporate structural information into knowledge representations.

A.3
Current Challenges

Interpretability vs. Accuracy Trade-off.

Deep learning approaches typically achieve higher predictive accuracy but at the cost of interpretability. Their latent representations are opaque high-dimensional vectors that provide limited insight into specific concepts a student has mastered or misconceptions they hold. This limits the usability of these models in practice.

Why do we need interpretability?

At first glance, predictive accuracy alone might seem sufficient for KT—an accurate model could, for example, analyze student responses across many questions to identify overall strengths and weaknesses. However, even highly accurate models can yield misleading or fragile predictions if the underlying reasoning is opaque. For instance,
[
22
]
showed that predictive accuracy alone might obscure models relying on superficial patterns like repeated questions, limiting practical usability. Thus, without interpretability, accurate models may fail to meaningfully support educators in diagnosing and intervening in students’ learning processes.

Cold-start Problem.

KT systems struggle with new students or questions where historical data is limited or non-existent: for any new question added to the dataset, traditional models require the observation of answers from many students to learn an accurate latent representation for this question. This "cold-start" problem poses significant challenges for the practical deployment of KT systems, particularly in dynamic educational environments where new content and students are regularly introduced.

Appendix C
Extended Discussion

C.1
Extended Discussion

Knowledge Tracing as an Inverse Problem

The reframing of Knowledge Tracing as an inverse problem represents a fundamental shift in how we conceptualize student modeling. Traditional KT methods adopt a forward modeling approach, where the goal is to learn a mapping from observed interactions to future performance predictions. While effective for prediction, this treats the student’s knowledge state as an opaque latent variable that serves primarily as an intermediate computational step. In contrast, our inverse problem formulation explicitly seeks to recover the underlying knowledge state that generates observed behaviors, making interpretability the primary objective rather than an afterthought. This aligns with educational assessment principles, where the main objective is to understand what a student knows, rather than merely predicting future behavior.

Parallel with Kolmogorov Complexity

Our inverse problem formulation of Knowledge Tracing draws a natural parallel with algorithmic information theory. The Kolmogorov complexity
K(x)
of a string
x
is defined as the length of the shortest program that outputs
x
when run on a universal Turing machine. Traditional KT methods focus on learning statistical regularities between questions and responses, without looking for parsimonious explanations for observed behavior. In contrast, LBMs explicitly search for minimal natural language descriptions that can both reconstruct past interactions and predict future responses. The knowledge state summary
\mathcal{S}
is akin to the minimal program, and the decoder LLM functions is akin to the universal interpreter that unpacks
\mathcal{S}
to generate the observed question-answer patterns. This connection suggests that effective knowledge state summaries should capture the algorithmic essence of student behavior—the underlying "program" of knowledge and misconceptions that generates observable responses—rather than merely fitting surface-level patterns. While true Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable, LBMs approximate this ideal through the natural language bottleneck constraint, encouraging summaries that balance compression with predictive power.

Computational Architecture and Design Choices

The encoder-decoder architecture reflects a deliberate separation of concerns: extraction versus interpretation of knowledge states. Our experiments demonstrate that these two tasks have different intrinsic complexity, with encoding (summary generation) being more challenging than decoding (prediction from summaries). This finding has practical implications for model selection and computational resource allocation. The use of GRPO for encoder training represents a novel application of reinforcement learning to interpretable AI, where the reward signal directly measures the downstream utility of explanations rather than their surface-level quality.

C.2
Extended Limitations

Context Length Constraints

A practical limitation of LBMs is the context length restrictions of current LLMs. Eventhough modern models can handle thousands of tokens, comprehensive student histories in real educational settings can easily exceed these limits. As student trajectories grow more complex and include more information, the context required for the encoder might go beyond what current LLMs are capable of. A natural solution could be an iterative encoding process, where the encoder iterates over the text bottleneck while going over the input data by windows.

Computational Cost and Resource Requirements

Since LBMs involve two LLMs as the encoder and decoder, they are typically more computationally intensive to run than traditional KT methods. Each inference call with LBMs requires two LLM calls (encoding and decoding) with extensive context windows. Training LBMs with GRPO is particularly costly, as it requires iteratively generating and evaluating multiple candidate summaries per training example. This cost structure may limit the practical deployment of LBMs to high-stakes educational contexts where the interpretability benefits justify the computational expense.

Dependence on Textual Question Content

Many existing KT datasets provide only question identifiers rather than full question text. This limits the direct applicability of LBMs, as they require the questions content in order to generate meaningful summaries about student knowledge. While question content is increasingly available in modern educational platforms, this dependency creates a barrier to applying LBMs to historical datasets or systems that rely primarily on item response theory frameworks.

Quasi-Knowledge State Assumption

The quasi-static knowledge state assumption underlying our inverse problem formulation, while reasonable for short diagnostic sessions, does not cover longer time horizons. Real learning involves continuous knowledge acquisition, forgetting, and misconception evolution that our current framework cannot capture. This limitation restricts current LBMs to diagnostic rather than contexts of assessment throughout the learning process. The assumption also fails to account for contextual factors (fatigue, motivation, external stressors) that can significantly impact student performance within even short sessions.

C.3
Extended Future Work

Iterative Encoding Strategies

A natural extension to address context length limitations involves iteratively applying the encoder while chunking input data. At each step, the encoder would process a portion of the interaction history
X
along with one or more previously generated bottleneck summaries, creating an updated summary that incorporates new information from the latest chunk. This approach could maintain both detailed recent context and compressed historical patterns, enabling LBMs to handle arbitrarily long student trajectories through sequential refinement of knowledge state representations.

Active Learning and Question Selection

Building on iterative encoding, active learning strategies could provide encoders with the most informative input questions. Rather than processing all available interactions, the system could strategically select questions that maximize information gain about uncertain aspects of student knowledge. This could leverage uncertainty quantification methods or use the encoder LLM itself to recommend the most diagnostically valuable questions. Such active sensing would improve both efficiency and diagnostic power by focusing computational resources on the most insightful student responses.

Dynamic Knowledge State Modeling

Extending LBMs to handle evolving knowledge states represents a significant next step necessary to account for progressive learning and forgetting effects. A natural relaxation of the static knowledge state assumption involves "piecewise-constant" knowledge states that can evolve between question sessions but remain static within sessions. This extension poses exciting challenges in developing training objectives that balance within-session consistency with between-session learning dynamics, potentially requiring new approaches to temporal modeling in natural language representations.

Multimodal Input Integration

Question-answer pairs represent a relatively narrow information source for inferring student knowledge states. Richer data sources such as student-tutor interactions, self-reported explanations for answers, timing patterns, or hint usage could provide deeper insights into student understanding. While LBMs naturally extend to text-based inputs, future work should investigate optimal strategies for combining these varied data sources and evaluate their relative contributions to knowledge state inference accuracy and interpretability.

Pedagogical Alignment

Recent work like LearnLM
[
28
]
demonstrates the potential for making LLMs more pedagogically aligned. Incorporating similar pedagogical principles into LBM components could help encoders better interpret educational interactions and generate summaries that align with expert teaching practices. This might involve training on educator-annotated examples, incorporating educational taxonomies into summary structure, or using reward functions that emphasize pedagogically relevant aspects of student knowledge states. Such alignment could bridge the gap between computational convenience and educational validity.
