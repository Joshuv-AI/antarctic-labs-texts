Strongly correlated Josephson junction: proximity effect in the single-layer Hubbard model
Don Rolih, Rok Žitko

Abstract
We study the proximity effect in the Hubbard model coupled to BCS superconductors describing a single-layer strongly correlated electron system in a phase-biased Josephson junction. We find two distinct gapped solutions, one Mott-like insulating (M-phase) and one proximitized superconducting phase (S-phase), separated by first-order transition with hysteresis. In the M-phase the large correlation charge gap strongly suppresses the critical current, while the S-phase behaves as a 0 0 -junction, with a proximitized gap that closes for ϕ = π \phi=\pi to yield a correlated metal. Phase bias and junction transparency can thus serve as tuning knobs to switch between conducting and insulating regimes. Working within the dynamical mean field theory using the numerical renormalization group as the impurity solver, we associate M- and S-phase solutions with the doublet and singlet fixed points of the underlying superconducting Anderson impurity problem. We obtain detailed insight into the spectral structure on all energy scales. In the M-phase, the self-energy has sub-gap resonances symmetrically located around the Fermi level resulting from the splitting of the “mid-gap pole” found in Mott insulators; this structure accounts for phase insensitivity.

Discussion.

The two distinct phases in the proximitized Hubbard model can be interpreted from two different perspectives: 1) as extensions of the metallic and Mott insulating phases of the isolated Hubbard region to finite proximity coupling
\Gamma
, and 2) as the singlet and doublet solutions of the effective quantum impurity problem (SAIM) in the DMFT picture. While this connection may appear natural, it is nontrivial: the self-consistency condition significantly modifies the effective hybridization function in the SAIM compared to the conventional impurity problem
1
, with drastic effects. In the quantum dot SAIM, the doublet ground state corresponds to a
\pi
-junction that still carries a sign-reversed Josephson current with somewhat weaker
\phi
-dispersion compared to the singlet ground state
45
;
50
. In the lattice problem, the self-consistency instead produces almost complete suppression of the critical current in the M-phase. The Mott charge gap provides a qualitatively different barrier than a band gap in conventional SIS junctions. In the Mott case, the gap is dynamically generated by the self-energy pole structure, and the absence of low-energy quasiparticle states means that the superconducting phase information cannot propagate across the barrier. This distinction between a single-particle gap and a correlation-driven gap is fundamental and should persist beyond single-site DMFT, since it relies on the local self-energy structure. Another striking effect occurs in the
\phi\to\pi
limit. In the impurity problem, the doublet phase persists to arbitrarily large
\Gamma
at
\phi=\pi
(the "doublet chimney"
5
;
38
;
55
), i.e., the spin cannot be screened. In the correlated junction problem, the proximitized gap in the S-phase collapses and one finds a correlated metal, while the M-phase is unaffected, which is a remnant of the doublet-chimney physics.

Several aspects deserve further investigation. We have restricted the analysis to half-filling, where particle-hole symmetry pins the chemical potential and maximizes the Mott gap. Away from half-filling, the Mott gap closes and the system becomes a doped correlated metal; the interplay between doping-induced carriers and the proximity effect is an open question. Nonlocal correlations beyond single-site DMFT on finite-dimensional lattices could modify the quantitative phase boundaries but are not expected to alter the qualitative distinction between M and S phases, which is rooted in the local self-energy structure. Magnetic ordering could introduce additional competing phases.

Our results are directly relevant to recent experimental platforms in van der Waals heterostructures involving Mott insulating layers sandwiched between superconducting contacts. The suppression of the Josephson current in the M-phase could be observed as an unusually small critical current compared to junctions with non-correlated barriers. The coexistence region in the phase diagram implies hysteretic switching between high and low critical current states as a function of parameters that tune
U/\Gamma
, such as pressure or interlayer spacing.

\epsilon^{\parallel}_{\alpha\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{\parallel}}
is the in-plane dispersion relation for a two-dimensional plane
\epsilon^{\parallel}_{\alpha\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{\parallel}}=-2t(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})
from now on taken to be independent of the plane index
\alpha
.
From
eq.

5
, one can see that the inverse of the non-interacting lattice Green’s function as a matrix in plane indices is a tridiagonal matrix.

The Dyson equation for the interacting lattice Green’s function in real-space is then

G_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)=G_{\alpha\beta}^{0}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)+\sum_{\gamma}G_{\alpha\gamma}^{0}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)\Sigma_{\gamma}(z)G_{\gamma\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)

(6)

where we have used the locality of the self-energy in
eq.

4
.
The equation for its matrix elements is then

\left[z+\mu-\Sigma_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}\right]G_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)+VG_{\alpha+1,\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)+VG_{\alpha-1,\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}.

(7)

This is a tridiagonal system of coupled equations for the lattice Green’s function
\bm{\mathrm{G}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)
with indices in
z
-direction
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
which can be solved directly via continued fraction expansion
13
;
17
.
Of course, in the actual implementation, one needs to truncate the system to a finite number of planes labeled by
\alpha=1,2,\ldots,N
and impose appropriate boundary conditions at the edges.
The local Green’s function on plane
\alpha
is expressed in terms of two continued fractions
L_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)
and
R_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)
as

G_{\alpha\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)=\frac{1}{L_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)+R_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)-Z_{\alpha}+\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}},

(8)

with
Z_{\alpha}(z)\equiv z+\mu-\Sigma_{\alpha}(z)
.
The left and right continued fractions for
1\leq\alpha\leq N
are defined by the following expressions

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}(\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}},z)=Z_{\alpha}(z)-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\cfrac{V^{2}}{Z_{\alpha-1}(z)-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\cfrac{V^{2}}{Z_{\alpha-2}(z)-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\cfrac{\ddots}{Z_{1}(z)-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\cfrac{V^{2}}{\frac{1}{2}(z+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(z+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})^{2}-4}}}}},

(9)

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}},z)=Z_{\alpha}(z)-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\cfrac{V^{2}}{Z_{\alpha+1}(z)-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\cfrac{V^{2}}{Z_{\alpha+2}(z)-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\cfrac{\ddots}{Z_{N}(z)-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\cfrac{V^{2}}{\frac{1}{2}(z+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(z+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})^{2}-4}}}}},

(10)

where the boundary conditions are set by assuming semi-infinite metallic leads at both ends of the interacting region

L_{0}(\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}},z)=R_{N+1}(\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}},z)=g_{1d}^{-1}(z,\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})=\frac{1}{2}(z+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(z+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})^{2}-4V^{2}};

(11)

the choice of sign in front of the square root is enforced by causality.

Now, let us consider the case of a single active layer. Using the same boundary conditions of semi-infinite metallic leads on both sides, the matrix inverse of the lattice Green’s function from
eq.

7
is

\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1d}(z-\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})&V&0\\
V&z+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\Sigma_{1}(z)&V\\
0&V&g_{1d}(z-\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})\end{pmatrix};

(12)

using the Schur complement formula [or directly
eq.

8
] the active layer’s local Green’s function (local in real-space) is

G_{11}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel},z)=\frac{1}{z+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}}-\Sigma_{1}(z)-2V^{2}g_{1d}(z-\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}})}.

(13)

This is exactly the same as the Green’s function of a single-layer Hubbard model with an additional self-energy term originating from the coupling to two semi-infinite metallic leads acting as reservoirs.
The last step is the sum over the in-plane momenta
\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\parallel}
to obtain the local Green’s function on the active layer which is performed as an integral over the non-interacting density of states for the two-dimensional square lattice

G_{11}(z)=\int\dif\epsilon\rho_{2d}(\epsilon)\frac{1}{z+\mu-\epsilon-\Sigma_{1}(z)-2V^{2}g_{1d}(z-\epsilon)}.

(14)

Instead of formulating a fully self-consistent inhomogeneous DMFT, we can simply model the effect of the electronic reservoirs via an additional self-energy term in the lattice Green’s function (see Ref.
3
).
On the level of the Hamiltonian, this corresponds to adding a coupling to a non-interacting bath to each site of the lattice

H_{i}^{\mathrm{c}}=\gamma\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},\sigma}(c_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{i,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.})+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},\sigma}\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}a_{i,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{i,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}.

(15)

Here,
a_{i,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}^{\dagger}
creates an electron in the bath coupled to site
i
with momentum
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
and spin
\sigma
and
\gamma=V
is the effective hybridization strength between the lattice site and the bath.

The Green’s function of the non-interacting bath is

g_{\mathrm{bath}}(z)=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\frac{1}{z-\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}}

(16)

and the effect of the bath on the lattice Green’s function is then an additional self-energy term on each lattice site

\Sigma_{\mathrm{bath}}(z)=V^{2}g_{\mathrm{bath}}(z).

(17)

In DMFT, the lattice model is mapped onto an effective impurity model. The impurity propagator is

G_{\mathrm{imp}}(z)=\frac{1}{z+\mu-\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}(z)-\Sigma_{U}(z)}

(18)

and the self-consistency condition is
G_{\mathrm{imp}}(z)=G_{\mathrm{loc}}(z)
, where
G_{\mathrm{loc}}(z)
is the local lattice Green’s function including the additional self-energy term from the bath.

In paramagnetic DMFT the lattice enters the problem only through its density of states. As concerns the Mott MIT, the detailed form of the DOS matters little
8
. For simplicity, instead of the square lattice DOS we thus take the Bethe lattice DOS. For DMFT calculations on the Bethe lattice, the self-consistency condition simplifies and the effective impurity hybridization function is

\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}(z)=t^{2}G_{\mathrm{loc}}(z)+\Sigma_{\mathrm{bath}}(z),

(19)

where
t
is the hopping parameter of the Bethe lattice.

In the following we will use the extension of this approach to the superconducing (SC) case using Nambu formalism.

II
Hamiltonian, superconducting region description, Nambu form

The system is composed of a strongly-correlated region weakly coupled to two SC regions.
The strongly-correlated region is modeled by the Hubbard Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{scr}}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}(d_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}d_{j\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.})-\mu\sum_{i,\sigma}d_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}d_{i\sigma}+U\sum_{i}n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow},

(20)

where
d_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}
creates an electron of spin
\sigma
on site
i
of the Bethe lattice,
t
is the nearest-neighbor hopping parameter,
\mu
is the chemical potential, and
U>0
is the on-site Coulomb repulsion. The Bethe lattice non-interacting density of states (DOS) is given by

\rho(\epsilon)=\frac{\sqrt{4t^{2}-\epsilon^{2}}}{2\pi t^{2}},\quad|\epsilon|<2t.

(21)

The coupling to SC regions
\lambda=\mathrm{L},\mathrm{R}
for each Bethe lattice site
i
of the Hubbard model is described by the Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{sc},\lambda}=V_{\lambda}\sum_{i,\bm{\mathrm{q}},\sigma}[(d_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}+\text{h.c.})+\sum_{i}H^{\mathrm{BCS}}_{i,\lambda}[c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}],

(22)

or, for a single lattice site
i
,

H_{\mathrm{sc},i\lambda}=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},\sigma}V_{\lambda}(d_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.})+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},\sigma}\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\left(\Delta_{\lambda}c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\lambda,-\bm{\mathrm{q}}\downarrow}^{\dagger}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right).

(23)

Here
\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}
is the dispersion relation in the superconductor,
\Delta_{\lambda}=|\Delta_{\lambda}|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{\lambda}}
is the SC order parameter with amplitude
|\Delta_{\lambda}|
and phase
\phi_{\lambda}
.

Defining the Nambu spinors
\psi_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\dagger}=(c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\uparrow}^{\dagger},c_{i\lambda,-\bm{\mathrm{q}}\downarrow})
for the SC region and
\phi_{i}^{\dagger}=(d_{i\uparrow}^{\dagger},d_{i\downarrow})
for the strongly-correlated region, the Hamiltonian can be written as

H_{\mathrm{sc},i\lambda}^{\mathrm{c}}=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}V_{\lambda}(\phi_{i}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}\psi_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}+\mathrm{h.c.})+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\psi_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{h}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\psi_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}},

(24)

with
\bm{\mathrm{h}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}
defined as

\bm{\mathrm{h}}_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}&\Delta_{\lambda}\\
\Delta_{\lambda}^{*}&-\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\end{pmatrix},

(25)

and
\tau_{3}
is the Pauli matrix Z.
The Green’s function of the superconducting region is given by

\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}(z)=\left[z\bm{1}-\bm{\mathrm{h}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\right]^{-1}=\frac{1}{z^{2}-(\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2})}\begin{pmatrix}z+\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}&\Delta_{\lambda}\\
\Delta_{\lambda}^{*}&z-\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\end{pmatrix}.

(26)

Assuming a constant DOS
\rho_{\mathrm{SC}}
in the SC region with half-bandwidth
D_{\mathrm{SC}}
, the integration over momenta
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
gives

\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda}(z)=-\frac{2\rho_{\mathrm{SC}}}{\sqrt{|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}-z^{2}}}\arctan\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{SC}}}{\sqrt{|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}-z^{2}}}\right)\begin{pmatrix}z&\Delta_{\lambda}\\
\Delta_{\lambda}^{*}&z\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

The effect of the SC regions on the Hubbard layer is an additional self-energy term on each lattice site

\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}(z)=\sum_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}^{2}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda}(z)\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}.

(28)

In the case of symmetric coupling,
V_{L}=V_{R}=V
, equal superconducting gaps
|\Delta_{L}|=|\Delta_{R}|=|\Delta|
and phases
\phi_{L}=-\phi_{R}=\phi/2
, this becomes

\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}(z)=-\frac{\Gamma}{\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Delta|^{2}-z^{2}}}\arctan\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{SC}}}{\sqrt{|\Delta|^{2}-z^{2}}}\right)\begin{pmatrix}z&-|\Delta|\cos(\phi/2)\\
-|\Delta|\cos(\phi/2)&z\end{pmatrix}.

(29)

We have defined the hybridization strength as

\Gamma=2\pi\rho_{\mathrm{SC}}(0)V^{2}.

(30)

In the wide-band limit,
D_{\mathrm{SC}}\to\infty
, and for
\phi=0
, we recover the standard superconducting hybridization function for a single SC contact:

\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}(z)=-\frac{\Gamma}{\sqrt{|\Delta|^{2}-z^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}z&-|\Delta|\\
-|\Delta|&z\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

III
Effective impurity model, self-consistency, discretization

The interacting lattice Green’s function for the Hubbard model in Nambu space is given by

\bm{\mathrm{G}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}d_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\uparrow};d_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\uparrow}^{\dagger}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\omega}&\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}d_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\uparrow};d_{-\bm{\mathrm{k}}\downarrow}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\omega}\\
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}d_{-\bm{\mathrm{k}}\downarrow}^{\dagger};d_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\uparrow}^{\dagger}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\omega}&\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}d_{-\bm{\mathrm{k}}\downarrow}^{\dagger};d_{-\bm{\mathrm{k}}\downarrow}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\omega}\end{pmatrix}

(32)

with the standard notation
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}A;B\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\omega}=-\mathrm{i}\int\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\Theta(t)\langle[A(t),B]\rangle\dif t
. The off-diagonal (anomalous) elements are non-zero only in the presence of SC symmetry breaking. For the repulsive Hubbard model considered here, the SC symmetry breaking arises from the proximity effect of the SC regions when
\Gamma>0
. (For attractive Hubbard model with
U<0
, the SC symmetry breaks spontaneously
6
.)

The interacting Green’s function including the effect of the superconducting regions is

\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega)=\omega\bm{1}-(\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}-\mu)\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}-\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}(\omega)-\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{U}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega),

(33)

where
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}
is given by
eq.

29
and
\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}
is the dispersion relation for the Bethe lattice.

We use dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) to solve the interacting lattice model.
The self-energy
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{U}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega)
is local

\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{U}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega)\approx\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{U}(\omega).

(34)

This is an exact expression for the Bethe lattice
20
.
The effective impurity model is the superconducting Anderson impurity model (SAIM)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{\mathrm{imp}}+H_{\mathrm{bath}}+H_{\mathrm{hyb}},

(35)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{imp}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{d}\sum_{\sigma}d_{\sigma}^{\dagger}d_{\sigma}+Un_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow},

(36)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{bath}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}},\sigma}\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}c_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\sigma}+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}\left(\Delta_{\mathrm{bath},\bm{\mathrm{k}}}c_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{-\bm{\mathrm{k}}\downarrow}^{\dagger}+\mathrm{h.c.}\right),

(37)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{hyb}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}},\sigma}V_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}(d_{\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.}).

(38)

Here,
\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}
,
\Delta_{\mathrm{bath},\bm{\mathrm{k}}}
and
V_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}
are the bath parameters to be determined self-consistently.
The non-interacting Green’s function of the impurity model in Nambu space is

\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{0,\mathrm{imp}}^{-1}(\omega)=\omega\bm{1}-\varepsilon_{d}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}-\bm{\mathrm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega).

(39)

The effective Weiss field
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}_{0}
is a matrix in Nambu space.
The DMFT self-consistency equation is

\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}_{0}^{-1}(\omega)=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{-1}(\omega)+\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(\omega).

(40)

We use the NRG to calculate the self-energy
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{U}(\omega)
of the effective impurity model.
From this we can obtain the local lattice Green’s function
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\omega)
using the Hilbert transform (see next section).
Using the self-consistency condition we can then update the effective hybridization function as

\bm{\mathrm{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\omega)=t^{2}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\omega)\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}+\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}(\omega).

(41)

The model partly accounts for the back-action of Hubbard layer on the superconductors. It does not include the local renormalization of the superconducting gap, which requires adding “buffer” layers with attractive interaction and a self-consistent calculation
15
;
35
;
16
;
17
. Phenomena in buffer layers add further complexity to the problem
15
and can be quantitatively important, but should not affect the dynamical correlation effects in the Hubbard layer that are the focus of this work. On the other hand, the inverse proximity effect is fully included in our method, because the calculation is based on an exact mapping with no further approximations, as explained in this section. For this reason, in the section on calculating the total system energy, we will include the corresponding energy terms. In other words, in our approach we take into account the back-action at the level of Green’s functions, but not at the level of Hamiltonian parameters.

IV
Hilbert transform in matrix space

Using the Nambu matrix form of the interaction self-energy
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega)
, the inverse of the interacting lattice Green’s function for the proximitized Hubbard model can be written as

\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega)=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{0}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega)-\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega)=\omega\bm{1}-(\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}-\mu)\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}-\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}-\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{U}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega).

(42)

The effect of the SC leads is to add an extra self-energy term
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}
to the expression of the standard Hubbard model.

The local Green’s function is obtained by summing over all momenta
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
for every matrix element

\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}\bm{\mathrm{G}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\omega)=\int\dif\varepsilon\rho(\varepsilon)\bm{\mathrm{G}}(\varepsilon,\omega)=\int\dif\varepsilon\rho(\varepsilon)\left[\omega\bm{1}-(\varepsilon-\mu)\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}-\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}(\omega)-\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{U}(\omega)\right]^{-1}.

(43)

Here,
\rho
is the non-interacting DOS. Following Ref.
6
, we first perform partial fraction decomposition of the integrand. We define

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(\omega)=\omega+\mu-\Sigma_{11}(\omega),

(44)

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}(\omega)=\omega-\mu-\Sigma_{22}(\omega),

(45)

where
\Sigma_{ij}
denotes the component of the matrix
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}=\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{U}+\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}
in row
i
and column
j
. Entries of the matrix
\bm{\mathrm{G}}(\varepsilon,\omega)
can then be written as

\displaystyle G_{11}(\varepsilon,\omega)=\frac{\zeta_{2}+\varepsilon}{(\zeta_{1}-\varepsilon)(\zeta_{2}+\varepsilon)-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{21}},

(46)

\displaystyle G_{12}(\varepsilon,\omega)=\frac{\Sigma_{12}}{(\zeta_{1}-\varepsilon)(\zeta_{2}+\varepsilon)-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{21}},

(47)

\displaystyle G_{21}(\varepsilon,\omega)=\frac{\Sigma_{21}}{(\zeta_{1}-\varepsilon)(\zeta_{2}+\varepsilon)-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{21}},

(48)

\displaystyle G_{22}(\varepsilon,\omega)=\frac{\zeta_{1}-\varepsilon}{(\zeta_{1}-\varepsilon)(\zeta_{2}+\varepsilon)-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{21}},

(49)

The roots of the denominator are

\varepsilon_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2}\pm\sqrt{(\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2})^{2}-4\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{21}}\right)

(50)

and the partial fraction decomposition yields

\displaystyle G_{11}(\varepsilon,\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta_{2}+\varepsilon_{+}}{(\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-})(\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon)}+\frac{\zeta_{2}+\varepsilon_{-}}{(\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon_{+})(\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon)}=\frac{A_{11}}{\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon}+\frac{B_{11}}{\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon},

(51)

\displaystyle G_{12}(\varepsilon,\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma_{12}}{(\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-})(\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon)}+\frac{\Sigma_{12}}{(\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon_{+})(\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon)}=\frac{A_{12}}{\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon}+\frac{B_{12}}{\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon},

(52)

\displaystyle G_{21}(\varepsilon,\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma_{21}}{(\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-})(\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon)}+\frac{\Sigma_{21}}{(\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon_{+})(\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon)}=\frac{A_{21}}{\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon}+\frac{B_{21}}{\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon},

(53)

\displaystyle G_{22}(\varepsilon,\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta_{1}-\varepsilon_{+}}{(\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-})(\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon)}+\frac{\zeta_{1}-\varepsilon_{-}}{(\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon_{+})(\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon)}=\frac{A_{22}}{\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon}+\frac{B_{22}}{\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon},

(54)

where we have defined
A_{ij}
as

\displaystyle A_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta_{2}+\varepsilon_{+}}{\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-}},\quad A_{12}=\frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-}},

(56)

\displaystyle A_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma_{21}}{\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-}},\quad A_{22}=\frac{\zeta_{1}-\varepsilon_{+}}{\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-}}

(57)

and
B_{ij}
are obtained from
A_{ij}
through the replacement
\varepsilon_{+}\leftrightarrow\varepsilon_{-}

\displaystyle B_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta_{2}+\varepsilon_{-}}{\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon_{+}},\quad B_{12}=\frac{\Sigma_{12}}{\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon_{+}},

(58)

\displaystyle B_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma_{21}}{\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon_{+}},\quad B_{22}=\frac{\zeta_{1}-\varepsilon_{-}}{\varepsilon_{-}-\varepsilon_{+}}.

(59)

The integral in
eq.

43
can now be performed using the standard Hilbert transform defined as

\mathcal{H}[f](z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dif\varepsilon\frac{f(\varepsilon)}{z-\varepsilon}.

(60)

The final expressions for the components of the local Green’s function are

\displaystyle[\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\omega)]_{11}=A_{11}\mathcal{H}[\rho](\varepsilon_{+})+B_{11}\mathcal{H}[\rho](\varepsilon_{-}),

(61)

\displaystyle[\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\omega)]_{12}=A_{12}\mathcal{H}[\rho](\varepsilon_{+})+B_{12}\mathcal{H}[\rho](\varepsilon_{-}),

(62)

\displaystyle[\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\omega)]_{21}=A_{21}\mathcal{H}[\rho](\varepsilon_{+})+B_{21}\mathcal{H}[\rho](\varepsilon_{-}),

(63)

\displaystyle[\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\omega)]_{22}=A_{22}\mathcal{H}[\rho](\varepsilon_{+})+B_{22}\mathcal{H}[\rho](\varepsilon_{-}).

(64)

For Bethe lattice DOS, the Hilbert transform can be evaluated analytically as

\mathcal{H}[\rho](z)=\frac{1}{2t^{2}}\left(z-\mathrm{i}\sign(\mathrm{Im}(z))\sqrt{4t^{2}-z^{2}}\right).

(65)

V
Energy of the lattice model

The expectation value of the Hamiltonian can be expressed in terms of local quantities obtained from the DMFT solution.
It has four contributions: the kinetic energy of the strongly-correlated region, the interaction energy of the strongly-correlated region, the coupling energy between the strongly-correlated region and SC regions, and the energy of the bulk SC regions.

The kinetic energy per site of the strongly-correlated region can be written as
20

\frac{E_{\mathrm{kin}}}{N}=T\Tr\int\dif\varepsilon\rho(\varepsilon)\varepsilon\bm{\mathrm{G}}(\varepsilon,\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}),

(66)

where
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
is the local Green’s function,
\omega_{n}
are the fermionic Matsubara frequencies, and the trace is both a Matsubara index summation and a matrix trace in Nambu space.
On the real-frequency axis, this becomes

\frac{E_{\mathrm{kin}}}{N}=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dif\omega n_{f}(\omega)\int\dif\varepsilon\rho(\varepsilon)\varepsilon\left(-\frac{1}{\pi}\right)\mathrm{Im}\,[\bm{\mathrm{G}}(\varepsilon,\omega)]_{11},

(67)

where the factor of
2
accounts for both spin species,
n_{f}(\omega)
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution function, and
\bm{\mathrm{G}}(\varepsilon,\omega)
is the full interacting lattice Green’s function.

The interacting energy per site of the strongly-correlated region can be calculated directly from the expectation value of the interaction term:

\frac{E_{\mathrm{int}}}{N}=\frac{U}{N}\sum_{i}\langle n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow}\rangle=U\langle n_{\uparrow}n_{\downarrow}\rangle.

(68)

For the coupling term between the strongly-correlated region and the SC regions, we need the expression for the mixed Green’s function. Using the equation of motion (EOM), this can be expressed as

\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\omega)\equiv\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\phi_{i},\psi^{\dagger}_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\omega}=V_{\lambda}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}\tau_{3}\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}},

(69)

where
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
is the local Green’s function of the strongly-correlated region and
\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}
is the Green’s function of the SC region given in
eq.

27
.
The expectation value is

\frac{E_{\mathrm{hyb},\mathrm{sc}}}{N}=\sum_{\lambda=\mathrm{L},\mathrm{R}}2V_{\lambda}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dif\omega n_{f}(\omega)\left(-\frac{1}{\pi}\right)\mathrm{Im}\,\Tr[\bm{\mathrm{G}}\tau_{3}\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda}\tau_{3}].

(70)

Using the symmetric choice of
V_{\lambda}
and
\Delta_{\lambda}
as before, this becomes

\frac{E_{\mathrm{hyb}}}{N}=2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dif\omega n_{f}(\omega)\left(-\frac{1}{\pi}\right)\mathrm{Im}\,\Tr\left[\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\omega)\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}(\omega)\right].

(71)

The SC region contributions are

\langle H_{i\lambda}^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},\sigma}\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\langle c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma}\rangle+\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\left(\Delta_{\lambda}\langle c_{i\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\lambda,-\bm{\mathrm{q}}\downarrow}^{\dagger}\rangle+\mathrm{h.c.}\right)=\langle H_{i\lambda,\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle+\langle H_{i\lambda,\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle.

(72)

This needs to be evaluated by taking into account the coupling to the strongly-correlated, even if we otherwise neglect the back-action of the interacting layer (the neglect of back-action merely implies that the SC Hamiltonians are fixed, rather than being self-consistently updated, but the inverse proximity effect is fully included in our calculation). The dressed Green’s function of the SC reservoir is (using EOMs):

\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{g}}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}(z)=\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}+V_{\lambda}^{2}\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}},

(73)

where
\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}
is the (bare) reservoir Green’s function, see
eq.

26
, and
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is the local Green’s function of the strongly-correlated region.
Considering only the second term, which arises from the coupling to this region, we get for the normal component

\langle H_{i\lambda,n}^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle=2V^{2}_{\lambda}\int\dif\omega n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega)\int\dif\varepsilon\rho(\varepsilon)\varepsilon\left(-\frac{1}{\pi}\right)\mathrm{Im}\,[\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}]_{11}

(74)

and the anomalous component

\langle H_{i\lambda,a}^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle=2V^{2}_{\lambda}\int\dif\omega n_{\mathrm{F}}(\omega)\int\dif\varepsilon\rho(\varepsilon)\Delta_{\lambda}\left(-\frac{1}{\pi}\right)\mathrm{Im}\,[\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{loc}}\bm{\mathrm{\tau}}_{3}\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{\lambda,\bm{\mathrm{q}}}]_{12}.

(75)

For completeness, we evaluate the constant energy for the isolated SC region (i.e., in the decoupled limit,
V_{\lambda}\to 0
).
We use Green’s functions in
eq.

26
evaluated at fermionic Matsubara frequencies. The first term (normal component) is

\langle H_{i\lambda,\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle=\frac{2}{\beta}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}0^{+}}\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\frac{\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}+\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}}{(\mathrm{i}\omega_{n})^{2}-(\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2})}=-\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\frac{\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{2}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}}}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}}}{2}\right);

(76)

here,
\beta=1/T
is the inverse temperature. For flat DOS and
T=0
, this becomes

\displaystyle\langle H_{i\lambda,\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2D_{\mathrm{SC}}}\int_{-D_{\mathrm{SC}}}^{D_{\mathrm{SC}}}\dif\varepsilon\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}}}=

(77)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2D_{\mathrm{SC}}}\left[D_{\mathrm{SC}}\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{SC}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}}-|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}\arcsinh\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{SC}}}{|\Delta_{\lambda}|}\right)\right].

(78)

The second term (anomalous component) is

\langle H_{i\lambda,\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle=\frac{2|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}}{\beta}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}0^{+}}}{(\mathrm{i}\omega_{n})^{2}-(\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2})}=-\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}\frac{|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}}}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}}}{2}\right).

(79)

At
T=0
and for flat DOS, this becomes

\langle H_{i\lambda,\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle=-\frac{|\Delta_{\lambda}|^{2}}{D_{\mathrm{SC}}}\arcsinh\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{SC}}}{|\Delta_{\lambda}|}\right).

(80)

The final expression for the BCS energy per site (for both regions) is then

\langle H^{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle=-\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{SC}}^{2}+|\Delta|^{2}}-\frac{|\Delta|^{2}}{D_{\mathrm{SC}}}\arcsinh\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{SC}}}{|\Delta|}\right).

(81)

VII
Dependence of the results on technical NRG and DMFT parameters

Unless stated otherwise, we choose the discretization parameter
\Lambda=4
, average the results over four discretization grids
N_{z}=4
and keep the states with energies up to
E=12
in units of rescaled energies, but never less than
600
states. This holds for the calculations presented in the main text as well as for the benchmarks discussed in this section.

To obtain the effective bath parameters for the impurity model, we use the discretization scheme described in Ref.
30
.
This approach allows one to discretize general hybridization functions with off-diagonal components in Nambu space. Our implementation of this algorithm in Julia programming language is available from the GitHub repository
42
.

Due to its nature, NRG performs best for ungapped hybridization functions, where the discretization yields Wilson chains with conventional asymptotic behavior of hopping constants (monotonic exponentially decreasing as
\Lambda^{-n/2})
. Frequency ranges where hybridization function has low values (i.e., spectral gaps) lead instead to unusual scaling of Wilson chain coefficients that break energy-scale separation, potentially resulting in artifacts in the computed spectra. For this reason, but also to fix possible numerical causality violations, one needs to perform “clipping”, i.e., restoring the values of self-energy or the hybridization functions to appropriate minimal values of correct sign. The threshold value needs to be chosen carefully. A too low clipping value may result in uncontrolled artifacts, while a too high clipping value will introduce spurious density of states in the gaps. A compromise must be made. Consequently, one can never obtain a truly gapped solution with zero density of states inside the gap, but instead some residual, and potentially noisy, values. In this work, we have implemented the clipping at the level of self-energy.
For a matrix-valued self-energy
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}
we define its spectral representation as

\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}}}}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\left[\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(\omega+\mathrm{i}\eta)-\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}^{\dagger}(\omega+\mathrm{i}\eta)\right].

(82)

Causality requires that the matrix
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}}(\omega)
is positive semi-definite for every
\omega
. Equivalently, its eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(\omega)
are non-negative. We perform the clipping by diagonalizing
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}}
and requiring
\lambda_{i}\geq\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}
, where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}
is the clipping value, typically set to
10^{-7}
. After obtaining the clipped spectral representation, we can reconstruct the value of the self-energy using Kramers-Kronig transformation

\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{clip}}(\omega)=\int\dif\omega^{\prime}\frac{\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}_{\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}}^{\mathrm{clip}}(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega+\mathrm{i}\eta-\omega^{\prime}}+\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(\infty);

(83)

the last term is the constant Hartree shift of the self-energy.

Another reason for obtaining finite density of states inside the gapped regions is spectral broadening. The raw results in NRG consist of
\delta
peaks that need to be broadened into a continuous spectral function. Typically one uses a log-Gaussian function with a width that is proportional to frequency. The overall width is controlled by parameter
\alpha
. To be able to reduce
\alpha
, one makes use of twist averaging, i.e., one performs the NRG calculation for several interleaved discretization grids, then the results are averaged. This is controlled by parameter
N_{z}
, the number of interleaved grids. The method works best when
N_{z}
is a power of 2, with commonly used values 2, 4, and 8, which represent a good compromise between the improvement in spectral resolution and computational requirements.

To assess the expected magnitude of artifacts due to clipping and broadening, we perform benchmark calculations with variable NRG parameters. The key finding is that the NRG artifacts are similar as finite-temperature effects. The effective temperature is much lower than the spectral gap, thus in practical calculations the gaps can be unambiguously identified and quantified with no difficulty.

In
fig.

, we first show the dependence of the local spectral function on (true) temperature and clipping of the superconducting self-energy
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{\mathrm{BCS}}
for (a, b)
\Gamma=0.05
and (c, d)
\Gamma=0.20
. The values at low frequencies do not depend on temperatures as long as
T\ll\Delta
. We find some differences between the two cases. In the
S
-phase the deviations from the
T=0
results are observed starting from

for clipping parameter
10^{-5}
. By reducing the value of the clipping parameter,
\mathcal{A}_{11}
values in the gap can be reduced by several orders of magnitude, see panel (d). In the M-phase the saturated values are higher and cannot be much reduced.

We also checked how the result change with the broadening parameter
