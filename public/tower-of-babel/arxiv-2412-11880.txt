Eckstein-Ferris-Pennanen-Robinson duality revisited: paramonotonicity, total Fenchel-Rockafellar duality, and the Chambolle-Pock operator
Heinz H. Bauschke, Walaa M. Moursi, and Shambhavi Singh

Abstract
Finding zeros of the sum of two maximally monotone operators involving a continuous linear operator is a central problem in optimization and monotone operator theory. We revisit the duality framework proposed by Eckstein, Ferris, Pennanen, and Robinson from a quarter of a century ago. Paramonotonicity is identified as a broad condition ensuring that saddle points coincide with the closed convex rectangle formed by the primal and dual solutions. Additionally, we characterize total duality in the subdifferential setting and derive projection formulas for sets that arise in the analysis of the Chambolle-Pock algorithm within the recent framework developed by Bredies, Chenchene, Lorenz, and Naldi.

Introduction

Throughout this paper, we assume that

X
and
Y
are real Hilbert spaces, and
L\colon X\to Y
is continuous and linear.

(1)

We also assume that

A
and
B
are maximally monotone on
X
and
Y
, respectively.

(2)

(See, e.g.,
[
3
]
for more on maximally monotone operators.)
A central problem in optimization and variational analysis is to

\text{Find }x\in X\text{ such that }0\in Ax+L^{*}BLx.

(3)

We emphasize that we do not assume that
A+L^{*}BL
or
L^{*}BL
is maximally monotone.
Following Eckstein and Ferris
[
16
]
,
we encode the
(primal) problem

3
by the triple

(A,L,B).

(4)

The
dual problem
is given by

\text{Find $y\in Y$ such that }0\in B^{-1}y-LA^{-1}(-L^{*}y),

(5)

which is now represented by the triple

(B^{-1},-L^{*},A^{-1})
.
This gives rise to the
duality
operation

(A,L,B)^{*}:=(B^{-1},-L^{*},A^{-1})

(6)

which features the pleasant
biduality
(see
[
16
, Section 2.1]
)

(A,L,B)^{**}=(B^{-1},-L^{*},A^{-1})^{*}=\big((A^{-1})^{-1},-(-L^{*})^{*},(B^{-1})^{-1}\big)=(A,L,B).

(7)

The set of solutions to the primal problem is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{sol}(A,L,B)

\displaystyle:=\big\{{x\in X}~\big|~{\text{$x$ solves the problem
given by $(A,L,B)$}}\big\}

(8a)

\displaystyle=\big\{{x\in X}~\big|~{0\in Ax+L^{*}BLx}\big\}.

(8b)

For ease of notation,
we abbreviate the set of the primal and the dual solutions by

Z:=\operatorname{sol}(A,L,B)=\big\{{x\in X}~\big|~{0\in Ax+L^{*}BLx}\big\}

(9)

and

K:=\operatorname{sol}(A,L,B)^{*}=\big\{{y\in Y}~\big|~{0\in B^{-1}y-LA^{-1}(-L^{*}y)}\big\},

(10)

respectively.
Closely related to these notions is the set of

saddle points

(see
Remark

3.2
below for the motivation of this name)
or
primal-dual solutions

for
(A,L,B)
, given by

{\mathbf{S}}:=\big\{{(x,y)\in X\times Y}~\big|~{-L^{*}y\in Ax\land Lx\in B^{-1}y}\big\}.

(11)

The inclusion

{\mathbf{S}}\subseteq Z\times K

(12)

is always true (see
Corollary

3.3
below).
However, equality in
12
may fail as
Eckstein and Ferris (
[
16
, page 69]
) observed:

Example 1.1
.

Suppose that
X=Y=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
L=\operatorname{Id}
,

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
\ 1&0\end{bmatrix},\;\;\text{and}\;\;B=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
\ -1&0\end{bmatrix}.

(13)

Then
A^{-1}=B=-A
,
B^{-1}=A=-B
, and
we have the strict inclusion

{\mathbf{S}}=\operatorname{gra}(-A)\subsetneqq\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}=Z\times K.

(14)

Our goal in this paper is to carefully study the relationship
between the primal problem
3
, the dual problem
5
,
the corresponding solution sets
9
,
10
, and the
set of saddle points
11
.

2
Traversing between primal and dual solutions

The following notation, which induces a way to traverse between
primal and dual solutions, will be used extensively throughout the paper:
Set

(\forall x\in X)\quad K_{x}:=(-L^{-*}Ax)\cap(BLx)\subseteq Y

(15)

and

(\forall y\in Y)\quad Z_{y}:=(L^{-1}B^{-1}y)\cap A^{-1}(-L^{*}y)\subseteq X,

(16)

where
L^{-*}=(L^{*})^{-1}
and the inverse is
in the sense of set-valued analysis.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

y\in K_{x}

\Leftrightarrow

x\in Z_{y}
.

(ii)

L^{*}(K_{x})=(-Ax)\cap L^{*}BLx
and

L(Z_{y})=(B^{-1}y)\cap LA^{-1}(-L^{*}y)
.

(iii)

K_{x}\neq\varnothing

\Leftrightarrow

x\in Z
, and

Z_{y}\neq\varnothing

\Leftrightarrow

y\in K
.

(iv)

K_{x}\subseteq K
and

Z_{y}\subseteq Z
.

(v)

K_{x}
and
Z_{y}
are closed and convex.

Proof
. We have the equivalences

\displaystyle y\in K_{x}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow y\in-L^{-*}Ax\land y\in BLx

(17a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow-L^{*}y\in Ax\land Lx\in B^{-1}y

(17b)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x\in A^{-1}(-L^{*}y)\land x\in L^{-1}B^{-1}y

(17c)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x\in Z_{y},

(17d)

and this proves
(i)
.

We now prove
(ii)
:
First, suppose that
v\in K_{x}
, i.e.,

v\in-L^{-*}Ax
and
v\in BLx
.
It follows that
L^{*}v\in-Ax
and
L^{*}v\in L^{*}BLx
, i.e.,

L^{*}v\in(-Ax)\cap L^{*}BLx
.
We’ve verified that
L^{*}(K_{x})\subseteq(-Ax)\cap L^{*}BLx
.
Conversely, suppose that
u\in(-Ax)\cap L^{*}BLx
.
Then there exists
v\in BLx
such that
u=L^{*}v
.
Hence
-L^{*}v\in Ax
and so
v\in-L^{-*}x
.
Thus
v\in K_{x}
and so
u=L^{*}v\in L^{*}(K_{x})
.
Altogether, we have shown that
L^{*}(K_{x})=(-Ax)\cap L^{*}BLx
.
The proof of the identity

L(Z_{y})=(B^{-1}y)\cap LA^{-1}(-L^{*}y)
is similar.

Turning to
(iii)
and
(iv)
,
suppose that

K_{x}\neq\varnothing
, and let
k\in K_{x}
.
It follows that

k\in-L^{-*}Ax\land k\in BLx.

(18)

First, this implies that

-L^{*}k\in Ax\land L^{*}k\in L^{*}BLx
, which upon adding, yields

x\in Z
.
Second, this also implies

x\in A^{-1}(-L^{*}k)\land Lx\in B^{-1}k
.
Hence
-Lx\in-LA^{-1}(-L^{*}k)\land Lx\in B^{-1}k
.
Upon adding, we learn that
k\in K
.
We have shown that
K_{x}\subseteq K
and this is also trivially true
if
K_{x}=\varnothing
.

Now assume
x\in Z
.
Then there exists
y\in BLx
such that

-L^{*}y\in Ax
, i.e.,
y\in-L^{-*}Ax
.
It follows that
y\in K_{x}
and so
K_{x}\neq\varnothing
.

The remaining statements in
(iii)
and
(iv)

are proved similarly.

Because
A
and
B
are maximally monotone,
the sets

Ax
and
B(Lx)
are convex and closed.
The linearity and continuity of
-L^{*}
then
yields the convexity and closedness of
-L^{-*}Ax
.
Upon intersecting, we obtain convexity and closedness
K_{x}
.
The set
Z_{k}
is treated similarly.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

We now associate with
15
and
16
the following
set-valued operators:

{\mathbf{K}}\colon X\rightrightarrows Y\colon x\mapsto K_{x}\;\;\text{and}\;\;{\mathbf{Z}}\colon Y\rightrightarrows X\colon y\mapsto Z_{y}.

(19)

Corollary 2.2
.

We have

\operatorname{dom}\,{\mathbf{K}}=Z
and
{\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{K}}=K=\bigcup_{z\in Z}K_{z}

(20)

as well as

\operatorname{dom}\,{\mathbf{Z}}=K
and
{\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{Z}}=Z=\bigcup_{k\in K}Z_{k}
.

(21)

Proof
. The domain statements follow from
Proposition

2.1
(iii)
.
This and
Proposition

2.1
(iv)

imply
{\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{K}}=\bigcup_{z\in Z}K_{z}\subseteq K

and
{\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{Z}}=\bigcup_{k\in K}Z_{k}\subseteq Z
.
Now let
k\in K=\operatorname{dom}\,{\mathbf{Z}}
.
Then
Z_{k}\neq\varnothing
.
Let
z\in Z_{k}
.
By
Proposition

2.1
(i)
,

k\in K_{z}\subseteq{\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{K}}
.
Hence

K\subseteq{\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{K}}
.
Altogether, we deduce that

{\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{K}}=K
. The range of
{\mathbf{Z}}
is treated similarly.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

The next result states that the operators
L{\mathbf{Z}}
and
L^{*}{\mathbf{K}}
are skew.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that
z_{i}\in Z
, and
k_{i}\in K_{z_{i}}
, for each
i\in\{0,1\}
.
Then

\left\langle{{Lz_{0}-Lz_{1}},{k_{0}-k_{1}}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{{z_{0}-z_{1}},{L^{*}k_{0}-L^{*}k_{1}}}\right\rangle=0.

(22)

Proof
. The left equality in
22
is trivial.
Now, the assumption implies that each
k_{i}\in(-L^{-*}Az_{i})\cap(BLz_{i})
.
Thus each
-L^{*}k_{i}\in Az_{i}
and
L^{*}k_{i}\in L^{*}BLz_{i}
.
Because
A
and
L^{*}BL
are monotone, we deduce that

0\leq\left\langle{z_{0}-z_{1}},{(-L^{*}k_{0})-(-L^{*}k_{1})}\right\rangle

and
0\leq\left\langle{z_{0}-z_{1}},{L^{*}k_{0}-L^{*}k_{1}}\right\rangle
.
Altogther,

0=\left\langle{z_{0}-z_{1}},{L^{*}k_{0}-L^{*}k_{1}}\right\rangle
which is the right equality in

22
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

3
Saddle points

Fact 3.1
.

The set of saddle points
{\mathbf{S}}
is convex and closed.

Remark 3.2
.

Fact

3.1
can be proved by observing that the points in
{\mathbf{S}}
are actually
the zeros of the
the maximally monotone operator on
X\times Y
, given by

(x,y)\mapsto(Ax\times B^{-1}y)+(L^{*}y,-Lx)
;
see
[
16
, Proposition 2]
(and also
[
3
, Proposition 26.33]
).
When
A=\partial f
and
B=\partial g
, for proper
lower semicontinuous convex functions
f
and
g
on

X
and
Y
, respectively, then
{\mathbf{S}}
is precisely the set of saddle point of the function

(x,y)\mapsto f(x)-g^{*}(y)+\left\langle{x},{L^{*}y}\right\rangle,

(23)

which is convex in
x
and concave in
y
.
This motivates the term “saddle points” for
11
.
One may also find the terminology “Kuhn-Tucker vectors” for
{\mathbf{S}}

in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
).

Corollary 3.3
.

The set

{\mathbf{S}}=\operatorname{gra}{\mathbf{K}}=\operatorname{gra}{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}
is a closed convex subset of
Z\times K
.

(24)

Moreover, the sets of primal and dual solutions

Z
and
K
are convex.

(25)

Proof
. Taking
(x,y)\in X\times Y
, we get the equivalences

\displaystyle(x,y)\in{\mathbf{S}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow y\in-L^{-*}Ax\land y\in B(Lx)

(26a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow y\in K_{x}

(26b)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(x,y)\in\operatorname{gra}{\mathbf{K}},

(26c)

which yields
{\mathbf{S}}=\operatorname{gra}{\mathbf{K}}
.
On the other hand,
Proposition

2.1
(i)
yields

{\mathbf{K}}={\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}
.
Combining with
Fact

3.1
, we obtain
24
.

Because
{\mathbf{S}}
is convex, so are the restrictions to
the first and second components, i.e.,
Z=\operatorname{dom}\,{\mathbf{K}}
and
K={\operatorname{ran}}\,{\mathbf{K}}
.
This proves
25
and we are done.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

Remark 3.4
.

The fact that
Z
and
K
are convex is somewhere surprising
because the operator sums occurring in their definition are
not assumed to be maximally monotone.

4
Common zeros

The next result characterizes when we have common zeroes for the problem

3
:

Proposition 4.1

(common zeros)
.

\operatorname{zer}A\cap\operatorname{zer}L^{*}BL\neq\varnothing\Leftrightarrow K\cap\ker L^{*}\neq\varnothing
.

Proof
. “
\Rightarrow
”:
Indeed, using
Proposition

2.1
(iv)
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{zer}A\cap\operatorname{zer}L^{*}BL\neq\varnothing

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\exists z\in X)\;\;z\in\operatorname{zer}A\cap\operatorname{zer}L^{*}BL

(27a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\exists z\in X)\;\;0\in Az\land 0\in L^{*}BLz

(27b)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(\exists z\in X)\;\;L^{-*}(0)\subseteq L^{-*}Az\land(\exists y\in BLz)\;\;L^{*}y=0

(27c)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\exists z\in X)\;\;\pm\ker L^{*}\subseteq L^{-*}Az\land(\exists y\in Y)\;\;y\in BLz\cap\ker L^{*}

(27d)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(\exists y\in Y)\;\;y\in\ker L^{*}\cap BLz\cap(-L^{-*}Az)

(27e)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\exists y\in Y)\;\;y\in\ker L^{*}\cap K_{z}\subseteq\ker L^{*}\cap K.

(27f)

“
\Leftarrow
”:
Conversely, assume that
K\cap\ker L^{*}\neq\varnothing
.
Let
k\in K\cap\ker L^{*}
.
By
Corollary

2.2
,
K=\bigcup_{z\in Z}K_{z}
.
Hence there exists
z\in Z

such that
k\in K_{z}\cap\ker L^{*}
.
Thus

k\in(-L^{-*}Az)\cap(BLz)\cap\ker L^{*}.

(28)

Hence

-L^{*}k\in Az\land k\in BLz\land L^{*}k=0
.
This implies
0=-0=-L^{*}k\in Az\land 0=L^{*}k\in L^{*}BLz
,
i.e.,
z\in\operatorname{zer}A\cap\operatorname{zer}L^{*}BL
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

Proposition 4.2

(
0
as a dual solution)
.

We have:

\operatorname{zer}L^{-*}A\cap\operatorname{zer}BL\neq\varnothing\Leftrightarrow 0\in K
.

Proof
. Indeed, using
Corollary

2.2
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{zer}L^{-*}A\cap\operatorname{zer}BL\neq\varnothing

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\exists z\in X)\;\;0\in(-L^{-*}Az)\cap(BLz)

(29a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\exists z\in X)\;\;0\in K_{z}

(29b)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow 0\in K=\bigcup_{z\in Z}K_{z},

(29c)

as claimed.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

The previous two results generalize
[
2
, Proposition 2.10]
;
moreover, these generalization are different from each other
as we now show:

Example 4.3
.

Suppose that
Y=X
and that
U
is a proper closed linear subspace of
X
.
Further suppose that
A=P_{U}
and
L=P_{U}=L^{*}
,
let
u^{\perp}\in U^{\perp}\smallsetminus\{0\}
,
and suppose that

B\colon y\mapsto u^{\perp}
.
Because
\ker L^{*}=U^{\perp}
, we have

(\forall x\in X)

x\in Z

\Leftrightarrow

0\in Ax+L^{*}BLx=P_{U}x

\Leftrightarrow

x\in U^{\perp}
; thus,

Z=U^{\perp}.

(30)

It thus follows with
Corollary

2.2
that

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{z\in Z}K_{z}=\bigcup_{z\in U^{\perp}}(-L^{-*}Az)\cap(BLz)=\bigcup_{z\in U^{\perp}}(-U^{\perp}\cap\{u^{\perp}\})=\{u^{\perp}\}.

(31)

Hence
0\notin K
, and, as predicted by

Proposition

4.2
,

\operatorname{zer}L^{-*}\cap\operatorname{zer}BL=\varnothing
.
On the other hand,

K\cap\ker L^{*}=\{u^{\perp}\}\cap U^{\perp}=\{u^{\perp}\}\neq\varnothing

and so

Proposition

4.1
guarantees that

\operatorname{zer}A\cap\operatorname{zer}L^{*}BL\neq\varnothing
.
Indeed,
\operatorname{zer}A\cap\operatorname{zer}L^{*}BL=\operatorname{zer}P_{U}\cap\operatorname{zer}P_{U}u^{\perp}=U^{\perp}
.

5
Paramonotonicity helps!

Recall that
A:X\rightrightarrows X
is
paramonotone
if it is monotone and

\left.\begin{array}[]{c}(x_{0},u_{0})\in\operatorname{gra}A\\
(x_{1},u_{1})\in\operatorname{gra}A\\
\left\langle{x_{0}-x_{1}},{u_{0}-u_{1}}\right\rangle=0\end{array}\right\}\Rightarrow(x_{0},u_{1})\in\operatorname{gra}A\text{ and }(x_{1},u_{0})\in\operatorname{gra}A.

The term “paramonotone” was introduced in

[
11
]
for functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and thoroughly studied in
[
19
]
.
The class of paramonotone operators is large: it includes subdifferential
operators, strictly monotone operators, displacement mappings, and others
(see also
[
3
]
and
[
7
]
).

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that

A
and
B
are paramonotone.
Then
(\forall z\in Z)

K_{z}=K
and
(\forall k\in K)

Z_{k}=Z
.

Proof
. Take
z_{0},z_{1}
in
Z
, and let each
k_{i}\in K_{z_{i}}

By
Proposition

2.3
,

\left\langle{Lz_{0}-Lz_{1}},{k_{0}-k_{1}}\right\rangle=0=\left\langle{z_{0}-z_{1}},{(-L^{*}k_{0})-(-L^{*}k_{1})}\right\rangle.

(32)

On the other hand, the definition of
K_{z_{i}}
yields

(z_{i},-L^{*}k_{i})\in\operatorname{gra}A
and
(Lz_{i},k_{i})\in\operatorname{gra}B
.
Altogether, in view of the paramonotonicity of
A
and
B
,
we deduce that

(z_{0},-L^{*}k_{1})\in\operatorname{gra}A
,

(z_{1},-L^{*}k_{0})\in\operatorname{gra}A
,

(Lz_{0},k_{1})\in\operatorname{gra}B
, and

(Lz_{1},k_{0})\in\operatorname{gra}B
.
Hence
k_{1}\in K_{z_{0}}
and
k_{0}\in K_{z_{1}}
.
It follows that
K_{z_{0}}=K_{z_{1}}
and thus
(\forall z\in Z)

K_{z}=K
.

The remaining assertion is proved similarly, after noting that an operator
is paramonotone if and only if its inverse is likewise.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

Remark 5.2
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are paramonotone, and that

k\in K
is an
arbitrary
dual solution.
Then

Theorem

5.1
guarantees that the
entire set of primal solutions

is recovered via

Z=Z_{k}=\big(L^{-1}B^{-1}k\big)\cap\big(A^{-1}(-L^{*}k)\big).

(33)

Similarly, a single primal solution
z\in Z
recovers all
dual solutions via
K=K_{z}=(-L^{-*}Az)\cap(BLz)
.
Without paramonotonicity, this fails even when
Y=X
and
L=\operatorname{Id}
—
combine
Example

1.1
with
Corollary

3.3
.

Corollary 5.3
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are paramonotone.
Then the following hold:

(i)

The sets of primal and dual
solutions,
Z
and
K
, are convex and closed.

(ii)

\operatorname{gra}{\mathbf{K}}={\mathbf{S}}
and
\operatorname{gra}{\mathbf{Z}}
are the rectangles
Z\times K
and
K\times Z
, respectively.

(iii)

\left\langle{\overline{\operatorname{span}}\,(Z-Z)},{\overline{\operatorname{span}}\,(L^{*}K-L^{*}K)}\right\rangle=0=\left\langle{\overline{\operatorname{span}}\,(LZ-LZ)},{\overline{\operatorname{span}}\,(K-K)}\right\rangle
.

Proof
.
(i)
:
Combine
Theorem

5.1
with
Proposition

2.1
.

(ii)
:
Combine
Theorem

5.1

with
Corollary

6
The subdifferential case and Fenchel-Rockafellar duality

Throughout this section, we assume that

f\colon X\to\,\left]-\infty,+\infty\right]\;\;\text{and}\;\;g\colon Y\to\,\left]-\infty,+\infty\right]\;\;\text{are convex, lower semicontinuous, and proper,}

(39)

and that

A=\partial f\;\;\text{and}\;\;B=\partial g.

(40)

The
Fenchel-Rockafellar primal problem
asks to minimize

f+g\circ L
.
Standard subdifferential calculus and Fermat’s rule yields the
following result:

Proposition 6.1
.

We have

Z=\operatorname{zer}(\partial f+L^{*}\circ\partial g\circ L)\subseteq\operatorname{zer}(\partial f+\partial(g\circ L))\subseteq\operatorname{zer}\partial(f+g\circ L)=\argmin(f+g\circ L).

(41)

Theorem 6.2
.

Suppose that
z\in Z
and
let
x\in\argmin(f+g\circ L)
.
Then
L^{*}(K_{z})\subseteq L^{*}(K_{x})
.

Proof
. Because of
Proposition

6.1
, we have

f(z)+g(Lz)=f(x)+g(Lx)\in\mathbb{R}
; hence,

f(z)-f(x)=g(Lx)-g(Lz).

(42)

We also have
K_{z}\neq\varnothing
and

\varnothing\neq L^{*}(K_{z})=(-Az)\cap L^{*}BLz=(-\partial f(z))\cap L^{*}\partial g(Lz)
by

Proposition

2.1
(iii)
&
(ii)
.
Let
u\in(-\partial f(z))\cap L^{*}\partial g(Lz)
.
Then there exists
v\in K_{z}
such that

u=L^{*}v
.
On the one hand, because
-u\in\partial f(z)
, we have

f(z)+\left\langle{x-z},{-u}\right\rangle\leq f(x)
or

f(z)-f(x)\leq\left\langle{x-z},{u}\right\rangle.

(43)

On the other hand, because
v\in K_{z}\subseteq\partial g(Lz)
,
we have
g(Lz)+\left\langle{Lx-Lz},{v}\right\rangle\leq g(Lx)

\Leftrightarrow

g(Lz)-g(Lx)\leq-\left\langle{Lx-Lz},{v}\right\rangle=-\left\langle{x-z},{L^{*}v}\right\rangle=-\left\langle{x-z},{u}\right\rangle

\Leftrightarrow

\left\langle{x-z},{u}\right\rangle\leq g(Lx)-g(Lz).

(44)

Combining
42
,
43
, and
44
,
we deduce that

f(z)-f(x)=\left\langle{x-z},{u}\right\rangle=\left\langle{Lx-Lz},{v}\right\rangle=g(Lx)-g(Lz).

(45)

Because
-u\in\partial f(z)
; equivalently,

\left\langle{z},{-u}\right\rangle=f(z)+f^{*}(-u)
,
we learn from
45
that

\left\langle{z},{-u}\right\rangle=(f(x)+\left\langle{x-z},{u}\right\rangle)+f^{*}(-u)
.
Thus
\left\langle{x},{-u}\right\rangle=f(x)+f^{*}(-u)
, i.e.,

-u\in\partial f(x).

(46)

And because
v\in\partial g(Lz)
,
we have
\left\langle{Lz},{v}\right\rangle=g(Lz)+g^{*}(v)

and we learn from
45
that

\left\langle{Lz},{v}\right\rangle=(g(Lx)+\left\langle{Lz-Lx},{v}\right\rangle)+g^{*}(v)
.
Thus
\left\langle{Lx},{v}\right\rangle=g(Lx)+g^{*}(v)
and so

which further yields

u=L^{*}v\in L^{*}\partial g(Lx).

(47)

Combining
46
,
47
,
with
Proposition

2.1
(ii)
,
we deduce that
u=L^{*}v\in L^{*}(K_{x})
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

.

If
\operatorname{zer}(\partial f+L^{*}\circ\partial g\circ L)\neq\varnothing

. Let
z\in\operatorname{zer}(\partial f+L^{*}\circ\partial g\circ L)=Z
and
let

.

,

2.1
(iii)

Remark 6.4
.

Consider

(51)

Theorem 6.5

(total duality)
.

We have the equivalence

Proof
. Either side of

“

.
Let

.
Then

3.3

and

.
It thus follows from

, Theorem 19.1]
that

solves the primal problem,

solves the dual problem,
and
\mu^{*}=-\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.

“
\Leftarrow
”:
Suppose

satisfies

.
By
[

, Theorem 19.1]
,

.
Hence

3.3
and

yield

(and

).

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

Remark 6.6
.

Theorem

can be viewed as a variant of

[
21
, Corollary 5.3]
which states that
if

is a primal solution and
\partial(f+g\circ L)(x)=\partial f(x)+L^{*}\partial g(Lx)

holds,
i.e.,
\mu^{*}=-\mu
and both the primal and the dual Fenchel-Rockafellar

We refer the reader to the paper
[
21
]
by Li, Fang, López, and López for a
comprehensive study of total duality.

In contrast, it is possible that there is no duality gap (
\mu^{*}=-\mu
) but neither
the primal nor the dual Fenchel-Rockafellar problem has a solution:

Example 6.7
.

Suppose that
X=Y=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
L=\operatorname{Id}
, and
f
and
g
are given by

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\exp(x_{1})+\exp^{*}(x_{2})\;\;\text{and}\;\;g(x_{1},x_{2})=\exp(x_{1})+\exp^{*}(-x_{2}).

(53)

Then
\mu^{*}=-\mu=0
but neither Fenchel-Rockafellar problem has
a solution.

Proof
. Recall that

\exp^{*}(\xi)=\begin{cases}+\infty,&\text{if $\xi<0$;}\\
0,&\text{if $\xi=0$;}\\
\xi\ln(\xi)-\xi,&\text{if $\xi>0$.}\end{cases}

(54)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\inf_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}(\exp(x_{1})+\exp(x_{1}))+0=0,

(55)

but
\mu
is not attained.
Because
f^{*}(y_{1},y_{2})=\exp^{*}(y_{1})+\exp(y_{2})=f(y_{2},y_{1})
and

g^{*}(y_{1},y_{2})=\exp^{*}(y_{1})+\exp(-y_{2})=g(-y_{2},-y_{1})
,
it follows that
The Fenchel-Rockafellar dual problem thus asks to minimize

(y_{1},y_{2})\mapsto g(-y_{2},-y_{1})+f(-y_{2},-y_{1})
; however,
again this problem has optimal value
\mu^{*}=0
yet no solution.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

We conclude this section with an excursion to the
unconstrained LASSO problem.

Example 6.8

(LASSO)
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\lambda>0
, and
suppose that
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
Y=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and

L\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
Furthermore,
we suppose that
f(x)=\lambda\|x\|_{1}-\left\langle{x},{L^{*}b}\right\rangle

and
g(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|b\|^{2}
.
Thus our maximally monotone operators are given by

Ax=\partial f(x)=\lambda\partial\|x\|_{1}-L^{*}b\;\;\text{and}\;\;By=\partial g(y)=\nabla g(y)=\operatorname{Id}.

(56)

The value of the primal Fenchel-Rockafellar problem is

\mu=\min_{x\in X}\big(f(x)+g(Lx)\big)=\min_{x\in X}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\|Lx-b\|^{2}+\lambda\|x\|_{1}\big),

(57)

which is the famous unconstrained LASSO problem
[
26
]
.
Note that we wrote “
\min
”, not “
\inf
”, which we justify now:
indeed,
the primal objective function
x\mapsto f(x)+g(Lx)
is the sum of the
nonnegative function
x\mapsto\tfrac{1}{2}\|Lx-b\|^{2}
and
the coercive continuous function
x\mapsto\lambda\|x\|_{1}
, which
guarantees that minimizers exist.
Next,
f^{*}(x)=\iota_{C}((x+L^{*}b)/\lambda)
and

g^{*}(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\|b\|^{2}
, where

C=[-1,1]^{n}
is the unit ball with respect to the max norm.
It follows analogously that the dual objective function

y\mapsto g^{*}(y)+f^{*}(-L^{*}y)
has a minimizer; moreover, it is
unique due to the strong convexity of
g^{*}
. Combining
with the fact that
\operatorname{dom}\,g=\operatorname{dom}\,g^{*}=Y
, we have total duality and
so
Z\neq\varnothing
and
K\neq\varnothing
.
Denote the unique dual solution by
k
:

K=\{k\}.

(58)

The inverses of
A
and
B
are given by
A^{-1}\colon x\mapsto N_{C}((x+L^{*}b)/\lambda)
and
B^{-1}=\operatorname{Id}
.
If
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, then

N_{C}(x)=N_{[-1,1]}(x_{1})\times\cdots\times N_{[-1,1]}(x_{n}),\quad\text{where}\;\;N_{[-1,1]}(\xi)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}_{-},&\text{if $\xi=-1$;}\\
\{0\},&\text{if $|\xi|<1$;}\\
\mathbb{R}_{+},&\text{if $\xi=1$;}\\
\varnothing,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(59)

Now

Z=Z_{k}=L^{-1}B^{-1}(k)\cap A^{-1}(-L^{*}k)
,
which turns in our current setting into

Z=L^{-1}(k)\cap N_{C}\big(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}L^{*}(b-k)\big).

(60)

We note that
60
immediately yields the following:

(i)

If
L
is injective (
\Leftrightarrow

{\operatorname{rank}}\,(L)=n\leq m
),
then
L^{-1}(k)=Z
is a singleton.

(ii)

If each

, i.e.,

, then

and so

is a singleton.

More general conditions ensuring that

is a singleton can be found in
Tibshirani’s
[
27
]
; see also

[
8
]
.

7
The Chambolle-Pock operator within the Bredies-Chenchene-Lorenz-Naldi framework

From now on we assume that

\sigma>0,\;\tau>0,\;\text{and}\;\sigma\tau\|L\|^{2}\leq 1.

(61)

Recall that the
Chambolle-Pock (a.k.a. Primal-Dual Hybrid Gradient) operator
for the problem
3
is
defined by (see
[
12
]
and also
[
13
]
)

{\mathbf{T}}\colon X\times Y\to X\times Y\colon\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}x^{+}\\
y^{+}\end{bmatrix}:=\begin{bmatrix}J_{\sigma A}(x-\sigma L^{*}y)\\
J_{\tau B^{-1}}\big({y+\tau L(2x^{+}-x)}\big)\end{bmatrix}.

(62)

Proposition 7.1
.

\operatorname{Fix}{\mathbf{T}}={\mathbf{S}}
is convex and closed.

Proof
. Let
(x,y)\in X\times Y
. Then

\displaystyle(x,y)\in\operatorname{Fix}{\mathbf{T}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}={\mathbf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}

(63a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x=J_{\sigma A}(x-\sigma L^{*}(y))\land y=J_{\tau B^{-1}}(y+\tau Lx)

(63b)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x-\sigma L^{*}y\in x+\sigma Ax\land y+\tau Lx\in y+\tau B^{-1}y

(63c)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow-L^{*}y\in Ax\land Lx\in B^{-1}y

(63d)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(x,y)\in{\mathbf{S}},

(63e)

and so
\operatorname{Fix}{\mathbf{T}}={\mathbf{S}}
.
Now invoke
Corollary

3.3
.

\hfill\quad\blacksquare

Following the recent framework proposed by
Bredies, Chenchene, Lorenz, and Naldi
[
9
]
,
we define block operators on
X\times Y
via

{\mathbf{M}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sigma}\operatorname{Id}_{X}&-L^{*}\\
-L&\tfrac{1}{\tau}\operatorname{Id}_{Y}\end{bmatrix}\;\;\text{and}\;\;{\mathbf{A}}:=\begin{bmatrix}A&L^{*}\\
-L&B^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(64)

Then
{\mathbf{A}}
is maximally monotone with

\operatorname{zer}{\mathbf{A}}=\operatorname{Fix}{\mathbf{T}}
while
{\mathbf{M}}
is a positive semidefinite preconditioner
and

({\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{M}})^{-1}\colon\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}J_{\sigma A}(\sigma x)\\
J_{\tau B^{-1}}\big(2\tau J_{\sigma A}(\sigma x)+\tau y\big)\end{bmatrix}.

(65)

Moreover,

{\mathbf{T}}=({\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{M}})^{-1}{\mathbf{M}}.

(66)

The preconditioner
{\mathbf{M}}
induces a seminorm on
X\times Y
via

\|(x,y)\|_{{\mathbf{M}}}^{2}=\left\langle{(x,y)},{{\mathbf{M}}(x,y)}\right\rangle
; it is a norm if and only
if
\sigma\tau\|L\|^{2}<1
, in which case
{\mathbf{T}}
is firmly nonexpansive
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathbf{M}}}
.
We also assume that there exist another real Hilbert space
Z
and
a continuous linear operator
C\colon Z\to X\times Y
such that

CC^{*}={\mathbf{M}}.

(67)

The associated reduced operator

\widetilde{T}:=C^{*}({\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{M}})^{-1}C,

(68)

which operates in
Z
, is (classically) firmly nonexpansive
and

\operatorname{Fix}\widetilde{T}=C^{*}(\operatorname{Fix}{\mathbf{T}})\;\;\text{is convex and closed.}

(69)

We now discuss various cases.

7.1
The general case

One way to find
C
is by first constructing
another operator
R
in the following manner:
Suppose that
Z=X\times Y
and
R\colon Y\to Y
satisfies

RR^{*}=\operatorname{Id}_{Y}-\sigma\tau LL^{*}\;\;\text{and set}\;\;C=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\operatorname{Id}_{X}&0\\
-\sqrt{\sigma}L&\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}R\end{bmatrix}.

(70)

For instance,
R
can be chosen as the principal square root of

\operatorname{Id}_{Y}-\sigma\tau LL^{*}
for which we even have
R=R^{*}
.
Another possibility is to use a Cholesky factorization of
\operatorname{Id}_{Y}-\sigma\tau LL^{*}

in the finite-dimensional case.

7.2
The case when
K\subseteq\ker L^{*}

For simplicity, we shall assume that
Y
is finite-dimensional,
R

is chosen as the principal square root of
\operatorname{Id}_{Y}-\sigma\tau LL^{*}
,
and
K\subseteq\ker L^{*}
.
Then
(\forall k\in K)

R^{2}k=k
.
If
k\in K\smallsetminus\{0\}
, then

k
is an eigenvector of
R^{2}
with eigenvalue
1
;
thus,
k
is also an eigenvector of
R
with eigenvalue

\sqrt{1}=1
. Hence

(\forall k\in K)\quad Rk=k,

(71)

and this hold obviously when
k=0
.

Now assume furthermore that
A
and
B
are paramonotone.
Combining
Proposition

7.1
with
Corollary

5.3
(i)

yields
\operatorname{Fix}{\mathbf{T}}=Z\times K
.
Thus
69
yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{Fix}\widetilde{T}

\displaystyle=\big\{{C^{*}(z,k)}~\big|~{(z,k)\in Z\times K}\big\}

(72a)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\big\{{(z-\sigma L^{*}k,\sqrt{\sigma/\tau}k)}~\big|~{(z,k)\in Z\times K}\big\}

(72b)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}Z\times\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}K.

(72c)

7.3
The scaled isometry case:
\sigma\tau LL^{*}=\operatorname{Id}_{Y}

In this subsection, we assume that

\sigma\tau LL^{*}=\operatorname{Id}_{Y}.

(73)

(Note that this corresponds to
R=0
in
70
.)
In this case, we can pick
Z=X
and

C=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\operatorname{Id}_{X}\\
-\sqrt{\sigma}L\end{bmatrix}.

(74)

a statement that trivially holds when

are pairwise orthogonal,

preserves the topology of

.

\displaystyle=P_{L^{*}(\rho V)}(x)=L^{*}P_{\rho V}(Lx)=L^{*}(\rho P_{V}(Lx/\rho))=\rho L^{*}P_{V}(Lx/\rho).

(78)

Now assume that
A
and
B
are paramonotone.
Combining

with

5.3
(i)

(79)

(81)

K=(V_{1}^{\ominus}\times\cdots\times V_{n}^{\ominus})\cap L^{-*}(U^{\oplus})
.

(iv)

If
U
and each
V_{j}
are linear subspaces, then

K=(V_{1}^{\perp}\times\cdots\times V_{n}^{\perp})\cap L^{-*}(U^{\perp})
.

(v)

If

U\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{-1}(\operatorname{int}V_{j})\neq\varnothing
,
then
K=\{0\}
.

Finally, to find primal and dual solutions, one may iterate the
Chambolle-Pock operator
{\mathbf{T}}
.
In the present set up, it is given by the following formula:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}\colon X\times(Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{n})

\displaystyle\to X\times(Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{n})

(113a)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}x^{+}\\
y_{1}^{+}\\
\vdots\\
y_{n}^{+}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}J_{\sigma A}\big(x-\sigma\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{*}y_{j}\big)\\
J_{\tau B_{1}^{-1}}\big(y_{1}+\tau L_{1}(2x^{+}-x)\big)\\
\vdots\\
J_{\tau B_{n}^{-1}}\big(y_{n}+\tau L_{n}(2x^{+}-x)\big)\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(113b)
