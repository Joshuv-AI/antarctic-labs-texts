A novel real-time assessment of intestinal perfusion with dual imaging using ICG fluorescence and capillaroscopy in colorectal surgery
Authors: Sadahito Iseki, Takeshi Aoki, Makoto Watanabe, Ryohei Watanabe, Tetsuya Kitajima, Keisuke Oura, Shun Miyasaka, Yusuke Uchida, Koya Sekiguchi, Yasuyuki Ohira, Kodai Tomioka, Hideki Shibata, Yoshihiko Tashiro, Koji Nogaki, Hiromi Date, Kazuhiro Matsuda, Kimiyasu Yamazaki, Hiroki Yamaue, Toshiko Yamochi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAnastomotic leakage (AL) is a major complication after colorectal surgery. Indocyanine green fluorescence imaging (ICG-FI) enables real-time assessment of intestinal perfusion, but remains subjective. This study quantitatively evaluated intestinal perfusion by combining ICG-FI with video capillaroscopy (VC).MethodsThirty-two patients undergoing colorectal surgery were enrolled. ICG was administered intravenously before anastomosis. Fluorescence time was defined as the interval from injection to initial fluorescence at the anastomotic site. Microvascular blood-flow velocity was measured intraoperatively using VC in fluorescent and non-fluorescent regions.ResultsAL occurred in 4 of 32 patients (12.5%). The AL group had significantly longer fluorescence time than the non-AL group (32.0 [25.3–34.3] vs. 20.0 [17.3–25.0] s, p = 0.014) and tended to have lower microvascular flow velocity in ICG-fluorescent regions (90.8 [49.0–249.8] vs. 231.7 [164.6–327.3] µm/s, p = 0.064). ROC analysis showed discriminative performance for fluorescence time (AUC 0.88, preliminary cutoff 32 s) and flow velocity (AUC 0.79, preliminary cutoff 101.6 μm/s). Fluorescence time and flow velocity were negatively correlated (r = − 0.41, p = 0.0195).ConclusionsDelayed fluorescence appearance on ICG-FI was associated with reduced VC-derived microvascular flow velocity. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate ICG-FI and VC intraoperatively for quantitative assessment of intestinal perfusion. Combined ICG-FI and VC assessment may provide physiological support for interpreting delayed fluorescence as a potential marker of microcirculatory impairment. Further validation is required to determine its clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-026-04094-3.

Introduction

Anastomotic leakage (AL) is a major complication of colorectal surgery, with reported incidences ranging between 3% and 29% and a significant negative impact on postoperative morbidity, mortality, and oncological outcomes [1, 2]. Inadequate anastomotic perfusion is a critical risk factor [3]; however, intraoperative evaluation has traditionally depended on subjective visual inspection, which varies considerably among surgeons.

Indocyanine green fluorescence imaging (ICG-FI) has been increasingly used to provide real-time, intraoperative assessment of intestinal perfusion. The feasibility of this approach was first demonstrated in the PILLAR II study [3], whereas the subsequent PILLAR III randomized controlled trial was terminated prematurely and did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in AL rates [4]. More recently, the Japanese EssentiAL study, which enrolled 839 patients with rectal cancer, showed a statistically significant decrease in AL with the use of ICG-FI compared with conventional assessment (7.6% vs. 11.8%, p = 0.041), thereby providing the strongest evidence to date for its clinical utility [5].

Several studies proposed quantitative ICG parameters, such as maximum fluorescence intensity (F_max), time to peak (T_max), and the time ratio (T_1/2/T_max), to establish objective cutoffs for predicting AL [6, 7]. Wada et al. reported that F_max ≤ 52 was strongly associated with AL [6], and Son et al. identified a time ratio > 0.6 as an independent predictor [7]. Hayami et al. demonstrated the feasibility of quantifying fluorescence intensity and slope parameters with near-infrared imaging, suggesting their potential utility for evaluating anastomotic perfusion [8]. Darwich et al. recently used spectrophotometric analysis with the O₂C® system to quantify fluorescence intensity during low anterior resection [9]. Hagiwara et al. applied the SPY-QP software program to quantify blood perfusion during minimally invasive rectal cancer surgery objectively, supporting the feasibility of standardized software-based perfusion assessment [10]. A systematic review by McEntee et al. highlighted substantial methodological heterogeneity and the lack of standardized thresholds across studies, underscoring the need for further research to validate and refine quantitative ICG-FI approaches [11]. Therefore, although quantitative ICG-FI is promising, its clinical application remains inconsistent.

ICG-FI reflects macroscopic perfusion but does not directly assess microvascular hemodynamics, which are crucial for anastomotic healing. Video capillaroscopy (VC) allows direct visualization and quantification of microcirculation in the serosal capillary network. In reconstructive plastic surgery, VC has been used to evaluate flap perfusion and detect early microvascular compromise [12, 13]. However, in gastrointestinal surgery, no study has yet integrated VC-derived measurements with ICG-FI to assess macroscopic and microvascular perfusion simultaneously.

In our preliminary observations, delayed ICG fluorescence on the bowel surface corresponded to congestion and sluggish red blood cell movement within serosal capillaries under VC observation, which indicated impaired microvascular perfusion. These findings suggest that combining ICG-FI with VC could provide a physiologically integrated, real-time evaluation of intestinal blood flow.

This study is the first prospective feasibility investigation combining ICG-FI and VC to evaluate macroscopic and microvascular perfusion in colorectal surgery quantitatively and to explore candidate quantitative parameters associated with AL.

Materials and methods

Patients

This prospective feasibility study enrolled 32 consecutive patients who underwent colorectal cancer surgery at Showa Medical University Hospital from February to August 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Showa Medical University (Approval No. 2024-252-B), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients were excluded if they had known hypersensitivity to ICG or iodine, a history of bronchial asthma, pregnancy, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, or severe cardiovascular or systemic comorbidities considered unsuitable for surgery or ICG administration. Anastomotic leakage was defined as clinically or radiologically confirmed leakage at the anastomotic site, regardless of whether therapeutic intervention was required. Clinical confirmation was based on findings such as feculent drainage or peritonitis, and radiological confirmation was based on computed tomography or water-soluble contrast studies.

Surgical procedure

All operations were performed by board-certified colorectal surgeons using either laparoscopic or robot-assisted approaches under general anesthesia. Laparoscopic surgery was performed using the VISERA Elite II/III system ® (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), whereas robot-assisted surgery employed the da Vinci Xi ® (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) or hinotori® (Medicaroid, Kobe, Japan) systems. A standard medial-to-lateral or lateral-to-medial dissection and lymphadenectomy were performed. For right-sided tumors, an extracorporeal functional end-to-end anastomosis was performed. For left-sided colon and rectal cancers, an intracorporeal anastomosis was constructed using the double-stapling technique.

Intraoperative perfusion assessment using ICG-FI and VC

After vessel ligation and bowel mobilization were completed, ICG (Diagnogreen®, Daiichi Sankyo, Tokyo, Japan) was administered intravenously at a dose of 5 mg/body before anastomosis. Near-infrared fluorescence imaging was performed under a darkened operative field. Fluorescence time was defined as the interval between ICG injection and the first visible appearance of fluorescence at the planned anastomotic site on the bowel serosa. Fluorescence time was recorded for each patient and subsequently compared between the non-AL and AL groups. Fluorescence time was selected as the primary ICG-FI parameter because it can be assessed intraoperatively in real time without dedicated fluorescence intensity analysis software and is less dependent on post hoc image-processing conditions than intensity- or slope-based parameters. In addition, this time-based parameter was considered suitable for comparison with VC-derived microvascular flow velocity, which was measured in the same intraoperative setting as a real-time physiological indicator of serosal microcirculation.

The final transection line was determined by the operating surgeon after confirming fluorescence with ICG-FI. Bowel transection was planned within the fluorescent bowel segment, as close as possible to the fluorescence demarcation line, while avoiding non-fluorescent areas. If the initially planned transection line before ICG-FI did not correspond to the fluorescent bowel segment, adjustment of the transection line to a fluorescent region was considered at the surgeon’s discretion. The exact distance between the fluorescence demarcation line and the final transection line was not prospectively measured.

Microcirculation was evaluated using a high-resolution video capillaroscopy system (Bscan-ZA; GOKO Imaging Devices, Kawasaki, Japan) (Fig. 1a–g). The device provides LED illumination and optical magnification up to 720×, enabling real-time visualization of the serosal microvasculature to a depth of approximately 1 mm. The observation field was 0.35 mm² at each site. During VC assessment, the handheld probe was gently applied to the bowel serosa near the planned anastomotic site under clinically stable hemodynamic conditions as judged by the anesthesiologist (Fig. 1b, c). Mean arterial pressure and vasopressor use at the time of VC assessment were recorded.

To minimize measurement variability, all VC measurements were performed by a single experienced operator. The probe was gently placed perpendicular to the antimesenteric serosal surface without intentional compression. Image acquisition was performed after transient stabilization of the bowel surface, avoiding periods of marked bowel movement, respiratory motion, or visible probe-induced compression of the microvasculature. Measurement sites were selected based on predefined fluorescence status, the antimesenteric location, proximity to the fluorescence demarcation line, and clear visualization of the capillary network.

Representative intraoperative images of the serosal capillary network were captured at ×200 and ×720 magnification (Fig. 1d–g). After ICG-FI, VC assessment was performed in both ICG-fluorescent [ICG(+)] and non-fluorescent [ICG(–)] regions. In ICG(+) regions, the handheld probe was applied to the antimesenteric side of the fluorescent bowel serosa immediately adjacent to the fluorescence demarcation line. In ICG(–) regions, VC assessment was performed on the antimesenteric side of the non-fluorescent bowel serosa immediately adjacent to the fluorescence demarcation line when technically feasible. Sites with clearly visible capillary networks were selected, and red blood cell (RBC) flow was recorded for several seconds. The exact distance between the VC measurement site and the fluorescence demarcation line was not prospectively predefined or measured. Using proprietary analysis software (GOKO-VIP, version 1.0.0.4; GOKO Imaging Devices, Japan) (Fig. 2), the mean RBC flow velocity (µm/s) was automatically calculated based on frame-to-frame displacement.

Both ICG-FI and VC assessments were performed before bowel transection and anastomotic construction in all patients. Post-anastomotic perfusion reassessment was not routinely performed in this study. Although post-anastomotic VC assessment may be technically possible in extracorporeal anastomosis for right-sided colon surgery, it could not be performed after intracorporeal double-stapling anastomosis in left-sided colon and rectal surgery because the current handheld VC probe is not designed for laparoscopic insertion into the abdominal cavity. Therefore, to maintain a consistent assessment protocol across procedures, VC assessment was performed before anastomotic construction in all patients.

Histopathological assessment

Pathologists routinely examined resected specimens. The proximal resection margin adjacent to the anastomotic site was evaluated for histopathological evidence of ischemia, including mucosal necrosis, congestion, or hemorrhage, to assess tissue viability at the anastomotic end.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), whereas categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Because of the small sample size and distributional characteristics of the variables, nonparametric tests were used for group comparisons. Comparisons between the AL and non-AL groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Comparisons of baseline characteristics between the AL and non-AL groups were considered descriptive and exploratory because of the small number of AL events. Paired comparisons of microvascular flow velocity between non-fluorescent [ICG(–)] and fluorescent [ICG(+)] regions were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Correlations between fluorescence time and microvascular flow velocity were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient because of the small sample size and because normality could not be assumed for all variables. Exploratory diagnostic performance and candidate cutoff values associated with AL were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with calculation of the area under the curve (AUC). Multivariable logistic regression was not performed, as the limited number of AL events (n = 4) could have led to model overfitting. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP software (version 18; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 32 patients were enrolled, with a median age of 73.5 years [64.3–79.8], and 19 patients were male (Table 1). The median BMI was 22.9 kg/m² [20.2–25.0]. Diabetes mellitus, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, and current smoking were observed in 6 (18.8%), 14 (43.8%), 15 (46.9%), and 4 (12.5%) patients, respectively. ASA physical status ≥ 3 was present in 10 patients (31.3%), and 2 patients (6.3%) received steroid or immunosuppressive therapy. No patients received neoadjuvant therapy. Low rectal anastomosis was performed in 7 patients (21.9%). The anastomotic location was right-sided colon in 16 patients (50.0%) and left-sided/rectal in 16 patients (50.0%). Laparoscopic and robot-assisted approaches were performed in 19 and 13 patients, respectively. The surgical procedures comprised 5 ileocecal resections, 11 right hemicolectomies, 2 sigmoidectomies, 7 high anterior resections, and 7 low anterior resections. AL occurred in 4 patients (12.5%), all of whom had undergone left-sided or rectal resection, including 3 after high anterior resection and 1 after low anterior resection. AL was diagnosed on postoperative days 4, 3, 4, and 4, respectively. All four patients with AL required reoperation and underwent temporary stoma creation, corresponding to Clavien–Dindo grade IIIb complications. No clinically or radiologically confirmed leaks without therapeutic intervention were observed in this cohort.

Comparison between ICG(-) and ICG(+) regions

In all 32 patients, blood-flow velocity was markedly higher in the ICG(+) region than in the ICG(-) region (median 16.2 [0–30.4] µm/s vs. 206.7 [142.2–302.9] µm/s; p < 0.0001) (Table 3; Fig. 3C). This finding indicates that ICG(-) regions corresponded to areas of near-stagnant microcirculation on VC observation.

Preliminary ROC analysis for AL

ROC analysis showed discriminative performance of fluorescence time for AL, with an AUC of 0.88, a preliminary cutoff of 32.0 s, sensitivity of 0.75, specificity of 0.96, and p = 0.044 (Table 4; Fig. 4A).

Correlation between fluorescence time and blood-flow velocity

A significant negative correlation was observed between fluorescence time and blood-flow velocity in ICG(+) regions (r = − 0.4109; p = 0.0195) (Table 5; Fig. 5).

Histopathological findings

Postoperative pathological examination of the proximal resection margin near the anastomotic site revealed no histological evidence of ischemia in any specimens, and no difference was observed between the AL and non-AL groups.

Discussion

AL remains a major challenge in colorectal surgery. In this cohort, AL occurred in 12.5% and was associated with significantly prolonged fluorescence time, along with a trend toward reduced microvascular blood-flow velocity. Quantitative analysis identified preliminary cutoff values of 32 s for fluorescence time and 101.6 μm/s for microvascular velocity, which may reflect impaired anastomotic perfusion. These findings suggest that quantitative intraoperative perfusion assessment may provide objective physiological metrics, although their clinical utility for surgical decision-making requires further validation.

ICG-FI has gained widespread applicability in gastrointestinal surgery, enabling real-time visualization of anatomical structures, tissue perfusion, and surgical landmarks. In hepatobiliary surgery, ICG-FI has been used for hepatic mapping and anatomical liver resection, intraoperative cholangiography, and fluorescence-guided tumor detection or resection [14–24]. Recent advancements include artificial intelligence-assisted fluorescence navigation during cholecystectomy [25] and ICG-guided delineation of extrahepatic lesions within the hepatoduodenal ligament [26]. In gastric surgery, applications include sentinel node mapping [27], fluorescence-based tumor localization [28], identification of dissection planes during total gastrectomy for the remnant stomach [29], and assessment of remnant gastric perfusion during pancreaticoduodenectomy after prior gastrectomy [30]. In colorectal and general surgery, ICG-FI has been used for fluorescent tattooing [31–33], evaluation of bowel viability in incarcerated or ischemic obstruction [34], and fluorescent gauze techniques to mark anatomical landmarks and prevent retained items [35]. Collectively, these diverse applications highlight the versatility and reliability of ICG-FI and support its use as part of intraoperative perfusion assessment.

Despite the widespread use, the clinical effectiveness of ICG-FI in reducing AL remains controversial. The PILLAR II study demonstrated feasibility and suggested a potential benefit [3], but the subsequent PILLAR III randomized controlled trial failed to show a statistically significant reduction in AL [4]. The EssentiAL reported a modest but significant decrease in AL using ICG-FI compared with conventional assessment [5]. More recently, two large multicenter randomized controlled trials—the AVOID trial [36] and the IntAct trial [37]—confirmed the safety and feasibility of ICG-FI but did not show a significant reduction in AL. These mixed results suggest that while ICG-FI effectively visualizes bowel perfusion, its macroscopic assessment alone may not sufficiently detect clinically relevant microvascular compromise, underscoring the need for complementary modalities.

A key advancement of the present study is the integration of VC with ICG-FI, enabling dual-level evaluation of intraoperative perfusion. Whereas ICG-FI visualizes macroscopic perfusion dynamics, VC captures real-time serosal microcirculation and RBC movement. In cases with delayed fluorescence appearance, VC demonstrated capillary congestion and reduced microvascular velocity, indicating microvascular stagnation. This complementary information bridges the gap between macroscopic and microvascular perfusion evaluation and provides a physiologically grounded, quantitative assessment of tissue viability at the anastomotic site. Although VC has shown clinical utility for flap monitoring in reconstructive surgery [12, 13], its intraoperative application in gastrointestinal surgery has not previously been reported.

In contrast, postoperative histopathological examination revealed no overt ischemic changes at the proximal resection margin, even in patients who developed AL. This finding suggests that VC-detected microvascular stagnation and reduced RBC velocity may represent functional microcirculatory impairment that precedes irreversible structural ischemic changes detectable by routine histopathology. In addition, histopathological evaluation was limited to the resected margin and could not assess dynamic post-anastomotic changes in perfusion or microcirculation. Therefore, the absence of histological ischemia does not necessarily exclude transient or functional microvascular impairment at the anastomotic site.

The combined use of ICG-FI and VC represents a novel dual-modality approach to intraoperative perfusion assessment. Fluorescence time serves as a quantitative macroscopic indicator of ICG arrival at the bowel serosa, whereas VC-derived microvascular velocity directly reflects capillary-level RBC movement. Importantly, the present findings suggest that even when the bowel eventually becomes fluorescent on ICG-FI, delayed fluorescence appearance may be associated with capillary-level RBC stagnation and reduced microvascular velocity within the serosal microvasculature. Thus, VC provides physiological support for interpreting prolonged fluorescence time not merely as delayed visual dye appearance, but as a potential marker of microcirculatory impairment. When delayed fluorescence and reduced VC-derived velocity are concordant, these findings may indicate both impaired macroscopic perfusion dynamics and microvascular stagnation. Conversely, potential discrepancies between the two modalities may provide additional physiological information; for example, delayed fluorescence with preserved microvascular velocity may reflect delayed macroscopic dye arrival without severe local capillary stagnation, whereas reduced microvascular velocity despite fluorescence may suggest localized microcirculatory congestion or impaired capillary flow not fully captured by macroscopic fluorescence appearance alone. However, because of the limited sample size and preliminary nature of this feasibility study, the clinical significance of such concordant or discordant patterns could not be determined. Further studies are needed to clarify whether and how combined interpretation of ICG-FI and VC provides clinically relevant information beyond ICG-FI alone. In the present study, VC findings were not used to modify the transection line or other intraoperative surgical decisions. Therefore, no definitive clinical decision-making algorithm can be proposed from the current data. Future studies should evaluate whether VC-derived microvascular information can guide intraoperative management, particularly in cases with delayed fluorescence appearance or discordant ICG-FI and VC findings.

This study has several limitations. The sample size was small, with only four AL cases, and the study was conducted at a single institution, which limits the statistical power. Therefore, this feasibility study was not powered to detect differences in AL rates or to establish definitive cutoff values for predicting AL. In addition, the cohort included anatomically heterogeneous procedures, including right-sided colon, left-sided colon, and rectal resections, which differ in vascular anatomy, baseline leakage risk, and perfusion characteristics. All AL events occurred after left-sided or rectal resection. Therefore, the observed associations between delayed fluorescence time, reduced microvascular velocity, and AL may have been influenced by anastomotic location, and this study could not determine whether these perfusion findings were independent predictors of AL or reflected anatomical differences in distal colorectal perfusion. Although bowel transection was performed within the fluorescent bowel segment as close as possible to the fluorescence demarcation line, the exact distance from the fluorescence border was not prospectively measured or standardized. Similarly, the exact distance between the VC measurement site and the fluorescence demarcation line was not prospectively predefined or measured. In addition, perfusion was assessed before anastomotic construction, and post-anastomotic reassessment was not routinely performed. Post-anastomotic VC assessment was not possible after intracorporeal double-stapling anastomosis in left-sided colon and rectal surgery because the current handheld VC probe is not designed for laparoscopic insertion into the abdominal cavity. Therefore, potential effects of stapling, bowel tension, or mesenteric orientation on post-anastomotic perfusion could not be evaluated. Although all VC measurements were performed by a single experienced operator using a standardized approach, microvascular flow velocity may still have been affected by subtle probe pressure, bowel peristalsis, respiratory movement, or site selection. Formal assessment of intraobserver or interobserver reproducibility was not performed. Multivariable analysis was not performed to avoid model overfitting. Furthermore, VC assessment was limited to the serosal layer and did not evaluate mucosal perfusion. Larger multicenter studies are warranted to validate these preliminary cutoff values and clarify the clinical utility of combined ICG-FI and VC assessment. Further refinement and standardization of quantitative fluorescence and microcirculatory parameters may support the development of an objective, reproducible perfusion assessment system in colorectal surgery.

Conclusion

This prospective feasibility study demonstrated that prolonged fluorescence time on ICG-FI and reduced microvascular flow velocity on VC were associated with impaired intestinal perfusion and the occurrence of AL after colorectal surgery. In ICG non-fluorescent regions, VC revealed RBC congestion within the serosal capillaries, indicating microcirculatory disturbance. In cases with delayed ICG fluorescence appearance, VC demonstrated sluggish RBC movement and decreased microvascular velocity, consistent with microvascular stagnation at the anastomotic site. These findings suggest that delayed fluorescence appearance on ICG-FI may reflect underlying microcirculatory impairment rather than merely delayed visual dye appearance.

The integration of ICG-FI and VC enables paired assessment of macroscopic fluorescence dynamics and serosal microvascular flow. This dual-modality approach provides physiological support for interpreting delayed fluorescence appearance as a potential marker of microcirculatory impairment. However, the clinical utility of combined ICG-FI and VC assessment, including its ability to improve intraoperative decision-making or reduce AL incidence, requires validation in larger multicenter studies to establish standardized quantitative parameters and cutoff values for clinical application.
