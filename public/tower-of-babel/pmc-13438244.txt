How to Manage the Spinoglenoid Notch Cyst: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Abdullah B. Chandasir, Ryan M. Lew, Lord J. Hyeamang, Asim A. Khan, Nurudeen K. Alli, Zaamin B. Hussain, Gregory P. Nicholson, Grant E. Garrigues
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Spinoglenoid notch cysts can cause suprascapular nerve compression, posterior shoulder pain, and infraspinatus weakness. Although multiple open and arthroscopic operative approaches have been described, the optimal management strategy remains unclear.Purpose:To (1) analyze existing evidence on the management of spinoglenoid notch cysts, (2) compare outcomes among different operative approaches, and (3) provide a practical management algorithm based on cyst characteristics.Study Design:Systematic review and meta-analysis; Level of evidence, 4.Methods:A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines, using the PubMed, Scopus, and Embase databases from inception to August 2025. Eligible studies included clinical reports on the operative or nonoperative management of spinoglenoid notch cysts, with postoperative outcomes. Data on patient characteristics, procedures, patient-reported outcome measures, recurrence, reoperation, and complication rates were extracted. Random- and common-effects models were used for pooled analyses. Risk of bias was assessed using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies criteria.Results:A total of 32 studies comprising 562 shoulders met the inclusion criteria. Overall risk of bias was moderate. Arthroscopic decompression with labral repair was the most common intervention (n = 271 [48%]). Across all studies, significant improvements were observed in the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons, Constant-Murley, and visual analog scale pain scores. Meta-analysis revealed the lowest cyst recurrence rate for arthroscopic decompression with labral repair (3% [95% CI, 0.02-0.06]), compared with arthroscopic decompression alone (12%), arthroscopic labral repair alone (6%), and open decompression (12%). Reoperation rates were lowest with open decompression (0%) and arthroscopic labral repair (3%-4%), and highest with arthroscopic decompression without labral repair (9%). Complication rates were uniformly low across all techniques (<5%).Conclusion:Arthroscopic decompression with concurrent labral repair demonstrated favorable outcomes and low recurrence rates in properly selected patients with spinoglenoid notch cysts. Both arthroscopic and open approaches yield excellent pain relief and functional recovery. Arthroscopic decompression with labral repair offers a safe, effective, and minimally invasive option for nonloculated cysts. For very large and/or loculated cysts, open decompression with removal of the cyst structure itself should be considered.

Spinoglenoid notch cysts represent a distinct cause of SSN entrapment in the spinoglenoid notch. The formation of these ganglion cysts can be associated with repetitive overhead activities. In some cases, a posterosuperior labral tear can cause a “1-way valve,” allowing for extravasation of synovial fluid from the glenohumeral joint.14,32,33 SSN compression at the spinoglenoid notch typically results in isolated infraspinatus atrophy, external rotation weakness, and a force-couple muscle imbalance, which indirectly causes posterior shoulder pain.19,23

Spinoglenoid notch cysts are relatively rare, with a reported prevalence34,35 of 1.3% to 1.5% and incidence 10 of 2% to 4%, and the indications, timing, and optimal treatment algorithm, depending on patient and cyst characteristics, have not been well defined. Indeed, the management of spinoglenoid notch cysts (SGNCs) may depend on symptom severity, the patient's activity demands, cyst size, and nerve involvement, as SGNCs can be incidental and asymptomatic. 6 Current management options include observation, 37 ultrasound-guided aspiration, 8 arthroscopic intra-articular decompression alone,16,22,32,36 arthroscopic labral repair without decompression,15,31 arthroscopic labral repair with decompression,9,14,24 and open extra-articular cyst excision.11,19 Outcome heterogeneity between studies, variability in study quality, and limited comparative analyses have made comparative synthesis of the literature challenging to date, with previous reviews focusing only on a limited range of treatment modalities.6,17

The purpose of this meta-analysis was to analyze the current literature on the management of SGNCs and to establish a clinically useful algorithm based on patient and cyst characteristics. We hypothesized that in the presence of a labral tear, arthroscopic cyst decompression with concurrent labral repair would yield superior patient-reported outcomes, clinical improvement, and a lower recurrence rate compared with open and nonsurgical approaches.

Methods

Search Strategy and Eligibility Criteria

A systematic review of the existing literature on the treatment of spinoglenoid notch cysts, in accordance with the 2020 PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement, was conducted in the PubMed, Scopus, and Embase online databases from database inception to August 2025 (Figure 1). The review was designed according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome framework, 29 and was registered on PROSPERO (1238412). The initial search queried studies reporting patient outcomes and complications after surgical or nonoperative treatment for spinoglenoid notch cysts. The search terms ‘((Spinoglenoid) OR (Spinoglenoid Cyst) OR (Paralabral Cyst) OR (Ganglion Cyst) OR (Suprascapular Nerve Entrapment) OR (Suprascapular Neuropathy) OR (Spinoglenoid Notch) OR (Suprascapular Nerve)) AND ((Excision) OR (Decompression) OR (Arthroscopic Decompression) OR (Arthroscopic Excision) OR (Surgery) OR (Aspiration) OR (Ultrasound Guided Aspiration) OR (CT Guided Aspiration) OR (Observation) OR (Conservative Management) OR (Arthroscopy) OR (Surgical Procedures))’ were utilized to populate studies in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus. No restrictions on publication date were placed on the search. Reference lists of relevant articles were searched, and any articles that applied to the treatment of spinoglenoid notch cysts were also included.

PRISMA search criteria. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies reporting on clinical management (operative or nonoperative) of spinoglenoid notch cysts; (2) full text availability; and (3) patients aged ≥18 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-English studies; (2) biomechanical studies; (3) cadaveric studies; (4) in vitro studies; and (5) studies involving concomitant procedures. There were no studies that included suprascapular notch cysts.

Screening and Data Extraction

Two independent authors (R.L. and L.H.) performed abstract and full-text screening of each article, where any discrepancies were resolved by a third independent author (A.C.). All authors who participated in full-text screening are medical students. Data were extracted from the remaining studies by 2 independent authors (A.K. and N.A.) using Microsoft Excel (Version 16.70; Microsoft Corp). Information regarding each included article, such as the author's name, publication date, Level of Evidence, patient and study characteristics, mean follow-up time, treatment type, concomitant procedures, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), was recorded. PROMs with at least 2 studies reporting on them in each cohort were included. Additionally, rates of postoperative complications and revisions were collected.

Data and Statistical Analysis

Weighted means of spinoglenoid cyst recurrence, reoperation, and complication rates, as well as outcomes at final follow-up, were calculated through subgroup analyses based on treatment approach, which included arthroscopic decompression and labral repair; arthroscopic labral repair only; arthroscopic decompression only; other treatment (including aspiration); and open decompression.

For binary outcomes (recurrence, complications, or reoperations), random-effects meta-analyses were performed to calculate pooled event rates. For continuous variables (range of motion, patient-reported outcomes), a formal meta-analysis was not feasible due to inconsistent reporting of standard deviations across studies. Instead, outcomes were pooled using sample-size-weighted means to provide descriptive summary estimates with ranges (minimum-maximum) to highlight study heterogeneity. All forest plots included 95% CIs and I2 values to assess heterogeneity. A 2-tailed Z test for proportions or a t test for means was performed to determine significant differences between treatment approaches. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Meta-analysis and descriptive statistics were conducted in RStudio (Version 4.3.2) using the meta and metafor packages to create forest plots.

Risk of Bias Assessment

To ensure the included studies met acceptable quality standards, the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized studies (MINORS) criteria were employed. The MINORS criteria are a scale with a maximal score of 16 points for noncomparative, nonrandomized studies and 24 points for comparative, nonrandomized studies. Each criterion is assigned a value of 0 if not reported, 1 if reported but inadequate, or 2 if reported and adequate. Two independent authors (L.H. and A.K.) assigned a score to each included study in accordance with these criteria. Both authors independently scored each article; any disagreements were resolved by a third investigator (A.C.).

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics

The initial literature search yielded 1212 studies. After screening and eligibility assessment in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines (Figure 1), 32 studies were included in the final quantitative synthesis, encompassing 562 shoulders treated for spinoglenoid notch cysts.

Studies were subdivided by treatment modality and categorized as “arthroscopic with labral repair and decompression”; “arthroscopic with labral repair, no decompression”; “arthroscopic with decompression, no labral repair”; “Open decompression”; and “Other.” The most common treatment for spinoglenoid notch cysts was arthroscopic decompression with concurrent labral repair; nearly half of the shoulders included in the analysis underwent decompression and labral repair (Figure 2). The demographic breakdown of included patients by treatment modality is summarized in Table 1. The mean patient age across studies ranged from 21 to 54, with the mean age relatively consistent across treatment methods. Most studies also had a predominance of male patients, with male proportions ranging from 33% to 100%. The mean follow-up time was 24.2 months, and the longest follow-up time was 15 years. The mean cyst size ranged from 0.5 to 6.8 cm, and larger cysts were found in shoulders that underwent arthroscopic labral repair only, as well as open decompression. Most treated shoulders exhibited concurrent labral pathology, with all treatment modalities showing labral tears in >90% of cases, except for arthroscopic decompression alone and those classified as other interventions.

A meta-analysis was performed to determine cyst recurrence, complication, and reoperation rates for each treatment modality. Arthroscopic decompression with labral repair yielded the lowest cyst recurrence rate, with the meta-analysis yielding a 3% (95% CI, 0.02-0.06) across 271 shoulders (Figure 3). Arthroscopic decompression of the cyst alone yielded a recurrence rate of 12% recurrence rate (95% CI, 0.05-0.24) (Figure 4). Arthroscopic labrum repair alone yielded a 6% recurrence rate (95% CI, 0.03-0.14) (Figure 5), and open decompression produced a 12% recurrence rate (95% CI, 0.08-0.87) (Figure 6). Other treatment modalities had the highest recurrence rate of 71% (95% CI, 0.60-0.80).

Complication rates were lowest in patients who underwent open repair, with no complications among any of the studies that reported complications, followed by patients who underwent arthroscopic decompression and labral repair, with a pooled complication rate of 2% (95% CI, 0.01-0.04). Isolated arthroscopic decompression yielded a 3% complication rate (95% CI, 0-0.19), and arthroscopic labral repair alone had a pooled complication rate of 5% (95% CI, 0.02-0.13). Complications, although rare, included postoperative capsulitis (33.3% of complications), persistent pain of the long head of the biceps (33.3%), and surgical site infections (16.7%).

Reoperation rates were highest among patients who underwent arthroscopic decompression alone, with a pooled reoperation rate of 9% (95% CI, 0.01-0.44). The lowest reoperation rates were found in open decompression, which reported no reoperations across all included studies. Arthroscopic decompression with labral repair, with a pooled reoperation rate of 4% (95% CI, 0.01-0.11), and arthroscopic labral repair alone, with a pooled reoperation rate of 3% (95% CI, 0-0.10), both had low reoperation rates.

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the MINORS criteria (Figure 7). Overall, the risk of bias across studies was moderate, in part reflecting the predominance of retrospective case studies among the included studies. Most studies clearly stated their objectives and defined relevant clinical endpoints, with points awarded for aim clarity and the appropriateness of outcome measures and endpoints. Strong point totals were also noted for appropriate follow-up periods and low loss-to-follow-up rates. However, prospective data collection was uncommon, and only a subset of studies included consecutive patients, increasing the risk of selection bias. In addition, no study performed a prospective sample size calculation.

Discussion

SGNCs are an uncommon cause of suprascapular nerve compression that can result in infraspinatus hypoactivity, external rotation weakness, muscular imbalance, and shoulder pain. The purpose of this meta-analysis was to analyze the current literature on the management of SGNCs and to establish a clinically useful algorithm based on patient and cyst characteristics to assist with clinical decision-making.

Our key findings across all treatment modalities showed that arthroscopic decompression with concurrent labral repair often yielded the most favorable outcomes, with the lowest pooled recurrence rate (3%), complication rate (2%), and reoperation rate (4%). Arthroscopic labral repair alone and open decompression alone showed low complication and reoperation rates but had higher recurrence (6% and 12%, respectively). Arthroscopic decompression alone also had a high recurrence (12%) and the highest reoperation rate (9%). “Other” interventions, including aspiration-based approaches, had the highest recurrence at 71%. Despite these differences in structural outcomes, all surgical groups achieved substantial improvements in pain and function, and most patients regained or maintained a good range of motion.

These findings suggest that arthroscopic decompression combined with labral repair, when a labral tear is present, may be beneficial, as it addresses both the anatomic cause of nerve entrapment and the pathway for cyst formation. Repairing the labral defect likely closes the “1-way valve” mechanism through which synovial fluid tracks into periarticular tissue, reducing the risk of reaccumulation in the cyst cavity. Previous work by Kim et al14,15 supports this concept, reporting high rates of complete cyst resorption and lower recurrence when decompression is combined with labral repair compared with labral repair alone. As such, surgeons should maintain a high index of suspicion for concomitant labral tears and carefully probe the labrum to ensure a complete evaluation. However, if not present, there is no indication to elevate a clinically normal labrum, and we do not recommend this.

Across the literature, 2 arthroscopic techniques dominate for treating spinoglenoid notch cysts: intra-articular decompression through a labral defect with concomitant labral repair and labral repair alone.14,15 Intra-articular decompression with labral repair is performed via standard posterior and anterosuperior portals, with cyst access achieved by instrumenting through the labral slit, allowing for evacuation of the cyst contents. Several reports emphasize that this approach leverages familiar arthroscopic portals and minimizes extra-articular dissection around neurovascular structures, such as the suprascapular nerve. This technique allows the surgeon to achieve cyst decompression and labral repair, making it safe, effective, and reproducible.25,32 Lichtenberg et al 18 described an all-arthroscopic strategy with favorable early clinical and imaging outcomes, popularizing arthroscopy as the default over open excision. Similarly, a 2017 therapeutic case series reported no surgical complications, no recurrence of the spinoglenoid notch cyst, and equal overall satisfactory clinical outcomes in patients who underwent arthroscopic decompression alone and in patients who underwent additional labral repair. 16 In contrast, an advantage of the open excision technique is direct visualization of the cyst, but this theoretically carries a higher risk of suprascapular nerve and vascular compromise, neglect of potential intra-articular pathologies if not performing a diagnostic arthroscopy, and excessive tissue trauma, potentially impeding patient recovery. 36 Despite these inherent risks, several studies, including our review, reported no complications after open excision, likely reflecting many factors such as careful patient selection and surgeon experience.21,23 Taken together, when a labral tear is present, arthroscopic labral repair with intra-articular decompression appears to be both the technically easiest and safest option in most patient situations. In contrast, repair alone may be reasonable when cysts are small or the nerve is not threatened. 38

Our pooled PROM findings, demonstrating improvements in ASES, Constant-Murley, and VAS scores, align with multiple primary studies on arthroscopic management. Kim et al 16 reported significant gains in ASES, VAS, Subjective Shoulder Value, and University of California, Los Angeles shoulder scores at 2-year follow-up after arthroscopic decompression of spinoglenoid cysts. Shon et al 32 observed excellent pain relief and functional recovery after all-intra-articular decompression with labral repair, with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-confirmed cyst resolution in most shoulders. 32 Notably, a comparative study from Kim et al 15 found similarly high final scores whether surgeons performed labral repair alone or combined with decompression, suggesting that PROMs may be driven primarily by addressing the labral defect, with decompression having a smaller effect on symptoms in patients. Consistent with our review, once the labrum is repaired when a tear is present, there are no significant differences in PROMs between techniques.

Our meta-analysis found the lowest recurrence with arthroscopic decompression with labral repair and a higher recurrence with decompression alone, consistent with the valve-closure rationale. In the broader literature, several series report complete cyst resolution and low recurrence after decompression, with or without labral repair, provided the cyst does not cause significant nerve compression, including Jeong et al 10 for inferior paralabral cysts and Youm et al 38 for spinoglenoid cysts treated with repair alone. Still, the 2018 systematic review by Schroeder et al 31 concluded that both labral repair alone and labral repair with decompression achieve similarly excellent results, with very low failure and recurrence rates. 31 This likely reflects a selection effect: patients with larger or neuropathy-associated cysts were more likely to undergo decompression, yielding outcomes comparable to patients without large cysts who required only labral repair. Our data also showed the highest reoperation rates when decompression was performed without labral repair, reinforcing that addressing the source tear may be critical to preventing recurrence.

Cyst size may strongly influence the risk of neuropathy and the choice of surgery. Larger lesions are more often associated with electromyography (EMG)-confirmed suprascapular neuropathy and greater preoperative external-rotation deficits. One study proposed a threshold of >2.2 cm for decompression, given the higher likelihood of EMG and nerve conduction study (NCS) findings. 26 In our review, larger cysts tended to appear in cohorts treated with labral repair alone or open procedures, which may reflect surgeons relying on valve closure without decompression when intra-articular evacuation is risky or choosing open access for very large cysts that are hard to access arthroscopically. Beyond cyst size as a factor in the management of spinoglenoid notch cysts, some surgeons have also proposed addressing the cyst lining in larger spinoglenoid notch cysts. However, current studies regarding cyst management do not endorse routine open excision of the cyst lining, regardless of size.15,30 In most cases, arthroscopic techniques that decompress the cyst, with or without labral repair, achieve resolution without the need to target the cyst lining specifically.22,31 Multiloculations or a medial cyst location that might not be easily decompressible from the joint, or failed arthroscopy, might be good indications for open decompression. 11 Indeed, although outside of the scope of this review, open SSN decompression is also a good option for SSN compression at the spinoglenoid notch without a cyst, which can occur from other causes such as spinoglenoid ligament hypertrophy and mobility of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus on either side of the scapula spine. It must be made clear that a cyst is not necessary for SSN compression. 19

Based on pooled outcomes and reported postoperative results from studies included in our search, we developed a clinical decision-making algorithm for the management of SGNCs (Figure 8). Asymptomatic patients or those without evidence of suprascapular nerve compression should initially be treated nonoperatively with observation and serial follow-up, given that SGNCs can frequently be incidental. We recommend that in patients who present with mild symptoms of suprascapular nerve compression without infraspinatus atrophy, a trial of 6 to 12 weeks of nonoperative treatment can be attempted, consisting of a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, physical therapy, and ultrasound-guided cyst aspiration. Piatt et al 23 described a 45% recurrence rate in patients who underwent physical therapy and ultrasound (US)-guided cyst aspiration, and Yoon et al 37 reported significant improvement in pain and mild improvement in range of motion in 12 cases of spinoglenoid notch cysts treated with physical therapy, NSAIDs, and US-guided cyst aspiration.23,37 Thus, a trial of nonoperative treatment could be warranted in cases of mildly symptomatic patients without evidence of overt nerve compression, but patients should be counseled on a high failure rate. Failure of conservative management was defined as persistent pain, weakness, or functional limitation after a structured trial of 6 to 12 weeks of nonoperative treatment, including NSAIDs, physical therapy, and/or image-guided aspiration. In particular, progression of symptoms, lack of improvement in pain or strength, or inability to return to the desired activity level were considered indicators of treatment failure.

In patients who present with symptoms of significant suprascapular nerve compression, as evidenced by infraspinatus atrophy with ER weakness on manual muscle testing, MRI evidence of nerve compression, muscle atrophy, or swelling, or EMG abnormalities, progression to surgical management is indicated. Arthroscopic labral repair with intra-articular cyst decompression was the most common approach and demonstrated superior outcomes with the lowest recurrence rates across studies; thus, it is recommended in our treatment algorithm, but only when a labral tear exists. There is no indication of an elevated clinically normal labrum. We recommend open surgical management in evidence of multilocular spinoglenoid notch cysts, large spinoglenoid notch cysts (>3 cm), or for recurrent cysts that have failed arthroscopic management. The senior authors (G.E.G. and G.P.N.) believe that for these very large and/or loculated cysts, the synovial lining of the ganglion can produce synovial fluid even if the original route of fluid egress through the labral tear is closed, with evidence that repeat trauma to the synovial lining can stimulate fibroblasts to produce hyaluronic acid, causing reaccumulation of fluid. 24 Thus, open cyst excision to remove the cyst lining beyond simple decompression is recommended.

The evidence presented in this review supports a structured, pathology-driven approach to managing spinoglenoid notch cysts, which we have summarized in a proposed treatment algorithm. Our pooled analysis showing the lowest recurrence and reoperation with arthroscopic labral repair and cyst decompression parallels the findings of Abboud et al, 1 who also reported that recurrence rates are lowest when both the labral lesion and cyst are addressed arthroscopically. The initial nonoperative branch of our algorithm, which includes NSAIDs, physical therapy, and US-guided aspiration for symptomatic patients without nerve involvement, is grounded in studies by Piatt et al 23 and Yoon et al 37 that demonstrated that aspiration-based strategies can provide short-term pain relief and modest gains in range of motion. When patients present with ER weakness, infraspinatus atrophy, EMG-confirmed suprascapular neuropathy, or MRI evidence of denervation, our pooled outcomes support proceeding with surgical decompression, combined with labral repair when a concomitant labral tear is present. This branch of our algorithm is consistent with the neurologic improvement reported by Shon et al. 32 Our recommendation to consider an open posterior excision for large or recurrent cysts reflects both our observation that larger lesions in open cohorts and previous reports by Mall et al, 19 Piat et al, 23 and Lee et al, 17 demonstrating reliable decompression and low recurrence when these specific cysts were managed through an open approach. Finally, the decision to observe incidentally detected or asymptomatic cysts is supported by case reviews that endorse continued observation as a treatment option in the absence of nerve compression. 3 Overall, the algorithm provides evidence-based guidance based on the included studies that balances patient symptoms, nerve involvement, and underlying pathology to optimize outcomes while minimizing unnecessary surgical morbidity.

This review is limited by heterogeneity in study designs, surgical indications, and technique details. Many cohorts lacked EMG and NCS, which may be useful for confirming suprascapular neuropathy and for tracking recovery after decompression or repair. Many cohorts also lacked standardized, uniform cyst-size measurements and clear thresholds for selecting repair alone versus repair and decompression. Postoperative PROMs and MRI or EMG data demonstrating resolution were reported inconsistently, with variable follow-up durations. This study elected not to include noncyst-related causes of SSN compression at the spinoglenoid notch. Finally, comparative data remain limited as most are derived largely from small, retrospective series with heterogeneous indications. As a result, current comparisons may be underpowered statistically.

Future work should focus on adopting uniform reporting of cyst dimensions, location, baseline nerve status, predefined surgical technique indications, and other defining factors. Patient outcomes should use a consistent core set of PROMs with satisfactory follow-up time. To close evidence gaps, direct comparisons of arthroscopic intra-articular decompression and open excision in larger cysts should include consistent metrics. Multicenter registries or prospective cohort studies may be the most realistic and reliable approach to deliver adequately powered findings.

Conclusion

Arthroscopic decompression with concurrent labral repair demonstrated favorable outcomes and low recurrence rates in properly selected patients with spinoglenoid notch cysts. While both arthroscopic and open approaches yield excellent pain relief and functional recovery, arthroscopic repair with decompression when a labral tear is present offers a safe, effective, and minimally invasive option for nonloculated cysts. For very large and/or loculated cysts, open decompression with removal of the cyst structure itself should be considered.
