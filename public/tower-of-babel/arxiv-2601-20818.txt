Quantum Memory and Autonomous Computation in Two Dimensions
Gesa Dünnweber, Georgios Styliaris, Rahul Trivedi

Abstract
Standard approaches to quantum error correction (QEC) require active maintenance using measurements and classical processing. The possibility of passive QEC has so far only been established in an unphysical number of spatial dimensions. In this work, we present a simple method for autonomous QEC in two spatial dimensions, formulated as a quantum cellular automaton with a fixed, local and translation-invariant update rule. The construction uses hierarchical, self-simulating control elements based on the classical schemes from the seminal results of Gács (1986, 1989) together with a measurement-free concatenated code. We analyze the system under a local noise model and prove a noise threshold below which the logical errors are suppressed arbitrarily with increasing system size and the memory lifetime diverges in the thermodynamic limit. The scheme admits a continuous-time implementation as a time-independent, translation-invariant local Lindbladian with engineered dissipative jump operators. Further, the recursive nature of our protocol allows for the fault-tolerant encoding of arbitrary quantum circuits and thus constitutes a self-correcting universal quantum computer.



Quantum information is fragile.
Noise rapidly degrades superpositions and corrupts entanglement.
Quantum error correction (QEC) counteracts this fragility
by encoding logical states into many physical qubits and repeatedly detecting and reversing errors. Today’s fault-tolerant architectures rely on
active
syndrome extraction and classical processing that impose a substantial resource overhead
14
;
9
;
17
. This raises the question of whether the protection and processing of quantum information could instead be achieved
passively
by embedding the corrections into the dynamics of the quantum hardware itself.

Such
self-correcting quantum memories
are known in four spatial dimensions (for example, the four-dimensional toric code under thermal noise), but are ruled out in two dimensions for local stabilizer Hamiltonian models
13
;
3
;
11
;
12
.
In three dimensions, prior works have explored a variety of codes with numerical indications for passive QEC capabilities but lacking analytical evidence
22
;
29
;
31
.
Hierarchical approaches have been rather limited.
Harrington’s construction
23
, inspired by a reduced variant
20
of Gács’s classical method, still required reliable classical processing. More recently, Balasubramanian et al. proposed a three-dimensional automaton (or two-dimensional scheme with explicit time-dependence) that decodes the toric code but crucially relies on global synchronization and spatially varying interactions
6
.
Similarly, Lake’s scheme
28
based on simulated confinement of spacetime defects locally uses noiseless classical processors that must grow with the system size.
Thus, whether scalable, passive stabilization of quantum information is achievable within realistic spatial dimensions has remained open.

Here, we answer this question conclusively by constructing and rigorously analyzing a two-dimensional, time-independent, translation-invariant quantum system with local interactions that autonomously performs error correction under a local noise model.
Our approach uses engineered
dissipation

37
where local, stationary couplings to an environment generate dynamics that continuously drive the system towards the desired code space
30
.
The error-correcting code realized by this is inherently non-local, with logical degrees of freedom supported on recursive structures, yet the enforcing interactions are strictly local (and uniform).

Our design is inspired by self-simulating cellular automata that were introduced by Gács to stabilize classical information
15
;
16
. We develop a universal quantum cellular automaton (QCA) that self-corrects across a hierarchy of scales
32
;
21
;
5
. This hierarchy supports a concatenated quantum code
1
;
18
whose syndromes are generated and processed autonomously. Local gadgets implement error correction at the lowest level, while higher levels enact coarse-grained stabilization and fault-tolerant logical computation through the same fixed, local interactions. The tower of simulations allows arbitrary quantum circuits to be embedded in the initial state and executed within the code, yielding self-correcting
computation

8
as well as memory.

We establish robustness by proving the existence of a noise threshold under a local noise model. Below this threshold, the logical errors are suppressed arbitrarily with increasing system size, and thus the memory lifetime diverges in the thermodynamic limit.
Our analysis adapts the extended rectangle formalism
4
to the autonomous dissipative setting and we show that any quantum circuit can be simulated efficiently, with overhead that scales polylogarithmically in the circuit size.
We also provide an implementation in continuous time that explicitly accounts for the absence of synchronized correction cycles.

Our work suggests several avenues for further development. The hierarchical, self-simulating approach is quite general and can be extended to other settings. In particular, we believe that more intricate structure-correction rules may enable self-correcting quantum computation even in one dimension.
Further, in relation to the theory of open quantum systems, we anticipate that performing a history-state embedding within our quantum memory directly renders a large class of quantum computation tasks stable to noise (in the sense of
36
).
Beyond computation, the construction has implications for non-equilibrium phases of matter.
When the encoded computation is a simple counter, the dynamics realize a dissipative time-crystal
40
that is protected against noise and provides a controlled setting in which long-lived temporal structure emerges without external signals.

I
Overview

We start with an overview of the main intuitions underlying our construction.
Firstly, we note that there exists a
classical
transition function which performs self-correction when applied globally in discrete time-steps on a two-dimensional lattice.
This is given by the remarkably simple Toom’s rule
35
:

Definition 1

(Toom’s rule)
.

A classical trajectory
\mathbf{s}(t,i,j)
on the periodic square lattice
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{2}
follows Toom’s rule if and only if

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}(t+1,i,j)=

\displaystyle\Toom(\mathbf{s}(t,\cdot),i,j)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Maj(\mathbf{s}(t,i,j),\mathbf{s}(t,i+1,j),\mathbf{s}(t,i,j+1))

at all time steps
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and all sites
(i,j)\in\Lambda
. The function
\Maj(a,b,c)
represents the majority vote: if at least two of its arguments coincide, then it returns their shared value, otherwise it returns the first argument. At each step, Toom’s rule thus computes the majority among the northern and eastern neighbors as well as the central site under consideration.

When we speak of applying Toom’s rule to a quantum register, we mean the channel obtained by first dephasing into the computational basis and then applying a classical Toom-update to the resulting diagonal state.

Toom’s rule stores a single bit encoded in the classical repetition code for an extended amount of time.
The intuition is that, in two or higher dimensions, taking the majority vote of a local neighborhood erodes islands of errors from their corners inwards.
Moreover, even in one dimension it is possible to reliably store information with a classical cellular automaton known as Gács’s construction
15
;
20
. This requires a complex update rule which ensures that the entire state is hierarchically structured and that regions of errors within it shrink whenever their internal structure has a smaller scale than that of the surrounding state.
For the sake of simplicity, we proceed with the two-dimensional case.

As a second ingredient to our proposal, we recall
active
error correction schemes with concatenated codes.
These have been used to prove the threshold theorem in Refs.
1
;
18
. We formulate their result as:

Lemma 1

(proof in the extended version of
1
)
.

For any fixed
t_{\mathrm{EC}}\geq 1
,
there exists a constant-size quantum error-correcting code
\mathcal{C}
and a complete, nearest-neighbor, measurement-free gadget set (state preparation, a universal gate set, error correction, and measurement) which satisfy the fault-tolerance conditions (Def.
7
in App.
A
).
These gadgets define a constructive map
\mathcal{E}
that transforms an ideal logical circuit
Q
into a one-level encoded circuit
\mathcal{E}(Q)
.
The
r
-level concatenated circuit
FT_{r}(Q)=\mathcal{E}^{r}(Q)
retains 1D nearest-neighbor locality and requires no intermediate measurements or classical control.

The proof of the error-correcting capabilities of such codes can be performed by tracking the rescaling of the logical error rate through each level with the method of extended rectangles

4
;
19
, which we explain in App.
A
.
We use a 1D nearest-neighbor version of the statement, implemented using a sequence of SWAP gates to realize the recursive gadgets locally. Following the extended version of
1
, we enforce locality already at the first encoding level and then concatenate. This increases the overhead by only a constant factor relative to the nonlocal scheme.
The gadgets are implemented in a measurement-free way via stabilizer applications to local ancillas that act as control qubits in subsequent operations
4
.
Each gadget uses
\mathcal{O}(1)
ancilla qubits per encoded block. Hence the ancillas can be counted as part of the constant block size of the base code.
Importantly, although such schemes require no intermediate classical processing, it is still necessary to store and control the structure of the protocol. To obtain a truly passive error correction method, we supply it with a clock mechanism that achieves this control in a time-independent manner whilst preserving the stability to errors.

The crucial task thus lies in finding a way to self-correct the structural information that is usually hard-coded in concatenated error-correcting schemes.
We achieve this by enforcing recursively layered Toom-like correction across scales.
Essentially, what we build is a self-correcting automaton that is capable of universal computation as in
16
. The universal control is then combined with a concatenated quantum error-correcting code for an overall self-correcting quantum system.

To make this rigorous, we utilize a universal quantum cellular automaton that replaces the temporal control in quantum computations with a fixed, local transition rule. It is known that there exist QCAs which achieve universality by shifting the computational complexity entirely to the initial state
32
;
38
;
33
;
39
;
26
.
Let
\Univ
denote the one-step unitary update rule of such a universal automaton.
Universality means that for any finite quantum circuit (or local transition rule)
R
there exists a product-state ‘program’ such that repeated application of
\Univ
on data registers initialized to hold this program implements
R
(up to a fixed encoding).
For example, there exists an initial state that represents the program with which
\Univ
implements Toom’s rule on a designated register.

However, Toom’s rule alone has limited error-correcting properties that allow for the storage of merely a single bit. The recursive application on several levels improves on this but brings the additional challenge that this hierarchical updating must itself emerge from a translationally invariant local rule. We note that a system which is capable of universal computation is thereby also capable of universal simulation. The simulation program can be engineered such that the system effectively simulates
the execution of its own update rule
on the states encoded in the level above.
It takes some care to achieve this self-simulation without disturbing lower levels and while still using only a finite state-space, but, once the self-simulation is robustly implemented, the error-correcting properties amplify themselves up to the highest level that fits in the system.
The hierarchical pattern of the states stored by this self-simulation is exactly the same as the structure needed in concatenated quantum error correction schemes.

Altogether, we construct a fixed set of interactions that simultaneously performs two tasks: Firstly, these interactions correct a constant number of errors locally within a quantum error-correcting code chosen according to Lemma
1
and within a classical error-correcting code that is a variant of Toom’s rule. These codes are located in separate subspaces and we consider the full system to obey a combined code. And secondly, the interactions enforce a simulation of an interacting quantum system whose qudits are represented by blocks of physical qudits. The interactions within these systems are applied on a slower scale than the physical interactions but, crucially, they are exactly the same as those of the system itself, i.e. the system exhibits
self-simulation
.
Together, these properties ensure that appropriate states can be stored in the system for an extensive amount of time. One may include additional interactions that perform gates of a chosen circuit and ‘carry them along’ through all levels of self-simulation. This way, the largest block size in the system undergoes an evolution that fault-tolerantly simulates a given circuit in the highest level of encoding.

II
Properties of the construction

Our main result is:

Theorem 1

(informal)
.

There exists a two-dimensional quantum system that enables self-correcting, fault-tolerant simulation of arbitrary quantum circuits.

Let us be more specific about the required implementation and mention some of its properties:

•

The interactions are fixed, independent of the desired accuracy and the physical system size. They are also translationally invariant.
Further, the interactions can be made independent of the simulated circuit by encoding the desired circuit in the initial state.

•

The operation of the self-correcting computer can be realized in one of two ways: Firstly, there exists a dissipative quantum cellular automaton given by a local transition rule that performs passive quantum error correction. In this setting, the corresponding interactions are applied throughout the system synchronously in
discrete time-steps
and we consider noise that produces local errors after each time-step.
Secondly, the self-correction can also be enforced in an open quantum system whose evolution in
continuous time
is given by a Lindbladian with local dissipative interactions and with noise that is modeled by a local unitary perturbation on the Lindbladian.

•

To simulate a translation-invariant circuit of depth
D
on
N
qubits to within error at most
\delta
, the scheme would use
N\mathrm{polylog}(ND/\delta))
qudits with physical noise strength below a constant threshold. Furthermore, the required initial state can be prepared fault-tolerantly with a circuit of depth
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{polylog}(ND/\delta))
.

•

The self-stabilization can be used to correct
any
concatenated coding scheme that admits nearest-neighbor, measurement-free, constant-ancilla gadgets as in Lem.
1
and that corrects sufficiently many errors.
Furthermore, because the hierarchical pattern enforced by recursive Toom’s rule application in fact reproduces the concatenated structure
along each row and column
of the two-dimensional system, this can be used to error-correct the structure of either a single 2D concatenated QECC or of several 1D concatenated QECCs in parallel. Each row can represent a separate one-dimensional logical system and nearest-neighbor gates can be performed between these.

III
Discrete time: Self-correcting quantum cellular automaton

We now lay out the details of the error-correcting procedure.
We start with the construction of a self-correcting quantum cellular automaton.
Thus, we consider operations that are applied throughout the system at integer times and allow errors to occur in between these time-steps.
Our notation as well as our proofs are heavily inspired by Gács’s classical construction in Ref.
16
.
The term
cells
refers to the local, finite-dimensional state placed at individual sites on a lattice.

III.1
Precise definition of the transition rule

The local states in our scheme carry two kinds of information that are stored in separate registers: a
structural state
(where is the site located on the lattice, what is the current time) and a
data-state
(what is the quantum information, including the local program, represented at this site). Both of these should be considered within a single level of the hierarchical structure, e.g. the location is given modulo the block size within a single level.

We will later fix the block size
M
and period
T_{0}
to absolute constants (independent of the simulated circuit, target accuracy and total lattice size). The simulator will execute one step of the simulated QCA over several repetitions of a fixed
T_{0}
-step cycle.
For now, we label these as parameters

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\text{linear first-level block size},

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle=\text{period of one first-level simulator cycle}.

The number of physical QCA steps required to simulate one step of the encoded higher-level QCA will be denoted as
T:=T_{0}T_{\mathrm{sim}}
.

Let
\mathbf{s}
be a pure-state trajectory of the system on the lattice
\Lambda
.
In the following,
(t,i,j)
refers to the
actual
space-time coordinate, so

is the number of updates that have been performed and
(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{2}
is the real-space lattice site.
The structure-state at each site represents the locally
believed
space-time location. For a trajectory
\mathbf{s}
, we denote this as

\displaystyle\tau(t,i,j)={}

\displaystyle\text{time-stamp stored in $\mathbf{s}(t,i,j)$},

\displaystyle(x(t,i,j),y(t,i,j))={}

\displaystyle\text{location stored in $\mathbf{s}(t,i,j)$},

taking values
\tau\in\mathbb{Z}_{T_{0}}
,
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}
.
The role of
\tau

Within one trajectory, the structural information in
\tau,x,y

is essentially classical in the sense that it can be measured at each step of the automaton without compromising the quantum information stored in the data-state.

We redefine Toom’s rule slightly so that it corrects periodic, instead of just constant, structural information.

Definition 2

(Toom’s rule for structural information)
.

Trajectories
\mathbf{s}(t,i,j)
of structural states
\mathbf{s}=(\tau,x,y)
follow Toom’s rule
(\tau,x,y)\mapsto\Toom((\tau,x,y))

\displaystyle\tau(t+1,i,j)=\Maj\big(\tau(t,i,j),\;

\displaystyle\tau(t,i+1,j),\;

\displaystyle\;\tau(t,i,j+1)\big)+1\bmod{T_{0}},

\displaystyle x(t+1,i,j)=\Maj\big(x(t,i,j),\;

\displaystyle y(t+1,i,j)=\Maj\big(y(t,i,j),\;

\displaystyle y(t,i+1,j),\;

Our framework starts with the transition function
\Univ
of a universal quantum cellular automaton.
For concreteness, one may take the QCA of Ref.
33
where each cell has constant dimension
d_{\mathrm{Univ}}=12
and splits into program and data bands that store the local operation to apply and the simulated system, respectively.
The global update is a product of a shift step which moves the program band relative to the data and a calculation step that applies the correct local unitary on the data, controlled by the neighboring program symbols.
(We extend the one-dimensional construction to two dimensions by composing additional shift steps along both lattice directions. The resulting
d_{\mathrm{Univ}}
remains constant.)
This system is programmable for universal quantum computation via a finite, spatially-varying product string which specifies the initial state.
The symbols
\gamma(t,i,j)
denote the local program degrees of freedom with constant-size alphabet, so that the full program information of the simulated QCA is spread out spatially across several sites.
Since
\Univ
is capable of arbitrary computation, it is also capable of implementing an arbitrary interacting quantum system (universal simulation).

We define from this a quantum cellular automaton
\R_{1}
that acts in each time-step as follows:

•

On the structure registers
\tau,x,y
it applies Toom’s rule according to Def.
2
.

•

On the data registers it applies the update channel
\Univ
with the local program state
\gamma
specifying the simulated rule.

Suppose that the system’s initial state contains the program of some other quantum cellular automaton
\R_{2}
.
We denote by
M
and
T_{\mathrm{sim}}
the block size and the number of steps needed by
\Univ
to simulate the application of
\R_{2}
to a single cell.
Noisy evolution under
\R_{1}
then simulates
\R_{2}
in a time-independent and translation-invariant system while the structural information is reliably stored with Toom’s automaton.
However, errors on the simulated state itself are never corrected if the automaton
\R_{2}
contains no error-correcting mechanism.
To address this, instead of implementing an arbitrary automaton, we let
\R_{1}
simulate itself, i.e. we give it a program that specifies
its own transition rule
.
This is well-defined if the sizes scale favorably.
We will later show that there indeed exists a self-consistent choice of
d,M,T_{0},T_{\mathrm{sim}}
.

With this,
\R_{1}
simulates itself recursively while correcting the initial structure-state on all levels of the code.
To correct the quantum state in the data registers, we replace the bare
\Univ
with a fault-tolerant encoding:
Fix an encoding scheme satisfying Lem.
1
. The associated concatenated code is corrected by a measurement-free,
T_{\mathrm{code}}
-length sequence of operations that we write in a time- and spatially
varying

\Concat(t,i,j)
be the resulting schedule of an encoded
\Univ

From this we obtain the transition function
\Concat^{\prime}:=\Concat(\Toom(\tau,x,y))
that replaces the space-time variation by a dependence on the (Toom-adjusted) local structure variables.
The QCA rule will include the application of
\Concat^{\prime}
to the data registers.
The description of the logical circuit instance
Q
is stored only in the initial data state (as a spatially distributed tape) and is read by
\Concat^{\prime}
when selecting the encoded logical gate to simulate.
Suppose that the initial state has correct structure variables at all sites and that the QCA successfully corrects errors occurring on this state, so that the probability of incorrect structure variables,
(\tau,x,y)\neq(t\bmod{T_{0}},i\bmod{M},j\bmod{M})
,
is small.
This means that the circuit schedule is carried along through the recursive pattern of the self-correcting state and the necessary error-correcting operations are performed at each step. Thus, the quantum state that is stored and computed on by the full system effectively undergoes the evolution of the fault-tolerant circuit
FT(Q)
.

To take noise into account and prove that the QCA indeed corrects errors, we need to modify its rules a bit:
Consider one iteration of the self-simulating procedure. We want to show that the
M
-sized block on which this is performed represents a single qudit of the level above and that this qudit experiences effective noise that is reduced in comparison to the physical noise on individual sites of the block (see Fig.
1
).
Note that not every block configuration corresponds to a valid encoded higher-level cell, i.e. a valid
codeword
. To nevertheless obtain an analytical renormalization of noise, we will need the property that the error-correcting procedure (= T steps of the QCA) yields states that are close to codewords whenever there are sufficiently few faults (see Sec.
III.2
).
The procedure generated by
\R_{1}
does not satisfy this. If the initial state is far from the desired subspace then it is possible for different parts of the block to disagree on what qudit this should represent so that the overall state does not yield a codeword.

Fig. 1:
Schematic depiction of the noise reduction.

Each level of the construction corrects local errors via Toom’s rule (structure layer) and a concatenated quantum error-correcting code (data layer) while simultaneously performing a simulation of the quantum cellular automaton acting on the level above.
A macro-location is the full space-time region containing operations on one block during the
T

The states of simulated higher-level cells are spread out over blocks of qudits on the level below and appear faulty whenever the number of errors within one such block can no longer be corrected by the code.
We show that the noise model is effectively rescaled at each level of self-simulation so that the logical noise can be suppressed with increasing system size.

We rectify this in our analysis by introducing the property
structural health
that captures approximate correctness of the structure layer.
Under sufficiently few faults, the structure registers remain close to the unique legal configuration
(\tau,x,y)=(t\bmod T_{0},\,i\bmod M,\,j\bmod M)
(structural codeword).
Since a single faulty local operation may corrupt all qudits in its constant-size support, we quantify structural closeness in terms of constant-size defect
clusters

We condition all structural correctness statements on health.
Health violations are treated as bad macro-locations (effective faults) at the next concatenation level and we will show that these occur rarely when starting from an initially healthy state.

Definition 3

(Structural health)
.

Consider the interaction-neighborhood of our quantum cellular automaton. The transitions are localized to a nearest-neighbor region which has spatial extent
\leq 3

We say that a site

A cluster of errors is a set of singular sites that could appear as a result of a single faulty operation in the QCA, meaning that it must be contained within a

A group of level-

The self-correcting QCA relies on structural health to coordinate the block-wise self-simulation.
To prevent structural errors within one block from inducing data faults on neighboring blocks, we condition all inter-block data operations on a local coordinate-consistency check.

III.2
Sufficient conditions for passive error correction

We establish here a general set of sufficient conditions that a quantum system may possess to ensure passive error correction. This adapts the extended-rectangle (exRec) method
4
;
19
to the self-correcting case.
The intuition is that any quantum system that performs a self-simulation while also satisfying recursive fault-tolerance conditions achieves self-correction because, on the one hand, any trajectory in which the operations are performed according to the usual concatenated fault-tolerant prescription is shown to correct errors below some threshold noise-rate via the exRec accounting. On the other hand, the prescribed pattern of operations is automatically self-preserved by the transition rules due to its self-simulation with a chosen initial state.

Let us start by describing our noise model. Because the QCA is updated synchronously at integer times, its ideal evolution can be viewed as a translation-invariant circuit where each QCA step applies a constant-depth pattern of local gates to the lattice. A
location
is the local operation applied in the QCA step at a specified space-time position. (In particular, a two-qudit gate is one location, not two.) We model noise as acting on each location by coupling the system to an arbitrary environment and replacing the ideal operation by a joint system-bath unitary.
This yields a
fault-path expansion
where the full noisy evolution is characterized by the subset of space-time locations at which a fault occurs.

We allow correlated, non-Markovian
local noise
in the sense of
4
;
34
;
2
:

Definition 5

(Local noise)
.

For a set
I_{r}
of
r

E(I_{r})
denote the sum over all fault paths in which faults occur at all locations in
I_{r}

The noise is local with strength
\eta

r
and
I_{r}
,
||E_{r}||\leq\eta^{r}

This model allows arbitrary correlations across space and time mediated by the bath.
It is automatically satisfied whenever the system-bath interaction is local, meaning that faults couple only to the qudits acted on at that location
4
.
We assume that the physical system evolves under synchronized applications of the QCA
\R
at discrete time-steps and experiences local noise.
For intuition one may picture the special case of independent stochastic faults with local error rate
p

\eta\sim\sqrt{p}

The exRec formalism provides a way to keep track of the rescaling of errors in concatenated error correction schemes. We review the method in App.
A

Following Refs.
4
;
19

Thus, any faulty block-crossing operation that lies between a good and a bad exRec is always counted in the bad macro-exRec. This is consistent whenever the block-crossing operations are not required for within-block error-correction, i.e. if they appear only in multi-block (2-qudit) logical operations.

We use:

Lemma 2

(Threshold theorem. Proof in

Consider a quantum computer subject to local noise.

In conventional concatenated error correction schemes the recursive simulation of the logical circuit is a hard-coded sequence of operations. The distinguishing property of our construction is that the hierarchical structure automatically builds itself when the engineered interactions are applied everywhere at the lowest level.

(Threshold for self-correcting QCA)

The time-slices of the QCA’s trajectories can be mapped to the hard-coded steps of a gadget’s circuitry in active QEC protocols.

We first establish some useful properties of the update rule

exRec is directly bad if, ignoring influence neighborhoods, it contains more than
t_{\mathrm{EC}}^{S}
clusters of errors. By counting the number of locations within one such exRec, there exists a constant
\tilde{A}_{S}
such that the corresponding effective noise strength satisfies
\tilde{\eta}_{l+1}^{S}\leq\tilde{A}_{S}\left(\eta_{l}^{S}\right)^{t_{\mathrm{EC}}^{S}+1}
.
We relate this to the stricter notion of badness which accounts for the entire influence neighborhood. One directly bad exRec can render at most
R
exRecs bad in this sense. Therefore, for any set of
r
level-
l
exRecs to be bad, at least
\lceil r/R\rceil
directly bad level-
l
exRecs are required. By the locality of noise, the resulting effective noise is bounded as
\eta_{l+1}^{S}\leq\left(\tilde{\eta}_{l+1}^{S}\right)^{1/R}\leq\left(\tilde{A}_{S}\left(\eta_{l}^{S}\right)^{t_{\mathrm{EC}}^{S}+1}\right)^{1/R}
.
We set
t_{\mathrm{EC}}^{S}=6\geq 2R
, so this indeed yields the recursive sparsity condition
\eta_{l+1}^{S}\leq A_{S}{\left(\eta_{l}^{S}\right)}^{t_{\mathrm{EC}}+1}
with
t_{\mathrm{EC}}=1
and constant
A_{S}
.
∎

We have shown that at good macro-locations the structure gadget is correct and that bad macro-locations can be treated as a local effective fault at the next level.
Together with Lem.
4
, this is sufficient for the threshold argument of Prop.
1
.
This concludes the proof of self-correction capabilities for the QCA with rule
\R
.

The proofs have so far assumed evolution on periodic boundary conditions.
We remark that the QCA can be directly extended to open boundary conditions by keeping four copies of the state at each site and letting the transition rules ‘fold over’ at the edges where they can detect an open boundary and re-direct interactions to the appropriate layers, so that the system emulates a two-dimensional projection of a torus.

III.4
Starting and stopping a self-correcting computation

We have seen that the self-simulating transition rule
\R
can fault-tolerantly encode an arbitrary quantum circuit. If one wants to obtain the output of a quantum circuit encoded in this construction, it is necessary to start and stop the self-correcting interactions (the automaton) in specific ways.
The same transition rules can also be used to build a self-correcting quantum memory by setting the simulated circuit to consist solely of identity gates.
Stopping the computation is not an issue in that case since there is no disadvantage in executing this circuit several times. In the thermodynamic limit this system can remember quantum states indefinitely. On any finite system, the memory time will be determined by the logical noise within the largest level of encoding that still fits inside the boundaries.

Let us first address the required initial state.
The self-correction of the automaton relies on the hierarchical structure that ensures error correction happens on all levels of the code, and thus it works only if the evolution starts in a state that is already encoded in the desired code. For the structure-state this demands a product state and for the data-state it depends on the specific concatenated code used in Lem.
1
. Magic-state distillation and verification protocols can be used to prepare these states
25
;
10
(and, if necessary, one may even control the preparation protocol within the self-correcting structure by carrying the corresponding instructions and the required ancilla qubits along with the circuit-scheme through all levels of concatenation).
Because there exists a constant-depth circuit for preparing the one-level encoded initial state and there are doubly-logarithmically many concatenation levels in total (in terms of the desired circuit size and accuracy), this yields a polylogarithmic circuit complexity for the fully encoded initial state.

Once the desired initial state is prepared, one can evolve under the self-correcting transition rule in order to apply the encoded circuit evolution. After some number of rounds, the highest-level encoded circuit will have undergone exactly one round of the simulated logical circuit. At that point, the computation is finished and one should measure the final state and decode classically to obtain the output of the computation. Alternatively, one could switch to different interactions so that the system stores the circuit’s final state within its capacity as a quantum memory.

We now explain how one may circumvent the need for externally timing the precise endpoint of the encoded computation.
Note that the believed time
\tau
on the highest level of the automaton correctly represents the overall time as steps performed in the encoded logical circuit.
We introduce a marker register at each site that can be used for the highest level to recognize its status as overall timer (even on periodic boundaries) and to switch from simulation of the circuit to memory, i.e. to apply encoded identity gates regardless of the program, after exactly one round: Suppose that this marker is correctly initialized and introduce transitions that remove all such markers whenever the adjacent cells do not also carry the same marking (cf.
15
). This prevents the marker states from appearing anywhere except on the highest level, since that is the only level with no other neighbors except itself. This way the interactions can be switched automatically at precisely the right moment and the output of the computation can be extracted at any point afterwards (with memory time scaling with the total system size).

IV
Continuous time: Self-correcting Lindbladian

The protocol above enables self-correction in two dimensions with a fixed, translationally invariant update rule in the form of a quantum cellular automaton. This requires discrete applications of the transition rule as well as global synchrony between these applications.
In the current Section, we provide an implementation that works even in continuous time and which allows for distant parts of the system to become asynchronous.
We build on top of the self-correcting QCA to engineer time-independent, translationally invariant dissipative interactions for passive quantum error correction.

IV.1
Passive error correction in asynchronous trajectories

Our first step is to define a version of the self-correcting automaton that runs under
discrete-time, asynchronous
application of the updates.
For this, we use several layers of states, placed in separate local registers, to represent many copies of the synchronized QCA, with each copy at a different time-step of the correction cycle.

Because each level of the hierarchy has a finite number
T_{0}
of time-steps before the error-correction cycle repeats, it is possible to store these steps redundantly in different parts of the local Hilbert space. The full state of one block in a single layer of the self-simulating scheme then consists of
M\times M\times T_{0}
qudits, which each represent a state of the original QCA from Def.
4
.

Updates are performed based on concurrent neighbor states and otherwise follow the rules of the synchronized QCA. The entire local state, made up of
1\times 1\times T_{0}
qudits, is updated simultaneously. Additionally, we keep track of how many updates have been performed at a site compared to its nearest-neighbors and never update more than one step ahead of these neighbors (cf. marching soldier scheme in
15
).
The reason for the latter restriction is that it guarantees neighboring sites undergo the same number of error correction cycles so that the order of the updates is irrelevant. Thus, the time-slices of the state are comparable throughout the whole system.

These updates then produce self-simulating patterns as before, but now there exist
T_{0}
copies of the automaton on all levels in the hierarchy of simulations. The same-time slices of the state experience the same updates as if they were acted on synchronously.
With this, noisy updates on the
M\times M\times T_{0}
state map to noisy updates on the
T_{0}
separate concurrent
M\times M\times 1
slices, so the synchronized-time analysis can be applied slice-wise.

One might worry whether it is indeed possible to store all of the time-slices as redundant copies since this complicates the automaton simulated by
\Univ
and thus lengthens the cycle period
T_{0}
which in turn requires more copies.
In our case, the redundancy affects the update rules only in the regard that the simulation is now applied separately on each time-layer. The additional overhead of the corresponding instructions is constant and thus it does not significantly increase the number of time-steps
T_{0}
in one cycle of the simulator.

IV.2
Discrete trajectories in continuous-time dynamics

We now extend the results on discrete correction steps with constant noise rate to a notion of error correction in
continuous time
.

Consider an open quantum system whose evolution
\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}(\rho)
is governed by a Lindbladian

\mathcal{L}(\rho)=\sum_{\alpha}\gamma_{\alpha}\left(\Phi_{\alpha}(\rho)-\rho\right)

built from
local, completely positive, trace-preserving update channels

\Phi_{\alpha}
.
In our case, these channels have constant rates
\gamma_{\alpha}=1
and are given by the local attempted correction updates that defined the asynchronous self-correcting automaton (including local dissipative resets that reinitialize the ancilla registers used by the measurement-free scheme).
Each
\Phi_{\alpha}
acts on
\mathcal{O}(1)
neighboring cells and is a translate of one of finitely many update channels, so the resulting Lindbladian is local, time-independent and translation-invariant.
For any given period of time, expectation values on the final state of the evolution can be determined by averaging over an ensemble of
trajectories
where the probability of each trajectory depends on the update events along the taken path.
Writing
\Phi_{\alpha}(\rho)=\sum_{s}K_{\alpha,s}\rho K^{\dagger}_{\alpha,s}
with
\sum_{s}K^{\dagger}_{\alpha,s}K_{\alpha,s}=\mathbb{I}\;\forall\alpha
, this yields independent Poisson processes with constant attempt rates for each jump
\alpha
, independent of the system state.

Let us introduce noise in this setting. The noise is realized by a perturbation
\delta\mathcal{L}
on the Lindbladian.
We adopt an error model

\delta\mathcal{L}(\rho)=\sum_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}(N_{\beta}\rho N_{\beta}^{\dagger}-\rho)

with local unitary noise jumps
N_{\beta}
and total rate
p=\sum_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}
. This encompasses, for example, local Pauli noise.
The trajectories then follow a stochastic process with two kinds of update channels, correction-updates and noise-updates, that are each applied randomly. The rates are state-independent except for the constraint that correction-updates are accepted only at sites whose local correction-step counter does not exceed any of its nearest-neighbors’ counters (marching soldier constraint).

Since noise-updates obey an independent Poisson process, the probability of having at least one noise-step in a time interval
\Delta t
is

\mathcal{O}(p\Delta t)
.
We compare this with the expected waiting time between two correction-updates.
These obey a stochastic process with each site carrying a constant attempt rate for performing the next correction step. An attempted correction-update at site
(i,j)
is accepted iff it satisfies the marching soldier constraint.
Thus, the instantaneous mean rate of
successful
correction-updates per site is
v=\mathbb{P}\{\text{$(i,j)$ is a local minimum of the update counter}\}
.
The expected density of local minima in this process is known to converge monotonically in time towards a positive constant that remains bounded away from zero in the limit of infinite system size
27
.
Thus, for small physical noise rate
p
, the probability (within the ensemble of trajectories) of at least one noise-step occurring between two successful correction-updates at a given site is
\mathcal{O}(p/v)=\mathcal{O}(p)
for fixed
v=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

In the trajectory picture, these faults generate a local noise process that corresponds to the discrete-time model of Sec.
III.2
with an effective fault strength
\eta=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{p})
per successful correction update.
We infer that noisy continuous evolution under this Lindbladian maps to discrete asynchronous steps with constant local noise strength. With the notes above, these steps precisely mirror the time-slices of the synchronized evolution, which enables a proof of the error-correction capabilities via the exRec formalism from before.
Lastly, we note that the Lindbladian dynamics can also be simulated within a Hamiltonian system by coupling the qudits to local ancillas that undergo dissipative resets
24
.

V
Discussion

We have shown that autonomous fault-tolerant quantum computation is possible in two spatial dimensions with strictly local, time-independent and translation-invariant dynamics. Our construction can be formulated either as a quantum cellular automaton with a fixed local update rule, or as a local Lindbladian generated by engineered dissipative jump operators. By combining a self-simulating hierarchical control architecture inspired by Gács’s classical schemes with a measurement-free concatenated code, we prove a non-zero noise threshold. Below threshold, logical errors are suppressed arbitrarily with increasing system size, so the memory lifetime diverges in the thermodynamic limit and arbitrary quantum circuits (encoded in the initial state) can be executed fault-tolerantly with polylogarithmic overhead.

These results contribute to our understanding of quantum error-correction without measurements, classical feedback, spatial inhomogeneity or external timing, and establish a 2D autonomous counterpart to the concatenated-code threshold theorems of actively controlled architectures.

Several open problems are suggested by our work.
A first direction concerns the open-system aspects of the construction in the presence of noise.
For any finite system size, logical faults ultimately mix between the encoded states, so the steady state of the unmodified memory is completely mixed in the logical degrees of freedom. It could be fruitful to formalize the intuition that adding a weak, translation-invariant bias yields a unique attractive fixed point whose local statistics can be post-selected to extract the outcome of an encoded computation.
On open boundaries, the highest level of the encoding can be detected from the absence of neighboring same-level cells.
With this, one may construct a translation-invariant Lindbladian that enforces the self-correcting dynamics with the highest level of encoding experiencing different interactions that apply the correction jumps both forwards and backwards to obtain a history-state logical Lindbladian. Perturbations on the physical Lindbladian are corrected so that the logical evolution effectively experiences reduced noise. We expect that one can increase the system size to make the expectation values of logical observables on the fixed point stable (in the sense of Ref.
36
), which would imply BQP completeness for the class of stable, size-independent Lindbladian fixed point problems.

Further, it would be interesting to see whether one can obtain a similar self-correcting quantum computation scheme even in one dimension. For this, we suggest embedding a quantum error-correcting code within the structure of a state stored in Gács’s one-dimensional robust universal automaton
15
. The proof should be roughly the same as the two-dimensional case, but the automaton would be significantly more complicated since the hierarchical structure cannot be error corrected so easily from comparing with a local environment.
This would provide a counterexample to dissipative variants of Ref.
7
.
In regard to classical computation, one may use a classical repetition code in the data layer of our scheme to obtain a simplified version of Gács’s two-dimensional automaton. Carrying our ideas over to one-dimension might similarly result in a streamlined proof of the classical, one-dimensional case.

Beyond concatenated codes, the hierarchical self-simulation framework is modular and could potentially be combined with other quantum code families.
In particular, one might use the self-correcting structure layer to eliminate explicit spatial dependence and global synchronization requirements in local-automaton decoders for topological codes (cf.
6
), potentially enabling fully translation-invariant correction of the toric code in two dimensions.

Another direction is to make the scheme self-organizing. Self-organization here is equivalent to asking whether the initial state can be prepared fault-tolerantly using the same fixed interactions. If achievable, this would provide an input-preparation mechanism where one needs to supply only the circuit description or the state to be stored as a translation-invariant product state, for example repeated locally across the lattice, and the dynamics autonomously generate the full hierarchical pattern. The main challenge is to ensure that this growth proceeds on a slower time-scale than the error correction inside already healthy regions, so that the act of building the hierarchy does not itself introduce and spread errors.

Finally, natural next steps are to generalize the noise model and optimize the efficiency of the scheme.
Our proofs treat local fault-path noise in discrete time and local unitary jump-operator perturbations in continuous time.
Extending the analysis to more general noise, in particular to non-unitary perturbations on the Lindbladian (e.g. via a fault-path expansion directly in continuous time) or non-local thermal noise, remains open.
Likewise, obtaining explicit threshold estimates, reducing the local dimension and simplifying the control mechanism are desirable steps towards practical realizability.

Definition 8

(Extended rectangles)
.

For every location in the logical circuit
Q
, we define the corresponding rectangle (Rec) in the encoded circuit
\mathcal{E}(Q)
to consist of the specific gadget followed by error correction. An extended rectangle (exRec) is defined as a rectangle along with the EC gadgets immediately preceding it. For example, the extended rectangle of a two-qudit gate consists of two leading EC gadgets, the two-qudit gate gadget, and two trailing EC gadgets.

The extended rectangles allow us to rescale the noise under the error-correcting encoding. Specifically, the leading EC gadgets ensure that the input to encoded operations reside in a subspace with bounded errors while the trailing EC-gadgets ensure that the output is error-corrected before passing to the next step. Thus, we can move the decoder from output to input through an entire circuit that may contain both good and bad exRecs.
For a good exRec, the corresponding Rec implements the ideal logical location (up to an operation acting only on the syndrome register carried by the *-decoder), whereas a bad exRec is treated as a single effective fault at the encoded level.

We now sketch how exRecs rescale local noise strength
\eta
(as defined in the main text) under one level of encoding, following
4
.
For any set
I_{r}
of
r
locations inside an exRec, local noise implies that the sum of all fault-path operators with faults at all locations in
I_{r}
has operator norm at most
\eta^{r}
.
Let
F^{(t_{\mathrm{EC}}+1)}
denote the sum of all fault-path contributions in the exRec with at least
t_{\mathrm{EC}}+1
faults (i.e., the bad part of the exRec).
Carefully counting all relevant fault paths with the inclusion-exclusion principle, one obtains a bound of the form

\|F^{(t_{\mathrm{EC}}+1)}\|\leq A\,\eta^{t_{\mathrm{EC}}+1}
,
where
A
is a combinatorial constant depending only on the maximal number of locations within an exRec and not on the simulated circuit.

Thus, a level-1 exRec simulates the ideal logical location with an effective encoded noise strength that is upper-bounded by
A\eta^{t_{\mathrm{EC}}+1}
.
It follows that repeated application of the fault-tolerant encoding scheme (concatenation) for
k
levels decreases the logical noise strength superexponentially to

\eta_{\text{log}}\leq\eta_{\text{th}}(\eta/\eta_{\text{th}})^{(t_{\mathrm{EC}}+1)^{k}}

whenever the physical noise strength
\eta
is below a constant threshold
\eta_{\text{th}}=A^{-1/t_{\mathrm{EC}}}
.
For an ideal circuit of depth
D
on
N
qubits, the norm of the sum of all bad fault-path contributions is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(ND\,\eta^{(k)})
, which upper-bounds the simulation error (in trace distance, hence also in total variation distance on measurement outcomes). Choosing

k=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\log\log(ND / \delta)\right)

achieves logical accuracy
\delta
with polylogarithmic overhead, proving Lem.
2
.
