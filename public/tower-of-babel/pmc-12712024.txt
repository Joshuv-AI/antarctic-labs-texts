From negligible to giant electrocaloric effect of poly(vinylidene fluoride)
Authors: Yibo Zhang, Zhiwei Ye, Fukun Niu, Chuanxi Xiong, Wei Wang, Yuheng Fu, Shixian Zhang, Shan Wang, Quanling Yang, Hongmei Qin, Shuang Liu, Qing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF), the most widely used ferroelectric polymer, typically exhibits a negligible electrocaloric effect (ECE) due to its symmetrical molecular configuration and bulky ferroelectric domains. PVDF-based ferroelectric terpolymers with giant ECE are expensive and have a narrow temperature window (Tw). Here, we report that PVDF films prepared by solid-phase pressure forming (PVDF-SPF) show a giant ECE of up to 12.8 K at 25 °C and a wide Tw of 60 °C. PVDF films underwent significant plastic deformation and extensive crystal fragmentation, forming loose and tiny β crystal grains under 30 MPa solid-phase pressure. The giant ECE primarily originates from orientation entropy changes of molecular chains associated with the reversible transition between loose and compact β crystals, as well as switching entropy changes of polar dipoles in tiny grains. Given the availability of PVDF, this work opens a route for the development of scalable solid-state cooling techniques.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Polymers, Devices for energy harvesting

Introduction

Our society heavily relies on cooling technologies, such as air conditioning, with the traditional vapor compression cycle being the dominant method. However, this technology suffers from low energy efficiency, large volume, and high complexity. Furthermore, there is an intensifying environmental concern regarding the ozone-depleting and greenhouse gas-emitting refrigerants utilized in vapor compression systems1. Solid-state cooling technologies based on caloric effects offer promising alternatives with superior energy efficiency and zero emissions2. Caloric effects can be classified into magnetocaloric3,4, mechanocaloric5–9 and electrocaloric effect10–12 according to the action of external fields. Electrocaloric cooling shows potential for applications in human thermoregulation, battery thermal management, energy harvesting and refrigeration13. To date, refrigerants with both high ECE and low cost remain a significant technical challenge for the large-scale application of electrocaloric cooling.

Entropy changes stemming from conformational transitions and dipole orientations are the main contributors to the ECE temperature drop (ΔTECE) in polymers14. PVDF-based ferroelectric terpolymers, such as poly(vinylidene fluoride-trifluoroethylene-chlorotrifluoroethylene) [P(VDF-TrFE-CTFE)], are well-studied relaxor ferroelectric terpolymers in electrocaloric. The large volume and physical pinning effect of the comonomers in the terpolymer restrict the ferroelectric domains to the nanoscale, creating polar nanoregions. The nanoregions facilitate filed-induced relaxor ferroelectric to ferroelectric conformation transitions, resulting in a slim hysteresis loop15,16. The non-polar phase of the terpolymer transform into the polar β phase when an electric field was applied, confirming that its ECE is primarily attributable to the structural phase transition11. The reversible reorientation of the molecular chain conformation contributes to the large entropy and temperature change of terpolymers17.

Despite the superior ferroelectricity of PVDF, it exhibits negligible ECE at room temperature due to bulky and compact ferroelectric domains, which result from its symmetrical and regular molecular configuration. To improve its electrical performance, strategies such as repeated pressing-and-folding have been employed to extend polymer chains multidirectionally, thereby boosting dielectric energy storage capability18. In addition, high-power ultrasonication was utilized to induce a hard-to-soft transition, increasing the piezoelectric coefficient d31 in PVDF19. The presence of oriented amorphous fraction structures has been reported to greatly improve the piezoelectric response of PVDF20. In terpolymers, the introduction of a small number of double bonds can generate numerous loosely correlated polar entities in a high-entropy state, which also favors the ECE11. This suggests that such loosely correlated intermediate phases with ordered conformations may significantly contribute to the polarization behavior. In general, the morphology of polymers is influenced by both chain structure and processing techniques21,22. We believe that optimized forming techniques can restrict the ferroelectric domains of PVDF to tiny grains with loosely assembled chains. Given that the dipole moment of the PVDF monomer is higher than that of the comonomers in PVDF-based terpolymer23,24, the maximum polarization (Pmax) of PVDF is consequently higher. Therefore, PVDF has the potential to exhibit giant ECE if the bulky polar dipoles are decoupled.

Here, we first performed uniaxial stretching for the PVDF films prepared by solvent casting (PVDF-C), achieving a stretched PVDF film (PVDF-S) with 96% β crystal. This process increased the long-range order while minimizing the initial dipole switching entropy, resulting in an entropic storage effect. The entropic storage effect boosted the piezoelectricity of PVDF but inhibited the ECE25. Subsequently, the PVDF-S underwent significant plastic deformation and extensive crystal fragmentation during solid-phase pressure forming (SPF), a pressure-forming method below the melting temperature of materials, to form PVDF-SPF film. The SPF technology exhibited a superentropic effect, effectively releasing the entropy stored during the stretching process. The variable-angle attenuated total reflection infrared (VA-IR) spectra revealed the internal orientation of the β phase. The atomic force microscopy with infrared spectroscopy (AFM-IR) images proved the decoupling of long-range order in the molecular chains of PVDF-SPF. In situ high-voltage infrared spectra confirmed the reversibility of dipole flipping, and in situ high-voltage 2D wide-angle X-ray diffraction (2D-WAXD) curves illustrated the reversible transition between loose and compact β crystals. The PVDF-SPF achieved a giant ΔTECE of 12.8 K at 25 °C and 200 MV m−1 (breakdown field strength, Eb = 617 MV m−1), surpassing PVDF-C by 20 times. The PVDF-SPF displayed a wide Tw of 60 °C and a high Young’s modulus (Y) of 1.7 GPa, demonstrating excellent overall performance and application potential.

Results

Solid-phase pressure formed PVDF for ECE

The PVDF-C is dominated by α and γ spherulites with large and weakly polar paraelectric dipole domains26 (Fig. 1a), which is mainly used as dielectric materials27. The PVDF-C demonstrates a slender hysteresis loop28 with a Pmax generally around 2 μC cm−2 at 200 MV m−1. Upon uniaxial stretching for PVDF-C, the α and γ spherulites can transform into fibrous β crystals with electroactivity29, which are mainly used as piezoelectric materials with a piezoelectric coefficient25 typically between 20 and 35 pC N−1. However, the ECE of these piezoelectric materials is generally poor. To achieve a giant ECE in PVDF, it is essential to maximize the β phase to increase the density of field-induced dipoles. Uniaxial stretching of PVDF-C to a strain of up to 1400% (Fig. 1b) yielded PVDF-S with approximately 96% β phase (Supplementary Note 1), forming fibrous β crystals with bulky and compact ferroelectric dipoles. Subsequently, the PVDF-S was gradually pressurized to 30 MPa at 85 °C by a hot press machine with adhesive gaskets for primary SPF. At 155 °C, we applied the same pressurization method for secondary SPF. The processes cause the film to undergo significant plastic deformation along the transverse direction (Fig. 1d) and extensive β crystal fragmentation, destroying the long-range ordered structure and forming loose and tiny β grains (Fig. 1c). The β phase content of PVDF-SPF was reduced to 94% (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The PVDF-C showed a negligible ΔTECE of 0.6 K. The PVDF-S exhibited a limited ΔTECE of 1.9 K, predicting a small amount of crystal fragmentation in the film by high-draw ratio stretching. The PVDF-SPF demonstrated a significantly higher ECE, with a maximum ΔTECE of 12.8 K at 25 °C and 200 MV m−1 (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 5a). This result was cross-validated using an infrared thermometer. Due to lower sampling rates of the infrared method, the measured temperature change was reduced (Supplementary Fig. 5). A high-resolution infrared thermal imager visually captured the temperature variations of the PVDF-SPF during a single electrocaloric cycle, confirming the giant electrocaloric effect (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Movie 1).

To investigate the intrinsic relationship between ECE and crystal morphology, we employed VA-IR30 to characterize the orientation and distribution of the β phase in PVDF membranes prepared by three processing methods (Supplementary Fig. 2). VA-IR is a characterization technique that captures conformational information across all azimuthal angles by rotating the film sample. This enables in situ visualization of the spatial distribution of molecular conformations within the film. The PVDF-C primarily contained the short-γ phase16 (at 833 cm−1), which displayed weak electroactivity and showed strong vibrational signals at all angles (Fig. 1g). Almost no intensity of the long-β phase16 (at 1275 cm−1) was observed. The PVDF-S revealed a strong long-β phase signal at all angles, while the short-β phase16 (at 837 cm−1) presented a pronounced signal near 0° (Fig. 1h). This observation indicated that the molecular chains in the PVDF-S possessed predominant regular long-range ordered structure with limited short-range ordering. The longer all-trans conformation necessitates a higher electric field strength for alignment with the field direction31, which impedes dipole orientation and reduces the switching entropy change during flipping.

The PVDF-SPF presented a distinct β phase distribution compared to the PVDF-S. The long-β phase became discontinuous, while the short-β phase became continuous at all angles, indicating an interruption of long-range order (Fig. 1i). The spectral width of the electroactive short-β phase increased, suggesting a greater variety of microscopic polar states and a more disordered short-β phase orientation32. Additionally, across the entire angle, the difference between the maximum and minimum intensities of the short-β phase decreased, suggesting to a more uniform distribution. Thus, the number of microscopic polar states in the PVDF-SPF was dramatically increased, which enhanced the dipole switching entropy change (ΔSdip) and positively impacted the ECE of the system.

Reversible transition in β crystals

To facilitate a more direct observation of the polar conformational distribution in the three films, we characterized the samples using AFM-IR33. An infrared wavelength of 1275 cm−1, associated with the all-trans conformation of PVDF, was selected for analysis34. The topographies of the films were measured, and the nanoscale infrared images were normalized to more clearly delineate the β phase distribution (Supplementary Fig. 6).

The surface morphology of PVDF-C exhibited spherical aggregation (Supplementary Fig. 6a), with a quite weak intensity of the all-trans conformation and only discernible in specific regions (Fig. 2a). In contrast, the PVDF-S displayed a clear molecular chain orientation, as evidenced by stretching marks on the surface (Supplementary Fig. 6b). The oriented molecular chains aggregated, reaching a width of approximately 730 nm (Fig. 2b). The infrared amplitude for all-trans conformation was observably enhanced, indicating strong long-range coupling. Despite the presence of oriented molecular chains in the PVDF-SPF, the long-range coupling was disrupted. The aggregation width of the short all-trans conformation was reduced to 150 nm (Fig. 2c), with a more uniform distribution and increased orientational randomness. This result was highly consistent with the measurements obtained from VA-IR (Fig. 1i). The peak intensities of the infrared amplitude for the PVDF-S and PVDF-SPF were 0.64 mV and 0.6 mV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6d), without a significant decrease in the molecular chain polarity during SPF. Additionally, Young’s modulus (Y) was characterized by AFM (Supplementary Fig. 7), with values of 1.1, 2.1, and 1.7 GPa for PVDF-C, PVDF-S, and PVDF-SPF, respectively. High modulus could notably improve the mechanical performance and fatigue durability of polymer electrocaloric devices.

Moreover, we employed high-voltage infrared spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 8) to analyze the evolution of internal polar conformation in the PVDF-SPF17,35. After applying 200 MV m−1 to PVDF-SPF, the infrared absorption of both the long-β phase and the short-β phase weakened (Fig. 2d). Given the high β phase content (94%) in PVDF-SPF, further increase in its concentration under the electric field is considered negligible. The reduction in absorption intensity is primarily attributed to the reorientation of polar dipoles along the field direction, which decreases their vibrational component along the direction of the infrared electric vector36,37 (Supplementary Fig. 9). This reorientation also leads to a decrease in dipolar entropy (Supplementary Note 3). The initial spectrum of PVDF-SPF was nearly identical to that after high-voltage removal, confirming the reversibility of the dipole flipping. Subsequently, high-voltage 2D WAXD characterization was performed (Fig. 2g)36. The peak position of PVDF-SPF shifted from 20.15° to 20.48°, decreasing the interchain spacing of the β crystal (Fig. 2e). The dispersion of the Debye ring reflects the orientational disorder among β crystals. A broad and diffuse ring indicates more random orientation, corresponding to a more disordered system with higher entropy. In contrast, a sharp and concentrated ring suggests increased orientational order and lower entropy. Based on this observation, we propose that changes in the orientational order of crystal domains may represent an additional and previously overlooked entropy contribution, namely the β phase orientation entropy change (ΔSorient) (detailed derivation in Supplementary Note 3). The broadening of 837 cm−1 in the VA-IR characterization (Fig. 1i) indicated that the initial PVDF-SPF had a large microstate number in the short-β phase. AFM-IR images (Fig. 2c) showed that lower β phase orientation corresponded to greater randomness. After applying a high voltage, the molecular chains in PVDF-SPF became more compact (Fig. 2f), resulting in a decrease in D, accompanied by an increase in relative crystallinity (Supplementary Fig. 10). The narrowed diffraction rings (Fig. 2g) indicated an increase in P. These changes significantly reduced the microstate number of the system, leading to a decrease in entropy. The minor shaded areas signified that the transition from loose to compact β crystal resulted in a negligible loss (Fig. 2f). As with ΔSdip, the orientation entropy was reversible under the electric field.

Electrical responses and structural analysis

We performed various electrical and structural characterizations on PVDF-C, PVDF-S and PVDF-SPF. The PVDF-C exhibited a slim polarization-electric field hysteresis loop (P-E loop), with a Pmax of only 1.86 μC cm−2 at 200 MV m−1 (Fig. 3a), insufficient for providing adequate switching entropy. The PVDF-S showed a fat P-E loop (Fig. 3b), with a high Pmax of 9.83 μC cm−2 and a large remanent polarization (Pr) of 5.85 μC cm−2 at 200 MV m−1, indicative of piezoelectricity38. However, the permanent dipoles with high density, which are proportional to large Pr, failed to follow electric field variations, sacrificing both ΔSdip and the ECE. In contrast, the PVDF-SPF displayed a distinctly different P-E loop (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 11) with a high Pmax of 9.13 μC cm−2 and a low Pr of 1.12 μC cm−2 at 200 MV m−1, favoring a high ΔSdip and a giant ECE. The unipolar P-E loop efficiency of PVDF-SPF was much higher than that of PVDF-S and comparable to that of the linear dielectric PVDF-C (Fig. 3d), explaining the excellent reversibility of ECE cycling for PVDF-SPF. This was further confirmed by the large electrostriction of up to 5% under 200 MV m−1 (Supplementary Fig. 13). When the field exceeded 250 MV m−1, dipole alignment approached saturation, and the polarization increase slowed markedly. The Pr remained nearly constant, suggesting that the reversible polarization component was nearly exhausted, and the material behavior became more linear (Supplementary Fig. 12).

To gain a more profound comprehension of the effects of processing temperature on the structure of PVDF-SPF, we examined the crystalline morphology using 2D-WAXD (Supplementary Fig. 14). The PVDF-SPF subjected only to low-temperature SPF at 85 °C (PVDF-SPF85) exhibited the same 2θ angle of 20.52° (β crystals, 200/110 plane39) as PVDF-S, but with a dramatically broader full width at half maximum. The crystallite size reduced to 6.22 nm (Supplementary Fig. 14f), In addition, the crystalline melting enthalpy of PVDF-SPF85 (54.8 J g−1) also be lower than that of PVDF-S (61.3 J g−1) (Supplementary Fig. 15c). The above results show that low-temperature SPF mainly crushed larger β crystals, decoupled polar dipoles and increase ΔSdip.

After further high-temperature SPF at 155 °C, the 2θ angle of PVDF-SPF decreased from 20.52° (PVDF-SPF85) to 20.16° without new peaks (Fig. 3e). The interchain spacing increased to 4.41 Å (Supplementary Fig. 14f). The DSC melting range of PVDF-SPF expanded from 13.2 °C (PVDF-SPF85) to 17.1 °C (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 15d). These results demonstrated that high-temperature SPF increased the polydispersity of the β crystals and expanded the molecular chain spacing within the β crystals, creating ΔSorient from loose to compact β crystals.

We then conducted measurements of the dielectric properties of the films. Both stretching and SPF methods increased the dielectric constant (k) of PVDF films, with PVDF-SPF exhibiting the highest k of 19.2 at 1 kHz (Supplementary Fig. 16a). The stretching process enhanced the number of polar dipoles and improved electroactivity. The SPF process decoupled larger polar dipoles, facilitating dipole flipping and increasing the k. Additionally, the PVDF-SPF achieved a higher Eb (617 MV m−1) (Supplementary Fig. 16b), making it suitable for higher voltage ECE devices.

Temperature-dependent ECE behavior

It was crucial to explore the effective Tw of PVDF-SPF. To this end, we systematically study the dielectric response of three films using variable-temperature dielectric spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 17). Compared to PVDF-C and PVDF-S, the k of PVDF-SPF increased with temperature (Fig. 4a), indicating higher polarizability and stronger electroactivity under an electric field. At low temperatures (approximately −30 °C), both k and dielectric loss increased significantly (Supplementary Fig. 18a, d). This behavior resembles the polarization enhancement mechanism reported previously40. The structure in PVDF-SPF, referred to as loose β crystals (similar to intermediate phases), can greatly improve the reversible ECE. In contrast to terpolymers (Supplementary Fig. 18c, f), no dielectric peak was observed in PVDF-SPF at room temperature, suggesting the absence of conformational transitions41,42. The drop of k around 90 °C (Fig. 4a) illustrates structural relaxation and partial melting (Supplementary Fig. 15a), which may seriously reduce the ECE of PVDF-SPF.

Variable-temperature infrared spectra show a clear redshift in the characteristic peak of the long-β phase with increasing temperature, indicating weakened interchain dipole-dipole interactions (Supplementary Fig. 19a, c). Its absorbance gradually decreases and drops sharply above 80 °C, reflecting the strong temperature sensitivity of the long-β phase content. In contrast, the peak position and absorbance of the short-β phase remain nearly unchanged below 80 °C, which demonstrates its great thermal stability (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 19b). However, once the temperature exceeds 80 °C, a rapid decrease in the short-β phase is observed. This behavior indicates that 80 °C serves as a critical threshold for the structural stability of the short-β phase. Below this temperature, the β phase in PVDF-SPF remains stable without conformational transformation41. The structural evolution of β crystals was directly observed via variable-temperature 2D-WAXD (Supplementary Fig. 20). Around 80 °C, both crystal size and relative crystallinity exhibit inflection points. This indicates the β crystals undergo considerable thermal expansion accompanied by partial disintegration, which leads to a rapid decrease in relative crystallinity (Fig. 4c). A weak endothermic peak in the DSC curve also reflects this transition (Supplementary Fig. 15a). Before the collapse, the increased interchain spacing43 favors the ΔSorient.

In the range of 20–80 °C, the polarization difference (ΔP = Pmax − Pr) gradually increased (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 22). The resulting increase in ΔSdip led to a total ECE entropy change (ΔSECE) of 52.5 J kg−1 K−1 at 80 °C (Fig. 4e). This enhancement was attributed to tensile residual stress (~15 MPa) introduced during the SPF process (Supplementary Fig. 23), which stabilized the loose β crystals and enabled a large ΔSECE below 80 °C. However, the ΔTECE of PVDF-SPF gradually decreased to 10.8 K at 80 °C (Supplementary Fig. 24a), due to the rapid increase in specific heat capacity (Supplementary Fig. 25) offsetting the gain in ΔSECE. Above 80 °C, the internal stress generated was gradually released. This induced molecular chain rearrangement and disorientation, along with partial melting of the loose β crystals. At high temperatures, the excessive thermal motion of molecular chains could not follow the electric field variation, and ΔSorient disappeared. At this point, the PVDF-SPF presented a high Pr and hysteresis loss (Supplementary Fig. 21), lowering the ΔSdip and ECE. Once the structure becomes unstable and collapses, the electrocaloric effect drops rapidly to nearly zero. The ΔSECE versus temperature curve of PVDF-SPF completely echoed the above variable-temperature tests, revealing an effective Tw of 60 °C for PVDF-SPF.

Considering the practical requirements for electrocaloric polymers, we selected five parameters: ΔTECE, Tw, Eb, Y, and Pmax as evaluation criteria (Supplementary Table 1). PVDF-SPF exhibited a large ΔTECE (12.8 K) and a high Pmax (9 μC cm−2), demonstrating significant potential for electrocaloric performance tuning. Its high Y (~1700 MPa) is expected to enhance the structural stability and operational durability of electrocaloric devices (Supplementary Fig. 26). Compared with previously reported electrocaloric materials (Supplementary Table 2), PVDF-SPF outperforms commercially available terpolymers in ΔTECE, ΔSECE, and Tw, highlighting its notable advantage in overall property balance (Fig. 4f). These results provide useful guidance for optimizing the trade-off between performance and fabrication in the design of future electrocaloric materials.

Discussion

The SPF technique was used to fabricate low-cost PVDF solid-state refrigerant with a giant ECE of 12.8 K at 25 °C. The technique disrupted crystallization morphology and increased interchain spacing, facilitating the cyclic flipping of dipoles and the reversible transition between loose and compact β crystals, thereby generating orientation entropy and enhancing switching entropy. The PVDF solid-state refrigerant displayed a wide Tw up to 60 °C, an impressive Eb of 617 MV m−1 and a robust Young’s modulus of 1.7 GPa, ensuring stable operation under high voltage. This processing strategy not only provides insights into polymer ECE enhancement but will also greatly promote the commercialization of solid-state refrigeration.

Methods

Materials and general film preparation

PVDF powder (Solef® 5130) was purchased from Solvay S.A. with weight-average molecular weights (Mw) of 1000 kg mol−1. N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.9%) was obtained from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. A 13 wt.% PVDF solution was prepared by dissolving 10 g of PVDF in 67 g of DMF. The solution was vigorously stirred for 12 h, then cast onto a smooth glass plate using a thick blade. The glass plate was placed in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 24 h to obtain the PVDF-C film. The PVDF-C was cut into rectangular strips and stretched to a strain of 1400% at 85 °C using an electromechanical test system (Model E44, MTS Systems Co., Ltd.) to obtain the PVDF-S film.

Solid-phase pressure forming

The PVDF-S was clamped using adhesive gaskets (PTFE with single-sided tape, Taizhou Chenguang Plastic Industry Co., Ltd.) and placed in a hot press (R-3201, Wuhan Qien Science & Technology Development Co., Ltd.) at 85 °C. The pressure was gradually applied to 30 MPa within 2 min. Cooling water was then circulated to bring the film to room temperature, resulting in the PVDF-SPF85 film. This film was subsequently subjected to the same process at 155 °C to obtain the PVDF-SPF film. The process of solid-phase pressure forming primarily involves transverse expansion of the film, with slight change in the original stretching direction (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Microstructure characterization

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Spectrum two, PerkinElmer Inc.) was employed to characterize the conformational composition of the samples with attenuated total reflection (ATR) accessory. The infrared spectra of the samples were collected using the ATR accessory with incident light parallel to the original stretching direction. For variable-angle infrared spectroscopy (VA-IR), the sample was placed at the center of the protractor, and infrared spectra were obtained in ATR mode at 15° intervals (Supplementary Note 3). For variable-temperature infrared spectroscopy, the sample was positioned on a custom temperature-controlled stage. The heating rate was 1–2 °C min−1, with measurements taken at 5 °C intervals. The temperature-controlled stage consisted of a temperature controller (TCM1030, Chengdu Yexian Technology Co., Ltd.), a polyimide heating plate (0402828, Yancheng Zhenglong Electric Heating Technology Co., Ltd.), and a platinum resistor (PT1000, Heraeus), which was positioned near the sample to monitor the temperature accurately. 2D wide-angle X-ray diffraction (Xeuss 3.0, Xenocs S.A.) with Cu/Kα radiation (wavelength 1.54189 Å) was performed to characterize the crystalline structure of the samples, with a pixel size of 75 μm. For variable-temperature WAXD, the temperature was controlled using a cooling-heating stage (HFSX350, Linkam), with a heating rate of 1–2 °C min−1 and measurements taken at 5 °C intervals. Thermal analysis was characterized using differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) (DSC 25, TA Instruments) with a heating/cooling rate of 10 °C min−1. The conformational distribution and morphology of samples were conducted using tapping mode with pixel scan of 256 × 256 by atomic force microscopy equipped with infrared spectroscopy (AFM-IR, Dimension IconIR, Bruker). The samples were excited with infrared light at 1275 cm−1 and scanned simultaneously. The Young’s modulus of the samples was measured using the same AFM.

Functional properties characterization

Permittivity spectroscopy was evaluated using a precision LCR meter (E4980A, Keysight Technologies) equipped with a temperature-controlled stage (same as the stage utilized in variable-temperature infrared spectroscopy). The heating rate was maintained at 1–2 °C min−1, with measurements taken at 2 °C intervals. Unipolar and bipolar ferroelectric hysteresis loops were measured by a ferroelectric tester (CPE1901, Poly K Technologies) with a modified Sawyer-Tower circuit at a frequency of 10 Hz. The electrostriction of PVDF-SPF was measured using a high-voltage amplifier equipped with a laser interferometer (SP-S120, SIOS Meßtechnik GmbH). In-situ FTIR and 2D-WAXD characterization

For In situ high-voltage infrared spectroscopy (HV-IR), the sample surfaces were coated with carbon nanotubes (CNTs, Suzhou Tanfeng Technology Co., Ltd.). The film was positioned on a custom high-voltage sample stage (Supplementary Fig. 7), and an external high-voltage source (TCM6000i, Dalian Teslaman Technology Co., Ltd.) was used to apply the electric field. Spectra were measured in transmission mode. For in situ high-voltage 2D wide-angle X-ray diffraction (2D HV-WAXD), silver electrodes were sputtered onto both sides of the film by a magnetron sputtering system (GVC-2000, Gevee Instruments Co., Ltd.). The film was then placed on the high-voltage sample stage, with other procedures following the same protocol as the HV-IR measurement. The thickness of the samples was limited to 10 μm.

ECE measurement

The ECE of the samples was captured using a heat flux sensor (HFS-5, OMEGA Engineering Inc.). A high-voltage amplifier (EEL1102.05, Electrical Energy Limited LLC) provided the required voltage, and data were collected using an oscilloscope (DHO914, RIGOL Technologies Co., Ltd.). The heating and cooling of the samples were directly observed using a high-precision infrared thermal imager (Testo SE & Co. KGaA). The time-resolved electrocaloric temperature change was measured using an infrared thermometer (CTlaser LT, Optris GmbH). Before infrared measurement, a layer of carbon nanotubes was sprayed onto the silver electrode surface to enhance the emissivity of film. The emissivity of the film (ε = 0.85) was measured using a dual-band emissometer (IR-2, Shanghai Institute of Technical Physics). The film thickness used for ECE measurement was approximately 10–15 μm. Detailed information is available in Supplementary Note 2.
