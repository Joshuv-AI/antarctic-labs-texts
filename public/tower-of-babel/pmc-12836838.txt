The efficacy and safety of benznidazole in adults with seropositive indeterminate form, Trypanosoma cruzi infection: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Joshua W. Trowell, Edward Mitre, Rachel Marcus, Anwar E. Ahmed, Barbara J. Fuhrman, Patrick W. Hickey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChagas disease (CD)-a parasitic infection caused by Trypanosoma cruzi, affects ~7 million people worldwide. Current first line therapies like Benznidazole and Nifurtimox have significant side effects, and limited data to support their efficacy when used to treat CD in its chronic indeterminate (asymptomatic) phase. This study aimed to critically assess and meta-analyze all clinical trials to date that address this specific phase of CD.MethodsPubMed, Cochrane, ClinicalTrials.gov, EBSCOhost, and SciELO (inception - December 2024) were searched for randomized, controlled trials (RCTs) including the efficacy or safety of treatment in adults with indeterminate form, chronic CD. English abstracts were required, with full text in English, Spanish or Portuguese. Studies had to have the potential to report disease progression (primary) or parasitological cure and adverse events (secondary) by treatment regimen. We define parasitological cure as T. cruziPCR negativity. Meta-analysis was conducted using random effects models with inverse-variance weighting of study-specific risk ratios (95% CI). Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots.ResultsFive out of 132 studies were unique RCTs that met the inclusion criteria. Inter-rater agreement on study selection was high (κ=0.83). Of all participants, most were female (428/653 or 66%). Summary estimates revealed significant benefit for achieving parasitological cure in treatment versus control groups (overall RR 5.93 [95% CI: 3.96, 8.86, p < 0.001]). A direct comparison of adverse events (AEs) experienced across trials was challenging (overall RR 1.47 [(95% CI: 0.90, 2.38), p <0.12]). Among all participants, 8.1% (53/653) discontinued treatment for various reasons after treatment initiation. Notably, no prospective RCTs of indeterminate form CD were identified that assessed for development of long-term cardiac or gastrointestinal complications.ConclusionAntitrypanosomal treatment can significantly improve parasitological cure rates in indeterminate (asymptomatic) form, chronic phase CD, despite high risk of bias and aggregated data limitations. Our analysis underscores the need for more rigorous, standardized randomized controlled trials with consistent inclusion criteria based on indeterminate form, chronic phase CD and clinical endpoints. This study provides a focused, clinically relevant perspective by emphasizing randomized trial data, complementing broader CD research.PROSPERO registrationCRD42024512886.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12231-4.

Background

Chagas disease (CD), also known as American trypanosomiasis, is a neglected disease caused by the protozoan parasite Trypanosoma cruzi (T. cruzi). Throughout this manuscript we use ‘Chagas disease’ consistent with current clinical practice guidelines (CDC, PAHO 2019), recognizing that this term encompasses the full spectrum from asymptomatic T. cruzi infection to organ-specific disease manifestations, with disease stage (e.g. chronic indeterminate form, with or without organ damage) specified where clinically relevant. CD affects more than 7 million people worldwide and more than 100 million people are considered at risk of infection [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 28,000 new cases and 10,000 to 12,000 deaths occur each year due to CD.

Previously CD was confined to rural areas of Latin Americas (including Mexico, Central America, and South America), but in recent decades it has spread to urban settings throughout this endemic region, and through migration, to non-endemic countries worldwide [2, 3]. Within the U.S., a recent CDC study classifies Chagas disease as hypoendemic, though significant underreporting persists due to inadequate screening and surveillance programs [4]. In non-endemic areas, an infected human host can transmit CD through blood transfusion, organ transplantation, or through congenital transmission from mother to child. The most common routes of transmission in endemic countries include vector transmission, oral transmission via contaminated food or water, transfusion, and in utero vertical transmission of T. cruzi parasites [5].

Currently there are two FDA-approved drugs for CD: nifurtimox (NFX) and benznidazole (BNZ). These drugs involve a prolonged course of treatment, require careful monitoring, and cause significant adverse effects [6]. The natural history of CD has two phases: acute and chronic. The acute phase typically lasts four to eight weeks, is associated with typically mild systemic symptoms, and is detectable by microscopically visible parasitemia [7]. In contrast, the chronic phase is divided into an indeterminate form lacking any signs or symptoms of disease, and a determinate form, in which signs and symptoms are present.

The indeterminate form is a period characterized by normal findings in the heart (electrocardiogram and echocardiogram) and digestive system (esophagography and radiopaque enema) examinations [8, 9]. The indeterminate form of CD frequently persists throughout the patient’s lifespan despite ongoing infection, and is associated with an excellent prognosis, though surveillance for disease progression is necessary. The symptomatic (determinate) form of CD will develop in 20–30% of cases. Cardiac manifestations include arrhythmia and conduction disorders, ventricular systolic dysfunction with or without heart failure, and thromboembolic phenomena. Gastrointestinal manifestations include peristalsis dysfunction of the esophagus and/or intestine and can lead to mega-syndrome presentations.

Challenges still persist in diagnosing asymptomatic T. cruzi infection [10, 11]. Serological diagnosis requires confirmation using two different tests with distinct antigens or methodologies. In the United States, this can be accomplished through major commercial laboratories offering multiple assays, or through the CDC. In many endemic countries, clinical laboratories routinely perform both diagnostic and confirmatory testing. Effective, early diagnosis and treatment in the indeterminate form is imperative. While 70–80% of people with indeterminate CD will suffer no ill effects if they remain undiagnosed and untreated, predicting the 20–30% that will progress to chronic disease and would benefit from intervention remains a key priority [7, 12, 13].

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in 2020 showed progression of chronic phase, indeterminate form CD to cardiac signs or symptoms in patients with an average annual rate of 1.9% [0.2% − 10.3%] [14]. Since there is currently no way to accurately predict which patients are more likely to progress to the determinate form of disease, it is generally recommended to offer treatment to all patients without contraindications under the age of 50 [15]. That stated, there remains uncertainty whether treatment of indeterminate CD reduces morbidity and mortality [16]. Moreover, seroreversion (reversal of detectable antibody response from positive to negative) can take many years, and a single negative PCR test does not necessarily signify complete parasitological cure. Thus, understanding trypanocidal treatment effects among patients with indeterminate form of Chagas disease remains an important area of research.

Four systematic review and meta-analytic studies have evaluated treatment efficacy through parasite-related outcome measures [17–20]. One review assessed 48 cohort studies, and six randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [17]. The goal of that study was to determine how children and adults with chronic T. cruzi infection responded to BNZ and NFX treatment by measuring parasitological, molecular, and serological outcomes. The study found that while parasitological markers (xenodiagnoses) showed initial improvement, PCR positivity remained high at 40% after 48 months. Serological markers, measured by ELISA, demonstrated gradual improvement with 15% negativization at 48 months and up to 45% in very long-term follow-up. An updated analysis using individual participant data from Sguassero and colleagues identified three randomized controlled trials involving 71 participants with PCR data from Argentina and Spain out of a total of approximately 500+ participants with PCR data across all studies [18]. However, this meta-analysis did not conduct a formal analysis of parasitological cure outcomes, instead focusing primarily on serological seroreversion, which was found to occur more frequently and within a shorter timeframe in children and adolescents compared to adults, with the likelihood of seroreversion varying by country with the highest rates observed in Brazil.

A 2014 review by Villar and colleagues primarily sought to identify and summarize any patient-related outcomes regarding efficacy and found that none consistently showed the same positive results across different studies [19]. Non-randomized and open-labeled studies were also included as part of the review and analysis. However, NFX or BNZ treatment results from at least two RCTs showed substantial reductions in infection markers based on completed laboratory tests (serology, xenodiagnoses or PCR). A qualitative synthesis review published by Vallejo and colleagues included only two RCTs that sought to compare the lowering or disappearance of parasitemia or anti-T. cruzi antibodies according to study criteria [20]. The authors concluded that the findings cast doubt about the use of nitrofuran and imidazole drugs solely targeting late-stage, symptomatic, chronic CD and whether they can be reliably applied to clinical practice.

While two drugs, BNZ and NFX, are FDA-approved for treatment of CD, their optimal efficacy and safety in the indeterminate form of the chronic phase requires ongoing research evaluation. This is evident by use of varied inclusion & exclusion criteria, treatment dosages, durations, and endpoints for both efficacy and adverse events.

To further add to the literature, in this study we addressed the following research question:What are the efficacy and safety profiles of Nifurtimox (NFX) and Benznidazole (BNZ) in adults with seropositive indeterminate form, chronic T. cruzi infection with regards to achieving clinical and parasitological cure as well as experiencing adverse events?

What are the efficacy and safety profiles of Nifurtimox (NFX) and Benznidazole (BNZ) in adults with seropositive indeterminate form, chronic T. cruzi infection with regards to achieving clinical and parasitological cure as well as experiencing adverse events?

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement to ensure transparency and comprehensive reporting [21]. The research protocol was registered on February 14, 2024 with the International Prospective Register of Systematic reviews (PROSPERO) under CRD42024512886 [22].

Search strategy and study selection

On February 26, 2025, we searched five total databases–PubMed, Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, EBSCOhost and SciELO, respectively from inception to December 31, 2024. The search strategies were completed using controlled vocabulary (e.g. MeSH, Subject Headings), free text terms such as ‘Chagas disease’, and ‘antiparasitic drug treatment’ and Boolean connectors (“AND”, “OR” and “NOT”). The database-specific search criteria are detailed in Supplementary Material 1. A minor protocol amendment (adding the SciELO database to the search) was made prior to finalizing the search protocol on February 25, 2025, followed by data retrieval. We screened abstracts for prospective RCTs evaluating the efficacy and safety profiles of BNZ and NFX treatment for indeterminate form, chronic phase, Chagas disease in adults (1), including only English-language abstracts (2) with full-texts either in English, Spanish or Portuguese (3). Several supplementary search methods were used to minimize potential publication and indexing bias. These included manual hand-searching of reference lists from relevant systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and key primary studies; screening of conference abstracts and non-indexed trial records; and searching trial registries and institutional repositories. We limited our search to studies with adult human participants. Full-text articles originally written in Spanish or Portuguese were professionally translated to English using Language Innovations (Washington, DC) before screening [23].

The systematic search of the literature was performed by JT who also uploaded the search results from each database into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), a web-based collaboration software platform that streamlines the production of systematic and other literature reviews. All identified studies were uploaded into Endnote 20 (Clarivate Analytics, Pennsylvania, USA), and Covidence, which automatically removed duplicate studies. Two reviewers (JT, PH) independently screened each title and abstract for eligibility and possible inclusion. If a study title and abstract was deemed eligible, the full text was obtained and assessed for inclusion. Disagreements on inclusion were resolved through discussion by a third reviewer (RM). After completion of the full-text screening process, a PRISMA flow diagram was generated—including the total number of records captured, the number of duplicates records removed, the number of records excluded during title/abstract screening, as well as the number of records excluded during the full-text review process with their exclusion reasons. All records that met final selection criteria were included in the final analysis.

Eligibility criteria

To be included, studies had to be prospective randomized controlled treatment trials of adults (18 years or older) with indeterminate form CD (1), defined as seropositivity for T. cruzi infection in the absence of clinical evidence of cardiac or gastrointestinal complications. Studies were required to report safety evaluations and adverse events (2), as well as exposure to standard anti-trypanosomal treatment (NFX or BNZ) regardless of dose and duration, or treatment with other experimental anti-trypanosomal drugs or placebo, with no limit on the duration of follow-up (3).

Studies had to report either a primary or secondary endpoint: rate of progression to cardiac or gastrointestinal manifestations by treatment regimen (primary), parasitological cure (secondary), as well as evaluation of safety and adverse events (secondary). Exclusion criteria were investigating only infants and children under 18 years of age, studies that only included cases of chronic phase, determinate form Chagas disease, and non-randomized observational studies including one treatment type.

Outcomes

The primary outcome for this study was defined as the frequency of progression to determine CD defined by development of cardiac manifestations (acute congestive heart failure, myocarditis, cardiomyopathy without other known cause, arrhythmia, and ventricular thrombus) and gastrointestinal disorders (megaesophagus/megacolon).

The secondary outcome of interest was parasitological cure, defined as frequency of negative blood PCR test, at any specified study timepoint. An additional secondary endpoint was treatment safety profile data in RCTs during treatment, specifically adverse events.

Data extraction

After building a modified template in Covidence, two reviewers (JT, EM) independently extracted all data from the manuscripts. Once extracted, downloaded data from Covidence software were converted to an excel format and uploaded to Stata software (Version 18.5, StataCorp, College Station, TX) for data cleaning, management and analysis. Aggregated data was extracted as published and included author, year of publication, country, type of sponsorship, study design, population, sample size (intervention vs. control), age (intervention vs. control), sex (male vs. female), primary outcome, secondary outcome, loss to follow-up counts, intervention (medication type with frequency and dose), as well as duration. Outcomes were analyzed and focused on patient populations with indeterminate form, chronic phase Chagas disease. Studies with indistinguishable patient populations regarding disease stage were noted as such. A third reviewer (RM) independently reviewed the extracted data and provided final adjudication to resolve any discrepancies between the two primary reviewers.

All extraction forms, tools, and meta code were pilot tested on two studies and reviewed by a biostatistician (AA). Necessary changes were completed to ensure all extraction and data were adequately included to answer the research question. Adjusted effect sizes were prioritized for inclusion in the meta-analyses. When adjusted estimates were not available or not reported, crude effect sizes were calculated manually from either the raw data provided, or event counts reported in the selected articles. These unadjusted estimates were included in the analysis and identified as crude calculations. Results were presented in summary tables and forest plots, with effect sizes reported as risk ratios (RR) as well as rates for parasitological cure and adverse events, both with 95% confidence intervals (CI), as deemed appropriate. Using the extracted frequencies and denominators, we computed RRs that quantify the relative risk of outcomes in the antitrypanosomal drug (treatment) versus control (placebo) groups.

Quality and risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (JT, EM) independently evaluated all included studies for risk of bias (RoB) analysis using a modified grading scale [24], and a third reviewer (RM) provided consensus adjudication for discordant assessments. Risk of bias results were displayed using Robvis (Visualization Software for RoB2 Tool, Bristol, England). RoB was classified as either having high risk, low risk, or some concerns, with further justification of grading available in the supplemental file.

Data analyses

Data were analyzed using Stata software (Version 18.5, StataCorp, College Station, TX). Cohen’s kappa (k) was calculated to quantify the level of agreement during the title and abstract screening phase as well as during the full-text screening phases. A random effects model using residual maximum likelihood (REML) estimation was our primary analytic approach due to anticipated heterogeneity among studies [25]. The inverse-variance method was used to weight studies according to their precision. The pooled rate of parasitological cure and adverse events was presented in terms of effect size and associated 95% CIs.

Forest plots were generated to visually represent pooled effect sizes as well as confidence intervals to compare cure rates between treatment and control groups across studies. To assess for publication bias, a contour-enhanced funnel plot was used to examine the relationship between effect sizes and standard errors.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of our findings. We assessed the impact of including unadjusted estimates on the overall pooled results and carried out leave-one-out analysis to identify any disproportionate influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate. We also assessed the impact of excluding studies contributing high heterogeneity to evaluate the impact of individual effect estimates.

All tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. The findings were discussed in the context of current clinical guidelines and their potential implications for treatment recommendations in patients with indeterminate form, chronic phase, Chagas disease.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The database search identified 132 abstracts from six databases (four major and two minor databases) (Fig. 1). PubMed yielded the highest number with 59 studies, followed by EBSCO with 35, and SciELO with 17. Manual hand searches, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov each contributed seven studies. Sixty-one references were excluded: two duplicates identified manually, ten duplicates identified by automation tools, and 49 references that did not meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The remaining 71 studies underwent manual screening of titles and abstracts, resulting in the exclusion of 58 studies that did not meet inclusion criteria. Thirteen studies were successfully retrieved for full-text review. An additional eight studies were excluded for various reasons (See Supplementary Material 1). Ultimately, five RCTs met all inclusion criteria and were meta-analyzed [26–30].Fig. 1PRISMA figure. Selection of records for inclusion in systematic review and meta-analysis

The level of agreement between two reviewers (JT, PH) (Table S1, supplement) was identified using a Cohen’s κ, revealing moderate inter-rater agreement after the title and abstract stage (κ = 0.57, 95% CI 0.41–0.60), consistent with expected initial screening variability, and high agreement in the full-text review stage (κ = 0.83, 95% CI 0.81–1.00). Reviewer agreements in the title and abstract versus full-text review stages were 83% vs. 92%, respectively. Ultimately, there was a strong consensus for final inclusion decisions, indicating robust reviewer calibration and consensus-building processes [31].

Three RCTs included different first authors, and two were published by the same first author (Torrico et al. [29], Torrico et al. [30]). Two RCTs [26, 27] were government funded, two (Torrico et al. [29], Torrico et al. [30]) were non-profit, and one [28] was industry funded.

Of the five included studies, two were conducted in Spain, two in Bolivia, and one enrolled participants from multiple countries. Benznidazole (BNZ) was used in all five trials, and although most studies had a treatment duration of 60 days, two studies (Torrico 2018, Torrico 2021) had treatment arms with shorter regimens (i.e. two, four or eight weeks). Posaconazole (POS) was used in two trials (Molina 2014, Morillo 2017) as monotherapy or combination therapy. Across the five RCTs, follow-up periods varied from 6 to12 months post-treatment or were measured at the end of treatment (EOT). There was considerable heterogeneity in dosing schedules, comparator groups, and secondary outcomes. Two RCTs were unblinded, two were double-blinded, and one was single-blinded.

Across all studies, there were a total of 653 participants, with study-specific sample sizes ranging from 14 to 231 (Table S2). The participant population was relatively homogeneous and included only adults with indeterminate form, chronic phase Chagas disease, with the exception of Molina et al. 2014 (65% indeterminate population). Overall, 66% of the composite study population were female, and the weighted mean age was 33.7 years. Across all studies, 8% (53/653) of participants discontinued treatment for any reason, and loss to follow-up ranged from 7% to 9%.

Quality and risk of bias assessment

All RCTs studied were assessed for risk of bias (Fig. 2). Overall, there was mixed methodology across included trials. Four out of five studies demonstrated an overall high risk of bias (80%), and only one study achieved a low risk of bias (20%). Domain two–Fig. 2Risk of bias quality assessment

intervention adherence (blinding and protocol compliance) was a major concern across the majority of included studies. However, domains one, four, and five, in at least 4 out of 5 (80%) studies contained low risk of bias.

A notable methodological concern was identified in Torrico 2021, which appeared to deviate from intent-to-treat (ITT) principles in reporting treatment outcomes. While the study randomized 210 participants (30 per group across seven treatment arms), eight patients discontinued and 202 completed the study. A true ITT analysis should have maintained denominators of 30 for all groups, with missing or lost patients typically counted as treatment failures to preserve the principle that all randomized participants remain in their assigned groups for analysis. Instead, the study appears to have excluded discontinued participants from the denominator when reporting treatment outcomes, though adverse events were reported for the full ITT sample. This deviation from ITT methodology may have inflated the apparent success rates and introduces attrition bias, potentially affecting the precision and validity of effect estimates derived from this study in the meta-analysis.

Overall risk of bias was scored as ‘high risk’ if a study was graded high risk in one domain or if a study was graded as ‘some concerns’ across multiple domains [24]. Using the RoB 2 tool, studies received a ‘critical high’ or ‘some concerns’ ratings for study domains 1, D1b, D2, and D3 (random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, and incomplete outcome data, respectively) because limitations in these specific domains have greater impact on RCT validity over other domains.

Model selection

A random-effects model was used despite anticipated convergence issues due to zero events in a treated, but not cured group found in the Vallejo 2016 study. This model selection and approach was applied considering the known sensitivity issues surrounding fixed-effects models that are more robust (less sensitive) to between-study variance and issues with regard to zero events and extreme values [32, 33]. This is a common methodological challenge among meta-analyses and is well supported by the literature [34].

Outcomes

Clinical cure outcomes

We identified no prospective, randomized, clinical therapeutic trials of indeterminate Chagas that evaluated for progression to cardiac or gastrointestinal disease. This includes the five trials included in this meta-analysis.

Parasitological cure outcomes

An adjusted random effects model accounting for time to parasitological cure (defined by sustained negative T. cruzi PCR in blood), demonstrated a statistically significant benefit for achieving parasitological cure outcomes in treatment versus control groups (Fig. 3a). Across the four included studies, individual risk ratios ranged from 4.57 (95% CI: 2.15, 9.71) to 25.47 (95% CI: 3.70, 175.10), with all studies favoring treatment. The pooled risk ratio of parasitological cure was 5.93 (95% CI: 3.96, 8.86, p < 0.001), indicating that patients receiving treatment were approximately six times more likely to achieve parasitological cure compared to controls. Notably, heterogeneity among studies was negligible (I2 = 0.00%, τ2 = 0.00, H2 = 1.00; Q(3) = 2.66, p = 0.45), suggesting consistency in treatment effects across different study populations and protocols.

The pooled absolute cure rates were 70.4% (266/378) in the treatment groups versus 9.5% (24/253) in control groups (Table 1). This yielded a risk difference of 0.57 (95% CI: 0.40, 0.75) and an attributable risk percent of 80.9%. (Fig. 3b, Table 1). The risk difference of 0.57 signifies a 57-percentage point absolute increase in PCR negativity in treatment compared to control groups. Cure Definition: Sustained parasitological clearance defined as negative qualitative blood PCR at the specified follow-up timepoint. All studies used intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, where patients lost to follow-up, those with treatment discontinuation due to adverse events, or those with positive PCR at any timepoint were considered treatment failures

RD (Risk Difference/Absolute Risk Reduction): Measures the absolute difference in PCR negativity rates between treatment and control groups, calculated as: Treatment cure rate - Control cure rate. Represents the excess proportion achieving parasitological cure attributable to treatment and is expressed as a proportion (range: 0 to 1.0). RD values can also be interpreted as the absolute risk reduction (ARR)

AR% (Attributable Risk Percent): Expressed as a percentage, measures the proportion of cure among the treated population that can be attributed to the treatment itself, calculated as: [(Treatment cure rate - Control cure rate (Risk Difference))/Treatment cure rate] × 100

Heterogeneity Considerations: All studies employed ITT analysis with similar definitions of treatment failure. Follow-up duration varied from 6 months (Morillo 2017, Torrico 2021) to 10 months (Molina 2014) to 12 months (Torrico 2018). Control groups differed by study design: placebo controls in Morillo 2017, Torrico 2018, and Torrico 2021; active comparator (posaconazole) in Molina 2014. In Torrico 2018, experimental E1224 arms (high dose) and standard-of-care benznidazole were pooled as active treatment to evaluate overall therapeutic efficacy against all other E1224 arms including placebo. Treatment effects were consistently favorable across all studies but varied in magnitude. Molina et al. (2014) reported cure rates of 61.5% (treatment) versus 13.5% (control), with a risk difference of 48% with an attributable risk percent of 78.0%. Morillo et al. (2017) showed cure rates of 60.0% versus 10.0%, yielding a risk difference of 60% and attributable risk percent of 83.3%. Torrico et al. (2021) reported the highest treatment cure rate at 84.9% versus 3.3% in controls, producing a risk difference of 82% and attributable risk percent of 96.6%. Across all studies, the attributable risk percent ranged from 78.0% to 96.6%, indicating that the substantial majority of parasitological cures in treated patients were directly attributable to the therapeutic intervention.

By study weight, the four included trials contributed relatively equally to the pooled estimate: Torrico 2021 (27.9%), Torrico 2018 (26.5%), Morillo 2017 (24.6%), and Molina 2014 (21.0%) (Fig. 3b). The Torrico 2021 trial, with the largest sample size (n = 202) demonstrated the highest risk difference of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.73, 0.90) and contributed the most weight to the overall pooled estimate. Considerable heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 87.0%, Tau2 = 0.03, p < 0.001), suggesting substantial variation in treatment effects across studies. Individual study risk differences ranged from 0.46 to 0.82 with all studies demonstrating statistically significant treatment benefits.

Adverse event outcomes

A risk ratio was calculated to compare the risk of experiencing at least one adverse event between treatment and control groups. Across the four studies, risk ratios ranged from 0.98 (95% CI: 0.86, 1.12) to 3.56 (95% CI: 1.82, 6.93) with three of four studies showing increased risk of adverse events in treatment groups (Fig. S5). The overall pooled risk ratio indicated that treatment groups had 1.47 times the risk of adverse events compared to controls (95% CI: 0.90, 2.38, p = 0.12), though this was not statistically significant. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed among studies (I2 = 90.2%, τ2 = 0.21, H2 = 10.2; Q(3) = 17.8, p < 0.001). Notably, Torrico 2018 showed a protective effect (RR = 0.98), while Molina 2014 demonstrated the highest risk (RR = 3.56). The CHAGASAZOL study (Molina 2014) also had the greatest reported number of non-graded symptoms (n = 70) [26]. All other trials each reported two or fewer non-graded symptoms [27–30]. The high heterogeneity among studies raises questions about adverse event definition consistency and reporting standards in RCTs, and may reflect differences in populations, treatment protocols, or follow-up procedures across studies.

Among 653 patients included among RCTs, 53 discontinued therapy (Table 2). Hepatotoxicity emerged as the most frequent cause of discontinuation, occurring in 26% (14/53) of cases across study arms. The hepatotoxic effects primarily manifested as elevated liver enzymes (ALT, AST). Dermatologic and skin reactions were marked as a safety concern that resulted in seven treatment discontinuations. Systemic disorders, specifically lymphatic system disorders led to three discontinuations in patients receiving combination therapy with BNZ and fosravuconazole (FOS), while infective cholecystitis contributed to one discontinuation. Sensitivity analyses

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated the robustness of parasitological cure findings (Fig. S6). When each study was systematically excluded, the pooled relative risk remained highly stable, ranging from 5.55 to 6.57 (all p < 0.001), with all confidence intervals excluding the null value. The narrow range of estimates (coefficient of variation = 7.0%) and minimal deviation from the full model (within 18%) indicate that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall treatment effect, supporting the reliability of the pooled estimate.

Publication bias for parasitological cure was assessed using a contour-enhanced funnel plot and Egger’s regression test (Fig. S4, Table S5). Visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed a generally symmetric distribution of studies around the pooled effect estimate (log RR ≈1.73), with all four studies falling within statistically significant regions. Egger’s regression test detected no significant small-study effects (β₁ = 1.48, p = 0.252), suggesting minimal publication bias. However, the absence of studies in non-significant regions and the limited number of included studies (n = 4) warrant cautious interpretation. Overall, the evidence suggests that selective reporting bias, if present, does not substantially influence the pooled risk ratio estimate for parasitological cure.

A contour-enhanced funnel plot was generated to assess publication bias for the secondary outcome of adverse events (Fig. S7). Egger’s regression test revealed statistically significant asymmetry (β₁ = 3.90, SE = 0.960, z = 4.07, p < 0.001), indicating small-study effects. The funnel plot displays marked asymmetry, with four studies clustered near the apex showing positive associations with adverse events: three studies fall within statistically significant regions (1% (0.01) < p < 10% (0.10) contour ranges), and one study in the p > 10% (0.10) range. One outlying study appears outside the funnel on the right with lower precision (SE ≈ 0.35) and a larger positive effect (≈1.5). The pronounced asymmetry–characterized by absence of studies showing protective or null effects on the left side and confirmed by the significant Egger’s test suggest potential publication bias or small-study effects that may overestimate the association between interventions and adverse events (Table S6).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of antiparasitic agents for treating indeterminate Chagas disease. Our goal was to identify and synthesize the findings from all available randomized, prospective controlled trials that met specific eligibility and quality criteria, focusing on treatment outcomes and associated adverse events. Importantly, our analyses identified no prospective RCTs of indeterminate form, chronic phase CD that assessed the effects of antitrypanosomal treatment on the development of cardiac and gastrointestinal manifestations of CD. Instead, the five included trials focused on surrogates, including parasitologic cure and adverse events. We found that, in the chronic phase, indeterminate form of Chagas disease, antitrypanosomal treatment positively improved the relative risk of achieving parasitological cure by ~ 6 times, denoted by a risk difference of 57 percentage points increase in PCR negativity in treatment arms compared to those in control arms. The weighted mean age of 33.7 years is representative of the chronic Chagas disease population commonly studied in RCTs. Treatment guidelines vary by region and organization: CDC guidelines strongly recommend treatment for adults up to age 50 without advanced cardiomyopathy, while individualizing decisions for those over 50. In contrast, PAHO 2019 guidelines suggest offering trypanocidal treatment to all adult patients with chronic T. cruzi infection who do not present with specific organ damage, without specifying an explicit upper age limit [35]. Additionally, trends across trials showed that mild, and moderate AEs were more frequently experienced among treated than control groups. Four of the five included studies were identified as having a high risk of bias. The data analysis, although limited to aggregated data, revealed vital information about parasitological cure and adverse events despite limitations. Results demonstrate a need for more robust RCT research to guide clinical decision-making for indeterminate form Chagas disease.

Moreover, the geographic distribution of included trials merits consideration when interpreting treatment efficacy and generalizability. Trials conducted in Spain (Molina 2014, Vallejo 2016) enrolled predominantly Bolivian-born immigrants, while other trials (Torrico 2018, 2021) were conducted in Bolivia; only the STOP-CHAGAS trial (Morillo 2017) included diverse populations from multiple countries. This concentration of Bolivian and Bolivian-immigrant populations–particularly in the heavily weighted Torrico 2018 study (53.5%)–reflects Bolivia’s high disease burden but raises generalizability considerations. Additionally, T. cruzi exhibits substantial genetic diversity across discrete typing units (DTUs), with TcV/TcVI predominating in Bolivia and among Bolivian migrants (~55 TcV), contrasting with TcI predominance (~86%) in Central and North America [36–39]. Whether parasite DTU influences treatment response remains incompletely characterized, though in vitro evidence suggests potential differences in drug susceptibility. Our pooled efficacy estimates may not fully capture treatment effectiveness in populations infected with DTUs underrepresented in these trials.

A recently published systematic review and meta-analysis by Rassi and colleagues assessed four main outcomes (ECG changes, disease progression, cardiovascular death, and overall mortality) among 8,972 patients [40]. Of the 23 studies included, five were RCTs (none of which are included in our meta-analysis) involving children and adults with chronic Chagas disease. While our study focused only on prospective RCTs examining indeterminate form CD to assess the full risk profile for this group on the progression of chagasic cardiomyopathy, Rassi and colleagues examined both retrospective and prospective studies to measure study outcomes. Researchers highlighted the significant positive association with only 5 mg/kg of BNZ versus higher dose regimens within the studied age range (childhood to middle adulthood). Trends indicated increased benefit with age, attributed to differences between pediatric and young adult populations rather than among adults of various ages. Their random-effects model analysis revealed changes in electrocardiogram (ECG) results with a risk ratio (RR) of 0.48 (95% CI: 0.36, 0.66, p < 0.001), I2 (heterogeneity) = 76.4%, with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 5, and disease progression with a RR of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.23, 0.51, p < 0.001), I2 = 72.4% and a NNT of 6.

Furthermore, Rassi and colleagues included 12 studies in a random effects analysis measuring disease progression and found an overall pooled risk ratio of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.23, 0.51), which indicated that treatment reduced the risk of disease by 65% compared to control [40]. Among the two RCTs analyzed in this category, Coura et al. (1997) contributed the least weight (0.94%) while Morillo et al. (2015) contributed the greatest weight (15.1%); however, both trials reported non-significant individual risk ratios [41, 42]. The substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 72.41%) was primarily driven by the various factors such as study design, heterogeneous populations, bias among studies and varying follow-up durations [40]. Our meta-analysis findings for those with indeterminate form, chronic phase CD demonstrated a marked parasitological cure achievement rate among the treated group with 70.4% (266/378) versus the control group with 9.5% (24/253). Our study fills a critical gap by establishing the treatment effect size specific in randomized trials of indeterminate form, chronic phase patients, informing evidence-based decisions for early intervention strategies.

Additionally, a systematic review published in 2022 by Crespillo-Andújar and colleagues included 17 studies (eight RCTS, and nine prospective observational studies), and out of 6640 patients, 44% (2,938) received BNZ originating mostly from Argentina and Brazil. Researchers found mixed evidence regarding the effectiveness of BNZ and NFX treatments in children and adults [43]. Out of 16 studies, only 50% (8) included patients with the indeterminate form, while the remaining evaluated chronic phase CD (one of patients with Chagas cardiomyopathy; seven of mixed populations with and without visceral involvement). Researchers evaluated antibody seroreversion in children treated with BNZ versus placebo and found an overall OR of 38.4 (95%CI: 10.73, 137.0) in two RCTs (Coura et. al 1997 and Sosa-Estani 1998) [41, 44]. In adults, researchers identified the relative effect among six RCTs [27–30; 42, 44] using evidence from 1,826 participants revealing that while treatment significantly improved parasitological outcomes [OR 41.7 (95% CI: 7.25, 238.15)], this did not translate into statistically significant clinical progression benefits [OR 0.93 (95% CI: 0.79, 1.09)] when the Morillo 2015 study was analyzed alone [42]. The clinical progression outcomes had higher quality evidence compared to achieving a parasitological cure (moderate vs. low) but showed minimal effect with only six fewer cases per 1,000 patients, whereas parasitological clearance showed substantial benefit with 637 more patients per 1,000 achieving clearance.

Our findings complement Crespillo-Andújar’s and colleagues’ 2022 systematic review, with both analyses demonstrating consistent evidence for BNZ parasitological efficacy despite different methodological approaches. Notably, while previous systematic reviews by Rassi et al. (2025) and Crespillo-Andújar et al. included RCTs with clinical outcomes that showed mixed results, our review applied more stringent inclusion criteria specifically focusing on the indeterminate form of chronic Chagas disease. While their broader inclusion of mixed efficacy designs across various disease stages showed treatment benefits, our RCT-specific analysis provides higher-certainty evidence with a pooled RR of 5.93 for parasitological cure, translating to a substantial 57 percentage point increase in cure rates with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 87.0%, p < 0.001). Despite this heterogeneity, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated remarkable stability of treatment effects, with pooled estimates ranging narrowly from 5.55 to 6.57 across all iterations (coefficient of variation = 7.0%), indicating that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall effect. The convergent findings across both reviews, despite our more restrictive inclusion, reinforce the robustness of BNZ therapy despite significant AEs that interrupt treatment completion. Our evidence strengthens the overall evidence base for early intervention for chronic Chagas disease cases.

While not included in this systematic review, observational and longitudinal studies examining the rate of CD progression from indeterminate to determinate form chronic phase still provide valuable context. One study found a rapid progression rate of 21% within a three-year period among an Argentinean, treatment-naive cohort with demonstrated risk within an endemic country [45]. Study findings, however, may not be generalizable to nonendemic settings due to several reasons including the role of reinfection in variability and severity of the clinical course of Chagas’ disease, as well as infection of different T. cruzi strains that could significantly influence disease progression patterns.

During the indeterminate form, chronic phase of CD, gradual cardiac injury is occurring through different factors and is related to the specific T. cruzi genotype, frequency of vector/parasite exposure, and parasitemia level. Furthermore, in vivo studies of patients with the indeterminate form of CD have demonstrated histopathological alterations in ≥ 50% of the patient population [46–48]. These findings stress the importance of considering regional epidemiology and host-parasite dynamics when evaluating progression risk and treatment strategies for individuals in the indeterminate form of CD [47, 48].

Three studies identified during initial review that had suitable study designs were ultimately excluded from our meta-analysis due to unavailable or incomplete results in the peer reviewed literature [49–51]. These include the EQUITY (CHICAMOCHA 3) and TRAENA trials, both of which evaluated benznidazole and nifurtimox in adults with chronic T. cruzi infection. While preliminary reports suggest parasitological effects without clear clinical benefit, full datasets were not accessible for inclusion. The third, an additional ongoing RCT (MULTIBENZ), aims to recruit participants from four countries measuring the efficacy for three different BNZ therapeutic schemes given for 15- or 60-days with 12 months follow-up in populations without severe cardiac forms [50]. Importantly, MULTIBENZ adopts Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events to standardize reporting. This is particularly relevant given that TRAENA reported 22% treatment discontinuation due to adverse events, EQUITY reported 7.3% treatment abandonment in active treatment arms versus 4.9% on placebo, and our meta-analysis revealed 53 total treatment discontinuations.

The experimental treatment arms evaluated in our meta-analysis included E1224 (a ravuconazole pro-drug), posaconazole (POS), and fosravuconazole (FOS). A fexinidazole (FEX) study was identified during the systematic search, but excluded because the double-blind, phase 2 trial compared FEX against placebo without benznidazole or nifurtimox as an active comparator [52]. While this compound showed initial antiparasitic activity, most patients experienced parasitic rebound during extended follow-up. For example, FEX showed only 19% sustained parasite clearance at four months compared to 13% in historical controls [52].

The selection of appropriate clinical endpoints represents a fundamental challenge in Chagas disease drug development given the long period of time to clinical disease progression. Four of the five studies we analyzed used PCR clearance as a surrogate endpoint for treatment efficacy. While PCR is a sensitive marker for identifying therapeutic failure of parasite clearance, it may not definitively indicate treatment success, as evidenced by the discordance between initial parasite clearance and sustained PCR negativity across multiple trials [26; 28–30]. Additionally, PCR negativity alone may not indicate treatment success with regards to clinical outcomes. The temporal aspect of endpoint assessment further complicates interpretation, as parasite loads frequently rebound after initial suppression, typically beginning around 10 weeks post-treatment [52]. This pattern suggests that short follow-up periods may overestimate treatment efficacy, while extended monitoring reveals true therapeutic impact. The field urgently needs consensus on standardized study designs that incorporate: (1) harmonized PCR methodologies and interpretation criteria, (2) standardized follow-up durations that capture both early treatment response and long-term sustained clearance, (3) prospective treatment RCTs of indeterminate Chagas that include clinical endpoints, and (4) development of robust biomarkers that correlate with clinical outcomes.

Limitations

The small number of included studies examining treatment of indeterminate form CD limits confidence in our findings. Additionally, readers should interpret findings cautiously given that four out of five studies demonstrated high risk of bias. Although most individual domains (D1, D4, and D5) showed low risk or some concerns, our strategic weighting criteria appropriately emphasized domains D1 to D3 as critically important for randomized controlled trials, which may not be apparent from surface-level assessment. Finally, our reliance on aggregate rather than individual participant data creates several analytical limitations. Study-level relationships may not reflect individual-level associations, creating potential for ecological fallacy. This approach also restricted our ability to explore sources of heterogeneity, as patient-level factors explaining treatment response differences remain hidden within study-level summaries. Although studies showed differences in methodology, with various outcome definitions, follow-up periods, and analytical approaches, there was no significant heterogeneity across the included study-specific estimates of relative risk. We found no evidence of publication bias in the secondary outcome of parasite clearance (assessed by PCR as a surrogate maker), but significant bias was detected in the secondary outcome assessment of adverse events; disease progression could not be evaluated due to insufficient data in the included trials.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlights the potential of antitrypanosomal treatment to significantly improve parasitological cure rates in patients with indeterminate form, chronic phase Chagas disease, despite the limitations posed by high risk of bias and reliance on aggregated data in many of the included studies. Ultimately, our analysis underscores the need for more rigorous and standardized RCTs as well as the need for clinical endpoints. Our study also contributes valuable insights into treatment-related adverse events, particularly liver toxicity and skin reactions that continue to challenge treatment adherence. By focusing specifically on the indeterminate phase and prioritizing randomized trial data, our study adds a targeted and clinically relevant perspective that complements broader investigations of Chagas disease progression.

Future research efforts, including large-scale trials like MULTIBENZ and EQUITY are expected to fill critical gaps in our understanding of optimal treatment regimens and tolerability in diverse patient populations. Moreover, the integration of DTU characterization in future studies will be vital for elucidating to what extent parasite genetics influence therapeutic efficacy across different endemic regions. Importantly, these ongoing studies are adopting necessary adverse event reporting standards, as well as exploring both shorter and combination therapies that may improve safety and adherence. However, persistent challenges remain, particularly the lack of validated biomarkers and long-term clinical and parasitological efficacy endpoints. Our findings support continued investment in well-designed clinical trials that incorporate regional epidemiologic factors (especially in varied global settings), patient-centered outcomes, and long-term follow-up to guide therapeutic decision-making in indeterminate form, chronic phase CD.
