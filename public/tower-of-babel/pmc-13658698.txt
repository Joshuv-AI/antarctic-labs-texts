Biomachining in manufacturing: mechanisms, processes, applications and future perspectives
Authors: Jingyu Song, Hui Huang, Weibin Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiomachining (BM) is an emerging bio-assisted manufacturing approach that uses microbial metabolism and bioelectrochemical reactions to remove material under mild conditions. Due to its low mechanical loading and limited thermal effects, BM has potential in low-damage microfabrication, surface functionalization, microfluidic mold fabrication, abrasive tool dressing, and functional device modification. This review critically summarizes BM with respect to removal mechanisms, process technologies, modeling and optimization strategies, manufacturing applications, and engineering challenges. The biological and electrochemical mechanisms of BM are discussed first, followed by conventional and hybrid BM processes and their comparison with conventional and non-conventional machining technologies. Current applications are reviewed with emphasis on the gap between laboratory demonstrations and scalable industrial implementation. Major challenges include low removal efficiency, limited dimensional accuracy, unstable microbial activity, weak process controllability, contamination risk, waste treatment, and biosafety management. Future development should focus on standardized evaluation, microbial stability control, mechanism-data fusion modeling, hybrid process intensification, and dedicated equipment. Introduction

Background of advanced manufacturing

With the rapid development of electronic information, advanced healthcare, energy, and environmental sectors, the demand for microscale manufacturing, functional surface engineering, and high-performance components has continued to increase (Liu et al. 2026a; Ince and Özel 2025; Song et al. 2023; Farooq et al. 2022). To meet the growing requirements of precision micromachining, non-conventional machining methods, including chemical machining, laser machining, and electrical discharge machining, have been widely developed (Revilla et al. 2025; Kuriachen 2024; Zhu et al. 2024). However, these technologies still exhibit limitations in machining costs and energy consumption (Shokrani et al. 2024; Hocheng et al. 2012b). In this context, sustainable and low-carbon manufacturing requires alternative machining approaches that are lower-cost, consume less energy, and cause minimal workpiece damage, thereby promoting the development of emerging technologies such as biomachining (BM).

Emergence and evolution of biomachining

Bioleaching, Microbiologically Influenced Corrosion (MIC), and BM all involve microorganism-mediated dissolution of materials (Knisz et al. 2023; Tezyapar Kara et al. 2023). These processes differ substantially in their engineering purposes and process controls. Bioleaching aims to recover valuable components from minerals, tailings, and waste metals and is generally regarded as a resource-extraction-oriented metallurgical process that focuses on improving metal dissolution efficiency (Elander et al. 2025). MIC refers to the accelerated corrosion and degradation of materials caused by microbial activity, with emphasis on corrosion failure mechanisms and protection strategies (Catherine et al. 2026). In contrast, BM is a manufacturing-oriented process that utilizes microbial metabolic characteristics to achieve controlled material removal. These characteristics include the transformation of highly oxidizing ions, secretion of catalytic enzymes, and formation of localized removal environments. Therefore, BM can be used to fabricate designed geometries, microstructures, and functional surface features, showing potential for precision micromachining (Hocheng and Jadhav 2015; Ting et al. 2000). In 1996, Uno et al. (1996) first used Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans to fabricate microgrooves on copper and proposed the concept of BM.

Research progress and historical development

A literature search was conducted using Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar for publications from January 1996 to March 2026. The main keywords included ‘biomachining’, ‘microbial machining’, ‘biological machining’, and ‘bio-etching’. To improve transparency, a systematic literature search and selection workflow was established, as shown in Fig. S1. Studies were included if they addressed BM mechanisms, microorganisms, culture conditions, material removal processes, process parameters, hybrid biomachining (HBM), modeling and optimization, manufacturing applications, sustainability, or future development. Studies related only to bioleaching, biomining, microbial corrosion, bioremediation, or general microbiology were excluded if they did not involve manufacturing-oriented material removal or BM applications.

As shown in Fig. 1, the development of BM can be summarized into three progressive stages. The early exploration stage from 1996 to 2005 demonstrated the feasibility of using microorganisms as material removal agents by revealing the roles of microbial oxidation and metabolic products in metal dissolution (Kikuchi et al. 1999; Uno et al. 1996). The mechanism and process development stage from 2006 to 2018 shifted the focus toward microorganism selection, material compatibility, and process parameter regulation to improve material removal rate (MRR), surface roughness (SR), and controllability (Muhammad et al. 2015; Díaz-Tena et al. 2014). The engineering exploration stage from 2019 to 2026 introduced external energy fields, hybrid strategies, and optimization methods to overcome the limitations of conventional BM and expand its laboratory-scale applications in microstructure fabrication and functional surface preparation (Bijimol et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025a; Santaolalla et al. 2023).

Overall, BM has evolved from feasibility demonstration toward mechanism clarification, process regulation, and engineering exploration. Its low mechanical loading, limited thermal damage, and potential environmental advantages have attracted increasing attention in microfabrication, surface engineering, and tool conditioning (Pradeep et al. 2024; Eskandarian et al. 2013; Hocheng et al. 2012a, b, c; Liu et al. 2009). However, the boundaries of its practical application remain unclear.

Aim and structure of this review

Several review articles have summarized BM from different perspectives, including microbial metal etching, process principles, operating parameters, micromachining feasibility, and manufacturing applications. However, most existing reviews focus on selected aspects of BM. At the same time, an integrated framework linking microbial metabolism, electrochemical reactions, mass transfer, material removal mechanisms, hybrid processes, process modeling, application maturity, sustainability, and industrial scale-up remains insufficient. As summarized in Table 1, previous reviews have provided valuable foundations, but further integration from fundamental mechanisms to controllable manufacturing is still needed.

Mechanisms of biomachining

The interaction among microbial metabolism, solution chemistry, interfacial electrochemical reactions, and material dissolution controls BM. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for explaining material removal behavior and improving process controllability. This section summarizes the representative microorganisms used in BM, the key factors affecting microbial activity and material removal, and the direct, indirect, and hybrid removal mechanisms. Thermodynamic and kinetic assessments are further used to evaluate the feasibility of material removal and the reaction-rate characteristics. This mechanistic foundation supports the subsequent comparison with other machining technologies and the development of controllable BM processes.

Microorganisms used in biomachining

Microbial metabolic activity determines material removal capability and process stability. Different strains promote material dissolution mainly through Fe2+ oxidation, organic acid, or the production of oxidative metabolites (Ma et al. 2025b). Representative microorganisms and their culture conditions are summarized in Table 2. Among them, Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans and Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans are currently the most widely used strains, as they promote metal dissolution primarily through the Fe2+ oxidation cycle (Suresh et al. 2025). Aspergillus niger mainly facilitates material removal through organic acids and glucose oxidase (Zhang et al. 2020; Jadhav and Hocheng 2015, 2014; Eskandarian et al. 2013). Staphylococcus sp. can create acidic or alkaline corrosive microenvironments through different metabolic pathways, thereby enabling localized material dissolution (Matlani and Kadam 2021; Shikata et al. 2009; Kikuchi et al. 1999). In addition, strains such as Paracoccus versutus, Leptospirillum ferrooxidans, and Sulfobacillus thermosulfidooxidans have also been applied to BM of different material systems (Pradeep et al. 2024; Díaz-Tena et al. 2018, 2016; Hoque and Philip 2011).

Unlike conventional machining, the material removal capability of BM depends not only on the species of microorganism but also on cell density, metabolic activity, and culture conditions. Microbial populations usually undergo lag, exponential, and decline phases, during which cell number and metabolic rate change with processing time, thereby affecting MRR and process stability (Singh et al. 2019). To maintain microbial activity and support continuous oxidant regeneration, recent studies have explored strain preservation, immobilized cultivation, online cultivation, and culture medium regulation (Song et al. 2025; Santaolalla et al. 2021, 2020; Díaz-Tena et al. 2018, 2017b). Although current strategies have improved microbial activity and stability, they still rely largely on empirical optimization.

In addition to culture regulation, biological stability is critical for BM. During long-term cultivation or repeated processing, genetic instability, mutation, metabolic variability, biofilm evolution, microbial competition, and contamination may change metabolite production, Fe2+/Fe3+ regeneration, cell adhesion, and material removal behavior, thereby reducing MRR stability and process repeatability. The key barrier is the lack of quantitative relationships among microbial metabolic behavior, mass transfer, material dissolution, and machining responses. Establishing these relationships is essential for stable, efficient, and scalable BM.

Factors influencing biomachining performance

The cultural environment directly affects microbial growth and metabolic capability. Factors such as culture temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), medium composition, and shaking speed influence cell density, metabolic rate, and metabolite production (Ma and Huang 2020a). Appropriate temperature and pH help maintain microbial activity, while sufficient oxygen supply and suitable medium composition promote the metabolism of aerobic microorganisms (Jadhav and Hocheng 2014; Yang et al. 2009; Johnson et al. 2007). Previous studies have shown that changes in culture parameters can significantly alter microbial oxidation capability and MRR (Muhammad and Ko 2014). However, most studies still focus on single-factor optimization, and the coupled effects among different culture parameters and their influence on the stability of material removal remain insufficiently understood.

Metabolite concentration is another key factor affecting MRR, but its effect does not increase monotonically. When microbial metabolic activity is insufficient, Fe3+ regeneration decreases, leading to a gradual reduction in MRR (Santaolalla et al. 2024). Conversely, excessive iron ion concentration or unsuitable pH conditions can promote the formation of precipitates such as jarosite (KFe3(SO4)2(OH)6), which consumes dissolved Fe3+ and inhibits material removal (Santaolalla et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2024; Díaz-Tena et al. 2018). In addition, Cu2+ accumulation and reduced oxygen availability may suppress microbial activity (Muhammad et al. 2015). Therefore, metabolite concentration should be properly regulated. Simply increasing oxidant or metal-ion concentration does not necessarily lead to sustained improvements in machining efficiency.

Material composition, microstructure, and surface state further determine the selectivity of removal and interfacial reaction resistance during BM. Compared with pure metals, alloys may exhibit stronger selective dissolution due to potential differences among constituent elements and micro-galvanic effects (Ma et al. 2020a). In sintered and cast materials, microstructural heterogeneity can also affect localized corrosion and MRR (Pradeep et al. 2023). However, dense oxide films, passive layers, and adhered reaction product layers on the material surface can hinder interfacial reactions between metabolites and the substrate, thereby reducing MRR (Lin et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2019). Therefore, BM is not governed solely by microbial metabolic capability but is also strongly constrained by surface chemistry and interfacial mass-transfer behavior.

Overall, BM is a multivariable, coupled process rather than one controlled by a single parameter. Although previous studies have identified the effects of individual factors on MRR, these isolated trends may not be directly applicable under coupled microbial, chemical, and material-interface conditions. The key barrier is the lack of quantitative models linking microbial metabolism, culture environment, metabolite concentration, mass transfer, and interfacial reactions. Therefore, multivariable modeling and process control are needed to ensure a stable, controllable BM.

Material removal mechanisms

BM of metallic materials is essentially an electrochemical dissolution process in which metal atoms lose electrons, forming soluble ions in a microorganism-mediated environment. Depending on whether microorganisms interact directly with the material surface or regulate dissolution via extracellular metabolites, three material removal mechanisms have been proposed: direct, indirect, and hybrid removal.

As shown in Fig. 3a, the direct removal mechanism involves microbial attachment and extracellular electron transfer between cells and the metal surface (Indianto et al. 2023; Díaz-Tena et al. 2014). As shown in Fig. 3b–d, microscopic observations have confirmed microbial attachment and participation in the dissolution process (Wang et al. 2014). However, efficient biofilm formation and electron transfer are difficult to maintain during machining, which results in lower removal efficiency (Lilova et al. 2007; Zhang and Li 1998). Therefore, the contribution of the direct removal mechanism to overall material removal remains limited.

Indirect removal is the dominant material removal pathway in BM. As shown in Fig. 3e, microorganisms can generate or transform Fe3+, oxalic acid, and H2O2 during metabolism (Zhang et al. 2020; Jadhav and Hocheng 2014). These extracellular acidic or strongly oxidizing species etch materials through redox reactions (Jadhav and Hocheng 2016, 2015; Eskandarian et al. 2013). The dominant role of indirect removal has been supported by variations in etching efficiency under different metabolite concentrations (Muhammad et al. 2015; Xenofontos et al. 2015; Jadhav et al. 2013; Yang et al. 2009). Compared with direct removal, indirect removal offers a more controllable reaction pathway because material dissolution is primarily governed by extracellular oxidizing species rather than unstable direct contact between microbial and the material surface.

The hybrid removal mechanism is a synergistic process in which microbial surface attachment and extracellular metabolite oxidation jointly contribute to material removal. As shown in Fig. 3f and g, Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans and sulfate-reducing bacteria can promote material dissolution through both extracellular electron transfer and oxidation cycles (Li et al. 2018; Istiyanto et al. 2011; Shikata et al. 2009). However, the relative contributions of direct and indirect removal vary among different microorganisms and processing conditions (Pradeep et al. 2022; Xenofontos et al. 2015). Therefore, the hybrid removal mechanism remains limited by insufficient in situ characterization and quantitative analysis of contributions, making it difficult to control material removal and predict machining quality.

Among these three mechanisms, indirect oxidation-mediated dissolution is currently regarded as the main pathway in BM, because extracellular oxidants and metabolites can continuously participate in metal dissolution without requiring stable microbial attachment. Direct removal provides fundamental evidence of microorganism-material interactions, but its contribution is often limited by low electron-transfer efficiency and insufficient biofilm stability. The hybrid mechanism combines these two pathways and shows potential for improving removal efficiency.

Thermodynamic and kinetic assessment

The feasibility assessment of BM has largely drawn on studies of bioleaching and MIC. Since indirect removal is generally the dominant pathway, thermodynamic and kinetic analyses can be used to evaluate the feasibility of material removal and the tendency toward dissolution (Zhang and Li 1999). Thermodynamic analysis supports material selection, prediction of preferential dissolution, and redox system design. In contrast, kinetic analysis reveals the effects of pH, oxidation–reduction potential (ORP), open circuit potential (OCP), metabolite concentration, and mass transfer on MRR, as shown in Fig. 4. However, direct removal depends on the interfacial microenvironment and microbial oxidation potential, making its key parameters difficult to quantify. Therefore, coupling thermodynamic and kinetic analyses with process parameter design is essential for developing BM from empirical regulation toward quantitative process design.

When mass transfer is the limiting factor, reactant transport can be enhanced through agitation, flow-field design, immobilized cultivation, or assisted energy fields.

Current thermodynamic and kinetic analyses in BM are still largely adapted from bioleaching and MIC studies, and their manufacturing-guiding capability remains limited. Thermodynamic analysis can assess the tendency of a reaction, but it cannot directly predict MRR. Kinetic models can describe interfacial reaction rates, but they often neglect microbial growth, metabolic fluctuations, biofilm evolution, and mass transfer. The key barrier is the lack of a quantitative framework linking electrochemical reactions, microbial metabolism, mass transfer, and machining responses. This limitation hinders process parameter design, online monitoring, and MRR prediction, thereby restricting the transition of BM from empirical regulation to model-driven development.

Comparison with other machining technologies

BM differs from mechanical machining, chemical etching, laser machining, electrical discharge machining, and electrochemical machining in material removal mechanism, energy input, surface integrity, and environmental impact. It removes materials primarily via microbial metabolites, redox reactions, or extracellular electron transfer, thereby offering low mechanical loading and limited thermal effects. However, its low MRR, insufficient dimensional accuracy, process instability, and limited industrial maturity remain major constraints. Therefore, its technical value should be evaluated using quantitative indicators such as MRR, SR, dimensional accuracy, energy consumption, cost, and environmental impact, as summarized in Table 4.

Comparison between biomachining and other machining technologies

Compiled by the authors based on the cited literature

Compared with conventional mechanical machining such as milling, grinding, and drilling, BM does not rely on strong tool-workpiece contact, thereby reducing mechanical loading, residual stress, and subsurface damage (Li et al. 2025). This makes it more suitable for thin-walled structures, brittle material surfaces, and low-damage microstructural fabrication (Taufiqurrakhman et al. 2021). However, conventional machining still has clear advantages in MRR, machining accuracy, equipment maturity, and batch stability. Because BM typically has a low MRR and long processing time, it is currently better suited to localized fine removal, surface functionalization, and tool dressing rather than high-efficiency bulk material removal.

Compared with chemical etching, BM is also a dissolution-based process, but it uses milder microbial metabolites or biological oxidation cycles instead of strong acids, alkalis, or oxidizing etchants. This can reduce risks associated with over-etching, safety hazards, and corrosive waste (Hocheng et al. 2012b ; Li et al. 2000). However, the environmental advantage of BM should not be assumed solely from its mild reaction conditions (Kohn et al. 2024; Hocheng and Jadhav 2011). It should be evaluated against competing processes under the same functional unit, considering energy consumption, chemical use, waste treatment, emissions, cost, machining quality, and process efficiency.

Compared with laser machining and electrical discharge machining, BM produces almost no heat-affected zone, recast layer, or thermal cracks, thereby preserving surface integrity. Laser machining and electrical discharge machining are efficient and controllable for microholes, microgrooves, and complex structures. However, their high local energy input can lead to surface alterations, microcracks, residual thermal stresses, high equipment costs, and increased energy consumption (Lesi et al. 2025). BM is more suitable for heat-sensitive materials, high-integrity surfaces, and functional microstructures. However, its low MRR, limited spatial selectivity, and slow process response still restrict its use in high aspect ratio structures, highly uniform arrays, and rapid manufacturing (Matlani and Kadam 2021; Díaz-Tena et al. 2014).

Compared with electrochemical machining, BM also involves interfacial electrochemical dissolution, but differs in driving force and control mode. Electrochemical machining uses an external electric field and electrolyte circulation to achieve high MRR and complex-shape machining. Still, it requires precise control of the machining gap, electrolyte state, and power parameters, with additional energy, electrolyte treatment, and equipment costs (Zhang et al. 2018). BM is mainly driven by microbial metabolism and redox cycles, which may reduce external energy input, but its reaction rate, stability, and controllability remain limited. Therefore, BM is more suitable as a complementary process for small-scale, low-damage applications than as a short-term replacement for electrochemical machining.

HBM offers a potential route to improve MRR and process controllability. External assistance, including electric fields, flow fields, jets, magnetic fields, and ultrasound, can enhance mass transfer, oxidant regeneration, and interfacial reactions. However, its effectiveness should not be judged solely by MRR improvement, as external fields may also affect microbial activity, metabolic stability, SR, and undercutting (Song et al. 2025; Uno et al. 1996). Therefore, the key issue is to clarify the relationships among external field parameters, microbial metabolism, interfacial reactions, and machining quality, rather than treating HBM as a universal solution.

Overall, BM is better suited to fine processing with low mechanical loading, limited thermal damage, and potential environmental benefits than to high efficiency bulk removal. Its promising applications include low-damage microstructures, functional surfaces, tool dressing, and biocompatible surface modification. However, limitations in MRR, dimensional accuracy, repeatability, equipment maturity, and verification of environmental impact remain. Therefore, the practical value of BM should be assessed against competing machining technologies under the same functional unit, considering energy consumption, cost, waste treatment, environmental impact, and machining performance.

Biomachining processes and technologies

BM processes can be classified into conventional BM and HBM according to the use of external assistance. Conventional BM relies on microbial metabolism and bioelectrochemical reactions to remove material. In contrast, HBM introduces external fields or auxiliary methods to enhance mass transfer, oxidant regeneration, and process controllability. Based on the mechanisms discussed above, this section reviews conventional BM, HBM, and related process modeling and optimization strategies, aiming to clarify the current technological framework and development direction of BM processes.

Conventional biomachining

Selective material removal is essential for applying BM to microfabrication. In most cases, BM is combined with masking techniques to confine the contact between microbial metabolites and the material surface, thereby enabling the fabrication of microgrooves, micro-pits, and complex patterns (Song et al. 2025; Muhammad et al. 2018; Díaz-Tena et al. 2014). Masking approaches such as digital lithography, maskless lithography, and laser ablation of resists have been used to prepare mask patterns for biomachined microstructures, thereby promoting their application in micromachining (Imran et al. 2017; Suwandi et al. 2014; Saragih and Ko 2013).

However, the accuracy of the mask pattern does not directly determine the final machining accuracy, as shown in Fig. 5a–c. Rounded photoresist edges, nonuniform mask adhesion, and pattern transfer errors can alter the initial machining boundary (Imran et al. 2017). In addition, metabolite diffusion, local pH and ORP fluctuations, and reaction product layer formation during BM can further cause the actual removal region to deviate from the designed pattern. Therefore, dimensional accuracy is governed not only by mask quality, but also by biochemical reactions and interfacial mass transfer.

Existing studies have shown that BM can fabricate microscale structures such as microgrooves, micro-pits, and complex surface patterns, as summarized in Table 5, thereby indicating its potential for low-damage microfabrication. However, shape errors and dimensional deviations remain common (Istiyanto et al. 2017, 2012; Muhammad et al. 2015; Hocheng et al. 2012c). Undercutting is one of the main causes of dimensional error, as shown in Fig. 5d and e. It results from the lateral diffusion of oxidative metabolites and ions near the mask edge, as well as the quasi-isotropic corrosion behavior of BM (Istiyanto et al. 2011). Longer processing times usually intensify undercutting, leading to lower aspect ratios, rounded edges, and reduced pattern fidelity (Singh et al. 2019). Therefore, undercutting control is essential for transforming BM from feasible microstructure fabrication into precise microfabrication.

In addition, the repeatability of biomachined microstructure fabrication has not been sufficiently validated. Fluctuations in microbial activity, medium composition, pH, ORP, OCP, metabolite concentration, surface oxide films, and mask adhesion can cause variations in MRR, machining depth, lateral corrosion, and surface quality. However, most studies still report representative morphologies or single experimental cases, with limited statistical evaluation of dimensional deviation, undercutting, morphology consistency, and surface integrity. The key limitation is the lack of standardized evaluation metrics for accuracy and repeatability, which hinders the transition of BM from feasible microstructure fabrication to controllable microfabrication.

Hybrid biomachining

HBM introduces external fields, auxiliary processes, or other machining methods to address the low MRR, limited mass transfer, and insufficient controllability of conventional BM (Chavoshi and Luo 2015). It generally includes two strategies: coupling BM with external fields, such as electric fields, flow fields, and jets, to enhance interfacial reactions or mass transport; and using galvanic coupling, acid assistance, or other methods to modify the electrochemical environment and promote selective dissolution. However, current studies remain limited to a few materials and assistance methods. Therefore, the role of HBM should be evaluated by both removal efficiency and machining quality.

Existing studies indicate that external assistance can improve material removal capability to some extent, as shown in Table 6. Electric-field assistance can promote anodic dissolution by altering the workpiece potential. As shown in Fig. 6, Uno et al. reported that the removal depth of copper increased by approximately 52% after applying a voltage (Uno et al. 1996). Flow or jet assistance can enhance mass transfer between microbial metabolites and the workpiece surface, thereby increasing MRR (Song et al. 2025). In addition, Galvanic coupling uses the potential difference between different materials to strengthen selective dissolution in the anodic region (Ma et al. 2020b ). Acid assistance can reduce interfacial reaction resistance by increasing the H+ concentration, thereby promoting metal dissolution (Sonia et al. 2021). These studies suggest that hybrid strategies have the potential to intensify BM processes.

It should be noted that an increase in the MRR does not necessarily improve machining quality (Lin et al. 2024). Stronger interfacial reactions and mass-transfer disturbances may increase SR, local over-corrosion, non-uniform product deposition, undercutting, or microstructural distortion. For example, some jet-assisted studies showed that SR and MRR may increase simultaneously, indicating that higher removal efficiency may be achieved at the expense of SR (Song et al. 2025). Therefore, HBM should be evaluated not only by MRR or removal depth but also by SR, dimensional error, undercutting, surface integrity, and batch repeatability.

Overall, HBM is a promising approach to overcoming the low efficiency of conventional BM, but its engineering feasibility has not been sufficiently verified. The main barriers include unclear removal mechanisms under multi-field coupling, limited understanding of the effects of external fields on microbial activity, the unresolved trade-off between MRR and SR, and immature equipment and control methods. Therefore, single-case demonstrations are insufficient, quantitative relationships among external fields, microbial metabolism, interfacial reactions, and machining quality are needed to support controllable and engineering-oriented applications.

Process modeling and intelligent optimization

For BM, process modeling should link biological variables and process parameters with machining responses. This is important for understanding material removal and improving process control (Chavoshi and Luo 2015). Relevant inputs include microbial activity, cell density, growth phase, culture environment, metabolite concentration, solution state, processing time, and material properties. In contrast, outputs include MRR, SR, dimensional accuracy, surface integrity, and environmental performance. Therefore, modeling and parameter optimization can help identify key variables and support the transition from empirical regulation to predictable machining.

Existing studies have used significance analysis, sensitivity analysis, orthogonal experiments, Taguchi methods, response surface methodology, linear regression, and intelligent optimization algorithms to model or optimize the relationships between culture/process parameters and material removal characteristics (Pradeep et al. 2024; Ma and Huang 2020a; Singh et al. 2019), as shown in Table 7. These methods help shorten experimental cycles, identify key parameters, and obtain improved process conditions. For example, response surface methodology and orthogonal experiments are commonly used to analyze the effects of pH, temperature, culture time, Fe2+/Fe3+ concentration, and processing time on MRR and SR (Pradeep et al. 2022; Muhammad et al. 2015).

However, current BM models remain largely based on empirical fitting and single-objective optimization, with limited predictive capability and engineering applicability (Pradeep et al. 2023). A major reason is that BM is governed not only by fixed process parameters, but also by dynamic biological variables. Microbial activity, cell density, growth phase, pH, ORP, DO, Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio, metabolite concentration, and biofilm state can directly affect material dissolution and machining stability. These variables are often time-varying and batch-dependent, making it difficult to define inputs consistently and transfer models.

In addition, most existing models focus mainly on MRR, while SR, dimensional accuracy, surface integrity, environmental impact, and cost are insufficiently considered. The lack of mechanistic constraints from microbial metabolism, electrochemical reactions, and mass transfer further limits model interpretability. These limitations become more significant in HBM, where external fields increase the complexity of parameter coupling and may also affect microbial activity.

Although direct applications of machine learning (ML) and Internet of Things (IoT) concepts in BM remain limited, related advances in optimization, bioleaching, and smart BM offer useful references for future process control (Shariatifar et al. 2025; Isoko et al. 2024; Naseri et al. 2023). To overcome the above limitations, BM modeling should move toward mechanism-data fusion frameworks that connect microbial metabolism, electrochemical reactions, mass transfer, and material dissolution with machining responses. Supported by online sensing of pH, oxidation–reduction potential, temperature, DO, ion concentration, and microbial activity, ML models can help describe nonlinear relationships among microbial state, culture environment, process parameters, and machining responses, such as MRR, SR, dimensional error, and surface integrity (Mondal et al. 2023). Further integration with digital twin-based closed-loop control may support anomaly detection, prediction of removal, and real-time process regulation. It may also enable multi-objective optimization of MRR, SR, dimensional accuracy, and environmental performance.

Applications of biomachining in manufacturing

BM uses microorganisms and their metabolites to remove materials and has attracted attention in microfabrication, surface engineering, and functional device fabrication due to its low mechanical loading, limited thermal damage, and potential green manufacturing characteristics (Hocheng et al. 2012a). However, most existing applications are still at the stage of laboratory validation or prototype fabrication, while cases for continuous industrial production remain limited. Machining of micro-components and microstructures

Micro-component and microstructure fabrication is one of the earliest application areas of BM. As shown in Fig. 7, structures such as micro-gears, microgrooves, micro-pits, and complex patterns have been fabricated, indicating the feasibility of BM for low-damage microstructure formation (Muhammad et al. 2018, 2015; Díaz-Tena et al. 2014; Zhang and Li 1998). However, these studies remain mainly at the laboratory-scale validation stage, and there is still a clear gap to industrial microstructure manufacturing. Key limitations include long processing time, low MRR, obvious undercutting, insufficient dimensional consistency, and limited repeatability verification (Istiyanto et al. 2012). Therefore, the key issue is no longer only whether microstructures can be fabricated by BM, but whether they can be fabricated stably, accurately, and repeatably.

Preparation of functional surfaces

Functional surface fabrication is a promising application of BM. By controlling surface microstructures and morphology, BM can improve tribological, wetting, and interface properties (Liu et al. 2026b; Song et al. 2025; Imran et al. 2017). As shown in Fig. 8, it has been used to fabricate friction-reducing textures, hydrophobic surfaces, and cell-adhesive structures (Qosim et al. 2018; Whulanza et al. 2016). However, most studies remain limited to laboratory validation on specific samples and mainly correlate biomachined surface morphology with short-term functional performance. The combined effects of surface chemistry, oxide layers, reaction residues, and service conditions are rarely evaluated. Therefore, evaluation criteria should move beyond single-sample functional testing and include large-area uniformity, durability, batch repeatability, and long-term performance stability.

Micro-channel fabrication

Microchannel structures are important for fluid transport, mixing, and heat transfer in microfluidic devices, cooling structures, and biochemical detection systems. As shown in Fig. 9, BM has been used to fabricate microchannel molds and prototype devices, such as polydimethylsiloxane microfluidic devices replicated from metal molds for directional droplet motion control (Santaolalla et al. 2023). However, current studies mainly remain at the stage of mold fabrication and prototype validation (Taufiqurrakhman et al. 2021). Since microchannels require high dimensional accuracy, controlled cross-sectional profiles, clean surfaces, and consistent flow resistance, long processing time, lateral removal, and reaction residues may affect channel accuracy and device performance. Therefore, the key issue is whether BM-fabricated microchannels can meet device-level requirements for cross-sectional accuracy, surface cleanliness, flow resistance, and long-term stability.

Tool dressing

Tool dressing is one of the BM applications closest to engineering use. Unlike direct microstructure fabrication, it aims to selectively remove metal bond or loading layers rather than precisely replicate microscale geometries, thereby improving abrasive protrusion, chip space, and cutting ability (Lin et al. 2026). As shown in Fig. 10, BM has been used to dress fixed abrasive lapping plates and metal-bonded diamond grinding wheels, thereby improving subsequent grinding performance (Ma et al. 2025a; Ma and Huang 2020b). However, dressing rate, uniformity, abrasive retention, and online integration remain key challenges. Insufficient dressing may leave residual bond or loading layers, while excessive dressing may weaken abrasive retention and shorten tool life. Therefore, BM-based dressing should be evaluated based on abrasive protrusion, grinding force, grinding temperature, SR, and tool life, rather than on bond removal efficiency alone.

Functional device fabrication

BM has also been used for structural regulation and interface modification of functional devices. As shown in Fig. 11, it has been applied to microfluidic chips, electrode structures, and microbial fuel cells (Suwandi et al. 2014). For example, porous anodes can increase specific surface area and enhance bacteria-electrode interactions and electron transfer, thereby improving microbial fuel cell performance (Bijimol et al. 2025). However, most applications remain at the proof-of-concept or prototype stage. Functional device fabrication requires structural accuracy, interface cleanliness, device consistency, and long-term stability. Some microstructures may enhance interfacial reactions but reduce substrate mechanical strength (Hemalatha et al. 2021). Therefore, the critical issue is determining whether BM-induced interface structures can improve device performance without sacrificing structural accuracy, interface cleanliness, substrate strength, or long-term stability.

Summary of application maturity and future requirements

Overall, BM has demonstrated potential in microstructure fabrication, functional surfaces, microchannels, tool dressing, and functional device modification. However, aside from tool dressing and some surface-modification applications, most studies remain at the prototype stage and have not yet established stable, repeatable, and scalable industrial processes. These limitations indicate that the main bottleneck is the lack of a stable and scalable process chain rather than the absence of isolated application demonstrations. Key issues include machining efficiency, dimensional accuracy, batch repeatability, and long-term performance, as well as equipment integration, contamination control, microbial stability, medium circulation, waste treatment, automation, inline monitoring, and process standardization.

Challenges, scale-up barriers, and future perspectives

Although BM has been demonstrated in various laboratory applications, including functional surfaces, tool dressing, and functional device modification. However, its transition toward engineering, continuous, and scalable manufacturing still faces significant barriers. Unlike conventional manufacturing processes, BM is governed by the coupled effects of microbial metabolism, electrochemical reactions, interfacial mass transfer, and material dissolution, making the process highly dynamic and uncertain. Therefore, building on the application maturity analysis above, this section summarizes the key barriers to scale-up and proposes future routes toward a controllable, engineering-oriented BM, as shown in Fig. 12.

Key challenges and scale-up barriers

At present, the transition of BM from laboratory validation to industrial application faces several major challenges.

First, machining efficiency and forming accuracy remain major technical constraints. The relatively low MRR leads to long processing times, which may aggravate undercutting, increase SR, and cause dimensional deviations. In addition, reaction product layers, passive films, and metabolite concentration gradients can lead to non-uniform material removal, making it difficult to control microstructural profiles, aspect ratios, and surface integrity. Therefore, improving removal efficiency must be balanced with dimensional accuracy and surface quality.

Second, microbial stability is a key bottleneck for scalable BM. During long-term cultivation or repeated processing, genetic instability, mutation, metabolic variability, biofilm evolution, microbial competition, contamination, nutrient depletion, and metal ion accumulation can change microbial activity and metabolite production. These changes may lead to fluctuations in MRR, SR, and machining repeatability. Therefore, scalable BM requires long-term culture maintenance, stable strain management, contamination control, medium renewal, microbial activity monitoring, and regulation of key solution parameters, including pH, ORP, DO, nutrient concentration, Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio, and metal ion concentration.

Third, manufacturing-oriented equipment and process integration remain insufficient. Most current studies still rely on beakers, culture dishes, or simple flow cells, which are difficult to connect with stable production lines. Dedicated reactor or machining-cell systems are needed to integrate microbial cultivation, machining reaction, medium circulation, temperature control, pH/ORP regulation, DO supply, waste treatment, metal ion recovery, and online monitoring. Continuous processing further requires stable microbial activity, nutrient supply, metabolite regulation, contamination prevention, and reaction product removal. In addition, inline monitoring of pH, ORP, DO, temperature, ion concentration, cell density, and machining depth is necessary for process diagnosis and closed-loop control. Process standardization, biosafety management, waste discharge control, regulatory compliance, economic feasibility, and technology readiness should also be evaluated before industrial implementation.

Finally, the sustainability and economic feasibility of BM still require systematic verification. Its mild reaction conditions, low mechanical loading, and limited thermal damage should not be directly equated with green manufacturing advantages. The actual environmental performance depends on the whole process chain, including medium preparation, sterilization, microbial cultivation, processing time, waste treatment, metal ion recovery, biosafety management, and equipment operation. Therefore, Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) and techno-economic analysis are needed to clarify the real environmental and economic benefits of BM in specific application scenarios.

Roadmap toward controllable and scalable biomachining

For engineering applications, BM should follow a route of biological system stabilization, mechanism-data fusion modeling, dedicated equipment development, digital twin-based closed-loop control, and sustainability validation, as shown in Fig. 13.

Integrated framework for controllable and scalable biomachining. Created by the authors

First, stable and repeatable microbial working windows should be established. For Fe2+/Fe3+ cycling systems, Fe3+ regeneration, pH, ORP, DO, and metal ion accumulation should be monitored. For organic acid or enzyme-mediated systems, metabolite production, medium stability, and product inhibition should be controlled. Strategies such as strain screening, strain preservation, immobilized cultivation, co-culture regulation, medium circulation, ion recovery, and online microbial monitoring can improve metabolic stability. Key indicators should include cell viability, metabolite concentration stability, pH/ORP fluctuation, continuous processing time, and MRR repeatability.

Second, predictive models that combine mechanistic constraints with data-driven methods should be developed. Kinetic models can describe charge transfer, diffusion resistance, and MRR, while data-driven models can map biological and process parameters to MRR, SR, dimensional error, and undercutting. However, future models should not only improve fitting accuracy but also explain the coupling among microbial metabolism, electrochemical reactions, mass transfer, and material dissolution. In addition, ML and IoT-based monitoring can provide useful support for this process. Online sensing of pH, ORP, temperature, DO, ion concentration, metabolite concentration, and microbial activity can be used to update ML models and digital twins. This enables prediction of removal, anomaly detection, and process-state estimation.

Third, dedicated BM equipment should be developed to enable stable, controllable, and scalable processing. A manufacturing-oriented BM system should evolve from simple laboratory containers to closed or semi-closed machining reactors integrating microbial cultivation, machining reaction, medium circulation, temperature control, pH/ORP regulation, DO supply, contamination prevention, sensor integration, waste treatment, and metal ion recovery. Continuous processing further requires stable microbial activity, nutrient supply, metabolite regulation, reaction product removal, and medium renewal over long operating periods. In addition, inline monitoring, process standardization, biosafety management, regulatory compliance, economic feasibility, and technology readiness should be evaluated before industrial implementation.

Fourth, digital twin-based closed-loop control should be introduced to link biological variability with manufacturing controllability. Real-time sensing data can provide dynamic process information, while physics-data fusion models can predict MRR, SR, dimensional error, undercutting, and process instability. Based on the target structure and quality requirements, key process variables such as medium renewal rate, flow rate, pH, nutrient supply, external-field parameters, and processing time can be adjusted to form a sensing-prediction-control-machining closed loop. Since microbial systems are inherently fluctuating, digital twin models should be updated online rather than relying on fixed parameters.

Finally, sustainability validation should be conducted using LCA and techno-economic analysis under clearly defined functional units, such as unit removal volume, unit machined area, or unit functional surface performance. The evaluation should consider energy consumption, medium and chemical use, sterilization, waste treatment, metal ion recovery, biosafety management, processing efficiency, equipment cost, and product quality. Such analysis can help identify application scenarios where BM offers real sustainability advantages.

Milestone goals for engineering implementation

Based on the above roadmap, BM can be advanced through short-term, medium-term, and long-term goals. In the short term, the priority is to improve the repeatability of laboratory processes by standardizing strain preservation, culture medium formulation, pH/ORP monitoring, material pretreatment, MRR/SR evaluation, and repeatability metrics. In the medium term, predictable and controllable BM systems should be developed through mechanism-data fusion models, online monitoring, dedicated machining-cell design, and closed-loop regulation of microbial activity, metabolite concentration, flow conditions, and machining time. In the long term, engineering validation should be conducted for specific application scenarios, such as large-area functional surface treatment, microfluidic mold fabrication, and electrode surface modification. The evaluation should emphasize continuous operation, batch repeatability, lifetime performance, equipment reliability, waste treatment, cost accounting, and regulatory compliance.

Overall, the future development of BM should not rely merely on introducing concepts such as microbial engineering, artificial intelligence, or hybrid energy fields. Instead, the key is to transform microbial reactions into manufacturing processes that can be monitored, modeled, controlled, and verified. Only by moving from laboratory feasibility demonstration to engineering process control can BM achieve sustainable, scalable, and industrial applications.

Conclusions

BM should be regarded as a complementary bio-assisted process for specific low-damage and function-oriented manufacturing applications, rather than a universal substitute for conventional or non-conventional machining. The main contribution of this review is to establish an integrated, manufacturing-oriented framework that links microbial activity, electrochemical dissolution, mass transfer, material response, process modeling, and application maturity. This framework clarifies the key barriers to BM development, including low MRR, dimensional error, poor repeatability, microbial instability, weak process controllability, and insufficient industrial verification. Overall, the broader manufacturing value of BM depends on transforming microbial reactions into processes that can be monitored, modeled, controlled, and validated through standardized evaluation, mechanism-data fusion modeling, dedicated equipment, inline monitoring, digital twin-based control, and sustainability assessment.
