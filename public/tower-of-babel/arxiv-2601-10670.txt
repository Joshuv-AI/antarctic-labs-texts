Real characters and real classes of GL 2 \mathrm{GL}_{2} and GU 2 \mathrm{GU}_{2} over discrete valuation rings
Archita Gupta, Tejbir Lohan, Pooja Singla

Abstract
. Let 𝔬 \mathfrak{o} be the ring of integers of a non-archimedean local field with residue field of odd characteristic, 𝔭 \mathfrak{p} be its maximal ideal and let 𝔬 ℓ = 𝔬 / 𝔭 ℓ \mathfrak{o}_{\ell}=\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p}^{\ell} for ℓ ≥ 2 \ell\geq 2 . In this article, we study real-valued characters and real representations of the finite groups GL 2 ​ ( 𝔬 ℓ ) \mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}) and GU 2 ​ ( 𝔬 ℓ ) \mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}) . We give a complete classification of real and strongly real classes of these groups and characterize the real-valued irreducible complex characters. We prove that every real-valued irreducible complex character of GL 2 ​ ( 𝔬 ℓ ) \mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}) is afforded by a representation over ℝ \mathbb{R} . In contrast, we show that GU 2 ​ ( 𝔬 ℓ ) \mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}) admits real-valued irreducible characters that are not realizable over ℝ \mathbb{R} . These results extend the parallel known phenomena for the finite groups GL n ​ ( 𝔽 q ) \mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}) and GU n ​ ( 𝔽 q ) \mathrm{GU}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}) .

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbf{F}
be a non-archimedean local field with ring of integers
\mathfrak{o}
, maximal ideal
\mathfrak{p}
, and residue field
\mathsf{k}
of odd characteristic
p
and cardinality
q
. Fix a uniformizer
\pi
of
\mathfrak{o}
. For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}=\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p}^{\ell}
.

Let
\mathrm{G}
be a classical group scheme defined over
\mathfrak{o}
. The group
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o})
of
\mathfrak{o}
-points is a maximal compact subgroup of
\mathrm{G}(\mathbf{F})
and plays a fundamental role in the representation theory of
\mathrm{G}(\mathbf{F})
. Since
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o})
is profinite, every continuous finite-dimensional complex irreducible representation of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o})
factors through a finite quotient
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
for some
\ell
. Consequently, the study of continuous representations of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o})
reduces to the study of finite-dimensional representations of the finite groups
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
.

The representation theory of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
has been studied extensively in recent years; see, for example,
[
12
,
5
,
13
]
and the references therein. Most of this work focuses on complex representations. In contrast, comparatively little is known about representations over other fields. The purpose of this article is to initiate a systematic study of real representations of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
.

We restrict our attention to the groups
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
and
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
. Our first main result is a complete description of the real-valued irreducible complex characters of these groups; see
Theorem 4.5
. An irreducible complex representation of a finite group that admits a realization over
\mathbb{R}
is called orthogonal (also called real in literature), and its character is called orthogonal. Every orthogonal character is real-valued, but the converse does not hold in general. It is well known that an irreducible character of a finite group is orthogonal if and only if its Frobenius–Schur indicator is equal to
1
; see
[
17
, Chapter 23]
or
[
16
, Chapter 4]
.
In
Theorem 5.2
, we consider whether every real-valued character of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is orthogonal. We prove that every real-valued irreducible complex character of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is orthogonal for
\ell\geq 1
. In contrast, this property fails for
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
. These results are parallel to the well-known situation for the finite groups
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
\mathrm{GU}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
; see
[
23
, Theorem 4]
and
[
6
, p. 415]
.

Our approach relies on the known construction of irreducible representations of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
as well on the analysis of real and strongly real classes. An element
g
of a group
G
is called real if it is conjugate to its inverse in
G
, and strongly real if it is conjugate to its inverse by an involution. Equivalently,
g
is strongly real if and only if it can be written as a product of two involutions. A conjugacy class is called real (respectively, strongly real) if it contains a real (respectively, strongly real) element.

The study of real and strongly real elements has a long history; see
[
21
]
for a comprehensive survey. For a finite group, the number of real classes equals the number of real-valued irreducible complex characters
[
15
, Theorem 18.6]
. In the finite field case, Gow
[
10
]
classified the real classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and showed that their number coincides with that of
\mathrm{GU}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
; see also
[
8
, Theorem 3.8]
. However, unlike
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, not every real class of
\mathrm{GU}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is strongly real. Strongly real classes in
\mathrm{GU}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
were enumerated for odd
q
in
[
7
]
.
We prove analogous results for
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
and
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
when
\ell\geq 2
, including explicit descriptions and enumerations of real and strongly real classes; see
Theorem 3.2
,
Theorem 3.5
,
3.7
, and also
Table 1
.

The relationship between strongly real elements and orthogonal characters has been studied extensively. For instance,
a conjecture of Tiep, proved by Vinroot
[
25
]
, states that for finite simple groups every irreducible complex character is orthogonal if and only if every element is strongly real.
This equivalence fails for finite groups in general; see
[
18
]
.
Furthermore, in contrast to
\mathrm{GU}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, it is known that for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
every real element is strongly real and every real character is orthogonal; see
[
21
,
23
,
6
]
.
Our results show that the same dichotomy holds for
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
and
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
when
\ell\geq 2
; see
Section 5
.

\mathfrak{p}

\pi

\mathfrak{O}

We denote by

the non-trivial Galois automorphism of

. The image of

Let

1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{O}_{\ell})
denote the permutation matrix corresponding to the longest Weyl element. Consider the involution on
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathfrak{O}_{\ell})
defined by

(2.1)

(a_{i,j})^{\star}\coloneqq W(a_{j,i}^{\circ})W^{-1},

and its associated Hermitian form on
\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{2}
given by:

\langle(u_{1},u_{2}),(v_{1},v_{2})\rangle\coloneqq v_{1}^{\circ}u_{2}+v_{2}^{\circ}u_{1}.

For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
the unitary group with respect to
\star
and its Lie algebra of anti-Hermitian matrices are given by

\begin{split}\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})&\coloneqq\left\{A\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{O}_{\ell})\mid A^{\star}A=\mathrm{I}_{2}\right\},\\
\gu_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})&\coloneqq\left\{A\in\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathfrak{O}_{\ell})\mid A+A^{\star}=\mathrm{O}_{2}\right\}.\end{split}

Throughout this paper we consider
\mathrm{GL}_{2}
and
\mathrm{GU}_{2}
as
\mathfrak{o}
-group schemes, where the
R
-points of the latter are the fixed points of
A\mapsto(A^{\star})^{-1}
for every
\mathfrak{o}
-algebra
R
and
A\in\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(R)
. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the lie algebra scheme of
\mathrm{G}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is either
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}
or
\gu_{2}
as
\mathfrak{o}
-Lie algebra schemes, the latter being the fixed points of
A\mapsto-A^{\star}
. The adjoint action of a group on its Lie algebra will be denoted by
\mathrm{Ad}
.
Recall
\mathfrak{o}_{1}=\mathbb{F}_{q}.

We use
R_{\ell}
as a common notation to denote
\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}
for
\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{GL}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}
for
\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{GU}_{2}
. For the uniformity in the proofs, we use
\epsilon
to denote
1
for
\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{GL}_{2}
and
\varepsilon
for
\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{GU}_{2}.
Similarly,
\partial_{\mathrm{G}}
is used to denote
-1
for
\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{GL}_{2}
and
1
for
\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{GU}_{2}
.

For
i\leq\ell
, let
\rho_{\ell,i}:\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}\rightarrow\mathfrak{o}_{i}
be the natural projection maps. The corresponding natural projection maps
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})\rightarrow\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{i})
are also denoted by
\rho_{\ell,i}
. For any matrix

\rho_{\ell,1}(A)

Let

be the
i
-th congruence subgroups of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}).
For
i\geq\ell/2,
the group
\mathrm{K}^{i}
is isomorphic to the abelian additive subgroup
\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell-i})
of
M_{2}(R_{\ell-i}).
Let
\psi:R_{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be a fixed primitive one dimensional representation of
R_{\ell}
. For

we assume that
\psi
satisfies
\psi(x+\epsilon y)=\psi^{\prime}(x)\psi^{\prime}(y)
for some primitive one dimensional representation

of
\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}
. Therefore,
\pi^{\ell-1}\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}\not\subseteq\ker(\psi)
by our choice of

.

For any
i\leq\ell/2
and
A=[a_{st}]\in\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{o}_{i}),
we will consider lifts
\tilde{A}=[\widetilde{a_{st}}]\in\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
of

such that
\rho_{\ell,i}(\tilde{A})=A
with

\widetilde{a_{st}}=0

. In this case, we say

is a

of

.
For any

and

, define
\psi_{A}:\mathrm{I}+\pi^{\ell-i}\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
by

\psi_{A}(\mathrm{I}+\pi^{\ell-i}B)\coloneqq\psi(\pi^{\ell-i}\bm{tr}(\tilde{A}B)),

for any lift
\tilde{A}
of
A
and

Then
\psi_{A}
is a well defined one dimensional representation of

Further, the following duality for abelian groups

and

.

(2.3)

\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell-i})\cong\widehat{\mathrm{K}^{i}}\,\,;A\mapsto\psi_{A}\,\,\mathrm{where},\,\,\psi_{A}(\mathrm{I}+\pi^{i}B)=\psi(\pi^{i}\bm{tr}(\tilde{A}B))\,\,\forall\,\,\mathrm{I}+\pi^{i}B\in\mathrm{K}^{i}.

We use
[
14
]
and
[
19
]

Lemma 2.1

An exhaustive list of

∎

\underset{h\in\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}}\backslash\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})/(\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}})^{g}}{\oplus}{\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}}\otimes({\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}})^{hg}=\mathbf{1}\text{ on }{\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}}\cap(\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}})^{gh}}

is equivalent to
{\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}}\otimes{\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}}^{g}=\mathbf{1}\text{ on }C_{\tilde{A}}
, where
g=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
for
\mathfrak{t}(A)=\mathbf{ss}
and
g=\begin{pmatrix}z&0\\
0&-z\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
for
\mathfrak{t}(A)=\mathbf{sns}
.

Proof.

For proving this lemma we consider
\mathfrak{t}(A)=\mathbf{ss}
and
\mathfrak{t}(A)=\mathbf{sns}
separately.
First we consider the split semisimple case. In this case,
\tilde{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\alpha}&0\\
0&-\tilde{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}
and
g=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. First we claim that
\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}}\backslash S_{A}/(\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}})^{g}=\mathrm{I}.
For
\begin{pmatrix}a&\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}b\\
\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}c&d\end{pmatrix}\in S_{A}
, take
X=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}c&(d-\pi^{2{\ell_{1}}}cba^{-1})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}}
and
Y=\begin{pmatrix}1&\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}a^{-1}b\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in(\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}})^{g}.
Then by direct computations we get
XY=\begin{pmatrix}a&\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}b\\
\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}c&d\end{pmatrix}
which proves our first claim. Next we claim that
\psi^{\prime}_{\tilde{A}}\otimes(\psi^{\prime}_{\tilde{A}})^{g}=\bf{1}
on
\mathrm{N}\cap\mathrm{N}^{g}
. Notice that
\psi^{\prime}_{\tilde{A}}=\psi_{\tilde{A}}
on
\mathrm{N}
. Take
\begin{pmatrix}1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}a&\pi^{{\ell_{2}}}b\\
\pi^{{\ell_{2}}}c&1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{N}\cap\mathrm{N}^{g}.
By direct calculations using
Equation 2.4
we get
(\psi_{\tilde{A}}\otimes(\psi_{\tilde{A}})^{g})(X)=1.
These two claims and the fact that
\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}}\cap\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}}^{g}=(\mathrm{N}\cap\mathrm{N}^{g})C_{\tilde{A}},
prove the lemma for this case.

Now we consider the split non-semisimple case. In this case,
\tilde{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\epsilon\pi\tilde{\beta}\\
\epsilon&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
and
g=\begin{pmatrix}z&0\\
0&-z\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}).
First notice that
(\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}})^{g}=\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}}.
The set of double coset representatives of
\mathrm{N}C_{\breve{A}}\backslash S_{A}/\mathrm{N}C_{\breve{A}}
is given by

\Gamma=\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&\epsilon\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}w\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},w\in\mathfrak{o}_{1}^{\times}\}.

The given equation becomes

(2.6)

\underset{h\in\Gamma}{\oplus}{\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}}\otimes({\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}})^{hg}=\mathbf{1}\text{ on }{\mathrm{N}C_{\tilde{A}}}

Now we claim that
{\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}}\otimes({\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}})^{hg}=\mathbf{1}\text{ on }{\mathrm{N}}
if and only if
h=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.
Observe that if this claim holds then using
Equation 2.6
, the lemma follows. We first note that the claim is true for
h=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.
Now we proceed to prove the other side. Take
h=\begin{pmatrix}1&\epsilon\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}w\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma.
By using
\psi^{\prime}_{\tilde{A}}=\psi_{\tilde{A}}
on
\mathrm{N}
and
Equation 2.4
we get

{\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}}\otimes({\psi_{\tilde{A}}^{\prime}})^{hg}\begin{pmatrix}1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}a&\pi^{{\ell_{2}}}b\\
\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}c&1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}d\end{pmatrix}=\psi(\pi^{2{\ell_{1}}}\epsilon^{2}w(d-a))

For
\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{GL}_{2},
using
\pi^{\ell-1}\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}\not\subseteq\ker(\psi)
we get that
\psi(\pi^{2{\ell_{1}}}\epsilon^{2}w(d-a))=1
for all
\begin{pmatrix}1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}a&\pi^{{\ell_{2}}}b\\
\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}c&1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{N}
if and only if
w=0\mod(\pi).
For
\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{GU}_{2},
by the defining conditions of
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
,
\begin{pmatrix}1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}a&\pi^{{\ell_{2}}}b\\
\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}c&1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{N}
implies
(1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}a)(1+\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}d^{\circ})=1.
Using this we get
\pi^{2{\ell_{1}}}(d-a)=\pi^{2{\ell_{1}}}(d+d^{\circ}).
Substituting this in
\psi(\pi^{2{\ell_{1}}}\epsilon^{2}w(d-a))
we get
\psi(\pi^{2{\ell_{1}}}\epsilon^{2}w(d+d^{\circ})).
Since
\epsilon^{2}w(d+d^{\circ})\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell},
using
\pi^{\ell-1}\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}\not\subseteq\ker(\psi)
, we get
w=0\mod(\pi).
This proves the claim.
∎

3.
Real and strongly real classes of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})

In this section, we describe and enumerate the real and strongly real classes in
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
. We also show that every real element of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is strongly real.

3.1.
Real
classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})

The following result regarding the conjugacy classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
was proved in
[
2
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Any
A\in M_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is similar to a unique matrix of the form

(3.1)

\displaystyle M(d,i,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dI+\pi^{i}\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha\\
1&\beta\end{pmatrix},

where
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell\},

d=\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}d_{k}\pi^{k}
with
d_{i}\in\mathfrak{o}_{1},
and
\alpha,\beta
are determined modulo
(\pi^{\ell-i}).

Further
M(d,i,\alpha,\beta)\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
if and only if
d_{0}\in\mathfrak{o}_{1}^{\times}
for
i\neq 0
and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times}
for
i=0
.

We use this result to describe the real and strongly real elements of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}).
By definition,
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is real if and only
A
is conjugate to
A^{-1}.
Therefore
A
is real implies that
\det(A)=\det(A^{-1})
and
\mathrm{tr}(A)=\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1}).
In our next result, we prove that the converse also holds for
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

A
is real.

(2)

A
is strongly real.

(3)

\det(A)\in\{\pm 1\}
and
\mathrm{tr}(A)=\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1})
.

(4)

A
is conjugate to one of the following matrices:

(a)

M(0,0,1,0)
;

(b)

M(0,0,-1,\beta)
with
\beta\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}
;

(c)

M(1,i,\alpha,\pi^{i}\alpha)
for
1\leq i\leq\ell
and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}
;

(d)

M(-1,i,\alpha,-\pi^{i}\alpha)
for
1\leq i\leq\ell
and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}
.

Proof.

The implication (1)
\implies
(3) is immediate, and (2)
\implies
(1) follows directly from the definitions.
The equivalence (3)
\iff
(4) follows from the parametrization in
Equation 3.1
, since the determinant and trace conditions precisely describe the families listed in (4). Thus, it remains to show that every matrix appearing in (4) is strongly real.

For
M(d,i,\alpha,\beta)
with
i=0
among the matrices in (4), the involution
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
satisfies

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,M(0,0,\alpha,\beta)\,\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=M(0,0,\alpha,\beta)^{-1}.

For
M(d,i,\alpha,\beta)
with
i>0
among the matrices in (4), the involution
\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
satisfies

\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,M(d,i,\alpha,\beta)\,\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=M(d,i,\alpha,\beta)^{-1}.

Hence, every matrix in (4) is strongly real, completing the proof.
∎

Observe that
M(d,i,\alpha,\beta)
is regular (non-regular) if and only if
i=0\,(1\leq i\leq\ell)
. As a direct consequence of
Theorem 3.2
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

(1)

Total number of real regular and real non-regular classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is
q^{\ell}+1
and
2\sum_{0}^{\ell-1}q^{i}.

(2)

The total number of real (strongly real) classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is
1+q^{\ell}+2\sum_{0}^{\ell-1}q^{i}.

3.2.
Real classes in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})

The following result regarding the conjugacy classes of
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
follows from
[
4
, Section 4.K]
.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
4
, p. 69]
)
.

Every element of
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is conjugate to one of the following matrices:

(A)

\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&x\end{pmatrix}
, where
xx^{\circ}=1
.

(B)

\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&{{x}^{-1}}^{\circ}\end{pmatrix}
, where
x{x}^{\circ}\neq 1
.

(C)

\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
y&x\end{pmatrix}
, where
y\neq 0
such that

xx^{\circ}+yy^{\circ}=1\text{ and }xy^{\circ}+x^{\circ}y=0.

(D)

\begin{pmatrix}x&\pi^{i+1}\beta y\\
\pi^{i}y&x\end{pmatrix}
, where
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
,
\beta\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell-i-1}
and
x,y
are units in
\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}
such that

xx^{\circ}+\pi^{2i+1}\beta yy^{\circ}=1\text{ and }\pi^{i}(xy^{\circ}+x^{\circ}y)=0.

Our next result characterizes the real elements of
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}).

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

A
is real.

(2)

\det(A)\in\{\pm 1\}
and
\mathrm{tr}(A)=\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1}).

(3)

Up to conjugacy,
A
has one of the following form:

(a)

\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&x\end{pmatrix}
, where
x\in\{\pm 1\}
.

(b)

\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&x^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
, where
x\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times}
such that
x^{2}\neq 1
.

(c)

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
or
\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
y&x\end{pmatrix}
,
where
y\neq 0
such that

xx^{\circ}+yy^{\circ}=1
,
xy^{\circ}+x^{\circ}y=0,
and
x^{2}-y^{2}=1
.

(d)

\begin{pmatrix}x&\pi^{i+1}\beta y\\
\pi^{i}y&x\end{pmatrix}
, where
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
,
\beta\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell-i-1}
,
x\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times}
, and
y\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
such that

x^{2}+\pi^{2i+1}\beta yy^{\circ}=1=x^{2}-\pi^{2i+1}\beta y^{2}\text{ and }\pi^{i}(xy^{\circ}+x^{\circ}y)=0.

Proof.

Here, (1)
\implies
(2) follows from the definition of real elements. We now prove (2)
\implies
(1). For this, we identify the conjugacy classes satisfying (2) from
Theorem 3.4
and show that each also satisfies (1).

(A)

Consider
A=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&x\end{pmatrix}
with
xx^{\circ}=1
satisfying (2). Then
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1})
implies
x=\pm 1
. Thus
A
satisfies (1).

(B)

Consider
A=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&{x^{-1}}^{\circ}\end{pmatrix}
with
x{x}^{\circ}\neq 1
satisfying (2). Then
\det(A)=x{x^{-1}}^{\circ}\in\{\pm 1\}
, so
x=x^{\circ}
or
x=-x^{\circ}
. From
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1})
, we obtain

(xx^{\circ}+1)(x-x^{\circ})=0.

The case
x=-x^{\circ}
yields
x^{2}=1
, contradicting
xx^{\circ}\neq 1
. Hence
x=x^{\circ}
, so

A=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&x^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad x\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times},\ x^{2}\neq 1.

The involution
\tau=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
satisfies
\tau A\tau^{-1}=A^{-1}
. Thus
A
satisfies (1).

(C)

Consider
A=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
y&x\end{pmatrix}
with
y\neq 0
,
xx^{\circ}+yy^{\circ}=1
, and
xy^{\circ}+x^{\circ}y=0
, satisfying (2). Then
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1})
yields two cases:

Case 1:
\det(A)=-1
.
Here
x=0
, so
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&y\\
y&0\end{pmatrix}
with
y\in\{\pm 1\}
. Thus
A
is conjugate in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
to the involution
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, hence satisfies (1).

Case 2:
\det(A)=1
.
Then
A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}x&-y\\
-y&x\end{pmatrix}
. For any
u+v\epsilon\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}
with
u,v\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}
such that
(u+v\epsilon){(u+v\epsilon)}^{\circ}=-1
, we have

\begin{pmatrix}v\epsilon&u\\
-u&-v\epsilon\end{pmatrix}A\begin{pmatrix}v\epsilon&u\\
-u&-v\epsilon\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=A^{-1}.

Such elements exist by surjectivity of the norm map. Thus
A
satisfies (1).

(D)

Consider
A=\begin{pmatrix}x&\pi^{i+1}\beta y\\
\pi^{i}y&x\end{pmatrix}
with
x,y
units in
\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}
satisfying (2). Then
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1})
implies
\det(A)=1
. As in Case C,
\begin{pmatrix}\omega&0\\
0&-\omega\end{pmatrix}A\begin{pmatrix}\omega&0\\
0&-\omega\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=A^{-1}
for any
\omega\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
with
\omega{\omega}^{\circ}=-1
. Thus
A
satisfies (1).

Therefore, (2)
\implies
(1). The above analysis of cases (A)–(D) also establishes the equivalence of (2) and (3). This completes the proof.
∎

Unlike
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
, not every real element in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is strongly real. The following result characterizes strongly real elements in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
and demonstrates that case (d) of
Theorem 3.5
contains no strongly real elements.

Theorem 3.6
.

A real element in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is strongly real if and only if it is conjugate to one of the elements described in parts (a)–(c) of
Theorem 3.5
.

Proof.

Since reality is preserved under conjugacy, for the sufficiency part we consider the involutions constructed in the proof of
Theorem 3.5
, which conjugate the elements described in parts (a)–(c) of
Theorem 3.5
to their inverses.

For the necessity part, it suffices to show that if
A
is a real element in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
conjugate to the matrices described in part (d) of
Theorem 3.5
, then
A
is not strongly real. We proceed as follows.

Consider

A=\begin{pmatrix}x&\pi^{i+1}\beta y\\
\pi^{i}y&x\end{pmatrix},

where
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
,
\beta\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell-i-1}
,
x\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
, and
y\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
such that

x^{2}+\pi^{2i+1}\beta yy^{\circ}=1=x^{2}-\pi^{2i+1}\beta y^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi^{i}(xy^{\circ}+{x}^{\circ}y)=0.

Suppose there exists

g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
such that
g^{2}=\mathrm{I}_{2}\text{ and }gAg^{-1}=A^{-1}.
Then we have

g^{2}=\mathrm{I}_{2}\iff a^{2}+bc=1,\quad a^{2}=d^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad b(a+d)=0=c(a+d)

and

gA=A^{-1}g\iff\pi^{i}y(a+d)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\pi^{i}y(b+\pi\beta c)=0.

Let
a+d=\pi^{k}u
and
b+\pi\beta c=\pi^{m}v
, where
0\leq k,m\leq\ell
and
u,v\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
. Depending on the values of
k
and
m
, we consider the following cases:

Case 1:

k=0
or
m=0
. From
gA=A^{-1}g
, we obtain
\pi^{i}y=0
. This implies that
A
is a scalar matrix, a contradiction.

Case 2:

1\leq k\leq\ell-1
. From
b(a+d)=0=c(a+d)
, we have
b=\pi^{\ell-k}b^{\prime}
and
c=\pi^{\ell-k}c^{\prime}
, where
b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
. Since
1\leq k
and
1\leq\ell-k
, the equations
a^{2}+bc=1
and
a{d}^{\circ}+c{b}^{\circ}=1
yield

a^{2}=1\pmod{\pi}\quad\text{and}\quad-a{a}^{\circ}=1\pmod{\pi}.

This implies
a=-{a}^{\circ}\pmod{\pi}
, so
a={\epsilon}a^{\prime}
for some
a^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times}
. Since
a^{2}=1\pmod{\pi}
, we deduce that
\epsilon^{2}\mod(\pi)
has a square root. Recall that the residue field is of odd characteristic. Therefore
\epsilon^{2}\in(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times})^{2}
if and only if
\rho_{\ell,1}({{\epsilon}^{2}})\in(\mathfrak{o}_{1}^{\times})^{2}.
Hence we get
\epsilon^{2}\in(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times})^{2}
, a contradiction.

Case 3:

k=\ell
and
1\leq m\leq\ell
. Then
a=-d
and
b=\pi^{m}v-\pi\beta c
, where
v\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
. The equations
a^{2}+bc=1
and
a{d}^{\circ}+c{b}^{\circ}=1
lead to a contradiction as in Case 2.

Therefore,
A
is not strongly real, and the proof is complete.
∎

The following result provides a count of real and strongly real classes in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
.

Proposition 3.7
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

The numbers of real regular and real non-regular classes in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})

coincide with those in
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
; hence, the two groups have the
same total number of real classes which is
1+q^{\ell}+2\sum_{0}^{\ell-1}q^{i}.

(2)

In
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
, the number of strongly real classes equals the number
of real regular classes.

(3)

The numbers of real regular and real non-regular classes in
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
are
given by

q^{\ell}+1\quad\text{and}\quad 2\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}q^{i},

respectively.

Proof.

We consider the following cases to count the total numbers of real regular and real non-regular classes in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
, using the characterization of real classes obtained in
Theorem 3.5
.

Case (i):
Let
A=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&x\end{pmatrix}
, where

. Both of these real classes are non-regular.

Case (ii):
Let
A=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&x^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
, where
x\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times}
such that
x^{2}\neq 1
. The total number of distinct real classes in this case is

\frac{|\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times}|-2}{2}=\frac{q^{\ell}-q^{\ell-1}}{2}-1.

Moreover,
A
is regular if and only if
x^{2}-1
is a unit. Therefore, the total number of distinct real regular and real non-regular classes is given by

\frac{(q-3)q^{\ell-1}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad q^{\ell-1}-1,\quad\text{respectively.}

Case (iii):
Let
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
or
A=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
y&x\end{pmatrix}
, where
y\neq 0
satisfies
xx^{\circ}+yy^{\circ}=1
,
xy^{\circ}+x^{\circ}y=0
, and
x^{2}-y^{2}=1
. For

y&x\end{pmatrix}

xx^{\circ}+yy^{\circ}=1
, and
xy^{\circ}+x^{\circ}y=0
, we get that
x+y
and
x-y
are eigenvalues of
A
with norm
1
. This implies

x=a\text{ and }y=b\epsilon\text{ such that }a^{2}-b^{2}\epsilon^{2}=1,

where

and
b\neq 0
. Let the norm map
\mathcal{N}:\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}^{\times}

for all

. Then
\mathcal{N}
is surjective and its kernel has order
q^{\ell}+q^{\ell-1}
. Therefore, the total number of matrices
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\epsilon\\
b\epsilon&a\end{pmatrix}
with
a,b\in\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}
and

such that

is
q^{\ell}+q^{\ell-1}-2
.

Note that
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
is a regular element with determinant
-1
, and
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\epsilon\\
b\epsilon&a\end{pmatrix}

b

q^{\ell-1}-1

.

b\epsilon&a\end{pmatrix}

is

, while the number with

(q^{\ell}+q^{\ell-1}-2)-2\bigl(q^{\ell-1}-1\bigr)=q^{\ell}-q^{\ell-1}=(q-1)q^{\ell-1}.

Since the norm map
\mathcal{N}
is surjective, we can choose

. Using

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

\frac{(q-1)q^{\ell-1}}{2}+1,\quad q^{\ell-1}-1,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{q^{\ell}+q^{\ell-1}-2}{2}+1,

respectively.

Real and orthogonal characters of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})

Recall that an irreducible representation
\sigma
of a finite group
G
has a real character if and only if
\sigma
is self-dual. It is well known that a representation
\sigma
of
G
is self-dual if and only if
\langle\sigma\otimes\sigma,\mathbf{1}_{G}\rangle\neq 0
. If
\sigma
is irreducible, then
\langle\sigma\otimes\sigma,\mathbf{1}_{G}\rangle\leq 1
. Consequently, an irreducible representation
\sigma
has a real character if and only if
\langle\sigma\otimes\sigma,\mathbf{1}_{G}\rangle=1
. We shall use this criterion to determine the real characters of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
.

Our objective is to characterize all regular and non-regular irreducible representations
\sigma
of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
satisfying
\langle\sigma\otimes\sigma,\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})}\rangle=1
. The next lemma describes the real non-regular characters of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

A non-regular representation
\sigma\in\irr(\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}))
has real character if and only if there exists
\sigma^{\prime}\in\Irr(\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell-1}))
with real character such that
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}\circ\rho_{\ell,\ell-1}
.

Proof.

It is clear that if there exists
\sigma^{\prime}\in\Irr(\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell-1}))
with real character such that
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}\circ\rho_{\ell,\ell-1}
then
\sigma
has real character. We prove the converse. Assume that
\sigma\in\Irr(\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}))
is a non-regular representation with real character. Then
\sigma_{|_{\mathrm{K}^{\ell-1}}}
must be a real character of
\mathrm{K}^{\ell-1}
. By definition of non-regular representations we have
\sigma_{|_{\mathrm{K}^{\ell-1}}}=\dim{(\sigma)}\psi_{A}
for a scalar matrix
A\in\mathfrak{g}(\mathfrak{o}_{1})
. Hence
\psi_{A}
has real character. Since
\mathrm{K}^{\ell-1}
is an odd order group, only real character of
\mathrm{K}^{\ell-1}
is trivial. This implies
\psi_{A}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{K}^{\ell-1}}
. Hence
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}\circ\rho_{\ell,\ell-1}
for some
\sigma^{\prime}\in\Irr(\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell-1}))
.
∎

Hence, it boils down to investigate real regular characters of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
. For any regular

Then

\displaystyle=

{\phi}_{\mid_{\mathrm{K}^{{\ell_{2}}}}}=q^{{\ell_{2}}-{\ell_{1}}}\psi_{A}

{\langle\phi\otimes\phi^{g},\mathbf{1}}\rangle_{\mathrm{K}^{{\ell_{2}}}}\neq 0\,\,\mathrm{if\,\,and\,\,only\,\,if}\,\,{\langle\psi_{A+gAg^{-1}},\mathbf{1}\rangle}_{\mathrm{K}^{{\ell_{2}}}}\neq 0.

we obtain
A+gAg^{-1}=0\mod(\pi^{{\ell_{1}}}).
This proves (T1).

To prove (T2), we choose the Ad orbit representatives

.

5.
Orthogonal characters

In this section, for
\ell\geq 2
, we prove that every real character of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is orthogonal, and that there exists a real character of
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
that is not orthogonal.

For a representation
\phi
of a group
G
, let
\upsilon(\phi)
denotes its Frobenius-Schur indicator. Recall that an irreducible representation
\phi
is self-dual and orthogonal if and only if
\upsilon(\phi)
is
\pm 1
and
1
, respectively. We shall use the following well known result to determine if every real character is orthogonal:

(5.1)

\sum_{\phi\in\Irr(G)}\upsilon(\phi)\dim(\phi)=|\{g\in G\mid g^{2}=e\}|.

Recall that an element
g\in\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is called an involution if
g^{2}=e
. We first determine the number of involutions in
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}).

Lemma 5.1
.

The number of involutions in
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is
(q-\partial_{\mathrm{G}})q^{2\ell-1}+2
.

Proof.

The notion of involution is a conjugacy class invariant, hence to determine the involutions in
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}),
we use the conjugacy class representatives of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
as given in
Theorem 3.1
and
Theorem 3.4
.

By
Theorem 3.1
, any element of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is conjugate to a matrix
M(d,i,\alpha,\beta),
with certain conditions on
d,i,\alpha,\beta.
The matrix
M(d,i,\alpha,\beta)
is an involution if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied:

(5.2)

\displaystyle 2\pi^{i}d+\pi^{2i}\beta=0,

(5.3)

\displaystyle d^{2}+\pi^{2i}\alpha=1.

For
i=0,
the above conditions give
d=\frac{-\beta}{2}
and
\alpha=1-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}.
This gives a class representative
\begin{pmatrix}\frac{-\beta}{2}&1-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\\
1&\frac{\beta}{2}\end{pmatrix}
and it represents the conjugacy class
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
for all
\beta.
The total number of involutions of this class type are
\frac{|\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})|}{|\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})}(g)|}=\frac{q^{4\ell-3}(q-1)(q^{2}-1)}{(q-1)^{2}q^{2\ell-2}}=(q+1)q^{2\ell-1}
.
For
i\geq 1,
it is easy to see from
Equation 5.2
that
i=\ell.
Using this in
d^{2}+\pi^{2i}\alpha=1
gives
d=\pm 1.
Hence we get two distinct conjugacy class representatives
\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
and the total number of involutions of this class type is 2. From the above, we get
(q+1)q^{2\ell-1}+2
number of involutions in
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}).

Next we consider the conjugacy class representatives of the group
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
as given in
Theorem 3.4
.

(1)

g=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
: In this case
g^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
implies
x=\pm 1
. Hence total number of involutions from these type of conjugacy classes is
2.

(2)

g=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&y\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
,
x\neq y
:
g^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
implies
x^{2}=y^{2}=1
. The condition
x\neq y
and
x^{2}=y^{2}=1
does not give any involution in this class type.

(3)

g=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
y&x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
,
y\neq 0
: In this case,
g^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
if and only if
xy=0
and
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
. Since
y\neq 0
, using
xy=0
we get either
y\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
and
x=0
or
y\in\pi\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}
and
x\in\pi\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}
. The case
y\in\pi\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}
and
x\in\pi\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}
is not possible because
\det(g)\neq 0\mod(\pi)
. Hence, we must have
x=0
. Therefore
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
gives
y^{2}=1
. The corresponding unique class representative is
g=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. This class has total
\frac{|\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})|}{|\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})}(g)|}=\frac{q^{4\ell-3}(q-1)(q+1)^{2}}{(q+1)^{2}q^{2\ell-2}}=(q-1)q^{2\ell-1}
involutions.

(4)

g=\begin{pmatrix}x&\pi^{i+1}\beta y\\
\pi^{i}y&x\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}),y\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
: In this case,
g^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
gives
\pi^{i+1}xy=0
. Since
y\in\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}^{\times}
, we get
x\in\pi\mathfrak{O}_{\ell}
, which is not possible since
\det(g)\neq 0\mod(\pi)
. Hence this class type does not give any involution.

From the above discussion, it is clear that the total number of involutions in
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is
(q-1)q^{2\ell-1}+2
. This proves the Lemma.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\ell\geq 1
. The following hold:

(1)

Any irreducible complex representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
with real-valued character is orthogonal.

(2)

There exists a self-dual representation of
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
that is not orthogonal. For
\ell\geq 2
, there exists a regular symplectic representaton.

Proof.

To prove (1), we show that the total sum of the dimensions of the self-dual representations of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is the same as the number of involutions in
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell}).
The result then follows from
Equation 5.1
.

We proceed by induction. It is a well known result for
\ell=1
, see
[
9
]
and
[
20
]
.
We assume it to be true for
k\leq\ell-1
and we prove for
k=\ell
. We partition the self-dual representations of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{k})
into four components, namely split semisimple (
\Sigma^{\mathbf{ss}}
), split non-semisimple (
\Sigma^{\mathbf{sns}}
), cuspidal (
\Sigma^{\mathbf{cus}}
) and non-regular (
\Sigma^{\mathbf{nreg}}
) representations.
As mentioned earlier, the non-regular self-dual representations of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
are the same as the self dual representations of
\mathrm{G}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell-1}).
Hence by induction,

\sum_{\phi\in\Sigma^{\mathbf{nreg}}}\dim(\phi)=(q+1)q^{2\ell-3}+2.

The dimension of any
\mathbf{ss}
,
\mathbf{sns}
, and
\mathbf{cus}
representations of

\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
are

(q+1)q^{\ell-1}
,
(q^{2}-1)q^{\ell-2}
, and
(q-1)q^{\ell-1}
respectively. By
Theorem 4.5
, we have

|\Sigma^{\mathbf{ss}}|=\frac{1}{2}(q^{2}-1)q^{\ell-2}
,
|\Sigma^{\mathbf{sns}}|=2q^{\ell-1}
and
|\Sigma^{\mathbf{cus}}|=\frac{1}{2}(q-1)^{2}q^{\ell-2}
. Then it is easy to see that the sum of dimensions of the all self dual representations of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
is
(q+1)q^{2\ell-1}+2
, and that is same as the number of involutions of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
by
5.1
. This proves (1).

For (2), let
a_{\ell}
be the sum of the degree of the characters of
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
with Frobenius Schur indicator
1
and
b_{\ell}
be the sum of the degree of the characters of
\mathrm{GU}_{2}(\mathfrak{o}_{\ell})
with Frobenius Schur indicator
-1
