REAP1/AtSWAP70 integrates RAB5 and ROP signalling during sexual reproduction
Authors: Emi Ito, Natalia Julia Rzepecka, Yoko Ito, Tomoko Hirano, Kazuo Ebine, Yoshihisa Oda, Masa H Sato, Akihiko Nakano, Tomohiro Uemura, Takashi Ueda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall GTPases of the Ras superfamily are critical regulators of diverse cellular processes. While crosstalk between their signalling pathways has been documented in animals, similar mechanisms remain unexplored in plants, where small GTPases have undergone unique evolutionary diversification. Here we identify REAP1/AtSWAP70 as an effector in Arabidopsis thaliana that interacts with the active forms of both canonical RAB5 and the plant-specific RAB5, ARA6. Remarkably, REAP1 also binds to active ROP7, a plant-unique Rac-type GTPase, via its Dbl homology domain. REAP1 localizes to endosomes and facilitates ROP7 recruitment from the plasma membrane, a process dependent on RAB5 activity. Genetic analyses reveal that the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 signalling cascade is essential for gametogenesis, impacting pollen viability and development. This study provides evidence of functional crosstalk between RAB and ROP signalling in plants, unveiling a layer of regulatory complexity in plant GTPase signalling during plant reproduction.Subject terms: Protein trafficking in plants, Pollen

Main

Small GTPases of the Ras superfamily are critical regulators of diverse cellular processes, acting as molecular switches that alternate between active, GTP-bound states and inactive, GDP-bound states. This cycling is regulated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs), which promote GDP–GTP exchange, and GTPase-activating proteins, which stimulate GTP hydrolysis. Upon activation, conformational changes in the switch I and II regions enable small GTPases to interact with effector proteins, initiating specific downstream signalling pathways1.

The Ras superfamily comprises five major subfamilies: Ras, Rho/Rac/Cdc42, Rab, Sar/Arf and Ran2. Each subfamily specializes in distinct cellular functions: Ras regulates cell proliferation and differentiation, Rho controls cytoskeletal organization and polarity, Rab and Sar/Arf mediate membrane trafficking and Ran facilitates nuclear transport. Crosstalk between these subfamilies often supports complex cellular functions, as observed in animal systems. For instance, Ras and Rho pathways interact to remodel the actin cytoskeleton and enhance cell motility in oncogenic RAS-transformed fibroblasts3. Coordination between Rab and Rho pathways has been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, where disrupted cytoskeletal dynamics and membrane trafficking contribute to pathogenesis4. However, the integration of small GTPase pathways in plants remains largely unexplored, mainly due to unique features of plant small GTPases.

Although small GTPase families are broadly conserved across eukaryotes, plants exhibit unique adaptations. The Ras subfamily, critical for cell proliferation in animals, was lost in plants during evolution, while the Rab and Rho families underwent extensive diversification. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the Rab family includes 57 members, comparable to the 60 in humans. However, plant Rabs are classified into only 8 subgroups, differing from the 39 in humans5. Plants lack certain Rab subgroups found in animals, such as Rab4, which mediates endosome-to-plasma-membrane (PM) recycling, and Rab9, which regulates endosome-to-trans-Golgi-network transport6,7. Instead, plants have uniquely expanded Rab subgroups, such as RAB11, which includes 26 members in Arabidopsis but only 2 in humans8. The plant-specific RAB5 homologue, ARA6 (also known as RABF1), coexisting with canonical RAB5, ARA7 and RHA1 (also known as RABF2a and b), further exemplifies the unique diversification of the Rab group6,7,9,10. ARA6 has distinct structural features and regulates different endosomal trafficking pathways from canonical RAB5, partially counteracting canonical RAB5 (refs. 11–14).

The Rho family exhibits similar plant-specific adaptations. While animals and yeast possess three subfamilies (Rac, CDC42 and Rho), plants lack Rho and CDC42 homologues, instead harbouring a unique Rho of Plants (ROP) family, most similar to Rac15. Plant ROPs regulate diverse processes, including tip growth of pollen tubes and root hairs, immune responses, leaf pavement cell morphogenesis, auxin-dependent polar growth, xylem pit formation, cell division through cytoskeletal reorganization and plant–microbe interactions16–18. ROPs are activated by multiple GEF types, including PRONE-domain-containing ROPGEFs unique to plants, and a small number of DBL-type Rho GEFs, which include homologues of SWAP70 (refs. 15,16,18).

DBL-type Rho GEFs are defined by their Dbl homology (DH) domain, which confers GEF activity, and a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain, required for phosphoinositide binding19. In Oryza sativa, SWAP70 has been shown to activate OsRac1, contributing to immune responses against chitin elicitors20. In Arabidopsis, the homologue SWITCHING B-CELL COMPLEX ASSOCIATED (AtSWAP70) also contains a DH domain, but its specific role in ROP activation remains unclear.

Despite the biological importance of Rab and ROP GTPases, whether their functions are integrated in plants has remained unknown. In this study, we identified RAB5 EFFECTOR OF ARABIDOPSIS WITH PH DOMAIN 1 (REAP1)/AtSWAP70 as an effector that interacts with active canonical RAB5 and ARA6. We also discovered that REAP1 binds to the active form of ROP7, but not to the inactive form, and facilitates its recruitment to endosomes under the regulation of RAB5. Further investigation revealed that this RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade is involved in pollen development and gametophyte function. Our findings uncover a previously unrecognized mechanism integrating Rab and ROP signalling in plants, with potential implications for understanding plant-specific adaptations of small GTPase-mediated signalling.

Results

REAP1 interacts with both plant-specific and canonical RAB5s

To better understand the molecular functions of plant RAB5, we focused on PH-domain-containing proteins, as these proteins are often involved in membrane trafficking21–23. The Arabidopsis genome encodes 53 PH-domain-containing proteins21. To investigate whether any of these proteins interact with ARA6, we performed a yeast two-hybrid screen using the constitutively active form of ARA6 (ARA6(Q93L)) as bait. This screening identified AT2G30880 as an effector candidate for ARA6, which we named REAP1 (Fig. 1a).

To determine whether REAP1 interacts with ARA6 in a nucleotide-state-dependent manner, the interactions of REAP1 with the wild-type, constitutively active (ARA6(Q93L)) and dominant-negative (ARA6(S47N)) forms of ARA6 were tested. REAP1 interacted with the wild-type and constitutively active forms but not with ARA6(S47N) (Fig. 1a). We next tested whether REAP1 also interacts with ARA7, a canonical RAB5 in Arabidopsis. Similar to its interaction with ARA6, REAP1 interacted with the wild-type and constitutively active forms of ARA7 (ARA7(Q69L)) but not with the dominant-negative form (ARA7(S24N)) (Fig. 1a).

To confirm these interactions, we conducted pull-down assays. HA-tagged REAP1 expressed in yeast was pulled down by bacterially expressed and purified GST-tagged ARA6(Q93L) and ARA7(Q69L), but not by GST-tagged ARA6(S47N) or GST–ARA7(S24N) (Fig. 1b). These findings indicate that REAP1 interacts with both ARA6 and canonical RAB5 in their GTP-bound states and functions as an effector for these RAB5 proteins.

REAP1, an orthologue of OsSWAP70, contains PH and DH domains20. To determine which region of REAP1 mediates its interaction with RAB5, we performed yeast two-hybrid assays using truncated REAP1 constructs (Fig. 1c). The results showed that the active form of ARA6 interacted with the region containing the DH domain together with its flanking sequences, whereas the active form of ARA7 was able to interact with the DH domain alone (Fig. 1d). Expression of all constructs used in these assays was confirmed by immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, several constructs used in this study have been previously validated to support detectable protein–protein interactions in yeast two-hybrid assays13,24,25.

REAP1 binds to PI3P and PI(3,5)P2

Given the known role of the PH domain in phosphoinositide interactions, we investigated whether REAP1 binds to specific phosphoinositides. An overlay assay using bacterially expressed and purified GST–REAP1 revealed that REAP1 specifically binds to phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P) and phosphatidylinositol 3,5-bisphosphate (PI(3,5)P2) (Fig. 1e).

To assess the functional relevance of these interactions, we examined the effects of wortmannin (Wm), a phosphatidylinositol 3- and 4-kinase inhibitor26–30, and YM201636, an inhibitor of PI3P 5-kinase/FYVE-type zinc finger containing (PIKfyve), also known as Formation of aploid and binucleate cells 1 (FAB1)31,32, on the subcellular localization of REAP1. Transgenic plants expressing mGFP-tagged REAP1 under the control of its native regulatory elements (promoter, introns and terminator) in a reap1 mutant background were treated with these inhibitors. In mock-treated cells, mGFP–REAP1 localized to punctate organelles (Fig. 1f). Upon Wm treatment, mGFP–REAP1 was dispersed into the cytosol. In contrast, YM201636 treatment did not completely alter the subcellular distribution of GFP–REAP1 in root epidermal cells, although the number of puncta labelled by GFP–REAP1 was significantly reduced in cortex cells (Fig. 1g). The stronger effect of Wm than of YM201636 probably reflects their distinct effects on phosphoinositide pools: Wm depletes PI3P, the precursor of PI(3,5)P2 (ref. 23), whereas YM201636 inhibits PI(3,5)P2 production while leaving PI3P largely intact, thereby allowing partial retention of punctate localization. These findings suggest that PI3P and PI(3,5)P2 are important for regulating the subcellular localization of REAP1.

Both the DH and PH domains are required for punctate localization of REAP1

On the basis of these findings, we next investigated which domain or domains of REAP1 are required for its punctate localization. To this end, we generated GFP-tagged truncated constructs corresponding to the DH domain and the PH domain and examined their subcellular localization using transient expression in Nicotiana benthamiana leaf epidermal cells. While full-length REAP1 localized to punctate structures, GFP–#1 (PH domain) and GFP–#2 (DH domain) were both predominantly observed as diffuse signals in the cytosol, with no obvious punctate localization as supported by quantitative analysis (Fig. 1h). These results indicate that neither the DH domain nor the PH domain alone is sufficient to drive punctate localization. Together with our interaction and lipid-binding analyses, these findings suggest that both RAB5-binding activity mediated by the DH domain and phosphoinositide-binding activity mediated by the PH domain are required for the proper subcellular localization of REAP1.

REAP1 colocalizes with canonical RAB5 and ARA6 on endosomes

To determine the organelle where REAP1 localizes, we compared its subcellular distribution with several organelle markers. REAP1 exhibited strong colocalization with both RAB5 members and VPS9A, the common activator of plant-specific and canonical RAB5s25 (Fig. 2a). However, REAP1 did not colocalize with markers for the trans-Golgi network (VHAa1–mRFP)33 or trans-Golgi cisternae (ST–mRFP)34 (Supplementary Fig. 2a,b). These results suggest that REAP1 localizes to multivesicular endosomes (MVEs) together with RAB5s and their activator VPS9A.

To investigate whether REAP1-labelled MVEs are sensitive to brefeldin A (BFA), an ARF GEF inhibitor35,36, we treated plants co-expressing GFP–REAP1 and mRFP-tagged RAB5s with BFA. The ARA6 domain is known to form aggregates called BFA bodies upon BFA treatment, although MVEs themselves were not detected within the BFA compartment by electron microscopy37. Given that REAP1 localizes to MVEs together with ARA6, we sought to determine whether REAP1-bearing MVEs also exhibit sensitivity to BFA. While ARA6- and ARA7-labelled structures aggregated into BFA bodies in response to BFA treatment as previously reported37, REAP1-labelled domains were not incorporated into these BFA bodies (Fig. 2b). This finding suggests that REAP1 localizes to a subset or subdomain of MVEs that is less sensitive to BFA.

Endosomal localization of REAP1 requires RAB5 activity

To determine whether the endosomal localization of REAP1 depends on RAB5 activity, we examined the subcellular localization of GFP–REAP1 in the ara6-1 (ref. 14) and vps9a-2 (ref. 25) mutants. In the ara6-1 mutant, GFP–REAP1 localized to punctate organelles similar to the pattern observed in the wild type (Fig. 2c). In contrast, in the vps9a-2 mutant, GFP–REAP1 was dispersed in the cytosol and partially mislocalized to the vacuolar membrane (Fig. 2c, arrowheads).

VPS9A is a common activator of ARA6 and canonical RAB5, promoting their recruitment to MVEs25. The altered localization of REAP1 in the vps9a-2 mutant, but not in ara6-1, led us to hypothesize that canonical RAB5, rather than ARA6, determines the subcellular localization of REAP1. To test this hypothesis, we performed a transient expression experiment using Arabidopsis root cultured cells11. GFP–REAP1 localized to punctate organelles overlapping with MVE markers, tagRFP–ARA7 and ARA6–tagRFP. A similar localization pattern was observed in cells expressing constitutively active forms of canonical RAB5 (ARA7(Q69L)) and ARA6 (ARA6(Q93L)). In contrast, when dominant-negative ARA7 (ARA7(S24N)) was overexpressed, GFP–REAP1 was not detected on punctate compartments and instead appeared dispersed in the cytosol. However, the expression of dominant-negative ARA6 (ARA6(S47N)) did not affect the subcellular localization of GFP–REAP1 (Fig. 2d), as supported by quantitative analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2c). These findings support the hypothesis that the MVE localization of REAP1 is specifically regulated by canonical RAB5.

REAP1 interacts with active ROP7

Previous studies reported that rice OsSWAP70, orthologous to REAP1, activates OsRac1 and that OsSWAP70 and OsRac1 function cooperatively during pathogen responses20. To investigate whether REAP1 interacts with ROP members in Arabidopsis, we performed yeast two-hybrid assays to test interactions between REAP1 and the 11 Arabidopsis ROP members (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The results showed that REAP1 interacts with both the wild-type and constitutively active forms of ROP7. Expression of the constructs used in the assay was confirmed by immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

It has also been reported that a DH-domain-like sequence is present in the amino acid region 203–401 of REAP1 (ref. 38; Fig. 3a). To determine whether this DH-domain-like sequence is required for the interaction between REAP1 and ROP7, we conducted a domain dissection analysis (Fig. 3a). Yeast two-hybrid analysis revealed that the DH-domain-like region of REAP1 is necessary and sufficient for its interaction with the constitutively active form of ROP7. This result suggests that the DH domain of REAP1 serves as a critical interface for both RAB5 and ROP7.

Next, we examined the interaction between REAP1 and ROP7 in vivo using a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay. Full-length REAP1 was fused to the amino-terminal portion of VENUS (nVENUS), and wild-type ROP7 was fused to the carboxy-terminal portion of VENUS (cVENUS). When these constructs were transiently expressed in tobacco leaf epidermal cells, reconstituted VENUS fluorescence was observed, indicating an interaction between REAP1 and wild-type ROP7 (Fig. 3b). Fluorescence was also detected with the combination of REAP1 and the constitutively active form of ROP7. Expression of all constructs used in these assays was confirmed by immunoblotting and PCR with reverse transcription (RT–PCR; Supplementary Fig. 3c,d). In contrast, no fluorescence was observed for combinations of REAP1 and the dominant-negative form of ROP7 (Fig. 3b). Notably, in contrast to the yeast two-hybrid results, reconstituted fluorescence was also observed between REAP1 and wild-type ROP1 and ROP2 (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

We then examined the intracellular distribution of the reconstituted VENUS fluorescence resulting from the interaction between nVENUS–REAP1 and cVENUS–ROP7. The fluorescence was observed to localize to both the PM of epidermal cells and punctate structures in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3c). These punctate structures were highly mobile along the cytoplasmic streaming (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Videos 1, 2 and 3).

Overexpression of REAP1 and RAB5s recruits ROP7 to endosomes

Many Arabidopsis ROP members have been reported to localize predominantly to the PM39,40. Since REAP1 localizes to MVEs and interacts with ROP7 at punctate structures, we hypothesized that REAP1 might recruit ROP7 to MVEs. To test this, we examined the effect of REAP1 overexpression on the subcellular localization of ROP7. When wild-type ROP7 was transiently expressed in tobacco epidermal cells, it localized predominantly to the PM. In some cells, ROP7 was also concentrated in the nucleus and nucleolus. Upon co-expression with REAP1, a subpopulation of ROP7 remained at the PM, while colocalization of REAP1 and ROP7 was observed in punctate structures where REAP1 localized (Fig. 4a), which was also supported by quantitative analysis (Supplementary Fig 4a).

Interestingly, the constitutively active form of ROP7 localized to both the PM and punctate structures even without co-expression of REAP1. These cytosolic punctate structures were also labelled by REAP1. In contrast, the dominant-negative form of ROP7 localized only to the PM, with no recruitment to punctate structures observed upon co-expression with REAP1. Similarly, wild-type ROP1 and wild-type ROP2 also localized to the PM in the absence of REAP1 co-expression, and no recruitment to punctate structures was observed with REAP1 co-expression. Furthermore, REAP1 overexpression did not affect the localization of the PM marker SYP132 (Supplementary Fig. 4a,b).

Given that REAP1 is an effector downstream of RAB5, we predicted that RAB5 overexpression would affect the subcellular localization of ROP7 similarly to REAP1 overexpression. To test this, we performed experiments analogous to those conducted for REAP1 overexpression (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 4a). The results showed that overexpression of wild-type ARA7 or wild-type ARA6 resulted in the marked recruitment of wild-type ROP7 to MVEs where RAB5 is localized. This effect was not observed with overexpression of RAB11s (RABA1b or RABA6a), nor with overexpression of another MVE marker, VAMP727 (Supplementary Fig. 4a,c). These findings suggest that both RAB5 and REAP1 play roles in the recruitment of ROP7 to endosomes.

RAB5, REAP1 and ROP7 act in a cascade to recruit ROP7 to endosomes

To determine whether REAP1 is involved in RAB5-mediated recruitment of ROP7 to endosomes, we examined the subcellular localization of ROP7 in Arabidopsis root-derived protoplasts isolated from wild-type and reap1 mutant plants. We successfully detected GFP signals in protoplasts prepared from transgenic plants expressing GFP–REAP1 under the control of its native promoter in the reap1 mutant background, confirming REAP1 expression in root protoplast cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a). The punctate localization of GFP–REAP1 also confirms that the protoplast preparation process has negligible effects on its subcellular localization. When tagRFP-tagged ARA7 was expressed under the control of the 35S promoter, ARA7 localized to enlarged ring-shaped endosomes in this system (Supplementary Fig. 5b).

We next compared the subcellular localization of ROP7 in wild-type and reap1 mutant protoplasts. In both genotypes, ROP7 predominantly localized to the PM under basal conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5c). However, upon overexpression of ARA7 in wild-type protoplasts, ROP7 exhibited a punctate localization pattern, with GFP–ROP7 signals within ring-like endosomes formed by ARA7 overexpression (Fig. 5). In contrast, in reap1 mutant protoplasts, ARA7 overexpression resulted in the formation of enlarged ring-like endosomes, but ROP7 failed to associate with these structures (Fig. 5). Quantitative analysis showed that the number of GFP–ROP7 puncta was significantly increased upon tagRFP–ARA7 overexpression in wild-type protoplasts, whereas this increase was not observed in reap1 mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. 5d). In addition, the proportion of ARA7-positive puncta bearing GFP–ROP7 signal was significantly reduced in the reap1 mutant (Supplementary Fig. 5e). In contrast, the size of ROP7-positive puncta was not significantly different between wild-type and reap1 mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. 5f). These results suggest that REAP1 mediates the action of ARA7 in the recruitment of ROP7 to MVEs, forming a RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 signalling cascade.

The RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade is required for gamete development

To investigate the role of the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade in plant growth and development, we analysed the phenotype of the reap1 mutant (Supplementary Fig. 6). Under standard laboratory conditions, no macroscopic differences were observed between reap1 and wild-type plants. We then obtained the rop7 mutant and generated the reap1 rop7 double mutant through crossing. The rop7 mutant harbours a transfer DNA insertion in the sixth intron (Supplementary Fig. 7a), and RT-PCR and real-time PCR analyses confirmed that ROP7 expression was reduced to approximately one tenth of the wild-type level (Supplementary Fig. 7b), indicating that it is a knockdown mutant. Phenotypic observations revealed no significant differences between the rop7 or reap1 single mutants and the wild type. Similarly, the reap1 rop7 double mutant exhibited no obvious macroscopic abnormalities under normal growth conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6).

We next examined the genetic interaction between REAP1 and RAB5. VPS9A has been shown to function as a major GEF for all three RAB5 members in Arabidopsis25; thus, vps9a mutants serve as a genetic tool to impair RAB5 activation, and we therefore used this mutant to assess the functional relationship between ROP7 and RAB5-mediated processes. The vps9a-2 mutant, a weak-allele mutant of VPS9A, exhibits multiple phenotypic defects, including impaired primary root elongation25. Previous studies have shown opposing effects of mutations in canonical (ARA7 and RHA1) and plant-specific RAB5 (ARA6) on the vps9a-2 phenotype; the abnormalities of vps9a-2 are exacerbated by ara7 but suppressed by the ara6 mutation14. The hemizygous mutation in PUF2 for a regulator of RAB5-mediated endosomal transport also exaggerates the effects of the vps9a-2 mutation13. On the basis of these findings, we investigated how the reap1 mutation affects the vps9a-2 phenotype. Interestingly, the reap1 mutation partially suppressed the vps9a-2 phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 6). This suppression was completely abolished when a genomic fragment of REAP1 or GFP-tagged REAP1 was introduced into the reap1 vps9a-2 double mutant (Supplementary Fig. 8), confirming that the suppression was due to the reap1 mutation.

We then analysed the genetic interaction between ROP7 and VPS9A. Notably, we were unable to recover the rop7 vps9a-2 double mutant. Although ROP7 and VPS9A are located on different chromosomes, self-pollination of rop7+/− vps9a-2+/− heterozygous plants failed to produce rop7−/− vps9a-2+/− or rop7+/− vps9a-2−/− progeny (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). To investigate this further, we constructed a DNA fragment to express mGFP-tagged ROP7 under the control of its native promoter (Supplementary Fig. 7c) and introduced it into rop7+/− vps9a-2+/−. In the T2 generation, we successfully obtained transformants expressing mGFP–ROP7 in the rop7−/− vps9a-2+/− background (Supplementary Fig. 7d,e). These results suggest that the combination of rop7 and vps9a-2 mutations impairs gametophyte function.

To test this hypothesis, we performed cross-pollination experiments (Supplementary Table 3). When rop7+/− vps9a-2+/− plants were crossed with wild-type plants, the rop7 vps9a-2 mutant combination was not transmitted to the next generation, regardless of whether the mutant plant was used as the female or male parent. This observation suggests that ROP7 and VPS9A function cooperatively in both male and female gametophyte development. To investigate this further, we focused on pollen development and generated the qrt1-4 rop7+/− vps9a-2+/− mutant for detailed analysis (Fig. 6a). The qrt1-4 mutant (control), as well as the qrt1-4 rop7 and qrt1-4 vps9a-2 mutants, formed normal pollen tetrads whose viability was confirmed by Alexander staining41. In contrast, pollen from qrt1-4 rop7+/− vps9a-2+/− plants exhibited notable abnormalities. Approximately half of the tetrads (51.3%) were similar to the control. Among the others, the majority of tetrads showed a 1:1 ratio of normal to aborted pollen grains (43.0%), while a small population contained three (3.2%) or four (2.5%) aborted pollen grains per tetrad (Fig. 6a). This result indicates that ROP7 and normal RAB5 activation are essential for gametophyte development after meiosis, and the rop7 vps9a-2 mutation may affect the development of microspores with a wild-type genotype within the tetrad.

To determine the developmental stage at which defects arise in the rop7 vps9a-2 mutant, we examined pollen development using DAPI staining to monitor nuclear progression. The wild type and rop7 and vps9a-2 single mutants showed no obvious abnormalities at any stage. In contrast, pollen from rop7 vps9a-2 plants appeared normal at the early unicellular stage, but defects became apparent from the late unicellular to bicellular stages, where two of the four pollen grains within a tetrad were aborted (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed that no obvious abnormalities were detected at the unicellular stage (wild type: 125/125 normal (100%), 0/125 abnormal (0%); rop7: 115/115 normal (100%), 0/115 abnormal (0%); vps9a-2: 66/66 normal (100%), 0/66 abnormal (0%); rop7+/− vps9a-2+/−: 77/77 normal (100%), 0/77 abnormal (0%)). However, at the bicellular stage, approximately one quarter of the pollen grains exhibited severe defects, including empty pollen grains or signs of cellular degeneration (wild type: 38/38 normal (100%), 0/38 abnormal (0%); rop7: 26/28 normal (92.9%), 2/28 abnormal (7.1%); vps9a-2: 114/117 normal (97.4%), 3/117 abnormal (2.6%); rop7+/− vps9a-2+/−: 111/141 normal (78.7%), 30/141 abnormal (21.3%)). The frequency of abnormal pollen grains did not significantly deviate from the expected Mendelian segregation ratio of 3:1 (χ2 = 1.04, d.f. = 1, P = 0.31) (Supplementary Fig. 9b). These observations suggest that pollen defects in the rop7 vps9a-2 mutant arise during the transition from the late unicellular to the bicellular stage, ultimately leading to pollen abortion.

Although the reap1 rop7 and reap1 vps9a-2 double mutants were successfully obtained, significantly higher proportions of aborted pollen grains (0.79% for reap1 rop7 and 1.78% for reap1 vps9a-2, P < 0.01, Fisher’s exact test) were observed in the pollen population from these mutants than in the wild type (0.14%) (Supplementary Fig. 10). To further determine the developmental stage at which defects occur in these mutants, we examined pollen development using DAPI staining. Although the frequency was low, aborted pollen grains were observed at the bicellular and tricellular stages in reap1 vps9a-2 plants (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 4). In addition, reap1 rop7 mutants showed a significantly higher proportion of aborted pollen grains at the tricellular stage than other genotypes. These findings suggest that REAP1 is required for the full functionality of ROP7 and its cooperation with RAB5 during pollen development.

RAB5 regulates REAP1 localization in pollen

To investigate the role of the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade in pollen, we first examined the expression and subcellular localization of REAP1 during pollen development. REAP1 was expressed in pollen and exhibited a punctate localization pattern at the unicellular stage. At the bicellular stage, REAP1 appeared enriched in the generative cell, and from the tricellular stage onwards, strong signals were observed in the sperm cells (Supplementary Fig. 12a). ARA7 showed a predominantly discrete punctate localization pattern, although slight variations were observed depending on the developmental stage (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Given that our genetic and cytological analyses suggested that the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade functions in the transition from the unicellular to the bicellular stage, we next examined REAP1 localization in ara6-1 and vps9a-2 mutants at the bicellular stage. In both mutants, the punctate localization of REAP1 was largely lost (Supplementary Fig. 12c). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in REAP1-positive puncta in these mutants. These results indicate that both ARA6 and VPS9A are required for the proper localization of REAP1 in the male gametophyte.

REAP1 regulates the localization of ROP7 in endothecium cells

In our experimental system, ROP7 expression was not detectable in pollen in the transgenic plants carrying the construct shown in Supplementary Fig. 7c (Supplementary Fig. 13a). We also observed an independently generated transgenic line expressing monomeric mGFP-tagged ROP7, in which mGFP was inserted within the ROP7 coding sequence, but again no detectable mGFP signal was observed in pollen grains (Supplementary Fig. 13b). However, the publicly available database ePlant42,43 indicates that ROP7 is expressed in pollen (Supplementary Fig. 14), suggesting that its expression may be below the detection limit under our experimental conditions.

Considering that sporophytic tissues such as the tapetum and endothecium also play important roles in pollen development and function44,45, we next examined the spatial expression pattern of ROP7 in anther sporophytic tissues. We analysed a series of developmental stages using anthesis (stage 0) as a reference and tracing earlier stages (−1, −2 and so on)46. ROP7 exhibited a highly stage- and tissue-specific expression pattern and was detected specifically in endothecium cells at stages −3 and −2, with little or no detectable signal at other stages or in other anther cell layers, in two independently generated transgenic lines (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 15a,b).

We next examined whether components of the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade are co-expressed in this context. ARA7, ARA6 and REAP1 were all detected in endothecium cells at stage −3 (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 15c), suggesting that the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade may function in this tissue. To test the functional relationship between these components, we analysed the subcellular localization of ROP7 in the reap1 mutant. In wild-type endothecium cells, ROP7 localized to the PM and was broadly distributed throughout the cytoplasm. In contrast, in reap1 mutant cells, ROP7 was predominantly restricted to the PM, with a marked reduction in intracellular signal (Fig. 6b). These results suggest that REAP1 contributes to the balance between PM and intracellular localization of ROP7.

We next examined the effect of RAB5 impairment on REAP1 localization. In the ara6-1 mutant, REAP1 exhibited a punctate localization pattern comparable to that in wild-type cells. In contrast, in the vps9a-2 mutant, REAP1 showed a more diffuse distribution with a reduction in punctate structures (Fig. 6c). Together, these results support a model in which canonical RAB5 regulates REAP1 localization, which in turn influences the subcellular distribution of ROP7 in endothecium cells during a specific developmental window.

Discussion

Crosstalk between distinct small GTPase families in plants

Small GTPases are key regulators of various cellular processes, but how their functions are integrated during developmental processes in plants remains unexplored. In this study, we identified REAP1 as an effector for both the canonical RAB5 and ARA6 in Arabidopsis. We also found that REAP1 interacts with the constitutively active form of ROP7 and facilitates the recruitment of active ROP7 to endosomes. Similarly, RAB5 promotes ROP7 recruitment to endosomes, and this process depends on REAP1. These findings reveal the existence of a RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 signalling cascade in Arabidopsis, in which ROP7 functions on endosomes to regulate developmental processes, including pollen development (Fig. 6d). This study demonstrates the functional interplay between two distinct types of small GTPases, RAB and ROP, in plants. Crosstalk between different small GTPase subfamilies has been previously reported in animal systems3,4. However, the cascade we identified involves ARA6, a plant-specific RAB5 GTPase uniquely acquired during plant evolution, alongside ROPs, which are uniquely diversified in plants, and canonical RAB5. This suggests that this regulatory system was specifically adapted in plants. Our findings underscore the universal importance of integrative interactions among different types of small GTPases, while also highlighting the plant-specific adaptations of small GTPase cascades shaped during evolution.

REAP1 interacts with active ROP7

REAP1 was previously identified as AtSWAP70 (ref. 38), a protein whose rice homologue has been reported to function as a GEF for OsRac1. This homologue plays a critical role in immune responses, including defence gene expression and reactive oxygen species production in response to chitin elicitors20. Similar to its rice counterpart, AtSWAP70 contains the DH domain, known to activate Rho GTPases in animal systems, suggesting that AtSWAP70 might also possess ROPGEF activity. However, our findings indicate that AtSWAP70 interacts specifically with the GTP-bound form of ROP7, and the DH domain alone is sufficient for this interaction (Fig. 3a). Given that GEFs typically interact with GTPases in their inactive GDP-bound state, our results suggest that the DH domain of AtSWAP70 may not function as a GEF, but may have evolved to serve as an interaction site with ROP proteins. This hypothesis warrants further experimental validation. Another intriguing question is whether REAP1 can simultaneously interact with both RAB5 and ROP7 or whether their binding is competitive. Our data suggest that RAB5 binds to REAP1 at its DH domain, raising the possibility of distinct or overlapping interaction mechanisms.

It has been reported that ROP7 is activated by ROPGEF2 and ROPGEF4 to regulate stomatal behaviour47. Additionally, ROPGEF8, which is activated by phosphatidic acid, also activates ROP7 (ref. 48). ROPGEF8 interacts with both canonical RAB5 and ARA6, regardless of their nucleotide-binding states, and cooperatively regulates pollen tube germination with ROP1 (ref. 49). The same research group also discovered that ARA6 and canonical RAB5 act synergistically during pollen germination, unlike their counteracting functions during vegetative development. These findings suggest that crosstalk between RAB5 and multiple ROP proteins forms an integral and specific mechanism underlying various reproductive processes. Exploring this mechanism further will be an exciting avenue for future research.

Physiological roles of the plant RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade

ROP7 has been reported to exhibit high expression during xylem differentiation, where it regulates the density of cell wall bands in protoxylem cells15,50,51. ROP7 is also expressed in guard cells, where it is activated by ROPGEF2 through interactions with PHYB, playing a downstream role in red-light-induced stomatal opening47. In this study, we uncovered a role for ROP7 in the development of both male and female gametophytes.

The segregation ratios of offspring from rop7+/− vps9a-2+/− mutant plants did not statistically support any of the three simple hypotheses we tested: (1) a 15:1 segregation ratio in double mutants, (2) lethality in either male or female gametophytes or (3) lethality in both male and female gametophytes (Supplementary Table 2). These findings suggest that the phenotypic abnormalities cannot be explained solely by simple gametophyte lethality. Together with the pollen viability assays, our data indicate that the rop7 vps9a-2 mutation causes more complex developmental defects, including effects on neighbouring microspores within the same tetrad.

An alternative, but not mutually exclusive, possibility is that the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade also functions in sporophytic anther tissues and thereby indirectly influences pollen development. In this study, we found that ROP7, REAP1/SWAP70, ARA6 and ARA7 are co-expressed in endothecium cells and that REAP1/SWAP70 regulates the intracellular localization of ROP7 in this tissue. These observations raise the possibility that the cascade functions not only in the gametophytic lineage itself but also in surrounding sporophytic tissues that support pollen development.

Sporophytic regulation of pollen development is well established, most notably through the tapetum, which supplies essential components required for pollen maturation45. The endothecium, in contrast, is best known for its role in anther dehiscence, where lignocellulosic secondary wall thickening generates mechanical force during anther dehydration, leading to pollen release52. ROP7 has also been implicated in secondary wall patterning in protoxylem cells, where it functions together with ROP8 and ROP11 to regulate the density of secondary wall bands51. In this context, the spatiotemporal expression of ROP7 coincides with the developmental window during which endothecium cells undergo secondary wall thickening in coordination with pollen maturation and tapetum degeneration, raising the possibility that the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade functions at this stage to regulate endothecium maturation. Alternatively, the cascade may have additional roles in the endothecium that influence pollen function. Consistent with this idea, enzymes involved in very-long-chain fatty acid biosynthesis, including CER2, CER2L2 and KCS6, are expressed in the endothecium, and the cer2 cer2l2 double mutant exhibits defects in pollen hydration despite normal pollen viability44. These findings support a model in which very-long-chain fatty acids supplied from the endothecium, probably after tapetum degradation, facilitate pollen hydration on the stigma. Together, these observations suggest that proper pollen development and function depend not only on the microspore genotype but also on the physiological state of the surrounding sporophytic tissues, reflecting a multilayered role of the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade during reproduction.

Interestingly, both ARA6 and canonical RAB5 interact with REAP1 in their active forms. Although the physiological significance of the interaction with ARA6 remains unclear, previous studies have shown that ARA6 and canonical RAB5 function antagonistically during vegetative growth, whereas they act more redundantly in gametophytes49. Considering these gametophytic functions, ARA6 may act to reinforce canonical RAB5 activity within the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade identified in this study. However, both ara6-1 and reap1 mutations partially suppress the root growth defects of the vps9a-2 mutant, although to different extents. These observations raise the possibility that ARA6 and REAP1 function in the same pathway during root development. This pathway is probably independent of ROP7, because rop7 mutants do not phenocopy the vps9a-2 root phenotype. As canonical RAB5 and ARA6 function in distinct trafficking pathways during vegetative growth13,14, REAP1 may preferentially operate with other interacting partners and participate in an ARA6-dependent trafficking pathway during root development. Further functional analyses will be required to clarify how ARA6 contributes to the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade through its interaction with REAP1 in gametophytic and sporophytic contexts, as well as the role of REAP1 in ARA6-dependent trafficking pathways.

Regarding ROP7’s role in female gametophyte formation, previous studies have reported its expression in synergid cells53, implicating ROP7 in synergid cell functions. To further elucidate the physiological significance of the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade during gametogenesis, future studies should include detailed phenotypic analyses of rop7+/− vps9a-2+/− gametophytes and observations of ROP7, REAP1 and RAB5 dynamics throughout gametophyte development.

Implications of endosomal recruitment of ROP7

Although ROP7 has been reported to localize to the PM54,55, its roles in endosomal functions have not been explored until now. In this study, we found that ROP7, but not ROP1 or ROP2, is recruited to endosomes via the expression of REAP1 or canonical RAB5. Since ROP7 interacts with REAP1 in its GTP-bound active state, it is possible that ROP7 initiates downstream signalling at endosomes, which may be critical for normal gametogenesis. However, the precise molecular mechanisms and biological significance of such endosomal signalling remain less understood in plants. Alternatively, endosomal recruitment of ROP7 could serve as a mechanism to downregulate its signalling activity. At the PM, ROP7 has been implicated in regulating the density of cell wall bands in protoxylem cells through interactions with ROP GTPase-activating proteins and ROPGEFs51. The recruitment of active ROP7 to endosomes may thus attenuate its functional activity at the PM. It is also possible that the recruitment of active ROP7 followed by ESCRT-mediated protein sorting at MVEs serves to degrade active ROP7. In our protoplast experiments, ROP7 was detected enclosed within the enlarged endosomes induced by ARA7 expression. Future research focusing on the downstream signals of the RAB5–REAP1–ROP7 cascade will be crucial to elucidate the biological significance of this intracellular signalling unit in plants.

Methods

Plasmid construction for yeast two-hybrid and BiFC experiments

For yeast two-hybrid experiments, the full-length complementary DNA (cDNA) sequence of REAP1 was cloned into the pAD-GAL4-GWRFC vector provided by T. Demura (NAIST) using the Gateway cloning technique (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cDNA sequences encoding constitutively active (QL) and dominant-negative (SN) forms of ARA6 and ARA7 were cloned into the SalI site of the pBD-GAL4-Cam vector (Agilent Technologies). Truncated REAP1 fragments were amplified using the primer sets listed in Supplementary Table 5 and subsequently cloned into pAD-GAL4-GWRFC using the Gateway cloning technique. Arabidopsis ROP coding sequences were amplified from open flower or root cDNA libraries and cloned into pBD-GAL4-GWRFC, also provided by T. Demura, using the Gateway cloning technique. Constitutively active (GV) and dominant-negative (TN) forms of ROPs39,56 were generated using PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 5. The expression of fusion constructs in yeast was verified by immunoblotting. Transformed yeast cells were grown in YPD or SD medium and harvested at log phase. Cell pellets were resuspended in 0.2 N NaOH and incubated at room temperature for 5 min with vortexing. An equal volume of 3× SDS sample buffer containing 1 mM DTT was then added, and the samples were heated at 95 °C for 5 min prior to SDS–PAGE. Fusion proteins were detected using anti-GAL4 AD (1:2,000) or anti-GAL4 BD (1:500) antibodies (Takara Bio). Mouse primary antibodies were detected using an HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Cytiva/Amersham, 1:10,000). Signal detection was performed using SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Signal enhancement reagent (Can Get Signal; Toyobo) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. BD–ARA6, BD–ARA6(Q93L), BD–ARA6(S47N), BD–ARA7, BD–ARA7(Q69L) and BD–ARA7(S24N) have been previously described to produce detectable interaction signals in yeast two-hybrid assays13,24,25.

For BiFC vector construction, cDNA sequences of REAP1, ROP7, ROP7(G15V), ROP7(T20N), ROP1 and ROP2 were first cloned into pENTR/D-TOPO using the Gateway TOPO reaction. The resulting constructs were digested with EcoRV and subsequently transferred into pDEST-VYCE(R)GW and pDEST-VYNE(R)GW vectors57 using Gateway LR reactions. The expression of BiFC fusion constructs was verified by immunoblotting. Infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves were homogenized in 1× PBS supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete, EDTA-free, Merck) to 1 g ml−1. The homogenate was centrifuged to remove debris, and the supernatant was mixed with SDS sample buffer containing 2-mercaptoethanol and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min prior to SDS–PAGE. Proteins were detected using HRP-conjugated anti-HA (MBL, M180-7, 1:1,000) and anti-Myc (MBL, M192-7, 1:3,000) antibodies.

Yeast two-hybrid interaction assay

Individual interaction assays were performed using GAL4 two-hybrid phagemid vector kits (Agilent Technologies) and strain AH109 (Takara Clontech). Colonies were grown in selective medium lacking leucine and tryptophan, diluted to an OD600 of 0.5 for testing interactions between REAP1 and RAB5s, or to an OD600 of 1.8 for testing interactions between REAP1 and ROPs. Cultures were spotted onto plates lacking leucine, tryptophan and histidine.

Expression and purification of recombinant proteins

ARA6(Q93L), ARA6(S47N), ARA7(Q69L), ARA7(S24N) and REAP1 were expressed as glutathione S-transferase (GST)-fusion proteins using the pGEX6P-1 vector (GE Healthcare). GST–RAB5 was expressed in Escherichia coli strain DH5α, and GST–REAP1 was expressed in Rosetta-gami 2 (DE3) (Merck). The recombinant proteins were then purified according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vitro pull-down assay

HA-tagged REAP1 was expressed in yeast strain YPH414 (MATa Δpep4:TRP1 ura3 lys2 ade2 trp1 his3 leu2) under the control of the GAL1 promoter. Yeast cells were collapsed by vortexing with glass beads in PSB supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete, EDTA-free, Merck). The collected cell lysate was mixed with 0.2 nmol GST or GST-tagged RAB5 proteins prebound to glutathione-Sepharose 4B resin. The mixture was incubated for 60 min at 4 °C in binding buffer (PBS buffer containing 0.05% Tween-20 and protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck)). After incubation, the beads were washed three times with binding buffer, and the bound proteins were subjected to immunoblotting analysis. HA-tagged REAP1 was detected using an anti-HA antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500) followed by an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Cytiva/Amersham, 1:10,000).

Lipid-binding assay using PIP strips

PIP strips (Echelon Biosciences Inc.) were blocked overnight at 4 °C in TBS buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 137 mM NaCl, 2.68 mM KCl) containing 3% fatty-acid-free BSA (Wako Fujifilm). After blocking, the strips were incubated with 10 nM GST or GST–REAP1 in TBS-T (TBS with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 hour at room temperature with gentle agitation. After incubation, the strips were washed three times for 15 min each in TBS-T with gentle agitation. The strips were then incubated with anti-GST antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) diluted 1:1,000 in TBS-T for 1 hour at room temperature with gentle agitation. After three washes with TBS-T, the strips were incubated with anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:10,000 in TBS-T for 1 hour at room temperature. The strips were washed three more times, and signals were detected using a chemiluminescent reagent (Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate; Millipore).

Generation of transgenic plants

Translational fusions between cDNAs for fluorescent proteins and Arabidopsis genes were generated using fluorescent tagging of full-length proteins. Briefly, the cDNA for monomeric GFP (mGFP) or mRFP were inserted in front of the start or stop codon of each gene with the following flanking sequences: 2,055 bp 5′ and 996 bp 3′ for REAP1, and 1,973 bp 5′ and 1,015 bp 3′ for ROP7. The primer sequences used to amplify genomic REAP1 and genomic ROP7 sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 5. The mGFP–ARA7 construct was generated on the basis of the previously reported GFP–ARA7 construct25, in which mutations were introduced into the GFP sequence to generate a monomeric variant using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 5. The resulting DNA fragment was subcloned into the pGWB1 vector, provided by T. Nakagawa (Shimane University). In addition, an alternative ROP7 construct was generated on the basis of Higa et al.51, in which mGFP was inserted internally within the ROP7 coding sequence after Gly134 under the control of a 2,520-bp promoter and a 500-bp terminator58. This construct was subcloned into the pGWB401 vector. For the reap1 complementation assay, a genomic fragment including REAP1 and the same 5′ and 3′ flanking sequences was subcloned into pGWB1 and transformed into the reap1 vps9a-2 double mutant. For the rop7 complementation assay, the pROP7::mGFP-ROP7 sequence was similarly subcloned into pGWB1. The construct was first transformed into the rop7+/− vps9a-2+/− double mutant, and the genotypes of the T2 lines were confirmed by PCR-based genotyping to validate complementation. Arabidopsis plants were transformed using the floral dip method with Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101:pMp90. Transgenic lines with a 1:3 segregation ratio for antibiotic resistance were selected, indicating a single-locus insertion of the transgene. These lines were used for subsequent experiments.

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana Columbia-0 (Col-0) was used as the wild type in all experiments. The transfer DNA insertion line reap1/Atswap70 (GABI_096E03) was provided by K. Yamaguchi and T. Kawasaki (Kinki University)38. The rop7 mutant (SALK_041927) was obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center and backcrossed three times with wild-type Col-0 before being used for the experiments. Other mutant lines, vps9a-2 (GABI_557C02)25, ara6-1 (SAIL_880-C07)14, puf2 (SAIL_24_C10)13, rop7/8/11 (ref. 51) and qrt1-4 (SALK_024104)59, and the transgenic lines expressing mRFP–ARA7 (ref. 60), ARA6–mRFP14, VPS9A–GFP25, VHAa1–mRFP33 and ST–mRFP34 have been described previously. Seeds were surface-sterilized by exposure to 70% ethanol for 5 min with gentle agitation, followed by rinsing with 100% ethanol. Sterilized seeds were plated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog medium including vitamins (Duchefa Biochemie), 1% sucrose, 0.05% MES-KOH (pH 5.8) and 0.5% (w/v) gellan gum (Wako Fujifilm) for observation using confocal laser scanning microscopy, or 1% (w/v) gellan gum for experiments examining overall macroscopic phenotypes and primary root elongation. Plates were incubated at 4 °C for 2 days for stratification and then transferred to a growth chamber maintained at 22 °C under long-day conditions (16-hour light/8-hour dark). After 14 days, seedlings were transferred to soil and grown at 22 °C under the same long-day conditions.

Microscopy

Confocal laser scanning microscopy was performed using an LSM780, LSM800 or LSM980 (Carl Zeiss) or a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope. Images were acquired using dry or oil immersion objectives as follows: on the LSM780, a dry objective (×20, NA 0.55) and oil immersion objectives (×40, NA 1.3; ×63, NA 1.4); on the LSM800, an oil immersion objective (×63, NA 1.4); and on the LSM980, a dry objective (×20, NA 0.8) and an oil immersion objective (×63, NA 1.4). Five-to-seven-day-old seedlings were observed. For imaging anther and pollen, floral tissues were dissected and observed using the lambda mode of the LSM980, and emission spectra from 490 to 667 nm were acquired. Reference spectra for linear unmixing were obtained with the same microscope settings using anthers or microspores/pollen from Arabidopsis plants that did not contain any fluorescent protein transgenes for autofluorescence spectra, and GFP single-colour transgenic plants for the GFP spectrum. Detailed confocal microscopy settings for each experiment are provided in Supplementary Table 6. Alexander staining was performed using an Alexander staining solution based on a previously described protocol41, containing 95% ethanol (10 ml), 1% malachite green in 95% ethanol (1 ml), 1% acid fuchsin in water (5 ml), 1% Orange G in water (0.5 ml), phenol (5 g), glacial acetic acid (2 ml), glycerol (25 ml) and distilled water (50 ml). The staining solution was diluted approximately twofold with distilled water before use, followed by the observation of pollen morphology and viability using a Zeiss Primostar 3 microscope.

The acquired images were processed and analysed using Fiji (version 2.16.0)61. Object-based colocalization analysis was performed using the DiAna plugin (version 1.54) in Fiji. Fluorescent puncta were segmented after Gaussian filtering and manual thresholding. Colocalization was defined as objects with centre-to-centre distances less than 0.24 µm as described previously62. For quantification, four regions of interest (ROIs) of identical size (18.45 µm × 18.45 µm) were selected from each of five independent roots. In total, 20 ROIs (16–55 GFP puncta per ROI) were analysed, and the proportion of colocalized objects was calculated for each ROI. Tracking was performed using TrackMate (version 7.14.0)63. The estimated particle diameter was set to 1 µm, with a detection threshold of 4 units. Spot detection was performed using the Laplacian of Gaussian algorithm, and tracking was conducted using the simple LAP tracker with a maximum linking distance of 10 µm.

For DAPI staining, anthers were extracted from flower buds or open flowers using a Zeiss Stemi 305 stereo microscope, then placed on a fresh slide glass with 30 µl of DAPI solution (1 μg ml−1 DAPI, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 M sodium phosphate (pH 7.0))64 and gently crushed to release microspores or pollen grains.

For transmission electron microscopy, anthers at various developmental stages were collected and were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.05 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 °C overnight, washed and post-fixed in 2% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C for 3 h. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in Quetol-651 resin (Nisshin EM). Semi-thin sections (1.5 µm) were stained with toluidine blue for light microscopy. Ultrathin sections (80 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome (Ultracut UCT; Leica) and collected on copper grids. Sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead stain solution. Transmission electron microscopy observations were performed using a JEM-1400Plus microscope (JEOL) at 100 kV. Images were acquired with a CCD camera.

Tobacco infiltration assay

Transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves was performed using Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration65. cDNA sequences of REAP1, ARA7, ARA6, RABA1b and RABA6a were subcloned into the binary vector pGWB405m or pGWB406m (a gift from S. Segami, NIBB) using Gateway LR reactions. cDNA sequences of ROP7, ROP7G15V, ROP7T20N, ROP1 and ROP2, N-terminally tagged with the mRFP sequence, were subcloned into pMDC7 using Gateway LR reactions. The expression vectors were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101:pMp90. Agrobacteria were co-infiltrated at OD600 = 0.2 with Agrobacterium carrying the p19 silencing suppressor. β-Oestradiol (2 μM) was infiltrated 24 hours after the initial infiltration to induce protein expression. All transient expression experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates. Quantification of punctate structures was performed using Fiji. Z-stack images (10 optical sections; total thickness, 4.43 µm) were projected using maximum intensity projection. Cells were manually segmented, and images were processed using Gaussian blur (σ = 1 µm) and background subtraction (2 pixels), followed by manual thresholding and watershed segmentation. Puncta were quantified using the Analyze Particles function, and the number of puncta per unit area was calculated. A total of 9–17 cells were analysed per condition.

Transient gene expression in Arabidopsis protoplasts

Transient expression of GFP- and/or tagRFP-tagged proteins in the Arabidopsis Col-0 suspension cultured cell line (Deep)66, provided by M. Umeda (NAIST), was conducted as described previously11,67. Z-stack images (10 sections; total thickness, 5.75 µm) were projected using maximum intensity projection in Fiji. Cells were manually segmented, and images were processed using Gaussian blur (σ = 2 µm), followed by manual background subtraction and thresholding. Puncta were quantified using the Analyze Particles function, and the number of puncta per unit area was calculated. A total of 10–13 cells were analysed per condition.

Preparation of wild-type (Col-0) and reap1 mutant protoplasts and transformation were conducted using a modified version of the tape–Arabidopsis sandwich method as described by Wu et al.68. Fourteen-day-old roots from Col-0 and reap1 mutant plants were collected and digested with enzyme solution (1% Cellulase R-10 (Kyowa), 0.25% Pectolyase-Y23 (Kyowa), 0.4 M mannitol, 10 mM CaCl2, 20 mM KCl, 0.1% BSA and 20 mM MES (pH 5.7)) for 2 hours at room temperature with gentle rotation. The released protoplasts were washed twice with W5 buffer (154 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl, 5 mM glucose and 2 mM MES (pH 5.7)) and resuspended in MMg solution (0.4 M mannitol, 15 mM MgCl2 and 4 mM MES (pH 5.7)). For transformation, 500 ng or 1 μg plasmid DNA was mixed with 2 μl salmon sperm carrier DNA (Invitrogen) and added to the protoplast suspension, followed by PEG solution (40% PEG6000, 0.1 M CaCl2 and 0.2 M mannitol). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min and then washed with W5 buffer. Transformed protoplasts were incubated for two days at 22 °C and then imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Z-stack images (optical section thickness of 0.48 µm; 10–41 optical sections) were acquired up to the equatorial plane of the cell and projected and processed as described above. Cells with mean tagRFP fluorescence intensity less than or equal to 70 were selected, followed by MaxEntropy thresholding and particle detection (minimum size, 3 pixels). The number of puncta per unit area and total GFP–ROP7 puncta area per unit area were quantified. A total of 20–23 cells were analysed. Using object-based colocalization analysis (DiAna, Fiji), the number of puncta in ARA7 and ROP7 colocalized was quantified as described above, and the proportion of ARA7-positive endosomes bearing ROP7 was calculated. Protoplast-based imaging experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, and quantification is presented from a representative experiment.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from open flowers of wild-type and rop7 mutant plants using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA integrity was evaluated by measuring the absorbance ratio at 260 nm/280 nm. cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of total RNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (Toyobo), and the synthesized cDNA was diluted tenfold for subsequent quantitative RT-PCR analysis. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed on a Light Cycler 96 system (Roche) with specific primer sets listed in Supplementary Table 5. Each 20-μl reaction mixture contained 2 μl of template cDNA, 0.4 μl of each primer (final concentration, 200 nM each) and 10 μl of KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Master Mix (2×) (KAPA Biosystems). The PCR reaction and the melting curve analysis were conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. Baseline correction and quantification cycle values were automatically calculated using Light Cycler 96 (Roche).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1)69. Normality of data was assessed with the one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test or the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using either a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test or a two-sample t-test, depending on the data distribution. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance was followed by the Tukey–Kramer test as a post hoc pairwise analysis. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by the Dwass–Steel–Critchlow–Fligner test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For analysis of proportions (the frequency of aborted pollen), a binomial generalized linear model was applied with genotype and developmental stages as explanatory variables, followed by Dunnett-type contrasts for multiple comparisons against the control genotype. Associations between categorical values were analysed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Groups not sharing the same letter were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed statistical results are shown in Supplementary Table 7 or indicated in the figures. Reproducibility information for the Supplementary Figures is summarized in Supplementary Table 8.
