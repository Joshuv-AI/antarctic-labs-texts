Exploring factors of physicians’ preferences for continuing medical education
Authors: Ruzica Nikolic Mandic, Vesna Bjegovic-Mikanovic, Zoran Bukumiric, Zorica Terzic-Supic, Jovana Todorovic, Sandra Grujicic, Dejan Nesic, Ulrich Laaser
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAchieving sustainability in continuing medical education (CME) involves regular assessment of the evolving needs of healthcare professionals regarding their competencies and updates in educational content accordingly. This study aimed to investigate the key areas and competencies that physicians in Serbia prioritize for their professional development and to analyze the factors associated with these competencies.MethodThis cross-sectional study was conducted among 2,625 physicians who are members of the medical chamber in Serbia. Besides similar studies, the Delphi process among medical experts served to create the research instruments, while obtained data were analyzed using factor analysis and robust regression analysis.ResultsThe results pointed to a significant association between the scores in all identified domains (management, clinical practice, communication, public health, essentials and clinical emergency) and female gender. The score in the communication domain was additionally associated with work in privately owned institutions (B = 0.563). The scores in the public health domain and the basic medical skills (essentials domain) were additionally associated with age (B = 0.077 and B = 0.100), work in a primary healthcare institution (B = 2.327 and B = 2.155) and being a specialist (B=-1.795 and B=-2.901). The score on the clinical emergency domain, besides the female gender, was associated with work in primary health care (B = 0.748), being a specialist (B=-1.592) and being a subspecialist (B=-1.023).ConclusionThis research identified gaps in CME/CPD program knowledge and design. It outlined competency groups developed with expert consensus. Proposed actions include integrating key competencies and developing online self-assessment tools for everyday practice. This can help physicians and program organizers focus on essential competencies and exclude non-evidence-based ones.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06374-2.

Background

The provision of sustainable healthcare requires healthcare professionals to improve their competencies continually and become more evident than ever during the COVID-19 pandemic [1]. Continuing medical education and professional development (CME/CPD) is commonly used to improve healthcare professionals’ knowledge and skills [2]. Achieving sustainability in continuing education in medicine involves implementing practices that promote long-term effectiveness, relevance, and accessibility [3]. Sustainable efforts in CME development are also pointed out in the “Global Strategy on Human Resources for Health: Workforce 2030”, indicating the need for “a more sustainable and responsive skills mix, harnessing opportunities from the education and deployment” [2]. However, investment in health workforce education and training is insufficient globally [2–5].

Although CMEs vary by country, they usually improve professional practice and help healthcare professionals achieve their goals [2, 4]. Modalities of conducting CMEs differ from traditional classroom-based lectures through different seminars or demonstrations to online-based educational programs [5].

Also, CME systems in Europe differ in mandatory nature, regulations, and accreditation structures. Some countries mandate CME at regional or national levels, while others do not legally regulate it [6]. Seventeen European Union (EU) member states have mandatory continuing education laws, while others recommend participation in CME activities. The required CME points for license renewal vary, with Bulgaria requiring 150 points annually and Slovenia 10.7 [7]. Out of the 27 EU countries, 21 countries used the services of the European Accreditation Council for CME (EACCME®) and its accreditation activities.

Based on evidence from numerous studies [8, 9], physicians need training to acquire competencies for quality improvement through “the combined and continuous efforts of health professionals, patients and their families, researchers, health insurers, planners, and educators to make changes that will lead to better outcomes for patients, better functioning of the system and better professional development” [10]. A European study on continuing education in primary health care found differences in content between European countries and organizations countries [11, 12]. This is due to the absence of a widely accepted model describing the competencies required of physicians to improve health care quality [13]. Competency models can enhance CME/CPD by focusing on learning outcomes and providing a basis for self-assessment and organizational structure for educational programs [14, 15].

Despite numerous studies on CME/CPD, gaps remain in the comprehensive competencies that are necessary for CME/CPD systems. A recent review revealed no comprehensive models for presenting competencies through CME/CPD systems [16, 17]. For instance, a US model [18] identifies key healthcare quality and patient safety, while the Canadian CanMeds framework defines six competency groups: medical expert, communicator, collaborator, manager, health advocate, scientist and professional [19, 20]. Greiner et al. [21] outlined five core competencies for healthcare professionals but excluded ethics and professionalism. The Bellagio model [22] highlights nine essential competencies for effective health care, including leadership, public trust and patient involvement. The American Board of Medical Specialties (ABMS) system focuses on competencies such as patient care, medical knowledge, professionalism, systemic practice, and practice-based learning, also adopted for undergraduate students [23].

Looking at Europe, there is no comprehensive competency model for physicians of all specialties. However, there are studies and recommendations for certain medical specialties: internal medicine [24], palliative medicine [25], obstetrics and gynaecology [26], general medicine [27, 28], occupational medicine [29], cardiology [30], as well as digital competences for physicians by the Standing Committee of European Doctors [31].

In Serbia, CME is crucial for improving the health system and is mandatory for the re-licensing of physicians, nurses, dentists, pharmacists, and biochemists [32]. Serbia has 333 publicly provided healthcare facilities and around 310 physicians and 610 nurses per 100,000 inhabitants [33]. In the Serbian health system, CME requirements were formalized in 2005 through the Health Care Law, Health Insurance Law, and Law on Health Professional Chambers. Re-licensing occurs every seven years in line with other regions [7], based on the accumulation of 20 CME credits. The process is overseen by the Health Council of Serbia, which manages the national accreditation of CME training events [34, 35]. These events must meet criteria such as evidence-based content, target group specification, and outcome evaluations.

From 2011 to 2020, significant efforts by the Ministry of Health, the Health Council of Serbia, and health profession chambers targeted and stabilized the CME system [36]. CME development aligns with European Association of Medical Specialists (UEMS) recommendations, involving diverse providers like public and private health organizations, educational institutions, and NGOs. In these efforts, developers or organizers of CMEs have to identify the significance of relevant topics to offer attendees the highest interest and most appropriate CMEs [37].

There are studies in the literature devoted to the competencies of Serbian pharmacists only among all healthcare workers [38, 39]. In Serbia and the countries of Southeastern Europe, there has been no combined research on the competencies necessary for post-graduate training of physicians so far. Examining the factors associated with the potential involvement of physicians in CME, their needs, and their experiences with the CMEs attended can, therefore, significantly improve the curricula of CMEs and adapt the topics and modalities to the actual needs of physicians.

This study aimed to investigate the key areas and competencies that physicians in Serbia prioritize for their professional development and to analyze the factors associated with these competencies.

Methods

The study was conducted as a cross-sectional study in two steps: (1) the development of a set of necessary and appropriate competencies for the continuing professional development of physicians by establishing a consensus of experts based on the Delphi process, and (2) the examination of competencies for continuous professional development of physicians, factors that determine them and expectations from continuous education through a cross-sectional study and a questionnaire as a research instrument.

Developing a set of necessary and appropriate competencies

The first step included developing the set of necessary and adequate competencies for the CMEs of physicians in Serbia through the Delphi process to generate consensus. Three rounds of the Delphi process were conducted to evaluate groups (clusters, domains) of competencies and make changes in the initial framework of the competencies draft (Fig. 1).

Experts, in total 51, were selected based on their extensive experience and roles in the CME Centers across all five medical faculties in Serbia (Belgrade, Kosovska Mitrovica, Kragujevac, Niš, and Novi Sad). CME Centers are expert bodies responsible for reviewing and quality control of continuing education programs within the institution, and if such a body does not exist with the organizer of CME, approval is given by the expert body of the corresponding association of health workers or the corresponding faculty of medicine. The background of experts differs based on clinical or public health specialisation: internal medicine, surgery, anesthesiology, gynaecology, psychiatry, paediatrics, pharmacology, epidemiology, social medicine, physical medicine, forensic medicine, immunology, infectious diseases, etc.

In the first round of the Delphi process, 35 experts responded, and the same number in the second round. Consensus development panels provided agreement in areas characterized by uncertainty or lack of definitive information. Also, consensus development panels helped bring experts together to directly comment on and develop research instruments and techniques.

The initial document in developing a set of competencies for physicians was an instrument used in Ireland – a survey of physicians to understand their needs for different aspects of CME [40], as well as Serbian national documents defining competencies in medicine. We selected the Irish instrument as available after thoroughly searching the literature. Also, the Ireland instrument is used in various specialities and primary care settings because it effectively captures essential factors related to patient care, outcomes, and physician performance. Its design allows adaptability beyond hospital physicians, making it valuable for assessing clinical practices across different healthcare environments. This versatility ensures that relevant insights can be gathered, promoting improvements in CME across all settings. The list of competencies and their definitions is in additional file 1 [34, 41–45]. In the first round of the Delhi process, the list of 53 competencies was provided to the panel members, who rated all listed competencies and could add competencies they considered important and not listed. In the following iteration, all initial competencies with scores above 5 were listed, along with the competencies added by the panel members in the first step to rate them again. This round was then repeated, and all the competencies with a median rating of 5 or more were included in the instrument for the quantitative analyses (in total 74 competencies presented in additional file 1). The final list was created based on the opinions of experts (75% and more agreement), factor analysis and mutual connection (correlation) of individual competencies.

Examining competencies for continuing professional development of physicians

The second step included distributing the questionnaire developed through the Delphi process in the first step and validated through a pilot study. The pilot version of the questionnaire was validated on 50 respondents with an average age of 45.3 ± 10.6 years; 26% were male, and 74% were female. Reliability was assessed using the Cronbach alpha coefficient, and the final version of the questionnaire had satisfactory reliability for all subscales (above 0.8).

The study population included all physicians in Serbia with a license to practice medicine, meaning all members of the Serbian Medical Chamber. Serbian Medical Chamber is the only licensing institution in Serbia for physicians, and a license issued by the Serbian Medical Chamber is necessary for medical practice [33, 34, 41].

The questionnaire consisted of five sections: (1) demographic characteristics of the respondents, (2) their needs for continuing medical education (CME), competencies that participants considered as important to be covered by the CME program, (3) topics that should be covered with the CME program, (4) type of education program and learning environment, and (5) attitudes and opinions on CME.

The first section of the questionnaire regarded the demographic characteristics of the respondents (age, sex – male/female), the type of healthcare institution where they work (University, clinical centre, clinical-hospital centre, general hospital, primary healthcare institutions, non-governmental organization, or other institution), work in private or state sector or both, and academic education (specialization, fellowship/ master or doctoral degree). The second section referred to competencies that participants consider necessary for daily work. The participants were asked to rate each competency on a scale from 1- of little importance to 7- of very high importance. The third section of the research instrument referred to topics that participants considered important for CPDs. The participants were asked to rate each offered topic on a scale from 1- of little importance to 7- of very high importance. The fourth section referred to organizers, modalities of courses (live educational events, online educational events, or e-learning materials, blended learning events), days in which the courses should be organized. All items are given on a seven-point scale from 1- completely disagree to 7- completely agree. The final section referred to the practices regarding regular attendance of CPD programs, assessment of needs for CPD programs, types of programs, and modalities of programs. All items were given on a seven-point scale, and the participants were asked to rate each item from 1- not interested at all to 7- completely interested.

The questionnaire was distributed online through the mailing list of the Serbian Medical Chamber, and the filling-in was open for 30 days with two reminders in the same period. The population of 27,029 physicians was contacted through email, and 2,625 physicians filled in questionnaires (the population response rate was 9.7%). Looking at the design of the study and the method of implementation, a multi-stage sample was not used due to a large number of specialisations (in total 47) and subspecialisations (in total 58)(see Additional 2). Instead, we classified all participants based on their roles and the level of their competencies in the group of medical doctors (general practitioners with a focus on primary healthcare), specialists (have advanced training in a specific field of medicine and provide specialised care), and subspecialists (have further specialized training, focusing on more specific areas within a speciality).

All the participants received written explanations about the purpose of the study and its organizers, along with the number of ethical permits (Faculty of Medicine, University of Belgrade and Medical Chamber of Serbia). It was also explained that participation is voluntary and anonymous. Finally, it was stated that the participants were informed and agreed to participate if they filled out and submitted the questionnaire. The data from the questionnaire was collected in a way that minimized the amount of personal information gathered. During the analysis, personal data was pseudonymized and encrypted in accordance with the Law on Protection of Personal Data (“Official Gazette of RS”, No. 87/2018). After each participant completed the questionnaire, the database was stored on a separate computer using encryption software to prevent unauthorized access or data transfer. We implemented appropriate technical, personnel, and organizational measures to ensure security. The Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Belgrade, approved the study (the decision of the Ethical Committee No. 1322/V-9).

Statistical analysis

All listed competencies were classified into six domains: management, clinical practice, communication, public health, essential, and clinical emergency (see Additional file 1). The classification was conducted using triangulation of the results from the exploratory factor analyses, correlation coefficients between different competencies, and the concept of each competency. The concept of competencies was based on previous studies [40, 46, 47]. For all the domains, the ratings were computed to obtain the total score for each domain. A total of 12 variables were analyzed: the dependent variables were scores on each domain (management, clinical practice, communication, public health, essential and clinical emergency). The independent variables were sex, age, type of health care institution the participants worked in (hospital, institute, primary health care), institutional ownership (public, private, or both public and private), highest professional degree (medical doctor, specialist, subspecialist), highest academic degree (medical doctor, Magister or PhD level).

Statistical analyses involved descriptive and analytical methods. Normality was examined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Robust regression analyses served to examine the association between the potential factors associated with each dependent numerical variable without normal distribution (scores on examined domains: management, clinical practice, communication, public health, essential and clinical emergency). All analyses were done using the R statistical package (v4.2.2).

Results

This analysis included 2,625 physicians in Serbia. The average age of the participants was 44.5 ± 11.5 years. Most of the participants were females (1,845–70.3%), specialists (1,204–45.9%), and a quarter of the participants had a PhD as the highest scientific degree (656 − 25.0%). More than half reported working in hospitals (1,397–54.8%), and more than two-thirds worked in publicly owned institutions (2,022–77.0%). Regarding the highest professional degree, the results pointed that those physicians with specialisation judged as less important competencies in public health (-1.795, p = 0.046), group of essentials competencies (-2.901 p = 0.002|) and clinical emergency domain (-1.592, p < 0.01). Discussion

The need for sustainable paths in learning, renewing and improving the knowledge and skills of health professionals is today conditioned by the intensive development of science and evidence-based health services, constant changes in the health sector with the application of digital technologies, but also demographic challenges, accelerated development of social relations and increased burden of diseases and injuries [47, 48]. Continuing professional development in medicine, public health and other fields enables the realization of personal, professional and scientific potential, and it is also an individual process that should be complementary to collective perspectives. Therefore, physicians and other health professionals accept CME as a professional and ethical obligation that brings personal progress and benefits users, patients and society as a whole. In this context, induction CME programs attract particular interest in improving learning outcomes among trainees with different backgrounds and expectations [49].

In preparing this research, we conducted three rounds of the Delphi method, and the experts, who were members of program councils of the CME Centres at every University in Serbia, reached a consensus on the necessary competencies. After deploying the questionnaire to physician population, we obtained an acceptable response rate, which is set by other studies for online questionnaires to be more than 500 participants [50, 51].

Interestingly, more than half of the listed competencies were rated with a maximum of 7, possibly indicating that the participants in the Delphi method understood the importance of sustainable learning and defined different competencies applicable in physicians’ everyday work that continuously needs improvement.

Our study indicates that Serbia faces a similar situation in the organisation of continuing educational events compared to other countries. Serbia has a regulatory perspective with a well-developed conceptual framework (CME/CPD objectives and methods), which reflects specific contextual factors obtained during educational needs assessment [5, 9]. Except for theoretical frameworks and legal and organisational aspects related to CME/CPD for physicians, only a few countries performed assessments of competencies [18–22].

Six groups of competency domains for physicians in our study embrace clinical medicine, public health and managerial skills. Our findings align with previous research highlighting the importance of clinical practice, system thinking, communication, and management competencies. Several authors obtained similar results, slightly different (in the number of domains and their wording) but also oriented to clinical practice, system thinking, communication and management [19, 21–23]. However, our study uniquely emphasises the significant role of gender and workplace setting in shaping these competency needs. The self-assessed needs are associated with the areas each of the physicians considers as the areas that require improvement, which may not correlate with their level of skills [52]. Our analyses showed the association of scores in every domain and female sex. One notable characteristic of medical doctors in Serbia is the significantly higher percentage of women among employed medical doctors compared to men (68% vs. 32%) [53]. With a significant proportion of female physicians in Serbia’s medical workforce, there’s a notable emphasis on learning and staying updated. This might be linked to personal dedication and the societal expectation for continuous improvement and reliability in female-dominated healthcare roles. Some other authors found additional relations between female physicians and career advancement [54, 55]. Also, working in privately owned institutions was positively associated with the score in the communication domain, probably due to the need for better patient-physician communication and higher requirements for higher levels of patient satisfaction in privately owned institutions [56–58].

Regarding the public health domain of competencies besides the female sex, age was positively associated and the work in primary health care institutions. As the competencies classified as ‘public health’ in our study included the competencies necessary for the adequate conduction of health education, lifestyle improvement, and behavioral change techniques, the physicians at the primary health care level may have recognized all these competencies as valuable as they are part of capitation formula and can influence the physicians’ salary. Another factor that may have influenced the association of the work at the primary health care level and the score on public health domain was the recognition of the need for improvements in vaccination uptake, physical inactivity, poor diet, and the need for promotion of smoking cessation among the general population which are all considered as public health issues [59]. In Serbia, due to the high prevalence of risk behaviour in the population [53], physicians in primary health care have pointed to the need for strong competencies in the domain of public health. On the other hand, being a specialist was negatively associated with the score in the public health domain, which may also be because most of the public health issues are faced at the primary healthcare level. Specialists are commonly focused on specific skills and illnesses and rely on primary health care to deal with their patients’ common poor lifestyle choices.

Similar results were obtained for the domain of essential medical knowledge. Again, physicians at the primary health care level are often forced to work in many disciplines, while the specialists working in larger institutions may see the competencies within essential medical knowledge as necessary for other professionals in their team. These results align entirely with the results obtained with clinical emergency domain. Again, primary healthcare physicians may need competencies to deal with emergencies independently; specialists and subspecialists usually work in larger institutions and feel that they can rely on colleagues from other medical areas in emergencies [60].

To confirm these associations, we also conducted sensitivity analyses with different classifications of domains, in accordance with the recommendations of the Institute of Medicine Core Competencies and only related to the concept of the competencies, and we obtained almost identical results [18].

Our study has a few possible limitations. The first is in the Delphi process organization, as this has led us to obtain a higher number of competencies considered important than the initial list. However, this has enabled us to thoroughly analyze the numerous competencies possible. One of the limitations of our study refers to the low response rate. It is known from other studies that the response rate for online surveys among physicians can be low [61]. The reasons may include physicians’ heavy workload, inadequate perception of the importance of CME/CPD, or a general negative attitude towards online questionnaires. However, the number of responses in our study is still sufficiently large to conduct multivariate analyses and to draw conclusions that can be generalized to the population. Additionally, the large sample enabled the recognition of all these competencies as relevant.

The association of the different domains with the female sex, especially in work in various institutions at different levels, can successfully guide the development of CME programs directed to different physician populations. To modify CME programs effectively, considering the study’s findings, several practical strategies can be implemented that reflect the Serbian [32, 36, 42, 53] and global context [2, 5]. These adjustments have to enhance learning outcomes for all physicians, acknowledging and accepting the preferences observed among female participants and addressing broader cultural and professional dynamics. For example, given female physicians’ solid preferences for communication and interpersonal skills, CME programs could incorporate more case-based discussions, role-playing, and interactive workshops. These approaches can foster collaborative learning that benefits both male and female participants, giving strengths in communication and empathy. Also, following our study results, CME programs should continue integrating core competencies such as patient-centred care, teamwork, and evidence-based practice into the CME curriculum. Competency-based training that includes real-life scenarios, patient simulations, and feedback loops can improve physicians’ ability to apply knowledge effectively in clinical settings [9, 12, 52]. The focus should also be on strengthening the CME competencies of primary healthcare physicians.

Conclusion

This research contributes to the recognition of gaps in knowledge and the design of continuous professional development and continuous medical education based on established competency groups developed with the consensus of experts. Our research recognized gaps in knowledge and design of CME/CPD programs and identified competency groups developed with the consensus of experts. The potential actions include integrating key competencies and developing online self-assessment tools for CME/CPD competencies in everyday practice for physicians and program organisers. Combining key areas with multiple models in a certain number of groups with consensus can enable a new organization of key competencies needed by physicians and the exclusion of imposed competencies that are more suitable for administrators and not evidence-based. Based on this research, online tools for self-assessment can be developed further to target the competencies of physicians necessary to integrate in CME/CPD programs.
