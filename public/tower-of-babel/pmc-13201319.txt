Development of a double-antibody sandwich ELISA for detecting Peste des petits ruminants virus infection
Authors: Haoyue Xu, Xueliang Zhu, Qian Li, Yuxi Liu, Qi Wang, Xuelian Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeste des petits ruminants (PPR) is an acute, highly contagious viral disease caused by Peste des petits ruminants virus (PPRV). PPR has inflicted severe economic losses on livestock industries across numerous countries in Africa, Asia, and Europe, posing a significant threat to the survival of endangered wild ruminant species. In this study, we performed prokaryotic expression of the PPRV nucleocapsid (N) protein and prepared rabbit-derived polyclonal antibodies (pAbs). We also identified the characteristics of two mouse-derived monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) against the PPRV N protein. The selected mAb was labeled with horseradish peroxidase (HRP). A double antibody sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (DAS-ELISA) was established to detect PPRV antigen using rabbit-derived pAbs and HRP-conjugated mAbs. The threshold for the DAS-ELISA was determined to be 0.227. The DAS-ELISA demonstrated minimum detection limits of 0.195 μg/mL for the recombinant PPRV N protein and 250 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) for PPRV. Of note, the assay exhibited no cross-reactivity with Capripoxvirus (CaPV), Orf virus (ORFV), Lumpy skin disease virus (LSDV), Foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), or Parainfluenza virus 5 (PIV5). The repeatability of the DAS-ELISA was validated with both intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV%) being less than 9%, indicating good repeatability. Compared to the results obtained using the commercial ID Vet kit (ID-Vet, Montpellier, France) for PPRV antigen detection on the 240 tissue samples, the DAS-ELISA showed a concordance rate of 93.75% and a kappa value of 0.86, demonstrating the reliability of the developed method. The results showed that the DAS-ELISA established in this study is a reliable diagnostic method for the PPRV detection.Key points• Polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies against PPRV N protein were prepared.• A DAS-ELISA based on mAbs and pAbs for detecting virus infection was developed.• The established DAS-ELISA exhibits excellent sensitivity and specificity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13824-y.

Introduction

Peste des petits ruminants virus (PPRV) is a member of the genus Morbillivirus within the family Paramyxoviridae. The virions exhibit a pleomorphic morphology, with particle sizes ranging from 400–500 nm (Haffar et al. 1999). The PPRV genome sequence is 15,948 nucleotides long and encodes eight proteins: nucleocapsid protein (N), phosphoprotein (P), matrix protein (M), fusion protein (F), hemagglutinin-neuraminidase protein (HN), and large RNA polymerase protein (L), along with two non-structural proteins C and V (Bailey et al. 2005). Furthermore, the N protein, being a highly conserved and abundant structural protein within the genus Morbillivirus, exhibits strong immunogenicity and is expressed at high levels. Consequently, the N protein is commonly employed as a target for serological screening in both naturally infected animals and those vaccinated against PPR. It also serves as an excellent candidate antigen for differentiating infected animals from those vaccinated with recombinant marker vaccines expressing the F or H proteins (Choi et al. 2005).

PPRV exhibits a broad host range, infecting 46 mammalian species across nine families, including Bovidae, Camelidae, Elephantidae, Suidae, and Canidae (Munir 2014). Infection typically manifests clinically as fever, ocular and nasal discharge, erosive stomatitis, and diarrhea (Bailey et al. 2005). The resulting disease, peste des petits ruminants (PPR), is characterized by high prevalence and mortality. It not only compromises the livelihoods of impoverished communities economically dependent on small ruminants globally (Banyard et al. 2010) but also threatens endangered wildlife populations, posing a significant risk of genetic depletion in susceptible species (Kamel and El-Sayed 2019). Consequently, PPR is classified as a notifiable disease by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) and the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO).

To safeguard food security in endemic regions, alleviate the burden on impoverished populations, and protect wildlife, WOAH and FAO have established a global strategy for the control and eradication of PPR (Legnardi et al. 2022). Central to the eradication program is large-scale vaccination aimed at achieving disease elimination. Consequently, pathogen screening in unvaccinated animal populations and wildlife reservoirs represents a critical component of this effort.

Currently, laboratory detection methods for PPRV antigens include direct detection techniques such as virus isolation, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Saravanan et al. 2006; Balamurugan et al. 2023), which target PPRV and its antigenic substances. Additionally, molecular biology methods such as reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), multiplex PCR, real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR (qPCR), and loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) are used for detecting PPRV genetic material (Parida et al. 2015). Among these, ELISA is widely employed for rapid detection due to its characteristics of high throughput, straightforward operational procedures, minimal requirement for sophisticated instrumentation, and relatively low cost. Therefore, in order to develop a convenient, cost-effective method suitable for large-scale clinical sample testing at the grassroots level, we established a double-antibody sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (DAS-ELISA) method for detecting PPRV and tissue samples by combining rabbit-derived polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) with mouse-derived monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). This method exhibits excellent specificity, sensitivity, and repeatability, making it applicable to both clinical detection and monitoring of PPRV.

Materials and methods

Cells, viruses, animals and samples

African green monkey kidney (Vero) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; CCL-81) and stored in our laboratory. The PPRV vaccine strain Nigeria 75/1, Capripoxvirus (CaPV), Orf virus (ORFV), Lumpy skin disease virus (LSDV), Foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), and Parainfluenza virus 5 (PIV5), recombinant expression vector pET30a-N carrying the PPRV N gene of strain Nigeria 75/1 (GenBank Accession No. X74443), hybridoma cell lines (clones 10–3 and 13–3) producing mAbs against the PPRV N protein, were obtained from the National Key Laboratory for Animal Disease Control and Prevention (Lanzhou, China). Rabbits and mice were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. 240 tissue samples including heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and lymph node were collected at commercial abattoirs, 100 samples were collected from goat in vaccine-immunized areas, and 140 samples from in PPR-free zones.

Antigen preparation

The recombinant expression vector pET30a-N carrying the PPRV N gene was transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) (Sangon, Shanghai, China). The expression of the recombinant PPRV N protein was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Aladdin, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 8 h in a fermenter, followed by optimization of temperature (16, 20, 28, and 37 °C), induction time (8, 12, 16, and 20 h), IPTG concentration (0.2, 0.4, 0.8, and 1 mM), and cultivation system (shaker vs. fermenter). Inclusion bodies and soluble fractions were separated. The recombinant PPRV N protein was purified by affinity chromatography using Chelating Sepharose Fast Flow resin (Cytive, Uppsala, Sweden), and eluted with an imidazole gradient (50–500 mM). Purified protein was analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and western blotting, and quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Preparation and characterization of pAbs and mAbs against the PPRV N Protein

PAbs and mAbs were generated using rabbits and mice obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Lanzhou, China). PAbs were generated by immunizing three 8–12-week-old rabbits with 500 μg/each of purified recombinant PPRV N protein emulsified in Freund’s complete adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), followed by two booster immunizations with Freund’s incomplete adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at two-week intervals. Sera were collected when the antibody titer reached 1:4,000 by indirect ELISA. MAbs were produced from hybridoma cell lines 10–3 and 13–3. Female BALB/c mice (8–10 weeks old), primed with sterile paraffin one week earlier, were injected intraperitoneally with hybridoma cells; ascites fluid was harvested approximately 10 days post-injection upon signs of abdominal distension and lethargy. Both rabbit sera and mouse ascites were precipitated with saturated ammonium sulfate and purified by Protein G column (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Purified antibodies were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, quantified by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and titrated by indirect ELISA.

Indirect ELISA

An indirect ELISA was developed and optimized to titrate antibodies against PPRV N protein. Plates were coated with 100 μL/well of recombinant PPRV N protein (0.2 μg/well) or PPRV (1:50, 1:100 dilutions) overnight at 4 °C, then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 1 h at 37 °C, and incubated with diluted pAbs or mAbs for 1 h at 37 °C. Bound antibodies were detected using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies—goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000; BBI, Shanghai, China) or donkey anti-mouse IgG (1:3000 ~ 1:5000; BBI, Shanghai, China) for 1 h and 3,3',5,5'-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Surmodics, Eden Prairie, MN, USA) for 15 min in the dark at 37 °C. The reaction was stopped with 2 M H₂SO₄, and OD450 values were measured using a SpectraMax Plus384 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Characterization of mAbs and peroxidase labeling

Physicochemical properties of mAbs

The physicochemical stability of each mAb was evaluated under acidic (pH 2.2 HCl), alkaline (0.05 mol/L Na2CO3-NaHCO3 buffer, pH 9.6), and thermal (56 °C) conditions. Antibodies were diluted 1:10 in acidic or alkaline buffers were stored at 4 °C, while those in PBS were incubated at 56 °C. Samples were collected at various time points (4 ~ 48 h), and antibody stability was assessed by measuring OD450 via indirect ELISA.

Antigenic epitopes of mAbs

The saturation dilution point for each mAb was determined by serial dilution corresponding to maximal antigen binding. In indirect ELISA, OD450 values for individual mAbs were recorded as A₁ and A₂, and that for their combination as A1+2. The additivity index (AI) was calculated as: AI = ((2 × A₁₊₂/(A₁ + A₂)) − 1) × 100% (Zhang et al. 2021). Epitope specificity was interpreted based on established thresholds: AI < 50% indicates recognition of the same epitope, while AI ≥ 50% indicates distinct epitopes.

Peroxidase-labeled mAbs

mAbs 10–3 and 13–3 were labeled using Peroxidase Labeling Kit-NH2 (DOJINDO, Kumamoto, Japan). Following the kit protocol, mAbs were incubated with the pre-prepared NH2-reactive peroxidase solution, thoroughly mixed, and then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After washing and centrifugation, the peroxidase-labeled mAbs were obtained and stored in storage buffer.

Western blotting

Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk-Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20 (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature, then sequentially incubated with primary antibodies (sheep anti-PPRV positive serum, mAb 10–3, pAb No.2) at 4 °C overnight and corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (rabbit anti-sheep IgG, donkey anti-mouse IgG, and donkey anti-rabbit IgG; all from Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 1.5 h at room temperature with shaking. Finally, chemiluminescence detection was performed with an ECL kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and the signals were visualized by exposure.

Establishment of a DAS‑ELISA

To screen antibody pairs for DAS-ELISA, an orthogonal assay was performed. Capture antibodies—mAbs (10–3 and 13–3) and pAbs (No.1, No.2, No.3)—were individually diluted to 2 μg/mL in carbonate buffer, then coated onto 96-well plates (100 μL/well) overnight at 4 °C, and blocked with 1% casein buffer (150 μL/well; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). After washing, plates were incubated with either PPRV diluted at 1:32 (positive control, P) or uninfected Vero cell supernatant (negative control, N) at 100 μL/well. Following additional washing, HRP-conjugated detection antibodies (heterologous to the capture antibodies) were added. For wells with HRP-conjugated mAbs as detection antibodies, chromogenic reaction was conducted directly; for wells using unlabeled pAbs as detection antibodies, HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG was added. OD450 was measured, and optimal pairs were selected based on maximal P/N ratios.

Subsequently, checkerboard titration was performed to optimize the DAS-ELISA conditions. The optimized parameters included capture antibody (pAb No.2) concentration (0.1 and 0.2 μg/well), blocking buffer type (1% BSA, 1% bovine serum, and casein buffer), and HRP-conjugated mAb 10–3 dilution (1:4000, 1:6000, and 1:8000). The same positive (PPRV diluted at 1:32) and negative (Vero cell supernatant) controls were used. Tissue homogenates were diluted in different sample buffers, including RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), PBS, and 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The optimal conditions were determined by comparing P/N ratios.

Cut-off value of the DAS‑ELISA

Under optimized assay conditions, 96 known-negative ovine tissue specimens were analyzed using the DAS-ELISA. Tissue samples were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer, with the resulting homogenates clarified by centrifugation prior to supernatant collection for testing. The assay cut-off value was established as the mean OD450 of these 96 negative control samples plus 3 standard deviations (mean OD450 + 3SD).

Sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility of the DAS‑ELISA

Gradient dilutions of PPRV (from 8000 TCID50 to 7.8125 TCID50) and serial dilutions of the PPRV N protein (from 100 μg/mL to 0.03 μg/mL) were separately assayed to determine the limit of detection of the DAS-ELISA method, which corresponds to the sensitivity of the assay. Using the optimized DAS-ELISA protocol, six viruses—including PPRV, PIV5, ORFV, CaPV, FMDV and LSDV—were tested to evaluate the specificity of the method. To assess the repeatability of the DAS-ELISA, the coefficient of variation (CV%) was analyzed for six tissue samples across both intra-batch (same plate) and inter-batch (different plates) assays. Each serum sample was tested in triplicate on each plate, with three plates processed simultaneously to determine inter-assay variability. The mean value of the sample OD450 (x̄) and the SD value were calculated. The coefficient of variation (CV%) was calculated as (SD/x̄).

Concordance rate test

240 tissue samples were concurrently analyzed using the established DAS-ELISA and the commercial ID Screen® Peste des Petits Ruminants Virus ELISA Antigen Detection Kit (ID-Vet, Montpellier, France). According to the detection results between the two methods, the samples were classified as: a, true positive (both methods positive); b, false positive (DAS-ELISA positive only); c, false negative (DAS-ELISA negative only); d, true negative (both methods negative). Methodological agreement was evaluated through calculation of both the concordance rate [(a + d)/n × 100%, n = total number of samples] and its 95% confidence interval (CI) using the Wilson Score method. Additionally, Cohen's kappa value was calculated from the formulas: PA = (a + d)/n; PC = [(a + b) (a + c) + (c + d) (b + d)]/n2; Kappa = (PA—PC)/(1—PC), with its 95% CI derived from the standard error. The Kappa values reflecting the diagnostic consistency level between two classification methods: 0.81 ~ 1.00 (almost perfect agreement), 0.61 ~ 0.80 (substantial), 0.41 ~ 0.60 (moderate), 0.21 ~ 0.40 (fair), and 0 ~ 0.20 (slight).

Statistical analysis

The DAS-ELISA employed a checkerboard titration method to optimize experimental conditions, and data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Preparation of PPRV N protein

Based on SDS-PAGE analysis, the PPRV N protein was expressed in inclusion bodies (Supplementary Fig. S1). Considering cost-effectiveness, the optimal conditions were determined to be: temperature 37 °C (Supplementary Fig. S2), induction time 8 h (Supplementary Fig. S3), IPTG concentration 0.8 mM (Supplementary Fig. S4), and using a fermenter (Supplementary Fig. S5). The high-purity recombinant N protein at 65 kDa with high-yield expression in the fermenter was obtained (Supplemental Fig. S6). Western blotting with sheep anti-PPRV positive serum as the primary antibody demonstrated that the purified PPRV N recombinant protein has good reactivity (Supplemental Fig. S7).

Development of an indirect ELISA

An indirect ELISA was developed to identify the characteristics of mAbs and detect the serum titer of immunized rabbits. The coating concentration of PPRV and N protein, enzyme-labeled antibody type, and its dilution fold were optimized using a checkerboard titration method (Supplemental Fig. S8). Based on the OD450 values and P/N values, samples with a P/N value > 2.1 were defined as positive. The optimal coating concentrations were 1:50 (PPRV) and 0.2 μg/well (PPRV N protein), while the optimal dilution of HRP-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG and goat anti-rabbit IgG were 1:3000 and 1:10,000, respectively.

Preparation and characterization of pAbs and mAbs against the PPRV N protein

Development of the DAS-ELISA

To screen optimal antibody pairs for DAS-ELISA, combinations of capture and detection antibodies were systematically evaluated. The highest P/N ratio (5.647) was achieved using pAb No.2 as capture antibody and HRP-conjugated mAb 10–3 as detector (Supplementary Table S2). Subsequent optimization identified the following conditions: capture antibody coating at 0.2 μg/well (P/N = 5.303; Fig. 2A), casein blocking buffer (P/N = 6.295; Fig. 2B), HRP-conjugated mAb 10–3 at 1:6000 dilution (P/N = 17.385; Fig. 2C), and RIPA as sample dilution buffer (P/N = 3.478; Fig. 2D).Fig. 2Optimization of DAS-ELISA reaction conditions. The optimized DAS-ELISA protocol was established as follows: 96-well plates were coated with pAb No.2 (2 μg/mL in carbonate buffer, 100 μL/well) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBST, plates were blocked with 1% casein buffer (150 μL/well) for 1 h at 37 °C. Samples (100 μL/well) were added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated mAb 10–3 (1:6000, 100 μL/well) for 1 h at 37 °C. After final washing, TMB substrate (100 μL/well) was added and incubated for 15 min in the dark at 37 °C. The reaction was terminated with 2 M H₂SO₄ (50 μL/well), and OD450 was measured.

The DAS-ELISA established in this study was applied to 96 known negative tissue samples (Fig. 3). The mean optical density at OD450 was 0.122 with a standard deviation (SD) of 0.035. Based on the cut-off value calculation formula (mean OD450 + 3SD), the diagnostic threshold for the DAS-ELISA was determined to be 0.227 (0.122 + 3 × 0.035). Inter-batch and intra-batch repeatability

Concordance rate test

Two hundred forty tissue samples were concurrently analyzed using the established DAS-ELISA and the ID-Vet kit. Methodological concordance was evaluated using percentage agreement and Kappa value. Discussion

The transboundary spread of PPRV outbreaks has generated a dual crisis: On the one hand, it threatens 300 million impoverished rural households globally that depend on small ruminants as their primary livestock resource, exposing them to risks of losing livelihoods, food security, and employment opportunities; on the other hand, it has caused a year-on-year decline in susceptible endangered wild animal populations. Between 2016 and 2017, for instance, PPR led to an 80% reduction in the population of the endangered antilope Saiga tatarica mongolica (Pruvot et al. 2020). Therefore, the development of cost-effective diagnostic technologies has become a key component for advancing the 2032 Global PPR Eradication Programme (Fine et al. 2020).

Current diagnostic methods for PPRV primarily include virus isolation (Fakri et al. 2016), RT-PCR (Forsyth and Barrett 1995), qPCR (Bao et al. 2008), LAMP (Mahapatra et al. 2019), and ELISA (Saravanan et al. 2004; Singh et al. 2004). Although virus isolation is recognized as the "gold standard" with high specificity, it is time-consuming and labor-intensive, making it unsuitable for rapid outbreak response and large-scale routine surveillance. Highly sensitive molecular techniques, epitomized by qPCR, have largely supplanted conventional RT-PCR. However, their stringent requirements for laboratory conditions, specialized equipment, and skilled personnel, coupled with a susceptibility to contamination and false positives, limit their utility in resource-constrained field settings (Banyard et al. 2010). In contrast, ELISA technology, based on the specific recognition of antigens-antibody, offers a favorable balance of sensitivity and specificity alongside operational simplicity. The DAS-ELISA, which employs capture and detection antibodies targeting distinct epitopes of the viral antigen, can effectively minimize cross-reactivity and enhance detection sensitivity (Butler 2000) Although commercial PPRV antigen detection ELISA kits (e.g., from ID-Vet) are available, their relatively high cost may hinder widespread application for grassroots-level and large-scale screening. Therefore, the present study developed a DAS-ELISA for PPRV detection by employing a pair of pAb and mAb. Parallel testing with the commercial ID-Vet kit on 240 clinical tissue samples demonstrated a concordance rate of 93.75% with a kappa coefficient of 0.87, confirming almost perfect diagnostic agreement and reliability of the developed DAS-ELISA. Combined with the cost advantage afforded by our self-developed antibodies, this method presents a high-performance and economical alternative to existing commercial kits.

The DAS-ELISA established in this study is based on a combination of rabbit-derived pAbs for capture and mouse-derived mAbs for detection. The use of pAbs, which recognize multiple epitopes, enables efficient and broad-spectrum capture of the antigen, thereby enhancing assay sensitivity. Simultaneously, the high specificity of mAbs for a single epitope ensures detection specificity and minimizes cross-reactivity (Getachew et al. 2018). The high sensitivity observed in this study (with limits of detection of 0.195 µg/mL for PPRV N protein and 250 TCID50 for the whole virus) and the absence of cross-reactivity against other susceptible animal viruses (such as FMDV and LSDV) corroborate the effectiveness of this assay.

The global PPR eradication strategy is structured around four sequential stages: assessment, control, eradication, and post-eradication (Zhao et al. 2021), and the DAS-ELISA developed in this study holds practical significance for each stage. During the initial assessment phase, it enables rapid confirmation of PPRV infection, providing direct evidence for mapping the disease situation and enabling timely response to outbreaks. In the control phase, integrated with large-scale vaccination campaigns, the method can support the establishment and monitoring of PPR-free zones (Balamurugan et al. 2024). In the eradication phase, it can be combined with highly sensitive molecular techniques such as qPCR, providing multi-layered evidence to verifying PPRV-free status. Even in the post-eradication phase, the assay remains valuable for ongoing surveillance at border points and in wildlife populations to prevent virus reintroduction (World Organisation for Animal Health 2024).

Despite its promising performance, several key limitations of this method need to be addressed in future research. First, during the development phase, the most relevant and readily standardized antigens—PPRV viral particles and purified N protein—were used to establish a consistent benchmark. However, for the assay to be applicable for clinical testing in both domestic and wild susceptible animals, validation across a wider range of clinical sample types is essential, including sera, oral/nasal swabs, and various tissue samples, to comprehensively confirm its universality and reliability. Consistent with this, previous antigen capture ELISA development studies have emphasized that validation across diverse clinical matrices is critical for establishing diagnostic utility, with evaluations primarily focused on sheep and goat specimens (Balamurugan et al. 2021). Second, reagent standardization and batch-to-batch consistency pose another challenge. The pAbs used in this study were sourced from a single rabbit, which could introduce variability between batches. To ensure reagent consistency, large-scale production of pAbs will be implemented, with standardized protocols for animal immunization, antibody purification, and titer determination to minimize such variability. Third, reaction conditions need further optimized to enhance field applicability, including shortening incubation times and expanding the incubation temperature range, which will improve usability in resource-limited settings. Additionally, a notable limitation of the DAS-ELISA is its inability to distinguish between infected and immunized animals, which is due to the use of pAbs and mAbs targeting the N protein of the PPRV Nigeria 75/1 vaccine strain. This limitation is widely recognized within the field—the immune response induced by current live attenuated vaccines is difficult to distinguish from natural infection in routine serological assays, underscoring the need for improved diagnostic approaches (Selvaraj et al. 2021). Nevertheless, with the development and deployment of novel PPR vaccines (e.g., engineered marker vaccines), the demand for matching DAS-ELISA assays will be increasingly urgent (Niedbalski 2024). By modifying the antibody pairs to specifically target infection-related antigen or markers, the DAS-ELISA platform can be adapted to establish diagnostic assays for differentiation of infected from immunized animals. This advancement is critical for monitoring the progress of eradication efforts and will strongly support the global PPR eradication target.

In conclusion, the present study successfully developed a cost-effective DAS-ELISA for PPRV detection that combines high sensitivity, specificity, and reliability. Given the use of PPRV N protein as the diagnostic target, the primary limitation is its inability to distinguish between infected and immunized animals. Future work focusing on expanding the assay's validation, standardizing reagents and reaction conditions will further strengthen its clinical and field application potential.
