Beam Cross Sections Create Mixtures: Improving Feature Localization in Secondary Electron Imaging
Vaibhav Choudhary, Akshay Agarwal, Vivek K Goyal

Abstract
Secondary electron (SE) imaging techniques, such as scanning electron microscopy and helium ion microscopy (HIM), use electrons emitted by a sample in response to a focused beam of charged particles incident at a grid of raster scan positions. Spot size—the diameter of the incident beam’s spatial profile—is one of the limiting factors for resolution, along with various sources of noise in the SE signal. The effect of the beam spatial profile is commonly understood as convolutional. We show that under a simple and plausible physical abstraction for the beam, though convolution describes the mean of the SE counts, the full distribution of SE counts is a mixture. We demonstrate that this more detailed modeling can enable resolution improvements over conventional estimators through a stylized application in semiconductor inspection of localizing the edge in a two-valued sample. We derive Fisher information about edge location in conventional and time-resolved measurements (TRM) and also derive the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) from the latter. Empirically, the MLE computed from TRM is approximately efficient except at very low beam diameter, so Fisher information comparisons are predictive of performance and can be used to optimize the beam diameter relative to the raster scan spacing. Monte Carlo simulations show that the MLE gives a 5-fold reduction in root mean-squared error (RMSE) of edge localization as compared to conventional interpolation-based estimation. Applied to three real HIM datasets, the average RMSE reduction factor is 5.4.

In this paper, we complement the framework of previous works with

Our main assertion is that nonzero beam cross-sectional area causes the SE count
due to a single incident particle to follow a mixture distribution.
This contrasts with a convolutional model that is intuitive because,

Indeed, convolutional modeling is conventional in scanning electron microscopy

As a prototypical problem in semiconductor metrology,

This simplified imaging-based inspection problem allows us to

Monte Carlo simulations

improvement relative a conventional interpolation method is by a factor of 5.
On experimental HIM data, we observe an an average RMSE reduction factor of 1.5 with respect to a convolution model and 5.4 with respect to interpolation.

II-A

Model of Incident Particle Beam

As shown in the cartoon of
Fig.

1
(a),
incident particles are haphazard in both transverse spatial positions and times of incidence.
We model the beam temporally as arrivals in
a Poisson process with a known rate
\Lambda
per unit time (see
Fig.

1
(b)).
Let
t
denote the pixel dwell time.
Then the number of particles
M
incident on a pixel is a Poisson random variable with parameter
\lambda=\Lambda t
.
We refer to
\lambda
as the
dose
;
normalization by beam cross-sectional area or raster scan density is helpful when assessing likelihood of sample damage.
Recall that
M
is not directly observable; its variation from
\mathrm{E}[\,{M}\,]=\lambda
is called
source shot noise
.

The position of an incident particle
deviates
from the nominal raster scan position.
We model this with a circularly symmetric 2D Gaussian distribution:

\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},W_{2})\sim{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}I_{2})}

(see
Fig.

1
(c)).
We will refer to the beam standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}
as the
beam width
;
we could instead use an equivalent specification of full width at half maximum (FWHM).

II-B

Model of Sample

As noted in the introduction,
SE imaging generally shows the variation in SE yield.
To work toward a precise understanding of the effect of the beam cross-section,
we make a distinction between the SE yield at a scan position
and the underlying SE yield of the sample at an incidence location.
For this, we require a continuous-space description of the sample even though data is collected by discrete raster scanning of the beam.

We normalize the transverse spatial dimensions of the sample by
the imaging raster scan spacing so that
raster scan locations are on the grid
\{0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,\ell-1\}^{2}
.
Our mathematical model of the sample
is not restricted to the raster scan grid
because we wish to model effects of the beam cross section and
the potential for sub-pixel resolution.
Thus, the sample is defined as a function

\eta:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty)

that maps from the ion incidence position
(which, due to nonzero beam width, is not exactly a raster scan position)
to the SE yield.

When an ion is incident at
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})
, the number of emitted SEs is not deterministically
\eta(\mathbf{s})
.
Distinct mechanisms of emission have been established.
Potential emission depends mostly on incident particle charge
and results in SE counts that can be modeled as binomial
[
35
,
36
]
;
it is especially significant with multiply-charged projectiles
[
37
,
38
]
.
Kinetic emission depends mostly on incident particle mass and velocity
and results in SE counts that can be modeled as Poisson when
cascade effects are minimal and Pólya more generally
[
39
]
.
Due to the combination of mechanisms and the crudeness of the theoretical models,
no single parametric model fits measured data well across a wide range of conditions.
Many studies of SE emission and all previous SE imaging methods,
to the best of our knowledge,
treat the number of SEs as a Poisson random variable, including effects of nonzero beam width.
Here, we assume the SE counts are Poisson distributed when effects of nonzero beam width are
not
included.

The problem of SE imaging in general is the estimation of the function
\eta
,
usually on a subset of the domain such as
[0,\,\ell-1]^{2}

or only on the scan grid
\{0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,\ell-1\}^{2}
.
In
Section

II-D
,
we will restrict
\eta
to model a two-valued sample with a single axis-aligned edge.

II-C

Models of SE Distribution for One Incident Particle

Under the beam and sample models in
Sections

II-A
and
II-B
,
one incident ion at grid scan location
\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},g_{2})

will actually strike the sample at
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{W}
,
where
\mathbf{W}\sim{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}I_{2})}
.
Since the sample has SE yield
\eta(\mathbf{s})
for this incidence position,
the random number of generated SEs is

X\sim\mathop{\mathrm{Poisson}}(\eta(\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{W})).

(1)

Since the random quantity
\eta(\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{W})
is the parameter of a Poisson random variable,
this
X
has
a
Poisson mixture

distribution
[
40
]
.

By iterated expectation,
the mean of
X
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{X}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{X\,|\,\mathbf{W}}\,\right]}\,\right]=\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{\eta(\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{W})}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\eta(\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{w})f_{\mathbf{W}}(w_{1},w_{2})\,\mathsf{d}w_{1}\,\mathsf{d}w_{2}.

(2)

The convolutional form in (
2
) is reminiscent of a linear blur kernel in an optical system.
It is important, however, to make the distinction between describing the full distribution
or merely the mean.
Since SE distributions are often assumed to be Poisson, we will refer to

X_{\rm convolution}\sim\mathop{\mathrm{Poisson}}(\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{\eta(\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{W})}\,\right])

(3)

as the
convolutional model
for
X
.

The mixture and convolutional models are defined to match in mean.
A simple way to illustrate that they are not equal in distribution is to compute variances.
Since the convolutional model is Poisson distributed, its variance is plainly

\mathrm{var}\!\left({X_{\rm convolution}}\right)=\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{\eta(\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{W})}\,\right].

(4)

For the mixture model, the variance is straightforward using the law of total variance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}\!\left({X}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{\mathrm{var}\!\left({X\,|\,W}\right)}\,\right]+\mathrm{var}\!\left({\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{X\,|\,W}\,\right]}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{\eta(\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{W})}\,\right]+\mathrm{var}\!\left({\eta(\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{W})}\right).

(5)

We will refer to

\mathrm{var}\!\left({X}\right)-\mathrm{var}\!\left({X_{\rm convolution}}\right)=\mathrm{var}\!\left({\eta(\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{W})}\right)

(6)

as the
excess variance
of the mixture model.
It
is nonnegative,
and it is zero only in the limit of
\eta
have no variation
in the neighborhood of the nominal scan location
\mathbf{g}
, where the relevant neighborhood size depends on
\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}
.

We will reinterpret
(
3
)–(
6
)
after restricting
our attention to samples with a single straight edge (
Section

II-D
).
With that restriction, we will examine the estimability
of
\eta
under the two models (
Section

II-E
).

II-E

SE Yield Estimation with Deterministic Number of Ions

Let us now consider how the mixture model improves the ability to estimate SE yield.
Suppose one is able to observe SE counts

X_{1},\,X_{2},\,\ldots,\,X_{m}

from
m
incident ions.
In the absence of a sophisticated model,
one would conventionally estimate the mean

\mathrm{E}\!\left[\,{X}\,\right]
using the sample mean of the observations,

\widehat{\eta}_{\mathrm{conv}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}.

(14)

The sample mean is unbiased, and by substitution
of (
11
),
its variance is

\mathrm{var}\!\left({\widehat{\eta}_{\mathrm{conv}}}\right)=\frac{1}{m}\left[(1-q)\eta_{1}+q\eta_{2}+q(1-q)(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}\right].

(15)

Furthermore, under a Poisson model as in (
12
),
the sample mean is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)
and the minimum-variance unbiased estimate
of the distribution’s parameter.

Using the mixture model,
we can improve the quality of the SE yield estimate.
To derive a Cramér–Rao bound (CRB),
we start by calculating the Fisher information (FI) about
q
in
X
, with
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
assumed given, which we denote

\mathcal{I}_{X}(q;\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
.

From (
9
),
the probability mass function (PMF) of
X
is

\mathrm{P}_{X}(x\,;\,q,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=(1-q)\frac{\eta_{1}^{x}e^{-\eta_{1}}}{x!}+q\frac{\eta_{2}^{x}e^{-\eta_{2}}}{x!}.

(16)

By substitution,
computing derivatives, and simplifying,
one can find

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{X}(q\,;\,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x!}\frac{\left(\eta_{1}^{x}e^{-\eta_{1}}-\eta_{2}^{x}e^{-\eta_{2}}\right)^{2}}{(1-q)\eta_{1}^{x}e^{-\eta_{1}}+q\eta_{2}^{x}e^{-\eta_{2}}}.

(17)

Since FI is additive,
the reciprocal of

m\,\mathcal{I}_{X}(q\,;\,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})

lower bounds the variance of an unbiased estimate for
mixture parameter

q
computed from the response to
m
ions.
Based on (
10
),
one would form an estimate of SE yield
\eta
from an estimate for

q

\widehat{\eta}=(1-\widehat{q})\eta_{1}+\widehat{q}\eta_{2}=\eta_{1}+(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})\widehat{q}.

(18)

With the variance scaling implied by
(
18

we obtain

\mathrm{var}\!\left({\widehat{\eta}}\right)\geq\frac{(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}}{m\,\mathcal{I}_{X}(q\,;\,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})}.

(19)

Measurement Models with Source Shot Noise

,

In a fixed dwell time

As illustrated in

Since the case of

From the oracle observation (

In

This type of two-valued sample is inspired by semiconductor inspection problems, where an edge could be between a metal nanostructure and a silicon substrate.
Having established several results in

The precision of such

The localization here is not limited to the grid scan locations and hence represents a type of super-resolution.

Current practice is represented by linear interpolation of the baseline estimate of SE yield at each scan location.
We also introduces an MLE formulation for TRM data under the conventional convolutional model

Comparisons based on Monte Carlo simulations appear in

V-B

Edge Location Estimates

Interpolation:

The current practice of SE imaging is based on computing the baseline estimate (
28
) at each pixel prior to any subsequent processing.
In the absence of an explicit model for the effect of the beam cross section,
one could declare an edge location to be where the estimated SE yield crosses a threshold value.
For example, the edge location could be estimated at the location where the linearly interpolated estimated SE yield equals
(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2})/2
.
In our specific case of left-to-right scanning from lower SE yield to higher SE yield,
as illustrated in
Fig.

2
(b),
the interpolated SE yield estimate function may cross the threshold multiple times.
When this happens, we use the sample mean of all the upward crossings.

Maximum likelihood estimate:

Formulating the MLE of
\gamma
from a full scan of TRM data,

\{(\widetilde{m}_{k},\{\widetilde{x}_{i,k}\}_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}_{k}})\}_{k=0}^{\ell-1}
,
where
k
indexes the scan location,
is a matter of collecting the likelihood expressions from earlier sections.
For any candidate edge location
\gamma
and each horizontal raster scan location
k
,
(
8
)
provides the corresponding mixing parameter
q_{k}
.
The likelihood of observing

\widetilde{m}_{k}

is given by the Poisson distribution with parameter

\lambda\rho_{k}
,
where

\rho_{k}

is given by (
53
); and
the likelihood for each
\widetilde{x}_{i,k}
is given by (
55
).
Combining these over all
\ell
raster scan positions gives the likelihood of the full data set,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scan}}(\gamma)=\prod_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\mathop{\mathrm{Poisson}}(\widetilde{m}_{k}\,;\,\lambda\rho_{k})\prod_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}_{k}}\mathrm{P}_{\widetilde{X}}(\widetilde{x}_{i,k}\,;\,q_{k},\eta_{1},\eta_{2}),

(70)

where
\rho_{k}
and
q_{k}
are functions of
\gamma
,
k
,
\eta_{1}
,
\eta_{2}
, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}
.
Maximizing this scalar function of
\gamma
gives the MLE:

\widehat{\gamma}_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\argmax_{\gamma}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{scan}}(\gamma).

(71)

Mismatched MLE:

To isolate the effect of the mixture model
from the effect of TRM, we also consider an estimator that maximizes the likelihood for Poisson-distributed data;
i.e., the estimator uses the conventional convolutional model for the beam cross-section rather than the mixture model.
As above,
for any candidate edge location
\gamma
and each horizontal raster scan location
k
,
we have a corresponding parameter
q_{k}
.
This implies a mean
\mu_{k}
given by
(
10
),
and each SE count distribution
X_{i,k}

\mathop{\mathrm{Poisson}}(\mu_{k})

12

Differing from above, the thinning parameter in the distribution of

\rho_{k}^{\prime}=1-e^{-\mu_{k}}

Also differing from above, each
\widetilde{X}_{i,k}
has a zero-truncated Poisson distribution
with parameter
\mu_{k}

\mathop{\mathrm{ZTPoisson}}(\mu_{k})

Combining the likelihoods over the scan positions gives

and

\gamma
,

\eta_{1}
,
\eta_{2}
, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}
.
Maximizing this function gives the mismatched MLE:

\widehat{\gamma}_{\mathrm{MMLE}}=\argmax_{\gamma}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{conv}}(\gamma).

V-C

Simulations of Edge Localization

We performed Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate the performances of the three edge estimators,
with the MLE and MMLE implemented by grid search with a grid spacing of 0.01.
Each Monte Carlo trial has pseudorandom data generated according to the model in
Section

II

on a one-dimensional scan grid of

\{0,1,\ldots,99\}

(i.e.,
\ell=100
)
Detector noise is not modeled, so a simulated TRM dataset is

\{(\widetilde{m}_{k},\{\widetilde{x}_{i,k}\}_{i=1}^{\widetilde{m}_{k}})\}_{k=0}^{\ell-1}
.
Simulation parameters include the
edge location
\gamma
,
beam width
\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}
,
dose
\lambda
,
and SE yields on each side of the edge,
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
.
Where applicable, the estimators
use

\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}
,

\lambda
,

\eta_{1}
, and
\eta_{2}
; only
\gamma
is unknown.
Sets of simulations vary one parameter while keeping others fixed.
Each reported result is based on 300 Monte Carlo trials,
which allows for computation of (estimated) bias,
(sample) standard deviation, and (estimated) root mean-squared error (RMSE).
The error bars in the bias plots represent
\pm 1
standard deviation of the sample mean.
We similarly computed
\pm 1
standard deviation interval endpoints for the sample variance and the sample MSE.
The marked error bars in the standard deviation and RMSE plots use
the square roots of these quantities.
The reciprocal of the Fisher information (
68
) is the Cramér–Rao lower bound on the MSE of any unbiased estimate of
\gamma
from a TRM dataset. The square root of this CRB is included in all RMSE plots.

In
Fig.

8
(a), we vary
\lambda
from 20 to 290 while fixing

\gamma=50.2
,

\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}=1
,

\eta_{1}=1
, and

\eta_{2}=10
.
The RMSE comparison shows uniform superiority of the MLE over the MMLE and interpolation.

The MLE has very low bias, and its standard deviation and RMSE follow the
\lambda^{-1/2}
dependence on
\lambda

predicted by
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{scan}}(\gamma\,;\,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\lambda,\sigma_{\mathrm{b}})

in (
68
)
being linear in
\lambda
.
Along with the
\lambda^{-1/2}
scaling behavior,
the quantitative match of the RMSE to the square root of the CRB is good here and in most subsequent simulation results,
showing that the MLE is approximately efficient.

The MMLE has a negative bias and slightly higher standard deviation than the MLE.
The bias being negative can be explained by the
excess variance (
13
)
as follows.
Just to the left of the edge (e.g., at 49 or 50),
the relatively frequent occurrences of
positive outliers in the SE counts,
when interpreted through the convolution model
(Poisson rather than Poisson mixture),
are best explained by the edge having already been reached.
In other words,
since the Poisson model requires the mean and variance to be equal,
the MMLE incorrectly imputes a larger mixture fraction
q_{k}

to account for these outliers.
Since the mixture fraction values and the edge location are linked by (
8
),
this overestimation of mixture fractions leads to a negative bias in edge estimation.

The interpolation estimator has very low bias at high doses—but significant bias at low doses—and higher standard deviation than the MLE and MMLE.
The bias being positive and vanishing at high enough doses can be explained as follows.
Gross inaccuracy of the interpolation estimator is most likely when there are multiple crossings of the threshold
(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2})/2=5.5
.
Far to the left of the edge,
\widehat{\eta}_{\rm baseline}
has mean
\eta_{1}=1
and variance
(\eta_{1}+\eta_{1}^{2})/\lambda=2/\lambda

(see (
38
) and (
39
)).
Thus, even at the lowest shown dose of
\lambda=20
, spurious threshold crossings (i.e.,
\widehat{\eta}_{\rm baseline}>5.5
) are very unlikely.
Conversely, far to the right of the edge,
\widehat{\eta}_{\rm baseline}
has mean
\eta_{2}=10
and variance
(\eta_{2}+\eta_{2}^{2})/\lambda=110/\lambda
,
and spurious threshold crossings (i.e.,
\widehat{\eta}_{\rm baseline}<5.5
) happen with nonnegligible probability unless
\lambda
is large enough.

\lambda=200

\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}

The RMSE comparison again shows the superiority of the MLE over the MMLE and interpolation.
The signs of biases match the results in panel (a),

In the standard deviation and RMSE plots of panel (b),

\gamma=50.5

\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}
