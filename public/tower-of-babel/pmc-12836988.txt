Cumulative displacement in old age: a phenomenological study of life-course trajectories to homelessness among older adults in Iran
Authors: Shiva Parvaei, Vahid Rashedi, Mohammad Hossein Sharifi Saei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHomelessness among older adults is an emerging social crisis in Iran, exacerbated by rapid population aging, inadequate welfare systems, and rising housing costs. Despite its growing prevalence, research on older homelessness in Iran remains limited. This study explores the lived experiences and underlying causes of homelessness among older adults to inform targeted prevention and intervention strategies.MethodsA qualitative phenomenological design was employed to investigate life-course trajectories leading to homelessness. In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted in 2025 with 27 homeless adults aged ≥ 60 years residing in municipal shelters in Tehran. Purposive and theoretical sampling ensured diversity across gender, duration of homelessness, and living arrangements. Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method. To ensure the trustworthiness of the findings, the study adhered to established qualitative criteria by addressing credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability through strategies such as member checking, maximum variation sampling, peer debriefing, and thick description.ResultsThis study examined homelessness among 27 older adults (18 men, 9 women; mean age 65.5 years) living in Tehran shelters, most of whom had very low education and pension coverage, with shelter stays ranging from months to two decades. Analysis identified five interconnected categories driving their homelessness: economic-livelihood challenges (housing deprivation, financial crises, unstable work); family-generational factors (intergenerational poverty, conflict, and heightened gender vulnerability for women denied property rights or dependent on unreliable children); harmful exposures (childhood trauma, addiction, violence, high-risk lifestyles, and health incapacitation); individual factors (self-perceived life choices and skill deficits); and institutional-support failures (inadequate pensions, weak welfare systems, and the collapse of traditional family support networks). These factors converged into two distinct pathways into homelessness: a gradual trajectory stemming from lifelong cumulative disadvantage, and a rapid onset triggered by acute old-age crises such as spousal death, severe illness, or sudden housing cost inflation.ConclusionOlder homelessness in Iran emerges through cumulative disadvantage across the life course, with distinct gendered pathways. Practical interventions must include: structural reforms in housing affordability and pension systems, particularly for informal workers; [2] gender-sensitive policies addressing women’s property rights and men’s addiction treatment needs; life-course preventive strategies beginning in childhood to disrupt intergenerational poverty cycles; and integrated service models combining healthcare, social support, and housing assistance specifically designed for older homeless adults.

Introduction

Population aging is one of the most pressing demographic challenges of the 21 st century. Demographic projections indicate that by 2030, one in six people globally will be aged 60 years or over, growing from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion. This trend is expected to accelerate, with the global population aged 60 years and older doubling to 2.1 billion by 2050 [1]. According to the latest national census (2016), the population of seniors aged 60 and above in Iran was 9.3% [2]. Based on UN population estimates, in 2030, 14.4% and in 2050, 31.2% of Iran’s population will be aged 60 and over [3].

While most developed nations have already undergone significant demographic transitions marked by aging populations, developing countries are now experiencing this phenomenon at a rapid pace [4]—often without the necessary infrastructure to support it [5]. The number of older persons in developing countries increased by 60% between 2000 and 2015. This growth rate is projected to rise by 71% from 2015 to 2030 [3]. It is projected that by 2050, two-thirds of the world’s population aged 60 and above will reside in low- and middle-income countries [1].The accelerated rate of population aging in these regions, combined with underdeveloped social welfare systems and insufficient age-friendly policies, has led to heightened vulnerability among older adults, particularly in terms of financial insecurity and poverty [6].

Income poverty is a persistent and widespread issue among older adults [7], and studies have shown that its prevalence in this age group often surpasses that among children and working-age individuals [8]. In Iran, for example, nearly 30% of the older population (aged 60 and above) are covered by support systems, suggesting a significant portion remains excluded from formal assistance mechanisms. According to the Ageing Watch Index, approximately 25% of Iranian older adults live in income poverty, and their poverty rate is nearly twice that of other age groups. Moreover, only 45% of Iranian seniors receive pension benefits, leaving over half without access to this critical safety net [9]. Numerous studies underscore the vital role pension systems play in mitigating poverty among older populations. Consequently, limited pension coverage significantly increases the risk of poverty and even homelessness among older adults [10–12].

International research from countries such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia indicates that a significant proportion of older homeless individuals—up to two-thirds—experience homelessness for the first time in later life, rather than as a continuation of long-term housing instability [13]. Scholars such as Greiner et al. [14] and Barnes and Sussman [15] have further emphasized that the risk of poverty and homelessness intensifies with age. Homeless older adults face unique vulnerabilities—ranging from health problems and mobility limitations to heightened social isolation—which distinguish their needs from those of younger homeless populations [16–19].

Given these factors, it is crucial to understand the distinct causes and experiences of homelessness among older adults. Such an understanding can inform the development of targeted policies, interventions, and support services. Importantly, by identifying the pathways that lead to homelessness in later life, this knowledge helps to both mitigate its future prevalence and serve as a foundation for preventive strategies and improved rehabilitation and social work initiatives.

Despite the growing urgency of this issue, there remains a significant gap in Iranian research on homelessness among older adults. Most studies have neglected this vulnerable subgroup, focusing instead on younger populations. A review of Iranian research indicates that studies on homelessness in Iran have primarily focused on cardboard sleepers or specifically on women [20–28]. In these studies, homeless older adults have not been examined. This oversight raises important questions: Is homelessness in old age primarily a result of the aging process, or does it reflect cumulative life-course disadvantages? What specific factors contribute to homelessness among Iranian older adults? The present study aims to address these questions through a phenomenological approach, exploring the lived experiences of homeless older adults.

This study employed a qualitative methodology as it constitutes the inaugural investigation into homelessness among older adults within the Iranian context. A qualitative design was deemed essential to generate foundational knowledge and delineate the complex dimensions of this phenomenon from the participants’ own perspectives. The primary objective was to amplify the voices of a marginalized population and capture their lived experiences, which are often difficult to quantify. Thus, the qualitative approach was not only methodologically appropriate but also necessary to develop a rich, contextualized, and emic understanding [29] of a heretofore unexamined phenomenon in the Iranian scholarly literature. The use of qualitative interviews enables a deeply human-centered investigation of these challenges, providing a space for participants to articulate their experiences in their own words and on their own terms [30, 31]. Furthermore, the specific nature of the population underscored the choice of a phenomenological framework. Older adults experiencing homelessness often face multifaceted challenges, including psychosocial, structural, and health-related issues that are best understood through in-depth narratives and personal accounts.

Materials and methods

This study employed a qualitative research design grounded in a phenomenological approach, aiming to explore the lived experiences of homeless older adults and identify the underlying causes of homelessness in later life. Qualitative methodologies are particularly well-suited for investigating complex, context-dependent social phenomena, as they provide rich, in-depth insights that cannot be captured through quantitative measures alone [29]. This study employed a qualitative phenomenological framework to explore the participants’ lived experiences. Phenomenology, as a research paradigm, focuses on subjective perception and individuals’ interpretation of their world. This approach is particularly well-suited to investigating how individuals construe significant life events and the meanings they ascribe to their personal experiences [32]. Central to this methodology is the premise that reality is constituted through individual experience, and its objective is to uncover the meanings embedded within everyday life [33]. The phenomenological approach, in particular, was selected for its capacity to illuminate how individuals make sense of their experiences and the meanings they ascribe to them—insights that are essential when working with vulnerable populations such as older adults.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews with participants aged 60 years and older who were either residing in municipal nursing homes or temporary shelters (hothouses) across the 22 municipal districts of Tehran in 2025 (in January and February). This dual interview format allowed for flexibility: a structured set of open-ended questions served as a guide, while participants were encouraged to freely elaborate on topics or raise new issues based on their personal experiences. This approach supported both consistency across interviews and responsiveness to emergent themes.

Each semi-structured interview lasted between 45 and 240 min. Furthermore, participants were not compensated for their involvement. Interviews were conducted in private settings to ensure participants’ comfort and psychological safety. All interviews were conducted by two of the study’s authors in a private room within the shelters’ administrative section, with only a single participant present at each session. Interviews were conducted individually. Each session began with a brief explanation of the study’s objectives in accessible language, followed by verbal and written informed consent. Participation was entirely voluntary, and interviewees were informed that they could decline to answer any question or withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. To maintain confidentiality and anonymity, all names were replaced with pseudonyms during transcription and analysis.

Inclusion criteria comprised individuals aged 60 years or older residing in municipal shelters in Tehran, who possessed adequate cognitive and mental health. In addition, willingness to participate in the study and consent for the interview to be audio-recorded were also part of the inclusion criteria. The exclusion criteria specifically targeted individuals exhibiting significant cognitive impairment or acute psychiatric conditions that would compromise their ability to provide informed consent, maintain coherent narrative responses, or endure the emotional demands of in-depth interviewing. This multi-stage assessment began with preliminary screening by municipal shelter staff, who provided observational insights regarding residents’ day-to-day cognitive functioning, emotional stability, and communication abilities based on established professional relationships. Subsequently, during the formal interview process, researchers employed clinical observation techniques to evaluate participants’ attention span, narrative coherence, reality orientation, and emotional regulation. This rigorous protocol proved essential, as three potential participants were excluded during active interviewing when they demonstrated inconsistent recall, disorientation regarding temporal elements of their narratives, or symptoms of acute psychological distress that indicated compromised capacity for meaningful participation.

Sampling strategy

A combination of purposive and theoretical sampling was used to recruit participants. Purposive sampling aimed to ensure the inclusion of a diverse range of older adults experiencing homelessness, accounting for variation in gender, duration of homelessness, and type of current residence (e.g., shelter vs. long-term care facility). Once initial participants were interviewed and preliminary data analyzed, theoretical sampling was employed to refine emerging categories and concepts. In this iterative process, subsequent participants were selected based on their potential to elaborate or challenge preliminary themes, until theoretical saturation was reached—that is, when no new themes or insights emerged from additional interviews. Interviews continued until data saturation, which was reached with 27 participants.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection to facilitate theoretical sampling and iterative refinement of the research focus. The data analysis started after the first interview, and it was not the case that the analysis and coding started after the end of the 27 interviews.

To manage and code the qualitative data systematically, MAXQDA software was employed. This facilitated multi-level coding and categorization, ensuring transparency and rigor in the analytical process. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and converted into textual format using Microsoft Word. Analysis followed the seven-step Colaizzi phenomenological method (1978), which includes the following stages:

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical standards for qualitative research were carefully followed throughout. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality was maintained through anonymization and secure data handling. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage, and all interviews were conducted individually in safe and respectful environments. To ensure participant confidentiality, each participant is coded starting from P1 to P27.

Results

The sample comprised 27 older adults (aged 60–90 years) residing in shelters in Tehran, with a mean age of 65.5 years. The cohort consisted of 18 men and 9 women, a disparity that reflects the lower prevalence of homeless older women in the city. Most participants were divorced or widowed, with a minority being married or never married (single). Educational levels were generally low, predominantly at the primary or lower secondary level, although a few individuals held a high school diploma or a university degree. Pension coverage was limited, with only a minority receiving a minimal social security pension; most lacked any stable retirement income. Shelter residency duration varied widely, ranging from a few months to over a decade, including one case of nearly 20 years (Table 1).

This study focuses on a specific subpopulation of older adults experiencing homelessness: those residing in temporary municipal shelters. Consequently, for this research, the term ‘homelessness’ refers specifically to this status. It is important to note that while all participants had a history of street homelessness (often referred to as “rough sleeping” or “cardboard sleeping”), they were transitioned into and residing in municipal shelters at the time of data collection, thus no longer living on the streets.

The results indicate that the causes of homelessness among older adults can be categorized into several themes: economic and livelihood factors, family and generational factors, harmful factors, individual factors, and institutional and support factors (Table 2).

Economic and livelihood factors

Among the key economic and livelihood factors contributing to homelessness are housing deprivation, financial crises, and precarious employment.

Housing deprivation

A major factor leading to homelessness among older adults—particularly women—is the lack of stable housing. Rising housing costs, especially over the past decade, have left many seniors without the financial capacity to afford exorbitant rental expenses. Some older adults, unable to find affordable housing, have resorted to shared living arrangements. For others, homelessness began after the death of their older parents, followed by the division of inheritance and the loss of their residence.

P3 illustrates this: “A good friend of mine also needs a place to live. We agreed to pool our money to rent a place together, with the lease under my name. My son helped me with the deposit, but we still can’t find anything. We’ve knocked on every door, but with this budget, it’s impossible—prices are outrageous now.”

Financial crises

Another factor that has led to homelessness among older adults is the financial crisis they experienced throughout their lives. Major financial crises include job loss, business bankruptcy, the depletion of assets due to unemployment and addiction, becoming a victim of economic fraud, and the financial pressures of family life.

Precarity and unstable employment

Another economic and livelihood factor that ultimately leads to late-life homelessness is a history of employment in precarious jobs during youth and middle age. The present study reveals that older adults with a history of homelessness were primarily engaged in insecure, fragile, and low-wage occupations, such as labor and service jobs, as well as other informal and unstable positions. Critically, these jobs often lacked retirement benefits, provided low income, and were inherently volatile. As a result, when facing declining physical health and the inability to work in their later years, these individuals were left vulnerable to homelessness. Family and generational factors

One of the causes of homelessness among older adults is family-related factors. This study identified three key categories: the intergenerational cycle of poverty, family conflicts, and gender vulnerability.

Intergenerational cycle of poverty

The intergenerational cycle of poverty refers to a process where poverty is systematically reproduced and sustained from one generation to the next. Findings indicate that school dropout during childhood, child labor, a weak family support system, and a lack of insurance and financial literacy are all manifestations of this cycle in both its economic and cultural dimensions. These factors perpetuate familial deprivation, as problems are passed down between generations. In effect, poverty is inherited.

One of the consequences of this poverty is school dropout in childhood, often caused by unfavorable family economic conditions. Poverty deprives individuals of educational opportunities. In many cases, children from impoverished families decide to leave school to help support the family’s finances.

P10 (Male, 76 years old) illustrates this: “I studied until the ninth grade. Then I dropped out because of my family’s poor economic situation. My father was a simple laborer in agriculture with a low income. Life was difficult, so I thought I would come to Tehran to work, hoping to make a change.” P22 (Male, 64 years old) similarly stated: “I was a failed high school student, so I just quit school. There was no one above me to tell me what to do. After I left school, I became an addict.”

Harmful factors

One of the reasons for homelessness among older adults is a history of traumatic social experiences during childhood, adolescence, youth, and middle age. Harmful factors such as childhood and adolescent traumas, high-risk lifestyles, traumatic life events, and incapacitation can ultimately accelerate homelessness in old age.

Individual narratives and self-perceived life patterns

Rather than conceptualizing individual factors as inherent personality traits, our analysis reveals how participants subjectively interpreted their life trajectories and made meaning of their circumstances. These self-attributed patterns represent retrospective narrative accounts rather than clinical personality assessments. Participants frequently used personal accountability frameworks to explain their homelessness, often internalizing structural disadvantages as personal failings.

Participants frequently described their experiences using self-critical narratives that attributed homelessness to perceived personal shortcomings. These accounts should be understood as subjective interpretations rather than objective personality assessments. Many participants retrospectively identified patterns in their decision-making that they believed contributed to their circumstances:

P5 spoke about her lack of foresight: “This situation is because of my shortsightedness, for not thinking about tomorrow. I thought I would always be young and energetic, that I could always work and earn money again.”

A further issue is the financial burden of illnesses in old age and gaps in insurance coverage. This means a large portion of income is spent on medical expenses. In this regard, P4 says: “I was saving my money, but I spent all of it on my knee. I had knee surgery, and it cost a lot.”

The lack of a family support network, family rejection (by a spouse, parent, or children), the loss of family cohesion after a parent’s death, and a lack of social capital are all key issues that demonstrate weak informal social support.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that the causes of homelessness among older adults can be categorized into several key areas: economic and livelihood factors (housing deprivation, financial crises, and precarious employment), family and generational factors (the intergenerational cycle of poverty, family conflicts, and gender vulnerability), harmful factors (childhood and adolescent traumas, high-risk lifestyles, traumatic events, and incapacitation), individual factors (Self-Attributed Life Patterns and Perceived Skill Deficits), and institutional/support factors (weak formal and informal social support). Thus, late-life homelessness is a multidimensional phenomenon stemming from the interaction of these five main categories of factors.

Homelessness in older adults appears to be the result of a series of cumulative hardships throughout their lives. Each of these factors, either individually or in combination, creates a trajectory that ultimately increases the risk of homelessness in old age. The identified factors show that homelessness in this population is the product of a combination of macro-level (institutional/support and economic/livelihood factors), meso-level (family and generational factors), and micro-level (individual factors) issues that have accumulated over a lifetime. Therefore, homelessness is not merely the result of structural deficiencies, but family and individual factors also play a significant role in causing homelessness in old age.

The findings of this study indicate that homeless older adults fall into two groups: the first group consists of those who experienced homelessness before old age, during their youth and middle age (primarily due to addiction and family rejection resulting from it), and the second group consists of those who first experienced homelessness in old age. Some older adults had a history of homelessness and rough sleeping before their senior years. In contrast, others became homeless in old age after experiencing a series of financial crises, illnesses, housing inflation, and other issues. For example, some older adults were financially and professionally vulnerable throughout their lives and only experienced homelessness in their later years due to financial crises, illness, incapacitation, and housing inflation. This finding aligns with the results of Brown et al. (2013) [35]. Some of the identified causes of homelessness are related specifically to the period of old age, such as housing inflation, homelessness resulting from the death of older parents and the division of inheritance, cohabitation problems with family members, violence against older adults, incapacitation, weak formal social support, and falling victim to economic fraud and deception. These are older adults who became homeless for the first time in their later years, but it is important to note that their homelessness is also the result of an accumulation of deprivations and problems throughout their lives, making it a gradual process.

Research by Burns and Sussman (2019) and Om et al. (2022) suggests two pathways to homelessness in older adults: a gradual path and a rapid one [15, 36]. However, the findings of this study indicate that homelessness in older adults is not just a sudden, rapid event but rather the result of a gradual process of cumulative deprivations throughout their lives. The interaction of individual vulnerabilities, the collapse of support networks, intergenerational cycles of poverty, and economic structures like fragile, insecure, and unstable employment gradually leads to homelessness in later life.

This finding can be analyzed using Dannefer’s (2003) theory of cumulative advantage and disadvantage [37]. This theory explains how deprivations intensify over time and how structural and social factors lead to an accumulation of disadvantages throughout one’s life. Based on this theory, old age is not a uniform experience; instead, it is profoundly influenced by the inequalities and deprivations accumulated over a lifetime. These multiple deprivations—including individual, economic, structural, institutional, and even gender-based inequalities—interact to cause fragility and compounded deprivation for older adults. Therefore, homelessness in old age is the result of a gradual, cumulative process of interconnected deprivations throughout a person’s life.

The lived experiences of the homeless older adults in this study show that they endured crises, harms, and sometimes traumas during childhood, adolescence, youth, and middle age. These experiences ultimately made them vulnerable in old age, with their homelessness rooted in these accumulated problems. The factors identified include financial crises like business bankruptcy and the depletion of assets due to addiction and unemployment; precarious employment in unstable, low-wage, and uninsured labor and service jobs; the intergenerational cycle of poverty, evidenced by school dropout, child labor, a weak family background, and a lack of insurance and financial literacy; childhood and adolescent traumas, especially addiction, high-risk lifestyles like rough sleeping, and traumatic life events such as child marriage, parental divorce, being orphaned, and premature parental death. All of these made them vulnerable professionally, financially, and personally. In old age, experiences with incapacitation, illness, lack of a retirement pension, housing deprivation, divorce, and economic crises—particularly in the areas of housing and rental inflation—pushed them into homelessness. Therefore, the foundations of homelessness in older adults were gradually formed and strengthened during previous life stages. Homelessness is the consequence of accumulated crises and harms experienced throughout earlier life, which gradually made these individuals more vulnerable to it.

A significant finding of this study is the addiction-centric nature of homelessness among older adults in Iran, which leads to occupational, financial, and familial harm. The consequences of addiction on family, professional, and economic life are severe, often culminating in homelessness. Studies by Kellogg & Horn (2012), Brown et al. (2016), Grenier et al. (2016), and Reynolds et al. (2016) also document substance abuse and dependence on drugs and alcohol among homeless older adults, identifying it as a contributing factor to homelessnesss [14, 38–40]. The results of this study show that addiction throughout a person’s life leads to financial crises such as asset depletion, an inability to afford housing, and precarious employment. Moreover, individuals with a history of addiction experienced family rejection, the breakdown of family relationships, and divorce, thereby losing their family support networks. They also experienced rough sleeping, which contributed to their long-term homelessness. The experience of addiction is also a social one, stemming from factors like an addiction-plagued family environment, a drug-infested neighborhood, and peer pressure.

Another analysis that can be drawn from the study’s results is the role of gender inequality, or gender vulnerability, in the homelessness of older women. Based on a feminist analysis of homelessness, patriarchal social structures and gender inequalities expose women to homelessness [41]. Feminist theory posits that women’s pathways to homelessness are often linked to domestic violence and ineffective welfare systems [42]. The present study’s results indicate that the causes of homelessness among older adults differ based on gender. The pattern of homelessness for older women is family-centric and age-centric, primarily stemming from weak family support networks and crises related to old age and economics. Widowhood and dependence on children, a lack of a retirement pension due to domestic and informal work, physical incapacity and an inability to work, divorce, housing inflation, being single, and housing deprivation after spousal death are among the most significant causes of homelessness in older women. The pattern for older men, on the other hand, is harm-centric and addiction-centric. Addiction, a history of incarceration, rough sleeping, divorce, and family rejection are the main causes of homelessness among older men.

Economic and livelihood factors, such as housing deprivation, financial crises, and precarious employment, as well as institutional/support factors like a lack of retirement pensions and an inadequate support system, are some of the causes of homelessness among older Iranians. Economic and livelihood factors such as job loss, unaffordable rent, and a lack of access to rental housing have also been identified as causes of homelessness in later life in studies by Petersen & Parsell (2014) and Llorente et al. (2021) [43, 44]. The research by Crane and Warnes (2010) also indicates that older adults become homeless in later life due to factors such as changes in housing markets, retirement, and increasing income inequality [9].

In general, the present study revealed that homelessness among older adults in Iran is not a sudden occurrence but rather the outcome of a gradual and cumulative process of structural, familial, individual, and social deprivations accumulated throughout the life course. The findings demonstrated that homelessness among older adults arises from the complex interplay of economic-livelihood, intergenerational-familial, traumatic, individual, and institutional-support factors. Each of these determinants, acting either independently or in combination, progressively increases vulnerability to homelessness in later life. At the macro level, inadequate social policies, the inefficiency of welfare and insurance systems, and economic fluctuations—particularly severe housing inflation and escalating rental costs—have deprived many older adults of secure housing. The absence of retirement pensions, inadequate stipends, and the high financial burden of illnesses have further reinforced cycles of chronic poverty. At the meso level, family disruptions, intergenerational conflicts, lack of informal support networks, and the fragility of social capital were identified as major triggers of homelessness. Gender inequality, lack of property ownership among women, domestic violence, and dependency on adult children have placed older women at heightened risk. At the micro level, individual characteristics such as lack of foresight, poor life and financial management skills, irresponsibility, and impulsive spending, coupled with early-life adversities—childhood abuse, addiction, family rejection, and traumatic events—laid the groundwork for homelessness later in life. These vulnerabilities accumulated over time and ultimately manifested as homelessness in old age.

Conclusion

Homelessness among older adults in Iran emerges from cumulative disadvantage across the life course rather than isolated late-life events. Our findings reveal two distinct pathways: a gradual trajectory shaped by compounding economic, family, and health vulnerabilities, and a rapid onset triggered by housing inflation, spousal death, or health crises. Gender differences were pronounced: women’s homelessness stemmed primarily from family disruption, property inequality, and widowhood, while men’s pathways centered on addiction, incarceration, and street experiences.

Practical interventions must address multiple life stages and systems. Prevention strategies should begin early in life through school retention programs targeting children in poverty, financial and insurance literacy curricula in secondary education, and early intervention for youth exposed to family addiction or violence. Mid-life protective measures must include mandatory pension enrollment for informal sector workers through simplified registration processes, gender-equitable property ownership counseling for married couples, and addiction treatment programs incorporating housing stability components. Late-life interventions require geriatric homeless screening protocols in primary healthcare settings, “Housing First” models with integrated healthcare for older homeless adults, and emergency housing vouchers triggered by specific risk events such as spousal death, eviction, or health crisis. Systemic reforms are equally critical, including rent stabilization measures specifically protecting older adults on fixed incomes, a minimum guaranteed income for all citizens over 60 regardless of employment history, co-located healthcare services addressing addiction, mental health, and geriatric care needs, and legal aid programs protecting older women’s inheritance and property rights. Implementation requires coordination between municipalities (housing), the Ministry of Welfare (social services), the Ministry of Health (healthcare integration), and the judiciary (property rights protection), with priority given to the 20% of at-risk older adults experiencing recent spousal loss, housing instability, or family breakdown, and interventions beginning within 30 days of identification. This multi-level approach recognizes that preventing late-life homelessness requires addressing root causes across the entire life course while providing immediate protection for those most vulnerable.

Limitations and directions for future research

While the phenomenological approach provides rich and contextual insights, this study is limited by its exclusive focus on participants residing in Tehran’s shelters. This suggests that the causes of homelessness among older adults who live exclusively on the streets—a reality often described as cardboard box dwelling or street dwelling—may be different. Furthermore, a notable limitation of this study was the challenge of ensuring the veracity of information provided by some participants. Several participants, due to issues related to substance abuse, were unable to provide accurate and coherent data. For instance, during the interview process with three homeless older adults, the collected data contained contradictory and unreliable information. Consequently, these interviews were excluded from the final analysis after careful review. Ultimately, it is recommended that future studies examine gender differences in the causes of older adult homelessness and conduct quantitative research to measure the intensity and impact of each factor. In addition, the recruitment of participants exclusively from shelters may have led to an overrepresentation of experiences related to institutionalized homelessness. Consequently, the findings may not fully capture the perspectives of unsheltered individuals or those in informal housing situations (e.g., hidden homeless living with relatives), whose pathways and experiences are likely distinct.
