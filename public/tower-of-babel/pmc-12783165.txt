An overview of types of antibacterial coatings and methods for creating them on orthodontic wires
Authors: Mona Mohajeri Tehrani, Pardis Chaboki, Reza Mohammadi, Shamim Chinian, Saman Motallebzadeh, Munthar Kadhim Abosaoda, Mayank Kundlas, Sudhakar Theerthagiri, Ali ebrahimi, Sina hamzehzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Orthodontic therapy utilizing fixed equipment frequently results in heightened plaque accumulation, which can cause enamel demineralization and periodontal issues. Recent breakthroughs in surface coating technology have brought various antimicrobial techniques designed to diminish bacterial adherence and biofilm formation on orthodontic components. This study thoroughly examines many types of antibacterial coatings, including metallic nanoparticles (such as silver, zinc, and titanium), polymeric layers, carbon-based materials, and bioactive chemicals, emphasizing their modes of action, microbial targets, and therapeutic significance. Silver-based coatings are among the most thoroughly researched due to their continuous ion release and broad-spectrum antibacterial effectiveness. Photocatalytic titanium dioxide and synergistic composite coatings demonstrate promising outcomes under particular activation conditions. The review examines biocompatibility concerns, long-term durability, and the limitations of existing methodologies, while suggesting future research avenues to connect laboratory innovations with clinical applications. The surface modification of orthodontic equipment offers a practical approach to reducing oral health hazards associated with therapy and improving patient outcomes.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Fixed orthodontic appliances alter the oral ecosystem by introducing new retentive niches that favor plaque accumulation and a shift toward acidogenic, biofilm-forming communities [1]. Surface irregularities at the bracket–archwire interface, together with high surface energy substrates such as stainless steel, facilitate early colonizer attachment and maturation of complex biofilms that drive enamel demineralization and gingival inflammation around appliances [2]. Early white-spot lesions can emerge within weeks of appliance placement and represent both an esthetic burden and an indicator of irreversible mineral loss that may progress to cavitation if unmanaged [3]. Conventional measures including toothbrushing reinforcement, fluoride delivery, and antimicrobial rinses provide only transient benefits because of limited intraoral retention, patient compliance challenges, and trade-offs such as staining or mucosal irritation [4, 5]. Antimicrobial additives confined to bonding resins have not consistently prevented biofilm accrual on hardware exposed to saliva and dietary fluxes [6–8].

Surface engineering has therefore gained attention as a strategy to suppress bacterial adhesion and biofilm maturation directly at the material–biofilm interface [9]. Approaches include metallic and metal-oxide coatings with ion release or photocatalytic activity (for example silver, titanium dioxide, zinc oxide) [10, 11], antiadhesive polymeric layers that modulate surface free energy and hydration (for example PEG, PTFE, MPC), carbon-based materials with contact-killing and barrier functions [12], and bioactive coatings that leverage enzymatic or protein-mediated effects [13]. These technologies promise continuous, device-bound protection without relying on patient behavior and may be tailored to preserve mechanical performance and esthetics of orthodontic components.

Despite rapid progress, several unresolved questions limit clinical translation. First, there is methodological heterogeneity across studies in bacterial models, growth media, and readouts, which complicates comparison of “strong antibacterial activity” claims in the absence of standardized quantitative endpoints such as CFU reduction, biofilm biomass, or zone of inhibition expressed with effect sizes and time profiles [14]. Second, durability under realistic thermo-mechanical and chemical stresses remains uncertain. Coatings must withstand archwire deflection, bracket ligation, masticatory abrasion, pH cycling, and fluoride exposure without loss of function or excessive roughening that could increase friction [15]. Third, safety considerations require rigorous evaluation of ion release, cytocompatibility, and the risk of dysbiosis. Silver-containing layers, nitrogen-doped TiO₂ that operates under visible light, and carbon-based films all raise distinct questions regarding long-term biocompatibility and oral microbiome balance [16]. Fourth, performance trade-offs are incompletely defined. Some titanium nitride variants may elevate friction, while photocatalytic systems often depend on light spectra and exposure durations that do not reflect chairside conditions [17]. Evidence for gold or rhodium coatings remains mixed, and optimal concentration windows are critical for graphene oxide to avoid compromising biocompatibility [12]. Finally, the rise of clear aligner therapy introduces new substrates with different wetting, wear, and optical constraints that have been sparsely studied [8].

This review synthesizes current evidence on antibacterial coatings for orthodontic applications with a materials-to-clinic perspective. We compare coating classes by mechanisms of action, microbial targets including Streptococcus mutans, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Actinomyces viscosus, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans, fabrication routes, and side effects on friction, hardness, wear, corrosion, and esthetics. Where available, we highlight quantitative outcomes and longevity, discuss in vitro versus in vivo concordance, and identify parameters that govern visible-light responsiveness for TiO₂ systems. We also outline reporting practices that can improve comparability, including standardized bacterial panels, exposure conditions, and effect size metrics.

This narrative review was conducted by searching electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for articles published between 2016 and 2025. The keywords used included “antibacterial coatings,” “orthodontic appliances,” “silver nanoparticles,” “titanium dioxide,” “biofilm prevention,” and “biocompatibility.” Studies were selected based on relevance to the topic, in vitro or in vivo experimental evidence, and clinical applicability. Reviews, original research articles, and experimental studies were all considered, while editorials, non-English papers, and studies without clear antibacterial outcome measures were excluded.

Antibacterial mechanisms of coating materials

Antimicrobial mechanisms of nanoparticles

Oxidative stress mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Nanoparticles (NPs) interact with molecular oxygen to produce a variety of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are essential to their antibacterial activity [18, 19]. For instance, photons with wavelengths shorter than about 385 nanometers, or UVA light, activate the semiconductor material titanium dioxide (TiO₂) [20, 21]. This activation facilitates the mobility of electrons by encouraging their excitation within the conduction band. These electrons are then taken up by oxygen and water molecules, which produce extremely reactive species like hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide anions (O₂⁻•) [18, 20]. These ROS have the ability to break down the plasma membranes and cell walls of bacteria, which can lead to intracellular contents leaking out and, eventually, bacterial cell death [19]. Furthermore, the produced ROS have the ability to enter bacterial cells, harming vital proteins and upsetting plasmids that are necessary to preserve normal physiological processes and cellular morphology [18, 20].

Release of dissolved metal ions

Positively charged metal ions can also be released by nanoparticles to produce antibacterial effects [22]. The normally compact and well-ordered membrane structure of bacteria is disrupted and disorganized as a result of these metal ions’ electrostatic interactions with negatively charged functional groups on the membranes. Bacterial cell death occurs as a result of this destabilization, which also jeopardizes membrane integrity [23]. In addition to membrane interactions, some metal ions have the ability to attach directly to protein and nucleic acid functional groups, reducing their biological activity [24]. Zinc ions, for example, exhibit a high affinity for thiol (mercaptan) groups, where they can function as inhibitors by oxidizing these groups and obstructing glycolysis-related enzymes, thus enhancing their antibacterial properties [23, 24].

Antimicrobial mechanisms of non-nanoparticle materials

Inhibition of bacterial adhesion

By generating a hydrophilic environment, some organic compounds can lessen the adherence of bacteria to surfaces. For instance, a thin and stable aqueous layer forms on the substrate’s surface when polyethylene glycol (PEG) and water molecules form hydrogen bonds. Under physiological conditions, this hydrated layer greatly reduces bacterial attachment and colonization by interfering with the interaction between the substrate and biofilm-forming bacteria [24].

Incorporation of antibacterial functional groups

Certain functional groups, such as long alkyl chains (LACs) and quaternary ammonium cations (QACs), can also be added to materials to give them antimicrobial qualities. The antibacterial efficacy of QACs is thought to result from the strong electrostatic attraction between the positively charged QAC ions and the negatively charged phospholipid head groups within bacterial membranes, even though the exact mechanisms underlying their antimicrobial action are still not fully understood [25]. Membrane integrity and function are disturbed by this interaction [26]. Similarly, by attaching to components of the cell wall, long hydrophobic alkyl chains can pass through bacterial cell membranes, allowing cytoplasmic contents to leak out, triggering bacterial self-destruction (autolysis), and ultimately resulting in cell death [27].

Antibacterial coatings

Silver (Ag) and zinc oxide (ZnO) coatings are the most widely used antimicrobial coatings on orthodontic devices because of their proven antimicrobial efficacy and established safety profiles in a variety of medical domains [28]. More recently, bioactive coatings that contain lysozyme, polymer-based coatings that were first intended for esthetic enhancement, and photosensitive titanium dioxide (TiO₂) coatings have started to attract interest and are being researched for their possible antibacterial uses in orthodontics [10]. There is currently little research on the antimicrobial properties of carbon-based coatings; Dai and colleagues are among the few to utilize graphene oxide (GO) coatings to investigate this topic. Their results showed that the coating did not completely cover the nickel-titanium (NiTi) substrate at low graphene oxide (GO) concentrations, resulting in insignificant improvements in both tribological and anticorrosion properties [29]. Streptococcus mutans survival was statistically significantly reduced by approximately 20%, but this had a minor effect on the antibacterial performance. The antibacterial efficacy increased with increasing GO concentration; however, the biocompatibility of the coated NiTi substrate decreased accordingly. Although the exact recommended concentration remains unknown, these observations suggest that maintaining the GO concentration within an ideal range is crucial during the fabrication process. Furthermore, it is still unclear whether GO coatings are biocompatible with human tissues. Conversely, Ag and ZnO coatings are currently the best materials for antibacterial coatings on orthodontic appliances because they not only offer dependable antimicrobial action but also have a wide range of applications and are safe in clinical medicine [30]. Comprehensive antibacterial coating materials

In addition to their antimicrobial properties, the physicochemical and mechanical characteristics of surface coatings play a decisive role in their clinical applicability. Morphological features, including surface roughness and topography, strongly influence bacterial adhesion, with smoother or nano-patterned surfaces generally reducing microbial colonization [31, 32]. Coating thickness and uniformity determine not only the durability of the antibacterial effect but also the mechanical compatibility with orthodontic wires. Adhesion strength to the substrate is equally critical, as weakly bonded layers may delaminate under functional stresses such as mastication or archwire activation [33]. Furthermore, long-term stability against corrosion, salivary pH fluctuations, and thermal cycling in the oral cavity must be evaluated to ensure sustained performance [12, 34].

Mechanical properties are another key consideration. High hardness and adequate wear resistance are necessary to withstand frictional forces during orthodontic tooth movement without increasing surface roughness that could compromise both esthetics and antibacterial efficacy [35, 36]. Coatings that maintain mechanical strength while providing antimicrobial action, therefore, represent the most clinically relevant candidates for orthodontic applications [37, 38]. Future studies should consistently report these physicochemical and mechanical parameters alongside antibacterial outcomes to provide a more comprehensive understanding of coating performance [39].

Silver and silver compound coatings

Through a variety of methods, silver’s broad-spectrum antibacterial qualities successfully stop microorganisms from adhering to material surfaces. The constant release of silver ions by silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) damages the membranes and cell walls of bacteria. These ions enter bacterial cells and disrupt essential functions like DNA and protein synthesis. Additionally, by deactivating respiratory enzymes and producing reactive oxygen species inside bacterial cells, AgNPs aid in the death of bacteria. Furthermore, the nanoparticles themselves have the ability to adhere directly to the membranes and cell walls of bacteria, rupturing intracellular signal transduction pathways and causing structural harm like membrane denaturation and perforation [40]. AgNPs further hinder bacterial growth and survival by modifying host inflammatory responses in addition to their direct antimicrobial effects. As a result, adding silver coatings to orthodontic appliances may considerably lessen the amount of bacteria that colonizes the surfaces of teeth, which will lower the risk of dental caries and plaque development while receiving orthodontic treatment [22, 41].

The antibacterial effectiveness of silver coatings on orthodontic materials has been shown in numerous studies. For example, NiTi and stainless steel (SS) archwires coated with silver plating by thermal evaporation demonstrated strong antibacterial activity and successfully decreased the adherence of Lactobacillus acidophilus [1, 24]. This reduction was confirmed using CFU counts and biofilm inhibition assays.In one study, silver-coated SS wires reduced L. acidophilus colony-forming units (CFU) from 836.6 ± 48.9 to 220.9 ± 30.7, and silver-coated NiTi wires reduced CFU from 748.9 ± 35.6 to 203.2 ± 41.9, representing nearly a 70% reduction in bacterial counts (Mhaske et al., 2015). Similarly, agar diffusion (zone of inhibition) tests and SEM imaging showed that, Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus mutans were significantly inhibited by AgNPs that were hydrothermally synthesized and coated onto SS archwires. Gil et al. (2020) reported that AgNP-coated NiTi wires achieved over 90% reduction in bacterial adhesion compared with uncoated controls. Significant antibacterial effects were also demonstrated by nanosilver coatings on stainless steel brackets made by electroplating or physical vapor deposition (PVD), which remained active against Streptococcus mutans for up to a month. Bącela et al. (2022) further showed that TiO₂:Ag-coated SS wires reduced S. mutans adhesion by 74% within 4 h (p < 0.05). These coatings showed antibacterial and antiadhesive qualities against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus distortus, among other oral pathogens. For example, Gonçalves et al. (2020) demonstrated that AgNP-coated SS wires significantly decreased biofilm biomass by more than 60% for both S. mutans and S. aureus. Crucially, ceramic and cobalt-chromium alloy brackets were also affected by the antimicrobial effectiveness of nanosilver coatings [19, 20, 39].

Silver-coated brackets have been shown to have antibacterial properties in in vivo animal studies. According to one study, orthodontic brackets coated with nanosilver decreased the incidence of smooth-surface caries, released trace amounts of silver ions into saliva, and prevented Streptococcus mutans from colonizing dental plaque. However, the effect on occlusal caries was not statistically significant. Notwithstanding these encouraging results, there is still a dearth of in vivo research of this kind, which emphasizes the necessity of additional animal research to fully comprehend the clinical suitability of surface-coated orthodontic appliances [42].

The level of antibacterial enhancement can vary depending on the silver coating method used. For instance, when applied to SS brackets, plasma-immersion ion implantation and deposition (PIIID) showed antibacterial properties similar to those of electroplated and PVD silver coatings, more successfully lowering the volume of biofilm and surface coverage [42]. These outcomes were typically measured through CFU assays and biofilm coverage analysis by SEM. Although more clinical evidence is needed to confirm its effect on white-spot lesions and plaque-induced gingivitis linked to orthodontic treatment, this method has demonstrated encouraging clinical antibacterial properties. Furthermore, Streptococcus mutans attachment and growth on SS surfaces have been demonstrated to be inhibited by silver coatings like TaAgB, with evidence from both CFU counts and morphological analysis under SEM indicating antibacterial activity [43].

A lot of research has been done on silver compound coatings in an effort to increase their antibacterial and corrosion resistance. For example, silver-platinum (Ag-Pt) alloy coatings on stainless steel surfaces have demonstrated good biocompatibility and potent antibacterial activity against Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Streptococcus mutans. The antibacterial activity in these studies was primarily assessed through CFU enumeration and inhibition zone tests. However, because of the structural complexity of brackets and how they interact with archwires, this study may not be as clinically relevant because it used stainless steel blocks instead of real orthodontic brackets [44, 45].

Some studies have coated SS brackets with titanium dioxide and silver to further improve their antibacterial qualities. Compared to silver alone, these composite coatings showed better antibacterial and antiadhesive properties against Porphyromonas gingivalis and Streptococcus mutans, indicating better prevention of dental caries and plaque buildup without sacrificing biocompatibility [39]. CFU reduction and biofilm formation assays confirmed these improvements. Coatings of ZnO and silver nanoparticles on stainless steel brackets have demonstrated antibacterial activity against Lactobacillus acidophilus and Streptococcus mutans, with at least three months of sustained effectiveness. The antibacterial properties of composite coatings that combined zinc oxide and silver nanoparticles were even more potent than those of either nanoparticle alone, as validated by CFU counts and biofilm inhibition assays [46].

Other silver composite coatings are still being researched. For instance, in tests that replicated varying sucrose concentrations, a silver-polymer coating on archwires did not show any discernible antibacterial activity against Streptococcus mutans. The application of this study to the complex oral microbiome is limited by its use of a single bacterial species, suggesting the need for additional research conducted in more clinically relevant settings [47]. Layer-by-layer deposition techniques have produced novel composite nanocoatings that combine polydopamine, functionalized poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT), and AgNPs. Antibacterial evaluation in these coatings was performed using CFU enumeration and biofilm assays, confirming their antifouling activity. These coatings exhibit encouraging antifouling and antibacterial qualities on stainless steel surfaces. These coatings are promising options for upcoming orthodontic bracket and archwire coatings because they release silver ions at biocompatible and non-toxic levels to people [44, 48].

Silver is a strong candidate for preventing colonization by cariogenic and periodontal pathogens, which will help to reduce smooth-surface caries during orthodontic treatment. This is due to its long history of use as an antibacterial agent and numerous indications of its biocompatibility [39]. Because brackets are rarely replaced, its long-lasting antimicrobial action is particularly beneficial. However, limited corrosion resistance, relatively low hardness, and high material costs are some of the drawbacks of silver coatings. The advancement of the functional performance of orthodontic antimicrobial surfaces could be achieved by overcoming these constraints while preserving antibacterial efficacy through the creation of silver composite coatings that incorporate additional materials [43]. Titanium and titanium-based compound coatings

When exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light, titanium dioxide (TiO2) produces extremely reactive hydroxyl radicals (OH) and reactive oxygen species (ROS), demonstrating its photosensitive properties. The antibacterial properties of TiO2 are supported by these reactive species’ easy interaction with organic molecules. As a result, researchers are becoming more interested in using TiO2 coatings’ antibacterial qualities [49, 50].

Numerous studies have shown that TiO2-coated stainless steel (SS) and nickel-titanium (NiTi) archwires have strong antiadhesive qualities against Streptococcus mutans in addition to bactericidal effects against Porphyromonas gingivalis, Candida albicans, Enterococcus faecalis, and Streptococcus mutans. Likewise, it has been demonstrated that TiO2-coated brackets decrease adhesion and eliminate Lactobacillus acidophilus, Candida albicans, and Streptococcus mutans. For instance, TiO2-coated brackets demonstrated a 2–3 log reduction in L. acidophilus and C. albicans counts in CFU assays, with inhibition zones averaging 12–15 mm in agar diffusion tests [51, 52].

There are various crystalline forms of TiO2, but the most common ones are rutile, anatase, and brookite. Anodic oxidation usually produces the anatase form, whereas thermal oxidation produces the rutile phase. According to certain research, anatase coatings on titanium or titanium-silver (TiAg) substrates are more effective than rutile films at inhibiting Streptococcus mutans. There are conflicting results, though. For example, some studies using various TiO2 crystal structures on stainless steel brackets found that the rutile phase had higher cytotoxicity and stronger antibacterial activity than anatase. Despite its antibacterial potency, the rutile form is generally regarded as less desirable due to this cytotoxic risk, which also compromises biocompatibility [49, 51].

Quantitative assays confirm TiO2 activity in vitro and in vivo. TiO2 nanoparticle coatings on NiTi archwires showed strong antibacterial and antiadhesive properties against Streptococcus mutans. However, after a month, a large amount of the coating was gone, and the surface roughness was comparable to that of wires without a coating. CFU assays demonstrated ~70% reduction in bacterial adhesion within the first two weeks, while SEM imaging confirmed partial coating loss by day 30. In one clinical trial, plaque index scores decreased by ~55% compared with uncoated wires after two weeks [53, 54]. Because of the antibacterial qualities of the nanoparticles, bacterial adhesion was still lower than that of uncoated wires in spite of this degradation. In the first few weeks after application, TiO2 coatings on stainless steel archwires demonstrated comparable anti-Streptococcus mutans properties. As a result, these coatings work well to reduce early bacterial colonization on SS and NiTi wires. Nevertheless, there is still insufficient evidence to support their ability to stop enamel demineralization, which emphasizes the need for more clinical experimental validation, particularly with regard to their impact on periodontal health [55, 56].

In addition to the direct application of TiO2 coatings, titanium and its alloys naturally form a thin layer of TiO2 oxide, which adds to their inherent antibacterial qualities. In an effort to increase antibacterial efficacy above and beyond that of pure TiO2, the incorporation of silver (Ag) into titanium alloys to produce TiAg composites has also been investigated [54, 56]. When compared to TiO2-coated pure titanium, studies show that TiAg alloy surfaces coated with TiO2 have stronger and faster bactericidal effects against Streptococcus mutans, achieving a ~ 90% reduction in viable CFUs within 24 h compared with ~65% for TiO2 alone. While neither coating showed cytotoxicity, TiAg coatings did not show better antibacterial activity against Lactobacillus acidophilus than Ti alone. This implies that both materials have potential for fixed orthodontic appliances; however, more research is necessary to determine the specific advantages of silver inclusion. There is currently a research gap because Ti and TiAg coatings have not been used on archwires or brackets in clinical practice [53, 55]. Furthermore, the durability of these coatings under functional loading has not been tested because prior research has not evaluated them under the mechanical stresses common to orthodontic use [57].

TiO2’s dependence on UV light to initiate the production of hydroxyl radicals is one of its noteworthy drawbacks; visible light, which is mostly found in clinical settings, dramatically reduces its photocatalytic efficiency [58, 59]. This has been addressed by the development of nitrogen-doped TiO2 (TiO2−xNy) thin films, which exhibit strong bactericidal activity in the presence of visible light and are sensitive to it. RF magnetron sputtering-fabricated TiO2−xNy coatings on stainless steel brackets have shown high inhibitory rates against a variety of oral pathogens, such as Lactobacillus acidophilus, Streptococcus mutans, Actinomyces viscosus, and Candida albicans. In a long-term antimicrobial assessment, CFU counts of S. mutans on coated brackets were ~37.7 ± 5.2 at 24 h and remained stable at ~38 CFU for up to 90 days, compared with ~401 ± 14 CFU in uncoated controls (p < 0.001) [43]. Another study reported antimicrobial efficacy rates of 95.2% against S. mutans, 91.0% against L. acidophilus, 69.4% against A. viscosus, and 98.9% against C. albicans under visible light for 24 h [44]. To optimize visible-light-driven photocatalytic and antibacterial performance, ideal coating parameters have been found, including sputtering temperature, argon-to-nitrogen ratio, and annealing temperature. TiO2 nanofilms have also been produced by physical vapor deposition (PVD) techniques, which consistently produce antibacterial results irrespective of thickness variations [60–62].

However, a lot of experimental designs require long exposure times to visible light (24 h, for example), which are not feasible in clinical settings. Therefore, to confirm the practical efficacy of nitrogen-doped TiO2 coatings in orthodontics, more animal models, simulation studies, and randomized clinical trials are required [63]. In fact, under standard clinical lighting conditions, TiO2-xNy-coated stainless steel brackets significantly reduced the rise in Streptococcus mutans for up to 60 days after application, with the strongest effects seen at 30 days, according to one randomized controlled clinical trial. However, no direct evaluation of gingival inflammation or enamel demineralization was carried out, highlighting the necessity of additional research to elucidate these clinical outcomes [64, 65].

TiO2 coatings exhibit improved antimicrobial properties when paired with other materials. For instance, TiO2-incorporated silver on stainless steel brackets demonstrated better bactericidal and antiadhesive properties against Poralso been investigated for their antibacterial propertiephyromonas gingivalis and Streptococcus mutans, reducing bacterial adhesion by ~82% compared with controls in biofilm assays [64, 66].

Although they seem less effective than TiO2 or carbon nitride coatings, titanium nitride (TiN) coatings have also been investigated for their antibacterial properties. Experimental data showed that TiN surfaces achieved less than 10% reduction in S. mutans adhesion compared to controls, and in some cases even increased surface friction, potentially favoring bacterial retention. As a result, more research is necessary to determine the clinical utility of TiN as an antibacterial surface modification [63, 67]. Promising antibacterial properties and enhanced biocompatibility have been shown by novel nanofunctional coatings that use plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition of fluorine (F) and oxygen (O) onto titanium surfaces. Fluorine-deposition coatings achieved complete elimination of Staphylococcus aureus within 24 h, while dual fluorine-oxygen deposition provided more sustained antibacterial effects in long-term testing [68, 69].

In general, the antibacterial properties of titanium-based coatings are primarily derived from the photocatalytic activity of TiO2. They have benefits like good biocompatibility and resistance to periodontal and cariogenic infections. TiO2 coatings, however, are typically better suited for short-term applications because they tend to limit long-term efficacy by reducing initial bacterial colonization but degrading quickly [68, 70]. Practical difficulties for clinical use are also presented by increased surface friction and reliance on light activation. Composite titanium coatings containing nitrogen, fluorine, oxygen, and silver enhance antibacterial performance; however, issues with coating durability and friction have not been addressed. Titanium-based coating types and clinical insights

Zinc and zinc compound coatings

The ability of zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles to prevent the formation of biofilms, combined with their low toxicity and good biocompatibility, makes them appealing options for use in biomedicine [73]. According to a 2015 study by Baratali Ramazanzadeh and associates, brackets coated with ZnO nanoparticles using spray pyrolysis showed antibacterial activity. Specifically, ZnO-coated brackets reduced S. mutans counts from 140.8 ± 65.8 CFU at baseline to 96.8 ± 46.2 CFU at 2 h, 55.9 ± 51.9 CFU at 6 h, but increased again to 219.3 ± 304.5 CFU at 24 h. However, their effectiveness was less than that of coatings made of copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticles or composite CuO-ZnO nanoparticles, which completely eliminated S. mutans (0 CFU) within 2–4 h, compared with the control group that increased to 921.3 ± 823.7 CFU at 24 h [74]. It’s crucial to remember that these CuO-containing coatings’ biocompatibility was not assessed. The safety profile of CuO needs to be carefully examined because, even with strong antibacterial effects, increased cytotoxicity may prevent clinical application. On the other hand, ZnO’s biocompatibility has been confirmed, allowing for a wider range of medical device applications [75, 76].

On the surfaces of NiTi archwires, ZnO nanoparticle coatings have been shown to have antibacterial properties against a variety of pathogens, such as Escherichia coli, Streptococcus mutans, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus pyogenes. Studies indicate that ZnO coatings reduced E. coli and S. aureus viability by more than 70–80% within 24 h, while inhibition of S. mutans was about 60–65% compared with uncoated wires. The physicochemical properties of ZnO particles are influenced by a number of fabrication processes, including sol-gel methods, chemical vapor deposition (CVD), electrospinning, chemical precipitation, and polymer composite coatings [77, 78]. There is a definite trend that antibacterial efficacy is increased by smaller nanoparticle size, which is correlated with a larger specific surface area. Furthermore, ZnO coatings’ esthetic appeal—which is usually white or light golden—maintains stability without fading over time, providing a visual appeal advantage [53, 79].

Despite these encouraging results, some studies have questioned ZnO’s antibacterial efficacy and found it to be insufficient. One such study was conducted in 2021 and used photocatalytic ZnO coatings on stainless steel (SS) brackets using magnetron sputtering. The need for additional study to elucidate the clinical feasibility of ZnO nanoparticle coatings on orthodontic appliances like brackets and archwires is highlighted by the discrepancy in reported results [53, 80]. In addition to ZnO and CuO, ZnO has also been combined with AgNPs to form composite coatings. When applied to SS brackets, such ZnO-Ag composite coatings showed better antibacterial qualities against Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus acidophilus than ZnO alone, according to research by Noha K. Zeidan et al. For example, ZnO-Ag composites achieved ~90% reduction in S. mutans adhesion compared with ~60% for ZnO alone. By combining the mechanical strength of ZnO with the strong antimicrobial properties of silver, this synergistic combination produces coatings that are more effective overall [81].

In conclusion, ZnO’s antibacterial qualities, friction-reducing effects, biocompatibility, and esthetic suitability make it a promising multifunctional coating for clinical orthodontic applications. However, there are still concerns about its long-term durability and resistance to corrosion, and there is ongoing discussion about ZnO’s antibacterial properties alone. Ag nanoparticle-containing composite formulations have demonstrated encouraging gains in antibacterial activity, which makes this a particularly interesting line of inquiry for further research. Polymeric and bioactive materials

Clinical reports of antibacterial properties of epoxy coatings applied as esthetic layers on NiTi archwires have been made; however, these results were based on total bacterial counts from plaque samples without bacterial species differentiation. Epoxy coatings on stainless steel or NiTi archwires have been shown in vitro to temporarily decrease Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus distans adhesion, according to studies on the bacteria [85, 86]. For example, CFU counts of S. mutans decreased from ~720 to ~310 within 24 h (p < 0.05), although the effect diminished by 48 h. Instead of surface roughness changes, a decrease in surface free energy is the suggested mechanism for this effect. However, other studies have shown that epoxy coatings do not significantly reduce bacterial colonization and may actually increase the accumulation of biofilm on NiTi archwires [87]. This disparity highlights the necessity of additional experimental verification in settings that closely resemble the oral environment, such as animal models and clinical studies.

Another traditional polymeric esthetic coating material is polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). According to clinical research, PTFE coatings can lessen the development of plaque biofilm on NiTi archwires and stainless steel brackets (biofilm quantification tests). One study reported ~30% reduction in S. mutans colonization on PTFE-coated wires compared with uncoated controls (p < 0.05), although other studies found negligible effects. Coatings made at lower temperatures also show better microbial resistance [88]. Nevertheless, some studies indicate that PTFE has no discernible effect on the initial bacterial adherence, which is consistent with inconsistent findings regarding epoxy coatings [87]. When PTFE and silver nanoparticles are combined, antibacterial reduction exceeded 45% compared to PTFE alone, highlighting a synergistic effect (CFU assays) [89].

Biofilm formation on brackets has been demonstrated to be decreased by other polymeric coatings, such as silicon oxide (SiOx) hydrophobic and superhydrophobic films made by sol-gel processes, with superhydrophobic versions outperforming hydrophobic ones. Biofilm assays in saliva models showed that superhydrophobic SiOx coatings had ~50% less bacterial coverage compared to hydrophobic coatings after 48 h exposure. These coatings’ hydrophobic properties provide a believable explanation for their antimicrobial effectiveness by forming an interface with saliva that prevents bacterial adherence [90]. Since the initial findings, no further research has thoroughly examined these coatings on orthodontic appliances, despite encouraging results.

Other antibacterial polymer coatings include chitosan (CTS), which is mainly used in orthodontic bonding resins to control enamel demineralization, but its potential as a direct surface coating has not yet been investigated, and 2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine (MPC), which prevents Streptococcus mutans from adhering to stainless steel archwires. Particularly at a molecular weight of 5000, PEG-coated stainless steel archwires showed ~60% reduction in S. mutans adhesion (from ~800 to ~320 CFU within 10 h, p < 0.01), demonstrating remarkable antiadhesive qualities [80, 91, 92].

In addition to improvements in corrosion resistance and biocompatibility, bioactive materials—like lysozyme coatings on orthodontic composite archwires—have demonstrated antibacterial properties, mainly against Staphylococcus aureus in artificial saliva. CFU assays in artificial saliva showed S. aureus decreased from 1.2 × 10⁶ to 3.1 × 10⁵ within 24 h (~74% reduction, p < 0.01). Their effectiveness against other oral pathogens, like Porphyromonas gingivalis and Streptococcus mutans, hasn’t been evaluated, though. Lysozyme’s propensity to be more effective against Gram-positive bacteria raises concerns about possible disruption of the oral microbial balance, which calls for additional in vivo research [45, 54].

Through antiadhesion mechanisms, other bioactive antibacterial coatings function. For example, bovine serum albumin (BSA)-coated wires reduced bacterial adhesion by ~45% compared to uncoated surfaces [93]. Additionally, a hydrogel made of polyethylene glycol and chitosan (CTS/PEG) has been created, and it has demonstrated notable enhancements in antimicrobial and antiadhesive qualities on coated archwires (biofilm and adhesion assays), suggesting that it may find use in dental materials in the future [94].

Polydopamine (PDA) coatings with blue fluorescent hollow carbon dots (HCDs) are another recent development that exhibits over 50% antimicrobial activity against Streptococcus mutans and Escherichia coli for up to two weeks. HCDs’ fluorescent qualities provide a novel approach to clinical monitoring and make it easier to replace coatings on time. Due to their high biosafety profiles, low toxicity, and low risk of drug resistance, these bioactive materials have a lot of promise for clinical translation [95].

Methods for fabricating antibacterial coatings

Physical and chemical deposition processes are the two main types of techniques used in the creation of antibacterial coatings. Techniques like evaporation and different sputtering methods, such as magnetron sputtering, radiofrequency sputtering, and high-energy ion scattering, are examples of physical deposition methods. However, there are two types of chemical deposition techniques: liquid-phase methods, which include spin coating, dip coating, sol-gel, and spray pyrolysis, and vapor-phase processes, which include electrochemical deposition and atomic layer deposition. A number of coating fabrication techniques that have been used specifically in the orthodontics field are described in the section that follows [96, 97].

Physical vapor deposition

A class of vacuum-based procedures known as physical vapor deposition (PVD) involves vaporizing solid materials and then condensing them onto a substrate surface to create a thin film. Typically, thermal evaporation or physical processes, such as glow discharge or arc discharge, are employed to achieve this. For instance, PVD can be used to deposit silver coatings on the surface of nickel-titanium (NiTi) and stainless steel (SS) orthodontic archwires [98]. The most common PVD method for applying titanium dioxide (TiO₂) layers to stainless steel brackets is radiofrequency (RF) magnetron sputtering. With this method, material is spewed from a target using plasma, and under high vacuum conditions, it deposits uniformly onto the substrate surface. Zinc oxide (ZnO) coatings are also made using magnetron sputtering. For example, a composite ZnO/CQDs coating can be made by sputtering ZnO layers after first applying a solution containing carbon quantum dots (CQDs) onto stainless steel brackets. This combines the special qualities of both materials [99].

Chemical vapor deposition

Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) is a process that creates a thin, homogeneous coating by reacting gaseous chemical precursors and depositing solid materials onto a substrate, usually under plasma excitation or high temperatures. The composition and thickness of the coating can be precisely controlled using this technique. When applied to NiTi archwires, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) made by CVD usually have a uniform particle size distribution, frequently reported to be between about 59 and 61 nanometers [100]. This results in coatings with high density and excellent particle dispersion. The antibacterial layer’s performance and consistency are improved by this homogeneity. CVD-synthesised coatings have been used in most studies on surface modification of clear aligners, demonstrating the method’s dependability and reproducibility in orthodontic device applications.

Chemical precipitation

Chemical precipitation is the process of forming solid particles from a solution by means of a regulated chemical reaction, usually involving the addition of a precipitating agent to a solution containing metals, under controlled pH and temperature conditions. As a result, the target metal compound nucleates and deposits on the desired carrier material’s surface. For example, a zinc nitrate solution can be made more alkaline by adding ammonia while vigorously stirring the mixture to create zinc oxide nanoparticles. Particle formation may be impacted by the existence or lack of an external electric field during this process. The resulting ZnO nanoparticles are uniformly distributed, typically have a hexagonal shape with rounded ends, and range in diameter from about 30 to 150 nanometers. Chemical precipitation-produced coatings on NiTi archwires have shown strong antibacterial activity; bacterial inhibition rates have been reported to be as high as 96% [101].

Sol-gel method

When substrates are submerged in the sol and then dried and heated, the sol-gel technique allows for the formation of nanoparticle coatings by transforming a system from a liquid “sol” (a colloidal suspension of particles) into a solid “gel” phase. ZnO nanoparticles can be applied to NiTi archwires using the dip-coating variation of this technique, creating layers with advantageous structural and functional characteristics. ZnO nanoparticles made using the sol-gel process typically have a higher surface-to-volume ratio and are smaller in size than those made using other fabrication methods. Silver’s well-known antimicrobial qualities combined with the synergistic effects of both metal oxides result in Ag/ZnO composites that have superior antibacterial activity when added to ZnO nanoparticles synthesized by sol-gel [102].

Discussion

The delicate balance of the oral microbiome is disrupted by the use of orthodontic appliances, which often leads to an increase in harmful bacteria. These microbes hinder the maintenance of oral health during orthodontic treatment because they play a significant role in the development of periodontal diseases and enamel demineralization [102]. Traditional antimicrobial methods, such as mouthwashes and toothpastes, struggle to provide a lasting antibacterial effect due to their temporary nature and poor retention in the oral cavity [103]. Likewise, antibacterial treatments applied solely to the adhesives that secure the brackets to the enamel surface have limited effectiveness in preventing bacteria from colonizing [104]. Consequently, there is growing consensus that directly modifying the orthodontic appliances themselves for antibacterial properties is the most effective strategy for long-term microbial control, ensuring ongoing antimicrobial activity throughout treatment [105].

This review summarizes the most recent developments in antibacterial coatings specifically designed for orthodontic devices. Important topics covered include the classification of various coating materials suitable for orthodontic use, the underlying antibacterial mechanisms of these coatings, and the different techniques employed in coating fabrication. To steer the development of more effective antibacterial techniques integrated into orthodontic appliances, the objective is to provide researchers and orthodontic practitioners with a comprehensive understanding of the range of materials available and their practical applications in clinical settings [32].

Despite significant advancements, many questions remain unanswered regarding the theory and application of antibacterial coatings on orthodontic appliances. Most current studies primarily evaluate mechanical properties and antibacterial efficacy over short periods, which constitutes a major limitation [106]. It is essential to assess the long-term stability and durability of these coatings, as orthodontic treatments often last from months to years [107]. Additionally, most available data comes from in vitro studies that fail to accurately represent the complex and ever-changing conditions of the oral environment. Continuous pH fluctuations, variations in salivary flow and composition, and the presence of various dietary chemicals all characterize the oral cavity, influencing the effectiveness and durability of antibacterial coatings in ways that laboratory models cannot replicate [108].

In addition to their antibacterial efficacy, other crucial functional characteristics of orthodontic coatings must be considered. These include their ability to reduce friction between moving parts, maintain or enhance the mechanical integrity of the appliances, and exhibit biocompatibility to prevent adverse tissue reactions [12]. To optimize coating formulations, future research should investigate the interactions between antibacterial properties and these other factors, which remain not fully understood [96]. Furthermore, thorough research is needed to identify any potential negative effects associated with specific metallic elements used in coatings, such as cytotoxicity from silver ion release or allergic reactions to nickel ions [109]. To avoid unintentional patient harm, prioritizing the monitoring of ion release profiles and ensuring the safety of these materials for clinical use is essential.

The increasing popularity of clear aligners as an alternative to traditional fixed orthodontic appliances presents both new opportunities and challenges. There is currently a lack of research on the use of antibacterial nanoparticles (NPs) in clear aligner materials to prevent microbial growth [32]. Given the rising popularity of clear aligners, more research is necessary to assess the viability, effectiveness, and safety of antibacterial nanoparticle coatings in this context. Additionally, few materials and surface modification techniques have been integrated into standard clinical practice, particularly regarding friction control and bacterial adhesion mitigation, even though numerous materials and surface modification techniques have been proposed and tested to enhance the mechanical and antimicrobial properties of dental materials [96, 110]. Thus, to validate the promising in vitro results and determine their practical applicability in orthodontic treatment, robust clinical trials and longitudinal studies are essential.

In conclusion, significant effort needs to be made to address the current constraints, even though antibacterial coatings for orthodontic appliances offer a promising method to enhance oral health outcomes during treatment. Long-term assessments, in vivo validation, thorough property evaluations, and safety considerations should be the main areas of future research [111]. To further advance the field, it will be essential to speed up the conversion of laboratory innovations into clinical applications and expand the investigation of antibacterial strategies to include clear aligners.

Conclusion and future perspectives

The incorporation of antibacterial coatings onto orthodontic appliances represents a transformative approach to mitigating the risks of enamel demineralization, plaque accumulation, and periodontal complications during treatment. Among the various strategies explored, nanoparticle-based coatings—particularly those utilizing silver, titanium dioxide, and zinc oxide—have shown promising antimicrobial efficacy without significantly compromising the structural or mechanical properties of orthodontic components. Additionally, the integration of bioactive and polymeric coatings provides new avenues for enhancing both functional and esthetic performance.

Despite these advances, several challenges persist. Many studies remain confined to in vitro settings, failing to replicate the dynamic, multifactorial environment of the human oral cavity. The long-term durability, cytotoxicity, and mechanical stability of these coatings under routine orthodontic forces are still inadequately understood. Moreover, the potential for bacterial resistance, coating degradation, and adverse interactions with oral tissues must be carefully evaluated through robust in vivo studies and clinical trials.

Future research should prioritize the development of multifunctional coatings that offer not only antibacterial protection but also reduced friction, biocompatibility, and resistance to corrosion. As clear aligners become more popular, targeted studies on nanoparticle applications for thermoplastic materials will also be essential. Ultimately, translating laboratory findings into clinically viable solutions requires interdisciplinary collaboration, standardized evaluation protocols, and long-term follow-up studies to ensure both safety and efficacy in real-world orthodontic care.
