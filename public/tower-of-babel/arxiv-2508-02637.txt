Instance-Optimal Uniformity Testing and Tracking
Guy Blanc, Clément L. Canonne, Erik Waingarten

Abstract
In the uniformity testing task, an algorithm is provided with samples from an unknown probability distribution over a (known) finite domain, and must decide whether it is the uniform distribution, or, alternatively, if its total variation distance from uniform exceeds some input distance parameter. This question has received a significant amount of interest and its complexity is, by now, fully settled. Yet, we argue that it fails to capture many scenarios of interest, and that its very definition as a gap problem in terms of a prespecified distance may lead to suboptimal performance. To address these shortcomings, we introduce the problem of uniformity tracking , whereby an algorithm is required to detect deviations from uniformity (however they may manifest themselves) using as few samples as possible, and be competitive against an optimal algorithm knowing the distribution profile in hindsight. Our main contribution is a polylog ⁡ ( opt ) \operatorname{polylog}(\operatorname{opt}) -competitive uniformity tracking algorithm. We obtain this result by leveraging new structural results on Poisson mixtures, which we believe to be of independent interest.

Introduction

You are stationed at a telescope station, tasked with monitoring a large array of sensors: every second, one of the many cells of the array goes off, recording the impact of a seemingly random particle. In normal times, there is no pattern to these impacts: each of the
n

The signal would show this as a deviation from uniformity, with the random impacts on the array cells showing a different statistical pattern. But access to such a state-of-the-art telescope is a valuable resource, and observation time is precious:
how quickly will you be able to detect a non-uniform signal? How many seconds do you need?

1.1
The Uniformity Tracking Setting

We now rigorously introduce the problem we study, before motivating our definitional choice and making a few crucial observations. Throughout, we fix a universe size
n\in\mathds{N}
, and denote by
\mathbf{u}
the uniform distribution over
[n]

uniformity tracking algorithms
, the main algorithmic notion of this work. We then describe and motivate how we will measure its performance, which will be a competitive analysis framework; before providing a simple reduction to a slightly different version of the problem, which we term
instance-optimal uniformity testing
. Finally, we show that many of the known uniformity testing algorithms, while optimal from a worst-case perspective, are not optimal in the competitive framework.

Definition 1

(Uniformity Tracking)
.

Fix the universe size
n\in\mathds{N}
and failure probability
\delta\in(0,1]

is a randomized algorithm with the following guarantees:

•

Input
: There is an unknown distribution
\bm{p}
supported on
[n]

.

•

Output
: The algorithms runs continuously and outputs
plausible

reject
after each sample. Once the algorithm outputs
reject

The algorithm runs continuously and should satisfy the following completeness and soundness guarantees.

•

Completeness

\bm{p}=\mathbf{u}
, the probability the algorithm ever outputs

is at most
\delta

•

Soundness

\bm{p}\neq\mathbf{u}

reject

For any
\bm{p}\neq\mathbf{u}
, the

t(\bm{p})\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}

As discussed earlier, unlike in traditional distribution testing, a defining feature of a uniformity tracking algorithm (Definition
1
) is that there is no notion of a proximity parameter (often denoted “
\varepsilon

always

\bm{p}
. As the algorithm tracks the samples from
\bm{p}
and non-uniformity remains undetected, it is plausible that the unknown distribution
\bm{p}
is uniform; however, the moment the algorithm has convincing evidence that
\bm{p}
is non-uniform, it must reject as soon as possible. A simple consequence is that an algorithm never fully “accepts”: for any finite

which is not uniform, but indistinguishable from uniform with fewer than

s

A crucial question is then how to quantify the performance of a uniformity tracking algorithm. In particular, if we require an algorithm to be competitive against “the best possible algorithm,” we must define a meaningful notion of what it means to be “best possible” without being vacuous. We introduce next the notion of competitiveness we will consider, before discussing and motivating it:

Remark 1

(Impossibility of a stronger goal.)

Worst-case optimal uniformity testers may not be competitive.

Consider the collision-based uniformity testing algorithm, which after taking
m
samples, counts the fraction of pairwise collisions, and outputs
accept
or
reject
based on whether this fraction is higher or lower than a fixed threshold. This tester is known to be optimal in the classical setting where performance is measured with respect to
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\bm{p},\bm{u})

[
DGPP19
]
.

Given
m
samples from the uniform distribution, the expected fraction of distinct pairs
(i,j)
which “collide”, i.e., where
\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{x}_{j}
, is exactly
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}=1/n
, since this is the probability that two draws from a uniform distribution are the same. Furthermore, the variance of this fraction is at most
4/(m^{2}n)
(see page 26 in
[
Can22
]
). Consider the following non-uniform distribution
\bm{p}
: the first
n-1
elements appear with probability
(1-\beta)/n
and the last element with probability
\beta+(1-\beta)/n
(for some free parameter
\beta
). Here, the expected fraction of pairs which collide is
\|\bm{p}\|_{2}^{2}=\beta^{2}+(1-\beta^{2})/n
.

•

For this case, the number of samples in a collision-based test must be large enough so that the variance on the fraction of collisions in the uniform case is smaller than
\beta^{4}
(so that there is a gap between the number of collisions observed in
\mathbf{u}
and
\bm{p}
).
5
5

5

Specifically, we need to have
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\sqrt{4/(m^{2}n)}\ll\|\bm{p}\|_{2}^{2}

Doing so requires
4/(m^{2}n)\leq\beta^{4}

m=\Omega(1/(\beta^{2}\sqrt{n}))
.

•

However, there is a better test for
\bm{p}

appears
m/n\pm O(\sqrt{m\log n/n})

m\gg n

m/n\pm O(\sqrt{m\log n/n})
times. In
\bm{p}
, the last element appears
\beta m
times in expectation, so it suffices to set
m=O(\log n/(\beta^{2}n))
, a factor of
\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n})
better than the collision test.

This is not just about not knowing “
\varepsilon

As the above remark on worst-case uniformity testers makes it clear, the main conceptual feature of uniformity tracking is not simply the absence of a distance parameter
\varepsilon

would suffice, at the cost of only a doubly-logarithmic factor in

Competitive closeness testing.

We conclude this related work section by discussing the two papers most relevant to ours: that of Acharya, Das, Jafarpour, Orlitsky, and Pan

The part of their work most relevant to us is their lower bound, which implies that departing from the uniform distribution to allow arbitrary reference

1.3
Future work: Beyond uniformity

This work shows that uniformity tracking and testing, can be performed with sample size competitive with the optimal samples needed for the unknown distribution
\bm{p}
, where
\mathrm{opt}(\bm{p})
is measured with respect to all relabelings of
\bm{p}
. This naturally prompts a number of followup distribution testing problems. For what properties
P
can we obtain an algorithm that determines whether
\bm{p}\in P
with sample complexity scaling nearly linearly with
\mathrm{opt}_{P}(\bm{p})
, and what is the right notion of
\mathrm{opt}_{P}(\bm{p})
for more general properties? In general, it seems appropriate that the notion of
\mathrm{opt}_{P}(\cdot)
should depend on the symmetry of the property
P
, as ours does for the uniform distribution.

We list two concrete directions to explore. The first is to consider properties that are still fully invariant under relabelings of the distributions (the so-called
symmetric properties
), but are broader than the property of “being uniform.” For example, is there an instance-optimal tester for the property of having high entropy, where
\mathrm{opt}
is still defined with respect to all relabelings? To the best of our knowledge, it is conceivable that all convex (i.e. properties closed under taking mixtures) symmetric properties admit a nearly instance-optimal tester.

The second direction is to consider properties that are not fully invariant under relabelings of the distribution. For example, if the domain is
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{n}
, is there an instance-optimal tester that determines whether an unknown distribution
\bm{p}
over
\mathcal{X}
is uniform over some subspace of
\mathcal{X}
? Here, part of the challenge is designing the right notion of
\mathrm{opt}
. Intuitively, that definition should only allow relabelings that respect the linear structure of
\mathcal{X}
. Does such an
\mathrm{opt}
suffice?

2
Technical Overview

A tester receives as input a list of frequencies
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}
, each corresponding to the number of times it sampled some particular element
i\in[n]
. We wish for our tester to be universal in the sense that, given
m^{\prime}=\tilde{O}(m)
samples from some
\bm{p}
, if there is any test that distinguishes
\bm{p}
and all its relabelings from
\bm{u}
using
m
samples, then our test should work using
m^{\prime}
samples without needing to know
\bm{p}
. A natural first attempt would be to (i) consider all possible tests
T\colon[m]^{n}\to\{0,1\}
which do not often reject the uniform distribution, and (ii) run all these tests in parallel, while increasing the success probability so as to union bound over the probability that any test incorrectly rejects the uniform distribution. Alas, the number of such tests is too large (namely,
2^{m^{n}}
), which means that this algorithm is inefficient, and the overhead incurred from the union bound is
O(m^{n})
; yielding only a
O(m^{n})
-competitive algorithm. One way to understand our algorithm is as providing a randomized discretization of this large set down to only polynomially many tests, a doubly exponential improvement.

The first step is the well-known “Poissonization” trick to make the frequencies independent. This allows us to assume, without loss of generality, that we receive independent frequencies, with the
i^{\text{th}}
frequency drawn from
\mathrm{Poi}(\bm{p}(i)\cdot m)
. Unfortunately, it will not suffice for our purposes to analyze fully independent frequencies. Instead, our notion of
\mathrm{opt}
-competitiveness requires us to understand distributions that are formed by first drawing
n
independent frequencies and then uniformly permuting them, which results in intricate correlations between frequencies.

Definition 3

(Permutation distribution)
.

For any
\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n}
, the distribution
\mathrm{Perm}(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n})
is defined as the distribution of
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}
obtained by the following process:

1.

Draw a uniform permutation of
[n]
,
(\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{n})
;

2.

For each
j\in[n]
, independently sample
\bm{x}_{i}\sim\bm{p}_{\bm{i}_{j}}
.

We will require our tester to succeed whenever
\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Poi}(m\cdot\bm{p}(1)),\ldots,\mathrm{Poi}(m\cdot\bm{p}(1)))
is “distinguishable” from the frequencies corresponding to the uniform distribution, which are simply
\mathrm{Poi}(m/n)^{n}
.

Definition 4

(Distinguishability)
.

We say that distributions
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
are

The structure of the proof.

The proof of
Theorem

3
has two main steps.

1.

Subsampling to reduce to independent mixtures:
The first step is a randomized reduction which allows us to move from dependent to independent frequencies. We show it suffices to design a tester that distinguishes a single known Poisson from an unknown mixture of Poissons with nearly optimal sample complexity. That is, the new tester must reject if given
m^{\prime}\coloneqq\tilde{O}(m)
i.i.d. samples from a Poisson mixture
\bm{p}=\frac{\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1})+\cdots+\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{k})}{k}
that is distinguishable from
\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)
using
m
samples. This simplifies our task as we no longer need to reason about the correlation structure of the frequencies.

To do so, we show the following more general result: For any distributions
\bm{q}
and
\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n}
for which
\bm{q}^{n}
and
\mathrm{Perm}(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n})
are distinguishable, there is some subsampling size
k\in[n]
for which, with “moderate” probability over indices
\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{k}
chosen uniformly without replacement from
[n]
, the mixture distribution
\frac{\bm{p}_{\bm{i}_{1}}+\cdots+\bm{p}_{\bm{i}_{k}}}{k}
is “moderately” distinguishable from
\bm{q}
. We note that this step does not necessarily hold
without subsampling
(i.e., if we fixed
k=n
): One can easily construct distributions
\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n}
whose mixture is exactly
\bm{q}
, and yet,
\mathrm{Perm}(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n})
is easily distinguishable from
\bm{q}
. Intuitively, in these cases, the distributions
\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n}
must be quite far apart, which is why one might expect a subsampled mixture to be far from
\bm{q}
.

2.

The interval tester for mixtures of Poissons:
We show for any single Poisson
\bm{q}\coloneqq\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)
and mixture of Poissons
\bm{p}\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1})+\cdots+\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{k})}{k}
, if
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
are distinguishable using
m
samples, there is a simple “interval tester” that distinguishes
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
using
\tilde{O}(m)
samples. This interval tester furthermore depends only on
\bm{q}
and need not know the parameters
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
. We formally give its pseudocode in
Algorithm

1
. Briefly, it enumerates over polynomially many intervals. For each interval, it counts how many of its samples appeared in that interval and rejects iff any of these counts deviate significantly from their expectation under
\bm{q}
.

2.1
The interval tester for mixtures of Poissons

We begin by sketching a warm-up result: For any single Poisson
\bm{q}\coloneqq\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)
and mixture of Poissons
\bm{p}\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1})+\cdots+\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{k})}{k}

\bm{p}
and

Hence, our assumption that

1.

Output

The difficulty is that the set

\bm{p}
which in turn depend on the parameters

or

Preliminaries

Throughout, we denote by
\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)
the Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda\geq 0
, by
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
the Bernoulli distribution with parameter
p\in[0,1]
, by
\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
the Binomial distribution with parameters
n\in\mathds{N}
and
p\in[0,1]
, and
\bm{u}
the uniform distribution over
[n]
(with
n
typically clear from context). Other than these standard distributions, we will use
\bm{p},\bm{q}
and their various subscripts to denote other distributions. Random variables will also be written in
boldfont
and use symbols other than
\bm{p}
or
\bm{q}
, (e.g.
\bm{x}\sim\bm{p}
).

For any distributions
\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n}
, we use
\frac{\bm{p}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{p}_{n}}{n}
to denote the uniform mixture over
\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n}
. In particular, it will often be convenient to refer to mixtures of Poisson distributions without specifying the parameters of the mixture.

Definition 5

(Poisson mixture)
.

We say a distribution
\bm{p}
is a
Poisson mixture
if there is some
n\in\mathds{N}
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
for which
\bm{p}=\frac{\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1})+\cdots+\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{n})}{n}
.

We recall the definitions of some notions of distances between probability distributions that our proofs will rely on. For simplicity, we focus on the discrete, finite domain case, as this will be sufficient for our purposes.

Definition 6

(Total variation distance)
.

For any two distributions
\bm{p},\bm{q}
on a discrete domain
\mathcal{X}
, the
total variation distance
(or statistical distance)
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\bm{p},\bm{q})
between
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
is defined by

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\bm{p},\bm{q})=\sup_{S\subseteq\mathcal{X}}(\bm{p}(S)-\bm{q}(S))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\bm{p}(x)-\bm{q}(x)|.

(1)

In particular,
d_{\mathrm{TV}}
is a metric, and bounded in
[0,1]
.

Definition 7

(Hellinger distance)
.

For any two distributions
\bm{p},\bm{q}
on a discrete domain
\mathcal{X}
, the
Hellinger distance

d_{\mathrm{hel}}(\bm{p},\bm{q})
between
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
is defined by

d_{\mathrm{hel}}^{2}(\bm{p},\bm{q})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\sqrt{\bm{p}(x)}-\sqrt{\bm{q}(x)}\right)^{2}

(2)

In particular,
d_{\mathrm{hel}}
is a metric, and bounded in
[0,2]
.
8
8

8

The Hellinger distance is often defined with a factor
1/\sqrt{2}
to make it take values in
[0,1]
. To ease notation, and as it is not necessary in our setting, we omit this normalization factor.

Definition 8

(Kullback–Leibler divergence)
.

For any two distributions
\bm{p},\bm{q}
on a discrete domain
\mathcal{X}
, the
Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
(or relative entropy)
\mathrm{KL}\left({\bm{p}}\,\middle\|\,{\bm{q}}\right)
between
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
is defined by

\mathrm{KL}\left({\bm{p}}\,\middle\|\,{\bm{q}}\right)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\bm{p}(x)\ln\frac{\bm{p}(x)}{\bm{q}(x)}\,,

(3)

where
\ln
is the natural logarithm, and by convention
0\ln 0=0
.
Note that the KL divergence is non-negative and unbounded, but not symmetric, and does not satify the triangle inequality.

Total variation distance can be related to Hellinger distance through the following standard inequalities (see, e.g.,
[
Can22
, Lemma B.1]
):

\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathrm{hel}}^{2}(\bm{p},\bm{q})\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\bm{p},\bm{q})\leq d_{\mathrm{hel}}(\bm{p},\bm{q})

(4)

for every distributions
\bm{p},\bm{q}
.

We will use the following form of Chernoff bounds.

Fact 3.1

(Chernoff Bounds)
.

Let
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}
be independent random variables bounded in
[0,1]
and
\overline{\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\bm{x}_{i}
their empirical mean. For
p\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\overline{\bm{x}}]
and any
q\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}[\overline{\bm{x}}\geq q]\leq e^{-n\cdot\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathrm{Ber}(q)}\,\middle\|\,{\mathrm{Ber}(p)}\right)}\quad\quad\text{ if }q\geq p,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}[\overline{\bm{x}}\leq q]\leq e^{-n\cdot\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathrm{Ber}(q)}\,\middle\|\,{\mathrm{Ber}(p)}\right)}\quad\quad\text{ if }q\leq p.

We will use two corollaries of the above bound. First, using the fact that
\mathrm{KL}\left({\bm{p}}\,\middle\|\,{\bm{q}}\right)\geq d_{\mathrm{hel}}^{2}(\bm{p},\bm{q})
and union bounding over the above two inequalities, we have the following.

Corollary 3.2

(Chernoff bounds: Hellinger distance version)
.

In the setting of
Fact

3.1
,

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}\left[d_{\mathrm{hel}}^{2}(\mathrm{Ber}(p),\mathrm{Ber}(\overline{\bm{x}}))\geq\tau\right]\leq 2e^{-n\tau}.

Then, using that
\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathrm{Ber}(q)}\,\middle\|\,{\mathrm{Ber}(p)}\right)\geq q/6
whenever
q\geq 2p
, we have the following.

Corollary 3.3

(Chernoff bounds in the tail)
.

In the setting of
Fact

3.1
, for any
\delta\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}\left[n\overline{\bm{x}}\geq 2pn+\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{6}\right]\leq\delta.

From standard arguments (applying the above bound to
\mathrm{Bin}(n,\mu/n)
, and taking the limit as
n\to\infty
), one obtains the analogous statement for Poisson random variables:

Fact 3.4

(Tail bound for Poisson random variables)
.

For any
\mu\geq 0
and
\delta\in(0,1]
,

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)}\left[\bm{z}\geq 2\mu+\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{6}\right]\leq\delta.

d_{\mathrm{hel}}^{2}(\mathrm{Perm}(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n}),\bm{q}^{n})\geq\Omega(1)
. Next, we apply
Theorem

7
, which gives that,

\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Perm}(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n})}\left[d_{\mathrm{hel}}^{2}(\bm{p}(\bm{x}_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{\leq i-1}),\bm{q})\right]\geq\Omega(1).

where
\bm{p}(\bm{x}_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{\leq i-1})
is the marginal distribution of the
i
th
coordinate of
\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Perm}(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n})
conditioned on the first
i-1
coordinates.

From the above, we have that there is some
k\in[n]
for which

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Perm}(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n})}\left[d_{\mathrm{hel}}^{2}(\bm{p}(\bm{x}_{k}\mid\bm{x}_{\leq k-1}),\bm{q})\right]\geq\Omega\left(\textstyle\frac{1}{(n-k+1)\log n}\right).

Recall a sample of
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\sim\mathrm{Perm}(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n})
can be drawn by first setting
\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{n}
to a uniform permutation of
[n]
and then drawing
\bm{x}_{j}\sim\bm{p}_{\bm{i}_{j}}
independently for each
j\in[n]
. The way in which conditioning on
\bm{x}_{\leq k-1}=x_{\leq k-1}
affects the distribution of
\bm{x}_{k}
is by shifting the distribution of
\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{n}
to no longer be uniform over all permutations of
[n]
. In particular, it affects the distribution of
\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{k-1}
, which means that
\bm{i}_{k}
will no longer be uniform over
[n]
. Unfortunately, this conditioning is quite complicated, and difficult to get directly analyze: to address this, we apply
Fact

5.5
to “add even more conditioning”, and fully condition on the values of
\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{k-1}
, leading to a quantity easier to analyze. Combined with the prior bound, this gives

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{k-1}\overset{\mathrm{wor}}{\sim}[n]}\left[d_{\mathrm{hel}}^{2}\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{i}_{k}\sim[n]\setminus\{\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{k-1}\}}\left[\bm{p}_{\bm{i}_{k}}\right],\bm{q}\right)\right]\geq\Omega\left(\textstyle\frac{1}{(n-k+1)\log n}\right).

Finally, we can “flip” this expression by setting
\bm{j}_{1},\ldots,\bm{j}_{n-k+1}
to be the elements of
[n]\setminus\{\bm{i}_{1},\ldots,\bm{i}_{k-1}\}
and conclude that

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{j}_{1},\ldots,\bm{j}_{n-k+1}\overset{\mathrm{wor}}{\sim}[n]}\left[d_{\mathrm{hel}}^{2}\left(\frac{\bm{p}_{\bm{j}_{1}}+\cdots+\bm{p}_{\bm{j}_{n-k+1}}}{n-k+1},\bm{q}\right)\right]\geq\Omega\left(\textstyle\frac{1}{(n-k+1)\log n}\right).

Reparameterizing with the change of indices
\ell\coloneqq n-k+1
yields the lemma’s statement.
∎

6
Reduction to
Theorem

3

We prove that the formal version of
Theorem

2
stated below follows from
Theorem

3
.

Theorem 8

(Formal version of
Theorem

2
)
.

For any
n,m\in\mathds{N}
and
\delta>0
, there is an efficient tester using
m^{\prime}=O(m\cdot(\log m)^{2}\cdot\log(m/\delta))
i.i.d. samples from an unknown distribution
\bm{p}
supported on
[n]
satisfying

1.

Completeness
: If
\bm{p}=\mathbf{u}
, it outputs
accept
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

2.

Soundness
: If
\bm{p}\neq\mathbf{u}
and
\mathrm{opt}(\bm{p})\leq m
, it outputs
reject
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

The proof of
Theorem

3
mostly follows from the standard “Poissonization” trick and
Theorem

6
. However, a direct application would have an overhead with both
\log m
and
\log n
terms. To eliminate the
\log n
terms, we observe that when
m\ll\sqrt{n}
, the optimal tester essentially just rejects if it finds any collision.

Claim 6.1

(Small
\mathrm{opt}
implies high collision probability)
.

For any distribution
\bm{p}
on
[n]
with
\mathrm{opt}(\bm{p})=m\leq\sqrt{n}/2
if we sample
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\bm{p}
, then with probability at least
2/3
there is some
i\neq j\in[m]
for which
\bm{x}_{i}\neq\bm{x}_{j}
.

Proof.

We show that if the probability of a collision in
m
samples is less than
2/3
, then
\mathrm{opt}(\bm{p})>m
. This implies the desired result by contrapositive. To do so, we consider any tester
T
using
m
samples and show that either
T
does not accept the uniform distribution with probability
9/10
or that it does not reject some relabeling of
\bm{p}
with probability
9/10
.

Let
\alpha
be the probability that
T
accepts given
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}
chosen uniformly from
[n]
conditioned on all
m
values being unique. We separate this analysis into two cases based on the value of
\alpha
.

Case 1:

\alpha\leq 1/2
. In this case, we will argue that
T
fails to accept given samples from the uniform distribution with probability at least
0.9
. Using linearity of expectation, if given
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\bm{u}
, the probability that all are unique is at least
1-\frac{m^{2}}{n}\geq 3/4
. On such samples,
T
rejects with probability at least
1/2
. Hence, given samples from the uniform distribution, it rejects with probability at least
3/4\cdot 1/2=3/8
.

Case 2:

\alpha\geq 1/2
. In this case, we will argue that
T
fails to reject given samples from some relabeling of
\bm{p}
with probability at least
0.9
. Suppose we first draw a
uniform
relabeling of
\bm{p}
, denoted
\tilde{\bm{p}}
, and then draw
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\tilde{\bm{p}}
. Then, by assumption, the probability that all of
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}
are unique is at least
1/3
. Furthermore, when they are unique, they are equally likely to take on any
m
distinct values (since
\tilde{\bm{p}}
is a uniform relabeling).

Then, since
\alpha\geq 1/2
, we can conclude that given samples from
\tilde{\bm{p}}
,
T
accepts with probability at least
1/2\cdot 1/3=1/6
. In particular, this implies there exists a single relabeling of
\bm{p}
on which
T
accepts with probability at least
1/6
, as desired.

Hence, we have shown that any tester using
m
samples cannot distinguish
\bm{u}
from all relabelings of
\bm{p}
unless the probability of a collision in samples from
\bm{p}
is at least
1/2
. The desired result follows by contrapositive.
∎

For the Poissonization case, we will invoke the following standard fact.

Fact 6.2
.

For any distribution
\bm{p}
, if we take
\bm{m}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(m)
and then draw
\bm{m}
independent samples from
\bm{p}
, then using
\bm{x}_{i}
to denote the number of times
i
appears in the sample,
(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n})
is distributed as the product distribution
\mathrm{Poi}(m\cdot\bm{p}(1))\times\cdots\times\mathrm{Poi}(m\cdot\bm{p}(n))
. Furthermore, this process can be inverted: There is an algorithm that, without knowing
\bm{p}
, takes as input
\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(m\cdot\bm{p}(1))\times\cdots\times\mathrm{Poi}(m\cdot\bm{p}(n))
and outputs
\mathrm{Poi}(m)
independent samples from
\bm{p}
.

Claim 6.3
.

If
\mathrm{opt}(\bm{p})\leq m
, then for
m^{\prime}=O(m)
,
\mu\coloneqq m^{\prime}/n
and
\lambda_{i}\coloneqq\bm{p}(i)\cdot m^{\prime}
for each
i\in[n]
,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)^{n},\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{n}))\geq 1/2.

Proof.

Let
T
be the test using
m
samples guaranteed to exist from the fact that
\mathrm{opt}(\bm{p})\leq m
. Using it, we will construct a test
T^{\prime}:\mathds{N}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
for which

1.

Given a sample from
\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)^{n}
,
T^{\prime}
will accept with probability at least
3/4
.

2.

Given a sample from
\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{n}))
,
T^{\prime}
will reject with probability at least
3/4
.

Such a guarantee implies the desired TV distance bound. This test
T^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
works as follows: First, it uses the “inversion” part of
Fact

6.2
to generate
\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(m^{\prime})
many independent samples from some distribution
\bm{q}
. If
\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)^{n}
, then it will generate samples from
\bm{q}=\bm{u}
. On the other hand, if
\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{n})
, then it will have generated samples from a uniform relabeling of
\bm{p}
.

Then, if the number of samples it has generated,
\bm{z}
, it at least
m
, it accepts iff
T
accepts on the first
m
samples. on the other hand, if
\bm{z}<m
, it simply defaults to rejecting. By the guarantees of
T
and
Fact

6.2
, the failure probability of this test
T^{\prime}
is upper bounded by the sum of the failure probability of
T
(which is
0.1
) and the probability
\bm{z}<m
. If we set
m^{\prime}\coloneqq\max(2m,20)
, then
\operatorname{{Pr}}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(m^{\prime})}[\bm{z}<m]\leq 0.1
, giving a good enough bound on the total failure probability.
∎

Finally, we prove our main result for uniformity testing.

Proof of
Theorem

8
.

First, we handle the case where
m\leq\sqrt{n}/2
using
Claim

6.1
. In this case, our tester will use
m^{\prime}\coloneqq O(m\log(1/\delta))
samples. It breaks these into
O(\log 1/\delta))
groups each containing
m
samples and rejects if and only if a majority of groups contain at least one collision.

By
Claim

6.1
, if
\mathrm{opt}(\bm{p})\leq m
, then the probability each group has a collision is at least
2/3
. On the other hand, if
\bm{p}=\bm{u}
, the probability of a collision in each group is at most
m^{2}/n\leq 1/4
. By a standard concentration inequality (e.g.,
Fact

3.1
), we can conclude that the failure probability of this tester is at most
\delta
in both cases.

Next, we handle the case where
m>\sqrt{n}/2
. In this case we use Poissonization and
Theorem

6
. By
Claim

6.3
we know that for
m^{\prime}=O(m)
,
\mu\coloneq m^{\prime}/n
, and
\lambda_{i}\coloneqq\bm{p}(i)\cdot m^{\prime}
that
\mathrm{Poi}(\mu)^{n}
and
\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{n})
are distinguishable. Hence for

s\coloneqq O((\log n)^{2}\cdot(\log n+\log\mu+\log(1/\delta)))=O((\log m)^{2}\log(m/\delta)),

the tester of
Theorem

6
distinguishes between
\mathrm{Poi}(s\cdot\mu)^{n}
and
\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Poi}(s\cdot\lambda_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{Poi}(s\cdot\lambda_{n}))
with probability at least
1-\delta
. By
Fact

6.2
, if we draw
\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(O(sm))
and then take
\bm{z}
samples from
\bm{u}
, the frequency vector will be distributed as
\mathrm{Poi}(s\cdot\mu)^{n}
. Similarly, if we draw
\bm{z}
samples from
\bm{p}
, the frequency vector will be distributed as
\mathrm{Poi}(s\cdot\lambda_{1})\times\cdots\mathrm{Poi}(s\cdot\lambda_{n}))
, which we can uniformly permute to obtain a sample of
\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Poi}(s\cdot\lambda_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{Poi}(s\cdot\lambda_{n}))
.

Hence, with
\bm{z}
samples, we can use the tester
Theorem

6
to distinguish the two cases. Here, we use
Fact

3.4
to upper bound
\bm{z}
: With probability at least
1-\delta
,
\bm{z}
is at most
O(sm+\log(1/\delta))
. Note that
s\geq\log(1/\delta)
, so we only need
O(sm)=O(m\cdot(\log m)^{2}\cdot\log(m/\delta))
samples to have enough samples with probability at least
1-\delta
.

This tester can only fail if (1) it does not have enough samples or (2) the tester from
Theorem

6
fails. Union bounding over these two failure probabilities gives a tester that fails with probability
2\delta
. We simply reparameterize
\delta=\delta^{\prime}/2
to obtain the desired result.
∎
