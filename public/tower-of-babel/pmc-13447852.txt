Adaptation of hypothalamic CRH neuron responses to stress and pup cues during motherhood
Authors: Joon S. Kim, Dharshini Ganeshan, David R. Grattan, Karl J. Iremonger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The adaptation of stress responses during motherhood is critical for the well-being of the mother and the offspring. Stress responses are controlled by corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) neurons in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus. Although it is commonly believed that the activity of CRH neurons is dampened during motherhood, this hypothesis has not been directly tested. Using fibre photometry, we obtain optical recordings of PVN CRH neuron activity in freely behaving mice. Contrary to expectations, CRH neuron excitation in response to external stressors is not suppressed during lactation compared to virgin mice. However, we observe significant reductions in Crh mRNA expression and corticosterone release following stress in the lactating mice. In a pup retrieval task, there are large reductions of CRH neuron activity upon pup retrieval only in lactating mice. When lactating mice are in the presence of an inaccessible pup that could not be physically retrieved, this induces an amplified level of CRH neuron activity. These data show that during motherhood, CRH neuron activity is not globally suppressed. More importantly, we show for the first time that during lactation, CRH neuron responses to pup cues are amplified. These adaptations likely facilitate maternal behaviours that promote offspring survival.

Introduction

There are numerous changes in the body and brain that occur during pregnancy and lactation that enable a mother to successfully raise their offspring1–3. The suppression of stress responses is one adaptation that develops during pregnancy in humans, sheep and rodents and persists through lactation in all of these species4–14. While pregnancy-induced adaptations have been shown to occurs at multiple levels of the stress axis5, alterations in the function of hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) neurons are thought to be particularly important3.

CRH neurons in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus are the master controllers of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis15,16. These neurons control the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) from the pituitary gland and the subsequent release of glucocorticoid stress hormones from the adrenal gland. In addition to their neuroendocrine role17, these neurons also control arousal state18–20 and stress-related behaviours21–24. In many situations, it is advantageous to coordinate the neuroendocrine and behavioural responses to stress. However, because stress hormone secretion from the pituitary and adrenal gland can be regulated independently from CRH neural activity17,25,26, there may exist situations where CRH neuron activity and endocrine output are dissociated.

During lactation, it is well established that stress-evoked corticosterone release is suppressed in rats and mice7,9,11–13. Past studies have also reported that Crh mRNA expression in the PVN is reduced during lactation6,7,12,27 and that stress-induced cfos expression in the PVN is also reduced27,28. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that reduced CRH neuronal activity is important for the reduced responsiveness of the HPA axis during lactation. Reduced CRH neural activity is proposed to be adaptive, as central injections of CRH impedes pup nursing and protective behaviours and has also been associated with pup neglect and infanticide29–32. Likewise, chemogenetic activation of CRH neurons in the PVN has been shown to inhibit maternal behaviour33. While there is a general agreement that the HPA axis is suppressed during motherhood, evidence for reduced CRH neuron excitability is only indirect and comes from whole PVN mRNA measurements. For this reason, we set out to record PVN CRH neuron activity in vivo from freely behaving virgin and lactating mice under both basal and stress states. We also set out to determine how CRH neuron responses to pup cues change between virgin and lactating states.

Using in vivo GCaMP6s fibre photometry, we report that basal activity of PVN CRH neurons is not different between virgin and lactating mice. We also found that CRH neuron responses to white noise or novel environment stress were also not markedly different between virgin and lactating mice. In a pup retrieval task, we observed suppression of CRH neuron activity upon pup retrieval exclusively in lactating mice. When lactating mice were in the presence of an inaccessible pup, this induced a greater level of CRH neuron activation. This study is the first to show that while CRH neuron responses to threats are not markedly changed during lactation, CRH neurons become more responsive to pup-related cues. These adaptations likely facilitate maternal behaviours that promote offspring survival.

Results

Basal CRH neuron activity dynamics are similar between virgin and lactating mice

Fibre photometry recordings of PVN CRH neuron activity were performed in virgin non-pregnant female mice (n = 12; henceforth referred to as virgins) and lactating mice on postpartum day 4 (n = 18). We first characterised the basal CRH neuron activity dynamics over a 4-h recording period while animals were in their home cage. Similar to previous studies in males17, photometry recordings in virgin and lactating mice revealed continuously active neural activity. In order to induce a peak stress-induced response, mice were picked up by the base of the tail by an experimenter. The GCaMP response to pick-up stress was not different between virgin and lactating mice (virgin 0.55 ± 0.11 ΔF/F, lactating 0.52 ± 0.10 ΔF/F, P = 0.57; Fig. 1B). We then normalised the amplitude of transient GCaMP events that were measured during basal recordings such that the relative fluorescence of all mice were normalised to their relative peak amplitudes (ΔF/Fn). This allowed for comparison of GCaMP event kinetics between mice. Using this approach, we observed no differences in amplitude (virgin 0.095 ± 0.004 ΔF/Fn, lactating 0.10 ± 0.004 ΔF/Fn, P = 0.46; Fig. 1C), frequency (virgin 3.32 ± 0.55 events per min, lactating 3.31 ± 0.43 events per min, P = 0.99; Fig. 1D) or rise slopes between groups (virgin 0.031 ± 0.007 ΔF/Fn*s-1, lactating 0.032 ± 0.002 ΔF/Fn*s-1, P = 0.75; Fig. 1E); parameters we have previously reported differences in CRH neuron activity in negative feedback states17. Comparison of stress-evoked CRH neuron activity between virgin and lactating mice

Following the basal recordings, half the lactating mice were separated from their pups (referred to as the “pups removed” group). Previous studies have shown that pup removal for 24 h restores lactational hormone levels and corticosterone stress responses back towards virgin levels6,34. The next day (postpartum day 5 for lactating and pup-removed groups), all mice were exposed to a 5-min white noise stress within their home cage. All groups, (virgin n = 12, lactating n = 9, and pups removed n = 9) exhibited similar patterns of CRH neuron activation to the white noise stress (Fig. 2A). The kinetics of white noise stress responses was also similar to that previously described in males17. Peak CRH neuron ΔF/F was not significantly different between groups (Fig. 2B, P = 0.99, see Supplementary Table for full statistical reporting) and we normalised each mouse’s response to their peak values from the pick-up handling (Fig. 1B). CRH neuron activity was averaged into 5 min bins and compared before, during and immediately after the white noise stress. Reduced Crh mRNA and stress-evoked corticosterone release in lactating mice

Past studies have shown that lactation is associated with reductions in Crh mRNA in the PVN and reduced stress-induced corticosterone release7,12. To confirm these changes in our experiments, a separate series of mice were exposed to 5 min white noise stress, without fibre photometry. As expected, Crh mRNA levels in the PVN were significantly different between groups (One-way ANOVA, P = 0.017). Specifically, mRNA levels were reduced in lactating mice (postpartum day 7), compared to virgin (P = 0.014) and pup-removed groups (P = 0.038, Fig. 4A). We next measured basal and stress-evoked corticosterone. A two-way ANOVA revealed a non-significant effect of group (F(2,24) = 1.64, P = 0.21), a significant effect of time (F(1,24) = 71.01, P < 0.0001) and a significant interaction (F(2,24) = 4.31, P = 0.025). Blood corticosterone concentrations following the white noise stress were significantly elevated in virgin and pup removed groups, relative to their baseline (before vs after white noise corticosterone ng/ml difference: virgin 165.8 ± 25.7, P < 0.0001, pups removed 131.4 ± 23.0, P < 0.001; Fig. 4C, D). While this was also the case for lactating mice (63.33 ± 24.6, P = 0.0499; Fig. 4C), the magnitude of the elevation was significantly less compared to virgin mice (One way ANOVA, P = 0.025, Fig. 4D). Together, these observations indicate a dissociation of CRH neuron activity with the endocrine arm of the HPA axis during lactation, where the CRH neurons remain acutely responsive to stress, but appear to be synthesizing less CRH. Novel object increases CRH neuron activity, but not in lactating mice

We next examined how PVN CRH neuron activity changed following interactions with either a novel object (golf ball) or pups. We compared between virgin (n = 6), lactating (n = 7), and pups-removed (n = 6) mice. Three separate tests were presented once per day in random order over postpartum days 7–9; novel object (golf ball), pup retrieval, and inaccessible pup. All pups from lactating mice were removed for 1 h prior to each session and all pups used in the experiments were obtained from donor dams. Across all tests, the peak ΔF/F during pick-up handling was not different between groups (P > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 1B, F, H) and all fluorescence changes were normalised to the peak signal.

Exposure to a novel object induced activation of CRH neurons that differed between groups (One-way ANOVA, P = 0.002). Specifically, virgin and pups-removed groups showed significantly more CRH activation compared to lactating mice (virgin 2.3 ± 0.50 vs lactating −0.40 ± 0.47, P < 0.002; lactating vs pups removed 1.4 ± 0.42, P = 0.042; Fig. 5B, C). We then plotted the changes in CRH neuron activity in 10 min bins (Fig. 5D). A two-way ANOVA showed a significant effect of group (F(2,16) = 6.76, P = 0.0074), a significant effect of time (F(2.37,37.9) = 4.69, P = 0.011), but no significant interaction (F(6,48) = 0.82, P = 0.56). Only the virgin mice displayed a significant increase in activity, which was observed during the initial 10 min of the stimulus (P = 0.036, Fig. 5D). We next time-locked the photometry data to the onset of first contact with the novel object (Fig. 5E) and plotted the data 30 s before and after novel object contact. A two-way ANOVA revealed no significant effect of group (F(2,16) = 0.198, P = 0.82), a significant effect of time (F(1,16) = 25.25, P = 0.0001) and no significant interaction (F(2,16) = 0.93, P = 0.41). Contact with the novel object increased mean CRH neuron activity only in virgin and pups removed groups (30 s before vs after contact, mean activity difference, virgin 0.14 ± 0.035 ΔF/Fn, P = 0.028; pups removed 0.17 ± 0.049 ΔF/Fn, P = 0.006; Fig. 5F). There was no difference in the frequency (Fig. 5G) and amplitude (Fig. 5H, Two-way ANVOA’s, all comparisons >0.05) of transients 30 s before vs after contact. Modulation of CRH neuron activity in response to pup interactions

We next examined the relationship between maternal behaviours and CRH neuron activity. In response to a pup placed in the cage, a sustained inhibition of CRH neuron activity was observed (Fig. 6A, B). Integrated ΔF/Fn over 30 min (Fig. 6C) revealed a significant difference between groups (One-way ANOVA, P = 0.035). The suppression of activity was greatest in lactating mice (virgin −0.30 ± 0.8 vs lactating −2.25 ± 0.496, P = 0.048; virgin vs pups removed −0.53 ± 0.266, P = 0.96). Mean changes in CRH neuron activity in 10 min bins supported this observation. There was no significant effect of group (F(2,16) = 1.30, p = 0.29), a significant effect of time (F(2.57,41.1) = 9.96, P < 0.0001) and a significant interaction (F(6,48) = 2.81, P = 0.02). Lactating mice displayed significantly reduced CRH neuron activity over the first 20 min from the onset of the pup stimulus (basal vs 0-10 min mean ΔF/Fn difference −0.1 ± 0.032, P = 0.041; basal vs 10–20 min, −0.13 ± 0.039, P = 0.036; Fig. 6D). Virgin mice also displayed this inhibition, however with a delayed onset (basal vs 20–30 min mean difference −0.078 ± 0.015 ΔF/Fn, P = 0.008; Fig. 6D). To determine which behaviours correlated with the inhibition observed, time-locked changes in GCaMP transient frequency, amplitude, and mean ΔF/Fn, 30 s before and after the following behaviours were analysed: 1) First time of contact with the pup, 2) first time of pup retrieval and return to nest, and 3) first time of crouching/nursing behaviour over the pup. All mice across the three groups displayed the three behaviours (times of behaviour onset in Supplementary Fig. 1C–E). See Supplementary Table 1 for full reporting of all two-way ANOVA results for these experiments. Upon first contact with the pup (Fig. 6E), no significant changes in mean fluorescence (Fig. 6F), fluorescent transient frequency (Fig. 6G), and amplitude (Fig. 6H) were observed across all three groups when 30 s bins were compared (all two-way ANOVA comparisons, P > 0.05). However, if we compared 10 s bins, a two-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of time (F(1,16) = 7.27, P = 0.0001) with post hoc tests showing significant difference pre vs post contact for lactating (P = 0.019) and pups removed groups (P = 0.003). Upon retrieval of the pup back to the nest, a time-locked inhibition of CRH neuron activity was observed exclusively in lactating mice (30 s before vs after pup in nest, mean activity difference, 0.28 ± 0.06 ΔF/Fn, P = 0.0014; Fig. 6I, J). While there was no difference in the frequency (P > 0.05, Fig. 6K), a reduction in fluorescent transient amplitude was observed in lactating mice (lactation before vs after pup in nest, mean amplitude difference, 0.2 ± 0.04 ΔF/Fn, P = 0.0006; Fig. 6L). In response to crouching over the pups, a selective inhibition was again observed in lactating mice (before vs after pup in nest, mean activity difference, −0.14 ± 0.03 ΔF/Fn; Fig. 6M, N). In addition, significant reductions in frequency (lactation before vs after crouch, mean frequency difference, 0.71 ± 0.24 ΔF/Fn, P = 0.027; Fig. 6O) and amplitude of fluorescent transients were observed (lactation before vs after crouch, mean amplitude difference, 0.53 ± 0.01 ΔF/Fn, P = 0.034; Fig. 6P). Latencies to first contact and crouching over the pups were not different across groups (P > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 1C, E). However, lactating mice retrieved pups back to the nest significantly faster than virgin mice (P = 0.0213, Supplementary Fig. 1D).

We next tested how CRH neuron activity changes in response to pups that were placed in the cage with the female, but remained physically inaccessible (pup in a tea-ball). Remarkably, this stimulus had the opposite effect in that it resulted in increased CRH neuron activity (Fig. 7A, B). Amplitude of the fluorescent transients remained unchanged (lactation before vs after contact, mean amplitude difference, 0.22 ± 0.06 ΔF/Fn, P = 0.172; Fig. 7H). To assess whether pup distress calls may be contributing to the CRH neuron activation, USV vocalisations were correlated with changes in CRH neuron activity. We time locked photometry responses to the first instance of a burst of USV calls, at least 30 s in duration with a minimum rate of 1 call per second (Supplementary Fig. 2A). For mean ΔF/Fn, a two-way ANOVA revealed a non-significant effect of group (F(2,16) = 0.039, P = 0.96), a significant effect of time (F(1,16) = 10.24, P = 0.006) and a non-significant interaction (F(2,16) = 3.23, P = 0.066). Only the lactating group showed a significant increase in mean activity (lactation before vs after contact, mean activity, 0.09 ± 0.03 ΔF/Fn; P = 0.002, Supplementary Fig. 2B). There was no difference in GCaMP transient frequency (lactation before vs after contact, mean frequency difference, 0.24 ± 0.13 ΔF/Fn; Supplementary Fig. 2C), however transient amplitudes were increased in lactating mice (lactation before vs after contact, mean amplitude difference, 0.14 ± 0.06 ΔF/Fn; P = 0.0079, Supplementary Fig. 2D). Finally, we observed a significant correlation between USV call rate and CRH neuron activity exclusively in lactating mice (r = 0.36, P < 0.0001) but not in virgins and pups removed groups (Supplementary Fig. 2E–G). Together, these data reveal that CRH neuron activity in response to pup cues undergoes marked plasticity during lactation.

Discussion

The HPA axis undergoes numerous adaptations in response to motherhood1–3. Here, for the first time, we report how the in vivo activity dynamics of PVN CRH neurons change during this important period. Contrary to expectations, PVN CRH neuron activity was not different between virgin and lactating mice in the basal, non-stress state. In addition, CRH neuron excitation in response to external stressors (white noise or novel environment) was not also suppressed during lactation compared to virgin mice. We did, however, observe a faster shut-off of activity following the termination of stress in lactating mice as well as a reduced CRH neuron response to a novel object. The most striking differences in CRH neural activity were revealed in behavioural tests involving pups. In a pup retrieval task, CRH neuron activity was suppressed to a greater extent in lactating mice. In the presence of an inaccessible pup, lactating mice showed the largest increase in CRH neural activity. Overall, these data reveal that CRH neural activity is not globally reduced during lactation but is instead modified in a context-dependent manner.

Past studies have demonstrated that basal levels of corticosterone are elevated and that stress-evoked corticosterone is suppressed during lactation in rodents6,8,11–13. The lack of a coincident change in either basal or stress-evoked CRH neuron activity observed in this current study points towards a dissociation between central and peripheral components of the HPA axis. Dissociation between CRH neuron activity and HPA axis output has been reported previously. We have previously shown that while fast corticosterone negative feedback robustly inhibits pituitary ACTH secretion, it has little impact on stress-evoked CRH neuron activity17. Dissociation also appears to occur over the circadian day-night cycle. Specifically, PVN cFos and corticosterone peak at very different times of day35. Likewise, photometry recordings of CRH neuron activity reveal elevations of activity that are not well aligned with the circadian corticosterone rhythm36. Dissociation between CRH neural activity and corticosterone secretion may be caused by several mechanisms during lactation. Firstly, Crh mRNA levels are markedly reduced during lactation as shown by this current study and past studies6,7,12,27. In addition, lactation is also associated with reduced pituitary responsiveness to CRH26 and reduced adrenal responsiveness to ACTH37. Together, this would result in suppressed ACTH and corticosterone release in response to stress even if CRH neuron excitation remained unchanged.

The PVN CRH neuron population is heterogeneous both in respect to its gene expression profile, activity patterns and projections22,24,38–40. In addition to axonal projections to the median eminence, PVN CRH neurons also have projections to other central brain regions18,19,22,41,42. These projections are important for regulating stress-associated behaviours19,21–24 and are thought to use both glutamate and CRH as neurotransmitters18,22,43. It is currently unclear if the cell bodies of CRH neurons that project to different targets occupy different locations within the PVN. While in vivo imaging techniques such as miniscopes would be able to distinguish between individual neurons with different responses, this is not possible with fibre photometry. As such, future studies which utilised both miniscope imaging and retrograde labelling could address the question of whether PVN CRH neurons that project to different targets respond differently to threat and social stimuli.

Dissociation between behavioural stress responses and corticosterone release have been previously reported. In the forced swim test, lactating rats have suppressed stress hormone release compared to virgins, however, exhibit the same stress-associated behaviour as virgin rats44. Additionally, behavioural response to white noise stress is unaltered in lactating rats12. In contrast, post-stress grooming behaviours, which are driven by CRH neuron activity22 are suppressed in lactating rats12. This is consistent with our photometry data where CRH neuron responses during white noise stress were unaltered during lactation, but post-stress activity was suppressed.

We speculate that the ability to dissociate hormonal from behavioural stress responses would be advantageous for animals. In the case of corticosteroid negative feedback, it is important to prevent excessive amounts of stress-evoked corticosterone release, hence negative feedback efficiently suppresses the hormonal stress response45–48. However, animals must maintain appropriate behavioural stress-responses even when corticosterone levels are high. One way to achieve this would be to leave stress-evoked CRH neuron activity unaffected by corticosterone negative feedback17. A similar scenario appears to occur during lactation. Despite changes in hormonal output, stress-evoked activity of CRH neurons appears largely unaffected in response to generic stressors such as white noise or novel environment. We speculate that this would allow for the hormonal stress response to be suppressed while maintaining the ability of CRH neurons to signal to central brain regions to control stress-associated behaviours. The reduction in Crh mRNA levels is consistent with the theory that reduced central CRH signalling is adaptive during lactation29–31. Continued regulation of stress-associated behaviours via central CRH projections may therefore be mediated via glutamate release22,43. This theory needs to be tested in future work.

This current study is the first to reveal plasticity of hypothalamic CRH responses to pup interactions during lactation. Despite CRH neuron responses to white noise or novel environment stress not being changed during lactation, CRH neuron responses to pup associated cues were markedly changed. Specifically, we found a significantly larger inhibition of CRH neurons in response to pup retrieval in lactating mice compared to virgin mice, and a significantly larger excitation of CRH neurons in response to an inaccessible pup. Past work has shown that CRH neuron activity is suppressed by rewarding stimuli24,38,49,50. Lactating dams find pups rewarding51,52 and are motivated to seek pups in both place preference, bar press, and barrier climbing tests52–54. In virgin mice, pups also appear to have positive valence55,56. Consistent with this, we observed a small reduction in CRH neuron activity in response to pup presentation to virgin females23. However, the suppression of activity was significantly larger in lactating mice consistent with pups being more rewarding during the lactational state. One limitation of our study is that we have only investigated changes in CRH neuron activity during a short time window of lactation. Our tests of basal and stress-evoked activity were conducted on postpartum days 4–6 while our test of novel object and pup interaction were conducted on postpartum days 7–9. In future studies, it would be important to determine the time course of changes in CRH neuron activity over both pregnancy and lactation. We also noted that the latency to contact and retrieve pups was longer in our study than in some previous work54. This difference is likely explained by the fact that fibre photometry requires the tethering of mice with an optic fibre, which may subtly interfere with some behaviors.

Despite the overall suppression of stress responses during lactation, it has been reported that such effects can be modulated in a context-specific way. If pups are present, and the applied stressor could adversely affect the pups, mothers will continue to show a stress response57,58. The present data may be similar to this. The inaccessible pup is likely to be perceived to be in danger. As the mother is unable to retrieve it, the stress response is amplified. In addition, there may also be a change in threat salience during lactation with a shift towards responding more robustly to pups calls. This is consistent with past work showing that auditory cortex responses to pup calls are enhanced in dams compared to virgin females59–61.

Together these data reveal unique adaptations and unappreciated plasticity of hypothalamic CRH neurons during motherhood. These changes in stress circuit activity, in conjunction with adaptations in other neural circuits, would be predicted to promote care and protection of pups and hence enhance survival of offspring.

Methods

Animals

All experiments were carried out in female Crh-IRES-cre mice62 (stock # 012704, on a C57BL/6 background) obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. All mice were housed under 12:12 h lighting schedule with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were generated from homozygous x homozygous crosses. For stereotaxic surgery, adult (10–12-week-old) female Crh-IRES-Cre mice were anesthetized with 2% isofluorane, placed in a stereotaxic frame and were then unilaterally injected with AAV9.Syn.Flex.GCaMP6s.WPRZ.Sv40 (1.3 × 1013 genomic copies/ml; University of Pennsylvania Vector Core, see Supplementary data file for sequence information), into the PVN via a Hamilton syringe (−0.8 mm AP, −0.25 mm ML, −4.5 mm DV) at a volume of 1 μL over 10 min. In the same surgery, a mono fibre optic cannula, (400 μm core, 0.48 NA; Doric Lenses) was implanted at the same coordinates and secured using adhesive dental cement. The tip of the fibre optic was targeted to the middle of the PVN (along the AP axis) to collect fluorescence from PVN CRH neurons throughout the PVN. All mice were given carprofen (5 mg/kg, subcutaneous) and lidocaine (2%, subcutaneous) during surgery and allowed to recover for at least 2 weeks. Following recovery, mice were paired with male wild-type C57BL/6J mice, until pregnancy was confirmed, upon which the male mouse was removed. C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the University of Otago Biomedical Research Facility. The minimum number of pups within a litter was limited to four pups to maintain normalized maternal hormone milieu induced by suckling63,64. The maximum number of pups was limited to eight pups. Any additional pups were cross-fostered to dams with fewer than 4 pups. Overall, average litter size was 7 ± 0.4 pups per dam. All experiments were performed between hours of ZT2-ZT10. In this project, we have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All experiments were approved by the University of Otago Animal Ethics Committee in accordance with the New Zealand Animal Welfare Act.

Fibre photometry

Optical recordings of GCaMP6s fluorescence were acquired using a custom acquisition system17,65, with optical components purchased from Doric Lenses. Excitation LEDs (465 nm blue and 405 nm violet) were sinusoidally modulated at 211 and 531 Hz, respectively. Excitation wavelengths were relayed through a filtered fluorescence minicube (spectral bandwidth: 460–490 and 405 nm) to a 400 μm 0.48 NA fiberoptic cable connected to the mouse. A single emission (filtered at 500–550 nm) was detected using a femtowatt photoreceiver (2151, Newport) with a lensed fibre cable adapter. All signals were acquired at 10 kHz, digitized with a demodulation bandwidth of ±5 Hz, and down-sampled to a rate of 10 Hz. All mice were habituated to the test chamber and fibreoptic tethering for at least 7 days and 3 days prior to recordings, respectively. All environmental stressors were exposed after postpartum day 4 to avoid disruptions to early postpartum maternal behaviours.

Basal activity and peak ΔF/F: Basal recordings of unstressed CRH neuron activity was obtained from lactating dams (postpartum day 4, with pups present) or random cycling virgin female mice over at least 4 h. All recordings were obtained from the home cage, placed within a white acrylic box (400 × 400 × 400 mm). At the end of the session, all mice were picked up to obtain a peak fluorescence signal, peak ΔF/F. Mice with peak response signals below 10% ΔF/F were excluded from the study. Following recordings on postpartum day 4, half of the lactating mice had their pups removed.

White noise and novel environment stressors: Over the next consecutive days, mice were exposed to a 5 min white noise stressor (85 dB; postpartum day 5) and a 10-min novel environment stressor (open field box, postpartum days 6). The lactating mouse group had access to the pups over the course of the two stress tests, except during the 10-min novel environment stressor, allowing for pup context separation between the two stress tests. In both sessions, an unstressed basal activity of at least 30-min was obtained prior to the onset of the stressor. Basal activity was averaged and subtracted from the stress baseline, response, and post-stress CRH neuron activity following ΔF/F calculation.

Pup interaction tests: A separate cohort of animals went through testing on postpartum days 4–6 (basal activity, white noise and novel environment), then on postpartum days 7–9, these mice were presented with either a pup from a donor dam, a pup enclosed in a wire-mesh tea-ball (inaccessible pup), or a novel object (a golf ball). A golf ball was used as it had similar dimensions to the tea-ball, but did not have any associated pup cues. The three stimuli were presented once per day, in randomised order. Lactating mice were separated from their pups 1-h prior to and for the duration of the three sessions. A baseline of at least 30-min was obtained prior to the presentation of the stimulus. This baseline was averaged and subtracted from the entire trace following ΔF/F calculation. Each stimulus was presented for 30-min and a top-view camera was used to capture behaviour. Ultrasonic vocalisations (USV’s) from the pups were recorded (Avisoft Bioacoustics) with time stamps that were synchronised with fibre photometry acquisition. At the end of each session, a peak ΔF/F was obtained by picking up the mouse undergoing photometry recording.

Blood sampling and ELISA

Blood samples were collected from the tail tip. A small 1 mm section of the tail tip was cut and blood was collected using heparinized capillary tubes 2 h before and 20 min following 5 min of white noise stress. All mice were habituated to tail handling for at least five days prior to sampling. Samples from lactating mice were collected on postpartum day 7. Plasma corticosterone concentrations were quantified using an ELISA (Arbor Assays) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Following the second tail tip blood sample, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) and then transcardially perfused with RNase-free 4% PFA for subsequent RNAscope labelling of brain tissue.

RNAscope

RNAscope in situ hybridization assay was carried out with RNAscope® 2.5 HD Duplex kit (ACDBio, #322430) and Crh probe (ACDBio, #318931) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with the following modifications. The probe was diluted (1:40) with diluent (ACDBio, #300041) and Haematoxylin Gill No.1 (Sigma Aldrich, GHS132) was diluted (1:5) with distilled water. Slides were mounted with xylene based permanent VectaMount (Vector Laboratories, H-5000) and air dried overnight before imaging. The assay included a positive probe control (ACDBio, #321651). To quantify the mRNA labels, images were segmented using Ilastik66,67 which was iteratively trained to recognize the positive mRNA labels (green), nuclei (blue) and the background. The trained algorithm was then applied across all images considered for analysis. Quantification of mRNA labels was performed using QuPath 0.4.368, by creating an automated workflow and setting intensity thresholds for mRNA labels, nuclear staining and the background. The cell area occupied by the mRNA label (intensity threshold: 50) is reported as a percentage of the total PVN area.

Data analysis

Photometry data was processed using a custom made Python pipeline17,69, similar to those previously described70. Briefly, the isosbestic 405 nm emission was scaled and fitted to the 465 nm signal using least square linear regression. The fitted 405 nm signal was then subtracted from the 465 nm signal, and subsequently divided by the 405 nm signal to generate a ΔF/F %. For each experimental recording, the photometry data were normalised to the peak ΔF/F within subjects. This presents changes in fluorescence as relative changes to each animal’s maximum fluorescence. Overall, the mean peak ΔF/F across all trials were 53.8% ±0.37 ΔF/F. Variability in peak ΔF/F within subjects across all trials were 7.38% ΔF/F (standard error of the mean). All peak ΔF/F values are provided in Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 2A, F, H. All baselines were group averaged and normalized to zero. For detection of spontaneous GCaMP6s transients, a custom python script was developed using scipy find_peaks function. Three thresholds were applied, including a raw ΔF/F threshold amplitude of 3% ΔF/F, 5% normalized ΔF/F threshold (for animal specific cut-offs), and temporal threshold of 0.5 s from trough to peak. This script was validated using previous manually analysed datasets17.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are presented as mean ± SEM and all statistical analyses were performed using Prism. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001. Full reporting of all statistical results can be found in Supplementary Table 1. N values are defined by animal number and are reported in each Figure legend.
