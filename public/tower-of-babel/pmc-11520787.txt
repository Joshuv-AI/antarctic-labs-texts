Prevalence of burnout syndrome among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and intensive care unit nurses in Palestinian hospitals: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fatima Hasan, Tasnim Daraghmeh, Mohammad Jaber, Ramzi Shawahna
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBurnout syndrome is marked by three primary domains: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and low personal accomplishment. This study assessed the prevalence of burnout syndrome among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and intensive care unit (ICU) nurses in Palestinian hospitals. In addition, this study also sought to assess associations and identify the factors that can predict higher burnout among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses.MethodsThis study was conducted in a cross-sectional design using a questionnaire between October 2023 and December 2023. The questionnaire collected the demographic variables of the healthcare providers, their living conditions, lifestyle, working conditions, job satisfaction, and intention to change the profession. The healthcare providers were included from 12 different hospitals. Burnout syndrome was assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey for Medical Personnel (MBI-HSS-MP).ResultsA total of 250 healthcare providers were invited to participate in this study, of those, 212 completed the questionnaire, giving a response rate of 84.8%. Of the healthcare providers, 72 (34.0%) were anesthesiologists, 41 (19.3%) were anesthesia technicians, and 99 (46.7%) were ICU nurses. Of all healthcare providers, 151 (71.2%) reported emotional exhaustion, 125 (59%) reported depersonalization and 145 (68.4%) reported a lack of personal achievement based on the MBI-HSS-MP criteria for burnout. Multiple linear regression showed that higher emotional exhaustion scores could be predicted by the number of working hours per day (p-value = 0.039) and higher depersonalization scores could be predicted by having children (p-value = 0.001). On the other hand, lower personal achievement scores could be predicted by having children (p-value = 0.006), not owning a car (p-value = 0.036), and having more night shifts (p-value = 0.049).ConclusionsThe findings of this study indicated that burnout syndrome was prevalent among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses in Palestinian hospitals. Healthcare authorities and professional bodies should consider designing interventions to reduce burnout and improve the psychological well-being of anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. Further studies are required to identify the most effective interventions for reducing burnout among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses in Palestine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06196-y.

Background

Burnout syndrome refers to a spectrum of positive or negative responses to internal or external factors that affect the physical and/or emotional well-being of the affected individuals [1]. This condition is marked by three primary domains: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and low personal accomplishment. These elements together can lead to challenges in managing the psychological aspects of personal relationships with patients and often result in negative attitudes and unfavorable outcomes [2].

Burnout is a significant health concern across various medical specialties [3–6]. A previous systematic review with meta-analysis showed that the overall prevalence of burnout among all healthcare providers was 35.1% [6]. The prevalence rate of burnout among anesthesiologists was notably higher compared to the prevalence rates among other healthcare providers [3, 6]. Anesthesiologists are more prone to burnout for several reasons, including working conditions, social isolation, long work hours, lack of clinical autonomy, and certain personality traits that make a career in anesthesia more appealing [3]. Similarly, working in an intensive care unit (ICU) can be stressful. This stress can increase the likelihood of developing burnout syndrome among the healthcare providers working in the ICU. A previous study showed that 31% of healthcare providers working in Portuguese ICUs had high levels of burnout [5].

A previous study reported a relatively high prevalence of burnout among Colombian anesthesiologists [2]. The study also showed that burnout was associated with various factors, including depression, anxiety, low job satisfaction, and long hours of work [2]. Similarly, a recent study reported a high prevalence of burnout among Palestinian trainees and residents in surgical and nonsurgical specialties [4]. The study showed that burnout was higher among those who were married, unemployed, faced financial challenges, consumed coffee or tea frequently, expressed dissatisfaction with their job or training, considered changing their career path, and worked long hours [4].

Anesthesiology and ICU nursing are highly demanding specialties that can cause a considerable amount of stress [7]. In these specialties, healthcare providers often work long shift hours and provide care for a large number of vulnerable patients. It has been argued that the negative consequences of burnout can affect the healthcare provider and the quality of healthcare services provided to the patients [8, 9]. Therefore, high burnout can lead to medical errors, and doctor-patient communication issues, and deteriorate the psychological well-being of healthcare providers [10].

There is a lack of information on the prevalence of burnout syndrome among Palestinian anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. Moreover, little is known about the predictors of higher burnout syndrome among Palestinian anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. Therefore, we conducted this study to assess the prevalence of burnout among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses in Palestinian hospitals. Another aim of the study was to identify the predictors of higher burnout in this population of healthcare providers.

Methods

Study design and settings

A descriptive cross-sectional study design was used in this study. This study design was selected because it was the best design to assess the prevalence of burnout syndrome among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses working in Palestinian hospitals. This study was conducted in the period between October 2023 and December 2023. The study was conducted in adherence to the guidelines of reporting standards for cross-sectional studies employing a questionnaire as the research tool [11–14]. The checklist ensured comprehensive reporting of the study design, objectives, settings, recruitment process, sampling methods, participant characteristics, study tool details, reliability and validity assessments, data analysis procedures, interpretation of results, discussion of strengths and limitations, and generalizability [11–13].

The study was conducted in 12 different hospitals in the governorates of Nablus, Jenin, Salfit, Qalqilya, Tulkarm, and Tubas in the West Bank of Palestine.

Study population and sample size

In this particular study, the target population was anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses working in Palestinian hospitals. The sample size needed for this study was calculated using an online sample size calculator (www.raosoft.com). The calculations were based on a 95% confidence interval (CI) and a 5% margin of error. Data from the Ministry of Health reported a population of 470 anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses working in Palestinian hospitals. As a result, 212 anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses were needed for this study. A convenience sampling approach was used in this study.

The anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses were recruited in this study when they fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: (a) having a minimum of two years of experience working in a Palestinian hospital, (b) demonstrating willingness to participate in the study, and (c) providing written informed consent.

Variables

In this study, the dependent variable was burnout syndrome among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. Burnout syndrome was defined as a condition of physical and mental weariness associated with caregiving duties or work. In this study, burnout syndrome was assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey for Medical Personnel (MBI-HSS-MP) [15–17]. The MBI-HSS-MP assessed emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment. Emotional exhaustion was defined as the sense of being emotionally overworked and weary by one’s job. For anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses, emotional exhaustion was assessed using 9 items. Depersonalization was defined as an emotionless response to those who receive one’s assistance, treatment, or guidance. For anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses, depersonalization was assessed using 5 items. Personal accomplishment was defined as one’s feelings of competence and accomplishment in work. For anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses, personal accomplishment was assessed using 8 items. On the other hand, the independent variables were the demographic characteristics of the healthcare providers, their living conditions, lifestyle, working conditions, job satisfaction, and intention to change the profession. The MBI-HSS-MP was previously translated and culturally adapted into Arabic language. In addition, the tool was previously used among healthcare providers in Lebanon [16], Saudi Arabia [17, 18], and other Arab countries [19]. The tool was shown to be valid and reliable [16–19].

The demographic variables included sex, marital status, and having children. The living conditions and lifestyle variables included living alone or with parents, having another paid job, owning a car, duration of sleep, smoking, and consumption of coffee. The working conditions, job satisfaction, and intention to change the profession variables included thinking about changing the profession, job satisfaction, working hours, working long duty hours, ability to support oneself financially, number of experience years, number of task duties, number of night shifts, number of patients under one’s care, and presence of conflict with other professionals or patients’ families.

Study tools, validity, and reliability

The study questionnaire was adapted for anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. The questionnaire consisted of two sections. The first section of the questionnaire collected the demographic characteristics of the healthcare providers, their living conditions, lifestyle, working conditions, job satisfaction, and intention to change the profession. The anesthesiologists and anesthesia technicians were asked to indicate the number of surgeries per day, the average time of each surgery, and the average number of surgeries per month. The ICU nurses were asked to indicate the number of patients under their care, their experiences with patient deaths, existence of conflicts with other professionals or patients’ families. The second section of the questionnaire contained the MBI-HSS-MP [15]. The MBI-HSS-MP was composed of 22 items divided into 3 subscales: emotional exhaustion (9 items), depersonalization (5 items), and personal accomplishment (8 items). Each item was answered using a 7-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (never) to 6 (daily). The questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Table S1.

A pilot study was conducted among 25 healthcare providers who did not take part in the larger study. To assess the reliability of scores over a short time, the test-retest method was employed. The healthcare providers first responded to the questionnaire and were then asked to complete the same questionnaire again two days later. The scores from the two rounds were correlated using Pearson’s correlation. It was decided a priori that a Pearson’s r of ≥ 0.80 would indicate acceptable stability of score over a short duration (test-retest reliability). In this study, Pearson’s r was 0.97 which indicated excellent stability of scores over a short time. The internal consistency of the MBI-HSS-MP was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. It was decided a priori that a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.70 would indicate acceptable internal consistency of the items. In this study, the emotional exhaustion items had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92, the depersonalization items had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91, and the personal accomplishment items had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84. The 22-item MBI-HSS-MP had an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86. These Cronbach’s alpha values indicated excellent internal consistency.

Data collection

The study participants were visited in hospitals by the field researchers who were final-year medical students. During the medical education/training, the researchers were trained to interview patients, interact, and communicate with healthcare providers in hospital settings. For this study, the researchers were trained by the principal investigators on how to conduct interviews with anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. The potential participants were visited by the researchers in their places of work. The researchers explained the study’s objectives to the potential participants and asked them if they would be willing to participate in the study. Before answering the questionnaire, the participants were informed that this study was voluntary and were asked to provide written informed consent. No incentives were offered to participants in exchange for their involvement in this study.

Data analysis

Scores of the healthcare providers in each domain were categorized according to the following criteria: (1) emotional exhaustion: a score of ≤ 26 indicated no emotional exhaustion and a score of > 27 indicated emotional exhaustion, (2) depersonalization: a score of ≤ 9 indicated no depersonalization and a score of > 9 indicated depersonalization, and (3) personal achievement: a score of ≤ 33 indicated no personal achievement and a score of > 33 indicated personal achievement. The collected data were coded, entered, and analyzed using IBM SPSS for Windows v. 21.0. The categorical variables were described as frequencies (n) with percentages (%). Categorical data were compared using Chi-square tests. The continuous data were tested for normality of distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test [20]. As the data were not normally distributed (p-value < 0.05), the data were expressed using medians with their interquartile ranges and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis test [21]. To control the potentially confounding factors and identify the predictors of higher burnout, the variables that were significantly associated with the univariate analyses were included in multiple linear regression models [4, 22–25]. The goodness-of-fit was assessed using a significant R2. A variance inflation factor of ≥ 5 and/or a tolerance value of < 0.2 indicated the presence of multicollinearity problems. The variables that were associated with multicollinearity were removed from the models. A p-value of < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant result.

Results

Characteristics of the healthcare providers

A total of 250 healthcare providers were invited to take part in this study, of those, 212 completed the questionnaire, giving a response rate of 84.8%. Of the healthcare providers, 72 (34.0%) were anesthesiologists, 41 (19.3%) were anesthesia technicians, and 99 (46.7%) were ICU nurses.

Table 1 shows the demographic variables of the healthcare providers, their living conditions and lifestyle, working conditions, job satisfaction, and intention to change the profession. There were no statistically significant differences between the anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses in marital status, having children, living with parents, owning a car, and intention to change their professions. Similarly, there were no statistically significant differences between the anesthesiologists and ICU nurses in the number of professional practice years in the ICU. Moreover, there were no statistically significant differences between the anesthesiologists and anesthesia technicians in the average number of surgeries per month and duration of surgeries. On the other hand, the anesthesiologists were more predominantly male (p-value = 0.007), more likely to sleep 6 h or less (p-value = 0.037), more likely to consume 2 or more cups of coffee per day (p-value = 0.003), more likely to be smokers (p-value = 0.007), more likely to report longer working hours per days (p < 0.001), more likely to report longer single duty hours (p < 0.001), and more likely to report more night shifts per months (p < 0.001). The ICU nurses were more likely to have another paid employment (p-value = 0.006), more likely to be able to support themselves financially (p-value = 0.001), and more likely to be satisfied with their jobs (p-value = 0.039). The anesthesia technicians had longer experience (p-value = 0.007) and were more likely to report more surgeries per day compared to anesthesiologists (p-value = 0.012).

Prevalence of burnout among healthcare providers

Table 3 shows the prevalence of emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and lack of personal achievement among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. Of all healthcare providers, 151 (71.2%) reported emotional exhaustion, 125 (59%) reported depersonalization and 145 (68.4%) reported a lack of personal achievement based on the MBI-HSS-MP criteria for burnout. The prevalence of emotional exhaustion was significantly lower (p = 0.002) among ICU nurses compared to anesthesiologists and anesthesia technicians. On the other hand, there were no statistically significant differences in the prevalence rates of depersonalization and personal achievement between the healthcare providers.

Associations between variables of the healthcare providers with emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and lack of personal achievement

Table 4 shows associations between the variables of the healthcare providers with emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal achievement scores. The univariate analysis showed that there were no statistically significant differences between the scores of the anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses in the emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal achievement domains of the MBI-HSS-MP. Emotional exhaustion scores were significantly higher for the healthcare providers who were male, married, had children, did not live with their parents, did not have another paid employment, were unable to support themselves financially, did not own a car, slept for 6 or less hours, consumed more coffee, were smokers, thought of changing their profession, were dissatisfied with their job, worked longer hours, longer duties, and more night shifts (Table 3).

Similarly, depersonalization scores were significantly higher for the healthcare providers who were married, had children, did not live with their parents, did not have another paid employment, were unable to support themselves financially, did not own a car, slept for 6 or less hours, consumed more coffee, were smokers, thought of changing their profession, were dissatisfied with their job, worked longer hours, longer duties, and more night shifts.

On the other hand, personal achievement scores were significantly lower for the healthcare providers who were married, had children, did not live with their parents, did not have another paid employment, were unable to support themselves financially, did not own a car, slept for 6 or less hours, consumed more coffee, were smokers, were dissatisfied with their job, worked longer hours, longer duties, and more night shifts (Table 3).

Discussion

Burnout is a global health issue with increasing prevalence among healthcare providers in different settings around the world [1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 26–29]. Although a previous study showed a high burnout among trainees and residents in surgical and nonsurgical specialties in Palestine [4], there is a paucity of data on the prevalence of burnout and the factors that predict burnout among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses in Palestinian hospitals. Moreover, burnout was poorly studied among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses [27]. For the first time, the prevalence of burnout as indicated by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and lack of personal achievement was assessed among Palestinian anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. In addition, the predictors of emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and lack of personal achievement were also identified. The results of this study may provide useful information to health administrators, hospital managers, directors of anesthesiology departments, directors of ICU departments, and those in charge of designing appropriate interventions to address this problem and enhance the safety of anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses employed in Palestine.

In this study, burnout syndrome was highly prevalent among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses as indicated by the emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and lack of personal achievement scores. These findings indicated that burnout was highly prevalent among the anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses working in Palestinian hospitals. In addition, these findings were consistent with those reported in previous studies among healthcare providers working in anesthesia and ICU settings [28, 29]. Moreover, high burnout was significantly associated with demographics, living conditions and lifestyle, working conditions, job satisfaction, and intention to change the profession. Factors including lack of support at work, working for more than 40 h per week, and shortage of staff were previously reported to be associated with burnout [26]. Moreover, systematic reviews reported that ICU nurses typically experience high levels of emotional exhaustion and low levels of personal accomplishment [29, 30]. Higher burnout was associated with age, being single, shorter length of practice experience, high workloads, and long working hours [29]. Interestingly, the ICU nurses reported lower emotional exhaustion compared to the anesthesiologists and anesthesia technicians in this study. These findings could be explained by differences in the work settings and the nature of the duties and responsibilities of those healthcare providers. These findings should be taken into consideration when recommending measures to reduce burnout among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses.

In this study, there was no association between gender and burnout. The findings of this study were consistent with what was previously reported on the lack of association between gender and burnout [31]. In this study, married anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses reported significantly higher emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and low personal achievement compared to their single counterparts. Apparently, married healthcare providers had more responsibilities compared to their single counterparts. These findings were not surprising given the responsibilities toward spouses, children, and the household [32]. In this study, the healthcare providers who had another paid employment reported significantly lower emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and high personal achievement compared to their counterparts who did not have another paid employment. Probably, the healthcare providers who had another paid employment felt more financially stable. This argument was backed by the higher burnout scores of the anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses who were unable to financially support themselves. In addition, owning a car was associated with lower burnout scores. This could be explained by the independence and convenience of traveling to and from workplaces. Similarly, higher burnout scores were associated with smoking, frequent consumption of coffee, shorter duration of sleep time, heavier workloads, and working night shifts. These findings were consistent with those reported in previous studies [4, 32, 33]. In this study, long working hours predicted higher emotional exhaustion. This relationship suggested that prolonged exposure to work environments can deplete emotional resources and lead to higher levels of burnout. Healthcare providers and hospital administrators should consider regulating working hours and providing mental health support for anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses who work long shifts. Moreover, having children predicted higher depersonalization scores. Our findings indicated that the demand to balance professional with parental responsibilities could impart a sense of detachment from work responsibilities. This might also indicate that family stressors can increase the challenges of keeping an empathetic and engaging approach to patient care among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. Moreover, having children, not owning a car, and working more night shifts predicted lower personal achievement scores. These results indicate that the cumulative responsibilities toward the family, limited resources, and irregular work hours can also contribute to a sense of reduced personal accomplishment among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. Hospital administrators and decision-makers should consider providing additional support to healthcare providers who have family responsibilities outside their work.

The findings of this study provided insight into emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and lack of achievement among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses in Palestine. These study findings can be a call to improve the mental health and emotional well-being of this important population of healthcare providers. Improving the mental health and emotional well-being of anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses might minimize the negative consequences of high burnout on the affected healthcare providers, and improve the quality of healthcare services provided, and outcomes of the patients.

Strengths and limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted considering the following strengths. First, this was the first study to assess the prevalence of burnout among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses practicing in Palestinian hospitals. Second, the study identified the predictors of higher burnout scores in the emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment domains. Third, the MBI-HSS-MP was used to assess burnout among the study participants. This version of the tool was adapted for medical personnel. This tool was previously tested for validity and reliability. In addition, the test-retest reliability and the internal consistency of the tool were also assessed in this study. The study was shown to be reliable and internally consistent in this particular population of healthcare providers. Fourth, the sample included in this study was diverse in terms of gender, place of work, and geographical distribution across the West Bank of Palestine. Fifth, this study was conducted in a large number of hospitals in Palestine. This should have improved the representativeness and external validity of the findings reported in this study.

However, the study also had certain limitations. First, this study was a cross-sectional survey. Cross-sectional surveys are limited by design and the findings are limited to a specific point in time. A larger longitudinal study might have provided further insight into the development and prevalence of burnout syndrome among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. Second, the short interval between the two rounds in the test-retest method may have introduced cognitive bias. However, it is worth mentioning that previous studies have shown that the MBI-HSS-MP was reliable and internally consistent. Third, the responses collected in this study were self-reported, which may have led some healthcare providers to downplay some of their burnout symptoms. Fourth, the study was conducted during the ongoing war and escalating conflict in the West Bank. Anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses had to work extra shift hours due to the increased influx of patients to hospitals. The increased workload during this time may have potentially contributed to higher levels of burnout. Fifth, the presence of checkpoints and the frequent incursions made it challenging to access numerous hospitals across different cities in the West Bank. Finally, the data collection could have been affected by the tone and body language of the interviewer and unintentionally posing questions in a leading manner.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicated that burnout syndrome was prevalent among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses in Palestinian hospitals. Healthcare authorities and professional bodies should consider designing interventions to reduce burnout and improve the psychological well-being of anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses. Further studies are required to identify the most effective interventions for reducing burnout among anesthesiologists, anesthesia technicians, and ICU nurses in Palestine.
