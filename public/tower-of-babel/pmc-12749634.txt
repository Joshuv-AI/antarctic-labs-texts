Stage-related changes of bone density and volume in treatment of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw
Authors: Quang-Huy Nguyen, Hieu Nguyen, Hai-Van Giap, Jeong Keun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis retrospective study evaluated changes in lesion bone density and volume in medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) at different disease stages following conservative and surgical treatment. Additionally, the study analyzed the correlations between these changes and influencing factors. A total of 54 patients (mean [SD] age, 75.43 [7.44] years) diagnosed with stage 0, 1, 2, or 3 MRONJ, according to the American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons’ guidelines, were included. Pre- and post-treatment CBCT images were superimposed to identify the lesion locations, and lesions were segmented manually using ITK-SNAP software. Lesion mean bone density and volume before and after treatment were calculated. Stage 0–1 lesions showed a 16.8% reduction in bone density and a 17.0% reduction in volume and, while stage 2–3 lesions exhibited a 20.8% increase in bone density and a 46.0% reduction in volume (P < 0.05). In conclusion, stage 2–3 lesions demonstrated better healing outcomes than stage 0–1 lesions. Notably, conservative treatment may potentially benefit from a longer drug holiday, while surgical treatment showed positive effects on both soft and hard tissues in stage 2–3 lesions, regardless of the drug holiday duration.Keywords: Medication-related osteonecrosis of jaws, MRONJ staging, Cone-beam computed tomography, Bone densitySubject terms: Oral diseases, Three-dimensional imaging

Introduction

Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) is a rare and potentially debilitating complication associated with the use of antiresorptive or antiangiogenic agents in patients with osteoporosis or malignant tumors1. Approximately 0.7–18% of all users of antiresorptive agents develop MRONJ during the course of their treatment; the rates of MRONJ development for bisphosphonates and denosumab are similar at approximately 0.9–3.1%2,3. The typical clinical finding of MRONJ is nonhealing necrotic bone, which may be accompanied by prolonged ache in the jaw, loose teeth, gingival swelling, fistula, soft tissue infection, and nonhealing mucosal ulceration1. Although MRONJ was initially reported in 2003 by Marx4, the pathophysiology of the disease remains unclear5.

Conservative treatment with antibiotics and surgical treatment with sequestra debridement and resection are the basic management approaches for MRONJ; however, a general consensus regarding treatment protocols according to the disease stage has not yet been established6. Conservative treatment focuses on pain and infection control7,8, whereas surgical modalities aim to achieve stable mucosal closure to prevent recurrent infection6,7. Both approaches mainly target mucosal healing and often neglect the healing of the underlying bone.

Common cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) findings in MRONJ patients include osteosclerosis, osteolysis, periosteal reaction, and sequestration9,10. Early signs may involve non-healing extraction sockets, lamina dura thickening, and widening of the periodontal ligament space11,12. Stage 0 lesions can also show alveolar bone loss, altered trabecular patterns by dense bone, and localized osteosclerosis involving the alveolar or basal bone13. Similar changes may appear in stages 1 and 2, where lesions are confined to the alveolar bone. In advanced stages, sequestration is often seen, with necrotic bone fragments (involucra) forming within sclerotic trabecular bone, surrounded by a resorptive margin that expands over time14. To assess the healing of the hard tissue in MRONJ, CBCT supports the use of manual, semi-automatic, or automatic segmentation tools on three-dimensional (3D) imaging software, allowing precise quantitative analysis of osseous changes, including radiodensity and volumetric measurements6,7. Vallaeys et al. were the first to use ITK-SNAP to compare the effectiveness of dentomaxillary osteolytic lesion volume analysis using manual and semi-automatic segmentation procedures. Their findings demonstrated that manual segmentation was more reliable and should serve as a reference8.

Previous studies have contributed to understanding the radiographic characteristics of MRONJ; however, limitations remain. Several cross-sectional studies failed to assess lesion progression over time or include lesion volume, only evaluating bone density7,15. Another study investigated bone density changes in and around MRONJ lesions using panoramic radiographs in surgically treated patients; however, the two-dimensional nature of panoramic imaging limited their ability to assess the lesion three-dimensionally16. Zirk et al.6 only focused primarily on the differences in lesion volume between the maxilla and mandible, without assessing changes over time. Zhou et al.17, although included both variables, examined volume changes in osteolytic lesion and bone density changes in osteosclerotic lesions separately. Overall, most existing studies have concentrated on either density or volume changes independently. A comprehensive study is therefore necessary to provide a three-dimensional, quantitative analysis of both lesion volume and bone density changes, comparing surgical and conservative management of MRONJ.

This retrospective study aimed to investigate changes in MRONJ lesion bone density and volume using CBCT at different disease stages, thereby deepening our understanding of MRONJ and providing information for evidence-based clinical decisions. The null hypothesis was that there were no differences in lesion bone density and volume changes between different stages of MRONJ. Additionally, we aimed to assess the relationship between these changes and the demographic features of patients. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess osseous changes with quantitative CBCT analysis using a segmentation method before and after MRONJ treatment.

Results

Demographic features

A total of 54 patients were enrolled, including four men and 50 women, with a mean age of 75.4 ± 7.4 years at the time the CBCT image was taken before treatment (T0). The average duration of antiresorptive agent exposure was 6.0 ± 5.1 years. The average drug holiday duration, which was counted from the last dose of the antiresorptive agent until the time the second CBCT image was taken at least 4 months after treatment (T1), was 15.04 ± 9.20 months. Osteoporosis was the underlying disease in all patients. Approximately 93.5% of patients who underwent s urgical treatment achieved complete healing compared with 52% of those who underwent conservative treatment. During observation, 10 patients (18.5%) achieved partial healing, two (3.7%) exhibited stable disease with no improvements, and one (1.9%) experienced disease progression. (Table 1).

Relationships between changes in lesion volume and density and demographic features of patients

The relationships between %∆D and %∆V and the demographic features of the patient are shown in Table 4. Overall, changes in lesion bone density were not significantly associated with any demographic features of patients (P > 0.05). However, %∆V showed significant correlations with the drug holiday duration (r = − 0.346, P = 0.001), interval between T0 and T1 (r = − 0.302, P = 0.03), and treatment outcome (r = − 0.358, P = 0.008). In the conservative treatment group, a negative correlation between %∆V and drug holiday duration was observed (r = − 0.517, P = 0.01). In the surgical treatment group, %∆V was negatively correlated with the interval between T0 and T1 (r = − 0.395, P = 0.03) and treatment outcome (r = − 0.638, P < 0.001). A significant association between the treatment outcome and treatment method was observed, with patients who underwent surgical treatment being more likely to achieve complete healing than those who underwent conservative treatment (P < 0.001). Additionally, treatment outcome was significantly associated with the MRONJ stage, with complete healing observed more frequently in stage 2–3 lesions than in stage 0–1 lesions (P < 0.001) (Table 5).

Discussion

It is widely acknowledged that successful MRONJ treatment should be adjusted to each patient and aligned with the disease stage13,18,19. Treatment success is typically evaluated based on pain reduction, infection control, and the epithelialization of soft tissue to limit exposed bone1. However, the healing patterns of hard tissue across different disease stages remain poorly understood. Our study takes a pioneering attempt to address this gap, offering valuable insights into the underlying tissue healing process. By analyzing changes in lesion bone density and volume, we explored their correlation with disease stages and demographic factors. Our findings demonstrate that MRONJ lesions, regardless of whether treated surgically or non-surgically, decreased in size across all stages. However, the incomplete healing of lesions signifies the importance of long-term follow-up, even when the overlying soft tissue has fully recovered. Additionally, our analysis discloses the difference in hard tissue healing, in terms of lesion bone density and volume, between disease stages. The null hypothesis was therefore rejected.

In our study, advanced-stage lesions showed an increase in bone density after treatment, whereas early-stage lesions exhibited a decrease. These changes in bone density may be influenced by biomarkers associated with bone remodeling20. In particular, serum C-terminal telopeptide (serum CTX), a commonly studied biomarker in MRONJ, may contribute to this contrasting healing pattern21,22. Marx et al. proposed that patients with morning fasting serum CTX levels < 100 pg/mL had a high risk of MRONJ development, while a level > 150 pg/mL indicated a minimal risk of MRONJ development21. Conversely, other studies argued that CTX level should not be used as a risk predictor for MRONJ23,24. However, there is evidence suggesting a relationship between CTX levels and the severity of MRONJ. Kwon et al. showed a negative correlation between serum CTX levels and MRONJ stages, using severity scores for each patient according to the number and stage of MRONJ lesions25. This revealed that advanced-stage MRONJ is associated with lower serum CTX levels, reflecting suppressed bone resorption26, which may contribute to an increase in bone density during the healing period of the lesion and could be an important radiographic feature.

Radiographic characteristics of MRONJ lesions have been described in many studies, showing distinct but variable features such as trabecular osteosclerosis, osteolysis, periosteal reaction, and sequestration9,10. These findings differ across disease stages and are not self-existent. Given these variable radiographic patterns, imaging must always be interpreted alongside clinical examination and medical history to avoid confusion with other conditions including alveolar osteitis, fibro-osseous lesions, chronic sclerosing osteitis, condensing osteitis, or idiopathic osteosclerosis13.

The radiographic appearance of sclerosis in MRONJ remains controversial. A previous study reported that a common manifestation of MRONJ lesions was a combination of osteolysis and surrounding sclerosis27, which was also observed in the present study. Other authors proposed that osteolysis and sequestration may develop from preexisting osteosclerosis28,29. Therefore, a discrete sclerotic area with increased bone density could be a negative sign and may indicate potential progression toward osteolysis. On the contrary, the sclerosis around the necrotic bone can reflect an early reparative response30. Obermeier et al.16 evaluated the change in sclerotic area around necrotic MRONJ lesions and found no corellation between pre- and postoperative bone density with healing behavior, indicating no corellation between higher bone density and non-healed bone condition, suggesting that sclerotic bone should not be removed.

Drug holiday is another crucial factor in MRONJ management and can significantly influence the treatment outcome. A negative correlation between drug holiday duration and the percentage change of lesion volume (%∆V) was observed in the conservative group (Table 4), possibly due to the changes in CTX levels before and after drug holidays, suggesting a recovery in the bone remodeling process, leading to a reduction in lesion size. However, in the surgical group, no correlation was found between %∆V and drug holiday duration (Table 4), suggesting that drug holidays may play a more significant role in conservative treatment than in surgical treatment.

The rationale for implementing a drug holiday lies in the regenerative potential of bone marrow stem cells and osteoclast precursors, which may recover sufficiently to remodel and renew bone despite the prolonged half-life of bisphosphonates (approximately 11.2 years)31. Zhu et al.32 demonstrated in an osteoporotic rat model that drug suspension restored osteoclastic activity, evidenced by increased osteoclast numbers and elevated serum CTX levels, compared with controls. Similarly, Roberts et al.33 reported significant increases in serum CTX and P1NP levels at 12–18 months after bisphosphonate discontinuation, supporting the potential for osteoclast recovery. Sawada et al.34 further demonstrated that the restoration of cathepsin K-positive mononuclear cells—responsible for expressing cathepsin K, a key enzyme in osteoclastic bone resorption35—was observed in denosumab-treated patients with a longer drug. Collectively, these findings suggest that extended drug holidays may promote osteoclast reactivation, bone remodeling, and improved tissue healing.

However, several studies have advised against an extended drug holiday. The discontinuation of denosumab is reported to result in a rebound increase in bone resorption, consequently elevating the risk of multilevel vertebral fractures36,37. Additionally, Campisi et al.38 stated that, due to a shorter half-life, a shorter holiday may be more meaningful for patient on denosumab, as it could balance between the positive drug effect and a reduction in the risk of skeletal related events and fragility fractures. Accordingly, any decision to interrupt antiresorptive therapy should be made with careful patient selection and thorough consideration of both the timing and duration of the drug holiday.

In contrast to conservative group, our study found no correlation between drug holiday duration and %∆V among surgical group (Table 4). In addition, complete healing occurred more often in patients who underwent surgical treatment than in those who underwent conservative treatment (Table 5). This could be attributed to the advantage of surgical approach, which facilitates the entire removal of the necrotic tissue and preserving only healthy bone for stable mucosal closure39. Fresh bone bleeding created at the end of the surgical procedure, as well as the introduction of recombinant human bone morphogenic protein-2 (rhBMP-2) into the surgical site, could induce the formation of healthy bone tissue, leading to an increase in bone density and better postoperative healing. Absorbable collagen sponge (ACS) soaked in rhBMP-2 was also used in all patients who underwent surgery in this study. Min et al. theorized that ACS soaked with rhBMP-2, when introduced into MRONJ lesions after surgical removal of defective tissues, could promote bone healing by enhancing the bone remodeling process. Accordingly, they performed surgery on 26 patients diagnosed with MRONJ and noted an increase of 11.4% in bone density in the experimental group compared with only a 3.27% increase in the control group40. These findings suggest that surgical treatment is more reliable for achieving complete mucosal healing and reducing lesion volume without being dependent on the duration of the drug holiday. However, because debridement and resection are invasive procedures, a drug holiday of 3–4 months before treatment should still be followed13.

This study has certain limitations. While the sample size of this study was not relatively small compared with that of previous studies on MRONJ, it had an unequal number of patients in each stage group. There was also a marked gender imbalance, with a predominance of female patients in the present study. This likely reflects the higher prevalence of osteoporosis and related conditions among women, particularly postmenopausal individuals. Additionally, owing to the retrospective nature of the study, variables such as antiresorptive exposure, drug holidays, and duration between T0 and T1 could not be controlled for all patients. The short follow-up period is another limitation, as lesions with delayed healing may be overlooked. Lastly, although metal artifacts can affect the quality of CBCT images and interfere with the diagnosis and assessment processes, recently introduced CBCT systems have been equipped with scatter subtraction or homogenization preprocessing algorithms, which were designed to reduce metal artifacts41. Despite the above limitations, this study provides scientific evidence and valuable insights into the healing patterns of MRONJ lesions. The significant improvement in bone quality and reduction in lesion volume were observed in MRONJ stage 2–3 compared to stage 0–1. However, complete healing of hard tissue was not achieved in any case, signifying the necessity of long-term follow-up, even when the overlying soft tissue is fully recovered. Thus, prospective studies with a larger, well-distributed sample size examining the long-term healing of MRONJ lesions are required to enhance our understanding of the healing pattern of MRONJ lesions.

Methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study included patients who underwent treatment for MRONJ at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery of the Ajou University Hospital between 2012 and 2024. The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Ajou University Hospital (IRB No. AJOUIRB-DB-2024-606) and the requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of this study.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with MRONJ stages 0, 1, 2, or 3 as defined per the guidelines of the American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons (update 2022), and (2) the availability of CBCT images acquired before and at least 4 months after treatment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) radiotherapy in the head and neck areas, (2) periapical lesions owing to pulpal infection resulting in osteomyelitis, (3) bone tumors of the jaw, and (4) CBCT images with artifacts in the area of interest, such as metal, aliasing, ring, and motion artifacts, which can affect segmentation (Fig. 3)42.

Flow chart of patient enrolment. CBCT, cone-beam computed tomography; MRONJ, medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw.

Patients were divided into two groups according to the treatment modality used: conservative group (23 patients) and surgical group (31 patients). Patients who underwent any surgical intervention, including long-term antibiotic therapy before surgery, were classified into the surgical group; and those who received only medication without surgery were classified into the conservative group. According to disease severity, patients were classified into stage 0–1 group (20 patients) and stage 2–3 group (34 patients) based on their diagnosis at the first visit, regardless of later disease progression (either improvement to a lower stage or worsening to a higer stage). All patients were evaluated in terms of age, sex, types of antiresorptive agents, initiation date, duration of exposure, the most recent dose, underlying diseases, co-morbidity, triggering of lesion development, drug holiday duration, disease stage, CBCT images at T0 and T1 and treatment outcome. A drug holiday was instructed for all patients at their first visit and its duration was calculated from the date of the last dose.

Image acquisition

All CBCT images were captured by a single technician using a CBCT scanner (Carestream Dental CS 9600, Carestream Dental LLC, Atlanta, GA, USA) with the following exposure protocol: tube voltage of 120 kVp, tube current of 5.0 mA, exposure time of 24 s, slice thickness of 0.3 mm, and 0.3 mm voxel size. Image acquisition was performed with the patient in a standing vertical position, the Frankfort horizontal plane parallel to the floor, and the patient’s head stabilized by a forehead and chin rest.

Segmentation process and measurements

The open-source software ITK-SNAP (version 4.2.0, Penn Image Computing and Science Laboratory, Philadelphia, PA, USA) was used to segment images of MRONJ lesions for volume and mean density calculations. The CBCT images at T0 and T1 were imported as digital imaging and communications in medicine files with anonymization and presented as sagittal, coronal, and axial slices (Fig. 4). First, the anterior cranial base was selected for active contour segmentation using a region-growing algorithm to obtain an anatomical reference for superimposition (Fig. 5A). Using the segmented anterior cranial base, CBCT images at T1 were superimposed onto CBCT images at T0 directly using the ITK-SNAP open-source software, as proposed in previous literature (Fig. 5B)43–47. Following superimposition, manual segmentation of MRONJ lesions was performed separately for CBCT images acquired at T0 and T1. First, the lesion margin was carefully determined. Then, the “polygon” mode was used to draw closed structure outlines on top of lesions (Fig. 7). Polygons were edited by moving the vertices in the slice plane (Fig. 7a). In areas where the lesion border was interrupted, the polygon tool was used to draw a straight line connecting the two ends of the border (Fig. 7c). A 1-pixel gap was maintained between the lesion and the adjacent teeth or implants. Once accepted, the polygon was assigned the current label (Fig. 6) and integrated into the 3D segmentation volume. The paintbrush mode was used for quick drawing and touchup editing with masks of different shapes and sizes (Fig. 8a). Manual segmentation was repeated slice-by-slice for all three orthogonal slices (Fig. 8a–c), and the results were visualized using a 3D display of the segmented structures (Fig. 8d). Following the completion of the procedure, the software automatically calculated the mean density (intensity unit) and volume (mm3) of the segmented structure using the “Volumes and Statistics” tool. The segmentation was performed by a single well-trained investigator with 3 years of experience with CBCT.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Herein, 20% of participants were randomly selected for remeasurements after 2 weeks using intraclass corellation coefficient (ICC) statistic to evaluate intra-observer reliability. The ICC was > 0.80, indicating good reliability of the measurements.

The normality of data from each subgroup was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on the normal distribution of the data, a paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed to compare the values between T1 and T0. Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn’s tests were used to compare the mean lesion density and volume changes among the four MRONJ stages. Independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests were used to compare osseous changes between stage 0–1 and stage 2–3. The correlation between osseous changes and demographic features of patients and treatment outcomes based on the healing behavior of soft tissues were identified using Pearson’s and Spearman’s rho correlation analyses. The Chi-square test was conducted to determine whether the treatment outcome was associated with the treatment method or the stage of the disease.

Based on the available sample size and statistical tests used, the effect size was computed to be 0.82 in the power analysis (G*Power 3.1.9.4, Dusseldorf, Germany), using a two-tailed significance level of 0.05 and a power of 80%.
