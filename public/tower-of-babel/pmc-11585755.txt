Possible differences in the performances of vibration-reducing gloves when used with hammers of different sizes
Authors: Guido Alfaro Degan, Andrea Antonucci, Gianluca Coltrinari, Dario Lippiello
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Protection provided by vibration-reducing gloves (VR) when used with impact tools can be considerably different from that measured following the ISO 10819 Standard. This paper investigates the transmissibility, at the palm level, of three different types of vibration-reducing gloves (air bubbles; gel; neoprene) and a working leather glove, while using 8 different models of electro-pneumatic hammers for chiseling rock in a limestone quarry plant. The capability to reduce the triaxial vibration as the average of all the tested hammers results limited: 12 % for both the gloves in gel and neoprene, and 7 % for the glove in air. The working leather glove shows a tri-axial transmissibility (Tc(T)) quite equal to the unit. Anyway, the capability to reduce vibrations is tool-dependent. Among the used hammers, the glove in air showed a Tc(T) variable from 0.71 to 1.02, the glove in gel from 0.76 to 1.01, and the glove in neoprene from 0.74 to 1.05, while for the glove in leather the transmissibility remains quite always close to the unit. Grouping the hammers in two different categories in terms of average weight and average impact energy: small-size (4.4 kg; 4 J) and middle-size (10.6 kg; 18.5 J), the average transmissibility results different: Tc(T) = 0.94 for middle-size hammers, and Tc(T) = 0.86 for small-size hammers (p-value <0.00001). This mean that VR gloves could be around 10 % more efficient when used with lightweight and small impact energy hammers. Anyway, mainly because of the relatively small number of hammers investigated, further studies are needed for confirming this result.

Introduction

Manual tools producing high levels of vibrations can induce several illnesses, generally called hand-arm vibration syndromes, that can involve joints and muscles, other than the circulation system and sensory nerves [1,2]. When manual tools are also heavy, and require effort to operate or assume non-ergonomic postures, the risk of developing pathologies affecting the osteo-muscle-tendon system can increase, generating a dangerous combination of factors that can mine the health and safety of workers [3].

Impact tools such as electro-pneumatic hammers, widely used especially among workers of construction and mining sectors, are known to produce high levels of vibration, and often require big effort to operate [4,5]. Solutions to reduce the risk of hand-arm vibration syndromes and other Musculo-skeletal disorders are hard to find, since these kinds of tools require high impact energy for being able to disaggregate rocks or concrete.

Providing workers with vibration-reducing gloves (VR) can be a convenient solution, as well as being easy to apply. When these gloves are certified as anti-vibration (AV) according to the standard ISO 10819:13 [6], they should reduce the vibration at least 10 % in the middle frequencies (25–200 Hz) and at least 40 % in the high frequencies (25–1250 Hz).

Anyway, the operative conditions prescribed by the Standard ISO for the gloves’ certification are dissimilar from what happens during the real use of the gloves, mainly because the Standard protocol is based on simulated laboratory tests. The protocol of the ISO 10819:2013 use standardized excitation curves in order to reproduce the vibration on the handle of a shaker, and fixes both the push and the grip forces applied, moreover it considers only the vibration in the forearm direction, imposing subjects to assume a predefined hand-arm posture. All these factors can be different from those revealed in the real use of vibrating tools, sometimes resulting in an overestimation of the protection provided by the gloves [7,8].

Characteristics of AV/VR gloves are been widely studied throw the years, trying to define the properties of the different damping materials used for attenuating the vibrations. Results of these studies show that this personal protective equipment are not in general able to reduce vibrations in the low frequency range [9,10]. This can represent a problem when AV/VR gloves are used with electro-pneumatic hammers, because these tools often produce high levels of vibrations ranging in the frequencies between 25 and 50 Hz [11,12].

Employers could think that providing workers with certified AV gloves always has positive effects in reducing vibrations, but as reported in some cases [13,14], the real protection is lower than declared, sometimes resulting similar to that provided by normal working gloves.

Since AV/VR gloves are often thicker and stiffener compared to ordinary working gloves, the force applied by workers may intensify, causing musculoskeletal problems, loss of dexterity, and affecting the ability to safely control the tool [[15], [16], [17]]. This represents another important issue, especially in those cases where the reduction of the transmissibility provided by the gloves is little, and an intensive force is required to manage the tools.

Few studies involving subjects using electro-pneumatic hammers in real field report that AV/VR gloves can have positive effects in protecting workers against vibration [18]. This is what we found in one of our investigations, in which a small (3 kg, 2.6 J) electro-pneumatic hammer was used for chiseling limestone rocks in real field [19]. In this case, results showed a tri-axial protection provided by an air-bubbles glove of around 29 %. Anyway, in another our investigation, in which the same air-bubbles glove was used by the same subjects following the same protocol, but in this case using an 11.8 kg, 20 J hammer, we did not find protection (average triaxial transmissibility of 1.02) [20]. Probably, the different characteristics of the tools, mainly in terms of weight, impact energy, and vibration spectra, can have influenced the capability of the gloves to protect against vibration.

Starting from this evidence, in the present work we aimed to investigate at possible differences, in terms of transmissibility provided by AV/VR gloves, between two different categories of hammers, characterized by having different average weight and impact energy. In the first part of the study, we collected some more measurements of the transmissibility of the gloves on 3 other hammers, all characterized by having small weight and impact energy. This was necessary for compose two different groups of hammers, made by 4 tools each one. For these new measurements, we used the same gloves, subjects and protocol of our previous investigations. Then, we utilized all the collected data to look at possible differences in terms of vibration reduction provided by the gloves among the two different groups of hammers: small size (in the range 3–6.3 kg; 2.4–8.3 J), and medium size (in the range 9.7–11.8 kg; 12.2–23 J).

Fig. 4 reports the protocol used for the measurement campaign for each of the tested hammers. Subject 1 began the first trial wearing glove 1 (e.g. air). The same hammer was then used by subject n. 2 wearing another type of glove (e.g. gel), and so on. When all the combinations of subjects and gloves were completed, so that every subject used the same hammer, for 1 trial of penetration and wearing all the types of gloves, the entire protocol was repeated another 3 times before changing the hammer. At least, four trials for each subject were performed using the same glove and hammer. As indicated in Fig. 4, every 3 trials performed by the different subjects, a resting time of around 10 min was disposed. Every trial lasted around 15 s, starting the measurements after the beginning of the penetration, without moving the chisel during the recording and without breaks. For stabilize the signal, measurements recordings started approximately 5 s after the tool was in function, lasting for another 10 s. Results

Vibration produced by the new tested “small-size” hammers

In Fig. 5 are reported the average weighted triaxial acceleration spectra, measured both on the handle and using the adaptor (bare-hand) for the new tested "small-size" hammers. These two values are generally different, mainly due to the different position of the accelerometers on the handle, to the biodynamic response of the hand, and to the variation of the force applied [21] (Clemm et al., 2021). The acceleration directly measured on the handle is generally higher, especially in the high frequencies range.Fig. 5Wh weighted triaxial vibration on the handle (aR) and bare-hand adaptor (ab) for the new tested hammers.Fig. 5

Wh weighted triaxial vibration on the handle (aR) and bare-hand adaptor (ab) for the new tested hammers.

The hammer producing the highest vibration, is the Bosch GSH 3E Prof (17.11 m/s2 – 2.6 J). As can be seen from data coming from the new measurements and those coming from the other previous investigations, reported in the Appendix, there is not a direct correlation between the vibration measured at the handle and the impact energy or the weight of the hammer. The reason is that the vibration depends not only by the power of the tool, but also on many other factors, such as the masses and the speed of the internal components, the mechanical architecture, the dimension and the shape of the handle, the stiffness of the material used for covering the handle and not list the presence of vibration-reducing systems, generally consisting of a spring connecting the handle to the hammer case. In some of the hammers, such as the Metabo 845 BL, the Bosh GSH 5CE Prof, and the Makita HM 1213 C, the vibration-reducing system is connected only to one side of the handle, producing a higher reduction of the vibration close to the trigger, in the area in which the bare-hand accelerometer is positioned. Generally, the hammers having the higher weight are also those characterized by having the lower main percussion frequency, around 31.5 Hz, while the hammers with lower weight and energy produce a peak of vibration generally over 50 Hz. These differences, as discussed later in the text, can also have a role in the capability of the gloves to reduce the vibration.

Looking at the results, tri-axial transmissibility for the air glove varies from Tc(T) = 0.93 when used with the Bosch GSH 5 CE to Tc(T) = 1.00 when used with the Makita HR 261 OT, without statistical differences (p-value = 0.08914), so an average reduction of 4 % can be considered representative. For the glove in gel, results show the same transmissibility when used with the hammers Makita HR 261 OT and Bosch GSH 3E, providing a protection of 20 %, while when used with the hammer Bosch GSH 5 CE it does not provide protection (Maximum p-value = 0.00164). The glove in neoprene shows a transmissibility varying from Tc(T) = 0.76 when used with the Makita HR 261 OT to 0.89/0.88 when used with the other two hammers (p-value = 0.00424). The working leather glove does not provide protection Tc(T) = 1.05 - 0.99 - 1.03, without statistical differences (Minimum p-value = 0.1336), so in this case an average transmissibility of 1.02 can be considered representative.

Looking at the transmissibility along the singles axes, in compression (z axis), statistical analysis does not reveal differences for the gloves in air and gel among the tested hammers (minimum p-value = 0.09894), so an average protection, respectively of 7 %, and an increment of the vibration of 5 % can be considered representative. This result shows as the glove in gel is in general less capable to reduce the vibration in compression compared to the other gloves. This trend was also observed in our previous investigations [20]. Regarding the glove in neoprene, statistical analysis confirms all the differences reported in Table 4 (maximum p-value = 0.0244), providing a protection of 31 % when used with the hammer Makita HR 261 OT and 10 % when used with the hammer Bosch GSH 3E, while it does not provide protection when used with the hammer Bosch GSH 5 CE. Regarding the transmissibility in shear, the differences reported in Table 4 are confirmed for the gloves in gel and neoprene along the x-axis, and only for the glove in leather between the hammers Makita HR 261 OT and Bosch GSH 3E (p-value = 0.00512).

Looking at the transmissibility in the middle frequencies as considered by the ISO 10819 (Tc(Mz)), the statistical analysis confirms only the differences for the glove in neoprene, that in our case provides a protection of 33 % when used with the hammer Makita HR 261 OT, and 12 % when used with the hammer Bosch GSH 3E, while it does not provide protection when used with the hammer Bosch GSH 5 CE (maximum p-value = 0.00782). For the air glove and the gel glove, an average protection of 5 %, and an increment of the vibration of 3 % can be considered representative for all the hammers (minimum p-value = 0.25848).

Regarding the transmissibility in the high frequencies (Tc(Hz)), for the glove in air an average protection of 22 % can be considered representative when used with the hammers Makita HR 261 OT and Bosch GSH 3E (p-value 0.4009), while with the hammer Bosch GSH 5 CE it does not provide protection. The glove in gel does not provide protection with the hammers Makita HR 261 OT and Bosch GSE 5 CE, while it provides a protection of 9 % when used with the hammer Bosch GSH 3E. The glove in neoprene provides a protection of 25 % when used with the hammer Makita HR 261 OT, while an average protection of 11 % can be considered representative when used with the other two hammers (p-value 0.0601).

In the case of the hammer Bosch GSH 3E, looking at the triaxial transmissibility, the glove in gel offers the best protection (20 %), compared to the others two AV/VR gloves (4 % air, 11 % neoprene). All the AV/VR gloves have a quite flat behavior in the range of frequencies 16–125 Hz. In this range, the gloves in air and neoprene can reduce around 12 % of the vibration, while the glove in gel around 23 %. For the frequencies over 315 Hz, the behavior of all the tested gloves results more similar, showing a marked decrease of the transmissibility. In compression, the glove in gel has less capability to reduce vibrations while in shear, mainly along the x-axes, it results more efficient. In some frequencies, both along the x and the y axis, the AV/VR gloves can also increment the vibration.

For the hammer Makita HR 261 OT, the triaxial transmissibility shows greater protection in the low frequencies for the gloves in gel and air compared to the glove in neoprene. In this case, also the glove in leather shows a reduction of the vibration. The opposite happens in the range of frequencies over 50 Hz, where the glove in leather shows a transmissibility some times higher than the unit, while the gloves in neoprene and gel have similar behavior. The glove in air is in general less able to reduce the vibration. In compression, the glove in gel increments the vibration in the range of frequencies from 125 to 500 Hz, while the gloves in neoprene and air have similar behavior. The glove in neoprene increments the transmissibility in the range of frequencies 20–50 Hz. In shear, the behavior of AV/VR glove is less categorizable, anyway the glove in neoprene seems is in general able to reduce some vibration in all the spectra, while the glove in air increments the vibration in the middle frequency range, both along the x and the y axes. The glove in gel is the more efficient along the x-axis, except at the frequency of 160 Hz.

For the hammer Bosch GSH 5 CE, the triaxial transmissibility shows that the gloves in gel and neoprene have similar behavior for the frequencies until 250 Hz, while the glove in air is less capable to reduce the vibration. Over 250 Hz, the glove in gel results the best in reducing the vibration. In compression the behavior is less categorizable, having the gloves a discontinuous trend in relation to the capability to reduce vibrations. In share, both for the x and y axes, the glove in gel results to reduce more vibration for the frequencies over 315 Hz.

Comparison in terms of glove performances among the two different groups of hammers

For making a comparison in terms of transmissibility of the gloves among hammers of different size and impact energy, we combined the data coming from the present and the previous investigations, into two different groups. The firs group (middle-size hammers) is composed by hammers having a weight in the range 9.7–11.8 kg and an impact energy from 12.2 to 23 J. The second group (small-size hammers) is composed by hammers having a weight in the range 3–6.3 kg and impact energy from 2.4 to 8.3 J.

The tri-axial protection provided by the gloves, measured as the average for all the tested hammers results in general limited. Both the gloves in gel and neoprene provide protection of 12 % (Tc(T) = 0.88), while the glove in air provides protection of 7 % (Tc(T) = 0.93). Looking at the differences of the transmissibility through the axes (Tc(x); Tc(y); Tc(z)) for each single type of glove, the statistical analysis does not show notable differences for the gloves in air and neoprene (p-values = 0.72962; 0.19202), while the glove in gel seems to protect better in shear, especially along the x axis, than in compression: Tc(x) = 0.78; Tc(y) = 0.93; Tc(z) = 0.97. The statistical analysis confirms all the differences (Maximum p-value = 0.0007). Regarding the glove in leather, although it does not provide protection, its behavior in terms of vibration reduction results a little better in compression Tc(T) = 0.98 than in shear Tc(x;y) = 1.04 (p-value = 0.006).

In order to make a comparison with the method of calculation indicated by the ISO 10819 Standard, the transmissibility along the z-axis, both in the middle frequencies Tc(Mz) (25–200 Hz) and in the high frequencies Tc(Hz) (200–1250 Hz), is also reported. In the middle frequencies, the protection provided by the AV/VR gloves is generally limited, varying from 3 % for the glove in gel to 11 % for the glove in neoprene. Statistical analysis confirms the differences (p-value = 0.0048), showing a better behavior of both the gloves in air and neoprene compared to the glove in gel. The latter in this case offers the same protection as the working leather glove (p-value = 0.93624).

As expected, in the high frequencies the protection provided by the AV/VR gloves is higher. For the gloves in air and neoprene the transmissibility is similar (p-value = 0.2983), so an average protection of 25 % can be considered representative. Minor protection is provided by the glove in gel (10 %) (p-value <0.00001). The leather glove doesn't provide protection both in the middle (Tc(Mz) = 0.98) and in the high frequencies range (Tc(Hz) = 1.03).

Even though the gloves in air and neoprene are classified by the manufacturers as antivibration (AV) according to the ISO 10819 Standard, the protection measured in our investigation results lower than that labeled. The declared transmissibility in the middle frequencies range is Tc(Mz) = 0.75 for the air glove and Tc(Mz) = 0.86 for the neoprene glove, while in our tests the transmissibility calculated as the average for all the 8 hammers results higher: Tc(Mz) = 0.91 and Tc(Mz) = 0.89. The declared transmissibility in the high frequencies is Tc(Hz) = 0.45 for the air glove and Tc(Hz) = 0.59 for the neoprene, these too resulting different from those observed in our measurements campaign: Tc(Hz) = 0.74 for the air glove and Tc(Hz) = 0.75 for the neoprene.

Considering the average transmissibility for all the tested AV/VR gloves in the entire frequency range, and grouping them in the two previously mentioned categories (144 trials for each group), the average transmissibility results to be Tc(T) = 0.94 for the middle-size hammers, and Tc(T) = 0.86 for the small-size hammers. Statistical analysis confirms the differences between the two different groups (p-value <0.00001).

In both cases, anyway, the average protection remains in general limited, varying from 7 % to 15 % when calculated on the entire frequency range.

Discussion

This paper investigates the properties of three different kinds of AV/VR gloves and a working leather glove, in terms of vibration reduction at the palm level, when used with 8 different models of electro-pneumatic hammers for chiseling limestone rock. Three models of hammers are investigated in the present work, while other data reporting the transmissibility of the gloves derive from our previous investigations. The purpose of the study is to discuss the properties of AV/VR gloves in terms of transmissibility when used in real field with this kind of tools, looking at possible differences among the different sizes and impact energy of the hammers.

Field investigations have the advantage to be more representative of the real job compared to laboratory simulations, anyway must be considered that reliable measurements are not always easy to obtain [8]. In standardized laboratory tests, shakers are often used to produce vibrations, resulting in a limitation of the variations among different trials; moreover, the accelerometers used for the measurements are positioned very close to each other, allowing also to measure the vibration inside and outside the gloves at the same time. Such situation cannot be reproduced in real field, since it is not possible to measure vibration at the palm level inside and outside the glove at the same time. For this reason, it is customary to use a reference accelerometer, but as already discussed, the vibration revealed in different points of the handle can also be different [22]. In real field investigation, the grip and the push force change frequently according to the way subjects operate. In our case for example, both the forces were not under control, introducing substantial variations among measurement data.

As reported by Dong et al., 2014 [23], this is mainly the reason because laboratory tests, such as those tests using the transfer function method, are often preferred to measure tool-specific performances of gloves.

In our investigations, the overall results calculated as the average transmissibility of all the eight tested hammers, reveal that the capability provided by AV-VR gloves to reduce vibrations is in general limited when gloves are used with these kinds of tools. Even though the properties of the gloves are different in terms of damping materials used (air bladder, gel, and neoprene), combining all the tests made for each type of glove (96 measurements for each glove), the gloves in neoprene and gel show a similar average tri-axial protection of around 12 % (Tc(T) = 0.88), while the glove in air provides protection of 7 % (Tc(T) = 0.93). The working leather glove does not provide protection Tc(T) = 1.02.

Results confirm the hypothesis that, even if in real field investigations the transmissibility of the gloves can be different from that obtained in laboratory tests, the efficacy of these types of gloves remains in general questionable when used with impact tools such as electro-pneumatic hammers.

Anyway, the transmissibility of the gloves can vary considerably among the used hammers. For example, as reported in the introduction, in one of our previous investigations (involving the same three subjects and gloves, on the same kind of rock) made on a light weight hammer (3 kg, 2.6 J), the tri-axial transmissibility of the air bubbles glove resulted Tc(T) = 0.71, being able to reduce the vibration of around 30 %, while the same glove resulted not able to provide protection (Tc(T) = 1.05) when used with a 11.8 Kg, 20 J hammer.

For this reason, in this paper we also divided the hammers into two different groups: small-size hammers (average weight: 4.4 kg – average power: 4 J) and medium-size hammers (average weight: 10.6 kg – average power: 18.5 J), looking for possible differences in terms of transmissibility of AV/VR gloves.

The average tri-axial transmissibility when gloves are used with small-size hammers, as the average of all the three types of AV/VR gloves, results Tc(T) = 0.86, while when they are used with middle-size hammers it is Tc(T) = 0.94. Statistical analysis confirms the differences (p-value <0.00001). This result could suggest that when antivibration gloves are used with small-size electro-pneumatic hammers, they could protect a little more than when used with bigger hammers.

Anyway, must be considered that the frequency response of the gloves is relatively insensitive to the magnitude of the vibration [10], moreover, the amplitude of the vibration produced by the hammers is not always related to their impact energy or weight. As can be seen in Fig. 5 in the paper and in Fig. 1 in the Appendix, even though in our case the hammer producing the higher vibration is also one of the most powerful and heavy, some of the small-size hammers produce an overall vibration higher than those categorized as middle-size hammers.

The differences in the transmissibility of the gloves between the two groups of hammers can also be due to the different percussion frequencies, which are in general lower for the bigger hammers, other than to the force applied by workers, which can vary when varying the power and/or the weight of the hammers. Looking at the transmissibility spectra reported in Fig. 10, the lower transmissibility revealed in the use of small-size hammers is mainly due to the differences in the range of frequencies below 40 Hz. As reported by Dong 2004 [24], increasing the force tends to increase the transmissibility at the frequencies lower than 31.5 Hz, so if the use of bigger hammers requires also higher force, this could explain the increment of the transmissibility at low frequencies. Anyway, we did not measure the force during our investigations, so we have no elements to confirm this hypothesis. Moreover, at these frequencies, the vibration produced by small-size hammers is generally very little, and this can have affected the results.

The gloves in air and neoprene used in our investigations are certified as antivibration according to the ISO 10819:13 Standard, so we also looked at the transmissibility along the z-axis both in the middle (25–200 Hz) and in the high (200–1250 Hz) frequencies. The overall results, calculated as the average of the 8 hammers, show that the protection provided by these gloves results lower than that declared by the manufacturers. In our case, the protection declared by the manufacturers in the middle and high frequencies are respectively 25 % and 55 % for the air glove and 14 % and 41 % for the neoprene glove, while results of our measurements report 9 % and 26 % for the air glove and 11 % and 25 % for the glove in neoprene.

Looking at the frequency analysis as the average of all the used hammers, the tested gloves are not in general able to reduce the triaxial vibration in the frequencies range below 40 Hz, especially when used with middle-size hammers. Moreover, in this case the transmissibility results similar to that provided by the working leather glove, confirming the difficulty to reduce the vibration in very low frequencies. A protection variable from 4 % to 20 % is generally provided in the range between 40 and 400 Hz, while over 400 Hz the protection increases around 50 %. Anyway, these results must be considered as a general trend, since as reported in the text, the behavior of the gloves can vary among the different hammer used.

The properties of the gloves can also vary among the different axes. Even though in this case is more difficult to categorize their behavior, results show that the glove in gel is less capable to reduce the vibration in compression (z-axis) compared to the other two AV/VR gloves, while in shear, it generally provides better protection. This result is consistent with that reported in another study [25].

Although we tried to place both the accelerometers used for the investigations as close as possible to each other, in order to considers for possible variations of the acceleration during the different trials, the reference accelerometer, directly fixed to the handle of the hammers, generally provides values higher than that inside the adaptor, especially in the high frequencies range. These errors in alignments, other than the reduced space for the hand holding the hammers, and the possible contact between the glove and the reference accelerometer, can have introduced some errors in the evaluation of the transmissibility.

Conclusions

Results of our investigations show that the efficacy to reduce the vibration provided by AV/VR gloves when used with electro-pneumatic hammers remains questionable.

We found some differences in terms of transmissibility when AV/VR gloves are used between the two different groups of hammers (small-size and medium-size). These differences can suggest that when antivibration gloves are used with small electro-pneumatic hammers, they could be more efficient in reducing the vibration than when used with heaviest and more powerful hammers. The reason can be attributed to the different percussion frequency, in general higher on small-size hammers. Anyway, as discussed in the paper, the transmissibility of the gloves depends on many other factors, such as the force applied by the subjects, that can be higher for more powerful and heavy tools. Further investigations, increasing the overall number of hammers, other than measuring the force applied by workers, are needed to confirm the hypothesis.

Results of this study confirms also that the transmissibility of the gloves is tool dependent, and cannot be deduced from the values declared by the manufacturers according to the ISO Standard 10819:2013. We found that transmissibility declared by manufacturers results in general smaller to that measured in real field.

Reliable results require real field investigations, or laboratory tests based on the vibration produced by every single hammer.
