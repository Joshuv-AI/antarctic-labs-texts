Breast cancer worry, risk level, and knowledge, attitudes, and screening practices among women attending healthy life centers in Türkiye: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Kezban Yavuz Emik, Mehmet Akif Sezerol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer is the most common cancer among women worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Although early detection through screening significantly reduces mortality, participation in screening programs remains suboptimal in many settings, including Türkiye. This study aimed to assess breast cancer worry (BCW), perceived risk, knowledge, attitudes, and screening behaviors among women attending Healthy Life Centers (HLCs), and to identify factors associated with participation in breast cancer screening.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between May and September 2024 among women aged 18 years and older attending a Healthy Life Center. Of the 802 women approached, 518 agreed to participate (response rate: 64.6%). Data were collected through face-to-face interviews, with responses recorded using a structured questionnaire via Google Forms and included 45 items along with the 6-item Breast Cancer Worry Scale. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, Student’s t-test, and Mann–Whitney U test were used for bivariate analyses. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of mammography participation.ResultsA total of 518 women participated (mean age: 35.3 ± 8.7 years), with 66.9% under the age of 40. While 42.9% reported ever performing breast self-examination, only 12.1% practiced it regularly. Clinical breast examination was reported by 24.7% of participants, and 28.1% had undergone mammography. The overall BCW level was low (mean score: 4.1 ± 5.1), with 88.6% of women classified as having low worry. In multivariate analysis, breast cancer worry was identified as an independent predictor of mammography participation (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02–1.12, p = 0.006), whereas sociodemographic and reproductive factors were not significant after adjustment.ConclusionsDespite relatively high levels of awareness, participation in breast cancer screening remains limited. Emotional factors, particularly breast cancer worry, appear to play a more prominent role in screening behavior than traditional sociodemographic variables. Interventions should integrate both informational and psychosocial components. Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, with breast cancer representing the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women and a major contributor to cancer-related deaths [1]. According to global estimates, approximately 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer and 666,103 associated deaths were reported among women in 2022 [2]. Notably, five-year survival rates exhibit substantial disparities across countries, reaching nearly 90% in high-income settings while remaining considerably lower in low- and middle-income countries, reflecting inequities in access to early detection and treatment services [3].

In Türkiye, breast cancer constitutes the most common cancer among women and remains a significant public health concern due to its associated mortality burden [4]. Longitudinal data suggest a progressive increase in breast cancer-related mortality over time, with lifestyle-related factors such as obesity, physical inactivity, and metabolic disturbances playing a contributory role in this trend [5]. These findings underscore the critical importance of implementing effective early detection strategies and strengthening organized screening programs.

Established risk factors for breast cancer include female sex and advancing age, with the majority of cases occurring in women aged 50 years and older, highlighting aging as a key determinant [2, 6]. In addition to non-modifiable factors such as genetic predisposition and hormonal influences, modifiable risk factors including obesity, alcohol consumption, smoking, and sedentary behavior have been shown to significantly contribute to disease etiology [5, 6]. Nevertheless, a considerable proportion of breast cancer cases arise in the absence of identifiable risk factors, further reinforcing the necessity of population-based screening approaches [6].

Early detection plays a pivotal role in reducing breast cancer mortality. Mammography is widely recognized as the most effective screening modality, given its capacity to detect malignancies in the preclinical phase, and has been associated with an approximate 40% reduction in mortality [7]. In contrast, clinical breast examination (CBE) and breast self-examination (BSE) primarily serve to enhance breast health awareness and promote timely healthcare-seeking behavior upon symptom recognition, rather than facilitating detection in the preclinical stage.

Within the framework of the National Cancer Screening Program in Türkiye, biennial mammographic screening is recommended for women aged 40–69 years, alongside annual clinical breast examinations beginning at age 40 and interventions aimed at increasing breast awareness from the age of 20 [4, 8]. These services are provided free of charge within primary healthcare settings [8]. Screening activities are predominantly conducted through Cancer Early Diagnosis, Screening and Training Centers, with recent integration into Healthy Life Centers (HLCs). HLCs are community-based primary healthcare facilities operating under district health directorates or community health centers, delivering comprehensive services including preventive care, health promotion, lifestyle counseling, and risk assessment through an integrated approach [4, 9].

Despite the availability of these services, the current screening system in Türkiye is largely characterized by opportunistic rather than organized approaches, with insufficient implementation of systematic invitation mechanisms targeting the eligible population [7]. However, participation rates in breast cancer screening in Türkiye remain suboptimal. Data from the Health Statistics Yearbook 2023 indicate that only 14.5% of women underwent mammography within the past two years [10]. Furthermore, a national study reported that 45.9% of women had never participated in breast cancer screening, while only 31.5% adhered to recommended screening intervals [8]. These findings highlight a critical gap between the availability and utilization of screening services. Nevertheless, the limited nationwide implementation of such organized screening models represents a substantial gap in current public health practice.

Previous research has demonstrated that participation in breast cancer screening is influenced by a range of individual-level factors, including age, educational attainment, marital status, and socioeconomic status, as well as structural factors such as access to healthcare services and interaction with the healthcare system [8, 11]. Additionally, engagement with other cancer screening programs and increased frequency of healthcare utilization have been associated with higher screening uptake [8]. Conversely, insufficient knowledge, misconceptions, and sociocultural barriers continue to represent significant impediments to participation [11].

Although the existing literature has identified key determinants of screening participation, there remains a paucity of studies that comprehensively examine the interplay between breast cancer-related anxiety, perceived risk, and screening behaviors, particularly among women attending Healthy Life Centers. Furthermore, there is a need for research that evaluates the real-world effectiveness of current policies while incorporating psychosocial dimensions. This gap highlights the necessity for more integrative and context-specific investigations.

Accordingly, the present study aims to examine breast cancer-related anxiety, perceived risk levels, and knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding breast cancer and screening among women attending a Healthy Life Center, and to identify the factors influencing screening participation. The findings are expected to inform the development of evidence-based policies and interventions aimed at improving participation in breast cancer screening programs. Despite existing screening policies in Türkiye, limited participation and insufficient integration of psychosocial determinants indicate a critical gap between policy implementation and real-world outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between May 6, 2024, and September 5, 2024, at a Healthy Life Center (HLC) affiliated with the Sultanbeyli District Health Directorate. These centers provide free preventive health services, including breast cancer screening. The study population consisted of women aged 18 years and older who attended the center during the study period. Women aged 18 and above were included to reflect the actual population utilizing HLC services and to assess awareness and attitudes prior to the recommended screening age. Subgroup analyses were then structured based on clinically relevant thresholds, particularly the recommended screening age of 40 years.

No formal sample size calculation was performed; therefore, the statistical power of the study may be limited. Instead, all eligible women who attended the center during the study period and agreed to participate were included. A total of 518 women participated in the study. As the study followed a convenience sampling approach, the statistical power to detect certain associations may be limited.

Population and sampling

All women attending the HLC during the study period were considered eligible. A total of 802 women were approached, of whom 518 agreed to participate, yielding a response rate of 64.6%. The remaining women declined participation primarily due to time constraints or lack of interest.

Inclusion criteria were: being aged 18 years or older, having sufficient cognitive ability to understand the questions, and providing informed consent. Exclusion criteria included inability to communicate in Turkish and the presence of physical, cognitive, or psychiatric conditions preventing participation.

Data collection

Participation was voluntary. Before data collection, participants were informed about the purpose, scope, procedures, and voluntary nature of the study, as well as confidentiality and anonymity principles. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants; however, consent was not audio-recorded, which is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire administered via Google Forms by trained interviewers. The use of interviewer-administered surveys ensured inclusion of participants with low literacy levels.

A total of five trained interviewers conducted the data collection. Each interview lasted approximately 20–25 min. Interviewers received standardized training prior to data collection. Data collection was carried out during working hours, with an average of five to seven participants interviewed per day, making the total sample size feasible within the study period. The study was conducted without external funding, and interviewers participated as part of their professional responsibilities.

Although Turkish is the primary language of communication in the study setting, the region includes individuals from diverse ethnic backgrounds. No interpreter services were available during data collection; therefore, only participants who were able to communicate in Turkish were included in the study. This may have limited the representativeness of the sample.

Questionnaire

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed by the researchers based on relevant literature and previously used instruments [12–15], as well as expert consultation.

Although formal psychometric validation was not performed, the questionnaire items were reviewed by field experts to ensure content validity and clarity. The questionnaire consisted of more than 50 items, which may have increased respondent burden and introduced the possibility of response fatigue, potentially affecting data quality and contributing to non-response bias. The questionnaire was administered through interviewer-guided self-report, ensuring that participants including those who were illiterate could understand and respond to all items.

The questionnaire included the following sections: sociodemographic characteristics (age, education, income, etc.), health status (chronic diseases, smoking, alcohol use), reproductive history, menstrual and menopausal history, breast cancer awareness and history, breast self-examination practices, screening behaviors (mammography, clinical examination), perceived barriers to screening, beliefs about breast cancer and early detection. Perceived breast cancer risk was assessed based on participants’ self-evaluation. Risk level was defined as perceived risk derived from self-reported responses.

Breast cancer worry scale (BCWS)

Breast cancer-related worry was assessed using the Breast Cancer Worry Scale, originally developed by Lerman et al. and validated in Turkish populations [16, 17]. The scale includes six items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), with total scores ranging from 0 to 24. Scores ≥ 12 indicate high levels of worry. The Turkish version demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.78).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots.

For bivariate analyses, the chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. For continuous variables, the Student’s t-test was applied when the normality assumption was met, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used otherwise.

To identify independent factors associated with mammography participation, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. Mammography status (ever vs never) was included as the dependent variable. All relevant sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral variables were entered into the model. Variables with potential multicollinearity were carefully evaluated, and adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

Ethical approval for the study was obtained on April 18, 2024, with decision number 407 from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul Medipol University.

Results

The average age of the women participating in the study was 35.3 ± 8.7 years, and 66.9% (n = 347) were under the age of 40. Of the participants, 59.2% (n = 307) were primary school graduates, and 55.7% (n = 289) reported that their income was equal to or higher than their expenses. The majority of the women were not employed (87.2%, n = 452), and 96.5% (n = 500) were married. A body mass index of 25 or higher was observed in 75.2% (n = 390) of the participants. Additionally, 22.9% (n = 119) had at least one chronic disease, and 23.5% (n = 122) reported current smoking (Table 1).

Among the participants, 76.6% (n = 397) had 1–3 children. Of the women with children, 19.6% (n = 102) reported a total breastfeeding duration of less than one year. Oral contraceptive use, either current or past, was reported by 30.8% (n = 160) of the women. The age at menarche was between 12 and 14 years in 76.4% (n = 396) of the participants. Irregular menstrual cycles were reported by 25.0% (n = 130), 7.1% (n = 37) were menopausal, and 13.7% (n = 71) had a family history of breast cancer (Table 1).

Nearly half of the women (47.2%, n = 245) stated that they did not know what breast self-examination (BSE) was. Among those who were aware of BSE, the most common source of information was nurses and other healthcare professionals (22.6%, n = 117). Overall, 42.9% (n = 222) of all participants reported having performed BSE at least once in their lifetime, while 12.1% (n = 63) of all women reported performing BSE regularly once a month. Among women who practiced BSE, the most common method was palpation during bathing (30.7%, n = 159). The most common reasons for not performing BSE were not having enough time (3.7%) and perceiving cancer risk as low (2.3%) (Tables 1 and 2).

Breast self-examinations and breast cancer screening behaviors and worry status among women

A total of 28.1% (n = 146) of the women reported having undergone mammography at least once in their lifetime. Among women who had never undergone mammography, the most commonly reported reason was not being in the appropriate age group for screening (43.6%, n = 226). Other frequently reported reasons included having no complaints or discomfort (14.9%, n = 77) and lack of knowledge about screening procedures (13.1%, n = 68) (Tables 1 and 2).

Clinical breast examination (CBE) was reported as being performed either irregularly or at least once a year by 24.7% (n = 128) of the participants. Among women who had never undergone CBE, the most common reason was the absence of complaints or not feeling the need for examination (29.3%, n = 152), followed by lack of knowledge about screening examinations (24.3%, n = 126) and not knowing where the examination could be performed (18.7%, n = 97) (Tables 1 and 2).

Overall, 70.8% (n = 367) of the women believed that breast cancer is preventable, and 83.0% (n = 430) believed that early detection can protect against breast cancer (Table 1).

Low levels of breast cancer worry (BCW) were observed in 88.6% (n = 459) of the participants, and the mean BCW score for the entire sample was 4.1 ± 5.1. Statistically significant differences in BCW levels were observed according to employment status, number of children, total breastfeeding duration, family history of breast cancer, frequency of BSE, history of mammography or breast ultrasonography, undergoing clinical breast examination, belief in the preventability of breast cancer, and belief that early detection is protective (Table 1).

The proportion of women with high BCW was significantly higher among employed women compared with unemployed women (χ2 = 5.17, p = 0.02). High BCW increased with the number of children and was particularly more frequent among women with four or more children (χ2 = 5.99, p = 0.05). Women who breastfed for one year or longer had higher levels of BCW compared with those who breastfed for less than one year (χ2 = 3.82, p = 0.05). A significantly higher proportion of high BCW was observed among women with a family history of breast cancer (χ2 = 5.65, p = 0.02). Higher BCW levels were also more common among women who performed regular BSE (χ2 = 4.17, p = 0.04), those who had undergone mammography or breast ultrasonography (χ2 = 26.51, p < 0.001), and those who had undergone clinical breast examination (χ2 = 42.88, p < 0.001). Women who believed that breast cancer is preventable had higher BCW levels (χ2 = 8.24, p = 0.02), and women who believed that early detection is not protective also exhibited significantly higher BCW levels (χ2 = 12.25, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

In multivariate logistic regression analysis, breast cancer worry score was identified as an independent predictor of mammography participation. Each one-unit increase in the breast cancer worry score was associated with a 7% increase in the likelihood of undergoing mammography (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02–1.12, p = 0.006). Other variables, including age, education level, employment status, family history, reproductive factors, and breast self-examination, were not significantly associated with mammography after adjustment. The overall model was statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between breast cancer worry, risk perception, and screening-related knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors among women attending a Healthy Life Center (HLC), using real-world data from a primary healthcare setting in Türkiye. It also evaluated the association between sociodemographic and clinical factors and levels of worry. The relatively young age distribution of the study population (mean age 35.3 ± 8.7; 66.9% under 40) indicates that HLCs are accessed not only by the target screening population but also by younger women. This finding highlights the need to extend breast health education beyond the recommended screening age group to the broader female population in a structured and age-appropriate manner. In line with national recommendations in Türkiye, breast health education may be appropriately initiated from the age of 20, focusing on breast awareness rather than cancer risk, and delivered in an age-sensitive manner to avoid unnecessary anxiety [4, 8]. Therefore, educational interventions should aim to provide balanced and evidence-based information, emphasizing both protective factors and realistic risk, in order to increase awareness without unnecessarily increasing cancer-related worry [11, 16, 18].

Previous studies conducted among university students in Malaysia [18] and Korean American immigrant women in the United States [19] have shown that younger women tend to associate breast cancer with older age and often underestimate their own risk, leading to lower engagement in screening behaviors. Although breast cancer is more common in older women, early education may help establish long-term health awareness and promote positive screening behaviors.

A large proportion of participants (88.6%) had low levels of breast cancer worry. However, awareness and worry were significantly associated with multiple factors. Higher levels of breast cancer worry were observed among employed women, women with higher parity, those with prolonged breastfeeding duration, individuals with a family history of breast cancer, and those who had engaged in screening behaviors. These findings suggest that screening behaviors are influenced not only by knowledge but also by emotional responses, and that participation in screening may both increase worry and reflect greater awareness.

The study population was characterized by low educational attainment and low labor force participation, which may negatively affect health behaviors. Evidence from a systematic review conducted in low- and middle-income countries [20] and studies from Türkiye [21] and Germany [22] consistently show that lower educational levels are associated with reduced participation in cancer screening and limited access to healthcare services.

Employed women demonstrated higher levels of breast cancer worry, which may be attributed to greater access to information and wider social networks. A study conducted in Türkiye [23] similarly reported that higher knowledge levels and regular screening behaviors were associated with increased breast cancer worry, and that this worry was positively related to protective health behaviors.

In this study, breast cancer worry increased with the number of children and duration of breastfeeding. This may reflect heightened risk perception associated with maternal roles and responsibilities. However, large-scale pooled analyses from multiple countries [24] and meta-analyses conducted in international populations [25] have consistently demonstrated that prolonged breastfeeding has a protective effect against breast cancer. Awareness of protective factors may paradoxically increase worry among informed women, as increased knowledge can enhance risk perception and personal relevance of the disease.

Consistent with previous findings, women with a family history of breast cancer reported higher levels of worry. A population-based study conducted in the United States [26] demonstrated that family history significantly increases perceived risk. Similarly, a study conducted in Türkiye [27] found that higher levels of breast cancer anxiety were associated with changes in health behaviors, although not all women with elevated worry participated in regular screening.

Knowledge and practice of breast self-examination (BSE) were found to be limited. Nearly half of the participants (47.2%) did not know what BSE is, and only a small proportion practiced it regularly. Similar low rates have been reported among seasonal female agricultural workers in Türkiye [28] and among university students in Malaysia [18]. In Türkiye, women are encouraged to perform BSE from the age of 20 as part of national health recommendations. BSE should not be considered a primary screening tool but rather a method to promote breast awareness. However, international evidence suggests that the effectiveness of BSE, particularly among younger women, remains controversial and may have limited impact on mortality reduction. Cultural beliefs and fear related to cancer, as reported in studies conducted among Turkish women [29], may further influence screening behaviors and limit regular practice.

The source and quality of information emerged as important determinants of behavior. Studies conducted in Türkiye [30] and Malaysia [18] indicate that women often rely on informal sources such as media and printed materials, while information obtained from healthcare professionals remains limited. In the present study, only a minority of participants reported receiving information from health personnel. This suggests that inaccurate or incomplete information may lead to inconsistent screening behaviors and highlights the need to strengthen the educational and counseling roles of healthcare providers.

Women who performed regular BSE had higher levels of worry, supporting the notion that a certain level of concern may motivate protective behaviors. Similar associations between awareness, worry, and screening behaviors have been reported in studies conducted among Malaysian students [18] and Korean American women [19].

Participation in mammography and clinical breast examination (CBE) was also low. National studies conducted in Türkiye [31] and systematic reviews including global populations [32] have reported similarly low participation rates, particularly in settings where screening is perceived as symptom-driven. In this study, the most common reasons for not undergoing screening included lack of symptoms, perceived lack of need, and insufficient knowledge, indicating that the concept of early detection has not been fully internalized.

Women who had undergone mammography or breast ultrasound reported higher levels of worry. A study conducted among women undergoing mammography in Malaysia reported that 41.8% experienced moderate to high levels of anxiety during the procedure [33]. While higher levels of worry have been associated with increased participation in clinical breast examination in studies conducted in Türkiye [34], evidence suggests that worry alone is not sufficient to ensure consistent screening behavior, and that structural, informational, and sociocultural factors must also be considered.

Health beliefs were also found to play a significant role. In this study, women who believed that breast cancer is preventable and that early detection is effective reported higher levels of worry. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Iran [35]. In contrast, studies among Arab women [36], Latina populations in the United States [37], and a recent meta-analysis of international populations [38] have shown that fatalistic beliefs are associated with lower screening participation and reduced levels of concern. Fatalistic beliefs refer to the perception that cancer is inevitable or predetermined and cannot be prevented or controlled, which may reduce motivation to participate in screening behaviors. Access to accurate information and awareness of early detection were consistently identified as key determinants of both worry and screening behaviors across these populations.

The findings of this study demonstrate that breast cancer worry is an independent predictor of mammography participation, remaining significant after adjustment for multiple sociodemographic and reproductive factors. This suggests that psychological determinants may have a greater influence on screening behavior than traditional demographic variables. Consistent with previous multivariate analyses, health beliefs and individual perceptions have been identified as key factors influencing screening uptake [30, 35, 36]. Moreover, evidence indicates that the effects of conventional risk factors, such as family history, may be mediated by individual perceptions [26]. Taken together, these findings underscore the central role of cancer-related worry in shaping screening behavior and highlight the need for screening strategies that incorporate psychological and behavioral components.

Strengths of the study

This study provides field-based real-world data by being conducted among women who visit HLCs, which provide primary healthcare services in Türkiye. The joint assessment of knowledge, attitudes, behavior, and emotional factors affecting participation in breast cancer screening is an important aspect that distinguishes this study in the literature. The objective measurement of emotional factors using a valid and reliable scale such as the BCWS enhances the scientific value of the results obtained. Furthermore, the study's association of levels of breast cancer worry with sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioural data enables a multidimensional assessment of health behavior. Other important aspects that strengthen the study are that the findings shed light not only on the level of knowledge but also on cultural, social, and psychological variables, and that they offer practical recommendations on how HLCs can play a more effective role in screening programmes. In this respect, the research has the potential to contribute to both the scientific literature and health policies.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study was conducted at a single Healthy Life Center located in the Sultanbeyli district of Istanbul and included only women who applied to this center during the study period. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to women in other regions, settings, or healthcare contexts.

Second, no probability-based sampling method or formal sample size calculation was performed. The use of convenience sampling and the absence of power analysis may limit the statistical representativeness of the findings and the ability to detect certain associations.

Third, participation was voluntary, which may have introduced selection bias, as women who chose to participate may differ systematically from those who did not, particularly in terms of health awareness and motivation.

Fourth, all data were collected using self-report methods, which may have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias. In addition, the assessment of barriers to screening relied on subjective responses.

Fifth, verbal informed consent was obtained but not audio-recorded, which may be considered a methodological limitation in terms of documentation.

Sixth, the study was conducted only among women who were able to communicate in Turkish. Given the ethnic diversity of the study region, the exclusion of non-Turkish speakers and the absence of interpreter support may limit the representativeness of the findings.

Seventh, the questionnaire, except for the Breast Cancer Worry Scale, was not formally validated as a whole instrument, which may limit measurement reliability and raise concerns regarding the validity of the measurements.

Eighth, the questionnaire included more than 50 items, which may have increased respondent burden and contributed to response fatigue, potentially affecting data quality and completeness.

Ninth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences between variables, and the observed associations should be interpreted as correlational.

Tenth, the age distribution of the study population represents an important limitation. A substantial proportion of participants (66.9%) were under the age of 40, which may limit the applicability of the findings to the target screening population.

Finally, the relatively low educational level of a large proportion of participants may have influenced comprehension of questionnaire items and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusions

This study examined breast cancer awareness, attitudes, screening behaviors, and levels of breast cancer worry among women attending a Healthy Life Center (HLC) and identified several findings specific to this study population. Although most participants stated that they believed breast cancer is preventable and that early diagnosis can be life-saving, this awareness was not consistently reflected in screening behaviors. In particular, the low participation rates in mammography and clinical breast examination suggest a discrepancy between knowledge and practice.

Breast cancer worry was found to be associated with screening behavior in a complex and variable manner. While higher levels of worry were associated with screening participation in some women, worry was also linked to avoidance behaviors in others, indicating that its influence differs across individuals. Similar patterns have been reported in the literature, where women often demonstrate moderate to high levels of knowledge about breast cancer but low screening uptake [35, 39]. Systematic reviews further indicate that screening behaviors are influenced by multiple interacting factors, including knowledge, perceived risk, self-efficacy, cultural beliefs, and social support [28, 39].

In this study, employment status, number of children, breastfeeding duration, and family history of breast cancer showed significant associations with both breast cancer worry and screening behaviors. These findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that sociodemographic and reproductive characteristics may be related to both emotional responses to cancer and preventive health practices. Evidence from Malaysia similarly indicates that breast cancer worry during mammography may function as both a motivating and an inhibiting factor, depending on individual context [29].

International discussions have highlighted potential limitations of mammography screening, including overdiagnosis, unnecessary interventions, and increased anxiety in certain populations [36, 40]. Within this framework, approaches that emphasize breast awareness and informed decision making, rather than a sole focus on screening participation, may be particularly relevant [40]. Balanced and realistic health messages may support women in understanding their own bodies and making informed choices regarding screening.

Overall, the findings of this study suggest that breast cancer worry alone does not determine individual screening behavior. The translation of concern into action may require targeted and context-specific interventions, particularly for women with lower educational levels, limited workforce participation, and lower health literacy. In this respect, HLCs may have a supportive role in facilitating behavioral change through structured educational activities and psychosocial support.

Programs implemented in HLCs should therefore consider not only the provision of information but also the management of emotional barriers related to cancer worry within a safe and accessible service environment. Addressing emotional factors alongside educational and service delivery interventions may strengthen preventive health efforts in primary care settings and support the translation of breast cancer awareness into appropriate health behaviors. These findings highlight the importance of delivering age-appropriate and psychologically balanced health education strategies.
