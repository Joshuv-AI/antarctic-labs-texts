Flavor entanglement in pion-pion scattering at one-loop in chiral perturbation theory
Victor Miguel Banda Guzmán, Rubén Flores-Mendieta, Johann Hernández

Abstract
One of the defining features of quantum mechanics is the ability of two systems to exhibit correlation of information even when they are spatially separated by large distances. This phenomenon, known as quantum entanglement, is a fundamental resource for quantum computation and emerging quantum technologies. Understanding the mechanisms responsible for the generation of entanglement in particle interactions requires a quantitative description of the entanglement produced by fundamental interactions. For the strong interaction, this work presents an analysis of the flavor entanglement generated in pion–pion scattering at one-loop order in chiral perturbation theory. The amount of entanglement is quantified using the quantum mutual information, expressed in terms of the von Neumann entropy. The calculation is performed under fixed pion momenta and initial flavor indices, with the incoming pions assumed to be in a pure, unentangled state. It is shown that the resulting flavor entanglement depends on whether the initial pion flavors are identical or distinct. At threshold, the quantum mutual information reaches approximately 2.8 in the identical-flavor case, close to the maximal value for a pair of qutrits, and equals 2 in the distinct-flavor case, corresponding to the maximal entanglement of two qubits. Analytical expressions for the mutual information at other kinematical regimes are also provided.

Introduction

Two of the fundamental features that distinguish quantum systems form classical systems are superposition and entanglement of quantum states. When two quantum systems are entangled the information contained in each of them becomes correlated, even if they are spatially separated by large distances.

As an illustrative example, consider a source that generates a pair of entangled quantum particles. Each particle can exist in a superposition of two basis states which can be labeled as 0 or 1. Each particle thus represents a qubit, denoted by
A
and
B
. Assume that the composite system
AB
is in the following Bell state,

\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}_{A}\otimes\ket{1}_{B}+\ket{1}_{A}\otimes\ket{0}_{B}\right).

(1.1)

After a measurement of the qubit
A
, the outcome is either 0 or 1, each with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. If the result for qubit
A
is 0, the measurement of qubit
B
would return a value of 1, and vice versa. In either case, the measurement outcomes of the two qubits are perfectly correlated: knowledge of one qubit immediately determines the other.

Individually, each qubit exhibits random behavior, but when considered jointly, their measurement results reveal strong correlations. This phenomenon, now known as quantum entanglement, suggests the possible existence of hidden local mechanisms responsible for such correlations. But as shown by Bell, no local hidden variable theory can reproduce all the measurement outcomes that predict quantum mechanics
[
1
]
.

This correlation property encoded in entangled particles is a fundamental ingredient of quantum computation and quantum technologies. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms responsible for the generation of entanglement is therefore of great importance for these applications.

Since quantum entanglement is a distinctive feature of quantum mechanics and plays a central role in quantum technologies, this work constitutes the first one of a series of articles focused on understanding how entanglement arises from the fundamental interactions of particles. In the context of the strong interaction, it has been demonstrated that entanglement suppression correlates with the emergence of approximate spin-flavor symmetries in low-energy baryon interactions, suggesting that entanglement suppression is a property of the strong interaction in the infrared regime
[
2
]
.

In this work, the entanglement mechanism is analyzed also for the strong interaction but restricted to the pseudo scalar meson sector. Specifically, the flavor entanglement generated in pion–pion scattering is computed at one-loop order through a quantum field theoretic analysis which employs the effective Lagrangian of chiral perturbation theory. To quantify this entanglement in the scattering process, the relevant operator for the analysis corresponds to the
T
-matrix operator of the
S
-scattering matrix. The incoming pions are assumed to be in a pure, unentangled state with fixed flavor indices. Hence, the entanglement observed in the final state originates solely from the strong interaction, and not by the initial conditions. Furthermore, the initial and final pion momenta are held fixed, so that only flavor entanglement is considered.

4
Flavor entanglement in the pion-pion scattering process

Following the approach introduced in Refs.
[
15
,
16
]
, the quantum mutual information function
S(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})
is used to quantify the degree of entanglement generated during the pion-pion scattering process. This function is defined as
[
17
]
,

S(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})=S(\rho_{A})+S(\rho_{B})-S(\rho_{AB}),

(4.1)

where
A
and
B
are the subsystems of the composite system
AB
and
S(\rho)
denotes the von Neumann entropy,

S(\rho)=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log\rho),

(4.2)

where
\rho
denotes the density matrix of a quantum system,
\mathrm{Tr}
denotes the trace operation and
\log(z)

is the logarithm base 2 of
z
.

If
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of the density matrix, the von Neumann entropy in Eq. (
4.2
) can be rewritten as,

S(\rho)=-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\log(\lambda_{i}),

(4.3)

with the convention
0\,\log(0)=0
.

If
\rho_{AB}
in Eq. (
4.1
) is obtained from a final pure state produced by a
2\rightarrow 2
scattering process, the mutual information for the two particles
A
and
B
in the scattering event simplifies to,

S(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})=2S(\rho_{A})=2S(\rho_{B}),

(4.4)

where
\rho_{A}
(
\rho_{B}
) represents the reduced density matrix for the particle
A
(
B
). Thus, either
S(\rho_{A})
or
S(\rho_{B})
can be used to quantify the degree of entanglement produced in the
2\rightarrow 2
scattering of particles
A
and
B
.

Consider now the pion-pion scattering process
\pi^{a}(p_{1})+\pi^{b}(p_{2})\rightarrow\pi^{c}(p_{3})+\pi^{d}(p_{4})
with fixed momenta and a given set of values for the initial flavor indices
a
and
b
. In addition, assume that the initial pions are unentangled and that the scattering event occurs. Under this conditions, the final (pure) state
\ket{\psi}_{ab}
of the scattering process is described by the
T
-matrix elements given in Eq. (
3.2
),

\ket{\psi}_{ab}=\frac{1}{N_{ab}}\sum_{c,d}T^{ab;\,cd}\;\ket{c}_{A}\otimes\ket{d}_{B},

(4.5)

where
N_{ab}
is a normalization factor. Since the final momenta are considered to be fixed in the scattering process, the final state corresponds only to a superposition on the flavor indices of the final particles
A
and
B
.

From Eq. (
4.5
), the density matrix
\rho_{AB}
of the composite system of the scattered particles becomes

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}(a,b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}_{ab}}\sum_{c,d,c^{\prime},d^{\prime}}T^{ab;cd}\,T^{\ast\,ab;c^{\prime}d^{\prime}}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\times(\ket{c}_{A}\otimes\ket{d}_{B})\,(\bra{c^{\prime}}_{A}\otimes\bra{d^{\prime}}_{B}).

Since
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)=1
for any density matrix
\rho
, the normalization factor
N_{ab}
in Eq. (
4.6
) is given by

N^{2}_{ab}=\sum_{c,d}|T^{ab;cd}|^{2}.

(4.7)

Taking the partial trace over the particle
B
in Eq. (
4.6
), the reduced density matrix
\rho_{A}(a,b)
is now

\rho_{A}(a,b)=\frac{1}{N_{ab^{2}}}\sum_{c,c^{\prime},d}T^{ab;\,cd}T^{\ast\,ab;\,c^{\prime}d}\,\ket{c}_{A}\bra{c^{\prime}}_{A}.

(4.8)

The nine possible reduced matrices

are listed in Appendix
E

\displaystyle S[\rho_{A}(3,3)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S[\rho_{A}(2,2)]=S[\rho_{A}(1,1)],

(4.9a)

\displaystyle S[\rho_{A}(2,3)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S[\rho_{A}(1,3)]=S[\rho_{A}(1,2)],

(4.9b)

Conclusions

In the present analysis, it has been shown that the strong interaction in pion-pion scattering processes produces a large amount of flavor entanglement (see Sec.
4
). To quantify this entanglement, the quantum mutual information function
S(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})
for a bipartite composite system, defined in Eq. (
4.1
), is used. The function
S(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})
is given in terms of the von Neumann entropy
S(\rho)
. For a fixed state of the composite system, the expression for
S(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})
reduces to Eq. (
4.4
) which shows that the mutual information is twice the von Neumann entropy of the reduced density matrix of the system.

For a pion-pion scattering process where the initial and final momenta of the particles are fixed and a set of initial flavors is given with initial pure states, the nine possible flavor reduced matrices of the composite system formed by the final pions are provided in Appendix
E
. According to these results, there are only two independent von Neumann flavor entropies,
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, related to the nine possible reduced density matrices.
S_{1}
is produced when the flavors of the initial pions are identical and corresponds to the case of a three-level system since the related flavor density matrices have three non-zero eigenvalues. Similarly,
S_{2}
is produced when the initial flavors are distinct and corresponds to the case of a two-level system. For a three-level system (qutrit) the maximum value of the von Neumann entropy is
\log(3)
, while for a two-level system (qubit) the maximum value is 1.
4
4

4

In general, for a system in a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space, the von Neumann entropy is at most
\log(d)
, (see for example
[
19
]
).
Compact formulas for the functions
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are given in Eq. (
4.12
) in terms of the scattering amplitude
A(s,t,u)
defined in Eq. (
3.2
).

At one-loop chiral order, the amplitude
A(s,t,u)
is given in Eq. (
3.28
). After substituting this expression in Eq. (
4.12
), the entropies
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
—expressed in terms of the Mandelstam variables, the LECs, and the Lagrangian chiral parameters
M
and
F
at one-loop order—take the forms shown in Eq. (
4.17
). Best-suited expressions for numerical computations in terms of the physical pion mass
M_{\pi}
and the pion decay constant
F_{\pi}
are given in Eq. (
4.20
). From these formulas, the entropy
S_{1}
for equal initial flavors at threshold is about 1.4, close to the maximum value of a qutrit, while the entropy
S_{2}
at threshold is 1, which is the maximum value for a qubit.
5
5

5

From Eq. (
4.4
) the corresponding values for the quantum mutual information are twice the values of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.
Thus, the strong interaction in the scattering event produces almost the maximal possible degree of flavor entanglement in this kinematical region.

The plots of these entropies are displayed in Fig.
1
, where the vertical axis represents the scattering angle in the center-of-mass frame and the horizontal axis represents the total energy of the scattering process given by the Mandelstam variable
s
. The plot for
S_{1}
shows that, as the energy is increased from threshold, the amount of flavor entanglement of the final pions decreases with no noticeable variation with respect to the scattering angle, producing a uniform gradient of colors in the density plot. In the energy scale of the map, the value of the entropy does not decrease below 1. Thus, in this kinematical regime the amount of entanglement is above a qubit system. For
S_{2}
, the plot shows a region, centered at the scattering angle
\theta=\pi/2
, whose entropy equals the maximum possible value of a qubit system. This region almost covers the entire kinematical region of the plot. Since the entropy almost vanishes at the upper- and lower-right corners of the kinematical region for values of
s
above
1\,\mathrm{GeV}^{2}
, two-loop corrections should be considered since they could substantially contribute to the small values predicted by the tree-level and one-loop results. In summary, the density plots of Figure
1
show that at least near threshold the amount of entanglement of the final pions in the scattering process maintains a considerable degree of flavor entanglement close to the maximum levels for qutrit and qubit pair of particles. Future work should gather more evidence in favor of the fundamental interactions between particles to generate large amount of entanglement.

Appendix A
Transformation properties of the external fields
l^{\mu}(x)
and
r^{\mu}(x)

Consider the space matrix field
A^{\mu}(x)
defined as

A^{\mu}(x)=\tau^{a}A^{a\,\mu}(x),

(A.1)

where
A^{a\,\mu}(x)
are real scalar fields.
A^{\mu}(x)
, just like the external fields
l^{\mu}(x)
and
r^{\mu}(x)
defined in Eq. (
2.16
), is a Hermitian and traceless
2\times 2
matrix field. If
V(x)
denotes an element of the gauge group
\mathrm{SU}(2)
, the transformation of
A^{\mu}(x)
under
V(x)
is defined analogously to Eq. (
2.17
),

A^{\prime\,\mu}(x)=V(x)A^{\mu}(x)V^{\dagger}(x)+iV(x)\partial^{\mu}V^{\dagger}(x).

(A.2)

Under this transformation,
A^{\prime\,\mu}(x)
also remain Hermitian and traceless. This can be demonstrated by proving that each term on the right-hand-side of Eq. (
A.2
) are Hermitian and traceless. For the first term, note that by the cyclic property of the trace operation,

\mathrm{Tr}\left[V(x)A^{\mu}(x)V^{\dagger}(x)\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\left[A^{\mu}(x)\right]=0,

(A.3)

and
\left(V(x)A^{\mu}(x)V^{\dagger}(x)\right)^{\dagger}=V(x)A^{\mu}(x)V^{\dagger}(x)
.

Now, rewriting
V(x)
as

V(x)=\text{e}^{ig\tau^{a}v^{a}(x)},

(A.4)

where
g
is a constant parameter and
v^{a}(x)
are real scalar fields, it can be shown that (see for example Ref.
[
3
]
),

\mathrm{Tr}\left[iV(x)\partial^{\mu}V^{\dagger}(x)\right]=0.

(A.5)

Finally, to prove that
iV\partial^{\mu}V^{\dagger}
is hermitian, note that

V\partial^{\mu}V^{\dagger}=-\left(\partial^{\mu}V\right)V^{\dagger},

(A.6)

which is obtained by applying the derivative operator to the identity
VV\dagger=1
.

From Eq. (
A.6
), the following result is obtained,

\left(iV\partial^{\mu}V^{\dagger}\right)^{\dagger}=-i\left(\partial^{\mu}V\right)V^{\dagger}=iV\partial^{\mu}V^{\dagger},

(A.7)

which corresponds to the Hermitian condition of the term
iV\partial^{\mu}V^{\dagger}
.
