Pathological fractures revealing Langerhans cell histiocytosis in an elderly female: a rare case report
Authors: Prashant Adhikari, Gaurav Bir Bajracharya, Deepak Shrestha, Bhaskar Raj Pant, Sandeep Bhandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare clonal proliferative disorder of dendritic cells, predominantly affecting children. Adult-onset LCH is uncommon and often misdiagnosed due to its atypical presentation. Pathological fractures as the initial manifestation are particularly rare in older adults.Case presentation:We report a 63-year-old female who sustained a pathological femoral fracture following minor trauma, with radiographs revealing cortical thinning and lytic changes. Subsequent imaging demonstrated multifocal osseous lesions involving the pelvis, vertebrae, and long bones. Histopathology confirmed LCH by characteristic morphology with immunohistochemical positivity for CD1a, S100, CD45, and Langerin (CD207). The femoral fracture was stabilized with intramedullary interlocking (IMIL) nailing after biopsy of the lesion. Two months later, she sustained a pathological humeral fracture with similar radiological features, which was managed with IMIL nailing without repeat biopsy because the lesion was considered clinically and radiologically consistent with the previously confirmed diagnosis of LCH. Systemic therapy with cytarabine and prednisolone was initiated for single-system multifocal osseous LCH.Clinical discussion:This case highlights the importance of considering LCH in the differential diagnosis of pathological fractures in adults, despite its rarity in the elderly. The occurrence of sequential pathological fractures of two long bones illustrates the aggressive skeletal manifestations that may occur in adult single-system multifocal LCH. Histological confirmation with immunohistochemistry remains the diagnostic gold standard. Surgical stabilization with IMIL nailing provided stable fixation, allowing early rehabilitation while systemic therapy was initiated for progressive multifocal skeletal disease.Conclusion:Adult-onset multifocal skeletal LCH should be considered in patients presenting with pathological fractures after low-energy trauma. Early histological diagnosis, multidisciplinary management, appropriate fracture stabilization, and timely systemic therapy may facilitate fracture healing and functional recovery. Longer follow-up with systemic imaging is required to determine long-term disease control.

Introduction

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare clonal proliferative disorder of dendritic cells characterized by the accumulation of CD1a-positive and Langerin-positive cells in affected tissues. Although it is predominantly a pediatric disease, adult-onset LCH is uncommon, with an incidence of approximately 1 to 2 cases per million. Skeletal involvement is the most frequent manifestation and may present as lytic lesions, cortical thinning, or pathological fractures. In elderly patients, pathological fractures due to LCH are particularly rare and may mimic osteoporosis, metastatic disease, or primary bone tumors[1–3].

Histopathological confirmation with immunohistochemistry remains the diagnostic gold standard. Although isolated osseous lesions are more commonly encountered in adults, single-system multifocal skeletal involvement presenting with sequential pathological fractures of different long bones is exceptionally rare, particularly in elderly patients. The present case describes a 63-year-old woman in whom sequential pathological fractures of the femur and humerus led to the diagnosis of single-system multifocal LCH, emphasizing the importance of considering this entity in the differential diagnosis of pathological fractures in adults.

This case is reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[4].

Case presentation

A 63-year-old postmenopausal female with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department following a fall down approximately three steps at home. She reported that she could not bear weight on the affected limb and that she had severe pain in her left thigh immediately. There was no history of systemic symptoms like fatigue, fever, or weight loss. She denied a history of prolonged corticosteroid use, metabolic bone disease, or malignancy.

The patient was hemodynamically stable upon physical examination. A local examination of the left thigh showed crepitus, pain, and swelling over the midshaft of the femur. There was no impairment of distal neurovascular status. A transverse mid-shaft fracture with cortical thinning and a mild lytic lesion at the fracture site was visible on plain radiographs of the left femur, as shown in Figure 1A. In view of the radiographic appearance suggestive of an underlying pathological process, further evaluation was undertaken before definitive fracture fixation. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the left femur demonstrated a displaced mid-shaft fracture associated with an enhancing soft-tissue mass extending into the adjacent musculature (Fig. 2A). Whole-body fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (FDG PET/CT) subsequently revealed multiple hypermetabolic skeletal lesions involving the pelvis, vertebral bodies, bilateral femora, and other long bones, consistent with multifocal osseous disease (Fig. 2B). An open biopsy of the femoral lesion was performed before definitive fracture fixation. Histopathological examination demonstrated fragments of bone, skeletal muscle, and fibro-fatty tissue infiltrated by numerous eosinophils, lymphocytes, plasma cells, along with scattered sheets of polygonal to oval cells with moderately eosinophilic cytoplasm and characteristic irregular, grooved (“coffee-bean”) nuclei. Occasional multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells were also identified in a background of chronic inflammatory infiltrate. These histological features were suggestive of LCH and were confirmed by immunohistochemistry, which showed diffuse positivity for CD1a, S100, CD45, and Langerin (CD207). The lesional cells were negative for cytokeratin, desmin, and CD68, supporting the diagnosis in conjunction with the characteristic morphology and immunophenotype.

Systemic staging demonstrated osseous involvement without evidence of other major organ system involvement. High-resolution CT of the chest showed no characteristic pulmonary LCH changes. Clinical evaluation revealed no abnormalities of the skin, oral cavity, peripheral lymph nodes, liver, or spleen, and there were no symptoms suggestive of pituitary or other endocrine dysfunction. Based on these findings, the disease was classified as single-system multifocal LCH with skeletal involvement only.

Following histological confirmation, the femoral fracture was stabilized using a reamed intramedullary interlocking (IMIL) nail (Fig. 1B). Intramedullary fixation was selected because it provides stable load-sharing fixation, protects the entire femur from subsequent fracture, allows early mobilization, and is an established treatment option for pathological diaphyseal femoral fractures. The patient was encouraged to begin early knee range-of-motion exercises and progressive weight-bearing as tolerated under physiotherapy supervision. The patient achieved independent ambulation approximately 3 months after surgery. The postoperative course was uneventful.

Approximately 2 months after the initial surgery, the patient sustained another low-energy fall and developed pain, swelling, and deformity of the left upper arm. Radiographs demonstrated a pathological mid-shaft fracture of the left humerus with radiographic features similar to those of the previously diagnosed femoral lesion (Fig. 1C). As the patient already had histologically confirmed multifocal LCH and the humeral lesion demonstrated similar clinical and radiological characteristics, a repeat biopsy was not performed. This should be recognized as a limitation because histological confirmation of the humeral lesion was not obtained. The fracture was treated with IMIL nailing (Fig. 1D).

Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, serum calcium, phosphate, and alkaline phosphatase, were within normal limits.

Whole-body PET/CT demonstrated multiple hypermetabolic skeletal lesions involving the right humerus, L1 vertebral body, right radius, right fifth rib, sacrum, right ilium, acetabulum, bilateral femora, bilateral tibiae, bilateral fibulae, right talus, calcaneus, and the left third metatarsal.

FDG-avid synovial thickening and joint effusions were also noted around both knees on PET/CT. However, because the patient had no corresponding clinical features of inflammatory arthritis and no dedicated rheumatologic evaluation or joint aspiration was performed, these findings were considered nonspecific imaging abnormalities rather than evidence of active inflammatory arthropathy.

The patient was subsequently referred to the hematology service for multidisciplinary management. Owing to the presence of progressive single-system multifocal skeletal LCH with sequential pathological fractures, systemic treatment was recommended to reduce the risk of further skeletal progression. Intravenous cytarabine (100 mg per cycle, according to the treating hematologist’s institutional protocol) was administered for a total of five cycles over approximately one year. Detailed information regarding the number of treatment days per cycle and the dosing interval was not available from the medical records. No dose reductions or clinically significant adverse events were documented during follow-up. Prednisolone (10 mg orally once daily for 28 days) was administered as an adjunct to systemic therapy to reduce inflammatory activity during initiation of cytarabine treatment. No tapering schedule was documented in the medical records. The treatment was well tolerated, and no clinically significant treatment-related adverse events or dose modifications were documented during the reported follow-up period.

Clinical and radiographic assessment at 1 and 3 months demonstrated satisfactory implant position, maintained fracture alignment, and progressive callus formation at both fracture sites. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Sections of the bone biopsy demonstrated sheets of round to oval Langerhans cells with irregular, folded (“coffee-bean”) nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli, and moderately abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm (Fig. 3). The background contained numerous eosinophils, lymphocytes, plasma cells, and scattered multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells. Although nuclear grooves and eosinophil-rich inflammatory infiltrates are characteristic histological features of LCH, they are not diagnostic in isolation and require immunohistochemical confirmation. The diagnosis of LCH was confirmed by immunohistochemistry, which demonstrated strong diffuse positivity for CD1a, S100, CD45, and Langerin (CD207). The combined morphological and immunophenotypic findings established the diagnosis of LCH. The lesional cells were negative for cytokeratin, desmin, and CD68. The absence of cytokeratin and desmin helped exclude epithelial and myogenic neoplasms, while the immunohistochemical profile supported the diagnosis of LCH in the appropriate clinicopathological context.

Pulmonary findings on PET/CT demonstrated fibrotic linear densities and atelectatic bands. However, high-resolution CT of the chest showed no characteristic centrilobular nodules or cystic changes suggestive of pulmonary LCH. Furthermore, clinical assessment did not identify involvement of the skin, oral mucosa, lymph nodes, liver, spleen, or endocrine system. Accordingly, the disease was classified as single-system, multifocal LCH with isolated skeletal involvement.

The pathological diagnosis was established only from the femoral biopsy specimen. The subsequent humeral lesion was considered clinically and radiologically consistent with LCH, based on the previously confirmed diagnosis and similar imaging characteristics; however, histological confirmation of the humeral lesion was not obtained, representing a limitation of this report.

Discussion

Adult-onset LCH is uncommon and may present diagnostic challenges because its radiological appearance overlaps with metastatic disease, multiple myeloma, osteomyelitis, and primary bone tumors. Skeletal involvement is the most common manifestation and frequently affects long bones, the pelvis, and vertebrae. Pathological fractures as a presenting feature are particularly rare in elderly patients[5,6].

In the present case, a low-energy pathological femoral fracture was the initial manifestation of the disease and ultimately led to the diagnosis of LCH. Approximately 2 months later, the patient sustained a second pathological fracture involving the humerus, indicating progressive skeletal involvement despite initial fracture stabilization. The occurrence of sequential pathological fractures affecting two different long bones highlights the aggressive skeletal behavior that adult single-system, multifocal LCH may occasionally demonstrate. To our knowledge, reports describing sequential femoral and humeral pathological fractures as presenting manifestations of adult-onset multifocal skeletal LCH are extremely limited.

Histopathological examination demonstrated Langerhans cells with characteristic nuclear grooves within an eosinophil-rich inflammatory background. These morphological findings, although highly suggestive, are not diagnostic by themselves and require confirmation by immunohistochemistry. Diffuse positivity for CD1a, S100, and Langerin (CD207) established the diagnosis in our patient. Because the humeral lesion was not biopsied, its diagnosis was based on the previously confirmed pathology together with similar clinical and radiological findings. This should be recognized as a limitation of the present report[5].

Whole-body FDG PET/CT demonstrated multifocal skeletal involvement without convincing evidence of extra-osseous disease. High-resolution CT of the chest did not demonstrate characteristic pulmonary LCH, and clinical evaluation did not reveal involvement of the skin, oral cavity, lymph nodes, liver, spleen, or endocrine organs. Accordingly, the patient was classified as having single-system multifocal LCH. Although bilateral long-bone lesions were identified on PET/CT, the overall clinicopathological findings favored LCH. Although bilateral long-bone lesions prompted consideration of Erdheim–Chester disease and mixed histiocytosis, the characteristic morphology, CD1a/Langerin positivity, eosinophil-rich infiltrate, and overall clinicoradiological findings were considered more consistent with LCH. However, molecular testing was unavailable, and therefore the complete exclusion of overlapping histiocytic disorders was not possible[5,7,8].

Surgical stabilization was indicated because both fractures involved weight-bearing or functionally important long bones with established pathological fractures. IMIL nailing was selected because it provides durable load-sharing fixation, protects the entire bone, permits early mobilization, and has demonstrated favorable outcomes in pathological diaphyseal fractures. Curettage was not performed because the primary objective of surgery was fracture stabilization, while systemic disease was addressed medically following histological confirmation. The remaining osseous lesions identified on PET/CT were assessed clinically and radiologically. As they did not demonstrate cortical destruction, mechanical instability, or other features suggestive of impending pathological fracture, prophylactic fixation was not considered. Instead, these lesions were managed with systemic therapy and close clinical and radiological surveillance.

The decision to initiate systemic therapy was based on the presence of progressive multifocal skeletal disease with sequential pathological fractures, placing the patient at substantial risk for further skeletal complications. Current international expert consensus recommends systemic treatment for adults with multifocal osseous LCH, whereas isolated asymptomatic lesions may be managed with local therapy or observation. Cytarabine has emerged as an effective therapeutic option in adults because of its favorable response rates and acceptable toxicity profile. Vinblastine-based regimens are more commonly used in pediatric practice, but are generally associated with lower response rates and greater neurotoxicity in adults. Alternative treatments, including cladribine, targeted therapy for MAPK pathway mutations, radiotherapy, and local treatment, may be considered in selected patients according to the disease extent and molecular profile[9].

At the time of manuscript preparation, BRAF V600E and other MAPK pathway mutation analyses were not available at our institution and therefore could not be performed. This represents an important limitation because molecular profiling is increasingly recognized as valuable for prognostication and may influence therapeutic decision-making in selected patients.

Another limitation is the relatively short clinical follow-up. Although radiographs demonstrated satisfactory fracture healing and functional recovery, repeat whole-body imaging was not available to assess systemic disease response or detect recurrent or new lesions. Consequently, the present report is limited to early orthopedic and functional outcomes rather than long-term oncological control.

Despite these limitations, this case contributes to the limited literature describing adult-onset multifocal skeletal LCH in elderly patients. It emphasizes the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating pathological fractures after low-energy trauma and highlights the value of a multidisciplinary approach integrating orthopedic stabilization, histopathological confirmation, comprehensive staging, and systemic therapy.

Conclusion

Adult-onset LCH should be considered in the differential diagnosis of pathological fractures, particularly when fractures occur after low-energy trauma and are associated with osteolytic lesions. Histopathological examination with appropriate immunohistochemical staining remains essential for establishing the diagnosis. This case demonstrates that multidisciplinary management, including timely fracture stabilization and systemic therapy for progressive multifocal skeletal disease, can achieve satisfactory early fracture healing and functional recovery. However, extended clinical follow-up with repeat systemic imaging is required to evaluate long-term disease control and recurrence.

Limitation

Immunohistochemical confirmation was performed using CD1a, CD207, S100, and CD45; unfortunately, representative immunohistochemical photomicrographs could not be retrieved because the original pathology slides were not archived for digital imaging.

Additional limitations include the absence of BRAF V600E or other MAPK pathway mutation testing, the lack of representative immunohistochemical photomicrographs for publication, as these were unavailable, and the relatively short duration of follow-up without repeat whole-body PET/CT to assess long-term systemic disease response.

Original digital PET/CT (DICOM) images were unavailable for retrieval from the imaging archive. Consequently, representative photographs of available printed PET/CT films were used for illustrative purposes; this may limit image quality and detailed interpretation.

Patient perspective

The patient expressed satisfaction with the treatment received and reported progressive improvement in pain, mobility, and the ability to perform daily activities during follow-up. She appreciated the multidisciplinary approach adopted for the diagnosis and management of her condition, and agreed to the publication of this case to improve awareness of this rare disease.
