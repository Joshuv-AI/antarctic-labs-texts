AYLA: Architecting a loss landscape in shallow neural networks to accelerate feature recovery
Behnam Gheshlaghi, Shahin Atakishiyev

Abstract
Feature learning in shallow neural networks exhibits rich yet fragile dynamics, including prolonged plateaus, abrupt phase transitions, and sensitivity to optimization hyperparameters. While recent theoretical work has characterized these behaviors through the geometry of loss landscapes, saddle escape mechanisms, and emergent scaling laws, practical methods for actively shaping these dynamics remain limited. In this paper, we introduce AYLA, a principled loss reparameterization framework that dynamically modulates gradient magnitudes during training without altering the location of stationary points or optimal solutions. AYLA applies a smooth, sigmoid-controlled power-law transformation to empirical loss, yielding a state-dependent effective learning rate that accelerates descent in flat or saddle-dominated regions while stabilizing late-stage optimization. Crucially, AYLA preserves all critical points of the original objective, acting solely as a monotone transformation that reshapes optimization trajectories rather than objectives. We evaluate AYLA in controlled teacher–student settings using two-layer tanh networks trained on synthetic Gaussian data. Across stochastic gradient descent and multiple loss-exponent schedules, AYLA consistently improves feature recovery. This evidence is observed in terms of weight alignment, per-neuron cosine similarity, hidden-activation correlation, and spectral properties of learned representations, while AYLA maintains competitive or faster loss convergence. Spectral analyses further demonstrate that AYLA mitigates rank collapse and promotes richer internal representations, signaling a transition from lazy to active feature-learning regimes. These results position AYLA as a lightweight, theoretically grounded mechanism for bridging analytical insights on shallow network dynamics with practical optimization improvements, particularly in resource-constrained or noise-sensitive training regimes.



1
Introduction

Shallow neural networks, characterized by architectures with one or two hidden layers, have long served as canonical models for elucidating the fundamental mechanisms underlying gradient-based learning
(
Malach and Shalev-Shwartz,, 2019
;
Lei et al.,, 2022
;
Bietti et al.,, 2022
)
. Despite their apparent simplicity, these networks exhibit rich and often fragile training dynamics, including extended plateaus, abrupt phase transitions, and nontrivial feature adaptation processes that mirror those observed in deeper architectures. Theoretical investigations have revealed that the optimization landscapes of shallow networks are frequently populated by saddle points and spurious valleys, where gradient descent may stagnate before eventually progressing toward global or near-global minima
(
Soltanolkotabi,, 2017
;
Venturi et al.,, 2019
;
Sagun et al.,, 2020
)
. In ReLU-like networks, directional stationary points lacking so-called
escape neurons
, defined through first-order conditions that enable loss-decreasing trajectories, constitute genuine local minima, whereas the presence of such neurons guarantees the possibility of saddle escape in scalar-output settings. This characterization refines the understanding of saddle-to-saddle training regimes commonly induced by vanishing initialization, which suppresses certain escape directions and yields a more comprehensive depiction of optimization dynamics in non-differentiable landscapes
(
Chatterjee,, 2022
)
.

Beyond landscape geometry, recent work emphasizes emergent scaling laws and phase transitions intrinsic to stochastic gradient descent (SGD). In wide-network regimes with power-law teacher coefficients, the recovery of individual neurons occurs through sharp transitions. However, aggregating many such trajectories produces a smooth power-law decay of mean-squared error (MSE) as a function of sample size, optimization steps, and model parameters
(
Ren et al.,, 2025
)
. Closely related staircase-like loss curves arise from alternating phases of neuron alignment and rapid norm growth, formalized via Alternating Gradient Flows (AGF), which describe feature acquisition sequences across linear, quadratic, and attention-based architectures
(
Kunin,, 2025
;
Kunin et al.,, 2025
)
. Large-batch gradient descent further clarifies neuron specialization phenomena: batch sizes of order
\mathcal{O}(d)
suffice to align with a single direction, while
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
or even
\mathcal{O}(d^{\ell})
(where
\ell
denotes the leap index) are required to recover multiple or “hard” directions
(
Dandi et al.,, 2024
)
. These insights extend to normalization effects, where asymptotic expansions demonstrate the absence of a leading-order bias–variance tradeoff under mean-field scaling, with variance diminishing as normalization approaches the mean-field limit
(
Yu and Spiliopoulos,, 2022
)
.

Feature learning beyond the neural tangent kernel (NTK) or random-features regime persists throughout training. Local convergence analyses of regularized objectives show that once the loss falls below a critical threshold, gradient descent begins assimilating ground-truth directions, indicating that late-stage adaptation complements early-stage learning
(
Zhou and Ge,, 2024
)
. Dynamical central limit theorems further constrain fluctuations around mean-field limits, tying variance to the
\ell_{2}
norm of the underlying measure and motivating regularization for improved generalization
(
Chen et al.,, 2020
)
. In quadratic activation models, sufficient over-parameterization eliminates spurious minima with high probability, enabling convergence to interpolating solutions with bounded generalization error
(
Sagun et al.,, 2020
)
.

Nevertheless, learning high-frequency components remains challenging: poor conditioning of representations and learning dynamics leads to irreducible error floors, elevated computational cost, and sensitivity to perturbations
(
Zhang et al.,, 2025
)
. Nonlinear activations exacerbate these difficulties. Leaky ReLU landscapes exhibit symmetry-breaking bifurcations as the leak parameter varies, inducing multimodal degeneracies at critical points independent of network width
(
Li et al.,, 2023
)
. While spurious valleys vanish for finite-dimensional activations under sufficient over-parameterization, they persist for infinite-dimensional activations under certain data distributions
(
Venturi et al.,, 2019
)
. Sequential principal component acquisition in nonlinear autoencoders further underscores the importance of biases and tied weights
(
Refinetti and Goldt,, 2022
)
.

Despite these advances, a notable gap remains: existing theories largely explain
why
shallow networks learn features, but offer limited guidance on
how
to actively enhance this process in practice. Adaptive optimizers such as ADAM mitigate some optimization pathologies but do not directly target the staircase dynamics or sharp transitions predicted by theory
(
Siegel and Xu,, 2020
)
. Loss scaling techniques, commonly used in mixed-precision training, are typically static and seldom examined from a dynamical perspective
(
Zhao et al.,, 2019
)
. Moreover, while theory highlights the coupling between task structure, batch size, and leap indices
(
Dandi et al.,, 2024
)
, few methods explicitly modulate effective learning rates to emulate these dependencies, often resulting in suboptimal feature recovery in resource-constrained regimes. To address this gap, we propose
AYLA
(
A
rchitecting
Y
our
L
oss Landscaping
A
pproach), a dynamic loss reparameterization scheme that scales the empirical MSE through a sigmoid-interpolated power-law exponent. This mechanism amplifies gradients during low-loss descent phases while damping them during plateaus, thereby reproducing theoretically predicted staircase dynamics and facilitating saddle escape without explicit noise injection. By adaptively modulating the effective learning rate, AYLA promotes neuron specialization analogous to large-batch training
(
Dandi et al.,, 2024
)
, enhances alignment in teacher–student settings, and mitigates high-frequency learning barriers
(
Zhang et al.,, 2025
)
. Empirical evaluations on Gaussian-input, tanh-activated networks demonstrate improved feature recovery, measured via weight alignment, per-neuron cosine similarity, and hidden activation correlations, across SGD optimizer, while preserving convergence efficiency. AYLA thus bridges theoretical insights and actionable training strategies, enabling more robust shallow-network learning under constrained computational budgets.

Overall, the main contributions of the paper are summarized as follows:

•

We propose AYLA, a new loss parametrization scheme that dynamically modulates gradient magnitudes during training without affecting the location of stationary points, and we establish AYLA’s theoretical foundations.

•

We show that AYLA significantly outperforms SGD, converging faster across experiments on two-layer neural networks with synthetic Gaussian data.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work that forms the foundation for our proposal. The theoretical background behind AYLA is presented in Section 3. Sections 4, 5, and 6 present the problem setup, first- and second-order analysis, and adaptive exponent and stochastic dynamics, respectively. The optimization advantages of AYLA are outlined in Section 7. Finally, Section 8 presents our experimental findings along with discussion, limitations, and future directions, and Section 9 concludes the paper.

2
Related Work

Theoretical studies of shallow neural networks have primarily focused on optimization landscapes, training dynamics, and feature learning mechanisms, forming the conceptual foundation for the AYLA framework. This section briefly reviews these foundations.

Optimization Landscapes and Saddle Points.

The non-convex nature of shallow-network objectives has motivated extensive landscape analysis.
(
Venturi et al.,, 2019
)
characterize spurious valleys in one-hidden-layer networks and introduce intrinsic dimension as a criterion for their elimination under sufficient over-parameterization.
(
Soltanolkotabi,, 2017
)
and
(
Oymak and Soltanolkotabi,, 2020
)
establish global convergence guarantees for ReLU networks and demonstrate favorable geometry under moderate over-parameterization. As a follow-up,
(
Chatterjee,, 2022
)
relaxes center-stable manifold assumptions to show that gradient descent avoids strict saddles in ReLU and leaky ReLU networks under mild initialization conditions. These works clarify
where
optimization may stall, but offer limited prescriptions for actively accelerating escape, an issue AYLA directly addresses through adaptive loss scaling.

Training Dynamics and Scaling Laws.

Recent efforts have uncovered sharp phase transitions and scaling laws governing SGD dynamics. Ren et al.
(
Ren et al.,, 2025
)
identify abrupt recovery thresholds for individual neurons in wide networks, whose aggregation yields smooth power-law decay of MSE. Sagun et al.
(
Sagun et al.,, 2020
)
show that over-parameterized quadratic networks avoid spurious minima with high probability, while Chen et al.
(
Chen et al.,, 2020
)
establish dynamical central limit theorems that quantify fluctuations around mean-field limits. Yu and Spiliopoulos
(
Yu and Spiliopoulos,, 2022
)
further demonstrate that normalization mitigates variance without inducing leading-order bias–variance tradeoffs. AYLA complements these analyses by offering a practical mechanism to modulate effective learning rates in accordance with emergent scaling behavior.

Feature Learning Mechanisms.

Feature learning beyond the NTK regime has been extensively studied. Dandi et al.
(
Dandi et al.,, 2024
)
quantify batch-size requirements for neuron specialization, while Kunin
(
Kunin,, 2025
)
introduces Alternating Gradient Flows to explain staircase loss profiles and feature acquisition sequences. Zhou and Ge
(
Zhou and Ge,, 2024
)
provide local convergence guarantees showing that gradient descent recovers ground-truth directions after crossing a loss threshold. Refinetti and Goldt
(
Refinetti and Goldt,, 2022
)
analyze sequential PCA-like learning in nonlinear autoencoders, and Zhang et al.
(
Zhang et al.,, 2025
)
highlight persistent difficulties in learning high-frequency components. AYLA sustains feature learning across optimization phases by dynamically amplifying gradients, aligning practical training behavior with these theoretical predictions.

In summary, while prior work delivers deep insight into shallow network dynamics, it rarely translates these findings into adaptive optimization strategies. AYLA fills this gap by operationalizing theoretical principles through a lightweight, loss-based reparameterization that empirically enhances feature recovery and convergence stability.

3
Theoretical Background

In this section, we establish the theoretical foundations of the
AYLA method, drawing on established
results on optimization landscapes, training dynamics, and feature learning
in shallow neural networks. We first review known properties of two-layer
networks, including saddle points, spurious valleys, and phase transitions.
We then derive the AYLA loss transformation, analyze its induced effective
learning rate, and prove key properties such as boundedness and enhanced
saddle escape. All derivations assume a teacher–student setting with
Gaussian inputs and mean-squared error (MSE) loss, consistent with

(
Ren et al.,, 2025
;
Dandi et al.,, 2024
;
Zhou and Ge,, 2024
;
Sagun et al.,, 2020
)
.

3.1
Optimization Landscapes and Saddle Points

Shallow neural networks with one hidden layer exhibit highly non-convex loss
landscapes populated by saddle points and spurious valleys

(
Venturi et al.,, 2019
;
Soltanolkotabi,, 2017
)
. For a two-layer model

f(x;W,a)=a^{\top}\sigma(Wx),

with activation
\sigma
(e.g.,
\tanh
or ReLU-like), the empirical MSE loss is

L=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\big(y_{\mu}-f(x_{\mu})\big)^{2},

where
(x_{\mu},y_{\mu})
are samples generated by a teacher network.

A key structural insight is that stationary points lacking

escape neurons
—neurons satisfying first-order loss-decreasing
conditions—are local minima, while the presence of at least one escape
neuron guarantees non-optimality in scalar-output settings

(
Venturi et al.,, 2019
)
. Under vanishing initialization, this induces
saddle-to-saddle training dynamics that persist until suitable directions
are activated.

For ReLU and leaky ReLU networks, relaxed center-stable manifold theorems
ensure that gradient descent escapes strict saddles under mild initialization
conditions, with explicit thresholds governing global convergence

(
Chatterjee,, 2022
)
. Spurious valleys vanish under sufficient
over-parameterization for finite intrinsic-dimensional activations, but may
persist for infinite-dimensional cases under adversarial data distributions

(
Venturi et al.,, 2019
)
. In quadratic networks, over-parameterization
eliminates spurious minima with high probability, enabling convergence to
interpolating solutions with controlled generalization

(
Oymak and Soltanolkotabi,, 2020
)
. Despite these guarantees, standard GD and SGD often stagnate on extended plateaus, motivating adaptive mechanisms that reshape gradient magnitudes
without altering critical-point structure.

3.2
Training Dynamics and Feature Learning

Beyond static landscape properties, shallow networks exhibit rich training
dynamics marked by scaling laws and abrupt phase transitions. In wide
networks with power-law teacher spectra, individual neurons undergo sharp
signal recovery transitions, while aggregate MSE follows smooth power-law
decay in optimization steps and samples

(
Ren et al.,, 2025
)
.

Large-batch gradient descent induces neuron specialization:
O(d)
samples
suffice to align a single direction, whereas
O(d^{2})
or
O(d^{\ell})
samples
are required for multiple or “hard” directions, where
\ell
denotes the
leap index
(
Dandi et al.,, 2024
)
. Alternating Gradient Flows (AGF) formalize
staircase-like loss trajectories as alternating phases of dormant neuron
alignment and rapid norm growth
(
Kunin,, 2025
)
.

Local convergence analyses show that, once loss falls below a threshold,
gradient descent recovers ground-truth features, implying persistent
late-stage learning beyond initial random-feature regimes

(
Zhou and Ge,, 2024
)
.

Normalization studies reveal that asymptotically,
variance decays toward mean-field limits without a leading-order
bias–variance tradeoff
(
Yu and Spiliopoulos,, 2022
)
, while dynamical central
limit theorems link fluctuations to the
2
-norm of underlying measures

(
Chen et al.,, 2020
)
.

Nevertheless, high-frequency components remain difficult to learn due to
poor conditioning of Gram matrices, leading to error floors and instability

(
Zhang et al.,, 2025
)
. These results collectively explain plateaus and
transitions, but offer limited guidance on
how
to adapt loss geometry
to accelerate progress. Our proposal also addresses this gap.

4
Problem Setup

Let
l:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a non-negative, twice continuously differentiable loss function. We define AYLA as

L(x)=\big(C^{*}\,l(x)+\varepsilon\big)^{\,n(l(x))},

(1)

where:

C^{*}>0
is a loss scaling coefficient that maps the transition region of the loss to the unit interval.

\varepsilon>0
is a small constant ensuring strict positivity.

n:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a smooth, adaptive exponent function.

The design goals are to:

(i)

Preserve all critical points and their local curvature types.

(ii)

Ensure smooth transition between regimes:
n(l)\approx n_{1}<1
for
l\ll 1/C^{*}
and
n(l)\approx n_{2}>1
for
l\gg 1/C^{*}
.

(iii)

Maintain the identity transformation at the pivot point:
n(1/C^{*})=1
.

5
First-Order and Second-Order Analysis

Using logarithmic differentiation, the gradient of the transformed loss is

\gradient L=(C^{*}l+\varepsilon)^{n(l)}\underbrace{\left(n^{\prime}(l)\ln(C^* l + \varepsilon)+\frac{n(l)C^{*}}{C^{*}l+\varepsilon}\right)}_{A(l)}\gradient l.

(2)

Proposition 1

(Critical Point Preservation)
.

For any finite
n(l)
and
n^{\prime}(l)
, if
x^{*}
satisfies
\gradient l(x^{*})=0
, then
\gradient L(x^{*})=0
.

Proof.

Direct substitution into Eq. (
2
) shows that
\gradient L
vanishes whenever
\gradient l=0
.
∎

The Hessian of the transformed loss is

\Hess L=(C^{*}l+\varepsilon)^{n(l)}\Big[A(l)\Hess l+\big(A^{\prime}(l)+A(l)^{2}\big)\gradient l\gradient l^{\top}\Big].

(3)

Theorem 1

(Curvature Preservation)
.

At a critical point
x^{*}
, the Hessian scales as
\Hess L(x^{*})=\gamma\Hess l(x^{*})
. If
A(l)>0
for all
l>0
, the signature of the Hessian is preserved, ensuring that minima, maxima, and saddles of
l
retain their nature in
L
.

6
Adaptive Exponent and Stochastic Dynamics

To satisfy the regime transition requirements, we define the exponent function using a sigmoid transformation over the log-scaled loss space:

n(l)=n_{1}+(n_{2}-n_{1})\sigma\Big(k\big[\ln(C^* l + \varepsilon)-\ln\tau\big]\Big),

(4)

where
\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
is the logistic function. This ensures that
n(l)
transitions smoothly from a compressive regime (
n_{1}<1
) to an expansive regime (
n_{2}>1
) around the threshold
\tau/C^{*}
.

The primary implication of this adaptive exponent is the modulation of the optimization trajectory. A gradient descent update
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta\gradient L
is mathematically equivalent to standard SGD performed on the base loss
l
with a state-dependent effective learning rate:

\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}(l)=\eta\cdot\underbrace{(C^{*}l+\varepsilon)^{n(l)}A(l)}_{\text{Gain Factor }\Gamma(l)},

(5)

where
A(l)
is the scalar gain factor. This shows that AYLA acts as an automated curriculum manager: it amplifies updates when the model is far from the manifold (high
l
) and dampens them as it approaches convergence (low
l
).

In all experiments, we use vanilla SGD as our baseline and compare AYLA to it.

8.5
Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work

Across all configurations, empirical results show that AYLA consistently outperforms SGD by promoting
active feature learning, accelerating convergence, stabilizing alignment, and
maintaining rich internal representations.
Variants such as
(1,1.3)
and
(0.7,1.2)
achieve the best balance between
early acceleration and late-stage refinement, particularly in high-complexity
and high-learning-rate regimes where SGD struggles.
AYLA thus offers a lightweight alternative to adaptive optimizers such as Adam
(
Kingma and Ba,, 2015
)
and LAMB
(
You et al.,, 2020
)
, without introducing additional state variables.

In this work, we adopt vanilla SGD as our sole baseline and compare our results only against it; this is a current limitation of the paper’s scope. Consequently, we plan to evaluate AYLA on real-world datasets and deeper architectures, compare it to other optimizers such as RMSProp
(
Tieleman and Hinton,, 2012
)
, and extend it to the Transformer architecture
(
Vaswani et al.,, 2017
)
. Moreover, further theoretical analysis of convergence rates, implicit
bias, and generalization properties under AYLA may also yield deeper insight into the relationship between loss geometry and feature learning. We leave these directions for future exploration.

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced AYLA, a lightweight and
principled loss reparameterization framework that reshapes optimization
dynamics without modifying the location of stationary points or introducing
additional optimizer state. By applying a smooth, state-dependent power-law
transformation to the empirical loss, AYLA induces an implicit, adaptive
effective learning rate that accelerates optimization in high-loss or
flat-curvature regimes while stabilizing late-stage training. Through controlled teacher–student experiments, we demonstrated that AYLA
consistently outperforms vanilla SGD across a broad
range of learning rates, model widths, output dimensions, and loss-scaling
factors. Empirically, AYLA accelerates convergence, improves alignment with
teacher features, increases exploratory weight movement, and preserves richer
hidden-layer spectra. These effects jointly signal a transition from lazy,
kernel-like dynamics toward active feature-learning regimes, aligning with
recent theoretical analyses of overparameterized neural networks. Importantly, AYLA achieves these benefits without altering the underlying
objective geometry: all critical points of the original loss are preserved,
and the method acts purely by reshaping optimization trajectories. This
distinguishes AYLA from adaptive optimizers that rely on momentum buffers or
second-moment estimates, positioning it as a minimal and interpretable
mechanism for controlling learning dynamics. Overall,
AYLA provides a simple yet effective bridge between theoretical understanding
of neural optimization dynamics and practical algorithmic design.
