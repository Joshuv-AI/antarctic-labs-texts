SLAyiNG: Towards Queer Language Processing
Leonor Veloso, Lea Hirlimann, Philipp WickeHinrich SchützeCenter for Information and Language Processing, LMU MunichMunich Center for Machine Learning (MCML) {lveloso,hirlimann,pwicke}@cis.uni-muenchen.de

Abstract
Knowledge of slang is a desirable feature of LLMs in the context of user interaction, as slang often reflects an individual’s social identity. Several works on informal language processing have defined and curated benchmarks for tasks such as detection and identification of slang. In this paper, we focus on queer slang. Queer slang can be mistakenly flagged as hate speech or can evoke negative responses from LLMs during user interaction. Research efforts so far have not focused explicitly on queer slang. In particular, detection and processing of queer slang have not been thoroughly evaluated due to the lack of a high-quality annotated benchmark. To address this gap, we curate SLAyiNG, the first dataset containing annotated queer slang derived from subtitles, social media posts, and podcasts, reflecting real-world usage. We describe our data curation process, including the collection of slang terms and definitions, scraping sources for examples that reflect usage of these terms, and our ongoing annotation process. As preliminary results, we calculate inter-annotator agreement for human annotators and OpenAI’s model o3-mini , evaluating performance on the task of sense disambiguation. Reaching an average Krippendorff’s alpha of 0.746, we argue that state-of-the-art reasoning models can serve as tools for pre-filtering, but the complex and often sensitive nature of queer language data requires expert and community-driven annotation efforts.

Introduction

Language is an
act of identity
, a tool for self-identification and recognition within one’s community
(
Foucault 1976
;
Cameron and Kulick 2006
)
. This aspect of language is especially meaningful for queer individuals, for whom queer slang (and its analogues) can be a form of protest and social cohesion, evolving along cultural and socio-political changes
(
Rodgers 1972
;
Kaabour 2025
)
.

Regardless of extensive research efforts in the fields of queer linguistics
(
Kulick 2000
)
and information activism targeted at archiving the collective language of the queer community
(
Cifor and Rawson 2023
)
, queer slang is not adequately represented in pretraining data for large language models (LLMs)
(
Tint 2025
)
. This has worrying effects on bias mitigation, since queer slang terms can be wrongly flagged as hate-speech
(
Dorn et al. 2024
)
and contribute to cisheteronormativity in language systems, data, and research
(
Vásquez et al. 2022
)
. The contributions of this in-progress work are twofold:

(i) We describe the ongoing curation of SLAyiNG, the first annotated dataset explicitly containing queer slang, derived from a manually curated list of terms and definitions. SLAyiNG contains usage examples of 695 terms (and respective variants), serving as a snapshot of the real-world usage of English queer slang;

(ii) As a preliminary experiment, we evaluate the
state-of-the-art reasoning model
o3-mini
on the
task of sense disambiguation, by calculating inter-annotator
agreement with 3 human annotators. We argue that reasoning
models can serve as annotation tools in a pre-filtering
stage, but a low agreement of
42%
with human annotators on our slang terms make expert and community-driven annotation necessary.

Related Work

2.1
Slang in Natural Language Processing

Inclusion of slang terms in the NLP pipeline has been shown to be beneficial for tasks such as sentiment analysis
(
Wu et al. 2018
)
and sarcasm detection
(
Wilson et al. 2020
)
. Knowledge of slang can also aid LLMs in navigating user interaction. For this purpose,
Mei et al. 2024
design a benchmark for measuring LLMs’ comprehension of newly-emerging idiomatic expressions and slang.
Sun et al. 2024
provide the first comprehensive probing analysis of LLMs on slang knowledge, establishing a benchmark for informal language processing.

2.2
Queer Language Processing

Recent years have seen an increase in NLP research efforts related to queer slang and queer language representation.

From a digital archival standpoint, resources such as the Homosaurus
(
Cifor and Rawson 2023
)
, the Gender, Sex, and Sexual Orientation ontology
(
Kronk and Dexheimer 2020
)
, and lgbtDB
1
1

1

https://lgbtdb.wikibase.cloud/wiki/Main_Page
work as a reliable source for queer language (and other queer-related resources). Regardless of these archival efforts, queer-related terms often do not reach crucial corpora, notably those curated to aid hate-speech detection and content moderation.
Ramesh et al. 2022
find that English lexicons aimed at content moderation do not make any distinction between pejorative and non-pejorative queer-related words. Furthermore, reclaimed slur usage in an ingroup context is often flagged as hate-speech
(
Dorn et al. 2024
)
. This is especially concerning if we take in consideration that many queer slang terms can be used derogatorily or have historically been considered slurs, as is the case with the term “queer” itself
(
Brontsema 2004
)
.

The intersection of queer linguistics and NLP also gains relevance within HCI and user interaction with LLMs. For instance,
Tint 2025
has shown that prompts containing queer slang elicit more negative responses from LLMs, relative to heteronormative prompts. In parallel,
Basoah et al. 2025
find that queer participants feel more social presence from an LLM that uses queer slang, and highlight the need to design LLMs that respect cultural and linguistic contexts.

3
On Queer Linguistics, Terminology, and NLP

The broad usage of the term “queer language” within the
field of queer linguistics can be traced back to

Kulick 2000
, where it is used in lieu of
“lingo”, “slang”, “argot”, or “sociolect” pertaining
to gay, lesbian, transgender, or any other LGBTQ+ community.

The latter half of the 20th century saw a rise in research
on gay and lesbian languages. Much of this research has
historically consisted of glossaries of ingroup terms and
discussions of possible etymologies of queer words, and it
has been criticized for overly relying on sexual identity
labels as a foundation for sociolinguistic analysis

Kulick 2000
. The rise of queer linguistics in the
1990s and 2000s was accompanied by a growing interest in
gender and sexual non-normativity, i.e., perspectives on
queer language as the discursive materialization of all
sexual identities, a tool for identity construction outside
of cisheteronormativity

(
Motschenbacher 2011
)
. However, these
foundational works in queer linguistics have also received
criticism from a transgender linguistics perspective, for
misconstruction of trans identities and non-normatively
gendered communities
(
Zimman 2018
)
. Informed
by collective experiences, specific groups within the queer
umbrella possess different languages. Notably,

Zimman 2018
highlights trans discourse as
being especially concerned with authenticity and with institutional powers as tools for oppressing trans individuals.

Acknowledging these nuances within the fields of queer (and trans) linguistics, as well as the difficulty in defining “slang”, we refer to our dataset as a dataset of
queer slang
. This decision is not without its caveats. First, we must acknowledge that many of the terms we have collected have their origins in African American English (AAE), often with roots in African-American gay/trans communities and ballroom, and there is a wider conversation around appropriation of these terms and erasure of the linguistic history of these communities
(
Crowley 2025
)
. Secondly, we recognize that many of these terms are derived from very specific sociolects, without much traction outside of specific queer subcommunities (see, for example, transgender slang originating on 4chan
(
Kotajärvi 1998
)
). Thirdly, these terms have also been categorized under queer argot, lingo, or sociolects. The term “slang” itself is not statically defined within linguistics, due to its nature as a “deviant” repertoire of phrases and words
(
Agha 2015
)
. In order to align ourselves with current NLP research, we categorize words, phrases, or metaphors predominantly used by queer individuals as “slang”
(
Basoah et al. 2025
;
Tint 2025
)
.

Sources

4.1.2
Cleaning and Curation

Very common terms (see “bear”, for example) are present and defined by multiple of our sources. This entails that, after scraping, we may have redundant definitions. Nonetheless, there are terms with multiple, distinct, valid definitions. A notable example is “angel”, defined in lgbtDB as both “
A gay man
” and as “
A vogue performer who is perceived as feminine or femme and who is perceived as having a daintier dance style or execution.
”, in the context of ballroom. Our term definitions should be as descriptive and concise as possible, while retaining the valid, distinct ones.

We embed all definitions using the
all-mpnet-base-v2

5
5

5

https://www.sbert.net/docs/sentence_transformer/pretrained_models.html

0.7

Section

A.4

4.2
Raw Dataset Curation

Given the list of queer slang terms described in
Section

4.1

It is comprised of examples derived from three sources: subtitles, social media posts, and podcasts, in an attempt to reflect real-world usage across contexts. In this section, we describe the process for gathering examples, the required annotation tasks, and a proposal for our future pre-filtering and annotation pipeline.

4.2.2
Annotation Tasks

Our complete raw dataset contains 197,958 entries. Filtering and annotation are required for 3 tasks:

(i) Sense disambiguation:
Several terms of the slang term list have common, not-queer related senses (see “mother”, or “read”). This entails that the biggest and most consuming annotation task is sense disambiguation, since the majority of the examples from our raw dataset are false positives. As such, during the pre-filtering stages of the data curation and annotation pipeline, we only annotate for this task. Some terms have several distinct definitions in the context of queer slang. For those cases, we consider all valid definitions during annotation.

(ii) Detection of harmful content:
Approximately 15% of our terms are described in their original sources as slurs, derogatory, or potentially offensive. This makes it likely that an example found in our raw data is harmful. These terms appear in our cited slang sources due to processes of reclamation
(
Popa-Wyatt 2020
)
and/or ingroup usage. Notably, some of these terms have appeared and been considered “queer slang” in other NLP works
(
Tint 2025
)
, such as “fag” and “homo”. The inclusion of these repurposed terms in data is important in the context of hate-speech detection, since classifiers that falsely identify harm run the risk of suppressing speech
(
Dorn et al. 2024
;
Anderson and Lepore 2013
)
. We recognize that whether a particular use of a term is considered offensive or not depends on the term’s target group, and even then, it is unclear how many members of a group must declare its offensiveness for the usage to be considered harmful. Details regarding the management of harmful and offensive terms and content can be found in
Section

A.3
.

(iii) Author ingroup membership:
We base our definition of
ingroup
and
outgroup
, as well as the setup for the annotation for this task, on the work of
Dorn et al. 2024
. In a sentence with an identity term or slur, we say the ingroup is the population referenced by the identity term or slur’s neutral correlate (e.g., the neutral correlate for “dyke” is “lesbian”). The outgroup is the population not referenced by the identity term or neutral correlate. Author ingroup membership has an impact on the intended and perceived harm of a sentence.
Dorn et al. 2024
find that ingroup usages of slurs are much less likely to be considered harmful. As such, this annotation task serves as additional context for filtering and annotating potentially harmful sentences.

4.2.3
Pre-filtering & Annotation Pipeline

The size of our raw dataset makes it infeasible to perform
crowdsourced annotation over all examples, especially when
taking into consideration that the vast majority of our
examples are false positives for sense
disambiguation. Taking this into account, we propose the
following pre-filtering and annotation pipeline, which we
plan to implement in future work.

1.

Three author annotators (consisting of one post-doctoral researcher and two graduate students of computational linguistics, henceforth referred to as AAs) annotate a small sample of 25 examples for the task of sense disambiguation, to calculate a first human inter-annotator agreement.

2.

If this agreement is high (
>0.6
), each AA
annotates up to 15 examples per slang term (which
corresponds to approximately 2200 examples per AA). We use OpenAI’s
o3-mini
as an LLM annotator (LLMA), and calculate agreement between the AAs and the LLMA.
Section

5
details preliminary results for this step.

3.

For each term, if the agreement is high,
we annotate all examples with
o3-mini
. If the agreement is low, we proceed to use the more capable
o3
model to re-filter the examples and repeat the annotation process. This step yields a complete pre-filtered version of SLAyiNG.

4.

After pre-filtering,
we follow an iterative approach where a principal annotator (PA) annotates the majority of the pre-filtered examples, while making periodic checks for agreement
(
Wicke and Bolognesi 2020
;
Wicke and Bolognesi 2025
)
. This is the first step of the pipeline where annotation is done for the three tasks previously described. According to
(
Klie et al. 2024
)
, to ensure the quality of the annotation process, we make sure that at least 500 instances are annotated by all 3 AAs, and that each periodic check results in improved annotation guidelines. The pre-filtered data is split into batches, calculated according to the size of the final raw dataset. Initially, 3 authors annotate a small batch of examples. Inter-annotator agreement is calculated, disagreements are discussed, and guidelines are adjusted. When agreement is high, the PA annotates a large batch of examples. The 3 AAs reunite to annotate another batch, and do another round of agreement calculation and disagreement discussion. This iterative process continues until the entire pre-filtered dataset has been annotated, yielding a completely manually annotated version of SLAyiNG for our three tasks: sense disambiguation, detection of harmful content, and author ingroup membership. This step optimizes the trade-off between annotation quality and computational efficiency, leveraging model-based filtering to improve the manual annotation pipeline without entirely removing human oversight: no LLM output is left unchecked.

5.

For validating the quality of the data and
involving the broad queer community,
we recruit annotators from the queer community to annotate
a sample of the
AA/LLMA-annotated version of SLAyiNG.
Annotators are recruited
through a questionnaire consisting of
demographic-related questions, a screener for
familiarity with English queer slang, and a small
batch of examples to annotate for the three
tasks. Interested annotators (ideally across a wide
range of identities and backgrounds) are found through
social media and organizations dedicated to queer
linguistics, history, and archival efforts. The proposed
annotation sheet can be found in
Section

B.1
.

Ideally, this process yields a clean dataset of 2000 examples or more, for easier comparison with other slang-based datasets
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
.

Conclusion & Future Work

In this paper, we describe the ongoing curation of SLAyiNG, the first manually annotated dataset explicitly containing queer slang. We describe the process for curating our noisy, raw version of SLAyiNG, and propose an annotation pipeline for a comprehensive and fair dataset that reflects real-world usage. This pipeline is complicated by the ever-changing, often ambiguous, and potentially harmful nature of queer slang.

As preliminary results of the first stages of our curation
and annotation pipeline, we present inter-annotator
agreement results between human annotators and the
state-of-the-art reasoning model
o3-mini
, for the
sense disambiguation task. Achieving an average
Krippendorff’s alpha of
0.7461, we argue that LLMs can be valuable tools for pre-filtering data. However, 42.52% of slang terms achieve a low agreement score, highlighting the need for expert and community-driven annotation.

Ethical Considerations

Harmful and/or Irresponsible Usage.

We recognize that SLAyiNG can be misused to reinforce bias and stereotypes against queer people. To mitigate this possibility, we intend on making the version of SLAyiNG complete with potentially offensive terms available only by contacting the authors.

Subjectivity.

Labeling for harmful content is inherently subjective due to the nature of the task. Ideally, every entry of SLAyiNG would be manually annotated by a representative and wide group of annotators. While the success of this ideal is highly dependent on the size of the final dataset, we aim for every potentially offensive example to be crowdsourced annotated.

Availability of Queer Vocabularies and Resources.

Projects such as SLAyiNG rely on digital archival for queer vocabularies. However, while online content is disapeearing at an alarming rate
(
Chapekis et al. 2024
)
, recent years have seen a rise in disinformation, misinformation, and politically-motivated attacks against individuals, communities, and the availability of queer resources
(
Ellis 2023
;
Hammer 2024
;
Mehra and Gray 2014
)
. While works towards inclusive NLP resources and fair models contribute towards a richer and less cisheteronormative research field, the impact of archival in the preservation of queer history cannot be overstated
(
Rawson 2010
)
.

Limitations

The main limitation of this work (at its current stage and at its completion) stems from its sole focus on the English language. Queer language and slang are varied across languages and cultures. Furthermore, queer slang in predominantly English-speaking countries, such as the USA, can and is influenced by other languages
(
Morgan 2017
)
.

A.2
Sources: Details

Due to the very extensive nature of GSSO, we only scrape specifically queer-related slang categories. For this purpose, we collect all classes containing the word “slang” in their title, excluding the following due to redundancy ot not being explicitily related to sexual/romantic/gender orientations: slang, polyamory slang, slang dictionary, sexual slang, drug slang, hotel slang, pornography slang, barebacking slang, internet slang, cannabis slang, BDSM slang, sex work slang, and gendered slang.

A.3
Slurs and Offensive Terms

Several of our terms can be considered slurs, derogatory, or offensive. The offensiveness of term is dependent on its contextual usage, and it’s often subjective. We preemptively consider as offensive:

•

All terms that derive from the “English 4chan /lgbt/ slang” category of Wiktionary;

•

Terms that are described as “slurs”, “derogatory”, “pejorative”, or “offensive“ in any of its raw defintions (before the definition cleaning stage).

•

Terms that are present in the Chew Glossary lists of slurs, but are present in our other sources as “slang”.

Out of our 695 terms, we obtain 108 offensive terms (15.54% of our slang list). Of course, other terms not flagged as offensive can be used in a harmful sentence. They are tagged as such during the manual annotation process. To ensure a high level of community approval, we propose that any sentence that includes a potentially offensive term, or that is tagged as harmful by a majority of our expert annotators, is automatically considered for crowdsourced annotation.

A.4
Definition Cleaning: Details

We test values for minimum cosine similarity
min=\{0.60,0,65,0.70,0,75\}
. We settled on
0.7
as the optimal value (
Table

2
), since it offers a balance between effective filtering and retaining distinct senses.

Having this in mind, we design and clean our slang term list according to the following:

(i) One entry of our slang term list contains a
term
, optional one or more
variants
, and one or more
definitions
.
Variants
are a catch-all field for synonyms or terms with the same stem. We do not manually adjust our sources in that regard: for Chew, we add other terms with the same definition as variants; for GSSO, we extract the
SYNONYM
property terms as variants (which can include terms with the same stem, or terms with the same definition). Additionally, terms with defintions consisting only of “
Synonym of X
” were re-categorized as variants of X. Note that, duritng the filtering process for the creation of SLAyiNG, there is no difference between
terms
and
variants
. We organize them as such in our slang term list for preservation of the relation between the terms.

(ii) We remove extra notes related to archival (such as “
Widely used, but should be enclosed in quotation marks and contextualized if needed in archival description.
”, from the Chew Glossary) and references to other terms “
see also: …
” while preserving the remainder of the definition.

(iii) Definitions can be highly redundant (“
To behave, speak, act, etc. in a flamboyant, affected, ostentatious, or exaggerated manner stereotypically associated with gay men.
” and “
A gay man who is considered to be ostentatious, over the top, theatrical, or flamboyant.
”, for example). We create a sentence embedding of each definition and remove definitions that have a cosine similarity
>0.7
, keeping the longest of the pair.

(iv) We remove completely circular definitions, or definitions that solely define that term by mentioning another term belonging to the slang list (for example, the definition “
A boydyke.
” for the term “lesboy” is removed).

(v) We adapt definitions that partially define a term by refering to another term pertaning to the slang list. For example, “
Being or resembling a twunk (“muscular twink”).
” becomes “
Being or resembling a twunk (“muscular twink”, "twink" meaning "young, attractive, slim male").
”. We acknowledge that this is only possible to an extent, since very well-known terms (such as “lesbian”) are widely used in definitions for other terms. As we recognize the limitations of our methodology for cleaning definitions, we release a version of our slang list containing unfiltered/raw definitions. The clean version is used for annotation.
