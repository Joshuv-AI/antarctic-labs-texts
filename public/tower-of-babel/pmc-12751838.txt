Comparing disparities in geographic proximity to exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation before and after decentralisation of services: a repeated cross-sectional study using individual-level register data
Authors: Kristine Bihrmann, Ann-Dorthe Zwisler, Hanne Søndergaard, Marie Louise Overgaard Svendsen, Annette Kjær Ersbøll
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite well-known benefits, cardiac rehabilitation (CR) programs suffer from non-participation, and travel distance is repeatedly reported as a participation barrier. Based on individual-level data, the objective of this study was to evaluate geographic proximity to exercise-based CR and assess how geographic and sociodemographic disparities were affected by decentralisation of services from hospitals to primary care.MethodsBased on nationwide individual-level Danish data, travel distance, calculated as the shortest route along the road network, was used to assess geographic proximity to exercise-based CR. Travel distance was mapped using Inverse Distance Weighting to produce a surface map. Boxplots were used to illustrate the distribution of travel distance within the study population, and sociodemographic disparities in median travel distance were evaluated by assessment of the concentration index.ResultsThe study included 2,460,639 addresses and 3,693,053 residents (≥ 30 years old). Overall, the median travel distance to exercise-based CR was reduced with ≥ 40% after decentralisation. Non-central and rural parts of the country benefitted most. Inequality in travel distance associated with older age and multimorbidity was eliminated, while inequality associated with low educational level was significantly reduced.ConclusionsBased on unique national data, this study is the first to demonstrate how decentralisation increased geographic proximity to exercise-based CR. Remote and rural areas, and certain vulnerable groups benefitted particularly, leading to reduced inequality in geographic proximity after decentralisation. With lack of geographic proximity being a known barrier to participation in CR programs, the study supports decentralisation of services as an instrument to be considered in an ongoing strive to improve CR participation, overall and within vulnerable groups. Moreover, the results may serve as a general illustration of what can be achieved by decentralisation, regardless of the specific service.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02704-y.

Background

Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a multidisciplinary, evidence-based intervention recommended as secondary prevention therapy for persons with cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. Following hospitalization, CR involves pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions with the latter including exercise training, patient education, psychosocial management, and support for changing health behaviours. The benefits from CR include reduced mortality and hospitalization, and improvements in health-related quality of life [3]. Despite the benefits, CR programs suffer from non-participation and drop-out [4, 5].

Non-participation has been associated with personal factors as older age, female sex, comorbidity, living alone, and low socioeconomic position, but also with factors related to organization of CR programs and healthcare systems [6–8]. Logistical factors as travel distance and lack of transportation are repeatedly reported as barriers to participation [6–10]. A Swedish study found distance to CR to be the strongest predictor of non-participation [11]. Moreover, geographic proximity to CR has been associated with sociodemographic characteristics [12, 13], potentially contributing to sociodemographic disparities in CR participation.

The low participation in CR despite its known benefits calls for implementation of effective steps to “open pathways that lead all eligible patients toward the best possible cardiac health” as recently concluded by Randal J. Thomas in New England Journal of Medicine [14]. One such step could be to implement exercise-based CR in primary care settings to increase geographic proximity as suggested by Resurrección et al. [7]. However, it remains to be evaluated how such a decentralisation of services actually affects geographic proximity, including its effect on sociodemographic disparities.

Geographic proximity is a key element of spatial accessibility which constitutes one of five dimensions of access to health care. The other dimensions are availability, accommodation, affordability and acceptability [15]. Spatial accessibility of healthcare services has been measured using a number of different approaches, including proximity (distance and travel time to the nearest service), kernel density estimation, gravity models, and the two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method with enhancements [16]. The 2SFCA approach combines supply and demand for health care with geographical elements like proximity and catchment areas, and has been used in numerous studies, mostly based on area-level data [17, 18]. However, in a setting where each individual is administratively assigned to a specific healthcare facility based on catchment areas, geographic proximity may pose a more adequate measure to assess individual-level spatial barriers.

Based on individual-level data, the objective of this study was to evaluate geographic proximity to exercise-based CR and assess how geographic and sociodemographic disparities were affected by decentralisation of services from hospitals to primary care.

Methods

Setting

In Denmark, exercise-based CR has been part of a strategical decentralisation of healthcare services from hospitals to primary care that aims to reduce barriers to attendance. Following a recommendation from the Danish Health Authority in 2018 [19], exercise-based CR, subject to international guidelines [1], are now located within primary care.

Denmark covers an area of approximately 43,000 km2 and offers tax-paid universal healthcare with services available free of charge to all six million residents. The country is divided into five administrative regions responsible for hospital services, and 98 municipalities responsible for certain primary care services, including exercise-based CR [20]. In the case of primary care-based CR, catchment areas follow municipality borders [21].

The Danish municipalities are classified according to degree of urbanisation (rural area, town and suburb, and city) using the definition by EuroStat [22]. For this study, information on degree of urbanisation was obtained from Statistics Denmark.

Exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation

Geographic location of exercise-based CR before decentralisation was assessed in 2013, whereas geographic location of exercise-based CR after decentralisation was assessed in 2023. The two specific years were chosen due to data availability at the time of data collection. In between the two years, only the location of exercise-based CR changed; the specific service was delivered according to guidelines that did not change during the time period from 2013 to 2023 [23].

Information on hospitals providing exercise-based CR in 2013, i.e., before decentralisation, was obtained from an assessment performed by the Danish Cardiac Rehabilitation Database [24, 25]. The geographical address of each hospital was obtained from the hospital webpage. The main address was used.

Information on exercise-based CR in 2023, i.e., after decentralisation, was obtained from the official portal for the public Danish Healthcare Services [26]. The portal provides information on healthcare services to both citizens and healthcare professionals, and we manually extracted information on the geographical address of CR facilities within each of the 98 municipalities. If no address was listed, we consulted the municipality webpage. In case of multiple addresses within a municipality, locations providing exercise training were included.

Addresses were geocoded using the Danish Address Register [27] which holds information on all Danish addresses, including GPS coordinates.

Geographic proximity

Geographic proximity was assessed as the travel distance to CR facilities. It was calculated as the shortest route along the road network. The assessment was based on all Danish addresses in 2018 and the location of CR facilities before and after decentralisation, respectively. The year 2018 was the year decentralisation was first recommended by the Danish Health Authority [19]. Thus, travel distance was based on one set of origin locations and two sets of destination locations to assess travel distance before and after decentralisation. The change in travel distance was also assessed at each origin address.

Travel distance to exercise-based CR before decentralisation was calculated as the distance to the nearest CR facility. Travel distance to exercise-based CR after decentralisation was calculated as the distance to the nearest CR facility within the municipality. The latter was done since patients must attend primary care-based CR within their residential municipality [21]. In some cases, however, municipalities had an agreement on joint CR services, and this was accounted for in the calculation of distance.

Addresses were obtained from the Danish Address Register (September 18, 2018) [27], and the road network was obtained from the Danish Agency for Data Supply and Efficiency (June 30, 2020) [28].

Travel distance was calculated using R [29] v.4.3.1 and the package cppRouting [30].

Study population

All Danish residents ≥ 30 years old on December 31, 2018, were included in the study population with information on their residential address. Only residents ≥ 30 years old were included to ensure that final educational level, as a measure of socioeconomic position, is achieved. Moreover, CR is rarely relevant to individuals of younger age.

The study population was obtained from the Danish Civil Registration System [31] that includes a unique personal identification number given to all Danish residents at birth or immigration. The personal identification number enables individual-level linkage of information from nationwide population registers.

Each individual in the study population was classified according to the degree of urbanisation (rural areas, towns and suburbs, and cities) of their residential municipality on December 31, 2018.

Based on residential address on December 31, 2018, each individual in the study population was assigned a travel distance to exercise-based CR before and after decentralisation, respectively. Each individual was also assigned a change in travel distance following decentralisation based on their residential address on December 31, 2018.

Individuals with missing information on travel distance to exercise-based CR before or after decentralisation were excluded from the study. Individuals with a travel distance of zero were also excluded. This occurred when e.g. an address serving as a residential location in 2018 changed into housing a CR facility during the process of decentralisation.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Individual-level sociodemographic characteristics of the study population included sex, age, number of chronic diseases, highest completed level of education, equivalized disposable household income, cohabitation status, and country of origin.

Information on sex (male, female), age (30–39, …, ≥ 70 years), cohabitation status (cohabiting yes, no), and country of origin (Danes, immigrants and descendants from Western Countries and non-Western countries, respectively) was obtained from the Danish Civil Registration System (December 31, 2018) [31].

Number of chronic diseases (0, 1, 2–3, ≥ 4) on December 31, 2018, was assessed based on the Danish National Patient Register with information on all hospital contacts [32] and the Danish National Prescription Register with information on all prescription redemptions [33]. Included diseases, based on the approach used by Møller et al. [34], are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Educational level (September 30, 2018) was obtained from the Population Education Register [35], and classified according to ISCED2011 (International Standard Classification of Education) [36]: elementary (ISCED 1–2; ≤9 years), short (ISCED 3–4, 10–12 years), medium (ISCED 5–6, 13–15 years), and long (ISCED 7–8, ≥ 16 years).

Equivalized disposable household income (2018) was obtained from income registers at Statistics Denmark [37], and categorized in 5 groups based on quintiles calculated among individuals < 65 years of age and ≥ 65 years of age, respectively, to account for potential income changes following retirement. Individuals from households with residence outside of Denmark anytime during 2018 were not assigned an income category.

Statistical analyses

Travel distance to exercise-based CR before and after decentralisation, as well as the change in travel distance following decentralisation, was mapped using Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW). IDW was used to produce a surface map since interpreting a map of 2.5 million points was deemed infeasible. IDW was based on a 1 km by 1 km grid and weighting within 1 km to limit the degree of smoothing and interpolation into any non-populated areas. Geographic disparities were evaluated by visual inspection of maps. This was based solely on location of addresses and did not involve the study population.

Descriptive statistics was performed by the means of medians and interquartile range, and boxplots were used to illustrate the distribution of travel distance within the study population.

Sociodemographic disparities in median travel distance and median change in travel distance were evaluated by assessment of the concentration index [38], in which a positive value indicates inequality in favour of more privileged individuals. The concentration index is only defined when the characteristic considered has at least three levels with a natural ranking. Thus, the concentration index was not assessed to evaluate disparities related to sex, cohabitation status, and country of origin. The concentration index was calculated using the R package rineq version 0.3.0 [39].

Statistical analyses were performed using R [29] v.4.5.0 and SAS 9.4.

Results

Before decentralisation, a total of 37 hospitals provided exercise-based CR. After decentralisation, exercise-based CR was provided at 119 primary care facilities. Location of CR facilities are mapped in Fig. 1a, which also displays municipalities classified according to degree of urbanisation.

The study included 2,489,360 addresses. Travel distance to exercise-based CR before decentralisation could not be assessed at 28,721 (1.2%) addresses. Among these, 18,419 addresses were located at four islands, each constituting their own municipality, with no hospital-based CR and no bridge connection to the mainland (Fanø, Læsø, Samsø, Ærø; Fig. 1a). Travel distance to exercise-based CR after decentralisation could not be assessed at 10,377 (0.4%) addresses. In total, travel distance to exercise-based CR both before and after decentralisation was assessed at 2,460,639 (98.8%) addresses.

Before decentralisation, travel distance to exercise-based CR was more than 40 km in some areas south of Zealand and in non-central parts of Jutland (Fig. 1c). After decentralisation, travel distance to exercise-based CR of more than 30 km was only seen in smaller areas in Jutland (Fig. 1d). On four islands (Fanø, Læsø, Samsø, Ærø), exercise-based CR was available after decentralisation.

In most of the country, travel distance was reduced or unchanged (defined as difference ≤ 0.5 km) after decentralisation (Fig. 1b). Reductions of more than 30 km were seen in areas with the longest travel distance to exercise-based CR before decentralisation. In some areas across the country, also in the Copenhagen area, travel distance increased. In a relatively large area in western Zealand, as well as in some areas in central Jutland, the increase was more than 10 km. In general, increased travel distance was primarily seen towards municipality borders.

The study population included 3,693,053 Danish residents aged 30 years or more after exclusion of 26,805 (0.7%) individuals with missing information on travel distance to exercise-based CR, and 439 individuals with a travel distance of zero. After decentralisation, the median travel distance to exercise-based CR was 5.3 km, which is 3.9 km (42.4%) shorter than the median travel distance to exercise-based CR before decentralisation (Fig. 2; Table 1). At individual-level, 75% of the population resided at an address where travel distance was reduced or increased with no more than 0.6 km (Supplementary Figure S3). In total, 10% of the population resided at an address where travel distance increased with more than 3.1 km, with 1% residing at an address where the increase was more than 10.4 km (not shown).

Before decentralisation, the median travel distance to exercise-based CR increased from 3.8 km among residents of municipalities with the highest degree of urbanisation to 20.4 km among residents of municipalities categorised as rural. The concentration index showed significant inequality associated with degree of urbanisation before decentralisation (concentration index 0.32, 95% CI (0.15; 0.49); Fig. 3). After decentralisation, the median travel distance was reduced to 9.4 km within rural municipalities, and the concentration index no longer indicated significant inequality associated with degree of urbanisation (concentration index 0.18, 95% CI (-0.13; 0.49)). Among residents of municipalities with the highest degree of urbanisation, median travel distance increased with 0.7 km after decentralisation. The concentration index of inequality in median change in travel distance could not be calculated since the median change was both positive and negative (Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S3).

Concentration index of inequality in median travel distance to exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation and median change in travel distance after decentralisation of services from hospital to primary care, among Danish residents (≥ 30 years old). The concentration index of inequality in median change associated with degree of urbanisation could not be calculated since the median change was both positive and negative

Before decentralisation, the median travel distance to exercise-based CR increased with increasing age until 70 years (from 6.1 km (30–39 years) to 10.5 km (60–69 years), and the concentration index showed significant inequality in median travel distance in favour of individuals of young age (concentration index 0.090, 95% CI (0.029; 0.15)). The median change in travel distance displayed inverse inequality (concentration index − 0.13, 95% CI (-0.22; -0.039)), i.e. individuals of older age benefitted most from the decentralisation, and no inequality between age groups was observed after decentralisation (concentration index 0.015, 95% CI (-0.012; 0.043)).

Before decentralisation, the median travel distance to exercise-based CR increased with the number of chronic diseases (from 8.4 km (0 diseases) to 10.1 km (≥ 4 diseases)), whereas only very minor differences were seen after decentralisation. This was confirmed by the concentration index showing significant inequality associated with the number of chronic diseases before decentralisation of exercise-based CR (concentration index 0.044, 95% CI (0.030; 0.057)), but not after (concentration index 0.0064, 95% CI (-0.0034; 0.016)).

Increasing educational level was associated with decreasing median travel distance to exercise-based CR before decentralisation (from 11.5 km (elementary education) to 4.8 km (long education)), and the concentration index showed significant inequality in favour of individuals with longer education (concentration index 0.12, 95% CI (0.034; 0.20)). After decentralisation, inequality in median travel distance to exercise-based CR was still observed, but individuals with shorter education still benefitted most from decentralisation, since the median change in travel distance was significantly in favour of individuals with shorter education (concentration index − 0.14, 95% CI (-0.25; -0.024)).

Comparing income groups, the group with medium income had the longest median travel distance to exercise-based CR before decentralisation (10.3 km), whereas the groups with the lowest and highest income had the shortest (7.6 km and 8.1 km, respectively). Median travel distance to exercise-based CR after decentralisation displayed a similar pattern, but with smaller disparities between groups. Due to the non-monotone pattern in median travel distance, the concentration index showed no inequality associated with income.

Both before and after decentralisation, individuals living alone had shorter median travel distance to exercise-based CR than individuals not living alone (6.8 km vs. 10.1 km before decentralisation and 4.6 km vs. 5.7 km after decentralisation). However, the disparity between the two groups were smaller before decentralisation than after decentralisation.

Comparing country of origin, individuals of Danish origin had the longest median travel distance to exercise-based CR before decentralisation, whereas individuals of non-Western origin had the shortest (10.0 km vs. 4.4 km). After decentralisation, median travel distance to exercise-based CR displayed a similar pattern, but with substantially reduced disparity between groups.

Only minor differences were seen between males and females (not illustrated in Fig. 2).

The change in travel distance to exercise-based CR stratified by sociodemographic characteristics is illustrated in Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S3.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This nationwide population-based repeated cross-sectional study demonstrated a reduced travel distance to exercise-based CR in most of Denmark after decentralisation of services from hospitals to primary care. Especially in non-central and rural parts of the country, substantially reduced travel distance was observed. Overall, the median travel distance among residents aged 30 years or more was reduced with more than 40%, and pronounced sociodemographic disparities were substantially reduced. Most importantly, social inequality with longer travel distance to exercise-based CR among individuals of older age or with multimorbidity was eliminated, while social inequality associated with low educational level was significantly reduced. Disparities in travel distance related to income, cohabitation, and country of origin were found to be in favour of vulnerable groups, i.e. longer travel distance was found among individuals with medium income, cohabiting, and of Danish origin.

Comparison with other studies

We believe this is the first study to investigate how decentralisation of CR services from hospitals to primary care affects geographic proximity to CR. Previous studies have evaluated geographic proximity in settings with no change in access [9, 10, 13, 40–42].

A number of previous studies have demonstrated social inequality in accessibility of different types of healthcare services [43–45]. However. only a few previous studies have evaluated social disparities in geographic proximity to CR [12, 13]. In accordance with the present results, a smaller Danish study including 302 patients found longer travel time to CR among patients with low educational level compared to patients with high educational level [13]. An American study found lack of geographic proximity to CR to be associated with racial and ethnic minorities, and low income in Los Angeles County [12]. This contrasts with the present study where longer travel distance was found among individuals with medium income and of Danish origin. This may be due to structural differences between the two countries. Besides, the American assessment of geographic proximity and sociodemographic characteristics was based on information on census tract level, not individual-level data as in the present study.

In contrast to previous studies on CR, we were able to show that decentralisation of services removed or significantly reduced social inequality in geographic proximity associated with characteristics that are known to be related to participation in CR [6–8]. Considering other services than CR, a previous study on geographic access to cancer centres in USA after introduction of satellite facilities found racial/ethnic differences in reduced travel time [46], whereas a Chinese study evaluated the improvement in accessibility after introduction of new hospitals [47].

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of the present study is the use of high-quality, nationwide data that enables assessment of individual-level travel distance and sociodemographic characteristics across an entire population, in a country where systematic decentralisation of CR services has taken place. The registers used have a high degree of completeness and validity [31–33, 35, 37].

Since the available data allowed assessment of travel-distance at individual level, in a setting where each individual is administratively assigned to a specific CR facility, geographic proximity was used as a measure of spatial accessibility. Geographic proximity was measured as the shortest travel distance from residential address to CR facility along the road network. Other studies may be limited to assessing the distance from the centre of e.g. a census tract [12] and thus not at individual level.

Travel distance as a measure of proximity does not require any assumptions about means of transportation, speed limits, rush hour etc. that are all relevant if geographic proximity is instead assessed by travel time, another measure commonly used [10, 41]. However, other factors such as access to a car, personal resources, and physical abilities may all influence the perception of travel distance and how it affects healthcare utilisation. These elements were not accounted for in the study. Travel time assessed using different means of transportation, e.g. car, public transport, bike, and walking, at different times of the day would provide a more nuanced picture of proximity, but these data were not available for the present study.

Location of exercise-based CR after decentralisation was obtained from the official portal for the public Danish Healthcare Services [26] and was not systematically validated. However, since the portal provides official information from the local authorities, and the location of especially training facilities is assumed to be fairly permanent once established, erroneous information on location of exercise-based CR after decentralisation is believed to be of minor concern. A noticeable increase in travel distance to exercise-based CR was observed in western Zealand, and here we verified the location with the local authorities. Location of exercise-based CR before decentralisation was obtained from a previous assessment by the Danish Cardiac Rehabilitation Database [24, 25].

Travel distance to exercise-based CR in 2013 and 2023 was assessed based on the road network in 2020. Although there most likely were changes in the road network over the considered time span (mainly of relatively minor extent), their potential effect on the present results at the given scale is presumably small.

Perspectives

The results of the present study show that decentralisation may not only increase geographic proximity in general, it reduces inequality in geographic proximity by favouring rural areas and certain vulnerable groups. Thus, the study demonstrates how decentralisation has resulted in a more equitable spatial distribution of healthcare resources and thereby improved spatial justice [48]. Since geographic proximity to CR is known to be associated with participation [6–8], decentralisation of services may also have the potential to reduce the well-known inequality in CR participation. Whether this is actually the case remains a topic for future research.

In some areas, reduced geographic proximity was observed. This highlights the importance of the location of the decentralised services, especially when they are associated with restricted catchment areas as in the present study. More importantly, consistency and quality of care after decentralisation are pivotal. Thus, proximity is not the only aspect to consider in a decentralisation process. Aspects like infrastructure and number of eligible patients should also be considered. Treatment guidelines and systematic monitoring of quality as it is done by the Danish Cardiac Rehabilitation Database [24] may aid in monitoring and ensuring quality after decentralisation.

The results of the present study may not generalize to other countries one-to-one, but the obvious positive effect on geographic proximity, including reduced social inequality, even in a country with relatively short travel distances as Denmark, suggests that decentralisation would be beneficial in other countries as well. Moreover, the results may certainly generalize to decentralisation of other services than CR.

In recent years, home-based cardiac telerehabilitation (CTR) programs have been introduced. These programs eliminate travel distance as a barrier to participation but may at the same time introduce new barriers such as lack of digital skills [49] and internet access [50, 51]. Like traditional CR, non-participation in CTR has been associated with sociodemographic factors like older age and low educational level [52]. This may suggest decentralisation of CR services as a currently better option than CTR if the aim is to reduce or eliminate travel distance as a barrier to participation, as long as the quality of care can be maintained.

Conclusion

Based on unique individual-level Danish data, this study demonstrated how decentralisation increased geographic proximity to exercise-based CR. Remote and rural areas, and certain vulnerable groups benefitted particularly, leading to reduced inequality in geographic proximity after decentralisation. With lack of geographic proximity being a known barrier to participation in CR programs, the study supports decentralisation of services as an instrument to be considered in an ongoing strive to improve CR participation, overall and within vulnerable groups. Moreover, the results may serve as a general illustration of what can be achieved by decentralisation, regardless of the specific service.
