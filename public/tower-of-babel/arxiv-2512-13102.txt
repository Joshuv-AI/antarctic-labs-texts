Socratic Students: Teaching Language Models to Learn by Asking Questions
Rajeev Bhatt Ambati, Tianyi Niu, Aashu Singh, Shlok Mishra, Snigdha Chaturvedi, Shashank Srivastava

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) excel at static interactions, where they answer user queries by retrieving knowledge encoded in their parameters. However, in many real-world settings, such as educational tutoring or medical assistance, relevant information is not directly available and must be actively acquired through interactions. An interactive agent would recognize its own uncertainty, ask targeted questions, and retain new knowledge. In this work, we investigate effective strategies to interactively query a teacher in seeking useful information. To improve question quality, we train the students by sampling good questions either from themselves or from stronger peers. We find that this guided training enables models to learn how to ask more effective questions, improving learning efficiency by reducing the number of interaction turns required. Across math and coding tasks, where baseline student models begin with near-zero performance, we show that student-led approaches consistently yield absolute P ​ a ​ s ​ s ​ @ ​ k Pass@k improvements of at least 0.5 over static baselines.

Introduction

As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in interactive applications, their ability to engage in
dynamic
information-seeking and learning becomes critical. Many real-world use cases, such as educational tutoring
(
19
;
33
;
45
;
25
)
and medical assistance
(
27
;
28
)
, require models not only to retrieve knowledge, but to identify uncertainties, ask clarifying questions, and adapt to newly acquired information. For example, a medical assistant diagnosing a patient must recognize missing information, ask targeted follow-ups, and reason with the answers provided. Similarly, in a tutoring setting, an interactive agent must provide appropriate hints and guidance to a student seeking pedagogical help.

Building such interactive agents poses three challenges. First, models must be able to recognize their own uncertainty
(
29
)
, a significant hurdle as LLMs are known to hallucinate when lacking knowledge
(
23
)
, and exhibit overconfidence in their responses
(
42
)
. Second, when an LLM is uncertain, it can ask many possible questions to obtain information, only a few of which meaningfully reduce uncertainty. However, current post-training methods such as SFT
(
32
)
or DPO
(
35
)
are primarily used for style, tone, and subjective quality of responses
(
2
)
but not for information seeking. Third, acquired information is typically stored only in-context
(
4
)
, but performance deteriorates as context length increases, making long-term retention inefficient and fragile
(
17
)
. We address these challenges with various prompting strategies and training models to explicitly identify uncertainty and ask good questions.

To investigate the interaction ability of LLMs, prior work
(
24
)
has studied a student-teacher setting to see if the performance of a weaker student can be improved by asking questions to a stronger teacher. They primarily studied teacher-led interactions, where the teacher identifies a student’s gaps and supplies lessons consisting of new knowledge. They have shown that such methods yield improvements on knowledge-driven tasks. This demonstrates that students can effectively utilize teacher-provided knowledge for self-improvement. However, for reasoning-heavy domains, such as math and coding, learning requires more than recalling and using facts. Also, while exploring optimal teaching strategies is useful, an equally worthy task is exploring good questioning strategies for a student.

In this paper, we investigate the complementary setting:
Can students guide the conversation by asking helpful questions to a teacher?
We show that while prompting and feedback help, explicitly training models for question asking makes them significantly more efficient, requiring fewer interaction turns.

We study this on math (GSM8K,
(
8
)
) and coding (HumanEval/OPC,
(
6
;
20
)
) tasks using three LLMs. We examine several interaction strategies: (i) an unguided setting, where the student receives no explicit guidance on how to formulate questions; (ii) chain-of-thought (CoT)–guided questioning
(
47
)
, where the student follows a structured reasoning prompt before asking a question; (iii) pre- and mid-assessment settings, where the student receives feedback on its previous answer; and (iv) guided data collection using either self-guidance or a stronger peer, which is used to explicitly train the student using DPO to ask more effective questions.

Our contributions are:

•

A framework for student-led interaction, enabling dynamic conversations where students actively query teachers.

•

A guided training approach that leverages self- and peer-generated questions to improve the quality of student questioning.

•

Empirical evidence across math and coding datasets, showing that student-led interaction improves performance beyond static baselines.

Together, our findings highlight a shift from LLMs as static retrievers toward interactive learners that can reason, ask, and adapt in real time.

Related Work

General Interactive Learning.

Early work on language-guided machine learning often relied on single-turn instructions

;
16
;

Interactive Learning in Medicine.

Research has explored interactive frameworks for medical decision-making.
25
proposed adaptive LLM collaborations, and
5

(

, though they lack the depth of doctor-style reasoning. Information-theoretic methods in clinical settings
(

)
often simplify outcomes to binary. Other work includes static pre-consultation question sets
(
48
)
or models that ask improved questions based on handcrafted attributes
(
28
)
. Unlike these, our framework focuses on interactive, student-led guidance and learning for reasoning tasks, avoiding predefined trees and static questions.

Interactive Learning in Education.

LLMs have also been evaluated as tutors. Studies show that GPT-3 underperforms human teachers on pedagogical measures
(
43
)
, often revealing solutions prematurely
(
30
)
or introducing reasoning errors
(
15
;
31
)
. While some work highlights potential benefits of process-focused feedback
(
11
)
, most existing approaches focus on data creation. This includes building educational datasets for fine-tuning
(
22
;
7
;
26
;
12
)
or collecting step-by-step guidance
(
38
;
46
;
10
)
. More recent work leverages preference data for SFT
(
39
)
, RLHF
(
45
)
, or DPO
(
37
)
. These methods improve pedagogical alignment but largely optimize for style. Our work instead focuses on how students can lead interactions with teachers, asking targeted questions to improve their reasoning on math and coding tasks.

3.1
Baseline Interaction Strategies

We begin with two baseline modes of student–teacher interaction, both instantiations of Algorithm
1
without any assessment (
t_{\text{assess}}=-1
). Prompts used for Unguided and CoT are provided in Appendix
E.1
.

Unguided:

In the unguided setting, the student (
\mathcal{S}
) and teacher (
\mathcal{T}
) alternate turns for 6 steps. At each student turn,
\mathcal{S}
must directly produce a question for
\mathcal{T}
without any explicit scaffolding. This setup measures how much performance can improve purely from dynamic interaction, with no additional guidance.

CoT-Guided:

In the CoT-guided setting, the student’s system prompt is augmented with a simple reasoning scaffold. Before posing a question,
\mathcal{S}
is instructed to (A) summarize what is already known, (B) identify the missing subgoals needed for solving the task, and (C) ask a single, precise follow-up to
\mathcal{T}
. This encourages structured reasoning and targeted information-seeking, while keeping the same overall interactive protocol.

3.2
Assessment Strategies

Similar to human learners, we hypothesize that timely feedback can help LLM students calibrate their understanding and guide subsequent questioning. In human tutoring, assessments ground learners by identifying errors and providing corrective signals; we examine whether a similar mechanism improves interactive learning for LLMs.

We evaluate two variants of Algorithm
1
that insert a one-shot assessment at different points in the interaction. In both cases, the student (
\mathcal{S}
) generates two candidate solutions, and the teacher (
\mathcal{T}
) provides feedback on their correctness. The exchange is then appended to the conversation history, grounding all subsequent turns. Appendix
E.2
are the prompts used for assessment.

Pre-Assessment:

Here the assessment is introduced before the student’s first turn (
t_{\text{assess}}=1
). This allows the student to be explicitly grounded from the very beginning of the interaction. This corresponds to Algorithm
1
with
t_{assess}=1
.

Mid-Assessment:

Here the assessment occurs halfway through the interaction (
t_{\text{assess}}=3
out of
N=6
turns). Unlike pre-assessment, the student can leverage the accumulated dialogue from the first five turns when producing its candidate solutions, allowing the check to redirect reasoning midway. This corresponds to Algorithm
1
with
t_{\text{assess}}=3
.

3.3
Guided Training Strategies

We extend Algorithm
2
with preference optimization to train students (
\mathcal{S}
) to ask better questions. At each turn, a guide model (
\mathcal{G}
) generates multiple candidate questions. The student then ranks these questions based on downstream task performance (
Pass@k
) after receiving teacher responses. The best-performing question is marked “chosen,” others “rejected.” We collect prompt–chosen pairs for an SFT dataset (
\mathcal{B}{\mathrm{SFT}}
) and chosen–rejected pairs for a DPO dataset (
\mathcal{B}{\mathrm{DPO}}
). Students are fine-tuned with SFT followed by DPO.

Self-Guided:

Here the guide
\mathcal{G}
is the student itself (
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{S}
). The goal is for the student to learn from its own exploration, discovering which of its own questions are most productive.

Peer-Guided:

Here the guide
\mathcal{G}
is a larger, more capable peer model (
\mathcal{G}\neq\mathcal{S}
). The student ranks and learns from these externally generated candidates, distilling the stronger model’s question-asking ability into its own parameters.

Conclusion

We investigated whether LLMs can be prompted and trained to behave as effective interactive learners. Our results show that even a simple student–teacher interaction yields substantial gains, with absolute improvements in
Pass@k
often exceeding
+0.5
and reaching as high as
+0.85
. Chain-of-Thought guidance further amplifies these gains. We also found that assessment timing matters: pre-assessment is highly beneficial for math tasks, whereas mid-assessment is most effective for coding tasks—reflecting fundamental differences in their problem structures (stepwise vs. non-stepwise).

Beyond prompting strategies, we demonstrated that the skill of asking good questions can be explicitly trained. Both self-guided and peer-guided DPO substantially improve students’ questioning policies, leading to stronger downstream performance and greater learning efficiency, often achieving the same performance as unguided students with three fewer turns. Our analysis further showed that stronger methods help models make more progress per turn and ask higher-quality questions earlier in the trajectory.

Overall, these findings indicate that LLMs can move beyond static information retrieval and operate as adaptive, interactive learners. A key challenge ahead is the reliance on brittle, in-context memory: developing more robust mechanisms for retaining and updating knowledge across turns is an important next step toward building truly adaptive learning agents.

Limitations

We evaluate our methods on math (GSM8K) and coding (HumanEval/OPC) tasks, which capture structured reasoning but do not cover broader interactive settings such as open-ended dialogue, long-horizon planning, or multimodal environments. The effectiveness of guided questioning in these domains remains an open question.
