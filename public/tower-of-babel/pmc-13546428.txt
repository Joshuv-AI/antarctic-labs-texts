Factors Associated With Return to Sport at Minimum 10-Year Follow-up in Athletes With Borderline Hip Dysplasia Undergoing Hip Arthroscopy With Capsular Plication
Authors: Ady H Kahana-Rojkind, Matthew J Strok, Elizabeth G Walsh, Roger Quesada-Jimenez, Benjamin D Kuhns, Benjamin G Domb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Literature on long-term return-to-sport (RTS) rates in athletes with borderline hip dysplasia (BHD) is limited.Purpose:To examine patient outcomes and RTS rates in athletes with BHD who underwent primary hip arthroscopy with capsular plication at a minimum 10-year follow-up.Study Design:Cohort study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:Data were retrospectively reviewed for all patients with BHD (lateral center-edge angle 18°-25°) who underwent primary hip arthroscopy with labral treatment and capsular plication between September 2008 and May 2014. Athletes in the BHD group were propensity matched 1:1 to a control group of patients with normal acetabular coverage based on age, sex, body mass index, follow-up time, Tönnis grade, and preoperative sport competition level. Patient-reported outcome scores (modified Harris Hip Score, Non-Arthritic Hip Score, 12-item International Hip Outcome Tool, Hip Outcome Score Sports Specific Subscale, visual analog scale for pain, and satisfaction), RTS, and continuation-of-sport rates were also analyzed.Results:There were 50 patients (53 hips) in the BHD group and 53 patients (53 hips) in the control group. Both groups demonstrated similar increases in all PROs from preoperative to 10-year follow-up and favorable RTS rates. A total of 46 patients in the BHD group attempted to RTS and 40 (87.0%) were successful while 48 patients in the control group attempted to RTS and 41 (85.4%) were successful at the short to midterm. Of the patients who successfully returned to sport, 28 from the BHD cohort (70.0%) and 30 (73.2%) from the control cohort attempted to continue playing their respective sport 10 years postoperatively. Of these, 23 patients (82.1%) from the BHD cohort and 25 patients (83.3%) from the control cohort successfully continued their sport at a minimum of 10 years postoperatively. Over the course of 10 years of follow-up, 8 patients (16%) out of 50 in the BHD group at some point discontinued their sport, not due to their hip, but because of lifestyle changes or loss of interest.Conclusion:Athletes with BHD who underwent primary hip arthroscopy with capsular plication and labral treatment demonstrated a high rate of RTS at short-term follow-up, high rate of continuation of sport at long-term follow-up, and favorable long-term results. Loss of interest or lifestyle transitions had a greater impact on continuation of sport at long-term follow-up than persistent symptoms related to the hip.Keywords: hip, hip arthroscopy, borderline hip dysplasia, return to sport

Borderline hip dysplasia (BHD), commonly defined by a lateral center-edge angle (LCEA) ranging from 18° to 25°, represents a challenging condition for hip preservation surgeons.4,17,24 While periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) is the gold standard treatment for more severe dysplasia (LCEA <18°),10,12,22,29,40 the optimal management strategy for BHD remains a topic of ongoing debate in the hip preservation field. While periacetabular osteotomy addresses the underlying structural instability in patients with acetabular dysplasia, hip arthroscopy is frequently performed in conjunction with PAO to address associated intra-articular pathology. 41 Arthroscopy allows treatment of labral pathology, microinstability, and femoroacetabular impingement syndrome (FAIS), which commonly coexist in patients with BHD. In carefully selected patients with BHD, arthroscopy alone has also been proposed as a less invasive treatment strategy. Recent research has demonstrated promising short-, mid-, and long-term outcomes for BHD patients treated with hip arthroscopy, particularly when emphasizing labral preservation and capsular plication.7,15,27,30 However, the literature remains sparse regarding long-term outcomes, particularly for athletes, a group for whom successful return to sport (RTS) is a critical measure of surgical success.

Athletes with BHD present unique demands on the hip joint, often requiring supraphysiologic range of motion and dynamic neuromuscular control to accomplish the unique tasks required for sport. Prior studies have shown encouraging midterm results, including favorable patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and high RTS rates. At a minimum 5-year follow-up, these results were comparable to those of athletes with normal acetabular coverage. 39 However, evidence beyond this time frame is limited, and long-term data are needed to evaluate the durability of these outcomes and assess the survivorship of arthroscopic treatment in this population. The purpose of this study is to report minimum 10-year PROs and RTS rates in athletes with BHD following primary hip arthroscopy with capsular plication and to compare these outcomes with a propensity-matched control group of athletes with normal acetabular coverage. We hypothesize that (1) athletes with BHD will demonstrate sustained improvements in PROs and RTS rates at a minimum 10-year follow-up and (2) outcomes in this group will be comparable with those observed in athletes with normal acetabular coverage. By addressing this gap in the literature, this study aims to provide valuable insights into the long-term viability of arthroscopic management for BHD, particularly in the context of the high physical demands of athletic populations.

Methods

Participation in the American Hip Institute Hip Preservation Registry

All patients included in this study consented to participating in the American Hip Institute Hip Preservation Registry. Although previous studies might have included similar patient data, this study represents a unique analysis with separate findings. This study received institutional review board approval.

Patient Selection

Data were prospectively collected and retrospectively reviewed for all athletes with BHD who underwent primary hip arthroscopy for the treatment of labral pathology and FAIS between September 2008 and May 2014. Labral treatment included repair, reconstruction, or debridement. Patients in the BHD study group self-reported participation in a sport within 1 year preoperatively and had an LCEA between 18° and 25° in the affected hip. Patients indicated their level of sport as recreational, organized amateur, high school, college, or professional. Patients were included in the study if they had preoperative and minimum 10-year follow-up data available for the following PRO measures: the modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS), Non-Arthritic Hip Score (NAHS), Hip Outcome Score Sports Specific Subscale (HOS-SSS), and visual analog scale (VAS) for pain.9,32 The 12-item international Hip Outcome Tool (iHOT) was introduced later during the study period and therefore was not available for all patients enrolled earlier in the registry. Patients who received revision surgery or converted to arthroplasty were accounted for. Patients were excluded if they had a prior ipsilateral hip surgery, prior hip conditions (eg, Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease, avascular necrosis, etc), Tönnis grade >1, LCEA <18°, or workers’ compensation status. Patients in the BHD study group were propensity matched to a group of athletes with normal acetabular coverage (LCEA 25° to 40°).

Preoperative Evaluation, Radiographic Imaging, and Surgical Indications

The senior author (B.G.D.) acquired a patient history, physical examination, and radiographic imaging to evaluate surgical candidates. During the preoperative visit, patient demographic information was collected and physical examination characteristics such as gait, range of motion, tenderness, strength, and mechanical symptoms were evaluated.

A series of radiographic images were obtained of the pelvis, including the supine and standing anteroposterior, modified Dunn 45°, and false-profile views. 11 The supine anteroposterior view was utilized to measure the LCEA, 17 Tönnis angle, 18 and Tönnis grade. 6 Anterior coverage was measured on the false-profile view. The modified Dunn view was utilized to measure the alpha angle with angles >55° representing cam deformity of the femoral head-neck junction. 2 Magnetic resonance imaging was obtained to identify intra-articular pathology such as labral pathology or chondral damage.

Prior to surgical indication, all patients attempted and failed to improve after ≥3 months of conservative treatment. Activity modification, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, physical therapy, rest, and/or injections were part of the conservative treatment regimen. Surgical indications for patients with BHD are further described in previous studies.

Surgical Technique

The senior author performed every procedure. Under general anesthesia, patients were placed in the modified supine position, and traction was applied to the operative hip. A simple interportal capsulotomy was performed between the anterolateral (12 o’clock) portal and the midanterior portal. A diagnostic arthroscopy to evaluate the labrum, intra-articular cartilage, and ligamentum teres (LT) was performed after the anterolateral and midanterior portals were created. Labral damage was graded according to the Seldes system. 42 Chondral pathologies were measured according to the acetabular labrum articular disruption (ALAD) and Outerbridge classification systems.33,38 The Domb and Villar classifications were used to grade LT tears.5,37

Under fluoroscopic guidance, acetabuloplasty and femoroplasty were performed to address pincer and cam deformities, respectively. In the setting of BHD, acetabuloplasty was performed selectively. In these cases, rim trimming was kept minimal to address focal impingement while preserving overall acetabular coverage. Additionally, limited decortication of the acetabular rim was performed to improve the biologic environment for labral healing, with careful attention paid by the senior surgeon (B.G.D.) to avoid excessive rim resection that could compromise acetabular coverage. Labral debridement was performed in some cases. In the majority of cases, labral tears were repaired with knotless suture anchors. Labral reconstruction was performed in the case of irreparable labral tears. Capsular plication was performed in all patients in this study. Capsular plication was achieved through imbrication of the capsule with an inferior capsular shift, taking larger bites of the distal capsule through the zona orbicularis and corresponding bites of the proximal capsule more laterally. This configuration produces capsular imbrication and increases capsular tension in extension and external rotation. Multiple sutures were placed to achieve adequate capsular tension. 8 Tears of the LT were debrided, and patients with recurrent painful internal snapping refractory to conservative treatment received an iliopsoas fractional lengthening.

Rehabilitation Protocol

After surgery, patients used crutches with a 20-lb heel-touch weightbearing restriction for the first 2 weeks and were placed in a hip brace (Donjoy VersaRom) to provide stability and limit hip flexion to 90° during this period. Range of motion was then gradually advanced within a pain-free range. To protect the capsular repair and plication, patients were instructed to avoid combined hip extension and external rotation, aggressive joint mobilization, and overstretching exercises for the first 6 weeks postoperatively. All patients participated in a structured physical therapy program with the goal of RTS by approximately 6 months postoperatively. 16 To prevent heterotopic ossification and thrombosis, celecoxib and aspirin were taken for 4 weeks postoperatively.

Patient Outcomes and RTS Analysis

Patients completed preoperative and postoperative questionnaires via email, telephone, or in the clinic. Questionnaires were sent to patients at 3 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter. These questions were used to measure mHHS, NAHS, iHOT-12, HOS-SSS, VAS pain, and patient satisfaction. Satisfaction was measured on a scale from 0 (not at all satisfied) to 10 (most satisfied). VAS pain was measured on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (extreme pain). Preoperative iHOT-12 scores were not collected for this patient cohort.

On a specific sports-related section of the questionnaires, patients identified their preoperative sport and their level of participation within 1 year prior to surgery. RTS was defined as returning to participation in their preoperative sport at any level. Postoperative RTS surveys were completed at 3 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter. Athletes who did not attempt to RTS for reasons unrelated to the hip (eg, lifestyle transition, loss of interest) were excluded from the RTS analysis. A subanalysis was performed in the BHD group to examine RTS rates for competitive level and sex.

Continuation of sport was assessed at the minimum 10-year time point. Continuation of sport was defined as continuing to participate in sport after having returned at an earlier time point postoperatively. Patients who were never able to RTS were excluded from the continuation of sport analysis. Patients who had successfully returned to sport and were continuing to attempt to RTS were included in the continuation of sport analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations while categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages. Chi-square and Fisher exact tests were used to assess differences between categorical variables. For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test assessed normality, and the F test was used to determine if the 2 groups had equal variance. A 2-tailed t test was used to determine statistically significant differences between normally distributed data with equal variance. A paired t test was used when the variable of interest had equal sample numbers for both the study group and the control group. For nonparametric data, equivalent tests such as the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Mann-Whitney U test, or Welch test were used to assess group means. A P value of .05 was set as the statistically significant threshold before conducting the study. Statistical analyses were performed with Microsoft Excel using the Real Statistics add-in pack. 43

Athletes in the BDH study group were propensity matched in a 1:1 ratio using the optimal matching method in RStudio (Version 4.3.2; Posit, PBC). Patients were match based on age, body mass index (BMI), follow-up time, sex, Tönnis grade, labral treatment, and preoperative sport participation level. An a priori power analysis was conducted to determine the required sample size to achieve 80% power using an expected mean difference of 15 for HOS-SSS and a standard deviation of 24. 35 It was found that the study group and control group each needed a minimum of 42 hips (G*Power).19,35

Postoperative RTS

Both the study group and the control group patients played a wide variety of sports preoperatively, with running being the most common sport in both groups (Figure 2). Of the 50 patients in the BHD group, 4 patients did not attempt to RTS at any time point postoperatively because of lifestyle transition. Of the 46 BHD patients who attempted to return, 40 (87.0%) reported a successful return at short- to midterm follow-up. For the 6 patients who attempted to return but were unable, all cited hip-related symptoms as their reason for not returning. Of the 53 patients in the control group, 5 patients did not attempt to RTS at any time point postoperatively. Reasons for not attempting to return in the control group included lifestyle transition (3 patients) and desire to prevent hip symptoms on the contralateral side (2 patients). Of the 48 control patients who attempted to return, 41 (85.4%) were successful at some point postoperatively. For the 7 patients who attempted to return but were unable, all cited hip-related symptoms as their reason for not returning. There were no differences in the RTS rates between groups (P = .83). In the 46 athletes with BHD who attempted to RTS at any point in time, competition level and sex did not influence RTS rates (Table 6). For the 9 patients who were excluded from the RTS analysis for not attempting to RTS, their minimum 10-year values for mHHS, NAHS, HOS-SSS, VAS pain, and satisfaction were 91.9 ± 10.4, 91.4 ± 15.1, 84.8 ± 28.4, 1.3 ± 1.4, and 8.9 ± 1.3, respectively.

Long-Term Sport Continuation

Of the patients reporting a successful RTS previously, 28 (70%) in the BHD cohort were still attempting to play their sport at the 10-year time point compared with 30 (73%) in the control cohort (P > .05). Twelve patients in the BHD group and 11 patients in the control group did not attempt to continue playing at the 10-year time point for various reasons including desire to prevent hip symptoms (3 BHD patients; 2 control patients), lifestyle transition (3 BHD patients; 7 control patients), loss of interest (1 BHD patient), physician recommendation (2 BHD patients; from physicians outside our institution and not necessarily related to the index hip), and other symptoms such as back or neck pain (3 BHD patients; 2 control patients). Of the 28 BHD patients who attempted to continue playing, 23 patients (82.1%) from the BHD cohort and 25 patients (83.3%) from the control cohort successfully continued their sport at a minimum of 10 years postoperatively. There were 10 patients (43.5%) in the BHD cohort and 13 patients (52.0%) in the control cohort who continued to play at the same competitive level 10 years postoperatively. The remaining 13 patients (56.5%) in the BHD group and 12 patients (48.0%) in the control cohort played at a lower level 10 years postoperatively. There was no difference in the 10-year postoperative competitive level between groups (P = .55). For the 5 patients in both groups who attempted to continue playing their sport at 10 years postoperatively but were unable to, all described hip-related symptoms as their reason for not returning. There were no differences in continuation of sport rates between groups (P > .99).

Complications and Secondary Procedures

Patients demonstrated similar rates of complication, revision arthroscopy, and conversion to arthroplasty. There were 3 (5.7%) superficial infections in the BHD group and 2 instances of heterotopic ossification (3.8%) and 1 (1.9%) superficial infection in the control group. In the BHD group and control group, there were 9 (17.0%) and 8 (15.1%) revision arthroscopies, respectively. The indications for revision arthroscopy were most commonly recurrent or persistent labral pathology (including labral retears). At the 10-year time point, there were 4 (7.5%) conversions to arthroplasty in the BHD group and 3 (5.7%) in the control group (Table 7).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that athletes with BHD undergoing primary hip arthroscopy for labral treatment with capsular plication achieved significant improvements across all PROs, with RTS rates that were comparable to a matched control group with normal acetabular coverage, with a short-term RTS rate of 87% (40/46) and long-term continuation of sport rate at 82% (23/28). Lifestyle changes and loss of interest in sport affected long-term continuation of sport rates more than persistent hip pathology at minimum 10-year follow-up.

Combined hip arthroscopy and PAO demonstrates favorable outcomes in patients with borderline or symptomatic dysplasia, particularly in the setting of more pronounced structural instability.23,26,41 However, hip arthroscopy with capsular plication also provides favorable outcomes in carefully selected patients with BDH. 15 These findings emphasize the importance of patient selection and the degree of structural instability when determining the optimal surgical approach.

A key distinction in this study is the differentiation between RTS rates and continuation-of-sport rates. Assessing continuation-of-sport rates alongside RTS rates provides a more comprehensive evaluation of the long-term success of the intervention. This study showed that while 87% of BHD athletes successfully returned to sport postoperatively, only 23 of 50 patients (46%) were actively participating in their sport at the 10-year mark. Importantly, just 5 of these patients (10%) discontinued long-term participation because of hip-related issues, such as pain or instability. Taking into account the additional 6 patients (12%) that were unable to RTS because of persistent pain, these results align with findings by Lamba et al 25 who reported that at minimum 8.5-year follow-up, only 9 of 37 (24%) of young amateur athletes undergoing primary hip arthroscopy cited their hip as the reason for stopping sport. Of the remaining patients, 17 of 28 (61%), continued playing their initial sport. These results highlight that non–hip related factors, such as lifestyle transition, often play a more significant role in long-term sport discontinuation than surgical outcomes. While RTS rates capture an athlete's ability to resume athletic activity shortly after recovery, continuation-of-sport rates reflect the durability of these outcomes over time. This distinction is particularly significant in the context of long-term outcomes in BHD, where the potential for progressive joint degeneration or instability poses challenges to sustained participation in high-level sports. Continuation-of-sport rates also offer critical insights into the long-term functional stability of the hip, the quality of life for athletes, and the avoidance of secondary surgeries such as revision arthroscopy or conversion to THA.

Athletes with BHD undergoing hip arthroscopy demonstrate high RTS rates and favorable outcomes in multiple studies.20,21 Alter et al 1 analyzed outcomes in a population of patients with BHD undergoing hip arthroscopy for FAIS and reported a 75.6% RTS rate within 8.3 months, with 45.2% returning at the same level, 51.6% at a lower level, and 3.2% at a higher level of activity. Of the high school and collegiate athletes in this cohort, 90.9% successfully returned to sport. Similarly, D'Ambrosi et al 13 highlighted the importance of addressing capsular laxity in BHD patients, reporting an 80% RTS rate at 2 years following T-shaped capsular plication, along with significant functional improvement and low complication rates. The present study extends and builds upon these findings by showing sustained improvement in PROs and RTS rates at a minimum 10-year follow-up, with 87% RTS. Additionally, it shows similar results to the RTS achieved by patients without BHD as displayed in the meta-analysis by O’Connor et al, 36 which reported a 75.6% RTP rate at 8.3 months, with 45.2% returning to the same activity level.

In contrast, Leopold et al 28 reported a higher RTS rate of 92.5% at 5 years among athletes undergoing PAO for BHD, suggesting that PAO may be better suited for athletes requiring definitive structural correction of acetabular undercoverage. However, a notable difference is that athletes treated with PAO were more likely to transition to lower impact sports over time. The authors of that study mentioned that the proportion of cases that participated in low-impact sports increased significantly from preoperatively (29.1% [16/55]) to postoperatively (47.3% [26/55]). whereas arthroscopy patients in the current study demonstrated sustained participation in high-level sports. This may explain the lower RTS rate observed, as this study focused on long-term outcomes rather than a 5-year follow-up.

Previous literature has reported favorable long-term outcomes for BHD patients undergoing hip arthroscopy with capsular plication. Beals et al 3 reported a 79% survivorship rate at 10 years for patients undergoing hip arthroscopy for FAIS with borderline dysplasia, with significant improvements in mHHS from 58 to 83 and >80% of patients achieving the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for activities of daily living and sport. Likewise, Domb et al 15 showed that the 10-year survivorship rate was 82.2%, with significant improvements observed in mHHS, NAHS, and HOS-SSS, with high rates of achieving the MCID. Survivorship and revision surgery rates were comparable with a propensity-matched control group without BHD. At the midterm follow-up, Owens et al 39 demonstrated comparable PRO improvements in BHD athletes, with significant increases in mHHS and HOS-SSS scores and RTS rates of 88.2% at 5 years, highlighting the durability of arthroscopic interventions. Jimenez et al 21 also reported durable improvements in mHHS and iHOT-12, emphasizing the role of labral preservation and capsular repair in achieving clinically significant functional outcomes. Similarly, a systematic review performed by Lee et al 27 demonstrated favorable mid- and long-term outcomes for patients with BHD undergoing primary hip arthroscopy, with significant improvement in PROs, including mHHS and HOS-SSS and ≥70% of patients achieving MCID. Midterm survivorship was 98.2% at 5.7 years, while long-term survivorship was 76.3% at 12.2 years, with low revision hip preservation surgery rates (2.1%-7.0%) and conversion to THA up to 24%. These findings complement the current study by emphasizing the durability PROs achieved through the arthroscopic treatment in the BHD population. Furthermore, these findings align with earlier reports suggesting that labral preservation and controlled capsular tensioning are critical to achieving optimal outcomes in this population.14,17,31 The revision rate in the study (17%) is higher than those reported in other papers; for instance, Beals et al 3 and Menge et al 34 reported a lower revision rate (approximately 10%) in the borderline and adolescent populations. However, the current study specifically focuses on athletes with BHD, a population that engages in high-impact activities, placing greater stress on the hip joint postoperatively. This could lead to an increased likelihood of requiring revision due to persistent symptoms or reinjury. Other studies may include more diverse populations or patients with lower physical demands, leading to lower revision rates. Moreover, criteria for revision surgery might differ between studies leading to additional differences in revision surgery rates.

Additional strengths of the present analysis further support its findings. This study is among the few to report at a minimum 10-year follow-up. Propensity score matching was utilized to isolate the influence of acetabular coverage on PROs and RTS while minimizing potential confounding factors such as sex, age, Tönnis grade, BMI, follow-up time, sport type, and competition level. Furthermore, the inclusion of multiple validated functional hip outcome scores tailored to assess active patients with nonarthritic hips mitigated potential ceiling effects and enhanced the generalizability of the findings to similar athletic populations.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations. The small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings, and the retrospective design introduces potential selection and recall bias. Additionally, patient classification was based on LCEA alone. While this approach is consistent with prior literature on BDH, contemporary evaluation of hip instability increasingly incorporates additional radiographic parameters such as the anterior center-edge angle, anterior wall index, Tönnis angle, and femoroepiphyseal acetabular roof index, which were not included in the present study. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported activity levels and RTS data, without the use of validated tools like the Tegner Activity Scale, restricts the precision of the outcomes. Future research should prioritize larger prospective studies with standardized activity metrics and explore emerging surgical techniques to further optimize outcomes for athletes with BHD. Moreover, a small number of patients underwent bilateral procedures, which may introduce a degree of non-independence between observations; however, given the limited number of such cases and that RTS outcomes were analyzed at the patient level, the overall impact is likely minimal. Finally, given the long-term nature of this study with a minimum 10-year follow-up, surgical techniques and treatment strategies have evolved since the time these procedures were performed. Certain procedures that were more commonly performed during the study period, such as iliopsoas fractional lengthening, are now utilized less frequently as newer techniques and treatment algorithms have emerged. As such, current surgical practice may differ from the techniques reflected in this cohort.

Conclusion

Athletes with BHD who underwent primary hip arthroscopy with capsular plication and labral treatment demonstrated a high rate of RTS at short-term follow-up, high rate of continuation of sport at long-term follow-up, and favorable long-term results. Loss of interest or lifestyle transition had a greater impact on continuation of sport at long-term follow-up than persistent symptoms related to the hip.
