Association of HPGDS Expression with Prognosis and Immune Microenvironment Remodeling in Pancreatic Cancer
Authors: Hao Ye, Minjuan Du, Chenjun Xie, Ge Gao, Qingwen Wang, Xiaoyun Wang, Huaxin Kang, Mengkui Han, Xichen Wang, Junjing Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PAAD) is a highly lethal malignancy characterized by metabolic remodeling and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Hematopoietic prostaglandin D synthase (HPGDS), a key enzyme involved in prostaglandin and lipid-associated inflammatory signaling, remains insufficiently characterized in PAAD.MethodsWe integrated public transcriptomic datasets, survival cohorts, immunotherapy-related datasets, single-cell RNA sequencing data, and in vitro validation assays to evaluate the expression pattern, clinical relevance, immune association, and functional role of HPGDS in pancreatic cancer.ResultsHPGDS was upregulated in pancreatic cancer tissues and cell lines and was associated with clinicopathological progression and unfavorable prognosis. HPGDS expression correlated with immune checkpoint molecules, immune cell infiltration, chemokine receptor networks, and cell-cell communication programs within the pancreatic tumor microenvironment. In public immunotherapy cohorts, higher HPGDS expression was associated with poorer response to immune checkpoint blockade, although the predictive performance varied across cohorts. Pharmacological inhibition and shRNA-mediated knockdown supported a role for HPGDS in pancreatic cancer cell proliferation, whereas effects on migration and invasion were less consistent between inhibitor-based and genetic approaches.ConclusionThese findings suggest that HPGDS is associated with prognosis and immune microenvironment remodeling in pancreatic cancer and may represent a candidate biomarker for further translational investigation. Because many findings are based on retrospective public datasets and correlation analyses, additional mechanistic, in vivo, and clinical validation studies are required.Keywords: HPGDS, pancreatic cancer, immune microenvironment, prognosis, biomarker

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is one of the most aggressive malignancies, with a dismal prognosis, late diagnosis, high metastatic potential, and resistance to conventional therapies.1–3 Globally, it is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 10%.4 This high lethality is attributable to its complex tumor microenvironment, genetic heterogeneity, and metabolic reprogramming.5,6 Altered lipid metabolism is a hallmark of pancreatic cancer progression, fueling tumor growth, promoting immune evasion, and enhancing metastatic dissemination.5,7,8

HPGDS is a key enzyme in the prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) biosynthetic pathway and plays crucial roles in lipid metabolism and inflammatory signaling. It is well known for its role in immune regulation and allergic responses.9 Emerging evidence suggests that HPGDS may contribute to metabolic reprogramming in cancer. By modulating lipid homeostasis and influencing the tumor microenvironment, HPGDS may act as an important driver of tumor progression.10 However, the specific role of HPGDS in pancreatic cancer, particularly in lipid metabolic reprogramming, remains poorly understood and warrants further investigation.

HPGDS plays a significant role in lipid mediator biology and immune regulation. Pancreatic cancer is generally resistant to immune checkpoint blockade, in part because of its dense stromal compartment, immunosuppressive myeloid populations, limited effector T-cell activity, and complex cytokine and chemokine networks within the tumor microenvironment. Recent evidence further suggests that activated T cells can relieve tumor immunosuppression through macrophage reeducation and that HPGDS-positive macrophage-related programs may be relevant to anti-PD-1 responsiveness.11 Therefore, biomarkers that reflect immune suppression, lipid-inflammatory signaling, and stromal-immune communication may help contextualize patient heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance. Given the aggressive nature and poor prognosis of pancreatic cancer, investigating HPGDS may provide new insights into the relationship between lipid-associated inflammatory signaling and immune microenvironment remodeling. In this study, we focused on HPGDS and conducted comprehensive analyses of its expression profile, biological significance, prognostic value, immune associations, and potential relationship with immunotherapy response in PAAD using multiple bioinformatics and experimental approaches. The study aimed to clarify HPGDS as a candidate biomarker while recognizing that its therapeutic relevance requires further mechanistic and clinical validation.

Methods

Reagents

HPGDS inhibitor 1 (MCE, HY-10439), β-actin (Biodragon, B8010) and an HPGDS rabbit polyclonal antibody (Biodragon, BD-PN0730) were employed in this study.

Cell Lines

hTERT-HPNE, PANC-1, and MIA PaCa-2 cell lines were purchased from Zhongqiao Xinzhou (Shanghai, China). hTERT-HPNE and PANC-1 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Zhongqiao Xinzhou) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Newzerum) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS; Proteinbio). MIA PaCa-2 cells were cultured in DMEM from the same supplier, supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% PS, 2.5% horse serum, and 100 mM sodium pyruvate to maintain cell growth and viability. All cell lines were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. In addition, all cell lines were authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination to ensure experimental reliability.

Bioinformatics Analysis

To compare tumor tissues with matched normal tissues across multiple cancer types, TNMplot (https://tnmplot.com/analysis/) and GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#analysis) were utilized. The BEST web server (https://rookieutopia.hiplot.com.cn/app_direct/BEST/) was used to evaluate the association of HPGDS expression with differentiation grade, TNM stage, prognosis, and response to immunotherapy. A protein-protein interaction network centered on HPGDS was generated using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/). Furthermore, the relationships between HPGDS and microsatellite instability, tumor mutation burden, clinicopathological characteristics, biological pathways, immune checkpoints, chemokine/receptor families, and infiltrating immune cell populations were comprehensively analyzed using the TCGAplot R package (v7.0.1). For correlation analyses, gene expression associations were assessed using rank-based correlation unless otherwise specified, and statistical significance was interpreted using the adjusted or platform-reported P values provided by the corresponding analysis tools. Survival analyses were performed using dataset-defined or tool-derived expression stratification, and results were interpreted across independent cohorts rather than from a single dataset alone.

Single-Cell Data Analysis

Four GEO scRNA-seq datasets, including GSE194247, GSE197177, GSE212966, and GSE235449, were downloaded, comprising 30 pancreatic tumor samples and seven matched normal tissue samples. Data preprocessing was performed using the dplyr version 1.1.1. Dimensionality reduction and object handling were performed using Seurat version 4.3.0 and SeuratObject version 4.1.3, respectively. Cells with low detected gene counts, low total transcript counts, or excessive mitochondrial transcript proportions were excluded according to standard single-cell quality-control procedures before downstream analysis. Gene expression matrices were normalized and scaled before dimensionality reduction, and public datasets were integrated to reduce dataset-specific batch effects prior to clustering and visualization. Data visualization was conducted using ggplot2 version 3.4.4 and ggraph version 4.2.2, and two UMAP packages (versions 1.8.0 and 0.2.10.0) were used to produce two-dimensional embeddings. Network construction and topological analyses were performed using igraph version 2.0.3, in which genes or cells were considered as nodes linked by similarity or correlation weights. Cell-type annotations were assigned using canonical marker genes and the annotations available from the original datasets where applicable.

Cell-Cell Communication

Intercellular communication was analyzed using CellChat version 1.6.1. HPGDS-high and HPGDS-low cell populations were compared to explore differences in ligand-receptor interaction strength and pathway activity among pancreatic tumor, stromal, and immune cell populations. The analysis was used to identify communication programs associated with elevated HPGDS expression rather than to infer direct causal signaling effects.

Western Blot Analysis

Pancreatic cancer cells were collected and lysed on ice for 15–30 min with pre-chilled RIPA buffer (ProteinBio, China) to extract total cellular proteins. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE at 80 V for 30 min, followed by 120 V for 60 min, and then transferred onto PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system. The membranes were blocked with Beyotime blocking buffer for 1–2 h and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using ECL reagent (Millipore, USA) and visualized by chemiluminescence imaging.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from the cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (R401, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using HiScript II Q Select RT SuperMix (R23301; Vazyme). Quantitative PCR was performed using an ABI 7500 system with SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q511-02, Vazyme). Target gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are presented in Table S1.

Cell Proliferation Assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Briefly, 100 μL of the cell suspension was seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to attach. After the indicated incubation period, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to evaluate the number of viable cells.

Transwell Assays

For migration assays, 5×104pancreatic cancer cells suspended in serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (Corning, USA), whereas medium containing 10% FBS was placed in the lower chamber. After incubation for 24–72 h, the cells remaining on the upper surface were carefully removed. The cells that migrated through the membrane were fixed, stained, and counted under a microscope to assess their migratory ability.

RNA Modification

RNA methylation patterns, including m1A, m5C, and m6A modifications, were systematically analyzed using SangerBox by profiling the expression of the corresponding writers, erasers, and readers. By examining the correlations between these RNA modification signatures and HPGDS expression across multiple tumor types, widespread associations were identified, suggesting that HPGDS participates in a regulatory axis influencing cancer-related RNA methylation.

Drug Sensitivity Analysis

An unbiased comparative analysis of the drug screening data was performed using the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (GSCA) platform. This analysis incorporated more than 10,000 genomic datasets from 33 cancer types derived from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), as well as over 750 small-molecule compounds from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database and Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal.

Colony Formation Assay

Pancreatic cancer cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 500–1000 cells/well and cultured in complete medium. After attachment, the cells were treated with the indicated concentrations of the HPGDS inhibitor or the corresponding vehicle control. The culture medium was replaced every 2–3 d. After 10–14 days, the colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted using a microscope. Representative images were captured, and the assay was independently repeated at least three times.

Statistical Analysis

All in vitro experiments were conducted at least three times unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). For comparisons among multiple experimental groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc testing was used. For two-group comparisons, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used when data met parametric assumptions; otherwise, non-parametric tests were considered. Data are presented as mean? Standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. For public transcriptomic and single-cell analyses, multiple-testing correction or tool-provided adjusted significance values were used where available.

Results

HPGDS Is Aberrantly Expressed in Multiple Cancer Types and is Upregulated in Pancreatic Cancer

To characterize the expression landscape of HPGDS in human cancers, we first performed a pan-cancer analysis using TNMplot and related public datasets. HPGDS expression differed substantially between tumor and normal tissues across multiple cancer types (Figure 1A–C). Notably, HPGDS was significantly upregulated in pancreatic cancer tissues compared to normal pancreatic tissues, indicating that HPGDS dysregulation may be involved in pancreatic tumorigenesis.

Next, we validated the expression pattern of HPGDS in independent pancreatic cancer cohorts. In the GSE62452 dataset, HPGDS expression was significantly higher in pancreatic tumor tissues than in normal pancreatic tissues (Figure 2A). Analysis of the ICGC_PAAD_AU_array cohort further showed that HPGDS expression differed significantly among the pathological grades (Figure 2B), suggesting a potential association between HPGDS and the tumor differentiation status. Consistently, in the E-MTAB-6134 cohort, HPGDS expression was significantly associated with the T stage (Figure 2C). Additionally, analysis of the GSE71729 dataset revealed that HPGDS expression was significantly higher in primary tumors than in metastatic lesions (Figure 2D). A similar trend was observed in the integrated comparison among normal, tumor, and metastatic samples, although the difference did not reach conventional statistical significance (Figure 2E). Collectively, these findings indicate that HPGDS is aberrantly expressed in pancreatic cancer and may be associated with clinicopathological progression of the disease.

To experimentally validate these bioinformatic observations, we examined the HPGDS expression in pancreatic cell lines. Western blot analysis showed that HPGDS protein levels were elevated in pancreatic cancer cell lines compared to hTERT-HPNE cells (Figure 2G). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis confirmed increased HPGDS mRNA expression in BxPC-3, PANC-1, MIA PaCa-2, AsPC-1, and CFPAC-1 cells compared to hTERT-HPNE cells (Figure 2H). These results support the conclusion that HPGDS is upregulated in pancreatic cancer at both the transcriptional and protein levels.

HPGDS Expression is Associated with KRAS-Related Signaling, Genomic Features, and Immune Checkpoint Landscapes

To explore the biological relevance of HPGDS, we investigated its association with oncogenic signaling pathways. Gene set enrichment analysis showed that high HPGDS expression was significantly enriched in the KRAS signaling pathway (Figure 2F), suggesting a close relationship between HPGDS and KRAS-driven oncogenic programs in pancreatic cancer.

Given the importance of genomic instability in shaping tumor behavior and therapeutic response, we further analyzed the correlation between HPGDS and microsatellite instability (MSI) and tumor mutational burden (TMB) across cancers. Pan-cancer analysis demonstrated that HPGDS expression was significantly correlated with MSI and TMB in several tumor types (Figure 1D and E). In PAAD, HPGDS showed a significant association with MSI, whereas no obvious association with TMB was observed in the present panel, indicating that the relationship between HPGDS and genomic instability may be tumor type-dependent.

We then assessed the association between HPGDS and the immune checkpoint molecules. A pan-cancer heatmap revealed that HPGDS expression was positively correlated with multiple inhibitory immune checkpoint genes, including HAVCR2, PDCD1LG2, SIGLEC15, CD274, CTLA4, TIGIT, LAG3, and PDCD1, in many cancer types (Figure 1F). A second immune checkpoint correlation analysis further supported the extensive positive associations between HPGDS and immunoregulatory genes, such as LGALS9, CSF1R, HAVCR2, LAG3, PDCD1, IDO1, TGFB1, TGFBR1, CD274, BTLA, CTLA4, TIGIT, IL10, PDCD1LG2, CD244, and CD96 (Figure 3G). These findings suggest that HPGDS may be linked to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and may be relevant to immune evasion.

High HPGDS Expression Predicts Unfavorable Prognosis in Patients with PAAD

To determine the clinical significance of HPGDS in pancreatic cancer, survival analyses were performed on multiple independent cohorts. In the E-MTAB-6134 cohort, patients with high HPGDS expression exhibited significantly worse overall survival than those with low HPGDS expression (Figure 4A). This finding was independently validated in the GSE71729 cohort, in which elevated HPGDS expression was also associated with shorter overall survival (Figure 4B).

(D) BEST database shows the correlation between HPGDS and pancreatic cancer Disease-specific survival (P = 0.017). (E) BEST database shows the correlation between HPGDS and pancreatic cancer Disease-free survival (P = 0.0024). (F). Correlation between CTRP drug sensitivity and HPGDS mRNA expression.

Next, we evaluated the survival outcomes in the TCGA-PAAD cohort. High HPGDS expression was significantly associated with shorter progression-free survival (Figure 4C), worse disease-specific survival (Figure 4D), and reduced disease-free survival (Figure 4E). Together, these results consistently indicate that elevated HPGDS expression is associated with poor clinical outcomes in PAAD, supporting its potential value as a prognostic biomarker.

HPGDS Expression is Associated with Drug Response and May Be Related to Immunotherapy Response

To investigate the therapeutic relevance of HPGDS, we first analyzed the relationship between HPGDS expression and drug sensitivity using the CTRP-based data. HPGDS expression was significantly correlated with the sensitivity to multiple compounds, including ibrutinib, alvocidib, BMS-345541, BRD-K45653223, BRD6940, cytarabine hydrochloride, dinaciclib, LX-2183240, ML203, leospeptide, omacetaxine mepesuccinate, ouabain, and triptolide (Figure 4F). These findings suggest that HPGDS may be useful for therapeutic response stratification.

Because HPGDS showed a strong association with immune checkpoint molecules, we further evaluated its potential relationship with immunotherapy response in public cohorts. In the Ascierto anti-PD-1 cohort, HPGDS expression was significantly higher in non-responders than in responders (Figure 3A). Similar results were observed in the IMvigor210 anti-PD-L1 (Figure 3B) and Wolf anti-PD-L1 (Figure 3C) cohorts, indicating that elevated HPGDS expression may be associated with an inferior response to immune checkpoint blockade.

Receiver operating characteristic analysis further showed that HPGDS had predictive value for immunotherapy response, with AUC values of 0.857 in the Ascierto cohort, 0.622 in the IMvigor210 cohort, and 0.723 in the Wolf cohort (Figure 3D–F). Collectively, these data suggest that HPGDS may serve as a candidate biomarker related to immunotherapy response, although its predictive performance varies across cohorts and requires prospective validation.

HPGDS-Associated Transcriptional Programs Suggest Links to Immune Regulation and Metabolic Processes

To further characterize the molecular context of HPGDS in PAAD, patients were stratified into high- and low-HPGDS expression groups, and co-expression analyses were performed (Figure 5A and D). Genes positively correlated with HPGDS expression were significantly enriched in immune-related biological processes, including lymphocyte, mononuclear cell, and leukocyte proliferation; leukocyte migration; and negative regulation of leukocyte activation (Figure 5B). In contrast, negatively correlated genes were enriched in metabolic and catabolic processes, including small molecule catabolic processes, regulation of reactive oxygen species metabolic processes, regulation of cysteine-type endopeptidase activity, aldehyde catabolic processes, and ligand-gated ion channel signaling pathways (Figure 5C).

To explore the potential molecular interactions involving HPGDS, a protein-protein interaction network was constructed using STRING. HPGDS was associated with several metabolism-related and prostaglandin-associated genes, including PTGES, PTGDS, GSR, GSTT2B, SLC06A1, GSTZ1, EPHX1, CYP3A4, CYP2E1, and CYP1A1 (Figure 5E). These observations further support the involvement of HPGDS in immune regulation and metabolic remodeling.

HPGDS is Associated with Immune Cell Infiltration and is Preferentially Enriched in Specific Immune-Related Cell Populations at the Single-Cell Level

To further investigate the immunological relevance of HPGDS, we analyzed the association between HPGDS expression and immune cell infiltration in different cancers. Pan-cancer immune infiltration analysis revealed that HPGDS expression was significantly correlated with multiple immune cell populations, including B cell subsets, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, mast cells, NK cells, neutrophils, eosinophils, and regulatory T cells (Figure 6A). Notably, in PAAD, HPGDS was positively correlated with several immune-related cell subsets, particularly monocytes/macrophages and other stromal-immune compartments, further suggesting a role for HPGDS in shaping the immune microenvironment.

To determine the cellular distribution of HPGDS in pancreatic cancer, we integrated four scRNA-seq datasets comprising pancreatic tumor and matched normal samples. UMAP visualization showed a clear transcriptional separation between tumor and normal tissues (Figure 6B), and cell annotation identified the major cell populations, including T cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, acinar cells, endothelial cells, B cells, and ductal cells (Figure 6C). Mapping of HPGDS expression onto the single-cell landscape revealed that HPGDS was not uniformly expressed across all cell types but was preferentially enriched in a restricted subset of cells (Figure 6D). Further subclustering of T-cell populations identified distinct T-cell states, including Th1, CD4+, CD8+, regulatory, memory, and an unclassified T-cell cluster (Figure 6E).

These findings indicate that HPGDS exhibits cell type-specific expression patterns in the pancreatic tumor microenvironment, further supporting its close association with immune regulation.

HPGDS-High Cell Populations Exhibit Extensive Intercellular Communication within the Pancreatic Cancer Microenvironment

Given the non-uniform expression of HPGDS across cell populations, we explored whether HPGDS-high cells participate in distinct intercellular communication networks. Single-cell density mapping revealed that HPGDS expression was concentrated in a defined subset of cells (Figure 7A). CellChat analysis demonstrated that both HPGDS-positive and-negative cell populations were involved in multicellular communication networks; however, HPGDS-positive cells displayed broader and stronger interactions with other stromal and immune cell populations, including macrophages, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and T-cell subsets (Figure 7B and C).

Further ligand-receptor analysis suggested that HPGDS-associated communication involves multiple signaling axes, including VEGFA/VEGFB-VEGFR, TGFB1-TGFBR1/TGFBR2, SPP1-CD44/integrin, MIF-CD74/CXCR4, LIF-LIFR/IL6ST, CSF1-CSF1R, AREG-EGFR/ERBB2, and ANXA1-FPR1 pathways (Figure 7D). These signaling interactions are closely related to angiogenesis, inflammation, myeloid cell recruitment, stromal activation and immune modulation. Collectively, these data suggest that HPGDS-high cells may contribute to the establishment of a highly interactive and potentially immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in PAAD.

HPGDS Is Associated with Chemokine Receptor Networks and RNA Methylation-Related Regulators

As immune cell trafficking is tightly regulated by chemokine signaling, we further evaluated the relationship between HPGDS and chemokine receptor expression. Pan-cancer analysis showed that HPGDS expression was positively correlated with multiple chemokine receptors in many tumor types, including CCR1, CCR2, CCR3, CCR4, CCR5, CCR6, CCR7, CCR8, CCR9, CCR10, CXCR1, CXCR2, CXCR3, CXCR4, CXCR5, CXCR6, and XCR1 (Figure 8A). In PAAD, HPGDS displayed predominantly positive associations with several chemokine receptors, further supporting its close link to immune cell recruitment and remodeling of the immune microenvironment.

We also examined the association between HPGDS and RNA methylation-related regulators in various cancers. Correlation analysis revealed widespread positive relationships between HPGDS and multiple m1A-, m5C-, and m6A-related genes, including writers, readers, and erasers (Figure 8B). These results suggest that HPGDS may be functionally connected to epitranscriptomic regulatory programs, although the precise mechanistic basis of this relationship requires further investigation.

Pharmacological Inhibition and Genetic Silencing of HPGDS Suppress Pancreatic Cancer Cell Proliferation

To explore the functional significance of HPGDS in pancreatic cancer cells, we first evaluated the effects of HPGDS inhibition in MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells. CCK-8 assays showed that HPGDS inhibition reduced cell viability in both cell lines in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 9A). Consistently, colony formation assays demonstrated a marked decrease in clonogenic growth following HPGDS inhibition (Figure 9C). These data indicate that HPGDS activity contributes to the proliferation of pancreatic cancer cells.

To further validate this effect, we knocked down HPGDS using shRNAs in PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells. qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed the efficient silencing of HPGDS in both cell lines (Figure 10A and B). Functionally, HPGDS knockdown significantly suppressed cell proliferation, as shown by CCK-8 assays in both PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells (Figure 10C). Collectively, these findings support the growth-promoting role of HPGDS in pancreatic cancer.

HPGDS Inhibition Reduces Invasive and Migratory Properties, whereas Genetic Knockdown Exerts Limited Effects on Transwell Migration Under the Tested Conditions.

Next, we examined whether HPGDS influences the migratory behavior of pancreatic cancer cells. Pharmacological inhibition of HPGDS markedly decreased transwell migration and invasion-related phenotypes in both MIA PaCa-2 and PANC-1 cells, with stronger effects observed at higher inhibitor concentrations (Figure 9B). These findings suggest that HPGDS activity may contribute to motility-associated phenotypes in pancreatic cancer cells.

However, when HPGDS was silenced by shRNA, the effects on transwell migration appeared limited under the experimental conditions used in the present study. In PANC-1 cells, one shRNA construct showed a modest reduction in transwell cell counts, whereas the second construct showed no significant difference; in MIA PaCa-2 cells, neither shRNA produced a significant change (Figure 10D). Thus, although pharmacological inhibition consistently impaired cell motility-related phenotypes, genetic knockdown produced weaker or less consistent effects in the transwell assays performed in this study.

Taken together, these data indicate that HPGDS is functionally involved in pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and may also contribute to motility-associated behavior, although the latter effect appears context-dependent and warrants further mechanistic investigation.

Discussion

Biomarkers are critical for the diagnosis and treatment of pancreatic cancer.7,12 Given the highly malignant nature of pancreatic cancer, its low rate of early detection, and poor prognosis, the identification of reliable biomarkers may provide new strategies for early diagnosis, prognostic evaluation, and therapeutic stratification.13–15 For early diagnosis, biomarkers such as CA19-9, S100P, MUC1, and MSLN have been used, although CA19-9 has limited specificity.16 In prognostic assessments, KRAS mutations and abnormalities in SMAD4 and TOP2A have been associated with poor outcomes.17–19 Biomarkers may also help define immune and metabolic states that influence therapeutic response; for example, MSLN has emerged as a candidate target for immunotherapy, whereas ERCC1 and RRM1 may predict responses to chemotherapy.20–23

Our study revealed that HPGDS is upregulated in pancreatic cancer tissues and cell lines and is associated with tumor differentiation, prognosis, immune checkpoint expression, and immune infiltration. These findings support the view that HPGDS is closely linked to immune microenvironment remodeling in pancreatic cancer. Trotta et al recently reported that activated T cells can break tumor immunosuppression through macrophage reeducation and identified HPGDS-expressing immunosuppressive macrophage states as relevant to anti-PD-1 treatment biology.11 This external evidence is consistent with our observation that HPGDS expression is associated with immune checkpoint molecules and stromal-immune communication programs. However, the immunotherapy-related findings in this study are primarily derived from retrospective public cohorts and correlation analyses. Therefore, HPGDS should be interpreted as a candidate biomarker associated with immune checkpoint response rather than as a clinically validated predictor or established therapeutic target.

Arachidonic acid (AA) plays a key role in tumor progression and inflammatory disease biology. Its metabolites, including prostaglandins, thromboxanes, and leukotrienes, promote tumor growth by activating signaling pathways involved in proliferation and survival, enhancing invasiveness, and modulating the tumor microenvironment to support angiogenesis and immune evasion.24–27 Recent work on arachidonate 15-lipoxygenase in acute pancreatitis further highlights the therapeutic relevance of arachidonic-acid-metabolizing enzymes in pancreatic inflammatory injury.28 In pancreatic cancer, TRIM21 inhibits EPHX1-mediated AA metabolism, reducing arachidonic acid levels and ferroptosis, thereby promoting cancer cell proliferation and gemcitabine resistance.29 HDAC5 deficiency also enhances AA metabolism by upregulating cPLA2, a key enzyme in this pathway.30 These findings suggest that AA metabolism and lipid-inflammatory signaling may provide biological context for studying HPGDS in pancreatic cancer.

HPGDS, a key enzyme in AA metabolism that catalyzes the conversion of PGH2 to PGD2, plays a multifaceted role in tumors. In lung cancer, HPGDS has been associated with lipid metabolism and tumor aggressiveness.10,31 In the present study, HPGDS was associated with immune-related transcriptional programs, chemokine receptor networks, and extensive intercellular communication involving stromal and immune populations. These observations are consistent with a possible role for HPGDS in shaping an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Nevertheless, these data do not establish that HPGDS directly drives immune evasion. Future studies should determine whether HPGDS acts mainly in tumor cells, macrophage-lineage cells, T-cell subsets, or other stromal-immune compartments, and whether modulation of HPGDS can alter anti-tumor immunity in vivo.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, many analyses were based on retrospective public datasets, which may contain cohort heterogeneity, treatment differences, sampling bias, and unmeasured confounding variables. Second, the association between HPGDS and immunotherapy response was inferred from public immunotherapy cohorts and requires validation in pancreatic cancer-specific clinical cohorts. Third, the functional experiments support a role for HPGDS in cell proliferation, but the migration and invasion findings were less consistent between pharmacological inhibition and shRNA-mediated knockdown. This discrepancy may reflect differences in assay conditions, inhibitor effects, knockdown efficiency, compensatory pathways, or cell-line-specific biology. Finally, this study did not include in vivo validation, and the influence of the pancreatic tumor mesenchyme and immune microenvironment should be evaluated in future animal and clinical studies.

Conclusions

Our study revealed that HPGDS is highly expressed in pancreatic cancer tissues and cell lines and is associated with low tumor differentiation, unfavorable prognosis, and immune microenvironment remodeling. Functional assays support a role for HPGDS in pancreatic cancer cell proliferation, whereas its contribution to migration and invasion requires further clarification. These findings suggest that HPGDS may serve as a candidate prognostic and immune-related biomarker in PAAD, but additional mechanistic, in vivo, and clinical studies are needed before HPGDS can be considered a validated therapeutic or immunotherapy target.
