Collaborative LLM Inference via Planning for Efficient Reasoning
Byeongchan Lee, Jonghoon Lee, Dongyoung Kim, Jaehyung Kim, Jinwoo Shin

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) excel at complex reasoning tasks, but those with strong capabilities (e.g., whose numbers of parameters are larger than 100B) are often accessible only through paid APIs, making them too costly for applications of frequent use. In contrast, smaller open-sourced LLMs (e.g., whose numbers of parameters are less than 3B) are freely available and easy to deploy locally (e.g., under a single GPU having 8G VRAM), but lack sufficient reasoning ability. This trade-off raises a natural question: can small (free) and large (costly) models collaborate at test time to combine their strengths? We propose a test-time collaboration framework in which a planner model first generates a plan, defined as a distilled and high-level abstraction of the problem. This plan serves as a lightweight intermediate that guides a reasoner model, which generates a complete solution. Small and large models take turns acting as planner and reasoner, exchanging plans in a multi-round cascade to collaboratively solve complex tasks. Our method achieves accuracy comparable to strong proprietary models alone, while significantly reducing reliance on paid inference. These results highlight planning as an effective prior for orchestrating cost-aware, cross-model inference under real-world deployment constraints.

Introduction

Language language models (LLMs) have achieved impressive performance on complex problem-solving tasks
(
Rae et al. 2021
;
Lewkowycz et al. 2022
)
. However, deploying them at scale presents a critical trade-off between reasoning ability and computational efficiency. Modern models vary widely in both capability and cost. As larger models continue to improve in capability, they also grow increasingly costly to run
(
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Schick et al. 2023
)
. Smaller models, in contrast, are far more deployable, but their limited capacity hinders them on complex tasks
(
Kojima et al. 2022
;
Wei et al. 2022
)
.

This tradeoff is especially clear in realistic deployment scenarios. For example, users typically interact with language models via mobile or edge devices, which cannot host large models
(
Leviathan et al. 2023
;
Chowdhery et al. 2023
)
. Instead, large models often reside in the cloud and incur substantial inference costs. In this setting, relying solely on large models for every request is both economically and practically infeasible. These constraints call for inference strategies that make effective use of small models when possible, while selectively leveraging large models only when necessary. For clarity, we use the terms free vs. costly, small vs. large, and weak vs. strong interchangeably to refer to models with different capacities and cost characteristics, depending on context.

While several works have explored small–large model collaboration at inference time, most focus on reducing latency rather than cost. For instance, speculative decoding
(
Stern et al. 2018
;
Leviathan et al. 2023
;
Xia et al. 2022
)
speeds up generation by having a small model draft token sequences that a large model verifies. However, the large model is still invoked on every query, and thus the overall cost remains high.

Relatively few methods address cost-aware inference, where large models are used sparingly at test time. Representatively, LLM Cascade
(
Yue et al. 2023
)
proposes routing between small and large models based on internal confidence signals. However, it remains a form of shallow delegation: the stronger model only steps in after the weaker model fails, and the two models do not communicate or coordinate their reasoning processes. This limits their ability to jointly solve complex problems in a structured and efficient manner. We discuss other methods that require task-specific training in Section
2
.

Figure 1:
Overall framework of COPE. The system proceeds in up to three rounds of inference, where small and large models alternate roles as planner and reasoner. In each round, a plan is generated to guide the reasoning process, and answers are obtained via sampling and voting. If no consensus is reached, the problem escalates to the next round. Plans generated in earlier rounds are retained and reused to inform reasoning in later rounds.

In contrast, we propose to structure this collaboration through
planning
. Instead of directly solving a problem, a model first produces a high-level abstraction of the task (such as a goal or a guideline), which we call a plan. A plan guides downstream reasoning by another model. This planning step serves as a lightweight, transferable intermediate that allows models to scaffold each other’s thinking without any additional supervision.

To realize this idea, we introduce COPE (Collaborative Planning and Execution), a modular inference framework in which free and costly models share the roles of planner and reasoner. The process begins with a free model attempting both planning and reasoning. If the model lacks confidence in its answer, planning and then reasoning are gradually escalated to a costly model. This structure allows models to collaborate adaptively: easy problems are handled by free models using simple plans, and harder problems are handled by costly models with more detailed ones.

Our contributions.

We summarize our main contributions as follows:

•

We show that planning can help reasoning. In particular, it addresses structural limitations of language models’ autoregressive generation (lack of global foresight). Planning introduces a task-level abstraction prior to token-level generation, enabling more coherent reasoning.

•

We propose COPE, a modular test-time inference framework in which small and large models take turns acting as planner and reasoner. COPE enables structured collaboration via intermediate plans without requiring any additional training.

•

COPE significantly reduces inference cost while matching or even surpassing the performance of much larger models. For example, on the MATH-500 dataset, COPE achieves 75.8% accuracy (higher than GPT-4o’s 75.2%) while reducing cost by nearly 45%.

Planning for reasoning.

Planning has been primarily studied in robotics, agent systems, and, more recently, in vision-language-action models as a core mechanism for structured decision-making
(
Kim et al. 2024
;
Black et al. 2023
)
. A high-level planning module decomposes a task into subgoals, which are then carried out through low-level execution by downstream components. This separation supports modularity, adaptivity, and efficient problem-solving across a wide range of settings.

In contrast, planning remains relatively underexplored in the context of LLM inference.
Wang et al.
propose the use of planning tokens prepended to each chain-of-thought step. However, this method requires supervised training to learn the tokens, and does not separate planning from reasoning. It focuses on local step-wise annotations rather than providing global guidance.

Saha et al. 2025
introduce EvalPlanner, a framework for LLM-as-a-Judge
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
evaluation. Given a pair of responses, the model generates a domain-specific evaluation plan to structure its judgment. The plan is produced after seeing the responses and is used post hoc to guide evaluation, rather than to inform the generation process. The method is trained over both plan construction and reasoning traces.

In contrast to these approaches, our method introduces planning as a zero-shot process that occurs before reasoning. The plan is a purpose-driven representation of the given problem (such as a goal or a guideline). Because planning is not learned and is decoupled from reasoning, it serves as a lightweight interface between models of different capacities. Planning can be seen as a feature of System-2 reasoning, supporting more structured thinking
(
Ji et al. 2025
)
.

Test-time compute scaling.

Test-time compute scaling
(
Snell et al. 2024
)
refers to how computational resources are allocated and utilized at inference time to improve accuracy. These efforts typically fall into two directions:
parallel sampling
, which generates multiple outputs and selects the best among them
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
, and
sequential sampling
(or iterative refinement), where a model revises its outputs over multiple passes
(
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Zelikman et al. 2022
;
Lee et al. 2025
;
Kumar et al. 2024
;
Muennighoff et al. 2025
)
.

These techniques typically aim to improve performance by allocating more compute to a single model. However, they remain limited by the capacity of the model itself. Once a model’s reasoning abilities are exceeded, performance saturates
(
Snell et al. 2024
)
. This motivates a different approach: rather than pushing a single model further, we explore collaboration across models of different capacities. This form of cross-model scaling can be seen as a new axis of test-time compute scaling.

Cost-aware LLM inference.

Some recent approaches aim to reduce the cost of LLM inference by selectively using powerful models only when necessary. LLM Cascade
(
Yue et al. 2023
)
leverages a mixture of reasoning formats (Chain of Thought (CoT) and Program of Thought (PoT)) by providing few-shot examples in each style and sampling outputs to estimate answer confidence. Based on this confidence signal, it routes between small and large models. However, this approach relies on curated few-shot examples and treats models as isolated units, without explicit collaboration.

FrugalGPT
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
learns both a scoring function to estimate answer reliability and a routing policy to decide when to escalate to stronger models. AutoMix
(
Aggarwal et al. 2024
)
similarly trains a routing policy, using confidence scores derived from verification prompts. The router is optimized under a POMDP formulation to balance cost and accuracy. However, both methods require task-specific supervision. In contrast, our approach involves no training or learned routing. We therefore exclude them from our baselines, as they target a different setup of supervised routing.

Motivation

In this section, we hypothesize that generating a simple plan can help guide reasoning, and introduce motivational examples and the insights drawn from them.
In this work, a
plan
refers to a high-level abstraction of the task that captures its essential structure. More precisely, it is a purpose-oriented representation that narrows the solution space and serves as a guide for reasoning. Plans may take different forms, and we present concrete examples such as goals and guidelines later.

Given a problem, we first prompt the planner model to generate a brief guideline, typically one or two sentences, that outlines how to approach the task. This guideline is then provided, along with the original problem, to the reasoner model. The following observations illustrate how this planning stage enables collaboration between models.

We use Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, and GPT-4o-mini, hereafter referred to as Llama-1B, Llama-3B, and GPT-mini, respectively. Llama-1B and Llama-3B are open-source and freely accessible, while GPT-mini is accessed via a paid API.

Observation 1.

Larger planners help smaller reasoners.

We begin by examining whether larger models can improve the performance of smaller ones by providing plans. As shown in Table
1
, when a larger model acts as a planner for a smaller reasoner, such as GPT-mini planning for Llama-3B or Llama-3B planning for Llama-1B, the reasoner’s accuracy improves over the no-plan baseline. For example, Llama-3B achieves 42.8% accuracy without planning, which rises to 53.0% when guided by GPT-mini. Likewise, Llama-1B improves from 25.2% to 36.4% when using plans from Llama-3B. This suggests that larger models are effective at guiding smaller models through planning.

Observation 2.

Smaller planners degrade larger reasoners.

We next examine whether small models can effectively serve as planners for larger reasoners. As shown in Table
1
, when small models such as Llama-1B or Llama-3B act as planners for more capable models like GPT-mini or Llama-3B, the resulting performance is often lower than the no-plan baseline. For instance, GPT-mini achieves 73.6% without a plan, but drops to 70.6% and 69.6% when using plans from Llama-3B and Llama-1B, respectively. This suggests that low-quality plans generated by smaller models can hinder the reasoning ability of larger models.

Observation 3.

A model benefits from plans aligned with its capacity.

We investigate whether models can effectively act as their own planners. For large models such as GPT-mini, self-generated plans improve performance: GPT-mini achieves 73.6% accuracy without planning, which increases to 76.2% when using its own plans (Table
1
). This suggests that large models can successfully scaffold their own reasoning through planning.

We introduce COPE, a collaborative inference framework in which small and large models alternate roles as planners and reasoners across multiple rounds. The core idea is to structure model collaboration by first generating an intermediate
plan
, which then guides downstream reasoning. Figure
1
illustrates the overall structure of COPE.

COPE operates in three rounds. In Round 1, a small model attempts to solve the problem on its own by generating both a plan and a solution. In Round 2, a large model provides a new plan, and the small model reattempts reasoning with this guidance. In Round 3, the large model assumes both roles to directly solve the problem. Each round is triggered only when the previous one fails to reach sufficient consensus, enabling adaptive allocation of compute. We now describe how each round operates in more detail. Figure
2
shows an example of plan generation and reasoning based on that plan, which serves as a core module in COPE’s multi-round inference.

Round 1: small model planning and reasoning

In Round 1, the small model acts as both planner and reasoner. Given an input
x
, it first samples
n
plans
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\sim P_{\text{small}}(g\mid x)
. For each plan
g_{i}
, it then generates a solution
y_{i}\sim P_{\text{small}}(y\mid x,g_{i})
, and extracts the corresponding answer
a_{i}=f(y_{i})
, where
f
denotes an answer extraction function.

The final answer is determined by majority vote over the answer set
\mathcal{A}:=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
, treating agreement among solutions as an indicator of correctness
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Du et al. 2023
)
. We define the
consensus ratio
as the fraction of samples that agree on the most frequent answer:

\text{Consensus}(\mathcal{A}):=\frac{\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\{i:a_{i}=a^{\prime}\}\right|}{n}.

(1)

If the consensus exceeds a threshold
\tau_{1}
, the answer is accepted. Otherwise, the problem is escalated to the next round. In that case, one of the plans
g_{i}
that produced the majority-voted answer is randomly selected and designated as
g^{\text{S}}
, which is passed to Round 2.

Round 2: large model planning, small model reasoning

If Round 1 fails to produce a confident answer, Round 2 begins with the large model generating a new plan
g^{\text{L}}\sim P_{\text{large}}(g\mid x)
. This plan is passed to the small model, which then attempts to solve the problem again by leveraging both the original plan
g^{\text{S}}
from Round 1 and the new plan
g^{\text{L}}
. These two plans are provided jointly as input to the small model in the prompt.

Then, the small model generates a solution
y_{i}\sim P_{\text{small}}(y\mid x,g^{\text{S}},g^{\text{L}})
and corresponding answer
a_{i}=f(y_{i})
. Majority voting is applied over the answer set
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
as in Round 1. If the consensus ratio exceeds a second threshold
\tau_{2}
, the answer is accepted. Otherwise, the problem is escalated to Round 3.

Round 3: large model planning and reasoning

If the small model still fails to produce a confident answer in Round 2, the problem is escalated to Round 3. Here, the large model takes full control of both planning and reasoning.

Specifically, it reuses the plan
g^{\text{L}}
that it generated in Round 2, and samples
y\sim P_{\text{large}}(y\mid x,g^{\text{L}})
. The final answer is extracted as
a=f(y)
. Since this is the final round, no further escalation occurs, and the answer is returned as the system’s output.

5
Experiments

We evaluate COPE on multiple reasoning benchmarks spanning mathematics and programming. Our experiments aim to assess both accuracy and cost-efficiency across tasks of varying difficulty. Unless otherwise noted, we fix
n=8
,
\tau_{1}=0.75
, and
\tau_{2}=0.5
throughout all experiments. An empirical study on the effect of these hyperparameters is provided in Appendix
A
. Our implementation builds upon and modifies the codebase from the
search-and-learn
repository by Hugging Face
(
Beeching et al.
)
.

Baselines.

As a baseline, we implement the method proposed in
Yue et al. 2023
, referred to as
Cascade
. This approach utilizes a two-stage prompting strategy involving verification and fallback. Initially, a small model generates multiple candidate answers, and confidence is estimated based on their agreement. If the confidence threshold is not met at this stage, the problem is immediately escalated to a large model
1
1

1

In our experiments, we apply few-shot prompting with 4-shot examples for the Cascade baseline. The original Cascade even applies majority voting for the large model, but we restrict majority voting to the small model and use single inference for the large model to ensure a fair comparison with our approach.

.

Unlike COPE, which enables iterative collaboration between small and large models across multiple rounds through zero-shot, model-generated plans, Cascade treats small and large models independently without direct interaction, typically operating in two stages. Additionally, Cascade relies on few-shot exemplars, whereas COPE uses zero-shot planning with explicit intermediate representations to guide reasoning.

Cascade includes two decision strategies: vote and verify. In the vote setting, multiple responses are sampled from two different few-shot prompts, and the final answer is selected by majority voting across all the responses. If the consensus ratio exceeds a threshold, the answer is accepted. Otherwise, the problem is escalated to a stronger LLM. In the verify setting, majority voting is applied separately to responses from each few-shot prompt, and the final decision is accepted only if the two majority-voted answers agree. Otherwise, the problem is escalated to the stronger LLM.

We also consider three simpler baselines. The first uses only the small model in a single-pass setting without any sampling or aggregation (
Small (single)
). The second baseline uses the small model to generate multiple outputs and applies majority voting to select the most frequent answer (
Small (majority)
). The third baseline runs the large model once without planning or verification (
Large
).

5.1
Mathematical reasoning

Datasets.

We use the MATH-500 dataset
(
Lightman et al. 2023
)
and the AIME-2024 dataset
(
Art of Problem Solving 2024
)
. MATH-500 is a filtered subset of the MATH dataset
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
consisting of 500 high-quality math problems. The problems cover a broad range of topics and difficulty levels. MATH-500 requires multi-step reasoning and structured inference, featuring significantly greater complexity and abstraction that make it a challenging testbed. Compared to MATH-500, AIME-2024 problems demand deeper insight, clever problem-solving strategies, and precise multi-step reasoning. AIME-2024 consists of 30 curated olympiad-level problems. It has recently emerged as a focal dataset in mathematical reasoning research, providing a challenging benchmark.

Evaluation metrics.

We evaluate accuracy (%) using exact match. For mathematical answers, we use symbolic comparison to accept equivalent expressions (e.g.,
1/2
and
0.5
). For non-mathematical outputs, exact string match is used. For cost, we use the actual per-problem API price based on real-world model pricing
(
OpenAI 2025
;
Google DeepMind 2024
)
. We scale the values by one million for readability, reporting cost in USD per million problems.

Models.

For the MATH-500 dataset, we use LG-EXAONE-3.5-2.4B-Instruct
(
Research 2024
)
as the small model, and GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini as the large models. For the more challenging AIME-2024 dataset, we use Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct
(
Team 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
as the small model, and Gemini-2.0-Flash as the large model, considering the increased difficulty of the problems.

Results.

Table
3
reports the results on mathematical reasoning. Interestingly, COPE outperforms the large model baseline in overall accuracy, with 75.8% vs. 75.2% for GPT-4o and 74.4% vs. 73.8% for GPT-4o-mini. This suggests that collaborative planning enables small models to solve certain problems that large models alone cannot. COPE also reduces inference cost significantly, with 2766 compared to 5060 for GPT-4o, and 202 compared to 355 for GPT-4o-mini. This corresponds to nearly a 50 percent reduction in cost while maintaining or improving accuracy.

Despite operating in a zero-shot setting, COPE outperforms few-shot baselines such as Cascade (vote) and Cascade (verify) in both accuracy and cost. For example, with GPT-4o-mini, COPE achieves 74.4 compared to 70.6 and 72.2 for Cascade (vote) and Cascade (verify), respectively, while also reducing cost from 226 and 250 to 202.

At Levels 3 through 5, COPE consistently outperforms Cascade baselines in both accuracy and cost. This indicates that COPE is more cost-efficient under increasing task complexity. We attribute this to the use of planning, which enables small models to reason more effectively and reduces unnecessary reliance on large models. The benefit is particularly evident on difficult problems. For example, at Level 5, small model accuracy improves from 34.3 to 55.2 and 52.2 under COPE with GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini, respectively.

5.2
Code generation tasks

For code generation tasks, we adopt a pass-based evaluation instead of majority voting, since multiple correct outputs may differ syntactically. Each generated output is executed against the task’s test cases. If it passes all test cases, it is considered correct. Among all passing outputs, one is randomly selected as the final answer. For the Cascade baseline, we adopt the same strategy: multiple outputs are generated, and one that passes all test cases is randomly selected as the final answer. If none pass, the task is escalated to the next round.

Datasets.

We use the 500-problem test set of the MBPP dataset
(
Austin et al. 2021
)
, which consists of crowd-sourced Python code generation tasks. Each task includes a natural language task description, a reference solution, and test cases.

Evaluation metrics.

We evaluate performance using the separate hidden test cases from the MBPP-ET dataset
(
Dong et al. 2025
)
. A generated program is considered correct if it passes all hidden test cases.

Models.

Similar trends hold for GPT-4o-mini, where cost drops from 159 to 77. These results confirm the effectiveness of COPE in a different domain beyond mathematical reasoning.

Theoretical Interpretation

Each inference round (Figure
3
) follows three steps: (i) sample a
plan

g
, (ii) generate a solution
y
conditioned on
g
, and (iii) aggregate the extracted answers by majority vote. We formalise the first round (the template for all rounds) with a concise latent
-
variable model.

Latent variable model

We formalize prediction as computing the most likely answer
a=f(y)
, where
y
is a solution and
x
is the input problem:

\hat{a}=f\left(\arg\max_{y}P(y\mid x)\right),

(2)

where
f(\cdot)
denotes an answer extraction function that maps a complete solution
y
(e.g., a reasoning trace) to its final answer
a
(e.g., a scalar result).

Although one could sample solutions directly from
P(y\mid x)
, we instead introduce a latent plan
g
to structure the generation process. This yields the decomposition:

\displaystyle P(y\mid x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g}P(y\mid g,x)\cdot P(g\mid x).

(3)

This decomposition enables exploration of diverse reasoning strategies, each corresponding to a distinct plan. We sample from the resulting process by drawing
g_{i}\sim P(g\mid x)
and then
y_{i}\sim P(y\mid g_{i},x)
.

This process yields a set of solutions
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
sampled from
P(y\mid x)
.
From each, we extract an answer
a_{i}=f(y_{i})
.
We then approximate the objective in Eq. (
2
) via majority voting:

f\left(\arg\max_{y}P(y\mid x)\right)\approx\text{mode}(f(y_{1}),\ldots,f(y_{n})).

(4)

This procedure offers a simple approximation to the most likely answer under
P(y\mid x)
.
Since higher-probability
y_{i}
are sampled more often, their corresponding answers
f(y_{i})
dominate the vote.
The latent plan
g
encourages diversity by inducing varied reasoning strategies.
This improves robustness, helping recover correct answers that might be missed by direct decoding.

Bayesian inference

From a Bayesian perspective, we are interested in the posterior predictive distribution
P(a\mid x)
over answers:

\sum_{g}\int_{y}\delta(f(y)=a)\,P(y\mid g,x)\,P(g\mid x)\,dy,

(5)

where
P(g\mid x)
serves as a prior over plans and
P(y\mid g,x)
is the likelihood of a solution path.

We approximate this distribution via Monte Carlo sampling:
g_{i}\sim P(g\mid x)
,
y_{i}\sim P(y\mid g_{i},x)
, and set
a_{i}=f(y_{i})
.
Majority voting over
\{a_{i}\}
yields an estimate
\hat{a}\approx\arg\max_{a}P(a\mid x)
.

We treat the consensus ratio as an empirical estimate of
P(a=\hat{a}\mid x)
, and use it to decide whether to accept the prediction or escalate.

7
Conclusions

We introduced COPE, a test-time inference framework where small and large language models collaborate via planning. By exchanging lightweight plans across planner–reasoner roles, COPE improves accuracy while reducing reliance on large models. These findings highlight cross-model planning as a promising direction for scalable and efficient LLM deployment.

Limitations

We focus on mathematics and code generation tasks, where goals and solution guidelines are relatively well-defined. These domains offer a natural fit for evaluating structured, planning-based collaboration, though generalization to more open-ended tasks may require task-specific adaptation.

Additionally, while COPE reduces overall inference cost by limiting reliance on large models, it may introduce additional latency in interactive applications due to its multi-round execution process.

Broader Impact

As AI systems increasingly span both edge devices and the cloud, collaborative inference frameworks like COPE may help shape efficient and scalable deployment strategies. By reducing unnecessary compute, this approach can also contribute to more sustainable AI usage.

In addition, COPE reduces reliance on expensive proprietary models, making high-quality language model inference more accessible to users with limited resources. This may support broader adoption of AI tools across diverse settings, including education, research, and local deployment.

Appendix B
Further Theoretical Interpretation

Our approach shares several structural similarities with mixture-of-experts (MoE) models
(
Jordan and Jacobs 1994
;
Cai et al. 2024
)
:

First, sampling a plan
g\sim P(g\mid x)
corresponds to selecting an expert based on the input, akin to input-dependent gating. Second, each plan defines a distinct strategy for solving the problem, much like how individual experts specialize in different regions of the input space. Third, given a selected plan, the model generates a solution via
P(y\mid g,x)
, which parallels how an expert produces an output conditioned on its assignment.

Appendix G
Qualitative Examples

Vanilla

Problem:

Six cars pull up to a red light, one at a time. At the light, there are three lanes, one left-turn lane, one straight-going lane, and one right-turn lane. How many ways can the cars stack up so that all three lanes are occupied? Note that if the first car turns left and the second goes straight, this is considered different from the first car going straight and the second car turning left. In other words, the cars are distinguishable, but pull up to the intersection in a fixed order.

Solution (reasoner):

To solve this problem, we need to determine the number of ways six distinguishable cars can be arranged such that each lane (left-turn, straight-going, right-turn) is occupied by exactly one car.
Step-by-Step Solution:
Step 1: Assigning Cars to Lanes

Since there are three lanes and six cars, one lane will remain empty.
We need to choose which lane will be empty. There are 3 ways to choose which lane will be empty (left-turn, straight-going, or right-turn).
Step 2: Arranging Cars in Chosen Lanes
Once we have chosen which lane will be empty, we need to arrange the remaining five cars in the three lanes. The number of ways to arrange
n
distinguishable objects in
k
distinguishable bins is given by
k^{n}
. Here, we have 5 cars to arrange in 3 lanes:
3^{5}=243

Step 3: Combining Both Steps
Since there are 3 ways to choose which lane will be empty and for each choice, there are
3^{5}
ways to arrange the cars in the remaining lanes:
3\times 243=729

Therefore, the total number of ways the cars can stack up so that all three lanes are occupied is:

\boxed{729}

COPE

Problem:

Six cars pull up to a red light, one at a time. [...]

Goal (planner):

The goal of the problem is to determine the number of distinct permutations of six distinguishable cars parking in three lanes (left turn, straight, right turn) such that each lane is occupied by at least one car.

Guideline (planner):

To solve this problem, first determine the total number of ways to assign the cars to the three lanes such that each lane has at least one car. This can be achieved using the
principle of inclusion-exclusion
: calculate the total arrangements of cars in the lanes, subtract the arrangements where at least one lane is empty, and then add back arrangements where two lanes are empty. Finally, ensure that each arrangement respects the order of the cars.

Solution (reasoner):

To solve this problem efficiently using combinatorial principles, we need to ensure that each lane (left-turn, straight-going, right-turn) has at least one car while accommodating six distinguishable cars in three lanes sequentially.
Step-by-Step Solution:
Step 1: Total Arrangements Without Restrictions.
First, calculate the total number of ways to assign six cars into three lanes without any restrictions. Each car has 3 choices (left, straight, right):
3^{6}=729
.
Step 2: Subtract Arrangements Where At Least One Lane is Empty.
Next, subtract the cases where at least one lane is empty. We use the
principle of inclusion-exclusion
for this purpose:
One Lane Empty: Choose which lane is empty (3 ways), and distribute the cars among the remaining two lanes (
2^{6}
ways for each choice):
\binom{3}{1}\cdot 2^{6}=3\cdot 64=192
.
Two Lanes Empty: Choose which two lanes are empty (
\binom{3}{2}
ways), and place all cars into the remaining lane (1 way):
\binom{3}{2}\cdot 1^{6}=3\cdot 1=3
.
Using
inclusion-exclusion
:
729-192+3=540
.
Step 3: Ensure Each Lane Has At Least One Car.
Since we already ensured each lane had at least one car through subtraction in Step 2, no further adjustment is needed here.
Conclusion.
Therefore, the number of ways to stack up the six cars such that all three lanes are occupied is:
\boxed{540}
