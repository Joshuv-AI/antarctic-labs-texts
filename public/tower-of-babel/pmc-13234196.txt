Construction of multi-parameter intelligent comprehensive estimation algorithms for soil compression modulus
Authors: Reza Sarkhani Benemaran, Erfan Khajavi, Amir Reza Taghavi Khanghah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of soil stress–strain behavior remains a major challenge in geotechnical engineering due to the inherent heterogeneity, nonlinearity, and sparsity of soil datasets. Conventional laboratory and in-situ testing methods are often expensive, time-consuming, and sensitive to sampling disturbances, which limits their efficiency in large-scale engineering applications. To address these challenges, this study proposes an optimized stacking ensemble framework that integrates advanced tree-based learning algorithms with metaheuristic optimization. The selected base learners Light Gradient Boosting (LGB), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Random Forest (RFR), and Histogram-based Gradient Boosting (HGB) were chosen for their complementary strengths in capturing nonlinear interactions, handling high-dimensional inputs, and maintaining robustness under sparse and heterogeneous data conditions. These models are optimized using the Puma Optimization (PO) algorithm and combined through a stacking strategy to enhance predictive stability and generalization performance. A dataset comprising 1,410 samples was compiled literature data, witch the K-fold cross-validation was employed to evaluate model robustness. The proposed stacking model, particularly the optimized XGBPO ensemble, achieved superior predictive accuracy with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9914 in the testing phase, outperforming individual and hybrid models. Interpretability and sensitivity analyses further identified dry density (), void ratio (e0), and degree of saturation () as the most influential factors governing soil compressibility behavior. The proposed framework provides a scalable, reliable, and computationally efficient alternative to traditional geotechnical testing methods, offering improved predictive accuracy and practical applicability for infrastructure design and decision-making under complex soil conditions.Keywords: Stress–strain behavior, Stacking ensemble, Metaheuristic algorithm, Individual conditional expectationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Literature review

The soil compression modulus () is a critical geotechnical parameter, defined as the ratio of additional vertical stress to the corresponding strain increment in a soil column under fully constrained lateral boundaries1,2. In urban subsurface strata, exhibits pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity, variability, and lateral discontinuity, making it a key factor in urban geohazards such as land subsidence, surface collapses, and earth fissures3,4. Additionally, significantly influences engineering challenges, including the entrapment of tunnel boring machines ( s) in soft strata5, which often results in considerable economic losses and safety risks6,7. As a primary determinant of soil compressibility, governs ground deformation and is essential for analyzing anisotropic deformation in soft soils. However, obtaining accurate values through traditional geotechnical methods, such as triaxial compression tests or in-situ pressure meter tests, is time-intensive, labor-intensive, and constrained by the limited number of samples that can be analyzed. These methods also face reliability issues due to potential disturbances during sample transportation and storage. To address these limitations, probabilistic inversion methods are increasingly utilized to estimate the spatial distribution of , providing more representative approximations of field conditions8–10.

Empirical correlations derived from readily measurable index parameters (e.g., water content, Atterberg limits), strength parameters (e.g., undrained shear strength), or in-situ test results (e.g., -values) offer a practical alternative for large-scale estimation11,12. Nevertheless, these approaches are challenged by the multivariate, uncertain, sparse, and occasionally incomplete nature of data. Advancements in computational technologies have positioned machine learning () as a transformative tool in geotechnical investigations13,14. enables the efficient processing and analysis of large, complex datasets, offering enhanced accuracy and reliability in estimating . By addressing the inherent complexities of data, methodologies are revolutionizing the field, providing new opportunities for improved geotechnical assessments and decision-making.

Recent computational geomechanics discoveries show that multiscale interactions and internal length effects greatly influence geomaterial deformation and failure15,16. Peridynamics-based simulations and length-scale insensitive nonlocal formulations have been used to simulate fracture evolution and compression-induced damage in rock masses, showing that local continuum assumptions miss mechanical responses17. Cohesive fracture modelling frameworks emphasize fracture process zone calibration for accurate material behavior prediction. These findings suggest that macroscopic stiffness characteristics like soil compression modulus derive from complicated micro-mechanical interactions rather than empirical correlations.

Time- and stress-history-dependent processes affect geomaterial stiffness in addition to geographical variability. Recent investigations on advanced cementitious and composite materials show stress-level-dependent creep transitions, fatigue-induced deterioration, and environmental elastoplastic reactions. These findings show that stiffness-related parameters change dynamically under mechanical and environmental loads. This intrinsic nonlinearity makes typical compression modulus testing less trustworthy18–20.

New research shows that geological fabric, anisotropy, pore structure evolution, and fracture network development greatly influence soil stiffness21–23. In aeolian sands and reconstituted clays, bedding orientation and microstructural pore properties strongly impact deformation behavior24. Large-scale research on underwater landslides and tunnel excavations emphasize geological discontinuities and subsurface variation in stiffness and stability25. These data imply that compression modulus is impacted by structural, environmental, and geological elements that are hard to describe deterministically26.

Alongside physics-based modeling, data-driven methods are being used to characterize geomaterial microstructures. Transformer-based deep learning models have been utilized to segment sedimentary rock pore networks in three dimensions, improving structural detail and mechanical behavior representation. These findings support the confluence of AI and geomechanics for capturing hidden patterns in complicated material systems27.

Research gap and contribution of the present study

This section outlines the key motivations and contributions of the present study. While previous research has explored machine learning applications in geotechnical modeling, limitations remain in handling data heterogeneity, sparsity, and generalization stability. To address these gaps, this study proposes an optimized stacking ensemble framework that integrates metaheuristic optimization with complementary tree-based learners to enhance predictive accuracy, robustness, and interpretability. Prediction of the soil compression modulus (), is one of the most challenging tasks in geotechnical engineering because of the inherent complexity, heterogeneity, and sparsity of soil datasets. Most conventional estimation methods may be effective in controlled settings but are usually very time-consuming and expensive; besides, they are prone to inaccuracies due to sample disturbances. While large and complete datasets have been used in most of the existing approaches, which often fail to provide suboptimal performance in capturing the complex nonlinear relationships inherent in multivariate soil data, ML models have shown promising alternatives. These limitations hence point to the need for one to have more robust, adaptive, and efficient predictive frameworks. Recent developments in ensemble-based ML models have shown improved prediction accuracy by combining the strengths of multiple base learners. However, stacking ensemble models that have shown success in geotechnical parameters estimation remain underexplored for the estimation. Furthermore, optimization of the stacking ensembles through advanced metaheuristic algorithms is seldom analyzed, leaving large room for using its potential in geotechnical predictions completely. Therefore, this research effort covers these research gaps by proposing, for the first time, a new framework of stacking ensembles optimized via a Puma Optimization Algorithm (PO). This work hugely integrates the advanced base learners LGB, XGB, RFR, and HGB in order to take full advantage of merits from each of them while solving the problem of heterogeneity and sparsity. By using PO to carry out hyperparameter tuning of the stacking model, the proposed algorithm has greater improvements in predictive accuracy, convergence efficiency, and robustness. Different from previous related works, this research strictly verifies the reliability of models with strict cross-validation and error analysis on various datasets. The proposed approach not only advances the state-of-the-art in prediction but also establishes a scalable methodology for applying optimized stacking ensembles in broader geotechnical and engineering contexts. The optimized stacking ensemble model, developed in this study for prediction, is an economical and efficient alternative to traditional geotechnical testing. Accurate and scalable estimation in support of real-world applications like infrastructure development and construction helps in the reduction of project delays, material waste, and costs.

The reliable and continuous dataset was obtained from the literature1, and provided a robust basis for the development and evaluation of the predictive models in a way that ensures the findings are reliable and can be applied to a wide range of soil conditions. Similarly, and reflect compaction and structural properties, respectively, both of which become critical in fixing the compressive behavior of soil. Randomization was employed to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the predictive models. The dataset was randomly split into training, validation, and testing sets to prevent overfitting and ensure unbiased model evaluation. Also, hyperparameter training and cross-validation processes were subjected to randomness for maximization of model performance. With its integration, bias in the distribution in the data was avoided, and its prediction of under changing soil conditions became reliable. All were divided, which means they separated the whole into training, validation, and test phases composed of 80%, 10%, and 10% of the total number, respectively, making 1128 samples and 141 other samples, to avoid any ordering bias, randomization was applied during the splitting process.

The Interquartile Range (IQR) method is effective in noticing and removing extreme deviations, especially in enhancing the accuracy and reliability of models50. All predictive models should be robust, outliers were removed using a threshold value for the IQR at 1.45. A total of 25 data points were removed in such a way that extreme values did not result in poor model performance. Besides that, for handling the empty-scale discrepancy in input variables, Min.-Max. normalization was applied to scale all features within the range of 0.0–1.0. Normalization avoids those features’ dominance that have a larger numerical range and ensures that all the variables have an equal contribution to the model’s learning process. For example, if the raw values of and are varying in very different ranges, then normalization will scale them into comparable ranges, which can strengthen model convergence and stability in training.

Base description of Histogram-based gradient boosting (HGB)

Boosting techniques based on Gradient Boosting of Histograms are among the most effective boosting strategies of recent years due to their effectiveness and ability to improve processing time and performance52. The first model is trained using the weighted data. Consequently, some of the traits might appear more frequently in the new sets of the other models. Following the identification of the data samples that it incorrectly classified, the first model then adjusts and increases their weights. To refocus its attention on those and correct the incorrect predictions, the second model is fed these modified weights.

One boosting method based on decision trees is gradient boosting. Applying this recently created technique to classification and regression problems yields remarkably good results on a variety of datasets. Training time is a major disadvantage of gradient boosting. Aside from the enormous quantity of attributes and samples, the problem here is intrinsically linked to the nature of a decision tree. It is necessary to split or divide all continuous values and continuous characteristics in order to construct a decision tree. It takes a lot of work and computation, especially when dealing with hundreds of features and data samples. HGB deals with problems pertaining to big datasets and efficiency.

This method condenses all of the features’ distinct values into a minuscule number of numbers. Consequently, before splitting or dividing the tree, a decision tree can concentrate on a small subset of the traits. In contrast to gradient boosting, it enhances and expedites the trees’ implementation. In this implementation, HGB is implemented using the Hist Gradient Boosting Classifier class from the sklearn package.

Base description of stacking ensemble (SE)

In machine learning (ML), stacking is a class of ensemble learning techniques that combines multiple base models to generate an overall improved predictive performance; the process is often referred to as stacked generalization. The intuitive idea of stacking is to transfer information from one set of learners, called base models, to another, called meta-models, before making the final prediction. This works amply for both regression and classification problems, while the leveraging of diverse strengths from models increases their accuracy and robustness. Stacking in regression problems is implemented through a hierarchical framework structured into multiple layers, where the base learners are usually on the first layer and independently trained based on the original dataset to make each one’s best prediction. These are combined into a new dataset, also called the meta-feature dataset, which then acts as the input for the second layer. The second layer consists of the meta-learner, designed to combine and refine these predictions. The meta-learner undergoes training aimed at learning the particular strengths and limitations of each base learner so as to assign optimal weights or adjustments to their respective contributions. The power of stacking lies in how it can capture the varied patterns and relationships that might have been missed by individual models operating in isolation. By combining the insights of multiple models, stacking mitigates overfitting, reduces prediction errors, and delivers more reliable results. This approach has been widely adopted across various domains, including finance, healthcare, and engineering, where precise predictions are crucial for decision-making.

Algorithm selection rationale and methodological framework

In this study, four tree-based ensemble learning algorithms XGB, LGB, HGB, and RFR were selected due to their complementary strengths in modeling nonlinear, heterogeneous, and high-dimensional geotechnical data. The prediction of soil compression modulus (Es) involves complex multivariate interactions between stratigraphic depth, density parameters, saturation conditions, and soil classification. These relationships are inherently nonlinear and often exhibit multicollinearity. Tree-based ensemble models are particularly suitable for such conditions because they:

Capture nonlinear feature interactions without explicit functional assumptions,

Capture nonlinear feature interactions without explicit functional assumptions,

Are robust to multicollinearity and non-normal feature distributions,

Are robust to multicollinearity and non-normal feature distributions,

Handle mixed-type inputs (categorical and continuous variables),

Handle mixed-type inputs (categorical and continuous variables),

Demonstrate strong performance on medium-sized datasets.

Demonstrate strong performance on medium-sized datasets.

XGB was selected for its regularized boosting structure, which minimizes both bias and variance through second-order gradient optimization and L1/L2 penalization. This makes XGB particularly effective in preventing overfitting while capturing complex nonlinear stress–strain patterns.

LGB improves computational efficiency through leaf-wise tree growth and histogram-based splitting, enabling rapid convergence even with large feature interactions. Its structure is advantageous for capturing localized stiffness variations in heterogeneous soil layers.

HGB reduces computational burden by discretizing continuous variables into histogram bins prior to splitting. This improves scalability and stability, particularly when feature distributions are skewed, as commonly observed in geotechnical datasets.

RFR, in contrast to boosting methods, relies on bagging and random feature selection to reduce variance and improve model stability. Its ensemble averaging mechanism enhances robustness against noise and prevents excessive sensitivity to localized anomalies in data.

Performance metrics: selection rationale and evaluation strategy

Accurate prediction of the soil compression modulus (Es) requires not only high numerical precision but also robust generalization, stability against data variability, and reliability under heterogeneous geotechnical conditions. Relying on a single performance metric may lead to incomplete or potentially misleading conclusions, particularly when modeling nonlinear stress–strain behavior influenced by multiple interacting variables. The selected metrics include the Coefficient of Determination (R2), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE), Mean Squared Logarithmic Error (MSLE), Root Mean Squared Logarithmic Error (RMSLE), Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE), Theil’s Inequality Coefficient (U), Scatter Index (SI), Willmott’s Index (WI), and Under-95th Percentile (U95). These metrics were intentionally chosen to capture complementary aspects of predictive performance. To provide a structured assessment, the evaluation metrics were categorized into four functional groups:

Variance-explanation metrics (R2) to assess the proportion of output variability captured by the model.

Variance-explanation metrics (R2) to assess the proportion of output variability captured by the model.

Absolute and scale-dependent error metrics (RMSE, NRMSE, MASE) to quantify the magnitude of prediction deviations.

Absolute and scale-dependent error metrics (RMSE, NRMSE, MASE) to quantify the magnitude of prediction deviations.

Logarithmic and proportional error metrics (MSLE, RMSLE) to evaluate relative error behavior, particularly relevant for moderate-to-high modulus variations.

Logarithmic and proportional error metrics (MSLE, RMSLE) to evaluate relative error behavior, particularly relevant for moderate-to-high modulus variations.

Agreement and reliability indices (WI, U, SI, U95) to assess prediction consistency, scatter behavior, and uncertainty bounds.

Agreement and reliability indices (WI, U, SI, U95) to assess prediction consistency, scatter behavior, and uncertainty bounds.

The soil compression modulus exhibits moderate skewness and heterogeneity across stratigraphic conditions. Therefore, evaluating both absolute deviations (e.g., RMSE) and normalized deviations (e.g., NRMSE, SI) ensures that model performance is not biased by scale effects. Logarithmic metrics prevent disproportionate penalization of larger modulus values, while uncertainty-based indicators (U95) provide insight into prediction reliability under engineering safety considerations. This comprehensive metric selection enables rigorous comparison among single, hybrid, and stacking models, ensuring that improvements in one metric (e.g., R2) are not achieved at the expense of increased prediction uncertainty or instability. Practical usages of the present study

The findings of the study are particularly useful in the practice of geotechnical engineering, especially the estimation of , significant for the design and analysis of the foundation system, embankment, and retaining structure.

Presently, this study presents a reliable, speedy, and economical method of prediction of by the use of ML models optimized by metaheuristic algorithms, unlike conventional laboratory-based testing methods, which are generally cumbersome and involve high resource consumption. Advanced ML models like LGB, XGB, RFR, and HGB and their hybrid and stacking versions ensure better predictive accuracies with reduced errors, hence making them apt for real-world applications. The model proposed herein provides practitioners with the possibility to estimate using minimum input parameters such as , , soil type, and . This simplifies the process of data acquisition by reducing reliance on cumbersome field or laboratory measurements. Besides, model reliability is improved with the application of optimization algorithms such as POA for the optimal selection of model parameters, a further performance enhancement. It can make the framework workable for various site soil conditions and project requirements in preliminary site assessment, rapid design iterations, and quality control during construction. Another critical application is the large-scale infrastructure projects whereby quick and reliable predictions of are of extreme importance for guaranteeing safety but also cost-effectiveness. It could, for example, provide the frame that will enable the engineer to predict the settlement-related problem in soft soils or to consider strategies for ground improvement. Further applications of the methodology of this study might be done for other geotechnical parameters as well; thus, this application is far wider in the field of soil mechanics.

Conclusions

This study introduced a novel optimized stacking ensemble framework for predicting the soil compression modulus () by advanced machine learning techniques. The proposed methodology combines four powerful base learners Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Random Forest (RFR), Histogram-based Gradient Boosting (HGB), and Light Gradient Boosting (LGB) optimized using the Puma Optimization (PO) algorithm and aggregated through a stacking strategy to enhance predictive robustness and generalization capability.

The results consistently demonstrated that the optimized stacking model, particularly Stacking (XGBPO), outperformed all single and hybrid counterparts across training, validation, and testing phases. The integration of metaheuristic optimization with ensemble learning significantly improved convergence behavior, reduced prediction errors, and enhanced overall model stability. These findings confirm that combining optimized base learners within a hierarchical stacking structure provides a reliable and scalable solution for modeling complex nonlinear soil behavior.

Interpretability analyses using FAST sensitivity analysis, SHAP values, Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE), and Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) revealed that dry density (), void ratio (), and degree of saturation () are the dominant factors influencing soil compressibility. This not only strengthens the physical consistency of the model but also provides meaningful geotechnical insight into stress–strain behavior under varying soil conditions. From an engineering perspective, the proposed framework offers a computationally efficient and cost-effective alternative to traditional laboratory and in-situ testing methods. By enabling accurate and scalable estimation of soil compressibility, the approach can support infrastructure design, risk mitigation, and construction planning while reducing time delays, experimental costs, and material waste.

Despite its strong predictive performance, the study has certain limitations. The dataset primarily relies on compiled literature data, which may not fully capture all site-specific field uncertainties. Future research should therefore focus on validating the framework using large-scale field measurements and real-world case studies across diverse geological conditions. Moreover, integrating reliability-based analysis and probabilistic machine learning models such as Bayesian ensemble learning or uncertainty-aware neural networks would enable explicit quantification of predictive uncertainty. Such advancements would enhance the framework’s applicability in risk-informed geotechnical design and decision-making processes.

Overall, this research advances the state-of-the-art in data-driven geotechnical modeling by demonstrating that optimized stacking ensembles can provide highly accurate, interpretable, and practically applicable solutions for complex soil behavior prediction problems.
