Introduction

Paragangliomas are rare neuroendocrine tumors arising from extra‐adrenal paraganglia and are often associated with catecholamine‐related symptoms and hypertension. Because of their rarity, evidence regarding optimal diagnosis and treatment remains limited. Approximately 15%–20% of pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas arise from extra‐adrenal paraganglia, mainly in the abdomen, but also in the thorax and pelvis [1]. Bladder paraganglioma is extremely rare, accounting for less than 0.05% of all bladder tumors [2].

Most bladder paragangliomas are localized at diagnosis, and complete surgical resection offers a high likelihood of cure. However, TUR of bladder tumors may provoke severe hypertensive crises due to catecholamine release during tumor manipulation. Acute respiratory distress syndrome following TURBT has also been reported, highlighting the potential hazards of inappropriate tumor manipulation [3]. Therefore, partial cystectomy is generally considered the preferred surgical approach for localized bladder paraganglioma [4]. In recent years, minimally invasive approaches, including laparoscopic and robot‐assisted surgery, have increasingly been adopted. However, because of the rarity of this disease, the role of robot‐assisted partial cystectomy has not been fully established [5, 6, 7].

Herein, we report a case of bladder paraganglioma that was suspected preoperatively based on cystoscopic and radiological findings, allowing avoidance of TUR and safe treatment with robot‐assisted partial cystectomy.

Case Presentation

A 58‐year‐old man with a history of psychiatric disorder presented with gross hematuria. Cystoscopy revealed a smooth elevated lesion covered with normal mucosa on the left lateral wall of the bladder, suggestive of a submucosal tumor (Figure 1). Contrast‐enhanced CT demonstrated a well‐defined approximately 20‐mm mass with peripheral enhancement (Figure 2A). MRI revealed an approximately 2‐cm submucosal bladder mass with a lobulated hyperintense central component on T2‐weighted imaging and a slightly hyperintense peripheral solid component relative to the muscular layer (Figure 2B). Diffusion‐weighted imaging demonstrated restricted diffusion in the solid component (Figure 2D).

Cystoscopic findings. A submucosal tumor was identified on the left lateral wall of the bladder.

Radiological findings. (A) Contrast‐enhanced CT demonstrated a tumor with peripheral enhancement. (B) T2‐weighted MRI revealed an approximately 2‐cm tumor with lobulated high signal intensity in the center. (C) Diffusion‐weighted imaging demonstrated restricted diffusion in the solid component of the tumor, whereas the adjacent muscular layer showed no restricted diffusion. (D) MIBG scintigraphy demonstrated radioisotope uptake in the bladder tumor.

Routine laboratory findings were unremarkable, although urinalysis showed microscopic hematuria. Plasma catecholamine levels were within normal limits. Despite the absence of biochemical evidence of catecholamine excess, bladder paraganglioma was suspected based on the imaging findings. Therefore, MIBG scintigraphy was performed. Imaging obtained 24 h after administration of I‐123 MIBG demonstrated radioisotope uptake in the left bladder wall mass, suggesting paraganglioma (Figure 2D).

Considering the risk of hypertensive crisis associated with tumor manipulation, TUR was considered inappropriate. Preoperative alpha‐adrenergic blockade with doxazosin was initiated. Robot‐assisted laparoscopic partial cystectomy was subsequently performed using a transperitoneal approach. Intraoperative ultrasonography was used to confirm the exact depth and location of the submucosal tumor from the extravesical aspect before bladder incision, complementing cystoscopic identification of the mucosal margin. After confirming the resection margin cystoscopically, the bladder wall was incised and the tumor was removed en bloc. The bladder defect was closed in two layers (Figure 3).

Intraoperative footages. (A) After dissection of the Retzius space, the tumor location was confirmed using intraoperative ultrasonography. (B) The bladder wall was incised while the resection margin was simultaneously confirmed cystoscopically. (C) The submucosal tumor was removed en bloc with full‐thickness bladder wall resection under intravesical observation. (D) The bladder defect was closed in two layers using a 3–0 barbed suture. (E) The bladder was confirmed to be completely closed by cystoscopy.

No significant hemodynamic instability occurred during tumor manipulation. The urethral catheter was removed on postoperative Day 7, and the patient was discharged uneventfully.

Histopathological examination revealed nodular lesions with a fibrous capsule beneath the bladder mucosa. The tumor cells were relatively large, with amphophilic cytoplasm, and showed trabecular and nested growth patterns. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for chromogranin A and synaptophysin, confirming the diagnosis of paraganglioma (Figure 4).

Discussion

Bladder paraganglioma is a rare neuroendocrine tumor accounting for less than 0.05% of all bladder tumors [2]. Typical manifestations include hematuria, hypertension, headache, palpitations, and micturition‐induced adrenergic symptoms [4]. However, some patients lack characteristic symptoms or biochemical evidence of catecholamine excess, making preoperative diagnosis difficult. In such cases, recognition of a submucosal bladder tumor with characteristic radiological findings may provide an important diagnostic clue [4, 5].

Accurate preoperative diagnosis is clinically important because TUR may provoke excessive catecholamine release and severe hemodynamic instability [4]. Although plasma catecholamine levels were within normal limits in the present case, bladder paraganglioma was strongly suspected based on the characteristic cystoscopic and radiological findings. Given the high clinical suspicion, urinary catecholamine levels were not additionally measured, and MIBG scintigraphy was performed to further support the diagnosis. This diagnostic process enabled avoidance of TUR and appropriate perioperative preparation with alpha‐adrenergic blockade.

Partial cystectomy is generally regarded as the preferred treatment for localized bladder paraganglioma because it allows complete full‐thickness excision while minimizing unnecessary tumor manipulation [8, 9]. Simultaneous cystoscopic guidance has recently been reported to facilitate accurate identification of the tumor location and resection margin during robot‐assisted partial cystectomy, thereby helping preserve bladder capacity while ensuring complete excision [10]. Robot‐assisted surgery also provides excellent visualization, precise dissection, and secure intracorporeal suturing [6, 10, 11]. In the present case, robot‐assisted partial cystectomy was performed safely without significant intraoperative hemodynamic instability and resulted in an uneventful recovery.

Although only a limited number of cases of robot‐assisted partial cystectomy for bladder paraganglioma have been reported, the available literature suggests favorable perioperative outcomes. In the present case, robot‐assisted surgery enabled safe tumor resection with stable intraoperative hemodynamics and an uneventful postoperative course. Our experience supports the feasibility of robot‐assisted partial cystectomy as a minimally invasive treatment option for selected patients with bladder paraganglioma.

Conclusion

Bladder paraganglioma should be included in the differential diagnosis of submucosal bladder tumors, even in the absence of catecholamine‐related symptoms. Recognition of characteristic imaging findings may facilitate preoperative diagnosis, avoid transurethral resection, and support safe surgical management. Robot‐assisted partial cystectomy appears to be a feasible minimally invasive treatment option for selected patients.
