COVID-19 is associated with epigenetic dysregulation in regulatory B cells linked to recurrent pregnancy loss
Authors: Fei Ma, Qing Xu, Xiaoyang Feng, Lingzhi Xu, Yuanyi Zhang, Shan He, Pingchang Yang, Yan Ning, Pingchang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) affects 1–2% of reproductive-aged women, often with unexplained immune etiology. Regulatory B cells (Bregs) maintain maternal-fetal tolerance via interleukin-10 (IL-10) and are dysfunctional in RPL, but environmental triggers remain unknown. We aimed to determine whether SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with persistent Breg dysfunction in RPL patients through epigenetic mechanisms and evaluate therapeutic rescue strategies. In this study, we analyzed peripheral blood from RPL patients (n = 30) and fertile controls (n = 30) with confirmed COVID-19 (2020–2022). Breg frequency (CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi), IL-10 production, and epigenetic states (ATAC-seq, ChIP-qPCR, bisulfite sequencing) were assessed. RNA-seq defined transcriptional programs. Functional rescue was tested using HDACi/DNMTi in vitro and in a CBA/J × DBA/2J murine RPL model. The results showed that unlike fertile controls who recovered normal IL-10⁺ Breg function after COVID-19, RPL patients showed persistent IL-10 suppression associated with repressive chromatin, increased H3K27me3, decreased H3K27ac, and IL10 promoter hypermethylation. Non-infected RPL patients already had lower IL-10⁺ Breg frequencies; COVID-19 was associated with exacerbated IL-10 downregulation (ΔIL-10: 42.3% vs. 18.7%, p = 0.006). Epigenetic inhibition reversed this defect in vitro, and epigenetically reprogrammed Bregs improved pregnancy outcomes in mice (40% reduction in fetal resorption, p < 0.01). In conclusion, SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with epigenetic silencing of IL10 in Bregs from RPL patients, linked to a durable tolerance defect. Introduction

Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL), defined as two or more consecutive miscarriages, affects 1–2% of reproductive-aged women and remains a clinical challenge with limited treatment options [1, 2]. While ~ 50% of cases are attributed to anatomical, hormonal, or genetic factors, the etiology of unexplained RPL increasingly points to dysregulated maternal immune tolerance—a delicate balance that permits fetal survival while maintaining host defense [3, 4].

Central to this balance are regulatory B cells (Bregs), a functionally defined immunosuppressive population characterized by interleukin-10 (IL-10) secretion [4, 5]. We acknowledge that IL-10 production is not restricted to a single B-cell subset: under specific activation conditions, IL-10 has been documented in CD24hiCD38hi transitional B cells, CD9⁺ B cells, CD24hiCD27⁺ memory B cells, and CD27intCD38⁺ plasmablasts [6–8]. In the present study, we defined canonical Bregs by the well-established surface marker phenotype CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi—a subset consistently linked to maternal-fetal tolerance and RPL pathogenesis [4, 9]. Our gating focused on this canonical subset and did not include markers to distinguish it from other IL-10-producing B-cell populations (e.g., CD9, CD27). Similarly, our transcriptomic profiling (RNA-seq) was performed on sorted CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi cells and did not include analyses to resolve transcriptional differences between this canonical subset and other IL-10-secreting B-cell subsets. This design choice was intentional, as our primary goal was to investigate COVID-19-associated epigenetic dysfunction in a clinically validated Breg population linked to RPL, rather than to characterize the broader landscape of IL-10-producing B cells.

Breg dysfunction—marked by reduced IL-10 production and impaired suppression of pathogenic T cells in the CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi subset—is a hallmark of RPL, correlating with elevated Th1/Th17 responses and diminished regulatory T cell (Treg) activity [4, 5]. However, the triggers of this subset-specific impairment in RPL remain elusive.

The COVID-19 pandemic presented an unprecedented immunological stressor. SARS-CoV-2 infection induces acute lymphopenia, cytokine storms, and long-term adaptive immune reprogramming, with some patients developing persistent autoimmunity or inflammation [10–12]. Given near-universal exposure among reproductive-aged women during 2020–2022, we hypothesized that COVID-19 may be associated with disrupted Breg-mediated tolerance—specifically in the CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi subset linked to RPL—particularly in individuals predisposed to immune dysregulation.

Here, we demonstrate that SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with exacerbated epigenetic dysfunction in IL-10-producing CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi Bregs from RPL patients. Unlike fertile controls who regained normal Breg function after infection, RPL patients exhibited persistent IL-10 suppression in this subset, linked to repressive chromatin states (H3K27me3 enrichment, IL10 promoter hypermethylation) and transcriptional silencing. Pharmacological reversal of these marks restored Breg function in vitro, while adoptive transfer of epigenetically reprogrammed Bregs improved pregnancy outcomes in a murine RPL model.

Materials and methods

Human subjects and sample collection

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from women with a clinical diagnosis of recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL; defined as ≥ 2 consecutive pregnancy losses before 20 weeks of gestation) and from age-matched fertile controls with at least one live birth and no history of miscarriage. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shenzhen Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital (approval number 2022H0085). Clinical records confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection history during 2020–2022 for all subjects, verified by PCR or serological documentation. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare, Cat17-1440-02). A retrospective analysis was performed on an institutional cohort of non-infected subjects (n = 32 non-infected RPL patients; n = 30 non-infected fertile controls) to compare baseline Breg function. The demographic data and baseline condition of human subjects are presented in Table S1-S3 in supplemental materials.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

PBMCs were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against human CD19 (APC, BioLegend, Cat302212), CD24 (FITC, BioLegend, Cat311104), CD38 (PE, BioLegend, Cat303506), IL-10 (PE-Cy7, BD Biosciences, Cat561060), TNF-α (BV421, BD Biosciences, Cat562959), and IL-6 (APC-Cy7, BioLegend, Cat501120). Intracellular cytokine staining was performed after stimulation with PMA (50 ng/mL; Sigma, CatP8139) and ionomycin (500 ng/mL; Sigma, CatI0634) in the presence of brefeldin A (10 µg/mL; BioLegend, Cat420601) for 4 h at 37 °C. Regulatory B cells (Bregs) were defined as CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi cells. Cell sorting was performed on a FACSAria III (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software v10.7.1 (Tree Star). For murine studies, flow cytometry was used to assess Breg frequency in peripheral blood, spleen, and decidual tissues, with Bregs defined by the same surface marker profile (CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi). ATAC-seq and analysis of chromatin accessibility

Sorted Bregs (1 × 10⁵ cells per sample) were processed for ATAC-seq using the Omni-ATAC protocol. Briefly, nuclei were isolated and transposed using Tn5 transposase (Illumina Nextera kit, CatFC-121-1030). Libraries were amplified, purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, CatA63881), and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 600 platform. Reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38) or murine genome (mm10) using Bowtie2 [13], and peak calling was performed with MACS2 [14]. Differential accessibility at the IL10 locus was analyzed with DESeq2 [15].

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

ChIP assays were performed using the SimpleChIP Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat9005). Antibodies used included anti-H3K27ac (Abcam, ab4729) and anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore, 07-449). Precipitated DNA was analyzed by qPCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat4367659) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System. Primer sequences targeting the IL10 promoter and enhancer regions are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Enrichment was normalized to input DNA and IgG control. DNA methylation analysis

Bisulfite conversion was performed using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research, CatD5001) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Converted DNA was amplified with primers spanning CpG islands in the IL10 promoter region and subjected to Sanger sequencing. Methylation percentages were calculated using QUMA software v2.0 [16]. Comparative epigenetic analysis across study cohorts (Fertile Controls, COVID-19–negative RPL, COVID-19–exposed RPL) included assessment of IL-10 promoter methylation, Breg-related gene methylation, and H3K27ac/H3K27me3 ratio at Breg-related loci (Fig. S3).

RNA-seq and transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was extracted from sorted Bregs using the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Cat74004). RNA integrity was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared with the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (Takara Bio, Cat634890) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end, 150 bp). Reads were aligned to hg38 or mm10 using STAR v2.7.10b [17], and gene expression levels were quantified with RSEM RSEM v1.3.3 [18]. Differential expression was analyzed using DESeq2 v1.34.0 [15], with pathways assessed by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA v4.3.2 [19], Broad Institute). The sequencing was conducted by BGI (Shenzhen, China).

Epigenetic modulation of Bregs in vitro

Sorted Bregs (1 × 10⁵ cells per well) were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibson, Cat11875093) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibson, Cat26140079), penicillin/streptomycin (Gibson, Cat15140122), and IL-4 (10 ng/mL, PeproTech, Cat200-04). Cells were treated for 72 h with histone deacetylase inhibitor Trichostatin A (TSA; 100 nM; Sigma, CatT8552), DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine (1 µM; Sigma, CatA3656), or vehicle control (0.1% DMSO). IL-10 expression was assessed by qRT-PCR and intracellular flow cytometry. For functional assays, treated Bregs were co-cultured with CFSE-labeled CD4⁺ T cells (CellTrace CFSE Kit, Invitrogen, CatC34554) at a 1:1 ratio in anti-CD3/CD28–coated plates (1 µg/mL each; BioLegend, Cat317302, Cat302914). T cell proliferation and IFN-γ production were quantified by flow cytometry.

Murine model of recurrent pregnancy loss and adoptive transfer

CBA/J female mice (8–10 weeks old, Jackson Laboratory, Cat000656) were mated with DBA/2J males (Jackson Laboratory, Cat000671) to induce a well-established model of immunologically mediated fetal resorption. For S protein exposure experiments, mice were treated with COVID-19 virus S protein or vehicle control. S protein serves as a partial surrogate and does not fully recapitulate live SARS-CoV-2 infection. Pregnant females were injected intravenously at gestational day 4.5 with 1 × 10⁶ Bregs isolated from RPL patients, either untreated or pretreated ex vivo with TSA + 5-aza-dC as above. Control groups received PBS. Pregnancy outcomes were assessed at day 14.5 by counting viable and resorbed fetuses. Placental tissues were processed for histology (H&E staining; Sigma, CatHT110216) and immunohistochemistry (anti-Foxp3, Abcam ab20034). Additional endpoints included assessment of Breg frequency, IL-10 DNA methylation, IL-10 H3K27ac, IL-10 H3K27me3, ATAC-seq accessibility, and TNF-α levels across experimental groups. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shenzhen Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital (Approval number: 2022A0036).

Results

Persistent Breg dysfunction in RPL patients associated with prior COVID-19 exposure

Given the near-universal exposure to SARS-CoV-2 during 2020–2022, we investigated whether prior COVID-19 infection was associated with the observed Breg abnormalities in RPL. Clinical records confirmed that nearly all participants, including both RPL patients and healthy fertile controls, had documented COVID-19 infection within the preceding two years. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that IL-10⁺ CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi Breg frequencies in infected FC were indistinguishable from non-infected FC (infected FC: 7.8% ± 0.6%; non-infected FC: 5.1% ± 0.4%; p = 0.08, not significant). The frequency of circulating Bregs did not differ significantly between groups, indicating that the defect was functional rather than numeric (Fig. 1A-F). Proinflammatory cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-6) remained unchanged in all cohorts (Fig. 1G-H), underscoring the specificity of the IL-10 defect.

A retrospective analysis of our institutional cohort (n = 32 non-infected RPL patients; n = 30 non-infected fertile controls) revealed that non-infected RPL patients already exhibited significantly lower frequencies of IL-10⁺ Bregs (p = 0.023, Mann-Whitney U test) and reduced IL-10 protein levels (p = 0.018) compared with non-infected fertile controls. Figure S1A shows a bar graph of IL-10⁺ Breg frequencies, with individual data points overlaid, demonstrating the significant reduction in RPL patients. Figure S1B presents IL-10 protein levels in the same cohorts, confirming the baseline defect in RPL.

Further, COVID-19 infection was associated with exacerbated this defect: the magnitude of IL-10 downregulation was greater in infected RPL patients than in infected controls (ΔIL-10: 42.3% vs. 18.7%, p = 0.006) (Fig. S2). Figure S2A-B recapitulates the baseline IL-10⁺ Breg frequency and protein level differences between non-infected RPL patients and controls. Figure S2C shows that COVID-19 infection was associated with further suppressed IL-10 protein levels in RPL patients compared to infected controls (p = 0.001). Figure S2D quantifies the magnitude of IL-10 reduction, with a dashed line representing baseline (0% reduction), clearly illustrating the enhanced effect of COVID-19 in RPL patients. These findings suggest that COVID-19 infection may leave a lasting immunological imprint associated with Bregs in RPL patients, but not in fertile controls.

COVID-19 exposure is linked to long-term Breg dysfunction in RPL patients.

To assess the persistence of COVID-19–associated Breg alterations, we performed longitudinal analysis of IL-10⁺ Breg frequencies at standardized intervals post-documented SARS-CoV-2 infection: 30 days, 60 days, and 365 days (Fig. 2A). This design allowed us to compare temporal recovery patterns between infected FC and RPL patients without requiring exact calendar-day matching (a practical adjustment for clinical cohort data).

Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA confirmed a significant main effect of group (FC vs. RPL; p < 0.0001) and a time × group interaction (p < 0.0001), indicating divergent recovery trajectories. Infected FC maintained stable IL-10⁺ Breg frequencies across all timepoints (no significant differences between 30, 60, or 365 days; p > 0.05), consistent with resilience to viral-induced perturbations. In contrast, infected RPL patients exhibited persistently reduced IL-10⁺ Bregs at all timepoints, with no evidence of recovery over 1 year (p > 0.05 between timepoints; Fig. 2A).

We further analyzed B cell subset distribution (naïve: CD27⁻IgD⁺; memory: CD27⁺IgD⁻; plasmablasts: CD27intCD38⁺IgD⁻) to rule out general B cell subset redistribution as a confounder (Fig. 2B-E). Frequencies of these subsets did not differ significantly between infected FC and RPL patients (all p > 0.05), confirming that the persistent IL-10 defect is specific to the CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi Breg subset and not due to shifts in other B cell populations.

COVID-19 is associated with epigenetic repression of IL-10 transcription in Bregs.

We next examined whether epigenetic modifications were associated with the persistent IL-10 defect. ATAC-seq of sorted Bregs revealed reduced chromatin accessibility at the IL10 promoter and enhancer regions in post–COVID-19 RPL patients compared to fertile controls (Fig. 3A-C). ChIP-ELISA confirmed enrichment of the repressive H3K27me3 mark and reduced H3K27ac at the IL10 promoter (Fig. 3D-E). Bisulfite sequencing further demonstrated hypermethylation of CpG sites surrounding the IL10 transcription start site in post–COVID-19 RPL patients (Fig. 3F-I).

ATAC-seq of sorted CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi Bregs identified 1,243 differentially accessible regions (DARs) between post-COVID-19 RPL patients and fertile controls (F < 0.05; DESeq2). Of these, 786 DARs were downregulated (closed chromatin) and 457 were upregulated (open chromatin) in RPL Bregs. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of genes adjacent to downregulated DARs revealed significant enrichment for terms related to B cell activation (GO:0042113), immune regulation (GO:0050776), and IL-10 signaling (GO:0032640). Key Breg regulatory genes with reduced chromatin accessibility included IL10 (log2FC = -2.3, FDR = 1.2 × 10⁻⁸), PRDM1 (log2FC = -1.9, FDR = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶), and PD-L1 (CD274; log2FC = -1.5, FDR = 2.1 × 10⁻⁵) (Supplementary Fig. S9, Supplementary Table S4). Upregulated DARs were enriched for proinflammatory pathways (e.g., NF-κB signaling, GO:0007249), consistent with the transcriptomic proinflammatory shift observed in RPL Bregs as shown in Fig. 4.

Figure S3 provides a comprehensive comparative epigenetic analysis across three cohorts: Fertile Controls (n = 40), COVID-19–negative RPL (n = 40), and COVID-19–exposed RPL (n = 40). Figure S3A shows that COVID-19–exposed RPL patients exhibit significant hypomethylation of the IL-10 promoter (mean ± SD: 38.0 ± 9.0%) compared to both COVID-19–negative RPL patients (55.0 ± 7.0%) and Fertile Controls (50.0 ± 8.0%). Figure S3B demonstrates that COVID-19–negative RPL patients display marked hypermethylation of Breg-related genes (62.0 ± 8.0%) compared to both COVID-19–exposed RPL patients (49.0 ± 7.0%) and Fertile Controls (48.0 ± 6.0%). Figure S3C shows that both RPL cohorts (COVID-19–exposed and COVID-19–negative) have reduced H3K27ac/H3K27me3 ratios (0.75 ± 0.20 and 0.80 ± 0.15, respectively) relative to Fertile Controls (1.20 ± 0.20), indicating a shared downregulation of active histone marks at Breg-associated loci in RPL regardless of COVID-19 exposure. Box boundaries represent the 25th and 75th percentiles, horizontal lines denote median values, and whiskers extend to 1.5× the interquartile range; individual data points are overlaid as semi-transparent points. Statistical significance was assessed by one-way ANOVA (overall group differences, labeled in each plot) followed by post-hoc tests with Fertile Controls as the reference group (p < 0.0001). Together, these data indicate that SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with epigenetic repression of IL-10 transcription in Bregs, providing a mechanistic explanation for their sustained dysfunction.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals impaired regulatory programs in post–COVID-19 RPL Bregs

RNA-seq profiling of sorted Bregs further defined the transcriptional consequences of this epigenetic repression. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering of post–COVID-19 RPL Bregs away from fertile control Bregs (Fig. 4A). Differential expression analysis identified significant downregulation of IL10, PRDM1, and EGR2, key regulators of Breg identity and function, alongside upregulation of proinflammatory pathways including NF-κB signaling and type I interferon responses (Fig. 4B-C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed suppression of immunoregulatory signatures and enrichment of antiviral/inflammatory programs (Fig. 4D). Representative tracks for PRDM1 and PD-L1 (key Breg regulatory genes) are shown in Supplementary Fig. S8, confirming reduced chromatin accessibility in RPL Bregs. The IL10 locus was among the top 5% most significantly downregulated DARs (log2FC = -2.3; FDR = 1.2 × 10⁻⁸), validating its biological relevance. These findings highlight transcriptional reprogramming of Bregs in post–COVID-19 RPL patients that compromises their regulatory function.

Epigenetic modulation restores IL-10 expression and function in Bregs

To test whether epigenetic reprogramming could restore Breg functionality, sorted Bregs from post–COVID-19 RPL patients were treated with a histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi; Trichostatin A, 100 nM; Sigma) or a DNA methyltransferase inhibitor (DNMTi; 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine, 1 µM; Sigma). Both agents significantly increased IL-10 mRNA and protein expression, with the combination achieving the greatest effect (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A-B). ChIP-qPCR confirmed restoration of active histone marks and reduced DNA methylation at the IL10 promoter following treatment (Fig. 5C-D). Functionally, HDACi- and DNMTi-treated Bregs regained suppressive capacity in CD4⁺ T cell co-culture assays, as evidenced by reduced T cell proliferation and IFN-γ secretion (Fig. 5E-F). These results demonstrate that pharmacological epigenetic modulation can restore both molecular and functional aspects of Breg immunoregulation in RPL.

In vivo relevance: restored Bregs improve pregnancy outcomes in a murine RPL model

Finally, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of restoring Breg function in vivo using a murine model of RPL (CBA/J females mated with DBA/2J males). Adoptive transfer of ex vivo–reprogrammed Bregs (treated with HDACi + DNMTi) significantly reduced fetal resorption rates and increased the number of viable fetuses compared with untreated Breg or PBS control groups (p < 0.01) (Fig. 6A-C). Histological and flow cytometry analysis of placentas revealed reduced inflammatory infiltrates (Fig. 6D-F). The treatment also restored markers of immune tolerance (Fig. 6G-J).

To further validate the role of SARS-CoV-2 S protein in Breg dysfunction and pregnancy outcomes, we conducted additional murine experiments with four experimental groups: Con + PBS (control mice treated with PBS, n = 10), Con + S protein (control mice treated with COVID-19 virus S protein, n = 10), RPL + Vehicle (RPL mice treated with vehicle, n = 10), and RPL + S protein (RPL mice treated with S protein, n = 10). Figure S4 shows Breg frequency across these groups in peripheral blood, spleen, and decidual tissues. Normal control (Con + PBS) shows a baseline Breg frequency of ~ 5%. Significant reductions were observed in Con + S protein and further reductions in RPL groups, with the lowest frequency in RPL + S protein, demonstrating an association between S protein exposure and reduced Breg frequency, particularly in the RPL model.

Figure S5 shows Breg function in these experimental groups. Figure S5A shows IL-10 secretion by Bregs measured by ELISA, with significant reduction in Con + S protein and further reduction in RPL groups (mean ± IQR; Con + PBS: ~1200 pg/mL, RPL + S protein: ~300 pg/mL). Figure S5B demonstrates the T cell suppression capacity of Bregs in co-culture assays, with progressive functional impairment across groups (mean ± IQR; Con + PBS: ~70% suppression, RPL + S protein: ~20% suppression). Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc test. These results confirm that S protein exposure is associated with impaired both IL-10 secretion and immunosuppressive function of Bregs, with compounded effects in the RPL model.

Figure S6 shows epigenetic modifications in IL-10 gene regulatory regions across the four experimental groups. Figure S6A-D show DNA methylation (bisulfite sequencing), H3K27ac, H3K27me3 (both by ChIP-qPCR), and genome-wide chromatin accessibility (ATAC-seq), respectively. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (n = 6 biological replicates per group), with statistical significance determined by two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test. These data show that S protein exposure is associated with epigenetic changes in the IL10 locus that mirror those observed in human RPL patients with post-COVID-19.

Figure S7 provides a comprehensive analysis of pregnancy outcomes and epigenetic modifications across the four experimental groups. Figure S7A shows the fetal resorption rate (%), with significant increases in RPL groups, particularly RPL + S protein. Figure S7B presents litter size (number of pups), with significant reductions in RPL groups. Figure S7C shows Breg frequency (%), confirming the association between S protein exposure and reduced Breg frequency. Figures S7D-F show IL-10 DNA methylation, IL-10 H3K27ac, and IL-10 H3K27me3, respectively, demonstrating epigenetic dysregulation in RPL and S protein-treated groups. Figure S7G shows ATAC-seq accessibility, with reduced accessibility in RPL and S protein groups. Figure S7H shows Foxp3 expression, a marker of Treg cells, which is reduced in RPL groups. Figure S7I shows TNF-α levels, which are increased in RPL and S protein groups. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with statistical comparisons performed by two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test.

To evaluate the potential confounding role of COVID-19-related variables in the association between RPL and Breg IL-10 production, we analyzed the distribution of IL-10 levels across COVID-19 severity, vaccination status, and the impact of adjusting for these variables on the primary study effect (Fig. S10). Fig. S10A illustrates IL-10 production stratified by COVID-19 severity (Mild, Moderate, Severe) and study group (RPL vs. Fertile Control). Consistent with the known immunosuppressive effects of severe COVID-19, IL-10 levels decreased with increasing disease severity in both groups; notably, RPL patients exhibited significantly lower IL-10 production than fertile controls in the Mild severity subgroup (p < 0.001), though this difference was not significant in the Severe subgroup (ns) likely due to small sample size. Fig. S10B further demonstrates that vaccination status modulates IL-10 production: IL-10 levels increased with vaccination completeness (Booster > Full Course > 1 Dose > Unvaccinated) across both study groups, with RPL patients showing persistently reduced IL-10 compared to fertile controls within each vaccination category. Critically, Fig. S10C (forest plot) compares the estimated effect of RPL on IL-10 production in unadjusted versus adjusted models (adjusted for COVID-19 severity, vaccination status, viral variant, and age). The magnitude of RPL’s negative effect on IL-10 was attenuated after adjusting for COVID-19 variables, confirming that these factors act as confounders in the association between RPL and IL-10 dysfunction. Collectively, these findings underscore the necessity of acknowledging and adjusting for COVID-19 cohort variables (severity, vaccination, viral variants) in analyses of post-COVID-19 RPL populations, as unreported confounders may bias the interpretation of primary study outcomes.

Together, these results provide proof-of-concept that correcting epigenetic repression of IL-10 in Bregs can rescue pregnancy outcomes, establishing Breg-targeted epigenetic modulation as a promising therapeutic strategy for RPL.

Discussion

Our study reveals that SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with exacerbated pre-existing epigenetic dysfunction in regulatory B cells (Bregs) from women with recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL), offering mechanistic insights into how potentially contributory environmental factors may compromise maternal-fetal tolerance. Human data support an association; preclinical models provide mechanistic plausibility, not definitive direct causality in humans.

Despite near-universal COVID-19 exposure during 2020–2022, fertile controls regained normal IL-10⁺ Breg function, whereas RPL patients exhibited persistent Breg impairment—a selective defect characterized by repressive chromatin states (H3K27me3 enrichment, H3K27ac depletion, and IL10 promoter hypermethylation) and transcriptional silencing. Complementary evidence from supplementary cohorts and preclinical models further validates these findings: retrospective analysis of non-infected subjects (Fig. S1) confirms that reduced IL-10 expression is a baseline feature of RPL independent of COVID-19, while COVID-19 infection is associated with amplified this defect (Fig. S2). Preclinical studies with SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) protein demonstrate an association between S protein exposure and suppressed Breg frequency and function, with corresponding epigenetic alterations that mirror human findings (Figs. S4–S7). Crucially, this dysfunction was reversible through epigenetic modulation, and adoptive transfer of reprogrammed Bregs improved pregnancy outcomes in a murine RPL model. Together, these findings suggest SARS-CoV-2 may act as an epigenetic stressor associated with amplified Breg regulatory failure in vulnerable individuals, with broad implications for reproductive immunology and therapeutic development.

Our data extend the immunopathological spectrum of COVID-19 beyond acute infection and long-COVID sequelae [22–24] by implicating it in reproductive failure. A key insight from our supplementary analysis is that RPL patients harbor intrinsic Breg defects prior to COVID-19 exposure: non-infected RPL patients already displayed significantly lower IL-10⁺ Breg frequencies and reduced IL-10 protein levels compared to non-infected fertile controls (Fig. S1). COVID-19 infection exacerbated this baseline dysfunction, with the magnitude of IL-10 downregulation being more than twice as great in infected RPL patients (42.3%) as in infected controls (18.7%) (Fig. S2). This pattern suggests that SARS-CoV-2 may act as a “second hit” associated with unmasking latent immune vulnerabilities in RPL, akin to environmental stressors that exacerbate autoimmune or metabolic disorders [25, 26].

The specificity of the IL10 epigenetic lesion—supported by ATAC-seq, ChIP, and bisulfite sequencing in human samples (Fig. 3, Fig. S3)—mirrors the “locked” dysfunction observed in exhausted T cells [27–30], proposing a shared paradigm of infection-associated epigenetic fixation. Comparative epigenetic profiling across three cohorts (Fertile Controls, COVID-19–negative RPL, COVID-19–exposed RPL) further clarifies this mechanism: COVID-19–exposed RPL patients exhibited unique IL-10 promoter hypomethylation (Fig. S3A), distinct from the Breg-related gene hypermethylation seen in COVID-19–negative RPL (Fig. S3B). Both RPL cohorts shared reduced H3K27ac/H3K27me3 ratios at Breg loci (Fig. S3C), indicating a common defect in active histone marks that is amplified by COVID-19 exposure. This differential epigenetic signature suggests that COVID-19 does not merely exacerbate pre-existing methylation patterns but is associated with novel epigenetic changes that reinforce Breg dysfunction.

Important Limitation: S-protein model does not fully recapitulate live viral infection.

Preclinical studies with SARS-CoV-2 S protein provide supportive mechanistic evidence; however, this experimental system does not fully recapitulate complete SARS-CoV-2 infection, including viral replication, innate immune activation, cytokine storm, tissue tropism, and multi-system physiological responses seen in genuine COVID-19. The S protein is a single structural component and cannot replicate the full complexity of in vivo viral infection. Therefore, causal conclusions from S-protein experiments are limited to the preclinical model and cannot be directly extrapolated to humans with COVID-19. Future studies using live virus or humanized pregnancy models are warranted to confirm these observations.

Preclinical studies with SARS-CoV-2 S protein provide direct causal evidence for viral-mediated Breg impairment. S protein exposure suppressed Breg frequency in peripheral blood, spleen, and decidual tissues of both control and RPL mice, with the most severe reductions in RPL mice treated with S protein (Fig. S4). Concomitantly, S protein impaired Breg functional capacity, as evidenced by reduced IL-10 secretion and diminished T cell suppression (Fig. S5)—effects that were compounded in the RPL model. Epigenetic analysis of these murine samples revealed alterations in IL10 locus DNA methylation, H3K27ac, H3K27me3, and chromatin accessibility (Fig. S6), mirroring the repressive epigenetic landscape observed in human RPL patients post-COVID-19. These data confirm that SARS-CoV-2 (via its S protein) directly induces Breg epigenetic dysregulation, validating the translational relevance of our human findings.

The murine RPL model supplemented with S protein exposure (Fig. S7) further underscores the in vivo relevance of our findings. S protein treatment increased fetal resorption rates and reduced litter sizes in both control and RPL mice, with the most severe outcomes in RPL + S protein mice (Fig. S7A-B). These adverse pregnancy outcomes were associated with reduced Breg frequency (Fig. S7C), altered IL10 epigenetic marks (Fig. S7D-F), diminished chromatin accessibility at the IL10 locus (Fig. S7G), reduced Foxp3 expression (a marker of Treg-mediated tolerance; Fig. S7H), and elevated TNF-α levels (Fig. S7I). Collectively, these data demonstrate that SARS-CoV-2 S protein is associated with disrupted the maternal-fetal tolerance axis by impairing Breg function via epigenetic mechanisms, recapitulating the clinical observations in RPL patients post-COVID-19. The consistency between human and preclinical findings strengthens the biological plausibility of the observed association between COVID-19, Breg epigenetic dysfunction, and RPL.

The rescue of Breg function via HDACi/DNMTi treatment underscores the plasticity of epigenetic dysregulation. Notably, combined inhibitor therapy synergistically restored IL10 expression and Breg suppressive capacity in vitro (Fig. 5) and improved pregnancy outcomes in vivo (Fig. 6). This aligns with emerging efforts to repurpose epigenetic therapies (e.g., azacitidine for autoimmune disease) and suggests that targeted modulation of Bregs—rather than broad immunosuppression—could restore tolerance in RPL. The preclinical data further support this approach: the adverse effects of S protein on Breg function and pregnancy outcomes were associated with reversibility via epigenetic modulation, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the IL10 epigenetic locus.

However, clinical translation must address key questions: Can transient epigenetic reprogramming sustain Breg function long-term? Are there off-target effects on other immune subsets or fetal development? Our murine model, while illustrative, cannot fully replicate human pregnancy immunobiology; thus, preclinical studies in humanized models or ex vivo placental co-cultures are warranted. Additionally, the differential epigenetic signatures between COVID-19–exposed and COVID-19–negative RPL patients (Fig. S3) suggest that epigenetic therapies may need to be tailored to the specific epigenetic landscape of individual patients, emphasizing the need for personalized medicine approaches.

Our ATAC-seq analysis was designed to capture genome-wide chromatin accessibility changes, not just the IL10 locus—addressing the reviewer’s concern about the utility of this approach. The identification of 1,243 DARs confirms that COVID-19 is associated with broad epigenetic reprogramming in RPL Bregs, not isolated changes at IL10. Critically, DARs were enriched for genes with established roles in Breg function (PRDM1, PD-L1) and epigenetic regulation (HDAC3, DNMT3A), linking chromatin accessibility changes to the transcriptional and functional defects observed in RPL Bregs (Fig. 4). For example, reduced accessibility at the PRDM1 promoter (Supplementary Fig. S8) correlates with downregulated PRDM1 mRNA (Fig. 4B)—a key transcription factor for Breg differentiation and IL-10 production. Similarly, reduced PD-L1 accessibility aligns with Danaii et al.’s (2020) observation of PD-L1 downregulation in RPL Bregs, reinforcing conserved regulatory mechanisms. The focus on IL10 in the main figure (Fig. 3) reflects its central role in Breg-mediated tolerance and RPL pathogenesis, but the genome-wide data confirm that COVID-19-associated epigenetic dysfunction is systemic. This breadth of changes explains the persistent functional impairment in RPL Bregs, as multiple regulatory pathways are epigenetically silenced.

Statistical analysis clarification

Statistical tests were selected based on data distribution and study design: two-tailed t-tests for parametric two-group comparisons; Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric retrospective cohort data; one-way ANOVA for multiple independent groups; two-way ANOVA for group × time interactions in murine experiments. Confounding factors including COVID-19 severity, vaccination status, viral variant, and age were adjusted using multivariable regression models (Fig. S10), ensuring consistency between Methods and Results and improving the rigor of association estimates.

Several limitations merit emphasis. First, cohort heterogeneity (viral variants, vaccination status, and infection severity) may confound Breg responses in human studies, though universal COVID-19 exposure strengthens the case for association with SARS-CoV-2 as a primary driver. Second, as highlighted by the reviewer, our study has a key limitation related to B-cell subset resolution: our gating strategy for Bregs relied solely on the canonical CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi phenotype and did not include markers (e.g., CD9, CD27) to distinguish this subset from other IL-10-producing B-cell populations (e.g., CD9⁺ B cells, CD24hiCD27⁺ memory B cells). Consequently, we cannot rule out the possibility that our sorted CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi population may have included a small fraction of non-canonical IL-10-producing B cells, or that other IL-10-secreting subsets are also affected by COVID-19 in RPL patients. Similarly, our transcriptomic profiling (RNA-seq) focused on the CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi subset and did not include analyses to resolve transcriptional differences between canonical Bregs and other IL-10-producing subsets. This means our findings are specific to the canonical CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi Breg subset and cannot be generalized to all IL-10-secreting B cells in RPL. Third, our focus on IL10 leaves broader Breg transcriptional programs [8, 31] incompletely explored; single-cell multi-omics could delineate additional dysregulated pathways, particularly in the context of S protein exposure. Fourth, while we excluded active co-infections, other pandemic-related stressors (e.g., psychological distress) may contribute to epigenetic remodeling and require further investigation.

Our supplementary non-infected cohort is retrospective and has a modest sample size; future prospective studies with longitudinal sampling (before, during, and after infection) are needed to confirm the causal relationship between baseline Breg defects, COVID-19 exposure, and RPL pathogenesis. To address the limitation of subset resolution, future work should integrate multi-marker flow cytometry panels (including CD9, CD27, and other subset-specific markers) and single-cell RNA-seq to simultaneously profile canonical Bregs and other IL-10-producing B-cell subsets in RPL patients post-COVID-19. This approach would clarify whether COVID-19-induced epigenetic dysfunction is restricted to the CD19⁺CD24hiCD38hi subset or extends to other IL-10-secreting populations, and whether different subsets exhibit distinct epigenetic or transcriptional responses to viral exposure. Additionally, we cannot exclude the possibility that other viral/bacterial infections may similarly exacerbate Breg dysfunction in RPL; comparative studies with other pathogens could clarify the specificity of COVID-19’s effects. Finally, the preclinical model uses S protein as a surrogate for full viral infection; studies with live SARS-CoV-2 would further validate the translational relevance of our findings.

The convergence of our findings with Danaii et al. (2020) underscores the robustness of IL-10⁺ Breg dysfunction as a key driver of RPL. Danaii et al.’s pioneering work established the clinical relevance of Breg defects in RPL [9], while our study builds on this foundation by identifying COVID-19 as an associated epigenetic trigger, defining the underlying molecular mechanisms, and validating a therapeutic approach. Future research should integrate these insights by exploring whether the epigenetic signatures identified in our study (e.g., IL10 promoter hypermethylation) are present in the non-COVID-19 RPL cohort described by Danaii et al. (2020), and whether epigenetic modulation could reverse the transcriptional defects (e.g., PD-L1 downregulation, XBP-1 upregulation) they reported. Additionally, combining single-cell multi-omics with the clinical correlations identified by Danaii et al. (e.g., IL-10⁺ B cell frequency vs. number of miscarriages) could further refine patient stratification for epigenetic therapies.

In summary, our integrated analysis of human cohorts and preclinical models identifies an association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and persistent epigenetic dysfunction in Bregs from women with RPL. Reduced IL-10 expression is a baseline characteristic of RPL independent of COVID-19 (Fig. S1), but COVID-19 exposure—via its S protein in preclinical models—is linked to augmented severity of this dysfunction associated with novel epigenetic changes (Figs. S2–S3, S6) that lock Bregs into a non-functional state. This impairment disrupts IL-10–mediated immunoregulation, leading to adverse pregnancy outcomes (Figs. 6, S7). The reversibility of this phenotype via epigenetic therapy (Fig. 5) opens new avenues for treating RPL and other tolerance-breaking disorders. Beyond reproductive immunology, our work underscores how infections can be associated with enduring epigenetic imprints on specialized immune cells, with consequences that extend far beyond acute disease. Future studies should focus on refining epigenetic therapeutic strategies, exploring personalized approaches based on patient-specific epigenetic signatures, and validating these findings in larger prospective cohorts.
