SUMMARY: Consistent with its jurisdiction over swaps, the Commodity 
Futures Trading Commission (``Commission'' or ``CFTC'') is proposing to 
further define the term ``swap'' to explicitly include event contracts, 
including those based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-
related events (the ``Proposal'').

I. Background

    Title VII of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer 
Protection Act (``Dodd-Frank Act'') established a comprehensive 
regulatory framework for swaps.\2\ Section 712(d)(1) of the Dodd-Frank 
Act provides that the Commission and the Securities and Exchange 
Commission (``SEC''), in consultation with the Board of Governors of 
the Federal Reserve System (the ``Federal Reserve Board''), shall 
jointly further define, among other terms, the term ``swap.'' \3\ 
Pursuant to this requirement, in 2012, the Commission and the SEC, in 
consultation with the Federal Reserve Board, issued a final rule 
further defining the term ``swap'' and other terms.\4\

    This Proposal seeks to clarify that event contracts, including 
those based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-related 
events, are swaps within the CFTC's jurisdiction. The CFTC has 
historically recognized that many event contracts are covered by the 
Commodity Exchange Act's (the ``CEA'') swap definition.\5\ Under 
section 1a(47)(A) of the CEA, the term swap is defined ``broadly.'' \6\ 
As discussed further in this section below, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(ii) 
defines ``swap'' to include ``any agreement, contract, or transaction . 
. . that provides for any purchase, sale, payment, or delivery (other 
than a dividend on an equity security) that is dependent on the 
occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of the occurrence of an event 
or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or 
commercial consequence.'' \7\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

CEA, including futures, options on futures, securities such as 
options on securities and indexes of securities, security-based 
swaps, and debt securities). Certain event contracts may be

    The broad nature of clause (ii) of the swap definition is in 
keeping with the broad definition of the term ``commodity'' in the CEA. 
The 1974 amendments to the CEA expanded the definition of the term 
``commodity'' to encompass ``all services, rights, and interests in 
which contracts for future delivery are presently or in the future 
dealt in.'' \8\ In 2000, Congress reaffirmed the broad scope of the 
commodity definition in adding the term ``excluded commodity'' to the 
CEA--which, despite the name, is a commodity.\9\ This term includes, 
among other indices and measures, any ``occurrence, extent of an 
occurrence, or contingency'' of financial, commercial, or economic 
consequence that is beyond the control of any party to the relevant 
derivatives contract and is not based on a change in the price, rate, 
value, or level of a physical commodity.\10\ Derivatives

[[Page 66024]]

contracts based on these `` excluded commodities'' are subject to the 
Commission's jurisdiction just as derivatives based on other 
commodities are.

    The definition of ``excluded commodity'' makes clear that events 
that fall within the definition, including those related to sports, 
politics, culture, and weather, are themselves commodities.\11\ The 
CFTC has a long history of regulating event contracts. ``Since 1992, 
Commission-regulated exchanges have listed for trading a variety of 
commodity futures and options contracts with payout terms based on'' 
events ``as diverse as regional insured property losses, the count of 
bankruptcies, temperature volatilities, corporate mergers, and 
corporate credit events,'' and in subsequent years, event contracts 
encompassed ``the accomplishment of certain scientific advances, world 
population levels, the adoption of particular pieces of legislation,'' 
and even ``the length of celebrity marriages.'' \12\ In 1993, the 
Commission issued a no-action letter to Iowa Electronic Markets, 
allowing the facility to list event contracts tied to U.S. and Canadian 
elections.\13\ In 2004, the CFTC designated the first contract market 
(``DCM'') dedicated to listing contracts based on events that are 
excluded commodities.\14\ Initially, these contracts were structured as 
commodity options known as ``binary options,'' the cash settlement of 
which was based on whether a specified event had occurred.\15\

    Under the plain language of the CEA, event contracts are implicated 
by four clauses of the ``swap'' definition.
    First, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(ii) defines ``swap'' to include ``any 
agreement, contract, or transaction . . . that provides for any 
purchase, sale, payment, or delivery (other than a dividend on an 
equity security) that is dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or 
the extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated with 
a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence.'' \16\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \16\ 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(A)(ii).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Second, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(i) includes binary options and other 
commodity options in the ``swap'' definition by including ``any 
agreement, contract, or transaction . . . that is a put, call, cap, 
floor, collar, or similar option of any kind that is for the purchase 
or sale, or based on the value, of 1 or more interest or other rates, 
currencies, commodities, securities, instruments of indebtedness, 
indices, quantitative measures, or other financial or economic 
interests or property of any kind.'' \17\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \17\ 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(A)(i). A binary option is thus a type of 
swap which is an ``option whose payoff is either a fixed amount or 
zero.'' See CFTC, Futures Glossary, available at https://www.cftc.gov/LearnAndProtect/AdvisoriesAndArticles/CFTCGlossary/index.htm#B.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Third, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(iv) includes in the ``swap'' 
definition ``an agreement, contract, or transaction that is, or in the 
future becomes, commonly known to the trade as a swap.'' \18\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \18\ 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(A)(iv).

    Finally, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(vi) makes clear that the ``swap'' 
definition includes ``any combination or permutation of, or option on, 
any agreement, contract, or transaction described in any of clauses (i) 
through (v)'' of CEA section 1a(47)(A).\19\

    \19\ 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(A)(vi).

    In addition to the ``swap'' definitions provided by the statute, 
the Dodd-Frank Act included several provisions that give the Commission 
authority to further define ``swap.'' First, Dodd-Frank Act section 
712(d)(2), 15 U.S.C. 8302(d)(1), directs the CFTC and SEC, in 
consultation with the Federal Reserve Board, to ``further define'' the 
term ``swap.'' \20\ Second, Dodd-Frank Act section 721(b), 15 U.S.C. 
8321(a)(2), permits the CFTC to define any term included in an 
amendment to the CEA in Subtitle A of the Wall Street Transparency and 
Accountability Act of 2010, which includes the term ``swap.'' Third, 
Dodd-Frank Act section 721(b), 15 U.S.C. 8321(b), directs the CFTC to 
further define ``swap'' to include transactions and entities that have 
been structured to evade Subtitle A.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \20\ As mentioned, the CFTC and SEC, in consultation with the 
Federal Reserve Board, first further defined ``swap'' in a 2012 
joint rulemaking. Further Definition Release, 77 FR 48208. In that 
rulemaking, the CFTC and SEC noted that, while ``[t]he statutory 
definitions of the terms `swap' and `security-based swap' are 
detailed and comprehensive, . . . the definitions could be read to 
include certain types of agreements, contracts, and transactions 
that previously have not been considered swaps or security-based 
swaps, and nothing in the legislative history of the Dodd-Frank Act 
appears to suggest that Congress intended such agreements, 
contracts, or transactions to be regulated as swaps or security-
based swaps.'' The CFTC and SEC thus used their authority ``to 
further clarify the treatment under the definitions of certain types 
of agreements, contracts, and transactions.'' Id. at 48211.

    In recent months, States have taken actions and courts have issued 
rulings that undermine the CFTC's jurisdiction over event contracts. 
The State of Minnesota enacted a criminal prohibition on the operation 
or facilitation of a ``prediction market,'' which purports to make it a 
felony to offer event contracts on athletic events, elections, 
policymaking decisions, and a variety of other events.\21\ The State of 
Arizona filed a ``twenty-count criminal information'' against a DCM in 
Arizona Superior Court for offering event contracts on sports and 
elections in the State.\22\ The State of New York sought a temporary 
restraining order in State court that would prohibit a DCM from 
offering event ``contracts relating to sports, culture, elections, and 
other events'' ``within or from New York or to persons in New York.'' 
\23\ Because that DCM is headquartered in New York, that relief 
threatens to prohibit it from offering event contracts nationwide. That 
same DCM has also been ordered by State trial courts to geofence its 
offering of sports-related event contracts in the States of Michigan, 
Washington, and Nevada.\24\

    To date, three Federal courts of appeals have issued rulings 
concerning whether an event contract whose underlying is related to an 
outcome of a sporting event is a swap under CEA section 1a(47)(A)(ii). 
In April 2026, a divided panel of the United States Court of Appeals 
for the Third Circuit held in KalshiEX, LLC v. Flaherty that because 
``sports-related event contracts are traded on a CFTC-licensed DCM and 
depend on event outcomes associated with economic consequences, they 
fit within the Act's definition of `swaps' subject to the CFTC's 
jurisdiction.'' \25\ The dissenting judge also agreed that a ``plain 
reading of the Act's text suggests that [ ] sports-event contracts fit 
comfortably within the statutory definition.'' \26\

    In August 2026, the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth 
Circuit adopted the opposite interpretation of CEA section 
1a(47)(A)(ii).\27\ After acknowledging that ``the CEA expressly 
preempts state law regulating swaps `traded or executed on' a DCM,'' 
the court rejected the ``dictionary definitions'' and ``plain-language 
appeal'' of the Third Circuit's interpretation and concluded that, as 
used in the CEA, ``event'' means something different from ``a thing 
that happens or takes place.'' \28\ The court stated that the 
``substance'' of the sports event contracts at issue was ``sports 
gambling, regardless of whether [the DCM] calls them swaps'' (and, by 
inference, regardless of whether the CFTC regulates them as swaps).\29\ 
It expressed concern that the plain-text interpretation of the

address whether sports event contracts could qualify as swaps under 
other prongs of the CEA swap definition, including sections 
1a(47)(A)(i) and (iv). A petition for rehearing and rehearing en banc 
has been filed in that case.\33\

    As the above discussion demonstrates, there is widespread confusion 
and disuniformity about the definition of swap. The Commission 
therefore proposes to exercise its statutory authority to ``further 
define'' swap and expressly clarify that event contracts, including 
those based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-related 
events, are swaps.

II. Proposed Rule

    The Commission is proposing to further define the term swap in the 
context of event contracts. Specifically, the Commission proposes to 
add new paragraph (2)(i)(G) to the definition of ``swap'' in Sec.  1.3 
of the Commission's regulations.\40\ This proposed addition identifies 
as swaps event contracts, including, in a non-exhaustive list, those 
based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-related events.\41\ 
As discussed in more detail below, event contracts, including those 
based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-related events, are 
financial instruments that are commonly known to the trade as swaps, 
structured as binary options, and/or structured to make payment 
dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of the 
occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential 
financial, economic, or commercial consequence. It is the Commission's 
view that the best reading of the statute is that event contracts based 
on sports-, political-, cultural-, or weather-related events qualify as 
swaps under each of these statutory prongs. In addition, the 
Commission's exercise of its express statutory authority to ``further 
define'' the term ``swap'' provides an independent legal basis to deem 
these contracts as swaps.\42\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \40\ 17 CFR 1.3.
    \41\ As described above in note 5, event contracts might be 
structured as other instruments such as futures contracts, which are 
excluded from the statutory definition of ``swap.'' Additionally, 
certain event contracts may be security-based swaps or other 
instruments subject to the SEC's jurisdiction. The event contracts 
at issue in this Proposal are those that are within the definition 
of ``swap'' subject to the CFTC's jurisdiction, and not those that 
might be futures or SEC-regulated instruments. Furthermore, the 
proposed addition would not reach casino-style gambling products, 
which, as the Commission describes in a separate rulemaking, are not 
swaps subject to the Commission's jurisdiction.

    First, the Commission believes that event contracts, including 
those based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-related 
events are ``commonly known to the trade as a swap'' within the meaning 
of prong (iv) of the swap definition.\43\ The Commission

[[Page 66026]]

understands the phrase ``commonly known to the trade as a swap'' as a 
residual, catch-all prong intended to cover instruments that, in the 
market's view, are swaps.\44\ The Commission further understands ``the 
trade'' in this context to mean those who participate in the 
derivatives markets, including the CFTC, its registrants, and traders, 
rather than some broader group, such as the general public.\45\ 
Furthermore, the Commission notes that this standard does not require 
universal recognition--``commonly known'' is not the same as 
``universally known.'' In this regard, the scope of prong (iv) should 
be informed by the actual practice of market participants and the 
longstanding regulatory treatment of instruments by the CFTC.

    Market participants have also overwhelmingly recognized that event 
contracts are swaps by complying with the CFTC's swap regulatory regime 
just as they do when listing, clearing, or trading other swaps. For 
example, over the past several years, DCMs have self-certified 
thousands of event contracts based on sports, politics, cultural 
events, and weather to the Commission.\53\ This includes both new 
entrants into the market and longstanding exchanges like CME Group 
(``CME''), which has self-certified numerous event contracts based on 
sports and elections as swaps.\54\ And in comments to the CFTC, CME has 
recognized that ``event contracts and swaps are not new and have been 
subject to the existing regulatory scheme for years without issue,'' 
and that the CEA's ``Core Principles[ ] provide[ ] the proper structure 
to regulate these products.'' \55\ Cboe Global Markets has likewise 
stated in comments that event contracts ``generally fall within the 
CEA's broad definition of `swap,' '' absent a statutory exception.\56\ 
Consistent with this commonly held market view, such Commission 
registrants have not sought to register at the State level or offer 
products under State gambling laws. Instead, they offer event contracts 
for multilateral trading on a national, competitive market, just like 
other derivatives.

    Second, the Commission believes that event contracts, including 
those based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-related 
events, fall within prong (ii) of the swap definition, which 
encompasses any agreement, contract, or transaction whose underlying is 
an occurrence or contingency ``associated with a potential financial, 
economic, or commercial consequence.'' \61\ The Third Circuit's 
Flaherty decision adopted this conclusion in the context of sports-
related event contracts, and the reasoning applies more generally as 
well. Applying the plain text of the statute, the court held that 
``[t]he outcome of a sports event certainly can be associated with a 
potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence,'' and that 
sports-related event contracts traded on a CFTC-licensed DCM ``fit 
within the Act's definition of `swaps' subject to the CFTC's 
jurisdiction.'' \62\ In reaching that conclusion, the court expressly 
endorsed the district court's identification of the broad range of 
economic stakeholders--including sponsors, advertisers, television 
networks, sports franchises, and local and national communities--whose 
financial interests are directly affected by sporting event outcomes, 
finding that identification of such stakeholders was sufficient to 
satisfy the ``associated with a potential financial, economic, or 
commercial consequence'' standard.\63\ Even the dissent conceded that a 
plain reading of the Act's text suggests that sports-related event 
contracts fit comfortably within the statutory definition.\64\ And the 
court confirmed that the Commission's statutory authority to further 
define ``swap'' would be valuable in this area.

    Third, the Commission believes that event contracts may 
independently satisfy the first prong of the swap definition. The 
Commission's preliminary reasoning is based on a plain reading of 
section 1a(47)(A)(i) of the CEA. Section 1a(47)(A)(i) explicitly 
defines a swap to include any agreement, contract, or transaction that 
is a ``put, call, cap, floor, collar, or similar option of any kind'' 
that is ``based on the value[ ] of 1 or more . . . commodities . . . or 
other financial or economic interests or property of any kind.'' \69\ 
In this regard, the Commission notes that many event contracts are 
structured as binary options, meaning that the contract's payout is a 
predetermined fixed amount (typically, one dollar) if the underlying 
condition is satisfied, and zero if it is not. Courts \70\ and the CFTC 
\71\ have repeatedly affirmed that binary option event contracts are a 
type of swap subject to the Commission's jurisdiction. Because, as 
described above, an event (such as a sports-, politics-, cultural-, or 
weather-related event) is an excluded commodity, a binary option event 
contract referencing such an event is a swap within the Commission's 
jurisdiction.

    The term ``excluded commodity,'' including ``an occurrence, extent 
of an occurrence, or contingency . . . that is--(I) beyond the control 
of the parties to the relevant contract, agreement, or transaction; and 
(II) associated with a financial, commercial, or economic 
consequence,'' has long been interpreted broadly. This definition was 
added to the CEA by the Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000 
(``CFMA'').\72\ The CFMA provided for a near-complete exemption from 
the exchange trading requirement for swaps in an ``excluded commodity'' 
between institutional traders.\73\ In other words, these commodities 
are defined as ``excluded'' because the CFMA exempted swaps involving 
them from the exchange trading requirement.

    Finally, the Special Rule added by Dodd-Frank to the CEA clarifies 
that the CFTC has jurisdiction over ``[e]vent contracts,'' including 
event contracts based on non-financial commodities. The Special Rule 
provides that ``[i]n connection with the listing of agreements, 
contracts, transactions, or swaps in excluded commodities that are 
based upon the occurrence, extent of an occurrence, or contingency 
(other than a change in the price, rate, value, or levels of a 
commodity described in section 1a(2)(i) of this title), by a designated 
contract market or swap execution facility, the Commission may 
determine that such agreements, contracts, or transactions are contrary 
to the public interest if the agreements, contracts, or transactions 
involve--(I) activity that is unlawful under any Federal or State law; 
(II) terrorism; (III) assassination; (IV) war; (V) gaming; or (VI) 
other similar activity determined by the Commission, by rule or 
regulation, to be contrary to the public interest.\80\ This rule 
confirms that the term ``excluded commodities'' includes 
``occurrence[s]'' that ``involve'' various non-financial events, 
including but not limited to terrorism, assassination, war, and gaming. 
It also confirms that ``swaps'' can be ``based upon'' such non-
financial activities. Thus, the notion that the ``event'' referred to 
in the second prong of the ``swap'' definition must ``be one inherently 
associated with a financial, economic, or commercial consequence'' is 
incompatible with the text of the Special Rule.\81\

potential financial, economic, or commercial consequences associated 
with underlying sports, political, cultural, and weather events that 
are the subject of event contracts. Importantly, the statute uses the 
word ``potential,'' which is broad.\87\ So even if the financial 
consequences of a particular event are not felt right away--indeed, 
even if they never actually occur--an event contract remains a swap so 
long as there is potential for commercial ramifications.\88\ For 
example, it may be difficult to empirically verify that there are 
financial consequences associated with a particular city reaching a 
temperature of 97 to 98 degrees on a given day, as opposed to 95 to 96 
degrees. By inserting the word ``potential'' into the swap definition, 
Congress signaled that it did not wish

causing to be obtained, or soliciting information, and includes 
required disclosure to third parties or the public, of facts or 
opinions, when the information collection calls for answers to 
identical questions posed to, or identical reporting or recordkeeping 
requirements imposed on, ten or more persons.\116\

C. Consideration of Benefits and Costs

1. Introduction
    The Commission is mindful of the economic effects, including the 
benefits and costs, of the Proposal. Section 15(a) of the CEA requires 
the Commission to consider the benefits and costs of its actions before 
promulgating a regulation under the CEA or issuing certain orders.\117\ 
As required by section 15(a), the Commission evaluates these benefits 
and costs evaluated in light of five broad areas of market and public 
concern: (1) protection of market participants and the public; (2) 
efficiency, competitiveness, and financial integrity of futures 
markets; (3) price discovery; (4) sound risk management practices; and 
(5) other public interest considerations.

1a(47) to include event contracts, including those based on sports-, 
politics-, cultural-, and weather-related events.\118\ It is the

Commission's preliminary belief that the Proposal would not change the 
legal rights or obligations of market participants under the CEA

nationwide treatment of event contracts under the exclusive 
jurisdiction of the Commission.

2. Baseline
    The baseline against which the benefits and costs of the Proposal 
are measured consists of the current regulatory framework and the 
current state of the event contracts market.
a. Regulatory Baseline
    The Commission has exclusive jurisdiction to regulate transactions 
involving swaps (other than security-based swaps, which the SEC 
regulates, and mixed swaps, over which it shares jurisdiction with the 
SEC).\119\ The Commission preliminary believes that under the plain 
language of the CEA, event contracts, including event contracts based 
on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-related events, are 
swaps.\120\ For example, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(ii) defines ``swap'' to 
include ``any agreement, contract, or transaction . . . that provides 
for any purchase, sale, payment, or delivery (other than a dividend on 
an equity security) that is dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, 
or the extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated 
with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence.'' 
\121\ It is the Commission's preliminary belief that this comprehensive 
definition captures many event contracts, which are a type of swap 
where payment is triggered by ``the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the 
extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a 
potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence,'' such as a 
political election or a sporting event.\122\

b. Current Event Contract Markets
    The number of DCMs listing event contracts and the volume of 
trading in such contracts have increased significantly in recent years. 
For example, as of September 1, 2026, at least seven DCMs registered 
with the CFTC offer contracts that include event contracts based on 
sports-related events to U.S. persons. More than fifteen pending DCM 
applications have been filed since 2025. Many of these newly designated 
DCMs and platforms with pending applications have stated they intend to 
offer contracts that could be considered event contracts. In August 
2026, average monthly trading volume in event contracts was about $1.5 
billion across all DCMs reporting to the Commission.\127\ Of that 
volume, approximately $1.2 billion was in event contracts based on 
sports-related events; approximately $11 million was in event contracts 
based on politics-related events, including elections-related event 
contracts; and approximately $4 million was in event contracts based on 
weather-related events.\128\

3. Benefits
    Because the Commission does not expect the Proposal to change the 
legal rights or obligations of market participants under the CEA, it is 
the Commission's preliminary belief that the primary benefit of this 
Proposal would be added certainty that event contracts, including those 
based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-related events, are 
swaps that are subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of the CFTC. 
Although the extent of the benefit may be limited by the ongoing 
efforts of certain state regulators to assert their jurisdiction over 
certain event contracts, market participants--including DCMs listing 
event contracts-- may benefit by spending less time and fewer resources 
determining which regulatory regime applies and by saving the costs of 
complying with various State regulatory regimes.
4. Costs
    It is the Commission's preliminary belief that there should be 
negligible costs from codifying that event contracts, including those 
based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and weather-related events, are 
swaps subject to the Commission's exclusive jurisdiction, as there 
would be no expected changes required of market participants under the 
CEA or the Commission's regulations.
    However, the Proposal may result in indirect costs to the extent 
that market participants respond to the added certainty by shifting 
activity toward the CFTC swaps framework. For example, States might 
receive less fee and other revenue if entities choose to structure 
their business operations as DCMs instead of structuring their 
operations within State regulatory frameworks. It is also possible that 
costs could also result from the potential transition, generating 
short-term frictions. For example, market participants could incur 
reconciling costs where Federal and State regulatory regimes overlap 
during the transition, such as unwinding or

[[Page 66032]]

maintaining State licenses in parallel with CFTC-regulated obligations 
during the transition.
5. Section 15(a) Factors
a. Protection of Market Participants and the Public
    The Commission preliminarily believes that classification certainty 
provided by the Proposal may allow market participants to more readily 
operate within the Federal swaps framework and its attendant 
protections. Insofar as this certainty leads to an increase in the 
number of event contracts listed on DCMs, the protection of market 
participants and the public may be advanced by application of the Core 
Principles to the market. However, certainty provided by the Proposal 
could be perceived as a safety signal by retail participants. This may 
raise risk appetite for certain categories of event contracts as retail 
market participants may update their beliefs about oversight of these 
products and under-weigh their inherent risk and increase their 
exposure to these products without parallel improvements in disclosure 
or education that could improve comprehension of risks related to event 
contracts based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, or weather-related 
events.
b. Efficiency, Competitiveness, and Financial Integrity of Futures 
Markets
    The Commission preliminarily believes that the Proposal is unlikely 
to affect the efficiency or financial integrity of the futures markets, 
but there could be effects on the swaps market. To the extent that 
firms restructure their operations from State regimes to the Federal 
swaps regime, the Proposal could facilitate direct competition between 
CFTC-regulated DCMs and State-regulated businesses. However, because 
the Federal swaps regime requires infrastructure with fixed costs, 
certainty provided by the Proposal might favor incumbent DCMs with 
established infrastructure and customer bases. Furthermore, the 
Commission preliminarily believes that clarifying that event contracts 
are swaps may advantage exchanges already configured for Federal 
compliance relative to state-licensed platforms, shifting costs 
unevenly where new entrants may face higher fixed costs to reach swaps 
compliance scale, potentially reducing competitive pressure on fees, 
service quality, and innovation diversity at the margin.
c. Price Discovery
    The Commission preliminarily believes that price discovery for 
event contracts will not be materially affected because the Proposal 
does not alter trading rules, exchange requirements, or information-
dissemination obligations for the contracts it covers, which the 
Commission already expects to trade on DCMs subject to existing Core 
Principles.
d. Sound Risk Management Practices
    The Commission preliminarily believes that risk management 
practices will not be materially affected because the Proposal does not 
alter the application of Commission requirements regarding risk 
management, including DCM risk-management Core Principles.
e. Other Public Interest Considerations
    Codification of the Commission's understanding that event 
contracts, including those based on sports-, politics-, cultural-, and 
weather-related events, are swaps may improve legal predictability and 
administrability, consistent with the CEA's purpose of encouraging 
effective self-regulation of trading facilities, clearing systems, and 
market participants. The Commission preliminarily believes that the 
Proposal may reduce the resources market participants devote to 
complying with various, sometimes-contradictory State regulatory 
regimes.
    But, insofar as the certainty provided by the Proposal increases 
the volume of event contracts listed on DCMs, the Commission 
preliminarily believes that retail market participants could be harmed 
because some event contract trading characteristics (such as outcomes 
occurring at unpredictable intervals, perception of skill-based 
decision making, near-miss experiences, and potential for loss-chasing 
behavior) can be associated with addictive potential. It is possible 
that these characteristics can lead to financial harm to participants 
who accumulate losses through high-frequency trading, allocate 
disproportionate resources to trading activity, and have trouble 
disengaging voluntarily.
6. Regulatory Alternatives
    Given the Proposal seeks to articulate the Commission's existing 
jurisdiction over swaps, the Commission has not identified any viable 
regulatory alternatives.
