CHI3L1 promotes cell division through activation of RhoA in lung cancer cells
Authors: Ji Eun Yu, Jung-Hyun Shim, In Jun Yeo, Dong Ju Son, Jaesuk Yun, Key-Hwan Lim, Hanseul Park, Yoon Seok Roh, Sang-Bae Han, Jin Tae Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCytokinesis, the final stage of cell division, is crucial for maintaining genomic stability. Dysregulation of cytokinesis can contribute to cancer development. This study aimed to investigate the role of CHI3L1 in cytokinesis and its influence on lung cancer cell growth.MethodsWe modulated CHI3L1 expression in lung cancer cells (overexpression and depletion) and normal cells, assessing effects on cytokinesis, mitotic duration, and actomyosin ring formation. In vivo studies were conducted using CHI3L1 knockout mice with tumor xenografts. Expression and activity of RhoA and Ect2 were analyzed using immunoblotting and immunohistochemistry.ResultsCHI3L1 overexpression in lung cancer cells accelerated cytokinesis, shortened mitosis, and enhanced cell proliferation. Conversely, CHI3L1 depletion in A549 cells increased multinucleation, impaired furrow formation, prolonged mitosis, and caused abnormal cytokinesis. Normal cells were less affected by CHI3L1 modulation. Mechanistically, CHI3L1 promoted formation and contraction of the actomyosin ring via RhoA and Ect2, effects more pronounced in cancer cells. In CHI3L1 knockout mice, tumor tissues exhibited reduced RhoA activity and Ect2 expression.ConclusionsCHI3L1 functions as a novel regulator of cytokinesis, promoting actomyosin ring formation and efficient mitosis, particularly in cancer cells. Its elevated expression in NSCLC and its influence on RhoA and Ect2 suggest that CHI3L1 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for disrupting aberrant cytokinesis in cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02601-5.

Background

Cell division is crucial for growth, development, and tissue maintenance. Cytokinesis is the process by which a cell divides into two daughter cells following mitosis. In animal cells, cytokinesis involves the formation of actomyosin ring. This ring is initiated during anaphase and starts to contract in telophase, leading to inward pinching of the cell membrane [1–3]. As the ring contracts, it exerts tension to the cell membrane, resulting in the formation of a cleavage furrow. The cleavage furrow gradually deepens, ultimately dividing the cytoplasm into two daughter cells [1, 3, 4]. The dynamic nature of the actomyosin ring and its interaction with actin and myosin proteins are critical for ensuring accurate and efficient cytokinesis.

RhoA, a small GTPase protein belonging to the Rho family, plays a crucial role in the formation and contraction of the actomyosin ring during cytokinesis [5]. It becomes activated and localizes to the site of actomyosin ring formation in response to signals from the mitotic spindle and central spindle, ensuring the proper timing and positioning of the ring [5–7]. Once activated, RhoA recruits and activates downstream effector proteins, including Rho-associated protein kinases (ROCKs) [8, 9]. These kinases, in turn, phosphorylate and activate myosin light chain (MLC) and other components of the actomyosin ring, ultimately leading to the contraction of actin-myosin filaments [10–12]. RhoA also contributes to stabilizing and maintaining the integrity of the actomyosin ring during cytokinesis [6, 13, 14]. It promotes the assembly of actin filaments and cross-linking proteins like anillin, ensuring the proper organization and structure of the actomyosin ring [15–17].

However, when the actomyosin ring becomes dysfunctional, it can lead to cytokinesis failure and the generation of abnormal cells. This dysfunction can occur due to various reasons, including mutations or alterations in the genes encoding actomyosin ring components or the regulatory proteins involved in its assembly and function [2, 18]. Notably, cancer cells often exhibit abnormalities in cytokinesis, and dysfunctional actomyosin rings have been observed in many types of cancer. The failure of proper cytokinesis can result in the formation of multinucleated cells or cells with an abnormal number of chromosomes, a condition known as aneuploidy. Aneuploidy is a hallmark of cancer and can contribute to genomic instability, thereby fostering the development of malignant characteristics in cells. Furthermore, impaired cytokinesis can also impact cancer cell migration and invasion. Dysfunctional actomyosin rings can disrupt the coordinated movement of cancer cells, promoting invasive behavior and metastasis [19, 20]. Therefore, identifying new proteins involved in cytokinesis regulation can potentially lead to the development of targeted therapies aimed at restoring normal cytokinesis and inhibiting cancer cell growth.

CHI3L1 is a glycoprotein that has been implicated in the development of various types of cancers including lung, breast, colon, and others [21–25]. It has been shown to be involved in various cellular processes such as cell proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, and immune regulation [21, 25–31]. Elevated expression of CHI3L1 has been reported to affect the expression or activity of various cell cycle regulators such as cyclin and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) [21, 28, 29, 32]. In addition, CHI3L1 interacted with several growth factor signaling pathways related to cell cycle regulation, such as epithelial growth factor receptor (EGFR) pathway and insulin-like growth factor (IGF) pathway [24, 33]. CHI3L1 is often upregulated in inflammation and inflammatory signals can affect cell cycle checkpoints and promote cell cycle progression. Given the crucial role of cytokinesis in maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing tumorigenesis, investigating the possible relationship between CHI3L1 and cell division could shed light on novel pathways influencing the accurate execution of this process. Understanding the impact of CHI3L1 on cytokinesis may offer new perspectives on the regulation of cell division and its relevance to disease states, paving the way for potential therapeutic strategies targeting this protein.

In this study, we investigate the impact of CHI3L1 on cell division and its implications in cancer development, building on its potential role in cell cycle regulation. Additionally, we explore the functional significance of CHI3L1 during cytokinesis and its potential interplay with well-established mitotic regulators.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

A549 lung cancer cells, H460 lung cancer cells, Hep3B liver cancer cells, MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells, and BEAS-2B lung normal cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA). A549, H460, BEAS-2B cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 µg/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Hep3B and MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 µg/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The cell cultures were maintained in an incubator with a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Reduced serum medium and X-tream GENE HP DNA transfection reagent (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) were used for transfecting plasmid and siRNA oligonucleotides following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Animal model

Animal experiments were performed as described previously [29]. For xenograft animal model, A549 human lung cancer cells were injected subcutaneously (s.c.) (1 × 107 cells/100 µL in PBS/animal) into upper dorsal region of CHI3L1−knock out (KO) mice and wild type mice. 4 weeks after injection, the animals were sacrificed, and the tumors were dissected and excised from the surrounding muscles and dermis.

For lung metastasis model, A549 cells were injected into the tail veins of CHI3L1−KO mice and wild type mice (1 × 106 cells in/100 µl phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) per animal). 7 weeks after injection, the animals were sacrificed, and the lung tissues were excised.

All protocols involving mice in this study were reviewed and approved by the Chungbuk National University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and complied with the Korean National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (CBNUA-2120-23-02).

Human samples

Human tissue samples from lung cancer patients and normal controls were obtained from Chungbuk National University Hospital Biobank, Keimyung University Dongsan Hospital Biobank and the Biobank of Ajou University Hospital, members of Korea Biobank Network. All studies using human samples were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Chungbuk National University Medical Center (IRB No. CBNU-201910-BR-941-01).

Cell treatment

To synchronize the cells, cells were treated with 2.5 mM thymidine for 18 h and released for 9 h with a fresh medium. The second 2.5 mM thymidine block was treated for 18 h and arrested them in the G1/S phase. Cell cycle progression was released at the indicated point after double thymidine block.

Cell lysates preparation and western blotting

Cells were harvested with 1x cold-PBS and lysed with lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.8, 0.1% NP-40, 200 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 5 mM EGTA with protease inhibitor, and protease inhibitor). The cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. In total, 10 µg of proteins were subjected to SDS–PAGE for separation and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies and subsequently with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The desired proteins were detected using an ECL substrate (#WBKLS0500, Millipore, Billerica, MA) and visualized using the FUSION Solo S chemiluminescence detection system (Vilber Lourmat, Collégien, France). The primary antibodies were as follows: anti-CHI3L1 (dilution 1:1000; #A3166, Abclonal, Wuhan, China), anti-PRC1 (dilution 1:1000; #sc-376983, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), anti-Aurora B (dilution 1:1000; #ab3609, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-RhoA (dilution 1:1000; #2117, Cell Signaling, Boston, MA), anti-Ect2 (dilution 1:500; #sc-514750, Santa Cruz), anti-α-tubulin (dilution 1:1000; #sc-5286, Santa Cruz), and anti-β-actin (dilution 1:5000; #sc-517582, Santa Cruz).

Immunoprecipitation

For immunoprecipitation, the cell lysates were incubated with specific primary antibodies for 4 h at 4 °C. Then protein A/G PLUS-Agrose (Santa cruz) were added to antibody containing cell lysates and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. Immunoprecipitated proteins were loaded to SDS-PAGE.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were arrested for 18 h with 2.5 mM thymidine (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and then released into fresh medium for 9 h. To enrich for prometaphase cells, cells were released into medium containing 200 ng/ml nocodazole (Sigma) for 18 h. Mitotic cells were collected by mitotic shake-off and subsequently released into fresh medium for 2 h to obtain telophase cells. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. The cells were then permeabilized with 100% cold-methanol for 5 min and blocked using 4% BSA in PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100 (PBST) for 1 h. The saples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then with Alexa Fluor 488 (#A32723, #A32731, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) or Texas Red (#T-862, #T-2767, Invitrogen)-conjugated secondary antibodies 1 h at room temperature. The cells were then incubated with 1 µg/mL DAPI (#D9542, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for 5 min at room temperature and subsequently covered with Fluoromount-G Mounting Medium (#0100-01, Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL). The cells were visualized using the Zeiss Axio Observer fluorescence microscope system (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), and the images were analyzed using ZEN 2.1 software (Carl Zeiss). Midbody fluorescence intensity was quantified using ZEN 2.1 software. ROI was placed at the center of each midbody, and background signals measured from the cytoplasm of the same cell were subtracted. Each midbody was treated as a single data point and analyzed in a random manner.

The primary antibodies were as follows: anti-CHI3L1 (dilution 1:100; #ab77528, Abcam), anti-PRC1 (dilution 1:100; #sc-376983, Santa Cruz), anti-Aurora B (dilution 1:100; #ab3609, Abcam), anti-RhoA (dilution 1:100; #sc-418, Santa Cruz), anti-Ect2 (dilution 1:50; #sc-514750, Santa Cruz), anti-α-tubulin (dilution 1:200; #sc-5286, Santa Cruz), and anti-γ-tubulin (dilution 1:200; #MA1-850, Invitrogen).

Immunohistochemistry

The tumor and lung tissue sections were blocked with 3% normal goat serum diluted in PBS, for 30 min; the sections were then incubated with antibodies for CHI3L1, RhoA, and Ect2, at the appropriate dilution in blocking serum, for overnight at 4 °C. The slides were washed in PBS, followed by the avidin–biotin–peroxidase complex (#PK-6101, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA). The slides were washed, and the peroxidase reaction was developed with diaminobenzidine and peroxide (#SK-4100, Vector Laboratories), mounted in Aqua-Mount, and evaluated under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Time-lapse imaging

A549 cells were seeded onto a µ-Dish (35 mm, high Glass Bottom, ibidi GmbH, Martinsried, Germany), and transfected with mCherry Histone H2B (Plasmid #20972, Addgene, Watertown, MA). Cells were monitored for 18 h after 8 h of transfection and observed on an Axio Observer Z1 inverted fluorescence microscope, equipped with a CO2 and a temperature controller. Digital images were obtained under an A-Plan20X/0.8ph2M27 objective, with snapshots acquired at 4 min intervals using the AxioCam MRm camera, operated by the ZEN v2.1 (blue edition) software. The imaging was performed under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The live cell movie generation and image analysis were performed using the ZEN v2.1 (blue edition) software.

FACS analysis

A549 cells were washed with PBS, harvested using trypsinization and incubated with RNase A for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were stained with 100 mg/mL propidium iodide (PI) for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. The FACS samples were analyzed using a BD FACS Calibur™ fow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), and the data were analyzed using the CellQuest Pro v6.0 software (BD Biosciences).

RhoA pull-down activation assay

RhoA activity was measured using Rho Activation Assay Biochem Kit (#BK036, Cytoskeleton, Los Angeles, CA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were lysed with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.5 M NaCl, and 2% IGEPAL CA-360). The cell lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Then, 20 µg/mL rhotekin-RBD beads were added to the cell lysates, and the samples wwere incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed with washing buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 30 mM MgCl2, 40 mM NaCl) two times, and 2x Laemmli sample buffer was added. After heating at 95 °C for 10 min, the immunoprecipitated proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)−2,5-diphenyl-2 H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (#M5655, Sigma-Aldrich), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, 5 µg/mL MTT solution was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well. The absorbance of each sample was detected using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 540 nm.

Midbody isolation

Briefly, the cells were centrifuged at 200 × g for 3 min at 4 °C and then resuspended in 25x hypotonic swelling solution (1 M hexylene glycol, 20 µM MgCl2, 2 mM PIPES, pH 7.2) at room temperature. Cells were centrifuged at 200 × g for 3 min at 4 °C and then lysed in 50 X lysis solution (1 M hexylene glycol, 1 mM EGTA, 1% NP-40, 2 mM PIPES, pH 7.2) for 30 s at 4 °C. The cell lysates were stabilized by chilling on ice and added to 0.3 × lower pH solution (1 M hexylene glycol, 30 mM MES, pH 6.3). To remove large debris, cell lysates were centrifuged at 250 × g for 10 min, and the supernatants were applied to glycerol cushion (40% glycerol (w/v) in 50 mM MES, pH 6.3). Midbody pellets were isolated by centrifugation for 40 min at 2,800 × g. The pellets were washed with 50 mM MES buffer (pH 6.3), and then 2× Laemmli sample buffer was added. After heating at 95 °C for 10 min, the isolated proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE.

Protein-protein interaction network

The protein-protein interaction network of CHI3L1 was analyzed by Humanbase database (https://hb.flatironinstitute.org/) [34] and GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) [35], which is publicly available sources of protein-protein interaction information.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5 software (La Jolla, CA). The error bars represent standard deviations (SDs), unless indicated otherwise. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test. Multiple comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s tests. Differences with P values lower than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

CHI3L1 is essential for the completion of cell division

Cancer remains a major health concern worldwide, with a high mortality rate and limited treatment options. Understanding the molecular mechanisms involved in cancer development and progression is crucial for the development of effective therapeutic strategies. Cell cycle dysregulation is a hallmark of cancer, and understanding the molecular events that drive aberrant cell division is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying oncogenesis. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of CHI3L1 in cancer, and the relevance of CHI3L1 in lung cancer has garnered significant attention [21, 27, 28, 32, 36–38]. The A549 lung cancer cell line is widely used in various research fields related to the cell cycle in lung cancer, molecular mechanisms of cancer, and studies on the efficacy of anticancer agents [38–40]. Therefore, we conducted this study to explore the role of CHI3L1 during cell division in A549 lung cancer cells. To investigate the role of CHI3L1 in cell division, we overexpressed CHI3L1 in A549 cells. As the expression of CHI3L1 increased, the growth of A549 cells also progressively increased (Fig. 1A). We next examined the cellular population changes of CHI3L1-overexpressing cancer cells through a thymidine block release experiment using FACS. Control cells mostly reached the G2/M phase after 12 h, whereas CHI3L1-overexpressing cells were already present in the G2/M phase after 9 h (Fig. 1B). This finding suggests that the overexpression of CHI3L1 promotes cancer cell division by shortening the time required for cells to progress through the cell cycle. To assess whether CHI3L1 overexpression actually promotes cell division, we conducted time-lapse imaging analysis in control (expressing empty vector) and CHI3L1-overexpressing cancer cells. Both control and CHI3L1-overexpressing cells successfully generated two daughter cells, completing the cell division (Fig. 1C). However, in CHI3L1-overexpressing cells, there was a reduction in the duration of mitosis from metaphase to telophase (control 75.12 ± 3.85 min vs. CHI3L1 58.57 ± 4.79 min) (Fig. 1C), suggesting that CHI3L1 overexpression promotes cancer cell division.

To assess whether the observed effects were specific to cancer cells, we performed additional experiments in BEAS-2B normal (non-tumorigenic) lung cells. CHI3L1-overexpressing in normal cells also resulted in increased cell growth (Figure S1A). In addition, time-lapse imaging analysis results showed that both control and CHI3L1-overexpressing normal cells successfully completed cell division. However, unlike cancer cells, CHI3L1-overexpressioning normal cells showed no significant changes in the duration of mitosis (control 89.16 ± 3.95 min vs. CHI3L1 87.99 ± 4.72 min) (Figure S1B). These results suggest that the effect of CHI3L1 on cell division may have a greater impact on cancer cells.

Conversely, we examined whether CHI3L1 depletion induces cell division failure in A549 lung cancer cells. One of the observed outcomes resulting from cell division failure is the presence of multinucleated cells. Although 0.9% of the control cells had multi-nucleation, this percentage significantly increased to 11% in CHI3L1-depleted cancer cells (Fig. 2A). In addition, we observed a significant increase in the number of cells forming intercellular bridges in CHI3L1-depleted cancer cells (Fig. 2B). These results indicate that CHI3L1 depletion induce multinucleation and abnormalities in cell division, such as the formation of intercellular bridges suggesting that CHI3L1 may have a significant impact on cytokinesis. Moreover, by quantifying cellular DNA content through FACS analysis, we found that CHI3L1-depleted cancer cells exhibited significant increases in the subG1 and multinucleated (> 4 N) cell populations (Fig. 2C). These alterations in cell populations further highlight the impact of CHI3L1 depletion on cell division. To further investigate the influence of CHI3L1 on cell division, we analyzed time-lapse imaging of CHI3L1-depleted cancer cells. The time taken for mitosis (from metaphase to telophase) in control siRNA-treated cancer cells was 82.95 ± 4.44 min. However, CHI3L1-depleted cancer cells showed a significantly longer duration of mitosis (226 ± 39.22 min) (Fig. 3D). While most control siRNA-treated cells successfully completed cell division, only 61.25% of CHI3L1-depleted cancer cells completed cytokinesis, exhibiting a significant reduction in the rate of successful cell division compared to the control siRNA-treated cells. Approximately 38.75% of CHI3L1-depleted cells exhibited abnormal cytokinesis, classified as “no furrowing” (15.30 ± 3.14%) or “regression” (23.45 ± 2.45%) (Fig. 2E).

Nest, we investigated whether normal cells also exhibit the abnormal cell division phenotypes observed in CHI3L1-depleted cancer cells. Similar to cancer cells, the multinucleated cells and the number of cells forming intercellular bridge cells were increased in BEAS-2B normal cells, but the increase was greater in cancer cells (Figure S2A and S2B). CHI3L1 localizes to the midbody of telophase during cell division

Cell division involves several stages, including DNA replication, chromosome segregation, and cytokinesis. The midbody is a transient structure that forms during cytokinesis, the final stage of cell division, playing a crucial role in its completion [41, 42]. To determine whether CHI3L1 is localized in the midbody during cell division, we utilized immunofluorescence microscopy to track CHI3L1 expression along with α-tubulin throughout the entire cell cycle in A549 lung cancer cells. Similar to our previous study, CHI3L1 was partially localized in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus during interphase [27]. CHI3L1 concentrates at the equatorial cortex in anaphase and eventually become focused at the midbody in telophase (Fig. 3A). To validate the midbody localization, co-immuno staining was performed with midbody markers, Aurora B, PRC1, and γ-tubulin, along with CHI3L1 in A549 lung cancer cells. As a result, CHI3L1 was co-localized with the midbody core marker proteins PRC1 and γ-tubulin (Fig. 3B). However, CHI3L1 is slightly separated from the midbody arm marker protein Aurora B. These results indicate that CHI3L1 is localized at the midbody core region during telophase cancer cells. To detect CHI3L1 in midobdy, we synchronized A549 cancer cells into interphase and telophase cells and then isolated the midbody. We observed that the expression of CHI3L1 was significantly higher in telophase midbody fraction compared to the interphase cells (Fig. 3C). Additionally, the expression levels of the midbody markers, Aurora B and PRC1 were also significantly increased in telophase in A549 lung cancer cells (Fig. 3C). To exclude the possibility of CHI3L1 midbody localization being specific to A549 lung cancer cells, we examined the localization of CHI3L1 in other cancer cell lines, such as H460, Hep3B, and MDA-MB-231 cells. CHI3L1 was localized in the midbody of telophase during cell division in these cancer cells (Figure S3A).

Next, we investigated the localization of CHI3L1 in BEAS-2B normal cells. Indeed, similar to cancer cells, CHI3L1 was also observed to be localized at the midbody during telophase in normal cells (Figure S3B). However, when comparing the intensity of CHI3L1 fluorescence at the midbody between A549 lung cancer cells and normal cells, the intensity of CHI3L1 fluorescence was found to be higher in cancer cells (Figure S3B). These data suggest that the accumulation of CHI3L1 at the midbody might be more prominent in cancer cells compared to normal cells, indicating a potential role of CHI3L1 in regulating cell division processes, specifically during the telophase stage.

CHI3L1 promotes actomyosin ring formation

The contractile activity of actomyosin ring during telophase is crucial for orchestrating the final stages of cell division, ensuring the accurate and efficient partitioning of cellular components into the two daughter cells [1–3]. This contractile structure, which is composed of actin filaments and myosin motor proteins, forms around the equatorial region of the cell [1, 3, 43, 44]. Therefore, we investigated the impact of CHI3L1 on actomyosin ring formation during telophase in A549 lung cancer cells. The phosphorylation of myosin light chain at ser19 (p-MLC) activates the ATPase activity of myosin II, which promotes binding to and motility along actin filaments [45, 46]. We first examined the intensity of the actomyosin ring components p-MLC and F-actin in CHI3L1 overexpressing cells. The results showed an increase in the fluorescence intensity of p-MLC and F-actin in CHI3L1-overexpressing cells compared to the vector control-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4A). We also examined the recruitment of the actomyosin ring scaffolding protein anillin, which concentrates at the cleavage furrow during cytokinesis [17, 47]. The fluorescence intensity of anillin was slightly increased in CHI3L1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4A). These results suggest that CHI3L1 overexpression promotes the formation of the actomyosin ring. On the contrary, we investigated the impact of CHI3L1 depletion on actomyosin ring formation in A549 lung cancer cells. Compared to the control siRNA-treated cancer cells, the fluorescence signals of p-MLC and F-actin were weakened, indicating that CHI3L1 depletion decreases actomyosin ring formation (Fig. 4B). In addition, the fluorescence intensity of anillin was also decreased in CHI3L1-depleted cells (Fig. 4B). This suggests that CHI3L1 may play a role in regulating the stability of the actomyosin ring.

During cytokinesis, furrowing is mediated by the contraction of the actomyosin ring, and changes in the actomyosin ring strength or efficiency can potentially influence the depth of furrowing [1–3, 44]. We measured the impact of CHI3L1 on furrow depth and found that CHI3L1 overexpression significantly increased furrow depth in A549 cancer cells (Fig. 4C). Conversely, CHI3L1 depletion led to a substantial reduction in furrow depth compared to the control cells (Fig. 4D). The increased furrow depth observed in CHI3L1 overexpressing cells suggests that CHI3L1 may enhance the contractile activity of the actomyosin ring, leading to more pronounced furrow formation.

Next, we investigated the impact of CHI3L1 on one of the actomyosin ring components, anillin, in BEAS-2B normal cells. In contrast to cancer cells, both CHI3L1 overexpression and CHI3L1 depletion in normal cells did not show any significant effect on the intensity of anillin (Figure S4A and S4B). These data indicate that CHI3L1 in cancer cells appears to have a significant impact on actomyosin ring formation and furrowing depth, potentially contributing to cancer cell division. However, in normal cells, CHI3L1 exerts less influence on these processes.

CHI3L1 is involved in RhoA-mediated cytokinesis

RhoA is a small GTPase protein that plays a key role in regulating the formation and contraction of the actomyosin ring during cell division. During telophase, RhoA becomes activated and localizes to the equatorial region of the cell, where it triggers the assembly and contraction of the actomyosin ring [6, 13, 14]. This contraction leads to the furrowing of the cell membrane, ultimately resulting in the physical separation to two daughter cells. Therefore, we investigated whether CHI3L1 regulates the signaling of RhoA as an upstream regulator in cytokinesis progression. We utilized an online database to identify proteins interacting with CHI3L1 and RhoA. The analysis showed that CHI3L1 is singificantly associated with RhoA and Ect2 (Fig. 5A and B). Ect2 functions as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for RhoA to promote RhoA activation [6–8]. To examine whether CHI3L1 interacts with RhoA and Ect2, we performed immunoprecipitation analysis. The results presented in Fig. 5C showed that CHI3L1 indeed interacted with RhoA and Ect2 in A549 cancer cells (Fig. 5C). In addition, both normal cells and cancer cells exhibited interactions between CHI3L1 and RhoA, but cancer cells exhibited a more pronounced interaction compared to normal cells (Figure S5A). CHI3L1 was colocalized with RhoA and Ect2 at the midbody during telophase of cancer cells (Fig. 5D). These findings were also observed in normal cells, showing that CHI3L1, RhoA and Ect2 were co-localized at the midbody in telophase, but cancer cells exhibited a greater intensity of colocalization compared to normal cells (Figure S5B).

We further investigated whether CHI3L1 influenced recruitment of RhoA and Ect2 into the midbody during telophase cells. CHI3L1-overexpressing cancer cells showed a significant increase in the fluorescence intensity of RhoA and Ect2 signals at the midbody (Fig. 5E and F). Next, we aimed to confirm the effect of CHI3L1 on RhoA activity. RhoA activity is a key determinant in orchestrating the contractile machinery during cytokinesis and plays a crucial role in driving successful cell division. To assess changes in RhoA activity, we performed pull-down assay in control (expressing empty vector)- and CHI3L1-overexpressing cancer cells. RhoA activity was significantly increased in CHI3L1-overexpressing cancer cells; however, the overall cellular level of RhoA was not elevated in CHI3L1-overexpressing cancer cells (Fig. 5G). Conversely, we investigated the effect of CHI3L1 depletion on the recruitment of RhoA and Ect2 in cancer cells. RhoA and Ect2 signals were weaker at the midbody of CHI3L1-depleted cells compared to the control cells (Fig. 5H and I). In addition, RhoA activity was also reduced in CHI3L1-depleted cells (Fig. 5J).

We also investigated whether CHI3L1 overexpression affects the activity and recruitment of RhoA in normal cells. In contrast to cancer cells, CHI3L1 did not affect the localization of RhoA in normal cells (Figure S5C). Additionally, there were no significant differences observed in RhoA activity between control and CHI3L1-overexpressing normal cells (Figure S5D). Similarly, in normal cells, CHI3L1 depletion did not lead to significant alterations in the recruitment or activity of RhoA (Figure S5E and S5F). These findings suggest that CHI3L1 plays a specific role in regulating the recruitment of RhoA and Ect2 at the midbody during cytokinesis, leading to increased RhoA activity and potentially influencing successful cell division, particularly cancer cells.

CHI3L1 knock-out suppresses RhoA activity in in vivo

Based on our findings, CHI3L1 plays an essential role in the progression of cell division, and its abnormal expression can impact the development, progression, and prognosis of cancer. Therefore, we investigated the association between CHI3L1 expression and the survival rate of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients using the Kaplan-Meier plotter [48]. Our analysis revealed a significant correlation between high CHI3L1 expression levels and decreased overall survival rates in NSCLC patients (Fig. 6A). Protein mutations that occur in cancer patients can alter or impair the process of cell division and may be associated with abnormal cell cycle regulation in cancer cells, impacting tumor growth and progression [49–51]. Therefore, we investigated whether CHI3L1 exhibits mutations in lung cancer patients. Using cBioPortal and GenomeAD analysis, we examined the alteration frequency of CHI3L1 in a cohort of 8,083 lung cancer patients and 135 healthy humans [52, 53]. The alteration frequency of CHI3L1 was found to be significantly higher in the lung cancer patients (Fig. 6B), suggesting that CHI3L1 could potentially play a significant role in the development and progression of NSCLC. Several studies have reported the upregulation of RhoA and Ect2 in lung tumor tissues [54–57]. These proteins play crucial roles in regulating signal transduction associated with cell division control in cancer cells. Their elevated expression may be associated with tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis. We assessed the expression of CHI3L1, RhoA, and Ect2 in NSCLC patient tissues. The results showed increased expression of RhoA and Ect2 in lung tumor tissues from NSCLC patients (Fig. 6C). Several studies have reported that CHI3L1 depletion inhibits lung tumor growth and metastasis [21, 27, 28, 32, 36–38]. Therefore, we investigated whether the CHI3L1 expression affects RhoA activity in an in vivo model. The expression levels of RhoA and Ect2 were decreased in the tumor tissues of CHI3L1-knock out (KO) mice. Furthermore, RhoA activity was significantly reduced in the tumor tissues of CHI3L1-KO mice (Fig. 6D). Immunohistochemistry analysis showed that the expression of Ect2 and RhoA was significantly increased in tumor tissues of CHI3L1 KO-mice (Fig. 6E), consistent with the in vitro results. Similar results were obtained in metastatic lung tissues, where the expression of RhoA and Ect2 was also reduced in CHI3L1-KO metastatic lung tissues, along with a decrease in RhoA activity (Fig. 6F and G). These findings suggest that CHI3L1 plays a role in regulating RhoA and Ect2 expression and activity not only in primary lung cancer cells but also in metastatic lung tissues. However, in normal lung tissues, there were no significant differences in the expression of RhoA and Ect2 (Figure S6A). Additionally, RhoA activity also remained unchanged between the normal lung tissues of WT and CHI3L1-KO mice (Figure S6A). Overall, these findings suggest that CHI3L1 may specifically influence RhoA activity in tumor tissues, contributing to tumor progression and cell division control. Discussion

Cell division plays a fundamental role in the growth and development of organisms. However, when cell division becomes uncontrolled or dysregulated, it can lead to the formation of cancer cells. CHI3L1 is an inflammatory glycoprotein that is overexpressed in several types of cancer, including lung cancer [21, 23, 24]. Previous many studies have mainly demonstrated the role of CHI3L1 in tumor progression, metastasis, and the tumor microenvironment [21, 24, 25, 27–29, 37]. Some studies suggest that CHI3L1 may regulate signaling pathways related to cell cycle and affect the expression of cell cycle regulators [21, 24, 28, 29]. However, the exact roles of CHI3L1 in cell division remain uncertain. In the present study, we focused on the novel role of CHI3L1 in cell division. We showed that CHI3L1 overexpression resulted in notable alterations in cell cycle distribution, thereby promoting cancer cell division. We observed that the growth of lung cancer cells was gradually increased as the increased expression of CHI3L1. Time-lapse imaging analysis confirmed that CHI3L1 overexpression reduced the duration of cell division in cancer cells. Conversely, CHI3L1 depletion delayed cancer cell division. In addition, CHI3L1 depletion resulted in prolonged cell division duration and a reduced rate of successful cancer cell division. Furthermore, CHI3L1 depletion led to multinucleation and abnormal cell division in cancer cells. These changes in cell division associated with CHI3L1 expression were more pronounced in cancer cells than in normal cells. These data indicate that CHI3L1 could be significant for cancer cell division.

Cell division is composed of various processes, including DNA replication, chromosome alignment, and cytokinesis. Cell division have been known to regulate and coordinate these stages to maintain accuracy and balance, playing a role in ensuring the precision and equilibrium of cell division [1, 41, 50]. Cytokinesis, which represents the final stage of cell division, is very important. Because it prevents errors by ensuring accurate distribution of genetic material for proper division of cell [19, 20]. Moreover, it plays a fundamental role in the growth and development of multicellular organisms, restoration of tissues, and maintenance of appropriate cell sizes [41, 42]. Cytokinesis occurs between anaphase and telophase during cell division. During telophase, the central structure known as the midbody ensures the thorough separation of daughter cells, ultimately facilitating the formation of new individual cells [1, 3, 4]. Many papers report that proteins localized midbody play a crucial role in maintaining the accuracy and genetic stability of cytokinesis [41, 42]. In this study, we confirmed that CHI3L1 is concentrated at the midbody during telophase, as shown using fluorescence staining and fractionation. The midbody localization of CHI3L1 was observed in various cancer cell lines and normal cells. It is worth noting that the fluorescence intensity of CHI3L1 at the midbody was more pronounced in cancer cells compared to normal cells. This observation suggests a significant involvement of CHI3L1 in the control of cell division, especially during telophase. The midbody helps regulate the localization and activity of the actomyosin ring, ensuring proper constriction and furrow formation during cytokinesis [8, 41, 42, 47]. In addition, abnormal expression of myosin, actin-binding proteins, or regulators of actomyosin contractility can impact cytokinesis. Therefore, we investigated the impact of CHI3L1 on actomyosin ring formation in cancer cells. CHI3L1 overexpression promoted the contractile activity of the actomyosin ring, leading to more pronounced furrow formation in cancer cells. Conversely, CHI3L1 depletion reduced the contractile activity of the actomyosin ring and diminished furrow formation. In addition, these effects were specific to cancer cells and were not observed in normal cells. These data indicate that CHI3L1-mediated promotion of cytokinesis could be significant for CHI3L1-induced cell division, and is associated with localization of CHI3L1 in the midbody in cancer cells, leading to increased contractile activity of the actomyosin ring and enhanced furrow formation. However, the precise mechanism by which CHI3L1 affects cytokinesis is not yet clear.

Therefore, we investigated whether CHI3L1 interacts with specific proteins. Though the GENEMANIA and Humanbase databases, we identified an interaction network between CHI3L1 and cell division-related proteins, RhoA and Ect2. In addition, we confirmed that CHI3L1 physically interacts with RhoA and Ect2. Numerous studies have highlighted the coordinated role of RhoA and Ect2 in cytokinesis [6–8, 13, 14]. Ect2 activates RhoA, which, in turn, regulates actin and myosin dynamics to promote the formation, contraction, and ingression of the actomyosin ring. Precise spatial and temporal regulation of RhoA and Ect2 is critical for successful cytokinesis and proper separation of daughter cells. We here provide evidence indicating CHI3L1 may stabilize the midbody, principally by regulating RhoA activity. The specific interaction of these molecules and the master role of CHI3L1 were supported first of all by localization studies. Our results revealed that overexpression of CHI3L1 led to an increase in the activation of RhoA and simultaneously the upregulation of Ect2, which could have significant implications for cancer cell division. Conversely, CHI3L1 depletion reduced the recruitment of RhoA and Ect2 into the midbody and potentially decreased RhoA activity in cancer cells. Based on these findings, CHI3L1 may more likely to colocalize and interact with RhoA at the midbody, rather than simply moving RhoA to the midbody. Thus, CHI3L1 appears to function as a modulator of RhoA activity and interacts with RhoA at the midbody, ultimately reinforcing RhoA activation. These effects were not observed to the same extent in normal cells, indicating that the impact of CHI3L1 depletion on RhoA-mediated processes were specific to cancer cells. We also observed that the expression of RhoA, Ect2, and CHI3L1 were upregulated in NSCLC patient tumor tissues. Furthermore, the activation of RhoA and Ect2 were decreased in tumor tissues and the lung metastatic region of CHI3L1-KO mice. On the other hand, in the normal lung tissue of CHI3L1-KO mice, there were no significant changes in the activation of RhoA and Ect2 expression. Many studies have reported that the overexpression of RhoA and Ect2 increases cancer cell division and invasion, promoting tumor growth and metastasis [57–60]. The overexpression of RhoA and Ect2 affects the formation and contraction of the actomyosin ring, inducing structural and functional changes during cytokinesis [6–8, 13]. Moreover, RhoA and Ect2 are involved in cell motility and cell cycle regulation, enhancing the mobility and invasive capabilities of tumors [57, 59, 60]. The alterations and modifications triggered by the overexpression of RhoA and Ect2 facilitate tumor infiltration into surrounding tissues and dissemination to distant sites. Our present study suggests that CHI3L1 overexpression may promote cytokinesis in cancer cells for cell division by increasing the expression of RhoA and Ect2.

The dysregulation of cell division is closely associated with cancer development and progression. Cancer cells manipulate the actomyosin ring, essential for cytokinesis, to promote invasion and migration by altering actin dynamics and myosin activity, facilitating cytoskeletal remodeling and acquiring invasive properties [16, 61]. Abnormal cytokinesis can lead to abnormal proliferation of cancer cells and contribute to the formation of tumors [62]. In our study, CHI3L1 overexpression promoted cytokinesis in cancer cells, leading to faster and more growth. As a result of Kaplan-Meier plotter analysis, reducing the survival rate of NSCLC patients with high CHI3L1 expression may be related to this cytokinesis. Dysregulated cytokinesis can induce genomic instability in cancer cells, leading to abnormal distribution of chromosomes and causing additional mutations and genetic abnormalities in cancer cells [63]. This genetic instability can influence the development and progression of cancer, resulting in irregular cell proliferation and accelerated tumor growth [64–66]. Our genetic alternation analysis revealed an increase in CHI3L1 mutations in NSCLC patients compared to healthy individuals. These mutations can alter the function of CHI3L1, a critical regulator of cytokinesis. As a result of CHI3L1 mutations, cancer cells may enhance cytokinesis, leading to increased cell proliferation and promoting cancer growth and metastasis. However, in normal cells, the impact of CHI3L1 appears to be minimal, possibly due to well-regulated signaling pathways and a relatively stable cytokinesis.

These findings indicate that CHI3L1 could be significantly involved in the cell division process of cancer cells, highlighting the importance of CHI3L1 as a target protein for cancer therapy.
