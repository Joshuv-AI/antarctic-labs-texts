Feature-Space Planes Searcher: A Universal Domain Adaptation Framework for Interpretability and Computational Efficiency
Zhitong Cheng+, Yiran Jiang+, Yulong Ge, Yufeng Li, Zhongheng Qin, Rongzhi Lin, Jianwei Ma

Abstract
Domain shift, characterized by degraded model performance during transition from labeled source domains to unlabeled target domains, poses a persistent challenge for deploying deep learning systems. Current unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) methods predominantly rely on fine-tuning feature extractors—an approach limited by inefficiency, reduced interpretability, and poor scalability to modern architectures. Our analysis reveals that models pretrained on large-scale data exhibit domain-invariant geometric patterns in their feature space, characterized by intra-class clustering and inter-class separation, thereby preserving transferable discriminative structures. These findings indicate that domain shifts primarily manifest as boundary misalignment rather than feature degradation. Unlike fine-tuning entire pre-trained models—which risks introducing unpredictable feature distortions—we propose the Feature-space Planes Searcher (FPS): a novel domain adaptation framework that optimizes decision boundaries by leveraging these geometric patterns while keeping the feature encoder frozen. This streamlined approach enables interpretative analysis of adaptation while substantially reducing memory and computational costs through offline feature extraction, permitting full-dataset optimization in a single computation cycle. Evaluations on public benchmarks demonstrate that FPS achieves competitive or superior performance to state-of-the-art methods. FPS scales efficiently with multimodal large models and shows versatility across diverse domains including protein structure prediction, remote sensing classification, and earthquake detection. We anticipate FPS will provide a simple, effective, and generalizable paradigm for transfer learning, particularly in domain adaptation tasks.

FPS optimizes decision planes by maximizing sample discriminability while minimizing class imbalance in the target domain. Within the fixed feature space, this task simplifies to extending decision boundaries from source-domain to target-domain regions, guided by prior knowledge. This framework enables rigorous examination of prior assumptions and their impact on transfer processes. By pre-extracting features, memory and computational costs are reduced, facilitating single-step optimization across the full dataset distribution.

Contributions

Our main contributions are:

1.

Our analysis reveals that cross-domain transfer challenges originate not from feature degradation, but from decision boundary misalignment. Capitalizing on the inherent intra-class clustering and inter-class separation of features extracted by pre-trained models, domain adaptation is achieved through exclusive fine-tuning of the final decision planes layer.

2.

We introduce FPS, a domain adaptation method that freezes the feature extractor while optimizing decision planes using structure-informed prior distributions. Critically, FPS attains effective domain adaptation without pseudo-labels or feature alignment, ensuring efficient refinement of decision planes.

3.

FPS surpasses or matches state-of-the-art unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) methods across diverse benchmarks, including protein structure prediction, remote sensing, and earthquake detection. The approach demonstrates efficiency, robustness, and compatibility with multimodal encoders.

Decision-Plane Refinement via Entropy and Geometry

A key UDA approach involves refining the decision planes of the classifier to reduce errors caused by domain bias in the target data. One fundamental idea is entropy minimization on unlabeled target samples, encouraging the decision surface to align with low-density regions
[
31
]
. MME
[

[
33

introduces a non-adversarial loss function that penalizes ambiguous predictions spanning multiple categories. MCC explicitly mitigates pairwise category confounding, promoting higher efficiency in domain adaptation. FPS also utilizes entropy minimization to position decision planes in low-density sample regions. However, we observed that entropy minimization alone can bias the decision boundary toward extreme cases, where samples cluster within a limited number of classes. To address this, we introduce balancing terms, such as class distribution equilibrium, to mitigate bias. Meanwhile, we employ statistical partitioning to justify the rationality of entropy optimization.

III
MOTIVATION AND PRELIMINARY ANALYSIS

Pretrained models such as ResNet
[
41
]
and Vision Transformers (ViT)
[
42
]
, trained on large-scale datasets, exhibit strong efficacy in improving domain adaptation, particularly under source-domain sample scarcity. These models typically serve as encoders, mapping samples (e.g., images) to high-dimensional feature representations, with each sample represented as a point in the embedding space. A set of decision boundaries are then leveraged in this feature space to classify different sample categories.

Conventional approaches often fine-tune the encoder to achieve a refined feature space, where source and target domain distributions align more closely. This relies on two core assumptions: (1) Performance degradation of source-trained models on target data stems from the encoder’s inability to extract target-domain features effectively, causing distributional divergence; (2) Under specific assumptions, a superior encoder can be derived. First, significant source-target domain discrepancies imply that samples exhibit distinct features beyond categorical similarities. When pretrained models extract diverse features effectively, source and target samples inherently follow distinct distributions. Forcing alignment may consequently impair the encoder’s feature extraction. Furthermore, fine-tuning high-parameter encoders with limited target samples raises reliability concerns. Post-fine-tuning, feature-space alterations in such encoders are poorly characterized.

If the original encoder yields sufficiently robust features, and a decision boundary maintains high source-target accuracy, freezing the encoder while refining this boundary is viable. To investigate this, we systematically analyzed feature distributions via t-SNE, quantified intra/inter-class distances, and conducted theoretical accuracy evaluations.

The t-SNE visualizations reveal consistent clustering patterns: Within a single domain, distinct classes form well-separated clusters (Fig.
1
). Cross-domain analysis shows preserved separation despite distribution shifts, with minimal inter-class overlap across domains (Fig.
1
). Distance metrics reinforce this observation: intra-class samples maintain closer proximity than inter-class samples, regardless of domain variations (Fig.
2
). This phenomenon is termed the intra-class clustering and inter-class separation pattern.

As shown in Fig.
1
with a black dashed line, the performance decline in the target domain mainly arises from boundary misalignment induced by domain shift. Therefore, cross-domain accuracy can be significantly enhanced by fine-tuning only the decision planes (as illustrated by the solid green line and green arrow in Fig.
1
).

To validate this hypothesis, we froze the encoder parameters and trained domain-agnostic linear classifiers using both source and target domain labels. As shown by Fig.
3
, the resulting classifiers consistently outperformed state-of-the-art domain adaptation methods across most tasks (Table
II
). The empirical success of these fixed-feature classifiers substantiates our theoretical proposition: inheritable decision boundaries exist in the original feature space, enabling effective domain adaptation through boundary finetuning rather than feature refinement. Moreover, the prevalent geometric pattern of intra-class clustering and inter-class separation often positions the decision planes in regions with lower sample density. Leveraging this characteristic may facilitate fine-tuning of the feature space.

IV
METHODOLOGY

In unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA), the labeled feature vectors in the source domain are denoted as
\mathbf{D}_{s}=\left\{(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{s}}^{i},y_{\mathrm{s}}^{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{s}}}
, where
N_{\mathrm{s}}
is the number of source domain samples. The unlabeled target domain samples are represented by
\mathbf{D}_{t}=\left\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{t}}^{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{t}}}
, where
N_{\mathrm{t}}
is the number of target domain samples. These feature vectors are assumed to be independently drawn from
p(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{s}},y)
and
p(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{t}})
, corresponding to independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples. Traditional UDA aims to learn an effective target-domain classifier by aligning feature representations between
\mathbf{D}_{t}
and
\mathbf{D}_{s}
through feature extractor optimization. In contrast, our approach minimizes modifications to the feature extractor, instead optimizing decision boundaries without requiring feature extractor fine-tuning.

We use a linear classification layer to model the decision boundaries. The layer is defined as:

\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{b},

(1)

where
\mathbf{W}
is the weight matrix,
\mathbf{b}
is the bias vector, and
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is the feature vector of the
i
-th sample. The class probability vector
\mathbf{p}_{i}
is computed via the softmax function:
\mathbf{p}_{i}=\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{y}_{i})
. The full set of model parameters is denoted as
\mathbf{\Theta}=\{\mathbf{W},\mathbf{b}\}
.

We first derive a mathematical formulation for fine-tuning decision planes, leveraging the prior distribution of latent variables in the target domain data. Next, we introduce latent variables derived from feature distribution patterns and their associated prior distributions. We then detail a framework for incorporating these latent variables into the decision plane optimization algorithm as well as necessary details.

IV-A

Latent Priors

Fig. 4
:
A Bayesian Rule-Based Cross-Domain Learning Pipeline. The diagram illustrates a three-stage machine learning workflow for domain adaptation: (1) Initial State contains model parameters (

) and unlabelled target domain data

alongside labelled source domain data
\{x_{\text{s}}^{i},y_{\text{s}}^{i}\}_{i}
; (2) Intermediate State reflects parameter updates via Bayesian inference, where source domain knowledge is propagated to adapt
\Theta
for the target domain; (3) Final Prediction generates target domain predictions
\{z_{\text{t}}^{i}\}_{i}
after full optimization. This process formalizes knowledge transfer between domains through probabilistic parameter refinement. In our study,
\Theta
solely represents the parameters of the final linear classification layer.

To advance our analysis, we reformulate unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) as an optimization problem for the decision surface. Specifically, UDA can be addressed by optimizing the decision planes. Given a joint distribution
p(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{D}_{s})
of source and target domain features and a prior
p(\mathbf{\Theta})
(e.g., uniform), the decision planes can be determined via maximum likelihood estimation:

\arg\max_{\mathbf{\Theta}}\log p(\mathbf{\Theta}\mid\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{D}_{s}).

(2)

This expression can be further simplified using Bayes’ theorem as follows:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{\Theta}\mid\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{D}_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{D}_{s}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})p(\mathbf{\Theta})}{p(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{D}_{s})}

\displaystyle\propto p(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{D}_{s}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{p(\mathbf{D}_{t}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})}_{\text{unsupervised term}}\cdot\underbrace{p(\mathbf{D}_{s}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})}_{\text{supervised term}},

(3)

where Eq. (
3
) assumes conditional independence between domains.

The optimization objective in Eq. (
9
) can thus be reformulated as:

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\mathbf{\Theta}}\log p(\mathbf{\Theta}\mid\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{D}_{s})

\displaystyle\propto\arg\max_{\mathbf{\Theta}}\left(\log p(\mathbf{D}_{s}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})+\log p(\mathbf{D}_{t}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})\right).

(4)

Since the source domain provides accurate labels, the supervised signal enables the maximization of
\log p(\mathbf{D}_{s}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})
via the standard cross-entropy loss. The primary challenge, therefore, lies in maximizing the unsupervised term
\log p(\mathbf{D}_{t}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})
. Without labels, directly estimating
\log p(\mathbf{D}_{t}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})
for the target domain is intractable. However, if latent variables
\mathbf{Z}(=\{\mathbf{z}_{\text{t}}^{i}\}_{i})
—which determine the prior distribution and are jointly influenced by
\mathbf{D}_{t}
and
\mathbf{\Theta}
—can be identified, they may serve as a means to optimize
\mathbf{\Theta}
.

When the optimized
\mathbf{\Theta}
is unknown, the latent variable
\mathbf{Z}
corresponding to
\mathbf{D}_{t}
is also unknown. Thus, we must account for all possible values of
\mathbf{Z}
, transforming the optimization objective
p(\mathbf{D}_{t}\mid\mathbf{\Theta})
into:

\displaystyle\int p(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{Z}|\mathbf{\Theta})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\mathbf{Z}}

\displaystyle=\int p(\mathbf{D}_{t}|\mathbf{\Theta},\mathbf{Z})p(\mathbf{Z}|\mathbf{\Theta})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\mathbf{Z}}.

(5)

Assuming
\mathbf{Z}
has a deterministic relationship with
\mathbf{\Theta}
and
\mathbf{D}_{t}
, defined by
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{\Theta})
, we have:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{D}_{t}|\mathbf{\Theta},\mathbf{Z})=\mathbf{\delta}\left(\mathbf{Z}-\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{\Theta})\right)p(\mathbf{D}_{t}).

(6)

The optimization objective can then be expressed as:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{D}_{t})p\left(\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{\Theta})|\mathbf{\Theta}\right)\propto p\left(\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{\Theta})|\mathbf{\Theta}\right).

(7)

Although
p\left(\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{\Theta})|\mathbf{\Theta}\right)
remains intractable, we approximate it using the prior distribution of
\mathbf{Z}
:

\displaystyle p\left(\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{\Theta})|\mathbf{\Theta}\right)\approx p_{\mathrm{prior}}\left(\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{\Theta})\right).

(8)

The overall optimization objective is:

\arg\max_{\mathbf{\Theta}}\log p(\mathbf{\Theta}\mid\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{D}_{s})+\log p_{\mathrm{prior}}\left(\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{t},\mathbf{\Theta})\right).

(9)

Thus, for the target domain data, our objective is to identify latent variables with estimable prior distributions, enabling fine-tuning of parameters derived from supervised learning in the source domain. An overview of our modeling flow is illustrated in Fig.
4
.

IV-A
1
Sample (Shannon) Entropy

To leverage the observed pattern of intra-class clustering and inter-class separation in the data, we first introduce Sample (Shannon) Entropy (SE) as a latent variable. For a given sample with predicted probabilities
\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{t}}^{i})
across all categories, SE is computed as:

\mathrm{SE}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\text{t}}^{i}\right)=-\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{t}}^{i})^{\mathrm{T}}\log\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{t}}^{i}).

SE has been widely adopted in domain adaptation tasks, though with varying motivations. Some approaches use the predicted probability distribution under current model parameters to generate pseudo-labels for self-training
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
, thereby encouraging higher confidence in assigned categories. Others minimize SE to position decision planes in low-density regions
[
31
]
.

Although we fine-tune only the final classifier, SE can also guide decision plane placement in low-density regions of the frozen feature space. Moreover, the fixed feature space allows for deterministic analysis of SE changes. As shown in Figure
5
, the target domain’s SE distribution shifted significantly after supervision: under random initialization, SE clustered around 2.5, while supervision with source-domain data reduced overall SE values. Further reductions occurred when using target-domain data or a combination of source and target data.

If the SE distribution under target-domain supervision were known a priori, it could optimize decision plane placement. However, obtaining this distribution is challenging in unsupervised settings. Nevertheless, we observe that SE distributions—whether randomly initialized or supervised by the source domain—are larger than those under target-domain supervision. Thus, SE reduction can guide decision plane optimization. Accordingly, we propose a sample entropy loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}}=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{\text{t}}^{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{t}}}\left[\mathrm{SE}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{t}}^{i})\right].

(10)

Fig. 5
:
Comparison of sample entropy (SE) distributions in the target domain of the VisDA-2017 dataset under different supervision. The feature vectors used in these experiments were extracted using ViT-B/16, a model pre-trained on ImageNet-1K. The results were visualized using kernel density estimation (KDE), wherein the sample entropy distribution was smoothed with a Gaussian kernel. The resulting probability density function was normalized such that the total area under the curve equals one.

Optimizing solely for SE reduction may lead to an undesirable solution in which all samples are predicted as one or a few extreme classes (APPENDIX Fig.S1). To prevent the decision boundaries from converging toward such a solution, we introduce the category (Shannon) entropy, CE:

\mathrm{CE}\left(\mathbf{D}_{t}\right)=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathrm{t}}\in\mathbf{D}_{t}}\left[\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})\right]^{\mathrm{T}}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathrm{t}}\in\mathbf{D}_{t}}\left[\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})\right].

We compute the mean class probabilities and then derive the Shannon entropy from these values. The corresponding category entropy loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
, is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}=-\mathrm{CE}(\mathbf{D}_{t}).

(11)

This loss maximizes the category entropy (CE), encouraging a uniform probability distribution across all classes and preventing overconcentration in a single or few categories. Combining
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
, the total entropy-related loss is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}}+(\alpha-1)\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}},

(12)

where
\alpha
is balance weight for two loss. This constitutes the core loss function of our method.

IV-A
2
Random Pooling Constraint

Beyond the entropy-related loss, we identify a latent variable linked to the pooling mechanism. In classification tasks, global pooling or a CLS (classification) token typically aggregates semantic representations of the input. Both methods effectively compute weighted averages of patch-wise features. A natural extension is to perturb these weights while preserving classification performance. We propose a random pooling layer, where weights (
\omega_{p}
) are stochastically assigned to each patch. The final feature is:

\mathbf{x}=\frac{\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}{\omega_{p}\mathbf{x}_{p}}}{\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}{\omega_{p}}},

(13)

where
\mathbf{x}_{p}
denotes the
p
-th patch, and
N_{p}
is the total number of patches. For ResNet backbones,
\omega_{p}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
; for ViT backbones,
\omega_{p}=\eta_{p}^{2}
, where
\eta_{p}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
.

Two random pooling operations on
\mathbf{x}_{p}
produce features
\mathbf{x}_{\text{t}}^{i}
and
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{t}}^{i}
, leading to predictions
\mathbf{y}_{\text{t}}^{i}
and
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{t}}^{i}
. The prediction discrepancy (CR) between perturbations is:

\displaystyle\text{CR}(\mathbf{y}_{\text{t}}^{i},\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{t}}^{i})=\|{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{t}}^{i}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{t}}^{i}\|_{2}.

(14)

To validate that perturbations minimally affect classification, we analyze the CR distribution across supervision regimes (Fig.
6
). When the supervised data approximates the target domain, the CR distribution converges to zero. This implies that robust decision boundaries reduce sensitivity to pooling variations, ensuring consistent classification for independently pooled features
[
46
]
.

Given these distributional differences, CR serves as a latent variable for optimizing decision boundaries. To circumvent estimating CR’s prior distribution, we define a loss function to minimize CR:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CR}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathbf{X}_{\text{t}}}\left[\|{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{t}}^{i}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{t}}^{i}\|_{2}\right]}{C},

(15)

where
C
is the number of classes. Unlike the entropy loss, which leverages feature-space distributions, CR acts like a data augmentation regularizer, refining decision boundaries with finer granularity. The name ”CR” is derived from the abbreviation of the Consistency Regularization term.

distributions in the target domain from the VisDA-2017 dataset under different supervision. Other settings remain consistent with those described in Fig.

.

\mathrm{CR}
is normalized by
(\|{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{i}\|_{2}+\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{i}\|_{2})/2

IV-B

Additional Detail

The two latent variables, SE and CR, introduced earlier form the core of our decision surface fine-tuning. Building upon this foundation, we will now introduce additional details that collectively constitute the entire algorithm.

IV-B
1
Tolerance for Decision Plane Shift

For tasks with severely misaligned decision boundaries, we allow a certain degree of divergence between the decision planes of the source and target domains. The linear layer in the target domain is adjusted as:

\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{W}})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}+\Delta_{\mathbf{b}}.

(16)

Here,
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{W}}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{b}}
represent the perturbations of weights and biases, respectively. The regularization constraint on perturbation magnitude is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}=\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{W}}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{b}}\|_{2}.

(17)

IV-B
2
Dynamically Weighted Loss Function

Currently, we have the supervised cross-entropy loss (SCE)
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SCE}}
for labeled source domain data, along with
\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}(=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}}+(\alpha-1)\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}})
,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CR}}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}
for unlabeled target domain data, all employed throughout the training process.
Through experimentation, we observed that prioritizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SCE}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}
initially, followed by gradually increasing the weights of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SE}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CR}}
, yields optimal results in most tasks. Specifically, focusing primarily on the categorical entropy loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}
) at the outset, followed by progressively increasing the weight of the sample entropy loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SE}}
), is advantageous. Therefore, we have designed a dynamically weighted loss function defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{SCE}}+(1-\beta_{t})\left[\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}\right.

(18)

\displaystyle\left.+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CR}}+\gamma_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}\right],

where,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}=\alpha_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}}+(\alpha_{t}-1)\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
and the hyperparameters

,
\beta_{t}
,

, and

(20)

,

Besides, to address the issue of class imbalance on overall performance, we assign weights to each sample. The core principle of this weighting method is to estimate sample distribution based on feature similarity—samples that are more similar to others receive lower weights. The weight for the
i
-th sample is determined as:

w_{i}=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{\mathrm{e}^{A\mathrm{CC}_{ij}}}}

(23)

where
\mathrm{CC}_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{x}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}}
represents the cross-correlation coefficient between the
i
-th sample (
\mathbf{x}_{i}
) and the
j
-th sample (
\mathbf{x}_{j}
), and
A
is a hyperparameter (typically set to 5) that adjusts sample weights. To ensure an average sample weight of 1, the following normalization is applied:

\tilde{w}_{i}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{t}}}w_{j}}w_{i}.

(24)

These weights are utilized in calculating
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CR}}
for the target domain.

IV-B
3
Intra-Class Distance Metric

Similar to methods such as k-means, if samples from the same category exhibit clustering tendencies, the optimal decision planes should minimize intra-category sample distances. If this assumption holds, it allows us to assess decision plane optimization performance and select optimal hyperparameter configurations without relying on labeled target-domain data.

Building on these insights, we propose a non-parametric metric, the Intra-Class Distance Metric (ICDM). The centroid of class
c
in the target domain is computed as:

\mathbf{\mu}_{c}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{c}}[\mathbf{x}^{i}],\quad\text{where }\mathcal{D}_{c}=\{\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbf{D}_{\text{t}}\mid\hat{y}_{i}=c\}.

(25)

Here,
\hat{y}_{i}
denotes the model’s predicted pseudo-labels. Intra-class similarity is measured as the squared Euclidean distance between a sample and its corresponding centroid:

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{\text{intra}}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{\text{t}}}[||\mathbf{x}^{i}-\mathbf{\mu}_{\hat{y}_{i}}||^{2}]}.

(26)

To provide a relative scale,
\hat{D}_{\text{intra}}
is normalized by the corresponding metric
D_{\text{intra}}
computed from true labels:

R=\frac{\hat{D}_{\text{intra}}}{D_{\text{intra}}}.

(27)

As shown in Figure
7
, R varies across tasks depending on supervision levels. Note that
D_{\text{intra}}
is solely used to normalize
\hat{D}_{\text{intra}}
across tasks, facilitating comparison, but is not required for parameter selection. Notably, when incorporating the source domain data,
R
significantly decreases compared to random class partitioning; furthermore,
R
declines even more after introducing the target domain data. This indicates that as the decision planes become more aligned with the target domain data,
R
generally decreases. In the

Feature Preprocessing

We precompute and store all feature vectors of the dataset, thereby fully leveraging the statistical information of the target domain. To maintain consistency in search strategies across different backbones, the features undergo standardization (zero-mean, with the standard deviation normalized to 2.5):

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathbf{x}-\mu}{\sigma}\cdot s

(28)

where

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathrm{t}}\in\mathbf{D}_{s}\cup\mathbf{D}_{t}}\left[\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}\right],

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{i,\mathrm{t}}\in\mathbf{D}_{s}\cup\mathbf{D}_{t}}\left[(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}-\mu)^{2}\right]}.

and
s
is set to the target standard deviation of 2.5. Note that for ResNet models, the final feature output (after ReLU and pooling layers, as shown in APPENDIX Fig.S2(a)) deviates more from a normal distribution compared to ViT-based models (APPENDIX Fig.S2(c)). Given the non-negativity of features, we take the square root of the original features before distribution transformation to make them more closely resemble a normal distribution (APPENDIX Fig.S2(c)).

VI
DISCUSSION and CONCLIUSION

In this work, we introduce the Feature-space Planes Searcher (FPS), a domain adaptation framework that optimizes decision planes within frozen pre-trained feature spaces using structural priors. Our theoretical analysis demonstrates that cross-domain performance degradation stems predominantly from discriminative boundary misalignment, not feature representation degradation. To mitigate this, we propose unsupervised optimization objectives—target conditional entropy minimization, category entropy maximization, and stochastic pooling-based consistency regularization—to enable precise boundary estimation. FPS challenges conventional domain adaptation approaches that rely on feature alignment or confusion, effectively reducing the problem to fine-tuning the final decision boundary. This approach not only highlights the inherent effectiveness of pre-trained model features but also facilitates quantitative analysis of domain shift constraints.

Extensive experiments across diverse benchmarks—including computer vision, protein structure prediction, remote sensing classification, and earthquake detection—show that FPS achieves superior or competitive performance over state-of-the-art unsupervised domain adaptation methods, while enhancing efficiency and interpretability. Notably, FPS remains compatible with multimodal foundation models without requiring source domain raw data, demonstrating strong scalability.

Relation to SFUDA
Source-Free Domain Adaptation (SFDA) aims to adapt pre-trained models to new, unlabeled scenarios without requiring access to the original sensitive data, thereby addressing critical privacy preservation challenges in domains such as healthcare. This area has garnered increasing attention from researchers in recent years. FPS operates under a similar problem setting, as it requires only access to the high-dimensional feature vectors associated with the source data, avoiding any direct dependence on the source domain and thus preserving data privacy. Therefore, we argue that FPS aligns with the core objectives of SFDA. However, most existing SFDA methods
[
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
]
achieve domain adaptation by catalyzing the process through techniques like pseudo-label refinement, prototyping, contrastive alignment, or uncertainty weighting. These methods typically focus on improving pseudo-label quality by iteratively updating classifiers or features based on the relationship between target samples. In contrast, FPS takes a fundamentally different approach by eschewing pseudo-labeling and auxiliary memory constructs. Instead, it directly optimizes decision planes in closed form through entropy-based regularization and geometric consistency, implemented via stochastic pooling. This makes FPS an interpretable and privacy-conscious solution for domain adaptation.
