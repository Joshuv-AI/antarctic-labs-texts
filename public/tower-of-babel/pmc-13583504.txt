Intraoperative high-resolution CT reveals electrode misplacement undetected by skull X-ray following cochlear implantation for IP-III inner ear malformation
Authors: Zhen Zhao, Jia Guo, Tianci Feng, Yan Huang, Wei Liu, Zhigang Zhang, Yu Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIncomplete partition type III (IP-III) malformation presents unique challenges for cochlear implantation. We report a critical intraoperative imaging comparison in a paediatric IP-III case where traditional skull X-ray failed to reveal the precise location of the cochlear electrode, while high-resolution CT (HRCT) precisely identified the site of electrode displacement. This finding demonstrates a significant limitation of routine radiography in these complex anatomies. Our experience underscores HRCT’s critical role in accurately verifying electrode placement for patients with IP-III. Recognising this imaging discrepancy is essential for preventing undiagnosed malposition, guiding surgical revision and optimising auditory outcomes. Our findings support considering intraoperative HRCT as a valuable adjunct for confirming electrode position in IP-III implantation. Further multicentre studies are warranted to establish evidence-based imaging protocols for this challenging patient group.Keywords: Radiology; Otolaryngology / ENT; Paediatric Surgery; Surgical Procedure, Operative

Background

Cochlear implantation is the standard treatment for children with severe to profound sensorineural hearing loss, but outcomes are often complicated by inner ear malformations. Incomplete partition type III (IP-III), linked to POU3F4 mutations, is one of the rarest and most challenging anomalies. The absence of the cochlear modiolus and abnormal communication with the internal auditory canal create a high risk of electrode misplacement, cerebrospinal fluid leak or gusher and surgical difficulties. Conventional intraoperative radiographs, including plain skull X-ray, may not reliably confirm electrode position in such cases. Failure to recognise misplacement can compromise hearing rehabilitation, cause serious neurologic sequelae and may require revision surgery. This report highlights the value of high-resolution CT (HRCT) in overcoming these limitations and ensuring safe implantation in patients with IP-III.

Case presentation

A boy in late childhood with congenital, bilateral, profound sensorineural hearing loss was referred for cochlear implantation. Preoperative evaluation with MRI of the internal auditory canal and CT of the temporal bone confirmed an IP-III malformation, showing absence of the modiolus with thin, incomplete interscalar septa.

Cochlear implantation was performed on the left side using a commercially available cochlear implant system (Mi1200, MED-EL, Innsbruck, Austria). Intraoperative skull X-ray showed a coiled electrode configuration without obvious malposition (figure 1A). In contrast, HRCT demonstrated that the electrode had entered the basal turn through the outer cochlear wall, with its tip extending into the internal auditory canal (figure 1B–D). Given the known high risk of electrode misplacement associated with IP-III malformations, intraoperative HRCT was performed as part of our institutional protocol for all IP-III cochlear implantation cases, irrespective of intraoperative electrophysiological results or perceived insertion resistance. While cone-beam CT (CBCT) is typically favoured in paediatric populations to minimise radiation exposure, intraoperative HRCT was employed in this case due to the lack of intraoperative CBCT equipment at our institution. Despite this equipment limitation, the radiation dose was carefully managed according to standard paediatric protocols, with a Volume CT Dose Index of 26.8 mGy and a dose-length product of 134.0 mGy cm. With parental consent, revision surgery was carried out immediately. During the revision, the lateral wall of the basal turn was extensively drilled to achieve direct microscopic visualisation. The electrode array was then carefully re-inserted and rotated toward the expected medial cochlear axis. Finally, a fascia graft was used to seal the basal turn defect and prevent cerebrospinal fluid leakage. Intraoperative testing with electrically evoked auditory brainstem responses and neural response telemetry confirmed satisfactory implant function.

Treatment

With parental consent, the child underwent immediate revision surgery, during which the electrode was re-inserted.

Outcome and follow-up

The postoperative course was smooth. Due to the significant geographic distance from our hospital, the patient underwent the initial cochlear implant activation at a local facility rather than our institution. Through telephone follow-up, we learnt that the child had no complications and has achieved a good improvement in hearing. One month after surgery, the implant was activated, with several apical electrodes switched off. The child had no complications such as hemifacial spasm or facial weakness and was able to return to normal daily activities, including school and play. Follow-up at 3 and 6 months showed stable electrode function, good wound healing and improving hearing responses. He remains under regular audiology review and continues to do well.

Discussion

The IP-III malformed cochlea, first systematically characterised by Sennaroglu et al,1 represents a distinct subclass of cochlear malformations attributable to mutations in the POU3F4 gene. This genetic defect results in the complete absence of the cochlear modiolus while preserving the interscalar septa, and the condition is associated with X-linked deafness. HRCT plays a pivotal role in delineating these anatomical anomalies by revealing a thin cochlear membrane that completely envelops the cochlea, in contrast to the thick bony internal layer outlining the membranous labyrinth. Given these unique structural features, cochlear implantation has emerged as a highly beneficial intervention for auditory rehabilitation in patients with IP-III malformations, as documented in previous studies.2 3 Furthermore, the detailed visualisation provided by HRCT not only aids in diagnosis but also assists in preoperative planning, ultimately contributing to improved surgical outcomes.

The complex anatomy of the IP-III cochlea predisposes patients to a relatively high incidence of electrode misplacement, particularly with electrodes inadvertently entering the internal auditory canal. In a review by Cheung et al, 28 cases of electrode misplacement into the internal auditory canal were reported, with 13 cases (46%) occurring in patients with IP-III type. Notably, the majority of these malpositions were detected only during the postoperative period, thereby highlighting the limitations of intraoperative assessments alone.4 Accordingly, routine intraoperative or postoperative skull X-ray examinations have been recommended to verify the precise positioning of the electrode array. Complementing these findings, the study by Dirr et al showed that restricting skull X-ray examinations to patients exhibiting increased resistance during electrode insertion could result in nearly half of the misplacement cases being overlooked, underscoring the importance of routine imaging irrespective of initial anatomical impressions.5

In our reported case, the patient underwent systematic skull X-ray examinations intraoperatively. These examinations initially confirmed optimal positioning of the cochlear electrodes, with no robust evidence of kinking or misplacement into the internal auditory canal, and intraoperative electrophysiological assessments yielded normal results. The initial intraoperative skull X-rays demonstrated a coiled configuration of the cochlear implant electrodes, resembling the radiographic appearance of successful electrode placement. This phenomenon may be attributed to dilatation of the internal auditory canal and its direct communication with the cochlear base in IP-III malformations.

However, subsequent intraoperative HRCT imaging identified instances where the electrodes had erroneously entered the internal auditory canal, suggesting that HRCT may provide superior sensitivity in detecting electrode malposition compared with conventional skull X-ray evaluations. Historically, skull X-ray imaging was the predominant method for intraoperative or postoperative assessment, as highlighted in the review by Vogl et al. Yet, with advancements in imaging technology, HRCT has emerged as a valuable tool, offering both precise evaluation of electrode contact positions and comprehensive morphological assessment of the cochlea.6 HRCT is now considered the preferred modality for detecting severe malposition, including those where electrodes encroach on the superior semicircular canal, horizontal semicircular canal, vestibule, Eustachian tube, internal auditory canal or even the internal carotid artery. In cases of particularly challenging temporal bone anatomy, intraoperative HRCT—as proposed by Kim et al—has proven invaluable. This real-time imaging modality allows for the accurate assessment of electrode positioning and immediate correction, if necessary, thereby ensuring both surgical safety and the accuracy of electrode implantation.7

Accepting the final electrode position in this case, with the distal tip remaining within the internal auditory canal, illustrates a recurring dilemma in the surgical management of IP-III malformations. The primary objective of revision surgery is not necessarily to achieve a completely intracochlear electrode array at all costs, but rather to obtain the safest position that provides effective neural stimulation while minimising surgical trauma.8 In IP-III, the absence of the modiolus and the abnormal communication between the cochlea and the internal auditory canal mean that limited intracanalicular extension of the distal tip may still permit satisfactory auditory outcomes, provided the majority of functional contacts remain within the malformed cochlea.9 Intraoperatively, this decision must rely on imaging, insertion trajectory and the anticipated risks of further manipulation. Repeated attempts to completely withdraw and reposition the electrode from the internal auditory canal increase the risks of severe cochlear trauma, cerebrospinal fluid leakage or facial nerve injury, particularly in this highly abnormal anatomy. An anatomically imperfect but functionally adequate position is therefore often the more prudent choice. Subsequent individualised programming, including the selective deactivation of poorly functioning or non-auditory stimulating apical electrodes, is relatively common and effective in these complex cases, providing a reliable alternative to further surgical intervention.8 10

Collectively, these findings underscore the need for a multimodal imaging strategy in cochlear implantation, particularly in patients with complex inner ear malformations. The integration of HRCT into both intraoperative and postoperative protocols not only enhances the detection of subtle electrode misplacements but also provides critical anatomical insights that can inform surgical decision-making. Since intraoperative CT is not yet universally available, an early (within 24 hours) postoperative CT or CBCT may serve as a reasonable alternative for confirming electrode position, although it cannot prevent an unnecessary return to the theatre. For patients with high-risk anatomy such as IP-III, considering referral or collaboration with a centre equipped with intraoperative imaging is highly recommended if logistics permit. A limitation of this report is the lack of objective postoperative electrophysiological data, as the patient’s activation and rehabilitation were performed at a remote facility without a shared data archiving system. Nonetheless, the favourable clinical course lends support to the functional adequacy of the accepted electrode position.

Patient’s perspective

We came to understand that our child had a malformed cochlea and that surgery could be difficult. When the first attempt was not successful, we agreed to immediate revision so the electrode could be reinserted. Although HRCT involves more radiation than an X-ray, we accepted this for a better chance of proper implantation and hearing recovery. We are deeply grateful to the medical team for their support and guidance during this difficult time.
