Protocol for studying macrophage lipid crosstalk with murine tumor cells
Authors: Daan J Kloosterman, Martina Farber, Menno Boon, Johanna Erbani, Leila Akkari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryLipid accumulation has recently emerged as a key feature underlying the pro-tumorigenic role of macrophages. Here, we present a workflow to study macrophage lipid crosstalk with tumor cells. We describe steps for the identification, purification, and multi-omics characterization of lipid-laden macrophages (LLMs) from murine tumors and outline protocols to assess the functional significance of LLMs in cancer malignancy. This approach has the potential to uncover the source of lipids that drives LLM formation and its pro-tumorigenic potential in multiple cancer types.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Kloosterman, Erbani, et al.1Subject areas: bioinformatics, sequence analysis, cell biology, single cell, flow cytometry, cancer, genomics, RNA-seq, immunology, microbiology

Before you begin

Lipid metabolism underlies the pro-tumorigenic phenotype of tumor-associated macrophages in multiple cancers.2,3,4 The protocol below was designed to identify and purify lipid-laden macrophages for multi-omics characterization in a brain tumor, glioblastoma mouse model, as described previously by Kloosterman, Erbani and colleagues.1 This workflow led to the discovery that macrophages acquire a lipid-laden phenotype through phagocytosis of lipid-rich myelin debris. These lipid-laden macrophages (LLMs) efflux myelin-derived lipids to tumor cells to fuel their proliferation.

The brain tumor tissue used in these protocols was obtained from a genetically-engineered mouse model of glioblastoma employing the RCAS/t-va technology, as previously described.5 Briefly, glioblastoma was induced by intracranially injecting DF-1 cells expressing an RCAS (Replication-Competent ASLV long terminal repeat (LTR) with a Splice Acceptor) vector encoding PDGF-B HA into 5- to 7-week-old Nestin-TV-a;Ink4a/Arf−/− mice (BL/6 background). Tumor progression was assessed by weekly MRI scans where tumor volumes were measured as previously described.6 The mice were euthanized and brains harvested for use in the below protocols once the tumor reached a volume of 90–120 mm3.

While this protocol was developed and used in a glioblastoma model, we also show an example of collecting and interpreting data from a hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) mouse model,7,8 indicating that this workflow can be applied to the study of lipid-laden macrophages in multiple murine cancer models.

Step-by-step method details

Preparation of single-cell suspension from murine tumor tissue for downstream LLM flow cytometry-based sorting and analyses, or for generation of cell lines

Timing: 2.5 h

This section outlines the standard procedure to process tumor tissues for use in all downstream analyses described later in this protocol. In addition, we describe how to simultaneously isolate tumor interstitial fluid (TIF) from the tumor sample for lipidomic analyses.

1.Isolate tumor tissue.a.Remove as much healthy tissue as possible using a scalpel as shown in Figure 1A. Brain tumor tissue can be recognized by its darker color due to aberrant blood vessel growth and formation of necrotic areas compared to healthy tissue.b.Place isolated tumor tissue in empty 15 mL tube, place on ice.

Representative flow cytometry gating strategy for analysis of the glioblastoma myeloid environment

(A) Gating strategy for identification of lipid-laden macrophages (LLMs) in murine glioblastoma. Total cells were gated on forward and side scatter (FSC-A and SSC-A) to exclude debris. Single cells were isolated from total cells using the FSC-H and FSC-A to exclude doublets. Live cells were gated based on Zombie-NIR exclusion. Myeloid cell populations were gated based on CD11b expression within CD45 positive live cells. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) were then gated as Ly6C− and Ly6G− cells and were separated into monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) and microglia (MG) based on CD49d expression (positive signal for MDMs and negative signal for MGs). LLMs can be identified for both TAM populations based on SSC-Ahigh and BODIPYhigh signal.

(B) Gating strategy for identification of LLMs in murine HCC. All gates are similar to murine glioblastoma samples with the exception of separating tissue-resident macrophages, known as Kupffer cells, from MDMs in the total macrophage population. Kupffer cells were gated as CD11bintermediateF4-80high, whereas MDMs were gated as CD11bhighF4-80intermediate.

(A) Dissect the skin/fur from the hind legs and remove both legs including the hip bone, femur and tibiae. Avoid breaking the bones.

(B) Remove all muscle tissues from the bones using a scissor (or scalpel).

(C) Separate the femur and tibiae and cut off a small piece of bone from both ends and flush it with a 251/2G needle through a 40 μm cell strainer.

(D) Wash the bone-marrow cell suspension and resuspend in 30 mL of maintenance media supplemented with 10 ng/mL CSF-1 before transferring the cells to a Teflon bag with a 10 mL pipette.

In vitro assay: Lipid transfer from macrophages to tumor cells

Timing: day 1: 1 h; day 2: 6 h

This section describes how to assess lipid transfer between macrophages and tumor cells using lipids with a click handle.19 Tracing lipids intercellularly using click chemistry, rather than using lipids tagged with large fluorescent molecules, is crucial to study their physiological function without interfering with their metabolic properties.20 It is important to assess the ability of LLMs to transfer lipids to tumor cells before further probing their functional significance during tumor progression.

40.Load BMDMs with clickable cholesterol.a.Put dTomato+ BMDMs (see Steps 25 to 28 for generating BMDMs) into 2 separate 7 mL Teflon culture bags. Use a concentration of 1.5 × 106 cells per mL; for instance, seed 9 × 106 BMDMs in 6 mL of RPMI lipid transfer media (see materials and equipment section).CRITICAL: To be able to distinguish the two co-cultured cell types (BMDMs and tumor cells) by flow cytometry, at least one of them should be fluorescently labeled, as most extracellular antibody staining for cell type identification are not compatible with this protocol.b.Load one bag of BMDMs with 50 μM alkyne (“clickable”) cholesterol and incubate overnight at 37°C; keep the second bag as negative control.Optional: At this step it is possible to add pharmacological inhibitors for further mechanistic insights, for instance to interfere with lipid efflux or transfer.Alternative: Other lipid species of interest with a click handle can be used instead of cholesterol.

Ex vivo assays to assess LLM formation and functional significance

Timing: day 1: 5 h; day 2: 2 h; day 3/4: 6 h

This section describes the steps to functionally validate the role of lipid uptake and transfer between macrophages and tumor cells in an ex vivo setting, in which in vivo tumors are used as single cell preparations in culture. After dissociation of the tumor, various parameters are changed through positive selection steps, including for instance removing/re-adding myelin debris or macrophages from the whole dissociated tumor, to assess their impact on the phenotypes observed. Alternative options have been included depending on the hypotheses generated in previous steps.

Expected outcomes

This workflow encompasses methodologies to identify and study lipid-laden macrophages in the context of glioblastoma, as extensively presented in Kloosterman, Erbani and colleagues.1 However, this protocol is also designed to be applicable to different tumor types, and the anticipated outcomes of experiments will be dependent on the tissue used. Alternatives were provided throughout the protocol to guide the reader through experimental adaptations where needed. Additionally, while these protocols were optimized for murine samples, they can be adapted to primary human tissue provided that the flow cytometry panels are adjusted, and that the reagents are compatible with human samples.

To illustrate these context-dependent results, we performed transcriptional analyses of LLMs in a hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) murine model.7 Based on the relative lipid pathway activity patterns of LLMs in HCC samples compared to those in glioblastoma, we observed an increase in cholesterol biosynthesis and lipid uptake pathway, suggesting LLMs in HCC acquire their lipids through a different mechanism (Figure 8A). Based on these results, we hypothesize that macrophages in HCC at least partly acquire a lipid-laden phenotype through de novo cholesterol biosynthesis or receptor-mediated lipid uptake.

The expected outcomes and proposed methods to validate these hypotheses in any tumor types are summarized in Figure 8B and are further detailed below.

TIF analysis

The interstitial fluid collected from the tumors, described at Step 2, can be used for a wide range of lipidomics analyses, such as untargeted lipidomic analysis by LC-MS/MS,21 sterol analysis using GC-MS22 or quantitative lipidomics on the Lipidyzer.23 The levels of various lipid classes can be compared to interstitial fluid from healthy tissue, or from tumors of animals treated with any drug of interest (for example, interfering with lipid metabolism and exchange). In combination with the bioinformatic analyses described above, increased levels of specific lipid species found in the TIF can provide an indication of the source of lipids for LLMs, or point towards efflux of lipids by specific cells such as LLMs (and potentially exchanges of lipids within the TME). These hypotheses can be further tested by the functional experiments described below.

In vitro lipid uptake assays

The in vitro assays described in this protocol have been designed to validate the potential lipid source of LLMs and their ability to transfer these lipids to tumor cells.

For example, to test the hypothesis emerging following scRNA-seq analysis of the HCC sample, we suggest performing Steps 29 to 39 to assess whether the lipid content of macrophages can be increased solely by exposure to TCM produced from cell lines generated in Step 8. Adding specific inhibitors for lipid receptors upregulated in HCC-LLMs (based on the scRNA-seq analysis) during these LLM formation assays will elucidate the mechanism underlying the lipid accumulation in HCC-resident macrophages.

Alternatively, if the LLM phenotype from macrophages isolated from other organs than the brain relies on enhanced phagocytosis, as seen in glioblastoma, it is likely not based on myelin uptake. Therefore, other lipid-rich structures present within that tissue may be responsible for LLM accumulation. For example, lipid-rich pulmonary surfactant in the lungs24,25 or lipid-rich necrotic cores in breast cancer26 are examples of such local lipid sources. In these cases, it is crucial to purify these sources of lipids and include them in the relevant protocols instead of myelin, to validate their contribution to LLM formation.

In vitro lipid transfer assays

Click chemistry-based lipid-transfer assays allow precise tracing of lipid classes without interfering with their physico-chemical properties and integration within cellular metabolism.20 In this protocol, we describe tracing of cholesterol with click chemistry, however multiple other lipid species with a click handle are commercially available, such as palmitic, stearic, oleic, palmitoleic, linoleic and arachidonic acids, which allows modification of the experimental setup based on specific hypotheses and scientific questions.

Ex vivo functional assays

To validate the pro-tumorigenic role of macrophages in tumor cell lipid metabolism, we designed an ex vivo system which closely resembles the in vivo environment, with interchangeable steps to expose the pro-tumorigenic function of LLMs. Importantly, adding pharmacological inhibitors (designed to target potential mechanisms identified in the previous steps of this workflow) to these ex vivo assays allows detailed mechanistic examination of selected processes, such as Valspodar to block ABCA1/ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Flow cytometry data was analyzed with FlowJo version 10.8.0. Prism version 10.0.3 was used to generate graphs. Data is presented as ± SEM. The single cell expression data was loaded, analyzed and visualized in R version 4.1.1 using Seurat v4.4. and SeuratObject v4.0.27

Limitations

This protocol was specifically optimized for the study of lipid-laden macrophages in glioblastoma tumor tissue. Applying this workflow to a different tumor type will require to adjust the sorting strategy, and possibly the tissue digestion method to ensure optimal cell viability for ex vivo assays. It is possible that some tumors originating from other tissues do not contain lipid-laden macrophages, and it is therefore crucial to first analyze lipid-laden macrophage content before performing functional assays. Moreover, the code and instructions for bioinformatic analyses described in Steps 17 to 24 of this protocol are designed only to provide an indication of the potential source of lipids driving the LLM phenotype, and thorough mechanistic validation is required to confirm the hypotheses stemming from these computational analyses. Here, we provided examples of such mechanistic validations (Steps 60 to 62) that are applicable for situations where lipid-laden macrophages acquire lipids from an external source of lipids present in the TME (myelin debris in this case). However, if the computational analyses of Step 24 point towards increased biosynthesis activity, rather than an external source of lipids (such as observed in HCC model7), the functional studies should be modified according to the researchers’ hypothesis. Suggestions for adjustments of the experimental setup have been stated throughout this protocol.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

At Step 16 the number of LLMs obtained from the sort is too low for downstream analyses.

Potential solution

Some applications, particularly lipidomic analyses for example, require a high number of LLMs (>3 × 105). It is possible to pool sorted LLMs from multiple biological replicates, as long as the matched non-LLMs from the same biological replicates are similarly pooled.

Problem 2

At Step 60, ex vivo cultured tumor cells do not grow.

Potential solution

Optimize the cell density and well size so that tumor cells are not too sparse, which can impact their growth.

Problem 3

Lack of consistency between batches of cell lines generated from the murine tumors (Step 8a).

Potential solution

To limit batch-to-batch variation between the cell lines, freeze down a large number of vials from a single tumor (>30 vials) at an early passage (p7-p10). Do not keep the cells in culture later than passage 20, and do not freeze down again, as another freeze-thaw may alter the cells’ phenotype. We recommend extensive characterization of each new cell line prior to use in functional assays, to ensure a consistent phenotype. We recommend regular preparation of cell lines from tumors to ensure reliable material for experiments.

Problem 4

After the washing steps of Step 41, there are not enough macrophages to perform a lipid transfer experiment.

Potential solution

Significant loss of macrophages can be expected during the extensive washing performed at Step 41. To anticipate for this loss, the number of macrophages to incubate with the clickable lipids should be approximately 10 times higher than what is needed for the experiment.

Problem 5

There are no EdU+ (proliferating) tumor cells in the readout for Step 58.

Potential solution

Seeding density must be optimized to ensure that the cells do not reach confluency before the end of the experiment, which can lead to cell cycle arrest; conversely, cells seeded too sparsely can also have altered proliferation. Since multiple freeze/thaw cycles of the EdU compound may affect its stability, we recommend preparing small aliquots of the stock solution and thawing each vial only once.
