THE DEDEKIND-HASSE CRITERION
IN

Abstract




Pre-Print Date: August 8, 2026

Abstract

We give a criterion for principal ideal orders, with the objective
of generalizing for an arbitrary order some known results about the
Hurwitz Integers, namely, the existence and uniqueness (up to
associates) of a left/right factor of a given norm and the existence
of a factorization in prime quaternions.

Keywords:
Quaternions, Hurwitz Integers, Quaternion Algebras,
Quaternion Orders, Non Commutative Principal Ideal Domains.

1
Introduction

Inside the rational quaternions, there is a maximal order, the ring of
Hurwitz integers,
\mathcal{H}
, named after Adolf Hurwitz, who studied this
ring in great detail in his book
[
8
]
. In particular,
Hurwitz proved a factorization theorem for
\mathcal{H}
that states that any
quaternion, whose coefficients have no common divisor, has a
factorization in Hurwitzian primes modeled on a rational
factorization of its norm, unique up to unit migration
(see
[
3
, Thm. 2, p. 57]
).

In this paper we focus on an arbitrary rational quaternion algebras

\mathbb{A}=\left(\frac{a,b}{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\mathbb{A}
is a division algebra, inside which one gets many different
orders worth studying. Our main contributions are: to show that the
Dedekind-Hasse criterion is valid for quaternionic orders, how to use
it to construct a finite algorithm that can be applied to prove that
an order is a principal ideal domain, and then to show the application
of the algorithm in two examples of orders that are not norm
Euclidean, but are principal ideal domains.

For a maximal order to be a principal ideal domain it is sufficiently
and necessary for it to have class number 1, that is the number of
isomorphism classes of invertible (left or right) ideals is one, as
all the the ideals are invertible. In 1980, in her book

[
13
]
, Vignéras determined the ten Eichler orders with class
number 1, which in particular include the maximal order with
discriminant equal to
2,3,5,7\text{ or }13
. Also, she remarked
that the only norm Euclidean ones were the ones with discriminant

2,3\text{ or }5
. Later, in 1995, Brzezinski showed that there
were exactly 24 isomorphism classes of orders with class number 1 in
rational definite quaternion algebras, in
[
2
]
, which
included the ten Eichler orders that Vignéras found. Here, we use the
Dedekind-Hasse criterion to provide an alternative proof of the fact
that the maximal order with discriminant
7\text{ and }13
are
principal ideal domains.

In 2009, Conway and Smith, in their book
[
3
]
, provided
more modern proofs of some of Hurwitz’s results, including the
factorization theorem. Nonetheless, in
[
9
, Thm. 2, p. 288]
,
Pall proved, using the theory of quadratic forms, that, in any maximal
order of a quaternion algebra, given an element of that order and an
integer that divides its norm, then that element has a unique up to
associates (right and left) divisor whose norm is that integer. From
this one can easily deduce unique factorization modeled on the
norm. In 2008, Deutsch, in
[
4
]
, set to find a quaternionic
proof of the universality of some quadratic forms of the form

t^{2}+ax^{2}+by^{2}+cz^{2}
, by studying the associated quaternionic
orders, which were exactly the ones that are Euclidean for the
norm. The author succeeded in studying the orders of discriminant
2

and
3
and was then followed by Fitzgerald, in 2011, who provided a
basis for the orders of discriminant
5,7
and
13
, in

[
5
]
. Together, these two articles provide an arithmetic
approach of the fact that the orders of discriminant
2,3
and
5

are Euclidean for the norm and thus principal ideal domains.

We start, in section
2
, by providing an arithmetical proof of
this result of Pall. In section
3
, we show that
Dedekind-Hasse criterion,
[
11
, Thm. 9.5, p. 124]
, is
also valid for quaternionic orders, and we use it to deduce that an
order is a left principal ideal domain if and only if it is a right
principal ideal domain.

In section
4
we use the Dedekind-Hasse criterion as the
basis of a finite procedure to check if a quaternionic order is a
principal ideal domain. Finally, we use this algorithm to give an
alternative proof that the orders of discriminant
7
and
13
,
although not norm Euclidean, are principal ideal domains.

2
Factorization in an Order

In the following sections, let
H
be an order in the rational
quaternion algebra
\mathbb{A}=\left(\frac{-a,-b}{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
, where

a,b>0
and such that
\mathbb{A}
is a division algebra, with the
\mathbb{Q}
-basis

(1,i,j,k)
such that
i^{2}=-a
and
j^{2}=-b
.

Definition 1
.

We say an element
\alpha\in H
is
primitive
if there is
no integer
n\neq-1,1
such that
n\mid\alpha
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
H
be a left (right) principal ideal domain,
\theta\in H
be a
primitive quaternion, and
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

m\mid\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\theta)
. Then there is only one right (left) divisor of

\theta
of norm
m
, up to left (right) associates.

Proof.

Let
H
be a left PID. The proof for right PID is analogous.
Let
\gamma\in H
be such that
H\theta+Hm=H\gamma
, and
let
\alpha,\beta\in H
be such that

\gamma=\alpha\theta+\beta m
. Then

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)=(\alpha\theta+\beta m)(\overline{\theta}\overline{\alpha}+\overline{\beta}m)=\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha)\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\theta)+m^{2}\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\beta)+m(\alpha\theta\overline{\beta}+\beta\overline{\theta}\overline{\alpha}),

which is divisible by

m
, by hypothesis. Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)=mt
. Since
\theta,m\in H\gamma
, there are

f,g\in H\colon\;\theta=f\gamma
and
m=g\gamma
. Then,
taking the norm, we have
m^{2}=\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(g)\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)=\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(g)mt
, and
thus
m=\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(g)t
. From this it follows that

g\gamma=m=\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(g)t=g\overline{g}t,

and so

\gamma=\overline{g}t
. But then

\theta=f\gamma=f\overline{g}t
, which implies that

t\mid\theta
, and thus we must have
t=1
, since
\theta
is
primitive. This shows that
\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)=m
, and since

\theta=f\gamma
, we have shown that
\theta
does indeed have a
right divisor norm
m
.

Now, if
\delta
is another right divisor of
\theta
of norm
m
,
then
\theta=\epsilon\delta
for some
\epsilon\in H
. But
then

\gamma=\alpha\theta+\beta m=\alpha\epsilon\delta+\beta\overline{\delta}\delta=(\alpha\epsilon+\beta\overline{\delta})\delta.

The fact that

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)=\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)
then entails that
\delta
and
\gamma

are left associates.
∎

Recall that, we say an element
\pi\in H
is a
prime
quaternion
(in
H
) if
\pi=\alpha\beta
implies that either

\alpha
or
\beta
is a unit, that is, if and only if the norm of

\pi
is a rational prime.

Let us now state the unique factorization theorem for any order which
is a principal ideal domain, first proved in In
[
3
]

by Conway and Smith for the Hurwitz integers.

Theorem 2

(Factorization Theorem)
.

If an order

H
is a (left or right) principal ideal domain, then to each
factorization of the norm
q
of a primitive quaternion
\gamma\in H

into a product
p_{0}p_{1}\dots p_{k-1}p_{k}
of rational primes, there
is a factorization

\gamma=\pi_{0}\pi_{1}\dots\pi_{k-1}\pi_{k}

of
\gamma
into a
product of prime quaternion modeled on that factorization of
q
,
that is, with
\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\pi_{i})=p_{i}
. Moreover, if

\gamma=\pi_{0}\pi_{1}\dots\pi_{k-1}\pi_{k}
is any one
factorization modeled on
p_{0}p_{1}\dots p_{k-1}p_{k}
, then all the
others have the form

\gamma=\pi_{0}u_{1}\cdot u^{-1}_{1}\pi_{1}u_{2}\cdot u^{-1}_{2}\pi_{2}u_{3}\dots u^{-1}_{k-1}\pi_{k-1}u_{k}\cdot u^{-1}_{k}\pi_{k},

where,
u_{i}
is a
unit for all
i=1,\dots,k
, which means that the factorization
on a given model is unique up to unit-migration.

Using Theorem
1
, one can easily adapt the original proof
by Conway and Smith for this case.

3
The Dedekind-Hasse Criterion

Let
H
be an order in the rational quaternion algebra
\mathbb{A}
, as in the
previous section. The usual
norm
map
\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{Q}_{0}^{+}
,
which sends
\alpha=x_{0}+x_{1}i+x_{2}j+x_{3}k
to

\alpha\overline{\alpha}=x_{0}^{2}+ax_{1}^{2}+bx_{2}^{2}+abx_{3}^{2}
,
plays a central role in studying the arithmetic of
H
. Recall that a
ring
R
is a said to be a
left (right) principal ideal
domain
(PID) if it is a domain and every left (right) ideal is
principal. The following criterion for an order to be a principal
ideal domain is based on the criterion attributed to Dedekind and
Hasse in
[
11
, Thm. 9.5, p. 124]
.

Theorem 3
.

The order
H
is a left principal ideal domain
if and only
if

\forall\rho\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H\;\exists\,\alpha,\beta\in H\colon 0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\rho-\beta)<1.

Proof.

Suppose
H
is a left PID, and let
\rho\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H
be
given. As
H
is an order, we have
H\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{A}
, thus
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\rho\in H
, with
n>1
. Let

\gamma
be a generator of the ideal
Hn+H(n\rho)
. Then

\gamma=\alpha(n\rho)-\beta n
for some
\alpha,\beta\in H
. From the fact that
n\in H\gamma
, we have

n^{2}=\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(n)=q\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)
for some
q\in\mathbb{N}
. Now if

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)=n^{2}
, we would have that
n
and
\gamma
are
associates, and as
n\rho\in H\gamma=Hn
, we would get

\rho\in H
, which is absurd. So
0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(n)
, which
upon dividing by
\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(n)
yields
0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\rho-\beta)<1
.

Conversely, assume the right hand condition holds, and let
I
be a
non-zero left ideal of
H
. Let
\gamma
be an element of the ideal

I
of minimal norm, that is, be such that

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)=\min\{\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(x):x\in I\setminus\{0\}\}
. Suppose that

I\not=H\gamma
. Then there exists an element

\delta\in I\setminus H\gamma
, so

\delta\gamma^{-1}\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H
, thus, by the hypothesis
made, there are two elements
\alpha,\beta\in H
such that

0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha(\delta\gamma^{-1})-\beta)<1
. This means that
the element

\eta:=\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma\in I\setminus\{0\}

satisfies
\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\eta)<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma)
, which yields the desired
contradiction.
∎

Remark
: Analogously, one shows that
H
is a right
principal ideal domain
if and only if

\forall\rho\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H\;\exists\,\alpha,\beta\in H\colon 0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\rho\alpha-\beta)<1
.

Corollary 1
.

H is a left PID
iff

H
is a right PID.

Proof.

Suppose
H
is a left PID, and let
\rho\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H
. As

H
is closed for conjugation,
\mathbb{A}\setminus H
is also closed for
conjugation. By the previous theorem, there are
\alpha,\beta
such
that
\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\overline{\rho}-\beta)<1
. Then, as

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\rho\overline{\alpha}-\overline{\beta})=\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\overline{\rho}-\beta)<1
, we conclude that
H
is a right PID.
The converse is analogous.
∎

This result allow us to concentrate our attention only on left
PIDs. We define

\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{A}}:=\{\rho\in\mathbb{A}\>|\>0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\rho)<1\}

and

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}:=\{\rho\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H\>|\>\exists\alpha,\beta\in H:\;\alpha\rho-\beta\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{A}}\},

so that
H
is a PID if and only if
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{A}\setminus H
.

The next result is well known but here we use the criterion to prove it.

Proposition 1
.

A norm Euclidean order is necessarily a PID.

Proof.

It is known that
H
is norm Euclidean if and only if for all

\rho\in\mathbb{A}
there exists a
\beta\in H
such that

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\rho-\beta)<1
. In particular, this is true for

\rho\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H
, and thus
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{A}\setminus H
.
∎

Proposition 2
.

If an order
H
in
\mathbb{A}
is a principal ideal domain, then
H
is
maximal.

Proof.

Suppose that
H
is not a maximal order, that is, there exists an
order
L
such that
H\subsetneq L\subset\mathbb{A}
. As
L
is an order,
by
[
12
, Thm. 10.1, p. 125]
, one has that

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(L):=\{\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha)\colon\alpha\in L\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
.

Pick
\ell\in L\setminus H
. Then, as
\ell\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H
,
there exist elements
\alpha,\beta\in H
such that

0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\ell-\beta)<1
. As

\alpha,\beta\in H\subsetneq L
,
\alpha\ell-\beta\in L
, and so

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\ell-\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

4
Reduction to a Finite Algorithm

If we now wish to apply the Dedekind-Hasse criterion to an order
H
,
we would need to verify if every element of
\mathbb{A}\setminus H
is in

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
. The aim of this section is to reduce the number of
quaternions that one would need to check.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\rho\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H
and
\delta\in H
. Then

1.

\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
if and only if

\rho+\delta\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
,

2.

If
\delta\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
, then
\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
. Then there are
\alpha,\beta\in H

such that
0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\rho-\beta)<1
. As

\alpha\delta+\beta\in H
, we have

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits\big(\alpha(\rho+\delta)-(\alpha\delta+\beta)\big)=\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\rho-\beta),

that is,
\rho+\delta\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
. The converse is proved using this implication for

\rho+\delta
and
-\delta
. For the second property, suppose
that
\delta\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
. Then, we have
\alpha,\beta\in H

such that

0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\delta\rho-\beta)<1,

but as

\alpha\delta\in H
, it immediately follows that

\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
.
∎

Corollary 2
.

For
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H
one has

\rho_{1}\equiv\rho_{2}\mod{H}\Longrightarrow(\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}\iff\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}).

Thus, in order to check if a quaternionic order is a PID, it is enough
to select one element from each non-zero class in
\mathbb{A}/H
, and then
verify if all such elements belong to
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
. Fixing a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
for
H
, which is then a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis for
\mathbb{A}
, we just need to check if all
non-zero elements of
\mathbb{A}
whose coefficients, in that basis, have
absolute value less or equal to
\frac{1}{2}
are in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
.

Moreover, we can further reduce the number of elements of

\mathbb{A}\setminus H
we need to check as follows. As
H
is an order, any
element of
\mathbb{A}
is of the form
\frac{1}{n}\delta
, for some

n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in H
. Thus, by Proposition
3
,
if
\frac{1}{p}\delta\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
, for a rational prime
p
, then

\frac{1}{pq}\delta\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
, for any
q\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, we need
only to check if
\frac{1}{p}\delta\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
for all primes
p

and
\delta\in H
. Now, if
\{1,v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis for

H
, it follows that one only needs to verify if

\frac{d_{0}}{p}+\frac{d_{1}}{p}v_{1}+\frac{d_{2}}{p}v_{2}+\frac{d_{3}}{p}v_{3}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}

for all positive rational primes
p
, and
for
d_{i}
with
d_{0}\geq 0
and
\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{2}
, a finite set
with no more than
\frac{1}{2}p^{4}
elements.

Furthermore, since
\frac{\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)}{\delta}=\overline{\delta}\in H

for any
\delta\in H
, we have that

p\nmid\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)\implies\exists_{r\in\mathbb{Z},0<\absolutevalue{r}<p}:\overline{\delta}\cdot\frac{\delta}{p}=\frac{\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)}{p}\equiv\frac{r}{p}\pmod{H},

and thus, for any
\delta\in H
,

p\nmid\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)\implies\frac{\delta}{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}

(since
\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\frac{r}{p})<1
)
.

All of the above proves the following:

Theorem 4
.

With the above notations,
H
is a PID
iff

\frac{\delta}{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
, for all positive rational primes
p
,
and for all
\delta=d_{0}+d_{1}v_{1}+d_{2}v_{2}+d_{3}v_{3}
such that

\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{2}
and
p\mid\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)
.

Next, we wish to bound the number of primes one needs to check. Let,
as before,
H=\mathbb{Z}[1,v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}]
be an order in the algebra

\mathbb{A}=\left(\frac{-a,-b}{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
where
a,b>0
. Define

d=(1+a)(1+b)
and

M=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq 3\\
0\leq s\leq 3\end{subarray}}\absolutevalue{a_{r,s}},\text{ where }v_{r}=a_{r,0}+a_{r,1}i+a_{r,2}j+a_{r,3}k.

Set
\delta=d_{0}+d_{1}v_{1}+d_{2}v_{2}+d_{3}v_{3}
with

\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{2}
. We are now going to show that, for
p

sufficiently large,
\frac{\delta}{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
trivially. More
precisely, we are going to show that there is some
\alpha\in H
such
that
\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits\left(\alpha\frac{\delta}{p}\right)<1
.

First, we show that if we had
\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{Q}
for
i=1,2,3
,
and for some appropriately chosen
Q\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits\left(\frac{\delta}{p}\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d_{0}}{p}+\frac{d_{1}}{p}a_{1,0}+\frac{d_{2}}{p}a_{2,0}+\frac{d_{3}}{p}a_{3,0}\right)^{2}+a\left(\frac{d_{1}}{p}a_{1,1}+\frac{d_{2}}{p}a_{2,1}+\frac{d_{3}}{p}a_{3,1}\right)^{2}+

\displaystyle+b\left(\frac{d_{1}}{p}a_{1,2}+\frac{d_{2}}{p}a_{2,2}+\frac{d_{3}}{p}a_{3,2}\right)^{2}+ab\left(\frac{d_{1}}{p}a_{1,3}+\frac{d_{2}}{p}a_{2,3}+\frac{d_{3}}{p}a_{3,3}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{Q}a_{1,0}+\frac{1}{Q}a_{2,0}+\frac{1}{Q}a_{3,0}\right)^{2}+a\left(\frac{1}{Q}a_{1,1}+\frac{1}{Q}a_{2,1}+\frac{1}{Q}a_{3,1}\right)^{2}+

\displaystyle+b\left(\frac{1}{Q}a_{1,2}+\frac{1}{Q}a_{2,2}+\frac{1}{Q}a_{3,2}\right)^{2}+ab\left(\frac{1}{Q}a_{1,3}+\frac{1}{Q}a_{2,3}+\frac{1}{Q}a_{3,3}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3M}{Q}\right)^{2}+a\left(\frac{3M}{Q}\right)^{2}+b\left(\frac{3M}{Q}\right)^{2}+ab\left(\frac{3M}{Q}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}+3\frac{M}{Q}+9d\left(\frac{M}{Q}\right)^{2}

Since

\frac{1}{4}+3\frac{M}{Q}+9d\left(\frac{M}{Q}\right)^{2}\leq 1\iff Q\geq M(2+2\sqrt{1+3d}),

one gets the following result:

Lemma 1
.

With the above notations, setting

Q=\left\lceil M(2+2\sqrt{1+3d})\right\rceil
, one has that if

\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{Q}
for
i=1,2,3
then

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits\left(\frac{\delta}{p}\right)<1
.

Now, we only need to guarantee that we can reduce to the case in
which, for
i=1,2,3
, one has
\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{Q}
. For this
purpose, we use Theorem 201 in
[
7
, Chap. IX, p. 170]
,
with a slightly modified statement to better suits our notations.

Theorem 5
.

Given
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and any
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we can find

u\in\{1,\dots,Q^{n}\}
so that, for all
i
,
u\xi_{i}
differs
from an integer by less than
\frac{1}{Q}
. In other words,

\absolutevalue{ u \xi_i - k_i}<\frac{1}{Q}

for some
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
for all
i
.

Let
\xi_{i}=\frac{d_{i}}{p}
, for some
p
prime and

\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{2}
, and let
Q
as in
Lemma
1
. Then this theorem implies that there is

u\in\{1,\dots,Q^{3}\}
, and there are
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, for
i=1,2,3
,
so that
\absolutevalue{u \frac{d_i}{p} - k_i}<\frac{1}{Q}
. Setting

r_{i}=ud_{i}-pk_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, one has
ud_{i}\equiv r_{i}\mod{p}
and

\absolutevalue{\frac{r_i}{p}}<\frac{1}{Q}
.

By Proposition
3
, in order to show that

\frac{\delta}{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
, it is enough to show that

u\frac{\delta}{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
. We have

u\frac{\delta}{p}=\frac{ud_{0}}{p}+\frac{ud_{1}}{p}v_{1}+\frac{ud_{2}}{p}v_{2}+\frac{ud_{3}}{p}v_{3}\equiv\frac{d_{0}^{\prime}}{p}+\frac{r_{1}}{p}v_{1}+\frac{r_{2}}{p}v_{2}+\frac{r_{3}}{p}v_{3}\mod{H},

where
\absolutevalue{r_i}<\frac{p}{Q}
and

d_{0}^{\prime}\equiv ud_{0}\mod{p}
is such that
\absolutevalue{d_0'}<\frac{p}{2}
. Let
\gamma
be the quaternion on the right hand
side. Then, by Lemma
1
, we have

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits\left(\gamma\right)<1
. Since
1\leq u\leq Q^{3}
, for

p>Q^{3}
, one sees that in this case one has that
\gamma\notin H
,
so that
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
. That is, we proved the following result.

Theorem 6
.

Let
H=\mathbb{Z}[1,v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}]
be an order in the rational quaternion algebra

\mathbb{A}=\left(\frac{-a,-b}{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
, where
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
. Let

d=(1+a)(1+b)
, and

M=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq 3\\
0\leq s\leq 3\end{subarray}}\absolutevalue{a_{r,s}},\text{ where }v_{r}=a_{r,0}+a_{r,1}i+a_{r,2}j+a_{r,3}k.

Then, to check if
H
is a PID, we only need to check if

\frac{\delta}{p}=\frac{d_{0}}{p}+\frac{d_{1}}{p}v_{1}+\frac{d_{2}}{p}v_{2}+\frac{d_{3}}{p}v_{3}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}

for

\delta\in H
with
d_{0}\geq 0
and
\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{2}
and such that

p\mid\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)
, and for

p\text{ prime}\text{ such that }p<\left\lceil M(2+2\sqrt{1+3d})\right\rceil^{3}.

Remark 1
.

In this last proof, we have only used Theorem
5

for the three last coefficients of
\delta
because, although
bounding all four coefficients would lead to a smaller bound in
Lemma
1
, that would entail a fourth power in the last
result. It turns out that, when working with a particular case, one
can get a smaller upper bound for the primes
p
either by bounding
the coefficients of
\delta
through tighter estimates than the
value
M
above, or by using Theorem
5
for only
two coefficients, which can be done if our algebra is of the form

\left(\frac{-1,-b}{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
.

We now have a criterion to check if a given order is a PID by
verifying if only a finite number of quaternions

\rho\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H
are in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
. However, checking if a
given element
\rho
is in this last set is still a problem, as there
exists an infinite number of possibilities for
\alpha,\beta
to
consider if
0<\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\alpha\rho-\beta)<1
. Let us now see how that this
can also be reduced to checking a finite number of possibilities.

Proposition 4
.

Let
H
be an order in a quaternion algebra
\mathbb{A}
. Let
p
be a
rational prime and
\rho=\frac{\delta}{p}
in
\mathbb{A}\setminus H
. For any
\alpha\in H
we have the following equivalence:

\exists\beta\in H\colon\alpha\rho-\beta\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{A}}\iff\forall\alpha_{1}\in[\alpha]\;\exists\beta_{1}\in H\colon\alpha_{1}\rho-\beta_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{A}},

where
[\alpha]
denotes
the equivalence class of
\alpha
in
H/pH
.

Proof.

Let us fix a prime
p
, and set

\rho=\frac{\delta}{p}\in\mathbb{A}\setminus H
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be elements of
H
. Let
\alpha_{1}\in H
be any
representative of
[\alpha]\in H/pH
, and let
\gamma\in H
be
such that
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+p\gamma
. Then

\alpha\,\frac{\delta}{p}-\beta=(\alpha_{1}+p\gamma)\,\frac{\delta}{p}-\beta=\alpha_{1}\,\frac{\delta}{p}-(\beta-\gamma\delta).

Thus, if
\alpha,\beta
are such that

\alpha\frac{\delta}{p}-\beta\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{A}}
, then we can just take

\beta_{1}=(\beta-\gamma\delta)
.
∎

Therefore, if we put the previous proposition together with Theorem

6
, we get the following result.

Theorem 7
.

To check if an order
H
in an algebra
\mathbb{A}
is a PID, we only need
to check if, for all
\rho=\frac{\delta}{p}
with
p
prime such
that
p<\left\lceil M(2+2\sqrt{1+3d})\right\rceil^{3}
and with

\delta\in H
such that
d_{0}\geq 0
and
\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{2}
, and
such that
p\mid\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)
, there exists
\alpha,\beta\in H
, where

\alpha=a_{0}+a_{1}v_{1}+a_{2}v_{2}+a_{3}v_{3}
with

\absolutevalue{a_i}<\frac{p}{2}
, such that
\alpha\rho-\beta\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{A}}
.

All that is left to consider is the best way to find if there is a
suitable
\beta
for each pair
\left(\rho,\alpha\right)
in the
previous theorem. In other words, we have to solve

\min_{\beta\in H}\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\gamma-\beta),

(1)

where
\gamma
runs over the possible pairs
(\rho,\alpha)
. To put it
another way, we want to know if the closest quaternion to

\alpha\rho
that belongs to the order is at a distance less than 1
or not. This is related to the closest vector problem, and it can be
solved using an algorithm presented by László Babai in
[
1
]
.

Let us first identify the algebra
\mathbb{A}
with
\mathbb{R}^{4}
as follows

\displaystyle\phi\colon\mathbb{A}=\left(\frac{-a,-b}{\mathbb{Q}}\right)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\ \mathbb{R}^{4}

\displaystyle t+xi+yj+xk\

\displaystyle\longmapsto\ (t,x\sqrt{a},y\sqrt{b},z\sqrt{ab}).

One can easily see that the bilinear form associated with the algebra

\mathbb{A}
, that is,

B(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\alpha\overline{\beta})
, is equal
to the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, that is,

\phi(\alpha)\cdot\phi(\beta)
. Thus, problem of finding the minimun
in (
1
) is equivalent to finding

\min_{v\in\phi(H)}\norm{u-v},

where
u=\phi(\gamma)
. If
\gamma=\sum_{i=0}^{3}u_{i}v_{i}
, then

u=\sum_{i=0}^{3}u_{i}\phi(v_{i})
, and Babai’s Algorithm works by
rounding each
u_{i}
to the nearest integer.

Now, Theorem 6.34 of
[
10
, p. 380]
tells us that this
algorithm finds an approximate solution to the closest vector problem
if the vectors in the basis are sufficiently orthogonal to one
another, which is to say that its
Hadamard Ratio

(cf.
[
10
, p. 373]
) is reasonably close to 1. This ratio is
defined for any given basis,
(v_{0},\dots v_{n})
, of a lattice in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\mathcal{H}(v_{0},\dots,v_{n-1})=\left(\frac{\absolutevalue{\det(A)}}{\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\norm{v_i}}\right)^{1/n}

where
A
is the matrix whose columns are the
coordinates of
v_{i}
.

Although this ratio might not be close to one for most bases, in our
case the algorithm did succeeded in find a suitable element, as we
will see in the next section. For this reason, the algorithm failing
to find a pair
(\alpha,\beta)
for a particular
\frac{\delta}{p}

does not imply immediately that the order is not a PID. But we can
then try to deal with each of those exceptions individually if the
criterion
3
fails, or not. However, this did not
happen for the orders we are concerned about in this paper.

5
The Maximal Orders of Discriminant 7 and 13

In this section, we will apply the Dedekind-Hasse Criterion, according
to Theorem
7
, to the maximal orders of
discriminant 7 and 13, in order to give an arithmetical proof that
they are both principal ideal domains.

5.1
Maximal Order of Discriminant 7

Consider the rational quaternion algebra

\mathbb{A}=\left(\frac{-1,-7}{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
, and its maximal order, of
discriminant 7,

\mathcal{H}_{1,7}=\mathbb{Z}[1,v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}]=\mathbb{Z}\left[1,i,\frac{1}{2}(1+j),\frac{1}{2}(i+k)\right]

with norm form

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(d_{0}+d_{1}v_{1}+d_{2}v_{2}+d_{3}v_{3})=\left(d_{0}+\frac{d_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(d_{1}+\frac{d_{3}}{2}\right)^{2}+7\left(\frac{d_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}+7\left(\frac{d_{3}}{2}\right)^{2}.

According to Theorem
6
, we have that we only need
to check if elements of the form

\frac{\delta}{p}=\frac{d_{0}}{p}+\frac{d_{1}}{p}v_{1}+\frac{d_{2}}{p}v_{2}+\frac{d_{3}}{p}v_{3}
are in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
, for the primes
p
such
that
p<\lceil M(2+2\sqrt{1+3d})\rceil^{3}
and for
\delta\in H

with
d_{0}\geq 0
and
\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{2}
and such that

p\mid\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)
. We easily see that, for this order,
d=16
and

M=1
, and thus it would be enough to deal with the primes
p<16^{3}
. Actually, this upper bound can be improved, as we are working
with a concrete order, and because our algebra is of the form

\left(\frac{-1,-b}{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
we can bound only the coefficients
of
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
in order to get a better bound for
p
.

Let us then reproduce, for this particular case, the calculations done
in the proof of Lemma
1
to find the smallest
Q
such
that
\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{Q}
, for
i=2,3
, implies

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits\left(\frac{\delta}{p}\right)<1
:

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits\left(\frac{\delta}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d_{0}}{p}+\frac{d_{2}}{2p}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{d_{1}}{p}+\frac{d_{3}}{2p}\right)^{2}+7\left(\frac{d_{2}}{2p}\right)^{2}+7\left(\frac{d_{3}}{2p}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle<\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2Q}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2Q}\right)^{2}+7\left(\frac{1}{2Q}\right)^{2}+7\left(\frac{1}{2Q}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{Q}+\frac{4}{Q^{2}}.

Hence, one sees that, when
Q\geq 4
, one has

\frac{\delta}{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
. Once again, using the same arguments as
before, we can see that we only need to check if

\frac{\delta}{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
for
\delta\in H
as above, and for
primes
p
with
p<4^{2}
, which is a better upper bound than
before. Therefore we just need to verify if
\frac{\delta}{p}
is in

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{A}}
for the primes
p=2,3,5,7,11,13
, and
\delta
with

d_{0}\geq 0
and
\absolutevalue{d_i}<\frac{p}{2}
and such that
p\mid\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(\delta)
.

Using the PARI/GP
[
6
]
code presented in the next section, we
were able to find how many quaternions of the form
\frac{\delta}{p}

need to be checked according to Theorem
6
. Note
that the Hadamard ratio of this basis is

\mathcal{H}(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})=\left(\frac{7}{8}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\approx 0.967,

which suggest that the Babai’s algorithm will work. The code was run
in a laptop of 16GB of RAM, and it took less than a second to check
102 quaternions over six primes.

Finally, using Theorem
7
and the corresponding
PARI/GP functions in Section
6
, we were able
to find, for each quaternion of the above form, pairs
\alpha,\beta

such that
\alpha\frac{\delta}{p}-\beta\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{A}}
, thus proving
the following:

Theorem 8
.

\mathcal{H}_{1,7}
is a Principal Ideal Domain.

5.2
Maximal Order of Discriminant 7

Consider now the rational algebra

\mathbb{A}=\left(\frac{-7,-13}{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
, with its maximal order of
discriminant 13

\mathcal{H}_{7,13}=\mathbb{Z}[1,v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}]=\mathbb{Z}\left[1,\frac{1}{2}(1+i),\frac{1}{7}(i+k),\frac{1}{14}(7+i+7j+k)\right],

and norm form

\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits(d_{0}+d_{1}v_{1}+d_{2}v_{2}+d_{3}v_{3})=\left(d_{0}+\frac{d_{1}}{2}+\frac{d_{3}}{2}\right)^{2}+7\left(\frac{d_{1}}{2}+\frac{d_{2}}{7}+\frac{d_{2}}{14}\right)^{2}+\\

\frac{\delta}{p}=\frac{d_{0}}{p}+\frac{d_{1}}{p}v_{1}+\frac{d_{2}}{p}v_{2}+\frac{d_{3}}{p}v_{3}

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm N}\nolimits\left(\frac{\delta}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d_{0}}{p}+\frac{d_{1}}{2p}+\frac{d_{3}}{2p}\right)^{2}+7\left(\frac{d_{1}}{2p}+\frac{d_{2}}{7p}+\frac{d_{2}}{14p}\right)^{2}+13\left(\frac{d_{3}}{2p}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\ +91\left(\frac{d_{2}}{7p}+\frac{d_{3}}{14p}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle<\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2Q}+\frac{1}{2Q}\right)^{2}+7\left(\frac{1}{2Q}+\frac{1}{7Q}+\frac{1}{14Q}\right)^{2}+13\left(\frac{1}{2Q}\right)^{2}+

, and for
