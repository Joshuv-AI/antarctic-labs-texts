Study on Carbon Dioxide Capture Using Ternary Betaine-Based Deep Eutectic Solvents
Authors: Chengfang Liu, Xuzhong Zhou, Shihong Guo, Shuie Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The application of existing ternary betaine (Be)-based deep eutectic solvents (DESs) faces inevitable limitations due to the low regenerability and water absorption capability of the DESs. In this study, three ternary Be-based DESs with different molar ratios were prepared by adding the promoter diethanolamine (DEA) to the binary system of Be and ethylene glycol (EG). The effects of DEA, temperature, and flow rate on CO2 absorption by the Be-based DESs were then investigated. At a molar ratio of 1:5:8 (Be:EG:DEA), the CO2 absorption capacity reached 0.207 g CO2/g DES at an absorption temperature of 30 °C and a gas flow rate of 50 mL/min. The addition of water with different contents triggered DES dissolution, forming a ternary Be-based DES + water system. The added water inhibited the absorption of CO2 by the ternary Be-based DESs. Evaluation of the regenerability of the ternary Be-based DESs showed that their CO2 absorption capacities remained above 0.176 g CO2/g DES after five cycles of absorption–desorption.

Introduction

Fossil fuel utilization leads to significant greenhouse gas emissions and global warming. Capturing carbon dioxide (CO2) from the environment has become critical for preventing global warming.1−3 Currently, chemical absorption technologies, due to their maturity, high decarbonization efficiency, and selectivity, represent the most promising approaches for postcombustion carbon capture in industrial applications.4,5 However, the high energy consumption involved in regenerating organic amine aqueous solutions has become a significant technical bottleneck, severely restricting the widespread industrial application of chemical absorption technologies.6 Issues such as the complex synthesis method of ionic liquids, high viscosity of the liquids after absorption, and high costs have greatly limited their industrialization.7

A deep eutectic solvent (DES) is a novel liquid system formed by hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs) and hydrogen bond donors (HBDs), with a melting point significantly lower than those of its components.8,9 Apart from biodegradability,10 recyclable performance,11 easy preparation,12 and low cost,13 DESs exhibit all the characteristics of ionic liquids, including low volatility, thermal stability, and designability. Abbott et al.14 were the first to report this solvent type. They successfully prepared liquid eutectic mixtures from choline chloride and urea at room temperature and named them DESs. Siani et al.15 studied the ability of amphoteric natural DESs based on N,N,N-trimethylglycine and carboxylic acids (oxalic acid, ethanol, and phenylacetic acid) to serve as eco-friendly solvents. Furthermore, CO2 solubility of these solvents was measured using the gravimetric method at different temperatures (298.15–333.15 K) and pressures (0.1–4 MPa), and the influence of experimental conditions on CO2 solubility was discussed. Altamash et al.16 mixed betaine (Be) with alanine, lactic acid, and malic acid with a molar ratio of 1:1 to synthesize natural DESs. They found that DESs synthesized with Be as the HBA had lower viscosities, which ensured a low mass-transfer resistance. Jangir et al.17 used novel Be hydrochloride as the HBA and urea, ethylene glycol (EG), diethylene glycol, triethylene glycol, and glycerin as HBDs to prepare ternary DESs. The molecular structure and hydrogen bonds of the prepared DESs were studied by using comprehensive spectral research methods. Thermal stability, density, viscosity, and other important thermophysical properties at different temperatures were also investigated. Be can be used as an HBA to prepare DESs to absorb CO2.18−20

The recyclability and water content of DESs are of great significance in their practical applications as water affects CO2 capture and recyclability affects the cost of practical application.21−23 However, there are currently a few relevant studies on ternary DES in this aspect.

In this study, the promoter diethanolamine (DEA) was added to a binary Be-based DES to prepare ternary Be-based DESs for CO2 absorption. The effects of the temperature and gas flow rate on CO2 capture by the ternary Be-based DESs were investigated, and the prepared Be-based DESs were systematically characterized. The absorption mechanisms of the binary and ternary Be-based DESs were studied. In addition, the effect of the ternary Be-based DESs with different water contents on CO2 capture was explored along with the regenerability of the DESs.

Experimental Section

Materials

Betaine (AR, 98%), EG (AR, 99%), and DEA(AR, 99%) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. All materials were used for the preparation of a betaine-based DES without further purification. CO2 (99.99%) and N2 (99.999%) were purchased from Guizhou Sanhe Gas Co., Ltd. in China.

Preparation of Betaine-Based DESs

The HBA and HBD were mixed in a round-bottom flask at a specified molar ratio and stirred vigorously with magnetic stirring in an oil bath at 60 °C until a clear liquid state was formed, and the method was similar to previously reported methods.24,25 Using Be as the HBA and EG as the HBD, binary Be-based DESs were formed by mixing different molar ratios (Be:EG = 1:4; Be:EG = 1:5; Be:EG = 1:6) at 60 °C for a certain period. Furthermore, ternary Be-based DESs were prepared using Be as the HBA, EG as the primary HBD, and DEA as the secondary HBD. Different molar ratios of the components (Be:EG:DEA = 1:4:8; Be:EG:DEA = 1:5:8; Be:EG:DEA = 1:6:8) were mixed at 60 °C for a certain period. Figure 1 shows the static state of the Be-based DESs synthesized in this study at room temperature over an extended period. All DESs were dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 12 h before use.

From left to right: (a) Be:EG = 1:4, Be:EG = 1:5, Be:EG = 1:6; (b) Be:EG:DEA = 1:4:8, Be:EG:DEA = 1:5:8, Be:EG:DEA = 1:6:8.

Characterization

The thermal stability of betaine-based DESs before and after CO2 absorption was determined using a TGA5500 thermogravimetric analyzer from TA Instruments, USA. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Thermo Scientific Nicolet is20, USA) was used to measure the infrared spectra of betaine-based DESs before and after CO2 absorption. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR, Bruker Ascend TM 600 MHz, Germany) was employed to analyze the 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra of betaine-based DESs before and after CO2 absorption, providing insights into the structural changes of the betaine-based DESs. The viscosity was measured with a falling ball-type viscometer (Lovis 2000 M).

CO2 Absorption

The CO2 capture experiments were conducted by using a gas absorption apparatus, as shown in Figure 2. The CO2 absorption capacity of the Be-based DES was determined using the gravimetric method. The mass of the Be-based DES was denoted as M0, and the total mass of the absorption bottle and Be-based DES was denoted as M1. The total weight of the absorption bottle and Be-based DES was measured every 10 min and recorded as Mn. The measurements were repeated three times, and the average value was taken. M represents the amount of CO2 absorbed (g CO2/g DES).

Regeneration of Ternary Betaine-Based DESs

The desorption of CO2 from the ternary betaine-based DES was achieved under the conditions of heating and N2 purging. The saturated absorbent was added to a desorption flask and placed in a constant-temperature water bath at 80 °C. The inlet of the desorption flask was connected to N2, and the release of CO2 was monitored by weighing the mass change during the desorption process. When the weight of the tube no longer changed, it was considered that desorption was complete. Results and Discussion

Thermal Stability

The heating process of the Be-based DESs involved the breakdown of hydrogen bond networks within the solvents. Subsequently, the HBD, with poor thermal stability, decomposed.26 As the temperature continued to rise, the HBA also decomposed. Thus, during heating, the Be-based DESs lost weight.

Figure 4a shows that for the binary Be-based DES with a molar ratio of 1:5, the first decomposition event occurred at 106 °C; after CO2 absorption, the first decomposition event occurred at 86 °C. Figure 4b shows that for the ternary Be-based DES with a molar ratio of 1:5:8, the first decomposition event occurred at 169 °C, and after CO2 absorption, the first decomposition event occurred at 82 °C. These results indicate that the Be-based DES can be recycled and reused and the ternary Be-based DES with the promoter exhibits better thermal stability.

(a) Thermogravimetric curve of the binary betaine-based DES. (b) Thermogravimetric curve of the ternary betaine-based DES.

According to Figure 4a, the 5% mass loss temperature of DES (BE:EG = 1:5) before and after CO2 absorption was 115 and 95 °C. According to Figure 4b, the 5% mass loss temperature of DES (BE:EG:DEA = 1:5:8) before and after CO2 absorption was 168 and 88 °C, respectively. It shows that the DES system has good thermal stability under the experimental working conditions. The good thermal stability is conducive to the desorption and recycling of DESs.

The Effect of Ternary Betaine-Based DESs on CO2 Capture Performance

Evaluation of the ability of the Be-based DESs to capture CO2 revealed that DEA, temperature, and flow rate affect the CO2 capture rate and capacity. Figure 5a,b shows the results of capture experiments performed at the same temperature and flow rate. Results also revealed that the CO2 capture capacity of the binary Be-based DESs with different ratios was low (Figure 5a). The physical absorption mechanism of the Be–EG system may have led to the low absorption capacity.

(a) Effect of Be–EG on CO2 absorption capacity at different molar ratios. (b) Effect of Be–EG–DEA on CO2 absorption capacity at different molar ratios. (c) Effect of Be:EG:DEA (1:5:8) on CO2 absorption capacity at different temperatures. (d) Effect of Be:EG:DEA (1:5:8) on the CO2 absorption capacity at different flow rates.

Analysis of the effect of different molar ratios of the ternary Be-based DESs on the CO2 capture capacity revealed high capture rates and good absorption capacities (Figure 5b). For instance, during the first 10 min, the CO2 absorption capacities were 0.102 g CO2/g DES (1:4:8), 0.102 g CO2/g DES (1:5:8), and 0.117 g CO2/g DES (1:6:8). After 60 min, the absorption capacities were 0.169 g CO2/g DES (1:4:8), 0.199 g CO2/g DES (1:5:8), and 0.181 g CO2/g DES (1:6:8). This was because the DEA molecules that were freed from the hydrogen bond networks of Be···EG···DEA in the Be–EG–DEA system reduced the viscosity of the system, which decreased the mass-transfer resistance during absorption and enhanced the capture rate.27,28 In addition, free DEA molecules possess the ability to capture CO2; hence, the absorption mechanism of the ternary Be-based DESs for CO2 involves the synergistic effect of physical and chemical absorptions. Thus, the ternary systems exhibited an increased CO2 capture capacity compared to that of the binary system.

Figure 5c shows the relationship between the CO2 capture capacity and the temperature of the Be–EG–DEA (1:5:8) system. This system exhibited a high CO2 capture capacity at 30 °C, with an absorption capacity of 0.199 g CO2/g DES. After passing CO2 gas for 30 min and increasing the capture temperature to 40, 50, and 60 °C, the CO2 absorption capacity of the system began to decrease. This was because an increase in the temperature reduces the viscosity of the ternary Be-based DESs, resulting in decreased mass-transfer resistance, which allows CO2 to enter the ternary Be-based DESs more easily.29,30 However, higher temperatures increase the kinetic energy of the gas molecules, and collisions between CO2 molecules within the ternary Be-based DESs become more intense, making it easier for CO2 molecules to escape from the ternary Be-based DESs.31

Figure 5d shows that the gas flow rate markedly affects the absorption of CO2 by the ternary Be-based DES. Peaked CO2 absorption rate and the greatest absorption capacity occurred at a gas flow rate of 50 mL/min. This is because as the gas flow rate increases, the amount of CO2 entering the ternary Be-based DESs system per unit of time increases. With the increase in the gas flow rate, the mass-transfer efficiency of CO2 in the ternary Be-based DESs also increases, and the time required for the absorption to reach equilibrium is shortened.

FT-IR Spectra of Betaine-Based DESs

Figure 6 shows the FT-IR spectra of Be–EG DES, Be–EG–DEA DES, and their three pure components. As shown in Figure 6a, the strong vibration of pure Be at 1631 cm–1 results from the asymmetric stretching vibration of the COO– group, and the vibration band at 714 cm–1 results from the symmetric deformation vibration of the H–C–N group. Pure EG is characterized by asymmetric and symmetric stretching C–H groups at adjacent 2935 and 2873 cm–1, respectively, and the bending vibration of the −COH group at 1430 cm–1. As shown in Figure 6b, the vibration band of pure DEA at 1470 cm–1 represents the bending vibration of secondary amine N–H, and the vibration bands at 1357 and 936 cm–1 represent C–N and C–C stretching.

Spectral diagrams of betaine-based DESs: (a) FT-IR spectrum of Be–EG and (b) FT-IR spectrum of Be–EG–DEA.

Figure 6a also depicts the binary Be-based DES formed with a Be:EG molar ratio of 1:5. All of the functional groups of the two pure components can be identified. Vibration band broadening at 3200–3500 cm–1 indicates hydrogen bond formation after binary Be-based DES generation.32Figure 6b also depicts the ternary Be-based DES formed with a Be:EG:DEA molar ratio of 1:5:8. The FT-IR spectrum results confirm ternary Be-based DES formation in which all functional groups of the three pure components can be identified in the reported spectrum. Vibration band broadening at 3200–3500 cm–1 indicates hydrogen bond formation after ternary Be-based DES generation.32 The peaks at 1470 and 1631 cm–1 represent the presence of the −NH and −COO– groups.

After CO2 absorption, the infrared spectrum results of Be–EG indicated that no new functional groups appeared, and no chemical reaction occurred between Be–EG and CO2, indicating that CO2 absorption by Be–EG is purely physical.

The structure of Be–EG–DEA underwent changes, and the infrared spectrum showed that Be–EG–DEA exhibited a bending vibration of C–H bonds at 1300 cm–1, suggesting a physical absorption process for CO2 absorption by Be–EG–DEA. In addition, a new characteristic peak appeared at 1540 cm–1, indicating the presence of new functional groups. This suggests that the absorption of CO2 by Be–EG–DEA involves chemical absorption, leading to carbamate formation. Therefore, ternary Be-based DESs and binary Be-based DESs exhibit disparate CO2 absorption mechanisms.

NMR Analysis of Betaine-Based DESs

The 13C NMR results shown in Figure 7a indicate that the peak at 52.6 ppm belongs to the −CH3 group of Be in the binary Be-based DES, while the peaks at 63.24 and 66.38 ppm correspond to −CH2OH and −NH2CH2, and the peak at 165.77 ppm corresponds to the −COOH group. In addition, the peaks at 51.39 and 60.66 ppm correspond to the −NHCH2 and −CH2OH groups of DEA in the ternary Be-based DES, respectively (Figure 7c).33 The 13H NMR results shown in Figure 7b reveal that the peaks at 3.12 and 3.36 ppm correspond to the −CH3 and NH2CH2 groups of Be in the binary Be-based DES, while the peak at 4.58 ppm belongs to the −CH2OH group of EG in the binary Be-based DES. In addition, the peak at 3.66 ppm belongs to the −NHCH2 group of DEA in the ternary Be-based DES (Figure 7d). Compared with the binary DES, no new peaks appear in the ternary DES, indicating that ternary Be-based DES formation is a physical process.

13C NMR analysis (a, c) and 13H NMR analysis (b, d) of betaine-based DESs.

After CO2 absorption, no new peaks appeared in the 13C and 13H NMR spectra of the Be–EG DES (Figure 7a), indicating no chemical reaction with CO2 and suggesting a physical absorption mechanism, which results in a lower absorption capacity. The characteristic peak of carbamate observed at 160.33 ppm in the 13C NMR spectrum of the Be–EG–DEA DES (Figure 7c) indicates a chemical absorption mechanism. A characteristic peak of carbamate was also observed at 5.48 ppm in the 13H NMR spectrum of the Be–EG–DEA DES (Figure 7d).34 This indicates that carbamate is formed after the absorption of CO2 by ternary Be-based DESs.

Therefore, by adding DEA to the binary Be-based DES, a ternary Be-based DES hydrogen bond network structure was successfully constructed, increasing the −NH– group. The CO2 absorption mechanism also changed from physical absorption to physicochemical synergistic absorption.

Effect of the Ternary Betaine-Based DES + Water System on CO2 Absorption

Water with a content of 10 and 20 wt % was added to the ternary betaine-based DESs to investigate the influence of the ternary Be-based DES + water systems on the CO2 absorption capacity.

As shown in Figure 8, the addition of water inhibited the absorption of CO2 by the ternary Be-based DESs, with the inhibition effect being proportional to the water content. This is because water destroys the hydrogen bond networks of the ternary Be-based DESs and reduces the concentration of DEA molecules that are freed from the hydrogen bond network of the Be···EG···DEA system in the ternary Be-based DESs. Water also interferes with the reaction of active sites of the ternary Be-based DESs with CO2.35,36 Therefore, water has a significant inhibitory effect on the absorption of CO2 by the ternary Be-based DESs containing secondary amines.

The Influence of Viscosity

Viscosity is a crucial factor influencing the capacity of the CO2 absorption of DESs. Figure 9a shows that the viscosity of the ternary Be-based DESs decreases with an increasing temperature. In particular, the viscosity decreases by approximately 76 and 86% from 30 to 60 °C before and after CO2 absorption, respectively. This phenomenon results from the weakening or breaking of hydrogen bonds in the Be···EG···DEA system due to the temperature increment. Figure 9b shows that before CO2 absorption, the viscosities of the ternary Be-based DESs with 10 and 20 wt % water contents are 88.256 and 46.528 mPa·s, respectively. After CO2 absorption, the viscosities increase to 291.9 and 100.3 mPa·s, respectively. Water addition reduces viscosity due to the disrupted hydrogen bond networks caused by water incorporation.37

Conclusions

In this study, ternary Be-based DESs containing DEA were prepared, and they exhibited excellent absorption performance for a molar ratio of 1:5:8 (Be:EG:DEA), an absorption temperature of 30 °C, and a gas flow rate of 50 mL/min, which reached 0.207 g CO2/g DES. Water content markedly affected the CO2 capture capacity of the ternary Be-based DESs, with CO2 absorption capacity decreasing with increasing water content. FT-IR and NMR analyses indicated that the binary Be-based DES underwent a physical adsorption process. After addition of the promoter DEA, the ternary Be-based DESs underwent physical–chemical synergistic adsorption, yielding carbamate. The ternary Be-based DESs exhibited cyclic regenerability and maintained a certain absorption capacity after five absorption–desorption cycles.
