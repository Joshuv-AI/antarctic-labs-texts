Outcomes of Laser Interstitial Thermal Therapy–Augmented Surgery in the Management of Subcortical Glioblastoma
Authors: Hsien-Chung Chen, Anudeep Yekula, Marcus W. Weimar, Eric T. Wong, Sasmit Sarangi, Heinrich Elinzano, Andrew S. Venteicher, Clark C. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:Subcortical glioblastomas present surgical challenges owing to their proximity to critical anatomy. In this study, we assessed the utility of laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) in the management of subcortical glioblastomas.METHODS:Clinical outcomes were evaluated in 80 consecutive patients with subcortical glioblastoma (isocitrate dehydrogenase wild-type, H3K27 nonmutant) treated surgically between 2017 and 2025. During this time period, LITT was increasingly incorporated into stereotactic needle biopsy (SNB) and surgical resection (SR) workflows, enabling comparisons between SNB vs SNB + LITT and between SR only vs planned subtotal resection (STR) + LITT.RESULTS:Among 46 treatments involving SNB, 24 underwent SNB only and 22 SNB + LITT. Lesion size was similar between these groups (1.72 ± 0.38 vs 1.92 ± 0.36 cm, P = .07), with comparable procedural morbidity (4.2% vs 4.5%, P = 1.0). In newly diagnosed glioblastoma, SNB + LITT was associated with improved overall survival compared with SNB alone (median 375 vs 162 days, P = .0012). Thirty-four patients underwent resections (18 SR, 16 STR + LITT). Lesion size was comparable between these cohorts (3.77 ± 0.68 vs 3.74 ± 0.83 cm, P = .9) as was the extent of cytoreduction (77.8% vs 93.8%, P = .34), but STR + LITT was associated with fewer permanent postoperative deficits (0% vs 27.8%, P = .047). Survival did not differ between STR + LITT and SR (median 368 vs 289 days, P = .43). Across all patients, postoperative motor deficits were associated with worse survival (112 vs 254 days, P < .001).CONCLUSION:Our results support incorporating LITT as a staged procedure into planned STR or in conjunction with SNB in the management of subcortical glioblastoma. Postoperative motor deficit is associated with poor survival outcomes.

Glioblastoma is the most common form of primary malignant brain tumor in adults.1 Once defined solely by histological features, glioblastoma is now understood to encompass biologically distinct entities defined by molecular features.2 Tumors harboring isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations3 or H3K27-altered gliomas4 are excluded from this category. Within molecularly defined glioblastomas, O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation is associated with improved survival and response to standard-of-care chemotherapy with temozolomide.5 As such, survival metrics should be interpreted in this molecular context.

Subcortical glioblastomas are glioblastomas located distant from the cerebral cortex. They have been subclassified based on their anatomic proximity to the thalamus or basal ganglia (Figure 1). Despite these anatomic distinctions, subcortical glioblastomas pose similar challenges that are addressed with broadly comparable strategies.6 Smaller subcortical glioblastomas are typically managed with stereotactic needle biopsies (SNB), with up to 10% surgical morbidity7; glioblastomas with mass effect are managed with surgical resection (SR), with 20% to 40% surgical morbidity.8 In general, survival outcomes for subcortical glioblastomas are worse than those involving the superficial cortex,9 with a median survival of <12 months.10,11

Laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) is a minimally invasive technique increasingly used in the management of subcortical glioblastomas.12 Guided by magnetic resonance (MR) thermometry, laser activation induces hyperthermic coagulative necrosis, selectively ablating the target tissue while preserving surrounding healthy structures.13 LITT and SNB demonstrate equivalent safety profiles, reflecting their procedural parallels.14 Moreover, LITT can be combined with SNB during the same procedure, without significant added risk.12 Notably, patients who have undergone both LITT and craniotomy favored LITT.15 Beyond its cytoreductive effects, LITT facilitates blood-brain barrier disruption16 and elicits a localized inflammatory response that may potentiate antitumor immunity.17

Maximal safe resection has been a central paradigm in surgical neuro-oncology.18 Recognizing the risks of aggressive resections in anatomically complex regions, contemporary practice increasingly supports a nuanced approach that balances the reduction of tumor burden with the preservation of neurological function.19 An accepted approach for a subset of skull base tumors involves safe, maximal subtotal resection (STR), followed by radiation.20 Building on this paradigm, we examined the role of LITT in subcortical glioblastomas to extend maximal safe cytoreduction. During the study period, LITT was increasingly adopted as a surgical adjunct to both SNB and STR. By 2021, the treatment preference for the senior author had evolved to the following: Large subcortical glioblastomas abutting the corticospinal tract, arcuate fasciculus, deep gray matter, and brainstem were treated with planned maximal STR, followed by LITT as a second-stage procedure (STR + LITT). We evaluated this experience to assess the adjunctive role of LITT for subcortical glioblastomas.

METHODS

Study Cohort

The retrospective study received institutional review board approval under IRB #816619 and STUDY00007479. Written informed consent for the surgical procedure was obtained from all patients by the senior author as part of standard clinical care. The study reviewed the consecutive experience of a single neurosurgeon (2017-2025). Inclusion criteria include the following: (1) age >18 years, (2) pathology diagnosis of glioblastoma, (3) MR finding of supratentorial tumor with epicenter in the deep gray matters (thalamus, caudate, lentiform nucleus, insula, or corpus callosum), and (4) underwent stereotactic needle biopsy (SNB), SNB + LITT, resection, or resection + LITT. Exclusion criteria include the following: (1) tumors harboring H3K27 or IDH mutation, (2) infratentorial tumors, (3) multifocal glioblastoma, (4) documentation of opting out for research, and (5) failure to complete standard-of-care concurrent chemoradiation treatment.

Before 2021, LITT was discussed when patients were explicitly referred for consideration of the procedure. As clinical experience grew, LITT was increasingly incorporated into surgical management strategies, particularly as an adjunct to SNB or as a second-stage procedure when STR was planned. By 2021, the treatment preference for the senior author had evolved to SNB + LITT and STR + LITT.

The following clinical information was collected: age (at presentation), sex, presenting symptoms, Karnofsky Performance Status, tumor size (maximal diameter), tumor location, treatment status (newly diagnosed vs recurrent glioblastoma), MGMT promoter status, surgical approaches, extent of cytoreduction, surgical morbidity, postoperative neurological condition, 30- and 90-day readmission, pre- and postoperative brain MRI, and overall survival (OS) (from the time of initial diagnosis).

Surgical Techniques, Medical Therapy, and Radiation Therapy

All resections were performed under general anesthesia using standard microdissection techniques, guided by either Stealth (Medtronic) or Brainlab (Brainlab) navigation systems, in conjunction with 5-aminolevulinic acid fluorescence. In recurrent cases where the previous surgical cavity provided a corridor to the lesion, this corridor was used (Figure 2A-2D). For newly diagnosed subcortical glioblastomas without such corridors, a trans-sulcal approach was taken (Figure 2E-2H). In general, STR was planned if the tumor was in proximity to the corticospinal tract, arcuate fasciculus, and deep gray matter. The LITT procedures were performed as previously described21 using either NeuroBlate (Monteris) or Visualase (Medtronic) within 2 weeks of the resection to avoid delay of subsequent concurrent chemoradiation therapy.

All patients received standard-of-care radiation therapy with concurrent and adjuvant temozolomide initiated within 6 weeks of surgery. At progression, salvage therapies included lomustine, temozolomide rechallenge, reirradiation, bevacizumab, and enrollment in a clinical trial. Therapeutic decisions were made on an individualized basis and were independent of the initial surgical approach.

Clinical and Imaging Collection

Postoperative imaging was performed on either a 1.5T or a 3T system within 48 hours of the procedure. Each postoperative MRI included T1-weighted, T2-weighted, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery, contrast-enhanced T1-weighted, and diffusion-weighted imaging sequences, with surveillance imaging at 3-month intervals thereafter. Patients with neurological decline or clinical concern were imaged earlier. The extent of cytoreduction is defined as follows. For SRs, the extent of cytoreduction is the extent of resection (based on response assessment in neuro-oncology criteria).22 For SNB + LITT or STR + LITT, the extent of cytoreduction is defined as the residual contrast-enhancing lesion after both procedures; the expected thin rim of “egg-shell” contrast enhancement post-LITT was not considered residual tumor in the assessment. All MR assessments were made by a board-certified neuroradiologist and the senior author (C.C.C.). The extent of cytoreduction was classified into gross total (GT), near-total (NT), and subtotal, defined as no evaluable residual, >90%, and <90% combined removal/ablation of the enhancing tumor, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, whereas continuous variables were summarized by mean ± SD, median with interquartile range (25th-75th percentile), and range (minimum to maximum). Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed or ordinal data. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. Survival data were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. OS was defined as the time from histopathological diagnosis to death. OS was analyzed using R (version 4.4.2) and RStudio (version 2025.09.0). Kaplan-Meier and log-rank tests were performed to assess statistical significance.

RESULTS

Overall Demographics

This study included 80 patients with glioblastoma, comprising 52 newly diagnosed and 28 recurrent cases. The mean ages in the SR, SNB, SNB + LITT, and STR + LITT groups were 55.8 ± 13.2, 58.7 ± 13.2, 59.4 ± 12.4, and 58.5 ± 12.0 years, respectively (P = .84). There was a slight predominance of men in each cohort [55.6% (SR), 58.3% (SNB), 54.5% (SNB + LITT), and 62.5% (STR + LITT)]. For patients who underwent a biopsy procedure (including SNB and SNB + LITT), headache and cognitive decline were the most frequent presenting symptoms. For patients who underwent SR (including SR or STR + LITT), hemiparesis was the most frequent presenting symptom. Baseline Karnofsky Performance Status was comparable across groups (median [IQR]: SNB 90 [80-95], SNB + LITT 80 [70-90], SR 90 [80-100], STR + LITT 90 [75-100]; P = .30), indicating no significant differences in preoperative performance status. Tumor Characteristics

Tumor locations were similar between LITT-treated and non–LITT-treated tumors (P = .64; Table 2). The maximum tumor diameter was substantially larger in the resection group relative to the biopsy group (3.76 ± 0.74 cm for SR and STR + LITT vs 1.81 ± 0.38 cm for SNB and SNB + LITT, P < .001). Within the biopsy group, the maximal diameter of the treated lesion was comparable between the LITT-treated and non–LITT-treated patients (1.92 ± 0.36 for SNB + LITT vs 1.72 ± 0.38 for SNB, P = .07). Similarly, the maximal diameter of the treated lesion was comparable between the LITT-treated and non–LITT-treated patients who underwent resections (3.77 ± 0.68 for SR vs 3.74 ± 0.83 for STR + LITT, P = .9). MGMT promoter methylation status was also similar between the LITT-treated and non–LITT-treated groups (42 and 38, respectively, P = .84).

For this cohort of 80 patients, there were 27 MGMT promoter–methylated glioblastomas and 53 MGMT promoter–unmethylated glioblastomas. Clinical Outcomes: SNB vs SNB + LITT

Procedural morbidity between SNB and SNB + LITT was comparable (Table 3). In the SNB cohort, 1 patient experienced worsened gait from a biopsy-site hematoma (4.2%). The mean duration of hospitalization was 1.83 ± 1.13 days, with 30- and 90-day readmissions of 16.7% and 20.8%. In the SNB + LITT group, 1 patient developed new aphasia (4.5%), a rate not statistically different from the SNB group (P = 1.0). Hospital stay (1.45 ± 0.60 days) and 30- and 90-day readmissions (4.5% and 9.1%) were likewise similar to the SNB group (P = .158, P = .35, and P = .42).

Comparing SR vs STR + LITT.

The proportion of newly diagnosed and recurrent glioblastoma patients was also similar between the SNB and SNB + LITT group (54.2% vs 68.2%, P = .38). A survival analysis comparing all SNB and SNB + LITT-treated patients revealed that SNB + LITT-treated patients exhibited improved survival compared with SNB-treated patients (mOS of 218 days [95% CI, 182-620] vs 192.5 days [95% CI, 162-223], P = .01; HR 0.41, 95% CI, 0.20-0.83, P = .013; Figure 3B). This survival difference was observed only in the newly diagnosed group, where SNB + LITT was associated with a mOS of 375 days (95% CI, 283-near total resection [NR]) relative to the 162 days (95% CI, 111-NR) for the SNB-alone group (P = .001; HR 0.24, 95% CI, 0.09-0.61, P = .003; Figure 3C).

Clinical Outcomes: Comparative Analysis of SR and STR + LITT

In the SR group, the extent of cytoreduction was 50% GT, 27.8% NT, and 22.2% ST. In the STR + LITT group, the extent of combined cytoreduction was 50% GT, 43.8% NT, and 6.3% ST. The extent of cytoreduction did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (P = .34, Table 3). Regarding procedural complications, there were 5 postoperative complications (5/18, or 27.8%) in the SR group, including 3 cases of hemiparesis, one case of hemianopsia, and one case of aphasia. There were no procedural complications in the STR + LITT group (0/16, or 0%). The difference in procedural complications between SR and STR + LITT-treated patients was statistically significant (P = .047).

The SR and STR + LITT groups showed similar mean lengths of hospital stay (4.67 ± 3.51 vs 4.26 ± 2.75 days, P = .706). The 30-day readmission rates were similar between the SR and STR + LITT cohorts (27.8% and 12.5%, respectively, P = .405). Finally, 90-day readmission rates were also comparable between the SR and STR + LITT cohorts (27.8% vs 6.3%, P = .18, Table 3).

The proportions of newly diagnosed and recurrent glioblastoma patients were similar between the SR and STR + LITT groups (13/5 vs 11/5, P = 1.0). In this context, we performed a comparative analysis of OS between these cohorts. This analysis showed no significant difference in mOS between SR and STR + LITT patients [mOS of 289 (95% CI, 166-468) vs 368 (95% CI, 278-NR), P = .43; HR 0.72, 95% CI, 0.31-1.64, P = .43; Figure 4A]. The comparability between survival was also seen in a survival analysis stratified by newly diagnosed vs recurrent subcortical glioblastomas (Figure 4B and 4C).

Permanent Neurological Deficit is Associated With Poor Survival

Postoperative neurological deficits have been linked to reduced survival for patients with malignant brain cancer.23 We hypothesized that a similar association exists for subcortical glioblastomas. We performed an analysis comparing the patients with and without postoperative deficit using the entire cohort of 80 patients. Consistent with our hypothesis, patients with postoperative motor deficits showed worse OS [112 (95% CI, 86-NR) vs 254 days (95% CI, 203-290); P < .001; HR 11.16, 95% CI, 2.93-42.43, P < .001] (Figure 4D).

DISCUSSION

Surgical management of subcortical glioblastomas remains challenging due to their proximity to eloquent white matter tracts and deep gray matter. This study presents a retrospective analysis of a single surgeon's experience integrating LITT into the treatment of subcortical glioblastoma. Before the incorporation of LITT, smaller tumors were managed with SNB, whereas larger lesions underwent trans-sulcal SR with intent for GT resection. Over the study period, treatment strategies evolved to SNB + LITT for smaller lesions and STR + LITT for larger tumors abutting the corticospinal tract, arcuate fasciculus, deep gray matter, and brainstem. By 2021, LITT had become routinely incorporated as an adjunctive treatment for subcortical glioblastomas.

For subcortical glioblastomas with mass effect, trans-sulcal resection was performed for all newly diagnosed tumors in this series. The extent of resection (50% gross total resection) and safety profile (27% surgical morbidity, 0% mortality) for this trans-sulcal series are comparable with those reported for transcortical approaches.19 The marked reduction in morbidity associated with planned STR followed by LITT (0%) compared with surgery with intent for GT resection (SR, 27.8%) supports the feasibility and safety of the former approach. The STR + LITT approach mitigates the risks associated with intraoperative localization uncertainty by intentionally leaving a small residual tumor as a margin of safety. Postoperative MRI then enables precise delineation of this residual tumor, facilitating targeted LITT. Importantly, the extent of cytoreduction and OS for these approaches were comparable, suggesting that STR + LITT warrants consideration. Of note, the development of postoperative motor deficits correlated with reduced OS (112 vs 254 days; P < .001; Figure 4D), emphasizing the “safe” in maximal safe resection.

A staged strategy incorporating STR followed by LITT presents distinct technical considerations. First, when a safe single-stage SR is feasible, it is preferred over a staged strategy. Second, resection of subcortical glioblastomas near eloquent structures requires millimeter-level accuracy, but intraoperative anatomic distortion and electric spread from subcortical stimulation during resection may compromise this accuracy. A STR + LITT approach can be considered in this context. If so, the initial surgical stage must achieve sufficient cytoreduction to maximize the likelihood of combined GT and to minimize the risk of malignant post-LITT edema.24 Finally, the dense, abnormal vascular architecture of glioblastomas increases the risk of hemorrhage.25 This study offers reassuring evidence that when meticulous hemostasis is achieved during the initial procedure, staged resection can be performed safely.18 For these staged procedures, strict postoperative blood pressure control and monitoring of coagulation status are warranted.26

Although LITT has seen rapid adoption in surgical neuro-oncology,27 data addressing its role as an adjunct to SNB remain sparse.28,29 In this series, the safety profile of SNB + LITT was comparable with SNB (∼4% complication rate). Regarding survival, newly diagnosed subcortical glioblastoma patients treated with SNB + LITT showed a longer mOS of 375 days than those treated with SNB (162 days; P = .001). These observations support SNB + LITT as a treatment for patients with newly diagnosed glioblastoma.30 That said, SNB + LITT conferred only modest survival gains, underscoring the need for combination strategies with synergistic potential.13

Historically, concerns have been raised about SNB due to limitations in tissue yield and sampling error, which have contributed to reported misdiagnosis rates.31-33 However, advances in MR interpretation and stereotactic techniques, including real-time MRI guidance34 and robotic-assisted biopsy systems,35,36 combined with integrated molecular diagnostics, have significantly enhanced diagnostic yield.37 In this experience, targeting of the contrast-enhancing region and systematic sampling at serial depths through a single trajectory consistently yielded sufficient tissue for definitive diagnosis and molecular analysis.

While progression-free survival is an important endpoint in neuro-oncology, it cannot be meaningfully assessed in LITT-treated patients because no validated radiographic criteria exist to characterize post-LITT tumor response. Postablation cavities exhibit dynamic, heterogeneous MRI changes driven by immune and inflammatory activity and alterations in the blood-brain barrier, some of which can resemble tumor progression. Currently, no consensus criteria exist to evaluate recurrence in this setting. As such, we relied on OS as the primary end point of this study. In this context, the finding that OS was superior in the LITT + SNB group compared with SNB alone among newly diagnosed patients is notable. This finding reinforces the peer-reviewed literature demonstrating that cytoreduction confers a survival benefit for glioblastoma patients. Although OS is suitable for newly diagnosed patients, it may introduce lead-time bias in recurrent cohorts. For instance, a potential benefit of LITT could be obscured if the intervention or diagnosis occurs later in this group than in patients who underwent SNB alone.

Limitations

To our knowledge, this study is the first to characterize survival patterns in subcortical glioblastoma through a molecular lens. By explicitly excluding IDH-mutant and H3K27M gliomas, we isolate the IDH-wildtype population and stratify survival analysis by MGMT promoter methylation status, enabling a focused evaluation of anatomically and biologically distinct subgroups. However, the study's retrospective, single-surgeon design introduces significant limitations in generalizability and potential selection bias. In addition, the extent of cytoreduction was determined qualitatively, despite independent reviews. While the temporal sequence of SR preceding STR + LITT introduces the possibility of a learning-curve effect, the comparable survival outcomes between these 2 groups suggest that this influence is unlikely. Furthermore, the small sample size restricts statistical power and precludes meaningful multivariate analysis, limiting the ability to adjust for confounding variables. Finally, the impact of salvage medical therapies was not evaluated in this study. However, the absence of a standardized salvage regimen suggests that these therapies perform comparably in this setting, so any differential impact on outcomes is likely minimal. Despite these limitations, our findings offer valuable insights into the role of LITT in subcortical glioblastoma management when combined with planned STR or SNB. Future prospective, multi-institutional studies with larger cohorts and robust statistical modeling are essential for validating and extending this paradigm.

CONCLUSIONS

This series highlights the potential role of LITT as an adjunct to SNB or as a staged addition to STR in the surgical management of subcortical glioblastomas. The observed correlation between postoperative motor deficits and worse survival reinforces the imperative to minimize surgical morbidity.
