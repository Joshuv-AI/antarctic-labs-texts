Waking-hour movement patterns and all-cause mortality: A multi-dimensional accelerometer analysis in the UK Biobank
Authors: Yutao Li, Weifeng Pan, Xiangyu Du, Hengguo Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the independent and joint associations of time spent at each physical activity intensity level during waking hours, fragmentation, and bout accumulation patterns with all-cause mortality.MethodsWe included 94,014 UK Biobank participants with valid wrist-worn accelerometer data. Waking-hour activity was characterized by light physical activity (LPA) time, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) time, activity fragmentation quantified by the active-to-sedentary transition probability (ASTP), and time accumulated in short (<5 min), medium (5-10 min), and long (≥10 min) bouts. Cox proportional hazards models, interpretable machine-learning analyses, and Gaussian mixture models were used.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 6.9 years, 4901 deaths occurred. Both LPA and MVPA were inversely associated with mortality, with a stronger association for MVPA (hazard ratio per 10 min/day: 0.99 for LPA and 0.96 for MVPA). Higher ASTP was associated with higher mortality risk (hazard ratio per 0.01 increase: 1.02). After adjustment for total activity time, greater time in short bouts was associated with lower mortality risk, whereas greater time in long bouts was associated with higher risk for both LPA and MVPA. Five composite activity patterns were identified; the pattern with high activity time, low fragmentation, and a moderate proportion of long bouts showed the lowest mortality risk.ConclusionBeyond total activity time, waking-hour fragmentation and bout accumulation patterns were independently associated with all-cause mortality. A balanced pattern with high activity time, low fragmentation, and a moderate proportion of long bouts was associated with the lowest mortality risk.Keywords: Physical activity, Bout accumulation, Activity fragmentation, All-cause mortality, UK Biobank

Introduction

Promoting physical activity is a central priority in global public health.1 Current guidelines recommend that adults accumulate at least 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) per week and increasingly acknowledge the independent health benefits of light physical activity (LPA).2,3 Notably, these guidelines emphasize total activity volume rather than how activity is accumulated across bouts; consistent with this shift, the 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour removed the previous recommendation that aerobic activity be accumulated in bouts of at least 10 min.4,5

In recent years, accelerometer-based studies have examined which patterns of physical activity are associated with more favorable health outcomes. With respect to bout accumulation, the evidence remains inconsistent. In the UK Biobank, Millard et al. reported no significant difference in all-cause mortality whether MVPA was accumulated in short (<15 min) or long (>40 min) bouts.6 Cai et al. found that sporadic MVPA was already associated with reduced mortality risk, with additional benefit from performing sustained bouts.7 Rowlands et al. further showed that the benefit of sustained accumulation may be modified by intensity, being more protective at moderate than at higher intensities.5 With respect to fragmentation, the active-to-sedentary transition probability (ASTP) characterizes activity continuity, with higher values indicating greater fragmentation.8 In Hispanic/Latino adults, Mediano et al. found that higher ASTP was associated with higher all-cause mortality after adjustment for total activity volume, suggesting that fragmentation may influence risk independently of volume.9

These studies approached the question from two perspectives—bout accumulation and fragmentation—both showing independent associations with mortality after accounting for total activity volume. However, total activity volume, bout structure, and the degree of fragmentation are not independent of one another. For example, a lower ASTP may itself reflect higher activity volume and longer bouts. Waking-hour physical activity is therefore best understood as the integrated expression of total volume, bout structure, and fragmentation, which are inherently intertwined and cannot be meaningfully considered in isolation. Accordingly, evaluating these three dimensions within a unified framework and examining their joint associations with mortality is of clear importance.

Moreover, prior research has largely relied on single-dimension, linear analytical frameworks (e.g., Cox models),10 limiting the detection of nonlinear associations and cross-dimensional interactions. In contrast, interpretable machine-learning methods can capture nonlinear dose-response relationships and interactions, and Gaussian mixture models can integrate these dimensions to identify composite activity patterns, providing a basis for more targeted recommendations.

Accordingly, using large-scale objectively measured accelerometer data from the UK Biobank, this study systematically addressed the following questions:

(1)the independent associations of total LPA and MVPA volume, activity fragmentation (ASTP), and activity accumulation across different bout lengths with all-cause mortality risk;

the independent associations of total LPA and MVPA volume, activity fragmentation (ASTP), and activity accumulation across different bout lengths with all-cause mortality risk;

(2)whether, after thorough adjustment for total activity volume, the associations of bout accumulation patterns with mortality risk differ between LPA and MVPA; and

whether, after thorough adjustment for total activity volume, the associations of bout accumulation patterns with mortality risk differ between LPA and MVPA; and

(3)whether composite activity patterns identified through interpretable machine-learning analyses and Gaussian mixture model clustering are associated with all-cause mortality risk.

whether composite activity patterns identified through interpretable machine-learning analyses and Gaussian mixture model clustering are associated with all-cause mortality risk.

We hypothesized that: (1) greater total physical activity volume would be associated with lower mortality risk, whereas greater activity fragmentation (higher ASTP) and a greater proportion of activity accumulated in longer bouts would be associated with higher mortality risk, independently of total volume; (2) after adjustment for total activity volume, the associations of bout accumulation patterns with mortality would differ between LPA and MVPA; and (3) composite activity patterns jointly characterized by total volume, fragmentation, and bout structure would be associated with mortality risk, with certain patterns showing lower risk than others.

Methods

Data extraction

UK Biobank is a prospective cohort study that recruited more than 500,000 participants aged 40 to 69 years at baseline between 2006 and 2010.11 The study received full ethical approval from the UK National Health Service (NHS) National Research Ethics Service (approval number: 16/NW/0274), and all participants provided written informed consent. Details of the recruitment procedures and measurement protocols are available on the UK Biobank website. Physical activity (PA) was assessed in approximately 100,000 participants using the Axivity AX3 (Axivity Ltd, Newcastle upon Tyne, UK) wrist-worn accelerometer, recording at 100 Hz continuously for up to 7 days.12 The median interval between baseline assessment and accelerometer measurement was 5.7 years (IQR: 4.9–6.5 years).

The following variables were extracted (Table 1, Table 2): age, sex, ethnicity (White/non-White), educational attainment, smoking status, alcohol intake frequency, self-rated health, walking pace, BMI, Townsend deprivation index,13 handgrip strength, history of diabetes, cancer, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, pulmonary disease, allergy/eczema/rhinitis, and season of accelerometer assessment. Measurements obtained closest in time to the accelerometer assessment were used whenever feasible. Most covariates remained broadly stable between baseline and follow-up visits, with the exception of unemployment and medication use.14 The cohort definition is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Beginning with 98,687 participants who had accelerometer data, we excluded 2753 with fewer than 3 valid wear days and 547 who died within 2 years of the accelerometer assessment; a further 1373 participants with missing data on any covariate were excluded. This complete-case approach yielded a final analytic sample of 94,014 participants; no imputation of missing covariate data was performed.

Accelerometer data

For each participant, acceleration was aggregated into 5-s epochs and expressed as the Euclidean Norm Minus One (ENMO), and data were processed using GGIR (version 3.3.1) following standard procedures12,15; all metrics were derived from waking-time data. Files were excluded for calibration failure, valid wear fewer than 3 days, or incomplete wear across the 24-h cycle.16 All accelerometer-derived exposure variables are presented in Table 2.

Daily time in movement behaviors

Daily time spent at each movement intensity category was derived from raw accelerometer data using GGIR: inactivity (IN), light-intensity activity (LIG), moderate-intensity activity (MOD), and vigorous-intensity activity (VIG), all expressed in minutes per day. LPA was defined as total daily LIG time, and MVPA as the sum of MOD and VIG time.

Activity fragmentation metrics

ASTP was defined as the reciprocal of mean activity bout duration (minutes), reflecting transition frequency from active to inactive states, with higher values indicating greater fragmentation.8 SATP was defined as the reciprocal of mean inactivity bout duration (minutes), reflecting interruption frequency of sedentary time.8 Mean bout durations for both activity and inactivity were also included in the analysis.

Activity accumulation patterns stratified by bout duration

Bouts of LPA and MVPA were identified using an 80% tolerance criterion, requiring at least 80% of bout time to meet the corresponding intensity threshold, and classified into three duration categories: short (<5 min), medium (5–10 min), and long (≥10 min). Total time accumulated within each category was expressed in minutes per day. The ≥10-min threshold reflects the traditional definition of a sustained activity bout in physical activity guidelines,4 while this categorization follows prior accelerometer-based studies of activity fragmentation and mortality that used the same classification,17 enabling characterization of activity across the full range from sporadic to sustained.

Outcome

Dates of death were ascertained through linkage of UK Biobank data with death registry records from NHS England (covering England and Wales) and the NHS Central Register for Scotland. Follow-up began at the accelerometer assessment visit and ended at the date of death or the censoring date (August 31, 2024), whichever came first.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by mortality status and are presented as mean (SD) for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables. Group differences were assessed using t tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, with Fisher's exact tests used when expected cell counts were <5. Associations of physical activity measures with all-cause mortality were examined using Cox proportional hazards models, with follow-up time as the timescale and death as the event. Each exposure was modeled separately, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Three progressively adjusted models were fitted: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity; Model 2 additionally adjusted for educational attainment, smoking status, alcohol intake frequency, BMI, and Townsend deprivation index; and Model 3 further adjusted for diabetes, cancer, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, pulmonary disease, self-rated health, maximal handgrip strength, walking pace, valid wear days, and season of assessment. Covariates were selected a priori as established or plausible confounders of the association between physical activity and mortality, on the basis of prior literature; all were measured at or before the baseline assessment. We recognize that some variables (e.g., walking pace, grip strength, BMI, cardiovascular disease, and self-rated health) may lie partly on the causal pathway; a directed acyclic graph illustrating these assumed relationships is provided in Supplementary Fig. S2. Models for fragmentation metrics were additionally adjusted for total activity time or total sedentary time, as appropriate, and models for LPA and MVPA variables stratified by bout duration were further adjusted for the corresponding total activity time. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals.

To address the compositional nature of waking-hour time, in which time spent in one bout category is necessarily offset by time in another, we additionally performed isotemporal substitution analyses using the standard modelling approach.18 For each intensity level (LPA and MVPA), total activity time and all but one bout component (short, <5 min; medium, 5–10 min; long, ≥10 min) were entered simultaneously into the fully adjusted model; under this parameterization, the coefficient of each included component estimates the effect of reallocating 10 min/day to that component from the omitted component while holding total activity time constant. The three bout components accounted for a median of 98.7% (LPA) and 95.8% (MVPA) of total activity time.

To examine potential nonlinear associations and interactions, we developed XGBoost-based survival models.19 Derived indicators (e.g., the proportion of LPA and MVPA accumulated in ≥10-min bouts, and total activity time) were combined with covariates in prespecified feature sets. Data were split into training and test sets (80:20), and hyperparameters were optimized by cross-validation (Supplementary Table S6). Model interpretation used SHAP values and joint risk surface plots.20, 21, 22 These analyses were intended to characterize predictive contributions and reveal nonlinear patterns and should not be interpreted causally.

To identify distinct physical activity patterns, we performed Gaussian mixture model clustering23 using total activity time (active total, LPA + MVPA), the proportion of total activity accumulated in bouts of ≥10 min, and ASTP. These variables were standardized before clustering. Models with 3, 4, and 5 clusters were fitted, and the optimal number of clusters was determined using lower Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values. Normalized entropy and the average posterior probability of class membership were additionally computed as descriptive indicators of classification certainty (Supplementary Table S1) but were not used for model selection. Participants were then assigned to clusters based on model predictions. Clusters were reordered by total activity time from low to high and descriptively labeled according to the standardized mean values of total activity time, the proportion of long-bout activity, and ASTP. Kaplan–Meier methods were used to estimate cumulative survival across the five pattern groups, with differences assessed by the log-rank test.

Results

A total of 94,014 participants were included, of whom 4901 died during follow-up. Compared with survivors, participants who died were older, more often male, had lower educational attainment, poorer self-rated health, slower walking pace, and higher BMI and Townsend deprivation index, as well as a higher prevalence of diabetes, cancer, hypertension, cardiovascular, and pulmonary disease (Table 1).

Compared with survivors, participants who died accumulated less daily LPA and MVPA and showed a more fragmented pattern, with higher ASTP, lower SATP, shorter mean activity bout duration, and longer mean inactivity bout duration. After stratification by bout length, those who died accumulated less LPA and MVPA in short and medium bouts; for ≥10-min bouts, MVPA was markedly lower among decedents, whereas LPA did not differ substantially (Table 2).

Cox regression results are shown in Table 3. After progressive adjustment, both LPA and MVPA were associated with lower all-cause mortality risk, with a stronger association for MVPA than for LPA (Table 3). Among fragmentation metrics, higher ASTP and longer mean inactivity bout duration were associated with higher mortality risk, whereas SATP and mean activity bout duration were not significantly associated with mortality after full adjustment. Although fragmentation and volume metrics were moderately intercorrelated, all variance inflation factors were <3, indicating no problematic collinearity (Supplementary Table S2). After adjustment for the corresponding total activity time, greater time in short (<5 min) bouts was associated with lower mortality risk, and greater time in long (≥10 min) bouts with higher risk, for both LPA and MVPA; for medium (5–10 min) bouts, the association was protective for LPA but not clear for MVPA (Table 3). Isotemporal substitution models were consistent with these findings: reallocating 10 min/day from longer to shorter bouts was associated with lower mortality risk (LPA and MVPA HR 0.89), and the reverse reallocation with higher risk (Supplementary Table S3).

In sensitivity analyses excluding participants with fewer than 4 years of follow-up (n = 93,169) or with any baseline chronic disease (n = 57,329), greater time in ≥10-min bouts remained associated with higher mortality risk, and this association was somewhat stronger in the disease-free subgroup (Supplementary Table S4). In contrast, the association for ASTP was attenuated to the null after excluding participants with chronic disease (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.99–1.02).

In the fully adjusted models, most exposures showed no clear violation of the proportional hazards assumption (Supplementary Table S5). LPA total and LPA in ≥10-min bouts showed only mild time dependence on inspection of the Schoenfeld residual plots, whereas MVPA total, ASTP, and mean inactivity bout duration showed no obvious time trend (Supplementary Fig. S3).

The machine-learning analyses are shown in Fig. 1 (model parameters in Supplementary Table S6). MVPA time showed a nonlinear inverse association with predicted mortality risk, declining steeply before plateauing (Fig. 1A), with a joint risk surface suggesting interaction between MVPA time and its accumulation pattern (Fig. 1B). LPA time showed a decrease-then-increase pattern (Fig. 1C), and a greater proportion of LPA in ≥10-min bouts was associated with higher predicted risk (Fig. 1D). Greater total activity time was associated with lower predicted risk, plateauing at higher levels, whereas higher ASTP was associated with higher predicted risk (Fig. 1E and F). These patterns were broadly consistent across levels of fragmentation, with no consistent monotonic trend for the proportion of ≥10-min bouts (Supplementary Fig. S4–S6).

Discussion

Using data from the large prospective UK Biobank cohort, this study evaluated the independent and joint associations of physical activity time, activity fragmentation, and bout accumulation patterns with all-cause mortality risk, and further identified composite physical activity patterns through interpretable machine-learning analyses and Gaussian mixture model clustering.

The main findings were as follows. First, both LPA and MVPA were associated with lower mortality, more strongly for MVPA. Second, greater fragmentation (higher ASTP) was independently associated with higher mortality. Third, at fixed total activity time, short bouts were associated with lower and long bouts with higher risk, for both LPA and MVPA. Finally, clustering identified five patterns, with the lowest risk in a pattern of relatively high activity, low fragmentation, and a moderate long-bout proportion (Cluster 3).

Physical activity time and mortality risk

Both LPA and MVPA were associated with lower all-cause mortality, consistent with previous UK Biobank and cohort studies. The stronger association for MVPA suggests that intensity may confer benefits beyond activity time alone, consistent with Strain et al., who reported lower mortality with a higher proportion of MVPA at a given total energy expenditure.14 The joint risk surface further suggested some interaction, with higher MVPA associated with lower predicted risk at a given LPA level, indicating potentially complementary effects of different intensities (Supplementary Fig. S6).

The machine-learning models revealed nonlinear associations for both MVPA and LPA.24 Predicted risk declined steeply with MVPA before plateauing, consistent with previously reported L-shaped dose-response curves,25 whereas LPA followed a decrease-then-increase pattern. The latter may partly reflect reverse causation, as individuals with poorer or subclinical health may reduce higher-intensity activity and spend more time in low-intensity activity, producing a combination of higher LPA and higher mortality; single time-point assessments are particularly susceptible to this bias.26 Although we excluded early deaths and adjusted for multiple health indicators, the median 5.7-year gap between baseline and accelerometer assessment means residual bias from subclinical disease cannot be excluded,26 and the LPA association at higher exposures should be interpreted with caution. In sensitivity analyses excluding early follow-up or baseline chronic disease, the long-bout association remained robust—and was stronger in the disease-free subgroup—whereas the ASTP association attenuated to non-significance, suggesting the latter may partly reflect underlying health status.

Activity fragmentation and mortality risk

Higher ASTP was associated with higher all-cause mortality independently of total activity volume, consistent with Mediano et al. in Hispanic/Latino adults.9 A longer mean inactivity bout duration was also associated with higher risk, supporting the possibility that a more sustained activity pattern may be beneficial. In contrast, SATP and mean activity bout duration were not significantly associated with mortality, suggesting that active-to-sedentary transitions (ASTP) may have greater predictive value than sedentary-to-active transitions (SATP). This difference may reflect both biological and methodological factors. Biologically, ASTP reflects the inability to sustain activity once initiated, closely linked to fatigability, reduced physiological reserve, and declining functional capacity—characteristics strongly associated with mortality in ageing populations. SATP instead captures the propensity to initiate activity, which is more shaped by behavioural and environmental factors less directly tied to physiological decline. Methodologically, because participants accumulated far more sedentary than active time, inactivity bouts were longer and more variable, potentially rendering SATP a less sensitive marker. The machine-learning analyses were consistent with these findings: at a given total activity time, more fragmented patterns showed higher predicted risk for both MVPA and LPA (Supplementary Fig. S4), and fragmentation appeared to interact with bout structure—at similar long-bout levels, lower ASTP was associated with lower predicted risk (Supplementary Fig. S5). Together, these suggest that fragmentation and bout structure may interact rather than simply add.

Bout accumulation patterns and mortality risk

After accounting for total LPA or MVPA time, greater time in short bouts (<5 min) was associated with lower mortality, and greater time in long bouts (≥10 min) with higher risk, indicating that how activity is distributed across bouts may be independently associated with mortality even at fixed total time. Previous studies have generally reported broadly similar benefits of short and long bouts, or protective effects of brief, sporadic activity.6,27 Consistent with this, current guidelines have removed the former ≥10-min minimum-bout requirement and emphasize that activity of any duration contributes to health,4 aligned with our protective association for shorter bouts. Our results do not contradict this guidance but refine it, indicating that at fixed total volume, the proportional allocation of activity across bout durations may carry additional prognostic information. The apparent discrepancy with the few studies reporting adverse long-bout associations likely reflects methodological differences: we examined the proportional reallocation of activity at fixed total time and modeled nonlinear threshold patterns, rather than absolute bout time under linear frameworks.

Because waking-hour time is finite, time in one bout category is constrained by time in others. To account for this, we conducted isotemporal substitution analyses, which hold total activity time constant while reallocating time between bout categories. Their consistency with our primary findings—reallocating time from longer to shorter bouts was associated with lower risk—indicates that the bout-accumulation associations are not artefacts of the finite structure of waking time. We did not adopt a full compositional data analysis (CoDA) framework, as our primary question concerned reallocation between bout categories within a given intensity, which isotemporal substitution addresses directly; future studies using CoDA could offer complementary perspectives.

The machine-learning analyses were consistent with this in the predictive models: the proportion of long bouts (≥10 min) showed a non-monotonic association with predicted risk for both MVPA and LPA, rising mainly when this proportion was high (Supplementary Fig. S5). One hypothesis for this pattern is exercise compensation, although this was not directly examined and remains speculative. Individuals concentrating activity into a few longer bouts may be more sedentary for the rest of the day, offsetting exercise-related energy expenditure by reducing non-exercise activity28; consistent with this, intervention studies have reported decreases in non-exercise activity and thermogenesis after structured exercise.29,30 However, because we did not directly assess sedentary or non-exercise activity in relation to bout patterns, this should be regarded as a hypothesis for future research rather than an established mechanism. The joint risk surfaces further showed that predicted risk was lowest at an intermediate proportion of long bouts and higher at either extreme, for both MVPA and LPA, with risk rising most clearly at the highest long-bout proportions.

Composite physical activity patterns and mortality risk

Cluster 0 (lowest activity, highest fragmentation) had the highest mortality risk, whereas Cluster 3 (relatively high activity, low fragmentation, moderate long-bout proportion) had the lowest—lower even than Cluster 4, which had the highest activity and the highest long-bout proportion (Table 4). This pattern was confirmed in the fully adjusted Cox models (Table 5) and was consistent with the nonlinear pattern from the machine-learning analyses (Supplementary Fig. S4). Thus, although higher total activity is clearly important, a pattern overly concentrated in long bouts may be less favorable than one with a similar total but a more even distribution across bout durations.

Notably, Cluster 1 (lower activity, lower long-bout proportion) and Cluster 2 (moderate activity, higher long-bout proportion) showed similar risk reductions, suggesting that within the low-to-moderate range of total activity, the proportion of long bouts may have limited influence, whereas total activity time remains more important—consistent with the stronger SHAP contribution of MVPA in the machine-learning models.

Limitations and public health implications

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, UK Biobank participants tend to be healthier and socioeconomically more advantaged than the general population, and ethnic minority groups are underrepresented; therefore, the generalizability of these findings may be limited. Because the cohort comprised mainly middle-aged and older adults, our findings may apply less directly to younger populations, warranting investigation across a wider age range. Second, accelerometer data were collected at a single time point, making it difficult to capture long-term changes in physical activity patterns and leaving the analysis more susceptible to reverse causation than repeated measurements. Third, as an observational study, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Although we adjusted for chronic conditions and for self-rated health, grip strength, and walking pace as partial proxies for functional capacity, we lacked detailed data on disease severity and medication use. Moreover, some covariates may lie partly on the causal pathway, raising the possibility of over-adjustment; reassuringly, associations for bout accumulation and fragmentation were materially unchanged across Models 1–3. Fourth, the analyses were partly exploratory without correction for multiple comparisons, and the machine-learning results reflect predictive rather than causal contributions; findings should therefore be interpreted from the overall pattern rather than isolated associations. Fifth, we used a complete-case analysis without imputation of missing covariate data; although excluded participants constituted a small fraction of the cohort and were broadly comparable to those included (Supplementary Table S7), bias from data not being missing completely at random cannot be entirely excluded. Sixth, several measurement-related factors warrant caution: wrist-worn accelerometers cannot capture activities such as resistance training or cycling and provide no behavioral context; the choice of bout-duration thresholds may influence classification, though our isotemporal substitution analyses support the robustness of the findings; and the moderate classification certainty indicates that clusters should be interpreted as representative patterns along a continuum rather than discrete subgroups.

Notwithstanding these limitations, these findings may have public health relevance. In this observational cohort, given a similar total amount of activity time, a more even distribution of activity throughout the day, rather than its concentration into a few prolonged bouts, was associated with lower mortality risk. We emphasize, however, that these are observational associations and do not establish that redistributing activity would causally reduce mortality. This may be particularly relevant for middle-aged and older adults who find it difficult to set aside extended periods for structured exercise, and, if confirmed in future studies, may support consideration of shorter, more frequent episodes of physical activity as a complement to structured exercise.

In conclusion, after adjustment for total activity time, this study showed that both activity fragmentation (ASTP) and bout accumulation patterns were independently associated with all-cause mortality risk, and that both associations showed nonlinear features and potential interaction. Gaussian mixture modeling further indicated that a composite activity pattern characterized by relatively high total activity time, low fragmentation, and a more even distribution of activity across bout durations was associated with the lowest mortality risk. These findings suggest that how activity is distributed across the day, in addition to total activity time, may be relevant to mortality risk and could warrant consideration in future physical activity research and guideline development. This may help inform more targeted physical activity recommendations for different populations, particularly middle-aged and older adults who may find it difficult to engage in prolonged continuous exercise.
