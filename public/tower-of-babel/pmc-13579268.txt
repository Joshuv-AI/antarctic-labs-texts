Infected thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm with vertebral osteomyelitis caused by invasive pneumococcal disease successfully managed with rifampicin-soaked in situ graft replacement
Authors: Yoshifumi Itoda, Yutaka Hasegawa, Hiroyuki Morishita, Shuichi Okada, Yasuyuki Yamada, Joji Hoshino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvasive pneumococcal disease complicated by infected aortic aneurysm is exceedingly rare and frequently fatal. We report the case of a 60-year-old man who presented with a 5-month history of fever and back pain. Imaging revealed a ruptured infected thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm with L1 and L2 vertebral destruction. Both blood and tissue cultures yielded Streptococcus pneumoniae sensitive to penicillin G. Emergency in situ thoracoabdominal aortic replacement using a rifampicin-soaked four-branched prosthetic graft under cardiopulmonary bypass was performed successfully. Although cardiopulmonary bypass carries additional risks in a septic state, its use was considered unavoidable given the extent of aortic involvement and the need for selective visceral perfusion. Postoperative orthopedic consultation was obtained for management of vertebral osteomyelitis, including corset fabrication and supervised rehabilitation. Prolonged intravenous penicillin G for 6 weeks achieved microbiological eradication. At 3 months postoperatively, the patient remains under follow-up with no evidence of graft reinfection. This case underscores the importance of early cross-sectional imaging in patients with persistent fever and back pain, the diagnostic value of culture-positive confirmation, and the role of preventable risk factors including smoking and lack of pneumococcal vaccination.Keywords: Infected aortic aneurysm, Invasive pneumococcal disease, S. pneumoniae, Rifampicin-soaked graft, Vertebral osteomyelitis

Infected aortic aneurysm, also termed mycotic aneurysm, accounts for fewer than 1% of all aortic aneurysms yet carries a mortality rate of 16% to 44% despite surgical intervention.1 The most common causative organisms are Staphylococcus aureus and Salmonella species. S. pneumoniae is a distinctly uncommon pathogen in this context; a recent systematic review identified fewer than 100 reported cases of pneumococcal aortitis spanning seven decades.2 The insidious clinical presentation—mimicking musculoskeletal or degenerative spinal disease—frequently delays diagnosis and allows the disease to progress to an advanced, life-threatening stage.

Established risk factors for invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) include advanced age, smoking, immunosuppression, and lack of pneumococcal vaccination.3 Surgical management of infected aortic aneurysms remains challenging; in situ replacement with a rifampicin-soaked prosthetic graft is a recognized strategy when extra-anatomic bypass is technically unfeasible or when the extent of aortic involvement demands complex reconstruction.4 We report the case of infected thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm because of IPD complicated by vertebral osteomyelitis and psoas abscess, which was treated successfully at our institution. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of his case.

Case report

A 60-year-old man with no significant medical history was referred for evaluation of fever and lower back pain persisting for 5 months. He had worked as a meat processing worker for 40 years, had a 40-pack-year smoking history, and had never received pneumococcal vaccination. He had been managed conservatively with analgesics at a local clinic, but his condition deteriorated progressively; he became unable to walk and was nearly bedridden before seeking further care. Computed tomography (CT) at initial referral to our institution revealed an irregularly shaped saccular aneurysm of the abdominal aorta measuring 60 × 68 mm, extending from just distal to the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) to just proximal to the inferior mesenteric artery. The aneurysm demonstrated marked posterior protrusion with destructive changes of the L1 and L2 vertebral bodies, disc space narrowing, and posterior displacement of the vertebrae into the spinal canal without spinal cord compression (Fig 1). Given the morphological features and clinical course, an infected aortic aneurysm with impending rupture was diagnosed and emergency surgery was performed on the same day.

The aneurysm demonstrates marked posterior protrusion with destructive changes of the L1 and L2 vertebral bodies (arrowhead), disc space narrowing, and posterior displacement of the vertebrae into the spinal canal without spinal cord compression.

On presentation, blood pressure was 123/88 mm Hg, heart rate 98 beats/min, and temperature 37.6 °C. White blood cell count was 10,690/μL, and C-reactive protein was 9.17 mg/dL. Two sets of preoperative blood cultures yielded S. pneumoniae sensitive to penicillin G.

Under general anesthesia in the right lateral decubitus position with single-lung ventilation, the chest was entered through the left fifth intercostal space and the diaphragm divided to expose the retroperitoneal space and descending aorta. Marked inflammatory neovascularization and wall thickening distal to the celiac artery precluded safe dissection. Although this was classified as a type IV thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, the extensive inflammatory involvement and the need for complex multivessel reimplantation made it technically unsafe to proceed without cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Furthermore, the septic state of the patient raised concerns about systemic coagulopathy and organ hypoperfusion; we therefore accepted the inherent risks of CPB in a septic patient—including heightened systemic inflammatory response and coagulopathy—in favor of the controlled, perfused surgical field it provided. CPB was established via the right femoral vessels, and the aorta was clamped at the Th10 level after cooling to a core temperature of 30 °C. Aortotomy revealed posterior wall rupture with destroyed vertebral bodies and intervertebral disc material visible from within the aortic lumen (Fig 2). Distal hemorrhage was controlled with balloon tamponade, and selective visceral perfusion was initiated via cannulae in the celiac artery, SMA, and bilateral renal arteries. The distal aorta was transected at the inferior mesenteric artery level.

Intraoperative photograph showing the opened aortic aneurysm with posterior rupture. Destroyed vertebral bodies and intervertebral disc material (asterisk) are directly visible from within the aortic lumen, confirming direct communication between the aortic lumen and the lumbar spine.

A 22 mm four-branched J-graft (Japan Lifeline) was soaked in rifampicin solution (1.2 mg/mL; 600 mg dissolved in 500 mL of normal saline) for 10 minutes before implantation. The graft was anastomosed distally with continuous 4-0 Prolene sutures. Branch vessels were reimplanted as button anastomoses using 5-0 Prolene in the following order: right renal artery and SMA before proximal anastomosis at Th11, then celiac artery and left renal artery after clamp release. Necrotic tissue was thoroughly debrided and irrigated with 2000 mL of pulsatile saline. Omental wrapping was considered but not performed due to difficulty transposing the intraperitoneal omentum into the retroperitoneal space via the thoracoabdominal approach. Operative time was 5.5 hours with 500 mL blood loss. Intraoperative cultures of necrotic vertebral tissue also yielded S. pneumoniae, sensitive to penicillin G.

Postoperative CT confirmed patent graft with flow in all visceral branches and no contrast extravasation (Fig 3, A). Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging revealed residual abscess at L1 and L2 and in the left iliopsoas muscle without spinal cord compression (Fig 3, B). The patient was extubated on day 2 and transferred from the intensive care unit on day 7. Intravenous penicillin G (24 million units/day) was administered for 6 weeks, followed by oral amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily. Histopathological examination showed typical findings of acute bacterial aortitis (Fig 4). Blood cultures at 2 weeks postoperatively were negative, confirming microbiological eradication.

B, Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; T2-weighted) revealing erosion of the anterior aspects of the L1 and L2 vertebral bodies with residual abscess formation (asterisk), without spinal cord compression.

Histopathological examination of the resected aortic wall [hematoxylin and eosin stain (HE), ×12.5]. Dense neutrophilic infiltration and destruction of the media are consistent with acute bacterial aortitis.

On postoperative day 7, orthopedic surgery was formally consulted for management of the residual vertebral osteomyelitis. The orthopedic team performed clinical assessment of thepatient’s back pain, reviewed the postoperative imaging, and directed the fabrication of a custom spinal orthosis. Nonweight-bearing restrictions were maintained for 4 weeks, after which supervised weight-bearing rehabilitation was initiated under orthopedic guidance. Surgical spinal stabilization was not deemed necessary at this time, given the absence of spinal cord compression and the clinical response to antimicrobial therapy; however, the need for future spinal fixation will be reassessed based on follow-up imaging findings.

Oral amoxicillin will be continued until both normalization of C-reactive protein and complete resolution of vertebral abscess on serial magnetic resonance imaging are achieved, in accordance with published guidelines for pneumococcal deep-seated infection.5 At 3 months postoperatively, the patient remains under active follow-up with no clinical or laboratory evidence of graft reinfection or recurrent bacteremia. CT and laboratory testing are scheduled at 6 and 12 months postoperatively to assess graft integrity and resolution of the spinal infection.

Discussion

S. pneumoniae is a rarely reported cause of infected aortic aneurysm. Among the fewer than 100 published cases of pneumococcal aortitis,2 most series note that the abdominal aorta is the most commonly affected segment, and that many patients lack an identifiable primary infectious focus. The absence of classic signs of pneumococcal infection—pneumonia, meningitis, or bacteremia detected incidentally—in our patient likely contributed to the 5-month diagnostic delay. This trajectory is consistent with previous reports5 and underscores the importance of including infected aortic aneurysm in the differential diagnosis of any patient presenting with unexplained persistent back pain and fever, particularly when risk factors for IPD are present. Early cross-sectional imaging is essential; CT findings of a saccular, irregularly shaped aortic aneurysm with adjacent soft tissue changes or vertebral erosion should prompt urgent surgical evaluation.6

In contrast to many reported cases of pneumococcal aortitis in which blood and tissue cultures are negative, necessitating molecular confirmation by 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or lytA gene PCR,5 both preoperative blood cultures and intraoperative tissue cultures from the destroyed vertebral body yielded S. pneumoniae in the present case. Culture-positive confirmation allowed unambiguous diagnosis and targeted antimicrobial therapy with penicillin G without reliance on empirical broad-spectrum regimens. When blood cultures are negative in a patient with suspected infected aortic aneurysm, 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid PCR or lytA gene PCR applied to surgical tissue specimens should be considered.7

Our patient harbored several well-established and modifiable risk factors for IPD: a 40-pack-year smoking history, no previous pneumococcal vaccination, and likely chronic occupational exposure to biological agents in a meat-processing environment.3 Smoking impairs mucociliary clearance and alveolar macrophage function, significantly increasing susceptibility to pneumococcal infection.3 Both 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine and 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine have been demonstrated to reduce the incidence of IPD in adults,8 and vaccination coverage in at-risk populations remains suboptimal in many settings. This case illustrates the potentially fatal consequences of unaddressed preventable risk factors.

Surgical options for infected aortic aneurysm include extra-anatomic bypass and in situ reconstruction using rifampicin-soaked grafts, cryopreserved allografts, or autologous femoral veins.9 The present case involved a type IV thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, which can often be repaired without CPB. However, the severe inflammatory involvement of the periaortic tissues and the complex multivessel reimplantation required made it technically unsafe to proceed without the controlled operative field afforded by CPB. We recognize that CPB in a septic patient carries additional risks, including amplification of the systemic inflammatory response, coagulopathy, and end-organ injury. These risks were mitigated by limiting CPB time, maintaining adequate perfusion pressure, and using selective visceral cannulation. Rifampicin impregnation of the graft provides high local antibiotic concentrations against gram-positive organisms including S. pneumoniae,10 and early outcomes with rifampicin-soaked grafts in infected aortic reconstruction have been reported as acceptable.11 The absence of graft reinfection at 3 months postoperatively supports the validity of this combined approach.

Vertebral osteomyelitis as a complication of IPD-associated aortitis represents a particularly challenging aspect of management that requires multidisciplinary involvement. In our case, orthopedic surgery was consulted on postoperative day 7 and played a central role in directing spinal immobilization, rehabilitation, and ongoing surveillance. The decision to manage the vertebral involvement conservatively—without surgical spinal stabilization—was based on the absence of spinal cord compression and the favorable microbiological response to antimicrobial therapy. Whether spinal fixation will ultimately be required will be determined by serial imaging follow-up.

Conclusions

IPD-associated infected aortic aneurysm is a rare but potentially fatal condition that may be delayed in diagnosis due to its nonspecific presentation. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with persistent fever, back pain, and risk factors for IPD and prioritize early CT imaging. Culture-positive diagnosis enables targeted antimicrobial therapy and should be actively pursued. In cases where CPB is deemed necessary despite the septic state, a controlled operative strategy with selective visceral perfusion can achieve acceptable outcomes. In situ thoracoabdominal aortic replacement using a rifampicin-soaked prosthetic graft combined with prolonged penicillin G therapy and multidisciplinary orthopedic management is a feasible and effective treatment strategy. The presence of multiple preventable risk factors emphasizes the critical importance of pneumococcal vaccination and smoking cessation programs.
