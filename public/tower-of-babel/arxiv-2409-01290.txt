A strong large deviation principle for the empirical measure of random walks
Dirk Erhard, Tertuliano Franco, Joedson de Jesus Santana

Abstract
. In this article we show that the empirical measure of certain continuous time random walks satisfies a strong large deviation principle with respect to a topology introduced in [ 15 ] by Mukherjee and Varadhan. This topology is natural in models which exhibit an invariance with respect to spatial translations. Our result applies in particular to the case of simple random walk and complements the results obtained in [ 15 ] in which the large deviation principle has been established for the empirical measure of Brownian motion.

60F10, 60J27, 60B05

1.
Introduction

Let
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Markov process on a Polish space
(\Sigma,d)
. In the 1970’s, Donsker and Varadhan
[
8
, II]
showed that under suitable conditions the empirical measure defined by

L_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{X_{s}}\text{\rm d}s

satisfies a large deviation principle in the space of probability measures equipped with the weak topology given that
\Sigma
is
compact
. When the state space
\Sigma
is
not compact
, the upper bound holds for
compact
sets rather than
closed
sets. Meanwhile, the lower bound remains valid under a certain irreducibility assumption which we will detail further below.

In certain scenarios, this
weak
large deviation principle can be upgraded to a standard (or
strong
) one, thereby recovering the large deviation upper bound for all closed sets. However, such cases do not encompass many natural examples. To address the lack of compactness in applications, a confining drift may be added to the Markov chain (or diffusion)
[
11
]
, or it may be folded onto a large torus
[
2
,
4
,
10
]
. Folding the Markov process on a large torus is often sufficient when one aims for more crude information like the behaviour of a partition function. Yet, if one is interested in finer properties of the model at hand the above mentioned methods are not enough to account for the lack of a strong large deviation principle.

Recently, Mukherjee and Varadhan
[
15
]
introduced a new approach by embedding the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into a larger space equipped with a certain topology, rendering it a
compact
metric space. Under this new topology, they established a
strong
large deviation principle for the empirical measure of Brownian motion
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
, which was then successfully applied to the
Polaron Problem

[
3
,
13
,
15
]
. The compactification of measures has also proven fruitful in the context of directed polymers, as demonstrated in
[
1
,
5
]
. For an overview of Large Deviation Theory, readers may consult
[
6
,
7
]
, and for the role of topology in this theory, refer to
[
16
]
.

1.1.
Topology of Mukherjee and Varadhan

In this section we will explain the topology of Mukherjee and Varadhan. In the original article
[
15
]
the authors constructed a metric space
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
as an enlargement of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathds{R}^{d})
, the space of probability measures on
\mathds{R}^{d}
. Since we are interested in a discrete context our construction starts with the space of probability measures
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
on
\mathds{Z}^{d}
. The changes that need to be done to adapt from the original framework are minor and we outline those changes whenever necessary.

Denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}=\mathcal{M}_{1}\ /\!\sim
the quotient space of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
under the action of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
(as an additive group on
\mathcal{M}_{1}
).
For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
, its orbit is defined by
\widetilde{\mu}=\{\mu*\delta_{x}:x\in\mathds{R}^{d}\}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
.
For
k\geq 2
, we define
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
as the space of all functions
f:(\mathds{Z}^{d})^{k}\to\mathds{R}
that are diagonally translation invariant, i.e.,

f(u_{1}+x,\ldots,u_{k}+x)=f(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\,\qquad\forall\,x,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}\,,

and vanishing once getting away from the diagonal, i.e.,

\lim_{\max_{i\neq j}|u_{i}-u_{j}|\to\infty}f(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})=0\,.

For
k\geq 2
,
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
and
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\leq 1}
, we define
\Lambda(f,\mu)
, the integral of
f
with respect to a product measure of
k
copies of
\mu
, by

\Lambda(f,\mu):=\int f(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}\mu(\text{\rm d}u_{i})\,.

Due to the translation invariance of
f
, the above expression actually only depends on the orbit
\widetilde{\mu}
. The class of test functions that is of special importance is defined via

{\mathcal{F}}=\bigcup_{k\geq 2}{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\,.

As noted in
[
15
]
, there exists a countable dense set for each
{\mathcal{F}}_{r}
(under the uniform metric). Taking the union of all of these subsets we obtain a countable set, which we will write as

\big\{f_{r}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k_{r}}):r\in{\mathbb{N}}\big\}\,.

The desired compactification of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{\leq 1}
is the space
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
defined via

\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}:=\Big\{\xi=\{\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}\}_{i\in I}\,:\,\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{\leq 1}\,,\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}(\mathds{Z}^{d})\leq 1\Big\}

equipped with the metric

{\bf D}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):=\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{1}{2^{r}}\,\frac{1}{1+\|f_{r}\|_{\infty}}\,\Big|\sum_{\widetilde{\alpha}\in\xi_{1}}\Lambda(f_{r},\alpha)-\sum_{\widetilde{\alpha}\in\xi_{2}}\Lambda(f_{r},\alpha)\Big|\,.

Theorem 3.1 in
[
15
]
reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

{\bf D}
is a metric on
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
.

The second key result in
[
15
]
is its Theorem 3.2 which reads:

Theorem 1.2
.

The set of orbits
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
is dense in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. Furthermore, given any sequence
(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})_{n}
in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
, there is a subsequence that converges to a limit in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. Hence,
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
is a compactification of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
. It is also the completion under the metric
{\bf D}
of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
.

Theorem
1.1
follows from the same proof as in
[
15
]
, Theorem
1.2
however requires some small modifications that we will outline in Section
2.1
.
Before we close this section let us give an illustrative example.
Define a measure

\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{2}{{\mathcal{U}}}(\{n-1,n,n+1\})+\frac{1}{3}{\mathcal{U}}(\{-n-1,-n,-n+1\})+\frac{1}{6}{\mathcal{U}}(\{-n,-n+1,\ldots,n\})\,,

where
{\mathcal{U}}(A)
denotes the uniform distribution on the set
A
.
Then,
(\mu_{n})
clearly does not converge in the weak topology. However, one can show that its orbit
(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})
converges in
(\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}},{\bf D})
to

\xi=\Big\{\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{U}}_{3}(\cdot),\,\frac{1}{3}{\mathcal{U}}_{3}(\cdot)\Big\}\,.

Here
{\mathcal{U}}_{3}(\cdot)
denotes the orbit of the uniform distribution on a set of three consecutive numbers.
We note that the contribution of
\tfrac{1}{6}{\mathcal{U}}(\{-n,-n+1,\ldots,n\})
vanishes in the limit as
n\to\infty
.

1.2.
Main result

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Markov chain on
{\mathds{Z}}^{d}
with generator
{\mathcal{L}}
acting on functions
f:\mathds{Z}^{d}\to\mathds{R}
via

({\mathcal{L}}f)(x)=\sum_{y\in{\mathds{Z}}^{d}}a_{x,y}[f(y)-f(x)]\,.

(1.1)

We denote the domain of
{\mathcal{L}}
by
{\mathcal{D}}
and we assume that the rates
(a_{xy})_{x,y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}
satisfy the following six assumptions.

\rm{(A1)}

The process
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Feller process.

\rm{(A2)}

\sup_{x\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\sum_{y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}a_{x,y}<\infty\,.

\rm{(A3)}

For all
x,y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
there exist
n\in{\mathds{N}}
and a sequence of points
x=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=y
such that for all
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
we have that
a_{x_{i},x_{i+1}}>0
.

\rm{(A4)}

For all
c,x,y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
we have that
a_{x,y}=a_{x+c,y+c}
.

\rm{(A5)}

There exists a positive function
(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfying
\lim_{t\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{t}\log u_{t}=0
such that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log{\mathds{P}}\Big(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|X_{s}|\geq u_{t}\Big)=-\infty.

\rm{(A6)}

The rates are symmetric in the sense that for all
x,y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
one has that
a_{x,y}=a_{y,x}
.

The third condition of course simply states that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an irreducible Markov chain.
Next, we define the quantity of interest, namely, the
normalized occupation measure

(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined via

L_{t}(A)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{A}(X(s))\,\text{\rm d}s\,,\qquad A\subseteq\mathds{Z}^{d}\,.

(1.2)

Note that defined in this way, for each
t\geq 0
, the quantity
L_{t}
is a random probability measure on
\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
We recall that in
[
9
]
, under the assumptions
\rm{(A1)}
–
\rm{(A3)}
, it was shown that
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies a weak large deviation principle with respect to the usual weak convergence of probability measures with rate function
I
defined on
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
via

I(\mu)=-\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u>0\\
u\in{\mathcal{D}}\end{subarray}}\int\Big(\frac{{\mathcal{L}}u}{u}\Big)(x)\mu(\text{\rm d}x)=\big\|(-{\mathcal{L}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\mu}\big\|^{2}\ =\sum_{x,y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}a_{x,y}(\sqrt{\mu}(x)-\sqrt{\mu}(y))^{2}\,.

(1.3)

where the norm appearing above is on the space
\ell^{2}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
. The second equality is thanks to Assumption
(\textrm{A6})
and is a consequence of
[
8
, I, Theorem 5]
, while the last equality follows from a straightforward computation.
We note at this point that the above definition also makes sense for sub-probability measures
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\leq 1}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
and we will make use of that without further mentioning it.
We remark that it might not be obvious to check from
[
8
]
that Assumptions
(\textrm{A1})
–
(\textrm{A3})
are the correct assumptions to obtain the weak LDP. Therefore, in the forthcoming Subsection
2.2
we will show that our assumptions are indeed the correct ones.

To proceed we extend the rate function
I
to
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. We define
\widetilde{I}:\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}\to\mathds{R}
for
\xi\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
via

\widetilde{I}(\xi)=\sum_{\widetilde{\alpha}\in\xi}I(\alpha)\,,

(1.4)

where
\alpha
is an arbitrary representative of
\widetilde{\alpha}
. This makes sense because we will show in Lemma
2.5
that, as a consequence of Assumption (A4), the rate function
I
depends on
\mu
only through its orbit. Finally denote by
\widetilde{L}_{t}
the embedding of
L_{t}
into
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
and denote its law by
Q_{t}
.

Our main result then reads as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that Assumptions
\rm{(A1)}
–
\rm{(A6)}
hold. Then, the family of measures
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined on
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
satisfy a large deviation principle with rate function
\widetilde{I}
and rate
t
.

Before we come to the end of that section we will shortly discuss our assumptions. As mentioned above, Assumptions
\rm{(A1)}
–
\rm{(A3)}
will guarantee that the family of empirical measures satisfies a weak large deviation principle in the usual weak topology in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
. Note that
the topology of Mukherjee-Varadhan is applied to contexts in which the model at hand exhibits a shift invariance in space. In particular, the large deviation principle in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
can then be seen as an extension of the weak large deviation principle on
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
, and one expects the new large deviation rate function to be related to the one on
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
. It is therefore natural to consider models in which the rate function
I
governing the weak large deviation principle actually only depends on the orbit of the measure at hand. Our Assumption
\rm{(A4)}
guarantees exactly that. Regarding Assumption
\rm{(A5)}
it should be satisfied in almost all examples and simply states that it is very costly for the random walk to travel exponentially large distances. This will come in handy for a coarse graining argument that will be used to establish the large deviation upper bound. Finally, we believe that Theorem
1.3
should hold also without the Assumption
\rm{(A6)}
. However, without Assumption
\rm{(A6)}
the second equality in (
1.3
) does not hold which makes the analysis way more delicate.

2.
Proof

In this section we prove Theorems
1.3
. Recall that to establish a large deviation principle three properties need to be established. The lower semi-continuity of the rate function, the lower bound for open sets and the upper bound for closed sets. Our first task is to check that our hypothesis indeed imply the hypothesis of Donsker and Varadhan
[
9
]
, which in its hand imply a weak LDP, that is, a large deviations principle for compact sets in the usual topology of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
.

2.1.
Adaptation of the proof of Theorem
1.2
and auxiliary results

We start with the proof of Theorem
1.2
, which is an adaptation of the proof of
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
. It is divided in two steps:

Step 1.

\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
is dense in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
.

Step 2.
Any sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n}
has a subsequence that converges to some
\xi\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
.

We start with Step 1. Let
\xi\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
.
We are going to show that there exists a sequence of
(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})
of orbits in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
such that
\widetilde{\mu}_{n}
converges to
\xi
in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
.
Let
\xi=\{\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}:\,i\in I\}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. As argued in
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
we can assume that
\xi
is a finite collection of orbits of sub-probability measures. For any
i\in I
define
p_{i}=\alpha_{i}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
and choose spatial points
(a_{i})_{i\in I}=(a_{i}(n))_{i\in I}
such that
\inf_{i\neq j}|a_{i}-a_{j}|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Also, let
\nu_{n}
denote the uniform distribution of
[-n,n]^{d}\cap\mathds{Z}^{d}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{(2n+1)^{d}}\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }x\in[-n,n]^{d}\cap\mathds{Z}^{d}\,,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2.1)

We then note that for any
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\prod_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{n}(\text{\rm d}x_{i})=0\,.

Indeed, this is a consequence of the fact that
(\nu_{n})
totally disintegrates in the sense that, for any
r>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\nu_{n}(B(x,r))=0\,,

and Lemma 2.3 in
[
15
]
whose proof carries over
mutatis mutandis
to the present context.
We then define

\mu_{n}=\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}*\delta_{a_{i}}+\Big(1-\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\Big)\nu_{n}\,.

Since
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
vanishes whenever the distance of two coordinates tends to infinity and
\inf_{i\neq j}|a_{i}-a_{j}|\to\infty
we see that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{n}(\text{\rm d}x_{i})=\sum_{i\in I}\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\prod_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}(\text{\rm d}x_{i})\,.

This shows that the sequence of orbits
(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})
converges to
\xi
in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
, and therefore
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
is dense in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
.

The second step of the proof in
[
15
]
shows that every sequence
(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})
has a converging subsequence in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. The proof in the discrete context works without any major adaptations, relying fundamentally on the following lemma (
[
15
, Lemma 2.2]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of sub-probability measures that converges vaguely to a sub-probability measure
\alpha
, then each
\mu_{n}
can be written as
\mu_{n}=\alpha_{n}+\beta_{n}
. Here
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in the weak topology to
\alpha
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in the vague topology to zero. Moreover,
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
can be chosen to have disjoint supports and such that the support of each
\alpha_{n}
is compact.

The above formulation of Lemma
2.1
is a bit more general than in its original version in
[
15
]
but its statement follows directly from the proof of
[
15
, Lemma 2.2]
. We also refer to
[
12
]
where this was already noted.
Before we formulate the next result we will provide one more definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Two sequences
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of sub-probability measures on
\mathds{Z}^{d}
are widely separated if for any function
W:\mathds{Z}^{d}\to\mathds{R}
that vanishes at infinity

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int W(x-y)\,\alpha_{n}(\text{\rm d}x)\beta_{n}(\text{\rm d}y)=0\,.

We note that, apart of the discrete scenario, the above definition differs from the one given in
[
15
, Subsection 2.4]
, which requires
W
to be strictly positive. However, as one can see in
[
15
, Lemma 2.4]
, this condition of strictly positivity can be dropped, and our definition is equivalent to theirs.
Lemma
2.1
together with the proof of the second step in
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
then yields the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})
be a sequence in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
, converging to
\xi=\{\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}\}_{i\in I}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. Then there exists a sub-sequence which we also denote by
(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})
such that for any
k\leq|I|
we can write

\displaystyle\mu_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{n,i}+\beta_{n}

so that

•

(\alpha_{n,i})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}},i=1,\ldots,k,
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
are sequences of sub-probability measures in
\mathds{Z}^{d}
;

•

for each
i=1,\ldots,k
there are sequences
(a_{n,i})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of elements of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,i}\ast\delta_{a_{n,i}}\Rightarrow\alpha_{i}\in\widetilde{\alpha}_{i},\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty\,,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\min_{i\neq j}|a_{n,i}-a_{n,j}|=\infty\,,

and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is widely separated from each
(\alpha_{n,i})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
;

•

The supports of
\alpha_{n,1},\alpha_{n,2},\ldots,\beta_{n}
are all disjoint and for each
i
there exists a sequence
(R_{n,i})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
tending to infinity such that

\Supp(\alpha_{n,i})\subset B(-a_{n,i},R_{n,i})

and

\Supp(\beta_{n,i})\subset\big[\cup_{i}B(-a_{n,i},R_{n,i})]^{\complement}.

We finish this section with one more result which is a consequence of the proof in
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume that the sequence
(\widetilde{\alpha}_{n})
converges in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
to
\widetilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
. Then
(\widetilde{\alpha}_{n})
converges in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
to
\widetilde{\alpha}
.

The proof of this result is a consequence of the analysis of the case
q=p
in
[
15
, Proof of Theorem 3.2]
and it will be omitted here.

2.2.
Weak LDP

We start by recalling the assumptions in
[
9
]
, keeping the original notation.
Denote by
p(t,x,\text{\rm d}y)
the transition probability of the Markov process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
under consideration whose state space is a Polish space
X
. We will denote its law by
{\mathds{P}}_{x}
when started at
x
, and the corresponding expectation will be denoted by
{\mathds{E}}_{x}
. The following four assumptions were put in place:

\rm{(DV1)}

The transition probability
p(t,x,\text{\rm d}y)
is Feller, having a density
p(t,x,y)
with respect to a reference measure
\beta(\text{\rm d}y)
.

\rm{(DV2)}

Denote by
(T_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the semi-group of
X
. Define

\displaystyle B_{0}=\{f\in C(X):\lim_{t\to 0}\|T_{t}f-f\|=0\},

and

\displaystyle B_{00}=\{f\in C(X):\lim_{t\to 0}\sup_{x}{\mathds{E}}_{x}\big[|f(X_{t})-f(x)|\big]=0\}.

Moreover, for
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(X)
such that
I(\alpha)<\infty
,
k\in\mathds{N}
and fixed
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in B_{00}
define a neighbourhood
\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}
of
\alpha
in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(X)
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}=\Biggl\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(X):\bigg|\int_{X}f_{j}(x)[\mu(dx)-\alpha(dx)]\bigg|<\varepsilon,1\leq j\leq k\Biggr\}\,.

It was then assumed that
p(t,x,\text{\rm d}y)
is such that every neighboorhod of
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(X)
contains a neighborhood of the form
{\mathcal{N}}_{\alpha}
.

\rm{(DV3)}

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(X)
and any bounded measurable function
f
, there exists a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in B_{0}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
\|f_{n}\|\leq\|f\|
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and such that
f_{n}\to f
almost everywhere with respect to
\mu
.

\rm{(DV4)}

For every
x\in X,\sigma>0
and
E\subset X
such that
\beta(E)>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\sigma t)p(t,x,E)\text{\rm d}t>0\,,

where
p(t,x,E)
is defined via

p(t,x,E)={\mathds{P}}_{x}(X_{t}\in E)\,.

Below we argue why our assumptions
\rm{(A1)},\rm{(A2)}
and
\rm{(A3)}
indeed imply
\rm{(DV1)},\rm{(DV2)},\rm{(DV3)}
and
\rm{(DV4)}
:

\rm{(A1)}
implies
\rm{(DV1)}
:

The Feller property is clear by assumption and the reference measure
\beta
is in our case simply the counting measure since our state space is discrete. Hence,
\rm{(DV1)}
holds.

\rm{(A2)}
implies
\rm{(DV2)}
:

We note that the usual topology of weak convergence of probability measures is induced by testing against continuous, bounded functions. Therefore, it remains to argue that
B_{00}
coincides with
C_{b}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
in our case. Fix
f\in C_{b}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
, assume that
X_{0}=x
and define the first jump time via

T_{1}=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}\neq x\}\,.

Since
T_{1}
has an exponential distribution with parameter
\sum_{y}a_{xy}
we can estimate

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{x}\Big[|f(X_{t})-f(x)|\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{x}\Big[|f(X_{t})-f(x)|\mathds{1}_{\{T_{1}>t\}}\Big]+\mathds{E}_{x}\Big[|f(X_{t})-f(x)|\mathds{1}_{\{T_{1}\leq t\}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0+2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathds{P}_{x}(T_{1}\leq t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\|f\|_{\infty}\sum_{y}a_{xy}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\Big\{-s\sum_{y}a_{xy}\Big\}ds\,.

By
\rm{(A2)}
the latter goes to zero uniformly in
x
as
t\to 0
. Thus,
f\in B_{00}
and
\rm{(DV2)}
holds.

\rm{(A2)}
implies
\rm{(DV3)}
:

Since the state space
\mathds{Z}^{d}
is a discrete space, we have that every function
f:\mathds{Z}^{d}\to\mathds{R}
is continuous, and if this function is bounded then by the above arguments it clearly belongs to
B_{0}
. Thus, it is sufficient to take
f_{n}=f
, and
\rm{(DV3)}
follows.

\rm{(A3)}
implies
\rm{(DV4)}
:

This is a direct consequence of the fact that
\rm{(A3)}
simply states that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is irreducible.

2.3.
Lower bound

In this section we give the proof of the large deviation lower bound. More precisely, we will show the following result.

Proposition 2.4

(Lower bound)
.

For any open set
G\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{t}\log Q_{t}(G)\geq-\inf_{\xi\in G}\widetilde{I}(\xi).

(2.2)

For the proof we will need some properties of the rate function that are provided in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

The rate function
I
defined in (
1.3
) on the space
{\mathcal{M}}_{\leq 1}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
is translation invariant, i.e., for any
c\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
one has that
I(\mu)=I(\mu*\delta_{c})
, and it is homogeneous of degree 1, i.e., for any
\lambda\geq 0
one has that
I(\lambda\mu)=\lambda I(\mu)
, hence it is convex and consequently sub-additive.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\xi\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
with
\widetilde{I}(\xi)<\infty
. Then, there exists a sequence
(\xi_{n})
in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{I}(\xi_{n})\leq\widetilde{I}(\xi).

(2.3)

The proofs of the above lemmas are deferred to the end of that section. We now provide the proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Note that to show (
2.2
) it is enough to prove that, given
\xi\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
with
\widetilde{I}(\xi)<\infty
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{t}\log Q_{t}(U)\geq-\widetilde{I}(\xi)

(2.4)

for any
open
neighbourhood
U
of
\xi
.
First of all note that for any open neighbourhood
U
of
\xi
the set
U\cap\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
is open in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
. Indeed, given any
\theta\in U\cap\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and any sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
that converges to
\theta
with respect to the topology in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
it follows from Lemma
2.3
that
\xi_{n}
converges also with respect to the topology of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
to
\theta
. Thus, since
U
is open in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
eventually
\xi_{n}\in U
, and since it is a sequence in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
we have therefore that
\xi_{n}\in U\cap\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
provided that
n
is sufficiently large. Thus,
U\cap\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
is open in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
as claimed. Since
\widetilde{L}_{t}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
we have that

\mathds{P}(\widetilde{L}_{t}\in U)=\mathds{P}(\widetilde{L}_{t}\in U\cap\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}).

Define

\displaystyle q:\;

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}

\displaystyle u\mapsto q(u)=\widetilde{u}\,,

as the canonical map from
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
to
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
.
Note that
q
is a continuous mapping, so that
q^{-1}(U\cap\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1})
is an open set.
Now consider a sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as in the claim above. Then for all
n
sufficiently large we have that
\xi_{n}\in U\cap\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{1}
. For those
n
we can estimate

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathds{P}(\widetilde{L}_{t}\in U)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathds{P}(L_{t}\in q^{-1}(U\cap\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}))

\displaystyle\geq-I(\xi_{n})

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{I}(\xi_{n}).

Here, we used in the inequality the fact that
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the large deviation lower bound in the usual weak topology of probability measures together with the fact that by Lemma
2.5
the rate function
I
only depends on the orbit of the measure, and the last equality uses that
\xi_{n}
as an element of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
contains only one element. To conclude it suffices to send
n
to infinity.
∎

We now come to the proof of Lemma
2.5
.

Proof of Lemma
2.5
.

We first show that
I
is translation invariant.
We write, using Assumption
\rm{(A4)}
in the last equality

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}a_{x,y}(\sqrt{\mu}(x+c)-\sqrt{\mu}(y+c))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}a_{x-c,y-c}(\sqrt{\mu}(x)-\sqrt{\mu}(y))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}a_{x,y}(\sqrt{\mu}(x)-\sqrt{\mu}(y))^{2}\,.

Homogeneity, and convexity are direct consequences of the definition of
I
. The sub-additivity is then a immediate consequence of the homogeneity and the convexity. Hence, we can conclude.
∎

We now come to the proof of Lemma
2.6
.

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

By our analysis at the beginning of Section
2.1
the sequence
\widetilde{\mu}_{n}
approximating
\xi
in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
can be chosen to be of the form

\mu_{n}=\sum_{i\in J_{n}}\alpha_{i}*\delta_{a_{i}}+\bigg(1-\sum_{i\in J_{n}}p_{i}\bigg)\nu_{n}\,,

where
\nu_{n}
is defined in (
2.1
).
Here, given
\xi=\{\alpha_{i}:\,i\in I\}
as at the beginning of the proof we denote by
(J_{n})
a suitable chosen sequence of subsets of
I
that tend to
I
in the limit as
n
tends to infinity (note that in Section
2.1
the first step we detailed was for the case in which
I
was finite).
By Lemma
2.5
the rate function
I
is sub-additive on
\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}(\mathds{Z}^{d})
, thus

\displaystyle I(\mu_{n})

\displaystyle=I\Bigg(\sum_{i\in J_{n}}(\alpha_{i}*\delta_{a_{i}}))+\bigg(1-\sum_{i\in J_{n}}p_{i}\bigg)\nu_{n}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in J_{n}}I(\alpha_{i}*\delta_{a_{i}})+\bigg(1-\sum_{i\in J_{n}}p_{i}\bigg)I(\nu_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in J_{n}}I(\alpha_{i})+\bigg(1-\sum_{i\in J_{n}}p_{i}\bigg)I(\nu_{n})\,.

A straightforward computation shows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}I(\nu_{n})=0
.
Since, by the definition of
\widetilde{I}
in (
1.4
)

\sum_{i\in J_{n}}I(\alpha_{i})\leq\widetilde{I}(\xi)\,,

we can conclude.
∎

2.4.
Upper bound

Proposition 2.7

(Upper bound)
.

For any closed set
F
in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{t}\log Q_{t}(F)\leq-\inf_{\xi\in F}\widetilde{I}(\xi).

(2.5)

Before diving into the proof we present some definitions and observations that will be useful for our purposes.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be the space of functions of the form
u=c+v
where
v
is a smooth non-negative function with compact support on
\mathds{R}^{d}
and
c>0
is a constant. Let
\varphi
be a function satisfying
0\leq\varphi(x)\leq 1,\ \varphi(x)=1
inside the unit ball and
\varphi(x)=0
outside the ball of radius 2. For any
k\geq 1,R>0,u_{1},...,u_{k}\in\mathcal{U}
and
a_{1},...,a_{k}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
and
c>0
we consider the function

\displaystyle g(x)=g(k,R,c,a_{1},...,a_{k},x)=c+\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}(x+a_{i})\varphi\bigg(\dfrac{x+a_{i}}{R}\bigg)

(2.6)

and define
F:\Omega\to\mathds{R}
by setting

\displaystyle F(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},c,R,t,\omega)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\,:\\
\inf_{i\neq j}|a_{i}-a_{j}|\geq 4R\end{subarray}}\ \dfrac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}(g(X(s)))}{g(X(s))}\text{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\,:\\
\inf_{i\neq j}|a_{i}-a_{j}|\geq 4R\end{subarray}}\ \int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}(g(x))}{g(x)}L_{t}(\text{\rm d}x).

(2.7)

Since the last expression depends only on the image
\widetilde{L}_{t}
of
L_{t}
in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
, we can write

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k},c,R,\widetilde{L}_{t}):=F(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k},c,R,t,\omega).

We will prove first that
\widetilde{F}(\cdot)
grows only sub-exponentially as
t\to\infty
.

Lemma 2.8
.

For any
k\geq 1,R>0,\ u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in\mathcal{U}
and
c>0
,

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{t}\log\mathds{E}\{\exp\{t\widetilde{F}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},c,R,\widetilde{L}_{t})\}\}\leq 0.

(2.8)

Proof.

The proof proceeds in two steps. In the first step we show that the result follows if there were no supremum over
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}
in the definition of
\widetilde{F}
. To be more precise we will actually show that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\biggl\{\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}g)(X_{s})}{g(X_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\bigg\}\biggr\}

is bounded from above uniformly in the choice of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
By the Feynman-Kac formula, the function

\displaystyle\Psi(t,x)=\mathds{E}_{x}\bigg\{g(X_{t})\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}g)(X_{s})}{g(X_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\bigg\}\bigg\}

is a solution to the initial value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(t,x)=(\mathcal{L}\Psi)(t,x)-\dfrac{(\mathcal{L}g)(x)}{g(x)}\Psi(t,x)\,,\\
\Psi(0,x)=g(x).\end{array}\right.

Moreover, it is known that the above problem has a unique solution.
It is immediate that
\Psi(t,x)=g(x)
solves the above heat equation. Furthermore, by definition (
2.6
) we have that
g(x)\geq c.

Therefore, by the uniqueness of the solution

g(x)=\mathds{E}_{x}\bigg\{g(X_{t})\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}g)(X_{s})}{g(X_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\bigg\}\bigg\}\geq c\mathds{E}_{x}\bigg\{\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}g)(X_{s})}{g(X_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\bigg\}\bigg\}\,,

and consequently

\mathds{E}_{x}\bigg\{\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}g)(X_{s})}{g(X_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\bigg\}\bigg\}\leq\dfrac{g(x)}{c}.

(2.9)

Since
g
is uniformly bounded from above in
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
the claim follows.
We come to the second step of the proof, namely we will deal with the supremum over
(a_{1},...,a_{k})
inside the expectation. To that end we will use a coarse graining argument.
First note that if the range of the random walk in the time interval
[0,t]
is
r_{t}
, once any
|a_{i}|
exceeds
r_{t}+2R
it will no longer affect the value of
g
, since in this case we have that

|\tfrac{X_{s}+a_{i}}{R}|\geq 2

and
\varphi
is supported in the ball of radius 2.
Thus, we can limit each
a_{i}
to the ball of radius
r_{t}+2R.

By Assumption (A5) there exists a positive function
(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
\lim_{t\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{t}\log u_{t}=0
and a function
M(t)
tending to infinity as
t\to\infty
such that

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\big(\sup_{0<s\leq t}|X_{s}|\geq u_{t}\big)\leq\exp(-tM(t))

for all
t\geq 0
.
We will see that this will allow us to ignore the contributions of those trajectories satisfying
\sup_{0<s\leq t}|X_{s}|\geq u_{t}
. We can assume that
2R\leq u_{t}
, so that we can restrict the
a_{i}
’s to balls of radius
2u_{t}
.
Moreover, the function

\displaystyle\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}g)(x)}{g(x)}

is bounded from above by some constant
c_{2}
. This is a consequence Assumption (A2) and the fact that
g
is bounded from above and is bounded from below by
c
.
With the above considerations in mind we can now estimate

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\Bigg\{\exp\Bigg\{\underset{|a_{i}-a_{j}|\geq 4R\ \forall i\neq j}{{\sup_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}}}\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}g)(X_{s})}{g(X_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\Bigg\}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\Bigg\{\exp\Bigg\{\underset{|a_{i}-a_{j}|\geq 4R\ \forall i\neq j}{{\sup_{|a_{1}|\leq 2u_{t},\ldots,|a_{k}|\leq 2u_{t}}}}\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}g)(X_{s})}{g(X_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\Bigg\}\Bigg\}+\exp({c_{2}t})\mathds{P}\Big(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|X_{s}|\geq u_{t}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\Bigg\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathds{Z}^{d}\,\\
|a_{i}-a_{j}|\geq 4R\end{subarray}}\exp\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}g)(X_{s})}{g(X_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\Bigg\}\Bigg\}+\exp(c_{2}t-tM(t))

\displaystyle\leq(2u_{t})^{dk}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathds{Z}^{d}\,\\
|a_{i}-a_{j}|\geq 4R\end{subarray}}\mathds{E}\Bigg\{\exp\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}(g(X_{s}))}{g(X_{s})}\text{\rm d}s\Bigg\}\Bigg\}+\exp(c_{2}t-tM(t))\,.

Note that by the first step, the expectation above can be bounded by
g(k,R,c,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},x)/c
which actually is bounded uniformly in the choice of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
. Hence, taking the logarithm above, dividing by
t
, sending
t
to infinity shows that the left hand side in (
2.8
) is at most zero.

∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})
be a sequence in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
which converges to
\xi=\{\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}\}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. For any
k\in\mathds{N},i=1,\ldots,k
and
u_{i,R}(x)=u_{i}(x)\varphi\bigg(\dfrac{x}{R}\bigg)
, where
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{F}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},c,R,\widetilde{\mu}_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in\xi}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sup_{b\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\int\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R})(x)}{c+u_{i,R}(x)}\alpha_{i}(dx+b)

\displaystyle=:\widetilde{\Lambda}(\xi,R,c,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}).

(2.10)

Before diving into the proof of the above lemma we need to formulate a technical result, which will be useful in the proof of Lemma
2.9
and for the forthcoming results.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
u
be a non-negative function with compact support and
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
be a sequence of sub-probability measures such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{dist}(\Supp(\alpha_{n}),\Supp(u))=\infty\,,

(2.11)

where
\mathrm{dist}(A,B)
denotes the distance in the
\ell^{1}
-norm between two sets
A,B\subseteq\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
Under the above condition the following holds for all
c>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\frac{-({\mathcal{L}}u)(x)}{c+u(x)}\alpha_{n}(\text{\rm d}x)=0\,.

(2.12)

We first prove Lemma
2.9
accepting the validity of Lemma
2.10
. The proof of the latter will be given afterwards.

Proof.

Fix
b\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in\xi
. We will follow the second step in the proof of
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
. By Corollary
2.2
we can find
\ell\geq k
and a subsequence
\mu_{n_{k}}
that we will suppress from the notation such that we have the decomposition

\mu_{n_{k}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{n,j}+\beta_{n}

so that for a suitable choice of
a_{n_{i,j}}
that satisfy

\lim_{n\to\infty}|a_{n,i}-a_{n,j}|=\infty\ \ \text{for}\ i\neq j

one additionally has that

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,j}*\delta_{a_{n,j}+b}\to\alpha_{j}*\delta_{b}\,,\qquad\text{for }\qquad j\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}\,,

(2.13)

and such that the supports of the
\alpha_{n,j}
’s and
\beta_{n}
’s are all disjoint.

For
n
large enough we have that
|a_{n,i}-a_{n,j}|\geq 4R
so that the supports of the
\{u_{i,R}(\cdot-a_{n,i})\}_{i}
are mutually disjoint.
Now defining

\displaystyle\Supp(u_{i,R}(\cdot-a_{n,i}))=U_{i},\ \forall i=1,\ldots,k

and fixing
x\in\mathds{Z}^{d}

we have the two following cases:

•

There exist
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that
x\in U_{j}
and consequently
x\notin U_{i}\ \ \forall i\neq j
. In this case we have that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}g(k,R,c,a_{1},\ldots a_{k},x)}{g(k,R,c,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},x)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}.

•

\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
we have that
x\notin U_{j}
. Then,
u_{j,R}(x-a_{i})=0
for all
j
and in this case we have that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}g(k,R,c,a_{1},\ldots a_{k},x)}{g(k,R,c,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},x)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}.

Summing up the two contributions plus some elementary calculations yield that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}g(k,R,c,a_{1},\ldots a_{k},x)}{g(k,R,c,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},x)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in U_{j}\}}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}\mathds{1}_{\{x\notin\cup_{j=1}^{k}U_{j}\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in U_{j}\}}\Biggl\{\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}+\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}\Biggr\}

\displaystyle+\mathds{1}_{\{x\notin\cup_{j=1}^{k}U_{j}\}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in U_{j}\}}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in U_{j}\}}\dfrac{\mathcal{L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}.

For
x\in U_{j}
we have that

\begin{split}\int_{U_{j}}\bigg|\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}\bigg|\mu_{n}(dx)&=\int_{U_{j}}\bigg|\dfrac{\sum_{y\in U_{i}}a_{x,y}[u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})-u_{i,R}(y-a_{n,i})]}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}\bigg|\mu_{n}(dx)\\
&=\int_{U_{j}}\dfrac{\sum_{y\in U_{i}}a_{x,y}u_{i,R}(y-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}\mu_{n}(dx).\end{split}

(2.14)

Using that
u
is non-negative and bounded from above and that each
\mu_{n}
is a sub-probability measure we can estimate the above by some proportionality constant times

\sum_{y:\;|y-x|\geq d(U_{i},U_{j})}a_{x,y}\,.

(2.15)

The latter goes to zero as
n\to\infty
by
\rm{(A2)}
.
By the same argument, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{U_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}\mu_{n}(dx)

tends to zero as
n\to\infty
. Thus,

\begin{split}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}g(x)}{g(x)}\mu_{n}(dx)=&\liminf_{n\to\infty}\bigg(\int\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in U_{j}\}}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}\mu_{n}(dx)\\
&+\int\sum_{j=1}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}\mu_{n}(dx)\\
&+\int\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{\underset{i\neq j}{i=1}}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in U_{j}\}}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}\,\mu_{n}(dx)\,\Big)\,.\end{split}

(2.16)

This can be lower bounded by

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in U_{j}\}}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{k}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}\mu_{n}(dx)

\displaystyle+\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int\sum_{j=1}^{k}\dfrac{(\mathcal{-L}u_{j,R})(x-a_{n,j})}{c+u_{j,R}(x-a_{n,j})}\mu_{n}(dx)

(2.18)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int\sum_{\underset{i\neq j}{i=1}}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in U_{j}\}}\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}{c+u_{i,R}(x-a_{n,i})}\mu_{n}(dx)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int\dfrac{\mathcal{-L}u_{j,R}(x)}{c+u_{j,R}(x)}\alpha_{j}(dx)\,.

(2.20)

Here, we used the considerations after Equation (

Since this argument works for any collection

Lemma 2.12
.

If
\xi_{n}\to\xi
in

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{I}(\xi_{n})\geq\widetilde{I}(\xi).

Proof.

The proof of this lemma follows largely along the lines of the proof of Lemma

\displaystyle\widetilde{I}(\xi)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}R,c>0,\ k\in\mathds{N}\\

established in Lemma

Therefore, we fix
R,c>0
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and we define

as in the formulation of Lemma

.

Let
(\widetilde{\mu}_{n})
be a sequence in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
, converging to
\xi=\{\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}\}_{i\in I}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. We suppose that
\widetilde{I}(\mu_{n})\leq l

Initially consider the case where for each

consists of a single orbit, so that

Restricting to a subsequence if necessary, for some

3.
Applications of Theorem
1.3

In this section we present two applications of Theorem
1.3
.

3.1.
Application 1

We consider the same assumptions as in Section
1.2
and we define the function

V(x,y)=\mathds{1}_{\{x=y\}}

(3.1)

on
\mathds{Z}^{d}\times\mathds{Z}^{d}
. For any element
\xi\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
we then consider

\Lambda(V,\xi)=\sum_{\alpha\in\xi}\int V(x,y)\,\alpha(dx)\alpha(dy)\,,

(3.2)

which is continuous in
\xi
be the definition of the metric
D
on
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. Note that

\Lambda(V,L_{t})=\frac{1}{t^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\,ds\,\int_{0}^{t}\,du\,\mathds{1}_{\{X_{s}=X_{u}\}}\,,

(3.3)

which is simply the (rescaled by
t^{2}
) intersection local time of the random walk
X
.
To continue define a new rate function
I^{\prime}
on
{\mathbb{R}}
via

I^{\prime}(y)=\inf\{\widetilde{I}(\xi):\,\xi\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}},\Lambda(V,\xi)=y\}\,.

(3.4)

Theorem
1.3
together with the contraction principle then imply the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

The process of rescaled intersection local times
(\Lambda(V,L_{t}))_{t\geq 0}
satisfies a strong large deviation principle with rate function
I^{\prime}
and rate
t
.

3.2.
Application 2

Consider a bounded non-negative function
V\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
which is non-zero, taking its maximum in
(0,0)
such that
V(0,0)>\sum_{y}a_{0,y}
, and define

Z_{t}={\mathbb{E}}_{0}\Big[\exp\Big(t\int V(x,y)L_{t}(dx)L_{t}(dy)\Big)\Big]\,.

(3.5)

Remark 3.2
.

The definition of
Z_{t}
is somewhat related to the Polaron problem
[
11
]
which corresponds to the choice
d=3
,
V(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{|x-y|}
and where the underlying process is a Brownian motion instead of a random walk. We see that this function does not fall into the class of functions considered here. The reason is simply that this choice of function renders
Z_{t}
meaningless. One could possibly circumvent this by studying an appropriate choice of approximations of the potential of the Polaron problem. The drawback would be that this would translate to sequences of empirical measures that depend on an additional approximation parameter
N
. In this work we prefer to refrain from it.

It follows from Laplace-Varadhan’s
[
6
, Theorem 4.3.1]
Lemma that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log Z_{t}=\sup_{\xi\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}}\big\{\Lambda(V,\xi)-\widetilde{I}(\xi)\big\}=:\lambda\,.

(3.6)

We will analyze the variational formula in the sequel and will show that there is a maximizer and that this maximizer is a probability measure. We will start with the existence of the maximizer.
Consider a sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n}
in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\big\{\Lambda(V,\xi_{n})-\widetilde{I}(\xi_{n})\big\}=\lambda\,.

By the compactness of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
we can extract a subsequence of
(\xi_{n})_{n}
that converges to an element
\xi\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{X}}}
. For ease of notation we denote the subsequence again by
(\xi_{n})_{n}
. Since
\widetilde{I}
is lower-semicontinuous and
\xi\mapsto\Lambda(V,\xi)
is continuous we have that

\lambda=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big\{\Lambda(V,\xi_{n})-\widetilde{I}(\xi_{n})\big\}\leq\Lambda(V,\xi)-\widetilde{I}(\xi)\,,

from which we can conclude that
\xi
is a maximizer.
We next claim that the maximizer has only one component. To see that assume that the maximizer
\xi
has at least two components, denoted by
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
. Consider representants
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
such that
\int V(x,y)\alpha_{1}(dx)\,\alpha_{2}(dy)>0
. This is possible since
V
is non-zero. We then define
\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
and

\widetilde{\xi}=\{\alpha_{3},\xi\setminus\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}\}\,.

Then,

\displaystyle\Lambda(V,\alpha_{3})

\displaystyle=\int V(x,y)\alpha_{1}(dx)\,\alpha_{1}(dy)+\int V(x,y)\alpha_{2}(dx)\,\alpha_{2}(dy)+2\int V(x,y)\alpha_{1}(dx)\,\alpha_{2}(dy)

\displaystyle>\Lambda(V,\alpha_{1})+\Lambda(V,\alpha_{2})\,,

by the choice of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
.
By Lemma
2.5

I(\alpha_{3})\leq I(\alpha_{1})+I(\alpha_{2})\,.

From these two observation it follows that

\Lambda(V,\xi)-\widetilde{I}(\xi)<\Lambda(V,\tilde{\xi})-\widetilde{I}(\tilde{\xi})\,,

from which the claim follows.
We next show that any maximizer is a probability measure.
To that end assume that
\beta
is a sub-probability measure whose orbit maximizes the variational formula in (
3.6
). Define
p=\beta(\mathds{Z}^{d})\in(0,1)
and consider the probability measure
\mu=\tfrac{\beta}{p}
(it will follow from the arguments further below that
p>0
).
Then,

\Lambda(V,\mu)-I(\mu)=\frac{1}{p}\Big(\frac{\Lambda(V,\beta)}{p}-I(\beta)\Big)\geq\frac{1}{p}(\Lambda(V,\beta)-I(\beta))>\Lambda(V,\beta)-I(\beta)\,,

contradicting the assumption that
\beta
maximizes (
3.6
). Here we used that the supremum must be positive. Indeed, define

\tau_{1}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,X_{t}\neq 0\}\,,

which is the first jump time of the random walk
X
and has an exponential distribution with parameter
\sum_{y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}a_{0,y}
. It then follows that

Z_{t}\geq{\mathbb{E}}_{0}\Big[\exp\Big(t\int V(x,y)L_{t}(dx)L_{t}(dy)\Big)\mathds{1}_{\{\tau_{1}>t\}}\Big]\geq\exp\Bigg(t\Big(V(0,0)-\sum_{y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}a_{0,y}\Big)\Bigg)\,,

and the claim follows by the assumption that
V(0,0)>\sum_{y\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}a_{0,y}
.
The above analysis implies the following result:

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider a function
V:\mathds{Z}^{d}\times\mathds{Z}^{d}\to\mathds{R}_{+}
satisfying the above assumptions, then

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log{\mathbb{E}}_{0}\Big[\exp\Big(\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\,ds\,\int_{0}^{t}\,du\,V(X_{s}-X_{u})\Big)\Big]=\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathds{Z}^{d})}\big\{\Lambda(V,\xi)-I(\xi)\big\}\,.

The above variational formula has at least one maximizer. Moreover, if
\mu
is a maximizer, then for any
x\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
, the shifted measure
\mu_{x}:=\mu*\delta_{x}
is a maximizer as well.

Remark 3.4
.

It would be interesting to know under which conditions the above variational formula has a unique (unique modulo translations in space) maximizer. This however seems a delicate problem. Even in the case in which
\mathds{Z}^{d}
is replaced by
\mathds{R}^{d}
this is not a trivial issue, see
[
14
]
. We do not pursue this question here.
