ABSTRACT
Highlights•All trial patients receiving PBI included, with high protocol adherence.•Target coverage consistently met with minimal high-dose volumes.•Cardiac and lung doses met protocol constraints in all treated patients.•Prospective DICOM data enabled comprehensive, multicentre QA.•Data support safe implementation of PBI in routine clinical practice.Keywords: DBCG, QA, Quality Assurance, Omitting radiotherapy, Partial breast irradiation, De-escalation, Breast cancer

Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT) after breast-conserving surgery (BCS) reduces the risk of any first recurrence by about half, and breast cancer mortality by around one-sixth [1]. While these proportional benefits are consistent across patient groups, the absolute benefit varies by age, tumour characteristics, treatment factors, and time of follow-up [1]. Importantly, the risk of distant recurrence following local recurrence is age-dependent [2]. Consequently, considerations regarding de-escalation of RT have primarily been focused on elderly patients with favourable tumour characteristics. In this context, strategies such as partial breast irradiation (PBI) and omission of RT have been explored to reduce radiation exposure and treatment-related side-effects without compromising oncological safety.

PBI is based on the observation that most local recurrences occur near the tumour bed, allowing the RT to be centred on this area of the breast [3], [4]. Clinical trials such as UK IMPORT LOW and the Danish Breast Cancer Group (DBCG) PBI trial have compared WBI versus PBI and demonstrated comparable local control, with comparable or reduced radiation-induced side effects in patients with relatively low-risk breast cancer [5], [6]. ASTRO and ESTRO have published PBI guidelines, and PBI is increasingly used as standard treatment.

The complete omission of RT has focused on subgroups with minimal absolute benefit from RT. Accordingly, trials such as LUMINA, PRIME II, and DBCG RT Natural (NCT03646955) have assessed whether RT could be safely omitted in selected low-risk breast cancer patients [7], [8]. The ongoing T-REX trial extends this de-escalation strategy to clinically node-negative patients with one to two sentinel node macrometastases [9].

The DBCG RT Natural Trial enrolled selected elderly women operated with BCS for low-risk breast cancer. Data from the IMPORT-LOW and DBCG PBI trials estimated the 5-year risk of local recurrence with PBI at 0.5% [5], [6]. A meta-analysis on RT gain after BCS estimates that omission of RT increases this risk threefold to 1.5–2%, comparable to the 5-year risk of 2% for a new contralateral breast cancer [5], [10]. The DBCG RT Committee decided that a maximum 4% absolute risk of ipsilateral recurrence without RT was acceptable. The trial started accrual in September 2018. In November 2023, the study was paused after the first pre-planned interim analysis and subsequently permanently closed following a second analysis one year later.

Publication of RT quality assurance (QA) studies is essential, enabling assessment of protocol adherence, identification of inter-centre variation, and promotion of consistent standards [[11], [12], [13]]. In large multicentre randomised trials, systematic QA reporting should be integral to dissemination, supporting reproducibility and a more confident interpretation of clinical outcomes [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Here, we present the protocol-specified QA data for all patients treated with PBI in the DBCG RT Natural trial, providing an overview of target coverage, protocol compliance, and treatment planning consistency across participating centres.

Materials and Methods

Eligibility and trial design

Women aged ≥ 60 years operated with BCS for unilateral, unifocal, node-negative, non-lobular invasive breast cancer ≤ 20 mm (pT1), grade I–II, with oestrogen receptor (ER) expression ≥ 10%, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-normal status, and resection margin ≥ 2 mm were eligible. Minimal ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) involving < 20% of the tumour volume, but not at a distance from the tumour, was permitted. Axillary staging required pN0 status; isolated tumour cells were allowed, but micro metastases were not. Exclusion criteria included previous breast cancer or DCIS, known hereditary breast cancer, previous breast or chest wall RT, or non-epithelial tumours [19].

Eligible patients were randomised 1:1 to PBI (40 Gy in 15 fractions over 3 weeks) versus no RT. Patients who declined randomisation due to a preference against RT were able to enrol in a self-selected no-RT cohort. All patients were offered annual screening mammography for 10 years, with the first assessment also including an ultrasound at 12 months post-surgery [19].

Planning CT and delineation

Only patients randomised to PBI underwent a planning CT scan in the treatment position, encompassing the breasts, lungs, and heart. The slice thickness was required to be ≤ 3 mm, and daily setup reproducibility ≤ 5 mm.

All target volumes and organs of interest were delineated in accordance with the DBCG guidelines for PBI as implemented in the DBCG PBI trial, and consistent with the IMPORT LOW definitions [5], [6]. The tumour bed was identified using surgical clips, seroma, scar tissue, pre-operative imaging, and the surgical report, serving as a reference structure for defining the PBI clinical target volume (CTVp_PBI). Explicit delineation of the tumour bed as a separate volume was not required by the protocol. A 15 mm margin was added around the tumour bed and cropped inside the whole breast volume to create the CTVp_PBI, approximating the affected quadrant. To account for setup variations, breast swelling, and respiratory motion, a 5–8 mm margin was added to create the PTVp_PBI, cropped 5 mm from the skin. Where required, an additional cranial and/or caudal slide was delineated to account for partial volume effects.

The whole breast and organs of interest were contoured according to DBCG RT guidelines and current ESTRO consensus recommendations for adjuvant breast RT [20]. Respiratory gating followed centre-specific guidelines, predominantly for left-sided tumours, with substantial inter-centre variation: some centres did not use gating routinely, others applied it selectively based on tumour location, laterality, or patient factors, and some used it for all patients.

Treatment planning and protocol dose guidance

Treatment planning primarily used two tangential fields and a single isocentre to cover the PTVp_PBI with 95–105% of the prescribed dose. RT was delivered as 40 Gy in 15 fractions over 3 weeks. Dose guidance for organs of interest, were applied in accordance with the DBCG HYPO Trial. Importantly, the DBCG Natural RT Trial was initiated before RT QA results were compiled and analysed in the DBCG HYPO and PBI trials. Doses of 105–110% were allowed in < 2 cm3 of the total volume, preferably deep in the breast. The dose guidance for the heart was V35Gy ≤ 1% and V17Gy ≤ 5%, respectively. For the ipsilateral lung, the mean dose (MLD) was restricted to 16 Gy, and V17Gy ≤ 25% [19]. Less than 50% of the whole breast volume should receive the full prescribed dose, with reduced stringency for patients with small breast volumes. The use of wedges, electronic compensation, intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), and multi-leaf collimators (MLCs) was allowed.

Data collection and statistical analysis

All treatment plans were prospectively collected and uploaded to DcmCollab, a national dose-plan repository providing secure access to complete DICOM-RT data [19], [21]. In cases where the upload was not completed at the time of treatment, the prospectively generated plan was subsequently uploaded prior to the aggregated quality assurance analysis. The plans were evaluated post-treatment as part of the retrospective analysis. All dose metrics are reported in the delivered schedule of 40 Gy in 15 fractions without conversion to equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2). Clinical, pathological, and RT data were obtained from the national DBCG database and the protocol-specific research database.

Statistics

Patient dose–volume histograms were recalculated in DcmCollab from the original planned doses and exported for analysis in Stata 19.0 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Pearson’s chi-square test were applied for statistical comparisons, with p < 0.05 defined as the criterion for significance. The RTOG-type conformity index (RTOG_CI) was calculated as the ratio of the body volume receiving ≥ 95% of the dose to the PTVp_PBI V95% [22], [23]. Patients with missing delineations required for specific calculations were excluded from the relevant calculations.

Results

All 238 patients randomised to and receiving PBI were included in the analysis from 19 RT centres across five countries (Table 1, Table 3). No patients randomised to no PBI opted for PBI. The median age was 68 years (range 60–83), and the median RT duration, including non-working days, was 20 days (interquartile range (IQR), 19–21) (Table 1).

All treatment plans had delineated CTVp_PBI, PTVp_PBI, and ipsilateral lung volumes. The heart was delineated in 221 patients (93%); the 17 patients without heart contours all had right-sided tumours. The whole breast and tumour bed were delineated in 213 (89%) and 201 (84%) patients, respectively, and surgical clips were present in 216 (91%). Patients with an explicitly delineated tumour bed had a larger median CTVp_PBI volume compared with those without a delineated tumour bed (133 cm3 vs 97 cm3; p = 0.011).

Patients were generally treated using two tangential fields (3D conformal technique). In total, 7% were treated with or supplemented by IMRT or VMAT (four IMRT, four hybrid IMRT, one VMAT). At one centre, some plans included minor contributions from short “butterfly” VMAT arcs. Dose calculation algorithms varied across centres (Table 3); however, most plans were calculated using advanced algorithms, and only 8 (3%) used a pencil-beam dose calculation algorithm.

All target structures (CTVp_PBI, PTVp_PBI, and tumour bed) received a median mean dose close to the prescribed 40 Gy, and dose coverage was consistently high (Fig. 1C, Table 2). Three patients (1%) had CTVp_PBI V95% < 95%, with the lowest coverage at 90%. For the PTVp_PBI, 53 patients (22%) had V95% < 95%, with a median volume of 12.9 cm3 (IQR, 8.2–17.5) and 9 patients (4%) had PTVp_PBI V95% < 90% (Table 2).

The dose distribution was uniform across the tumour bed, CTVp_PBI, and PTVp_PBI, with a median V105% of 0%. PTVp_PBI dose above 105% was recorded for 67 patients (28%) with a median V105% of 1.1 cm3; 24 patients (10%) exceeded the protocol-specified V105% < 2 cm3 (Table 2). Among these patients, whole-breast delineations were available for 16 patients. No patients had volumes receiving > 110% of the prescribed dose (Table 2).

For the ipsilateral whole breast, the median V105% was 0%. Overall, 34 patients (16%) had V105% >2 cm3, including 18 in whom PTVp_PBI V105% remained < 2 cm3. Among those with whole-breast V105% >2 cm3, the median high-dose volume was 8 cm3. No correlation was observed between V105% and breast volume.

Dose conformity was assessed using the RTOG_CI. For the Body V95%/PTVp_PBI relative to the body, the median RTOG_CI was 2.0 (IQR, 1.7–2.4), indicating that the volume encompassed by the 95% isodose was approximately twice the size of the PTVp_PBI (Fig. 2). The median whole-breast V40Gy was 21.2% (IQR, 14.6–29.0), and the median V95% was 41.8% (IQR, 33.3–52.0) (Table 4).

The median ipsilateral lung volume was 1681 cm3 (IQR, 1322–2000). For left-sided cancers, median lung volumes were 2095 cm3 (IQR 1892–2379 cm3) with gating and 1452 cm3 (IQR 1189–1765 cm3) with free-breathing (FB). For right-sided cancers, the median volumes were 2542 cm3 (IQR 1853–2867 cm3) with gating and 1552 cm3 (IQR 1284–1764 cm3) with FB (Fig. 1B). Overall, gating was associated with a statistically significant increase in lung volume (p < 0.001). The MLD and V17Gy were comparable between the right and left-sided tumours. No significant differences in lung doses were observed between gated and FB for either laterality. No patients exceeded the dose guidance of MLD ≤ 16 Gy or V17Gy ≤ 25% (Table 4).

Patients with right-sided tumours had a lower median MHD compared with those with left-sided tumours (p < 0.001). Among patients with left-sided tumours, gating was associated with a non-significant lower median MHD compared to FB (Fig. 1D, Table 4). The median V17Gy and V35Gy for the heart was 0% (IQR, 0–0) across all patients, irrespective of laterality or gating status. Only 11 patients, all with left-sided tumours, had V17Gy > 0, with a median of 0.3% (IQR, 0.1–1,5%), and none exceeded 5% dose guidance (Table 4), and no patients had V35Gy > 0.

Discussion

PBI was delivered with high protocol adherence, achieving consistent target coverage and minimal high-dose volumes. Minor deviations were observed, mainly in PTVp_PBI coverage and V105–110% high-dose volumes. Doses to the heart and ipsilateral lung were low, with dose guidance for the heart and lung met in all patients. Overall, the QA findings confirm that PBI was delivered reliably and in accordance with the protocol-specified planning strategy.

QA findings from the DBCG RT Natural trial provide insight into planning and treatment quality. Target coverage was comparable or superior to that in comparable trials, with doses to organs of interest consistent with the American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) and the German Society for Radiooncology (DEGRO) PBI guidelines [17], [24], [25], [26]. Compared with the earlier DBCG PBI trial, modest reductions were observed in ipsilateral lung dose, V40Gy_whole breast, and MHD, while the remaining doses to organs of interest and target coverage remained similar. This improvement may reflect increased clinical experience obtained with the PBI technique since the DBCG PBI trial was conducted [6]. Respiratory gating did not lead to a clinically meaningful reduction in MHD, consistent with previous analyses from the DBCG PBI trial, supporting the DBCG RT committee decision to omit routine gating for left-sided PBI [27].

Although overall protocol adherence was high, 53 patients (22%) did not achieve the recommended 95% PTV_PBI dose coverage. Minor deviations may be clinically acceptable where strict adherence would increase exposure to adjacent normal tissues. This approach has also been reported in trials such as IMPORT HIGH, which applied a V90% criterion of the PTV outside the tumour bed [28]. Five patients had ipsilateral breast V40Gy > 50%, meaning that over half of the ipsilateral breast received the full prescribed dose, approaching − but not equivalent − to whole-breast irradiation (WBI), which would represent the clinical alternative to PBI. Importantly, these cases were managed and analysed as PBI and were not converted to WBI. This is consistent with clinical practice, as expanding the irradiated breast volume has been associated with an increased risk of breast induration [17]. While these plans may slightly increase doses to organs of interest and the risk of late effects, they are not expected to compromise oncological safety [6].

Importantly, even for dosimetric outliers, cardiac and pulmonary doses remained consistently low and within existing dose guidance. These findings indicate that current dose limits for organs of interest may be more permissive than necessary for PBI. Beyond demonstrating protocol adherence, the QA data provide insight into dosimetric levels consistently achievable in routine clinical practice. They also support the feasibility of adopting lower, more stringent dose guidance for organs of interest without compromising treatment robustness.

Large-scale RT QA offers the opportunity to generate valuable evidence on treatment outcomes and radiation-induced side effects. Future studies should aim to link RT QA metrics with clinical outcomes, including side effects and local control, to further elucidate dose–volume relationships and patient-specific susceptibility. In the present study, the number of events was too low to allow meaningful correlation between RT QA parameters and outcomes such as local recurrence; however, this remains highly relevant in larger cohorts where such analyses are feasible. Supporting this approach, data from the DBCG HYPO and PBI trials demonstrated that smoking, irradiated volume, and patient age influence the risk and severity of late effects. These findings highlight the importance of QA data for understanding dose–volume relationships and patient-specific susceptibility [17], [29]. In the present cohort of elderly patients, the median whole breast volume was larger than in the DBCG Skagen and HYPO trials, supporting the relevance of PBI in this population due to both more advanced age and increased treated volumes, which elevate the risk of late effects [14], [29].

This study has several strengths. First, all treatment plans, including those from non-Danish centres, were included in the QA analysis, exceeding the protocol requirement, which required submission of only a limited number of non-Danish plans for central review. Second, data were collected prospectively via DcmCollab, ensuring uniform and auditable documentation. Third, precise localisation of the tumour bed is essential for accurate delineation and relies on markers such as surgical clips, surgical notes, and postoperative imaging. In this cohort, surgical clips were present in 91% of patients, providing a reliable foundation for target definition. Fourth, the cohort characteristics closely match commonly applied eligibility criteria for low-risk breast cancer, supporting the generalisability [[5], [6], [26]]. When comparing QA between centres, differences in patient selection, treatment parameters (dose, fractionation, field design, delivery technique), and target definition should be considered. Tumour bed volume is particularly relevant for PBI, with median volumes in contemporary trials ranging from approximately 13 cm3 in IMPORT HIGH to 22 cm3 in DBCG HYPO trials [17], [28]. Smaller target volumes provide a dosimetric advantage, reducing doses to organs of interest and the risk of late effects, including breast induration [17]. Overall, these results indicate that PBI can be implemented safely and with consistently across multiple centres. As a result of the FAST-Forward trial, among others, many centres currently deliver PBI using a 26 Gy in 5 fractions schedule for node-negative breast cancer. In the present study, patients were treated with 40 Gy in 15 fractions. To provide an evidence-based foundation for adopting shorter fractionation schedules, the DBCG RT committee is planning a randomised controlled trial comparing the two regimens.

Certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, a small number of plans were missing delineations for selected structures, including the heart (7%, all rightsided), tumour bed (16%), and whole breast (11%). Target volume delineation was the responsibility of the treating oncologist, and plan approval implied acceptance of the delineated volumes. The absence of tumour bed contours largely reflected inter-centre differences in workflow: most centres delineated the tumour bed before generating the CTV_PBI, whereas others delineated the CTV_PBI directly, sometimes supported by auxiliary anatomical structures. Importantly, if the tumour bed could not be visualised, patients were treated with WBI in accordance with protocol. Overall, the extent of missing data was limited and unlikely to have influenced the overall QA results. Second, although the number of patients was relatively modest, the dataset is considered representative of routine clinical practice and sufficient to evaluate protocol adherence, target coverage, and planning consistency. Formal centre comparisons were not performed due to the small number of patients per site. Finally, this QA study assessed treatment planning rather than delivery; therefore, potential delivery-related deviations cannot be excluded [30]. Identified outliers involved limited dose deviations and are unlikely to translate into clinically measurable events. Clinical outcomes will be reported separately.

Conclusion

PBI in the DBCG RT Natural trial was delivered with high protocol adherence, achieving consistent target coverage and minimal high-dose volumes across multiple centres. Cardiac and ipsilateral lung doses remained uniformly low, and minor deviations in PTV coverage were limited. These QA data document that PBI can be implemented reliably in routine practice and provide benchmarks for achievable doses to organs of interest. Systematic, prospective QA is essential to ensure treatment consistency and to interpret trial outcomes with confidence.
