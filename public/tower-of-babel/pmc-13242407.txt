The invisible “cuneus vein”…does it even exist?
Authors: Goetz Benndorf
Published: 2026

Gurse et al. describe a previously unreported cortical venous structure—the “cuneus vein”—and propose techniques for its preservation during posterior interhemispheric approaches [4]. The study is based on a retrospective review of 303 surgical cases performed over a 20-year period, identifying the variant in 12 patients (4%). Because preservation of venous flow is crucial in neurosurgical practice, this is a clinically relevant topic [9].

While a key strength of this study is the extensive operative experience of the senior neurosurgeon and the beautifully illustrated, detailed intraoperative observations, which offer valuable practical surgical insights, the work also reveals several notable methodological limitations. The retrospective design introduces potential bias; the study is confined to a single operator and institution, reducing its generalizability. Additionally, the small number of cases somewhat weakens the conclusions.

The main drawback, however, is the inability to visualize the venous variant in preoperative MRI scans of 303 patients, including the 12 cases with positive intraoperative findings. The authors' explanation that the vessel is probably too small is unconvincing, as is the suggestion to assume its existence in cases with a “thinner than expected parieto-occipital vein (POV).” The question is: what is the “expected” thickness of a POV, a vessel that, like most superficial cortical veins, is highly variable itself?

The figures provided by the authors, which display volume renderings of superficial cortical veins in unfavorable projections to detect this particular vein, do not support their assumption.

No detailed information on the methodology and results of their retrospective MRI analysis is provided. That should incorporate the type of scanner, imaging protocols (sequences), and post-processing methods, all of which are likely to have changed over 20 years. It would also include findings such as the frequency of a normal or variant POV, its course as well as its diameter to define what would constitute an “expected thickness.” However, no such results or measurements are presented.

Why is all this relevant?

MRI technology has advanced significantly over the past 20 years, resulting in better image quality and more accurate diagnoses. In the 2000s, venous MRI was limited to 1.0 T and 1.5 T scanners, which had long scan times and moderate spatial resolution. The primary focus in venous studies was on dural sinuses to detect thrombus formation and monitor its resolution in patients with sinus venous thrombosis. Artifacts were common, and cortical veins were difficult to visualize with standard T1 sequences, 2D time-of-flight (TOF), or phase-contrast (PC) sequences. Limited computing power and post-processing tools hampered the imaging of small, slow-flow structures such as cortical veins, often reducing diagnostic confidence and necessitating cerebral angiography for confirmation [8].

In contrast, today, 3T systems have become standard equipment in most academic settings, offering higher resolution, faster acquisition times, reduced susceptibility to motion artifacts, and advanced post-processing techniques. Newer MRI sequences dedicated to venous imaging, including 3D contrast-enhanced MRV (CE-MRV), Susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), and Time-resolved (dynamic) MRV (4D MRV), have been developed and are widely utilized. Furthermore, advanced post-processing tools (MIP, MPR, AI assistance) have become available. These modern techniques enable visualization of particularly small, slow-flow vessels, such as cortical, pial, and medullary veins. 4D MRV even allows hemodynamic assessment, not just anatomical descriptions [6].

Unfortunately, the authors' figures show that none of the currently available advanced MR imaging techniques for cerebral venous anatomy were used or are discussed. Based on the authors’ surgical findings, the vessel is not only small but also has a trajectory that would prevent it from being reliably identifiable using the image post-processing mode and VRT projections shown by the authors. This raises concerns about the validity of the imaging part of this study.

Is it realistic to suggest that the future incorporation of 7T scanners into the standard of clinical care for preoperative MRI imaging will solve this problem?

Certainly, higher signal-to-noise and contrast-to-noise ratios improve spatial resolution and contrast, allowing for clearer visualization of smaller vessels, including small cortical veins [3]. However, in addition to likely limited availability at least in the near future, 7T MR imaging also presents several disadvantages, including increased susceptibility that can cause additional artifacts [2].

Furthermore, and most importantly, the future implementation of ultra-high-field MRI scanners should not be a substitute for inadequate utilization of current technologies or imaging methods to study cerebral venous anatomy.

In addition to advanced MRI techniques, alternative methods such as CT venography (CTV) have emerged as high-quality clinical tools for venous imaging. Therefore, whether or not the introduction of the described venous variant as a true novel anatomical structure is warranted, remains debatable. More validation using appropriate techniques in both, cadaveric studies [5] and clinical cerebral venous imaging may be prudent.
