Conjunctival myxoid stromal tumor: Case series of a rare neoplasm
Authors: Emma C Stenz, Lucy Evans, Mukul K Divatia, Kathryn P Scherpelz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report three cases of conjunctival stromal tumor (COST) with myxoid features and describe their microscopic characteristics and provide a review of the pertinent literature.ObservationThis case series consists of three patients who were found to have conjunctival growths consistent with conjunctival stomal tumors with myxoid features. Two patients underwent full excision without local recurrence, while one patient underwent local biopsy without post-procedure worsening of the lesion. All three cases demonstrated prominent myxoid stroma with spindle cell morphology and immunohistochemical staining with positive expression of CD34, and Factor XIIIa, and had negative immunoreactivity for S100 protein and smooth muscle antigen (SMA).Conclusion and importanceConjunctival myxoid stromal lesions represent a rare growth affecting the bulbar conjunctiva, with less than 30 described cases since initial recognition as a distinct entity in 2012. These lesions are histologically characterized by a spindle cell morphology with variably prominent myxoid stroma and immunohistochemical positivity for CD34, factor XIIIa, and vimentin. Complete excision is the definitive treatment for these tumors, however incomplete resection or local biopsy may be reasonable given the clinically indolent behavior of these lesions and lack of recurrence or locally invasive disease as documented in the literature. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold to consider systemic evaluation given possible syndromic associations with conjunctival myxomas (CM), a lesion with markedly overlapping microscopic and immunohistochemical features with COSTs. Further characterization of these rare lesions will allow for greater diagnostic clarity and guidance regarding optimal management.Keywords: Conjunctival, Stroma, Myxomatous, Tumor

Introduction

Conjunctival neoplasms arising from mesenchyme represent a rare class of lesions affecting the conjunctiva. Herwig et al.1 initially described a series of 4 patients with mesenchymal conjunctival stromal tumors (COSTs) in 2012 as distinct clinical entities from other conjunctival tumor categories, with <30 reported cases of COST reported since.2,3

Some controversy exists regarding whether COST represents a separate entity from conjunctival myxomas (CM) given a significant degree of overlap in their histologic, immunohistochemical (IHC) and clinical features. CMs are also rare conjunctival lesions derived from primitive mesenchyme with varied incidence rate estimates reported in the literature. A review of 2455 conjunctival lesions at a tertiary referral center with histopathologic confirmation diagnosed only four (0.16%) lesions as conjunctival myxoma.4 CM has known associations with Zollinger Ellison Syndrome (ZES), characterized by gastrin-producing neuroendocrine tumors, and Carney complex, an autosomal dominant disorder with systemic myxomas, hyperpigmentation of the skin, and endocrine overactivity.5 As such, an accurate diagnosis of these conjunctival lesions indicates whether further systemic evaluation is warranted.

This case series aims to provide in-depth description of three distinct cases of conjunctival stromal tumors with myxoid features and argues that there is a role for the intermediate term “conjunctival myxoid stromal tumor” (CMST) as previously proposed by Qin et al.6

Findings

Case 1

A 46-year-old female with past medical history of iron deficiency anemia and unremarkable past ocular history presented with an enlarging pale conjunctival lesion affecting the left eye for five years, with a one-year history of eye discomfort secondary to challenges closing the eye. Her initial best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/15. Examination was notable for 9 mm temporal cystic conjunctival lesion with mild regional injection without hyperpigmentation or telangiectatic vessels (Fig. 1A).

Case 2

A 45-year-old man with past medical history of pseudogout presented for evaluation of localized erythema of the temporal bulbar conjunctiva of the right eye which had been stable for two years. His past ocular history was notable for Demodex- and rosacea-associated blepharitis for which had been treated successfully with tea tree oil and oral doxycycline, as well as “episcleritis” of his right eye, which was attributed to rosacea conjunctivitis, but did not improve with topical steroids or oral nonsteroid anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs. He had already undergone extensive work-up with unremarkable autoimmune and infectious testing. Initial BCVA of the affected eye was 20/20. His initial eye exam was notable for temporal injection abutting the palpebral fissure without notable nodular components or feeder vessels associated with the lesion (Fig. 1D), and he was started on ketorolac drops and fluoromethalone ointment for one month.

Given his minimal response to anti-inflammatory treatments and concern for conjunctival lymphoma, he underwent incisional biopsy which was sent for flow cytometry and permanent sectioning. Flow cytometry was negative for any atypical or clonal B-cell, T-cell, or plasma cell populations. Histopathologic evaluation was notable for a minute specimen with a mesenchymal proliferation comprising spindled cells with few intranuclear pseudoinclusions, and multinucleated cells with prominent myxoid stroma (Fig. 1E and F). Significant nuclear pleomorphism, necrosis and atypical mitotic activity were absent. IHC testing demonstrated positive CD34 and factor XIIIa staining, variable CD68 staining, and negative pancytokeratin expression (Fig. 1L–Table 1). Given the small size of the biopsy and the overlap between the features of COST and CM, a comment described the difficulty of distinguishing between these entities. Lesional excision and next generation molecular sequencing was suggested to aid in diagnostic evaluation but were not ultimately performed. The option to pursue further complete excision and systemic evaluation to rule out Carney complex was additionally offered, but the patient deferred. At the one-month follow-up, the lesion was healing well with mild residual conjunctival injection without any interval enlargement.

Case 3

A 33-year-old man with unremarkable medical and ocular history presented with six months of right eye irritation secondary to a nodular lesion affecting the temporal bulbar conjunctiva, diagnosed as nodular episcleritis by an outside ophthalmologist (Fig. 1G). He was initially trialed on dexamethasone, neomycin and polymyxin B ointment for one month without improvement in size, after which he was referred to our institution. His initial BCVA of the affected eye was 20/25 and his exam was notable for a 12 mm × 12 mm non-pigmented lesion with a temporal feeder vessel affecting the inferotemporal conjunctiva.

Ultrasound biomicroscopy revealed possible scleral invasion concerning for malignancy, so he underwent conjunctival excision with cryotherapy and conjunctivoplasty with autografting one month later (Fig. 1H).

Histopathologic examination demonstrated benign bulbar conjunctival mucosa with a bland spindle cell stromal proliferation in a myxocollagenous stromal background with mild chronic lymphoplasmacytic inflammation (Fig. 1I and J). Extensive myxoid stromal change, prominent nuclear pseudo-inclusions in lesional cells and multinucleated floret-like giant cells were not identified. Significant nuclear pleomorphism, necrosis, and atypical mitotic activity were also not identified. CD163 staining was focally positive in the tumor cells and admixed histiocytes with co-expression of CD34 and factor XIIIa (Fig. 1M–Table 1), while desmin, CD1a and BRAF (V600E) were negative. Overall, the histologic features and immunohistochemical findings were consistent with those of a conjunctival myxoid stromal tumor.

At his two-week post-operative visit, the operative site was healing well without any initial concern for early recurrence. The patient was subsequently lost to follow-up.

Discussion

The classification of myxoid lesions in the conjunctiva has evolved significantly with the introduction of the term "conjunctival stromal tumor" (COST) by Herwig et al., in 2012.1 COSTs represent a rare entity that arises almost exclusively from the bulbar conjunctiva and are unilateral, slow-growing, plaque-like or cystic, and pink to pale yellow in color. Although they rarely involve the cornea or invade adjacent structures, isolated cases of large lesions causing exotropia or extending into extraocular muscles have been documented.3,7 COSTs generally have a negligible impact on visual acuity. Their histogenesis may include inflammatory or environmental triggers given their bulbar location and documented associations with blepharitis both in the literature and in 1 of 3 patients in the current series, suggesting a possible reactive or hyperplastic proliferative process.1 Other studies have postulated that their sporadic presentation and indolent clinical behavior suggest a primary benign neoplastic process.1,7

Key histologic features include variably myxoid stroma, prominent ropey collagen, and spindle cell morphology.3,8 Other microscopic identifying features include nuclear pseudoinclusions and multinucleated cells in a collagenous-to-myxoid stroma with a notable lack of malignant features such as high nuclear:cytoplasmic ratio, increased cellularity, increased mitotic activity with atypical mitotic figures, and cellular pleomorphism.1 Immunohistochemically, COSTs demonstrate positivity for CD34, vimentin, factor XIIIa and variable CD68. They lack expression of markers of neural and myofibroblastic differentiation, including S100 protein and smooth muscle antigen (SMA).6

Treatment for COSTs involves complete excision. However, some reports have queried the necessity of full excision given the tumor's noninvasive features, lack of documented malignant transformation, and low probability of recurrence.6 In a review of 22 cases of COST by Lam et al.,2 there were no cases of lesional recurrence, locally aggressive disease or metastasis after excision with an average follow-up time of 32 month except one case of an 11-year-old boy who developed a recurrence three years after primary incomplete excision.9 Among this series, two patients underwent full excision without local recurrence (though with limited follow-up), while one patient underwent incisional biopsy.

Despite the classification guidelines, distinguishing CMs from COSTs remains a diagnostic challenge. Clinically, both entities share overlapping features such as age at presentation, tumor size, and symptomatology. Histologically, abundant myxoid stroma, fibrillary collagen, and frequent stellate cells with relatively frequent accompanying cystic change favor a diagnosis of CM.8 While there have been attempts to distinguish the two based on appearance and lack of CD34 expression in conjunctival myxomas,1, 2, 3,7 other studies have demonstrated a lack of statistically significant differences in the previously mentioned points of differentiation. Notably, CD34, a marker historically used to exclude myxoma, has been found to show strong, diffuse positivity in numerous well-documented myxoma cases.8,10 The overlapping immunostaining patterns suggests that current immunohistochemical workup may be insufficient for differentiation between these entities. Certain peer-reviewed studies have proposed testing these conjunctival myxoid lesions for mutations in the PRKAR1A gene, given the well-documented association of conjunctival myxomas with Carney complex.8,11

Considering this diagnostic ambiguity, the more inclusive term “conjunctival myxoid stromal tumor” (CMST) has been proposed to encompass COSTs and CMs for small conjunctival biopsies, where limited tissue is available for morphologic evaluation.6 The adoption of CMST as a unifying term could improve diagnostic consistency in the short term akin to COST terminology, and in the longer term, could facilitate collection of sufficient cases for development of more reproducible criteria for classification. More generic terminology may encourage clinicians to have a low threshold to consider whether additional workup for any syndromic association is appropriate, given associations of CM in conjunction with Carney complex and Zollinger Ellison syndrome (ZES).5,7,12 However, we do suggest rendering a diagnosis of CM where classic morphologic features and findings of ancillary testing, including molecular next-generation sequencing, permit an unequivocal diagnosis of that entity.

The present series adds detailed histopathologic data and descriptions of three diverse clinical presentations to a sparse body of literature in which this rare tumor is otherwise variably described. While limited by short follow-up times, these case descriptions of conjunctival myxomas contribute to a more robust comprehension of the nuance between an isolated lesion versus a tumor with systemic implications.

Conclusion

Conjunctival myxoid stromal tumors are rare bulbar conjunctival lesions. These lesions are histologically characterized by proliferation of spindled lesional cells with variably prominent myxoid stroma and immunohistochemical positivity for CD34, factor XIIIa, and vimentin. Complete excision is recommended as the definitive treatment for these stromal tumors; however, incomplete resection or local biopsy may be reasonable given its indolent nature, low rate of recurrence and lack of locally invasive aggressive behavior. We recommend consideration of using the diagnostic terminology of CMST in cases where differentiation between these entities is challenging, such as in small biopsies or cases with intermediate morphologic features in the absence of ancillary molecular testing. Further characterization of these relatively rare lesions will allow for greater diagnostic clarity and guidance regarding optimal surgical and medical management.
