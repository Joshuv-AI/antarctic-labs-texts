Nanoscale multi-gradient ordered architectures driven exceptional strength and ductility in low thermal expansion magnesium alloy
Authors: Yuhang Hai, Kaijie Gu, Yadong Huang, Sujuan Wu, Zhihua Dong, Jiangfeng Song, Shuailing Ma, Bin Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlloys with low thermal expansion, high strength, and superior plasticity are crucial for critical applications in industries such as aerospace. Although the in-situ formation of low or negative thermal expansion particles presents a promising strategy, these materials generally suffer from limited ductility. Here, we demonstrate that the construction of nanoscale gradient ordered architectures can effectively addresses this limitation. By facilitating the diffusion and reaction of aluminum (Al) atoms into boronized manganese (Mn-B), we induce a gradient ordering architecture between rare-earthed magnesium (Mg) alloys and MnB phase, achieving a near-zero thermal expansion coefficient of 0.8 × 10-6·°C-1 within the temperature range of 280–320 °C. As fully transitioning from Mn-B to a multi-gradient ordered Mn-Al-B configuration results in a stabilized thermal expansion coefficient of 23 ×10-6·°C-1 across a broad temperature range (25–400 °C). This nanoscale architecture not only mitigates brittle interfacial fractures by maintaining the mechanical integrity but also enables the Mg alloy to reach an ultrahigh compressive strength of 507 MPa, with a 23.8% compressive strain. Our findings highlight the potential of designing gradient ordered architectures as a strategic approach to enhance the mechanical properties of lightweight alloys.Subject terms: Metals and alloys, Structural properties, Composites

Introduction

Thermal expansion is a widely observed phenomenon with significant implications for materials science and engineering1,2. This behavior is quantitatively characterized by the thermal expansion coefficient (CTE)3,4. An excessively high CTE presents substantial challenges for metals and alloys, such as the risk of hot cracking during casting processes and dimensional instability caused by interface misalignments under service conditions5–9. These challenges are particularly pronounced in lightweight metals like magnesium (Mg). Owing to its relatively low atomic mass, Mg experiences substantial atomic vibrations at elevated temperatures, resulting in a higher CTE compared to conventional metals such as aluminum (Al), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), and titanium (Ti)10–13. Consequently, thermal cracking during casting or processing becomes a pronounced issue, seriously restricting its production ratio and structural applications in engineering fields, such as aerospace, automotive14–16.

A variety of strategies have been explored to reduce the CTE in metals, including alloying, composite design, and processing optimization17–21. Among these strategies, the incorporation of low or negative thermal expansion (NTE) materials, guided by composite theory, has proven to be the most effective approach22–26. Nevertheless, such strategies often compromise the ductility of metals due to undesirable insufficient interfacial bonding27,28. At high temperature, these issues are further compounded by detrimental chemical reactions between NTE phases and matrices, as well as the inherent limitations of NTE materials, which typically display anomalous expansion behavior only within narrow temperature windows linked to specific phase transformations29,30. Therefore, the challenge of reducing the CTE of lightweight alloys at high temperatures while simultaneously preserving superior mechanical properties, particularly room-temperature plasticity, remains a critical and unresolved issue in the field of materials design.

In this study, we propose a strategy for the in-situ construction of a nanoscale multi-gradient ordered architecture to reduce the thermal expansion while enhancing the mechanical properties of a rare-earthed WE43 alloy (composed of Mg-4%Y-3.3%RE(Nd, Gd)−0.5%Zr by weight). The WE43 alloy was selected as a representative model system to address its inherent susceptibility to thermal cracking during processing, a key limitation hindering its broader application31–33. By optimizing the Al content in a boronized manganese (Mn-B) reinforced Mg matrix, we aim to achieve a serious transformation from Mn-B to various Mn-Al-B phases within multi-gradient configuration through Al diffusion and reaction. The in-situ thermal expansion strain analysis is used to elucidate the critical role of restructuring in alleviating internal stresses and strains during thermal exposure. This distinctive configuration enables the development of lightweight structural alloys with low thermal expansion and excellent mechanical properties.

Results

Microstructure characterization

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the Al-modified rare-earthed Mg-Mn-B alloy show that the absence of distinctive diffraction peaks associated with Mn, MnB, and MgAl phases (Fig. 1a). Notably, the diffraction peaks indexed as (101), (002), and (100) of the Mg matrix exhibit a pronounced shift towards lower angles upon the incorporation of 5 wt.% Al. However, these peaks remain unchanged with an increase in Al content to 10 wt.%. This behavior indicates a release of lattice strain in the Mg matrix as the Al concentration increases to 10 wt.%. Although the formation of Al2Y phases is evident with 5 wt.% Al, no discernible diffraction signals from the 7 wt.% Mn-B constituents are observed. This absence suggests either the presence of nanocrystalline Mn-B that fall below the detection limit of conventional XRD or the existence of amorphous Mn-B nanoparticles.

Micromorphological analysis (Fig. 1b) indicates that the refinement of Mg grains remains minimal with the addition of Mn-B and Al34–36. We found that a significant reduction in pore density is observed as the Al content increases (Supplementary Fig. 1). The detailed Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping shows that Al is predominantly located at the interfaces and partially diffuses into the Mn-B particles as increasing its contents (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3), significantly enhancing interfacial cohesion37. We also found that a diffusion of Al within the Mn-B particles (Fig. 1c), with complete diffusion occurring in particles smaller than 15 μm (Supplementary Fig. 4). Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM) analysis confirms strain accommodation in the 10 wt.% Al system, with substantially reduced strain localization at interfaces compared to conventional ceramic-reinforced Mg composites (e.g., SiCp/Mg and AlN/Mg)38,39 or Mg composites containing negative thermal expanded LaFe9.8Co1.1Al2.1 particles40, highlighting the unique interfacial compatibility achieved through this alloy design.

Interface observation

The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) analysis (Fig. 2) elucidates atomic-scale interfacial structures and chemical distributions at the interface. Elemental mapping demonstrates complete Mg-Al-Mn intermixing at the interfaces (Fig. 2a–d), with notable Al diffusion into Mn-B particles. Atomic-scale imaging shows perfect lattice continuity across Mg/AlMn-B interfaces (Fig. 2e–g) with two distinct strain effects. One is a lattice compression in Mg adjacent to interfaces, and the other one is an expansion near Mn-B particles (Fig. 2i). This strain coupling effectively mitigates interfacial embrittlement through elastic energy dissipation. Most remarkably, we directly resolved a 4–10 nm gradient ordered architecture within AlMn-B, generated via Al-Mn-B reaction, exhibiting continuous lattice spacing evolution (Fig. 2j–m). This configuration coherently accommodates the lattice mismatch while maintaining perfect crystallographic registry, enabling a unique structural configuration achievement in Mg matrix composites.

The diffraction pattern reveals that the AlMn-B structure can be indexed to either the [110] zone axis of the Mn4Al11 phase or the [001] zone axis of the MnB phase (Fig. 2h). Partial overlap between the (001¯) diffraction spots of Mn4Al11 and the (01¯0) spots of MnB indicates symmetry breaking along this orientation. This can be attributed to the Al/B defects during the formation of nanoscale gradient metastable AlMn-B. In the 5 wt.% Al sample, regions without interfacial cracks reveals that the interfacial formed Mn2Al3 phase preserves gradient-varying interfacial contact (Supplementary Fig. 5) suggesting the AlMn-B phase maintains strong cohesion with the Mg matrix and mitigates brittle interfacial fracture. This further supports our expectation that varying Al contents induce distinct phase formations.

In the Mg matrix adjacent to the interface, geometric phase analysis (GPA) and dislocation density comparisons between 0 wt.% and 10 wt.% Al samples reveal a stark contrast (Supplementary Fig. 6). The former displays pronounced interfacial strain concentrations and high dislocation density, whereas the latter exhibits a near dislocation-free interface with dramatically reduced strain. The absence of deformation dislocations in Mg matrix, variable lattice spacings, and asymmetric spot broadening in AlMn-B confirm that these chemically nanoscale ordered transition layers effectively relieve interfacial stresses. Real-space HRTEM and reciprocal-space SEAD data consistently demonstrate this Al diffusion and reaction drive the formation of nanoscale multi-gradient ordered architectures with progressive crystallographic symmetry variation. The above results suggest that the nanoscale gradient ordered interfacial architecture is established and have a good cohesion within Mg matrix.

Mechanical property and Thermal expansion behavior

The compressive mechanical properties and thermal expansion characteristics of the samples were systematically investigated (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. 7, 8, Supplementary Tables 1, 2). With increasing Al content, both the ultimate compressive strength and compressive strain of the alloys demonstrated a progressive enhancement. Notably, at an Al concentration of 10 wt.%, the alloy attained a compressive strength of 507 ± 8 MPa accompanied by an elongation of 23.8 ± 0.6%, indicative of significant plasticity improvements in the Mg alloy (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 9). The tensile performance improvement is less significant than the compressive performance (Supplementary Fig. 10), which reflects the typical tension-compression asymmetry of magnesium alloys due to their hexagonal close-packed crystal structure. Nevertheless, compared with the WE43 alloy containing only 7 wt.% Mn-B, the addition of 10 wt.% Al raises the yield tensile strength from 157 ± 4 MPa to 204 ± 2 MPa, while maintain an elongation of 8 ± 0.5%, which is a notable enhancement compared with the nearly complete loss of ductility observed without Al addition (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Interestingly, thermal strain analysis revealed a near-zero expansion regime in samples with 0 wt.% and 5 wt.% Al (Fig. 3b), analogous to behaviors observed in alloys solely addition of pure MnB phase (Supplementary Fig. 12). This regime coincides with the phase transition temperature of MnB, approximately 280–300 °C41,42. The results suggest the potential formation of the MnB phase during alloy preparation, as corroborated by EDS point analyses (Supplementary Fig. 13). In comparison to magnesium alloys previously reported in the literature for their low thermal expansion and robust plasticity7,21,43–52, the alloy synthesized through our approach exhibits superior mechanical strength and plasticity (Fig. 3c). This highlights the critical role of interface control through alloying in achieving outstanding thermal expansion and mechanical load transfer properties.

The formation of the MnB phase typical requires high pressure, indicating significant thermal stress during synthesis. Interestingly, alloys modified with 5 wt.% Al display an expanded near-zero thermal strain range compared to those influenced predominantly by MnB alone. We propose that this anomalous thermal stability arises from a synergistic effect among Al-induced deformation recovery, the presence of porosity in the Mg matrix, and interactions between MnB and Mg, collectively broadening the thermal stability range40. This hypothesis is supported by XRD analysis, which indicates significant residual strain in the sample with 5 wt.% Al. In contrast, the sample with 10 wt.% Al shows reduced residual strain, as evidenced by TEM and GPA characterization of the Mg matrix (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating strain relaxation and a stable CTE of 23 × 10-6 °C-1. Remarkably, this CTE aligns with that of many Al alloys, yet the material maintains a substantially lower density of 1.83 g/cm³ compared to 2.7 g/cm³ of conventional Al alloys10,21. This research advances our understanding of macroscopic thermal strain, phase transitions, and residual strain during processing by examining variations in the CTE across different temperature ranges, providing a scientific basis for optimizing manufacturing processability and operational reliability of lightweight alloys.

Discussion

Formation mechanism of the gradient ordered architecture

Three-dimensional atom probe tomography (3D-APT) and proximity histogram analysis across the Mg/AlMn–B interface reveal a nanoscale intermixing region (2–5 nm wide) exhibiting near-perfect spatial overlap of B, Mn, and Al elemental distributions (Fig. 4a, b). This colocalization confirms that B atoms dissolve atomically into the Mn4Al11 lattice, forming a compositionally graded metastable phase rather than discrete MnB precipitates at the interface. To accommodate local strain from solute B atoms or Al deficiencies, Mn atoms undergo coordinated lattice adjustments, consistent with stress-activated structural and electronic adaptations7,53,54. The segregation of solute B atoms generates strain accommodating regions through atomic-scale structural modulation every few lattice periods55,56, which contributes to the coherent interfacial architecture observed in Fig. 2.

a 3D atom map reconstruction of the Mg/Mn-Al interface, showing the distribution of Mg, Al, Mn and B atoms. b One-dimensional compositional profile across the Mg/Mn-Al interface, illustrating the nanoscale (2–5 nm) co-segregation and perfect overlap of B, Mn, and Al elements. c Calculated free energy profiles of MnB, Mn4Al11, Mn2Al3, Mn2Al3-B and Mn4Al11-B phases as a function of temperature. The results show that B incorporation stabilizes Mn₄Al₁₁ over competing phases, supporting the formation of a B-stabilized ordered structure at the interface. The inset schematically illustrates the interdiffusion-driven Mn–Al reaction that promotes progressive ordering, induces boron supersaturation, and produces the observed compositional gradient at room temperature. Source data for (b, c) are provided as a Source Data file.

To further elucidate the formation mechanism of AlMn-B, we conducted first-principles calculations of temperature-dependent free energies for MnB, Mn2Al3, Mn4Al11, Mn2Al3-B, and Mn4Al11-B phases. Our calculations (Fig. 4c) indicate that Mn4Al11-B possesses the lowest free energy at 520 °C, elucidating its preferential formation in Al-rich interfacial regions. In contrast, at room temperature, Mn2Al3 and Mn4Al11 are thermodynamically more stable, suggesting that the metastable AlMn-B phase undergoes decomposition or phase separation during cooling57–59. The final phase assemblage depends strongly on the initial Al content, which governs both the primary phase formed and the composition of precipitates generated upon cooling.

We thus proposed the following formation pathway to elucidate the evolution of this distinctive compositional gradient ordered architecture and its effect on Mg matrix (inset of Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 14). During hot-pressing at 520 °C, interfacial Al atoms rapidly diffuse into Mn-B, leading to the formation of a B-supersaturated AlMn-B phase. Upon cooling, this phase undergoes decomposition via B precipitation, while spatial variations in Al content and localized stress promote the development of a compositional gradient through coordinated B segregation. In the absence of Al, direct formation of MnB particles at the Mg/Mn–B interface induces high residual strain due to mechanical and thermal mismatch. With limited Al addition, although the stable Mn2Al3 enhances interfacial bonding, the mismatch-induced localized stress concentration still promotes MnB precipitation and residual strain accumulation in the Mg matrix. At higher Al contents, however, the high-temperature Mn4Al11-B phase decomposes under cooling through a defect-mediated restructuring process. Enrichment of solute B atoms effectively suppresses MnB formation and facilitates discontinuous transformation within the Mn4Al11, dissipating interfacial energy and minimizing microcracking. Consequently, in the 10 wt.% Al alloy, MnB is eliminated, strain concentration is absent, and no zero-thermal-expansion regime is observed. This collectively demonstrate the role of the nanoscale gradient structure in achieving interfacial stability and thermodynamic equilibrium.

Mechanism of strength and ductility synergy

Microstructural analysis of the 10 wt.% Al composites deformed to 24% strain reveals the emergence of Mn5Al8 phase (Fig. 5a–d). GPA shows pronounced strain localization within the Mg matrix adjacent to the Mn5Al8 regions, while the original Mn4Al11-B phases maintain substantially lower strain levels (Supplementary Fig. 15). Moreover, dislocations observed within Mn5Al8 further confirm plastic activity in the secondary particles. First-principles calculations demonstrate that applied strain drives the transition from metastable Mn4Al11-B to Mn5Al8 (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 16), revealing a stress-induced phase transformation mechanism.

Mn5Al8 possess the highest energy than Mn4Al11-B, Mn4Al11, and cannot stable under equilibrium condition. Under applied strain ( ~ 2%), the energy of Mn4Al11-B rises above that of Mn5Al8, providing the thermodynamic driving force for the stress-induced phase transformation. f Schematic illustration of the deformation mechanism: (Step 1) dislocations (┴) glide and pile up around the particles. (Step 2) Upon reaching a critical stress, the outer layer of the particle undergoes a stress-induced phase transformation, effectively releasing the accumulated strain and suppressing crack initiation. (Step 3) With continued loading, dislocations pile up again, triggering further phase transformation in the inner layers. Based on the observed microstructural evolution, we propose the following strengthening and toughening mechanism (Fig. 5f). Stress induced phase transformation and plastic activity in the secondary particle enable high density dislocation storage for strengthening and work hardening60, while simultaneously improving ductility through enhanced dislocation mobility. The mitigation of brittle interfacial fractures achieved by the nanoscale gradient ordered architecture, along with reduced residual matrix strain, facilitates mechanically synergistic particle-matrix interactions. This process is accompanied by various phase transformations mediated volumetric adjustments that reinforce interfacial cohesion and delay fracture initiation. Furthermore, dislocation activity within the newly formed phases at higher strain levels provides additional enhancement of both strength and ductility27,61,62. This nanoscale compositional gradient architecture effectively converts conventional weak interfaces into active energy dissipation zones, ultimately enabling the exceptional strength-ductility synergy observed in this system.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the formation of a nanoscale gradient ordered Mn-Al-B architecture in Mg alloys. This unique structural feature effectively mitigates stress concentration at the interface, resulting in an exceptional combination of high strength (507 MPa) and ductility (23.8% compressive strain) in boronized manganese particle reinforced Mg matrices, surpassing the performance of conventional alloys reported to date. The atomic level gradient ordered architecture engineering strategy facilitates the development of lightweight structural alloys with low thermal expansion and tunable residual thermal strain. The remarkable combination of low thermal expansion and favorable elastic properties positions these alloys for applications in high-precision devices requiring stable elastic rigidities, such as mechanical chronometers for space missions. This work introduces a design strategy for composites, with the nanoscale gradient architecture formed via reaction and segregation gradients laying the groundwork for future composite material studies.

Methods

Sample preparation

The Mn powder and B powder were mixed in a planetary ball mill at an atomic ratio of one to one for 60 min, and the rotation speed was 180 r/min. Then the vacuum sealing tube was placed in a crucible filled with graphite powder. The crucible was placed in an insulating brick and heated in a microwave oven for 5–10 minutes to obtain Mn and B mixed powder, denoted as boronized manganese (Mn-B). Different mass ratios of Mn-B powder, Al powder and WE43 powder were mixed in a planetary ball mill at a speed of 180 r/min for 2.5 h. Then it was sintered in a vacuum hot-press sintering furnace with a vacuum degree of 1 × 10−5 Pa, a pressure of 20 MPa, a temperature of 520 °C, and a holding time of 1 h. The sample is then cooled to room temperature in the furnace.

Microstructure characterization

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of samples was recorded by the D/Max 2500 pc X-ray diffractometer with monochromatic Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). The microstructure was examined by a JEOL-7800F type scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and FEI Talos F200X type field emission transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and FEI 200. The Fast Fourier transform pattern was processed using DigitalMicrograph software (version 3.43.3212.0) with a Wiener filter applied for noise reduction. The APT measurements were conducted on a CAMECA LEAP 5000 XR. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) mapping was conducted utilizing an Oxford Instruments AZtec system (version 2.1.2), with step sizes varying between 0.26 and 0.46 µm. The specific step size and resultant indexing rate for each scan are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Subsequent analysis of the EBSD data was performed using the MTEX toolbox (version 5.8.0) operating within MATLAB R2024A. For grain reconstruction, a misorientation threshold of 15° was applied to delineate grain boundaries, which then underwent smoothing with a parameter of 5. Final sample preparation was performed by argon ion polishing, and the shooting instrument was JEOL JSM-7800F.

Mechanical properties and thermal expansion performance test

The dimension of the specimens for CTE measurement was Ø 3 mm × 3 mm. The linear thermal expansion data (dL/L) of samples were measured using a NETZSCH 402 C Dilatometer under a heating rate of 10 °C/min and temperature ranging from room temperature to 200 °C. The room temperature compressive mechanical properties were measured by a CMT-5305-300 KN universal testing machine. The size of the compressed sample was Ø 4 mm × 8 mm.

First-principles Calculations

Theoretical investigations were conducted to understand the mechanisms behind the structure evolution and its effect on the mechanical properties. The calculations of phonon dispersion spectra and related thermodynamic properties were conducted using density functional perturbation theory (DFPT)63. All first-principles calculations were performed employing the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP, version 6.3.2)64,65,66]. The projector augmented-wave (PAW) pseudopotentials67 and the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE)68 generalized gradient approximation functional were used to describe the electron exchange-correlation interactions69.

We began with the geometric optimization of the pristine MnAl unit cell, using a Brillouin zone k-point mesh of 3 × 3 × 4. The energy convergence criterion for the electronic self-consistent cycle was set to 10−8 eV, and the ionic relaxation was considered converged when the forces on atoms were below 0.001 eV/Å. Upon obtaining the optimized host structure, we constructed a supercell, introduced B atom doping, and built a supercell model containing a total of 64 atoms. The energy cut-off of the plane-wave basis sets was 450 eV.

Based on this doped supercell, the first-order force constants required for the dynamical matrix were computed directly using the DFPT method implemented in VASP. Since this 64-atom supercell is sufficiently large to capture the relevant atomic interactions, it was treated as a 1 × 1 × 1 primitive cell for the phonon calculations. A q-point mesh of 7 × 7 × 4 was used to sample its Brillouin zone. The force constants obtained from the DFPT calculations were then processed using the Phonopy software to obtain the phonon dispersion relations and the phonon density of states (PDOS). Finally, within the harmonic approximation, the thermodynamic properties of the system, including the Helmholtz free energy (F), entropy (S), and constant-volume heat capacity (Cv), were derived by integrating the PDOS and are presented as functions of temperature.

The atomic coordinates of all optimized computational models are provided in the Source Data file.
