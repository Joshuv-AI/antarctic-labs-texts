Arthroscopic Bony Bankart Fixation Using Single-Row Technique with Knotless Suture Anchors
Authors: George F Matta, Benjamin Brej, Samuel Taylor, Gregory L Cvetanovich, Jennifer Kurowicki, Ryan C Rauck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Bony Bankart lesions involve detachment of the anteroinferior labrum with an associated glenoid rim fracture after traumatic anterior shoulder instability. Compared with isolated soft-tissue Bankart lesions, osseous involvement increases the risk of recurrent instability and progressive glenoid bone loss. Arthroscopic fixation techniques have evolved as minimally invasive alternatives to open repair and bone augmentation in appropriately selected cases.Indications:Arthroscopic single-row fixation is indicated for reducible bony Bankart lesions involving small to moderate fragments, typically <25% of the glenoid surface, with adequate fragment viability and an intact capsulolabral sleeve. Larger defects or irreducible fragments may require open fixation or bone augmentation procedures.Technical Description:The patient is positioned in the beach-chair position, and standard posterior, anterior, and anterosuperior portals are established. A systematic diagnostic arthroscopy is performed. The capsulolabral complex and attached osseous fragment are thoroughly mobilized using an elevator to achieve anatomic reducibility. The native anterior glenoid rim is lightly decorticated to create a bleeding bone bed while preserving fragment integrity. Fixation proceeds from inferior to superior. A knotless suture anchor is placed at the 5:30 position, and the suture is passed through the anteroinferior labrum at the base of the fragment, restoring inferior glenohumeral ligament tension. A second anchor is placed at the 3:00 position to secure the superior portion of the fragment. Knotless fixation permits incremental tensioning and retensioning to optimize reduction while minimizing fragment malrotation. Final probing confirms stable anatomic reduction.Results:Stable fixation with restoration of the anterior-inferior labral bumper and glenoid contour was achieved arthroscopically. Early follow-up demonstrated maintained stability, minimal pain, and progressive return of range of motion.Discussion/Conclusion:Arthroscopic single-row fixation using knotless anchors provides a reproducible, minimally invasive technique for selected bony Bankart lesions. Key technical principles include complete mobilization, preparation of the native glenoid bed, inferior-to-superior fixation, and controlled tensioning. Background

Patient History

In this case, we have a 62-year-old right-hand dominant female who presented to an outside emergency room with a chief complaint of a mechanical fall, stating she fell forward onto an outstretched arm, reaching for a shopping cart. She immediately had pain in her right palm and pain with active abduction. Initial radiographs were read as normal. She then presented to the Ohio State University 1 month after the initial injury with continued shoulder pain and recurrent instability. Past medical history includes uncomplicated hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, obstructive sleep apnea, depression, and obesity. Examination of the right shoulder demonstrated a limited range of motion, with 130° of passive forward elevation and 70° of active forward elevation. External rotation measured 80°, and internal rotation was limited to L3. Strength was grossly intact, although assessment was limited by pain. On special testing, the patient had positive mid-range apprehension and relocation tests.

Plain Radiographs

Repeat radiographs showed a fracture of the anterior glenoid rim, which had been initially missed at an outside hospital one month prior.

Computed Tomography (CT) Scans With 3-Dimensional Reconstruction

The CT scan showed an anterior glenoid fracture with displacement. Fracture fragment measured at approximately 20% of the glenoid.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

MRI again shows the displaced bony Bankart fracture. As we scroll inferiorly, the anteroinferior glenoid rim demonstrates contrast extravasation between the glenoid and labrum, indicating detachment of the labrum from the bony rim. The labrum also appears medially displaced, no longer seated on the glenoid face, consistent with loss of the bumper effect. All rotator cuff tendons were intact, with no evidence of tearing, as seen here in the coronal view.

Bony Bankart Lesions

For some background on the pathology, Bony Bankart lesions involve detachment of the anteroinferior labrum with a fracture of the glenoid rim. This typically results from a traumatic anterior shoulder dislocation, where the humeral head impacts the glenoid edge and shears away a fragment of bone along with the labrum. Compared with soft-tissue Bankart lesions, bony involvement carries a higher risk of recurrent instability, particularly in young and contact athletes. 1

Indications

Operative Treatment

Nonoperative management may be appropriate for minimally displaced bony Bankart lesions, particularly in older or lower-demand patients without recurrent instability. However, operative treatment is indicated for most bony Bankart lesions, especially in active or high-demand individuals, to reduce the risk of recurrent shoulder instability and chronic glenoid bone loss. It is important to accurately characterize the fracture fragment for surgical decision-making. For most patients, arthroscopic repair of the bony Bankart lesion is effective. However, those with large fracture fragments of >25% of the glenoid may require open repair with bony augmentation. For those without large fracture fragments, arthroscopic repair is preferred because it offers similar outcomes and is minimally invasive. Arthroscopic approaches do not interfere with the subscapularis like an open procedure would. 6

Arthroscopic Bony Bankart Repair

In 2002, Porcellini et al 5 introduced the first arthroscopic technique for bony Bankart repair, the single-row technique shown on the upper right-hand side. Suture anchors are placed in the glenoid rim, and sutures are passed medial and lateral to the bony Bankart fragment to surround the fragment and secure it with a single point of fixation. This technique utilizes the labrum attached to the fragment to achieve indirect reduction and stabilization of the fracture through capsulolabral tensioning. Alternatively, in 2009, Millett and Braun 4 described the double-row bony Bankart bridge technique, which uses 2 suture anchors placed medial and lateral to the fracture rim for 2 points of fixation. Since then, various similar techniques have been described. This slide compares single-row and double-row arthroscopic Bankart repair techniques. Single-row fixation is technically straightforward and efficient, making it a reliable option for routine, lower-demand cases. In addition, a single-row approach minimizes implant burden and preserves the glenoid bone for future reconstruction if needed. However, because fixation is achieved along a single line of anchors, this technique does not provide true compression of an associated bony fragment against the glenoid rim.

In contrast, double-row fixation allows for circumferential compression of the bony Bankart fragment, which may improve fragment stability and surface contact. The trade-off is increased technical complexity, and if not carefully tensioned, there is a risk of unintended rotation of the glenoid fragment and chondral irritation from sutures crossing the articular margin.

Case Assessment and Operative Plan

This patient is a 62-year-old right-hand dominant female who presented with persistent right shoulder pain and recurrent instability after a fall on her outstretched hand. Given the patient's persistent symptoms, recurrent instability, and imaging findings consistent with anterior-inferior labral pathology and bony Bankart fracture, operative management was recommended with arthroscopic fixation of the glenoid fracture using a single-row technique with knotless suture anchors.

Technique Description

Patient Positioning

The patient was induced under general anesthesia and positioned in the beach-chair position. While lateral decubitus positioning is also reasonable, the beach-chair position offers the advantage of facilitating management of concomitant rotator cuff or biceps pathology, which is frequently encountered in this patient population. Examination under anesthesia revealed grade 2 anterior instability, with no posterior instability and a negative sulcus sign. A posterior portal is created using an 11-blade scalpel, followed by atraumatic insertion of a blunt trocar and arthroscope. An anterior portal is then established just superior to the upper border of the subscapularis using an outside-in spinal needle technique, and an 8-mm cannula is introduced. A second anterosuperior viewing portal is placed high within the rotator interval to optimize visualization down the glenoid face. A 5-mm cannula is placed into the posterior working portal, and a systematic diagnostic arthroscopy is performed.

Arthroscopic Findings

Arthroscopic evaluation demonstrated pathology primarily involving the anterior glenoid rim and labrum, with approximately 20% anterior glenoid bone loss and associated cartilage delamination. The inferior fragment showed partial medial healing, while the superior fragment remained unhealed and medially displaced. The anterior labrum was torn and displaced from approximately the 2 to 6 o'clock positions. Importantly, although detached from the native glenoid rim, the labrum remained attached to the osseous fragment, allowing indirect reduction and stabilization using a soft-tissue–based repair technique. The posterior labrum, biceps tendon, subscapularis insertion, axillary recess, and capsule were intact. The humeral head demonstrated moderate degenerative changes without a Hill-Sachs lesion. The rotator cuff undersurface showed mild articular-sided fraying without a full-thickness tear. A superior labrum anterior and posterior tear was noted at the superior labrum and biceps anchor and was not operatively treated.

Knotless Anchor Technique

An arthroscopic shaver is introduced to gently debride frayed labral tissue. A Bankart elevator is then used to mobilize the anterior and inferior labrum until fibers of the subscapularis musculature are visualized, confirming sufficient release. Tissue quality is good, and the inferior labrum remains attached to the bony glenoid fragment. Using a soft-tissue grasper, the fracture fragment is manipulated to confirm reducibility. The anterior glenoid rim is lightly shaved to create a bleeding bony surface, optimizing healing potential.

Inferior fixation with 5:30 position anchor placement is then begun. A 1.8-mm knotless FiberTak anchor loaded with No. 2 repair suture is placed at the 5:30 position on the glenoid face. A suture lasso is used to pass the repair stitch through the anteroinferior labrum at the base of the fracture. The stitch is then retrieved through the anchor's shuttling mechanism and tensioned in a knotless fashion. This restores appropriate tension to the inferior glenohumeral ligament and reduces the inferior portion of the glenoid fracture. The limbs are left untensioned for final adjustment later in the procedure.

Attention is then directed to the superior portion of the fracture and the more superior anterior labrum. A second anchor is placed at the 3 o'clock position. The repair suture is passed around the labrum and capsule at the superior margin of the fracture. Under direct visualization and with a grasper maintaining reduction, the repair is tensioned to secure the superior portion of the glenoid fragment. The fracture site is probed and found to be well reduced and stable against the native anterior glenoid contour, completing the arthroscopic-assisted fixation.

Remaining soft tissue and bony debris are irrigated thoroughly. All arthroscopic portals are closed using 3-0 Monocryl. A sterile dressing is applied, and the arm is placed in a postoperative sling.

Discussion/Conclusion

Surgical Pearls

Fragment assessment is critical and involves careful arthroscopic evaluation of fragment size, mobility, and cartilage viability. Small to moderate fragments with an intact capsulolabral sleeve are well suited for soft-tissue–based fixation, thereby avoiding hardware-related complications; mobilization before fixation is recommended. Adequate liberation of the capsulolabral complex and bony fragment from medial scar tissue is essential. The fragment must reduce anatomically to the glenoid face without tension before anchor placement. Use the labrum as a reduction tool. Sutures passed through the capsulolabral tissue act as a tension band, indirectly reducing and stabilizing the bony fragment against the glenoid when tied or tensioned with knotless anchors.

Inferior-to-Superior Fixation Strategy

Begin anchor placement at the 5:30 to 6 o'clock position and progress superiorly to restore the anterior-inferior bumper and address capsular redundancy simultaneously. Knotless anchors help control tension. Knotless fixation allows incremental tensioning with the bone reduced and allows retensioning after placing all anchors for further compression. This improves fragment reduction while minimizing the risk of overconstraint or fragment comminution.

Follow-up

The patient was placed in a sling for 4 weeks, and an anterior stabilization physical therapy protocol began 4 weeks post-procedure. At 3 months postoperatively, the patient reported no recurrent instability symptoms. Active range of motion was steadily improving with adherence to a structured home exercise program and supervised physical therapy. Pain was minimal. At the 6-month follow-up, she continued to deny any episodes of instability. Active range of motion had further improved, measuring 60° of external rotation, 160° of forward elevation, and internal rotation to the level of L2. Pain remained minimal, and she demonstrated continued functional progress.

Results

Systematic reviews evaluating arthroscopic bony Bankart repair have similarly demonstrated excellent clinical outcomes, including high patient satisfaction, improved shoulder function, and return-to-sport rates exceeding 85%, supporting arthroscopic fixation as an effective treatment strategy for small to moderate anterior glenoid rim fractures. 3 Single-row fixation remains an effective and technically efficient option for bony Bankart repair. Although double-row constructs may provide greater biomechanical compression of the fragment, clinical studies have demonstrated comparable short-term outcomes and recurrent instability rates between techniques in appropriately selected lesions. Further comparative studies with larger cohorts and longer-term follow-up are needed to better define indications and clinical outcomes relative to alternative fixation techniques. 2 Here is the patient demonstrating her active range of motion 3 months postoperatively.
