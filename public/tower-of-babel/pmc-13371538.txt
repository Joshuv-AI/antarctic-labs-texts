Designing an antimicrobial stewardship curriculum for nurses to strengthen knowledge and clinical practice
Authors: Fatima Aldawood, Nour Shamas, Majid Alshamrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a public health threat. Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) provides a promising solution to mitigate AMR risk. Despite the recognition of AMS interventions, educational curricula tailored to nurses are scarce. This study aimed to develop an evidence-based nursing AMS curriculum.MethodsA descriptive design study used combined approach to guide the development of an AMS curriculum for nurses working at the Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs using Kern’s six-step framework and shared governance model. We have provided the detailed steps and collaborative approach used to develop the curriculum from inception to implementation.ResultsOur curriculum comprises 12 topics delivered in a nursing AMS course. The course was designed following AMS evidence-based practices and recommendations, with the aim of improving nurses’ engagement in AMS.ConclusionApplying Kern-six steps framework in combination with a shared governance model, and supported by interdepartmental collaboration was instrumental in the development of the nursing AMS curriculum and facilitated joint ownership, shared decision-making, and effective implementation of the course. The curriculum addressed the identified educational needs and gaps in current nursing practice at Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs and created opportunities for future AMS collaboration initiatives with nursing department, However, further evaluation is required to assess its impact on nurses’ knowledge, competencies, and learning outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09314-4.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Antimicrobial stewardship, Nursing, Curriculum

Background

Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) is a global public health threat, projected to cause 10 million deaths by 2050. A Lancet series on AMR estimated that between 1990 and 2021, AMR caused approximately 1.14 million deaths, and an additional 4.71 million deaths were associated with it [1]. This underscores the urgent need for targeted actions to mitigate AMR by enhancing Antimicrobial Stewardship (AMS) programs. Within healthcare systems, the stakeholders involved in implementation of AMS programs include physicians, pharmacists, nurses, infection control specialists, quality specialists, epidemiologists, case managers, patients, and their families [2]. Successful implementation of AMS programs require collaboration among these stakeholders to ensure alignment of efforts and effective interventions [3].

Studies have demonstrated that nurses’ engagement in AMS interventions improves clinical outcomes of patients [4, 5]. The nature of their daily work create valuable opportunities to implement AMS interventions, and this unique advantage can be leveraged to meet AMS program goals [6, 7]. Therefore, strengthening nurses’ knowledge of their important role in AMS is crucial for their full participation and engagement within multidisciplinary teams [8]. Similar perspectives have been shared in a position statement on AMR released by nurse-led organizations such as the International Council for Nurses (ICN), as well as in a joint white paper by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and American Nurses Association (ANA) on AMR; these perspectives emphasize the importance of nurses’ active participation in AMS programs [9, 10]. Additionally, multiple reviews worldwide have identified nurses’ participation in AMS as a pillar and a missed opportunity [7, 11–19]. International hospital accrediting bodies, such as the Joint Commission International Accreditation (JCIA) and local Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Health Institutions (CBAHI), necessitate AMS education for all healthcare providers, including nurses [20, 21].

Although literature and international recommendations advocate for nurses’ involvement in AMS, several recent studies have revealed the need to improve nurses’ knowledge to enhance their practice and contribution to AMS programs [15, 22–24]. This is consistent with findings from a local study that recommended education and training for nurses to improve their knowledge of AMR and enhance their sense of being an integral part of the AMS team for better patient care [18]. Furthermore, registered nurses do not have prescriptive authority, which has led many nurses and other healthcare professionals to overlook the significant contributions that nurses can make to AMS efforts. This perception excludes nurses from the antimicrobial treatment discussions that usually occur between physicians and pharmacists or clinical pharmacists [25]. Nurses can lead or support a range of AMS interventions that enhance clinical management and patient outcomes. For example, ensuring adequate filling volumes of blood culture bottles can significantly increase the sensitivity of bloodstream infection detection [26]. Additionally, flushing the tube at the end of an intravenously infused antimicrobial ensures complete dose administration and may avoid losses of up to 20% of the total dose. Therefore, providing tailored education to nurses in AMS is essential to enhance their existing contributions [27].

Due to these factors, the AMS team at the Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs (MNGHA) planned to develop an AMS training curriculum for nurses, as part of nursing-focused AMS initiatives. The MNGHA consists of six tertiary medical campuses located in different regions within the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, along with primary healthcare centers. The hospital’s AMS program was established within the infection prevention and control department and included an infectious disease (ID)/AMS physician, ID/AMS pharmacist, and AMS nurse. The AMS team collaborates with other sections within the department, such as public health, infection control, and environmental health, and with other departments in the hospital, including the nursing department, to facilitate AMS intervention implementation. Similar to many established hospital AMS programs, the program at MNGHA focused on improving physician and pharmacist behaviors and practices, with limited emphasis on nurses’ roles beyond raising awareness. A 2018 survey was conducted in Jeddah to evaluate the nurse’s perception of their role in AMS [28]. Nevertheless, nurses have contributed greatly to AMR and AMS awareness activities during the World Antimicrobial Resistance Awareness Week organized at MNGHA since 2017. In this paper, we provide a detailed evidence-based methodology used to develop the nursing AMS curriculum, an outline of the resulting curriculum, and an example of a co-developed innovative AMS tool for nurses.

Curriculum development methodology

This descriptive study outlines the development of the AMS nursing curriculum, conducted between March 2022 and April 2024. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) (Approval No.NRR25/032/12). The timeline roadmap of the curriculum development from inception to implementation is outlined in Fig. 1. We used Kern’s six-step framework, recommended for designing healthcare curricula to enhance the educational intervention [29]. The framework consists of six steps: (1) problem identification and general needs assessment; (2) targeted needs assessment; (3) goals and objectives; (4) educational strategies; (5) implementation; and (6) evaluation and feedback [30]. The steps are listed sequentially; however, the implementation approach offers flexibility and dynamic interactions between the steps. This framework has been widely adopted in medical education in various specialties [31–34]. We also adopted a shared governance model to collaborate with nursing departments in curriculum planning and implementation as it facilitates participative management [35, 36]. The model ensures shared decision-making, accountability, and ownership. The use of this model in nursing has been shown to improve quality of care, patient outcomes, patient satisfaction, and nurse engagement [37]. Combining the framework and model was necessary for shared decision-making and fostering responsibility and ownership among the departments involved in this initiative. Kern’s framework is used to guide implementation by considering the factors that influence curriculum outcomes, while the shared governance model guides the step-by-step planning of the process until implementation by identifying key stakeholders, facilitators, and barriers [38].

Nursing antimicrobial stewardship curriculum development timeline roadmap

Initially, the AMS team met with top-level nursing leadership, where we proposed a collaboration on two initiatives. The first is the nursing AMS curriculum development, and the second is a quality improvement project aimed at improving microbiological culture collection practices. We shared project charters and proposed a lesson plan; this resulted in the formation of two task forces. The first task force comprised nurse experts from middle-level nursing leadership and nurse educators representing different specialties to address the learning needs based on patient age groups and specialty care. The main objective of this task force was to ensure the relevance and agreement of the nursing department at all stages of curriculum development. The second task force, named “Enhancing Microbiological Culture Collection Practices,” was composed of representatives from nursing, microbiology laboratory, and respiratory therapy departments. The recommendations of the second task force were used to enhance microbiological culture collection in the diagnostic stewardship pre-analytical phase and led to the incorporation of this important topic into the objectives of the curriculum.

The nursing antimicrobial stewardship curriculum development and content review task force held a total of seven meetings led by the AMS nurse and AMS clinical pharmacist, with participation of nurse experts. In the introductory meeting, we shared the project charter, lesson plan, and aim of the task force with the team. We then presented international recommendations pertaining to nurses’ roles in AMS and explored the challenges nurses may encounter when implementing AMS interventions. In subsequent meetings, we shared the proposed content through presentations and group discussions. At each meeting, we reviewed one to three topics. The group discussions elicited constructive feedback that could be included in the curriculum, and some will be used to optimize AMS interventions using the electronic health records. Step 1: problem identification and general needs assessment

In this step, we used various methods to identify possible gaps, gain a comprehensive understanding of the current practice, and guide curriculum development. A non-systematic literature review was conducted to identify studies assessing nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding AMR and AMS [18, 23, 27, 39–44]. Recurring themes were identified, including the need for education on different aspects of AMR and AMS, as well as the role of nurses. These studies identified the need for greater collaboration with the other AMS stakeholders. An impediment to this collaboration was the nurses’ unfamiliarity with certain topics, such as microbiology and antimicrobial pharmacotherapy. In line with these findings, a survey had been conducted in an MNGHA hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, which identified that most respondents, all whom were nurses, did not perceive that they had a role in AMS, and suggested that addressing their knowledge gap through the development and implementation of a targeted nursing educational curriculum could lead to improved antimicrobial use [28].

Step 2: targeted nursing AMS learning needs assessment

A survey entitled “Assessment of nurses’ knowledge, attitude, and practices towards antimicrobial resistance and stewardship” was used to assess nurses’ knowledge, attitude, and practices (KAP) regarding AMR and AMS. The survey was developed based on brief literature review, taking into consideration concerns discussed in the task force meetings [18, 45, 46]. The survey consisted of three sections which are knowledge, attitude, and practice. A total of thirty questions used to cover topics related to AMR and AMS concepts, inappropriate use of antimicrobials, infection prevention and control, diagnostic stewardship, allergy assessment and labelling, and nurses’ role in AMS. The survey questions are provided in Supplemental file 1 (Assessment of Nurses’ Knowledge, Attitude, and Practices towards Antimicrobial Resistance and Stewardship). The survey result is not presented in this paper, as the survey was conducted without obtaining the hospital’s ethical review board approval. However, it is worth mentioning that our findings are consistent with those in previously published literature [18, 45, 46].

Step 3: goals and objectives

Based on the gaps identified through the survey and task force discussions, we were able to identify learning needs which we listed and then grouped together in themes that could be consolidated into presentation topics. This is presented in Supplemental file 2 (Examples of identified learning needs grouped into themes and assigned topics). Subsequently, the task force co-developed the lesson plan. The lesson plan was structured into eight components: topics, with each supported by aligned learning general objectives (the overall aim); specific objectives (steps to achieve the aim); content outline; teaching strategies; educational materials; methods for formative evaluation; modifications; and the estimated time needed to deliver each topic. The modification section of the lesson plan accommodates different skill levels and variations in learning needs among learners, highlighting special considerations for certain specialties and patient age groups. For example, the signs and symptoms that can indicate sepsis early in neonates, pediatric patients, and adult patients were described. The lesson plan comprises 12 topics: (1) AMS and nurses’ role; (2) Basic microbiology; (3) Basic pharmacology and antibiotic classes; (4) AMS strategies and MNGHA policies; (5) Nurses’ role in improving antimicrobial allergy labelling; (6) Role of infection prevention and control in AMR and AMS; (7) Optimal microbiology testing and drug level sampling techniques; (8) Safe antimicrobial storage and administration Practices; (9) AMS in sepsis; (10) AMS clinical pearls for nurses; (11) Patient education on AMR and antimicrobial use; (12) Nursing empowerment in AMS. The topics were intentionally arranged such that the course starts with foundational concepts, including AMR, AMS, microbiology, pharmacology, and antimicrobial classes. Topics requiring greater clinical judgment and assertiveness, such as AMS clinical pearls for nurses, and nursing empowerment in AMS, were placed in the second section of the curriculum. This approach intended to build knowledge and enable learners to connect concepts with informed clinical reasoning [47].

One of the curriculum goals was to enhance readiness for patient counseling and empower patients regarding antimicrobial treatment during hospitalization. However, because most nurses at the MNGHA are non-Arabic speakers caring primarily for Arabic-speaking patients, language barriers were carefully considered during curriculum development. To address this, we created a badge-sized transliteration card to support communication by listing the most commonly used terms related to antimicrobial therapy, as presented in Table 4.

Step 4: educational strategies

Successful learning outcomes depend on the use of various teaching styles and conducive learning environments. This approach creates a teaching environment with high levels of engagement and motivation [48]. Thus, we decided that the course would be delivered face-to-face instead of online or through recorded videos to allow for personalized attention and help instructors modify their teaching style based on the learning style of learners. To maximize the benefits of the course, we shared pre-course reading materials 2 weeks before the scheduled date. The material includes an overview of the course content, objectives, a historical overview of antibiotic discovery and evolving responses to AMR, microbiological concepts, pharmacological terms and definitions, and key terms in drug reactions and allergies. The completion time of the educational material was approximately 45 min; however, this may vary based on participants’ background knowledge. Course instructors were carefully selected because the topics were unfamiliar to our nurse educators who would have otherwise delivered the training sessions. The instructors were selected based on their specialties, and the topics were distributed to the AMS nurse, who was the primary instructor and nurse specialists. For instance, the topic on “antimicrobial stewardship in sepsis” was delivered by a nurse expert in sepsis, and the topic on “nurses’ role in improving antimicrobial allergy labeling” was delivered by a nurse who is an expert in medication safety and allergy education. The course lasted 8 h; hence, we utilized various teaching styles to keep learners engaged and increase their confidence in discussing their perspectives and experiences during the course. We selected teaching methods based on objectives, content, and available resources; some examples are highlighted in Table 5.

Step 5: implementation

For implementation planning, the AMS team held three meetings with leaders across all nursing leadership levels in the nursing management and education teams. The AMS team, with the top-level nursing leadership and nursing education department, agreed that the course would become a requirement for all nurses and that Continuing Medical Education (CME) credits would be applied. The nursing education department agreed to facilitate the participation of all the nurses in the groups. Another meeting was held with the nursing education department and middle-level nursing leaders, where the AMS nurse provided an overview of the course goals and objectives. The AMS team explored potential approaches to implementing the course and provided recommendations. This included incorporating a brief AMR and AMS program overview in the nurse induction program organized by the nursing education department. Attendance by nurse leaders was prioritized as their participation will enable them to effectively empower frontline staff to attend sessions on the course and support the application of the learnt AMS concepts. Furthermore, another meeting with first-line nursing leaders was conducted to ensure engagement and obtain buy-in. Thus, the AMS team met with the first-line managers, where an overview of AMR and AMS programs was shared, and it was emphasized that this education is an accreditation requirement. First-line managers were invited to share their ideas for successfully implementing the course and optimizing their attendance. The registration process was flexible to accommodate nurses’ changing schedules and clinical needs, therefore, we permitted replacement across units and departments. This approach ensured accountability and maintained a high attendance throughout the courses. The educational curriculum was delivered through a 1-day course entitled “Nursing Antimicrobial Stewardship Course” at the MNGHA Postgraduate Training Center (PGTC). The program was launched in April 2024, and subsequently delivered on two occasions in the following months with a total capacity of 200 participants in each course.

Step 6: evaluation and feedback

In coordination with the PGTC, we organized for participants to complete a mandatory electronic course evaluation survey at the end of the course. The survey included sections that assessed participants’ overall satisfaction, meeting the required objectives, educational materials and teaching strategies, logistics, feedback, and recommendations for future courses. Most nurses expressed satisfaction with the course content and its relevance to their everyday practice. However, some nurses found some lectures difficult, including “basic microbiology” and “basic pharmacology and antibiotic classes.” This feedback was used to improve course content using tools that summarize some content. For instance, a “Cheat Sheet” was incorporated in the course content to help nurses recall antibiotic classes and drug–bug coverage. Moreover, many nurses mentioned that the course content was dense, and it would be better if the content were delivered over 2 days; however, this adjustment would be difficult given nurse staffing shortages and the large number of nurses who need to take the course.

Informal qualitative discussions with the attendees suggested that the course helped clarify many medical decisions and interventions that had previously been unclear. They reported feeling more comfortable discussing the possibility of switching from intravenous antimicrobials to oral antimicrobials and appreciated the emphasis on antimicrobial-associated adverse drug events, such as electrolyte imbalances, hematologic dyscrasias, and organ toxicities. They found it eye-opening to observe certain functions and laboratory results to ensure patient safety.

Discussion

This paper describes the step-by-step development of a nursing AMS curriculum using Kern’s framework of curriculum development and a shared governance model. The use of established framework provided aliment with established systematic curriculum design, ensuring integration of learning objectives, teaching strategies, and evaluation. While the shared governance model facilitated structured collaboration and joint decision-making, as the content developers were not from nursing department but were led by an AMS nurse. The integration of educational framework with system-based model provided practical insights on curriculum development and implementation in complex setting. To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe the development of a nursing AMS curriculum using a shared governance model. Throughout the process, we followed implementation science principles to ensure the curriculum not only systematically designed, but also realistic to implement, relevant to nursing practice, modifiable to various settings, and sustainable This approach provide the foundation to translate the acquired knowledge into collaborative initiatives on various levels to improve nursing AMS interventions within healthcare system [49].

Most published studies related to nursing AMS focused on KAP surveys; however, detailed reports on the development of nursing-specific AMS curricula remain scarce. The developed curriculum aligns with the WHO curricula guide for health workers’ education and training on antimicrobials resistance for nurses and midwives [50]. We also reviewed topics and approaches from multiple sources, to strengthen the implementation of AMS interventions within our institution [10, 51–53]. A notable strength of our curriculum is the inclusion of an overview of the “One Health” approach which is recommended for mitigating AMR and emphasizes the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health through cross-sectoral collaboration [54]. While relevant, the concept is rarely included in traditional nursing education. Its formal integration into pre-service nursing curricula is therefore essential and has been recommended as it broadens students’ perspective beyond their immediate discipline and clinical environment [55, 56]. Since it is not commonly included in most formal nursing education, we decided to include it in the curriculum. Additionally, our program linked microbiological concepts to infection prevention and control practices. This focus emphasized the importance of nurses’ understanding of mobile genetic elements as major contributors to the emergence and spread of multidrug-resistant organisms within healthcare settings [57]. Therefore, the curriculum not only describes optimal actions, but also reinforces nurses’ understanding of interventions such as hand hygiene and disinfection of high-touch surfaces to decrease colonization pressure.

Another notable strength of the developed curriculum is the inclusion of guidelines for optimal blood serum level sampling for antimicrobials which necessitate Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM). This topic is important because the serum antimicrobial level in a sample determines dosing decisions and directly affects patient safety and clinical outcomes. Moreover, safe antimicrobial administration was addressed in detail, including common challenges encountered by nurses in their daily practice that are often overlooked, but have the potential to cause treatment failure and adverse effects. For example, the importance of discussing whether an intravenous antimicrobial dose should be administered following infiltration and extravasation incidents. Another example is not providing important instructions for patients for certain antimicrobials, such as tetracyclines; which necessitate drinking at least 250 ml of water and the need to stay upright for at least 30 min after taking oral tetracyclines to prevent esophageal irritation and erosion [58]. Finally, our curriculum addressed the importance of AMS in sepsis management to ensure that nurses perceive AMS as a supportive intervention that aligns with sepsis guidelines and dispel the potential perception that AMS can compromise patient care in urgent situations. We emphasized the importance of early recognition of sepsis and timely bundle implementation. We explained the difference between proven, probable, and possible sepsis, as this distinction is important to better understand treatment decisions related to the timing of antimicrobial administration and improve resource allocation depending on the severity of sepsis.

A major concern prior the implementation of the course was the complexity of certain topics. However, the level of attendees’ engagement suggested that they saw the relevance of the objectives in their daily practice. Changes in perception and behavior were evident from the feedback we received after the course and will be leveraged to improve AMS interventions in our hospital in the future. For example, the nurses reported that after taking the course, they started initiating discussions with prescribers on switching from intravenous to oral antimicrobials in eligible patients. However, the nurses requested support to increase awareness within the multidisciplinary team regarding this AMS intervention and the increasing awareness among the prescribing teams on the relevant role of nurses in this intervention. This challenge is one of many challenges that cannot be resolved by education alone and requires exploring organizational barriers to implementing AMS guidelines and identifying further educational needs for all relevant healthcare providers [59].

Another strength of the curriculum is the use of different teaching strategies and matching these strategies with the learning objectives presented in Table 5. The most effective teaching strategy in the course was the use of case scenarios and facilitated discussions, which appeared to resonate with nurses by allowing them to relate to real clinical experiences while reducing the cognitive load associated with abstract concepts. Other nurses appreciated the use of role play in nursing empowerment topic to demonstrate non-effective versus effective communication, specifically when there was a need for the nurse to discuss or escalate concerns related to AMS interventions and physician orders. We also learned that nurses had a strong interest in topics that had the potential to enhance their workflow and directly influence patient outcomes, such as topics explaining optimal sampling practices in microbiological culture and blood serum levels for antimicrobials requiring TDM.

Many AMS quality indicators may reflect the collaboration among disciplines, therefore, we identified that there is a need to develop nursing-specific AMS quality indicators to identify gaps and guide future improvement. Nursing-specific quality indicators are important for assessing nursing team compliance. This approach aligns with protocols of hospitals that adopt nurse-sensitive indicators, including but not limited to pain management, pressure injury prevention, and patient fall prevention, to monitor the quality of care provided by nurses and achieved the desired nursing care outcomes [60]. Additionally, the ANA/CDC workgroup strongly recommends identifying nursing quality measures related to AMS [10]. Examples of these indicators could include the contamination and rejection rates of microbiological culture specimens, number of patients who were switched from intravenous to oral antimicrobial based on a prompt by a nurse, promoting daily review of antimicrobial necessity during clinical rounds.

Many nurses reported that the course was long with dense content and suggested delivering it over 2 days. They also suggested that the course should include both physicians and pharmacists in the same setting. Future studies should consider these suggestions to maximize the benefits of the course and ensure that it is multidisciplinary. Another plan is to perform summative assessments after course completion to assess learning outcomes and improve curriculum content. Additionally, the course was implemented in only one of the MNGHA regions, with a plan to expand to other regions within the institution. This curriculum was developed considering the scope of practice of our hospital’s nurses and to meet the needs of our institution. This may limit generalizability to different settings and structure. The course delivery depended on limited number of instructors; however, the limited availability of subject-matter expert may limit teaching styles, variety in viewpoints, and cause speaker-fatigue. To ensure course sustainability, we plan to train more instructors in topics they feel comfortable presenting. Further efforts are also underway to secure sustainable funding to cover costs of the continuing medical education credit registration. This work can be leveraged to extend the collaboration with undergraduate nursing programs to integrate AMR and AMS to their curriculum, however, different approach such as the modular design can be used to cover the objectives and learning outcomes [61].

Conclusion

The nursing AMS curriculum co-development process demonstrates an evidence-based approach to ensure nursing engagement and empowerment in developing institution-tailored nursing-focused materials that can support AMS intervention efforts and mitigate AMR in healthcare facilities. Although the implementation encountered many challenges, such as the length of the course and logistics of course delivery, the process provided insights on how to address these challenges and guide future modifications to improve the robustness of the developed curriculum and its delivery to support effective multidisciplinary participation in AMS and patient care.
