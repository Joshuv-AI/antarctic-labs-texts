“Lost in translation?“: A qualitative exploration of digital perinatal mental health resource use among migrant women
Authors: Areni Altun, Melissa Oxlad, Rochelle Hine, Andrea Reupert, Shameran Slewa-Younan, Delaram Ansari, Levita D’Souza, Shazia Syed, Malavika Kadwadkar, Helen Skouteris, Jacqueline A Boyle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital platforms such as websites play an increasingly important role in supporting perinatal mental health, yet their reach, cultural responsiveness, and effectiveness among women of migrant backgrounds remain under-explored. This study aimed to understand how women from diverse migrant communities engage with five digital perinatal mental health resources and to identify factors influencing their access, engagement, and overall experiences.MethodsWe conducted qualitative analysis of user interactions with five digital perinatal mental health platforms. Data were drawn from focus groups and individual interviews with women from Chinese, Arabic and Indian-language speaking backgrounds. Thematic analysis was guided by iterative coding to derive key themes and sub-themes reflecting barriers, facilitators, and user engagement patterns among migrant mothers.ResultsFour overarching themes were identified. Participants highly valued access to content in their first language but frequently encountered confusing translations, hidden language options, and complex navigation, which discouraged sustained engagement even when content was relevant. Visual and audio formats such as videos and podcasts were preferred over text-heavy content, particularly when paired with culturally representative imagery and accessible language options, while tokenistic visuals and overly clinical website designs were viewed as less appealing. Digital resources were often introduced late in the perinatal journey, typically postpartum, when many women already felt overwhelmed. Participants recommended earlier and more frequent integration of these tools during antenatal care, facilitated through trusted professionals encountered throughout the perinatal period, such as general practitioners, midwives, and maternal and child health nurses. While digital tools provided privacy, convenience, and a sense of emotional safety, particularly when accessed via mobile phones, persistent inequities in digital literacy, internet access, and device availability continued to limit equitable use.ConclusionsWhile digital perinatal mental health resources offer valuable support, their uptake by women from migrant communities is limited by systemic failures to account for language diversity, cultural relevance, and poor integration into routine care pathways. Enhancing accessibility requires multilingual and culturally responsive design, early promotion through trusted providers, and strategies to mitigate digital disparities. Co-designed, inclusive digital resources may strengthen engagement and support perinatal mental health equity for migrant communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02715-9.Keywords: Perinatal health, Mental health, Migrant women, Digital equity, Health equity

Background

Over the past five decades, the rate of migration has increased, with international migrants accounting for more than 3.6% of the global population [1]. Today, many women live outside their country of birth and face complex, layered challenges, particularly when navigating unfamiliar maternal and mental health care systems that may not be culturally responsive or well connected to appropriate support networks. Across institutional systems, including culturally misaligned healthcare and exclusionary policies, persistent disparities remain in terms of perinatal mental health and maternal outcomes between migrant and non-migrant women. These disparities are reflective of broader social and structural inequities, such as socioeconomic disadvantage, settlement challenges, and access limitations [2, 3]. Evidence suggests these differences are not a reflection of individual or cultural deficiencies, but rather markers of system-level failure to support diverse populations equitably [4–6].

Globally, perinatal mental health conditions affect up to 20% of women [7, 8]. However, women from refugee, refugee-like, or migrant backgrounds (hereafter referred to as migrant) experience significantly higher risks, with prevalence rates for perinatal depression, anxiety, or post-traumatic stress disorder reported as high as 40% in some subgroups, reflecting stressors experienced before, during, and after migration and interacting social disadvantages such as isolation, precarious employment, and limited access to supportive services [7, 9]. While both social and clinical support (psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy) are effective in treating perinatal mental health conditions, access to these services remains inequitable due to numerous factors such as high costs, lack of trained professionals, and stigma [10]. Compounding stressors, such as adjusting to new settlement systems, encountering language and digital access barriers, and managing caregiving responsibilities, demonstrate how health systems and social policies often fall short of addressing the needs of migrant mothers [11, 12].

Beyond cultural considerations, it is critical to acknowledge the social and structural dimensions of inequity that shape migrant women’s perinatal experiences. Factors such as socioeconomic disadvantage, precarious employment, social isolation, and limited access to supportive services interact with language, migration pathways, and settlement contexts to influence both mental health outcomes and engagement with care [2, 3]. These intersecting social determinants can compound barriers to timely support and digital health engagement, highlighting how perinatal inequities are not solely cultural in nature.

Research on migration and superdiversity highlights that perinatal experiences are shaped not only by cultural background but also by intersecting social, economic, and structural factors [2, 3]. Digital health initiatives must therefore be designed with attention to both cultural relevance and social accessibility. Principles of Cultural safety extend beyond the acknowledgment or respect of cultural identities; they require ongoing reflection and deliberate action to address systemic racism, power imbalances, and institutional inequities that shape women’s experiences of care [13, 14]. Complementing this, Cultural humility involves a continuous, reflexive practice through which health professionals and researchers recognise their own positionality and work collaboratively with communities to uphold their self-determined needs and perspectives [13]. Together, these frameworks guide the project’s commitment to equity and accountability in the design and delivery of perinatal mental health resources for migrant women [15]. Recognising these dimensions ensures that perinatal mental health resources do not reinforce essentialising or “othering” assumptions about culture. Additionally, understanding migrant women as “connected migrants” navigating both local and transnational digital networks [16, 17] further contextualises how engagement with online resources intersects with social, cultural, and technological realities [15]. In response to these barriers, healthcare systems have increasingly turned to digital technologies to expand access to health education, mental health support, and self-management strategies, with growing recognition that such tools can effectively supplement, rather than replace, face-to-face or other traditional models of care [18, 19]. Digital health can be defined as the use of information and communication technologies within healthcare to provide users with services relating to the prevention, detection, diagnosis and management of diseases and other health conditions [20]. Digital tools, ranging from mobile apps and websites to telehealth platforms, offer promising options for scalable and flexible care, especially for underserved populations [20, 21]. They have the potential to empower users with personalised information, community support, and on-demand services [22]. For migrant women, digital health may offer an accessible alternative to in-person services, particularly when language or cultural stigma may serve as deterrents for traditional professional care pathways [23, 24]. However, there is potential for digital interventions to exacerbate inequity if issues such as limited health or digital literacy, language barriers, cultural incongruence, community distrust of unregulated information, and lack of access to the internet or private devices are not addressed [16, 17].Digital tools must therefore be designed and deployed with attention to both cultural and social equity, guided by principles of cultural safety and cultural humility [25, 26].

The current study explores five digital platforms in Australia that have made efforts to incorporate cultural and linguistic adaptations to improve inclusivity through translated pages. These websites were identified in our formative scoping review by first consulting with migrant women in Hindi, Mandarin and Arabic speaking-language groups to understand how they typically search for perinatal mental health support online. Full details of this search and selection method are described in a separate manuscript currently under review.

The five platforms identified in our scoping review, and further explored in the present study were The Centre of Perinatal Excellence (COPE), Perinatal Anxiety & Depression Australia (PANDA), Raising Children Network, RAHMA Health, and the Multicultural Centre for Women’s Health (MCWH) (see Supplementary Table 1 for platform details and URLs).Only two (RAHMA Health and MCWH) of these five platforms engaged migrant women’s lived and living experiences in content development, highlighting a lack of evaluation of how migrant women actually interact with such resources. Without such insight, digital health resources risk reinforcing existing inequities by overlooking the nuanced needs of multicultural communities.

While migrant populations are diverse, this study focuses specifically on women from Chinese, Arabic, and Indian-language speaking backgrounds. We use the terms ‘Chinese-‘, ‘Arabic-’, and ‘Indian-language speaking backgrounds’ to broadly refer to the range of languages commonly spoken in China, Arabic-speaking countries, and India, while acknowledging the significant linguistic diversity that exists within each of these groups. We selected these groups due to their demographic significance and documented health inequities. India is currently the leading country of birth for migrants to Australia, while Mandarin and Arabic are the most commonly spoken languages at home after English [27]. Women from these communities have also been shown to experience notable disparities in perinatal mental health outcomes and service access [28–32].

A Community and Consumer Involvement (CCI) Advisory Group comprising women from ‘Chinese-‘, ‘Arabic-’, and ‘Indian-language communities guided study design, ensuring the research remained culturally and linguistically appropriate from the outset. By adopting an equity-oriented lens and drawing on consumer voices, this study aims to investigate how migrant women from Chinese, Arabic, and Indian-language speaking communities engage with five key Australian digital platforms that have incorporated some level of cultural adaptation. This research seeks to understand how digital tools (websites) function in supporting migrant women during the perinatal period (if at all), with a view to informing enhancements that promote culturally safe and social accessible mental health care. Findings may offer valuable insights for other countries aiming to develop or adapt digital perinatal resources that address the needs of diverse migrant populations.

Methods

Study design

This study, reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [33], employed a qualitative research design involving semi-structured interviews and reflexive thematic analysis to explore migrant women’s experiences with digital resources for perinatal mental health support [34, 35]. The study methodology was co-designed in collaboration with a CCI Advisory Group to ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness. The CCI Advisory Group comprised one woman from each of the three priority populations – Chinese-, Arabic- and Indian-language speaking backgrounds - ensuring representation aligned with the focus of the research. CCI members were recruited through partnerships with multicultural health organisations, community networks, and maternal health services to ensure diverse representation in terms of migration pathways (e.g., recent migrants, refugees), parity, English proficiency, and digital literacy levels.

The CCI Advisory Group played a central role in shaping the study design, refining culturally sensitive interview guides, and advising on inclusive recruitment strategies. Specifically, they provided input on the wording and culturally appropriate framing of recruitment materials, recommended appropriate community engagement channels, and advised on ethical considerations, such as privacy, stigma, and trust when discussing perinatal mental health. CCI Advisory Group members attended research meetings and were compensated for their time and expertise in line with national consumer participation guidelines [36]. The Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee provided ethical approval, in June 2024 (Project ID 43519).

Study participants and recruitment

Eligibility criteria included being a woman aged 18 years or older at the time of the study (not necessarily at the time of birth), residing in Victoria, who had migrated to Australia via skilled, family, or humanitarian visa pathways, and who self-identified as having experienced perinatal mental health challenges, based on their own reports of symptoms or concerns. Participants were required to be conversant in Chinese, Arabic, Hindi (or another Indian language), or English, and willing to use a professional interpreter if they required language support.

The CCI Advisory Group supported recruitment by reviewing and advising on the cultural appropriateness and clarity of recruitment materials (e.g., flyers, emails, social media posts), recommending trusted community organisations and platforms for dissemination, and sharing materials within their networks. Several members facilitated introductions to community leaders and helped organise language-specific focus groups to ensure safe and culturally responsive environments. We utilised a purposive sampling strategy to recruit ten women from each cultural group, aiming for diversity in age, length of time in Australia, visa pathways, level of English proficiency, and recency of childbirth. This approach ensured the inclusion of a range of experiences related to digital health engagement. We also used snowball sampling to increase the likelihood of obtaining enough women in each language group, including those less familiar with health or digital services.

Recruitment materials, available in Arabic, Mandarin, Hindi, and English, were distributed through trusted community organisations, maternal health services, and at multicultural community events. Interested women could contact the designated researcher (AA) directly or give permission to a CCI member for AA to contact them by telephone or email. All participants received a plain language statement and provided written informed consent prior to participation. Women were informed that participation was voluntary, they could choose not to answer any question or stop the interview at any time. To acknowledge their time and contributions, each participant received a $30 AUD grocery voucher.

Data collection

This study explored migrant women’s experiences and perceptions of five Australian digital mental health websites designed to support women during the perinatal period. We use the term ‘migrant background’ to refer to women who were born overseas or who speak a language other than English at home. While some participants may have had refugee or asylum-seeking experiences, we did not specifically collect data about refugee status in this study. We acknowledge that such experiences may carry legal, social, or trauma-related dimensions that were not disclosed or explored in this context.

We used a combination of semi-structured individual interviews and focus group interviews to gather a breadth and depth of perspectives across cultural groups. To ensure inclusive engagement, participants could choose their preferred format, either a focus group or individual interview, based on what best suited their availability, and communication preferences. Participants who required language support could choose individual interviews or language-specific focus groups in Arabic, Mandarin, or Hindi. Professional interpreters, arranged through a National Accreditation Authority for Translators and Interpreters (NAATI) accredited service, joined via secure video conferencing. Only one interpreter per session was used to maintain consistency and confidentiality. Interpreters facilitated introductions, translated participants’ responses, clarified questions or culturally specific terms, and assisted with written consent when necessary.

The interview guide was developed based on a review of existing literature and input from clinical researchers and consumer partners from the CCI Advisory Group. The interview guide (see Supplementary Material) was iteratively refined throughout data collection to allow for conversational flow and cultural appropriateness.

Data collection occurred between June and October 2024. A total of 30 women participated, with eight focus groups and three individual interviews conducted. Group sizes ranged from two to four participants. The mean duration was 70 min for focus groups (range: 60–90 min) and 55 min for individual interviews (range: 45–65 min), see Table 1. Participant demographic characteristics

Data collection was conducted online via Zoom and facilitated by a trained qualitative researcher. Participants were encouraged to review the websites prior to their interview or focus group and to take notes during this process. At the start of each session, participants were asked to indicate which websites they had reviewed. For participants who had not engaged with all of the platforms beforehand, the facilitator provided a brief screen-sharing walk-through to ensure familiarity and support discussion. Participants were welcome to rely on either prior review or reflection and recall during the discussion, allowing for flexible engagement with the resources. Sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim into English. For non-English sessions, interpreters provided real-time translation, and transcripts preserved culturally meaningful expressions. Literal translations were prioritized where possible, and culturally significant terminology was reviewed in consultation with the CCI Advisory Group to ensure accurate interpretation of meaning [37]. Transcripts were not returned to participants for member-checking due to language barriers and participants’ preference for minimal follow-up. Credibility was enhanced through researcher debriefing, detailed field notes, and peer review among the multilingual research team.

All transcripts were imported into NVivo version 14 for coding and analysis. We adopted the principle of meaning saturation [34], whereby data collection continued until sufficient depth and diversity of perspectives were obtained to support meaningful interpretation, in line with our purposive sample of 30 women across three cultural-linguistic groups.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis [34], following an inductive and iterative approach to ensure interpretations remained grounded in participants’ accounts. Initial readings and open coding of the transcripts were conducted independently by AA. Coding was then reviewed and refined through regular team meetings involving a research assistant, a senior research fellow, and members of the CCI Advisory Group.

Codes were subsequently organised into themes, each representing a coherent and recurring pattern of meaning. Thematic development was supported by close reading of field notes and memos to contextualise findings. Themes were iteratively revised in consultation with the wider research team and CCI Advisory Group members, ensuring that the final interpretations accurately reflected participants’ lived experiences and culturally specific perspectives. To enhance analytical rigour, a double-coding process was used in which two researchers independently coded the same transcripts. While this approach can risk reducing interpretive richness or oversimplifying meaning, it supports transparency and consistency [38]. Discrepancies were discussed collaboratively, allowing for deeper reflection and shared understanding before reaching consensus.

Reflexivity

The intersecting identities and relationships between participants and researchers played a meaningful role in shaping data collection and analysis [35]. All members of the research team brought relevant professional expertise in migrant health, perinatal mental health, and qualitative research. AA, a Research Fellow and clinical osteopath; JB, an academic gynaecologist and obstetrician; and RH, a social worker with extensive experience in women’s health and rural and mental health care, contributed diverse clinical and academic perspectives. The broader research team also included researchers from disciplines such as public health, psychology, digital health, and implementation science with experience in migrant and perinatal health who contributed to study conceptualisation, design, and interpretation.

AA, who led both data collection and analysis, brought important contextual insight to the study. As a woman born to migrant parents, AA shared cultural touchpoints with many participants which facilitated rapport-building. While this closeness enhanced depth and richness in the data, it also required ongoing critical reflection to balance empathy and analytic distance.

The CCI Advisory Group (MK, SS, and ML), composed of women with lived experience of migration and perinatal mental health challenges, provided ongoing guidance and critical insights. Guided by Dahinden [15], we reflected on the categories applied to avoid a culturalist framing, ensuring that participants’ experiences (not assumptions about culture) drove our analysis [15]. We acknowledge that no migration studies specialist was part of the team, which may have foregrounded a culturalist lens; this was mitigated through engagement with relevant migration studies literature [2, 3, 15] and iterative reflexive discussions to situate participants’ experiences within broader social and structural contexts. These reflexive practices supported methodological rigor, equity-oriented interpretation, and grounding in participants’ lived realities.

Findings

Participant characteristics

A total of 30 women participated, with equal representation across the three language groups (10 participants per group). Most participants lived in metropolitan areas within Victoria, with a smaller subset residing in rural regions (n = 7). Ages ranged from 25 to 55 years, with most participants falling within the 25–34 and 35–44 age brackets, and one participant in the 45–55 age bracket. Participants had lived in Australia for varying durations, from six months to over 29 years. Educational backgrounds ranged from early high school qualifications to university degrees, with many (n = 26) holding tertiary-level qualifications. Most participants were married or in a relationship, with family sizes ranging from one to four or more children. Additional demographic characteristics of the study participants are outlined in Table 2.

This study examines how women of migrant background interacted with and responded to perinatal mental health content on five digital platforms provided for review. We generated four key themes: (1) Engaging with perinatal mental health websites; (2) Designing for connection; (3) Health system integration, and (4) Digital resource as a practical solution. Engaging with perinatal mental health websites

Language and digital literacy

Participants consistently stressed the importance of accessing content in their first language. While many could read English-language materials, the effort required to interpret unfamiliar medical or psychological terms made it difficult to meaningfully engage with website content.

Even though I can read in English, I still sometimes struggle with understanding the terminologies… I have to translate the terminologies into my own language in order for me to understand the meaning of it. (Chinese Focus Group B)

Language representation was a key driver of both initial engagement and sustained use of websites. Participants emphasised that when their preferred language was not available on a website, they were less likely to return, even if the content itself was relevant or evidence-based.

Because frankly speaking, the languages that I speak are not on the multicultural [MCWH] listing, and if I’m a person who cannot read English very fluently, I may not go back. Whereas the COPE website has a very massive list of languages it can convert to, that would be… why I would go to COPE rather than [MCWH]. (Indian Focus Group B)

Even when information on websites was available in their preferred language, women often perceived it as confusing, or inconsistent when compared with the quality of the English version. This affected participants’ ability to fully rely on the translated material, particularly when navigating sensitive perinatal mental health content.

…I still really prefer to read the Chinese version at first and then if I have something I feel it not makes sense. You know some translation sometimes doesn’t make sense. So, I will [go] back to the English version (Chinese Individual Interview).

While multilingual options were at times available, clunky navigation and unnoticed icons often obscured them, excluding speakers of other languages before they even began engaging. According to one participant, meaningful inclusion required design that prioritised language access from the outset, rather than concealing it behind icons or interfaces that may be unclear to users unfamiliar with standard web conventions.

This website called PANDA …my observation is about the language selection. It’s not obvious to select Chinese language and you have to find this icon, that looks like a globe and then you have to select the pulldown menu to select the different languages. But for someone who does not understand English we cannot read or speak English, it’s very difficult or inconvenient to find the language selection menu. It could feel inaccessible or too complex. (Chinese Focus Group B).

Participants described how language preferences shaped their online search behaviours. Even when residing in Australia, women often defaulted to health websites from their countries of origin due to linguistic familiarity and the absence of visible, accessible local content in their preferred language.

When we search on the internet or Google we find websites from Egypt, from Saudi Arabia. But if I were there I don’t have any idea about RAHMA or PANDA… and if we go on Google we try to find information on Arabic websites. (Arabic Focus Group A)

Participants also mentioned challenges with understanding medical terminology and navigating information in English, with some suggesting the importance of having a bilingual glossary.

…detailing all the terminology for pregnancy and also after birth, breastfeeding… Chinese against English… that will be very helpful (Chinese Focus Group B).

Navigating concerns about trust and information overload

While the five reviewed websites were generally viewed as credible and trustworthy, participants discussed how their broader online experiences shaped their engagement with these resources. Many migrant women first encountered the reviewed sites through general Google searches, where they were simultaneously exposed to unverified, contradictory, and overwhelming information. This broader digital landscape influenced their perceptions of trust and made it difficult to discern which sources were reliable. “I tried to find a few things on Google and sometimes I find more than one opinion and – but I don’t know who’s right, who’s wrong. Sometimes you find these opinions are opposites to each others and a lot of information that we feel that you are lost.” (Arabic Focus Group A)

Some women expressed scepticism toward unverified sources and described feeling overwhelmed by the deluge of unsubstantiated content, which made decision-making around parenting and mental health care more difficult.

Too much information makes you puzzled… the correct information should come from the GP or community, not Google (Indian Focus Group A).

Participants described a strong preference for digital resources that were trustworthy, evidence-informed, and connected to official government or medical bodies. The MCWH website was valued not only for its parenting information but also for its perceived transparency in communicating broader health system updates. For some women, especially those navigating unfamiliar healthcare systems, accessing content grounded in public policy and state-level medical guidance reinforced trust in the resource.

Yes they do have [the] latest information, what [the] government is doing about the overall health, especially on a policy level. So if I want to read what’s happening in the medical sector of my state I would go for that one [MCWH]. (Indian Focus Group B)

Designing for connection

“Read less, watch more”

Across all focus groups, women consistently expressed a preference for visual and audio-based formats over dense or lengthy written content. While such preferences may be common relating to website design, participants specifically linked these formats to the cognitive load, time constraints, and emotional demands of the perinatal period. For migrant women, these challenges were often compounded by navigating health information in a second language.

The video and photos stick to the brain more than the written one… ‘this feeling is normal, this feeling is abnormal, you should seek medical help’ (Arabic Focus Group A).

Participants highlighted the value of audio-visual content such as podcasts or videos. In the early postnatal period, when exhaustion and emotional turbulence are high, these formats were seen as particularly effective in reaching women who may otherwise disengage from text-heavy resources.

If there’s a podcast or video of the article, that would be more helpful (Chinese Individual Interview).

Participants also noted practical barriers, such as limited time and capacity to engage with lengthy written materials, making short-form visual and audio content preferable.

I may not have time to read long articles… just a few pictures or a one-minute video makes more sense …Anything longer than a minute or two… I don’t think we have the attention span anymore (Indian Focus Group B).

While all participants discussed a strong preference for video-based content due to its ease of use, its effectiveness was often undermined by language barriers and a lack of cultural representation. Many digital resources were available only in English, with limited or no options for subtitles or voiceover in community languages. Participants also noted that much of the content lacked diversity in imagery, scenarios, and cultural values, making it difficult to relate to or fully trust the information presented.

Videos are really helpful… but unfortunately [they are] fully [in] English. No subtitles, not options in Chinese. And again… both are being Caucasian experience, interviewers and interviewees…If you were [a] immigrant who is very new onboard… you might not be able to relate. (Chinese Focus Group A)

Culturally responsive design

Women reported that design choices such as grey and white were too clinical and emotionally off-putting, and in some cases culturally inappropriate, as certain colour combinations (for example, black and white in Chinese culture) are traditionally associated with mourning or funerals rather than wellbeing. “COPE had a particular colour scheme… very grey… quite stressful… if you’re looking at the bright colours it makes you feel a little brighter but if you’re looking at black and white, sometime in Chinese culture, we use black and white in funerals. So … it’s not very culturally sensitive to us because when I was looking at that I was thinking about a negative thought. (Chinese Focus Group A).

Authentic representation was highlighted as a critical component of trust and engagement with digital mental health content. Some participants noted that using stock images did not feel genuine or relatable and was “tokenistic”. To generate real engagement, participants wanted imagery that reflected everyday mothers from their communities, featuring real women and varied body types.

The Caucasian women look relatable, like someone next door. The CALD women don’t always feel authentic. When they use faces of CALD women… they use supermodels… not relatable (Indian Individual Interview).

Women also wanted to see what help looked like, who provides it, where it happens, and how to access it. Such visual cues were reported as essential for building confidence in unfamiliar systems, especially for women of migrant background navigating new health and social care environments.

So I feel like there should be lots of pictures where they are really doing what they wrote over there in the website. Like they are helping with the resources and everything. What kind of resources they have, how we can, where we can access it? (Indian Focus Group C)

Interestingly, participants emphasised the value of culturally informed content that bridges traditional and ‘Western’ postnatal practices, particularly to help navigate intergenerational differences. They suggested that having reliable, accessible explanations online could reduce conflict or confusion with family members and support them to feel more confident in their choices.

If they had a section on confinement, like why some say don’t wash your hair or drink cold water - I could just show my mum, instead of having to explain… A video or article explaining the origins would help a lot (Chinese Focus Group A)

Design features that enhanced usability such as clearly labelled tabs, filtering by postcode, and brief summaries were consistently appreciated when identified on a website.

It’s a very user-friendly website… different tabs for planning a family, expecting a baby… you know what to expect and what you want to read (Indian Focus Group B).

Even when women turned to search engines to find answers to their perinatal concerns, culturally relevant and tailored content was often lacking. While high-ranking placement in Google search results increased the likelihood that a woman might click on a website, participants stressed that the usefulness of websites hinged on the relevance and cultural inclusivity of the content.

If I Google my question and these websites come up at the top, I might click… but the information is still very general (Chinese Focus Group B).

Health system integration

Timing of digital support

Participants consistently highlighted that access to perinatal mental health websites did not align with their immediate needs. Many reported that these resources (if introduced) were only after childbirth, at a point when they felt physically exhausted, emotionally overwhelmed, and less able to meaningfully engage.

Mental health-related resources are only handed out after birth… not during pregnancy or antenatal appointments… as [a] new mother, we are already busy enough looking after our newborn. We have no time to look after ourselves. (Chinese Focus Group B)

Many participants discussed a lack of continuity in maternal care following childbirth, noting that health system interactions became almost exclusively focused on the infant.

After the first two weeks, all other MCH appointments were for the kid. There wasn’t any follow-up for the mother, nothing in terms of ‘How are you? Do you have support? Do you have resources?’ (Indian Focus Group C)

This was widely viewed as a missed opportunity.

Because after delivery you’re not going to actually use those [websites], you really don’t have the time. I guess when you’re pregnant, I remember I had a good pregnancy, if I was given those resources at that time, I would have sat and read. I would have gone through those websites, I would have at least known. (Indian Focus Group C).

Participants emphasised that timely access to trustworthy digital information, particularly early in the perinatal journey, could also prevent unnecessary stress and reliance on fragmented or delayed healthcare appointments.

If we know about this website like before, like years before which help us, we can find everything, we can find [the] answer for any answer, better than making an appointment and go to the doctor and wait. So, we can find everything… on the website. (Arabic Focus Group C)

Even when introduced to the websites after birth, participants emphasised that one-off exposure was insufficient to support ongoing use. Instead, they highlighted the importance of repeated prompts and reinforcement across multiple healthcare touchpoints, such as antenatal classes, discharge summaries, and via maternal child health (MCH).

I was first given about these two websites was when I went back to my obstetrician for my six-week postnatal appointment… That’s when I first was aware of mental health issues and relevant information. So during my pregnancy or even when I went to see my GP nobody ever told me about these two websites, such information was only given to the mothers after birth. (Chinese Focus Group B)

Participants also suggested discreet, low-effort strategies such as posters with QR codes in clinic waiting rooms to provide a private and convenient entry point to websites.

If you have these posters put up in the hospitals with the QR code… that goes directly to the website. (Indian Focus Group B)

Bridging awareness and access

Despite having interacted with a range of health professionals (including GPs, midwives, MCH and obstetricians) many women remained unaware of the existence of key websites until much later in the postpartum period. This lack of coordinated promotion by healthcare providers limited awareness and early engagement.

I’ve visited my obstetrician, my GP, my midwife… not a single one of them mentioned those websites. (Chinese Focus Group B)

Most had not encountered many of the websites prior to the study.

The website[s] that you sent for us… we didn’t hear about them before, only the Raising Children [website] from the hospital. The others- we didn’t hear about them. (Arabic Focus Group C)

Several participants described being unable to recognise postnatal mental health issues, such as depression or anxiety, particularly in the early postpartum period.

I had a bit of postnatal depression, which I didn’t know… you’re just living in the moment, you’re too busy to think… So if you knew you had issues then you go online… but most people wouldn’t know until someone points it out. (Chinese Focus Group A)

Some participants explained that in their countries of origin, postpartum mental health was rarely acknowledged or openly discussed, leading to a persistent knowledge gap that continued after migration. For example.

If you’re talking about in Iraq I don’t think there’s any, I don’t think they know that this happens, the depression after birth happens. So if they don’t know it happens, I don’t think they think about it, I don’t think it exists in Iraq. (Arabic Focus Group C)

Participants noted that websites are only useful if women first recognise they need support, something that was not always the case. Even when professionals recommended websites, follow-up was often lacking.

If the MCH nurse thinks that mum’s not coping… they give us links and websites… but it would be helpful if they had an application or place where they could refer the mums to, who could follow up…at least for the severely suffering mums… Because so many times Mums don’t even know that they’re going through postnatal depression for start, they may not be aware or they’re too embarrassed to ask for help or to even say that they think they’re not doing okay (Indian Focus Group B)

Participants highlighted that self-recognition of postnatal mental health challenges was often limited amongst migrant women, particularly in the early postpartum period when exhaustion and role adjustment were most intense. Thus, help-seeking via websites was not something done independently or proactively, but typically occurred only after concerns were externally identified and validated.

For myself and new mums it’s very difficult for them to have the self-awareness of their mood swing after birth. And we really rely on… a reminder from people around us including the MCH nurse, GP’s or even our husband to remind us that we start showing symptoms or signs of the postnatal depression. (Chinese Focus Group B)

Endorsement by health professionals

While many women described themselves as digitally literate and confident in navigating social media or smartphones, this did not necessarily translate to trust in online health information. Instead, credibility was strongly linked to the source, with medical endorsement playing a crucial role in legitimising digital content.

I think digital literacy is there…but where it is coming from is the question… I think during the midwife appointments, doctors’ appointments, that’s where you give that [website]…coming from your medical practitioner, people you trust. (Indian Focus Group B)

MCH nurses were also key advocates for website awareness, providing mothers with key resources such as PANDA to access much-needed support.

That’s how I know PANDA… the maternity health nurse said ‘go and look at that website’… imagine if I didn’t see her or didn’t make it to the appointment, that would be a different story. (Chinese Focus Group A)

Participants strongly advocated for a more integrated model in which trusted healthcare providers routinely introduced, endorsed, and explained websites.

GP would be a very good access point for the woman to access the resource… I don’t have too many information from my GP. I collect those information from the MCH. If their local GP’s provide them with that kind of resource, that’s really helpful because especially when you’re in pregnancy or when you’ve just given birth, we tend to trust the doctors or the medical fraternity more at that point of time. (Indian Individual Interview)

Digital resources as a practical solution

Accessibility and practical usability

Participants underscored the value of digital access in overcoming geographic constraints, particularly for women with limited mobility, transport, or childcare support. Online platforms served as low-barrier entry points for support, especially during the perinatal period when time, energy, and transportation are often limited.

I think anything online is helpful… we are in a very big country… it’s hard to move from area to area. So, anything online makes it easy for us. (Arabic Focus Group A)

Beyond convenience, several women emphasised the emotional safety and privacy that digital platforms afford. In cultural contexts where discussing mental health or personal distress may feel shameful or taboo, even within families, women described online resources as empowering.

In our country… you are shy to ask or if you have some problem… sometimes you are shy to ask your mum even. So the online resources are so important for me… they helped me so much after birth, especially my first daughter. (Arabic Focus Group B)

Mobile phones emerged as the dominant mode of access across all groups. Participants explained that smartphones offered both convenience and discretion, making them particularly well-suited to the realities of new motherhood.

We can’t hold a laptop while breastfeeding, but we can use a phone (Chinese Individual Interview)

Mobile access was not only viewed as convenient and discreet, but also as ubiquitous and deeply embedded in everyday life. Participants highlighted how smartphones served as a primary gateway to health information, reinforcing the potential reach of well-designed perinatal websites.

Everything we need is on the internet… women are using their phones (Arabic Focus Group A).

Digital inequity

Despite the widespread ownership of smartphones, participants explained that digital access and literacy were far from uniform. Owning a device did not necessarily equate to being digitally connected or confident. Many women lacked consistent internet access, had limited experience navigating websites, or faced language-related barriers, particularly with English-dominant content.

Most of the women have a mobile phone, but they don’t have access to the internet… they don’t know about these websites (Chinese Focus Group C).

Others drew attention to broader inequities in device access even within communities often assumed to be digitally connected.

Mostly women will access from their phone… [but] not everyone has a device (Indian Focus Group A).

Language barriers compounded these challenges, particularly for newly arrived women or those still developing English proficiency.

Some migrants, like students, definitely have the skills… but for others still learning English, it’s harder (Chinese Individual Interview).

In these contexts, peer support networks often became essential for navigating digital information. Instead of independently searching for resources, women frequently turned to each other to bridge gaps in knowledge and access.

The mothers talk together… they don’t know how to search… they just ask each other (Chinese Focus Group C).

Despite the growing availability of online mental health resources, digital inequity remains a significant barrier to access for many migrant women. Participants described how limited literacy, both digital and general, constrained the ability to independently navigate or benefit from web-based platforms. For some, the issue was not access to devices, but the skills and confidence required to search for information, particularly when compounded by low literacy in their primary language.

Most of them have mobile phone, but they don’t know how to go on internet and make, do search and find what they’re looking for because they don’t even read or write in Arabic. (Arabic Focus Group C)

Features that promote connection and follow-up

Participants highlighted the potential for perinatal mental health websites to go beyond information delivery and act as gateways to peer-connection with other mothers, particularly for those who were socially isolated or recently arrived in Australia.

For recently arrived women, or women who are the only mothers in their friend circles… that might be a really nice way to connect with other women from similar backgrounds via the website… like a national mothers group, I think that would be amazing. (Arabic Focus Group B).

Others proposed that responsive tools, such as chatbots, could help personalise this experience by directing users to relevant resources or local supports.

…when you type a keyword then the chat will automatically search relevant information… or recommend a local mothers’ group (Chinese Focus Group B).

Participants also noted a gap in content acknowledging socio-political stressors impacting their mental health, such as grief related to conflicts overseas.

I think this is something that is definitely impacting a lot of women… it isn’t acknowledged on the websites (Arabic Focus Group B).

The emotional tone and affirming language used on digital mental health websites played a significant role in participants’ sense of validation and psychological safety. Statements such as “you’re not alone” were described as profoundly comforting, particularly for women navigating perinatal mental distress in isolation. This kind of inclusive messaging helped normalise their experiences and offered a sense of belonging, critical factors for women from migrant backgrounds who may already feel culturally or socially marginalised. One participant explained.

…when I read through the COPE website and they said, ‘you’re not alone,’ I felt really relieved (Chinese Individual Interview).

Safety and privacy concerns

For many participants, mobile phones were not only practical tools for accessing digital perinatal content, they also played a crucial role in maintaining safety, control, and discretion within the home. In the context of shared domestic environments, where conversations about mental health may be culturally sensitive or stigmatised, mobile devices allowed for quiet, unobtrusive engagement with support resources. As one participant explained, the ability to access and conceal digital content at will was critical.

Mobile is… more discreet. Just hit the button and the screen is black (Chinese Individual Interview).

Others elaborated on how digital privacy tools such as private browsing or deleting search histories enhanced their sense of control and confidentiality.

…you can delete the data, use private mode… not obvious for others to know what you’re doing (Chinese Focus Group B).

Some women expressed concerns about privacy and safety when seeking mental health support online. For those experiencing family violence, these concerns were particularly pronounced. One participant suggested incorporating protective features, such as a quick-exit button, to enhance safety.

If PANDA had a button to exit quickly… that could address the safety issue (Indian Individual Interview).

However, most participants agreed that accessing websites was generally safe for migrant women in stable households, acknowledging that this may not be the case in coercive or violent domestic settings, where even digital access could be surveilled or restricted.

Unless they are in an environment with domestic violence, it should be safe (Chinese Focus Group B).

Discussion

This study highlights how migrant women from Chinese, Arabic, and Indian-language speaking backgrounds perceive and engage with digital tools for perinatal mental health support. While participants recognised the value of digital platforms as a useful and private source of information, many reported that current offerings fell short in meeting their needs. In particular, gaps were identified in the availability of in-language content, culturally affirming narratives, and visual formats that reflected users’ lived experiences and sociocultural contexts. Participants emphasised the importance of digital platforms not only as sources of information but also as gateways to social connection, especially for those experiencing isolation during the perinatal period. Participants’ trust in digital health information was closely linked to the reputability of its source, with women more likely to engage with content endorsed by a healthcare provider or trusted institution. Finally, the timing and integration of digital tools into routine perinatal care shaped their perceived value, with early and ongoing referrals playing a critical role in supporting access and engagement. Together, these findings point to the need for a more inclusive, community-informed, and system-integrated digital health environment for women of migrant background during the perinatal period.

While participants’ reflections primarily centred on cultural and linguistic aspects of digital access, their experiences also reflected underlying social and structural determinants shaping perinatal mental health and digital engagement. Factors such as migration pathway, employment precarity, visa insecurity, and limited social networks intersected with language and culture to influence women’s ability to access and act on health information [2, 3, 39, 40]. It is important to acknowledge that our participant sample, while culturally diverse, was predominantly highly educated, which may have limited the representation of more severe social disadvantage such as financial insecurity, low health or digital literacy, and housing instability. This interplay between cultural and structural inequities echoes broader migration and health scholarship, which cautions against a solely culturalist framing of health disparities [2, 3]. As Fassin and others have argued, social inequities (including those embedded in policy and welfare systems) fundamentally shape migrant women’s opportunities for care [41, 42]. Our findings extend this literature by showing how these layered inequities manifest within digital health environments.

Digital technologies are playing an increasingly central role in how healthcare is accessed and experienced during the perinatal period [43–45]. For migrant women, these tools offer both critical opportunities for support but also highlight significant concerns around equity, cultural safety, and inclusivity in care delivery [45]. In our study, most participants reported using their mobile phones as the primary way of accessing perinatal mental health information, making mobile-friendly and easily navigable formats particularly important. Despite this, many perinatal mental health websites were described as linguistically inaccessible, culturally generic, and overly reliant on text-heavy content, highlighting a need for digital design that is more culturally and linguistically responsive. However, there were some exceptions. For example, resources developed by the MCWH were viewed more positively, as they provided multilingual materials and, in the case of MCWH, ensured content was checked by bilingual health educators to enhance cultural and linguistic accuracy.

This speaks to the value of community-led and co-designed approaches in developing resources for migrant women. While co-design processes support cultural relevance and responsiveness, it is important to recognise that shared language or nationality does not encompass the full diversity of experiences within migrant populations [28, 46]. Factors such as age, migration pathway, education, and social position also shape how women engage with digital tools and mental health resources. Incorporating these intersecting influences helps ensure that resource development remains inclusive and reflective of the varied realities within migrant communities [28]. Some participants also appreciated visible efforts to represent diverse cultural backgrounds through inclusive imagery and content endorsed by trusted institutions; however, others highlighted that such representations can feel tokenistic when they fail to reflect the complexity of lived experiences within their communities. These findings echo emerging evidence that cultural expertise alone does not ensure cultural accuracy or inclusivity design [47, 48]. Studies of perinatal care among minority ethnic and refugee-background women have similarly emphasised the importance of recognising power differentials between “cultural experts,” participants, and the broader migrant population, and the need for cultural humility and reflexivity in co-design [13, 14, 47, 48]. Moreover, international work demonstrates that structural inequities, including those related to migration status, racialisation, and socioeconomic precarity, intersect with culture to shape how women experience perinatal care and digital engagement [49]. Acknowledging this diversity within and across cultural groups is critical to developing digital health resources that are both representative and equitable [47–49].

Digital health tools often fail to accommodate the complex realities of diverse communities [50, 51]. This aligns with broader evidence highlighting challenges in ensuring high-quality, culturally relevant translations in healthcare contexts. For example, Al Shamsi et al. (2020) reported persistent issues with the accuracy and contextual relevance of translated health information [52]. In digital contexts, this can undermine users’ ability to engage meaningfully with the content or to identify when and how to seek help. For this reason, it is essential to engage trained bilingual health educators in supporting resource development to ensure accuracy and contextual relevance of translated resources. Many participants also described how digital tools served as critical entry points to community-based programs, such as local parenting groups, cultural gatherings, workshops, or peer-led sessions. These platforms had the potential to connect women to culturally aligned services and peer networks that might otherwise remain invisible [53]. This finding aligns with international evidence showing that perinatal peer support improves maternal mental health, confidence, and bonding, largely by providing emotional validation and normalising shared experience [53–56]. Similarly, culturally familiar spaces and shared identities can foster belonging and reduce loneliness during early motherhood [57, 58].

Culturally resonant content where the framing, tone, and visual representation aligned with participants everyday realities and beliefs was valued. When discussing cultural representation, women were interested in non-tokenistic depictions that reflected the complexity of their lived experiences. They called for content that was practical, nuanced, and evidence-based, while still being sensitive to their cultural values and needs. These preferences align with international findings, including recent Chinese research, which demonstrated that multimedia formats, such as short videos and livestreaming, significantly enhance user engagement and comprehension by delivering health information in ways that are relatable, accessible, and contextually meaningful [59].

Genuine co-design with migrant communities is essential to ensure that digital platforms not only reflect diverse ways of understanding health but also deliver information through formats, such as video, storytelling, and other multimedia approaches, that resonate more deeply than written text alone [60]. However, to move beyond informal, unpaid consultation, there is a need to formally resource and recognise the contributions of bilingual health educators, bicultural workers, and multicultural organisations. Adequately funding these stakeholders would ensure that in-language, culturally responsive resources are developed in partnership with the communities they are intended to serve. Without such structural investment, digital innovation risks reinforcing, rather than reducing, existing health inequities [61]. Our research highlights systemic inadequacies in the design and delivery of digital mental health care and underscore the need for national digital health strategies to go beyond surface-level translation and establish cultural relevance and community partnership as a standard of care. As digital health solutions become increasingly integrated into routine practice, it is integral that they are co-designed and implemented collaboratively with appropriately supported community partners.

Trust and credibility emerged as important considerations, fundamentally shaping women’s willingness to engage with digital perinatal mental health resources. Accordingly, participants expressed a clear preference for content that was recommended by healthcare professionals or endorsed by trusted national (Australian) institutions, such as government or reputable health organisations. This preference was driven by concerns around the broader digital environment including the pervasiveness of misinformation and the general lack of clinical oversight on the internet [62]. These concerns were particularly pronounced when accessing information via social media or unfamiliar websites, where content is often unregulated. Marginalised communities, including women of migrant background, are particularly vulnerable to misinformation and may experience heightened concerns around digital privacy, safety, and the credibility of online health information [63–67]. Similar patterns have been reported in national research on South Asian women’s use of pregnancy apps and social media in Australia, which found that digital platforms became vital sources of information and social support amid times that restrict or limit in-person cultural and familial connections [68]. Similarly, Khorana et al. (2024) reported that many women relied heavily on informal social media groups for pregnancy and parenting information, which increased their exposure to misinformation and mirrored the confusion and anxiety expressed by participants in our study. These findings also align with the concept of the “connected migrant,” which highlights how digital spaces both alleviate and reproduce inequities, offering vital transnational connection while reflecting broader social hierarchies in access, trust, and digital literacy [16, 69].

Beyond concerns of trust and credibility, our findings highlight a missed opportunity for early intervention in perinatal mental health, as information about relevant websites and support services was often provided only postpartum. By this stage, many women were already navigating significant fatigue, emotional strain, and competing caregiving responsibilities, reducing their capacity to engage meaningfully with available resources [70]. This underscores the importance of introducing mental health information during pregnancy, when women may be better positioned to process and act on support options. International evidence by Iturralde et al. (2021) reinforces this need, highlighting that early engagement with perinatal mental health resources improved awareness and uptake, particularly among racial and ethnic minority women who face structural barriers to care [71]. Similarly, a Swiss study demonstrated that both the timing and volume of information strongly influence its relevance; when delivered too late or in an overwhelming manner, perinatal resources risk becoming ineffective [72]. Globally and within Australia, postpartum care traditionally centres on infant health, often sidelining maternal recovery and emotional wellbeing [70, 73–75]. This systemic imbalance may disproportionately affect migrant women, who must also navigate additional language, cultural, and access barriers, further compounding inequities in perinatal mental health care.

Recommendations

Our findings underscore the importance of integrating digital perinatal platforms into routine care pathways, where they can be regularly endorsed by midwives, GPs, and other trusted health professionals. This integration has the potential to enhance awareness, engagement and uptake among migrant mothers experiencing mental health challenges. Digital mental health platforms should be co-designed in partnership with multicultural and ethno-specific organisations, bilingual health educators, bicultural workers and women from diverse cultural backgrounds who have recent experience of navigating the perinatal period, thus ensuring that resources are in-language, culturally responsive, and reflect diverse ways of understanding health.

Safety features, such as quick-exit buttons and clear privacy protections, should be incorporated to safeguard women who may be at risk of coercive control or digital surveillance. Platforms should also account for digital literacy, accessibility, and social determinants of health, including migration pathways, employment precarity, and social isolation, to avoid reinforcing inequities. While participants raised concerns about the credibility of online health information, direct experiences of digital surveillance or technology-facilitated coercive control were not widely reported in our study. However, this does not contradict broader evidence showing that digital technologies can be used to monitor, restrict, or control help-seeking in the context of family and domestic violence [55–57]. Our findings suggest that while awareness of the risks of gendered violence exists, such experiences may be underreported and barriers to openly discussing these experiences during the perinatal period persist, despite compelling evidence of elevated risk of all types of family violence during the perinatal period [76]. To address these concerns, digital mental health platforms should incorporate safety features, such as quick-exit buttons and clear privacy protections, ensuring they are safe and trustworthy for women who may be at risk.

Policymakers and health services should provide sustainable funding to support the ongoing development, implementation, and maintenance of culturally and socially inclusive digital resources, enabling earlier engagement, improving service navigation, and ultimately reducing perinatal mental health disparities among migrant populations. Collectively, these recommendations aim to ensure that digital tools function as equitable, trustworthy, and effective components of perinatal mental health care. Future research and policy should focus on establishing evidence-based standards for cultural and social inclusivity in digital perinatal platforms, evaluating the impact of co-designed interventions, and monitoring engagement across diverse migrant populations to inform scalable and sustainable digital health solutions.

Limitations

While we recruited participants from Chinese, Arabic, and Indian-language speaking backgrounds, our sample does not reflect the full diversity of migrant women’s perinatal experiences. Variability within and beyond these language groups, such as differing migration pathways, visa categories, and levels of acculturation, may influence digital engagement and perinatal mental health needs in ways not fully captured in our study. In addition, the data generated by Arabic-speaking participants appeared comparatively limited in detail and scope. This outcome may reflect a range of intersecting factors, including language dynamics, interpreter-mediated communication, cultural norms around disclosure and stigma related to mental health. Our sample also included some women with higher levels of education and greater confidence in navigating health systems, which may not reflect the experiences of more marginalised communities such as those with limited formal education, lower health or digital literacy, or more acute social disadvantage. Furthermore, recruitment and interviews conducted via digital platforms may have unintentionally excluded participants facing significant technological or infrastructural barriers, precisely those groups who stand to benefit most from accessible and inclusive digital health solutions.

While participants raised concerns about the credibility of online health information, direct experiences of digital surveillance or technology-facilitated coercive control were not widely reported in our study. However, this does not contradict broader evidence showing that digital technologies can be used to monitor, restrict, or control help-seeking in the context of family and domestic violence [77–79]. Reports of personal experiences with domestic or family violence were limited, despite substantial evidence indicating that women from migrant backgrounds are at heightened risk during the perinatal period [80, 81]. It is possible that the focus group format limited disclosure of such sensitive experiences, as noted in prior research [82]. This absence likely reflects an underrepresentation of women facing safety concerns, influenced by both the group setting and the broader recruitment strategy. While participants acknowledged that specific digital safety features, such as quick-exit buttons, may be necessary in the context of family violence, we could not explore the topic in depth. It is also important to acknowledge that family violence may go unreported or unrecognised due to desensitisation of controlling behaviours in some cultural or familial contexts. The lack of disclosures should therefore be interpreted with caution and not assumed to indicate the absence of risk. Future research would benefit from utilisation of appropriate methodologies, such as individual interviews or anonymous formats, to more safely explore the intersections of digital health, coercive control, and structural inequities, particularly among women in more precarious or isolated circumstances.

Conclusion

This study explored how women from Chinese, Arabic, and Indian-language speaking migrant backgrounds engage with five national perinatal mental health websites, revealing both the potential and the limitations of current digital health offerings. While some platforms provided helpful information, many lacked the cultural, linguistic, and contextual relevance needed to offer meaningful or timely support. Maternal mental health remains systematically underprioritised in policy and service delivery, often overshadowed by infant-centred care models. For migrant women, intersecting barriers such as language exclusion, digital and health literacy gaps, social isolation, and the impacts of structural disadvantage, including, in some cases, forms of institutional bias within healthcare systems shape access to culturally safe perinatal mental health care. Addressing these issues requires a shift from individualised solutions to systems-level reform. Digital mental health tools must be developed with broader, equity-oriented perinatal care models that are co-designed with communities, and that centre maternal identity, agency, and wellbeing. Such an approach must include efforts not only to improve usability and representation, but also to integrate digital solutions within trusted health and community systems. Importantly, digital platforms should integrate peer-based connection and pathways to in-language, community-led programs, recognising the critical role of peer support in maternal mental health and recovery.
