Classification of ruled surfaces in Lorentz-Minkowski space that are stationary for the moment of inertia
Muhittin Evren Aydin, Rafael López

Abstract
. In this paper, we study hypersurfaces in Lorentz-Minkowski space 𝕃 n + 1 \mathbb{L}^{n+1} that are stationary for the moment of inertia with respect to the origin. After giving examples and applications of the maximum principle, we classify, in 𝕃 3 \mathbb{L}^{3} , all stationary surfaces that are ruled surfaces. We prove that planes are the only cylindrical stationary surfaces. If the surface is not cylindrical, the classification depends on the causal character of the rulings. In addition, we provide explicit parametrizations of all ruled stationary surfaces.

53A10, 49K05, 74G65

1.
Introduction of the problem

In 1744, Euler proposed the study of curves
\gamma
in the Euclidean plane that are critical points of the energy functional

(1)

E_{\alpha}[\gamma]=\int_{\gamma}|\gamma(s)|^{\alpha}\,ds,

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter, and
s
is the arc-length parameter
[
7
]
. When
\alpha=0
, the energy
E_{0}[\gamma]
is the length of
\gamma
. For
\alpha=2
, the energy
E_{2}[\gamma]
corresponds to the moment of inertia of
\gamma
with respect to the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Critical points of
E_{\alpha}
are called
\alpha
-stationary curves
, and they are characterized in terms of the curvature
\kappa
of the curve, namely,

(2)

\kappa=\alpha\frac{\langle N,\gamma\rangle}{|\gamma|^{2}},

where
N
is the unit normal vector of
\gamma
. Notice that the differentiability of the functional
E_{\alpha}
requires that
\gamma
does not cross the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Recently, Dierkes and Huisken extended this problem to arbitrary dimensions, considering the energy functional

(3)

E_{\alpha}[\Sigma]=\int_{\Sigma}|p|^{\alpha}\,d\Sigma,

where
\Sigma
is a hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

[
3
]
and
p\in\Sigma
. The corresponding Euler-Lagrange equation is similar to (
2
), where the curvature
\kappa
and the unit normal
N
replaced by the mean curvature
H
and the unit normal vector of
\Sigma
, respectively. Recent progress in the study of
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces can be found in
[
2
,
4
,
5
,
9
]
.

In this paper, we extend this problem in Lorentz-Minkowski space
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
. The space
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
is
(\mathbb{R}^{n+1},\langle,\rangle)
, where the metric is
\langle,\rangle=dx_{1}^{2}+\ldots+dx_{n}^{2}-dx_{n+1}^{2}
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})
are the canonical coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. In comparison with the Euclidean case, some important differences appear in the Lorentzian context. First, in order for
d\Sigma
in (
3
) to make sense, it is necessary to restrict the study to spacelike hypersurfaces.

The second difference concerns the term
|p|^{\alpha}
of the energy
E_{\alpha}
in (
3
). In
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
, the term
\langle p,p\rangle
is not necessarily positive. Thus by
|p|
we will indicate
|p|=\sqrt{|\langle p,p\rangle|}
to ensure that the power
|p|^{\alpha}
is well-defined. As in the Euclidean case, we require that
0\not\in\Sigma
to guarantee differentiability of the functional
E_{\alpha}
. However, in the Lorentzian setting we must also impose that
\Sigma
does not intersect the lightlike cone
\mathcal{C}=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{n+1}\colon\langle p,p\rangle=0\}
because of the denominator in (
2
).

The lightlike cone
\mathcal{C}
separates
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
in two sets,

\mathcal{C}^{+}=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{n+1}\colon\langle p,p\rangle<0\},\quad\mathcal{C}^{-}=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{n+1}\colon\langle p,p\rangle>0\}.

If
n\geq 2
, the set
\mathcal{C}^{-}
is connected, but
\mathcal{C}^{+}
has two components. Consequently, it is necessary to distinguish if
\Sigma
is included in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
or in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
.

Following standard calculus of variations (see Sect.
2
), a spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma
is a critical point of the energy
E_{\alpha}
if and only if the mean curvature
H
of
\Sigma
satisfies

(4)

H(p)=-\alpha\frac{\langle N(p),p\rangle}{|\langle p,p\rangle|},\quad p\in\Sigma.

To distinguish whether
\Sigma
lies in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
or in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
, we can write (
4
) as

(5)

H(p)=\epsilon\alpha\frac{\langle N(p),p\rangle}{\langle p,p\rangle},\quad p\in\Sigma,

where
\epsilon=1
if
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{C}^{+}
and
\epsilon=-1
if
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{C}^{-}
.

Eq. (
4
) gives an interesting observation. Although the problem was initially posed only for spacelike hypersurfaces, in order for the integral in (
3
) to make sense, all terms of Eq. (
4
) do for timelike hypersurfaces. This allows to extend the notion of
\alpha
-stationary hypersurface to the timelike case.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a spacelike or timelike hypersurface in
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
satisfying
\Sigma\cap\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
. We call
\Sigma
an
\alpha
-stationary hypersurface if

(6)

H(p)=-\alpha\frac{\langle N(p),p\rangle}{|\langle p,p\rangle|},\quad p\in\Sigma.

If the value of
\alpha
is not to be emphasized, we simply say stationary hypersurfaces.

Recently, the authors of the present paper have studied stationary hypersurfaces in the particular case
n=1
, that is, stationary curves in the Lorent-Minkowski plane obtaining explicit parametrizations of these curves
[
1
]
.

The objective of this paper is to classify all ruled stationary surfaces in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
. First, in Sect.
2
we derive the Euler-Lagrange equation of the energy
E_{\alpha}
defined in (
3
), and we extend to timelike hypersurfaces. In this section, we also show examples in the class of hyperbolic hyperplanes and pseudospheres. In Sect.
3
we give an application of the maximum principle for stationary spacelike hypersurfaces and, via inversions, relate the value
\alpha
for different stationary hypersurfaces. The classification of ruled stationary surfaces in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
is done in Sects.
4
,
5
and
6
. The discussion depends on whether the surface is cylindrical and on whether the rulings are lightlike or not. Suppose that a ruled surface
\Sigma
is parametrized by
X(s,t)=\gamma(s)+tw(s)
,
s\in I
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Notice that for a spacelike ruled surface, it is necessary (but not sufficient) that both the base curve
\gamma
and the rulings
w
must be spacelike. However, in case that the surface is timelike, the situation is richer. As summary of these results, we prove:

(1)

Vector planes are the only cylindrical stationary surfaces in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
(Thm.
4.1
in Sect.
4
).

(2)

If
w
and
w^{\prime}
are not lightlike, then
\alpha\in\{-1,1\}
. Moreover, the curve
w=w(s)
is a geodesic of a pseudosphere (Thm.
5.1
in Sect.
5
).

(3)

Vector planes are the only ruled stationary surfaces in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
such that
w
is not lightlike and
w^{\prime}
is lightlike (Thm.
5.2
in Sect.
5
).

(4)

If
w
is lightlike, then
\alpha\in\{-4,2,4\}
. Moreover, if
\alpha=2
, then
\gamma(s)=w^{\prime}(s)
(Thm.
6.1
in Sect.
6
).

2.
The Euler-Lagrange equation and examples

In this section, we begin with the derivation of the Euler-Lagrange equation of the energy (
3
), next we show examples of stationary hypersurfaces and finally, we give an application of the maximum principle.

First, we obtain the Euler-Lagrange equation for (
3
) using standard arguments from the calculus of variations and we point out the differences with the Euclidean case. Let
\Sigma
be a spacelike hypersurface in
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
. Since the arguments are local, we may assume that
\Sigma
is locally the graph of a function
u\colon\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, where we identify
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the hyperplane
x_{n+1}=0
. Set
p=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})=(q,x_{n+1})
. Consider the immersion of
\Sigma
given by

X\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{L}^{n+1},\quad X(q)=(q,u(q)).

As a first step, we suppose
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{C}^{+}
. This implies
\langle p,p\rangle=|q|^{2}-u(q)^{2}<0
. Hence,
|p|=\sqrt{u^{2}-|q|^{2}}
. Consider the unit normal vector on
\Sigma
given by

(7)

N=\frac{(Du,1)}{\sqrt{1-|Du|^{2}}}.

Since
\Sigma
is spacelike, then
|Du|^{2}<1
. The energy functional (
3
) can be written as

E_{\alpha}[\Sigma]=\int_{\Omega}(u^{2}-|q|^{2})^{\alpha/2}\sqrt{1-|Du|^{2}}:=\int_{\Omega}J(q,u,Du).

As usual, the Euler-Lagrange equation is given by the expression

\frac{\partial J}{\partial u}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial J}{\partial u_{i}}\right)_{x_{i}},

where
u_{i}:=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}
. A straightforward computation gives

(8)

\alpha\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}u_{i}-u}{\sqrt{1-|Du|^{2}}}=-(u^{2}-|q|^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{u_{i}}{\sqrt{1-|Du|^{2}}}\right)_{x_{i}}.

The first parenthesis on the right-hand side is
-\langle X,X\rangle
and the second one is the mean curvature
H
of
\Sigma
with respect to the orientation (
7
). Along the paper, the mean curvature
H
is understood as the trace of the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
. On the other hand, we have

\langle N,X\rangle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}u_{i}-u}{\sqrt{1-|Du|^{2}}}.

Definitively, Equation (
5
) can be written as

(9)

H=\alpha\frac{\langle N,X\rangle}{\langle X,X\rangle},

which coincides with (
6
) because
\langle X,X\rangle<0
.

If
\Sigma
lies in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
, the energy becomes

E_{\alpha}[\Sigma]=\int_{\Omega}(|q|^{2}-u^{2})^{\alpha/2}\sqrt{1-|Du|^{2}},

and we derive the same expression (
9
) except with a minus sign on the right-hand side. Thus, the Euler-Lagrange equation coincides again with (
6
).

If
\alpha=0
, then
E_{0}
is the
n
-area of a spacelike hypersurface. It is known that the critical points of the
n
-area are maximal hypersurfaces, that is, hypersurfaces whose mean curvature
H
vanishes. This case will not be considered further.

Although the energy (
3
) does not make sense for timelike hypersurfaces, Eq. (
6
) still holds. Recall that the mean curvature
H
is the trace of the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
and that the Weingarten map may fail to be diagonalizable. So principal curvatures are not, in general, well defined. In the case that it is diagonalizable, the eigenvalues are called principal curvature and
H
coincides with their sum.

We can do a similar argument for timelike hypersurfaces and we will see that we obtain the same Eq.
6
. Let
\Sigma
be a timelike hypersurface given locally as
u=u(q)
. The timelike condition writes as
1<|Du|^{2}
and the mean curvature
H
and the unit normal
N
are

H=\mbox{div}\left(\frac{Du}{\sqrt{|Du|^{2}-1}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{u_{i}}{\sqrt{|Du|^{2}-1}}\right)_{x_{i}},\quad N=\frac{(-Du,-1)}{\sqrt{|Du|^{2}-1}}.

Suppose that
\Sigma
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. Then
|q|^{2}-u^{2}>0
and define the energy

E_{\alpha}[\Sigma]=\int_{\Omega}(|q|^{2}-u^{2})^{\alpha/2}\sqrt{|Du|^{2}-1}.

We deduce that
\Sigma
is a critical point of
E_{\alpha}
if and only if

\mbox{div}\left(\frac{Du}{\sqrt{|Du|^{2}-1}}\right)=\alpha\frac{\langle Du,q\rangle-u}{(|q|^{2}-u^{2})\sqrt{|Du|^{2}-1}}.

Since
\langle N,X\rangle=-(\langle Du,q\rangle-u)/\sqrt{|Du|^{2}-1}
, we obtain

H=-\alpha\frac{\langle N,X\rangle}{|q|^{2}-u^{2}}=-\alpha\frac{\langle N,X\rangle}{\langle X,X\rangle}.

Thus this equation coincides with (
6
). In case that
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{C}^{+}
, the argument is similar, obtaining (
6
) again.

Remark 2.1
.

We point out that linear isometries and dilations of
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
preserve the solutions of (
6
) for the same value of
\alpha
.

We present some examples of stationary hypersurfaces. Before, we introduce the following notation. A hyperbolic hyperplane in
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
with radius
r>0
and center
p_{0}
is one of the two components of

\mathbb{H}^{n}(p_{0},r)=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{n+1}:\langle p-p_{0},p-p_{0}\rangle=-r^{2}\}.

This hypersurface is spacelike and its mean curvature is
H=n/r
for the orientation
N=(p-p_{0})/r
.
Similarly, a pseudosphere of radius
r>0
and centered at
p_{0}
is defined by

\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n}(p_{0},r)=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{n+1}:\langle p-p_{0},p-p_{0}\rangle=r^{2}\}.

A pseudosphere is a timelike hypersurface and the mean curvature is
H=n/r
for the orientation
N=-(p-p_{0})/r
. For both hypersurfaces, if the center is the origin, we denote by
\mathbb{H}^{n}(r)
and
\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n}(r)
.

We have the following examples of stationary hypersurfaces.

(1)

Vector hyperplanes. These hypersurfaces are
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces for all values of
\alpha
.

(2)

Hyperbolic planes
\mathbb{H}^{n}(r)
. They are
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces with
\alpha=n
for all values of
r
and contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
.

(3)

Pseudospheres
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{1}(r)
. They are
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces with
\alpha=n
for all values of
r
and contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
.

In the following result, we find all hyperplanes, hyperbolic hyperplanes and pseudospheres that are
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces.

Proposition 2.2
.

(1)

Vector (non-degenerate) hyperplanes are the only (non-degenerate) hyperplanes that are
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces. This holds for any value of
\alpha
.

(2)

The only hyperbolic hyperplanes that are
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces are:

(a)

Hyperbolic hyperplanes
\mathbb{H}^{n}(r)
(
\alpha=n
) and the component of
\mathbb{H}^{n}(p_{0},r)
with
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=-r^{2}
contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
(
\alpha=2n
). This holds for all
r>0
.

(b)

The component of
\mathbb{H}^{n}(p_{0},r)
with
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=-r^{2}
contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
(
\alpha=-2n
). This hypersurface crosses
0
. This holds for all
r>0
.

(3)

The only pseudospheres that are
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces are:

(a)

Pseudospheres
\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n}(r)
(
\alpha=n
) and
\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n}(p_{0},r)\cap\mathcal{C}^{-}
with
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=r^{2}
(
\alpha=2n
). This holds for all
r>0
.

(b)

Intersections
\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n}(p_{0},r)\cap\mathcal{C}^{+}
with
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=r^{2}
(
\alpha=-2n
). The pseudosphere crosses
0
. This holds for all
r>0
.

Proof.

The result for hyperplanes is straightforward.

(1)

We are looking for which hyperbolic hyperplanes are
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces. Suppose that
\mathbb{H}^{n}(p_{0},r)\subset\mathcal{C}^{+}
. Then (
6
) is

(2n-\alpha)\langle p,p_{0}\rangle+(\alpha-n)(r^{2}+\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle)=0.

If
p_{0}=0
, this identity holds for
\alpha=n
. If
p_{0}\not=0
then
\alpha=2n
and
r^{2}+\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=0
.
In such a case, the equation of the hyperbolic plane is equivalent to
\langle p,p\rangle-2\langle p,p_{0}\rangle=0
. One of the components crosses
0
but this component is included in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. The other component is included in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
. This proves the item (2a).

Suppose now
\mathbb{H}^{n}(p_{0},r)\subset\mathcal{C}^{-}
. A similar argument proves that (
6
) is

(2n+\alpha)\langle p,p_{0}\rangle-(\alpha+n)(r^{2}+\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle)=0.

Notice that if
p_{0}=0
, then
\mathbb{H}^{n}(r)
is included in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
, hence that this case is not possible. Thus
p_{0}\not=0
,
\alpha=-2n
and
r^{2}+\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=0
. The component of
\mathbb{H}^{n}(p_{0},r)
that crosses
0
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
but the other is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
, which proves the item (2b).

(2)

Let
\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n}(p_{0},r)
be a pseudosphere contained in
\mathcal{C}^{-}
. Then (
6
) gives

(n-\alpha)(r^{2}-\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle)+(2n-\alpha)\langle p,p_{0}\rangle=0.

If
p_{0}=0
, then
\alpha=n
. If
p_{0}\not=0
, then
\alpha=2n
and
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=r^{2}
. This proves the item (3a).

Finally, assume that
\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n}(p_{0},r)
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
. We have

(n+\alpha)(r^{2}-\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle)+(2n+\alpha)\langle p,p_{0}\rangle=0.

The case that
p_{0}=0
is not possible because
\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n}(r)\subset\mathcal{C}^{+}
. Hence
\alpha=-2n
and
\langle p_{0},p_{0}\rangle=r^{2}
, proving the item (3b).

∎

A this point, it deserves to highlight two differences with the Euclidean case.

(1)

Spheres of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
centered at
0
are stationary for
\alpha=-n
. The analogous case in
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
is given by
\mathbb{H}^{n}(r)
and
\mathbb{S}_{1}^{n}(r)
. In
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, spheres crossing
0
are stationary for
\alpha=-2n
. This is analog to (2b) and (3b). But, in
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
there are hyperbolic hyperplanes and pseudospheres centered at points other than the origin that do not cross
0
. These examples have no counterpart in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

(2)

On the other hand, there are pseudospheres not centered at the origin which contain stationary parts for different values of
\alpha
, the item (3) of Prop.
2.2
. This is because such pseudospheres intersect both
\mathcal{C}^{-}
and
\mathcal{C}^{+}
. Since they are timelike, no counterpart exists in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

3.
The maximum principle and inversions

In this section, we give an application of the maximum principle for elliptic equations and show that inversions of
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
preserve
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces but change the value of
\alpha
.

In case that
\Sigma
is a spacelike hypersurface, Eq. (
6
) is elliptic and the maximum principle can be applied. The argument is the same that in the Euclidean context: see
[
4
, Prop. 2.5]
. The maximum principle in
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
asserts that given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and two spacelike hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{i}
,
i=1,2
, which are tangent at a common point
p\in\Sigma_{1}\cap\Sigma_{2}
, if
\Sigma_{1}
lies above
\Sigma_{2}
around
p
according the the orientation by the (coincident) normal vectors at
p
,
N_{1}(p)=N_{2}(p)
, then
\widetilde{H}^{1}(p)\geq\widetilde{H}^{2}(p)
, where

\widetilde{H}^{i}=H_{i}+\alpha\frac{\langle N_{i},p\rangle}{|\langle p,p\rangle|},\quad i=1,2.

If, in addition,
\widetilde{H}^{i}
are constant and
\widetilde{H}^{1}=\widetilde{H}^{2}
, then
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{2}
in a neighborhood of
p
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the maximum principle allows to classify all closed stationary hypersurfaces which are spheres centered at
0
(
\alpha=-n
). In the Lorentzian ambient space it is not possible to get a similar result because there are not closed spacelike hypersurfaces. However, we can use hyperbolic hyperplanes
\mathbb{H}^{n}(r)
centered at
0
to estimate the value
\alpha
of some
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces. First, we introduce a definition. A set
S\subset\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
is said to be far away from the lightlike cone if there is
\delta>0
such that

S\subset\mathcal{C}_{\delta}:=\{p\in\mathbb{L}^{n+1}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-(x_{n+1}-\delta)^{2}<0\}.

The set
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
is formed by two connected components. Notice that the set
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-(x_{n+1}-\delta)^{2}=0
is a cone with vertex at
(0,\ldots,0,\delta)
. We also point out that the hyperbolic hyperplanes
\mathbb{H}^{n}(r)
are asymptotic to
\mathcal{C}
with two vertex
(0,\ldots,\pm r)
for each of the components of
\mathbb{H}^{n}(r)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a properly immersed
\alpha
-stationary spacelike hypersurface and contained in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
. If
\Sigma
is far away far from the lightlike cone, then
\alpha>n
.

Proof.

Since
\Sigma
is connected, we can assume that
\Sigma
is contained in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{+}:=\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\cap\{x_{n+1}>0\}
. In case that
\Sigma
is included in
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\cap\{x_{n+1}<0\}
, the arguments are similar. Let
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r)
be the component of
\mathbb{H}^{n}(r)
contained in
x_{n+1}>0
. Let also
r>0
be sufficiently small so that
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r)\cap\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{+}=\emptyset
. This is possible because
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r)
is asymptotic to
\mathcal{C}
,
(\mathcal{C}\cap\{x_{n+1}>0\})\cap\mathcal{C}_{\delta}^{+}=\emptyset
, and the vertex of
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r)
goes to the origin as
r\searrow 0
.

Let
r\nearrow\infty
until the first value of
r
, say
r_{0}
, between
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r_{0})
and
\Sigma
. The existence of
r_{0}
is assured because
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r)
follows being asymptotic to
\mathcal{C}
but the vertex goes vertically to infinity. Let
p_{0}\in\Sigma\cap\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r_{0})
be a common point. Take the orientation
N(p)=p/r
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r_{0})
. Then the hypersurface
\Sigma
lies above
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r_{0})
. For the parameter
\alpha
, the maximum principle at
p_{0}
yields

0=\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(p_{0})\geq\widetilde{H}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r_{0})}(p_{0})=\frac{n}{r_{0}}-\alpha\frac{r_{0}}{r_{0}^{2}}=\frac{n-\alpha}{r_{0}}.

This implies
\alpha\geq n
. The case
\alpha=n
cannot occur. In such a case,
\widetilde{H}^{\Sigma}(p_{0})=\widetilde{H}^{\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r_{0})}
and equal to the constant
0
. The maximum principle would imply
\Sigma=\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r_{0})
, which it is not possible because
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{+}(r_{0})
is not far away from the lighlike cone. This proves the result.
∎

In the second part of this section we consider inversions and see that they have a well behaviour with stationary hypersurfaces. We define the inversion in
\mathbb{L}^{+1}
by

\phi\colon\mathbb{L}^{n+1}\setminus\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{L}^{n+1},\quad\phi(p)=\frac{p}{\langle p,p\rangle}.

We point out that
\phi^{2}=1_{\mathbb{L}^{n+1}}
and that
\phi
preserves the sets
\mathcal{C}^{+}
(assuming
n\geq 2
) and
\mathcal{C}^{-}
, although it reverses the components of
\mathcal{C}^{-}
.

Let
\Sigma
be a hypersurface in
\mathbb{L}^{n+1}
, and let
\Sigma_{\phi}=\phi(\Sigma)
. If
v\in T_{p}\Sigma
, then

d\phi_{p}(v)=\frac{v}{\langle p,p\rangle^{2}}-2\frac{\langle p,v\rangle}{\langle p,p\rangle^{4}}p,\quad p\in\Sigma.

Thus
\langle d\phi_{p}(v),d\phi_{p}(v)\rangle=\langle v,v\rangle/(\langle p,p\rangle^{4})
. This proves that
\Sigma
is a spacelike (resp. timelike) hypersurface if and only
\Sigma_{\phi}
is a spacelike (resp. timelike) hypersurface.
From now, all quantities of
\Sigma_{\phi}
will be denoted with the subindex
\phi
.

If
N
is the unit normal vector of
\Sigma
, then

N_{\phi}(\phi(p))=N(p)-2\frac{h(p)}{\langle p,p\rangle}p,\quad h(p)=\langle N(p),p\rangle.

Let
A
be the Weingarten map of
\Sigma
. Let also
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{p}\Sigma
, where all vectors
v_{i}
are spacelike except
v_{n}
, which is spacelike or timelike depending on whether
\Sigma
is spacelike or timelike. If
(v_{i})_{\phi}=d\phi_{p}(v_{i})
, then

(10)

(A_{\phi})_{\phi(p)}(v_{i})_{\phi}=A_{p}(v_{i})+2\frac{2h}{\langle p,p\rangle}v_{i}-\left(2\frac{\langle Av_{i},p\rangle}{\langle p,p\rangle}+\frac{4h\langle p,v_{i}\rangle}{\langle p,p\rangle^{4}}\right)p.

If
\varepsilon=\langle N,N\rangle=\langle N_{\phi},N_{\phi}\rangle
, then using (
10
), one arrives at

(11)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\langle p,p\rangle^{4}}H_{\phi}&=\frac{\varepsilon\,\mbox{trace}(A_{\phi})}{\langle p,p\rangle^{4}}=\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\langle A_{\phi}(v_{i})_{\phi},(v_{i})_{\phi}\rangle+(-1)^{\varepsilon}\langle A_{\phi}(v_{n})_{\phi},(v_{n})_{\phi}\rangle\\
&=\frac{H}{\langle p,p\rangle^{2}}+\frac{2nh}{\langle p,p\rangle^{4}}.\end{split}

Remark 3.2
.

In case that
v_{i}
is a principal direction at
p
, so is
(v_{i})_{\phi}
at
p_{\phi}
and the relation between the principal curvatures is
(\kappa_{i})_{\phi}=\langle p,p\rangle\kappa_{i}+2h
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be an
\alpha
-stationary hypersurface. Then
\Sigma_{\phi}
is a
-(2n+\alpha)
-stationary hypersurface if
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{C}^{+}
and a
(2n-\alpha)
-stationary hypersurface if
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{C}^{-}
.

Proof.

It is immediate that
\langle p,p\rangle\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle=1
and
h=-\frac{h_{\phi}}{\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle}
. From (
11
), we have

H=\frac{H_{\phi}+2nh}{\langle p,p\rangle}=\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle H_{\phi}-2nh_{\phi}.

Since
\Sigma
satisfies Eq. (
6
), then

\begin{split}\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle H_{\phi}-2nh_{\phi}&=H=-\alpha\frac{\langle N,p\rangle}{|\langle p,p\rangle|}=-\alpha|\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle|\langle N_{\phi}-\frac{2h_{\phi}}{\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle}p_{\phi},\frac{p_{\phi}}{\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle}\rangle\\
&=\alpha|\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle|\frac{h_{\phi}}{\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle}.\end{split}

Thus

H_{\phi}=\left(2n+\alpha\frac{|\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle|}{\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle}\right)\frac{\langle N_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle}{\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle}.

Then we have

H_{\phi}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(2n+\alpha)\frac{\langle N_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle}{|\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle|}&\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle>0,\\
-(2n-\alpha)\frac{\langle N_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle}{|\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle|}&\langle p_{\phi},p_{\phi}\rangle<0,\end{array}\right.

which proves the result.
∎

4.
Ruled surfaces: cylindrical case

In this and the next sections, we give a full classification of ruled stationary surfaces. A ruled surface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
is parametrized by

(12)

X(s,t)=\gamma(s)+tw(s),\quad s\in I,t\in\mathbb{R},

where
\gamma:I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{L}^{3}
,
\gamma=\gamma(s)
, is the base curve and
w=w(s)\in\mathbb{L}^{3}
is a vector field along
\gamma
,
w(s)\not=0
, which indicates the rulings of
\Sigma
.

A first case is when
w=w(s)
is constant along
\gamma
. Then, we say that
\Sigma
is a cylindrical surface. In such a case, the corresponding classification of the stationary surfaces will be done in this section. When the surface is not cylindrical, the types of ruled surfaces depend on the causality of
w
and
w^{\prime}
and are as follows (see
[
6
]
):

(1)

w
is not lightlike:

(1a)

w^{\prime}
is not lightlike,

(1b)

w^{\prime}
is lightlike.

(2)

w
is lightlike.

Case (1) will be studied in Sect.
5
, and Case (2) in Sect.
6
.

In this section, we classify the ruled stationary surfaces of cylindrical type. First, we give some local computations which we need in the rest of sections. To be precise, we derive an explicit expression for (
6
) when a ruled surface
\Sigma
is parametrized by (
12
).

With respect to
X
, the coefficients of the first fundamental form are

E=\langle X_{s},X_{s}\rangle,\quad F=\langle X_{s},X_{t}\rangle,\quad G=\langle X_{t},X_{t}\rangle.

Since the following identity holds in
\mathbb{L}^{3}

\langle X_{s}\times X_{t},X_{s}\times X_{t}\rangle=-\langle X_{s},X_{s}\rangle\langle X_{t},X_{t}\rangle+\langle X_{s},X_{t}\rangle^{2},

the unit normal vector field is given by

N=\frac{X_{s}\times X_{t}}{\sqrt{|EG-F^{2}|}}.

The normal vector
N
is timelike (resp. spacelike) if
\Sigma
is spacelike (resp. timelike). Let

\langle N,N\rangle=\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}.

Denote by
(\vec{u},\vec{v},\vec{w})
the scalar triple product in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
, i.e., the determinant of the matrix formed by the vectors
\vec{u},\vec{v},\vec{w}\in\mathbb{L}^{3}
. The coefficients of the second fundamental form are given by

e=\frac{(X_{s},X_{t},X_{ss})}{\sqrt{|EG-F^{2}|}},\quad f=\frac{(X_{s},X_{t},X_{st})}{\sqrt{|EG-F^{2}|}},\quad g=\frac{(X_{s},X_{t},X_{tt})}{\sqrt{|EG-F^{2}|}}.

Hence, the mean curvature
H
is given by

H=\varepsilon\frac{eG-2fF+gE}{EG-F^{2}}.

In the denominator of both sides of Eq. (
6
), the factor
\sqrt{|EG-F^{2}|}
cancels and Eq. (
5
) reduces to

(13)

H_{1}=\alpha\epsilon\varepsilon(EG-F^{2})\frac{(X_{s},X_{t},X)}{\langle X,X\rangle},

where
H_{1}
is defined by

H_{1}:=G(X_{s},X_{t},X_{ss})-2F(X_{s},X_{t},X_{st})+E(X_{s},X_{t},X_{tt}).

The classification of cylindrical stationary surfaces is given in the next result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Vector planes are the only cylindrical stationary surfaces in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
.

Proof.

Let
\vec{w}
the direction of the rulings. We distinguish two cases according to whether
\vec{w}
is lightlike or not.

(1)

Case
\vec{w}
is not lightlike. We may assume
|\vec{w}|=1
, and that
\gamma
is contained in a plane
\Gamma
orthogonal to
\vec{w}
. Let
\langle\vec{w},\vec{w}\rangle=\delta\in\{-1,1\}
. By reparametrizing, we can assume that
\langle\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle=\mu\in\{-1,1\}
. Hence, Eq. (
13
) is

\delta(\gamma^{\prime},\vec{w},\gamma^{\prime\prime})=-\alpha\epsilon\frac{(\gamma^{\prime},\vec{w},\gamma)}{\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle+2t\langle\gamma,\vec{w}\rangle+\delta t^{2}}.

This equation is satisfied only if
(\gamma^{\prime},\vec{w},\gamma^{\prime\prime})=0
and
(\gamma^{\prime},\vec{w},\gamma)=0
. The first equation implies that
\gamma
is a straight line because
\gamma
is contained in a plane orthogonal to
\vec{w}
. Since
\Sigma
is a stationary surface, then
0\in\Sigma
.

(2)

Case
\vec{w}
is lightlike. Then
H=0
and Eq. (
13
) yields
(\gamma^{\prime},\vec{w},\gamma)=0
. Since
\Sigma
is non-degenerate, we must have
EG-F^{2}=-\langle\gamma^{\prime},\vec{w}\rangle^{2}\neq 0
on
I
. By applying a dilation followed by a rotation, we may assume that
\vec{w}=(1,0,1)
. Consequently,
\gamma
lies in the plane spanned by
(0,1,0)
and
(1,0,-1)
. If
\gamma
is a straight line
of the form
s\mapsto(s,c,-s)
, then
\Sigma
is a plane, and the condition
(\gamma^{\prime},\vec{w},\gamma)=0
implies
c=0
, i.e.,
0\in\Sigma
. Otherwise, we may have
\gamma(s)=(f(s),s,-f(s))
, for a smooth function
f
on
I
. In this case, the condition
(\gamma^{\prime},\vec{w},\gamma)=0
gives
f(s)=cs
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\gamma(s)=s(c,1,-c)
is a straight line, hence
\Sigma
is a vector plane.

∎

5.

Theorem 5.1

(Case

Without loss of generality, we can choose

E=\langle\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\rangle+\mu t^{2},\quad F=\langle\gamma^{\prime},w\rangle,\quad G=\delta.

Notice that

or
w(s)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}(1)
, according to the sign of
\delta
, and denote by
\kappa_{g}^{w}=(w^{\prime\prime},w^{\prime},w)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\delta Fw-\delta\mu Q(w\times w^{\prime}),

\displaystyle w^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\delta\mu(-w+\kappa_{g}^{w}(w\times w^{\prime})),

\displaystyle=QF\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle+\alpha\beta\mu Q^{2}(\gamma^{\prime},w,\gamma),
