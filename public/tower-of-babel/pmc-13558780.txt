Diversity–productivity relationships in ericaceous plants are influenced by interactions with ericoid mycorrhizal fungi
Authors: Camille S Delavaux, Laura G van Galen, Alice S Neves, Luisa Zumsteg, Martina Peter, Elena Martino, Martin Vohník, Janneke Hille Ris Lambers, Thomas W Crowther
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe positive effect of plant diversity on ecosystem productivity is among the most widespread trends in ecosystem ecology. Most studies suggest that niche differences (complementarity) play a significant role in shaping these effects. As most plant species require mutualistic mycorrhizal fungi to access limiting nutrients, recent evidence suggests that these mutualisms may mediate the complementarity detected in plant studies. Yet, until now, previous studies have focused primarily on the role of mycorrhizal presence in altering these relationships, but no studies have examined the combined effects of plant and mycorrhizal diversity in shaping productivity. Here, we use a mesocosm experimental system to manipulate ericoid mycorrhizal richness (ranging from one to seven fungal isolates) and plant richness (ranging from one to eight plant species) to assess how plant and fungal diversity interact to influence plant productivity. We find clear support for the positive effect of plant diversity on productivity, but this effect only emerged at higher levels of fungal diversity. Within plant monocultures, we found that increasing fungal diversity reduced plant productivity, consistent with antagonism between symbionts limiting plant productivity. Moreover, we found that although most species follow community‐level patterns, plant species vary in their response to changing plant and fungal diversity. These results suggest that plant productivity within the ericoid mycorrhizal system is impacted by both plant and fungal diversity, with implications for the use of ericoid mycorrhizal fungi in both agriculture and restoration.Keywords: biodiversity ecosystem functioning, complementarity, Ericaceae, mycorrhizal growth response

INTRODUCTION

In recent decades, a considerable number of large‐scale manipulative experiments has highlighted the positive role of plant diversity in increasing plant productivity. These studies generally show a positive diversity–productivity relationship that saturates (i.e., plateaus) beyond a certain threshold (Cardinale et al., 2011; Hart et al., 2003; Tilman et al., 2014; Zheng et al., 2024). Early efforts were made to establish whether this pattern was the result of fundamental differences among species (e.g., niches) that can be linked to statistical complementarity (Lambers et al., 2004; Schnitzer et al., 2011; Schwartz et al., 2000) or a few highly productive species (i.e., selection, or sampling effects; Huston, 1997; Tilman et al., 2001). However, across these studies, the role of complementarity consistently emerged as an important driver of long‐term patterns (Cardinale et al., 2007), which was interpreted as evidence that diversity leads to increased productivity because diverse communities can make more effective use of available resources via niche partitioning.

Although complementarity has been largely interpreted as more complete use of resources by plants themselves, nutritional mutualisms may represent an important mechanism underlying these patterns (Barry et al., 2019). Most plant species depend on a key nutritional mutualism with mycorrhizal fungi (van der Heijden et al., 2015). Via mining of essential nutrients, mycorrhizal fungi have a large impact on plant fitness (Smith & Read, 2008), with the potential to promote plant growth by a factor of 50 (Hoeksema et al., 2010; Maherali & Klironomos, 2007). This substantial fitness benefit and variation therein suggests that the composition and diversity of mycorrhizal fungi could have an important impact in shaping plant diversity–productivity relationships. Emerging research suggests that the presence of mycorrhizal fungi can directly influence the diversity–productivity relationship (Koziol & Bever, 2017; van der Heijden et al., 1998; Vogelsang et al., 2006). Klironomos et al. (2000) and Schnitzer et al. (2011) evaluated how this relationship varied in the presence of the dominant type of mycorrhizal fungi—arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF)—revealing that the addition of one or several AMF species increased productivity, particularly in low plant diversity systems.

Because fungal diversity can vary greatly in nature, attention has shifted in the past decade to examine the role of fungal diversity in shaping plant diversity–productivity relationships (Afkhami et al., 2021; Larimer et al., 2014; Magnoli & Bever, 2023). However, most previous work has focused on fungal diversity within one static plant diversity level, often a single plant individual. When considering a single plant associating with a few or many fungal symbionts, three outcomes are possible: (1) synergism (or symbiont complementarity), (2) sampling effects, or (3) interference (antagonism) (Magnoli & Bever, 2023). Synergism describes the nonadditive benefit of multiple symbionts: The addition of multiple symbionts increases plant productivity more than the average of each symbiont alone. Sampling effects can drive responses when the influence on productivity is simply that of the best or worst symbiont, such that productivity can increase or decrease when symbiont diversity is higher simply because there is a greater chance of a more beneficial or detrimental symbiont being present in a larger species pool. Finally, interference or antagonism is possible when the benefit of multiple symbionts is worse than the average and worst symbiont. This can be the result of direct competition of symbionts in mixture. A recent meta‐analysis of synergism suggests that symbiont sampling emerges as the dominant driver (Magnoli & Bever, 2023). Finally, some work has manipulated mycorrhizal symbiont diversity across plant communities; however, this work still only considers a single diversity level (Koziol & Bever, 2017; van der Heijden et al., 1998, Vogelsang et al., 2006). Together, these studies show that changing mycorrhizal diversity can indeed impact plant productivity at the same plant diversity level. However, limited and sometimes contradictory results restrict our ability to generalize on the impact of mycorrhizal diversity on plant community productivity in more diverse plant communities.

Despite progress showing that mycorrhizal fungal presence mediates the plant diversity–productivity relationship and that multiple mycorrhizal partners can impact productivity of plant communities at one diversity level, to our knowledge, no study has manipulated both plant and mycorrhizal fungal diversity to test their relative importance or interactive effects on plant productivity. Here, we leverage the ericoid mycorrhizal (ErM) symbiosis between the ErM fungi (ErMF) and members of the core Ericaceae to explore the relative importance of fungal and plant diversity in impacting plant productivity. Using a mesocosm greenhouse experiment, we manipulated both plant and ErMF diversity to test the impact of increasing diversity in both groups on plant productivity. Specifically, we used mesocosms of eight plant individuals to test the impact of four levels of plant diversity and five levels of ErMF inoculum diversity on plant productivity (Figure 1). In total, we examined above and belowground productivity across 599 and 192 mesocosms, respectively. We tested for (1) the effect of plant diversity on plant productivity in this ErM system for the first time, including in both above and belowground biomass, (2) the effect of ErMF diversity in mediating the plant diversity–productivity relationship, and (3) whether (2) varied with plant species identity.

Experimental set up. Our experiment consisted of mesocosms of eight plant individuals with varying plant and fungal diversities. At plant diversity 1, we included 33 replicates (3 each of the 11 plant species used in the experiment); at plant diversities 2, 4, and 8, we included 30 replicates, with plant species randomly drawn from the 11 species pool. This was repeated at five fungal diversity levels: 0, 1, 2, 4, and 7. Fungal isolate combinations for a given diversity were again drawn at random from the 7 isolate pool. Green dots represent plant individuals in mesocosms, with different colored dots representing distinct plant species; background shading represents fungal diversity, where white represents the control treatment (i.e., fungal diversity zero), yellow fungal diversity of 1, and multicolored fungal diversity of 7.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study system

Although relatively understudied, the ErM symbiosis represents the dominant mycorrhizal association in heathland systems and high latitude (i.e., temperate and boreal) forest understories (Read et al., 2004; Smith & Read, 2008). This symbiosis is formed between ErMF and certain ericaceous species (i.e., plants from the early anther inversion clade of the Ericaceae family, often collectively termed core Ericaceae) in these systems, providing key nutritional services—mostly in the form of accessing organic N (Leopold, 2016; Vohnik, 2020). However, with rising temperatures and land use change, these systems are also under mounting threat, with renewed interest in their restoration (Mitsch et al., 2013). Therefore, the concurrent urgent need for restoration and the shortage of research on this important mutualism in these habitats, makes understanding the joint impact of plant and fungal diversity on plant productivity vital.

Plant species

Eleven core Ericaceae species were used in the experiment (Table 1). Seeds were purchased from Semences du Puy (www.semencesdupuy.com) and Jelitto (www.jelitto.com). We germinated seedlings in an autoclaved 1:1 mix of sieved white peat and vermiculite in a glasshouse at 20°C. Additionally, four species (Gaultheria procumbens, Vaccinium angustifolium, V. macrocarpon, and V. vitis‐idaea) required cold stratification to break seed dormancy. After sowing, trays of these species were kept moist and stored in the dark at 4°C for 3–4 weeks (see Table 1 for species‐level details) before being moved to the greenhouse to germinate with the other species.

Experimental design

The experiment comprised 615 mesocosm pots (1.5 L) each containing eight individual plants inoculated with communities of ErMF (or deionized water for the controls, Figure 1). This maximum plant diversity level was chosen to ensure a broad enough range of diversity to detect plant diversity patterns, while reasonably representing diversity levels in ericoid systems (Fernández‐Guisuraga et al., 2025). Plant diversity of each pot was either one, two, four, or eight; ErMF inoculum diversity (hereafter “fungal diversity”) was either zero (controls), one, two, four, or seven. Each level of plant diversity contained 33 replicates of the control (no fungal inoculum) and 30 replicates of each ErMF diversity treatment. The exact combination of plant species and ErMF isolates used in the treatments was drawn randomly from the 11 plant species and the seven ErMF isolates. Due to variation in the availability of seedlings, some plant species were slightly more represented than others (Table 1). However, assessment of plant species and ErMF isolate proportional representation across both plant and fungal diversity levels showed no signs of skew (Appendix S1: Figure S1).

To prepare the ErMF inoculum for the experiment, we grew fungal cultures in 2 L of MLA liquid broth per isolate at room temperature on a shaker (120 rpm) for 2 weeks. Subsequently, residual liquid was drained and the mycelium of each isolate was pulse‐blended three times with 150 mL of deionized water for 1 s each time. The blender was sterilized in a 10% bleach solution for 15 min and thoroughly rinsed with running water between preparation of each isolate. Each isolate was then diluted to an equal volume (4 L) and inoculation mixes of 40 mL were prepared for each mesocosm pot by mixing equal volumes of the randomly selected isolates together. Individual isolates may differ in growth rates under both laboratory and natural conditions. Since we did not know the exact growth rates of the tested fungal strains in the experimental substrate, attempting to measure and apply identical mycelial volumes would not be meaningful and could introduce additional error. We therefore applied a consistent inoculation protocol across treatments without quantifying exact mycelial volumes. As a result, some variation in biomass per unit volume among isolates may have occurred, and we acknowledge this as a possible limitation of the study.

Each mesocosm was established in a 1.5‐L square pot (10 × 10 × 13 cm) filled with a 1:1 mix of white peat (pH = 5–6.5) and vermiculite (pH = 6–8, grain size = 2–3 mm). We chose this soil mix to match the nutrient‐poor acidic conditions of natural core Ericaceae habitats. To prepare the soil mix, we (1) soaked the peat for at least 1 h in a 1:1000 solution of wetting agent and water, (2) mixed it with an equal volume of vermiculite, and (3) autoclaved twice using cycles that sterilized at 120°C for 30 min. We planted eight seedlings per pot, using the species that had been randomly chosen for each mesocosm. After planting, 40 mL of fungal inoculum mix (or deionized water for the controls) were carefully poured over the base of seedlings in the mesocosm. Extreme care was taken to avoid contamination by regularly sterilizing equipment and the potting area. Mesocosms were randomly allocated to five benches in a greenhouse and grown for 5 months between July and November 2024. Glasshouse conditions were semi‐controlled using ventilation, with the target temperature set at 20°C. The seedlings were exposed to natural light (average day length of approximately 12 h over the duration of the experiment). Each mesocosm was individually watered three times per week using a gentle rain hose to avoid water splashing between pots. Each pot was placed on an individual underpot to reduce any possible cross‐contamination and in alternating positions so that pot sides were at least 16 cm apart. We measured the initial height of each seedling 1–2 weeks after planting (from the base of the soil to the height of the tallest leaves when pinched). At this stage, we also replaced any dead seedlings.

Mesocosm harvest

At the end of the 5‐month growing period, we scored plants as either dead or alive and harvested both the aboveground and belowground biomass. The majority of diversity–productivity studies focus on aboveground biomass only (Ma & Chen, 2016); however, whether these patterns are mirrored belowground is unclear. Furthermore, mycorrhizal associations in particular can alter belowground root architecture and allocation (Koziol & Bever, 2015), suggesting that above‐ and belowground responses may vary. Out of 4920 initial plant individuals, 4561 individual plants survived across 599 mesocosms. Survival was too high to allow for formal statistical analyses of mortality patterns, but the majority of death occurred in non‐inoculated control mesocosms, concentrated within Gaultheria miqueliana and Vaccinium corymbosum (Appendix S1: Figure S2). The experiment was designed to equally represent plant species and ErMF isolates across all diversity treatments, but we reassessed this after individuals that did not survive were removed and confirmed that both plant and inoculated ErMF isolates were evenly distributed among our treatments (Appendix S1: Figure S2).

To harvest aboveground biomass, each plant was cut at the base of the soil, dried for at least 1 week in a drying oven at 60°C, and weighed. Some individuals were too light to register on the scale (mostly for G. miqueliana); these were assigned the minimum detectable mass of 0.0001 g. Belowground biomass was measured at the pot level rather than the individual level as we could not confidently separate individual plant root systems. Due to the intense labor required for removing soil from roots, we only prepared and weighed belowground biomass for one third of the experiment (191 pots). Roots of all plants in a mesocosm were rinsed over a sieve using a rain hose to remove any soil particles and then placed in 15‐mL Falcon tubes filled with 50% ethanol. Roots were then dried in a drying oven for at least 1 week at 60°C and weighed. A random subset of roots from 80 mesocosms was taken for the colonization assessment to verify inoculation treatments.

Root colonization assessment

We assessed colonization in the roots of 20 replicates across the plant diversity levels (10 at zero fungal diversity level, and 3 at each of the remaining four levels) to check whether plants had formed the typical ErM plant‐fungal nutrient exchange interfaces, that is, dense intracellular hyphal coils in the rhizodermis of the hair roots. A representative subsample of root segments was taken from the roots of the relevant mesocosm (across all species in the pot). We stained the subsamples with trypan blue using a modified protocol (Vohnik, 2020). We cleared the roots by autoclaving at 121°C for 20 min in a 10% KOH solution, rinsed with tap water three times, and acidified in a 2% HCl solution at room temperature for 30 min. We then stained the samples with a 0.05% solution of trypan blue in lactoglycerol (1:1:1 glycerol, lactic acid, deionized water) overnight at room temperature. The samples were then rinsed with tap water 10 times and de‐stained in lactoglycerol at 4°C for 4 days. We mounted the samples on microscope slides with polyvinyllactoglycerol. We used the magnified intersections method (McGonigle et al., 1990) (400× magnification) to quantify the ErM colonization, which involved assessing vertical transects (minimum 20) along each slide that intersected with the root segments, and each intersection was scored for the presence or absence of the hyphal coils. We expressed colonization as the percentage of intersections colonized.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2024). Models were run using lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) and visualized using ggplot2 (Wickham et al., 2016). Estimated means were calculated from models and statistically compared using emmeans (Lenth et al., 2019). All analyses can be found in the Zenodo repository at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20043640 (Delavaux, 2026). Plant and fungal diversity were treated as categorical variables as preliminary analyses showed that there were not enough diversity categories to robustly treat the variables as continuous.

Root colonization

We tested for root colonization differences between (1) all non‐inoculated and inoculated and (2) non‐inoculated and each fungal diversity level. We used a generalized linear model (GLM), with a binomial distribution to model proportion colonization (cbind(colonized intersects, non‐colonized intersects)). This allowed observations to be accurately weighted when the number of intersects evaluated differed (weighting those with more intersects more) (Zuur et al., 2009).

Diversity effects on plant productivity

To test for the effect of plant and fungal diversity on mesocosm plant productivity, we ran models for either aboveground or belowground mesocosm biomass as explanatory variables. Each of these models was run as a linear mixed‐effects model. Fixed effects included plant and fungal diversity (as categorical explanatory variables) and an interaction of these two variables. We additionally included the final number of plant individuals remaining in the pot (reduced in some instances due to plant death) and a random effect of experimental block to account for nonindependence of observations. Because initial height may influence biomass results, but cannot be directly included in mesocosm level models, we used an initial height corrected aboveground biomass (predicted aboveground biomass) in the corresponding model. To generate this predicted aboveground biomass, we used a model with natural log of individual plant aboveground biomass as the response, fixed effects of plant diversity, fungal diversity, an interaction of the two, and initial height, and a random effect of block.

To test for the effect of plant and fungal diversity on individual plant productivity, we ran analogous models that additionally included plant species identity as a predictor. However, this was only possible for aboveground biomass as we did not measure individual belowground biomass. This aboveground individual plant model was run as a linear mixed‐effects model, with the natural log of predicted aboveground biomass as the response. Fixed effects included plant and fungal diversity, plant species identity, and all two and three‐way interactions among these variables. We included a nested random effect of block and mesocosm number within block to account for non‐independence of observations.

Complementarity and selection

We calculated statistical complementarity, selection, and net biodiversity effects using approaches from previous work (Loreau & Hector, 2001). Specifically, statistical complementarity represents a diversity effect on productivity; selection represents a sampling effect, where only a few species underlie the observed plant diversity–productivity patterns. The net biodiversity effect (delta Y) is defined as the sum of complementarity and selection, representing the deviation of mixture yield from monoculture, while accounting for species abundances. We ran subsequent models estimating either complementarity or selection and how these effects varied with diversity. These models were linear models conducted for aboveground biomass only. Fixed effects included plant and fungal diversity, plant species identity, and all two and three‐way interactions among these variables. We included a random effect of block to account for the non‐independence of observations.

Overyielding

We also assessed overyielding in plant species by plant and fungal diversity treatments. Overyielding tests for changes in individual plant performance as a result of these diversity treatments relative to monoculture. Overyielding was determined by calculating productivity by plant species in monoculture. This null expectation was then extrapolated to each plant species in mixed plant communities (the expected productivity). Finally, the difference between realized (measured) productivity in mixture and expected productivity from monocultures was calculated per species. The model structure to assess species overyielding followed that for individual aboveground biomass described above, except that the response was overyielding grouped at the species × mesocosm level.

Mycorrhizal growth response

To assess whether inoculation with different ErMF species/isolates differed in the effects on individual plant aboveground biomass, we modeled the natural log of the mycorrhizal growth response (MGR) using a linear mixed‐effects model. MGR is used as a proxy for mycorrhizal benefit and represents the difference in biomass between the control and inoculated treatments (i.e., the change in biomass caused by inoculation). We focused on fungal diversity to isolate the effect of fungal isolate identity; higher fungal diversity treatments necessarily have multiple ErMF isolates and therefore attributing effects to individual ErMF species is not possible for these treatments. Our model included the fixed effects of plant diversity, plant species, inoculum identity, and initial height. The random effects were the block number and the nested mesocosm (pot) number within block. We found no effect of isolate identity (likely due to low replication), and therefore proceeded to test for variation in MGR across all fungal diversity levels. This second model included the fixed effects of plant diversity, plant species, and fungal diversity. Random effects were block and nested mesocosm number within block. Although significant shifts in MGR could be assessed using our biomass model (via interactions), we opted to directly test MGR as a response as this metric is well established in mycorrhizal ecology and is more easily interpretable as mycorrhizal benefit.

RESULTS

Root colonization

Inoculation was confirmed as successful through root colonization assessment. In particular, inoculated mesocosms showed significantly higher colonization than non‐inoculated control treatments (p < 0.001, Appendix S1: Figure S3). Overall, mean colonization in inoculated mesocosms was 39%, whereas it was 9% in control mesocosms. When testing the difference in colonization between control and inoculated seedlings separately within each fungal diversity level, we detected a significant difference in colonization for fungal diversity levels 2, 4, and 7 compared to control (p < 0.001, Appendix S1: Figure S3, estimated means: inoculum level 2 = 45%, level 4 = 38%, level 7 = 67%). Small amounts of contamination are common in inoculation experiments with ErMF (Vohnik, 2020), as these fungi are ubiquitous and relatively motile (especially through anemochory and zoochory). However, differences detected in treated mesocosms/individuals relative to non‐inoculated controls can be attributed to the inoculation treatment. As fungal diversity level 1 was not significantly differently colonized compared to the control (7% vs. 9%), interpreting results at this level should be done with caution (i.e., nonsignificant differences between diversity level 1 and the control may be due to background contamination boosting growth in the control).

Diversity effects on plant productivity

Aboveground productivity was predicted by an interaction between plant and fungal diversity (p = 0.002, Figure 2A; Appendix S1: Table S1). For fungal diversity 0 and 1, we found no effect of plant diversity on plant productivity; that is, in these inoculation treatments, plant diversity did not increase productivity. At fungal diversity 2, 4, and 8, classic positive plant diversity–productivity patterns emerged. Differences were only significant between specific treatments (Figure 2A), but trends qualitatively matched the positive relationship expected. In particular, at fungal diversity 2, plant diversity 2 and 8 resulted in significantly higher plant productivity than plant diversity 1 (p = 0.01 and 0.05, respectively). At fungal diversity 4, plant diversity 4 showed higher productivity than at 1 or 2 (p < 0.001 and 0.05, respectively). Finally, at the highest fungal diversity in the experiment (7), we found that productivity was higher in plant diversity 4 than 1. These patterns show that increasing fungal diversity (at least with two fungi) leads to positive plant diversity–productivity patterns. Across inoculum diversities in which plant diversity positively predicts productivity (diversity 2 and above), we found a saturation effect at plant diversity level 4.

When exploring the effect of fungal diversity on aboveground biomass within plant diversity levels (Figure 2C), additional patterns emerged. At a plant diversity of 1 (i.e., monoculture), increasing fungal diversity led to a reduction in productivity (Figure 2C). That is, there was an antagonistic effect of fungal diversity. Specifically, we found that biomass decreased across the fungal diversity gradient of 0, 1, and 2, and then remained consistently low across fungal diversity levels 4 and 7 (Figure 2C; p < 0.01, Appendix S1: Table S1B). This pattern only occurred in plant monocultures and was absent from higher plant diversity levels (i.e., no significant effects).

Belowground mesocosm productivity was only predicted by plant diversity (Figure 2B); no effect of fungal diversity or interaction between plant and fungal diversity was detected (Appendix S1: Table S1). Specifically, increasing plant diversity in mesocosms increased belowground productivity (p = 0.013). Pairwise comparisons between plant diversity levels showed that there was a significant increase between plant diversity level 1 and 4 (p = 0.007, Appendix S1: Table S1B), with productivity more than doubling. The difference between plant diversity level 4 and 8 was no longer significant (p = 0.58), signifying saturation in productivity gains with diversity beyond this level. Although we did not find a significant inoculum by plant diversity interaction, visualizing these effects reveals trends consistent with aboveground patterns (Figure 2D). We posit that the lack of significance may be due to the relatively lower representation of belowground mesocosm biomass measured compared to aboveground (191 vs. 599 pots).

As we weighed aboveground biomass on a per individual basis, we were able to analyze species‐specific responses to increasing plant and fungal diversity on productivity. Aboveground productivity was significantly predicted by the three‐way interaction between plant diversity, fungal diversity, and plant species identity (Appendix S1: Table S2). When we examined the impact of plant diversity across varying levels of fungal diversity on plant productivity across different plant species, we found that most plant species followed the mesocosm‐level patterns, but some showed divergent responses. Species generally fit into two major patterns, represented well by patterns observed in Calluna vulgaris and G. miqueliana (Figure 3). C. vulgaris broadly showed a pattern of decreasing productivity with increasing plant diversity in zero or low inoculum diversities, but this correlation shifted toward positive with increasing fungal diversity; other species showing this pattern were V. corymbosum, Vaccinium myrtillus, Pieris japonica, and V. angustifolium. G. miqueliana broadly showed a pattern of decreasing productivity with increasing plant diversity in zero or low inoculum diversities, but this correlation shifted toward neutral with increasing fungal diversity; other species showing this pattern were Gaultheria shallon, G. procumbens, V. vitis‐idaea, Kalmia latifolia, and V. macrocarpon (Figure 3A). Although there are subtle differences in these patterns, these species‐specific assessments confirm that in non‐inoculated control mesocosms, plant diversity impeded productivity, whereas increasing fungal diversity, especially to a medium level, buffered (removed) or reversed this trend. Across individual plant species, fungal diversity reduced productivity in monoculture (e.g., C. vulgaris) or across plant diversities (e.g., V. macrocarpon, Figure 3B; Appendix S1: Table S2B).

Complementarity and selection

Results show that complementarity increases with increasing levels of plant diversity (Figure 4A; Appendix S1: Table S3) across all inoculum diversities. However, we also find that as fungal diversity increases, complementarity decreases (Figure 4B) across all plant diversities. Plant species identity was not found to be important in predicting complementarity. Selection effects were in the opposite direction to complementarity effects, decreasing with increasing plant diversity and increasing with fungal diversity. The net biodiversity effect, determined by both complementarity and selection effects, was also influenced by both plant and fungal diversity levels, switching from negative to positive across plant diversity in low fungal diversities to high diversities.

Overyielding

Although representation of plant species is even across our experiment, plant species‐specific responses could vary. Therefore, we assessed overyielding of each plant species (i.e., the additional realized compared to additive effect of productivity of mesocosms). We tested whether this depended on plant and fungal diversity level and plant species identity. We found a significant three‐way interaction (Appendix S1: Figure S4, Table S4). In non‐inoculated control communities, overyielding increased with increasing plant diversity. However, as fungal diversity increased, this pattern became weaker. This weakening effect was less pronounced for some species with increasing fungal diversity (e.g., C. vulgaris, G. macrocarpon, G. procumbens, V. angustifolium), but was sustained for others (e.g., G. shallon, K. latifolia, P. japonica).

Mycorrhizal growth response

We did not find any effect of ErMF identity on MGR. This is likely due to exceedingly low ErMF species by plant species representation in our dataset, as previous work using the same plant species and fungal inocula confirms that plant and fungal identity mediates MGR (Neves et al., 2025). Although we represent ErMF isolates evenly across the experiment, we only had 2–30 plants with each fungal isolate (mean = 11.12) at fungal diversity level 1 where we tested this effect. Therefore, we proceeded to investigate how MGR shifts with fungal diversity. This allows an understanding of the mycorrhizal growth benefit by plant species and how it changes with plant and ErMF diversity. Here, we find a significant three‐way interaction between plant and fungal diversity, and plant species (Appendix S1: Table S5). Notably, we find that K. latifolia MGR goes from negative to positive with increasing plant diversity across each fungal diversity; however, this is most striking for fungal diversity level 1 (Figure 5).

DISCUSSION

Plant diversity–productivity in the ErM system

We find that both above and belowground biomass respond positively to plant diversity in most situations, confirming a long‐established positive relationship between plant diversity and productivity. The positive above and belowground responses suggest that these patterns are not due to shifting allocation between shoots and roots but represent an increase in overall biomass. Root biomass may represent a substantial portion of plant biomass, representing up to 67% in grasslands (Ma et al., 2021), but studies exploring belowground biomass responses to plant diversity are scarce. A meta‐analysis found that root biomass increased in mixture, but the respective studies were conducted in forests and grasslands (Ma & Chen, 2016). Therefore, our study extends previous findings by showing that both above and belowground productivity respond positively to plant diversity in the ErM system. These increases are likely to be the result of increased complementarity relative to selection, as we found that net biodiversity effects shift from negative to positive across plant diversity with increasing fungal diversity. Moreover, we find that species‐specific overyielding consistently increases with increasing plant diversity. Together, these findings provide evidence that the positive plant diversity–productivity relationship detected in our experiment is predominantly a consequence of complementary use of resources across plant species.

Fungal diversity effects on plant diversity–productivity relationships

Our results suggest that mycorrhizal diversity plays a prominent role in shaping the effects of plant diversity on productivity, with ErMF diversity mediating the positive plant diversity–productivity relationship. Specifically, we found a significant interaction of plant and ErMF diversity in predicting plant productivity: plant diversity–productivity patterns were not present when no inoculum was present (i.e., non‐inoculated pots), but only emerged with increasing fungal diversity. This effect was largely consistent belowground, although the effects of fungal diversity were not significant, likely due to low sample size. This may also be due to slowed growth in mesocosms with fungal diversity of zero or one preventing competition and/or partitioning of resources.

Interestingly, our results suggest antagonistic mutualist effects within plant monocultures. Specifically, when monocultures were inoculated with increasing levels of ErMF diversity, their biomass was reduced. For V. macrocarpon, this pattern was also seen at higher plant diversity levels. ErMF isolates vary dramatically in host plant MGR (Neves et al., 2025; Ważny et al., 2022), even in some cases acting as pathogens by reducing plant growth. Therefore, it is possible that negative ErMF sampling effects (increasingly likelihood of the least beneficial ErMF “selected” at high diversity) may lead to this effect in monocultures. This is consistent with previous work which showed that antagonism can occur with increasing symbiont diversity due to sampling of a particularly harmful/less beneficial symbiont (Magnoli & Bever, 2023). Since we do not have quantitative information on our individual ErMF isolate biomass or relative abundance in mixture, we cannot directly assess overrepresentation by specific isolates as a mechanism for these patterns. Alternatively, competition between symbionts may also reduce their benefit in mixture, resulting in antagonism.

Plant species‐level variation in diversity impacts on productivity

Our analyses of individual plant species largely supported community‐level patterns, with the productivity of most individual plant species increasing with plant diversity. This was consistent with our analyses of overyielding, where most plant species showed higher overyielding with shifting plant and fungal diversity. However, we also uncovered species‐specific variation in these patterns; some plant species exhibited positive diversity–productivity relationships with increasing fungal diversity, while others did not. Some of the mesocosm‐level patterns could also be attributed to shifting within‐plant species MGR (Figure 4). In particular, for K. latifolia, MGR shifted from negative to positive with increasing plant diversity, suggesting that plant productivity gains with plant diversity may be due to more beneficial mycorrhizal partnerships.

These species‐specific results have important implications if managers or practitioners have a particular interest in promoting biomass of specific plant species. This could be the case, for example, in restoration efforts, where the goal might be to support specific late‐successional species or species of conservation concern. This might also be the case where diversity is used to promote a particular crop species. Understanding plant species‐specific responses and dependence on plant and mycorrhizal diversity will then be key to successful management efforts.

Limitations and future research

To our knowledge, this work represents the first fully factorial experiment manipulating both plant and mycorrhizal fungal diversity to assess their impacts on productivity. However, this work has several limitations. One important limitation is that our plants and fungi are from ErM systems, but not all of these species necessarily coexist in nature. Future work should therefore work with co‐evolved plant and ErMF from the same ecosystem. Such work will help address whether such co‐evolved host–mutualist systems are more or less likely to show positive diversity–productivity relationships. Although we focus on the influence of beneficial mycorrhizal fungi on these relationships, understanding the relative influence of both beneficial and antagonistic (i.e., pathogens or herbivores) interactions with plants will also improve our ability to apply these findings in situ in agriculture and/or in restorations. Finally, we chose to conduct this experiment within the ErM system as it is understudied and under elevated threat, especially across Europe (Zedler & Kercher, 2005). This makes research around promoting plant productivity in this system urgently needed. However, only ca. 2% of plant species associate with ErMF (van der Heijden et al., 2015). In contrast, about 80% of vascular plant species (and most crop species) associate with AMF. Therefore, we suggest that future experiments should also be extended to the arbuscular mycorrhizal system.

CONCLUSIONS

Our results show that both plant and mycorrhizal fungal diversity can impact plant productivity outcomes. In our ErM system, we confirmed that plant diversity promoted productivity, but that this trend emerged only when a higher diversity of ErMF were present. Interestingly, we found that increasing fungal diversity reduces productivity in single‐plant systems (i.e., monocultures), as antagonism between symbionts likely limited plant biomass production. These effects varied depending on plant species analyzed. Therefore, this work (1) confirms that plant diversity–productivity relationships exist in understudied ErM systems—both above and belowground, (2) highlights the important yet overlooked role of fungal mutualists in mediating this pattern, and (3) elucidates plant species‐specific responses to combined plant and fungal diversity. Together, this work suggests that both plant and mycorrhizal fungal diversity are critical to maintaining the productivity of ErM ecosystems. Consideration of both plant and fungal diversity will be necessary to design conservation and restoration efforts that aim to promote biodiversity and carbon storage within these critical ecosystems.
