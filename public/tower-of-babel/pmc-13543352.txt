The effects of resistance band training on strength, vertical jump and sprint performance in young male basketball players: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Tahir Volkan Aslan, Sakir Tufekci, Fatih Harun Turhan, Sacide Tufekci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to investigate the effects of a six-week resistance band training programme on strength, vertical jump and sprint performance in young male basketball players.MethodsThe study employed a pre-test–post-test experimental design with a control group. Twenty male basketball players (age: 16.35 ± 0.67 years; height: 177.95 ± 8.10 cm; body weight: 70.52 ± 10.69 kg) were randomly assigned to two groups (experimental, n = 10; and control, n = 10). The experimental group underwent a six-week resistance band training programme in addition to their basketball training, whilst the control group continued with their routine basketball training only. The players underwent testing for leg strength, back strength, vertical jump height, 30-metre sprint performance and handgrip strength before and after the six-week intervention. Data meeting the assumptions of normality were analysed using Repeated Measures ANOVA.ResultsLeg strength (F = 12.909, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.418), back strength (F = 7.385, p = 0.014, ηp² = 0.291), vertical jump performance (F = 11.780, p = 0.003, ηp² = 0.396) and dominant hand grip strength (F = 13.179, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.423). In contrast, no significant group × time interaction was observed for 30 m sprint performance (F = 1.099, p = 0.308, ηp² = 0.058) or non-dominant hand grip strength (F = 0.249, p = 0.624, ηp² = 0.014).ConclusionsIn conclusion, the findings of the present study suggest that a six-week resistance band training programme may improve leg strength, back strength, dominant-hand grip strength and vertical jump performance in young male basketball players. No significant improvements were observed in non-dominant hand grip strength or 30-m sprint performance. Overall, these findings indicate that resistance band training may represent a practical, low-cost and feasible strategy for supporting the development of strength- and power-related performance during the in-season period in young male basketball players.Keywords: Basketball, Resistance band, Strength, Vertical jump, Sprint

Introduction

Success in basketball requires a high level of physical fitness to support the effective application of technical and tactical skills [1]. Basketball, which involves technical and tactical manoeuvres such as repeated sprints, changes of direction, accelerations and jumps, is a team sport that demands high aerobic and anaerobic capacity, as well as advanced neuromuscular characteristics and muscle strength [2–4]. Lower-limb strength, in particular, is one of the key determinants of agility performance and shows a significant correlation with agility skills in female basketball players [5]. Although these physical characteristics develop naturally during the growth and maturation process [6], appropriately planned training programmes can further enhance this development [7].

In this regard, targeted strength training is one of the fundamental practices that support performance development in young athletes. Previous studies have shown that strength training, when carried out under appropriate loading and supervision, improves performance safely and effectively [8, 9]. Consequently, resistance training which is also recommended by various professional organisations can enhance athletic performance whilst reducing the risk of injury when performed using the correct technique [10, 11]. Among resistance training methods, elastic bands stand out as a suitable option for young athletes due to the low joint load they impose and the variable resistance they provide. Furthermore, their low cost, portability and versatility have contributed to their widespread use [12]. It has been reported that resistance training using elastic bands improves muscle strength, power and endurance [13] and can elicit neuromuscular adaptations similar to those of traditional resistance training [14, 15].

It has been shown that resistance training using elastic bands produces positive effects on physical fitness, body composition and health-related parameters in various populations [16, 17]. Unlike traditional fixed-load resistance training, elastic bands provide a load that is more in line with the natural muscle force curve by creating variable resistance that increases gradually as the band stretches [18, 19]. It has been reported that this loading profile increases the rate of force development [20], supports muscle activation [21] and can improve motor coordination and neuromuscular adaptation [19, 22]. These characteristics highlight resistance band training as a safe and effective method for strength development [23–25].

The impact of these biomechanical and neuromuscular characteristics on performance has been demonstrated in various studies. A meta-analysis reported that this method can improve lower-limb explosive strength, the ability to change direction and sprint performance in team sport athletes [26]. Similarly, a systematic review has shown that resistance training using elastic bands improves performance parameters such as jumping and change of direction in athletes across different age groups and training levels [27]. Furthermore, the systematic review and meta-analysis by Barahona-Fuentes et al. provided strong evidence that resistance band training is effective in improving key components of physical fitness in child and adolescent handball players [28].

However, existing studies have largely been conducted on different sports and the general population, and experimental evidence relating to young basketball players is limited. Given the specific physical demands of basketball and the developmental characteristics of young athletes, there is a need for studies examining the effects of resistance band training on performance in this population. Consequently, this study aims to investigate the effects of a six-week resistance band training programme on strength, vertical jump and sprint performance in young male basketball players. The hypothesis of the study is that a six-week resistance band training programme will significantly improve strength, vertical jump and sprint performance in young male basketball players.

Methods

Participants

Twenty male basketball players (age: 16.35 ± 0.67 years; height: 177.95 ± 8.10 cm; body weight: 70.52 ± 10.69 kg; BMI: 22.10 ± 2.90 kg/m² and training experience: 5.15 ± 1.53 years) aged 16–18 years, all with at least three years of regular training experience in an amateur basketball team, voluntarily participated in this study. Eligibility criteria included: (a) a minimum of three years of basketball experience, (b) absence of musculoskeletal injuries or health conditions that could affect performance, and (c) willingness to attend all training sessions and testing procedures. Participants who experienced injuries during the intervention or failed to complete the follow-up assessments were excluded. Prior to recruitment, sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power software [29] for a repeated-measures design with two groups and two measurement points. Assuming a moderate effect size (f = 0.25), 80% statistical power, and a significance level of 0.05, the analysis indicated that at least nine participants per group were required. Initially, 24 athletes were recruited for the study; however, two participants were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria, whilst two others were excluded because they did not take part in the training sessions or testing procedures. Consequently, the final sample consisted of 20 participants (10 per group).

Participants were randomly allocated to either the experimental or control group using a simple randomisation procedure performed by an independent blinded researcher. This method was employed to create groups of equal size and to minimise potential bias between groups. Allocation was carried out using sealed opaque envelopes. Twenty envelopes were prepared, with 10 containing the number “1” and 10 containing the number “2”. After confirming eligibility and consent, each participant selected one envelope, and the researcher assigned the athlete according to the number revealed (1 = experimental; 2 = control). Following randomisation, participants in both groups were numbered sequentially from 1 to 10. (Table 1) (Fig. 1).

Study protocol

The study employed a pre-test–post-test experimental design with a control group. All sessions were conducted during the final phase of the team’s competitive season. At the outset, participants in both the experimental and control groups underwent anthropometric measurements (height, body weight), as well as tests of leg strength, back strength, vertical jump height, 30-metre sprint time and handgrip strength. With the exception of the anthropometric measurements, a standard warm-up protocol comprising 10 min of light jogging, dynamic movements with and without a ball, and static and ballistic stretching exercises was carried out prior to all other measurements [30]. Prior to the study, participants underwent three preparatory phases to familiarise themselves with the equipment and experimental procedures. Over a period of 6 weeks, participants underwent a total of 18 sessions of resistance band training, conducted three days a week on non-consecutive days, in addition to their routine basketball training. The additional training sessions were added to the end of the participants’ routine training sessions. Participants in both the control and experimental groups continued with their regular basketball technique and tactics training sessions, held five days a week, and took part in regular official league matches as part of the competitive season throughout the study period. Whereas the control group did not perform any strength training, participants in the experimental group were put through a resistance band training programme comprising 10 exercises for the upper and lower extremities (Squat, Body Jump, Biceps Curl, Triceps Push Down, Incline Chest Press, Forward Lunge, Chest Press, Standing Row, Lat Pull Down, Standing Hamstring Curl). No adverse events were observed during the training sessions. At the end of the six-week period, both groups completed the data collection process by repeating the tests for leg strength, back strength, vertical jump height, 30-metre sprint time and handgrip strength. All tests were carried out under similar conditions (temperature: 20 ± 2 °C; relative humidity: 45–55 per cent), at the same time of day (10:00–12:00) to minimise the effect of the diurnal rhythm, and at least three days after the last match, in an indoor sports (basketball) hall (Fig. 2).

Research study protocol

Training protocol

Resistance band training sessions were conducted three days a week on non-consecutive days. The athletes were asked to avoid strenuous physical activities for at least 24 h prior to each test session and to refrain from any additional resistance training for at least 24 h prior to training sessions. To ensure that no excessively intense training sessions took place on test days or prior to resistance training sessions, training loads were monitored through communication with the coaching staff. Each training session included a 5-minute low-intensity run (~ 120 bpm) monitored via Polar devices, 10 min of dynamic warm-up exercises, and a 5-minute recovery period before resistance band training commenced. The training method and resistance band exercise programme were designed in line with previous studies in the literature and current recommendations for strength training practices [18, 31, 32]. All training sessions were supervised by a coach with at least 10 years’ experience in strength and conditioning. In the resistance band training protocol, each exercise was performed as 2 sets of 10–12 repetitions, with 30 seconds’ rest between exercises and 2 minutes’ rest between sets (Table 2). The exercises were performed using blue resistance bands (CLX TheraBand®, Hygenic Corporation, Akron, OH, USA) for the first two weeks. In accordance with the principle of progressive loading and based on the graduated resistance levels specified by the manufacturer of the resistance bands [31, 32], all participants were switched to black resistance bands in weeks 3 and 4, and to grey resistance bands in weeks 5 and 6. Thus, the external load applied throughout the training period was increased in a systematic and planned manner by utilising the resistance bands’ increasing resistance levels. Throughout the intervention period, participants were provided with guidance on proper nutrition and recovery practices.

Measurements

Anthropometric measurements

Height measurements were obtained using a SECA stadiometer (Germany; 0.01 m precision), and body mass was assessed with a Tanita BC-418 MA electronic scale (Japan; 0.1 kg precision). Participants wore lightweight clothing and removed accessories during testing. All measurements were recorded under standardised conditions to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Leg strength test

Leg muscle strength was assessed using a back-leg dynamometer (Takei TKK 5402, Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Japan), a device commonly used in strength testing. Dynamometers have been used to determine strength for many years, and numerous studies have demonstrated that dynamometer-based strength measurements are both reliable and valid [33, 34]. Prior to the measurements, participants completed a standardised 5-minutes warm-up protocol consisting of low-intensity general exercises and dynamic stretching movements targeting the lower limb muscles. The test protocol was explained in detail, and each participant performed a practice trial before data collection. During the test, participants stood on the dynamometer platform with full plantar contact. Their knees were positioned at approximately 120° of flexion, with the back kept straight and the torso leaning slightly forwards. Participants were instructed to pull the bar vertically upwards with maximum force using only their legs, whilst fully extending their arms, keeping their elbows fixed and gripping the bar firmly with their hands [35]. The test was repeated twice with a 3-minutes rest interval between repetitions, and the best result was recorded.

Back strength test

Back muscle strength was measured whilst standing with the lower back flexed at 30°, using a back and leg dynamometer (Takei TKK 5402, Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Japan). Prior to the measurements, participants completed a standardised 5-minutes warm-up protocol consisting of low-intensity general exercises and dynamic stretching movements. The test protocol was explained in detail, and each participant performed a practice trial before data collection began. The athletes were instructed to grip the dynamometer’s handle with both hands and pull the bar upwards as straight as possible, without bending their knees or lifting their heels [36]. The test was repeated twice with a 3-minutes rest interval between repetitions, and the best result was recorded.

Vertical jump test

The athletes’ vertical jump measurements were taken using an electronic Fusion Smart Jump mat. The participants stepped onto the jump mat wearing sports clothing and trainers. On the mat, athletes were instructed to place their hands on their hips, stand with both feet on the mat, bend their knees to a 90-degree squat, quickly lower themselves down, and then jump as high as possible [37]. Each participant performed two jumps, with a 3-minute rest period between jumps to allow the athlete to demonstrate their maximum performance. The test was repeated twice for each participant to ensure reliability, and the best performance value was recorded in seconds.

30 m sprint test

To assess sprint performance, a 30-m sprint test was conducted on a basketball court. Prior to the measurements, the participants performed a comprehensive warm-up consisting of 10 min of jogging at 60–70%HRmax, followed by 5 min of exercises involving fast leg movements over short distances of 5–10 m (e.g. skipping, carioca), followed by 2 min of passive rest and a comprehensive warm-up comprising 3–5 sets of 15-m shuttle sprints. Participants took their positions 50 cm behind the first photocell gate and were instructed to start when ready. Participants were encouraged to complete the 30-m distance as quickly as possible and to continue running for a further 5 m after crossing the finish line to prevent their speed from dropping before the sprint ended. Sprint times were measured in seconds using a dual-beam electronic timing system (Smart Speed; Fusion Sport, Australia). Each participant completed two attempts, with a 3-minutes rest period between runs to ensure maximum effort was exerted. The best performance time was recorded [38].

Hand grip strength

Grip strength was measured using a hand grip dynamometer (Takei, model TKK 5401, Japan). The device was adjusted so that each participant could comfortably grip the handle, ensuring proper contact between the palmar ridges and the middle phalanges. Participants stood upright with the arm to be tested extended and not touching the body; measurements were taken whilst the arm was positioned at a 45° angle to the body. The procedure was repeated three times for both the right and left hands, with a one-minute interval between each repetition, and the highest value was recorded [39].

Statistical analysis

All statistical procedures were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test, confirming that all variables were normally distributed, while homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. Variables satisfying these assumptions were analysed through a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with group (experimental vs. control) and time (pre-test vs. post-test) as factors. This approach was used to minimise the risk of Type I error. When significant group × time interactions were identified, Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons were applied. Since the time factor consisted of only two measurement points, the assumption of sphericity was inherently satisfied and Mauchly’s test was unnecessary. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared (ηp²) values (small: 0.01; medium: 0.06; large: 0.14) [40]. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05, and all data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Differences in veriables between control and experimental groups in pre and post test

A significant increase was observed in the leg strength parameter in the experimental group (Δ = +3.50 kg), whilst the change in the control group remained limited (Δ = +0.50 kg). The group × time interaction was found to be significant and of large effect size, and the mean difference in change between groups was calculated as + 3.00 kg (F = 12.909, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.418; 95% CI: 1.24 to 4.75). This large effect size clearly demonstrates the programme’s strong impact on leg strength. In terms of back strength, the experimental group showed an increase of Δ = +3.11 kg, whilst the control group recorded Δ = +0.46 kg. The group × time interaction was significant and had a large effect size, with the difference in change between groups determined to be + 2.65 kg (F = 7.385, p = 0.014, ηp² = 0.291; 95% CI: 0.60 to 4.71).

Whilst the experimental group showed a marked improvement in vertical jump performance with Δ = +5.02 cm, the control group recorded Δ = −0.77 cm. The group × time interaction was found to be significant and to have a large effect size, and the mean difference in change between groups was calculated as + 5.79 cm in favour of the experimental group (F = 11.780, p = 0.003, ηp² = 0.396; 95% CI: 2.24 to 9.33). This high effect size indicates that the programme has a significant effect on lower limb explosive power and neuromuscular performance.

In contrast, although both groups showed slight improvements in 30 m sprint performance (experimental group Δ = −0.10 s; control group Δ = −0.04 s), the group × time interaction was not found to be significant (F = 1.099, p = 0.308, ηp² = 0.058; 95% CI: −0.15 to 0.05).

The experimental group showed an increase of Δ = +2.70 kg in dominant-hand grip strength, whereas the control group showed a decrease of Δ = −0.79 kg. The group × time interaction was significant and had a large effect size, with the difference in change between groups determined to be + 3.49 kg (F = 13.179, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.423; 95% CI: 1.48 to 5.56). The high effect size highlights the programme’s strong impact on upper limb functional strength. No significant improvement was observed in non-dominant hand grip strength (ΔExp–ΔCon = + 0.27 kg; F = 0.249, p = 0.624, ηp² = 0.014; 95% CI: −0.87 to 1.41).

Overall, the findings indicate that the training programme administered produced strong and significant effects, particularly on strength and explosive power production; these gains, supported by high effect sizes, significantly increased dominant hand functional strength, but did not show a significant effect on sprint performance or non-dominant hand strength. Effect sizes (ηp²) and ΔExp–ΔCon values indicate that the programme induced a high level of adaptation, particularly in large muscle groups.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of a six-week resistance band training programme on strength, vertical jump and sprint performance in young male basketball players. The findings showed that resistance band training led to significant improvements, with large effect sizes, in leg strength, back strength, vertical jump performance and dominant-hand grip strength; however, it did not result in any significant changes in 30-metre sprint performance or non-dominant-hand grip strength. These results demonstrate that resistance band training is effective in developing performance components related to strength and explosive power in young basketball players; however, they also suggest that different physical performance characteristics may not respond to this training stimulus to the same extent.

Resistance band training resulted in significant improvements with large effect sizes in both leg strength and back strength in the experimental group compared with the control group (Leg strength: F = 12.909, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.418; Back strength: F = 7.385, p = 0.014, ηp² = 0.291). These findings indicate that a six-week resistance band training programme supports strength development in young male basketball players. Although studies examining the effect of resistance band training on strength development in basketball players are limited, Joy et al. [41] reported that resistance band-assisted strength training resulted in greater improvements in peak power and strength parameters compared to traditional weight training alone. This finding is consistent with our results, which suggest that the use of resistance bands may support strength development. Similarly, research conducted across different athletic populations has also shown that resistance band-based training increases upper and lower limb strength. Kamandulis et al. [42] and Asif and Sidiq [43] reported that elastic band training improved lower-limb strength; whilst studies conducted on young handball and volleyball players also reported significant improvements in back strength, upper-limb strength and other strength performance indicators [24, 25, 44, 45]. Furthermore, studies examining different resistance band protocols have similarly reported significant improvements in upper and lower limb strength [21, 23, 46, 47]. Given the limited number of studies conducted on basketball players, these findings from team sports with similar neuromuscular requirements contribute to the interpretation of our results. The observed increases in strength can be explained by the neural mechanisms that underpin early-stage adaptations to resistance training. The progressive mechanical load generated by the resistance band may have contributed to the strength gains observed in the experimental group by increasing motor unit activation, enhancing intra- and intermuscular coordination, and improving strength production capacity.

Consistent with the notion that improvements in lower limb strength translate into functional performance, resistance band training also produced a significant improvement in vertical jump performance with a high effect size (F = 11.780, p = 0.003, ηp² = 0.396). The 5.79 cm increase observed in the experimental group compared to the control group demonstrates that the programme has a clinically significant effect in enhancing explosive power capacity. Although studies examining this topic in basketball players are limited, existing research supports our findings. It has been reported that resistance training using elastic bands improves jump performance in basketball players [48, 49] and that squat exercises performed with high-resistance bands, in particular, result in significant increases in counter-movement jump (CMJ) height [50]. Similarly, studies conducted with footballers, volleyball players and tennis players, as well as various other athletic populations, have shown that resistance band-based training improves vertical jump, explosive lower-body performance and strength-speed characteristics [47, 51–53]. Given the limited literature on basketball, findings from these sports which share similar neuromuscular requirements support the interpretation of our results. The marked increase in vertical jump height observed in the experimental group can be explained by the more effective development of motor unit activation, the rate of transition of muscle fibres from the eccentric to the concentric phase, and intramuscular coordination through plyometric and explosive strength-focused training. The significant Group × Time interaction with a high effect size indicates that the training programme provided a strong stimulus on lower-limb explosive strength and neuromuscular performance.

Despite the significant improvements observed in strength and vertical jump performance, resistance band training did not have a significant effect on 30-metre sprint performance (F = 1.099, p = 0.308, ηp² = 0.058). This finding is consistent with the results reported by Januševičius et al. [54]. However, some studies using different resistance band protocols have reported significant improvements in 30 m sprint performance [55, 56]. This discrepancy is likely to stem from differences in the loading characteristics of the training protocols applied, the duration of the intervention and the profiles of the athletes. Sprint performance is the result of an integrated combination of sport-specific neuromuscular characteristics, such as high rate of force development, effective horizontal force application, short ground contact time and running mechanics. Although the resistance band exercises implemented in our study supported general strength development, they may not have sufficiently stimulated horizontal force production and the force-velocity characteristics at high movement speeds, which are of critical importance for sprint performance. Furthermore, the progressive increase in elastic band resistance throughout the movement may have provided only limited support for the sudden force production required, particularly during the initial acceleration phase of the sprint. Furthermore, it is possible that the six-week intervention period and the weekly training volume were insufficient to elicit measurable adaptations in sprint performance. These findings suggest that, if resistance band training is to be used to improve sprint performance, it may be more effective when supplemented with sprint-specific acceleration drills and exercises targeting horizontal force production.

Findings regarding handgrip strength indicate that resistance band training leads to different adaptations in the dominant and non-dominant hands. Whilst a significant improvement with a large effect size was observed in grip strength in the dominant hand (F = 13.179, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.423), no significant change was detected in the non-dominant hand (F = 0.249, p = 0.624, ηp² = 0.014). Although studies examining the effects of resistance band training on upper limb performance in basketball are limited, it has been reported that resistance band-assisted push-up training significantly increases arm muscle strength in basketball players [57]. Similarly, studies conducted on handball and volleyball players have shown that elastic band-based training improves upper limb strength, strength-speed performance and muscular endurance [24, 51, 58, 59]. Furthermore, studies conducted on young football players show that resistance band training supports the development of upper and lower extremity strength and increases the need for muscle stabilization by providing dynamic resistance [60, 61]. These findings support the improvements we observed in dominant-hand grip strength.

However, the fact that no significant development was observed in the non-dominant hand may be related to movement patterns specific to basketball. As technical skills such as dribbling, passing and shooting are largely performed using the dominant limb, this may have led to the development of more pronounced neuromuscular adaptations on the dominant side during the long-term training process. Although a significant proportion of the exercises performed were bilateral in nature, the fact that athletes utilised their dominant side more effectively whilst performing the movements may have limited the equal distribution of load between the two limbs. The literature reports that unilateral resistance training may also lead to strength gains in the non-dominant limb via neural adaptations (the cross-training effect) [62, 63]. However, the absence of a similar improvement in grip strength in the non-dominant hand in the present study may be attributed to the training programme consisting predominantly of bilateral exercises, the limited duration of the intervention, and basketball-specific side-use habits potentially suppressing the cross-training effect. These findings suggest that whilst resistance band training is effective in improving upper limb strength, more targeted training approaches incorporating unilateral exercises may be necessary to improve strength balance between the sides.

Limitations

Although the findings of this study provide valuable insights, certain limitations should be borne in mind to guide future research. Firstly, the short-term nature of the intervention limits the assessment of the long-term effects of resistance band training; sustainable effects should be investigated in future studies using longer-term protocols. Secondly, the generalisability of the findings is limited, as this study focuses solely on male footballers; research should be conducted across different genders, age groups, competitive levels and sports disciplines. Another limitation of the study is that variables which could potentially influence the results such as diet, fluid intake, sleep quality and previous training load were not objectively monitored or controlled for, and this may have affected the physical outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of the present study suggest that a six-week resistance band training programme may improve leg strength, back strength, dominant-hand grip strength and vertical jump performance in young male basketball players. No significant improvements were observed in non-dominant hand grip strength or 30-m sprint performance. Overall, these findings indicate that resistance band training may represent a practical, low-cost and feasible strategy for supporting the development of strength- and power-related performance during the in-season period in young male basketball players.
