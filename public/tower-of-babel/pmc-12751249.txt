Fund contraction challenges for supplies for maternal and newborn health services in low- and middle-income countries: a scoping review
Authors: Edosa Amente Gutema, Seman K Ousman, Ababe Tamirat, Masresha Tesemma, Zewdie Aderaw Alemu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth services and supplementation for pregnant women in Low and middle-income countries (LMICs) remain a vital public health challenge. Global efforts are hindered by collapsing health systems in many regions, largely due to international aid cuts that exacerbate mortality and suffering. This scoping review examines the challenges of funding cuts for supplies for maternal and newborn health (MNH) in resource-limited settings.ObjectiveTo examine the challenges of fund cuts/contractions on supplies for MNH in LMICs.MethodsFor this scoping review, a thorough search strategy was created using words found in titles and abstracts. Studies published in English from 2015 to 2025 were included in the search. The search results and study selection process were reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram. Eligible studies were screened and analyzed using Rayyan at https://new.rayyan.ai/rev. Data extraction was led by the studies included in Microsoft Excel spreadsheets. The findings were presented in narration, table, and figure to aid in data presentation.ResultsFor this scoping review, four databases were searched. The search yielded 982 records across all databases, and 169 duplicates were excluded. When screening titles and abstracts, 770 from 813 studies were excluded. Of the 43 remaining studies that underwent full-text review, 28 were excluded. and 15 articles were included. Key challenges of funding cuts were exacerbated by weak domestic financing, policy gaps, and heavy reliance on external donor support, which worsens maternal and newborn morbidity and mortality.ConclusionIdentified challenges underscore the need for strategic solutions for filling gaps and challenges for innovative financing to sustain maternal and newborn health programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25721-6.Keywords: Fund cut, Health financing, Maternal health services, Pregnant woman, Supply

Introduction

Background

Historically, donor support has been a notable part of Africa’s health financing, yet it is unsustainable as a primary source. It contributes up to 20 per cent of healthcare expenditure in most Sub-Saharan African nations (SSA) [1]. Global funding is provided by various international organizations and initiatives [2], aimed to address global health challenges, strengthen health systems, and support preparedness and response to health emergencies in LMICs [3]. For example, the pandemic fund provides a dedicated stream of long-term financing to strengthen pandemic prevention, preparedness, and response capabilities and address critical gaps in LMIC [2, 4].

In the last 30 years (1990–2020), substantial progress has been made in reducing maternal mortality [5]. However, with recent global maternal death estimates of 211/100,000 live births due to pregnancy and postpartum-related complications, the world is still not on track to achieve the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target of less than 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030 [5]. Most of these deaths are preventable if pregnant women receive proven, high-impact, evidence-based interventions when they need them [5].

As a report of the WHO policy on MNH, one-third of 130 LMICs had reached the SDG plan. Every Newborn Action Plan (ENAP) 2030 targets maternal mortality, stillbirth rates, and neonatal mortality [6]. The social and public health problems with micronutrient deficiencies, which are compounded by high fertility rates, repeated pregnancies, and short intervals between pregnancies, contribute to the poor MNH in developing countries [7]. A staggering 25-billion-dollar gap appeared between the funds needed for United Nations appeals and the contributions received in 2024, which is projected to be more than double in the future [8].

Globally, more than 240 million women of reproductive age in LMICs are underweight, 613 million women are anemic, and an estimated 450 million are stunted, which is a sign of chronic undernutrition [9]. Despite global efforts, health systems are on the verge of collapse in many regions due to significant cuts in international aid, leading to increased mortality and suffering. This crisis is worsened by ongoing conflicts, climate change, and poverty, creating a complex web of challenges that hinder aid delivery and may destabilize affected nations. The consequences include the closure of vital health programs, disrupted education and social services, and a heightened risk of pandemics [8].

The United States (US) contributed nearly 30% of the global aid budget and funded 43% of worldwide humanitarian needs [8]. The reliance was disrupted when the U.S. abruptly suspended USAID, a major conduit for American financial support to these nations, signaling a significant shift in external funding dynamics [10]. USAID has been quite instrumental in closing funding gaps in most developing countries. Therefore, the United States’s decision to shut down USAID to developing countries highlights the urgent need for Africa to lessen its reliance on external assistance [11]. Radical changes, introducing new radical thinking, can easily emerge in donor countries [10], which can affect the smooth inflow of foreign aid that had previously seemed a normal and acceptable part of government funding. If donor funding stopped due to human rights violations or financing of terrorism, it would result in the cessation of US funding and aid [11]. In 2025, aid cuts, along with reductions by other nations, have had an outsized impact, jeopardized essential services, and weakened global health security [8].

The US withdrawal from the World Health Organization (WHO), coupled with significant aid cuts, is causing global health systems to collapse, leading to increased mortality and jeopardizing decades of progress in areas like maternal and child health, particularly impactful as it represents a substantial reduction in critical funding for the WHO [12]. In early 2025, the U.S. announced its withdrawal from the WHO, stripping away nearly 20% of critical funding [13]. Due to this, globally, health systems are collapsing from massive aid cuts, and people are suffering from the decision’s consequences [13].

Rationale for the review

Historically, many maternal and child health programs and initiatives in SSA have relied heavily on external donor funding, such as from the USAID, Global Fund, Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunization (Gavi), and other international agencies, to support procurement, distribution, and program implementation [14]. The shifts in global health funding priorities, economic constraints, and the COVID-19 pandemic have led to reductions in external support, threatening the sustainability of these programs [15]. The massive fund cuts have had a severe impact and are challenging the health programs in SSA, even before the US President froze funding since February 2025 [10, 16, 17].

Supplies and medical care during pregnancy contribute significantly to maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality [18, 19]. Financial support is essential for the effective care of all women giving birth in medical facilities, reducing the quality gap, and providing antenatal care (ANC) and pregnancy-related services, which are key factors in preventing maternal morbidity and mortality [20]. However, these programs often struggle with sustainability due to challenges in funding, health system capacity, logistics, policy backing, and patient adherence.

Support typically comes from a mix of government budgets, international agencies, and private donors. Initiatives backed by global funding are instrumental in reducing maternal and child morbidity and mortality through comprehensive efforts that encompass prenatal services, safe delivery practices, postnatal care, and immunization programs [21]. The funding for Maternal and Newborn remains an essential feature for ensuring an integrated approach to improved MNH [22].

As external support diminishes, it becomes increasingly urgent to investigate how countries can keep these critical interventions through enhanced domestic ownership, targeted policy reforms, and innovative financing strategies. In resource-limited settings, uncertainty persists around sustaining supplies for maternal and newborn health efforts without relying heavily on donor support. Accordingly, this scoping review was expected to review the global fund cuts challenges in the supplies for MNH, and map out existing evidence and strategies that countries might adopt to preserve maternal health interventions amidst shifting global funding landscapes.

Why a scoping review?

A scoping review is an ideal tool to determine the scope or coverage of a body of literature on a given topic and give a clear indication of the volume of literature and studies available, as well as an overview of its focus [23]. Scoping reviews are useful for examining emerging evidence when it is still unclear what other, more specific questions can be posed and valuably addressed by a more precise systematic review. The general purpose of conducting scoping reviews is to identify and map the available evidence [24].

Significance of the study

This scoping review aimed to explore the challenges posed by funding contractions and related systemic barriers to the sustainability of supplies for pregnant women in LMICs. By synthesizing current evidence, the review seeks to identify strategic policy options, refine guidelines, and propose cost-effective supplementation approaches to improve maternal and newborn outcomes. The findings will be of critical relevance to policymakers, development partners, and scholars working toward sustainable maternal health systems and progress toward the SDGs.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The scoping review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews, which provides a rigorous and standardized approach for identifying, mapping, and analyzing existing literature on a particular topic [25, 26]. This method supports transparent and reproducible processes for defining objectives, selecting studies, extracting data, and presenting results. Following the JBI guidelines ensured the credibility and methodological robustness of the review process.

In addition, the review was reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for the PRISMA-ScR checklist. This reporting guideline promotes clarity, completeness, and transparency in presenting scoping review findings, aligning with best practices in evidence synthesis. The use of PRISMA-ScR enhances the reliability of the review and eases its integration into policy and practice.

To ensure further transparency and research integrity, the review title has been submitted for registration on an international database of Open Science Framework (OSF) with a DOI of 10.17605/OSF.IO/5ZTFP. This step underscores commitment to methodological rigor and public accountability in the evidence synthesis process.

Data sources

Electronic databases (Google Scholar and PubMed) were used to retrieve information from studies. To assess the challenges of the fund cut/contraction on supplies for maternal and newborn health in LMICs. The article search was conducted between 1 st and 15th July 2025.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

This review considered both observational and experimental study designs, as well as protocols, guidelines, policy briefs, and fact sheets reporting on the challenges of funding contractions on the sustainability of supplies for pregnant women. In addition, case reports, conference proceedings, and national survey reports were analyzed to identify best practices and challenges. In this review, we considered studies and reports that revealed the challenges of funding cuts and were written in the English language.

Population

Maternal-newborn health services, Pregnant women attending antenatal care.

Concept

Fund cuts for supplies.

Context

Low-and middle-income countries (LMICs).

Time frame/publication year

This review considered publications from the past ten years (2015–2025). While the primary focus was on recent studies, limited evidence has emerged since the February 2024 announcement of funding reductions by the current US President. As a result, select studies published in the past ten-year window were also included to capture relevant data and context.

Types of evidence

peer-reviewed articles, policy briefs/reports, evaluations, and grey literature.

Exclusion criteria

Studies published in languages other than English were excluded. Additionally, studies conducted outside of LMICs, those not addressing the challenges of the fund cuts, were excluded.

Search strategy

The search looked for studies that have been published, unpublished studies, and reports. For this review, a three-step search approach was applied. To find literature on the subject, a preliminary, constrained search of PubMed and Google Scholar was conducted, with modifications made as needed. Next, a comprehensive search strategy was developed using the index keywords used to characterize the articles and the text words found in the titles and abstracts of pertinent publications. Every database and/or information source that is included in the search strategy has its own set of keywords and index phrases that are specifically chosen for it. Lastly, further research was conducted in the reference list of all incorporated sources of evidence. Studies were assessed and incorporated references from single studies or systematic reviews on related subjects.

This review aimed to assess the challenges of the fund cuts on the sustainability of supplies for maternal and newborn health during pregnancy, focusing on best practices and implementation challenges. PubMed, Google Scholar, PubMed Central, and Bookshelf databases were searched. A search was performed according to PRISMA guidelines, limited the search to humans, and the complete search strategy was developed by using MeSH terms (Table 1, Annex II).

Study screening and selection

All found citations were gathered and imported into the Rayyan software at https://new.rayyan.ai/rev after the search, with duplicates being eliminated. Subsequently, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts and compared them to the review’s inclusion criteria. Complete retrievals of potentially pertinent studies were made, and the reference manager was imported with the details of those citations.

A study selection tool was developed for title and abstract screening and full-text review. The tool was first practiced by two independent reviewers on a random sample of literature. The level of agreement between the two reviewers was checked by calculating the kappa statistic. Disagreements were discussed to modify the screening tool, and the tool was applied until near-perfect agreement was realized (kappa statistic > 0.8).

Studies screening

Two investigators independently evaluated the entire text of the chosen citations against the inclusion criteria described in the PCC framework. The screening was conducted in two phases. The first is title and abstract screening, followed by full text review. Any point of disagreement was resolved through discussion and consensus between the two reviewers.

The difference in study selection was solved by discussion. The review documented and described the reasons behind the exclusion of full-text papers that do not match the inclusion criteria. The results of the search and the study inclusion process were reported in full in the final review with the PRISMA flow diagram [27] (Figs. 2 in the results section).

PRISMA- SC flow diagram for the scoping review process

Data extraction and charting

The data extraction was done by Arksey and O’Malley’s (2005) framework [28], and further refined by Levac (2010 [25]. Data extraction involves systematically collecting key information from selected studies to ensure consistency, transparency, and replicability. The data extraction included the authors’ names, publication year, study location, aims/objectives, and main findings (Table S2 in the supplementary file).

Charting the data is the process of organizing the extracted information into a coherent and accessible format, often using spreadsheets or data matrices. This step transforms raw data into a structured form, easing the identification of themes, patterns, and knowledge gaps. Data charting was done according to Peters et al. (2020) [26]. We did the chart as an iterative process, allowing flexibility to adapt the data categories as the review progresses and a deeper understanding is gained. During this phase, clear documentation was done to enhance the transparency and reliability of the review’s conclusions of this scoping review. The data were extracted from studies included in the review by the reviewers using a Microsoft Excel sheet.

Data synthesis and presentation

The synthesis stage of a scoping review involves collating and summarizing the data by including numerical mapping of study characteristics and thematic interpretation of content [29]. The results are presented in tabular and textual formats, supported by visual aids such as charts or concept maps. A well-executed synthesis contextualizes findings within broader literature, highlighting gaps, emerging themes, and areas for further exploration [30].

This scoping review employed a thematic synthesis approach to analyze the extracted data. Using a narrative framework, key themes were identified, coded, and synthesized to highlight patterns related to the challenges in the sustainability of supplies for maternal and newborn health during pregnancy.

To aid in the presentation and interpretation of findings, tables and figures were developed to illustrate implementation challenges, enabling factors, and system-level dynamics. Additionally, a mapping exercise was conducted to align sustainability components such as financing, supply chain mechanisms, and policy integration with corresponding elements of the health system. This integrative process provided a comprehensive overview of the evidence base and informed the development of thematic categories.

Quality assurance

To maintain data quality in this scoping review, we focused on a well-defined protocol, developed a search strategy, and a logic grid for transparency and reproducibility of search, rigorous screening, and structured data extraction by two independent reviewers. We created a clear data extraction form, training reviewers/online, piloting the form, and documenting any changes or decisions. Double-checking the extracted data and ensuring consistency among reviewers were also performed as crucial steps.

Ethical consideration

As the scoping reviews are a synthesis of existing literature, no ethical approval was received. Rather, we focus on the ethical conduct of the review process rather than participant-related ethics.

Results

Literature search and duplication check

The literature search was conducted from four databases and yielded 982 entries from all databases. One hundred sixty-nine (169) duplicate research and reports were removed. After duplicates were removed, 813 records were screened, and 770 studies were excluded based on title and abstract screening. Out of all the investigations, a total of 43 publications were sought for retrieval through full-text screening. As a result, 28 papers were excluded. Ultimately, this scoping review included 15 articles (as shown in Fig. 2) below.

All 15 included studies were carried out between 2015 and 2025. This scoping review examined the range and nature of the literature on the global fund cut. The studies and briefs on fund cuts on supply sustainability for maternal-newborn health in the LMIC context have covered different aspects, including fund cut challenges to supplies for pregnant women in LMIC. Identified studies were focused on the maternal health challenge of aid cuts and ways forward.

According to this review, the demand and supply gap widened due to the supply bottlenecks that arose from the fund cut. Discussion

Summary of evidence

In this review, challenges identified for the sustainability of supplies for maternal-newborn health outcomes during pregnancy include aid sanctions, which are a form of financial punishment imposed on a country by other nations or international organizations in response to a political coup, armed conflict, or human rights abuses.

Humanitarian catastrophes in many countries have brought aid sanctions to the center of the foreign affairs strategy debate because of their inadvertent negative effects on human health [43]. The uptake of micronutrients has varied globally due to challenges with acceptability, supply and distribution, counselling and knowledge, and access to health services [44]. This shows consistent results with studies conducted in Ethiopia [45], Cambodia [46], Ghana, and Malawi [47].

Supplies for pregnant women during ANC programs in SSA are challenged by fluctuating external funding, infrastructural limitations, and competing priorities. Countries that have successfully integrated maternal and newborn health services into national health policies and fostered local ownership demonstrate greater resilience [48]. For this, the local manufacturing and diversified financing are promising avenues for reducing dependence on donor support. However, political commitment remains vital. Advocacy efforts should emphasize the long-term health and economic benefits of maternal nutrition, aligning with broader development goals [49].

Loss of donor funding is also jeopardizing the procurement and distribution of supplies, a life-saving treatment for malnutrition. The micronutrient product comes as a dense micronutrient paste, and USAID supported half of the world’s supply [34]. Maternal mortality rates among LMICs declined at an average annualized rate of 2%. Aid sanction episodes would thus negate nearly 30% of the overall improvements in infants and under-5 mortality seen in the average LMIC over this period, and approximately 60% of the improvements in maternal mortality [43]. This indicates that when there is a total cut of funding for maternal health services and supplies, the death will more likely be prominent, and it will inadvertently compound human suffering. Henceforward, the challenges related to financial sustainability and the political economy of global health governance affected the sustainability of supplies directly or indirectly. This might be due to a lack of an independent funding source for implementation and sustainability challenges, inadequate stakeholder engagement, and the absence of clear policy and financial constraints [50].

Many programs, including micronutrient supplements during pregnancy for women attending ANC, have been funded and supported by international donors and global health initiatives [9]. However, the long-term sustainability of these programs remains uncertain due to factors including health system limitations, funding, the current global fund cuts, logistics, adherence, and policy support. Therefore, the Global Fund contraction or cut from the 2nd Trump administration will inadvertently compound human suffering. Health information and women’s beliefs about their treatment, as well as ANC attendance and the provision of prenatal services, are indicators of the quality of prenatal care received [9].

Donald Trump’s decision to shut down USAID to developing countries highlights the urgent need for Africa to lessen its reliance on external aid. Despite efforts toward economic independence, many African nations face conflicting priorities, such as the push for decolonization while still depending on foreign funds, and struggle with fostering meaningful investment amid corruption and political self-interest [11]. To overcome these barriers, the study suggests depoliticizing support for private enterprises, empowering entrepreneurs, and strategically reinvesting natural resource revenues into underserved sectors to diversify and strengthen national economies.

USAID has been quite instrumental in closing funding gaps in most developing countries. Introducing new radical thinking can easily erupt in donor countries [10]. These fund cuts can affect the smooth inflow of foreign aid that had previously seemed a normal and acceptable part of government funding. Enhanced regional collaboration could further boost productivity and guide the continent toward lasting economic self-sufficiency [12].

A growing body of evidence shows that reductions in external funding have disrupted program delivery. For instance, in Malawi, donor withdrawal resulted in stock-outs and cessation of some supplementation efforts [51]. Similar trends have been seen in Ethiopia and Nigeria, threatening the sustainability of ongoing interventions [52]. The funding reductions led to program disruptions, and pregnant women and children are vulnerable in the current program models [43].

International donor funding has accounted for approximately 40% of HIV funding in LMIC, with the US serving as the largest historical contributor. SSA stands to withstand the worst of these cuts, as critical prevention initiatives, including distribution of ART and PMTCT for HIV positive women and children, pre-exposure prophylaxis programs, face imminent discontinuation [10]. The repercussions could reverse decades of progress, particularly in curbing mother-to-child HIV transmission and reducing pediatric HIV mortality. The HIV transmission and mortality show how USAID funding cuts affect supplies and drugs important for the reduction of morbidity and mortality due to HIV in LMICs. The global fund cut can affect the sustainability of funding and make programs vulnerable. This problem is compounded by weak domestic financing and policy gaps, competing health priorities, and weak supply chains and infrastructural deficits.

The crisis due to fund cuts also presents a pivotal opportunity: the potential for Africa to reclaim agency over its developmental trajectory, unshackled from external conditionalities and structural dependencies. This juncture could catalyze an era of self-reliance, marked by regional collaboration, endogenous innovation, and economic sovereignty principles embodied by initiatives such as the African Continental Free Trade Area. To navigate this transition, African governments could reorient policy toward home-grown solutions, empowering local entrepreneurs, smallholder farmers, and domestic industries as engines of equitable growth [10].

To support local, in-country, or regional procurement and production of MMS, the possible production scenarios are: straight ingredients of vitamins and minerals forms imported or locally produced by a local manufacturer; import or local production of a vitamin and minerals premix encapsulated and packaged locally; import of a bulk, finished product that is branded; and import of a branded packaged product [40]. Furthermore, regional cooperation can facilitate resource sharing and policy harmonization [49]. Therefore, it emphasizes the need for multi-sectoral approaches to ensure sustainability.

As a solution for the fund cut challenges and gaps identified, harnessing diaspora capabilities for resilience of health systems and communities requires recognition, integration, and evidence-based action [41]. Embedding MMS into routine antenatal and reproductive health services enhances sustainability. Ethiopia’s work to integrate MMS into its national antenatal care guidelines has shown promise, leveraging existing health infrastructure [53].

Training health workers and engaging communities foster local ownership. In Ghana, community health volunteers have played a key role in keeping supplementation coverage despite reduced external support [54]. Countries are exploring domestic resource mobilization, including reallocating government budgets and setting up social enterprise models for supplementary production. Rwanda’s efforts to locally produce micronutrient powders have reduced reliance on imports and local production [55].

Investments in supply chain infrastructure and local manufacturing mitigate risks of stock-outs [53]. Local production and cost-effective intervention to enhance affordability and program ownership [56]. Cost-effective strategies, such as bulk procurement and government subsidies, can improve access [57]. Ensure uninterrupted, high-quality supplies and strengthen health worker capacities to effectively deliver, counsel, and support women in receiving and consuming nutrients [58]. For policy and programmatic implications of funding cuts, MNH incorporates nutrition into national health strategies. Innovative financing mechanisms, such as results-based financing, regional collaborations for resource pooling, and enhanced monitoring and evaluation systems, help demonstrate program impact on women and adolescent girls, who are key influencers of the importance of micronutrients and nutritious diets during pregnancy [56].

Limitations & strengths of this scoping review

The existing literature largely consists of descriptive studies, program evaluations, briefs, and policy reports, with limited availability of longitudinal and quantitative data examining the impact of funding cuts on the sustainability of maternal health supply. Although many of the included studies were from reports and briefs, the findings offer firsthand insights into the challenges associated with funding contractions during antenatal care programs and their implications for maternal health and nutrition outcomes. Also, as an inclusion criterion of English-language restriction, the review has a potential publication bias. Despite this, the review extensively used existing methodology and searched multiple databases to generate evidence on the fund cut by ensuring quality in the process.

Conclusion

Records, including published and unpublished data, which were not peer-reviewed articles, like reports and media briefs, were identified through systematic literature searches, encompassing 15 studies. The review highlights the major challenges posed by global funding cuts to the supplies for ensuring maternal health outcomes. Generally, fund cuts could challenge the supplies for maternal and newborn health services. Factors that can affect the program’s supply sustainability were weak domestic financing and policy gaps, supply chain vulnerabilities, health system factors, threat of funding cuts, policy, regulatory and governance challenges. The other barriers included limited political commitment and competing health priorities, dependence on external donor funding, and weak supply chains and infrastructural deficits.

Furthermore, this review outlines key sustainability challenges related to supplies of micronutrients, PMTCT, and immunization for pregnant women. The review stresses the need for strategic solutions to sustain maternal health programs. It also calls for urgent policy and research efforts to build resilient systems and safeguard long-term maternal and child health gains. In addition to this, there is an evidence gap, and to maintain progress, policymakers must prioritize maternal nutrition within broader health agendas and need to create resilient, scalable solutions. This shows there is limited evidence that shows the impact and challenges of fund cuts specific to the supply of maternal care during pregnancy in LMICs, indicating a need for further study.

Recommendations

Based on the findings from this scoping review, we recommend that governments establish domestic financing mechanisms through health insurance reforms. Additionally, it needs policy alignment to adopt maternal health services and supplements within the essential medicines lists. In addition to this, using community-led models to scale community care approaches and peer support systems, and institutionalizing contingency and emergency funds are important. Allocation of a small percentage of health budgets to rapid‑response reserves for commodity procurement during crises or donor delays will play a paramount role in tackling the challenges.

Implications for policy, practice, and program implementation

To promote implementation research, it needs to prioritize studies on the cost‑utility of supplementations, adherence interventions, and supply‑chain innovations; and integrate findings into policy cycles.

Future research

There is a need to conduct more research using higher-quality study designs and quantitative research and evaluation for decision-making and policymaking.

Operational definitions

Challenge refers to the significant problems, obstacles, or barriers that make it difficult to provide quality care to achieve maternal and newborn health.

Fund contractions” can also describe this phenomenon, referring to a period of economic decline where a company, industry, or the economy shrinks and reduces output.

Supplies

Include the essential medicines, medical devices, and other health materials needed to ensure the well-being of a mother and her baby throughout pregnancy, childbirth, and the post-natal period.
