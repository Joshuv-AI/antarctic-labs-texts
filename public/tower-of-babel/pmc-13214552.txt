Wafer-scale bias-reconfigurable optoelectronic micro-synapses with integrated functions of red emission and self-powered detection
Authors: Jie Yin, Jiabao Hu, Linrui Cheng, Binbin Hou, Mingmin Zhong, Xu Wang, Jianya Zhang, Fangren Hu, Yukun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDespite the evident advantages of multifunctional integrated devices in compactness and integration density, achieving efficient coexistence of photodetection, synapse-like memory and red-light emission in one monolithic unit remains an extraordinary challenge due to profound conflicts in carrier recombination, separation, and storage. Here, we effectively mitigate these long-standing limitations by developing a wafer-scale gallium nitride (GaN)-based optoelectronic micro-synapse that integrates red light emission, self-powered photodetection, and bias-reconfigurable synaptic plasticity within a single micro-device. Red emission is realized through a simple quantum-dot color-conversion layer on a blue GaN micro-LED, avoiding the efficiency bottleneck of native red emitters while preserving device scalability and color stability. Beyond light emission, the same junction operates as a zero-bias self-powered photodetector. Under optical stimulation and global bias modulation, the device further demonstrates rich optoelectronic synaptic behaviors. The demonstrated platform provides a scalable and manufacturable route toward integrated neuromorphic vision systems.Subject areas: applied sciences, engineering, materials science, devices

Introduction

Photodetectors (PDs) are foundational components widely employed in imaging, sensing, and optical communication systems.1,2 Unlike other colors, efficient red-light emission is crucial for full-color displays, as red constitutes one of the three primary red-green-blue colors required for wide color gamut and precise color fidelity in light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and next-generation imaging systems.3,4 Moreover, the human neural network exhibits remarkable parallelism and fault tolerance, enabling simultaneous data processing, evaluation, and storage.5,6,7,8 Integrating multiple functionalities into a single device is critical for advancing next-generation optoelectronics,9,10 which can simplify the supporting systems, reduce the size and greatly expand the application scenarios.

Artificial synapses demand prolonged signal retention and memory of prior inputs, whereas traditional PDs are engineered for ultrafast photoresponse and rapid photocurrent decay.1,9 Mechanistically, PDs generate electron-hole pairs upon photon absorption, which are swiftly separated and collected under an applied electric field to produce transient photocurrent.1 On the contrary, attempts to achieve native red emission by increasing indium content in indium-gallium-nitride (In, Ga, N) quantum wells inevitably induce severe lattice mismatch, strong quantum-confined Stark effect, and significant efficiency droop.11,12,13 In other words, the photodetection requires minimal carrier recombination for high-speed operation, whereas synaptic plasticity and red emission depend on prolonged carrier lifetime or efficient recombination. Hence, these fundamental conflicts create irreconcilable contradictions in carrier dynamics, response speed, and recombination/separation requirements. These conflicts have hindered true multifunctional integration within the same semiconductor active region. Consequently, monolithically integrating photodetection, artificial synaptic behavior, and active light emission within a single device has long been considered a formidable challenge.14,15,16,17 Therefore, up to date, it is urgent but quite difficult to integrate such three functions into a monolithic micro-device.

In our previous work, we have tried to combine the functions of detection and memory.2,18 However, the red emission function has not been explored in such multifunctional micro-devices. Here, we sidestep the red-emission bottleneck by employing quantum-dot color conversion while achieving the other two functions within the same micro-device. By leveraging III-nitride micro-device technology combined with straightforward quantum-dot color conversion, such wafer-scale multifunctional micro-devices circumvent the fundamental limitations of native red emission and demonstrate practical coexistence light emission, photodetection, and synaptic functions. It can thereby open new avenues for next-generation intelligent optoelectronic micro-systems in artificial intelligence, human-machine interaction, and advanced robotic vision applications.

Function as both red micro-LED and self-powered micro-PD

As the core functional layer of LEDs, MQWs can facilitate carrier recombination luminescence under electrical or optical excitation through bandgap engineering.19 When the current density increases from 60 to 200 Acm−2, the peak wavelength shifts slightly by 3.6 nm, demonstrating its excellent peak wavelength stability (Figure 2A). As shown in Figure 2B, the 1931 commission international de l’Éclairage (CIE) chromaticity coordinates are highly concentrated, which are calculated from the electroluminescence (EL) spectra under different current densities ranging from 60 to 200 A cm−2, indicating negligible color shift. The bare device without QDs exhibits a sharp emission peak at ∼455 nm (Figure S3). Upon coating with the red QDs, this blue emission is absorbed and converted to a red emission peak at ∼622 nm (Figure 2A). The EL spectra and the lighting image in Figures 2A and 2C confirm the red-light-emitting function of the micro-device successfully. The color conversion efficiency of red quantum dots is determined using the integration method, calculated as the color conversion ratio (CR) of the converted red emission intensity to the absorbed blue intensity.20

∫Ired(λ)dλ and ∫Iblue(λ)dλ represent the integrated areas of the converted red emission and the residual blue emission in Figure 2A, respectively. The calculated CR reaches 97.6% at 200 A/cm2, indicating that the QD color-conversion layer can effectively suppress blue light leakage even under high carrier injection levels. This high color CR ensures excellent color saturation and stability for high-quality lighting applications.

Figure 2D shows the cyclic photocurrent stability of the device under continuous 385 nm light pulse stimulation. As operating at 0 V bias, the micro-device exhibits self-powered detection functionality.21,22 Note that the photocurrent data are presented as absolute values to clearly illustrate the signal magnitude, given that the self-powered photovoltaic current flows in the reverse direction compared to the forward injection current. The photocurrent maintains consistent on/off amplitudes in each cycle without obvious drift or attenuation, indicating that the device exhibits excellent photoresponse stability and reliable operational repeatability in the self-powered detection mode. The rise time (trise) of the micro-device is defined as the time it takes for the photocurrent to increase from 10% to 90% of its peak value, while the decay time (tfall) is that decreases from 90% to 10%.23 As shown in Figure 2E, trise and tfall are both about 91 ms. As the incident light power intensity (Pinc) increases, the photocurrent (Iph) gradually rises (Figure 2F). Under self-powered mode, the responsivity of multifunctional device reaches 87 μAW−1.

The multifunctional micro-device exhibits robust performance in both emission and self-powered detection modes. Under forward bias (Figure 2G), electrons and holes are injected from the n-GaN and p-GaN regions, respectively. They undergo radiative recombination within the (In,Ga)N/GaN MQWs, releasing light photons and generating EL. Building upon this blue-emitting device, a red QD conversion layer is subsequently coated on the device surface to achieve red-light output. As shown in Figure 2H, the blue EL from the underlying GaN LED serves as the excitation source and is absorbed by the QDs in the conversion layer, promoting carriers to excited states. Subsequently, radiative recombination occurs from the ground state, emitting red photoluminescence. In this configuration, the QDs function solely as a photoluminescent color-conversion layer without electrical injection. It is noted that the QD color-conversion layer may absorb a portion of the incident ultraviolet (UV) light. However, due to the thin nature of the spin-coated film, a sufficient number of photons can penetrate to the GaN layers to generate electron-hole pairs. Although the absolute photocurrent might be attenuated compared to a bare device, the resulting photocurrent remains sufficient to trigger stable responses (Figure 2D).

In the self-powered detection mode (Figure 2I), incident photons with energy higher than the bandgap are absorbed by the MQWs and surrounding GaN layers, generating electron-hole pairs. The built-in electric field of the p-n junction drives the spatial separation of photogenerated carriers. The device demonstrates a millisecond-scale response, which is governed by the coupled effects of polarization-modified band alignment and trap-assisted carrier dynamics. The strong spontaneous and piezoelectric polarization fields tilt the energy bands, while the conduction and valence band offsets create periodic potential barriers. These heterojunction barriers spatially confine the photogenerated carriers-particularly holes with larger effective masses-thereby prolonging their residence time before being swept out by the built-in field.

Optoelectronic micro-synapse performance

Synaptic plasticity, the activity-dependent modification of synaptic efficacy, underpins learning and memory and includes short-term plasticity (STP) and long-term plasticity (LTP).24,25 In this work, synaptic behaviors are emulated by a micro-LED device under UV stimulation and different electrical bias conditions. Under a forward bias of 2.8 V, the device lights up and exhibits significant excitability when irradiated with 385 nm UV light (Figures 3A and 3B). When stimulated with a single UV pulse of varying duration (0.5 Hz), the triggered excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC) rises rapidly (Figure 3A). Both the peak amplitude and decay duration of the EPSC increase with increasing pulse width, which reflects the enhanced persistent photoconductivity caused by accumulated trapped holes.26,27,28 Paired-pulse stimulation shows obvious paired-pulse facilitation (PPF): the EPSC evoked by the second pulse (A2) is significantly larger than that evoked by the first pulse (A1), resulting in that a PPF index (A2/A1 × 100%) is higher than 100% (Figure 3B). This behavior indicates a typical short-term synaptic facilitation arising from incomplete carrier relaxation between successive stimuli.

Under a forward bias of +2.8 V, the device can operate in lighting and detection modes simultaneously. When driven as a micro-LED, electrons are injected from the n-GaN side into the MQW region and recombine with holes to emit light. Under 385 nm irradiation, photons are absorbed primarily in the p-GaN layer to generate electron-hole pairs (Figure 3C). The residual built-in potential and the applied external field jointly promote carrier separation. The photogenerated electrons are collected at the n-electrode, while holes are driven to the p-GaN, thereby producing a significant photocurrent and a persistent synapse-like response.

When the forward bias is reduced to 2 V, the peak EPSC decreases (Figures 3D and 3E). This bias-dependent modulation reflects weakened carrier injection and reduced electric-field-assisted extraction, which enhance the relative contribution of trap-assisted carrier storage. Under continuous light stimulation, photons continuously excite and generate new charge carriers, which are collected by electrodes to form a photocurrent. Compared with the strong forward bias condition in Figure 3C, the reduced forward bias in Figure 3F leads to less effective band flattening and a weaker internal electric field across the p-n junction. As a result, photogenerated carriers experience reduced drift-driven transport and are more likely to be captured by defect states in the p-GaN layer and at the MQW interfaces. The weakened extraction of carriers suppresses fast recombination pathways, allowing trapped carriers to accumulate and be released gradually after the removal of optical stimulation. Consequently, the energy band configuration in Figure 3F favors long-lived carrier storage and slow detrapping dynamics, giving rise to a significantly longer discharge time than that observed under the +2.8 V condition (Figure 3C).

At +2.0 V, EL is suppressed due to insufficient injection current below the turn-on threshold, yet stable synaptic behaviors persist. Under reverse bias (−2.0 V), light emission is absent but pronounced synaptic responses remain via bias-modulated photoresponse (Figures 3G–3I). Under a reverse bias of −2 V, although the direction of the electric field is reversed, the device still generates an absolute excitatory photocurrent (reverse). Notably, the EPSC amplitude under −2 V is approximately one order of magnitude smaller than that under +2 V. This asymmetry stems from the intrinsic rectification of the p-n junction: the forward bias (+2 V) reduces the potential barrier to allow efficient carrier injection (Figure 3F), whereas the reverse bias (−2 V) suppresses injection and limits the current to drift-driven and trap-assisted transport of photogenerated carriers (Figure 3I). Compared with that measured under +2.8 V condition, the EPSC under −2 V bias decays more slowly in the first 2 s stage (Figures 3A and 3G), which may result from the trap-assisted persistent photoconductivity.27,28,29 The prolonged residence time enhances the probability of carrier capture by localized defect states originating from interface roughness, polarization-induced charge accumulation and threading dislocations (Figure 2I). Such interplay between polarization-modified band alignment and trap-assisted carrier storage is intrinsic to the GaN-based heterojunction. While these dynamics result in a millisecond-scale photodetection, they are intrinsically advantageous for enabling synapse-like temporal integration and memory behaviors.26,27,28,30,31 To evaluate the ability of device to balance the inherent conflict between rapid detection and prolonged synaptic memory, the decay time in sensing mode (Tdetection) and the retention time in synaptic mode (Tsynaptic) has been analyzed as follows:

For the micro-device, Tdetection (decay time at zero bias) is about 91 ms (Figure 2E), while Tsynaptic (retention time) reaches ∼41 s (Figure 3D). The resulting speed-difference ratio (α) is approximately 450, which reflects the highly tunable nature of carrier dynamics within the monolithic (In, Ga) N/GaN heterostructure. Specifically, at zero bias, the strong built-in electric field of the p-n junction facilitates accelerated carrier extraction compared to biased synaptic states, resulting in a 91 ms response time. This speed is sufficient for biomimetic visual perception, matching the temporal resolution (typically 10–20 Hz) of biological vision systems. In contrast, under applied bias (e.g., ±2.0 V), the modified energy band configuration enhances carrier capture by interface traps and localized states, extending the carrier residence time by 450-fold to achieve stable synaptic plasticity. Such a broad temporal window, achieved through straightforward bias-reconfiguration, effectively improves the inherent trade-off between the rapid sensing and the long-term storage needed for neuromorphic memory.

When paired-pulse stimulation is performed at a bias of −2 V, the PPF index exhibits a facilitation effect (Figure 3H). This dual-mode behavior (coexistence of facilitation and slow decay) is fundamentally supported by the broad timescale discrimination ratio (α≈450), confirming the device’s capability to govern carrier dynamics across different temporal regimes. As illustrated in Figure 3I, under reverse bias and UV illumination, electron-hole pairs are generated when the photon energy exceeds the bandgap. The strong reverse electric field efficiently separates these carriers and drives them toward defect-rich regions near the p-n junction, where a substantial fraction becomes trapped.

As illustrated in Figures S4A–S4C, the device exhibits pronounced bias-dependent dual-mode optoelectronic synaptic characteristics that emulate the excitatory-inhibitory interplay observed in biological retinal pathways. In the initial state, the defect-related trap states exist within the GaN and MQW regions, which are capable of capturing photogenerated carriers. Under continuous electrical pulsing alone (without UV illumination), the device shows clear current suppression at all three biases, manifesting as inhibitory postsynaptic current-like behavior due to enhanced carrier extraction and reduced trapping efficiency. When the same continuous electrical pulses are applied concurrently with 385 nm UV illumination, robust EPSC curves emerge under 2 and −2 V conditions (Figures S4B and S4C). Reducing the bias from 2.8 to 2.0 V yields moderated yet still excitatory EPSC (Figures S4A and S4B). Most strikingly, even under −2.0 V bias, simultaneous UV illumination generates pronounced negative-going but unequivocally EPSCs (Figure S4C). This light-triggered transition from electrical-pulse-induced inhibition to photoelectrically activated excitation arises from the dominance of photogeneration and trap-assisted transport over the reverse-field extraction.

The continuous electrical pulses alone produce inhibition, while co-stimulation with 385 nm light reliably elicits tunable excitatory plasticity across forward and reverse biases. This unique electro-optical dual-mode synaptic behavior recapitulates the light-gated excitatory-inhibitory dynamics of retinal bipolar and ganglion cells, providing a powerful hardware foundation for retina-inspired adaptive perception and contrast modulation within a single monolithic micro-device. The energy consumption per synaptic event (∼12 μJ) is calculated by integrating the EPSC over time under a single 385 nm optical pulse at −2 V bias. In biological vision, photons are detected by photoreceptors in the retina, where initial signal preprocessing occurs before transmission via the optic nerve to the visual cortex for higher-level processing (Figure 4A). This pathway emulates a complete visual perception and learning system, from light sensing to synaptic integration in neural circuits. At the synaptic level, information is encoded and stored through dynamic changes in connection strength (termed synaptic weight), which is manifested as variations in postsynaptic current or potential. Presynaptic action potentials trigger neurotransmitter release across the synaptic cleft, modulating postsynaptic responses.40

To intuitively demonstrate the bias-reconfigurable synaptic plasticity at the array level, we simulated the photocurrent distribution of a 5 × 5 array when a Y-shaped pattern is projected onto the device plane under uniform 385 nm illumination (Figure 4A). The response of each pixel is generated by sampling from a Gaussian distribution (σ ≈ 15%) centered on the experimentally measured single-device EPSC peak values at +2.8, +2.0, and −2.0 V, respectively, faithfully reproducing the typical device to device variation observed across the wafer-scale micro-synapse array. Under +2.8 V bias, the array exhibits sustained and stable high-amplitude response along with pronounced memory retention characteristics, yielding a bright and persistent “Y” pattern that is particularly well-suited for weak-light imaging scenarios requiring prolonged signal accumulation (Figure 4B). At +2.0 V, a balanced excitatory response is obtained, representing standard detection (Figure 4C). Remarkably, applying −2.0 V reverse bias inverts the photocurrent polarity while preserving excitatory synaptic behavior, yielding a dark-background bright “Y” with reversed contrast (Figure 4D). It shows a capability that enables programmable polarity switching and contrast inversion without additional inhibitory neurons or circuits.

To simulate “learning-forgetting-relearning” processes, a repetitive pulse train is applied on the artificial micro-synapse (Figure 4E). Under forward biases of 2.8 V, the initial “learning” phase shows that the EPSC gradually amplifies after approximately 15 pulses, followed by spontaneous decay (“forgetting”). Subsequent re-stimulation only requires 1 pulse to achieve faster and stronger recovery (“relearning”), which mimics a hallmark of memory consolidation and the saving effect observed in biological systems. As shown in Figure S5A, with the increase in the number of learning cycles, the clarity of the “memory” of the cat image is significantly improved. In the initial state (N = 0), the image is almost completely dark. After 15 pulses of learning (N = 15), the image outline is clearly visible. After 5 s forgetting (T = 5 s), the image becomes slightly blurred. Only 1 pulse of relearning (N = 1) can restore the image to even exceed the brightness and contrast achieved after the first learning. This memory enhancement and image quality improvement dependent on the number of pulses simulating the plasticity and associative memory behavior of biological synapses.

The similar “learning-forgetting-relearning” processes can be achieved in Figures 4F and 4G, corresponding to Figures S5B and S5C, respectively. Specifically, the Δw of a device is spatially mapped to the contrast and brightness of the reconstructed image pixels. A higher synaptic weight (Δw) corresponds to a stronger channel conductance (or enhanced absolute negative conductance), yielding brighter image features. Conversely, a reduced synaptic weight leads to faded image details. Therefore, the spatial image evolution directly visualizes the temporal synaptic modulation. During the initial “learning” phase, optical pulses gradually build up Δw, enhancing the image brightness. When the stimulation is paused (“forgetting”), the stored image information gradually fades. Crucially, when the stimuli are reapplied, the device rapidly retrieves the forgotten pattern, requiring significantly fewer pulses (e.g., 1 pulse at 2.8 V, 2 pulses at 2.0 V, and 3 pulses at −2.0 V) to recover the peak image contrast. The evolution of Δw fundamentally reflects the carrier accumulation and retention capability within the micro-device.

To further assess the spatial uniformity and manufacturing reproducibility of the devices, the device to device variation in EPSC peak amplitude has been characterized. 10 independent micro-devices have randomly selected from different regions across the 2-inch wafer. As presented in Figure S6, the statistical distribution reveals a mean current of 3.80 mA with a remarkably narrow standard deviation of ±0.07 mA. Notably, the coefficient of variation (CV) is as low as 1.84%. Such a tightly concentrated distribution demonstrates excellent device to device uniformity and consistent synaptic performance.

Conclusions

By unifying light emission, self-powered sensing and synaptic processing within an identical physical device layer, this work provides a way to resolve the intrinsic functional conflicts that have long hindered multifunctional optoelectronic integration. Through combining the blue LED technology with straightforward spin-coating of red quantum dots, such wafer-scale micro-devices achieve bright and color-stable red EL. Operating as a self-powered PD, the multifunctional micro-device exhibits stable and repeatable photoresponse enabled by the built-in electric field. Furthermore, the device exhibits pronounced relearning behaviors under different bias conditions. These bias-dependent differences in the number of relearning pulses highlight the versatile capability of micro-device in emulating synaptic plasticity. Obtained through straightforward bias switching of a monolithic GaN-based p-n junction, a single device is demonstrated to replicate three core retinal adaptation mechanisms, which are balanced perception and polarity-tunable excitatory processing. This bias-reconfigurable paradigm establishes our multifunctional micro-synapses as a hardware primitive for next-generation retina-inspired neuromorphic vision systems.

Limitations of the study

Our study is limited by the lack of direct, atomic-level probing techniques for the deep-level trap states within the (In,Ga)N/GaN heterostructures. As a result, we cannot perform site-specific defect mapping or time-resolved spectroscopic measurements to causally link specific physical defects (e.g., specific point defects, interface roughness, or threading dislocations) to the bias-reconfigurable synaptic plasticity. The trap-assisted carrier storage and release dynamics are known to be modulated by a wide range of material growth parameters and structural variations. Trap characterization based solely on macroscopic electrical and photoelectric responses is not physically selective.41 As a result, macroscopic electrical manipulations do not allow unambiguous identification of the exact microscopic defect entities underlying the prolonged synaptic retention times. Furthermore, our measurements of image recognition and retina-like adaptive perception focused on a simulated subset array (5 × 5 pixels) and statistical sampling of isolated individual micro-devices.

Method details

Preparation of micro-devices

Figure 1A shows the structural diagram of the epitaxial wafer coated with red-light quantum dots. The epitaxial layers of a 2-inch GaN wafer were grown on a patterned sapphire substrate (PSS) by metalorganic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD). The layers include a 2800 nm Si-doped GaN layer (8 × 1018 cm−3), 12 cycles of (In,Ga)N/GaN (3/5 nm) multiple quantum wells (MQWs), Mg-doped aluminum gallium nitride (Al, GaN) layers (1 × 1020 cm−3), ∼80 nm Mg-doped p-GaN layers ([Mg] ≈ 3 × 1019 cm−3). After that, a 125 nm indium tin oxide (ITO) layer was sputtered onto the p-GaN as the current spreading layer. Subsequently, the devices underwent treatment with inductively coupled plasma etching (ICP) and ion beam etching (IBE). To isolate the positive and negative terminals of the device, SiO2 was deposited by plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD). Using an electron beam evaporation system (Ei-5Z), Ti/Al/Ni/Au (20/120/60/200 nm) metal layers were deposited.

Preparation of red-light quantum dots

In this study, the red-emitting quantum dots (QDs) used for color conversion were commercially available from Funa Technology Company, which were CdZnS-based core-shell QDs (Model R4.8-01). These QDs exhibit a photoluminescence (PL) emission peak at 625 ± 5 nm and a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of <32 nm. The QDs were dispersed in a propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) matrix (200 mg/mL) to form the QD solution.

To prepare the QD photoresist solution, the QD solution was mixed with poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) negative photoresist at a volumetric ratio of 1:2 (QD: PMMA) using a micropipette. The mixture was then placed in an ultrasonic cleaner with an ice bath for 40 min to ensure thorough dispersion and uniform mixing of the QDs within the photoresist matrix. After that, approximately 15 μ L of the QD photoresist solution was precisely dropped onto a pre-fabricated blue micro-LED array. The sample was then placed on a spin coater and spin-coated at 200 rpm for 100 s to form a uniform QD thin film. Finally, ultraviolet (UV) light irradiation was employed to cure the photoresist, thereby immobilizing the red QD layer on the micro-LED surface. This process achieved the integration of red-emitting QDs with the blue micro-LEDs, facilitating subsequent optoelectronic performance measurements.

Characterization and measurement methods

The structural properties of the epitaxial layers were analyzed using AC-STEM (Talos F200X, FEI) combined with high-resolution energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). STEM lamellae were prepared by focused ion beam milling (FIB, Scios, FEI). Optical performance was evaluated through electroluminescence (EL) measurements using an integrating sphere spectrometer system (SLS-10W). The electrical characteristics were measured with a semiconductor parameter analyzer (4200-SCS), including current-time (I-T) responses. For optoelectronic measurements, the micro-devices were used as the stimulation sources, which were modulated by a function generator (FY6900-20M). The surface chemical composition of the red-emitting QDs was characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI 5000 Versaprobe III). The surface morphology and uniformity of the QD film and micro-device arrays were observed using optical microscopy (SANQTID, 4KHU). The absolute photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of the solid-state QD film was measured using the fluorescence spectrometer (FLS1000) in the Nano-X platform following the “Direct Excitation” method.
