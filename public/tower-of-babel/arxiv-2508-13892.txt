Real-time prediction of geomagnetic storms using Solar Orbiter as a far upstream solar wind monitor


Abstract
We present the first real-time predictions of coronal mass ejection (CME) magnetic structure and resulting geomagnetic impact at Earth for two events using far-upstream observations from Solar Orbiter during March 2024. Despite the large heliocentric distance between measurement at Solar Orbiter and L1 (0.53 and 0.60 AU), our predicted magnetic structures closely matched subsequent observations. Geomagnetic index predictions were made in real-time with 7 and 15 hour lead-times, presenting a large improvement over current L1-based warning times of less than one hour. We successfully captured the observed geomagnetic response profiles, although underestimated the intensity of the storm during already disturbed conditions. Our results show that predictions with high accuracy and actionable lead-times can be made with far upstream spacecraft, even with longitudinal separations up to 10 ∘ from the Sun-Earth line, over heliocentric distance ranges where radial evolution effects dominate over longitudinal effects. Our findings demonstrate the substantial value of real-time upstream solar wind measurements for enhancing geomagnetic forecasting accuracy at Earth and provide critical validation for future dedicated upstream space weather missions.

Plain Language Summary

Solar storms, or coronal mass ejections (CMEs), can significantly disrupt technological infrastructure in both space and on the ground, from satellite communications to power grid blackouts. Increasing warning times of not only when the CME will arrive, but also how strong the resulting impact may be at Earth, represents an essential advancement towards protecting against these natural hazards. To assess their impact at Earth, knowledge of the internal magnetic field structure of a CME is essential but can only be gained by in situ spacecraft measurements. In this study, we take advantage of magnetic field measurements taken by the Solar Orbiter spacecraft located less than halfway between the Sun and Earth for a two-week window during March 2024. Using our real-time procedure we first use Solar Orbiter measurements to constrain the predicted arrival time of the CME at Earth. We show that simple statistical scaling relationships can be applied to produce accurate predictions of the CME magnetic structure, despite the processes affecting CME propagation and evolution. Predictions of the Earth-impact were made with lead times greater than 7 hours, presenting a large improvement over current warning times of less than one hour. The real-time procedure developed in this study demonstrates the value of real-time upstream measurements and provides a useful framework for future dedicated space weather missions.

Introduction

Coronal mass ejections (CMEs) with strong southward magnetic fields are the primary drivers of intense geomagnetic activity affecting Earth
Kilpua et al. (2017)
. Accurately forecasting their arrival and magnetic structure remains one of the most important challenges in space weather
Kilpua et al. (2019)
;
Vourlidas et al. (2019)
, and is essential for mitigating impacts to space and ground infrastructure
Eastwood et al. (2017)
;
Hapgood et al. (2021)
.

CMEs are thought to comprise a magnetic flux rope structure, which is driven outwards from the Sun and propagates through the heliosphere, compressing the solar wind ahead to form a turbulent sheath region in front and further driving a forward shock if traveling faster than the local magnetosonic speed
(
Zurbuchen & Richardson (2006)
, e.g., Figure 2 in)
. However, not all CMEs are observed to have such a magnetic flux rope structure at their core when measured by spacecraft in situ
Gosling (1990)
, and therefore the more general term of magnetic ejecta is often used to describe this region. Alterations to the structure of the magnetic ejecta are likely due to CMEs undergoing complex evolution during propagation
Scolini et al. (2022)
;
Davies et al. (2022)
, including deformations and flattening of the flux rope cross section
(
Owens (2006)
;
Savani et al. (2010)
;
Davies, Möstl et al. (2021)
, e.g.)
, erosion
(
Ruffenach et al. (2012)
;
Ruffenach et al. (2015)
, e.g.)
, rotations
(
Nieves-Chinchilla et al. (2013)
, e.g.)
, and interactions with other solar wind transients
Lugaz et al. (2017)
. These processes may alter the idealized magnetic flux rope structure of a CME, as discussed in a recent review by
Al-Haddad & Lugaz (2025)
to a more distorted shape with overall less twist than previously assumed.

Currently, the exact magnetic configuration of CMEs can only be determined through direct in situ spacecraft measurements. To better understand their evolution, previous studies have used multi-spacecraft observations of individual CMEs close to radial alignment to track how their properties change over various heliocentric distance ranges
Good & Forsyth (2016)
;
Salman et al. (2020)
;
Davies et al. (2022)
;
Möstl et al. (2022)
. However,
Owens et al. (2017)
suggested that CMEs are not coherent structures but may display locally apparent coherence, and therefore longitudinal separations between observing spacecraft must also be taken into account, as even small longitudinal separations of less than a few degrees can lead to significant discrepancies in observations and CME properties
Davies et al. (2020)
;
Regnault et al. (2024)
.

Current forecasting relies heavily on in situ measurements from spacecraft at the Sun-Earth L1 point, which provide a limited warning time of only 10–60 minutes before a CME impacts Earth. To extend this warning period, research has increasingly focused on the efficacy of spacecraft positioned closer to the Sun than L1, increasingly known as sub-L1 monitors
Weiler et al. (2025)
;
Lugaz, Lee et al. (2024)
;
Lugaz et al. (2025)
, which offer the potential to increase lead times by several hours, depending on their orbital distance from the Earth at the time of the CME observation.

Knowledge of CME arrival time is an important factor, but it does not determine whether a CME will be geoeffective; other factors such as CME speed and magnetic structure are essential for increasing the value of space weather forecasts
Owens et al. (2020)
. Between late 2022 and mid 2024, STEREO-A was well positioned to act as a sub-L1 monitor, passing
\sim
0.05 AU ahead of L1. From these observations, studies have found that the extent of the CME magnetic ejecta may be smaller than previously assumed, with angular widths of
\sim
20
∘
–30
∘

Lugaz, Zhuang et al. (2024)
;
Banu et al. (2025)
.
Weiler et al. (2025)
also demonstrated that STEREO-A observations could be used to predict the geomagnetic impact of CMEs during this time, applying the Temerin & Li model
Temerin & Li (2006)
to time-shifted observations of the May 2024 geomagnetic superstorm. If an operational sub-L1 monitor had existed during the event,
Weiler et al. (2025)
showed that the onset of the geomagnetic storm could have been predicted 2.57 hours earlier, with a predicted geomagnetic impact within 11% of observed Dst values and 8% of observed SYM-H values.

The optimal orbits for sub-L1 missions remain undetermined
Lugaz et al. (2025)
, with a clear trade-off between lead time and accuracy of predictions. Weiler et al. (in prep, private communication) conducted a statistical study of all CMEs observed by STEREO-A as it crossed the Sun-Earth line during 2022–2024, which resulted in geomagnetic storms. They find that for distances upstream between 0.01 (L1) and 0.05 AU, the gain in lead time was negative (i.e. the CME arrived at L1 before STEREO-A) for a significant number of events (approximately one third), showing that these distances may not be far enough upstream of L1. Varying types of mission have been proposed that allow for different upstream separations. Spacecraft in distant retrograde orbits
Henon (1969)
include the European Space Agency (ESA) HENON mission
Cicalò et al. (2025)
scheduled to launch with PLATO at the end of 2026, the ESA SHIELD mission concept, and the NASA MIIST concept
Lugaz, Lee et al. (2024)
. Also previously proposed are spacecraft on a Venus-like orbit
Ritter et al. (2015)
and spacecraft powered by solar sails to stay in stationary positions along the Sun–Earth line
Lindsay et al. (1999)
;
Eastwood et al. (2015)
.

The ESA/NASA Solar Orbiter mission
Müller et al. (2020)
has crossed the Sun-Earth line yearly since its launch in April 2020. Such crossings have presented rare opportunities to observe CMEs far upstream of the Earth and allowed better constrained predictions to be made about their arrival times and magnetic structure at Earth
(
Laker et al. (2024)
, e.g.,)
. From 12 to 25 March 2024, there was an approximate two-week window in which Solar Orbiter and Earth were longitudinally separated by
\pm
15
∘
, the range in which two spacecraft are likely to observe the same CME
Good et al. (2019)
;
Lugaz, Zhuang et al. (2024)
;
Banu et al. (2025)
.

In this study, we present the two CME events that occurred during this window and the resulting predictions of their geomagnetic impact made in real time. During this time, Solar Orbiter was located at heliocentric distances between 0.53–0.37 AU, and close to the ecliptic plane with very small latitudinal separations between
\pm
1.1
∘
with respect to the Earth. Gravity assist maneuvers at Venus from February 2025 have since raised the mission’s inclination, making the March 2024 Sun-Earth line crossing Solar Orbiter’s last whilst in the ecliptic plane. These measurements therefore provide a rare and final opportunity to predict the geomagnetic impact of a CME measured far upstream of the Earth.

2
Real-time Data

The real-time spacecraft measurements of the magnetic field and plasma at L1 were made by the magnetometer (MAG) and Faraday Cup (FC) instruments onboard the Deep Space Climate Observatory, DSCOVR
Burt & Smith (2012)
. These data were obtained via the data products released on the NOAA SWPC Services webpage (see Open Research Section), where the magnetic and plasma data are available in 1-min resolution for the previous seven days up to the current time.

The magnetic field measurements made by the magnetometer
(
Horbury et al. (2020)
, MAG;)
instrument onboard Solar Orbiter
Müller et al. (2013)
;
Müller et al. (2020)
were provided by the Imperial College London MAG team directly with a cadence of 1 minute. As Solar Orbiter is a science mission which does not supply a continuous stream of data to Earth, for this study, relevant data from the MAG team was sent as quickly as possible after each pass with the spacecraft had been completed. For each event, we received two sets of data: the first including the start of the CME and partially revealing its structure, and the second including the complete CME structure after it has passed the spacecraft. We note that since this work was completed, the MAG team has automated the processing and distribution of the MAG low-latency data to space weather forecasters, bringing latency down to around 1 hour during passes.

To compare our final geomagnetic predictions, we also use the NOAA real-time Dst index. These values are similarly obtained from the NOAA SWPC Services webpage (see Open Research Section), where hourly values are given.

3
Real-Time Procedure

In this study, we employ a procedure to first predict the arrival time of the CME at L1, use the magnetic data obtained from Solar Orbiter to predict the magnetic observations of the CME at L1, and then use these to predict the expected geomagnetic impact. As more Solar Orbiter data is received, we update our predictions accordingly.

3.1
Constraining the Arrival Time at L1

The CME arrival time at both Solar Orbiter and later L1 is predicted using the ELliptical Evolution model
(
Möstl et al. (2015)
, ELEvo;)
. The model assumes an elliptical front for the CMEs and takes initial parameter estimates of the time the CME is observed at 21.5 R
⊙
, initial speed, and propagation direction, to propagate the CME outward using a simple drag-based model
Vršnak et al. (2013)
. The drag-based model defines the rate at which a fast (slow) CME is decelerated (accelerated) relative to the ambient solar wind as it propagates through the heliosphere, and therefore requires the ambient solar wind speed and the drag parameter, which in this case, we assume to be constant and set to 400 km s
-1
and
0.1\times 10^{-7}
km
-1
, respectively. An ensemble is used to estimate the arrival time error, with the ambient solar wind and initial CME speed varying by 50 km s
-1
and the drag parameter varying by
0.025\times 10^{-7}
km
-1
along a normal distribution.

We make two CME arrival time predictions at L1. The first initial prediction uses input parameters taken from the Space Weather Database Of Notifications, Knowledge, Information (DONKI;
https://kauai.ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/DONKI/
) provided by the Moon to Mars (M2M) Space Weather Analysis Office and hosted by the Community Coordinated Modeling Center (CCMC;
https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/
) shortly after the CME is launched from the Sun. The second updated L1 arrival time prediction is then made using the observed CME arrival time at Solar Orbiter to further constrain the model results.

3.2
Predicting the ICME Magnetic Structure

The geomagnetic impact of an Earth-directed CME depends on its magnetic structure; crucially, the southward direction of the magnetic field vector. To quantitatively predict the geomagnetic impact of the CME, we first must predict the magnetic structure of the CME at the Sun–Earth L1 point. To do this, we first transform the data from radial-tangential-normal (RTN) coordinates to geocentric solar magnetospheric (GSM) coordinates, so that the x-axis points toward the Sun (origin is Earth center) and the z-axis is the projection of the Earth’s magnetic dipole axis to the plane perpendicular to the x-axis. We then time-shift the transformed magnetic data observed by Solar Orbiter so that the CME shock front aligns with the updated predicted L1 arrival time given by the ELEvo model.

As CMEs propagate away from the Sun, they expand due to their high internal magnetic pressure until they reach equilibrium with the solar wind background through which they propagate. In situ, such CME expansion often results in both larger radial widths of CMEs and lower measured magnetic field magnitudes. To achieve more realistic CME magnetic field observations at L1, we therefore scale the Solar Orbiter magnetic field data in terms of magnitude and stretch the data temporally.

Statistically, CME magnetic field strength tends to decrease with increasing heliocentric distance, providing a measure of their global expansion. The rate at which their magnetic field strength decreases can be fit with a power law,
B\propto r^{\alpha}
, where
r
is the heliocentric distance and
\alpha
is the constant to be determined. Previous statistical studies of ICME properties have found a range of
\alpha
values, with steeper rates of expansion over heliocentric distance ranges closer to the Sun
Gulisano et al. (2010)
;
Winslow et al. (2015)
;
Good et al. (2019)
, and slower rates of expansion beyond 1 AU
Richardson (2014)
;
Davies, Forsyth et al. (2021)
. In this study, we use an
\alpha
value of -1.64 to scale the magnetic field values measured at Solar Orbiter, as found by
Leitner et al. (2007)
, where a simple magnetic field model was fitted to Helios 1 and 2 observations to obtain the expansion rate of the axial field. We set the upper and lower uncertainty range to values of -1.2 and -2, respectively, reflecting the slowest rate of CME expansion found
Richardson (2014)
;
Davies, Forsyth et al. (2021)
, and the theoretical steepest rate of expansion for a self-similarly expanding cylindrical force-free magnetic flux rope, where the axial field would be expected to decrease as
r^{-2}

Farrugia et al. (1993)
.

After scaling the magnitude of the magnetic field values, we then consider the temporal expansion of the CME. The dimensionless expansion parameter
Démoulin & Dasso (2009)
;
Gulisano et al. (2010)
provides a measure of local expansion, typically denoted as
\zeta
, which is independent of radial distance. This parameter can therefore be related to the global expansion of CMEs: where the magnetic field strength would be expected to decrease as
r^{-2\zeta}
(
\alpha=2\zeta
) and the radial size of the CME expected to increase as
r^{\zeta}
.
\zeta
has been found to be
\approx
0.8 for non-perturbed magnetic clouds
Gulisano et al. (2010)
. We know the heliocentric distance of Solar Orbiter (r
1
) and the radial width of the CME (D
1
), so we can therefore apply this power law with
\zeta
= 0.8 to calculate the estimated radial width (D
2
) at L1 (r
2
). Assuming a constant expansion within the CME, we apply a time delta to each datapoint based on the ratio of the L1 and initial radial widths (D
2
/D
1
), effectively stretching the data timestamps linearly to fit the calculated larger radial width (D
2
).

3.3
Prediction of the Geomagnetic Indices

The geomagnetic impact of CMEs can be quantified with geomagnetic indices, such as the disturbance storm time (Dst) index
Sugiura (1964)
;
Nose et al. (2015)
or the SYM-H index, which is a higher resolution (1 minute) version of the hourly Dst index
Wanliss & Showalter (2006)
. Previous studies have found empirical relationships between measured magnetic field and plasma parameters and the resulting geomagnetic indices
Burton et al. (1975)
.

One such model, the Temerin and Li model
Temerin & Li (2006)
, is a semi-empirical model optimized using eight years of solar wind data. It uses inputs of solar wind parameters measured at L1, including the magnetic field, solar wind speed, and density. It also requires past magnetic field and
Dst
index values, with initial
Dst
value estimates needed to initialize the model. Since the model also depends on local time, it is essential to ensure that the input data, in this case the in situ Solar Orbiter data, is time-shifted to match the expected arrival time of the CME at L1.

Here, we input the predicted magnetic field structure for L1 to the model to produce a predicted 1-minute resolution SYM-H index, similarly to the procedure used by
Weiler et al. (2025)
. As the Temerin & Li model also depends on solar wind plasma parameters which we did not have in real-time from Solar Orbiter, we make best estimates of the speed and density profiles prior to and during the event. Before the arrival of the CME, we assume ambient solar wind conditions: here we set the density to a constant 5 cm
-3
and the solar wind speed based on the average conditions at L1 over the 12 hours prior to when the prediction is made. The density profile is then kept constant throughout the event. We then take the mean of the initial and updated ELEvo model estimates of the arrival speed at L1: if this speed is more than 100 km s
-1
faster than the average ambient solar wind speed, we increase the input speed to this mean estimate from the predicted arrival time onward, creating a stepped speed profile. To estimate the error when calculating the SYM-H index, we randomly vary the scaled magnetic field components (within a lower bound, i.e.
\alpha=-2
, and an upper bound, i.e.
\alpha=-1.2
, as described in Section
3.2
), our estimates for the initial
Dst
index values, the speed (
\pm
50 km s
-1
), and density (
\pm
30%) along a normal distribution using 10,000 ensemble members. We also randomly vary the time-shift obtained from the ELEvo model within the arrival time error.

The complete pipeline is therefore well suited for real-time applications. 5
Discussion, Conclusions and Implications for Future Space Weather Prediction Missions

In this study, we present the first real-time predictions of CME magnetic structure and geomagnetic impact using far-upstream Solar Orbiter observations at
\sim
0.4 AU. During the two-week window in which Solar Orbiter crossed the Sun-Earth line, two CME events were observed in situ. We used these observations to assess our real-time procedure, demonstrating how measurements from future dedicated upstream solar wind monitors could be used to improve space weather forecasting.

Our results demonstrate remarkably similar predictions of the CME magnetic structure at Earth in comparison to observations for both CME events despite the early CME-CME interactions that occurred for both events. Given the large heliocentric distance separation between Solar Orbiter (
\sim
0.4 AU) and L1 (
\sim
1 AU), it is perhaps surprising how effective the application of general statistical power laws to Solar Orbiter magnetometer data was at capturing in situ observations at L1, considering the numerous physical processes that can alter the structure of the CME during propagation. Over the radial distance separations in this study, we suggest that radial expansion effects likely dominated over longitudinal effects.

A limitation identified in our approach concerns the different expansion behaviors of CME sheath regions compared to the magnetic ejecta of CMEs. The scaling laws applied in this study, derived from CME magnetic ejecta and magnetic flux rope observations, do not accurately capture the true compression and accumulation of solar wind ahead of the CME as it propagates. This is likely reflected by the longer predicted sheath durations in comparison to the observed values.

While our in situ CME magnetic structure predictions were successful, arrival time predictions remain challenging, with errors of several hours persisting even with updated constraints from Solar Orbiter observations. For Event 1, the CME arrived 7.3 hours later than predicted, while Event 2 arrived 2.6 hours earlier than the updated prediction. While the updated arrival times constrained by the observed CME arrival time at Solar Orbiter are slight improvements over the initial model estimates using the DONKI input parameters, the improvements are not significant, suggesting that observations so far upstream still leave plenty of margin for error. ELEvo is a simple drag based CME propagation model, so improvements to the arrival time predictions likely require better characterization of the ambient solar wind environment that account for dynamic solar wind conditions. However, we note that the arrival time errors in this study are within the 10–13 hour uncertainty margins in Sun-to-L1 propagation found across all types of CME propagation models which represent a fundamental limitation that has not significantly improved over time
Riley et al. (2018)
;
Kay et al. (2024)
. The best solution to the arrival time problem is likely a solar wind monitor placed closer to L1 than Solar Orbiter in this study, but still at a significant enough distance for increased warning times.

Our study also highlights the essential role of plasma parameters in accurate geomagnetic impact modeling. The Temerin & Li model requires inputs of the solar wind speed and density for which we made assumptions of constant density (5 cm
-3
) and simplified speed profiles for the CME events. Realistic speed and density profiles, particularly the enhanced densities typically observed in CME sheath regions and the decreasing speed profiles observed within CME magnetic ejecta, produce more accurate geomagnetic storm onset values and stronger overall responses in the Temerin & Li model (Weiler et al., private communication). The input of more realistic plasma parameter profiles would likely improve the geomagnetic index predictions for this study, and are therefore essential measurements that should be provided by future upstream monitoring missions.

We also show that geomagnetic impact predictions can be complicated by pre-existing disturbed geomagnetic conditions, particularly evident for Event 2 where there was enhanced geomagnetic activity prior to CME arrival. It is likely that these complications would be lessened with continuous data input to the Temerin & Li model, as would be the case with future space weather missions, where prior conditions would be taken into account.

Using our real-time procedure, we demonstrate that even with relatively simple scaling laws, models and limited plasma data, accurate predictions of the CME magnetic structure and geomagnetic impact can be made, with actionable warning times of 7–15 hours. Our findings directly support proposed and future dedicated upstream space weather missions
(
Lugaz, Lee et al. (2024)
;
Cicalò et al. (2025)
, e.g.)
and the ESA SHIELD concept, demonstrating that useful predictions can be made with longitudinal separations up to 10
∘
over heliocentric distance ranges where radial evolution effects dominate.
