JAK-1 inhibitors in the management of atopic dermatitis in patients over 65 years old: a descriptive cohort study
Authors: Jiayue Zheng, Karla Robles-Velasco, Sonia Sharma, Isabel Canzi Almada de Souza Lima, Veronica Ferris Pasquini, Rodrigo G Ducati, Hermenio Lima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtopic dermatitis (AD) can affect older adults. Janus kinase inhibitors (JAK-1 inhibitors), such as upadacitinib and abrocitinib, have emerged as effective treatments for moderate-to-severe AD. However, data on their safety and efficacy in older adults remain limited due to underrepresentation in clinical trials. This retrospective cross-sectional study examined the efficacy and safety of oral JAK-1 inhibitors in patients aged 65 years or older. Data was extracted from electronic medical records. Disease control was assessed using the Atopic Dermatitis Control Tool (ADCT), while adverse events were analyzed. Statistical comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test. The study included 31 patients. Thirteen patients (42%) received abrocitinib, and 18 (58%) upadacitinib. The mean reduction in ADCT scores was − 84.4%, with a slightly greater improvement observed in abrocitinib users (-5.1%, p < 0.001). Mild adverse events were reported in 21 patients (68%), while 26% experienced no adverse effects. Two patients discontinued treatment due to adverse events. Both upadacitinib and abrocitinib demonstrated substantial efficacy in achieving long term AD control among older adults, with manageable safety profiles. These findings highlight the potential of JAK-1 inhibitors in this population while underscoring the need for personalized treatment approaches and further research in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32163-y.Keywords: JAK inhibitors, Atopic dermatitis, Elderly, Safety, EfficacySubject terms: Inflammation, Immunology, Medical research, Outcomes research

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common chronic inflammatory skin disease affecting both children and adults1. AD presents with a range of symptoms, including xerosis, pruritus, and pain; these symptoms often lead to sleep disturbances and significantly impair patients’ quality of life2. The burden of AD extends beyond physical symptoms, as it is associated with mental health comorbidities such as anxiety, depression, and suicidality. A 2019 study estimated the prevalence of adult AD to be 7.3% in the United States3. While AD has been typically common in younger ages, data on the prevalence of AD in older adults is recently growing. A 2021 population-based study estimated that 11.6% of adults over 45 years of age in the United Kingdom are affected by AD1. AD in elderly patients might differ in the immunopathogenesis of the disease in comparison with younger ones, and AD in middle-aged and older groups might be associated with an increased risk of cognitive dysfunction, particularly dementia of Alzheimer’s type and all-cause dementia4.

The introduction of Janus kinase inhibitors (JAK inhibitors), both topical and oral, has transformed the management of AD5. Their exact mechanism of action in treating generalized atopic dermatitis involves suppressing the transcription of pro-inflammatory and immune-regulatory genes by inhibiting of the Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP)-binding site of JAK enzymes, preventing their phosphorylation and activation6. Two oral JAK inhibitors, upadacitinib and abrocitinib, both selective for JAK-1, have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of moderate-to-severe AD in adults7.

Despite the potential of JAK-1 inhibitors (upadacitinib and abrocitinib) for achieving control, low activity of the disease and improvement in quality of life of AD patients, shown in several clinical trials and real-world studies8–10, there is still limited evidence on their use in treating AD in older adults (defined here as age 65 and above). Some studies have raised concerns about potential adverse events associated with these medications, including herpes zoster, serious infections, malignancies, and thrombosis11,12, but on the contrary, other studies in adult AD patients also have supported a favorable benefit-risk profile in up to 4 years of treatment10 and one Italian study in patients aged >60 years followed during 52 weeks showed a significant efficacy and an acceptable safety profile9. As the evidence continues to grow and given that older adults are underrepresented in trials of systemic immunomodulatory treatment for atopic dermatitis13, it is needed more real-world studies showing the safety and efficacy of upadacitinib and abrocitinib in this special population and in longer follow up14. Therefore, this study aims to describe our real-world, single-centre experience regarding the efficacy and safety of upadacitinib and abrocitinib in older adults during an average follow up of 548 days.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective descriptive cross-sectional study evaluated the use of Janus kinase inhibitors (JAK-1 inhibitors) in managing AD in older adults. The study involved a review of electronic medical records from Pitanga Clinic in Hamilton, Ontario, focusing on patients aged 65 years and older who were treated with JAK-1 inhibitors for AD.

Data from electronic medical records comprised patient demographics and treatment specifics, usually conducted as part of patient care. To evaluate the control level of AD, we employed the Atopic Dermatitis Control Tool (ADCT)15, a valid and reliable instrument for detecting clinically significant changes in disease control over time, as indicated by the HOME guidelines for AD16,17. Each ADCT question is assigned a score between 0 and 4 points, with total scores ranging from 0 to 24 points. An individual’s AD may be inadequately managed if the ADCT total score is 7 points or greater, with elevated values signifying diminished control of AD.

This tool, introduced in 2019, became a standard component of patient visits; nevertheless, not all patients documented this information in their records. Patients who satisfied the inclusion criteria and lacked a registry of ADCT scores were contacted by telephone and requested to evaluate their ADCT scores retrospectively. Initially, by recalling their symptoms before starting treatment with JAK-1 inhibitor, and subsequently by reporting their current ADCT ratings. Patients were inquired about significant cardiovascular risk factors, encompassing smoking history, history of thrombosis, and other relevant aspects.

Participants

Participants eligible for this study were 65 years or older and had a documented diagnosis of atopic dermatitis in their electronic medical records (EMR). They must have received treatment with an oral JAK-1 inhibitor, specifically upadacitinib or abrocitinib, and have taken at least one dose of either medication. Individuals were be excluded from the study if there was no follow-up data available related to their JAK-1 inhibitor therapy, if they were using an oral JAK-1 inhibitor for a condition other than atopic dermatitis, or if they had used more than one type of JAK-1 inhibitor for treating their atopic dermatitis.

Data collection

All data were extracted from a Hamilton center’s EMR Accuro and stored on a password-protected computer. The extracted data included patient demographics (i.e., gender, age, and comorbidities), disease characteristics (i.e., severity, duration, and prior treatments), and details of the JAK-1 inhibitor therapy (i.e., type of JAK inhibitor, dosage, and treatment duration). The primary outcomes assessed were changes in disease severity, via tracking changes to ADCT scores. Adverse events were assessed as secondary outcomes.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the data collected. Inferential analysis through the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test for paired samples was used to evaluate the differences in population mean ranks between baseline and follow-up ADCT scores. A similar method was carried out between subgroups for each JAK-1 inhibitor.

Ethical statement

This study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Retrospective data from electronic medical records were de-identified to maintain patient confidentiality. As no experimental intervention was administered and only de-identified secondary data were used, Research Ethics Board review was not required, in accordance with Article 2.4 of the Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (TCPS 2).

Results

The analysis included 31 participants, 65% female and 35% male, with a mean age of 72.6 years (SD = 5.3). Thirteen patients (42%) were treated with abrocitinib, while eighteen patients (58%) were treated with upadacitinib. The average duration of days receiving any JAK-1 inhibitor was 548 days (SD = 271), see Table 1.

Safety of JAK-1 inhibitors in older patients with atopic dermatitis

Eight individuals (26%) demonstrated an absence of adverse effects while undergoing treatment with JAK-1 inhibitors. Twenty-one patients experienced new or worsened symptoms across dermatologic, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and miscellaneous categories, all defined by the investigators as mild, all of them were taking a dose of either upadacitinib 15 mg or abrocitinib 100 mg daily. None were deemed serious adverse events related to the administered medicine (e.g. Table 2). The two remaining patients experienced cardiovascular and mild musculoskeletal adverse events, respectively, which resulted in the cessation of therapy. Both patients were under upadacitinib 15 mg daily.

Discussion

This study provides preliminary insight into the use of JAK-1 inhibitors, specifically abrocitinib and upadacitinib, in older adults with atopic dermatitis. For most patients, both upadacitinib and abrocitinib have demonstrated significant efficacy for improving symptoms and achieving control of the disease, as evidenced by a reduction in atopic dermatitis control test scores.

Atopic dermatitis can impact individuals of any age, including the elderly. A study conducted in the United Kingdom revealed that the incidence of AD exhibits a bimodal age distribution, with the initial peak occurring in children under one year of age and the subsequent peak in individuals aged 80 years and older18. Consequently, a comparable peak in the number of patients with AD in these age groups is anticipated during our consultations.

In addition, the degrees of disease severity vary. Some individuals with mild cases of AD may require only topical treatment, while others might need different interventions. In these cases, the necessary treatment may include biologics/monoclonal antibodies, ultraviolet B (UVB) light therapy, and small molecule immunosuppressants, specifically JAK inhibitors (abrocitinib 100–200 mg, baricitinib 2–4 mg, and upadacitinib 15–30 mg), as well as cyclosporine for refractory cases, typically moderate-to-severe, according to the latest guidelines of AD management19.

AD in elderly individuals is currently under investigation and may be influenced by a relapse or continuation of the condition from childhood, a sustained presence from young adulthood, or a de novo beginning of the disease11. The immunopathogenesis is uncertain but it is described that in aging processes, there is a decrease in barrier function of the skin, which might predispose to disruption in immune cells, decline of innate immune system and disbalance in the adaptative immune system, leading to activations of a Th2 response that allows the development of AD in the elderly11.

The difficulty arises in choosing the most appropriate treatment agent based on the severity of the patient’s AD, while also considering their medical history, including any comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, hypertension, or reduced kidney function, which contraindicates cyclosporine use. Additionally, a history of diabetes, osteoporosis, or skin fragility at that age raises concerns regarding the application of systemic or topical corticosteroids11.

Within the family of JAK inhibitors, Tofacitinib—one of the earliest and most extensively studied agents—has a well-documented safety profile. Based on available data, in 2017, the European Medicines Agency recommended the use of JAK inhibitors (in rheumatoid arthritis) only when no suitable alternative treatments were available20. More recently, in January 2022, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a boxed warning for certain JAK inhibitors due to potential risks of serious cardiovascular events, malignancies, thrombosis, and death in the treatment of chronic inflammatory conditions21.

This FDA warning was primarily informed by large, randomized safety trials of Tofacitinib in rheumatoid arthritis, which demonstrated a higher incidence of these adverse outcomes22. In contrast, similarly robust safety analyses were not available for Baricitinib and upadacitinib. Nevertheless, the FDA extended the warning to these agents as a precautionary measure, partly due to the limited number of randomized studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of selective JAK inhibitors in older adults. As highlighted by Sreekantaswamy et al., only 2.5–8% of clinical trial participants are over 65 years of age23, an underrepresentation that restricts the generalizability of trial findings to this demographic23. Although all three agents share the same fundamental mechanism of action—JAK inhibition—they differ in their selectivity profiles: Tofacitinib predominantly targets JAK1 and JAK3, with modest effect on JAK2; Baricitinib selectively inhibits JAK1 and JAK2, whereas both upadacitinib and abrocitinib have excellent selectivity for JAK16.

Nevertheless, in 2023, newer evidence including anecdotal studies and clinical trials results presented positive results illustrating an excellent safety profile of JAK inhibitors in AD, exemplified by Piscazzi et al., who reported on seven elderly patients administered upadacitinib for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis over 104 weeks24. The study indicated disease control, as assessed by the Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI), alongside an excellent safety profile, notwithstanding the considerable medical histories of some patients, including malignancies, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular conditions24. A similar study by Gargiulo et al.25 in 52 weeks, in a younger population but with comparable comorbidities showed a good efficacy and safety profile with AEs such as acne, blood count abnormalities and herpes zoster. Another study by Silverberg et al.10 presented an integrated analysis of the Measure Up 1, Measure Up 226, and AD Up27 phase 3 trials, with a mean participant age of 34 years and minimal inclusion of patients ≥ 65 years, supporting a favorable benefit–risk profile for upadacitinib in adolescents and adults with moderate-to-severe AD over up to four years of treatment. Six malignancies were reported in patients aged ≥ 50 years—most diagnosed within 30 days of treatment initiation—and none were considered related to the study drug.

In our study, 31 patients were monitored for an average of 548 days (with some exceeding 1000 days), demonstrating long-term treatment with remarkable efficacy in controlling their illness. Regarding the safety of JAK-1 inhibitors, it was noted that 4 patients (13%) discontinued treatment due to serious adverse events (sudden death: 2 cases, cardiovascular disease: 1 case, general ill health: 1 case). In the case of an unspecified cardiovascular issue that required hospitalization, there were no fatal consequences, and this patient had a prior history of myocardial infarction before initiating upadacitinib therapy. Consistent with these findings, a systematic review conducted by Ertus et al. examined major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in over 18,000 patients with atopic dermatitis treated with oral Janus kinase inhibitors, including acute coronary syndrome, ischemic stroke, and cardiovascular mortality28. Four MACE events were identified in the initial period, along with eight primary and 13 secondary cardiovascular events in the subsequent investigation period. The authors concluded that cardiovascular events are rare in patients utilizing JAK-1 inhibitors for AD, and that they exert minimal to no influence on the incidence of MACE28.

According to Chu et al. upadacitinib in high dose might increase the frequency of any adverse event and for serious adverse events the direct evidence was uncertain29. However, six cases of infections were reported among our patients: 5 of them were under upadacitinib 15 mg (two urinary tract infections, two cases of herpes zoster, one pseudomonas infection), and one respiratory tract infection in a patient on abrocitinib 100 mg. All of which were managed on an outpatient basis were minor and recovered promptly. JAK-1 was halted during the infection phase and subsequently reinstated without problems. A large study published in 2023 by Burmester et al.30. that analyzed the safety profile of upadacitinib in 15.000 patients across different inflammatory conditions including AD, showed that the risk of serious infections was similar across all inflammatory conditions, and they infrequently lead to discontinuation. Herpes zoster showed an upward trend at a dose of upadacitinib 30 mg, but affecting only one dermatome and no involvement of the central nervous system and patients restarted the treatment after resolution of the episode, as happened in our cases. Also, they reported that deaths, MACE, venous thromboembolism, and malignancies were observed, with rates generally lowest in AD but a high rate of acne was observed, in our study we reported three cases of acne in patients receiving upadacitinib 15 mg. Similar findings have been reported in other studies showing no heightened risk of serious or opportunistic infections in individuals on JAK inhibitors compared to placebo25,31–33.

Another study by Potestio et al.9 evaluated JAK inhibitor use over 52 weeks in 72 patients (mean age 68 years). Despite our smaller cohort (n = 31), we studied an older population (mean age 72.6 years) with a longer follow-up (78 weeks on average, some > 1,000 days). Both studies confirmed treatment efficacy. Compared with Potestio et al., we observed fewer cases of nausea (1 vs. 4) but more arthralgia (4 vs. 1), herpes zoster reactivation (2 vs. 1), and, notably, treatment discontinuations and five serious adverse events. While Potestio et al. reported no treatment interruptions or common AEs such as upper respiratory infections—likely influenced by retrospective design, patient selection, and exclusion of early dropouts, as noted per the authors—our study provides a detailed account of all adverse events and reasons for discontinuation, strengthening the relevance of our investigation in elderly patients.

Moreover, our report underscores the importance of vigilant monitoring in older patients. It is important to note, that causality cannot be inferred as many of the patients have existing comorbidities that predispose them to various medical events and that the study has a small sample size. This study contributes to the sparse literature on JAK-1 inhibitor safety and efficacy in older adults and provides valuable groundwork for future prospective larger-scale studies that could better define the risk-benefit profile of these medications.

This study has some limitations. First, this analysis was not statistically powered to examine the efficacy and safety of JAK-1 inhibitors in older adults, and it considered a follow-up of approximately 78 weeks. Moreover, the results may not be fully generalizable to older adults, as it is a retrospective, single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other settings and patient populations. The small sample reduces the power to detect less common adverse events. Furthermore, the reliance on patient recall for ADCT scores before treatment introduces recall bias, which may impact the accuracy of symptom severity assessments. Another limitation is the absence of other clinical outcome measures such as EASI, SCORAD, or Itch-NRS; we based our evaluation on the ADCT following HOME guideline recommendations17 to provide general insights on long-term disease control and safety. Future studies should adopt prospective designs with larger cohorts, control groups, and complementary clinician- and patient-reported measures to better evaluate efficacy and safety in older adults with AD.

Conclusions

This study supports the efficacy and safety of JAK-1 inhibitors, namely abrocitinib and upadacitinib, in elderly patients with atopic dermatitis. Both medications markedly improved disease control, as seen by decreases in ADCT scores, with slight advantages for abrocitinib. Adverse events were predominantly mild, and while cardiovascular concerns are infrequent, they underscore the necessity for vigilant patient monitoring. Although most patients tolerate these drugs effectively throughout prolonged usage, our findings emphasize the necessity of personalized treatment that considers comorbidities. Although the sample size is small and generalizability is limited, this study offers significant insights into applying JAK-1 inhibitors in an underrepresented group, recommending more extensive studies to confirm these findings.
