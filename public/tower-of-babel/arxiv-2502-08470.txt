Numerical Schemes for Signature Kernels
Thomas Cass, Francesco Piatti, Jeffrey Pei

Abstract
Signature kernels have emerged as a powerful tool within kernel methods for sequential data. In the paper “The Signature Kernel is the solution of a Goursat PDE” [ Sal+21 ] , the authors identify a kernel trick that demonstrates that, for continuously differentiable paths, the signature kernel satisfies a Goursat problem for a hyperbolic partial differential equation (PDE) in two independent time variables. While finite difference methods have been explored for this PDE, they face limitations in accuracy and stability when handling highly oscillatory inputs. In this work, we introduce two advanced numerical schemes that leverage polynomial representations of boundary conditions through either approximation or interpolation techniques, and rigorously establish the theoretical convergence of the polynomial approximation scheme. Experimental evaluations reveal that our approaches yield improvements of several orders of magnitude in mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) compared to traditional finite difference schemes, without increasing computational complexity. Furthermore, like finite difference methods, our algorithms can be GPU-parallelized to reduce computational complexity from quadratic to linear in the length of the input sequences, thereby improving scalability for high-frequency data. We have implemented these algorithms in a dedicated Python library, which is publicly available at: https://github.com/FrancescoPiatti/polysigkernel .

Introduction

Kernel methods are powerful tools in machine learning and have been widely applied in areas such as image classification and structured data analysis, achieving significant impact. They form the backbone of several machine learning techniques, including Support Vector Machines
[
Hea+98
]
and Gaussian processes
[
Ras03
]
, where they allow the use of high-dimensional feature sets without some of the disadvantages associated with working in high-dimensional spaces. A kernel
\kappa:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a positive semidefinite symmetric function defined on a set
\mathcal{X}
. Each such kernel has associated to it a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}
consisting of functions on
\mathcal{X}
and containing as a dense subspace the linear span of functions
\left\{\kappa_{x}:x\in\mathcal{X}\right\}
with
\kappa_{x}:=\kappa(x,\cdot):y\rightarrow\kappa(x,y)
. If
\mathcal{X}
is a topological space, then a universal kernel is one for which
\mathcal{H}
is dense in an appropriate space of continuous functions. In many applications, including those mentioned above, the kernel-based approach allows one to work with high-dimensional feature mappings without explicitly constructing those features, as only the kernel evaluations
\{\kappa(x_{i},x_{j}):i,j=1,...,n\}
are required for a finite sample
\{x_{i}:i=1,...,n\}
.

Sequential data are inherently high-dimensional and require efficient data representation and suitable methods to mitigate the impact of dimensionality. Kernel methods tailored to ordered inputs have become increasingly widely used in fields such as finance
[
NAT24
]
, speech recognition
[
May+19
]
, and bioinformatics
[
Zou+21
,
Qi+21
,
Hay+21
]
. Popular kernel examples include the Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) Kernel
[
LS07
]
and the Global Alignment Kernel (GAK)
[
Cut+07
,
Cut11
]
. An alternative approach introduced by
[
KO19
]
, is based on the (ordinary) signature kernel, a universal kernel on the space of unparameterised paths. This uses the signature transform to represent a continuous path of finite length
\gamma
as a hierarchy of coefficients that are determined by the collection of all iterated integrals of
\gamma
. If
\gamma
takes values in a Hilbert space
V
, then its signature
S(\gamma)
takes value in the free algebra of tensor series over
V

[
Reu03
]
:

T((V))=\left\{\mathbf{A}=(a^{0},a^{1},\dots)\ |\ a^{0}\in\mathbb{R},\forall k>0,a^{k}\in V^{\otimes k}\right\}.

where
\otimes
denotes the (classical) tensor product of vector spaces.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
V
be a Banach space, and let
\gamma:[0,t]\to V
be a continuous path of finite
p
-variation
[
LCL07
]
, with
p<2
. Then the signature transform
S(\gamma)_{[0,t]}
of the path
\gamma
is defined as the following collection of iterated integrals:

S(\gamma)_{[0,t]}=\left(1,\underset{0<u_{1}<t}{\int}dx_{u_{1}},\dots,\underset{0<u_{1}<\dots<u_{k}<t}{\int\dots\int}dx_{u_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes dx_{u_{k}},\dots\right)

or, equivalently, as the unique solution to the following controlled differential equation

dS(\gamma)_{t}=S(\gamma)_{t}\otimes d\gamma_{t},\qquad S(\gamma)_{0}=1

It is known that the map
\gamma\mapsto S(\gamma)
is a faithful representation of the group of unparameterised paths
[
HL10
]
. By equipping
T((V))
with an inner product, we can define a kernel on the space by

k_{x,y}=\langle S(x),S(y)\rangle_{T((V))}

When the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V^{\otimes k}}
on each
V^{\otimes k}
is chosen as the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product induced by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V}
, the resulting inner product on
T((V))
can be defined by linearity as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{T((V))}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\langle v_{k},w_{k}\rangle_{V^{\otimes k}}
.
This choice not only yields a universal kernel but also connects the signature kernel to a Goursat problem
[
Sal+21
]
. While alternative inner products (e.g., weighted inner products
[
CLX24
]
) could be considered, they are not the focus of this paper.

The effectiveness of the signature kernel has been demonstrated in numerous studies across a variety of problem domains. Its applications include time series classification and regression
[
Sal+21
,
Gu+24
]
, Bayesian modeling
[
Lem+21
,
TO20
]
, theoretical deep learning
[
Fer+21
]
, and generative modeling
[
Dye+22
,
CO18
,
Iss+24
]
. Furthermore, it has been successfully applied to distribution regression
[
Lem+21a
,
Sal+21a
,
Coc+21
]
and numerical analysis to solve path-dependent PDEs
[
PS24
]
. More recently, its connections to the scaling limits of residual neural networks have been explored
[
CLS23
]
, further showcasing its versatility and theoretical importance.

On the other hand, the practical implementation requires a way of evaluating the inner product efficiently. The first attempt was made by
[
KO19
]
, who proposed a truncated approximation, replacing the full signature with its truncated version up to level
N
, leveraging the factorial decay of the signature terms
[
LCL07
]
. Specifically, they approximated the kernel as
\langle S(\gamma),S(\sigma)\rangle\approx\langle S^{N}(\gamma),S^{N}(\sigma)\rangle
where
S^{N}(\cdot)
lies in the truncated tensor algebra
T_{N}(V)=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{N}V^{\otimes k}
. The direct evaluation of truncated signatures presents a major drawback: the number of coefficients in the signatures grows geometrically with the dimension of the time series, making it computationally expensive for large
N
. To mitigate this limitation, they introduced Horner-type schemes that exploit dynamic programming and low-rank approximations, significantly improving the computational efficiency of evaluating the truncated signature kernel.

Another approach to approximate the truncated signature kernel is to consider Random Fourier Signature Features
[
TOS23
]
.
This method extends the framework of Random Fourier Features
[
RR07
]
, which relies on Bochner’s theorem stating that any shift-invariant kernel can be expressed as the Fourier transform of a probability measure, to sequential data via signature transforms. By leveraging this framework, the signature kernel is approximated using randomized Fourier basis functions. Although this method still requires truncation of the signature, it offers a significant computational advantage as its complexity scales linearly with the length of the input paths, making it efficient for large-scale datasets.

The untruncated signature kernel was first identified by
[
Sal+21
]
, who demonstrated that for differentiable paths
x
and
y
, the kernel
k_{x,y}
satisfies a linear hyperbolic Goursat partial differential equation.

Theorem 1.1

(The signature kernel PDE
[
Sal+21
]
)
.

Let
I=[u,u^{\prime}]
and
J=[v,v^{\prime}]
be two compact intervals and let
x\in C^{1}(I,V)
and
y\in C^{1}(J,V)
be differentiable functions. Then, the signature kernel
k_{x,y}
is a solution of the following linear, second order, hyperbolic PDE:

\frac{\partial^{2}k_{x,y}}{\partial s\partial t}=\left\langle\dot{x}_{s},\dot{y}_{t}\right\rangle_{V}k_{x,y},\qquad k_{x,y}(u,\cdot)=k_{x,y}(\cdot,v)=1

(1)

where
\dot{x}_{s}
and
\dot{y}_{t}
are the derivatives of
x
and
y
at time
s
and
t
respectively.

The numerical scheme proposed by the authors employs finite differences methods on a refined grid (Definition
2.1
in this paper), interpolating time series data to improve accuracy. However, this approach can require a high-resolution grid and tends to become numerically unstable for paths at large
p
-variation.

Rough path theory
[
Lyo98
]
provides a natural framework to handle highly oscillatory paths, mitigating the numerical instability of finite difference methods. Additionally, it extends the computation of signature kernels to paths that may not have finite length.
[
LL24
]
demonstrated that the signature kernel for smooth rough paths
[
Bel+22
]
satisfies a system of coupled PDEs. Their approach approximates input paths using piecewise log-linear paths (also known as piecewise abelian paths
[
FL15
]
), parameterized by a time partition and an approximation degree. Degree-1 approximations reduce the problem to solving a PDE similar to (
1
), while higher degrees result in systems of coupled PDEs with coefficients derived from log-signatures over the partition intervals. While this method enhances stability, it introduces hyperparameters and scalability challenges, particularly for large datasets.

Our contribution

In this paper, we study the untruncated signature kernel and we propose two novel numerical schemes to solve PDE (
1
) for piecewise linear input paths. First, we focus on the solution of the PDE on a general rectangle
[a,b]\times[c,d]
where
\left\langle\dot{x}_{s},\dot{y}_{t}\right\rangle_{V}=C
is constant for
s\in[a,b]
and
t\in[c,d]
. We show that given analytical initial boundary conditions in the form of power series expansions
k(s,c)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}p_{n}(s-a)^{n}
and
k(a,t)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}q_{n}(t-c)^{n}
the coefficients of the power series that describe the solution on the opposite edges of the rectangle, i.e.
k(s,d)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}\tilde{p}_{n}(s-a)^{n}
and
k(b,t)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}\tilde{q}_{n}(t-c)^{n}
can be derived analytically

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}\frac{(C(t-c))^{n-k}\ k!}{(n-k)!\ n!}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}\frac{C^{n}(t-c)^{n+k}\ k!}{(n+k)!\ n!}

\displaystyle\tilde{q}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q_{k}\frac{(C(s-a))^{n-k}\ k!}{(n-k)!\ n!}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\frac{C^{n}(s-a)^{n+k}\ k!}{(n+k)!\ n!}

This result, formalized and proven in Proposition
3.1
, provides an analytical tool to propagate the boundary conditions across the grid and compute the signature kernel recursively. Building on this result, we develop two numerical schemes that leverage polynomials to approximate (
1
). The first scheme, referred to as the polynomial approximation scheme, truncates the power series above at order
N
. For this scheme, we derive an explicit error bound of the form:

|k(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})-\hat{k}^{N}(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})|\leq f(\ell_{x}+\ell_{y}-1)\frac{(K\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}\left[\frac{(\ell_{x}+\ell_{y}-1)^{N+2}}{N+2}+(\ell_{x}+\ell_{y}-1)^{N+1}\right]

where
\Delta=\max_{i,j}\{s_{i+1}-s_{i},t_{j+1}-t_{j}\}
,
\sup\left|\left\langle\dot{x}_{s},\dot{y}_{t}\right\rangle\right|\leq K
,
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function defined as
f(k):=\prod_{m=0}^{k}I_{0}(2\sqrt{mK}\Delta)
, and
\ell_{x}
,
\ell_{y}
are the number of linear segments of
x
and
y
.

The second scheme, termed the polynomial interpolation scheme, approximates the solution through polynomial interpolation and achieves faster convergence, particularly for oscillatory paths. By leveraging these polynomial-based approaches, we significantly reduce the need for high-resolution grids required by finite differences methods, improving numerical stability, especially for paths with complex oscillatory behavior.

Both numerical schemes have been integrated into an open-source software library, which also includes tools for practical applications of the signature kernel, such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) computation and statistical hypothesis testing.

In this section, we briefly review the finite differences scheme proposed by
[

]

Suppose

In

. By approximating the double integral in (

\displaystyle k_{x,y}(s,t)

\displaystyle\approx k_{x,y}(s,v)+k_{x,y}(u,t)-k_{x,y}(u,v)+\frac{1}{2}C\big(k_{x,y}(s,v)+k_{x,y}(u,t)\big)(s-u)(t-v)

Remark 2.1
.

Higher-order quadrature methods can provide more accurate approximations for the double integral in (
3

\displaystyle k_{x,y}(s,t)

\displaystyle\approx k_{x,y}(s,v)+k_{x,y}(u,t)-k_{x,y}(u,v)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}C\big(k_{x,y}(u,v)+k_{x,y}(s,v)+k_{x,y}(u,t)+k_{x,y}(s,t)\big)(s-u)(t-v)

They also introduce a second-order explicit scheme:

\displaystyle k_{x,y}(s,t)\approx k_{x,y}(s,v)

\displaystyle+k_{x,y}(u,t)-k_{x,y}(u,v)+\frac{1}{2}C\big(k_{x,y}(s,v)+k_{x,y}(u,t)\big)(s-u)(t-v)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{12}C^{2}\big(k_{x,y}(s,v)+k_{x,y}(u,t)+k_{x,y}(u,v)\big)(s-u)^{2}(t-v)^{2}

\mathcal{D}_{I}\times\mathcal{D}_{J}
into smaller rectangles via linear interpolation of the time series. This refinement results in a new grid

Definition 2.1

(Dyadic finite differences approximation scheme,

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\cap\left([u_{i},u_{i+1}]\times[v_{j},v_{j+1}]\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(u_{i}+k2^{-\lambda}(u_{i+1}-u_{i}),v_{j}+l2^{-\lambda}(v_{j+1}-v_{j})\right)\;:\;0\leq k,l\leq 2^{\lambda}\right\}

\displaystyle\hat{k}(s_{i+1},t_{j+1})=\begin{aligned} &\hat{k}(s_{i+1},t_{j})+\hat{k}(s_{i},t_{j+1})-\hat{k}(s_{i},t_{j})\\

3
Polynomial-based schemes for the Goursat PDE

In this section, first we derive an analytical solution for the signature kernel of two piecewise-linear paths in the form of a power series with coefficients that can be computed recursively, and we establish bounds for these coefficients. We then introduce two numerical schemes which leverage polynomials to approximate the signature kernel: the first scheme approximates the signature kernel by truncating the aforementioned power series at order
N
, while the second scheme employs polynomial interpolation to numerically solve the Goursat PDE.

\displaystyle|(n!)^{2}\tilde{p}_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(k!)^{2}p_{k}(|C|\Delta_{c,d})^{n-k}+n!(|C|\Delta_{c,d})^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(k!)^{2}q_{k}\Delta_{c,d}^{k}}{(n+k)!k!}

\displaystyle\leq(|C|\Delta)^{n}\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\gamma^{k}+(|C|\Delta)^{n}\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(|C|\gamma)^{k}\Delta^{2k}}{(k!)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(|C|\Delta)^{n}\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}(\gamma+1)^{n}+(|C|\Delta)^{n}\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}(I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)-1)

where in the second inequality we have used
(n+k)!\geq n!k!
.
This implies that:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(n!)^{2}\tilde{p}_{n}}{(C\Delta)^{n}(\gamma+1)^{n}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}+\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}\frac{(I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)-1)}{(\gamma+1)^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)

A similar argument holds for
\tilde{q}_{n}
, leading to
\|(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})\|_{\gamma+1}\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)
.∎

Remark 3.2
.

The bound on

guarantees that the power series expansions in (

[a,b]
and
[c,d]
, respectively, which is essential for a recursive application of Proposition (

In the one-step estimate we derived analytical formulas to solve PDE (

Remark 3.3
.

The function

\|(p^{1},p^{2})\|_{\gamma}=\max_{i=1,2}\left\{\max_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}\left\{\left|\frac{(n!)^{2}p^{i}_{n}}{(\gamma K\Delta)^{n}}\right|\right\}\right\}

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Lambda_{ij}
be as introduced in Definition
3.1
, and define the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\gamma}
as in (

). Then, for all

it holds that

\|\Lambda_{ij}\|_{i+j+1}\leq f(i+j)

where
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
s.t.

f(k):=\prod_{m=0}^{k}I_{0}(2\sqrt{mK}\Delta)

(16)

with
K=\max_{i,j}|C_{ij}|
and
\Delta=\max_{i,j}\{s_{i+1}-s_{i},t_{j+1}-t_{j}\}
.

Proof.

To begin, notice that

\displaystyle p^{01}_{n}=\frac{C_{00}^{n}t_{1}^{n}}{(n!)^{2}},\qquad q^{10}_{n}=\frac{C_{00}^{n}s_{1}^{n}}{(n!)^{2}},\qquad\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}

which implies
\|\Lambda_{00}\|_{1}=\|(p^{01},g^{10})\|_{1}\leq 1
, as
C_{00}\leq K
. By a simple induction,
\|\Lambda_{0j}\|_{j+1}\leq f(j)
and
\|\Lambda_{i0}\|_{i+1}\leq f(i)
. In fact, for
\|\Lambda_{0j}\|_{j+1}=\|(p^{0,j+1},q^{1,j})\|_{j+1}
notice that

p^{0,j+1}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p^{0j}_{k}\frac{(C_{0j}(t_{j+1}-t_{j}))^{n-k}\ k!}{(n-k)!\ n!}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad q^{1,j}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p^{0j}_{k}\frac{C_{0j}^{n}s_{1}^{n+k}\ k!}{(n+k)!\ n!}

where we have used
p^{0j}_{0}=q^{0j}_{0}
and that
q^{0j}_{n}=0
for
n\geq 1
. By Proposition
3.2
,
\|\Lambda_{0,j+1}\|_{j+2}\leq\|\Lambda_{0j}\|_{j+1}I_{0}(2\sqrt{(j+1)K}\Delta)
, leading to
\|\Lambda_{0,j+1}\|_{j+2}\leq f(j+1)
, by definition of
f
. Similarly we can show that,
\|\Lambda_{i0}\|_{i+1}\leq f(i)
.

Next, proceed by induction on
m=i+j
. Assume
\|\Lambda_{ij}\|_{i+j+1}\leq f(i+j)
for all
i,\ j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
i+j=m
and
\Lambda_{ij}
is defined. This implies
\|\Lambda_{ij}\|_{m+1}\leq f(m)
. Exploiting the symmetry of the problem, it suffices to show
\|\Lambda_{i+1,j}\|_{i+j+2}\leq f(i+j+1)
, i.e.
\|\Lambda_{i+1,j}\|_{m+2}\leq f(m+1)
.
By definition

\Lambda_{i+1,j}=(p^{i+1,j+1},q^{i+2,j})=\Lambda_{C_{i+1,j};[s_{i+1},s_{i+2}]\times[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}(p^{i+1,j},q^{i+1,j})

where
(p^{i+1,j},q^{i+2,j-1})=\Lambda_{i+1,j-1}
and
(p^{i,j+1},q^{i+1,j})=\Lambda_{i,j}
.

By the inductive hypothesis
\|\Lambda_{i+1,j-1}\|_{m+1}\leq f(m)
and
\|\Lambda_{i,j}\|_{m+1}\leq f(m)
, implying
\|(p^{i+1,j},q^{i+1,j})\|_{m+1}\leq f(m)
. Then by Proposition
3.2
, we obtain
\|\Lambda_{i+1,j}\|_{m+2}\leq I_{0}(2\sqrt{(m+1)K}\Delta)f(m)=f(m+1)
.∎

This result provides bounds on the coefficients of the power series describing each boundary of the grid, which in turn allows us to prove the error bounds of the numerical scheme proposed in the next section.

3.2
Polynomial approximation numerical scheme

We present an approximation scheme that leverages the results of Proposition
3.1
to numerically solve the Goursat PDE (
1
). This scheme approximates each boundary of the grid using an order-
N
polynomial obtained by truncating the power series (
11
), (
12
). This approach provides an efficient and accurate method for computing the signature kernel.

To proceed, we need to introduce additional notation to represent truncated sequences of coefficients, as well as sequences starting from an index greater than 0.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
g=(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
and
h=(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
, then define

g^{\leq m}:=(g_{0},g_{1},\dots,g_{m},0,\dots)

and
(g,h)^{\leq m}:=(g^{\leq m},h^{\leq m})
. Similarly, define the sequence
g^{>m}
starting from index
m+1
as

g^{>m}:=g-g^{\leq m}=(0,\dots,0,g_{m+1},g_{m+2},\dots)

In an obvious way we also write
g^{\geq m}=g^{>m-1}
.

Next, we define the space

E_{N}=\{e\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}:e=e^{\leq N}\}\equiv\{(e_{0},e_{1},\dots)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}:e_{n}=0\ \textrm{for}\ n>N\}

(17)

which encodes the order-
N
polynomial coefficients, embedded in the space of sequences. This framework enables a more efficient comparison between the polynomial coefficients of the numerical scheme and the power series coefficients of the exact solution. Finally, we define the projection map that allows us to move from the untruncated space
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
to
E_{N}
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
(g,h)\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
. We define the truncation operator
\pi_{m}:(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to E_{m}\times E_{m}=E_{m}^{2}
such that
\pi_{m}(g,h)=(g,h)^{\leq m}
, where
E_{m}
is defined in (
17
).

With this machinery in place, we can now formally define the polynomial approximation scheme.

Definition 3.4

(Polynomial approximation numerical scheme)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{I}=\{s_{0}<s_{1}<\dots<s_{\ell_{x}}\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{J}=\{t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{\ell_{y}}\}
be partitions of the compact intervals
I
and

For fixed

Error bounds for the polynomial approximation scheme

In this subsection we define and bound the local and global truncation error estimate for the polynomial approximation numerical scheme.

The local truncation error quantifies the error introduced in a single approximation step, assuming the initial boundary conditions are exact. More precisely, consider the Goursat PDE on the rectangle
[a,b]\times[c,d]
as in (
5
) and let
(p,q)\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be the coefficients of the power series describing
k(\cdot,c):[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
and
k(a,\cdot):[c,d]\to\mathbb{R}
respectively. The exact solution at
(b,d)
is given by

k(b,d)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{p}_{n}\Delta_{a,b}^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{q}_{n}\Delta_{c,d}^{n}

with
(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})
from Proposition
3.1
. The approximated solution at order
N
,
\hat{k}^{N}(b,d)
, is here defined as

\hat{k}^{N}(b,d):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\hat{p}_{n}\Delta_{a,b}^{n}+\hat{q}_{n}\Delta_{c,d}^{n}\right)

where
(\hat{p},\hat{q})=\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C,\,[a,b]\times[c,d]}(p,q)^{\leq N}
as in Definition
3.4
. The local truncation error (LTE) is then defined as

LTE:=\left|k(b,d)-\hat{k}^{N}(b,d)\right|

(18)

Proposition 3.4

(Local Truncation Error)
.

Consider the Goursat problem (
5
) on the rectangle
[a,b]\times[c,d]
. Let
(p,q)
be the coefficients of the power series defining
k(\cdot,c)
and
k(a,\cdot)
as in Eq (
7
) and let
\|\cdot\|_{\gamma}
be the norm defined in (
10
). Assume that
\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}
for some
0<\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\Delta=\max\{b-a,d-c\}
. Then the local truncation error (LTE), defined in (
18
), can be bounded as follows:

LTE\leq 2\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)I_{0}(2\sqrt{(\gamma+1)|C|}\Delta)\frac{(|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}(\gamma+1)^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}

Proof.

Fix
2\leq N\in\mathbb{N}
and let
(p,q)\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be given. We define the error coefficients:

(e^{1},e^{2}):=(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})-(\hat{p},\hat{q})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}

with
(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})
as defined in Proposition
3.1
, and
(\hat{p},\hat{q})\equiv(\hat{p},\hat{q})^{\leq N}
representing the approximation of
(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})
obtained by applying the polynomial approximation scheme, i.e.
(\hat{p},\hat{q})=\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C,\,[a,b]\times[c,d]}(p,q)^{\leq N}
.
Furthermore, the error terms
(e^{1},e^{2})
can be decomposed as:

(e^{1},e^{2})=(e^{1},e^{2})^{\leq N}+(e^{1},e^{2})^{>N}

with

\displaystyle(e^{1},e^{2})^{\leq N}

\displaystyle:=\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C,\,[a,b]\times[c,d]}(p,q)^{>N}\equiv(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})^{\leq N}-(\hat{p},\hat{q})

(19)

\displaystyle(e^{1},e^{2})^{>N}

\displaystyle:=\Lambda^{>N}_{C,\,[a,b]\times[c,d]}(p,q)\equiv(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})^{>N}

(20)

where
\Lambda^{>N}_{C,\,[a,b]\times[c,d]}=\Lambda_{C,\,[a,b]\times[c,d]}-\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C,\,[a,b]\times[c,d]}
.

In summary,
(e^{1},e^{2})^{\leq N}
accounts for the truncation error arising from truncating the exact initial boundary coefficients
(p,q)
up to order
N
- i.e.
(p,q)^{\leq N}
, when generating the new coefficients of order
\leq N
- i.e.
(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})^{\leq N}
. Instead,
(e^{1},e^{2})^{>N}
captures the error from truncating the new coefficients
(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})
at level
N
.

Based on the definitions of
(e_{1},e_{2})
and local truncation error (Eq. (
18
)), the latter can be expressed as the following series:

LTE\ =\ \left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(e^{1}_{n}\Delta_{a,b}^{n}+e^{2}_{n}\Delta_{c,d}^{n}\right)\right|

To prove the bound on the LTE we will proceed in two steps. First, we calculate the bound for
\|(e^{1},e^{2})^{\leq N}\|_{\gamma+1}
for a fixed
1\leq\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
, then we focus on
\|(e^{1},e^{2})^{>N}\|_{\gamma+1}
.

Step 1
: We aim to bound the norm
\|(e^{1},e^{2})^{\leq N}\|_{\gamma+1}
. For
n\leq N
, observe that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(n!)^{2}e^{1}_{n}}{(\gamma+1)^{n}(C\Delta)^{n}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq n!\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{(k!)^{2}q_{k}\Delta_{c,d}^{k}}{(n+k)!k!(\gamma+1)^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq n!\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{|(k!)^{2}q_{k}(C\Delta)^{-k}\gamma^{-k}|(\gamma|C|)^{k}\Delta^{2k}}{n!(k!)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}\frac{(\gamma|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma|C|)^{k}\Delta^{2k}}{(k!)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}\frac{(\gamma|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)

A similar argument holds for
e^{2}_{n}
. Hence,

\|(e^{1},e^{2})^{\leq N}\|_{\gamma+1}\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}\frac{(\gamma|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)

(21)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(e^{1}_{n}\Delta_{a,b}^{n}+e^{2}_{n}\Delta_{c,d}^{n}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)\frac{(\gamma|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}\frac{(\gamma+1)^{n}(|C|\Delta)^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\Delta^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)\frac{(\gamma|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma+1)^{n}(|C|\Delta)^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\Delta^{n}

\displaystyle=\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)I_{0}(2\sqrt{(\gamma+1)|C|}\Delta)\frac{(\gamma|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}

(22)

Step 2
: Next, consider
(e^{1},e^{2})^{>N}
, recalling that we have already established the relation
(e^{1},e^{2})^{>N}=(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})^{>N}
- Eq. (
20
). From Proposition
3.2
, for all
n\geq 0

\|(\tilde{p},\tilde{q})\|_{\gamma+1}\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)

This implies

\max\{\tilde{p}_{n},\tilde{q}_{n}\}\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)\frac{(\gamma+1)^{n}(|C|\Delta)^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}

Therefore, for
n>N
and
\gamma\geq 1

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\left(e^{1}_{n}\Delta_{a,b}^{n}+e^{2}_{n}\Delta_{c,d}^{n}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)\frac{(\gamma+1)^{n}(|C|\Delta)^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\Delta^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma+1)^{n}(|C|\Delta)^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\Delta^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)\frac{(\gamma+1)^{N+1}(|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma+1)^{n}(|C|\Delta)^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\Delta^{n}

\displaystyle=\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)I_{0}(2\sqrt{(\gamma+1)|C|}\Delta)\frac{(\gamma+1)^{N+1}(|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}

(23)

Combining the results (
22
) and (
23
), by the triangle inequality, we obtain:

LTE\leq 2\|(p,q)\|_{\gamma}I_{0}(2\sqrt{\gamma|C|}\Delta)I_{0}(2\sqrt{(\gamma+1)|C|}\Delta)\frac{(\gamma+1)^{N+1}(|C|\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}

∎

To assess the overall performance of the order-
N
polynomial approximation scheme across the entire grid, we analyze the global truncation error (GTE), which provides a measure of how well this scheme captures the true solution of the signature kernel PDE (
1
). The GTE is defined as:

GTE:=\left|k(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})-\hat{k}^{N}(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})\right|

(24)

where
k(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})
is the signature kernel for two piecewise linear paths
x
and
y
and
\hat{k}^{N}(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})
its polynomial approximation at order
N
obtained applying the scheme introduced in Definition
3.4
.

Theorem 3.2

(Global Truncation Error)
.

Let
\hat{k}^{N}
be a numerical solution obtained by applying the polynomial approximation scheme truncated at order
N
(Definition
3.4
) to the Goursat problem (
1
) on
D_{I}\times D_{J}
, where the paths
x
and
y
are piecewise linear with respect to the grids
\mathcal{D}_{I}
and
\mathcal{D}_{J}
. Recall that
\ell_{x}=|\mathcal{D}_{I}|
,
\ell_{y}=|\mathcal{D}_{J}|
, and assume there exist a constant
K\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{I}\times\mathcal{D}_{J}}\left|\left\langle\dot{x}_{s},\dot{y}_{t}\right\rangle\right|\leq K

Then

|k(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})-\hat{k}^{N}(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})|\leq f(\ell_{x}+\ell_{y}-1)\frac{(K\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}\left[\frac{(\ell_{x}+\ell_{y}-1)^{N+2}}{N+2}+(\ell_{x}+\ell_{y}-1)^{N+1}\right]

where
f
is defined in Eq. (
16
) and
\Delta=\max_{i,j}\{s_{i+1}-s_{i},t_{j+1}-t_{j}\}
.

Proof.
To begin, we present the polynomial approximation scheme using a notation consistent with the one introduced in Definition
3.1
for the exact signature kernel. Let

\hat{\Lambda}_{ij}=(\hat{p}^{i,j+1},\hat{q}^{i+1,j})=\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};[s_{i},s_{i+1}]\times[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}(\hat{p}^{ij},\hat{q}^{ij})

where
(\hat{p}^{ij},\hat{q}^{ij})\equiv(\hat{p}^{ij},\hat{q}^{ij})^{\leq N}
by definition. To simplify notation,
\mathcal{D}_{ij}=[s_{i},s_{i+1}]\times[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
. Furthermore, let
K=\max_{i,j}|C_{ij}|
, as for piecewise linear paths
\sup_{\mathcal{D}_{I}\times\mathcal{D}_{J}}\left|\left\langle\dot{x}_{s},\dot{y}_{t}\right\rangle\right|=\max_{i,j}|C_{ij}|
. The error coefficients are introduced as the differences between the exact and approximated coefficients, and are defined as follows:

\Xi_{ij}=(\mu^{i,j+1},\nu^{i+1,j}):=\Lambda_{ij}-\hat{\Lambda}_{ij}=(p^{i,j+1}-\hat{p}^{i,j+1},q^{i+1,j}-\hat{q}^{i+1,j})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}

Notice that by construction, since
(p^{ij},q^{ij})\in E_{N}\times E_{N}
- where
E_{N}=\{e\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}:e=e^{\leq N}\}
- it follows that
(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{>N}=(p^{ij},q^{ij})^{>N}
. Then by the definition of the global truncation error (Eq. (
24
)), and the polynomial approximation scheme (Definition
3.4
):

\left|k(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})-\hat{k}^{N}(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}})\right|=\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\mu^{l_{x}-1,l_{y}}_{n}(s_{l_{x}}-s_{l_{x}-1})^{n}+\nu^{l_{x},l_{y}-1}_{n}(t_{l_{y}}-t_{l_{y}-1})^{n}\right)\right|

This can be decomposed into two terms using the triangle inequality, leading to the bound
GTE\leq GTE_{1}+GTE_{2}
, where:

GTE_{1}:=\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\mu^{l_{x}-1,l_{y}}_{n}(s_{l_{x}}-s_{l_{x}-1})^{n}+\nu^{l_{x},l_{y}-1}_{n}(t_{l_{y}}-t_{l_{y}-1})^{n}\right)\right|

and

GTE_{2}:=\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\left(\mu^{l_{x}-1,l_{y}}_{n}(s_{l_{x}}-s_{l_{x}-1})^{n}+\nu^{l_{x},l_{y}-1}_{n}(t_{l_{y}}-t_{l_{y}-1})^{n}\right)\right|

Now we are interested in analysing the coefficients
(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
for all
i,j
for which are defined. Notice that for all
i=0,1,\dots,\ell_{x}-1,j=0,1,\dots,\ell_{y}-1
, it holds that

\Xi_{ij}=\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{\leq N}+\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{>N}+\Lambda^{>N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(p^{ij},q^{ij})

(25)

where
\Lambda^{>N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}:=\Lambda_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}-\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}
.

Equation (
25
) means that at each step in the approximation scheme three types of error contribute to
\Xi_{ij}=(\mu^{i,j+1},\nu^{i+1,j})
:

1.

Error propagation from previous steps: this affects coefficients up to order N and is represented as

\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{\leq N}

2.

Error from truncating initial coefficients: this error arises within the current rectangle due to truncation of the exact initial boundary coefficients
(p^{ij},q^{ij})
at level
N
. It affects the terms of
\Xi_{ij}
up to order
N
and is represented by:

\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{>N}\equiv\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(p^{ij},q^{ij})^{>N}

as by construction
(p^{ij},q^{ij})^{>N}\equiv(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{>N}
.

3.

Error from truncating the exact solution beyond order
N
: this captures errors from omitting higher-order terms and is represented as

\Lambda^{>N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(p^{ij},q^{ij})=(p^{i,j+1},q^{i+1,j})^{>N}

Notice that only the first two terms in the RHS of (
25
) contribute to
\Xi_{ij}^{\leq N}
. To better understand the behaviour of these first two terms, first let

\xi:=\frac{(K\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}

where recall that
K=\max_{i,j}|C_{i,j}|
. Then,

\displaystyle\|\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{\leq N}\|_{i+j+1}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{\leq N}\|_{i+j}I_{0}(2\sqrt{(i+j)K}\Delta)

\displaystyle\|\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(p^{ij},q^{ij})^{>N}\|_{i+j+1}

\displaystyle\leq\|(p^{ij},q^{ij})\|_{i+j}\ \xi I_{0}(2\sqrt{(i+j)K}\Delta)\ (i+j)^{N+1}

where the first line follows from Proposition
3.2
, which also applies to the coefficients of the approximation scheme since
\|\Lambda^{\leq N}_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}\|_{op}\leq\|\Lambda_{C_{ij};\mathcal{D}_{ij}}\|_{op}
, and the second line follows from the proof of the local truncation error - Eqs. (
19
) and (
21
).
By the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\|\Xi_{ij}^{\leq N}\|_{i+j+1}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{\leq N}\|_{i+j}I_{0}(2\sqrt{(i+j)K}\Delta)+\|(p^{ij},q^{ij})\|_{i+j}\ \xi I_{0}(2\sqrt{(i+j)K}\Delta)\ (i+j)^{N+1}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mu^{ij},\nu^{ij})^{\leq N}\|_{i+j}I_{0}(2\sqrt{(i+j)K}\Delta)+\xi f(i+j)\ (i+j)^{N+1}

with
f
defined in (
16
) and where we have used the result
\|(p^{ij},q^{ij})\|_{i+j}\leq f(i+j-1)
from Proposition
3.3
. Moreover, a straightforward computation shows that
\|\Xi_{00}^{<N}\|_{1}=0
.

Using a similar induction to the one in the proof of Proposition
3.3
, we get

\|\Xi_{ij}^{\leq N}\|_{i+j+1}\leq\xi f(i+j)\sum_{m=0}^{i+j}m^{N+1}\leq\xi f(i+j)\ \frac{(i+j+1)^{N+2}-1}{N+2}

This leads to the following bound:

\displaystyle GTE_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\mu^{l_{x}-1,l_{y}}_{n}\Delta^{n}+\nu^{l_{x},l_{y}-1}_{n}\Delta^{n}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq f(l_{x}+l_{y}-2)\xi\ \frac{(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)^{N+2}-1}{N+2}{}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)^{n}(K\Delta)^{n}\Delta^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq f(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)\frac{(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)^{N+2}(K\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{(N+2)[(N+1)!]^{2}}

On the other hand, we have established that
\Xi_{ij}^{>N}=\Lambda_{ij}^{>N}=(p^{ij},q^{ij})^{>N}
. From Proposition
3.3
, we have

\displaystyle\|\Xi_{ij}^{>N}\|_{i+j+1}

\displaystyle=\|\Lambda_{ij}^{>N}\|_{i+j+1}\leq\|\Lambda_{ij}\|_{i+j+1}\leq f(i+j)

Thus, for
i=l_{x}-1
and
j=l_{y}-1

\displaystyle GTE_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\left(\mu^{l_{x}-1,l_{y}}_{n}\Delta^{n}+\nu^{l_{x},l_{y}-1}_{n}\Delta^{n}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq f(l_{x}+l_{y}-2)\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)^{n}(K\Delta)^{n}\Delta^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq f(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)\frac{(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)^{N+1}(K\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}

Then, as
GTE\leq GTE_{1}+GTE_{2}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle GTE\leq f(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)\frac{(K\Delta^{2})^{N+1}}{[(N+1)!]^{2}}\left[\frac{(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)^{N+2}}{N+2}+(l_{x}+l_{y}-1)^{N+1}\right]

∎

Theorem
3.2
provides the convergence result for our numerical scheme. For a fixed constant
K\in\mathbb{R}
, taking the limit as
N\to\infty
yields convergence to the true solution. In Section
4
, we empirically demonstrate that increasing the hyperparameter
N
in the polynomial approximation scheme leads to faster convergence compared to increasing the dyadic order (Definition
2.1
) in the finite differences scheme. Moreover, the finite differences scheme and the polynomial approximation scheme are not mutually exclusive; they can be combined to produce a hybrid method by introducing a refined grid within the polynomial approximation scheme.

3.3
Polynomial interpolation numerical scheme

In Proposition
3.1
, we derived an analytical representation of the solution to the Goursat problem on a rectangular domain - Eq. (
5
), which involved expressing the initial boundary conditions as power series. By truncating these series, we constructed the polynomial approximation scheme - Definition
3.4
, which outperforms finite differences methods.

Alternatively, when the initial boundary conditions are polynomials, the exact solution on the opposite boundaries can be expressed in terms of special functions, specifically the confluent hypergeometric limit function
[
And+99
]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Consider the Goursat problem on a rectangle
[a,b]\times[c,d]
as in (
5
). Further, assume that the initial boundary conditions
k(\cdot,c):[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
and
k(a,\cdot):[c,d]\to\mathbb{R}
are polynomials at
s=a
and
t=c
respectively, such that

k(s,c)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}g_{n}(s-a)^{n}\qquad k(a,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}h_{n}(t-c)^{n}

with the constraint
g_{0}=h_{0}
. Then the solution
k(s,t)
can be written as

k(s,t)=g_{0}I_{0}(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)})+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(g_{n}(s-a)^{n}+h_{n}(t-c)^{n}\right){}_{0}F_{1}(n+1;\,C(s-a)(t-c))

(26)

with
s\in[a,b]
,
t\in[c,d]
. Here,
{}_{0}F_{1}
is the confluent hypergeometric limit function
[
And+99
]
defined by:

{}_{0}F_{1}(k;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{(k)_{n}n!}

where
(k)_{n}
is the Pochhammer symbol, which represents the rising factorial.

Remark 3.7
.

From the definition of the confluent hypergeometric limit function and the modified Bessel function (Remark
3.1
), we observe that for
z\geq 0
, the identity
{}_{0}F_{1}(1,z)=I_{0}(2\sqrt{z})
holds.

Remark 3.8
.

See Chapter 3 of the Ph.D Thesis of the third-name author
[
Pei24
]
.

The proof of Proposition
3.5
can be found in Appendix
A
.

Moreover, note that for bounded coefficients
(g_{n},h_{n})_{n=0,\dots,N}
, the function described by (
26
) is infinitely differentiable. To streamline the forthcoming analysis, we introduce specific notation to explicitly represent the map
(k(\cdot,c),\,k(a,\cdot))\mapsto(k(\cdot,d),\,k(b,\cdot))
, both in the case where the inputs are polynomials (as outlined in Proposition
3.5
) and when they are differentiable functions, as described in Theorem
3.1
.

Definition 3.5
.

Consider the Goursat problem on a general rectangular domain
[a,b]\times[c,d]
with associated constant
C\in\mathbb{R}/\{0\}
as in Eq. (
5
). Let
\sigma:=k(\cdot,c)\in C^{1}([a,b],\mathbb{R})
and
\tau:=k(a,\cdot)\in C^{1}([c,d],\mathbb{R})
denote the boundary conditions at the bottom and left edges of the rectangle. Let
\sigma_{new}:=k(\cdot,d)\in C^{1}([a,b],\mathbb{R})
and
\tau_{new}:=k(b,\cdot)\in C^{1}([c,d],\mathbb{R})
represent the corresponding solutions at the top and right edges, respectively.

We define the operator
\Phi_{C,[a,b]\times[c,d]}:C^{1}([a,b],\mathbb{R})\times C^{1}([c,d],\mathbb{R})\to C^{1}([a,b],\mathbb{R})\times C^{1}([c,d],\mathbb{R})
such that:

(\sigma_{new},\tau_{new})=\Phi_{C,[a,b]\times[c,d]}(\sigma,\tau)

Similarly we define the restriction of
\Phi_{C;[a,b]\times[c,d]}
to the space of polynomials of degree at most
N
with real coefficients, denoted as
P_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, as

\Phi^{N}_{C;[a,b]\times[c,d]}:P_{N}(\mathbb{R})\times P_{N}(\mathbb{R})\to C^{\infty}([a,b],\mathbb{R})\times C^{\infty}([c,d],\mathbb{R})

which is constructed by applying Proposition
3.5
to the polynomial initial data.

This construction and notation can be naturally extended to any rectangle
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]\times[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
in a given grid, with an associated constant
C_{ij}
, to solve the Goursat PDE on a partitioned domain.

Remark 3.9
.

When the initial boundary conditions
\sigma
and
\tau
can be expressed in the form of power series, the map
\Phi_{C;[a,b]\times[c,d]}
is similar to the map
\Lambda_{C;[a,b]\times[c,d]}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}\to(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. The key distinction lies in their domains and outputs: while
\Phi
and
\Phi^{N}
operates on the input functions directly,
\Lambda
acts on the coefficients of their power series and outputs the coefficients of the power series describing the solution.

This setup allows us to define an alternative numerical scheme based on polynomial interpolation.
More precisely, on each rectangle, the polynomial interpolation scheme operates by first constructing the interpolating polynomial of the initial boundaries, and then by using Proposition
3.5
to evaluate the solution on the opposite boundaries.

To construct the interpolating polynomial, we use the
N
-Chebyshev nodes of the second kind, also called
Chebyshev extrema

[
Tre19
]
, which, when scaled to a compact interval
[u,v]
, are defined as

\omega_{m;[u,v]}=\frac{v-u}{2}\cos(\pi m/N)+\frac{u+v}{2},\qquad m=0,1,\dots,N-1

(27)

This set of points minimizes the problem of Runge’s phenomenon
[
Run+01
]
, which is the large oscillation that can occur at the edges of an interval when using polynomial interpolation. Chebyshev nodes thus provide a near-optimal distribution, reducing interpolation error and enhancing the stability of the polynomial approximation. For convenience, we define a general interpolation operator based on the Chebyshev extrema.

Definition 3.6
.

Define
\Omega^{N}_{[a,b]}:C([a,b],\mathbb{R})\to P_{N}(\mathbb{R})
as a general interpolation operator that, given a continuous function
f\in C([a,b],\mathbb{R})
, evaluates
f
at the
N+1
Chebyshev extrema rescaled to the interval
[a,b]
, i.e.
\{\omega_{m,[a,b]}\}_{m=0,\dots,N}
as defined in (
27
), and returns the unique polynomial of degree at most
N
that interpolates these points.

Building on these definitions and Proposition
3.5
we define the polynomial interpolation scheme for the signature kernel PDE (
1
).

Definition 3.7

(Polynomial interpolation numerical scheme)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{I}=\{s_{0}<s_{1}<\dots<s_{\ell_{x}}\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{J}=\{t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{\ell_{y}}\}
be partitions of the compact intervals
I
and
J
and let
x:I\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y:J\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two piecewise linear functions on
\mathcal{D}_{I}
and
\mathcal{D}_{J}
respectively.
Here, let
\hat{k}^{N}
be the solution of the order-
N
polynomial interpolation scheme to the Goursat PDE (
1
) on the grid
\mathcal{D}_{I}\times\mathcal{D}_{J}
.

On each rectangular subdomain
\mathcal{D}_{ij}=[s_{i},s_{i+1}]\times[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
, for a fixed
N\geq 2
, we define:

\hat{\sigma}_{ij}:=\hat{k}^{N}(\cdot,t_{j}):[s_{i},s_{i+1}]\to\mathbb{R}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\hat{\tau}_{ij}:=\hat{k}^{N}(s_{i},\cdot):[t_{j},t_{j+1}]\to\mathbb{R}

Furthermore, let

C_{ij}=\frac{\langle x_{s_{i+1}}-x_{s_{i}},\ y_{t_{j+1}}-y_{t_{j}}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}{(s_{i+1}-s_{i})(t_{j+1}-t_{j})}

Starting from the initial conditions
\hat{\sigma}_{i,0}=1
and
\hat{\tau}_{0,j}=1
, we proceed recursively as follows: for
i\in\{0,\dots,\ell_{x}-1\}
,
j\in\{0,\dots,\ell_{y}-1\}

\displaystyle(\hat{\sigma}^{ch}_{ij},\hat{\tau}^{ch}_{ij})

\displaystyle=(\Omega^{N}_{[s_{i},s_{i+1}]}(\hat{\sigma}_{i,j}),\ \Omega^{N}_{[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}(\hat{\tau}_{i,j}))

\displaystyle(\hat{\sigma}_{i,j+1},\hat{\tau}_{i+1,j})

\displaystyle=\Phi^{N}_{C_{ij};[s_{i},s_{i+1}]\times[t_{j},t_{j+1}]}(\hat{\sigma}^{ch}_{ij},\hat{\tau}^{ch}_{ij})

where
\Phi^{N}
is introduced in Definition
3.5
, and
\Omega^{N}
is the interpolation operator introduced in Definition
3.6
. Consequently,
\hat{\sigma}^{ch}_{ij},\hat{\tau}^{ch}_{ij}\in P_{N}(\mathbb{R})
are the polynomial interpolants of
\hat{\sigma}_{ij}
and
\hat{\tau}_{ij}
, respectively.
The endpoint of our scheme is then defined as:

k(s_{\ell_{x}},t_{\ell_{y}}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{\ell_{x}-1,\ell_{y}}(s_{\ell_{x}})+\hat{\tau}_{\ell_{x},\ell_{y}-1}(t_{\ell_{y}})\right)

Computationally, the polynomial interpolants
\hat{\sigma}^{ch}_{ij},\hat{\tau}^{ch}_{ij}\in P_{N}(\mathbb{R})
are stored by their coefficients, while the evaluation of
\hat{\sigma}_{ij}\in C^{\infty}([s_{i},s_{i+1}],\mathbb{R})
and
\hat{\tau}_{ij}\in C^{\infty}([t_{j},t_{j+1}],\mathbb{R})
is required only at the scaled Chebyshev extrema for interpolation on each respective interval.

Experiments

In this section, we present the results of our numerical schemes and compare them to the finite differences scheme proposed by

]
and implemented in the

Python library
[

]
- the JAX version of the original Pytorch-based

Remark 4.1
.

The finite difference scheme implemented in the
sigkerax
package
[
Sal23
]
introduces a modification from the original scheme (Definition
2.1
) by replacing the dyadic refinement hyperparameter with a more general refinement factor. Specifically, while the dyadic hyperparameter
\lambda
increases the grid size by a factor of
2^{2\lambda}
, in the modified algorithm, the refinement factor
\gamma
interpolates each time series by adding a number of points to the grid proportional to
\gamma^{2}
.

Additionally, the finite differences implementation in the
sigkerax
package employs the second-order explicit finite differences scheme detailed in Eq. (
4
).

Python library

The code for this paper generated a Python library named Polysigkerax, which provides all the necessary tools to utilize the signature kernel. The code is freely available for download at the link
https://github.com/FrancescoPiatti/polysigkernel
. The implementation is written in JAX, enabling multi-threading and multi-processing for large batches. Similarly to the original library, additional features to perform statistical tests and compute mean maximum discrepancy have also been incorporated.

All the experiments have been run using a MacBook Air 2022 with M2 chip (for CPU) and a NVIDIA GTX 3090 (for GPU).

Error analysis and computational performance analysis

We first compare the numerical error of our schemes against the benchmark, which is the truncated signature kernel at level 21. The factorial decay of the signature ensures machine-level precision at this truncation, offering a highly accurate benchmark. However, due to the curse of dimensionality of the signature transform, this benchmark can only be computed for low-dimensional time series, hence our analysis focuses on 2-
d
paths. Since the dimensionality of the paths enters the algorithms only through the (weighted) dot product of the derivatives, we do not expect higher dimensions to significantly impact the performance of our scheme.

Figure
3
illustrates the decrease in the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) for both the polynomial approximation and interpolation schemes as the polynomial order increases, compared to the finite differences scheme against growing refinement factors. For shorter time series, our algorithm easily achieves machine precision (approximately
5\times 10^{-16}
), making it
\approx 10^{8}
times more accurate than the finite differences scheme, even at high refinement levels. For longer time series, our algorithm maintains robust performance, with the slightly larger errors likely due to the numerical inaccuracies of the benchmark given the extensive computations involved. Tables
1
,
2
and
3
in Appendix
B
provide the numerical values for this experiment. Additionally, Appendix
B
includes results from a complementary experiment conducted on stochastic paths other than Brownian motions.

Figure 3
:
Comparison of MAPE against polynomial order for the polynomial approximation and interpolation schemes is shown on the

and

batch of 2-

As mentioned in Section
3.2
, the computational performance of the polynomial approximation scheme is comparable to that of the finite differences scheme. In contrast, the complexity of the polynomial interpolation scheme depends heavily on the choice of the interpolation function
\Omega^{N}
(Definition
3.6
). In our experiments, we use
jax.numpy.polyfit

[
Bra+18
]
, which computes the monomial basis coefficients via a least-squares problem. This choice, combined with our implementation of the hypergeometric function
{}_{0}F_{1}
, introduces additional computational complexity as the interpolation order increases, rendering it slower compared to the other two schemes. While more efficient implementations of
\Omega
and
{}_{0}F_{1}
could mitigate this issue, the computational time analysis presented below is focused exclusively on the polynomial approximation scheme.

The key result of our experiments is that the polynomial approximation scheme achieves significantly higher accuracy while being more computationally efficient than the finite differences scheme. Figure
4
shows the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) against computational time for both schemes: the polynomial approximation method with increasing polynomial orders and the finite differences method with increasing refinement factors.

To further investigate the complexities of the two algorithms, we distinguish between theoretical and empirical complexities. Theoretically, the polynomial approximation scheme has a complexity of
O(d\ell_{x}\ell_{y}N^{2})
, where
\ell_{x}
and
\ell_{y}
are the lengths of the two time series, while the finite differences scheme has complexity
O(d\ell_{x}\ell_{y}\gamma^{2})
. Both schemes exhibit quadratic complexity with respect to their hyperparameters,
N
and
\gamma
. However, these theoretical estimates are asymptotic and do not always reflect practical performance, especially when the hyperparameters are relatively small. Empirically, our experiments reveal that for smaller datasets, the polynomial approximation scheme exhibits near-linear complexity in
N
, giving our algorithm a significant performance edge over the finite differences scheme on the CPU. This is illustrated in Figure
7
and further discussed in Appendix
B
, where a more detailed analysis of the computational time for our scheme is also presented.

Furthermore, in the context of GPU acceleration, enabling the parallelization described in Section
3.2
and Figure
2
breaks the quadratic complexity in relation to the time series lengths, reducing it to linear complexity (see Appendix
B
).

Appendix A
Proofs

In this section, we provide the proofs for the key results presented in this paper, specifically Proposition
3.1
and
3.5
, where we derive exact solutions to the Goursat problem on a rectangle for piecewise linear time series.

We begin by stating and proving Proposition
A.1
, which serves as a foundational result.

Proposition A.1
.

Consider the Goursat problem (
5
) on the rectangular domain
\mathcal{D}_{R}=\left\{(s,t)\mid a\leq s\leq b,c\leq t\leq d\right\}
. Assume that
\sigma
and
\tau
are (real) analytic functions at
s=a
,
t=c
respectively and are determined by their power series

\sigma(s)=k(s,c)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}p_{n}(s-a)^{n}\qquad\tau(t)=k(a,t)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}}q_{n}(t-c)^{n}

(28)

with the constrain
\sigma(a)=\tau(c)\implies p_{0}=q_{0}
, as
\sigma(a)=\tau(c)=k(a,c)
. Furthermore, assume that these power series converge uniformly on
[a,b]
and
[c,d]
respectively. Then

\displaystyle k(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m}(s-a)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q_{n}\frac{[C(s-a)]^{m}(t-c)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}

(29)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle k(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m}(s-a)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q_{n}\frac{[C(s-a)]^{m}(t-c)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}

(30)

Proof.

The proof is organised in two steps. In the first step we demonstrate that

k(s,t)=-\sigma(a)I_{0}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\right)+\sigma(s)+\tau(t)+Q_{1}(s,t)+Q_{2}(s,t)

(31)

where

\displaystyle Q_{1}(s,t)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sigma((s-a)\cos^{2}\theta+a)I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\sin\theta\right)\cos\theta d\theta

\displaystyle Q_{2}(s,t)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\tau((t-c)\cos^{2}\theta+c)I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\sin\theta\right)\cos\theta d\theta

In the second step we substitute the power series (
28
) for the initial boundaries into Eq. (
31
), recovering (
29
) and (
30
).

Step 1
: From Theorem
3.1
, for
s\in[a,b]
,
t\in[c,d]
, we have

\displaystyle k(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma(a)I_{0}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\right)+\int_{a}^{s}\sigma^{\prime}(r)I_{0}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-r)(t-c)}\right)dr+\int_{c}^{t}\tau^{\prime}(r)I_{0}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-r)}\right)dr

where
\sigma(\cdot)=k(\cdot,c)
and
\tau(\cdot)=k(a,\cdot)
. Integrating by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle k(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma(a)I_{0}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\right)+\sigma(s)I_{0}(0)+\tau(t)I_{0}(0)

\displaystyle-\sigma(a)I_{0}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\right)-\tau(c)I_{0}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\right)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{C(t-c)}\int_{a}^{s}\frac{\sigma(r)}{\sqrt{s-r}}I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-r)(t-c)}\right)dr+\sqrt{C(s-a)}\int_{c}^{t}\frac{\tau(r)}{\sqrt{t-r}}I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-r)}\right)dr

Given that
\tau(c)=\sigma(a)
and recalling that
I_{0}(0)=1
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle k(s,t)

\displaystyle=-\sigma(a)I_{0}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\right)+\sigma(s)+\tau(t)

(32)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{C(t-c)}\int_{a}^{s}\frac{\sigma(r)}{\sqrt{s-r}}I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-r)(t-c)}\right)dr+\sqrt{C(s-a)}\int_{c}^{t}\frac{\tau(r)}{\sqrt{t-r}}I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-r)}\right)dr

Now define:

Q_{1}(s,t):=\sqrt{C(t-c)}\int_{a}^{s}\frac{\sigma(r)}{\sqrt{s-r}}I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-r)(t-c)}\right)dr

Q_{2}(s,t):=\sqrt{C(s-a)}\int_{c}^{t}\frac{\tau(r)}{\sqrt{t-r}}I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-r)}\right)dr

Using the change of variables:

\displaystyle\sqrt{s-a}\sin\theta=\sqrt{s-r}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\sqrt{s-a}\cos\theta=\sqrt{r-a}

we obtain

Q_{1}(s,t)=2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sigma((s-a)\cos^{2}\theta+a)I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\sin\theta\right)\cos\theta d\theta

A similar change of variables for
Q_{2}(s)
yields:

Q_{2}(s,t)=2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\tau((t-c)\cos^{2}\theta+c)I_{1}\left(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}\sin\theta\right)\cos\theta d\theta

Step 2:
Substituting the power series expansion (
28
) for
\sigma
into
Q_{1}(s)
and expanding the Bessel function, we obtain

\displaystyle Q_{1}(s,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2p_{n}\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)}(s-a)^{n}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cos^{2n+1}\theta\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{[C(s-a)(t-c)]^{m+\frac{1}{2}}}{m!(m+1)!}\sin^{2m+1}\theta d\theta

The interchange of the summation over
n
and the integral is justified by the uniform convergence of the partial sums of (
28
) on the interval
[a,b]
. Similarly, we exchange the order of the summation over
m
and the integral, yielding:

\displaystyle Q_{1}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2p_{n}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m+1}(s-a)^{n+m+1}}{m!(m+1)!}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos^{2n+1}\theta\sin^{2m+1}\theta d\theta

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m+1}(s-a)^{n+m+1}}{(m+1)!}\frac{n!}{(n+m+1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}p_{n}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m}(s-a)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}

where in the second line we applied the following identity

\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos^{2n+1}\theta\sin^{2m+1}\theta=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Beta}(n+1,m+1)=\frac{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(m+1)}{2\Gamma(n+m+2)}

Similarly for
Q_{2}
we obtain:

\displaystyle Q_{2}(s,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}q_{n}\frac{[C(s-a)]^{m}(t-c)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}

Combining these results and expanding the remaining terms in Eq. (
31
), we have

\displaystyle k(s,t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p_{0}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m}(s-a)^{m}}{(m!)^{2}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}(s-a)^{n}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}p_{n}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m}(s-a)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q_{n}(t-c)^{n}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}q_{n}\frac{[C(s-a)]^{m}(t-c)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m}(s-a)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q_{n}\frac{[C(s-a)]^{m}(t-c)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}

Given that
k(a,c)=\sigma(a)=\tau(c)
, which implies
p_{0}=q_{0}
, Eq. (
29
) can also be written as

k(s,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p_{n}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m}(s-a)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q_{n}\frac{[C(s-a)]^{m}(t-c)^{n+m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}

∎

Proposition
A.1
establishes the foundational framework necessary to prove both Proposition
3.1
(also included here in the appendix as Proposition
A.2
) and Proposition
3.5
(included here as Proposition
A.3
).

Proposition A.2
.

Consider the Goursat PDE on the rectangle
[a,b]\times[c,d]
as in (
5
), with initial boundaries condition given by (
7
), then
\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
the coefficients of (
8
) are given by

\tilde{p}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}\frac{(C\Delta_{c,d})^{n-k}\ k!}{(n-k)!\ n!}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{k}\frac{C^{n}\Delta_{c,d}^{n+k}\ k!}{(n+k)!\ n!}

(33)

and, symmetrically

\tilde{q}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q_{k}\frac{(C\Delta_{a,b})^{n-k}\ k!}{(n-k)!\ n!}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\frac{C^{n}\Delta_{a,b}^{n+k}\ k!}{(n+k)!\ n!}

(34)

where
\Delta_{a,b}=(b-a)
and
\Delta_{c,d}=(d-c)
.

Proof.

The proof follows directly. To establish (
33
), we set
t=d
in (
30
) and then rearrange the sums in terms of
(s-a)
. Similarly, by substituting
s=c
into (
29
), we can prove (
34
). ∎

Proposition A.3
.

Consider the Goursat problem on a rectangle
[a,b]\times[c,d]
as in (
5
). Further, assume that the initial boundary conditions
k(\cdot,c):[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
and
k(a,\cdot):[c,d]\to\mathbb{R}
are polynomials at
s=a
and
t=c
respectively, such that

k(s,c)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}g_{n}(s-a)^{n}\qquad k(a,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}h_{n}(t-c)^{n}

(35)

with the constraint
g_{0}=h_{0}
. Then the solution
k(s,t)
can be written as

k(s,t)=g_{0}I_{0}(2\sqrt{C(s-a)(t-c)})+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(g_{n}(s-a)^{n}+h_{n}(t-c)^{n}\right){}_{0}F_{1}(n+1;\,C(s-a)(t-c))

(36)

with
s\in[a,b]
,
t\in[c,d]
. Here,
{}_{0}F_{1}
is the confluent hypergeometric limit function
[
And+99
]
defined by:

{}_{0}F_{1}(k;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{(k)_{n}n!}

(37)

where
(k)_{n}
is the Pochhammer symbol, which represents the rising factorial.

Proof.

Again the proof is straightforward. According to assumption (
35
), we focus on the truncation at level
n=N
of the power series in (
30
), which rearranged yields:

\displaystyle k(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_{n}(s-a)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m}(s-a)^{m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}q_{n}(t-c)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{[C(s-a)]^{m}(t-c)^{m}}{m!}\frac{n!}{(n+m)!}

Using Pochhammer notation, this simplifies to:

\displaystyle k(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_{n}(s-a)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{[C(t-c)]^{m}(s-a)^{m}}{m!(n+1)_{m}}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}q_{n}(t-c)^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{[C(s-a)]^{m}(t-c)^{m}}{m!(n+1)_{m}}

(38)

From the definition of the confluent hypergeometric function
{}_{0}F_{1}
- Eq. (
37
), we recover equation (
36
). ∎
