A note on spectral Monte-Carlo method for fractional Poisson equation on high-dimensional ball Journal: Elsevier
Lisen Ding, Mingyi Wang, Dongling Wang

Abstract
Recently, a class of efficient spectral Monte Carlo methods was developed in FSX 25 for solving fractional Poisson equations. These methods fully consider the low regularity of the solution near boundaries and leverage the efficiency of walk-on-spheres algorithms, achieving spectral accuracy. However, the underlying formulation is essentially one-dimensional. In this work, we extend this approach to radial solutions in general high-dimensional balls. This is accomplished by employing a different set of eigenfunctions for the fractional Laplacian and deriving new interpolation formulas. We provide a comprehensive description of our methodology and a detailed comparison with existing techniques. Numerical experiments confirm the efficacy of the proposed extension.



1
Introduction

We consider the fractional Poisson equation with exterior Dirichlet boundary conditions:

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}u(x)=f(x),&x\in\Omega,\\
u(x)=g(x),&x\in\Omega^{c},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain and
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a given function. The integral fractional Laplacian for
s\in(0,2)
is defined as:

(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}u(x)=C_{n,s},\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+s}},\mathrm{d}y,\quad C_{n,s}=\frac{2^{s}\Gamma\left(\frac{n+s}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\Gamma\left(1-\frac{s}{2}\right)},

(2)

where P.V. denotes the Cauchy principal value. This model has garnered significant attention for its theoretical and practical relevance
FRRO 24
,
DDG+ 20
,
BBN+ 18
.
A key feature of this operator is the singularity of its kernel, which induces low solution regularity near the boundary regardless of the smoothness of
f
or
\partial\Omega
. This inherent singularity, combined with the nonlocal nature of the operator, poses fundamental challenges for numerical computation, particularly in high dimensions.

Conventional numerical methods, such as finite difference
HO 14
,
HZD 21
,
MY 20
,
DZ 19
,
HS 24
,
HW 22
and finite element
ABBM 18
,
BBN+ 18
,
BFM 24
methods, often lead to complex discretization and dense matrices. The standard spectral method
HLZZ 21
,
XD 18
also faces significant challenges in high-dimensional settings.

To address these issues, a walk-on-spheres (WOS) algorithm was introduced in
KOS 17
for two-dimensional problems. This method leverages a probabilistic representation of the solution, which is then computed via Monte-Carlo simulation. Its key advantage lies in handling complex geometries effectively. This approach has since been extended to higher dimensions (
n\geq 3
) and more complex semilinear models
SSX 23
,
SSX 24
,
JLWZ 23
,
YH 25
,
NSD 25
. However, the Monte Carlo nature of WOS typically limits its convergence rate to half-order accuracy.

A recent breakthrough in
FSX 25
achieved spectral accuracy by constructing sophisticated interpolation operators that exploit the precise Hölder regularity of the solution near the boundary. Nevertheless, this method is fundamentally one-dimensional in its construction (see Section
3.2
). In this article, we generalize this high-accuracy approach to compute radial solutions in high-dimensional spherical domains.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
discusses radial solutions for problem (
1
) and outlines the relevant stochastic algorithms. Section
3.1
provides necessary background on Jacobi polynomials. The one-dimensional interpolation formula from
FSX 25
and our new interpolation formula for radial solutions on the unit ball are presented in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
, respectively. A comparative analysis and discussion of these two approaches are given in Section
3.4
. Numerical experiments in Section
4
demonstrate the effectiveness of our method. Finally, concluding remarks are offered in Section
5
.

Preliminaries

In what follows, we define
B_{r}^{x_{0}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x-x_{0}|\leq r\}
for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
|\cdot|
denotes the standard Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

2.1
Radial solutions

Radial solutions serve as an important tool in the study of partial differential equations (PDEs), often enabling explicit expressions of solutions. For equations with complex structure, radial solutions offer a simplified approach, and their analysis can lead to deeper insights into the behavior of such equations. The fractional Laplacian (
2
), defined as a hypersingular integral operator, has been extensively studied in the context of radial solutions; see, for example,
FW 14
,
CWNH 21
,
FLS 16
,
Dyd 12
. In what follows, we discuss some key aspects of radial solutions related to the integral fractional Laplacian.

It is well-known that the fractional Laplacian (
2
) reduces to the classical negative Laplacian
-\Delta
when
s=2
. As noted in
Eva 22
, the rotational invariance of
-\Delta
implies that the equation
-\Delta u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
admits radial solutions. Specifically, if
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an orthogonal matrix and
v(x)=u(Rx)
, then
-\Delta v=0
.
This property extends to the fractional operator
(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}
, which is also rotationally invariant due to the dependence of its kernel
|x-y|^{-(n+s)}
solely on the distance between points. To verify this, suppose
(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}u=0
and define
v(x)=u(Rx)
. Then,

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}v(x)

\displaystyle=C_{n,s}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{v(x)-v(y)}{|x-y|^{n+s}}\mathrm{d}y=C_{n,s}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{u(Rx)-u(Ry)}{|x-y|^{n+s}}\mathrm{d}y.

Making the change of variables
z=Ry
and using the orthogonality of
R
(which gives
|x-y|=|Rx-z|
and
\mathrm{d}y=\mathrm{d}z
), we obtain

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}v(x)

\displaystyle=C_{n,s}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(Rx)}\frac{u(Rx)-u(z)}{|Rx-z|^{n+s}}\mathrm{d}z=(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}u(Rx)=0.

Hence,
(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}v=0
, confirming that the equation is rotationally invariant and therefore admits radial solutions. Explicit forms of such radial solutions can be found in
CLM 20
.

However, for a bounded domain
\Omega
with a non-trivial source term
f\neq 0
, the existence of a radial solution to problem (
1
) requires both
\Omega
and
f
to satisfy specific symmetry conditions. A common framework, widely adopted in the literature and in this work, is to impose the homogeneous Dirichlet condition
g=0
.
Leveraging the rotational invariance of
(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}
, if
\Omega
is a ball (e.g., the unit ball
B_{1}^{0}
) or other spherical domain and
f
is a radial function, then the solution to (
1
) is also radial
FW 14
,
Dyd 12
. Since this article is restricted to a ball, it suffices to assume that
f
is radial to guarantee a radial solution.

2.2
Stochastic algorithm for fractional Possion problem

For spherical domains
\Omega=B_{r}^{0}
, the solution to problem (
1
) admits the representation

u(x)=\int_{B_{r}^{0}}f(y)Q_{r}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}^{0}}g(z)P_{r}(x,z)\mathrm{d}z,\quad x\in B_{r}^{0},

(3)

where
Q_{r}
is the fractional Green’s function and
P_{r}
is the fractional Poisson kernel. As shown in
SSX 23
, this representation can be reformulated in terms of expectations:

u(x)=\zeta(x)\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{Q}r}[f(Y)]+\mathbb{E}_{P_{r}}[g(Z)],\quad Y\in B_{r}^{0},\ Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}^{0},

(4)

where the weight function
\zeta(x)=\int{B_{r}^{0}}Q_{r}(x,y)\mathrm{d}y
for
x\in B_{r}^{0}
, and
\widetilde{Q}r(x,y)=Q_{r}(x,y)/\zeta(x)
. Combining this expectation formula with the walk-on-spheres algorithm, the solution on a general domain can be expressed as a conditional expectation. The corresponding Monte-Carlo discretization yields the numerical scheme:

u{M}(x)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}S^{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\zeta(X_{k}^{i})f(Y_{k+1}^{i})+g(X_{L}^{i})\right],

(5)

where
S^{i}
denotes the
i
-th sample path in a total of
M
Monte Carlo trials. For each sample, the walk-on-spheres algorithm generates a sequence of balls
\big\{B^{X_{k}}_{r_{k+1}}\big\}_{k=0}^{L-1}
, with radii
r_{k+1}=\mathrm{dist}(X_{k},\partial\Omega)
. Here,
X_{k}
represents the position of the Lévy process
X_{\tau}^{s}
at time
\tau_{k}
, and
Y_{k+1}
is a point sampled inside the ball
B^{X_{k}}_{r{k+1}}
. The algorithm terminates after a finite number of steps
L
, ensuring its practical feasibility.

A variant of this scheme is presented in
JLWZ 23
, which differs from (
5
) in the choice of the weight function and the treatment of the source term. Defining

\tilde{f}(Y_{k+1})=\left[1-B\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{r_{k}^{2}};\frac{n-s}{2},1-\frac{s}{2}\right)\right]f(Y_{k+1}),\quad\eta(X_{k})=\frac{B\left(\frac{n-s}{2},\frac{s}{2}\right)}{2^{s-1}\Gamma^{2}(s/2)}r_{k+1}^{s},

where
B(a,b)
is the beta function and
B(\cdot;a,b)
is the incomplete beta function with
B(1;a,b)=1
, the numerical formula in
JLWZ 23
is obtained by replacing
\zeta(X_{k})
and
f(Y_{k+1})
in (
5
) with
\eta(X_{k})
and
\tilde{f}(Y_{k+1})
, respectively. A key advantage of this modified scheme is that the weight
\eta(X_{k})
is given in closed form, eliminating the need for numerical integration. For this reason, we adopt the formulation from
JLWZ 23
in the present work. Further details on the stochastic algorithms for (
1
) can be found in
SSX 23
,
JLWZ 23
.

3
Spectral Monte Carlo Method

In this section, we always employ
t\in\mathbb{R}
as one-dimensional variables, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as high-dimensional variables.
We recall some basic notation and properties of Jacobi polynomials
STW 11
.

3.1
Jacobi Polynomials

The Jacobi polynomials
\left\{P_{m}^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)\right\}
with
\alpha,\beta>-1
are mutually orthogonal with respect to the weight function
\omega^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)=(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}
:

\int_{-1}^{1}\omega^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)P_{m}^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)P_{q}^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t=\gamma_{m}^{\alpha,\,\beta}\delta_{mq},

(6)

where
\delta_{mq}
denotes the Kronecker function, and

\gamma_{m}^{\alpha,\,\beta}=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Gamma(m+\alpha+1)\Gamma(m+\beta+1)}{(2m+\alpha+\beta+1)\Gamma(m+\alpha+\beta+1)m!}.\

These polynomials can be recursively defined by:

\begin{cases}P_{0}^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)=1,\\
P_{1}^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)=\frac{\alpha+\beta+2}{2}t+\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2},\\
P_{m}^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)=(A_{m}t+B_{m})P_{m-1}^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)-C_{m}P_{m-2}^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)\text{ for }m\geq 2,\end{cases}

where the coefficients

A_{m}=\frac{(2m+\alpha+\beta-1)(2m+\alpha+\beta)}{2m(m+\alpha+\beta)},B_{m}=\frac{(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(2m+\alpha+\beta-1)}{2m(m+\alpha+\beta)(2m+\alpha+\beta-2)},C_{m}=\frac{(m+\alpha-1)(m+\beta-1)(2m+\alpha+\beta)}{m(m+\alpha+\beta)(2m+\alpha+\beta-2)}.

Leveraging the weighted orthogonality, we employ the Jacobi-Gauss quadrature. Let

\left\{t_{k}^{\alpha,\,\beta},\omega_{k}^{\alpha,\,\beta}\right\}_{k=0}^{N_{t}}

denote the Jacobi-Gauss quadrature nodes and weights, which satisfy:

\int_{-1}^{1}\psi(t)\omega^{\alpha,\,\beta}(t)\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}\psi\left(t_{k}^{\alpha,\,\beta}\right)\omega_{k}^{\alpha,\,\beta},\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{2N_{t}+1},

(7)

where
\mathcal{P}_{N}
represents the space of polynomials of degree up to
N
.

3.2
Eigenfunctions and interpolation formula used in
FSX 25

When
n=1
and
\Omega:=\Lambda=(-1,1)
, the eigenfunctions of the fractional Laplacian with respect to the weight function
\rho(t):=(1-t^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}
are Jacobi polynomials
P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\,\frac{s}{2}}(t)
according to
MCS 16
, i.e.,

(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}\left[\rho(t)P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}(t)\right]=\lambda_{m}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}(t),\quad\lambda_{m}=\frac{\Gamma(m+\alpha+1)}{m!},\quad t\in\Lambda.

(8)

This is the crucial starting point of spectral Monte-Carlo method stated in
FSX 25
.

Due to the low regularity of solutions to fractional Poisson equations near the boundary of the region, conventional interpolation operators are unable to achieve high-precision approximation. To overcome this difficulty, a class of generalized interpolation operators with fractional order factors are introduced in
FSX 25
. Using Jacobi-Gauss quadrature nodes
\left\{t_{k}^{\alpha,\,\beta},\omega_{k}^{\alpha,\,\beta}\right\}_{k=0}^{N_{t}}
defined in (
7
) with
\alpha=\beta=\frac{s}{2}
, one can define the generalized Lagrange interpolation operator:

\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}^{\frac{s}{2}}v(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}v(t_{k})l_{k}^{\frac{s}{2}}(t),

(9)

with interpolating basis functions

l_{k}^{\frac{s}{2}}(t)=\psi(t)\cdot h_{k}(t),\quad\psi(t)=\left(\frac{1-t^{2}}{1-t_{k}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}},\ h_{k}(t)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq N_{t}\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}\frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{k}-t_{i}},

(10)

where
h_{k}(t)
are standard Lagrange basis polynomials. To simplify the notation, we have denoted
t_{k}:=t_{k}^{\frac{s}{2},\,\frac{s}{2}}
. Next, we choose generalized Jacobi functions
J^{\frac{s}{2}}_{m}(t)=(1-t^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}(t)
as the basis function, then
l_{k}^{\frac{s}{2}}(t)
can be expressed as the following expansion by the Jacobi-Gauss quadrature formula (
7
), that is,

l_{k}^{\frac{s}{2}}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}c_{mk}J^{\frac{s}{2}}_{m}(t),\quad c_{mk}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}}(1-t_{k}^{2})^{-\frac{s}{2}}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}(t_{k})\omega_{k}^{\frac{s}{2},\,\frac{s}{2}}.

(11)

Therefore, the interpolation function
\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}^{\frac{s}{2}}v(t)
can be rewritten as

\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}^{\frac{s}{2}}v(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}v(t_{k})\left(\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}c_{mk}J^{\frac{s}{2}}_{m}(t)\right),

(12)

and combine it with (
8
), we have

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}^{\frac{s}{2}}v(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}v(t_{k})\left(\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}c_{mk}(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}J^{\frac{s}{2}}_{m}(t)\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}v(t_{k})\left(\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}c_{mk}\lambda_{m}P^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}_{m}(t)\right)

(13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}\hat{v}_{m}P^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}_{m}(t),\quad\hat{v}_{m}=\lambda_{m}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}c_{mk}v(t_{k}).

3.3
Eigenfunctions and interpolation formula of radial solutions

The algorithm presented in Algorithm
1
for solving problem (
1
) is inherently one-dimensional. This limitation arises from the definition of the integral fractional Laplacian. In full space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the operator (
2
) admits an equivalent definition via the Fourier transform:

\mathcal{F}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}u](\xi)=|\xi|^{s}\mathcal{F}[u](\xi),\text{ where }\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n}).

When
s=2
, this reduces to the standard Fourier characterization of the classical Laplacian. While the identity
|\xi|^{2}=\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\xi_{n}^{2}
allows the Laplacian to be naturally extended to
n
dimensions, the fractional power
|\xi|^{s}
does not permit a similar separable decomposition. Consequently, the spectral Monte-Carlo method based on the one-dimensional eigenfunction relation (
8
) cannot be directly generalized to higher dimensions.

However, by treating
|\xi|
as a single variable, we can instead consider the radial solutions of problem (
1
). This perspective opens a pathway for extending the spectral Monte-Carlo method to higher-dimensional scenarios. To realize this, the one-dimensional eigenfunction relation (
8
) must be replaced with a radial analogue. The derivation of such a formula is the focus of this section.

Let
\Omega:=B_{1}^{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In
DKK 17
, the eigenfunctions of the fractional Laplacian with respect to the weight function
\rho(x):=(1-|x|^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}
are Jacobi polynomials with indices
(\alpha,\beta)=(s/2,n/2-1)
, i.e.,

(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}\left[\rho(x)P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)\right]=\mu_{m}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2|x|^{2}-1),

(14)

where
\mu_{m}=\frac{2^{s}\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}+m+1\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{s+n}{2}+m\right)}{m!\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+m\right)}.

Using (
6
), we can verify the orthogonality of Jacobi polynomials
P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)
with weight
\rho(x)
:

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}^{0}}\rho(x)P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)P_{q}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)\ \mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}r^{n-1}(1-r^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2r^{2}-1)P_{q}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2r^{2}-1)\ \mathrm{d}r\cdot\int_{\partial B_{1}^{0}}\mathrm{d}V

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\cdot 2^{-\frac{n+s}{2}-1}\int_{-1}^{1}(1+t)^{n/2-1}(1-t)^{s/2}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t)P_{q}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{2^{\frac{n+s}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\gamma_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}\delta_{mq},

(15)

where
\gamma_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}
is the Jacobi orthogonality constant from (
6
).

Under the condition of problem (
1
) exists radial solutions, our goal is to make the interpolation operator (
9
) suitable for high-dimensional space with the help of variable relation
t=2|x|^{2}-1
for
x\in B_{1}^{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Based on this variable relation and using Jacobi-Gauss quadrature nodes
\left\{t_{k}^{\alpha,\,\beta},\omega_{k}^{\alpha,\,\beta}\right\}_{k=0}^{N_{t}}
with index
\alpha=s/2
and
\beta=n/2-1
, we construct the new factor term
\hat{\psi}(t)=\left(1-t\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}/\left(1-t_{k}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}
as displayed in (
10
), where
t_{k}:=t_{k}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}
. This consideration is the difference between our approach and the one in
FSX 25
. For more detailed explanations and analyses, please refer to Section
3.4
below.

In order to use the orthogonality relation (
15
), we firstly need to build the connection between Lagrange polynomials
h_{k}(t)
and Jacobi polynomials
P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t)
. Expanding
h_{k}(t)
as
h_{k}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}\widehat{C}_{m,k}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t)
,
the coefficients
\widehat{C}_{m,k}
are determined via orthogonality:

\int_{-1}^{1}(1+t)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-t)^{\frac{s}{2}}h_{k}(t)P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t=\widehat{C}_{m,k}\int_{-1}^{1}(1+t)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-t)^{\frac{s}{2}}\left[P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t)\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}t=\widehat{C}_{m,k}\gamma_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}.

(16)

Applying Jacobi-Gauss quadrature (
7
), we have

\int_{-1}^{1}(1+t)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}(1-t)^{\frac{s}{2}}h_{k}(t)P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{t}}\omega_{i}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}h_{k}(t_{i})P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t_{i}).

Noting the nodal property
h_{k}(t_{i})=\delta_{ik}
, we obtain that

\widehat{C}_{m,k}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}}\omega_{k}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t_{k}).

In addition, considering the low regularity of solutions to high-dimensional fractional Poisson equations near the boundaries, we need to factorize the factor term
\hat{\psi}(t)
as

\hat{\psi}(t)=\left(\frac{2}{1-t_{k}}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}\left(\frac{1-t}{2}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}.

(17)

This factorization allows us to recover the weight
\rho(x)=(1-|x|^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}
by the variable relation
t=2|x|^{2}-1
for
x\in B_{1}^{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
It gives us the opportunity to include weight factors
\rho(x)
when calculating the fractional Laplacian of the interpolation operator in (
9
) by the continuity equation (
14
).
The above analysis inspired us to define a new interpolation operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}^{\frac{s}{2}}v(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}v(t_{k})\hat{\psi}(t)h_{k}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}v(t_{k})\left(\frac{2}{1-t_{k}}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}\left(\frac{1-t}{2}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}\cdot\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}\widehat{C}_{m,k}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t)

(18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}v(t_{k})\cdot\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}\widetilde{C}_{m,k}\left(\frac{1-t}{2}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t),

where the modified coefficients

\widetilde{C}_{m,k}=\left(\frac{2}{1-t_{k}}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}\widehat{C}_{m,k}=\frac{2^{\frac{s}{2}}}{\gamma_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}}(1-t_{k})^{-\frac{s}{2}}\omega_{k}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(t_{k}).

Through the substitution
t=2|x|^{2}-1
, and letting
v(t)=v(2|x|^{2}-1)
in (
18
). For convenience, we denote
u(x)=v(2|x|^{2}-1)
and
u(|x_{k}|)=v(2|x_{k}|^{2}-1)

Then we have the radial spectral approximation:

\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}^{\frac{s}{2}}u(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}u(|x_{k}|)\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}\widetilde{C}_{m,k}(1-|x|^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2|x|^{2}-1),\quad x\in B_{1}^{0}.

(19)

Here,
u(|x_{k}|)
implies that we only need to select points
\left\{x_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{N_{t}}
at a random angular direction.
Combining with (
14
), we get the fractional Laplacian action as

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{N_{t}}^{\frac{s}{2}}u(x)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}u(|x_{k}|)\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}\widetilde{C}_{m,k}\,\mu_{m}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{N_{t}}\hat{u}_{m}P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{n}{2}-1}(2|x|^{2}-1),\text{ where }\hat{u}_{m}=\mu_{m}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{t}}\widetilde{C}_{m,k}u(x_{k}).

(20)

According to (

), we can obtain the spectral Monte Carlo method in high-dimensional ball
B_{1}^{0}
. The implementation process is similar to Algorithm

, where the main difference is that the points to be calculated and the key formulas (

). For the Jacobi-Gauss points

, then we get
|x_{k}|=\sqrt{(t_{k}+1)/2}
and plus a random angular direction to generate

3.4
Comparisons, Explanations, and Remarks

1.

As established in
Gru 15
, the solution
u
of (
1
) admits a decomposition into a boundary singular term and a smooth component. Specifically, for a smooth source term,

f(x)\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})\Longleftrightarrow u\in\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{s/2}C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega}),\quad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

(21)

where
\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
denotes the Euclidean distance from
x\in\Omega
to the boundary. This characterization precisely captures the limited regularity of the solution near
\partial\Omega
.

In the one-dimensional case with
\Omega=(-1,1)
discussed in Section
3.2
, the weight function
\rho(t)=(1-t^{2})^{s/2}
in (
8
) exactly matches the boundary singularity described in (
21
). Furthermore, since the Jacobi polynomials
P_{m}^{s/2,s/2}(t)
are smooth, the generalized Jacobi polynomials
J^{s/2}_{m}(t)
inherently satisfy the decomposition structure:

J^{s/2}_{m}(t)\in\mathrm{dist}(t,\partial\Omega)^{s/2}C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})\Longleftrightarrow(-\Delta)^{s/2}J^{s/2}_{m}(t)=\lambda_{m}P_{m}^{s/2,s/2}(t)\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega}).

(22)

A key distinction is that (
21
) holds in arbitrary dimensions, while (
22
) and the associated eigenfunction relation (
8
) are intrinsically one-dimensional. Consequently, to implement the spectral Monte Carlo method in higher dimensions (
n\geq 2
), a new eigenfunction relation analogous to (
8
) must be developed for radial solutions.

2.

In Section
3.3
, we derived a new eigenfunction relation (
14
). Its weight function
\rho(x)=(1-|x|^{2})^{s/2}
for
x\in\Omega=B_{1}^{0}
correctly captures the boundary singularity
\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{s/2}
. It remains to verify the smoothness of the Jacobi polynomials
P_{m}^{s/2,n/2-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)
.

Let
h(x)=|x|
. Its partial derivatives are
\partial h(x)/\partial x_{i}=x_{i}/|x|
for
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})
. Since
1/|x|
is not differentiable at the origin, any function involving odd powers of
|x|
may lack smoothness there. However, the polynomial
P_{m}^{s/2,n/2-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)
is an even function of
|x|
, as its argument depends on
|x|^{2}
. This even symmetry ensures that the singularities in the derivatives cancel, guaranteeing that the function is smooth on
\bar{\Omega}
.
Therefore, both the weight
\rho(x)
and the polynomial
P_{m}^{s/2,n/2-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)
conform to the regularity structure in (
21
), satisfying:

\displaystyle\rho(x)P_{m}^{s/2,n/2-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{s/2}C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(-\Delta)^{s/2}\left[\rho(x)P_{m}^{s/2,n/2-1}(2|x|^{2}-1)\right]\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega}).

(23)

3.

We also note a connection established in
OLBC 10
:

\frac{P_{2m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}(t)}{P_{2m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}(1)}=\frac{P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{1}{2}-1}(2t^{2}-1)}{P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{1}{2}-1}(1)},

(24)

with the corresponding eigenvalues satisfying
\lambda_{2m}=\mu_{m}
. This identity demonstrates that the
n
-dimensional eigenfunction relation (
14
) for the special case
n=1
reduces to the one-dimensional result (
8
), but specifically for the even-indexed eigenfunctions.

Furthermore, an equivalent formulation to (
8
) is presented in Corollary 3.15 of
ABBM 18
, expressed in terms of the Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{m}^{\frac{s}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}(t)
. The two polynomial bases are related by the identity

P_{m}^{\frac{s}{2},\frac{s}{2}}(t)=\frac{(\frac{s}{2}+1)_{m}}{(s+1)_{m}}C_{m}^{\frac{s}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}(t),

(25)

where
(a)_{m}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+m-1)
denotes the Pochhammer symbol.

5
Concluding remarks

The spectral Monte Carlo method for fractional Possion problem in one dimensional spaces was first developed in
FSX 25
. Inspired by this work and another radial eigenfunction relation, we provide a new interpolation formula to make spectral Monte Carlo method suitable for high-dimensional ball region. Numerical results demonstrate our method is effective.
