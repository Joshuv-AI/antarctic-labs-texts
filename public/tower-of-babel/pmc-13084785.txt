Association between dietary patterns and the risk of breast cancer in South Asian countries: a protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Mantaka Rahman, Imtiaz Abdullah, Sharmin Sultana, Afroza Tamanna Shimu, Humayra Anjum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo date, breast cancer is the fourth leading cause of mortality globally, while Asian women have the highest mortality reporting ∼47.3%. Epidemiological evidence suggests that the association of dietary pattern is closely linked to the risk of breast cancer. A comprehensive analysis of underlying mechanisms could help to identify modifiable risk factors, ultimately reducing a significant disease burden. The review protocol aims to explore the critical association across subtypes and narratively synthesise the possible mechanism between dietary patterns and breast cancer development among South Asian adult women (≥18 years).Methods and analysisThe research team will search the MEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, Web of Sciences, Embase and Google Scholar databases from inception to 30 November 2025 using truncated and phrase-searched keywords and relevant subject headings. Observational studies published in English language, and reporting the association between dietary patterns, cancer subtypes and breast cancer risk among adult women in South Asia using validated assessment tools, will be included. Two reviewers will screen the retrieved papers independently, while any disagreements between them will be resolved by a third reviewer. Demographics, breast cancer type and dietary patterns, along with their associations, will be extracted for meta-analysis and the findings will be summarised furthermore. Forest plots will be constructed in R (V.4.3.2), with heterogeneity assessed using I² statistic, complemented by publication bias assessment (funnel plot) and sensitivity analysis. The included studies will be critically assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute’s critical appraisal tools. A quantitative synthesis will also be conducted if the included studies are sufficiently homogeneous using a random-effects model and risk factors will be narratively synthesised. In addition, meta-analysis will be completed by subgroups and sensitivity analysis.Ethics and disseminationThe review will synthesise evidence extracted from published studies. As the review will not involve the collection of primary data, ethical approval will not be required. Findings will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed publication.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42025644702.Keywords: Epidemiologic Factors, Epidemiologic Methods, Epidemiology, Systematic Review

Introduction

Breast cancer is a significant global public health issue and the most common malignancy among women, accounting for 24.5% of all female cancer cases in 2020.1 According to GLOBOCAN (The Global Cancer Observatory), 2022 breast cancer is the second highest cancer with an incidence of 2 296 840 cases and fourth leading cause of mortality with the number of 666 103 cases.2 In Asia, the age-standardised incidence rate of breast cancer was 36.8 per 100 000 females, lower than the global average. However, the region’s mortality-to-incidence ratio was 0.32, higher than the global average of 0.28, indicating a higher proportion of women in Asia succumb to the disease compared with other regions3 (figure 1). While breast cancer incidence worldwide is shaped by genetic, environmental and lifestyle-related factors, dietary patterns have emerged as crucial modifiable risk factors influencing its development and progression.4 In 2025, evidence reported in The Lancet Obstetrics, Gynaecology & Women’s Health showed that a survey of more than 2000 women in the USA found only 25% identified diet as a factor in reducing breast cancer risk; among them, just 5% mentioned limiting meat consumption and 1% recognised plant-based diets as a preventive strategy.5

In South Asia, the incidence of breast cancer has risen sharply in recent years, driven by a dual burden of traditional diet and increasingly Westernised dietary habits.6 Dietary patterns, which reflect the overall consumption of foods and nutrients, influence cancer risk through mechanisms including oxidative stress, inflammation and hormone regulation through different pathways.7 South Asian diet, characterised by high intake of lipids, carbohydrates and legumes, along with varying levels of physical activity, can either heighten or reduce the risk of breast cancer depending on their composition and balance.8 Adherence to a healthy dietary pattern, such as the Mediterranean DASH diet, which is rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains and healthy fats, has been associated with a reduced risk of breast cancer.9 Similarly, a plant-based diet abundant in phytonutrient-rich foods like soy and legumes has shown protective effects.3 10 In contrast, the Western dietary pattern, characterised by high consumption of red meat, processed foods and refined carbohydrates, is consistently associated with an increased risk of breast cancer.11 A diet high in fat and low in fibre, in particular, may contribute to obesity, a well-known risk factor for postmenopausal breast cancer.12

Emerging evidence also emphasises the protective role of bioactive compounds found in fruits and vegetables, such as polyphenols and antioxidants, which may further help to prevent breast cancer.13 14 These compounds, presenting in foods like berries, tea and leafy green vegetables, are believed to regulate inflammatory and oxidative pathways that potentially contribute to cancer development (as carcinogens).15 16 Additionally, micronutrients like folate, vitamin D and calcium have been associated with a lower risk of breast cancer.6 17 In South Asia, unhealthy eating habits brought on by rapid urbanisation, environmental factors and Westernisation have sparked concern about their role in escalating breast cancer rates.18 Studies conducted in India and Pakistan, in particular, have shown a correlation between high-fat rich diet and a higher risk of breast cancer, while other studies suggest that plant-based diet may offer protective benefit against the disease onset and progression.19 Traditional South Asian diet typified by high carbohydrate, diverse plant-based meals, whole grains, varying levels of food processing, cultural fasting/feasting practices and spices may offer protective benefits which markedly differ from Western dietary profiles. Although this advantage is eroding due to shifting food preferences and lifestyle changes.20 21 South Asia’s diverse dietary habits, shaped by cultural traditions and economic disparities, make it a rich context for such investigations. These unique exposures may influence breast cancer risk through mechanisms such as inflammation, glycaemic load and oxidative stress. Despite these differences, existing global systematic reviews rarely include sufficient evidence from South Asian data or reported region-specific analyses. At the same time, South Asia is experiencing a rapid rise in breast cancer incidence, especially among younger women, underscoring the need for context-specific prevention strategies. Thus, the paucity of region-specific systematic reviews limits our understanding of how dietary interventions might reduce the risk of breast cancer. The role of diet in modulating breast cancer risk has yet to be fully explored.22 23 Understanding the relationship between these diverse dietary patterns and breast cancer risk is essential to develop region-specific prevention strategies. The need to explore how these patterns influence breast cancer risk through robust observational studies has never been more pressing.24 By focusing exclusively on South Asian women (≥18 years) and distinguishing between empirically derived and predefined dietary patterns, this review will provide a nuanced and methodologically robust synthesis tailored to the region’s unique epidemiological landscape, offering insights not addressed by previous global summaries. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to explore the relationship between dietary patterns and the risk of breast cancer among adult women (≥18 years) in South Asia. Providing a dedicated South Asia-focused synthesis will enable more precise evaluation of local dietary exposures and generate context-appropriate evidence to guide culturally relevant prevention strategies. Additionally, given the established roles of inflammation, oxidative stress and hormonal pathways in mediating diet–cancer relationships, incorporating these mechanisms will further strengthen the conceptual basis for investigating how dietary patterns and breast cancer risk using validated assessment tools across the region for context-specific evidence. By synthesising the evidence from existing studies, this review seeks to gather evidence on the association of breast cancer risk linked to dietary patterns.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study would be to systematically review and meta-analyse observational studies to explore the relationship between dietary patterns and the risk of developing breast cancer among women (≥18 years) of South Asian countries, considering established diet-related mechanisms such as inflammation and oxidative stress. Where data will be available, the analysis will also investigate the associations across specific breast cancer subtypes (eg, triple-negative breast cancer, hormone receptor-positive cancers) and different histopathological types (eg, ductal carcinoma in situ, and invasive ductal carcinoma) to enhance the clinical relevance of the findings. As a secondary objective, the review will also narratively synthesise the possible biological mechanisms through which dietary patterns may influence breast cancer development.

Review question

What is the association between dietary patterns (empirically derived or pre-defined) and the risk of breast cancer among adult women (≥18 years) in South Asian countries based on observational studies?

Methods

Study design and methodological innovations

This systematic review protocol will be conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) 201525 and the Meta-analysis of observational studies in Epidemiology guidelines for systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies.26 The protocol has been prospectively registered in PROSPERO27 with the registration number CRD42025644702.

Beyond the conventional methodological approaches, this systematic review and meta-analysis will incorporate methodological depth by separately analysing empirically derived and predefined dietary patterns, allowing clearer interpretation of context-specific exposures. The review will also include South Asia-focused subgroup analyses and a narrative synthesis of underlying biological mechanisms, providing a more comprehensive and regionally tailored assessment than previous reviews.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria for this study will include observational study designs, including cohort, cross-sectional and case–control studies, focusing on adult female patients (aged ≥18 years) with diagnosed breast cancer, encompassing both benign breast lesions (eg, fibroadenoma or other benign breast conditions) and malignant breast neoplasms, including carcinoma in situ and invasive breast cancer. Breast cancer cases must be diagnosed using histopathological confirmation, biopsy or other standard clinical diagnostic methods, and studies focusing exclusively on recurrent breast cancer will be excluded accordingly. Studies will evaluate dietary patterns derived from factor analysis and/or principal component analysis (PCA) as the primary exposure of interest. Eligible studies will report breast cancer cases diagnosed using pathological biopsies or other standard methods, excluding recurrent cases. Controls or non-cases included females without a breast cancer diagnosis, encompassing all breast cancer types (eg, in situ or invasive cancer). Additionally, studies will need to provide relative risks (RRs), HRs or ORs with 95% CIs comparing the highest to the lowest dietary pattern categories. Where possible, benign and malignant outcomes will be analysed separately to better understand the differential association between dietary patterns and breast disease progression. Only restrictions on the publication period (from inception of each database, up to 30 November 2026) and language (English) will be applied, ensuring comprehensive inclusion of relevant research.

Information sources

Using comprehensive and advanced search strategies, the research team will search the major databases including MEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, Web of Sciences, Embase and Google Scholar. The search strategy will include key search terms related to exposure and outcome, and built-in filters in the databases will be used to customise the final search results.

The population, exposure, control, outcome and types of studies strategy

Population/participants

The review will include studies focusing on prevalent breast cancer (in situ, invasive) of all ethnicities, female genders and all over the South Asian countries (Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka) aging ≥18 years of age.29

Exposure

Women with no specific dietary pattern or unhealthy diets (eg, lowest adherence to dietary patterns while dietary patterns will include both empirically derived patterns (eg, factor or cluster analysis) and predefined patterns based on established dietary indices (eg, Mediterranean or Western patterns). In addition, included studies must assess dietary patterns using validated assessment tools, including food frequency questionnaires, 24-hour recalls, diet records or other predefined dietary indices.

Comparator (s)/control

Women with specific dietary pattern or healthy diets (highest adherence) assessed via validated dietary assessment tools.

Outcome

The primary outcome would be to assess the risk of breast cancer among adult women (≥18 years) in South Asia complemented by subgroup analysis based on breast cancer subtypes (benign and malignant), disease stage, histopathological subtypes and hormone receptor status. The secondary outcome would be to narratively synthesise the possible mechanisms.

Type of studies

Observational studies (cohort, cross-sectional and case–control studies) using validated assessment tools and using the effect measures (eg, RR, HR or OR with 95% CI).

Study records

Data management

EndNote V.21.0 reference management software (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, USA) will be used to compile all the included papers retrieved from the electronic databases.30 The search results from the databases and relevant references will be combined in the reference managing software and duplicates will be removed accordingly. The remaining papers were exported to a web-based application ‘Rayyan QCRI’ to facilitate article title-abstracts and full-text screening in collaboration among researchers involved in the screening process.31

Selection of studies

To identify the potential studies that qualify, two independent authors (IA, SS) will independently screen the search result using the titles and abstracts of the retrieved papers in the Rayyan software. For final inclusion, authors will examine the full text of each paper of the qualifying research. A third reviewer (ATS) will settle any disagreements if the authors have. There will be a log of the reasons for exclusion accordingly. PRISMA-protocols flow diagrams (figure 3) will be used to illustrate a summary of the research papers list for inclusion and exclusion criteria.25

PRISMA-P flow diagram of the study-selection process. The diagram outlines the study identification, screening, eligibility assessment and inclusion of studies in the review. PRISMA-P, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols.

Data extraction and management

A data extraction form will be developed and pre-tested, based on the Cochrane instrument recommendation. Data from each included study will be extracted, using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, Washington, USA) by two independent reviewers (IA and SS) and any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion with another reviewer (MR). The extracted data will include information on the author name, publication year, study location, sample size, characteristics of the population (including age, sex and other demographics), study objectives, eligibility, type of breast cancer, cancer subtypes, histopathological subtypes and dietary pattern. Further, data will be analysed using R statistical package v.4.3.2 in-built ‘meta-packages’.32

Addressing missing data

In cases of any missing data from the included studies, the corresponding author (MR) of this manuscript will contact the corresponding authors or coauthors of the particular articles by email or phone, whichever would be available. If, within the due time period, no required information can be obtained, the data will be excluded from the analysis of the review paper and will be covered in the discussion section as required.

Risk of bias (quality) assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklists will be used to assess the quality of the study.33 Two independent researchers (IA and SS) will independently assess the risk of bias (RoB) in included studies being considered after full-text review. Disagreements between the researchers over the RoB in a particular study will be resolved by discussion, with the involvement of a third review author (MR) where necessary.

Assessment of heterogenicity

The Cochran’s Q statistic and the I2-statistic will be used to assess between-study heterogeneity at a significance level of p<0.01. Heterogenicity will only be evaluated if a meta-analysis is warranted. The I2-statistic <25% represented low heterogenicity, 25–50% represented moderate heterogenicity while I2-statistic >50% is indicated by high heterogenicity.34 In cases of substantial heterogenicity among the included studies (I2>50%), the random-effects logistic model will be applied and in cases of low heterogenicity, the fixed-effects model will be used for analysis.35 Heterogeneity will be evaluated using prediction intervals for a comprehensive assessment. The findings will be represented using forest plots and funnel plots to visually assess overall publication bias.

Strategy for data synthesis and analysis

An electronic search will be performed using PubMed (MEDLINE), Scopus, Web of Sciences, Embase and Cochrane Library databases combining the key elements of the Population, Exposure, Comparator and Outcomes based on inclusion criteria.29 36 To develop the search strategy, a list of relevant index terms (MeSH) and keywords will be derived from the existing database, relevant literature and combined with Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT), truncations and explode functions. In consultation with a systematic review expert, the search strategy will be refined as required. A total of almost 174 studies were yielded from a preliminary search on the PubMed database conducted on 30 November 2025. All included studies will be summarised and tabulated for data extraction at the final stage. Egger’s test and funnel plots will be conducted to examine the possibility of publication bias.37

However, a sensitivity analysis will be conducted to explore the robustness of the study key findings regarding study quality and sample size, performed during meta-analysis. The result of the sensitivity analysis will be summarised in a tabular form. If the meta-analysis cannot be completed for all or some of the included studies, the remaining characteristics of the research and other findings will be summarised narratively (narrative synthesis). Furthermore, if possible, meta-analysis will be completed by subgroup analysis (dietary pattern type, study settings, age group, study design, geographical region, breast cancer subtype (eg, triple-negative breast cancer, hormone receptor status: ER+/ER−, PR+/PR−, HER2+if reported) and histopathological types (eg, ductal carcinoma in situ and invasive ductal carcinoma), effect measures and adjustment level). Dietary patterns (eg, PCA or predefined; dietary indices) will be analysed by subgroup meta-analyses for each type. Differences in associations and heterogeneity between pattern or types will be assessed, with sensitivity analyses conducted where possible and reported accordingly.

Grading quality of evidence

The certainty of the evidence will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation method,38 39 or an alternative methodology will be explicitly outlined and documented in the final review.

Ethics and dissemination

To conduct the systematic review and meta-analysis, ethical review is not necessary because this review will draw secondary data from the published and publicly available scientific literature. The results of the systematic review will be published in an internationally accepted peer-reviewed journal following the standard guidelines for systematic review and meta-analysis, and if sufficient new evidence becomes available to warrant a revision in the review’s conclusion, updates will be carried out accordingly. Any modifications to the protocol made during the review process will be noted in the final manuscript. Moreover, the summary findings will also be verbally disseminated through (eg, national or international conferences, seminars or webinars).

Status and timeline of the study

The systematic review is currently underway and remains in the very preliminary phase, which includes protocol development and comprehensive literature searching. The review process started on November 2025 and is anticipated to be completed by December 2026. This timeline encompasses all stages of the review, including literature searching, protocol registration, study screening, data extraction, quality assessment, data synthesis, meta-analysis and final manuscript preparation. All included databases will be searched for studies published up to 30 November 2025. Any amendments or deviations from the registered protocol will be clearly documented, reported and justified in the final review manuscript.

Limitation of the study findings

This systematic review provides an updated synthesis of observational studies, addressing a critical gap in the evidence on how dietary patterns may be associated with breast cancer risk, while acknowledging that findings reflect associations and not causality due to inherent limitations of observational designs. Although RoB assessment tools will be applied to assess the quality of the included studies, the findings might be influenced by confounding factors and possible selection bias due to the nature of study inclusion criteria.
