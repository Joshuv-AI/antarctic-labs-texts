Adaptation and Validation of the Couple Flourishing Measure Among Spaniards
Authors: Alejandro Saavedra‐Roa, Claudia Pineda‐Marin, Pablo Vallejo‐Medina, Adrián Montesano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFlourishing in couple relationships represents an optimal state of relational well‐being that integrates satisfaction, meaning, and mutual growth. Although related instruments exist, few measures have been specifically developed to assess couple flourishing as an integrated construct within intimate relationships. This study aimed to adapt and validate the Couple Flourishing Measure in a sample of 543 Spanish adults aged 18–75 years (M age = 49.59; SD = 12.81). For this purpose, we used the Couple Flourishing Measure and other related scales to observe convergent validity, such as the Dyadic Adjustment Scale, the Global Measure of Relationship Satisfaction of the Interpersonal Exchange Model of Sexual Satisfaction Questionnaire, and the Satisfaction with Life Scale. The scale was adapted and translated into Spanish. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were conducted, as well as assessments of convergent validity and temporal stability. The scale demonstrated a unidimensional internal structure, high reliability (α = 0.94) for assessing Couple Flourishing, expected associations with related measures, and temporal stability over one‐ and two‐month intervals. Additionally, percentiles for the scale were calculated for different age groups (i.e., 18–35, 36–55, and 56+). The Spanish CFM provides researchers with a brief and reliable instrument for assessing relational flourishing and examining changes in relational well‐being in nonclinical populations.

Introduction

Flourishing refers to a state of optimal human functioning characterized by positive emotions, meaningful relationships, personal growth, and a sense of purpose. Within positive psychology, this construct has been extended beyond individual well‐being to include relational contexts. Couple flourishing (CF) describes intimate relationships that provide satisfaction and foster mutual growth, emotional resilience, and a shared sense of meaning. Despite its relevance for understanding high‐quality couple relationships, research on couple flourishing remains limited, particularly with respect to integrated psychometric instruments specifically designed to assess it as a unified construct (Sanri et al. 2021). Despite its theoretical relevance, CF has often been conflated with relationship satisfaction. Satisfaction has been conceptualized as a subjective evaluative judgment of one's relationship (Fincham et al. 2018), whereas CF reflects couples' broader, process‐oriented capacity to thrive, grow, and build shared meaning (Sanri et al. 2021). Although empirically related, these constructs are conceptually different. The CF integrates both affective (hedonic) and developmental (eudaimonic) dimensions of relational well‐being.

From a theoretical perspective, relationship satisfaction has traditionally been framed within evaluative and outcome‐based models, emphasizing perceived rewards, costs, and overall judgments of relationship quality (e.g., adjustment, stability, or contentment; Fincham et al. 2018; Spanier 1976). In contrast, contemporary relational frameworks conceptualize flourishing as a process‐oriented construct grounded in mutual growth, ethical responsiveness, and shared meaning (Fishbane 2023; Fowers 2000; Galovan et al. 2022). From this perspective, high satisfaction does not necessarily imply flourishing, as relationships may be evaluated positively while lacking developmental depth, relational purpose or transformative capacity. Therefore, CF presents a theoretically distinct construct that complements—but is not reducible to—traditional satisfaction‐based models.

Building on the philosophical foundations of hedonia and eudaimonia, which represent the pleasure‐seeking and virtue‐oriented aspects of well‐being, respectively (Ackrill 1981; Kahneman et al. 1999; Waterman et al. 2008). Sanri et al. (2021) proposed an integrative model of CF. According to this framework, the hedonic component involves frequent and intense positive emotions and shared pleasurable experiences, whereas the eudaimonic component emphasizes mutual growth, compassion, and the co‐construction of shared meaning within the relationship. While some frameworks conceptualize CF as comprising distinct hedonic and eudaimonic dimensions (Fowers et al. 2016; Waterman et al. 2008), others argue for a more integrated, unidimensional structure that reflects the co‐occurrence of affective and developmental processes in intimate relationships (Sanri et al. 2021). Relational theories emphasize that well‐being is not exclusively an individual phenomenon but is inherently relational, emerging from shared experiences, mutual responsiveness, and the co‐construction of purpose (Fishbane 2023; Fowers 2000; Montesano and Ness 2019). These frameworks challenge traditional views of relationship functioning based solely on satisfaction and advocate for models that foreground relational growth, ethical responsiveness, and emotional depth. For example, the Strong Relationality Model (Galovan et al. 2022) proposes that relationships can not only be satisfied but also flourish, a state characterized by mutual inspiration and connection, even in the presence of challenges or difficulties.

Several studies have shown that CF is related to several important domains of people's lives, not just marital satisfaction alone. For example, previous research has found that CF could be related to self‐esteem if positive emotions mediate this relationship (Prameswari‐Edwina et al. 2022). Furthermore, life satisfaction has been shown to have a direct and positive relationship with CF (Rosta‐Filep et al. 2023). Given that sexual satisfaction is related to marital satisfaction and quality of life (Byers 2005; Dehghani‐Champiri and Dehghani 2023; Freihart et al. 2020; Sánchez‐Fuentes et al. 2014, 2015), it can be expected that CF may be linked to sexual satisfaction. These considerations underscore the importance of directly assessing CF as a construct that extends beyond evaluation satisfaction. Furthermore, standard tools focused on satisfaction often fail to capture the higher spectrum of relationship quality, hindering insights into thriving dynamics and the outcomes of psychotherapy.

However, the measurement of CF lags behind its theoretical development. Most existing instruments focus on marital satisfaction (e.g., DAS; Spanier 1976) or assess hedonic and eudaimonic components separately (e.g., Positive Feelings Questionnaire; Fowers et al. 2016; O'Leary et al. 1983; Relationship Flourishing Scale). Until the development of the Couple Flourishing Measure (CFM; Sanri et al. 2021), no instrument had integrated these components into a unified, psychometrically robust assessment tool.

The CFM was created to operationalize this integrative conception of flourishing relationships. This unifactorial questionnaire, available in three versions of 4, 8, and 16 items, was developed using a combination of classical test theory and item response theory. It has shown high reliability coefficients ranging from 0.91 to 0.97 (Sanri et al. 2021). Each item is answered on a 7‐point visual analog Likert scale, where 1 reflects a distressed relationship, 4 a satisfied relationship, and 7 a flourishing relationship, respectively. An example item is: “My life with my partner is…” where 1 = drained (distressed), 4 = content (satisfied), and 7 = plethoric (flourishing).

Thus, while CF and satisfaction are empirically related, they are not necessarily redundant. CF provides empirical data to support this conceptual distinction of relational functioning. The current study is relevant because it provides empirical data to support this conceptual distinction by examining convergent validity with well‐established satisfaction measures and testing the sensitivity of the CFM to relational difficulties. To date, this instrument has not been made available in Spanish. Therefore, this study aimed to adapt and validate the CFM in a sample of Spaniards and evaluate its test–retest reliability.

Method

Participants

Three independent samples were analyzed. A total of 673 Spaniards participated in the study. Of those, 543 participants were used to perform the majority of the analysis; this sample was randomly divided into two subsamples of comparable size for factor analytic procedures; the first subsample (n = 271; 49.9%) was used for the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), and the second subsample (n = 272; 50.1%) was used for the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). The remaining (n = 130) divided into two independent groups of 67 and 63 participants, which were used to evaluate the test–retest reliability at 1 and 2 months, respectively. Participants for the initial adaptation phase (i.e., expert review and cognitive interviews) were recruited from a university. For the main validation and test–retest phases, recruitment was conducted via open online calls posted on social media platforms and professional mailing lists. Participation was voluntary and anonymous in nature.

The 543 Spaniards' ages ranged from 18 to 75 years, distributed as under 30 years (20.68%), between 30 and 50 years (53.89%), and over 50 years (25.43%): 37.30% (M age = 41.34; SD = 12.87) considered themselves cisgender men, 62.30% (M age = 40.22; SD = 12.78) cisgender women, 0.20% (M age = 32.00) transgender men, and 0.20% (M age = 21.00) gender nonconforming or queer. Nearly the entire sample (99.80%) was in an exclusive partnership of more than 6 months duration, while 0.20% had more than one partner with this duration. The average relationship duration was 14.27 years (SD = 17.71). Regarding sexual‐affective orientation, the majority identified as exclusively heterosexual (86.10%), while the remaining participants identified as mostly heterosexual, bisexual, homosexual, asexual, or of other orientations (13.90%). Additionally, 13.62% (74 participants) were considering ending their relationship when answering the survey.

For the test–retest reliability analysis, two independent samples were evaluated at two points in time: one at one and the other at 2 months. The one‐month follow‐up sample consisted of 67 Spanish participants with a mean age of 40.47 years (SD = 11.78), distributed in 36.92% cisgender men, 61.50% cisgender women, and 1.50% belonging to other genders (e.g., transgender, nonconforming). Regarding sexual‐affective orientation, the majority identified themselves as exclusively heterosexual (75.40%), followed by mostly heterosexual (9.20%), bisexual (4.60%), and other orientations (10.80%). Similarly, the 2‐month follow‐up sample included 63 Spanish participants with similar age and orientation characteristics but a different gender distribution. The mean age was 38.60 years (SD = 9.02), with a gender distribution of 11.11% cisgender men and 88.89% cisgender women. As in the one‐month sample, sexual‐affective orientation was mostly represented by exclusively heterosexual (90.48%) and mostly heterosexual (7.94%) individuals. A priori statistical power analysis indicated that the sample sizes were sufficient to detect stability coefficients with high power (1–β > 0.95), assuming conservative effect sizes.

In all samples, participants had to be of legal age, Spanish, in an affective relationship for more than 6 months, and give their voluntary consent to participate in the study.

Measures

Psychosexual and Sociodemographic Information

An ad hoc questionnaire was designed to collect psychosexual and sociodemographic data. Information was collected on age, residence during the research, gender, relationship with a partner lasting more than 6 months, relationship duration, and sexual orientation. Participants were asked to self‐identify their gender (e.g., “man,” “woman,” “non‐binary/other”) and sexual orientation using the Kinsey Scale (cf. Kinsey et al. 1948) through multiple‐choice questions with the option to specify another response if none of the categories applied. No additional sociodemographic data (e.g., socioeconomic status, education, or regional background in Spain) were collected.

Couple Flourishing Measure

The Couple Flourishing Measure (CFM; Sanri et al. 2021) is a self‐report instrument designed to assess couple flourishing as an integrative construct encompassing both hedonic and eudaimonic dimensions of well‐being within intimate relationships. The full version consists of 16 items, although shorter versions (4‐ and 8‐item forms) have also been developed. Items are rated on a 7‐point Likert‐type scale, where higher scores indicate greater levels of couple flourishing. Total scores are computed by summing item responses, with higher values reflecting more optimal relational functioning. The original validation study reported high internal consistency (α ranging from 0.91 to 0.97) and supported a unidimensional factor structure (Sanri et al. 2021). In the present study, the Spanish adaptation of the 16‐item version was used.

Dyadic Adjustment

In this study, we used the brief Spanish version of the Dyadic Adjustment Scale (DAS; Santos‐Iglesias et al. 2009). This instrument consists of 13 items that assess dyadic adjustment across three dimensions: consensus, satisfaction, and cohesion. Items are rated on Likert‐type scales with five, six, or seven response options depending on the item format. The total score ranges from 5 to 70, with higher scores indicating greater adjustment. In the present sample, the total scale demonstrated adequate internal consistency (α = 0.83). An example item is: “Do you participate together in activities outside the relationship?”

Sexual Satisfaction

In this study, we used the Spanish version (Sánchez‐Fuentes et al. 2015) of the Interpersonal Exchange Model of Sexual Satisfaction Questionnaire (IEMSSQ; Lawrance et al. 2011). This scale assesses sexual satisfaction using four independent measures that evaluate the components of the Interpersonal Exchange Model of Sexual Satisfaction (IEMSS). For this research, only the Global Measure of Relationship Satisfaction (GMREL) was used, which assesses overall relationship satisfaction through five bipolar scales with scores ranging from 1 = very poor to 7 = very good. Scores ranged from 5 to 35, with higher scores indicating greater affective relationship satisfaction. In our sample, the GMREL demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.93). An example item would be, “How would you describe your relationship with your partner?”

Life Satisfaction

For this study, we used the Spanish version (Vázquez et al. 2013) of the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS; Diener et al. 1985). This is a unidimensional questionnaire with five items answered on a Likert‐type scale with 7 response options (1 = completely disagree; 7 = completely agree). The total score ranges from 5 to 35 points, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction with life. An example item is: “My life conditions are excellent.” The scale showed favorable reliability indices (α = 0.88).

Procedure

Cultural Adaptation

We conducted a rigorous cultural and linguistic adaptation of the questionnaire following a structured double‐translation protocol, in accordance with the guidelines proposed by Muñiz et al. (2013). First, two independent bilingual translators produced separate Spanish translations of the original items. The translations were then reviewed and reconciled by a team of adapters to ensure semantic consistency and conceptual equivalence. To ensure cultural appropriateness, a panel of two subject‐matter experts—bilingual (Spanish–English) members of the research group with at least 3 years of experience in the field of sexual and couple therapy—critically evaluated the translated version. This step went beyond linguistic equivalence; experts assessed whether the thematic content, cultural connotations, and implicit meanings of each item were preserved and appropriate for the target population. Each item was evaluated in terms of cultural relevance, representativeness, clarity, ease of interpretation, and comprehensibility. When necessary, minor wording refinements were introduced to improve linguistic naturalness and avoid overly literal translations while preserving the conceptual meaning of the original items. It should be noted that the adaptation did not include a formal back‐translation procedure; instead, conceptual equivalence was ensured through the iterative expert review process, consistent with the flexible adaptation framework described by Muñiz et al. (2013).

Subsequently, a cognitive debriefing procedure was conducted with 20 university students to evaluate the clarity, comprehension, interpretation, and completion time of the adapted items from an end‐user perspective. This step was designed to detect linguistically or culturally problematic wording that might not be apparent to experts, rather than to evaluate psychometric performance. Participants were asked to read each item aloud, paraphrase its meaning, and flag any phrasing they found confusing or unnatural. Their feedback informed final wording adjustments to ensure that the instrument was linguistically accessible and culturally appropriate for Spanish speakers in Spain. Finally, online sampling was performed using the three samples. The average time to complete the battery was 46 min, and it was administered within the Qualtrics platform.

Data Analysis

All analyses were carried out using the R programming language (R Core Team 2024; Version 4.4.2) in the R Studio environment (RStudio Team 2023; Version 2023.06.1 + 524). The use of the following packages for data analysis is highlighted: psych (Revelle 2024; Version 2.4.6.26), psychometric (Fletcher 2022; Version 2.4), lavaan (Rosseel 2024; Version 0.6–19), semPlot (Epskamp 2022; Version 1.1.6), semTools (Jorgensen et al. 2021; Version 0.5–6), psycho (Makowski 2022; Version 0.6.1) and ggplot2 (Wickham 2016; Version 3.5.1). The syntax and data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Missing data within the CFM items were minimal (0.24% across items; range per item = 0%–0.74%). A total of 9 cases (1.66%) contained incomplete responses on at least one CFM item and were excluded via listwise deletion for the factor analytic procedures. Given the very low proportion of missingness and the absence of systematic concentration in specific items, listwise deletion was deemed appropriate. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) were conducted to test the dimensionality of the scale in two random subsamples (1/2 for each analysis). Considering the distribution of the scores and the fulfillment of sampling adequacy assumptions (KMO and Bartlett's test (Bartlett 1937)), maximum likelihood (ML) estimation on the Pearson correlation matrix was used to estimate the number of factors to be extracted and the factorial solution. Although multivariate normality was not met (see Results), ML estimation was retained because of its relative robustness under moderate deviations from normality and the adequate sample size. We employed 19 commonly used factor retention procedures, including parallel analysis (Horn 1965), minimum average partial (Velicer 1976), the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues > 1; Kaiser 1960), scree plot inspection (Cattell 1966), acceleration factor, optimal coordinates, Very Simple Structure (VSS), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and additional criteria implemented. On the other hand, CFA was performed on the covariance matrix using ML as an estimator, although this was in its robust version. To evaluate the fit of the model to the data, four indicators were used: Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Tucker‐Lewis Index (TLI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Standardized Residual Mean Square Root (SRMR). RMSEA values below 0.06, SRMR below 0.08, and TLI and CFI values above 0.95 indicate a good fit of the data to the hypothesized model (Hu and Bentler 1999).

To observe convergent validity, Pearson correlations were estimated between the total score of the CFM scale and the DAS, GMREL, and SWLS. The significance and magnitude of the correlations were analyzed. In addition, their psychometric properties were examined. Likewise, the possible prediction of the CFM to discriminate the termination of the relationship was observed using a t‐test for independent samples, assuming unequal variances.

Finally, two main strategies were employed to assess the test–retest reliability of the CFM. First, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to estimate the degree of consistency between the pre‐test and post‐test measurements at two time intervals (i.e., one and 2 months), considering the values deemed acceptable if they fell within the recommended range. Second, Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were applied to compare the pre‐ and post‐test scores within each time interval.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 1983, by the research ethics committee. All participants voluntarily decided to participate in the study, and the ethical committee evaluated the procedures for obtaining consent. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the university (Universitat Oberta de Catalunya), which operates independently of the Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences under the approval number CE23‐PF02.

Results

Cultural Adaptation Process

The expert review and cognitive debriefing procedures indicated that the conceptual content of the original items was largely appropriate for the Spanish context. The two expert reviewers evaluated all 16 items independently and reached consensus on the need for minor linguistic refinements in four items. These adjustments primarily addressed expressions that sounded overly literal when translated directly from English, without modifying the conceptual meaning of the original items.

Two items warranted particular discussion. First, item 8 (“Las diferencias individuales que mi pareja y yo tenemos son ___”) presented a grammatical correction (pronoun order: “mi pareja y yo” rather than “yo y mi pareja”, consistent with standard Spanish usage) and a deliberate retention of the term “diferencias individuales”. Although this expression is more common in technical psychological language than in everyday Spanish, experts determined that it was sufficiently clear and interpretable in the context of the questionnaire, and that available natural‐language alternatives (e.g., “diferencias como personas”) would alter the construct's meaning by reducing its breadth. Second, item 5 (“El tiempo de calidad con mi pareja a solas es ___”) retains the expression “tiempo de calidad”, which is a direct translation of “quality time”. Although “calidad” in isolation typically refers to grade or excellence in Spanish, the compound expression “tiempo de calidad” has become a well‐established colloquial phrase in contemporary Spanish, particularly in relational and parenting contexts, to describe shared time intentionally spent together. Importantly, both expert reviewers and participants in the cognitive debriefing did not interpret the expression as inherently positive. Instead, it was understood as referring to dedicated shared time, the quality of which could vary. Within this interpretation, response options such as “monótono” were perceived as evaluating the subjective experience of that shared time rather than contradicting the expression itself. This interpretation supported the semantic coherence of the item within its evaluative structure.

Participants in the cognitive debriefing interviews reported that all items were generally clear and easy to interpret. No item required conceptual modification or replacement. Overall, the adaptation process confirmed the linguistic adequacy and cultural interpretability of the Spanish version of the CFM. The final version can be seen in the Supporting Information.

Exploratory and Confirmatory Analysis

Before conducting the factor analyses, neither sample showed a normal distribution according to Mardia's test (p < 0.01). In turn, the KMO indices (i.e., 0.91 and 0.97) and Bartlett's test (p < 0.01) showed the adequacy of the data to perform the respective analyses in both subsamples. Of the 19 methods employed, six (31.58%) supported a two‐factor solution, while five (26.31%) suggested a unidimensional model. However, the two‐factor exploratory solution did not demonstrate a clear simple structure. Specifically, several items loaded substantially on both factors, indicating a lack of conceptual differentiation between the extracted dimensions. These cross‐loadings suggest the presence of a dominant general factor rather than two substantively distinct constructs. Furthermore, unidimensional and two‐factor solutions were compared using both oblique and orthogonal rotations. Fit indices for these models are presented in Table 1. Although the two‐factor model showed improved statistical fit, the factors were highly correlated and the solution lacked interpretability. Moreover, the orthogonal rotation did not resolve these issues, as multiple items continued to load on both factors. Taken together, these findings suggest the presence of a strong general factor underlying the item set, while not fully excluding the possibility of multidimensional structures.

Convergent Validity

Convergent validity analysis was performed using Pearson correlations between the CFM total scores and those of the other measures; in all cases, significant (p < 0.001) and positive correlations were observed (see Figure 2). Significant differences (t (91.79) = −10.72, p < 0.001, 95% CI [16.08, 31.78], d = −1.40) were observed in the total CFM scores of those who were thinking about ending their relationship (M = 61.67; SD = 18.87) and those who were not (M = 85.60; SD = 16.48).

Psychometric Properties

Cronbach's alpha was obtained for the 16 items and for the entire sample, with α = 0.94 (95% CI [0.94, 0.95]), demonstrating high internal consistency and reliability of the scale. In all cases, item–total corrected correlations were moderate to high and positive; item 12 showed the lowest value (0.60), whereas item 5 presented the highest (0.76). The factor loadings in the EFA were consistently strong (all λ ≥ 0.61), indicating that each item contributed meaningfully to the general factor. Communalities ranged from 0.36 to 0.62, suggesting that a substantial proportion of the item variance was explained by the latent construct. Although Item 12 showed the lowest communality (𝒉2 = 0.361), its factor loading (λ = 0.63) and corrected item‐total correlation (0.60) remained within acceptable ranges, indicating an adequate contribution to the general factor. No item demonstrated psychometric weaknesses warranting removal. Overall, the item‐level analyses supported the internal coherence and structural adequacy of the CFM. Couple Flourishing Measure Scores and Percentiles

Finally, the percentile ranking of the scores of the Spanish‐adapted version of the CFM was obtained and differentiated into three age groups: 18–35, 36–55 and over 56 years (see Table 4). These intervals were defined to ensure adequate subgroup sizes and stable percentile estimation and are consistent with age stratifications commonly used in previous psychometric studies (e.g., Gómez‐Lugo et al. 2024; Saavedra‐Roa and Vallejo‐Medina 2020). The age bands are not intended to reflect discrete developmental stages but rather to provide practical normative references for descriptive comparisons. The skewness and kurtosis values across age groups fell within the acceptable ranges for psychological data (|skew| < 1; |kurtosis| < 1), indicating mild negative skewness consistent with the ceiling tendencies typically observed in well‐being measures. For interpretive purposes, percentile values allow situating an individual's score relative to others within the same age group. For example, a score corresponding to the 25th percentile in the 56+ group indicates that the individual reports lower levels of couple flourishing than approximately 75% of peers in that age range, reflecting comparatively lower perceived relational well‐being within the normative distribution. Conversely, scores above the 75th percentile reflect relatively higher levels of perceived relational flourishing. These comparisons are intended solely for descriptive contextualization and should not be interpreted as indicators of dysfunction or clinical thresholds.

Test–Retest Reliability and Temporal Stability of the CFM

The test–retest reliability analysis at the 1‐ and 2‐month intervals is presented in Figure 3. The Wilcoxon signed‐rank test showed no statistically significant differences between the pre‐ and post‐test scores at each time period. At the two‐month interval, the median scores remained stable between the measurements (p = 0.71). Similarly, for the one‐month interval, no significant differences were observed between the pre‐ and post‐test scores (p = 0.86). These analyses were intended to provide preliminary evidence of temporal stability rather than clinical validation. Furthermore, the intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for both intervals support the temporal stability in the measure provided by the CFM: ICC = 0.86, 95% CI [0.78, 0.91] at 2 months, and ICC = 0.84, 95% CI [0.74, 0.90] at 1 month.

Discussion

This study aimed to adapt and validate the Couple Flourishing Measure (CFM) for use with Spanish couples. The results show that the CFM is a reliable and valid tool for assessing couple flourishing (CF) in Spaniards, providing initial psychometric evidence regarding the integrative construct of couple flourishing in intimate relationships. To achieve this, the internal consistency of the scale was obtained, factor analysis was performed, and convergent and test–retest validity were evaluated. Additionally, percentiles of the instrument were obtained for the Spanish population.

The CFM showed excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha (α) values exceeding 0.90, indicating high reliability. This is consistent with previous findings from the original validation study of the CFM (Sanri et al. 2021), in which reliability coefficients ranged from 0.91 to 0.97. This consistency supports the structural robustness of the construct, as operationalized by the CFM.

Both exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) conducted in this study supported the unidimensional structure of the CFM. Although several retention methods suggested a potential two‐factor solution, the absence of a clear simple structure and the presence of substantial cross‐loadings indicated that the extracted dimensions did not represent conceptually distinct constructs. In the CFA, both unidimensional and bifactor models were estimated. While the bifactor model showed acceptable global fit, it presented clear signs of inadmissibility and lack of interpretability, with weak and inconsistent loadings in the specific factors. In contrast, the general factor showed strong and consistent loadings across all items. Taken together, these findings suggest that the common variance is primarily accounted for by a dominant general factor, supporting a predominantly unidimensional interpretation of the construct.

The unidimensional model explained 48% of the total variance, which is lower than the 73.6% reported by Sanri et al. (2021). This difference may reflect variability in how couple flourishing manifests within a broader general population sample. The original validation was conducted on a more homogeneous sample of couples, which may have increased shared variance across items. In contrast, greater heterogeneity in relational experiences may reduce inter‐item covariance, resulting in lower explained variance. However, this interpretation should be considered tentative. The lower explained variance could also reflect structural limitations of the adapted version of the instrument, including potential differences in item functioning or cultural nuances not fully captured in the adaptation process. In the absence of formal measurement invariance analyses or cross‐cultural comparisons, it is not possible to determine whether the observed discrepancy is attributable to sample characteristics, measurement factors, or a combination of both. From a psychometric perspective, explained variance values between 40% and 50% are generally considered acceptable for complex relational constructs that integrate affective and developmental processes (Hair et al. 2019). The RMSEA value (0.068) and its 90% confidence interval [0.045, 0.074] suggest that model fit is acceptable but not optimal, as the interval spans both good‐fit and above‐threshold values. However, the convergence of multiple fit indices (CFI, TLI, SRMR) supports the overall adequacy of the model. Moreover, the robust factor loadings and internal consistency indicate that the general factor remains substantively coherent despite the more moderate variance explained. This pattern is consistent with previous psychometric research indicating that constructs integrating hedonic and eudaimonic components often exhibit a strong general factor structure.

The convergent validity of the CFM was supported by significant positive correlations with related constructs such as dyadic adjustment, sexual satisfaction, and life satisfaction. These associations are theoretically consistent, given the central role of intimate relationships in broader well‐being frameworks (Prameswari‐Edwina et al. 2022; Rosta‐Filep et al. 2023). However, the magnitude of the correlations—particularly with dyadic adjustment—suggests substantial overlap between constructs. While couple flourishing has been theoretically conceptualized as encompassing not only evaluative satisfaction but also shared meaning, emotional resilience, and mutual growth, the present findings do not provide direct empirical evidence of discriminant validity. Accordingly, although life satisfaction represents a global cognitive evaluation of one's life as a whole and couple flourishing refers more specifically to relational processes within the dyad, the extent to which these constructs are empirically distinct remains to be established. Thus, the observed correlations support convergent validity but do not allow firm conclusions regarding construct distinctiveness.

Although CF integrates hedonic elements (e.g., positive emotional experiences), the CFM items assess global and enduring evaluations of relational functioning rather than momentary affective states. In this sense, couple flourishing may be conceptualized as a relatively stable dispositional perception of relational thriving that can coexist with short‐term emotional fluctuations. The strong test–retest coefficients observed over one‐ and two‐month intervals are therefore consistent with the theoretical framing of CF as a construct reflecting enduring relational quality rather than transient mood states. However, long‐term longitudinal research is needed to explore potential fluctuations across significant relational transitions.

From an applied perspective, the present findings support the use of the CFM primarily as a research instrument for assessing relational thriving at the group level. The scale demonstrated sensitivity to differences between individuals who had considered ending their relationship and those who had not, suggesting that it can discriminate levels of relational well‐being in nonclinical populations. In research settings, the CFM may be particularly useful as an outcome variable in studies examining CF, relational enhancement programs, or preventive interventions aimed at fostering relational growth. Its high internal consistency and temporal stability also support its suitability for longitudinal designs that assess changes in perceived relational flourishing over time.

Although the instrument shows potential relevance in clinical contexts, the present study did not establish diagnostic thresholds or clinical cutoff scores. Therefore, its use in therapeutic decision‐making should await formal clinical validation in therapy‐seeking samples. The normative percentile values provided in this study are intended for descriptive reference only and should not be interpreted as indicators of clinical impairment or of diagnostic status.

Limitations and Future Research

Despite these robust results, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample, although diverse in age and sex, was highly homogeneous in terms of relationship structure and sexual‐affective orientation. This overrepresentation limits the generalizability of the findings, and the psychometric evidence presented here should therefore be interpreted primarily as applicable to predominantly heterosexual, cisgender, and monogamous relationships within the Spanish general population. Such homogeneity may also inflate the scale's reliability and validity estimates, especially in relation to its potential clinical use. In addition, although the instrument was adapted for use in Spain, regional linguistic variations within the country and across other Spanish‐speaking populations (e.g., Latin America, Equatorial Guinea, etc.) were not examined. Therefore, the present findings cannot be generalized to all Spanish‐speaking contexts without further cultural validation.

Second, no data were collected on relevant sociodemographic variables such as socioeconomic status, education level, or regional background within Spain, which could provide further nuance in interpreting the results. Moreover, this study did not assess measurement invariance across demographic groups (e.g., gender or sexual orientation), nor did it examine discriminant validity using theoretically unrelated constructs (e.g., personality traits, general distress, or social desirability). As such, although strong correlations with dyadic adjustment support convergent validity, the present study does not provide formal evidence of incremental or discriminant validity beyond established satisfaction measures. Future research should incorporate theoretically unrelated constructs and conduct hierarchical regression or structural equation modeling analyses to determine whether the CFM explains unique variance in relational or well‐being outcomes beyond traditional satisfaction‐based instruments. These steps are crucial for establishing the broader applicability and psychometric robustness of the CFM.

Additionally, the two‐month test–retest subsample showed a marked gender imbalance, with a predominance of cisgender women (88.89%) and a relatively low representation of cisgender men (11.11%), in contrast to the more balanced distribution observed in the main validation sample. Although the stability coefficients obtained were satisfactory, this imbalance may limit the generalizability of the test–retest findings and could potentially influence the observed estimates of temporal stability.

Importantly, the present study does not constitute a clinical validation. No ROC analyses, sensitivity/specificity estimates, or clinical cutoff scores were established. Therefore, although preliminary group differences suggest potential clinical sensitivity, formal clinical validation requires independent samples of therapy‐seeking couples and appropriate diagnostic benchmarking procedures.

Although the unidimensional model demonstrated acceptable fit, the RMSEA value approached conventional cutoffs for optimal fit. Future research should therefore examine measurement invariance and alternative structural specifications (e.g., second‐order models) to further clarify the dimensional structure of the CFM across samples and contexts. Additionally, the study did not assess potential practice effects or significant relational events occurring between measurement intervals. Although the absence of significant mean differences and the high ICC values suggest temporal stability, future research should examine whether major relational transitions or repeated exposure to the instrument influence CFM scores over time.

Future research should attempt to validate the CFM in more diverse samples, including a greater representation of sexually diverse individuals, and explore the application of the CFM in different cultural contexts beyond Spain to further validate its cross‐cultural replicability. It is also important to test the clinical discrimination that the scale can achieve between affective‐sexual relationships that seek therapy and those that do not. In addition, longitudinal studies can examine the stability of CF over longer time intervals. Given the gender differences in relational perceptions are plausible, future research should examine the stability of the CFM across more demographically balanced samples and assess measurement invariance across gender groups. Clinical longitudinal research will also be needed to determine whether the Couple Flourishing Measure (CFM) is sensitive to psychotherapy outcomes that traditional satisfaction‐based measures may fail to detect due to ceiling effects. This line of inquiry is particularly relevant given the growing value placed on intimate relationships as a source of personal fulfillment and meaning in contemporary society (Herrera Gómez 2016; Montesano and Ness 2019). Cross‐cultural comparative studies could provide a deeper understanding of the universal aspects of CF.

Conclusion

The present study provides initial psychometric evidence supporting the adaptation of the Couple Flourishing Measure (CFM) for use in a Spanish‐speaking population. The findings indicate that the instrument demonstrates adequate reliability, factorial coherence, and convergent validity within a predominantly heterosexual, monogamous general sample. These results extend the availability of tools designed to assess relational thriving beyond traditional satisfaction‐based measures and offer a preliminary foundation for future research on couple flourishing in Spanish contexts. However, the current findings should be interpreted within the scope of a non‐clinical validation study. Further research is required to examine measurement invariance, discriminant and incremental validity, and formal clinical utility in therapy‐seeking populations. Taken together, this study represents a step toward expanding the empirical assessment of relational flourishing while highlighting the need for continued theoretical and methodological refinement of the construct across diverse relational and cultural contexts.
