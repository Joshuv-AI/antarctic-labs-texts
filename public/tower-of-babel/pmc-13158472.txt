Surgical Positioning in Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair: Comparable Outcomes but Longer Operative Times in the Beach-Chair Position
Authors: Madeline M McGovern, Omar A Protzuk, Kristen N Reikersdorfer, Nathan Sherman, Jillian L Mazzocca, Jamie Collins, Elizabeth G Matzkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (RCR) can be performed with patients in either the lateral decubitus (LDP) or beach-chair position (BCP). There are limited data exploring the effect of patient positioning on outcomes after RCR.Hypothesis:There will be a difference in patient-reported outcomes (PROs) between patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR in LDP versus BCP, and a significantly shorter operating room time in LDP to account for positioning.Study Design:Cohort study: Level of evidence, 3.Methods:Patients were propensity score matched for tear size and demographic variables including age, body mass index, sex, and workers’ compensation status. Patients were included who had an isolated arthroscopic primary RCR with ≥2 years of PRO data available.Results:A total of 160 patients, 80 in each of LDP and BCP, were included in the study. There were no significant differences in American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons function scores, and both groups improved at 2 years from 14.0 to 26.2 (95% CI, 12.7-15.3; 25.1-27.3) in BCP and 13.9 to 25.8 (95% CI, 24.8-26.9) in LDP (P = .64). No significant differences were found in PRO measures, including Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation score, Simple Shoulder Test, and Veterans RAND 12-Item Health Survey (VR-12) Physical Component Summary. The VR-12 Mental Component Summary score improved from 0 to 24 months with the BCP group scoring significantly better than LDP (P < .05). No difference in surgical time from incision to close was identified (BCP, 87.4 ± 23.1 minutes; LDP, 82.4 ± 31.4 minutes; P = .30); however, BCP cases had significantly longer total operating room time from in-room to end of procedure (BCP, 122.3 ± 25.7 minutes; LDP, 110.7 ± 30.3 minutes; P = .038). Two-year complication rates were similar between groups and identified in 9 (11.3%) patients in the BCP group and 11 (13.8%) patients in LDP (P = .63).Conclusion:Overall, PROs and complication rates were largely equivocal between the 2 groups at 2 years. Total operative time was shorter with LDP, reflecting the effect of setup for BCP. Patient positioning does not appear to affect patient outcomes, and surgeons should pick the positioning they are most facile with performing arthroscopic RCR.Keywords: rotator cuff repair, positioning, lateral decubitus, beach-chair

In the United States, approximately 250,000 rotator cuff repairs (RCRs) are performed annually. 27 Prevalence increases with age, and approximately 54% of adults in their 60s have rotator cuff tears visualized on magnetic resonance imaging.25,27 Failure of arthroscopic RCR occurs in up to 35% of small tears and 94% when the tears are larger.14,16,17 Certain risk factors are associated with increased risk of retear including increasing patient age and tear size. 16 Revision rotator cuff surgery is associated with worse patient outcomes and failure of healing between 36% and 39%.2,17 Optimization of patient comorbidities and operative techniques remains a focus to improve outcomes in primary repair.

Two techniques of patient positioning in shoulder arthroscopy have emerged with an apparent coastal bias. 15 Historically, West Coast surgeons positioned their patients in a lateral decubitus position (LDP), whereas East Coast surgeons performed shoulder arthroscopy in the beach-chair position (BCP). 15 Positioning preference is frequently dictated by training; however, there are advantages and limitations to the respective setups. LDP proponents argue an easier setup, improved visualization of the articular side of the rotator cuff, and less obscuration of view by electrocautery bubbles. There is a risk of injury to brachial plexus due to traction on the arm.6,10,13,21,23 LDP places the shoulder in a nonanatomic orientation that can be difficult to navigate and additionally requires general anesthesia.21,23,28

BCP provides surgeons with an anatomic view of the joint and permits access to the glenohumeral joint and subacromial space with minimal manipulation of the arm and allows for better exposure of the subacromial space.13,26 The setup does not require traction, limiting the risk of neurapraxia. 23 Patients can tolerate procedures with either general or regional anesthesia in the BCP. Unfortunately, there are serious risks associated with BCP—specifically, hypoperfusion and stroke.5,15,23 Excessive head extension can reduce vertebral artery flow leading to posterior brain infarct, while cervical flexion can block cerebral venous drainage.5,15

Patient positioning is a critical portion of any procedure, but its effect on RCR outcomes has limited analysis with only 1 comparative study to our knowledge exploring the topic. Zhang et al 28 reviewed outcomes of patients who were treated with arthroscopic RCR in either BCP or LDP. Postoperatively, patients treated in LDP had improved pain, function, and strength scores compared with the BCP cohort with a shorter operative time. Moreover, the study suggested LDP may be better in patients who have small, medium, and large tears but no difference was identified with partial tears. The authors theorized that improved outcomes with LDP may be secondary to direct visualization of anchor depth, ease of rotation of the arm for appropriate anchor positioning along the full rotator cuff footprint area, decreased operative time, and improved visualization of the glenohumeral and subacromial spaces without bubble obscuration to facilitate optimized medial and lateral row anchor placement and cuff reduction.

With the general lack of investigative evidence, our study intends to further explore the effect of positioning on patient outcomes. Our primary aim is to evaluate how patient positioning in arthroscopic RCR affects patient-reported outcomes (PROs). Our secondary aim is to evaluate pain scores, complication rates, and strength and range of motion (ROM) changes. We hypothesized that there would be differences in PROs between patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR in LDP versus BCP and that there would be a significantly shorter operating room time in LDP compared with BCP to account for positioning.

Methods

A retrospective review of patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR between August 2012 and August 2023 was performed. All patients underwent surgery within a large academic medical center. Surgery was performed by 1 of 2 fellowship-trained and board-certified orthopaedic sports medicine surgeons and patient positioning was dictated by surgeon preference, with one surgeon utilizing LDP and the other utilizing BCP. All patients were positioned by the surgical team, who were experienced in the setup of both positions.

In LDP, patients were positioned with a bean bag, axillary roll, hip post, and a pillow between the legs, with the down leg appropriately padded with foam and gel. The arm was secured utilizing a balanced weighted 3-point shoulder distraction system (Arthrex) and disposable sleeve with adherent wrap. For BCP, patients were placed on a compatible table with T-MAX beach-chair attachment (Smith & Nephew) with disposable foam face mask (Smith & Nephew). The operative arm was placed in a Spider2 pneumatic limb positioner (Smith & Nephew).

Inclusion criteria consisted of patients aged ≥18, minimum of 1 year of follow-up, and those who underwent arthroscopic RCR. Exclusion criteria included previous shoulder surgery, neurovascular injury of the operative extremity, revision RCR, irreparable or partial irreparable repair, concomitant instability procedure including labral repair or capsulorrhaphy, humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament repair, fracture, and concomitant tendon transfer.

All data were collected from the electronic medical record. Demographic variables including age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, diabetes, workers’ compensation status, laterality of injury and patient hand dominance, race, and ethnicity were collected. Perioperative data were also retrieved including tear size as defined by Cofield classification, number of rotator cuff tendons torn, subscapularis tear with repair, tear acuity, concomitant biceps procedure, and concomitant subacromial decompression.

Operative time data were obtained from the nursing records. Total time in the operating room was defined by the time elapsed from when the patient was brought into the operating room until the end of the procedure. Surgical time was calculated by subtracting the time of incision by the time of skin closure.

Postoperative outcomes were assessed via PROs. Outcome scores included the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE) score, Simple Shoulder Test (SST), Veterans RAND 12-Item Health Survey (VR-12) Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) and visual analog scale (VAS). These were collected both preoperatively and postoperatively at 2 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years. Preoperative and final follow-up shoulder ROM and strength measurements were performed at clinic visits and were documented. Strength was assessed by manual muscle testing and ROM was assessed visually by the surgeons of this study. Any perioperative and postoperative adverse events and complications were recorded.

Postoperative Rehabilitation Protocol

All patients followed a standard rehabilitation protocol. Initially, patients were immobilized with a sling for 4 to 6 weeks followed by ROM and strengthening with return to full activity expected between 6 and 8 months postoperatively.

Statistical Analysis

Patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR during the study time frame were identified and grouped according to whether their operation was performed in LDP or BCP. Propensity score matching was performed to ensure that the exposure groups were balanced on key covariates. Age at treatment, BMI, sex, and workers’ compensation status were included in the propensity score model, and the model was constrained to match exactly on Cofield tear size. We used greedy nearest-neighbor matching with caliper width 0.15 (3.81 mm). The association between longitudinal PROs and position was assessed using linear mixed-effects models with a time × position interaction term to assess whether change over time (ie, improvement from preoperatively) was different for patients with LDP versus BCP. Models were adjusted for BMI, age, and symptom duration. Analyses were restricted to matched pairs in which outcome data were available for both members. There were significant gaps in available data for ROM and strength outcomes with more data available in the LDP cohort. Thus, we did not perform statistical analysis for these variables to prevent inappropriate conclusions and interpretation.

A power calculation found that 80 patients per group (160 total) would have >80% power to detect between-group differences of effect sizes of approximately 0.45 SD.

Results

There were 631 patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR between August 29, 2012, and August 3, 2023. A total of 42 were missing data on key covariates, 60 did not provide baseline PROs, and 110 did not provide follow-up PROs at 1 or 2 years, leaving 419 to be considered for analysis. There were 113 patients who underwent surgery in BCP and 306 with LDP. A similar number were excluded from each group due to lack of follow-up PROs (37% BCP vs 32% LDP) (Figure 1).

Patient-Reported Outcomes

There were no significant differences found of ASES function at any time point with index ASES scores at time 0 of 14.0 (95% CI, 12.7-15.3) and 13.9 (95% CI, 12.6-15.2) in the BCP and LDP group, respectively, with improvement at 2 years to 26.2 (25.1-27.3) BCP and 25.8 (24.8-26.9) in LDP (P = .64) (Figure 2). Both the LDP and BCP groups improved with respect to time-zero scores over the 2-year period. There were no differences in 2-year postoperative PROs including SANE, SST, VAS, and VR-12 PCS (Table 2, Figures 3 -6). SANE scores were statistically equivalent in both groups at all time points (Figure 3). VAS pain scores significantly improved between time zero and 24 months with plateauing occurring around month 6 (Figure 4). The only other finding of significance was that of VR-12 MCS score improvement from 0 to 24 months with the BCP group scoring significantly better than LDP (P < .05) (Figure 7).

There were 66 matched pairs available for analysis for forward flexion ROM with improvement in BCP of 16°± 42° and 26°± 43° in LDP compared with preoperative ROM (Table 4).

There were 37 pairs analyzed for change in internal rotation (IR) strength. At baseline, 84.1% of BCP patients started with full strength in IR compared with 76.9% of LDP patients. A total of 33 (89.2%) BCP and 20 (54.1%) LDP patients had no change in strength grade, while 4 (10.8%) BCP patients and 14 (37.8%) LDP patients had strength improvement postoperatively; 3 (8.1%) LDP patients had a worse postoperative grade of IR strength. There were no differences in postoperative strength noted between the 2 groups.

In total, 54 matched pairs were analyzed for changes in external rotation (ER) ROM with a mean improvement of 4°± 26° in BCP and 11°± 26° in LDP. ER strength was analyzed in 46 matched pairs with 57.7% of BCP and 31.6% of LDP patients with full ER strength at baseline. Eleven (23.9%) BCP and 28 (60.9%) LDP patients demonstrated an improvement in strength postoperatively, while 33 (71.7%) BCP and 18 (39.1%) LDP patients had no change in ER strength. Two (4.3%) BCP patients had a worse ER strength grade postoperatively (Table 4).

Complications

Complications were reported in 9 (11.3%) BCP patients and 11 (13.8%) LDP patients (P = .63). Seven (8.8%) patients in the BCP group sustained retears or repair failure versus 4 (5.0%) patients in the LDP group. In the immediate perioperative period, 3 (3.8%) patients experienced medical complications in the LDP group. One patient experienced severe vertigo in the postanesthesia care unit and was admitted overnight. One patient experienced intraoperative atrial fibrillation, and 1 patient had a congestive heart failure exacerbation, which resolved with treatment. Four patients (5%) in the LDP group and 1 (1.3%) in the BCP group had either persistent pain or developed adhesive capsulitis postoperatively. The sample size was too small for statistical analysis of specific adverse events, but qualitative details of complications are listed in Table 5.

Discussion

This study analyzed the differences in PROs, operative time, postoperative clinical strength and ROM, and complications in patients undergoing RCR based on patient positioning either in BCP or LDP. Overall, PROs were largely equivocal between the 2 groups, without any statistical difference in SANE, VAS, SST, or VR-12 PCS. VR-12 MCS scores favored the BCP group at 2 years. There were no differences at any time point in our outcome of primary interest, ASES function scores, which is supported by the only other study of this subject. 28

We hypothesized that positioning setup would result in a longer time in the operating room in BCP versus LDP. We did identify that overall operating room time was shorter with LDP with a mean of 12 fewer minutes reflecting the effect of setup for BCP. However, there was no difference identified in surgical time between the 2 positions. Similar to this study, work by Zhang et al 28 demonstrated shorter overall operative time in LDP in settings of small, medium, and large tears. A study by Glazier and colleagues 10 found significantly shorter operative times and time between intubation and incision reflecting a shorter setup time.

With respect to postoperative ROM, patients in both groups improved in comparison with their preoperative values in every category. The majority of patients either kept their baseline strength or demonstrated improvement. We did not run statistical analysis for these data points because of incomplete data. The lack of improvement in IR strength in the BCP cohort likely is related to the high proportion of full strength on manual muscle testing at baseline, as there were only 3 subscapularis repairs in the BCP group. Zhang et al 28 similarly found no significant difference in postoperative flexion between the LDP and BCP groups but identified that ER ROM exhibited greater improvement in the LDP group.

Our overall complication rate was 11.3% in BCP and 13.8% in LDP. There was no difference in overall complication rate between the 2 groups. Complications included retear, persistent pain, and perioperative medical complications. The patient who experienced atrial fibrillation on induction followed up with the cardiologist without recurrence. Vertigo in the postoperative care unit was experienced by 1 patient who underwent RCR in LDP. This led to an overnight stay but no further issues. Interestingly, we did not have any patients experience complications related to their specific positioning. Positioning complications such as neurapraxia in LDP and cerebrovascular events are relatively rare with incidence between 0.2% and 10% and 0.004% and 0.005%, respectively.1,4,7,9,18-20,22,23 Considering how infrequent these positioning-related complications occur, a larger sample size is required to more adequately assess statistical significance. Seven (8.8%) patients in the BCP group sustained retears with 4 (5%) patients in the LDP group with either a retear or failure (Table 5). Retears are a common complication of rotator cuff surgery with one meta-analysis finding a retear rate of 22% in patients followed for a mean of 27.5 months postoperatively.12,24

There is a paucity of data exploring the effect of positioning on patient outcomes in arthroscopic RCR. Largely, positioning is predicated on surgeon training and comfort. LDP positioning may be advantageous with respect to recurrent instability rates in shoulder instability patients and arthroscopic labral repair. 8 Visualization is critical during arthroscopic surgery, and there is argument that LDP may permit easier visualization of the critical 6-o’clock position of the glenoid during treatment of anterior shoulder instability. 8 The traction and abduction from the LDP setup permits better anchor placement at the 6-o’clock position compared with BCP. 3 In the case of rotator cuff tears, adequate visualization in the subacromial space is most critical for repair. While LDP may provide better visualization and greater working room in the subacromial space, 11 we did not find that this affected overall PROs between the groups in our study.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study that should be taken into account. First, all procedures were performed by 1 of only 2 surgeons. We acknowledge there may be concerns about generalizability and comparison with only 2 surgeons included. Both surgeons have been in practice for >20 years and are respectively skilled in arthroscopic management of rotator cuff tears. Furthermore, this is a retrospective study with relatively short-term follow-up. These factors detract from the overall generalizability of our findings. Moreover, there was a difference in tear size before matching with 31% of BCP having massive rotator cuff tears and only 4% of the LDP group. While we were able to mitigate this discrepancy with matching, this resulted in a smaller sample size with only 80 participants per cohort. Finally, there were some missing time-specific PRO data and significant gaps in retrospectively collected data leading to smaller subgroup analysis.

Conclusion

Surgical positioning in LDP or BCP for arthroscopic RCR results in similar PROs at 2 years with comparable postoperative clinical strength and ROM. Complication rates were similar in our study with increased total operative time associated with BCP compared with LDP reflecting longer setup time for BCP. Surgeons should choose the positioning that best fits their skillset and preferences.
