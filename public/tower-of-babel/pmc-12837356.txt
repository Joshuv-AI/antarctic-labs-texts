Surgery versus stereotactic body radiotherapy for oligometastatic colorectal cancer: a prospective cohort study with propensity score matching
Authors: Daohai Wang, Zhili Zhao, Weijie Zhao, Guanglong Chen, Wei Du, Zhi Li, Jian Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal local therapy for oligometastatic colorectal cancer (CRC) remains undefined, with radical surgical resection and stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) serving as the primary modalities. High-quality comparative evidence is scarce. This study aimed to prospectively compare overall survival (OS), local tumor control (LTC), quality of life (QoL), adverse events, and healthcare resource utilization between these two treatments.MethodsIn this prospective, observational cohort study, 478 patients with 1–3 oligometastases from CRC were enrolled. Treatment assignment was determined by a multidisciplinary tumor board. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to adjust for baseline imbalances, creating 152 well-balanced pairs (n = 304) for analysis. The primary endpoint was OS; secondary endpoints included LTC, QoL (EORTC QLQ-C30), adverse events (CTCAE v5.0), and resource utilization.ResultsAfter PSM, the surgery group demonstrated a superior median OS compared to the SBRT group (65.1 months vs. 53.4 months; HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.04–2.10, p = 0.030). However, 2- and 3-year LTC rates were comparable between surgery and SBRT (87.3% vs. 86.1% and 79.8% vs. 78.2%, p = 0.220). Surgery was associated with a significantly higher incidence of major adverse events (27.6% vs. 7.9%, p < 0.001) and a greater initial decline in QoL. By 12 months, the surgery group reported superior scores in global health status and key functional domains (all p < 0.05). Healthcare resource utilization was substantially higher for surgery, with longer hospital stays, higher readmission rates, and greater costs.ConclusionIn oligometastatic CRC, surgical resection is associated with a significant overall survival advantage compared to SBRT, albeit with increased upfront morbidity, resource use, and initial QoL impairment. SBRT offers a favorable safety profile and minimal short-term disruption with comparable local control. The choice between modalities represents a critical trade-off, underscoring the need for personalized, patient-centric decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15448-3.Keywords: Colorectal neoplasms, Stereotactic body radiotherapy, Metastasectomy, Oligometastasis, Propensity score

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with metastatic dissemination being the primary determinant of poor prognosis [1]. A distinct subset of patients presents with oligometastatic disease [2], a state first proposed by Hellman and Weichselbaum [3], characterized by a limited number of metastases with an intermediate metastatic potential, which may be amenable to aggressive local therapy with curative intent. Furthermore, contemporary classifications, such as that by Guckenberger et al. [4], help to further stratify this heterogeneous population based on timing, history, and dynamics of disease, which can inform prognosis and treatment selection. The management of this condition relies on two principal modalities: radical surgical resection and stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT). For patients with resectable oligometastatic disease, particularly in the liver, the established paradigm involves a combination of radical surgical resection and perioperative systemic chemotherapy, which offers the best chance of long-term survival [5]. However, a significant proportion of patients are not candidates for radical resection due to anatomical constraints, medical comorbidities, or patient preference. For these individuals, SBRT has emerged as a potent non-invasive alternative for achieving local disease control with curative intent [6].

Despite the widespread use of both strategies, high-level evidence from randomized trials directly comparing their long-term outcomes is scarce. Most existing data derive from single-arm studies or retrospective series, which are inherently susceptible to selection bias and confounding by indication [7–10]. For instance, studies focusing specifically on SBRT for oligometastatic CRC, such as those by Nicosia et al. [11]. and Cuccia et al. [12], have demonstrated its efficacy in achieving high local control rates and delaying the progression to polymetastatic disease. Patients selected for surgery often have significantly different baseline characteristics—such as tumor burden, lesion location, and performance status—compared to those referred for SBRT. Furthermore, while overall survival (OS) remains the most clinically meaningful endpoint, conventional use of progression-free survival is limited by its uncertain correlation with OS and susceptibility to assessment bias. Consequently, well-designed observational studies that employ robust methodology to minimize bias are urgently needed to generate reliable comparative evidence.

This prospective, observational cohort study was designed to address this evidence gap by directly comparing radical surgical resection and SBRT in patients with oligometastatic CRC. We specifically focused on OS as the primary endpoint to ensure clinical relevance. Secondary outcomes including local tumor control, quality of life, treatment-related toxicity, and healthcare resource utilization provide a comprehensive evaluation of efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness. By incorporating propensity score matching, we aimed to balance key prognostic factors between the treatment groups and reduce confounding. This study aims to provide robust, real-world evidence to guide clinical decision-making for patients with oligometastatic colorectal cancer.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, observational cohort study compared the long-term outcomes of radical surgical resection versus SBRT in patients with oligometastatic colorectal cancer. Eligible patients were adults with histologically confirmed primary colorectal cancer and 1–3 measurable metastatic lesions (in the liver, lung, or bone) confirmed by CT or MRI. Both patients with synchronous metastases (diagnosed at the same time as the primary) and those with metachronous metastases (developing after curative treatment of the primary) were eligible. Participants were required to have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1. Key exclusion criteria included uncontrolled comorbidities, prior treatment of the current metastatic lesions, or contraindications to either intervention.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Henan Cancer Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Treatment assignment

Treatment assignment (surgery vs. SBRT) was determined through a multidisciplinary tumor board discussion, which considered the treating surgeon’s experience, the patient’s personal preference, and the anatomical feasibility of each option. Efforts were made to ensure balanced baseline characteristics between the two groups. Given the non-randomized assignment, propensity score matching was employed to minimize selection bias and confounding.

Study outcomes

The primary endpoint of the study was OS, measured from the time of treatment initiation until death from any cause. Secondary endpoints encompassed efficacy, safety, patient-reported outcomes, and economic impact. These included Local Tumor Control (LTC), defined as the absence of progression within the treated metastatic lesions as assessed by independent radiological review using RECIST version 1.1; Quality of Life (QoL), evaluated with the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire (EORTC QLQ-C30) at baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months post-treatment; the incidence and severity of Treatment-related Adverse Events (AEs), systematically recorded and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0; and Healthcare Resource Utilization, for which data on hospitalization days, readmission rates, and total treatment-related expenses were collected to facilitate a comprehensive economic evaluation.

The EORTC QLQ-C30 is a robust and extensively validated 30-item patient self-report questionnaire designed specifically to assess the health-related quality of life of individuals with cancers [13]. Its comprehensive structure captures a multi-dimensional profile of the patient’s experience, encompassing five functional scales (physical, role, cognitive, emotional, and social), three symptom scales (fatigue, pain, and nausea/vomiting), a global health status/quality of life scale, and several single-item symptom measures assessing issues like dyspnea, insomnia, appetite loss, constipation, diarrhea, and financial difficulties.

AEs were categorized as minor or major based on their CTCAE v5.0 severity grade. Minor AEs were defined as those corresponding to Grade 1 (mild) or Grade 2 (moderate), representing events that are typically transient, require minimal or no intervention, and do not significantly disrupt activities of daily living. In contrast, major AEs encompassed Grade 3 (severe), Grade 4 (life-threatening), and Grade 5 (fatal) events. These necessitate significant medical intervention, often including hospitalization, result in persistent disability or self-care limitations, or are directly life-threatening.

SBRT technique and prescription

Patients in the SBRT group underwent a standardized simulation and treatment planning protocol. Immobilization was achieved using a vacuum cushion or body frame specific to the treatment site (thorax or abdomen). Four-dimensional computed tomography (4D-CT) simulation scans were acquired for all patients to account for respiratory motion and to define the internal target volume (ITV). The gross tumor volume (GTV) was delineated on the average intensity projection of the 4D-CT, fused with diagnostic contrast-enhanced CT and/or liver-specific MRI for optimal visualization. The clinical target volume (CTV) was defined as identical to the GTV, with no elective nodal coverage. The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by expanding the ITV by an isotropic margin of 5 mm. Treatment was delivered using volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) on a linear accelerator equipped with daily cone-beam CT (CBCT) for image guidance. Given the known radioresistance of colorectal metastases, an aggressive, ablative dosing strategy was employed. The prescription dose was individualized based on lesion location, size, and proximity to serial organs-at-risk (OARs), adhering strictly to established OAR constraints. The predominant dose-fractionation schemes were: 50 Gy in 5 fractions for liver and lung lesions > 2 cm or adjacent to critical structures, and 60 Gy in 3 fractions for peripheral lung lesions ≤ 2 cm. The prescription isodose line ranged from 70% to 80%, ensuring that at least 95% of the PTV received 100% of the prescription dose. This corresponds to a median biologically effective dose (BED10), using an α/β ratio of 10 Gy, of 100 Gy (range, 83.3–180 Gy), confirming the delivery of definitively ablative radiotherapy across all treated metastases.

The multidisciplinary tumor board documented the primary reason for recommending SBRT over surgical resection for each patient in the SBRT group, and the primary reasons were documented: anatomically unfavorable lesions (48.8%), medical inoperability (29.5%), patient preference (15.7%), and extensive prior surgery (6.0%).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline patient characteristics. Time-to-event endpoints were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves, with between-group comparisons performed with the log-rank test. Overall survival was the primary time-to-event endpoint. LTC was analyzed both as a time-to-event outcome and by comparing the LTC rates between groups using Chi-square tests. To address confounding and selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was implemented. Propensity scores were generated using a logistic regression model that included the following covariates: age, primary tumor stage, number of metastases, location of metastases, and ECOG performance status. A one-to-one nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations was used to create balanced cohorts.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were applied to the matched cohorts to adjust for potential residual confounders for time-to-event outcomes. Quality of life data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models for repeated measures. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 28.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline data

A total of 478 patients with oligometastatic CRC were enrolled in the study between January 2015 and January 2025. Prior to matching, 312 patients (65.3%) underwent radical surgical resection (Surgery group), while 166 patients (34.7%) were treated with SBRT (SBRT group). In the surgical group, an R0 resection was achieved in 278 patients (89.1%), while an R1 resection was documented in 34 patients (10.9%). No R2 resections were performed. Before PSM, Significant imbalances were observed in the baseline characteristics of the two groups, reflecting the non-randomized treatment assignment. Patients in the Surgery group were significantly younger than those in the SBRT group (median age 62 vs. 71 years, p < 0.001) and had a superior performance status (ECOG 0: 78.2% vs. 58.4%, p < 0.001). The distribution of metastatic sites also differed substantially; the Surgery group had a higher proportion of patients with liver-only metastases (68.6% vs. 42.2%), whereas the SBRT group had a higher prevalence of lung-only metastases (31.3% vs. 18.9%) and a greater number of patients with metastases in challenging surgical locations, such as perivascular hepatic lesions or central lung lesions (12.0% vs. 3.8%, p < 0.001). Furthermore, patients in the SBRT group had a slightly higher median number of metastases (2 vs. 1, p = 0.012) and were more likely to have metachronous disease presentation (65.1% vs. 54.2%, p = 0.020). The SBRT group had a higher prevalence of poor-prognosis BRAF V600E mutations (9.0% vs. 3.2%, p = 0.007) and right-sided primary tumors (42.8% vs. 29.5%, p = 0.003) compared to the Surgery group. But propensity score matching successfully created two well-balanced cohorts of 152 matched pairs (n = 304 total). After matching, there were no statistically significant differences in any of the key covariates included in the model, confirming the success of the matching procedure in creating comparable groups for biological aggressiveness (Table 1).

OS

The median follow-up time for the entire cohort was 52.3 months (interquartile range, 36.1–70.8 months). In the overall population before matching, the Surgery group demonstrated a superior survival outcome compared to the SBRT group, with a median OS of 68.4 months (95% CI, 61.2–75.6) versus 42.1 months (95% CI, 36.5–47.7), and a 3-year OS rate of 82.1% versus 58.3% (log-rank p < 0.001). Following propensity score matching, the survival difference between the groups was attenuated but remained statistically significant. In the matched cohort, the median OS was 65.1 months (95% CI, 57.9–72.3) for the Surgery group and 53.4 months (95% CI, 46.8–60.0) for the SBRT group. The 3-year OS rates were 80.5% and 70.1% for the Surgery and SBRT groups, respectively (log-rank p = 0.0013). The Kaplan-Meier survival curves for the matched cohorts are presented in Fig. 1.

Overall survival in patients treated by surgery or SBRT before and after PSM

To further adjust for potential residual confounding, a Cox proportional hazards model was fitted to the matched data. In the univariate analysis, treatment with SBRT was associated with a significantly higher risk of death compared to surgery (Hazard Ratio [HR] 1.52, 95% CI 1.07–2.15, p = 0.019). Other factors including metastasis location significantly associated with worse OS in univariate analysis included the presence of 2–3 metastases (vs. a single metastasis), right-sided primary tumor location, and the presence of a BRAF mutation. In the multivariable analysis adjusting for these significant covariates, SBRT remained an independent predictor of inferior OS (Adjusted HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.04–2.10, p = 0.030). The presence of a BRAF mutation was the strongest prognostic factor for poor survival (Adjusted HR 2.95, 95% CI 1.65–5.28, p < 0.001), followed by having 2–3 metastases (Adjusted HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.15–2.37, p = 0.007) and right-sided tumor origin (Adjusted HR 1.49, 95% CI 1.04–2.13, p = 0.028) (Table 2).

LTC

Local tumor control analysis in the propensity score-matched cohort (n = 304) revealed comparable outcomes between surgical resection and SBRT. The 2-year LTC rates were 87.3% for surgery and 86.1% for SBRT, with 3-year rates of 79.8% and 78.2%, respectively (Fig. 2, log-rank p = 0.220). In univariate analysis, treatment modality was not associated with local control (SBRT vs. Surgery: HR 1.12, 95% CI 0.83–1.51, p = 0.220). However, several lesion-specific factors significantly predicted local failure, including multiple metastases (2–3 vs. 1 metastasis: HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.02–1.98, p = 0.038), larger lesion size (per 1-cm increase: HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.02–1.30, p = 0.024), and high-risk anatomical locations (HR 1.82, 95% CI 1.17–2.83, p = 0.008). Multivariable analysis confirmed that treatment modality remained non-significant (Adjusted HR 1.08 for SBRT vs. Surgery, 95% CI 0.79–1.48, p = 0.628), while multiple metastases (Adjusted HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.02–1.98, p = 0.039), and larger lesion size (Adjusted HR 1.16 per 1-cm increase, 95% CI 1.02–1.30, p = 0.025) persisted as independent predictors of local failure (Table 3).

A subanalysis was conducted on the 149 patients in the matched cohort with metastases confined to the liver. On multivariable analysis of this liver-only sub-cohort, treatment modality (SBRT vs. Surgery: Adjusted HR 1.21, 95% CI 0.78–1.87, p = 0.394) was not an independent predictor of local failure. The only significant predictor of local recurrence in the liver was larger lesion size (Adjusted HR 1.24 per 1-cm increase, 95% CI 1.08–1.43, p = 0.002) (Supplementary Table 1).

QoL

There was a distinct pattern of QoL recovery between the two treatment groups (Table 4). The SBRT group experienced minimal short-term disruption, with most scales showing little change from baseline at the 3-month follow-up. In contrast, the Surgery group exhibited a significant initial decline in physical domains (Physical, Role, and Social Functioning) and a concomitant increase in related symptoms (Fatigue, Nausea/Vomiting, Appetite Loss, Constipation), consistent with the expected impact of a major surgical intervention and recovery. By the 6-month assessment, the Surgery group showed substantial recovery, with most scores returning to near-baseline levels. At the 12-month endpoint, a clear long-term trend emerged: patients in the Surgery group reported statistically superior scores in key functional domains, including Global Health Status/QoL (p = 0.013), Physical Functioning (p = 0.007), Role Functioning (p = 0.021), Emotional Functioning (p = 0.045), and Social Functioning (p = 0.038), compared to the SBRT group. All significant symptom differences observed at 3 months had resolved by the 12-month mark, with no remaining clinically relevant disparities in pain, fatigue, or gastrointestinal symptoms.

Adverse event

Patients in the surgery group experienced a significantly higher overall incidence of both minor (75.7% vs. 55.9%, p < 0.001) and major (27.6% vs. 7.9%, p < 0.001) adverse events. The invasive nature of surgical resection was reflected in a higher burden of perioperative symptoms such as pain, fatigue, and nausea/vomiting, as well as specific major complications including postoperative hemorrhage (9.9%), anastomotic leak (6.6%), and bile leak (5.9%). In contrast, the toxicity profile for SBRT was notably more favorable, characterized predominantly by mild-to-moderate, transient events such as transaminitis (26.3%) and radiation dermatitis (14.5%). Although rare, the most serious AEs for SBRT were symptomatic radiation pneumonitis (3.9%) and gastrointestinal ulceration (3.3%) (Supplementary Table 2).

Healthcare resource utilization

The initial treatment burden was substantially higher for surgery, with a median hospitalization duration of 8 days (IQR 6–12) compared to just 1 day (IQR 1–2) for SBRT (p < 0.001). This was compounded by a more than threefold higher 30-day readmission rate in the surgical cohort (18.4% vs. 5.9%, p = 0.001), primarily for managing postoperative complications. Consequently, the mean total treatment-related cost for surgery was significantly greater than for SBRT ($45,200 ± $12,500 vs. $28,700 ± $8,900, p < 0.001). These findings underscore that the long-term survival benefit associated with surgery comes at the cost of greater initial morbidity and a higher consumption of healthcare resources (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

Our study was conducted within the contemporary clinical context where combined modality treatment, involving radical surgical resection and polychemotherapy, represents the cornerstone of management for resectable oligometastatic CRC. The central question addressed by our analysis is not to challenge this standard for clearly resectable disease, but to rigorously evaluate the comparative effectiveness of SBRT in a propensity-matched cohort where both options were considered viable by a multidisciplinary team. Our principal findings illuminate a critical therapeutic trade-off. Surgical resection was independently associated with a superior OS benefit, with a median OS of 65.1 months versus 53.4 months for SBRT. However, this potential survival advantage comes with significant costs: a markedly higher burden of major adverse events, a substantial initial decline in QoL, and significantly greater healthcare resource utilization. In stark contrast, SBRT demonstrated non-inferior LTC, a vastly superior safety profile, minimal short-term disruption to QoL, and a dramatically lower initial treatment burden. These results underscore that the choice between these modalities is not a simple determination of superiority but a complex, patient-centric decision that must balance the pursuit of long-term survival against the acceptance of upfront morbidity and resource expenditure.

The observed survival advantage for surgery, which persisted even after rigorous adjustment for key prognostic factors via propensity score matching and multivariable analysis, reinforces the long-held oncological principle that complete macroscopic and microscopic resection offers the best chance for long-term disease control and potential cure in oligometastatic disease. This finding is consistent with the vast corpus of retrospective data that has historically formed the evidence base for metastasectomy. The biological rationale is compelling: surgery physically eradicates all visible disease, potentially removing reservoirs of resistant clones and preventing further metastatic seeding [14]. Furthermore, it provides a complete pathological specimen for analysis, allowing for confirmation of diagnosis, assessment of treatment response to prior systemic therapy, and enabling comprehensive genomic profiling, which can guide subsequent systemic treatment choices [15] However, our results appear to stand in contrast to the findings of the systematic review by Gooijer et al. [16], which concluded that pooled five-year OS rates for patients with oligometastatic colorectal lung metastases were comparable between metastasectomy and SBRT (52.2% vs. 45.0%, p = 0.213). This discrepancy warrants careful analysis. First, the Gooijer review [16] highlighted a critical limitation: the included studies were of low quality on average (per GRADE assessment) and the patient cohorts were fundamentally different. Patients selected for SBRT were often older, had a lower performance status, had a higher prevalence of previous liver metastases, and received less adjuvant chemotherapy. This significant selection bias, which their analysis could not fully adjust for, likely negatively impacted the observed outcomes for SBRT. Our study, by employing propensity score matching, sought to create more comparable groups, thereby providing a less confounded estimate of the treatment effect. Second, the Gooijer review [16] aggregated data across many heterogeneous studies, whereas our prospective cohort, while observational, followed a standardized protocol. The survival difference we observed may thus more accurately reflect the relative efficacy of the two modalities in well-matched, modern patient cohorts.

Another layer of complexity is added by the pan-cancer SABR-COMET trial, which demonstrated an OS benefit for SBRT plus standard of care versus standard of care alone in an oligometastatic population [15]. The divergence from our findings can be partly explained by the unique tumor biology of CRC. Colorectal metastases are known to be relatively radioresistant compared to metastases from primaries like breast or prostate cancer, potentially due to hypoxic fractions and specific molecular pathways. Therefore, while SBRT is highly effective across many cancer types, its ability to achieve durable, long-term control in CRC may be inherently lower than that of surgery, a factor that could ultimately translate into an OS difference.

A pivotal finding of our study is that LTC was statistically comparable between the two modalities in the matched cohort, with 2-year and 3-year rates differing by less than 2%. This demonstrates the remarkable efficacy of modern, image-guided SBRT, which delivers ablative radiation doses with high precision, effectively sterilizing a high proportion of treated lesions. This challenges the historical notion that surgery is unequivocally superior for local disease eradication and validates SBRT as a highly potent local therapy. Our reported LTC rates are consistent with previous institutional experiences. Nicosia et al. [11] reported a 2-year local progression-free survival of 80% for lung oligometastases from CRC treated with SBRT, while a broader analysis by Cuccia et al. [12] that included various primary histologies showed 2- and 4-year LTC rates of 92.4% and 89.8%, respectively, for pulmonary oligometastases. This collective evidence challenges the historical notion that surgery is unequivocally superior for local disease eradication and validates SBRT as a highly potent local therapy. Nevertheless, a deeper analysis suggests caution in interpreting long-term equivalency. Our analysis identified that factors such as larger lesion size and the presence of multiple metastases were independent predictors of local failure, regardless of treatment modality. This aligns with the historical data on thermal ablation. For instance, Nishiwada et al. [17] reported a dramatically higher local recurrence rate (LRR) for radiofrequency ablation (RFA) compared to surgical resection (46.9% vs. 13.3%), with efficacy diminishing sharply for lesions larger than 20 mm. Similarly, the analysis of EORTC trials by Tanis et al. [18] suggested that while local control for small metastases (≤ 3 cm) can be comparable between RFA and resection, this parity is lost with increasing tumor size. Although SBRT is technologically superior to RFA, the principle may hold: its therapeutic window is most favorable for smaller lesions. Crucially, the Gooijer systematic review [16] reported a pooled LRR that was significantly higher following SBRT than surgery (28.1% vs. 10.5%, p < 0.001). This stark difference suggests that while SBRT can achieve excellent initial control, its long-term durability for CRC metastases may be inferior to the permanence of a surgical resection. In our study, with a median follow-up of 52.3 months, we may not yet have captured the full extent of late local recurrences in the SBRT arm, which could potentially impact longer-term survival outcomes. The “salvageability” of local failures, as mentioned by Gooijer et al. [16], where 52% of SBRT failures could undergo salvage surgery, is a critical mitigating factor that may blunt the impact of higher LRR on OS, but it also reintroduces the morbidity of surgery at a later date.

Perhaps the most striking differential between the two treatments lies in their safety and impact on patients’ lives. Our data paint a clear and expected picture: the invasive nature of surgical resection carries a significantly higher risk of complications. Patients in the surgery group experienced a more than threefold higher rate of major adverse events (27.6% vs. 7.9%), including serious, specific complications like postoperative hemorrhage, anastomotic leak, and bile leak, which were absent in the SBRT group. The SBRT toxicity profile was predominantly mild and transient, characterized by issues like transaminitis and dermatitis, with serious events like radiation pneumonitis being rare. The trajectory of QoL further elucidates the patient experience. The SBRT group experienced minimal short-term disruption, reflecting its non-invasive, outpatient nature. In contrast, the surgery group suffered a significant initial decline across multiple physical and social functioning domains, a predictable consequence of a major operation and its recovery period. This initial disadvantage for surgery is a key consideration for patients for whom rapid return to normal life is a priority.

However, the long-term QoL findings are provocative and add a crucial layer of complexity. By the 12-month mark, the surgery group not only recovered but reported statistically superior scores in key domains, including Global Health Status, compared to the SBRT group. This suggests that for patients who successfully navigate the perioperative period, there may be a sustained psychological and physical benefit. This could stem from the psychological reassurance of having undergone a definitive, “curative-intent” procedure with the tangible removal of disease, a feeling that may be less pronounced with a non-invasive treatment like SBRT. This nuanced QoL finding underscores that the assessment cannot be limited to the immediate post-treatment period. The potential for long-term enhanced well-being following recovery from surgery is an important factor in the decision-making process and is rightly a key secondary endpoint in ongoing trials like the PUCC trial [14].

The economic implications of our findings are substantial and align directly with the clinical outcomes. The initial treatment burden was overwhelmingly higher for surgery. The median hospitalization duration was eight times longer (8 days vs. 1 day), and the 30-day readmission rate was more than three times higher, driven by the need to manage postoperative complications. Consequently, the mean total treatment cost for surgery was significantly greater. This analysis provides a clear economic argument for SBRT, demonstrating its efficiency as a high-tech, low-touch procedure. In healthcare systems with constrained resources, the significantly lower upfront cost of SBRT is a powerful consideration. However, a true cost-effectiveness analysis would need to model long-term outcomes, including the costs of managing local recurrences after SBRT and the potential salvage therapies required, which could narrow the economic gap over a patient’s lifetime.

Our results, when synthesized with the broader literature, do not support a one-size-fits-all approach but rather advocate for a highly personalized treatment strategy. The ongoing PUCC trial protocol explicitly acknowledges this clinical equipoise, particularly for patients with multiple (≥ 3) metastases, and aims to provide level-one evidence [14]. Until such data mature, our study offers a framework for multidisciplinary decision-making. The choice between surgery and SBRT hinges on a careful evaluation of tumor factors, patient factors, and system factors. For the patient with a low comorbidity burden, excellent performance status, and a strong psychological preference for the most definitive local therapy, surgical resection may be the preferred option. The potential for a long-term survival advantage and the possibility of superior long-term QoL, despite the higher upfront risks and costs, may be acceptable. For the older patient, one with significant comorbidities, a lesion in a surgically challenging location, or a strong desire to avoid major surgery and its recovery, SBRT represents an outstanding alternative. It provides high rates of local control with minimal morbidity and a negligible impact on short-term QoL. Its role is also crucial in treating patients with metachronous recurrences after prior surgeries, where repeat resection may carry prohibitive risks. Furthermore, a combined-modality approach should be considered. For patients with bilateral lung metastases, a strategy of resecting the larger or more accessible lesion on one side (to obtain pathological confirmation and genomic data) while treating contralateral lesions with SBRT represents a rational synthesis of the advantages of both modalities.

Our study utilized the conventional numerical definition of oligometastasis (1–3 lesions). While the Guckenberger classification was not formally applied at enrollment [4], its principles are highly relevant to our findings. Retrospectively, our cohort likely comprised a mix of states, including genuine (synchronous) and induced/recurrent (metachronous) oligometastasis. We hypothesize that the survival benefit of surgery may be most pronounced in the former group. Our data, which identified the number of metastases and lesion size as key predictors of outcome, support the classification’s core premise that oligometastatic disease is biologically heterogeneous. Applying such a framework prospectively could significantly improve predictive value, better identifying patients who benefit most from surgery versus those for whom SBRT provides an optimal balance of efficacy and tolerability.

Our study has several limitations. Despite the use of propensity score matching, residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as detailed molecular profiles beyond BRAF/RAS or subtle differences in disease volume, may persist. While our matching incorporated ECOG performance status, a more granular comorbidity index was not available. Patients selected for SBRT are often medically less fit, and thus, a higher rate of non-cancer-related deaths in the SBRT group could have contributed to the observed overall survival difference. In this context, an analysis of cancer-specific survival would have been highly informative to isolate the pure antitumor efficacy of each modality; the unavailability of cause-of-death data for cancer-specific survival analysis is a limitation of our study. The non-randomized design means that unquantifiable selection biases inherent in multidisciplinary board decisions could influence the outcomes. The follow-up, while substantial, may still be insufficient to capture very late recurrences after SBRT, which could affect long-term OS. Our cohort included mixed metastatic sites (liver, lung, bone); while this increases generalizability, it may obscure site-specific nuances in treatment efficacy. Furthermore, the 10-year enrollment period, while enabling adequate patient accrual, means that changes in systemic therapy regimens, surgical techniques, and SBRT technology over time could have influenced outcomes, although our treatment protocols were standardized throughout the study period.

In conclusion, while combined modality therapy with radical resection remains the benchmark for resectable oligometastatic CRC, our comparative analysis demonstrates that in well-matched patients where both modalities are considered, surgical resection is associated with a significant OS advantage compared to SBRT. However, this benefit is counterbalanced by SBRT’s superior safety profile, more favorable short-term quality of life trajectory, and significantly lower initial healthcare resource utilization. Therefore, for patients in whom radical resection is not feasible or preferred, SBRT provides a highly effective alternative with comparable medium-term local control. The decision requires a shared process that thoughtfully weighs the pursuit of maximal survival against the imperative to minimize treatment-related morbidity and cost.
