Resource-Free Quantum Hamiltonian Learning Below the Standard Quantum Limit
Bora Baran, Timothy Heightman

Abstract
Accurate and resource-efficient estimation of quantum Hamiltonians is crucial for developing practical quantum technologies, yet current methods typically demand entanglement resources or dynamical control. Here, we demonstrate a method that surpasses the standard quantum limit without requiring entanglement resources, coherent measurements, or dynamical control. Our method relies on trajectory-based Hamiltonian learning, in which we apply local, randomized pre-processing to probe states and apply maximum-likelihood estimation to optimally scheduled Pauli measurements. Analytically, we establish the emergence of a transient Heisenberg-limited regime for short-time probes for our procedure. Furthermore, we outline how to estimate all Hamiltonian parameters in parallel using ensembles of probe states, removing the need for parameter isolation and structural priors. Finally, we supplement our findings with a numerical study, learning multiple disordered, anisotropic Heisenberg models for a 1D chain of spin-1/2 particles, featuring local transverse fields with both nearest- and next-nearest-neighbour interactions, as well as a gapless XXZ Hamiltonian. Our numerics show that our method needs only one shot per Pauli measurement, making it well-suited for experimental scenarios. The code for our method is available online and open-source.

2.1
Defining Fisher information and Cramér-Rao bound

Consider a quantum system governed by a Hamiltonian, whose spectral decomposition in the Pauli basis reads,

H(\theta)=\sum_{j}\theta_{j}P_{j},\quad P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}=\bigl\{\sigma_{i_{1}}\otimes\sigma_{i_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{i_{n}}\;\big|\;\sigma_{i_{k}}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}\bigr\},

(1)

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes a set of unknown parameters, and
\mathcal{P}_{n}

is the
n
-qubit Pauli group. The primary objective of Hamiltonian learning is to estimate the coefficients
\theta_{j}
through experimental observations and, if necessary, to infer the Hamiltonian’s operator structure. Learning scenarios are generally categorized based on whether prior information about the Hamiltonian’s structure is available. When this is the case, only the coefficients in a given operator basis need to be tuned, and we arrive at the White-box scenario. On the other hand, without prior knowledge about the Hamiltonians structure, we have the Black-box scenario, where both the Hamiltonians coefficients and structure (often in the Pauli basis) must be uncovered
[
24
]
. To understand the conditions in which HL parameters are optimally learnable, we can resort to the Fisher information
[
63
,
64
]
.

The Fisher information tells us the amount of information about the parameter
\theta
that can be extracted from observation of a probabilistic observable, and is given by
[
63
]
,

\mathcal{I}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p(x|\theta)\right)^{2}\right],

(2)

where
p(x|\theta)
is the likelihood function
[
65
]

[
66
]
. In the case of classical measurements, the general Fisher information becomes the classical Fisher information (CFI). The CFI associated with a probability distribution of measurement outcomes is given by,

F_{C}(t)=\sum_{j}\frac{1}{p_{j}(t)}\left(\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial\theta}{(t)}\right)^{2},

(3)

where
p_{j}{}(t)=|\langle m_{j}|\psi_{t}^{(b)}\rangle|^{2}
denotes the probability of obtaining a measurement outcome
j
at time
t
for a given initial state
|\psi_{t}^{(b)}\rangle

[
56
,
55
]
. The collected information can be used to estimate a governing Hamiltonian’s true parameters up to a precision dependent on the amount of Fisher information. A fundamental limit on the precision of an estimator
\hat{\theta}
is given by the Cramér-Rao (CR) bound,

\Delta\theta\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{I}(\theta)}}

(4)

where
\Delta\theta
is the estimation error and
\mathcal{I}(\theta)
is the Fisher information
[
66
]
.

Hence, at the level of Fisher information, the SQL scaling of estimator in the parameter
\theta
is linear,
F\propto t
, since this gives (by the CR bound)
\Delta\theta=\mathcal{O}(t^{-1/2})
. In contrast, Heisenberg limited scaling at the level of Fisher information requires
F\propto t^{2}
, so that by the CR bound, we get
\Delta\theta=\mathcal{O}(t^{-1})
. In the following we will make use of the Bachmann-Landau notation to describe the asymptotic scaling behaviour of functions (see appendix
A
).

2.2
Time Evolution of Fisher Information under Local Randomness

In this section we investigate how the classical Fisher information scales in the presence of randomness in both the initial quantum state and the measurement basis. Specifically, we sample from ensembles of one-local Haar random initial states and Pauli product measurement bases. We formalize the preparation of randomized initial states with the following definition:

Definition 1

(Spread State)
.

Let
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
be any fixed
n
-qubit basis state of the Hamiltonian of interest. For each qubit
j
, draw Euler angles
(\xi_{j},\chi_{j},\phi_{j})
i.i.d. from the single-qubit Haar measure, and define

U_{\rm spread}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}R_{z}(\xi_{j})\,R_{y}(\chi_{j})\,R_{z}(\phi_{j}),\qquad{|\psi_{\rm spread}\rangle}=U_{\rm spread}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}.

(5)

States of this form resemble the
delocalized
states considered in prior work on Hamiltonian learning
[
33
]
, where broad spectral support was shown to enhance learning accuracy and robustness. However, unlike that work, we define a concrete and experimentally simpler subclass using only local Haar-random unitaries, without loss of generality. Next to that, we also consider random measurements taken in a local Pauli-product basis
[
67
]
.

Under these ensembles of local Haar-random rotations, and randomly chosen Pauli measurements, we now seek to characterise the time-dependent behaviour of the classical Fisher information,

\mathcal{I}_{C}(t)=\sum_{j}\frac{(\partial_{\theta}p_{j}(t))^{2}}{p_{j}(t)},

(6)

where
p_{j}(t)
is the probability of outcome
j
after evolution for time
t
under
H(\theta)
. The time evolution under our scenario is summarised by the following theorem

Theorem 1

(Time Evolution of Fisher Information under Local Randomness is Heisenberg-Limited)
.

Let the Hamiltonian be
H(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{j}\theta_{j}P_{j}
, with
P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and let
d=2^{n}
be the Hilbert space dimension. Fix any parameter component
\theta_{k}
. Then, under spread-state initializations (Definition
1
) and random local Pauli measurements, the expected classical Fisher information satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{I}_{C}(t)\right]=\Theta(t^{2}),

(7)

for all fixed
t=o(1)
, before saturation or revival effects occur. Moreover, this scaling becomes increasingly typical across realizations as the system size increases.

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.
∎

Building on the expected behavior established in Theorem
1
, we formalize how this Heisenberg-limited scaling emerges empirically through averaging. As the number of sampled initial states and measurement bases increases, the empirical Fisher information converges to its expected value, which is captured in the following corollary:

Corollary 1

(Empirical Convergence of Fisher Information)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{rk}(t)
denote the classical Fisher information computed from the
r
-th spread state
\psi_{0}^{(r)}
and the
k
-th Pauli measurement basis
p_{k}
at time
t
. Then, the empirical average over all such realizations satisfies:

\lim_{R,\,K\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{RK}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{I}_{rk}(t)\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{I}_{C}(t)\right]=\Theta(t^{2}),

(8)

where the expectation is taken over the spread-state ensemble (Definition
1
) and the uniform distribution over random Pauli measurement bases.

This result shows that even with fully separable experiments, random product states and local Pauli measurements, we can collectively extract information with optimal time scaling. The Fisher information grows quadratically in
t
, consistent with Heisenberg-limited sensitivity
\Delta\theta=\mathcal{O}(t^{-1})
, despite the absence of entanglement.

Remark 1
.

Notably, the expected Heisenberg-limited scaling in Theorem
1
also becomes increasingly sharp as the system size grows. This sharpening arises from the suppression of statistical fluctuations in the measurement probabilities
p_{j}(t)
, which become tightly concentrated around their mean values. As a result, Hoeffding-type concentration bounds apply with increasing strength
[
68
]
, and the classical Fisher information
\mathcal{I}_{C}(t)
becomes sharply peaked around its expectation value. This ensures that the
t^{2}
scaling predicted by the theorem is increasingly typical across random realizations as the number of qubits grows, and more difficult to observe at very small systems.

2.3
Harnessing the Heisenberg-limited Scaling

In the previous section (Sec.
2.2
), we showed that each Hamiltonian parameter can achieve Heisenberg-limited scaling with respect to the evolution time
t
. However, in the case of using dynamical data for Hamiltonian learning, multiple time stamps are being used. Naturally, the scheduling of these measurements are a part of the process, but it is more then a mere practical detail. Here, we show, that the choice of timing affects how the Fisher information scales with total experiment time. This will allow us to show analytically that one can achieve Heisenberg-limited scaling in total experiment time by optimising this choice with respect to the Fisher information.

This approach is related in spirit to the measurement time optimization considered in Ref.
[
47
]
, where a single fixed time is optimally chosen to minimize reconstruction error. However, in contrast to that work, we consider the cumulative effect of distributing measurements across multiple distinct time stamps, and show explicitly how their scheduling, can be optimized to asymptotically recover Heisenberg-limited scaling

We first introduce a family of sampling schedules
t_{k}=\Delta t\,k^{\alpha}
, derive the resulting cumulative Fisher-information scaling in Proposition
1
, and then discuss practical factors for choosing
\alpha
.

Our aim is to choose
\{t_{k}\}
so that, for a fixed total experiment time,

T_{\rm tot}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}t_{k},

(9)

the cumulative Fisher information,

I_{\rm tot}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}\mathcal{F}(t_{k}),

(10)

grows as quickly as possible. We may generally write Fisher information’s scaling in time as
\mathcal{F}(t)=\Theta(t^{\gamma_{0}})
, so that we can say that
\gamma_{0}=2
corresponds to Heisenberg-limited scaling. For our non-uniform sampling schedule, we take

t_{k}=\Delta t\,k^{\alpha},\qquad k=1,\dots,m_{t},

(11)

with
\Delta t>0
and
\alpha>-1
. This leads us to the following proposition:

Proposition 1

(Cumulative Fisher-Information Scaling)
.

If
t_{k}=\Delta t\,k^{\alpha}
for
k=1,\dots,m_{t}
, and each
t_{k}
lies within the regime where the Fisher information obeys
\mathcal{F}(t_{k})=\Theta(t_{k}^{\gamma_{0}})
(with
\gamma_{0}=2
when with Heisenberg-limited scaling), then the total Fisher information

I_{\rm tot}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}\mathcal{F}(t_{k}),

(12)

scales with the total experimental time
T_{\rm tot}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}t_{k}
as

I_{\rm tot}=\Theta\bigl(T_{\rm tot}^{\,p}\bigr),\qquad p=\frac{\alpha\gamma_{0}+1}{\alpha+1}+O(m_{t}^{-1}),

(13)

where
p
is the effective scaling exponent that includes finite-
m_{t}
corrections.

Proof.
See Appendix
C
.

With the presented scheduling it is possible to tune
\alpha
while
\gamma_{0}=2
, so that one can continuously move from uniform sampling with
\alpha=0
, giving
I_{\rm tot}=\Theta(T_{\rm tot})
(standard quantum limit), to strongly non-uniform sampling with
\alpha\to\infty
, giving

I_{\rm tot}=\Theta(T_{\rm tot}^{p})

p\to 2
. In other words, we asymptotically recover the Heisenberg limit also with respect to total experiment time when
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
.

2.4
Fisher Information Diagonalization

When learning a Hamiltonian with multiple components
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})
, different components can influence the measurement outcome in overlapping or entangled ways. In such cases, adjusting or estimating one parameter effectively depends on the values of other parameter. Hence, many HL strategies must be carefully tailored to isolate or selectively probe each parameter one by one, which usually requires some kind of structural prior such as locality sparsity or commutativity
[
57
,
31
]
. Ideally, one would like a learning strategy where all parameters can be estimated independently and simultaneously. Here we benefit again from the randomness of state spreading. Firstly, when spread states are used as initial states, they produce measurement statistics that are sensitive to all components of the Hamiltonian, regardless of structure. That is, for a generic measurement outcome
i
, the partial derivatives satisfy

\frac{\partial p_{i}}{\partial\theta_{j}}\neq 0\quad\text{for all }j,

(14)

meaning all parameters influence the observed dynamics. We hence arrive at the following proposition,

Proposition 2

(Generic Sensitivity)
.

For a product state
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}{|\phi_{j}\rangle}
, where
{|\phi\rangle}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}{|\phi_{j}\rangle}
is any fixed product eigenstate of a Pauli string Hamiltonian and each local unitary
U_{j}\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
is independently drawn from the Haar measure, the short-time measurement probability

p(b,t)\approx|\langle b|\psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}-2t\,\mathrm{Im}\left[\sum_{a}\theta_{a}\langle b|\psi_{0}\rangle^{*}\langle b|P_{a}|\psi_{0}\rangle\right]

(15)

is generically sensitive to
every
Hamiltonian parameter
\theta_{a}
. That is, with overwhelming probability, the choice of the
\{U_{j}\}
, the overlaps
\langle b|\psi_{0}\rangle
and
\langle b|P_{a}|\psi_{0}\rangle
are non-zero for all
a
, so that no term in the Hamiltonian is missed at first order in time.

Proof.

See Appendix
D
.
∎

We note that these states can still produce parameter interdependence in the Fisher information due to cross terms of the form,

\frac{1}{p_{i}}\frac{\partial p_{i}}{\partial\theta_{j}}\frac{\partial p_{i}}{\partial\theta_{k}}\neq 0\quad\text{for }j\neq k,

(16)

as discussed in the general theory of quantum multi parameter estimation
[
69
]
.
However, using an ensemble of spread initial states asymptotically renders the Fisher information matrix diagonal, meaning that these cross-terms vanish in expectation and the parameter directions become statistically uncorrelated.
This is in line with related works that discuss similar ideas in the context of the quantum Fisher information matrix (QFIM) for parameter estimation
[
69
]
, with our work showing how the classical Fisher information diagonalises in the specific context of Hamiltonian learning.

Theorem 2

(Fisher Information Diagonalization)
.

Let
H(\theta)
be a Hamiltonian parametrized by
d
real parameters
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})
, so that
H(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{j}\theta_{j}H_{j}
, and let each experiment prepare an initial state via local Haar-random unitaries applied to a fixed product state, with measurements in a fixed Pauli product basis. Let
\mathcal{I}_{r}(\theta)
be the Fisher information matrix resulting from the
r
-th experiment, for
r=1,\dots,R
. For small times
t>0
, the ensemble-averaged Fisher information matrix converges to a diagonal form,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathcal{I}_{r}(\theta)\right)=\mathrm{diag}(c_{1},\dots,c_{d}),\quad\text{with }c_{j}>0.

(17)

Proof.

See
Appendix
E
.
∎

This removes the need to design separate experiments for each Hamiltonian term, and allows all Hamiltonian components to be learned simultaneously from the same dataset, without needing to isolate or separate them one at a time, which becomes important if we want to learn all parameters at once with Heisenberg-limited scaling.

Ground Truth Hamiltonian Models

5
Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced a Hamiltonian learning strategy that surpasses the Standard Quantum Limited (SQL) scaling in total experiment time, and asymptotically approaches Heisenberg scaling. To do so, we used one-local Haar-random unitaries, and scheduling of projective measurements in a fixed-basis, without requiring entanglement, coherent measurements, or dynamical multi-qubit control.

Our first theoretical insight was the derivation of a transient Heisenberg-limited regime in the time evolution of the classical Fisher information when using ensembles of random initial states and random Pauli measurement bases. Here, there is a clear parallel to classical shadows
[
72
]
, which also rely on one-local Haar-random unitaries to enhance measurement statistics of states and observables. However, we note that this strategy differs from classical shadows because our algorithm pre-rotates the probe state
before
it evolves in time. Non-spread initial states might fail to activate the smallest pairwise eigenvalue differences. As such, our strategy would fail if using one-local rotations
after
evolving a fixed probe state, as might be done in classical shadows.

We established that the timing of measurements in dynamical data collection as a fundamental factor influencing how the Fisher information scales with total experiment time. We proved that, while individual measurements follow Heisenberg-limited scaling with their individual evolution time
t
, the cumulative scaling limit may vary significantly. We provided a class of non-uniform measurement time schedules that asymptotically recover the Heisenberg-limited scaling with respect to total experiment time. We showed that tuning the characteristic sampling exponent
\alpha
allows continuous interpolation between standard quantum limit and Heisenberg-limited scaling. Furthermore, we addressed the challenge of multi-parameter learning by showing that ensembles of one-locally Haar-random
product
states also lead to rendering the Fisher information matrix asymptotically diagonal. This diagonalization removes statistical interdependence between parameters. It enables that all Hamiltonian components to be learned simultaneously from a shared dataset without any prior knowledge of the operator structure or selective probing of individual terms.

Our theoretical predictions were validated by numerical simulations across disordered, anisotropic many-body Hamiltonians with nearest-neighbour and next-nearest-neighbour interactions and local transverse fields, as well as a specifically gapless model.
We observed the predicted scaling laws, confirmed the effectiveness of non-uniform scheduling for learning rates with total experiment time, and demonstrated that increasing the spread-state ensemble size leads to statistical decoupling between parameters, enabling simultaneous full-Hamiltonian learning with uniform time efficiency. Taken together, our results show that optimal scaling in Hamiltonian learning is achievable without entanglement or control overhead, using tools compatible with near term quantum devices. This approach provides a practical path forward for resource efficient quantum system characterization, adaptive scheduling strategies, and experimental realization in noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) systems. Of note is the shot efficiency of our results, which used only one shot per iteration per Pauli measurement. Combined with the fact that the only other resource needed is one-local unitaries, our method is therefore experimentally friendly.

Looking forward, it is important to quantify how the protocol tolerates measurement noise, imperfect state preparation, and decoherence during evolution, factors that will weaken the effective Heisenberg limited scaling in practice since we have assumed to be in the ideal, noiseless limit. Extending initial-state spreading to time-dependent Hamiltonians, whose eigenvalues and eigenvectors vary during the experiment, will require adaptive ensembles or sequential updates. Finally, for larger system sizes, full -likelihood reconstruction becomes intractable; integrating compressed-sensing, tensor-network methods, or Bayesian inference to exploit any known structure is a key next step toward scalability
[
73
,
74
]
. However, the novel methods uncovered here are scalable in the sense that they require only a change in measurement scheduling and one-local pre-rotations to the probe states. As such, we believe them to be widely applicable to Hamiltonian learning algorithms.

Proof.

Appendix C

Proof.

We consider sampling times
t_{k}=\Delta t\,k^{\alpha}
, with
\alpha>-1
. The total experiment time is

T_{\rm tot}=\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}k^{\alpha}.

(41)

As

\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}k^{\alpha}=\int_{1}^{m_{t}}x^{\alpha}\,dx+O(m_{t}^{\alpha})=\frac{m_{t}^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\quad(m_{t}\to\infty),\qquad\alpha>-1,

\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}k^{\alpha}=\frac{m_{t}^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}+O(m_{t}^{\alpha}),

(43)

as

(44)

Solving for

, we find the leading-order behavior:

(45)

Next, consider the case where the Fisher information from time

scales as
I(t)=\Theta(t^{\gamma_{0}})
for each time stamp
t_{k}
. Then the total Fisher information becomes

I_{\rm tot}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}t_{k}^{\gamma_{0}}=(\Delta t)^{\gamma_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}k^{\alpha\gamma_{0}}.

(46)

Using a similar expansion as in Equation
43
, we approximate:

\sum_{k=1}^{m_{t}}k^{\alpha\gamma_{0}}=\frac{m_{t}^{\alpha\gamma_{0}+1}}{\alpha\gamma_{0}+1}+O(m_{t}^{\alpha\gamma_{0}}),

(47)

so the total Fisher information becomes

I_{\rm tot}=\Delta t^{\gamma_{0}}\cdot\frac{m_{t}^{\alpha\gamma_{0}+1}}{\alpha\gamma_{0}+1}+O(m_{t}^{\alpha\gamma_{0}}).

(48)

Substituting the expression for
m_{t}
in terms of
T_{\rm tot}
, we obtain

I_{\rm tot}=C\,T_{\rm tot}^{\,p}\left(1+\epsilon(m_{t})\right),

(49)

where

p=\frac{\alpha\gamma_{0}+1}{\alpha+1},\qquad\epsilon(m_{t})=O(m_{t}^{-1}).

(50)

To absorb the finite-
m_{t}
correction into the exponent, we define an effective exponent:

p_{\rm eff}(m_{t}):=\frac{\ln\bigl(I_{\rm tot}/C\bigr)}{\ln T_{\rm tot}}=p+\frac{\ln(1+\epsilon(m_{t}))}{\ln T_{\rm tot}}.

(51)

Using the expansion
\ln(1+\epsilon(m_{t}))=\epsilon(m_{t})+O(\epsilon(m_{t})^{2})
and noting that
\ln T_{\rm tot}=\Theta(\ln m_{t})
, we conclude

p_{\rm eff}(m_{t})=\frac{\alpha\gamma_{0}+1}{\alpha+1}+O(m_{t}^{-1}).

(52)

This yields the observed scaling:

I_{\rm tot}\approx T_{\rm tot}^{\,p_{\rm eff}(m_{t})},

(53)

with the effective exponent incorporating finite-
m_{t}
corrections.
∎

Appendix D
Generic Sensitivity

Proof.

Let
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}{|\phi_{j}\rangle}
, where
{|\phi\rangle}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}{|\phi_{j}\rangle}
is any fixed product eigenstate of a Pauli string Hamiltonian, and each
U_{j}\sim\text{Haar}(\mathrm{SU}(2))
independently.

For any fixed Pauli string
P_{a}=\sigma_{a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{a_{n}}
and fixed Pauli product basis state
{|b\rangle}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}{|b_{j}\rangle}
, the overlap can be written as

\langle b|P_{a}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\langle b_{j}|\sigma_{a_{j}}U_{j}|\phi_{j}\rangle=:\prod_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(U_{j}).

(54)

Since
\mathrm{SU}(2)
is uncountable and with a continuous Haar measure, the probability of sampling a unitary
U_{j}
such that
f_{j}(U_{j})=0
is zero, because the zero set is negligible relative to the rest of the space:

\mathbb{P}_{U_{j}}[f_{j}(U_{j})=0]=0.

(55)

Furthermore, since the
U_{j}
are sampled independently, the total overlap vanishes only if at least one of the
f_{j}(U_{j})
vanishes. Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left[\langle b|P_{a}|\psi_{0}\rangle=0\right]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}[f_{j}(U_{j})=0]=0.

(56)

The same argument applies to the overlap
\langle b|\psi_{0}\rangle
, which corresponds to the case
P_{a}=I
. Since the set of Pauli strings
P_{a}
is finite (with cardinality
4^{n}
), the union over all
a
of the probability-zero events where
\langle b|P_{a}|\psi_{0}\rangle=0
still has total probability zero:

\mathbb{P}\left[\exists a:\langle b|P_{a}|\psi_{0}\rangle=0\right]=0.

(57)

Hence, both
\langle b|\psi_{0}\rangle
and all
\langle b|P_{a}|\psi_{0}\rangle
are zero with probability zero. Therefore, the first-order-in-time measurement signal

p(b,t)\approx|\langle b|\psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}-2t\,\mathrm{Im}\left[\sum_{a}\theta_{a}\langle b|\psi_{0}\rangle^{*}\langle b|P_{a}|\psi_{0}\rangle\right]

(58)

contains non-zero contributions from every
\theta_{a}
, except on a measure-zero subset of initial states.
∎

Remark 2
.

Although the initial state
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=\bigotimes_{j}U_{j}{|0\rangle}
is constructed using only local unitaries, the result holds for
all
Pauli-string Hamiltonian components
P_{a}
, including those that act nontrivially on multiple qubits. This is because the sensitivity condition concerns global overlaps such as
\langle b|P_{a}|\psi_{0}\rangle
, which are nonzero even when
P_{a}
is nonlocal.

For instance, in a two-qubit system with
P_{a}=X\otimes Y
and
{|b\rangle}={|00\rangle}
, the overlap

\langle 00|(X\otimes Y)|\psi_{0}\rangle=\langle 0|XU_{1}|0\rangle\cdot\langle 0|YU_{2}|0\rangle

(59)

is the product of two independent random complex numbers, each nonzero with probability one. Hence, the global overlap is generically nonzero even in the case of
P_{a}
being nonlocal.

Appendix E
Fisher Information Diagonalization

Proof.

We consider the classical Fisher information matrix associated with measurement outcome probabilities
\{p_{i}^{(r)}(\theta)\}
, obtained from an ensemble of experiments. Let
H(\theta)
be a Hamiltonian parametrized by real parameters
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})
, so that
H(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{j}\theta_{j}H_{j}
. In each experiment, the initial state
|\psi_{r}\rangle
is generated by applying independent single-qubit unitaries
U_{\ell}^{(r)}\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
to a fixed product reference state
|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
, i.e.,

|\psi\rangle=\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{n}U_{\ell}|0\rangle,\quad\text{with each }U_{\ell}\sim\text{Haar on }\mathrm{SU}(2).

(60)

This defines an ensemble of spread states via local Haar-random sampling. Each state is then evolved under the Hamiltonian
H(\theta)=\sum_{j}\theta_{j}H_{j}
for a short time
t>0
, yielding

|\psi_{r}(t)\rangle=e^{-iH(\theta)t}|\psi_{r}\rangle.

(61)

Measurement is performed in a fixed Pauli product basis with projectors
\{\Pi_{i}\}
, and the outcome probabilities are given by

p_{i}^{(r)}(\theta)=\langle\psi_{r}(t)|\Pi_{i}|\psi_{r}(t)\rangle.

(62)

For small evolution times
t>0
, the measurement probabilities admit a first-order expansion:

p_{i}^{(r)}(\theta)=\langle\psi_{r}|\Pi_{i}|\psi_{r}\rangle+it\langle\psi_{r}|[H(\theta),\Pi_{i}]|\psi_{r}\rangle+O(t^{2}),

(63)

so that the derivatives with respect to the each of Hamiltonians
H(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{j}\theta_{j}H_{j},
coefficients are:

\frac{\partial p_{i}^{(r)}}{\partial\theta_{j}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\left(\langle\psi_{r}|\Pi_{i}|\psi_{r}\rangle+it\sum_{k}\langle\psi_{r}|[\theta_{k}H_{k},\Pi_{i}]|\psi_{r}\rangle+O(t^{2})\right)=it\langle\psi_{r}|[H_{j},\Pi_{i}]|\psi_{r}\rangle+O(t^{2}).

(64)

For small evolution times
t>0
, the measurement probabilities admit a first-order expansion:

p_{i}^{(r)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\langle\psi_{r}|\Pi_{i}|\psi_{r}\rangle+it\langle\psi_{r}|[H(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\Pi_{i}]|\psi_{r}\rangle+O(t^{2}).

(65)

With the Hamiltonian being parameterized as
H(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{j}\theta_{j}H_{j}
,
we have the derivatives with respect to the Hamiltonian coefficients satisfy:

\frac{\partial p_{i}^{(r)}}{\partial\theta_{j}}=it\langle\psi_{r}|[H_{j},\Pi_{i}]|\psi_{r}\rangle+O(t^{2}).

(66)

The Fisher information matrix for the
r
-th member of the ensemble is:

[\mathcal{I}_{r}(\theta)]_{jk}=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{p_{i}^{(r)}(\theta)}\frac{\partial p_{i}^{(r)}}{\partial\theta_{j}}\frac{\partial p_{i}^{(r)}}{\partial\theta_{k}}.

(67)

Substituting the linear expansion in
t
, we obtain:

[\mathcal{I}_{r}(\theta)]_{jk}=t^{2}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{p_{i}^{(r)}(\theta)}\langle\psi_{r}|[H_{j},\Pi_{i}]|\psi_{r}\rangle\langle\psi_{r}|[H_{k},\Pi_{i}]|\psi_{r}\rangle+O(t^{3}).

(68)

We now average over the ensemble of Haar-random product states:

\mathbb{E}_{\psi}[\mathcal{I}_{jk}(\theta)]=t^{2}\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}_{\psi}\left[\frac{1}{p_{i}^{(r)}(\theta)}\langle\psi_{r}|[H_{j},\Pi_{i}]|\psi_{r}\rangle\langle\psi_{r}|[H_{k},\Pi_{i}]|\psi_{r}\rangle\right]+O(t^{3}).

(69)

Each commutator
[H_{j},\Pi_{i}]
is a Hermitian operator that can be expanded as a linear combination of Pauli strings:

[H_{j},\Pi_{i}]=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{(j,i)}Q_{\alpha},

(70)

where
Q_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
are
n
-qubit Pauli strings and
c_{\alpha}^{(j,i)}\in\mathbb{R}
are coefficients depending on the structure of
H_{j}
and
\Pi_{i}
. Thus, the product of expectation values appearing in the Fisher information becomes a sum over Pauli string contributions:

\langle\psi|[H_{j},\Pi_{i}]|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|[H_{k},\Pi_{i}]|\psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}c_{\alpha}^{(j,i)}c_{\beta}^{(k,i)}\langle\psi|Q_{\alpha}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|Q_{\beta}|\psi\rangle.

(71)

Due to the unitary invariance of the Haar measure on
\mathrm{SU}(2)
and its independent application across qubits, the ensemble of one-local Haar-random product states satisfies:
-
\mathbb{E}_{\psi}[\langle\psi|Q|\psi\rangle]=0
for any non-identity Pauli string
Q
,
-
\mathbb{E}_{\psi}[\langle\psi|Q_{\alpha}|\psi\rangle\cdot\langle\psi|Q_{\beta}|\psi\rangle]=0
for
Q_{\alpha}\neq Q_{\beta}
.

it follows that for
j\neq k
, the expectation of the product of commutator terms vanishes in the ensemble average, leading to:

\mathbb{E}_{\psi}[\mathcal{I}_{jk}(\theta)]=0\quad\text{for }j\neq k,

(72)

For the diagonal entries
\mathcal{I}_{jj}
, the Fisher information contains terms of the form

\mathbb{E}_{\psi}\left[\langle\psi|Q_{\alpha}|\psi\rangle^{2}\right],

(73)

which correspond to the variances of Pauli string expectation values. Since
Q_{\alpha}
is nontrivial and
|\psi\rangle
is Haar-random on each qubit, these variances are strictly positive. As a result,

\mathbb{E}_{\psi}[\mathcal{I}_{jj}(\theta)]>0.

(74)

By the matrix law of large numbers, the empirical average over
R
independent members of the ensemble converges to the expectation:

\lim_{R\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathcal{I}_{r}(\theta)\right)=\mathrm{diag}(c_{1},\dots,c_{d}),\quad\text{with }c_{j}>0,

(75)

as claimed.
∎

Appendix F
Extended Parameter Embedding

To smooth the optimization landscape and mitigate spurious local minima, while keeping a fully agnostic representation of each Hamiltonian entry, we embed the
n^{2}
real parameters into a slightly higher
-
dimensional space via a compact feedforward network. Crucially, the network’s input is fixed, so it does not learn a mapping but solely serves as an “extended parameter embedding” that outputs the Hamiltonian matrix entries directly in the Pauli basis. Because this embedding is implemented as a standard neural network, its parameters can be updated via back
-
propagation, avoiding the extra computational overhead that would arise from explicitly managing a larger parameter vector.

•

Fixed input independence.
We feed the network a constant vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
with uniform entries

x_{\ell}=\left(\frac{c}{\mathrm{dim}}\right)^{p},\quad\mathrm{dim}=n^{2},

(76)

typically
c=1
,
p=0
. Because
x
never changes, the network parameters
\theta
fully determine the output, making the network a pure embedding of
\theta
.

•

Network architecture.
A two
-
hidden
-
layer feedforward network with
\tanh
activations, the first hidden layer containing 200 nodes and the second one containing 400 nodes, maps the fixed
x
through modest
-
width layers to a real
-
valued output of dimension
n(n+1)/2
.

•

Lower
-
triangular output for Hermiticity.
The network’s output vector is interpreted as the entries of a complex lower
-
triangular matrix
A(\theta)
. The full Hermitian estimate
\hat{H}(\theta)
is then constructed by

\hat{H}_{ij}(\theta)=\begin{cases}A_{ij}(\theta),&i\geq j,\\
\overline{A_{ji}(\theta)},&i<j,\end{cases}

(77)

enforcing self
-
adjointness in one step.
