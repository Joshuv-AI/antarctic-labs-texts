A simple method for rapid reconstruction of 3D animal trajectory from monocular video
Authors: Weihao Qi, Kangkang Zhang, Jiqian Li, Jianan Ding, Nina Ma, Weiyi Lin, Xinwen Xu, Ru Wang, Zhuomin Niu, Aiqing Lin, Jiang Feng, Ying Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundQuantification of locomotion is central to the study of animal movement ecology. Although technological advances have enabled researchers to acquire high-resolution kinematic data, the associated methods often require multiple cameras and complicate the analysis process. Quantifying complex animal locomotion in three-dimensional space lacks an accurate, user-friendly method.MethodsBy combining deep learning tools and the pinhole camera model, we develop a novel method for reconstructing three-dimensional animal motion trajectories from monocular videos and analyzing kinematic data. We tested spatial precision and occlusion robustness in both aerial-based and ground-based scenarios. Subsequently, the method was applied to a bat-predation biomechanics study to demonstrate its capabilities. The application is based on low-cost single camera and does not require multiple devices or precise calibration.ResultsOur method rapidly reconstructs 3D trajectories for various animal movements, including flight, walking, and preying. The estimated 3D coordinates have an average bias of 0.09 m for aerial motion and 0.044 m for ground motion. Moreover, our method is extremely robust in distance estimation when faced with foreground occlusion. We extracted kinematic parameters from the 3D trajectory and gait frequencies from pixel area changes. Applying these parameters to biomechanical analysis, the results show that the obtained parameters can accurately describe the animal’s movement.ConclusionsThis lightweight and cost-effective approach allows the analysis of animal locomotion in the natural environment. It also allows researchers to flexibly adapt it to their specific needs, facilitating intelligent monitoring of the wild animals and enhancing the understanding of their locomotion data.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-025-00608-8.Keywords: Monocular camera, Animal tracking, Animal monitoring, Kinematics, Gait analysis

Background

Animal behavior and locomotion are vital in ecology and evolution. They directly affect individual fitness through processes like migration, reproduction [1], predator avoidance [2], and prey selection [3]. The use of computer vision techniques in wildlife monitoring has grown rapidly recently because they enable minimized-disturbance, high-precision animal identification, localization, and tracking through advanced imaging analysis [4–7]. Furthermore, computer vision techniques can be employed as the gold standard for ground truth in three-dimensional localization and tracking tasks [8].

Current 3D video tracking technologies predominantly rely on costly multiple frame-synchronized cameras [9]. These methods can offer high spatial precision, but the configuration and calibration of multi-camera setups may limit them in field animal monitoring [10]. Additionally, post-processing of multi-view data demands expertise and significant time investment from researchers [11]. Therefore, the development of cross-species 3D kinematic quantification frameworks using monocular solutions at a low cost has become a critical research frontier.

Monocular vision offers simpler hardware for wildlife monitoring than stereo systems [12]. However, it struggles to estimate missing depth information. Current solutions exhibit tradeoffs. For example, monocular depth estimation by deep learning with alignment procedures can obtain the absolute depth of the scene, but the error is difficult to control [13]. Using cameras installed perpendicular to the study area to capture overhead views can circumvent the necessity for depth estimation [14] by pixel-to-metric conversion of known dimensions, but it is constrained in field deployment [15]. In conclusion, accurate, user-friendly methods that can be applied to a wide range of species remain undeveloped. Additionally, conservation biology has entered the era of big data. Various sensors are generating an ever-increasing amount of data, while advances in machine learning empower researchers to decode the raw data quickly and automatically [16]. While the integration of these technologies offers the potential to expand and enhance the precision of wildlife conservation [7], the development of such technologies in conservation efforts remains rare.

This study established a computational workflow for 3D-locomotion reconstruction in free-moving animals from monocular videos. The proposed method integrates deep learning-based image segmentation for rapid feature extraction, coupled with an optimized photogrammetric approach for trajectory computation and gait pattern quantification. The spatial precision of this method was evaluated across ground and aerial motion datasets, while the robustness against foreground occlusion was validated through simulation experiments. Furthermore, we demonstrate how kinematic information can be extracted from the reconstructed 3D trajectories to enable biomechanical analysis. The lightweight and user-friendly system would provide potential practical advantages for wildlife conservation applications and intelligent animal monitoring.

Methods

Dataset

The method was developed based on a video dataset of flying horseshoe bats during predation. The videos were obtained from Lin et al. [17], with a detailed description of the experimental design and procedure provided in their work. A total of 94 videos were utilized in our study. The videos captured the entire hunting process of two Chinese horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus sinicus), two Osgood’s horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus osgoodi), and three Big-eared horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus macrotis). Among flying vertebrates, bats are renowned for their exceptional maneuvering ability [18, 19] attributed to factors such as relatively low wing loading [20], the intricate wing morphology [21], and lift generated by stabilizing leading-edge vortices during flight [22]. Horseshoe bats, being typical insectivorous bats, predominantly engage in aerial hawking for prey capture [23]. We chose the horseshoe bat video dataset for its complex hunting motion. This complexity challenges and suits 3D reconstruction systems. Additionally, the flapping flight behavior of bats serves as a focal point in biomechanical research [22].

The videos were captured using an FDR-AX700 HD digital video camera (Sony, Tokyo, Japan) operating in infrared mode at a frame rate of 100 fps and a resolution of 1920 * 1080p. The camera’s optical axis was aligned parallel to the ground; along this axis, the optical center was positioned 3.5 m away from the bat’s take-off point. Throughout the experiment, the camera remained semi-permanently fixed in place at the designated camera position.

To test if the method can be further extrapolated to other animals and ground motions, the 3D-POP video dataset was downloaded from Edmond (the Open Research Data Repository of the Max Planck Society). The 3D-POP is a dataset of walking pigeons with accurate keypoint annotations in 2D and 3D, along with the bounding box [24]. Furthermore, the 3D-POP dataset was also used for estimating the spatial precision of the present method. In addition, to compare the spatial precisions between the 3D trajectories constructed from the monocular videos by the proposed method and from the binocular videos by the stereo vision method, the aerial motion data from Ma et al. [25] (where the flight of the bats was videotaped by two frame-synchronized digital cameras at 100fps, 1920 * 1080p) was also employed.

Video processing

The monocular videos of flying bats [17] were digitized using Python via the OpenCV-Python package (version 4.7.0). We first decomposed the original videos into frames at a frame rate of 100fps using the OpenCV-Python package. Then, we selected the frames between the bat’s take-off and prey capture to create motion sequences (Fig. 1). To reduce strong reflections in the background, the brightness of the motion sequences was decreased by 50 units (optional, used only to enhance target contrast in noisy environments). The bats in the motion sequences were recognized and tracked using the Track Anything Model (TAM) [26]. The TAM is a pre-trained video object segmentation model integrated with Segment Anything [27] (SAM, a zero-shot generalized image segmentation model) and XMem (a video object segmentation model [28]). These pre-trained models continuously generate masks of the targets in the videos, requiring no manual architecture design, parameter setting, or model training. We implemented the TAM using the official codebase [29]. First, a motion sequence with an annotation of the bat in a single frame was input into the TAM, which then automatically recognized the bat and generated masks for the bat in all frames. The output masks were reorganized into binarized sequences using NumPy, where the background is black and the bat is white (Fig. 1). All pixel coordinates of the bat within each frame were exported to TXT files using OpenCV and NumPy, and the animal target was digitized (Fig. 1).

Workflow of the proposed method. In TAM, targets can be tracked throughout the video with a single selection. The output video is processed by Python programs to complete the feature extraction and 3D reconstruction

The challenge in executing 3D reconstruction from monocular video arises from the need to extract distance or depth information from a 2D image. We resolve this via the pinhole camera model, which reliably projects the distance, size, position, and speed of animals from camera trap data [30], even with unknown physical specifications of the camera [31]. Determining the intrinsic matrix of the camera constitutes the foundational step in using the pinhole camera model. If the camera intrinsic matrix is known or the camera could be calibrated [32], it could be used directly in the pinhole camera model. In the absence of camera parameters, as in the monocular dataset of horseshoe bats [17] in the present study, a simple estimation method can be employed, suitable for standard lenses with a typical horizontal field of view (FOV) of 40° to 60°. This method assumes the optical center at the frame’s center and derives the focal length f from the frame resolution and an assumed camera FOV of 55°, which falls within the standard lens range. Specifically, the focal length is calculated as: f = 0.5·W/tan (0.5·FOV·(π/180)) [33], where W is the frame width in pixels (e.g., 1920 pixels), and FOV is in degrees. In this relationship, varying the FOV by ±5° from 55° results in approximately a 10% change in the focal length and distance estimation. As FOV increases, the nonlinear behavior of the tangent function reduces changes in focal length, improving accuracy in wide-angle scenarios. Subsequently, the previously extracted pixel coordinates were used to calculate the pixel area of the convex polygon enclosing the target edge using the scipy.spatial package. Due to the periodic movement of the animal, the pixel area fluctuates periodically throughout the entire sequence.

To obtain the missing dimensional information in the two-dimensional image, the distance between the target animal and the camera was calculated based on the pinhole camera model. For the aerial motion of bats, we used a third-order polynomial smoother to capture the changes in pixel area to estimate distances to all positions in a trajectory based on size scaling. At a certain position during the movement, the perpendicular distance between the plane where the bat is located and the camera (the optical center) is known (e.g., the distance between the take-off point and the camera) (Fig. 2a). Since the focal length of the camera and the actual area of the target are constant, the distance to the target is inversely proportional to the square root of the pixel area based on the pinhole camera model (Fig. 2a):

where d is the distance (e.g., millimeters) from the camera’s optical center to the target, a is the area of the target on the image (e.g., in pixels), C is a constant representing the product of the focal length and the square root of the actual area of the target. C can be solved using one set of data (d, a).

For the ground motions of pigeons, we introduced a technique for estimating the position of a target with reference to the ground plane in the field of autopilot [34]. When the motion of the target is constrained to the ground, the distance information is projected on the height axis of the pixel coordinate system (Fig. S1a in Additional file 1), and the mapping between the two should be inverse proportion [30]. In the limited range of the camera’s field of view, this relationship can be approximated as a linear function (Fig. S1b in Additional file 1). This linear relationship can be modeled simply by knowing the distance from any two points on the ground in the camera’s field of view (at different distances) to the camera’s optical center, and distance estimation can be performed on any animal that passes through the camera’s field of view. The advantage is that there is no need to obtain additional information about the target animal.

Reconstruction of the three-dimensional trajectory

To derive the 3D trajectory of the animal, a coordinate transformation is conducted across the pixel coordinate system, the image coordinate system, and the camera coordinate system. We employed the centroid of the animal target, defined as the mean pixel coordinates of all points within the enclosing convex polygon, as a digitized representation of the target in each frame. The working principle for reconstructing the 3D position involves combining the pixel coordinates of the centroid with corresponding distance estimations. Specifically, for each frame in the motion sequence, the two-dimensional pixel coordinates (u_b, v_b) of the centroid are extracted from the image, while the depth along the Z-axis (Z_b) is determined using monocular distance estimation based on the pinhole camera model. This model leverages auxiliary parameters, including the camera’s focal length (f) and the distance from a point in the trajectory to the camera, to compute the spatial position. The transformation from pixel coordinates to 3D camera coordinates is governed by the following equations: X_b/Z_b = (u_b - u_O)/f for the X-axis, and similarly Y_b/Z_b = (v_b - v_O)/f for the Y-axis, where (u_O, v_O) denotes the principal point of the image plane (Fig. 2b). These calculations enable the reconstruction of the target’s 3D position in each frame throughout the entire motion sequence. To enhance trajectory smoothness, the 3D trajectories of aerial motion were processed using a polynomial smoother, while those of ground motion were refined with a Savitzky-Golay filter to preserve local variations.

Through the above steps, the 3D trajectory of a single animal in a monocular video can be reconstructed. The proposed method has been implemented as a Python package to accelerate the data processing in the application. The details were shown in Additional file 2.

Spatial precision and occlusion robustness test

We conducted ground truth tests of the proposed method under two scenarios, aerial motion and ground motion, to evaluate the spatial precision. For the aerial motion, we used binocular videos from Ma et al. [25], where the 3D positions of the bat were obtained using the stereo vision method of Theriault et al. [35] as the ground truth positions. Ten flights containing a total of 945 frames were used for this test.

Performance on ground motion was tested on the 3D-POP dataset of walking pigeons forementioned. We extracted the clips from a subset of the 3D-POP videos (the file folder is named “pigeon01” in the dataset), with the 4 RGB camera views (30 Hz, 3840x2160p) being randomly selected. A total of 35 video clips, each ranging in length from 2 to 284 seconds, were obtained, comprising a total of 23,091 frames. We computed the centroid of 4 markers on the back of the pigeon as its ground truth position for each frame.

The method conducted in the present study was used to estimate the 3D position of the animal within a single camera view. The ground truth positions were transformed into the camera coordinate system via the extrinsic parameters of the cameras. The Euclidean distance between the estimated position and the ground truth position within each frame was used as the bias to evaluate the spatial precision of the method.

Given the prevalence of target occlusion in real ecological data, the robustness of the method to foreground occlusion of targets was evaluated. Artificial noise was introduced into the original data to simulate pixel changes resulting from the animal passing through the blind spots. The blind spot lengths were set at 25%, 50%, and 75% of the animal’s body length (300 pixels for the pigeon and 120 pixels for the bat). We randomly selected one motion sequence from each of the two datasets for the occlusion robust test. The relative error (RE) between the distance estimated under foreground occlusion and the actual distance without occlusion was calculated for each frame as RE = ([Estimated − Actual]/Actual) × 100 [31], with the result expressed as a percentage.

To compare the performance of the proposed methods with existing technologies, we selected 6 representative methods among different types of visual 3D perception approaches. The selection criteria included high spatial precision (error < 10 cm, effective working range > 100 m), a high number of citations (citation count > 100), and comprehensive information available for comparison.

Gait feature extraction and biomechanical analysis of bat predation based on the reconstructed 3D trajectories

To test whether the present method and the reconstructed 3D trajectories can be employed in the locomotion analyses, two application examples were shown. One example is employing the digitized animal target to extract the gait features of animal locomotion. In the videos of a walking pigeon in the 3D-POP dataset [24] and a flying bat in the monocular dataset [17], we integrated the enclosing convex polygon of the animal target and the appearance-based method [36] to obtain the gait features (details in Additional file 1).

The other example is comparing the kinematic differences of bats between different predation situations based on the reconstructed 3D trajectories. All the reconstructed 3D trajectories of flight bats were categorized into two groups (i.e., stationary vs. mobile prey) based on the locomotor status of prey during bat-prey interactions, and then five generalized kinematic parameters including speed, linear acceleration, radial acceleration, turn rate, and turn radius were extracted (Additional file 1). Three machine-learning models and statistical analyses were conducted to classify and compare the kinematic parameters between the two groups (details in Additional file 1).

Results

Reconstructed 3D trajectories

Based on the estimated positions of digitized targets frame-by-frame, the 3D flight trajectories of a total of 94 predation sequences from seven individuals of three horseshoe bat species, encompassing 8670 frames, were reconstructed (Fig. S2 in Additional file 1). Additionally, the binocular dataset [25] of bat flight trajectories with ground truth, comprising 945 frames, was reconstructed (Fig. 3a). The sampling rate of all 3D trajectories was consistent with the original video frame rate of 100 Hz. Furthermore, the 3D ground motion trajectories of pigeons were reconstructed based on the estimated target positions with reference to ground truth (Fig. 3b).

Spatial precision and occlusion robustness

In aerial motion of bats, the average 3D Euclidean distance between the 945 estimated positions and the ground truth positions is 0.090 ± 0.038 m, ranging from 0.003 to 0.221 m. In ground motion of pigeons, the average 3D Euclidean distance between the 23,091 estimated positions and the ground truth positions is 0.044 ± 0.035 m, ranging from 0.002 to 0.253 m, with 92.8% of the bias below 0.1 m. Monocular localization exhibits higher spatial precision in ground motion scenarios than in aerial motion scenarios.

The occlusion robustness test shows that the proposed method is extremely robust to foreground occlusion as a monocular vision method. When the blind spot occupies 25%, 50%, or 75% of the target’s body length, it can cause errors in distance estimation of up to 0.37%, 1.95%, and 3.97% in aerial motion (Fig. 4a), occlusion durations range from 0.15s ~ 0.25s; −1.14%, 2.23%, and −2.96% in ground motion (Fig. 4b), occlusion durations range from 2s ~ 3.5s.

Gait features and kinematic parameters derived from the reconstructed trajectories

The gait features of animals were extracted from the pixel area curves using the proposed method to demonstrate the relationship between pixel area and gait phase (Fig. S3 in Additional file 1). For bats’ flapping flight, the peaks indicated the instant of wing unfolding during the downstroke, with one wave representing a single gait cycle due to the synchronized movement of the wings (Fig. S3a in Additional file 1). For pigeons’ bipedal walking, the peaks in the pixel area curve corresponded precisely to the end of the double support phase. Due to the symmetry of bipedal walking, adjacent peaks represented different leading feet, allowing two adjacent waves to describe a complete walking cycle (Fig. S3b in Additional file 1). Building on these gait features, wingbeat characteristics were extracted from 10 predation sequences of Rhinolophus sinicus and Rhinolophus macrotis (five predation sequences per species) (Fig. S4 in Additional file 1). In these sequences, both bat species used 5–11 flaps to capture prey. As the bats approached their prey, the wingbeat frequency generally increased (Fig. S4 in Additional file 1). At greater distances, bats occasionally glided for short periods, resulting in wingbeat frequencies as low as 3.57 Hz. When closely pursuing prey, the bats executed complex maneuvers with rapid flapping, achieving wingbeat frequencies up to 16.67 Hz (Fig. S4 in Additional file 1).

Five kinematic parameters (Speed, Turn rate, Acceleration, Radial acceleration, Turn radius) were extracted from the 94 predation sequences (Table S1 in Additional file 1). The results of the importance of the permutation showed that speed, turn rate, and acceleration had positive effects on the classification task, while the radial acceleration, turn radius, and species had negative effects on the classification task (Fig. S5a in Additional file 1). The order of the importance of the parameters, in descending order, was: speed (0.1172), turn rate (0.0586), acceleration (0.0207), radial acceleration (−0.0103), turn radius (−0.0103), and species (−0.0379).

The kinematic parameters that contributed positively to the model’s classification performance were selected for further classification and ranking (Fig. S5b in Additional file 1), and parameter selection based on permutation importance improved the model’s classification accuracy: Before parameter selection (logistic regression 62%, support vector machine 62% and random forest 76%); After parameter selection (logistic regression 79%, support vector machine 79% and random forest 83%). The classification performance of each of the models was better than the random guessing level: 82% for logistic regression, 80% for the support vector machine, and 91% for the random forest (Fig. S5c in Additional file 1).

Statistical analysis reinforced the outcomes of machine learning models. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed to compare kinematic parameters between the two groups, with effect sizes (Cohen’s d) mirroring the permutation importance ranking derived from random forest classification. The parameters, ordered by descending effect size, were: speed (Cohen’s d = 1.36, 95% CI [0.218, 0.363]), turn rate (Cohen’s d = 0.85, 95% CI [167.45, 388.28]), acceleration (Cohen’s d = 0.29, 95% CI [−0.114, 1.401]), turn radius (Cohen’s d = 0.19, 95% CI [−0.013, 0.047]), and radial acceleration (Cohen’s d = 0.07, 95% CI [−0.683, 1.071]). In the random forest model, variables with a positive effect on the classification task—speed (p < 0.001), turn rate (p < 0.001), and acceleration (p = 0.035)—exhibited significant differences between the moving and stationary groups. In contrast, variables with a negative effect on the classification task—turn radius (p = 0.258) and radial acceleration (p = 0.834)—demonstrated no significant differences between the groups (Fig. S6 in Additional file 1). Additionally, a Chi-square test indicated no significant difference in the frequency of flight experiments for each species between the two groups (χ2 = 4.95, p = 0.084).

Discussion

In the present study, we propose a method integrating deep learning tools and the pinhole camera model to calculate the 3D coordinates of a single target’s locomotion trajectory based on mask size from monocular videos. Furthermore, we implemented the proposed method as a Python package, establishing an end-to-end framework. A zero-shot generalized model trained on millions of images enables rapid target extraction in complex environments. Since the universal computational principles for determining target locations were employed, the generality of the proposed method could be ensured. Our method shows that monocular distance measurement based on target size works for rapidly deforming targets like animals. Previously, this approach focused mainly on rigid objects. Therefore, the present study proposes a potentially efficient, low-cost, and operationally simple reference methodology for animal ecology research.

Occlusion has always been a challenging issue that affects the performance of monocular methods [11]. The test results demonstrate our method maintains high robustness against foreground occlusion in both aerial and ground motion scenarios. This is attributed to the combined effect of the enclosing convex polygon and the smoother. The enclosing convex polygons buffer the effect of occlusion on the area and shape of the target mask, and the smoother removes this effect using the information before and after the occlusion. In aerial motion, flying animals pass through the blind spot faster, and the body deformation during flapping flight is larger, which makes the noise induced by occlusion cause less influence. In ground motion, the animal’s distance information is contained in the position of the centroid of the enclosing convex polygon, and the centroid is only slightly perturbed when the animal is partially occluded.

Comparative analysis (Table 1) reveals the advantages of the proposed method in terms of speed, convince, and cost. While the existing photogrammetric techniques achieve high spatial precision, they often demand more hardware, labor, and technical expertise. The rotational stereo videography system developed by de Margerie et al. [39] exemplifies this trade-off, delivering exceptional precision and an extensive 3D tracking range (Table 1) but requiring €5,000 in equipment (20 kg) and continuous operator alignment. Wearn et al. [30] necessitated 180 person-days to manually mark 88,014 animal pixel locations, while Theriault et al. [35] recommended synchronized operation of ≥ 3 cameras for 3D tracking of animals. Simplified monocular alternatives (e.g., Srinivasan, Vo, and Schiffner [38]) introduced overhead perspective constraints and species specificity (flapping-flight birds). In addition, these methods are limited in large-scale monitoring activities. In contrast, our monocular approach attains spatial precision comparable to multi-view systems through adaptive convex modeling, eliminating complex deployment needs while preserving cross-species applicability. We also found that direct comparisons of precision across methods are often challenging due to dataset incompatibilities. This is because these studies incorporate distinct data acquisition processes and evaluate methods using different types of animal targets with varying body sizes and expected 3D displacement levels. In this study, we relied on the spatial precision values reported in the original publications for each method. Given the heterogeneity in animal targets and experimental conditions, we emphasize that these comparisons are indicative rather than definitive evidence of significant differences in performance. We hope that future high-quality 3D localization datasets for wildlife will help address this issue.

In monocular photogrammetry, distance estimation traditionally relies on mask size measurements, which work effectively for rigid bodies but face challenges with animals due to their shape variations. For example, Marks et al. [37] established a mask size-animal 3D position correlation of primates in a room with a maximum distance of 7 meters, yet reported a 0.43 m localization error. Our approach reduces hardware requirements while attaining centimeter-level spatial precision across a 9-meter range through optimized distance estimation algorithms.

Traditional photogrammetry relies on synchronized multi-view data and manual annotation. Recent deep learning advances infer 3D animal positions from camera trap imagery, reducing hardware and labor needs. Current methods predominantly reconstruct 3D point clouds from pixels, a computationally intensive process often yielding meter-level localization errors. For instance, wildlife-specific models like the DeepChimpact competition winner [40] achieved 1.62 m mean absolute error, and Johanns et al. [13] reported 0.99 m error (Table 1). The general monocular depth models like PatchFusion [41] require ~30 seconds per 4K image on an A100 GPU. In contrast, our framework automates animal tracking and feature extraction through deep learning-based target segmentation, enabling processing 200 frames in ~ 10 minutes (a few seconds per frame) on equivalent hardware while maintaining operational scalability across diverse scenarios.

Several advanced markerless pose estimation frameworks, such as DeepLabCut (DLC) [42], enable precise reconstruction of 3D poses and positions by tracking multiple anatomical keypoints across a broad range of scales, from fruit flies to cheetahs. In DLC, 3D keypoint estimation is accomplished through 2D predictions using convolutional neural networks, followed by photogrammetric triangulation across multiple camera views. Consequently, when processing data from novel species not included in the training set, manual annotation of extensive training datasets is required, which can be highly labor-intensive in multi-view scenarios. Furthermore, photogrammetry necessitates numerous cameras. For example, DLC’s cheetah 3D tracking case employed six fixed cameras [42]. As a result, the overall approach exhibits substantial complexity. In contrast, the proposed method offers a simpler and more accessible solution for 3D tracking. Although less detailed than multi-point pose estimation, the proposed method is particularly suited for applications requiring robust, training-free tracking of overall animal motion in diverse environments.

Gait analysis serves as a critical computer vision feature for individual animal identification and health assessment [36], while additionally supporting footprint identification technology (FIT) – a conservation research tool widely employed in monitoring endangered species [43]. The enclosing convex polygon in the proposed method accurately reflected the gait cycle of the animals (Fig. S3 in Additional file 1) with the pixel area profile precisely mirroring the current phase of motion, which enables efficient extraction of critical kinematic parameters like wingbeat frequency or walking frequency. Appearance-based gait analysis techniques can also efficiently extract gait features through video contour analysis while achieving high recognition accuracy [44]. Combined with our framework, this approach enables rapid extraction of kinematic parameters, providing a new angle of technical support for wildlife monitoring, i.e., future research would conduct gait analysis for accurate individual identification and tracking of wildlife from the monocular videos.

Kinematic analysis confirmed our method’s trajectories can quantify precise parameters. The data align with established bat biomechanical studies [45, 46]. Rhinolophus sinicus exhibited significantly higher mean values in speed, turn radius, and radial acceleration compared to smaller bat species, aligning with allometric principles [47] that associate larger body sizes with higher speeds but reduced maneuverability and increased turning forces. Across all species, bats pursuing moving prey exhibited higher average speed and turn rate than those targeting the stationary group (Fig. S6 in Additional file 1). This suggests that bats approach stable prey at low speeds using a stealth strategy, as prey stability typically indicates the predator remains undetected. This behavioral pattern is further corroborated by variations in wingbeat frequency: bats reduce wingbeat frequency or adopt gliding during long-distance approaches to minimize detection by prey, then increase wingbeat frequency during terminal phases to enhance capture precision (Fig. S4 in Additional file 1). This ambush strategy is also observed in aquatic and terrestrial predators. For instance, lionfish approach prey with slow, inconspicuous movement patterns [48], while pumas employ covert stalking followed by rapid pounces [49]. In addition, the turn radius as a kinematic prameters also plays a crucial role in predation outcomes. As demonstrated by Corcoran et al. (2016) [45], captured moths showed significantly larger turn radius (0.34 m) than successful escapees (0.15 m). The average turn radius across all studied bat species was 0.21–0.35 m (Table S1 in Additional file 1), which is the potential predation success range. According to the predator–prey maneuvering theory, the prey would use maneuvering strategies to escape capture. The bats might use various flight speeds and wingbeat frequencies to increase the predation success rate. These findings confirm the reliability of the proposed method for cross-species biomechanical comparisons and its applicability to diverse locomotion contexts, demonstrating robust performance in generating actionable ecological insights.

While the present study focuses on monocular-based 3D trajectory reconstruction for single individual subjects, the proposed method can be extended to track multiple animals. By assigning unique identifiers (IDs) to each animal in the initial video frame, TAM consistently segments all specified targets across the video sequence. The existing Python-based pipeline, developed for single-animal 3D trajectory reconstruction, already incorporates photogrammetric calculations to transform pixel coordinates of a target’s centroid into 3D camera coordinates using the pinhole camera model. Extending this pipeline to multiple animals requires only iterative application of the same reconstruction functions, processing the centroid coordinates and depth estimations for each animal’s mask in a loop. From a computational perspective, multi-target trajectory reconstruction is a straightforward repetition of the single-target process, with no significant modifications to the core algorithm. Nevertheless, technical challenges, including inter-animal occlusion and overlapping in multi-object scenarios, necessitate supplementary processing strategies—such as enhanced prompt engineering or fusion with advanced tracking algorithms—to preserve persistent ID consistency.

The proposed method faces several general limitations. Distance estimation accuracy depends on an animal’s frame proportion and posture, with stance/orientation variations causing amplified errors in larger animals [31]. Using Euler rotations to describe these effects, we note that the impact on distance estimation depends on the animal’s flight direction and orientation. When an animal flies primarily along the image’s width axis (horizontal) or height axis (vertical) with its side facing the camera, pitch variations have minimal impact on accuracy, whereas roll and yaw variations noticeably affect distance estimation. Conversely, when the animal flies along the optical axis (toward or away from the camera), roll variations have a reduced effect, while pitch and yaw variations have a more pronounced impact. For animals flying along the height axis with their ventral or dorsal side facing the camera, yaw variations have a lesser impact, while pitch and roll variations moderately affect accuracy. Larger animals exacerbate these errors due to their greater spatial footprint, amplifying the error space caused by posture changes. In practical flight scenarios, aerodynamic and gravitational forces typically result in smooth trajectories, which mitigate the impact of posture-induced errors to some extent. Short-term rapid maneuvers may introduce transient errors, but these are often reduced through smoothing operations applied during data processing. However, in cases where an animal significantly alters its primary flight direction and sustains this change for an extended period, smoothing may not suffice to maintain a consistent target size reference across the entire trajectory. In such scenarios, we recommend segmenting the data into distinct intervals corresponding to different flight phases or discarding segments where posture changes are too pronounced to ensure reliable analysis. If the experimenter has a sufficiently large dataset about the subject, a convolutional neural network can be trained. The network can serve as an alternative to the filter to recognize different postures of the target for a reliable distance reference. However, this approach significantly increases the method’s complexity and reduces its generalizability. For ground-based motion, posture variations have a lesser impact on distance estimation, as measurements rely on projecting the target’s 2D position onto the real world ground plane, independent of the target’s mask size.

The advancement of monocular vision tracking has spawned diverse methodological approaches for tracking the 3D shape and pose of objects. These technological evolution responds to growing demands in various fields, particularly in autonomous driving, robotic manipulation, virtual reality, and augmented reality. Techniques like 6D pose tracking can estimate an object’s three-dimensional position and orientation (pitch, roll, yaw) in real time but typically assume rigid objects [50]. The Shape-from-Template (SfT) methods track 3D shape deformations by registering a rest shape, requiring clear visuals for keypoint identification and geometric simplicity – a limitation that becomes particularly pronounced when handling large-scale deformations [51]. These methodologies demonstrate limited practicality in wildlife monitoring scenarios due to two inherent challenges: (1) the frequent occurrence of high-velocity non-rigid body deformations characteristic of animal movements, and (2) the inevitable degradation of image resolution caused by extended camera-to-subject distances in field conditions. The primary limitation stems from the historical scarcity of 3D pose datasets for animals, which has constrained research interest in this domain. Based on the above situation, the proposed method does not incorporate pose and shape estimation of animals to improve 3D localization accuracy. Instead, it uses an enclosing convex polygon to enhance robustness to animal deformation. Future work could integrate photogrammetric localization with animal pose estimation to resolve pose ambiguities in monocular settings. Animal body geometry provides critical cues for depth and pose estimation. For example, Srinivasan et al. [38] estimated the roll angle of flying birds using the distance between wingtips and the body center, whereas the 3D Menagerie constructs a statistical shape model from 3D scans of quadruped toy figurines [52]. This framework facilitates both pose normalization and effective generalization to real-world animal imagery.

Our experimental validation covered 4K/30fps and 1080p/100fps (under low-quality infrared lighting) configurations. It should be noted that the effective resolution range remains fundamentally determined by the performance of the Segment Anything Model (SAM). The method would be effective if SAM could accurately generate target masks. According to the SA-V dataset [53], SAM performs robustly across resolutions from 240p to 4K, indicating that our method is viable for most video resolutions. For high-fidelity motion reconstruction, we recommend using high-resolution video data, such as 4K or 1080p, although this increases GPU demands and data collection challenges [26]. In resource-constrained settings, traditional algorithms, such as temporal difference methods, can serve as alternatives to deep learning models for continuous video segmentation, but they require expert-driven noise management [54]. Compared to spatial resolution, temporal resolution (i.e., frame rate) is more critical for accurate motion reconstruction, particularly in gait analysis. Our research recommends a frame rate at least five times the animal’s locomotion frequency to ensure reliable analysis [55–58]. Suboptimal frame rates can degrade data quality in two ways: first, motion blur obscures key visual features of targets, particularly for rapidly moving appendages such as legs or wings, impeding accurate detection of pixel area variations indicative of motion phases; second, low frame rates result in sparse data points in gait analysis, which fails to form complete gait cycles and miss critical points, such as transitions between stance and swing phases. To address these issues, techniques like blur reduction and frame interpolation can be integrated into video processing pipelines to enhance data quality and analytical precision across varied hardware configurations.

Our objective is to provide an improved approach for 3D wildlife tracking that balances lightweight design, generality, and accuracy, thereby facilitating precise intelligent monitoring of animals in natural environments. We offer a practical set of procedures and scripts covering all steps, from video processing and monocular distance estimation to 3D reconstruction of animal trajectories. The approach retains a high degree of scalability. For instance, SAM can be replaced with traditional target segmentation algorithms or other models to further reduce computational resource demands. Alternatively, generality can be partially sacrificed for higher accuracy, such as by training a neural network to learn the mapping between distance and mask area for specific species, replacing the filters used in our ranging method. Our biomechanical analysis results further demonstrate that, even in the absence of precise calibration, the proposed method can reliably estimate target motion. Consequently, this approach will inspire and enhance the development of improved methods rather than being replaced by them.

Conclusions

Previously, there has been a scarcity of methods to recover 3D motion information from monocular videos of animals. Existing approaches often lack generality or capture limited motion information, making relevant studies costly. We found that combining deep learning tools can quickly and efficiently extract image features of target animals to obtain data valuable for movement ecology. The methodology described in this study is lightweight, fast, and easy to implement. We also discussed methods and opportunities for enhancing the method’s precision and conducting deeper analyses of kinematic data, and provided recommendations for researchers wishing to further refine their methods. Ultimately, as there is a growing demand for the extraction of information from data, the approach combining deep learning and traditional techniques we advocate here can aid in facilitating data exploitation and integrative understanding of animal movement ecology.
