Liouville–Type Results for Infinity Elliptic Equations Involving Gradient and Hardy–Hénon Nonlinearities
Tan-Dat Khuu, Trung-Hieu Huynh, Hoang-Hung Vo

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate Liouville properties for degenerate elliptic equations involving the infinity Laplace operator with nonlinear lower-order terms, of the form Δ ∞ β ​ u − c ​ H ​ ( u , ∇ u ) − λ ​ f ​ ( | x | , u ) = 0 ​ in ​ ℝ n , \displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u-cH(u,\nabla u)-\lambda f(|x|,u)=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}, where β ∈ [ 0 , 2 ] , Δ ∞ β \beta\in[0,2],\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta} denotes the fractional infinity Laplacian, and the nonlinear terms H H and f f are Hamiltonian and Hardy-Hénon type models, respectively. Our work extends existing Liouville frameworks for the classical and normalized infinity Laplacian by developing a new weighted comparison principle and locally Lipschitz regularity estimates. To obtain Liouville type theorems, we establish growth conditions for bounded nonnegative viscosity solutions when f f corresponds to increasing power-type nonlinearities, that is, when f f grows as u γ u^{\gamma} . Furthermore, we analyze the case of an exponential nonlinearity f f , which grows as e u e^{u} , showing that this problem exhibits a strongly supercritical behavior; under suitable growth assumptions on u u at infinity, only partial Liouville-type conclusions can be obtained. Our techniques combine radial analysis, barrier constructions, and refined comparison arguments, providing a unified framework connecting regularity, comparison principle, and Liouville properties.

1.
Introduction

The infinity Laplace operator is a second-order nonlinear PDE operator that describes the functional behavior of functions minimizing the Lipschitz norm. Formally, it is defined by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}u=\langle D^{2}u\,\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}},

where
\nabla u
and
D^{2}u
denote the gradient and the Hessian matrix of
u
, respectively. The corresponding equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}u=0,

is known as the infinity Laplace equation, which governs the class of
\infty
-harmonic functions. The notion of infinity Laplacian originally comes from the pioneering works of Aronsson
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
in the 1960s to study
absolutely minimizing Lipschitz
functions. More precisely, the infinity Laplacian equation arises as the Euler-Lagrange equation associated with the
L^{\infty}
-variational problem

\displaystyle\min_{u}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},

which describes absolute minimizers for the supremal functional
I(u)=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
with prescribed boundary data. Notably, Jensen in
[
35
]
proved the uniqueness of
absolutely minimal Lipschitz extensions
by considering viscosity solutions to the infinity Laplacian equation, which has motivated many researchers to study such equations. Infinity Laplacian also arises in stochastic tug-of-war games
[
48
]
, in which two players try to move a token in an open set
U
toward a favorable spot on the boundary
\partial U
corresponding to a given payoff function
g
on
\partial U
. It is worth noting that the payoff is not necessarily fixed; specifically, it may change each time the token is moved, which can be controlled by probabilistic methods (see
[
43
]
). Additionally, other applications of infinity Laplacian can be found in mass transfer problems
[
31
]
and image processing
[
21
]
.

In recent years, attention has shifted toward more general and hybrid operators combining
\Delta_{\infty}
with nonlinear gradient and absorption terms, which appear naturally in models of optimal control, stochastic games, and reaction-diffusion phenomena. A particularly interesting class of such operators is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u=\frac{1}{|\nabla u|^{\beta}}\Delta_{\infty}u=\frac{1}{|\nabla u|^{\beta}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\mbox{ for all }\beta\in[0,2].

This operator interpolates between the classical infinity Laplacian for
\beta=0
and the normalized infinity Laplacian for
\beta=2
, providing a useful framework for multi-scale optimization and degenerate diffusion problems. In addition, the combination with Hamiltonian terms and Hardy-Hénon type nonlinearities leads to rich mathematical structures involving nonhomogeneous weights and singular absorption-reaction balances.

In this paper, we aim to study Liouville theorems, addressing the nonexistence or existence of positive solutions to the nonlinear equations

(1.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u-cH(u,\nabla u)-\lambda f(|x|,u)=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\beta\in[0,2]
and
\lambda>0
. Here
c
is chosen for each model of
H(u,\nabla u)
so that
cH(u,\nabla u)\geq 0
for all relevant
H(u,\nabla u)
; in particular, if
H
is negative,
c
is also taken to be negative, and the nonlinearities are modeled by

\displaystyle H(u,\nabla u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ |\nabla u|^{m},\\
-u^{q}|\nabla u|^{m},\\
\penalty\ \penalty\ u^{q}|\nabla u|^{m},\end{array}\right.\mbox{ and }f(|x|,u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|x|^{\alpha}(u^{+})^{\gamma},\\
(1+|x|^{2})^{-\alpha}u^{\gamma},\\
|u|^{\gamma}e^{u}.\end{array}\right.

where
m,q
are positive constants and the principal objective is to identify optimal relations among the parameters
\gamma,\alpha
that ensure nonnegative viscosity solutions of (
1.1
) satisfying suitable conditions are trivial (
u\equiv 0
), thereby establishing a unified Liouville property for this broad class of fractional infinity Laplacian equations.

Our study is inspired by the series of recent developments in the theory of Liouville-type theorems for infinity Laplace equations and their extensions. As is known, Liouville properties play a fundamental role in the qualitative study of nonlinear partial differential equations due to their crucial role in providing information about natural phenomena
[
9
,
10
]
and regularity theory
[
25
,
15
,
4
,
38
]
. This line of research has developed rapidly over the years for a variety of different equations involving fully nonlinear elliptic operators. Related publications include works on symmetry and overdetermined problems for the infinity-Laplacian
[
17
,
27
]
, inhomogeneous infinity Laplacian
[
13
,
28
,
41
,
42
]
, Hörmander operators
[
12
]
, and truncated Laplacian
[
16
]
. For the standard
\infty
-Laplace equations, Crandall, Evans and Gariepy
[
25
]
showed that any infinity harmonic supersolutions which are bounded below are necessarily constant. Further well-known results were proved by Araújo, Leitão, and Teixeira in
[
3
]
, concerning the Liouville properties for the infinity Laplace equations with strong absorptions

(1.8)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}u=\lambda(u^{+})^{\gamma}\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\lambda>0
and
0\leq\gamma<3
. More precisely, if
u
is a viscosity solution to (
1.8
) and satisfies the following growth condition

\displaystyle\limsup_{|X|\to\infty}\frac{u(X)}{|X|^{\frac{4}{3-\gamma}}}<\sqrt[3-\gamma]{\lambda\cdot\frac{(3-\gamma)^{4}}{64(1+\gamma)}},

then
u\equiv 0
. Turning to the combination of the infinity Laplacian with gradient, this formulation stems from tug-of-war games (see
[
48
]
). We mention
[
16
,
4
,
47
,
13
]
as works related to this problem. The Liouville theorems for fractional infinity Laplacian were first proposed by Lu and Wang
[
41
,
42
]
. These authors applied the standard viscosity solution techniques, including comparison principles and Perron’s methods, to establish both the existence and uniqueness of solutions to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u=f(x)\text{ in }\Omega,

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain,
\beta\in\{0,2\}
with Dirichlet boundary condition, provided the data
f
has a constant sign. Recently, Biswas and Vo in
[
14
]
established Liouville results for fractional infinity Laplacian equations involving gradient. Their result asserts that any supersolutions
u
of

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u=c|\nabla u|^{4-\beta}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

with
c\geq 0
, which are bounded below are necessarily constant.

In addition to the fractional infinity Laplacian, Hardy-Hénon nonlinearities are also a notable aspect in our study. Over the past few decades, there have been several works on Liouville results for Hardy-Hénon type equations. For instance, the works
[
49
,
29
,
50
,
24
]
proposed Liouville type results for Hardy-Hénon equations involving Laplace operators. Furthermore, the nonexistence and existence of solutions to higher-order Hardy-Hénon equations have also captured much attention. In the recent work of Chen, Dai, and Qin
[
23
]
, the authors studied Liouville-type theorems, a priori estimates, and existence results for critical and super-critical order Hardy-Hénon type equations of the form

(1.9)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{m}u=|x|^{\sigma}u^{p}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
m\in(0,1],\sigma>-2m,
and
p>1
. In addition, we mention Giga and Ngô
[
46
]
, who established Liouville properties for the problem (
1.9
) with
m\geq 2,\sigma>-2m
, and
p>1
. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are not many well-known publications concerning this class of equations for the infinity-Laplacian, only
[
37
]
has treated it with the infinity Laplacian. It motivates us to consider these equations with the fractional infinity-Laplacian, aiming to generalize known results.

Furthermore, we prove Liouville results for a class of generalized Lane-Emden-Matukuma (LEM) equations, which can be informally considered the non-singular counterpart to the Hardy-Hénon class, as it involves the nonlinearity
f
of the form
(1+|x|^{2})^{-\alpha}u^{\gamma}
. In the case
\alpha=0
, this equation reduces to the standard Lane-Emden equation. On the other hand, if
\alpha=1
, it becomes the Matukuma equation, which was proposed by the astrophysicist Takehiko Matukuma in 1930 in
[
44
]
, who, based on his physical intuition, introduced it as a mathematical model to describe the dynamics of globular clusters of stars, see also
[
45
]
. Consequently, Lane-Emden and Matukuma equations have been extensively investigated by mathematicians as they describe several phenomena in astrophysics and mathematical physics, see
[
44
,
40
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
39
]
and references therein for further details. It is remarkable that LEM equations have been comprehensively studied in
[
19
,
20
]
for all positive values of the parameter
\alpha
. Our results in this article regarding these equations also cover a broad range of
\alpha
, including the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
in
[
19
]
and a part of the regime
\alpha>1
in
[
20
]
.

For exponential nonlinearity (the third type of nonlinearity above), equations involving this type of nonlinearity arise in the theory of gravitational equilibrium of polytropic stars, see
[
22
,
36
,
30
]
. Therefore, Liouville results for such equations have attracted many researchers over the years. Let us list some typical publications in this field. Firstly, Alarcón, Melián, and Quaas
[
2
]
established optimal Liouville theorems for supersolutions of the Laplace equation involving exponential nonlinearity, particularly having the form

\displaystyle-\Delta u=e^{u}\text{ in an exterior domain of }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Moreover,
[
1
]
studied Liouville results for the Laplace equation with gradient terms. Additionally, Wang and Ye
[
51
]
proposed Liouville type results for the Hénon elliptic problem

\displaystyle-\Delta u=|x|^{\alpha}e^{u}\text{ in }\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

whenever
\alpha>-2
and
n\geq 2
. In this article, we introduce a new result for this class of nonlinearity in the fractional infinity Laplacian framework, which contributes to extending the applicable scope of exponential nonlinearity.

Motivated by these results, we investigate the analogous conditions under which the equation (
1.1
) admits only trivial viscosity solutions. The structure of Hamiltonians in the equation (
1.1
) is inspired by the models considered in
[
15
]
, where nonlocal diffusion and gradient nonlinearities were analyzed in the context of fractional Pucci-type nonlinear operators. Our first contribution is to develop the idea in
[
3
, Theorem 5.1]
to cover increasing power-type nonlinearities and to include the fractional infinity Laplacian with Hamiltonians above. The fundamental tool of this approach is the comparison principle, which was first applied in
[
25
]
and later enhanced in
[
4
]
and
[
14
]
for equations with gradient terms and in
[
43
]
for weakly coupled systems. It is worth mentioning that we obtain a new Liouville result for equation (
1.1
) with the LEM nonlinearity (type II), for which the methods used in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
cannot be applied. Furthermore, the exponential-type nonlinearity
|u|^{\gamma}e^{u}
exhibits a much stronger supercritical growth, rendering comparison-principle methods for Liouville-type results inapplicable. Therefore, we overcome this difficulty by adopting the doubling method from
[
15
]
, which was used there to handle nonlocal equations with fractional Pucci-type operators. This method is widely used in the theory of viscosity solutions to nonlinear equations and we suitably adapt it to obtain regularity and Liouville-type results for these equations.

Regarding regularity, it is one of the primary challenges when studying the infinity Laplace equations. The best regularity results until now are
C^{1,\alpha}
for infinity harmonic functions in planes proposed by Evans and Savin
[
32
]
, and everywhere differentiability for infinity harmonic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with

due to Evans and Smart
[
33

[

that solutions of (
1.1
) are locally Lipschitz continuous under mild structural conditions on
H
and

. The comparison principle developed in this paper is also influenced by the approaches of
[
14
, Theorem 2.3]
, which combined viscosity solution techniques with carefully designed coupling functions to deal with the degeneracy of the fractional infinity Laplacian and the nonlinearity of gradient terms.

Our weighted version adapts these arguments to the presence of the Hardy-Hénon term
|x|^{\alpha}
, for which we impose conditions inspired by the recent work of Bezerra Júnior et al.
[
37
]
to ensure the consistency of the weight in the elliptic setting. Following the weighted Hardy-Hénon framework in
[
37
]
, we assume that the function
f(|x|,u)
satisfies the structural growth condition

\displaystyle f(|x|,1)=\mathcal{H}(x),

where
\mathcal{H}
acts as a type of weight in our diffusion model and satisfies the following growth condition: there exist positive constants
a_{1},a_{2}
such that

\displaystyle a_{1}|x|^{\alpha}\leq\mathcal{H}(x)\leq a_{2}|x|^{\alpha}.

This condition allows the exponent
\alpha

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce some notation, fundamental definitions, as well as several tools to facilitate the proofs of the main results.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; we denote by
\partial\Omega
its boundary.
We use
B_{r}(x_{0})
to denote the open ball of radius
r>0
centered at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,
B_{r}(x_{0})=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},|y-x_{0}|<r\right\}
. When
x_{0}=0
, this ball is denoted by
B_{r}
. The notation
u\prec_{z}\varphi
signifies that
\varphi
touches
u
from above exactly at the point
z
, i.e., for some open ball
B_{r}(z)
around
z
we have
u(x)<\varphi(x)
for
x\in B_{r}(z)\backslash\{z\}
and
u(z)=\varphi(z)
. Similarly,
u\succ_{z}\varphi
denotes that
\varphi
touches
u
from below exactly at the point
z
. Moreover, for any
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we now define the notions of viscosity subsolution and supersolution. It will be useful to have the notations

\displaystyle\mbox{USC}(\Omega)=\left\{u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R};\penalty\ u\text{ is upper semicontinuous}\right\},

\displaystyle\mbox{LSC}(\Omega)=\left\{u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R};\penalty\ u\text{ is lower semicontinuous}\right\}.

To state the results in a general setting, we introduce a Hamiltonian. Let
H:\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function with the following properties:

•

Growth condition:

|H(x,p)|\leq C(1+|p|^{m})
for some
m\in[0,3-\beta)
and
(x,p)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

•

Continuous:

|H(x,p)-H(y,p)|\leq\omega(|x-y|)(1+|p|^{m})
where
\omega:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous function with
\omega(0)=0
.

In this article, we deal with viscosity solutions to equations of the form

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u-cH(u,\nabla u)-\lambda f(|x|,u)=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\mbox{ and }u=g\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

Here
g
is assumed to be continuous and
f
is one of the three types of Hardy-Hénon nonlinearities defined in (
1
). Additionally, we use the notation
\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}
to denote the set of
n\times n
real symmetric matrices. Moreover, for a symmetric matrix
A
, we define the maximal and minimal eigenvalues of
A
, respectively, by

(2.2)

\displaystyle M(A)=\max_{|x|=1}\langle x,Ax\rangle,\quad m(A)=\min_{|x|=1}\langle x,Ax\rangle.

Definition 1

(
Viscosity solution).

An upper-semicontinuous (or lower-semicontinuous) function
u
on
\overline{\Omega}
is said to be a viscosity subsolution (or supersolution) of (
2.1
) if the following statements are satisfied:

(i)

u\leq g
on
\partial\Omega
(
u\geq g
on
\partial\Omega
);

(ii)

If
u\prec_{x_{0}}\varphi
(
u\succ_{x_{0}}\varphi
) for some point
x_{0}\in\Omega
and a
C^{2}
test function
\varphi
, then

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}\varphi(x_{0})-cH(u(x_{0}),\nabla\varphi(x_{0}))-\lambda f(|x_{0}|,u(x_{0}))\geq 0,

\displaystyle\left(\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}\varphi(x_{0})-cH(u(x_{0}),\nabla\varphi(x_{0}))-\lambda f(|x_{0}|,u(x_{0}))\leq 0\right);

(iii)

For
\beta=2
, if
u\prec_{x_{0}}\varphi
(
u\succ_{x_{0}}\varphi
) and
\nabla\varphi(x_{0})=0
then

\displaystyle M(D^{2}\varphi(x_{0}))-cH(u(x_{0}),\nabla\varphi(x_{0}))-\lambda f(|x_{0}|,u(x_{0}))\geq 0,

\displaystyle\left(m(D^{2}\varphi(x_{0}))-cH(u(x_{0}),\nabla\varphi(x_{0}))-\lambda f(|x_{0}|,u(x_{0}))\leq 0\right).

We call
u
a viscosity solution if it is both a subsolution and a supersolution to (
2.1
).

As is known, one can replace the requirement of strict maximum (or minimum) above by non-strict maximum (or minimum). We also recall the notion of superjet and subjet from
[
26
]
. A second order superjet (subjet) of
u
at
x_{0}\in\Omega
is defined as

\displaystyle J^{2,+}_{\Omega}u(x_{0})=\left\{\left(\nabla\varphi(x_{0}),D^{2}\varphi(x_{0})\right):\varphi\mbox{ is }C^{2}\mbox{ and }u-\varphi\mbox{ has a maximum at }x_{0}\right\},

\displaystyle\left(J^{2,-}_{\Omega}u(x_{0})=\left\{\left(\nabla\varphi(x_{0}),D^{2}\varphi(x_{0})\right):\varphi\mbox{ is }C^{2}\mbox{ and }u-\varphi\mbox{ has a minimum at }x_{0}\right\}\right).

Moreover, another definition of subjet at the point
x_{0}\in\Omega
is the set of all
\left(\nabla\varphi(x_{0}),D^{2}\varphi(x_{0})\right)
satisfying the following inequality

(2.3)

\displaystyle u(y)\geq u(x_{0})+\langle\nabla\varphi(x_{0}),y-x_{0}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle D^{2}\varphi(x_{0})(y-x_{0}),y-x_{0}\rangle+o(|x_{0}-y|^{2})\mbox{ as }y\to x_{0}.

To define the superjet, we reverse the inequality in (
2.3
). The closure of a superjet is given by

\displaystyle\overline{J}^{2,+}_{\Omega}u(x_{0})=\left\{(p,X)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}:\exists(p_{n},X_{n})\in J^{2,+}_{\Omega}u(x_{n})\mbox{ such that }\left(x_{n},u(x_{n}),p_{n},X_{n}\right)\to\left(x_{0},u(x_{0}),p,X\right)\right\}.

Similarly, we can also define the closure of a subjet, denoted by
\overline{J}^{2,-}_{\Omega}u
. See for instance,
[
26
]
for more details.

At the end of this section, we will state a lemma concerning the
\mathcal{O}(n)
invariance of
\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}
to facilitate the radial symmetry argument in Section

. Here,
\mathcal{O}(n)
stands for the set of

Lemma 2

(
\mathcal{O}(n)
invariance of
\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}
).

Let
O\in\mathcal{O}(n)
be an orthogonal matrix, and set
v(x):=u\left(O(x)\right)
. Then the following identity holds

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}v(x)=\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u\left(O(x)\right),\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

We can view
O
as a linear function, thus its Jacobian is constant and
O^{\prime}=O
. Applying the chain rule in vector form
v=u\circ O
gives us

\displaystyle\nabla v(x)=O^{T}\nabla u\left(O(x)\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Combining the above identity with the fact that
O
is an orthogonal matrix, we deduce

(2.4)

\displaystyle|\nabla v(x)|=|\nabla u(O(x))|,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Also, by the Hessian chain rule and
O^{\prime\prime}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle D^{2}v(x)=O^{T}\left[D^{2}u\left(O(x)\right)\right]O,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This yields that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\langle D^{2}v(x)\nabla v(x),\nabla v(x)\rangle=(\nabla v(x))^{T}(D^{2}v(x))\nabla v(x)=\left[O^{T}\nabla u\left(O(x)\right)\right]^{T}\left[O^{T}D^{2}u\left(O(x)\right)O\right]\left[O^{T}\nabla u\left(O(x)\right)\right].

We know that
(AB)^{T}=B^{T}A^{T}
and
OO^{T}=I
, then

(2.5)

\displaystyle\langle D^{2}v(x)\nabla v(x),\nabla v(x)\rangle=\langle D^{2}u\left(O(x)\right)\nabla u\left(O(x)\right),\nabla u\left(O(x)\right)\rangle.

Combining (
2.4
) with (
2.5
), we easily confirm

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}v(x)=\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u\left(O(x)\right)\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

which completes the proof.
∎

3.
Locally Lipschitz estimates and Comparison principle

In this section, we state and prove two key results for this paper: improved regularity estimates and a comparison principle. Now, we introduce a lemma about the regularity of viscosity solutions to problem (
1.1
), which helps us to establish a comparison principle for solutions of this equation. The techniques used to prove the following results are an adaptation of methods in
[
14
]
for our fractional infinity Laplacian equations.

Lemma 3
.

Let
f:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy the condition

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\Omega\times I}f(|x|,t)<\infty,\text{ for every compact set }I\subset\mathbb{R}.

Assume that
v
is a bounded viscosity solution of
\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}v\geq-c_{1}|\nabla v|^{m}-c_{2}f(|x|,v)
in
\Omega
with
c_{i}\geq 0
for
i\in\{1,2\}
and
m\in(0,3-\beta]
. Then
v
is locally Lipschitz in
\Omega
with Lipschitz constant depending on
c_{i},\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, and
\|f_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\sup\limits_{\Omega}f(|x|,v)
.

Proof.

By using Young’s inequality, we only need to consider
m=3-\beta
. In this case,
v
is a solution to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}v\geq-c_{1}|\nabla v|^{3-\beta}-c_{2}f(|x|,v)\text{ in }\Omega.

Then we obtain in the viscosity sense

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}v\geq-\tilde{c}_{1}|\nabla v|^{3}-\tilde{c}_{2},

for some nonnegative constants
\tilde{c}_{1},\tilde{c_{2}}
depending on
c_{i}
and
\|f_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.

There is no loss of generality in assuming
v\geq 0
. Now, we choose
\Lambda>0
sufficiently small such that
\Lambda\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<1
. We consider the function
h(x)=v(x)+\dfrac{\Lambda}{2}v^{2}(x)
.
A straightforward calculation reveals that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}h

\displaystyle=(1+\Lambda v)^{3}\Delta_{\infty}v+\Lambda(1+\Lambda v)^{2}|\nabla v|^{4}.

Applying the estimate (
3.1
), we derive that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}h\geq(1+\Lambda v)^{3}\left[-\tilde{c}_{1}|\nabla v|^{3}-\tilde{c}_{2}+\dfrac{\Lambda}{1+\Lambda v}|\nabla v|^{4}\right].

Applying Young’s inequality again, we deduce

(3.3)

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{1}|\nabla v|^{3}\leq\dfrac{1}{4}(\tilde{c}_{1})^{4}\left(\dfrac{1+\Lambda v}{\Lambda}\right)^{3}+\dfrac{3}{4}\dfrac{\Lambda}{1+\Lambda v}|\nabla v|^{4}.

Substituting (
3.3
) into (
3.2
), noting that
1\leq 1+\Lambda v<2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}h

\displaystyle\geq(1+\Lambda v)^{3}\left[-\dfrac{1}{4}(\tilde{c}_{1})^{4}\left(\dfrac{1+\Lambda v}{\Lambda}\right)^{3}+\dfrac{1}{4}\dfrac{\Lambda}{1+\Lambda v}|\nabla v|^{4}-\tilde{c}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq-\dfrac{1}{4}(\tilde{c}_{1})^{4}\dfrac{(1+\Lambda v)^{6}}{\Lambda^{3}}-\tilde{c}_{2}.

Since
1\leq 1+\Lambda v<2
, it follows

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}h\geq-\left[\dfrac{16}{\Lambda^{3}}(\tilde{c}_{1})^{4}+8\tilde{c}_{2}\right].

Therefore, we conclude that
\Delta_{\infty}h\geq-\Gamma
in
\Omega
, where
\Gamma=\dfrac{16}{\Lambda^{3}}(\tilde{c}_{1})^{4}+8\tilde{c}_{2}\geq 0
, from which we conclude that
h
is locally Lipschitz in
\Omega
thanks to
[
11
, Theorem 2.4]
. Observe that
v=\dfrac{1}{\Lambda}(\sqrt{1+2\Lambda h}-1)
, and this allows us to complete the proof.
∎

With the local Lipschitz estimate in hand, we next establish a comparison principle

Theorem 4

(
Comparison principle).

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain,
\lambda>0
. Let
f:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous and non-decreasing function in the second variable, in particular, it covers the forms (I) and (II). Suppose
u\in\mathrm{USC}(\overline{\Omega})
is a viscosity subsolution of

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u-cH(u,\nabla u)-\lambda f(|x|,u)=h_{1}(x)\text{ in }\Omega,

and
v\in\mathrm{LSC}(\overline{\Omega})
is a viscosity supersolution of the same equation with
h_{1}
replaced by
h_{2}
. Assume
v\geq u
on
\partial\Omega
and
h_{1}>h_{2}
in
\overline{\Omega}
. Then
v\geq u
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let us suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists
A>0
such that
A=\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{\Omega}}(u-v)
. Consider

\displaystyle\omega_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=u(x)-v(y)-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}|x-y|^{4}\mbox{ for }x,y\in\overline{\Omega}.

For each
\varepsilon>0
small, we define the maximum of
\omega_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}=\sup_{\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{\Omega}}\left(u(x)-v(y)-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}|x-y|^{4}\right)<\infty.

Note that
A_{\varepsilon}\geq A
for all
\varepsilon
. Let
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\in\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{\Omega}
be a point for
\omega_{\varepsilon}
where the maximum is attained. It is then standard to show that
[
26
, Lemma 3.1]

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{4}=0,\mbox{ and }\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}A_{\varepsilon}=A.

In particular, there exists
z_{0}\in\Omega
such that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}x_{\varepsilon}=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}y_{\varepsilon}=z_{0},\text{ and }u(z_{0})-v(z_{0})=A.

Moreover, one observes that

\displaystyle A>0\geq\sup_{\partial\Omega}(u-v),

i.e, the maximizer cannot move towards the boundary. As a result,
x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon}\in\Omega_{1}
for some interior domain
\Omega_{1}\Subset\Omega
and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. Since
u,v
are Lipschitz continuous in
\Omega_{1}
(Lemma
3
), there exists
L>0
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle|u(z_{1})-u(z_{2})|+|v(z_{1})-v(z_{2})|\leq L|z_{1}-z_{2}|,\quad z_{1},z_{2}\in\Omega_{1}.

From
\omega_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon})\leq\omega_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})
, one obtains that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{4}\leq v(x_{\varepsilon})-v(y_{\varepsilon}).

Combining (
3.5
) with (
3.6
), we deduce that

(3.7)

\displaystyle|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{3}\leq 4\varepsilon L.

Set
\eta_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|^{2}(x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon})\mbox{ and }\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}|x-y|^{4}
. Following
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
, there exist symmetric matrices
X,Y\in\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}
such that

\displaystyle(\eta_{\varepsilon},X)\in\overline{J}^{2,+}_{\Omega}u(x_{\varepsilon}),\quad(\eta_{\varepsilon},Y)\in\overline{J}^{2,-}_{\Omega}v(y_{\varepsilon}),

and

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{lc}X&0\\
0&-Y\end{array}\right)\leq D^{2}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\left[D^{2}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\right]^{2}.

In particular, we get
X\leq Y
. If
\eta_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
, from the viscosity subsolution property of
u
, it gives us

\displaystyle h_{1}(x_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq|\eta_{\varepsilon}|^{-\beta}\langle X\eta_{\varepsilon},\eta_{\varepsilon}\rangle-cH(u(x_{\varepsilon}),\eta_{\varepsilon})-\lambda f(|x_{\varepsilon}|,u(x_{\varepsilon}))

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq|\eta_{\varepsilon}|^{-\beta}\langle Y\eta_{\varepsilon},\eta_{\varepsilon}\rangle-cH(u(x_{\varepsilon}),\eta_{\varepsilon})-\lambda f(|x_{\varepsilon}|,u(x_{\varepsilon})).

Similarly, applying the definition of supersolution, we obtain

(3.11)

\displaystyle h_{2}(y_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\geq|\eta_{\varepsilon}|^{-\beta}\langle Y\eta_{\varepsilon},\eta_{\varepsilon}\rangle-cH(v(y_{\varepsilon}),\eta_{\varepsilon})-\lambda f(|y_{\varepsilon}|,v(y_{\varepsilon})).

From (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we have the following estimate

(3.12)

\displaystyle h_{1}(x_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq h_{2}(y_{\varepsilon})+cH(v(y_{\varepsilon}),\eta_{\varepsilon})-cH(u(x_{\varepsilon}),\eta_{\varepsilon})+\lambda f(|y_{\varepsilon}|,v(y_{\varepsilon}))-\lambda f(|x_{\varepsilon}|,u(x_{\varepsilon})).

From the condition on
H
, (
3.12
) implies that

\displaystyle h_{2}(y_{\varepsilon})-h_{1}(x_{\varepsilon})+\lambda\left[f(|y_{\varepsilon}|,v(y_{\varepsilon}))-f(|x_{\varepsilon}|,u(x_{\varepsilon}))\right]+c\omega(|x_{\varepsilon}-y_{\varepsilon}|)(1+|\eta_{\varepsilon}|^{m})\geq 0.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, using (
3.4
) and (
3.7
), we conclude that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{\Omega}}\left(h_{2}-h_{1}\right)\geq\lambda\left[f(|z_{0}|,u(z_{0}))-f(|z_{0}|,v(z_{0}))\right].

Since
u(z_{0})>v(z_{0})
and
f
is non-decreasing in the second variable, then

\displaystyle\sup_{\overline{\Omega}}\left(h_{2}-h_{1}\right)\geq 0,

which contradicts the assumption
h_{1}>h_{2}
on
\overline{\Omega}
.

Otherwise, if
\eta_{\varepsilon}=0
then
x_{\varepsilon}=y_{\varepsilon}
. Therefore, from (
3
), it yields

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{lc}X&0\\
0&-Y\end{array}\right)\leq\left(\begin{array}[]{lc}0&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right),

which leads to
X\leq 0\leq Y
. Combining this with (
2.2
), we infer that

(3.14)

\displaystyle M(X)\leq 0\leq m(Y).

For
\beta\in[0,2)
, we can apply similar techniques as the above case to get a similar contradiction. Finally, we address the case
\beta=2
and
\eta_{\varepsilon}=0
. By Definition
1
for this case, we have

\displaystyle\mbox{Subsolution: }M(X)-cH(u(x_{\varepsilon}),0)-\lambda f(|x_{\varepsilon}|,u(x_{\varepsilon}))\geq h_{1}(x_{\varepsilon});

\displaystyle\mbox{Supersolution: }m(Y)-cH(v(y_{\varepsilon}),0)-\lambda f(|y_{\varepsilon}|,v(y_{\varepsilon}))\leq h_{2}(y_{\varepsilon}).

Subtracting the subsolution inequality from the supersolution inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle h_{2}(y_{\varepsilon})-h_{1}(x_{\varepsilon})\geq m(Y)-M(X)+c\left[H(u(x_{\varepsilon}),0)-H(v(y_{\varepsilon}),0)\right]-\lambda f(|y_{\varepsilon}|,v(y_{\varepsilon}))+\lambda f(|x_{\varepsilon}|,u(x_{\varepsilon})).

From (
3.14
), we readily obtain

(3.15)

\displaystyle h_{2}(y_{\varepsilon})-h_{1}(x_{\varepsilon})\geq\lambda f(|x_{\varepsilon}|,u(x_{\varepsilon}))-\lambda f(|y_{\varepsilon}|,v(y_{\varepsilon})).

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
3.15
), we get the estimate (
3.13
) again, which is a contradiction. The proof is completed.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain existence and uniqueness of solutions under suitable conditions. This result hinges on the possibility of sandwiching the solution between two special functions, known as a supersolution and a subsolution. Specifically, we demonstrate that if a pair of ordered supersolution and subsolution can be constructed that satisfy appropriate regularity and boundary conditions, then the equation admits a unique solution within the region delimited by this pair.

Theorem 5

(
Existence and Uniqueness).

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded
C^{1}
domain. Suppose that
h
and
g
are continuous functions in
\overline{\Omega}
. Let
f:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. We consider the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{clcl}\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u-cH(u,\nabla u)-\lambda f(|x|,u)&=&h(x)&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=&g&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Assume that
\overline{u}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
is a supersolution and
\underline{u}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
is a subsolution to the problem (
5
) such that
\overline{u}\geq\underline{u}\geq 0
. We also suppose that
\underline{u}=g
on
\partial\Omega
. Then, the equation (
5
) admits a unique solution
u
satisfying the enclosure

\displaystyle 0\leq\underline{u}\leq u\leq\overline{u}\text{ in }\overline{\Omega}.

Proof.

The proof for uniqueness, on the other hand, relies entirely on Theorem
4
. The existence result is obtained via the established Perron’s method, coupled with the requirement for a carefully constructed barrier function near the boundary. A brief outline of the proof is included here. We introduce the set
\mathcal{T}
, defined as the collection of all supersolutions that are bounded above by
\overline{u}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\left\{\nu\in\mbox{LSC}(\overline{\Omega}):\penalty\ \nu\mbox{ is a supersolution to }\eqref{barrel-method}\mbox{ and }\nu\leq\overline{u}\right\}.

Also, we put
v(x)=\displaystyle\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{T}}\nu(x)
and

\displaystyle v^{\ast}(x)=\lim\limits_{r\to 0}\penalty\ \inf\left\{v(y):\penalty\ y\in\overline{\Omega},|x-y|\leq r\right\},

be the lower semicontinuous (LSC) envelope of
v
(see
[
26
, Page 22]
), ensuring that
v^{\ast}
is the largest LSC function less than or equal to
v
. Because every function in
\mathcal{T}
is a supersolution,
v
satisfies the boundary condition
v\geq g
on
\partial\Omega
. The primary goal is to show that
v^{\ast}
belongs to the set
\mathcal{T}
which would then imply
v=v^{\ast}
. By Lemma
3
, we also deduce
v
is locally Lipschitz continuous in
\Omega
. Now suppose that
v^{\ast}
is not a supersolution. Assume that for some
\varphi\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, we have
\varphi\prec_{x_{0}}v^{\ast}
for some
x_{0}\in\Omega
and

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}\varphi(x_{0})-cH(\varphi(x_{0}),\nabla\varphi(x_{0}))-\lambda f(|x_{0}|,\varphi(x_{0}))>h(x_{0}).

Using continuity we can find a ball
B(x_{0})
around
x_{0}
satisfying

(3.18)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}\varphi(x)-cH(\varphi(x),\nabla\varphi(x))-\lambda f(|x|,\varphi(x))>h(x)\mbox{ in }\overline{B(x_{0})}.

Now, for every
\varepsilon>0
we can find a pair
\left(\nu_{\varepsilon},x_{\varepsilon}\right)\in\mathcal{T}\times B(x_{0})
satisfying

\displaystyle\nu_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})-\varphi(x_{\varepsilon})=\displaystyle\inf_{B(x_{0})}\left(\nu_{\varepsilon}-\varphi\right)<\varepsilon.

Note that
x_{\varepsilon}\to x_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Also,
\varphi+\nu_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})-\varphi(x_{\varepsilon})
touches
\nu_{\varepsilon}
at
x_{\varepsilon}
from below. Hence, by the definition of supersolution we must have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}\varphi(x_{\varepsilon})-cH(\varphi(x_{\varepsilon}),\nabla\varphi(x_{\varepsilon}))-\lambda f(|x_{\varepsilon}|,\nu_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon}))\leq h(x_{\varepsilon}),

and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain a contradiction to (
3.18
). To fully establish this claim, the only remaining step is to demonstrate that
v^{\ast}\geq g
on
\partial\Omega
. Thus the boundary condition is satisfied because, by Theorem
4
, every function
\nu
in the set
\mathcal{T}
is greater than or equal to
\underline{u}
, which in turn satisfies the condition
\nu\geq g
on
\partial\Omega
. Therefore, we conclude that both
v\geq\underline{u}
and
v^{\ast}\geq g
on
\partial\Omega
.

We omit the detailed proof that
v
is a subsolution in
\Omega
, as this is a standard result in the literature. The interested reader may refer to the arguments detailed in
[
41
, Theorem 1]
. To complete the proof we must check that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{x\in\Omega\to z}v(x)=g(z)\text{ for all }z\in\Omega.

Pick
z\in\partial\Omega
. To begin, we extend the boundary data
g
continuously to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For a given
\varepsilon>0
, we select a radius
r>0
small enough such that

\displaystyle|g(x)-g(z)|\leq\varepsilon\mbox{ for }x\in B_{r}(z).

We now proceed to construct a barrier function. Fix any
r_{1}\in(0,r\wedge 1)
where
r\wedge 1=\min(r,1)
and we also define the auxiliary function

(3.19)

\displaystyle\chi(x)=|x|^{\alpha}-r_{1}^{\alpha}\mbox{ for some }\alpha\in\left(0,\frac{m}{m+q}\right),

is a classical barrier employed to establish boundary regularity via the comparison principle. The choice of a radial power-law form,
|x|^{\alpha}
, is motivated by the rotational symmetry of the infinity Laplacian operator. The constant term
-r_{1}^{\alpha}
is subsequently subtracted to enforce the geometric condition that the function vanishes on the sphere
\partial B_{r_{1}}(z)
, i.e.,
\chi(x)=0
for
|x|=r_{1}
. Next, we calculate the following function for the specific forms of Hamiltonians
H

(3.20)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}\chi-cH(\chi,\nabla\chi).

Substituting (
3.19
) into (
3.20
) with each specific form of the operator
H
, we obtain for each
x\in\Omega

(3.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&|\nabla\chi(x)|^{-\beta}\langle D^{2}\chi(x)\nabla\chi(x),\nabla\chi(x)\rangle-c|\nabla\chi(x)|^{m},\\[5.0pt]
&|\nabla\chi(x)|^{-\beta}\langle D^{2}\chi(x)\nabla\chi(x),\nabla\chi(x)\rangle+c(\chi(x))^{q}|\nabla\chi(x)|^{m},\\[5.0pt]
&|\nabla\chi(x)|^{-\beta}\langle D^{2}\chi(x)\nabla\chi(x),\nabla\chi(x)\rangle-c(\chi(x))^{q}|\nabla\chi(x)|^{m}.\end{split}

On the other hand, a simple calculation shows that

\displaystyle\nabla\chi(x)=\alpha|x|^{\alpha-2}x\text{ and }D^{2}\chi(x)=\alpha|x|^{\alpha-2}I+\alpha(\alpha-2)|x|^{\alpha-4}xx^{T}.

Continuing the analysis, we obtain

\displaystyle|\nabla\chi(x)|=\alpha|x|^{\alpha-1},

\displaystyle D^{2}\chi(x)\nabla\chi(x)=\left(\alpha|x|^{\alpha-2}I+\alpha(\alpha-2)|x|^{\alpha-4}xx^{T}\right)\left(\alpha|x|^{\alpha-2}x\right)=\alpha^{2}(\alpha-1)|x|^{2\alpha-4}x,

\displaystyle\langle D^{2}\chi(x)\nabla\chi(x),\nabla\chi(x)\rangle=\left(\alpha^{2}(\alpha-1)|x|^{2\alpha-4}x\right)\left(\alpha|x|^{\alpha-2}x\right)=\alpha^{3}(\alpha-1)|x|^{3\alpha-4}.

This allows us to deduce

\displaystyle|\nabla\chi(x)|^{-\beta}\langle D^{2}\chi(x)\nabla\chi(x),\nabla\chi(x)\rangle=\alpha^{3-\beta}|x|^{(3-\beta)\alpha-4+\beta}(\alpha-1).

As a result, three expressions in (
3.21
) become

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\alpha^{3-\beta}|x|^{(3-\beta)\alpha-4+\beta}(\alpha-1)-c\alpha^{m}|x|^{(\alpha-1)m},\\[5.0pt]
&\alpha^{3-\beta}|x|^{(3-\beta)\alpha-4+\beta}(\alpha-1)+c\left(|x|^{\alpha}-r_{1}^{\alpha}\right)^{q}\alpha^{m}|x|^{(\alpha-1)m},\\[5.0pt]
&\alpha^{3-\beta}|x|^{(3-\beta)\alpha-4+\beta}(\alpha-1)-c\left(|x|^{\alpha}-r_{1}^{\alpha}\right)^{q}\alpha^{m}|x|^{(\alpha-1)m},\end{split}

for
|x|>r_{1}
. With
m<3-\beta
and given the pre-specified condition
\alpha<\frac{m}{m+q}<1
, since
q>0
, and the exponent of
|x|
can be easily analyzed as follows

\displaystyle(3-\beta)\alpha-4+\beta=3\alpha-4-\beta(\alpha-1)<(3\alpha-3)-\beta(\alpha-1)=(3-\beta)(\alpha-1)<0,

and

\displaystyle(\alpha-1)m<0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \alpha q+(\alpha-1)m<0.

We can choose
r_{1},\psi>0
small so that for
r_{1}\leq|x|\leq r_{1}+\psi
, which allows us to obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}\left(\zeta\chi(x)\right)-cH(\zeta\chi(x),\zeta\nabla\chi(x))-\lambda f(|x|,\zeta\chi(x))\leq-\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

for some large
\zeta
. To handle the geometry near the boundary point
z
, we rotate the domain
\Omega
such that the ball
B_{r_{1}}
is tangent to
\partial\Omega
at
z
from the exterior. This arrangement ensures that for any
x\in\Omega
in a neighborhood of
z
, we have
|x|>r_{1}
. Consequently, our auxiliary function (
3.19
) is strictly positive in this region. Next, we introduce the function

\displaystyle w=\min\left\{g(z)+\varepsilon+\zeta\chi,\overline{u}\right\}.

In fact, it is also a supersolution, and hence
v\leq w
in
\Omega
, we take the limit as
x\to z
. Since
z
is the point of tangency for the exterior ball
B_{r_{1}}
, which implies
\chi(z)=0
. Thus, it follows that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{x\to z}\sup v(x)\leq\lim\limits_{x\to z}w(x)=g(z)+\varepsilon.

Since this holds for any

, we have
\lim\limits_{x\to z}\sup v(x)\leq g(z)
. A similar argument using a subsolution yields
\lim\limits_{x\to z}\inf v(x)\geq g(z)
, which completes the proof.
∎

. The proof follows by applying analogous techniques to those employed in

for equations involving the infinity Laplacian and gradient terms. This result will play a crucial role in establishing Theorem

Lemma 6

(
Stability).

Let
\left\{\xi_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of lower-semicontinuous functions on
\Omega
such that
|\xi_{j}|\leq K
for all
j\geq 1
and some
K>0
. Suppose that
\xi_{k}\to\xi
locally uniformly in
\Omega
for some
\xi\in\mathrm{LSC}(\overline{\Omega})
, and for each
k
,
u_{k}
is a viscosity solution of the following Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{clcl}\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u_{k}&=&c|\nabla u_{k}|^{m}+\xi_{k}&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
u_{k}&=&g&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Then
\left\{u_{k}\right\}
has a subsequence that converges locally uniformly in
\Omega
to a solution
u
of the Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{clcl}\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u&=&c|\nabla u|^{m}+\xi&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
u&=&g&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

4.
Radial analysis

In this intermediate section, we pause to analyze the radial boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u&=&cH(u,\nabla u)+\lambda f(|x-x_{0}|-\rho,u)&\mbox{in }B_{R}(x_{0}),\\[5.0pt]
u&=&d&\mbox{on }\partial B_{R}(x_{0}),\end{array}\right.

where
\beta\in[0,2]
, the parameters satisfy
\lambda>0,d>0
and the constant
c
is chosen depending on
H
such that the quantity
cH
remains positive, and the nonlinearities are modeled as in (
1
).

The direct method to solve this problem was introduced and analyzed in the paper ”Infinity Laplacian equation with strong absorptions” by Araújo, Leitão, and Teixeira
[
3
, Section 5]
, where the authors treated the infinity Laplace equation
\Delta_{\infty}u=\lambda(u^{+})^{\gamma}
and they employed a specific line of reasoning to demonstrate the radial symmetry of
u
via uniqueness and
\mathcal{O}(n)
invariance. Similarly, for our class of problems, by uniqueness and Lemma
2
, it is plain that the solution of such a boundary value problem is radially symmetric. Indeed, for any
O\in\mathcal{O}(n)
, the function
v(x-x_{0}):=u(O(x-x_{0}))
solves the same boundary value problem. Combined with the uniqueness of solutions, this yields
v(x)=u(x)
. Since
O\in\mathcal{O}(n)
was taken arbitrary, it follows that
u
is radially symmetric.

On the other hand, let us put
u(x)=h(r)
with
r=|x-x_{0}|
. We calculate the gradient and the Hessian matrix of
u
for

Theorem 7

(

) after adding the solution to the penalized problem
u_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u_{\varepsilon}&=&cH(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla u_{\varepsilon})+\lambda\left(|x-x_{0}|-\rho\right)^{\alpha}_{+}(u_{\varepsilon}^{+})^{\gamma}+\varepsilon&\mbox{in }B_{R}(x_{0}),\\[5.0pt]

(5.12)

Theorem 14

(
Liouville-type result III).

Let
\rho,\lambda>0
and
\alpha\in\left[0,\frac{(4-\beta-m)(3-\beta-\gamma)}{2(3-\beta-m)}\right)
. Assume that
u
is a nonnegative entire viscosity solution to

(5.16)

\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u=cH(u,\nabla u)+\lambda\phi_{\rho,x_{0}}(|x|)\,u^{\gamma},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

where the weight
\phi_{\rho,x_{0}}(|x|)\lesssim[1+(|x-x_{0}|-\rho)^{2}]^{-\alpha}
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the conditions on the parameters
m,q,\gamma
are given in Table
1
. If the following condition holds

(5.17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{u(x)}{|x|^{p^{*}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{\alpha}{3-\beta-\gamma}}}<\tau^{*},

where
p^{*},\tau^{*}
are respectively defined in (
4.40
), then every nonnegative viscosity solution of (
5.16
) is trivial, that is
u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Fixing
R>\rho>0
, let
v:\overline{B_{R}(x_{0})}\to\mathbb{R}
, we consider the radial solution to the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}v&=&cH(v,\nabla v)+\lambda\left[1+(|x-x_{0}|-\rho)^{2}\right]^{-\alpha}v^{\gamma}&\mbox{in }B_{R}(x_{0}),\\[5.0pt]
v&=&\displaystyle\sup_{\partial B_{R}(x_{0})}u&\mbox{on }\partial B_{R}(x_{0}).\end{array}\right.

By comparison principle, Theorem
4
,
u\leq v
in
B_{R}(x_{0})
(note that the same proof works since the function
f(x,t)=(1+x^{2})^{-\alpha}t^{\gamma}
is strictly increasing in the second variable). It follows by hypothesis (
5.17
) that, taking
R
sufficiently large (
R\gg 1
) such that

(5.18)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{\partial B_{R}(x_{0})}\frac{u(x)}{R^{p^{*}}(1+R^{2})^{-\frac{\alpha}{3-\beta-\gamma}}}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi\tau^{*},

for some
0<\Phi<1
. Thanks to (
4.44
), we obtain the supersolution
v
as follows

(5.19)

\displaystyle v(x)=\chi_{\{p_{2}^{*}>p_{1}^{*}\}}\tau^{*}\left[|x-x_{0}|-R+\left(\frac{\displaystyle\sup_{\partial B_{R}(x_{0})}u}{\chi_{\{p_{2}^{*}>p_{1}^{*}\}}\tau^{*}}\right)^{1/p^{*}}\right]^{p^{*}}_{+},

where
\chi_{\{p_{2}^{*}>p_{1}^{*}\}}
is defined in (

). Finally, combining (
5.18
) and (
5.19
), we deduce

(5.20)

\displaystyle u(x)\leq\tau^{*}\left[|x-x_{0}|-R\left(1-\Phi^{1/p^{*}}\right)\right]^{p^{*}}_{+}.

Letting

in (

), we conclude the proof of Theorem

.
∎

Remark 1
.

Let us explain the domain of the parameter
\alpha
in Theorem
14
. The mentioned condition of
\alpha
ensures that the function
h(x)=|x|^{p^{*}}(1+|x|^{2})^{-\frac{\alpha}{3-\beta-\gamma}},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
converges to
+\infty
when
|x|\to+\infty
. It allows the growth condition (
5.17
) to cover a broad class of viscosity solutions, including unbounded solutions.

Finally, we establish a Liouville result for nonlinearity
|u|^{\gamma}e^{u}
. Our proof of this theorem is based on the doubling method from
[
15
]
, along with a suitable adjustment of the coupling function. Furthermore, in order to state our result in a general sense, we allow for a functional coefficient
a(x)
for the gradient term. As a result, our theorem deals with a more general framework compared to
[
14
, Theorem 2.6]
.

Theorem 15

(
Liouville-type result IV).

Suppose that
u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a viscosity solution to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u=a(x)(|x|+1)^{\alpha}|\nabla u|^{m}+\lambda|u|^{\gamma}e^{u}\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
m\in(0,2-\beta]
,
a:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
is a function satisfying
\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a>0
,
\lambda,\gamma\geq 0
, and
\alpha\in(-1,0]
.
If the following condition holds

(5.21)

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}{\dfrac{\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}u}{R}}=0,

where
\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}u=\sup_{B_{R}}u-\inf_{B_{R}}u
, for each
R>0
, then
u
is necessarily constant.

Proof.

Let
L_{R}=\frac{\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}u}{R}
and
\kappa_{R}=\frac{L_{R}}{R}
, for every
R>0
. We consider the function

\displaystyle\Phi_{R}(x,y)=u(x)-u(y)-\kappa_{R}\omega_{R}(|x-y|)-2\kappa_{R}(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}),

where
\omega_{R}(t)=-t+\vartheta t^{2}
. Let us choose
\vartheta=\vartheta(R)>0
small enough so that

\displaystyle-1\leq\omega_{R}^{\prime}(t)\leq-\dfrac{1}{2}\text{ and }\omega_{R}^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq 0,\text{ for }t\in[0,R].

Since
\Phi_{R}
is continuous in
\overline{B_{R}}\times\overline{B_{R}}
,
\Phi_{R}
attains its maximum value on this domain. We consider two separate cases in this proof.

Case 1
: There exists
R_{0}>0
such that
\max\limits_{\overline{B_{R}}\times\overline{B_{R}}}{\Phi_{R}}\leq 0
, for every
R>R_{0}
.

Fixing two points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we are able to find
R_{1}>R_{0}
such that
x,y\in B_{R/2}
, for
R>R_{1}
. Thus, we derive the following estimates

\displaystyle|u(x)-u(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{R}\omega_{R}(|x-y|)+2\kappa_{R}(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})

(5.22)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{R}|x-y|^{2}+2\kappa_{R}(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}),\text{ for all }x,y\in B_{\frac{R}{2}}\text{ and }R>R_{1}.

Let
R\to\infty
in (
5.22
), noting that
\kappa_{R}\to 0
by assumption (
5.21
), we find that
u(x)=u(y)
for all
x,y\in B_{R/2}
and
R>R_{1}
. Since
x,y
are taken arbitrarily, we conclude
u
is constant.

Case 2
: There exists a sequence
R_{i}\to\infty
such that
M_{i}=\max\limits_{\overline{B_{R_{i}}}\times\overline{B_{R_{i}}}}{\Phi_{R_{i}}}>0
, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

We remark that
L_{R_{i}}>0
, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed, if there is a number
j\in\mathbb{N}
so that
L_{R_{j}}=0
, that means
\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R_{j}}}u=0
and
\kappa_{R_{j}}=0
. Combining it with the estimate

\displaystyle\Phi_{R_{j}}(x,y)=u(x)-u(y)-\kappa_{R_{j}}\omega_{R_{j}}(|x-y|)-2\kappa_{R_{j}}(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})=u(x)-u(y)\leq\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R_{j}}}{u},\penalty\ x,y\in\overline{B_{R_{j}}},

we deduce that
M_{j}\leq 0
, which is a contradiction.

For simplicity, throughout the rest of this proof, let us use the notation

\displaystyle L_{i}:=L_{R_{i}},\kappa_{i}:=\kappa_{R_{i}},\Phi_{i}:=\Phi_{R_{i}},\omega_{R_{i}}:=\omega_{i},\penalty\ i\in\mathbb{N}.

Assume that the function
\Phi_{i}
attains its maximum value on
\overline{B_{R_{i}}}\times\overline{B_{R_{i}}}
at
(x_{i},y_{i})
. Since
\Phi_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})>0
then

(5.23)

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}\omega_{i}(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)+2\kappa_{i}(|x_{i}|^{2}+|y_{i}|^{2})<u(x_{i})-u(y_{i}).

It follows from (
5.23
) that

\displaystyle 2\kappa_{i}|x_{i}|^{2}<u(x_{i})-u(y_{i})+\kappa_{i}|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq 2\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R_{i}}}u,

which leads to
|x_{i}|<R_{i}
. With similar arguments, we also obtain
|y_{i}|<R_{i}
. This reveals that
(x_{i},y_{i})
is a local maximum of
\Phi_{i}
over
\overline{B_{R_{i}}}\times\overline{B_{R_{i}}}
. Additionally, thanks to (
5.23
), we obtain

\displaystyle u(x_{i})-u(y_{i})>2\kappa_{i}(|x_{i}|^{2}+|y_{i}|^{2})-\kappa_{i}|x_{i}-y_{i}|,

thus we must have
x_{i}\neq y_{i}
.

Then applying
[
26
, Theorem 3.2]
, there exists
X_{i},Y_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n\times n}
such that
(p_{i},X_{i})\in\overline{J}^{2,+}_{B_{R_{i}}}(u(x_{i})),\\
(q_{i},Y_{i})\in\overline{J}^{2,-}_{B_{R_{i}}}(u(y_{i}))
and

(5.24)

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}X_{i}&0\\
0&-Y_{i}\end{matrix}\right)\leq A+\varepsilon A^{2},\text{ for each }\varepsilon>0,

where
A=D^{2}\Theta_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})
and
\Theta_{i}(x,y)=\kappa_{i}\omega_{i}(|x-y|)+2\kappa_{i}(|x|^{2}+|y|^{2})
. A direct calculation shows that

(5.25)

\displaystyle A=\left(\begin{matrix}Z&-Z\\
-Z&Z\end{matrix}\right)+4\kappa_{i}I_{2n\times 2n},

and

\displaystyle Z=\kappa_{i}\omega_{i}^{\prime\prime}(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)\dfrac{(x_{i}-y_{i})(x_{i}-y_{i})^{T}}{|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{2}}+\kappa_{i}\dfrac{\omega_{i}^{\prime}(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)}{|x_{i}-y_{i}|}\left(I-\dfrac{(x_{i}-y_{i})(x_{i}-y_{i})^{T}}{|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{2}}\right).

From (
5.24
) and (
5.25
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}p\\
q\end{matrix}\right)^{T}\left(\begin{matrix}X_{i}&0\\
0&-Y_{i}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}p\\
q\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\begin{matrix}p\\
q\end{matrix}\right)^{T}A\left(\begin{matrix}p\\
q\end{matrix}\right)+\varepsilon\left(\begin{matrix}p\\
q\end{matrix}\right)^{T}A^{2}\left(\begin{matrix}p\\
q\end{matrix}\right).

Choosing
(p,q)=(p_{i},0)
and
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we deduce that

(5.26)

\displaystyle\langle X_{i}p_{i},p_{i}\rangle\leq C(\langle Zp_{i},p_{i}\rangle+\langle\kappa_{i}p_{i},p_{i}\rangle),\text{ for some }C>0.

Moreover, using
\omega^{\prime}<0
and
\omega^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
, we derive the following estimates

\displaystyle\langle Zp_{i},p_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\kappa_{i}\omega_{i}^{\prime\prime}(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)\left\langle\dfrac{(x_{i}-y_{i})(x_{i}-y_{i})^{T}}{|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{2}}p_{i},p_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\kappa_{i}\dfrac{\omega_{i}^{\prime}(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)}{|x_{i}-y_{i}|}\left\langle\left(I-\dfrac{(x_{i}-y_{i})(x_{i}-y_{i})^{T}}{|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{2}}\right)p_{i},p_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{i}\omega_{i}^{\prime\prime}(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)|p_{i}|^{2}

(5.27)

\displaystyle\leq C\kappa_{i}|p_{i}|^{2}.

From (
5.26
) and (
5.27
), we obtain

(5.28)

\displaystyle\langle X_{i}p_{i},p_{i}\rangle\leq C\kappa_{i}|p_{i}|^{2}.

Applying the definition of viscosity subsolution, we obtain

(5.29)

\displaystyle|p_{i}|^{-\beta}\langle X_{i}p_{i},p_{i}\rangle\geq a(x_{i})(|x_{i}|+1)^{\alpha}|p_{i}|^{m}.

Thanks to the choice of the function
\omega_{i}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle|p_{i}|=\left|\kappa_{i}\omega_{i}^{\prime}(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)\dfrac{x_{i}-y_{i}}{|x_{i}-y_{i}|}+4\kappa_{i}x_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{i}|\omega_{i}^{\prime}(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)|+4\kappa_{i}|x_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{i}+4\kappa_{i}R_{i}

\displaystyle\leq 5L_{i}.

Combining estimates (
5.28
) and (
5.29
) gives

(5.30)

\displaystyle a(x_{i})(|x_{i}|+1)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C\kappa_{i}|p_{i}|^{2-\beta-m}.

Note that
\kappa_{i}=\dfrac{L_{i}}{R_{i}}
, we derive from (
5.30
) that

(5.31)

\displaystyle a(x_{i})(|x_{i}|+1)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C\dfrac{L_{i}^{3-\beta-m}}{R_{i}}.

From (
5.31
), it follows that

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{a}\leq a(x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq C\dfrac{L_{i}^{3-\beta-m}}{R_{i}}(|x_{i}|+1)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C\dfrac{L_{i}^{3-\beta-m}}{R_{i}}(R_{i}+1)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C{L_{i}^{3-\beta-m}}\dfrac{(R_{i}+1)^{-\alpha}}{R_{i}^{-\alpha}}R_{i}^{-\alpha-1}.

Let
i\to\infty
, we obtain
\inf\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{a}\leq 0
. This is a contradiction, thus the
Case 2
cannot hold. The proof is completed.
∎

Corollary 16
.

The conclusion of Theorem
15
is violated if
u
is a supersolution. For example, take

u(x)=1-e^{-|x|^{2}}
,
a(x)=a
, and
m\in(0,2-\beta]
where
a
is a positive constant chosen later. A direct calculation reveals that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u=2^{3-\beta}|x|^{2-\beta}e^{(\beta-3)|x|^{2}}(1-2|x|^{2}),

\displaystyle a(x)(|x|+1)^{\alpha}|\nabla u|^{m}=a(x)(|x|+1)^{\alpha}2^{m}|x|^{m}e^{-m|x|^{2}}.

For
x=0
, we easily arrive at

(5.32)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u(0)\leq a(0)(|0|+1)^{\alpha}|\nabla u(0)|^{m}+\lambda|u(0)|^{\gamma}e^{u(0)}.

For
|x|\geq\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
, we obtain
\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u\leq 0
, thus

(5.33)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u\leq a(x)(|x|+1)^{\alpha}|\nabla u|^{m}+\lambda|u|^{\gamma}e^{u},\text{ for }|x|\geq\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.

For
|x|\leq\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2},x\neq 0
, we derive the following estimates

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u}{a(x)(|x|+1)^{\alpha}+\lambda|u|^{\gamma}e^{u}}\leq\dfrac{\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u}{a(x)(|x|+1)^{\alpha}|\nabla u|^{m}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2^{3-\beta-m}}{a}(|x|+1)^{-\alpha}|x|^{2-\beta-m}e^{(m+\beta-3)|x|^{2}}(1-2|x|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2^{3-\beta-m}}{a}2^{-\alpha}=\dfrac{2^{3-\beta-m-\alpha}}{a}.

Choose
a=2^{3-\beta-m-\alpha}
, we readily obtain

(5.34)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u\leq a(x)(|x|+1)^{\alpha}|\nabla u|^{m}+\lambda|u|^{\gamma}e^{u},\text{ for }|x|\leq\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\text{ and }x\neq 0.

From (
5.32
), (
5.33
), and (
5.34
), we conclude

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}^{\beta}u\leq a(x)(|x|+1)^{\alpha}|\nabla u|^{m}+\lambda|u|^{\gamma}e^{u},\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

However, a straightforward calculation demonstrates that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}{\dfrac{\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}u}{R}}=\lim_{R\to\infty}{\dfrac{1-e^{-R^{2}}}{R}}=0.

Corollary 17
.

The result of Theorem
15
