Fasciculoventricular pathways (FVPs) are rare and benign variants of ventricular preexcitation that result from direct connections between the His bundle or proximal fascicles and the ventricular myocardium. Unlike typical atrioventricular accessory pathways, FVPs do not participate in reentrant tachycardias and are generally considered innocent bystanders.1,2Take-Home Messages•A fixed HV interval across multiple pacing maneuvers is diagnostic of a fasciculoventricular pathway and distinguishes it from true atrioventricular accessory pathways.•Recognition of this benign form of preexcitation is essential to avoid unnecessary and potentially harmful ablation near the His-Purkinje system.

On surface electrocardiography (ECG), FVPs may present with subtle delta waves, a short PR interval, and a near-normal QRS duration, often mimicking septal accessory pathway–mediated pre-excitation.1,2 As a result, patients with FVPs are frequently referred for invasive electrophysiological evaluation with suspected Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome.

Accurate recognition of this entity is clinically important because ablation of an FVP is unnecessary and may pose a risk of atrioventricular block due to its close proximity to the His-Purkinje system.3 We report a case of an FVP presenting with subtle preexcitation that closely mimicked WPW syndrome, highlighting key electrocardiographic and electrophysiological features that help avoid inappropriate ablation.

Case Presentation

A woman in her 40s was referred to our institution after an abnormal ECG detected during a routine health checkup suggested WPW syndrome. She reported occasional palpitations but denied syncope or documented tachyarrhythmias. There was no family history of sudden cardiac death.

Given the presence of symptoms and suspected ventricular preexcitation, an electrophysiological study was performed.

ECG Findings (ECG Challenge)

The baseline 12-lead ECG demonstrated a short PR interval of 115 milliseconds with subtle ventricular preexcitation. The QRS complex was isoelectric in lead V1, whereas small positive delta waves were observed in leads V5 and V6 (Figure 1). Management and Clinical Course

Although the FVP itself was considered benign and unlikely to account for the patient's palpitations, dual atrioventricular nodal physiology was demonstrated during isoproterenol infusion. Given the patient's occasional palpitation symptoms, slow pathway ablation was performed.

No ablation targeting the FVP was attempted. The patient had an uneventful postprocedural course and was discharged the following day.

Discussion

FVPs represent a distinct and uncommon cause of ventricular pre-excitation, characterized by direct conduction from the His bundle or proximal fascicles to the ventricular myocardium.1,4 Because these pathways bypass only a short segment of the normal conduction system, they do not support atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia and are typically benign.1,2 Previous case reports have noted that detailed anatomical or activation mapping of FVPs is inherently challenging because these tracts lack a discrete, targetable ventricular insertion and are closely integrated with the His-Purkinje system.1,2,5 Accordingly, the diagnosis of an FVP relies primarily on characteristic electrophysiological findings rather than on localization by mapping. Given the limited diagnostic yield and the absence of therapeutic implications, detailed mapping was not attempted in the present case.

Surface electrocardiographic findings in FVP are often subtle and nonspecific. A short PR interval with minimal preexcitation, near-normal QRS duration, and an isoelectric QRS complex in lead V1 with small positive delta waves in the lateral precordial leads have been described and may closely resemble septal accessory pathways.1,2 This overlap frequently leads to misdiagnosis and unnecessary concern for malignant arrhythmias associated with WPW syndrome.

From an electrophysiological perspective, a fixed HV interval regardless of atrial pacing, junctional rhythm, or adrenergic stimulation is the hallmark feature distinguishing FVPs from atrioventricular accessory pathways.1,2 In the present case, the HV interval remained constant across multiple pacing maneuvers and during isoproterenol infusion, strongly arguing against the presence of an atrioventricular bypass tract.

The disappearance of ventricular preexcitation during atrial extrastimulus pacing with the emergence of complete right bundle branch block morphology reflects functional refractoriness of the right bundle branch rather than loss of accessory pathway conduction. This phenomenon has been previously described in patients with FVP and should not be misinterpreted as evidence of a septal accessory pathway.2

Recognition of an FVP has important clinical implications. Ablation targeting these pathways is unnecessary and potentially harmful due to the risk of injury to the His-Purkinje system and subsequent atrioventricular block.3,6 In our patient, slow pathway ablation was performed to address dual atrioventricular nodal physiology, whereas the FVP was appropriately left untreated, resulting in an uneventful clinical course.

This case underscores the importance of careful integration of surface electrocardiographic findings with intracardiac electrophysiological assessment to correctly identify benign causes of ventricular preexcitation and avoid inappropriate ablation.
