Wireless IoT-enabled microneedle electroceutical for personalized and connected pain management
Authors: Heesoo Kim, Se Kyun Bang, Suk-Yun Kang, Sung Woo Kim, Jae-Soon Yang, Daehyuk Kim, Choong Yeon Kim, Sangeun Han, Yeji Jang, Juhyun Lee, Karen-Christian Agno, Jun-Bo Yoon, Sanghun Lee, Jae-Woong Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic pain has historically been managed with pharmacological therapies, including opioids that provide potent analgesia, but their sustainability is limited by dose-dependent risks of misuse, addiction, and overdose. Electroceuticals offer a non-pharmacologic alternative by modulating neural pathways through electrical stimulation, but current invasive systems require surgery and clinical supervision, whereas non-invasive devices suffer from poor therapeutic efficacy caused by unstable skin-electrode impedance. Here, we present a thermoresponsive, electrically conductive adhesive microneedle (TEAM) electroceutical that integrates low-impedance microneedle arrays and conductive hydrogel coatings within a compact wireless platform enabling IoT-based telemedicine for safe, remotely managed pain therapy with robust therapeutic outcomes. Experimental and analytical results demonstrate that TEAM improves charge injection efficiency and neural activation while maintaining electrical and thermal safety. The device achieves superior analgesic efficacy compared to conventional gel-based transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation in preclinical models, with performance comparable to pharmacological analgesics. IoT-enabled demonstrations further validate the feasibility of remote and scheduled therapy, as well as proof-of-concept automatically triggered neuromodulation guided by physiological pain-induced stress markers in human subjects. TEAM provides a foundation for scalable electroceutical therapy and holds promise for future preclinical-to-clinical translation toward real-world digital pain management.Subject terms: Health care, Quality of life, Biomedical engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Chronic pain is one of the most widespread yet least effectively managed medical conditions1–4; in the United States, 24.3% of adults experience chronic pain and 8.5% of adults live with symptoms that severely restrict daily activities5. Beyond persistent nociception, it manifests as sleep disturbance6, fatigue7, depression8, and loss of mobility9, profoundly diminishing quality of life. The U.S. Institute of Medicine has estimated the annual economic burden of pain at $560–635 billion10. This negative trend is expected to intensify due to global population aging, rising diabetes prevalence, and improved survival following cancer therapy11. Since chronic pain, by definition, cannot be permanently cured by current biomedical approaches, long-term management that maintains symptoms at tolerable levels has become a critical clinical priority12.

Pharmacologic therapy is the most common treatment due to its rapid and inexpensive pain relief12–14. Although the analgesic ladder (transitions from non-opioids to opioids according to the patient’s pain intensity) is the global standard guideline for pain management15, opioid use carries dose- and duration-dependent risks of misuse, addiction, and overuse that pose threats to patient health. Approximately 15–26% of patients prescribed opioids experienced abuse and addiction-related problems13,16,17, and 4% of individuals with prescription opioid addiction progress to heroin use18. Consequently, an estimated 40.5 million people live with opioid dependence, with over 109,000 overdose deaths recorded globally19. These alarming statistics underscore the urgent need for safer, non-addictive strategies for chronic pain management20.

Electroceuticals have emerged as a promising alternative for on-demand pain relief through electrical modulation of neural pathways1,21,22. The electrical stimulation modulates the activity of peripheral and central neural circuits, enhancing inhibitory neural pathways and suppressing nociceptive signal transmission, thereby inducing analgesia21. These approaches include invasive systems (e.g., peripheral nerve, spinal cord stimulators) and non-invasive systems (e.g., transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)). Although implantable electroceuticals can effectively target deep neural structures, they remain a last-resort treatment because they pose surgical risks23,24 and require precise electrode placement by medical specialists25. Non-invasive approaches such as TENS mitigate risks and are widely used; however, they mainly reach superficial nerves22, and rely on gel electrodes whose skin-electrode impedance varies with sweat, pressure, and signal frequency, reducing efficacy and reproducibility of therapy26–29. Moreover, because their performance depends heavily on user settings, improper use can lead to skin irritation, burns30,31, or reduced analgesic effect32. Therefore, consistent real-world benefits require a reliable current delivery interface for targeting neural pathways alongside professional oversight.

Microneedle electrostimulation systems may offer a promising strategy to balance the trade-off between implantable and wearable interfaces. By penetrating the stratum corneum and directly accessing skin tissue, these electrodes can provide a relatively stable electrical interface under varying skin conditions. Although many conventional microneedles for electrostimulation33–37 exhibit sufficient conductivity, their lack of adhesion and electrical safety strategies, combined with a sharp needle tip geometry prone to current crowding, can compromise their capability to deliver high-current stimulation while maintaining a robust interface, thereby constraining their application to low-current regimes such as wound healing34,35 or localized immunomodulation33,36.

These interface-level limitations and the need for clinical supervision underscore the importance of developing electroceuticals that are not only effective and safe but also inherently compatible with remote, connected care. Studies suggest that nearly 40% of patients with chronic pain do not receive adequate treatment38, partly due to barriers to in-person care stemming from mobility limitations, geographic distance, or public health restrictions39,40. Telemedicine has thus become increasingly indispensable for such populations. Effective pain therapy requires seamless connectivity, objective monitoring of physiological pain markers41–43, and personalized protocols that can be clinician-supervised or automated through closed-loop systems44–46. To address these challenges, IoT-enabled approaches for pain assessment and management have been developed41; however, these systems remain limited to data collection via sensors47–50 or self-reporting51,52 and treatment suggestions53, failing to integrate into closed-loop platforms for active therapeutic intervention. Moreover, many existing platforms have limited ability to remotely coordinate multiple devices or to maintain seamless functionality during user mobility, despite the World Health Organization’s call for digital health infrastructure for equitable healthcare access54–56.

To address these technological gaps, we present the thermoresponsive, electrically conductive adhesive microneedle (TEAM) electroceutical that combines a safe and robust charge-delivery interface with Internet-of-Things (IoT)-enabled telemedicine frameworks for personalized pain therapy. TEAM integrates microneedle arrays with conductive, temperature-tunable adhesive hydrogel coatings. The microneedle array penetrates the resistive stratum corneum and enhances charge-injection efficiency, enabling effective stimulation of pain-modulating neural pathways. The conductive adhesive hydrogel not only prevents current crowding at needles by providing an additional conductive path but also maintains stable coupling during prolonged wear. When temperatures exceed safety thresholds, the hydrogel’s adhesion decreases, facilitating safe detachment and reducing electrical burn risk. Embedded within a compact wireless patch with current-controlled stimulation, smartphone-enabled charging and Bluetooth/cellular connectivity, TEAM supports both patient-controlled therapy and clinician-supervised telecare. Integrated photoplethysmography (PPG) sensing and internet cloud-based analytics further enable closed-loop operation guided by cardiac dynamics related to pain-induced stress, supporting continuous and personalized pain management.

In this work, we demonstrate the translational potential of TEAM through systematic material, electrical, and thermal characterization, preclinical validation of neural activation and analgesic effects, remote IoT-enabled trials, and pilot human testing. While maintaining a consistent skin-electrode impedance regardless of skin conditions—a clear advantage over conventional gel interfaces—the TEAM also exhibited enhanced and safer high-current injection capabilities compared to existing microneedle platforms. In vivo experiments in rodents demonstrate that TEAM enhances target nerve activation and analgesic efficacy compared to gel electrode interfaces, achieving pain relief comparable to pharmacologic analgesics. Proof-of-concept human studies reveal that the IoT telecare system enables clinician-administered therapy for freely moving subjects, including outdoors, and highlight the potential for personalized pain management via closed-loop therapy based on physiological monitoring and clinical oversight. These findings collectively support the preliminary feasibility of the TEAM system as a wireless, wearable electroceutical platform for personalized and remotely accessible pain management.

Results

Concept and operation principle of the wireless TEAM system

Figures 1a, b provide an overview of the wireless TEAM system, whose central component is a TEAM patch for advanced pain management (Fig. 1a). The patch connects to ubiquitous wireless networks to enable patient-directed or clinician-assisted therapy. The microneedle array in the TEAM patch penetrates the highly resistive stratum corneum ( ~ 105 Ω∙cm2)57, creating a low-impedance skin interface that enables efficient and stable current delivery to subcutaneous nerves at various depths. This strategy allows effective analgesic stimulation while minimizing discomfort. A conductive hydrogel coating with temperature-responsive adhesion on the flexible microneedles ensures an electrical burn-free, conformal, and durable skin interface. This design overcomes the limitations of both invasive and non-invasive electroceutical modalities by simultaneously achieving long-term wearability and efficient charge injection (see Supplementary Table 1 for detailed features and comparison with contemporary microneedle electrostimulation systems). To complement this therapeutic core, the wireless TEAM system also incorporates a wireless PPG sensor that monitors physiological parameters associated with pain-related stress. The recorded PPG signals are streamed in real time to a cloud server, where heart rate and heart rate variability are analyzed to infer pain status58,59. Based on this, the system can deliver personalized therapeutic interventions through either clinician-initiated control or automatically triggered mode.

The TEAM system leverages smartphones as gateways, seamlessly connecting the TEAM and PPG patches to a cloud-based server (i.e., Firebase, Google LLC) through Bluetooth and cellular networks. In this system, two primary modes of operation are supported: (i) local treatment mode, in which patients directly control their patches via a Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) piconet (a short-range wireless network connecting one primary device to multiple secondary devices via Bluetooth protocol60); and (ii) telecare mode, in which smartphones function as secure relay nodes, enabling remote clinician monitoring and real-time or scheduled therapy initiation (e.g., through synchronized calendars (e.g., Google Calendar)). Telecare mode additionally supports closed-loop automation, whereby pain-related stress signals derived from PPG analysis directly trigger adaptive electrostimulation via TEAM patches. Because smartphones remain ubiquitously connected through cellular technologies (e.g., LTE, 5G), the TEAM system maintains full functionality during outdoor activities and travel. This ubiquitous connectivity may substantially expand access to care for elderly or rural patients who face geographical and financial barriers to in-person consultations, and may also mitigate habituation phenomena frequently observed during indiscriminate long-term TENS therapy32.

Figure 1c shows the hardware configuration of the TEAM patch. The lightweight and flexible device incorporates a custom stimulation control circuit (Fig. 1d, e) that integrates a biphasic current driver, a wireless power transfer module, a BLE system-on-chip (SoC; EYSHSNZWZ, Taiyo Yuden), and a miniature lithium-polymer battery (TW401012, Thehan). The microneedle electrodes are connected to the circuit via a miniaturized neodymium magnetic connector, which is electrically connected with Ag conductive epoxy, aided by the intrinsic conductivity of the magnets themselves, enabling easy replacement of the electrodes while allowing semi-permanent reuse of the main circuitry. The skin-device interface features an adhesive silicone coating (Silbione RT Gel 4717, Bluestar Silicones) with a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) protective layer (15:1 mixing ratio) on the top side, ensuring intimate, conformal contact with curved body surfaces for imperceptible long-term wear (Supplementary Fig. 1a)61. Similarly, the PPG patch (Supplementary Fig. 1b), fabricated on a flexible printed circuit board (fPCB), integrates a photodiode and a light-emitting diode (LED) and is coated with black-dyed silicone to suppress ambient light interference.

At the system level (Fig. 1f), the cloud web server employs smartphones as access nodes, with TEAM and PPG patches connected via BLE. Optimized throughput and connection management allow simultaneous operation of more than ten TEAM patches and up to four PPG sensors. Given that conventional TENS therapy typically targets one to four anatomical sites62,63, this scalability enables multi-site and parallelized electrostimulation therapies across diverse pain sites. To preserve reliability, TEAM patches pair with the registered smartphone only during active therapy sessions, thereby preventing unintended connections. Collectively, the system architecture supports daily pain management in real-world contexts and holds potential to improve quality of life for patients with chronic pain who otherwise lack access to continuous medical care.

Material, electrical, and thermal characterization of TEAM

The TEAM comprises four layers (Fig. 2a, b): (i) a flexible polyimide (PI) microneedle array, (ii) a conductive gold (Au) layer, (iii) a Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS)/Polyurethane (PU) interlayer, and (iv) a conductive hydrogel coating. The PI microneedle array (200 μm protruding height above the hydrogel coating), coated with titanium (Ti)/Au (20/200 nm) and PEDOT:PSS/PU ( ~ 1 μm), penetrates only the stratum corneum and upper epidermis without reaching nociceptors located deeper than 200 μm beneath the skin surface64–66, thereby enabling pain-free insertion while markedly reducing contact impedance. Here, the Au layer is amine-functionalized and chemically bonded to a hydrophilic PU matrix, forming an interpenetrating polymer structure that ensures robust adhesion to the PEDOT:PSS conductive layer67 and prevents delamination between the metal-coated microneedle array and the hydrogel. The PEDOT:PSS distributed at the microneedle surface and within the overlying hydrogel, owing to its high capacitance, lowers interfacial impedance and increases the charge storage and charge injection capacity of TEAM68,69 (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). While conductive microneedles have been previously used for bioelectronic stimulation, they were typically constrained to low-current regimes ( < 10 μA)33,34 due to current crowding at the sharp needle tips. TEAM overcomes this limitation by combining a sharp needle geometry with a uniform conductive hydrogel coating (Fig. 2a, c). This hydrogel features an interpenetrating double-network architecture, formed by in situ polymerization of thermally responsive N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM) and acrylamide (AAm) within a physically crosslinked PEDOT:PSS network (Supplementary Fig. 4). To avoid PEDOT:PSS aggregation caused by conventional radical initiators, a redox-initiated polymerization system based on hydrogen peroxide and ascorbic acid was used, which enables rapid and uniform hydrogel formation at room temperature70.

This conductive hydrogel layer establishes a parallel current pathway along the skin surface (Fig. 2c), alleviating current crowding at the needle tips and lowering interfacial impedance (see Supplementary Fig. 5 for the electrode-skin equivalent circuit model, and Supplementary Fig. 6 for the current density distribution through microneedle and hydrogel conduction pathways). As a result, TEAM exhibits significantly lower and more stable skin-electrode impedance (10.3 kΩ∙cm2 at 100 Hz, tested on human skin; Fig. 2d) across various skin conditions–including clean, sweaty, hairy skin, and skin contaminated with baby powder and oil (Supplementary Fig. 7)–compared to other electrode types–including commercial gel electrodes, Au-coated PI microneedle electrode (Au MNE), and conductive hydrogel electrodes. Importantly, this low impedance was maintained over 5 days, whereas conventional gel electrodes degraded rapidly due to skin secretions such as sebum, sweat, and dead skin cells26 (tested on human skin; Fig. 2e). This prolonged stability is partly attributed to the minimal dehydration of the hydrogel coating, which preserved conformal skin contact and minimized impedance fluctuations over time (Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover, under applied pressures ranging from 0 to 100 kPa, TEAM remained highly stable, showing only ~2 kΩ∙cm² impedance variation, while gel electrodes exhibited much larger variations (61 kΩ∙cm²) (Supplementary Fig. 9). Detailed contact impedance and adhesion characterization in harsh environments such as active motion condition and perspirative condition is provided in Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11.

The hydrogel coating also imparts excellent conformability and adhesion to the skin, ensuring mechanical stability during long-term wear. However, to avoid skin irritation and potential tissue damage caused by excessive adhesion or electrical stimulation-induced heat generation, the electrode must detach cleanly when necessary. The NIPAM-AAm polymer backbone facilitates strong yet reversible skin adhesion due to its abundant polar groups, which mediate van der Waals forces and electrostatic interactions70. By tuning the lower critical solution temperature (LCST) of the polymer network above body temperature through hydrophilic AAm incorporation (Supplementary Fig. 12), the hydrogel remains adhesive at room temperature (0.22 N/cm), comparable to commercial medical tapes (0.16–1.2 N/cm)71, but becomes non-adhesive (0.02 N/cm) at elevated temperature (43 °C), consistent with the IEC 60601-1 thermal safety standard72 for skin-contact medical devices worn over 10 min (Fig. 2f, g). The temperature-triggered safety mechanism was confirmed to operate reliably across an ambient temperature range of −10 °C to 45 °C, owing to the thermoregulatory properties of the human body that maintain skin surface temperature homeostasis (Supplementary Fig. 13). Detailed thermal characterization, including the thermal safety margin before tissue damage onset, detachment time as a function of skin temperature, and skin surface temperature during stimulation, is provided in Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15.

Figure 2h–j present the multiphysics simulation results illustrating the TEAM’s electrical and thermal performance. As shown in Fig. 2h and i, electric field simulations confirmed that TEAM-based stimulation achieves significantly greater field penetration across dermal layers that host A-β (type II afferent fibers from ultrafast nociceptors and stretch receptors73,74), A-δ (type III afferent fibers of nociceptors73), and C fibers (type IV afferent fibers of nociceptors and warmth receptors73), with an enhancement of up to 19% and 49% in the median nerve and peroneal nerve regions, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 16). These fibers are known mediators of TENS-induced analgesia75–78. For electrical performance of TEAM in an animal model, see Supplementary Fig. 17. Additionally, thermal simulations demonstrated negligible tissue heating during stimulation (maximum temperature: 36.9 °C), confirming thermal safety for transcutaneous applications (Fig. 2j). Furthermore, ex vivo experiments on porcine skin confirmed no occurrence of electrical burns under typical stimulation protocols (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Finally, biocompatibility was verified through 24-h cell viability assays with TEAM, which exhibited comparable cell viability to the phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) control group (Supplementary Fig. 19). Together with conformal reversible adhesion, stable electrical performance, and thermal safety, this high biocompatibility demonstrates TEAM’s translational potential as a clinically viable interface for safe, user-friendly, long-term electroceutical pain therapy.

Design and validation of the TEAM patch circuit

Figure 3a illustrates the circuit architecture of the wireless TEAM patch, which integrates a Qi wireless power transfer module and a programmable transcutaneous stimulation circuit for chronic pain therapy. Qi wireless charging is the most widely adopted wireless power transfer standard, offering seamless compatibility with most mobile device chargers and smartphone-based battery sharing systems, thereby enhancing user convenience without the need for dedicated or bulky equipment79.

In the TEAM patch, a Qi power management circuit with a 13-turn antenna coil (21.2 μH, resonance at ~120 kHz) wirelessly harvests power via inductive coupling to generate DC charging current for the LiPo battery. The battery then powers an onboard microcontroller unit (MCU), which controls stimulation and control circuitry, including a DC boost converter, BLE SoC, biphasic switch, and feedback modules. This design enables fully programmable stimulation waveforms while supporting safe, wireless, and continuous operation without the need for external cables or chargers. The MCU controls the generation of customizable TENS waveforms by managing a switch-mode DC converter that boosts the LiPo battery voltage. A biphasic switch circuit, implemented with bipolar junction transistor (BJT)-based polarity inverters, alternates the waveform polarity at each pulse phase. Such biphasic stimulation signals, whether anodic- or cathodic-leading, prevent net charge accumulation and mitigate electrochemical damage to both tissue and electrodes caused by electrolyte and pH imbalances during prolonged electrical therapy80–82.

As shown in Fig. 3b, c parameters such as frequency, amplitude, and pulse-width can be flexibly tuned to accommodate inter-patient variability83. During 100 cycles of 4.7 mA biphasic stimulation at 100 Hz, the leakage current remained below 10 μA, well within the IEC 60601-1 standard for wearable devices (Fig. 3d)84. This result demonstrates that the circuit delivers safe stimulation over extended use while maintaining precise charge balance. In the rare event that the TEAM interface becomes unstable, the circuit can switch from voltage control mode to constant current driving mode to maintain therapeutic consistency, implementing a dual-safety system. In this mode, the voltage change across a sense resistor is continuously monitored and fed back to the BLE SoC. Pulse width modulation (PWM) duty cycles are dynamically adjusted to ensure stable current output. As shown in Fig. 3e, Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21, the circuit successfully maintains target current regardless of skin conditions and load changes, demonstrating robust closed-loop regulation under variable skin impedance. Mechanical robustness is also essential for wearable electroceuticals. The TEAM circuit maintained stable resistance during bending tests down to a 1 cm radius, confirming that mechanical deformation does not compromise electrical stability (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 22).

The integrated Qi wireless charging system also allows smartphone-based charging, ensuring user-friendly operation. The antenna, with a quality factor (Q) of 37, enables efficient power transfer up to a 10 mm distance and under bending radii as small as 10 mm (Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24). Considering that the curvature of most body regions rarely exceeds a 10 mm bending radius, this level of flexibility is sufficient for typical skin deformation during daily wear. Under standard Qi-based charging, the battery reached maximum battery voltage ( ~ 4.1 V) in 15 min. Once fully charged, the device operated continuously for 10 h during stimulation at 2 Hz, 2.5 ms pulse width, and 80 V peak-to-peak amplitude (Fig. 3g). Power delivery characteristics of our TEAM patch circuit under different load conditions are shown in Fig. 3h, with a maximum power output of 27.5 mW at 180 Ω load resistance, optimized by the wireless power management system. The Qi-integrated charging system with smartphone compatibility thus eliminates the need for bulky, dedicated RF equipment and represents a major step toward practical deployment by lowering barriers to routine use and enhancing patient adherence (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 25).

In addition to the TEAM patch, Supplementary Fig. 26 illustrates the circuit of PPG patch for real-time physiological monitoring. A red LED (632 nm) and photodiode detect a blood flow signal, which is processed via a low-pass filter and transimpedance amplifier with DC offset control for optimal dynamic range. This signal helps infer pain-induced stress response58,85, enabling future closed-loop pain modulation. Although not the therapeutic core, the PPG circuit provides an important pathway toward integrated feedback control, linking physiological monitoring with electrostimulation.

Altogether, the integration of programmable stimulation, adaptive current regulation, robust mechanical stability, and smartphone-enabled wireless charging establishes the TEAM patch as a reliable and scalable foundation for wearable electroceutical systems.

Analgesic effects in neuropathic pain

The therapeutic efficacy of electrical therapy is associated with its ability to activate specific spinal cord pathways that regulate nociceptive processing. As part of the TEAM system, we first examined whether TEAMs could reliably engage spinal pain-modulatory circuits. Electrical stimulation (2 Hz, 0.5 mA, 0.1 ms pulse width, 10 s duration) was delivered via TEAMs or gel electrodes positioned below the knee joint, and wide dynamic range (WDR) neuronal activity was recorded in the lumbar-sacral spinal cord (L4–S1) (Fig. 4a). Peripheral afferent activation was quantified by comparing firing rates during a 1 s baseline period before stimulation with those in the 1 s response window after stimulation (Fig. 4b). Both electrode types significantly increased WDR activity: firing rate in the gel group rose from 1.8 ± 0.3 spikes/s to 3.9 ± 0.2 spikes/s, whereas the TEAM group showed a greater increase, from 1.9 ± 0.2 spikes/s to 5.0 ± 0.2 spikes/s (mean ± s.e.m.; Fig. 4c). The normalized relative firing rate in the TEAM group reached 128.2 ± 5.9% (**p = 0.0038), significantly higher than the gel group (100 ± 5.2%), demonstrating superior stimulation efficacy of TEAMs (Fig. 4d). Importantly, additional electromyography (EMG) control experiments showed that this enhanced firing was unlikely to be explained by differential motor fiber recruitment, as quantitative root mean square (RMS) analysis of EMG signals revealed no statistically significant difference between the TEAM and gel groups, and no visible muscle contractions were observed throughout the stimulation period (Supplementary Fig. 27).

Figure 4e shows the behavior test setup for evaluating the analgesic effects of the TEAM therapy in a mouse model of neuropathic pain. A chemotherapy-induced pain model was established by paclitaxel (PTX) injection. The TEAM patch, attached to the mouse’s back, was connected to either conventional gel electrodes (PTX+Gel) or TEAMs (PTX + TEAM) placed on the lateral aspects of the knee joint to deliver stimulation (2 Hz, 0.3 mA, 5 ms pulse width, 180 s duration). Duloxetine treatment (PTX+Dulo), an analgesic pharmaceutical intervention, served as a positive control. Mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia were assessed using the von Frey filament test and the Hargreaves test, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4f, PTX significantly increased paw withdrawal frequency (PWF), confirming mechanical allodynia. All treatments reduced PWF, but TEAM and duloxetine produced greater analgesic effects than gel electrodes (gel: 2 h, *p < 0.05; duloxetine: 2–8 h, *p < 0.05; TEAM: 2–4 h, **p < 0.01 vs. PTX). By 24 h post-treatment, PWF values in all groups had returned to baseline levels. In Fig. 4g, PTX significantly shortened paw withdrawal latency (PWL) to noxious heat, indicating thermal hyperalgesia. TEAM stimulation reversed this effect, producing significantly longer PWL at 1–2 h (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 vs. PTX) and outperforming duloxetine at the 2 h peak (*p < 0.05 vs. PTX). Gel electrodes, in contrast, produced only modest, non-significant benefit, underscoring the advantage of TEAMs for thermal pain relief. Representative traces of TEAM-induced analgesia at peak time points are shown in Fig. 4h, highlighting its robust efficacy in behavioral assays. Additionally, quantitative RMS analysis of EMG signals revealed that the therapeutic intensity of 0.3 mA elicited no abnormal muscle contractions or behavioral suppression during or after stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 28)

To examine effects on central sensitization, we measured Fos protein expression (a neuronal activation marker) in the superficial dorsal horn (SDH), nucleus proprius (NP), and neck region of the dorsal horn (NECK) (Supplementary Fig. 29). Quantitative analysis (Fig. 4i) showed that PTX markedly increased Fos-positive cells in all regions compared to normal controls. Both treatments reduced Fos expression, but TEAM showed superior efficacy: in the SDH region, gel (*p < 0.05) and TEAM (**p < 0.01) both significantly reduced Fos; in the NP region, only TEAM reached significance (*p < 0.05); in the NECK region, neither was significant. These results suggest that TEAM stimulation more effectively suppresses central sensitization, particularly in key nociceptive relay regions such as the SDH and NP86,87.

Safety evaluation using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay confirmed that TEAM attachment and stimulation did not cause morphological damage or apoptosis in mouse skin tissue (Fig. 4j). Both TEAM groups, with and without stimulation, showed no significant differences in epidermal thickness or apoptotic cell count compared to normal controls. Collectively, these results indicate that TEAM provides robust analgesic effects in a preclinical neuropathic pain model, with efficacy comparable or superior to pharmacological treatment. They also effectively attenuate central sensitization and maintain excellent cutaneous biocompatibility, thereby outperforming conventional gel electrodes in both electrophysiological and behavioral outcomes.

Evaluation of IoT-enabled remote analgesia using the wireless TEAM system

The IoT control feature of our TEAM system enables not only local on-demand device activation but also remote, double-blind experimentation for unbiased pain assessment. In the double-blind test shown in Fig. 5a, 12 mice were equipped with TEAM patches and six were randomly selected to receive remote electrical stimulation therapy. Stimulation commands were sent by a researcher located ~14 km away from the animal laboratory via the TEAM web server, while a smartphone beside the mouse cage acted as a BLE hub connected through the cellular network (Fig. 5b). This double-blind testing eliminates observer bias, ensuring more accurate behavioral data measurements88.

In addition to enabling double-blind operation, this remote control framework was evaluated for its responsiveness and scalability. Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31 and Supplementary Movies 1, 2 and 3 illustrate three control modes: (i) local treatment mode (local piconet), (ii) telecare mode (Internet-based control), and (iii) scheduled control. Local mode achieved the fastest average command transmission ( ~ 36 ms), followed by Internet ( ~ 223 + α ms) and scheduled ( ~ 528 + α ms), where α represents the propagation delay between the remote operator and hub device (Supplementary Fig. 30a). When eight TEAM devices were controlled simultaneously, latency increased due to device scanning, connection, and command verification steps, yet it averaged 1.7 s in the worst-case scenario under schedule mode (Supplementary Fig. 30b)–far shorter than the timescale of typical 15–30 min electrotherapy sessions62. BLE latency remained negligible even during ongoing stimulation for up to 10 devices (Supplementary Fig. 31), confirming that the system can reliably synchronize multiple devices within a ~ 1 s control window.

With the communication system validated, we evaluated the contribution of electrical stimulation to TEAM’s analgesic efficacy. Neuropathic pain mice were treated with TEAMs either with (PTX + TEAM) or without stimulation (PTX + TEAM w/o stim). In mechanical sensitivity testing (Fig. 5c, d), the PTX + TEAM group produced significantly greater reduction in PWF at 1, 2, and 4 h (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 vs. PTX), indicating robust anti-allodynic effects with electrical stimulation. PWF returned to baseline by 24 h in the PTX + TEAM group, whereas the PTX + TEAM w/o stim group showed only modest improvement. In thermal sensitivity test (Fig. 5e, f), the PTX + TEAM group significantly increased PWL at 1–2 h post-treatment (*p < 0.05 vs. PTX), indicating stronger attenuation of thermal hyperalgesia. The PTX + TEAM w/o stim group showed only slight, generally non-significant changes. Although the PTX + TEAM effect gradually declined after 8 h, it remained higher than that of the PTX + TEAM w/o stim group through 24 h.

Finally, to examine potential anti-inflammatory effects of our TEAM therapy, we measured plasma levels of proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in PTX-induced neuropathic pain mice using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Fig. 5g). Both cytokines were elevated in all PTX-treated groups, but the PTX + TEAM group showed a modest reduction in IL-1β (*p < 0.05) and a similar trend for TNF-α (**p < 0.01) compared to PTX alone. These findings suggest that TEAM-based electrical stimulation not only alleviates pain but may also partially suppress PTX-induced proinflammatory signaling. Taken together, these results demonstrate the potential of the wireless TEAM system as a scalable IoT-enabled platform capable of delivering remotely controlled, multimodal pain relief through both neuronal and anti-inflammatory mechanisms in preclinical settings.

Pilot human testing of feedback-enabled pain management with the wireless TEAM system

Effective pain relief requires not only reliable stimulation interfaces but also feedback-enabled control systems that adapt therapy to the patient’s dynamic physiological state. Such feedback integration is essential because conventional open-loop approaches cannot provide continuous or personalized adjustment, which often leads to inconsistent therapeutic outcomes. Figure 6a illustrates two complementary modes of feedback-enabled control within our TEAM system: (i) clinician-in-the-loop mode, where healthcare providers remotely monitor patient physiology and deliver individualized therapy either in real time or on a scheduled basis, and (ii) automatically triggered mode, where electrotherapy is triggered directly from PPG-derived cardiac activity that reflects pain-induced stress. Together, these modes provide a continuum of care that balances clinical oversight with automated responsiveness, while supporting cumulative and sustained analgesic effects over the course of repeated scheduled treatment89,90. In addition, all treatment logs are stored in the cloud, enabling continuous monitoring to prevent overuse and to ensure the safety of long-term electroceutical pain management (Supplementary Note 1).

Figure 6b presents a preliminary human study that assessed the analgesic potential of TEAM under the clinician-in-the-loop mode. Quantitative sensory testing (QST) is a standardized psychophysical method that measures sensory thresholds by quantifying patient responses to controlled stimuli91,92. The method detects both reduced and enhanced sensory function, enabling assessment of nociceptive properties of distinct afferent nerve fibers and their central pathways93,94. Before electrical therapy and QST, healthcare providers individually conducted in-person assessments of stimulation parameters by adjusting the current intensity between 1.5 and 10 mA at a frequency of 100 Hz, until each subject reported a strong but non-painful level of sensation, consistent with current clinical practice for determining TENS stimulation intensity89,95. For the scheduled electrical therapy sessions in clinician-in-the-loop mode, TEAM patches were placed on the lateral and anterior knee to stimulate the common and superficial peroneal nerves, and on the anterior ankle and foot dorsum to target the deep peroneal nerve and its branches. Scheduled electrical stimulation was then remotely set up by healthcare providers via an internet-based calendar with preset parameters. Subjects were free to move to different locations, including outdoor environments, during 20-min scheduled stimulation sessions, after which QST was conducted in person. For the sham control, patches were placed at the same locations, and stimulation was delivered for 1 minute before the device was turned off for the remainder of the session, without informing the subjects of the early termination. Detailed QST and electrical therapy protocols are provided in Methods and Supplementary Fig. 32. Following therapy, thermal detection and pain thresholds showed significant shifts. Under cold stimulation, both detection and pain thresholds decreased by 1.73 and 1.70 just noticeable difference (JND) units on average across hand and calf measurements (Fig. 6c), respectively, while heat stimulation increased thresholds by 1.46 and 1.64 JND units, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 33), indicating that subjects required a more extreme cold/heat stimulus to elicit a pain response. In contrast, the sham control group showed no statistically significant difference following sham therapy. Notably, no adverse skin events were observed during stimulation, supporting the preliminary safety of TEAM therapy in human use (Supplementary Fig. 34). These results support the feasibility and analgesic potential of TEAM therapy in human settings, suggesting performance on par with more invasive electroceutical approaches78,96.

Beyond direct subjective pain reporting, pain states are also reflected in cardiac dynamics through autonomic nervous system modulation. Although individual variability exists, pain and stress typically shift the autonomic balance towards sympathetic dominance97, characterized by elevated heart rate and reduced heart rate variability (HRV)58,85—both extractable from PPG signals. Common HRV indices include the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) and standard deviation of NN intervals (SDNN), which respectively reflect vagal-mediated parasympathetic activity and combined sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system activity58,85. To model pain-induced stress, a cold pressor test was performed. Compared with baseline, the cold-induced pain state produced a 13% increase in HR and 16% and 14% reduction in RMSSD and SDNN, respectively (Fig. 6d). Using these features, normal and pain-induced stress states were classified with 89.1% accuracy via linear discriminant analysis (LDA), demonstrating the feasibility of automated pain-induced stress detection (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 35). As the QST and cold pressor test was a pilot human study with a small cohort, further validation in larger populations and longer-term sessions will be necessary to establish clinical utility.

Figure 6f demonstrates proof-of-concept closed-loop operation of the TEAM system. The device automatically delivered programmed electrical therapy within ~10 s when PPG-derived HR and HRV parameters exceeded predefined thresholds based on cold-induced pain-associated cardiac activity. These results demonstrate the feasibility of automated electroceutical therapy, with scalability contingent on robust and accurate pain-induced stress-state monitoring. Overall, the integration of miniaturized electroceuticals with IoT telecare systems – combining clinician oversight with automated feedback – addresses inherent limitations of conventional care and suggests a potential path toward clinically accessible and intelligent pain management for medically underserved populations.

Discussion

This work presents the design and validation of the wireless TEAM system for advanced pain management within an IoT-enabled telemedicine framework. By combining TEAMs with miniaturized stimulation circuits featuring adaptive current regulation, the device achieves (i) stable current injection under variable skin and environmental conditions, (ii) thermally and electrically safe electrotherapy, and (iii) reversible skin adhesion that ensures long-term wearability without irritation. These features address the limitations of conventional invasive and non-invasive electroceuticals27,98, positioning TEAM as a promising interface for daily, user-friendly pain management.

This study demonstrates that TEAMs reduce interfacial impedance and enhance charge injection efficacy, thereby promoting stronger neural activation and leading to robust analgesia in both preclinical electrophysiological and behavioral models. These analgesic effects may involve neuro-immunological mechanisms, as localized peripheral nerve stimulation has been reported to modulate systemic inflammatory and neuroinflammatory responses99,100. Importantly, these outcomes were achieved while maintaining biocompatibility and avoiding skin damage, underscoring their potential for prolonged use. Beyond local efficacy, integration with IoT connectivity enables real-time or scheduled remote control and automatically triggered neuromodulation, supporting both clinician-supervised therapy and unbiased double-blind testing. Such ubiquitous connectivity can broaden access to precision electroceutical care for patients facing geographic, economic, or mobility barriers.

In addition to pain treatment, this work demonstrated the preliminary feasibility of a closed-loop framework for automated pain-induced stress sensing and management using a PPG patch, which classified pain-induced stress states via HR/HRV changes and triggered electrotherapy accordingly in a pilot study. Looking forward, the TEAM platform can be extended to electroceutical applications beyond pain, including neurological and psychiatric disorders such as depression and anxiety, highlighting the potential of wireless wearable patches as generalized closed-loop electroceuticals.

In conclusion, this work provides preliminary evidence for the foundational design principles of wireless, feedback-enabled, and clinically translatable electroceuticals that combine high-performance skin interfaces with ubiquitous healthcare infrastructure. Together, these studies suggest the potential of TEAM as a clinically translatable electroceutical interface that combines material innovation with digital health infrastructure to support scalable, personalized, and equitable pain management. By potentially addressing the socioeconomic burden of pain – through reducing direct medical costs, improving productivity, and offering non-opioid alternatives1–this technology holds promise for reshaping the landscape of chronic pain management, provided that its clinical efficacy and long-term therapeutic performance are established in future studies.

Despite this promising progress, several challenges remain in the current implementation. First, the pilot human study was conducted in a small cohort of healthy subjects, which limits statistical power and generalizability to broader patient populations. Furthermore, sensory and pain threshold modulation was assessed using QST, which quantifies psychophysical responses to standardized stimuli but does not directly measure clinical pain relief; these findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary evidence of pain threshold modulation rather than conclusive analgesic efficacy in humans. Large-scale clinical trials across diverse acute and chronic pain populations (e.g., postoperative pain and chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy patients) are necessary to enable translation from pilot feasibility to routine clinical care. Additionally, the human QST protocol relies on subjective sensation levels to determine stimulation intensity, which may limit the ability to establish a quantitative and reproducible electrotherapy dose. Incorporating more objective calibration markers, including baseline skin impedance, electrical sensory threshold, and early physiological responses to stimulation, could improve the objectivity of intensity calibration and mitigate inter-subject variability in response.

Second, the operational robustness of the TEAM system warrants further investigation. While the hydrogel–skin interface was validated under individual stressors including sweating, motion and skin stretching, and long-term use for up to 5 days, its performance under the simultaneous combination of these factors—across diverse skin properties and body morphologies—remains to be established. Whether reliable adhesion and stable electrode-skin contact for consistent stimulation performance can be maintained under such combined real-world conditions requires further validation. Additionally, although the thermoresponsive detachment was characterized under controlled laboratory conditions (over a temperature range of −10–45 °C), real-world perspiration levels vary with ambient humidity and inter-subject physiology, which may narrow the effective safe operating temperature range. Environmental temperature may also influence detachment behavior, potentially increasing the risk of premature detachment under hot conditions or delayed detachment under cold conditions. Additional safety design considerations may therefore be warranted to ensure robust performance across a wider range of environmental and user-dependent conditions.

Finally, although recent studies have reported that PPG-derived cardiac activity can be utilized for pain monitoring101,102, the assessment in this study was limited to short-term observation and relied on a narrow set of cardiac indices (RMSSD and SDNN). Consequently, these metrics may not fully capture pain-specific physiological responses as they also reflect general arousal or stress and are susceptible to non-pain-related confounding factors such as physical activity, psychological stress, and hydration status59. In addition, substantial inter-individual variability in physiological characteristics—such as bidirectional heart rate responses during the cold pressor test—poses challenges for robust pain-induced stress classification. Therefore, the LDA-based classification should be interpreted as a preliminary detection indicator of pain-associated physiological arousal rather than a definitive quantification of pain, and the system may therefore be susceptible to false-positive triggering in home-care settings, for example during brisk walking, household activities, or emotional stress. Overall, these limitations could be mitigated by developing more accurate pain classification models incorporating multimodal sensing103,104, including blood pressure, skin conductance, facial expression, and patient-reported outcomes1,47,105. Integrating this multimodal sensing platform with advanced motion artifact reduction methods106,107 and large cohort datasets sourced directly from chronic pain patients will be essential for developing a more robust and reliable pain management platform for future real-world applications108.

Methods

Design and fabrication of wireless TEAM and PPG circuits

TEAM circuit design

The TEAM circuit enables wireless electrical stimulation that can be controlled either directly via a smartphone or remotely through IoT-based Internet communication, facilitated by BLE connectivity. The coil antenna captures the alternating resonant signal ( ~ 120 kHz) from a wireless charger and induces an AC voltage, which is rectified and regulated by the Qi power receiver IC (BQ51013B, Texas Instruments) and the charge management controller (MCP73831T-2DCI/MC, Microchip Technology) to charge a LiPo battery (TW401012, Thehan). The battery supplies power to the BLE SoC (EYSHSNZWZ, Taiyo Yuden) through a low-dropout regulator (NCP1X300TBG, Onsemi). A metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET; DMN62D1LFB-7B, Diodes Incorporated), connected to the general-purpose input/output (GPIO) pin of the BLE SoC, controls the boosted voltage amplitude using pulse width modulation. Biphasic stimulation signals are generated by combining alternating clockwise and counterclockwise control signals from the BLE SoC through bipolar transistors (PMBT2222AMBYL, Nexperia). The current delivered to the target tissue is sensed across a 10 Ω sense resistor connected to ground. The resulting voltage is amplified by an operational amplifier (MCP6001, Microchip Technology) and read by the BLE SoC’s GPIO pin, providing real-time feedback to regulate stimulation amplitude and maintain the desired current level.

PPG circuit design

The PPG circuit acquires optical pulse signals and transmits them in real time to a smartphone for subject monitoring. The wireless charging and BLE SoC power management are identical to those of the TEAM circuit. A red LED (APG0603SEC-E-TT, Kingbright; peak wavelength: 632 nm) is connected in series with a current-limiting resistor (100 Ω) to the GPIO pin of the BLE SoC, enabling controlled illumination of tissue. The reflected light is detected by a photodiode (ALS-PT19-315C/L177/TR8, Everlight; peak sensitivity wavelength: 630 nm), and the resulting photocurrent is amplified by an operational amplifier (LMC6482IST, STMicroelectronics). The amplified signal is then read by the BLE SoC’s analog input pin and wirelessly transmitted to a smartphone for continuous monitoring of pulse waveforms.

Circuit fabrication

All circuit components were assembled on flexible printed circuit boards (PI/Cu/PI, 50 μm/36 μm/ 50 μm) fabricated by commercial vendors (e.g., PCBWay, China). Components were mounted using low-temperature solder paste (SMDLTLFP10T5, Chip Quik) and reflowed in an oven (AS-5060, SMTmax) at 130 °C for 90 s. A modular LiPo battery (TW401012, 30 mAh/90 mAh, Thehan) was connected to the circuit board via male (380-10-102-00-002000, Mill-Max) and female (315-93-102-41-001000, Mill-Max) pin connectors, which were soldered using solder paste and a soldering iron. For skin-contact safety and comfort, the bottom side of the TEAM circuit–excluding the electrode connector region–was encapsulated with a 500 μm-thick soft silicone (Silbione RT Gel 4717, Bluestar Silicones), while the top side was coated with 1 mm-thick PDMS (15:1 mixing ratio). The PPG circuit was encapsulated using silicone (Ecoflex 00-30, Smooth-On Inc.) mixed with black dye (Silc Pig, Smooth-On Inc.) on the top surface with the aid of a 3D-printed mold (B9 Core Med 550, B9Creations), and with a 500 μm-thick layer of soft silicone (Silbione RT Gel 4717, Bluestar Silicones) on the bottom surface.

Fabrication of TEAM

TEAM fabrication involved producing flexible microneedles, followed by sequential coating with a conductive Au layer, a PEDOT:PSS/PU interlayer, and a conductive hydrogel layer. The fabrication of the microneedle array began with UV treatment of a PDMS mold (MicroPoint) for 2 min. A PI solution (Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared by mixing PI with ethanol (2:1 weight ratio) and stirring for 30 min. The solution was applied to the negative PDMS mold, degassed in a vacuum desiccator for 5 min, and then scraped with a razor blade to remove residual bubbles. The PI was soft-baked at 100 °C for 30 min and cooled to room temperature for 30 min. A second PI coating was applied, scraped, soft-baked again at 100 °C for 30 min, and then hard-baked at 160 °C for 45 min. The resulting microneedle array was peeled from the mold, and a Ti/Au layer (20 nm/200 nm) was sputtered onto both sides.

For the PEDOT:PSS/PU interlayer preparation, the microneedle array was immersed in a 1 mM aqueous cysteamine solution for 1 h, rinsed with deionized (DI) water, and dried with nitrogen gas. A hydrophilic PU solution was prepared by dissolving 5 wt% PU (HydroMed D3, AdvanSource Biomaterials) in a 95:5 ethanol:water mixture (v/v)67. The array was dip-coated in the PU solution, dried under gentle nitrogen flow, and annealed at 80 °C for 1 h. For PEDOT:PSS electrodeposition, EDOT (the monomer of PEDOT; 0.01 M; Sigma-Aldrich) and PSS (2.5 wt%; Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in DI water and mixed thoroughly. The microneedle array was immersed in the electrodeposition solution and connected to the positive terminal of an electroplating rectifier through an electrode clamp, while a platinum-coated Ti mesh electrode was connected to the negative terminal. Electrodeposition was carried out by applying 3-4 V for 15-20 s at four different positions, with the electrode clamp repositioned to each corner of the array between applications.

For the conductive adhesive hydrogel synthesis, 40 mg PEDOT:PSS dry pellet (Sigma-Aldrich), 300 mg NIPAM (Sigma-Aldrich), 27 mg AAm (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mg N,N’-methylenebisacrylamide (MBAA, Sigma-Aldrich), and 50 μL of 20% (w/v) ascorbic acid (AA, Sigma-Aldrich) were combined and ground with 1.5 mL water using a mortar and pestle to form a homogeneous suspension. The suspension was coated onto the microneedles to a thickness of ~100 μm using a custom-designed silicone brush, then immersed in 5% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, Fisher Scientific) for 15 s. After hydrogel gelation, the electrodes were rinsed with DI water, immersed in glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min, rinsed again with DI water, and stored in sealed plastic bags at 4 °C to prevent water evaporation.

Skin contact impedance measurement

Electrode-skin contact impedance was measured using an LCR meter (E4980AL, Keysight Technologies) over a frequency range of 20 Hz to 10 kHz. All electrodes had uniform dimensions of 9 mm × 9 mm. Electrode pairs were positioned on human forearm skin with an inter-electrode distance of 3 cm.

Adhesion force measurement

Adhesion strength between the device and skin was evaluated by a standard 90° peel test using a force gauge (ESM303, Mark-10). Sample devices with a width of 9 mm were applied to the forearms of human participants. To prevent stretching of the device during peeling, a PI film was attached to the top surface of the samples, and TEAMs were bonded to the PI film with epoxy (Permatex). For temperature control, both the TEAM and skin were heated to the target temperature (43 °C) on a hot plate immediately prior to testing. Adhesive strength was calculated by dividing the mean peel force (N) by the contact width (cm) of the bonding area.

Electrical and thermal modeling

The electrical field and temperature distribution data for the TEAM (Fig. 2h-j and Supplementary Fig. 17) were obtained through simulations using commercial software (Version 6.3, COMSOL Multiphysics, COMSOL, Inc.). A multilayer tissue model was constructed to represent the human skin and underlying structures, consisting of the stratum corneum, epidermis, dermis, subcutaneous tissue, and bone. The electrical and thermal properties of each tissue layer, including electrical conductivity, relative permittivity, density, heat capacity, and thermal conductivity, were assigned based on reported literature values and an open-source tissue database (IT’IS, Zurich43)109–111. Specifically, the electrical conductivities of the stratum corneum, epidermis, dermis, subcutaneous tissue, and bone were set to 1.67 × 10⁻⁴, 1.67 × 10⁻⁴, 1.4 × 10⁻³, 3.03 × 10⁻², and 2.01 × 10⁻² S ∙ m⁻¹, respectively, while their relative permittivities were set to 3 × 10⁴, 3 × 10⁴, 6 × 10³, 2.5 × 10⁴, and 5.85 × 10³. The corresponding densities, heat capacities, and thermal conductivities109–111 were also incorporated to enable coupled electro-thermal analysis. Both the electric field and thermal simulations were conducted by applying a voltage of 50 V to the electrode located on the left side of the model, with the opposite electrode set to ground (0 V). Electrical insulation boundary conditions were applied to the outer boundaries, and continuity conditions were imposed at the interfaces between adjacent tissue layers. In the thermal simulations, the ambient air temperature was set to 20 °C and the body temperature was fixed at 36.5 °C to represent human physiological conditions. Mesh refinement was performed near the electrode and tissue interface regions to ensure numerical accuracy. The spatial distributions of electric field intensity and temperature within the tissue were extracted for analysis.

Animals

Male ICR mice (20–25 g; Samtako) and Sprague-Dawley rats (250–350 g; Samtako) were used in all the experiments. Animals were housed in standard colony cages under controlled conditions: a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 07:00), ambient temperature of 24 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity maintained at 40–60%. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All animals were acclimated to the housing environment for at least one week prior to the start of the experiments.

Extracellular recording of spinal cord neural activation

Adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (250–350 g) were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction: 3–4%, maintenance: 1.5–2%) and underwent laminectomy to expose the L4–S1 spinal cord. Animals were placed on a feedback-controlled heating pad to maintain core body temperature at 37 °C and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. To prevent spinal cord movement and desiccation, the exposed cord was covered with mineral oil. Extracellular recordings of wide dynamic range (WDR) neurons were obtained using carbon filament glass microelectrodes (0.4–1.2 MΩ, Carbostar-1, Kation Scientific), inserted into the dorsal horn of the L4–S1 spinal cord at a depth of 500–700 µm (based on Paxinos and Watson atlas). WDR neuron identity was confirmed following the criteria described in a previous study112, based on graded responses to mechanical stimuli including gentle brushing, von Frey filament application, and noxious pinch applied to the receptive field. After confirmation, TEAMs and gel electrodes were bilaterally affixed, and electrical stimulation (2 Hz, 0.5 mA, 0.1 ms pulse width) was applied for 10 s (n = 7 per group). To eliminate electromagnetic noise, neuronal activity was quantified by comparing spike counts recorded during 1-s periods before and after stimulation. Signals were amplified and filtered (0.1–10 kHz, ISO-80, World Precision Instruments), recorded using a CED 1401 system, and analyzed using Spike2 software (Version 7.20, Cambridge Electronic Design) with waveform-based template matching for spike sorting accuracy and unit consistency. The recording sites were histologically verified using Toluidine Blue staining and were primarily localized within Lamina V of the dorsal horn (Supplementary Fig. 36).

In vivo EMG recording

To evaluate potential motor fiber recruitment and muscle contraction during electrical stimulation, EMG was recorded in separate groups of rats and mice (n = 5 animals per group). After anesthesia with isoflurane, disposable 28 G monopolar needle electrodes (MMT Medimaxtech) were inserted into the thigh muscles innervated by the sciatic nerve branches. To minimize direct muscle fiber stimulation, the stimulation electrodes (TEAM and gel electrodes) were positioned specifically over the sciatic nerve branch region rather than along the longitudinal axis of the muscle belly. EMG signals were acquired using an ix-TA recorder equipped with an iWire-B3G isolated EMG recording module (iWorx). The stimulation parameters were set to match the respective experimental conditions: 0.5 mA (2 Hz, 0.1 ms pulse width, 10 s duration) for rats, consistent with spinal cord recordings; and 0.3 mA (2 Hz, 5 ms pulse width, 180 s duration) for mice, consistent with behavioral assays. Data analysis was performed using LabScribe software (Version 26, iWorx) to calculate the RMS amplitude of the waveforms generated during the stimulation period. Baseline noise floor in control site was also measured, confirming the absence of background electrical interference.

PTX-induced neuropathic pain

Paclitaxel (PTX; Fluka) was dissolved in 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich) and administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at a dose of 4 mg/kg once daily for five consecutive days. Mice in the vehicle control group received an equivalent volume of 5% DMSO following the same schedule. Duloxetine (Dulo; a serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, 10 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally 30 min prior to the application of TEAM therapy. Duloxeine treatment was selected based on previously published studies113.

Evaluation of pain behavior in rodent models

All behavioral experiments were performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines established by the IASP. To assess paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathic pain, including mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia, paw withdrawal responses were measured using a von Frey filament (0.4 g; North Coast Medical) and a plantar analgesia meter (IITC Life Science Inc.), as in a previous study114. Baseline sensitivity to mechanical and thermal stimuli was recorded prior to paclitaxel administration. Mice were then randomly assigned to treatment groups, and all behavioral assessments were conducted by an experimenter blinded to group allocation. Following confirmation of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN), behavioral testing was conducted at 1, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h post-intervention, with all assessments performed at the same time each day to control for circadian variability.

The electrical stimulation was delivered at 2 Hz, 0.3 mA, with a 5 ms pulse width for 180 s, based on a previously validated low-frequency stimulation protocol for chemotherapy-induced neuropathic pain77,114. To minimize stress-related variability, all animals were habituated to handling and TEAM application for at least one week before the study. TEAM stimulation was applied to the lateral aspect of the knee joint, adjacent to the anterior tubercle of the tibia, targeting the underlying sciatic nerve.

Mechanical allodynia was evaluated by applying a 0.4 g von Frey filament to the plantar surface of each hind paw through a metal mesh platform. The filament was applied 10 times per paw, and the number of paw withdrawal responses was recorded. Mechanical sensitivity was expressed as the paw withdrawal frequency (PWF, %), calculated as the percentage of positive responses out of 10 trials. Thermal hyperalgesia was assessed using a plantar analgesia meter. Mice were placed individually in transparent plastic chambers (15 cm in diameter, 20 cm in length) atop a glass platform and allowed to acclimate for 30 min. A radiant heat source was directed toward the plantar surface of the hind paw from beneath the glass floor, and paw withdrawal latency (PWL) was recorded to the nearest 0.1 s. A cut-off time of 20 s was set to prevent tissue damage. All behavioral testing was performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental conditions.

Immunohistochemistry for c-Fos

A total of 20 mice were euthanized at the endpoint of each treatment group, and lumbar spinal cord segments (L4–L6) were harvested for c-Fos immunohistochemistry. Two h following TEAM administration, animals were deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and transcardially perfused with calcium-free Tyrode’s solution, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Spinal cords were post-fixed in the same fixative for 4 h at 4 °C, then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PBS for 48 h. Transverse sections (40 μm thick) were cut using a cryostat and collected for free-floating immunohistochemical staining. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide, and non-specific binding was blocked with 3% normal goat serum in PBS. Sections were incubated with a mouse monoclonal anti-c-Fos (E8) primary antibody (1:1000; sc-166940, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at 4 °C for 48 h. After thorough washing, sections were incubated with a biotinylated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:200; PK-6102, Vector Laboratories) and subsequently processed using the avidin–biotin–peroxidase complex method (ABC kit; Vector Laboratories). Immunoreactivity was visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Sigma-Aldrich), enhanced with 0.2% nickel chloride for signal intensification. For quantification of c-Fos-immunoreactive neurons, three representative transverse sections from the L4–L6 spinal segments were randomly selected per animal. Sections were scanned using a cooled charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Micromax Kodak 1317; Princeton Instruments) connected to a computer-assisted image analysis system (MetaMorph, Version 7.10, Universal Imaging Corp.). To ensure consistency in detection and compensate for variability in immunostaining intensity, neurons were counted if they exhibited optical densities at least 70% darker than the mean gray level of each image, following background subtraction and shading correction. The average number of c-Fos-positive neurons per section was calculated for each animal and then averaged across animals within each group. c-Fos expression was analyzed in three defined regions of the ipsilateral dorsal horn: (1) the superficial dorsal horn (SDH; laminae I–II), (2) the nucleus proprius (NP; laminae III–IV), and (3) the neck region (NECK; laminae V–VI). All analyses were conducted by an investigator blinded to the experimental conditions.

In vitro biocompatibility studies and histological analysis

Cytotoxicity was evaluated using the human keratinocyte cell line HaCaT (Cellular Engineering Technologies, Inc., catalog no. CR1017-500). The complete medium was made with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% of penicillin-streptomycin in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium. TEAM extracts were prepared in accordance with ISO 10993-12115, using PBS as the extraction vehicle in an inert vessel at 37 °C for 24 h. During the extraction, electrical stimulation was applied to the microneedles with a commercial low-frequency electrical stimulator (STN-111, StraTek) at 300 Hz and 8.5 mA for the initial 2 h. HaCaT cells were seeded in 12-well plates and exposed to 10% (v/v) extract in 1 mL of culture medium for 24 h. The experimental group consisted of cells treated with TEAM extract, while the blank control group received culture medium and the negative control group received PBS. Cell morphology was examined under microscopy, and cell viability was quantified using the MTS assay with absorbance measured at 490 nm.

For histological analysis, mouse skin samples (10 × 10 × 10 mm) were excised from the tibialis anterior muscle below the outer knee 2 h after TEAM stimulation or attachment. Tissues were perfused with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and stabilized in formaldehyde. Samples were dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and mounted on gelatin-coated slides. For H&E staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (BBC Biochemical), washed in PBS for bluing, counterstained with alcoholic eosin (BBC Biochemical), dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene, cover slipped, and imaged using CaseViewer software (3DHISTECH). For TUNEL assay, sections were similarly processed, treated with proteinase K, incubated with recombinant terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (rTdT) mixture at 37 °C, blocked with hydrogen peroxide in PBS, developed with DAB, and imaged using a microscope (Axioscan 7, Zeiss).

Quantification of TNF-α and IL-1β levels via ELISA

Plasma concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) were measured using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits: Mouse TNF-α and IL-1β ELISA Kit (MyBioSource Inc.), according to the previous study116. Briefly, whole blood was collected via cardiac puncture into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-coated tubes and centrifuged at 895 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain plasma. Standards and plasma samples were added to 96-well microplates pre-coated with specific capture antibodies for TNF-α or IL-1β. Following incubation and washing steps to remove unbound material, biotinylated detection antibodies were applied, followed by streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate. After incubation with the substrate solution, the enzymatic reaction was stopped with a stop solution, and the optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cytokine concentrations were determined by interpolation from standard curves generated in each assay. All samples and standards were analyzed in duplicate.

Quantitative sensory testing in humans

The thermal tests were conducted using a custom-built device equipped with a Peltier module (TEC1-12705, Uxcell) and negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistor (NCP15XH103F03RC, Murata). The Peltier element provided a contact area of 16 cm², with cut-off temperatures of 10 °C and 50 °C. Warm detection and heat pain thresholds (WDT, HPT) were measured first, followed by cold detection and pain thresholds (CDT, CPT). JND units for heat and cold were measured by gradually increasing or decreasing the temperature from baseline until subjects first detected a thermal change, with thresholds recorded as the minimum perceivable deviation from the baseline (body temperature). Each temperature threshold was calculated as the mean of three repeated measurements, with a 10 min washout period between measurements as determined by preliminary testing. All assessments used ramped thermal stimuli at 1 °C/s, terminated immediately when the subject reported pain.

Cold pressor test and signal analysis in humans

For the cold pressor test, subjects underwent a 3-min cold pressor test consisting of two phases: a 1.5-min baseline period at room temperature (25 °C) and a 1.5-min cold exposure phase with one hand immersed in an ice water bath (0–1 °C). PPG signals were obtained at a sampling rate of 100 Hz, band-pass filtered (0.5–6 Hz), and these signals were stored on a personal computer using an IoT web server. Peak detection errors were identified using a threshold of ±20% deviation from the local median pulse interval and replaced by linear interpolation. The data, consisting of 165 samples (2 s each) for the training set, underwent feature extraction to yield three parameters: HR, RMSSD, and SDNN, which were subsequently processed using an LDA classifier, selected for its structural simplicity and robust generalization capabilities in small-sample conditions117. A five-fold cross-validation scheme was used in classification models. Data processing, machine learning training and evaluation were completed using MATLAB R2025b (Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox; MathWorks).

Human subjects

For QST, a total of 10 healthy subjects (7 males and 3 females, age 23–32 years) voluntarily participated. For the cold pressor test, a total of 4 healthy subjects (2 males and 2 females, age 24–28 years) voluntarily participated. All subjects were healthy, without any known medical conditions, and were asked to avoid caffeine, alcohol, and intense exercise for 2 h before the experiment.

Ethics

All experiment involving animals, human participants, or clinical samples were carried out following a protocol approved by an ethical commission. For animal experiments, all procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine under protocol numbers 24-011 and 24-066. Human subject research was conducted following approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (protocol number: KH2021-070), with informed written consent for participation and publication obtained from all participants. All subjects received no compensation for their participation.

Data presentation and statistical analysis

All plots, curves, and statistical graphs were generated using Origin software (Version 2019, OriginLab Corporation). In animal studies, all data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (Version 6, GraphPad Software Inc.). Behavioral data and c-Fos analysis were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with treatment and time as factors for behavioral data and treatment and spinal cord region as factors for c-Fos analysis. When significant main effects or interactions were detected, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons between groups. For the cytokine level analysis, data were compared using a one-way ANOVA. Post hoc analysis was performed using Šídák’s multiple comparison test for the pre-planned pairwise contrasts of the active treatment group against the disease control and sham control groups. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Paired and unpaired Student’s t-tests were used for within-subject (e.g., pre- vs post-stimulation) and between-group (e.g., TEAM vs Gel) comparisons, respectively. Data normality was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and exact P values (e.g., P = 0.0038) were reported where appropriate.
