An original less-invasive surgical treatment for arterial thoracic outlet syndrome caused by cervical rib
Authors: Samer Alhames, Mike Ghabally, Bana Alafandi, Nicola Sayegh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThoracic outlet syndrome affects 0.3-8% of the population, with arterial thoracic outlet syndrome being particularly rare. The management of arterial thoracic outlet syndrome requires complete thoracic outlet decompression along with arterial reconstruction when necessary, a procedure that is typically associated with significant risks, complications, and symptom recurrence. Our study aims to introduce a less invasive surgical method for treating arterial thoracic outlet syndrome caused by a cervical rib, which involves only cervical rib resection and scalenotomy.MethodsPatients diagnosed with isolated arterial thoracic outlet syndrome attributed to an ipsilateral cervical rib who required thoracic outlet decompression between 2015 and 2022 were included. All patients underwent supraclavicular cervical rib resection and scalenotomy without first thoracic rib resection or sympathectomy. Outcomes were assessed based on symptom recurrence and functional improvement at a two-year follow-up.ResultsThe cohort included 10 patients with a mean age of 29.1 years. Postoperatively, the results were promising, with no recurrence of arterial thoracic outlet syndrome symptoms, no complications, and excellent functional outcomes according to Derkash’s classification. Our modified surgical approach demonstrated favorable outcomes with minimal invasiveness, suggesting a viable alternative to traditional methods.ConclusionThis less invasive surgical method for treating arterial thoracic outlet syndrome caused by a cervical rib involving only cervical rib resection and scalenotomy had excellent functional outcomes and lower complication rates. However, given the small sample size, further studies are warranted to validate these findings and to establish long-term benefits.Keywords: Surgical technique, Thoracic outlet syndrome, ATOS, Cervical rib, Thoracic outlet decompression surgery

Background

Thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) is a multifaceted condition that occurs in 0.3–8.3% of the population. It arises from compression of the neurovascular bundle within the superior thoracic aperture [1], upon which it is classified into different types: arterial (aTOS < 1% of cases), venous (vTOS 3–5%), and neurogenic (nTOS > 90%) [2].

TOS is triggered by a variety of causes: chronic repetitive motion, traumatic injuries or accidents, and structural abnormalities. These structural abnormalities involve fibrous bands, muscular anomalies, and various bony irregularities, such as residual or rudimentary ribs, clavicular anomalies, fused or fractured ribs, and the presence of cervical ribs (CR) [3]. Although a CR increases the likelihood of experiencing TOS symptoms, CRs are commonly discovered incidentally during routine chest radiographs (CXR) and are present in less than 1% of the population. With only 10% of patients with a CR being symptomatic, a CR alone is not an indication for intervention and should only be resected if the patient is symptomatic on the side of the CR [3, 4]. Considering the low prevalence of both CRs and aTOS as separate clinical entities, the incidence of isolated aTOS in patients with cervical ribs is quite uncommon.

The treatment of aTOS requires complete thoracic outlet decompression surgery (TOD) combined with arterial reconstruction if there is significant damage to the artery that warrants intervention. Despite ongoing controversies surrounding the management of aTOS, current surgical protocols commonly advocate for supraclavicular TOD. This procedure typically involves anterior and middle scalenotomy, brachial plexus neurolysis, cervical rib resection (CRR), and first thoracic rib resection (FRR) [5]. Complete FRR is recommended during the initial operation, as it effectively helps prevent the recurrence of symptoms [6]. The management of aTOS remains controversial, making it difficult to determine the best course of action and long-term outcomes. Our study aims to propose a unique, less invasive surgical technique that has shown encouraging preliminary results in patients diagnosed with arterial TOS, which only includes a supraclavicular CRR and scalenotomy.

Methods

This single-center study was performed prospectively between 2015 and 2022. The inclusion criteria included all patients presenting to our hospital with isolated aTOS caused by an ipsilateral CR requiring TOD. Arterial TOS was defined as the presence of subclavian artery pathology (aneurysmal dilation, occlusion, stenosis, or embolizing ulceration).

Exclusion criteria included patients with mixed TOS (due to the difficulties in comparing outcomes of neurological TOS), aTOS that was not caused by a CR, the presence of a combined etiology of aTOS, or cases of patient refusal. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients after explaining the new surgical technique and the traditional surgery. Ethical approval was obtained from our institute’s ethics committee.

A detailed clinical examination was performed for all patients. Blood pressure and pulse were obtained and compared between the arms. Symptoms ranged from pain and muscle weakness to intermittent claudication, cold limb, and ischemic changes in the fingertips. Moreover, Roos and Adson tests were positive in all patients. Subclavian artery assessment was performed using Doppler ultrasonography, CTA (CT angiography) (Figure 1), and venography scans. Doppler ultrasonography was performed by experienced vascular sonographer using a high-frequency linear probe. Dynamic maneuvers, including arm abduction and external rotation, were routinely employed to assess positional changes in subclavian artery flow. Ultrasonographic findings were interpreted in conjunction with CTA to overcome technical limitations in the clavicular and subclavian regions.

CXR was obtained for all patients to confirm bony abnormalities. Additionally, electroneurography ruled out nTOS and ensure isolated arterial pathology in all patients without delaying surgical intervention in urgent cases. Arterial lesions were determined by radiological studies and direct intraoperative observation. The CR was classified using the Gruber classification (Table 1) [7] preoperatively via radiological studies and intraoperatively.

TOD was performed via a supraclavicular approach in all cases. Cervical rib resection via disarticulation was performed, followed by disinsertion of the anterior and middle scalenus muscles from the first thoracic rib. The first thoracic rib and the transverse process of the 7th CR were not resected, and sympathectomy was not performed in any patient. Intraoperative assessment of arterial lesions was carried out; the presence of intimal ulceration, thrombosis, or embolization required arterial repair with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) grafts. Following surgery, all patients had regular outpatient follow-up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months.

Results

A total of ten patients were included in this study (8 females [80%] and 2 males [20%]). The mean age was 29.1 ± 8.5 years (range 18–47). The dominant arm was affected in 7 out of 10 cases (70%). Three patients had bilateral CRs; however, only one side was symptomatic and required surgery (Table 2).

All patients underwent TOD within 2–30 days of presentation due to the patients’ choice of conservative therapy initially. Patients at high risk of thrombosis who could not undergo surgery within 48 h received anticoagulation therapy for 7 days. Seven patients (70%) received preoperative anticoagulation: five (50%) were started on rivaroxaban (20 mg daily) and two (20%) received enoxaparin (1 mg/kg).

According to the intraoperative classification of CR, five patients had class 3 CR, four patients had class 4 CR, and one patient had class 2 CR (Table 2). Arterial lesions included subclavian artery aneurysm (6 patients), subclavian artery atherosclerosis (3 patients), subclavian artery thrombosis (1 patient), and brachial artery thrombosis (3 patients), while in one patient there was only subclavian artery compression and angulation. Subclavian artery aneurysm diameters (n = 6) averaged 2.40 ± 0.58 cm (range 1.5–3.0 cm). PTFE grafts were indicated in 6 patients (60%). Only patients who underwent PTFE grafting were prescribed anticoagulation therapy, which included rivaroxaban 20 mg daily for 6 months and lifelong aspirin (81 mg).

Surgeries were uneventful, and no patient required blood transfusion. All patients were discharged after 2 days postoperatively. A suction drain was placed and removed after 24 h in 6 patients (60%) (Table 3). Postoperative analgesia consisted only of paracetamol, and none of the patients required nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or opioids.

None of our patients exhibited perioperative complications. Postoperative CXR was performed to rule out possible complications such as hemothorax, pneumothorax as well as phrenic nerve injury.

On follow-up, none of the patients experienced recurrence of aTOS symptoms or pain. Mild paresthesia over the supraclavicular fossa was reported by four patients, and two patients reported mild, nonspecific neck pain that did not necessitate pain-relieving medication or impede daily activities. At 2-year follow-up, none of our patients experienced permanent neurological injuries, vascular complications, or recurrence of symptoms. Among patients with bilateral cervical ribs, none required contralateral cervical rib resection during the follow-up period. The postoperative assessment of functional outcomes for all patients, according to Derkash’s classification [8], revealed excellent results in all patients.

Discussion

The thoracic outlet is a confined and dynamic anatomical space in the lower neck that serves as the conduit through which the subclavian vessels and brachial plexus travel from their central origins to their peripheral terminations [2, 9]. It is subdivided into three distinct anatomical areas: the scalene triangle, costoclavicular space, and subcoracoid space. TOS is caused by anatomical variations that compress the brachial plexus, subclavian, and axillary vessels in the thoracic outlet region, specifically in the interscalene triangle, costoclavicular space, and retro-coraco-pectoral space [2, 9].

The majority of TOS cases are diagnosed between the ages of 20–50 years; however, it can occur in teenagers or, rarely, in pediatric patients. Women are three to four times more likely to develop nTOS, while the incidence of vTOS is more equal between non-athletic men and women but is more pronounced in competitive athletic men [4].

Approximately 70% of TOS cases are associated with congenital muscular abnormalities. The remaining 30% are often caused by bony variations such as ectopic cervical ribs or prominent C7 transverse processes [10–12]. Cervical ribs (CR) are anatomical variants first described by Galen and Vesalius. They are relatively uncommon, with a prevalence of less than 1% of the population. However, the true prevalence remains unknown as they are often found incidentally; around 90% are asymptomatic [13].

Notably, CR is strongly associated with vascular TOS development and is responsible for almost 50% of cases [13]. In addition to detailed clinical examination, the diagnosis of aTOS is mainly radiologic, including CXR and CT scanning. Arterial evaluation is mostly via Doppler ultrasonography, CTA, magnetic resonance angiography, or conventional angiography [14]. The conduction speed in the normal ulnar nerve also helps in the diagnosis of TOS, typically ranging from 50 to 70 m/s. In patients with thoracic outlet syndrome, conduction velocity may be significantly reduced, often falling below 50 m/s due to nerve compression or irritation [3].

Despite advances in radiological imaging since thoracic outlet decompression was first performed in 1861, which have made it easier to identify the exact etiology preoperatively, they have not changed the traditional surgical techniques. Surgical treatment of TOS is indicated only in symptomatic cases that have been confirmed and after failure of conservative physical therapy [2], along with arterial TOS cases causing arterial thrombosis, embolization, significant stenosis, or post-stenotic aneurysmal degeneration of the subclavian artery [15]. In addition, aTOS presenting with acute limb ischemia may require revascularization via thrombectomy or thrombolysis. Subclavian artery reconstruction is usually reserved for subclavian aneurysms greater than 2 cm, while smaller aneurysmal changes are monitored annually by ultrasonography [15]. TOD surgery includes supraclavicular, infraclavicular, and trans-axillary approaches; however, when arterial repair is indicated, the supraclavicular approach is usually favored because it provides the best visibility and exposure [14, 15]. On the other hand, when CRR and FRR are planned, transaxillary access is preferred because it allows removal of both ribs via one incision [14].

Traditional TOD consists of CRR, FRR, anterior and middle scalenotomy, with or without resection of the 7th cervical transverse process. Although sympathectomy is debatable, it is often performed in aTOS to achieve arterial flushing after vascular decompression [2, 15–17]. CRR with FRR can also be performed laparoscopically via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, except in cases requiring arterial intervention. Prior to adopting our modified approach, patients with CR-associated aTOS at our institution were managed using the conventional supraclavicular TOD with arterial reconstruction when indicated. Our modified technique spares FRR, 7th cervical transverse process resection, and sympathectomy. PTFE grafts were selected for arterial reconstruction due to their availability, durability, and suitability for open surgical repair. Endovascular stenting was not used because persistent extrinsic compression in thoracic outlet syndrome carries a high risk of stent fracture, thrombosis, and restenosis.

According to the literature, complication rates after TOD surgery vary widely, from less than 5% to almost 40% across different studies, including pneumothorax, hemothorax, nerve injuries, and wound infections. However, severe complications and death are extremely rare, suggesting that surgical treatment of TOS is generally safe [11]. Pneumothorax is considered the most common complication of thoracic decompression surgery, with an incidence estimated at up to 33% [17]. However, none of our patients developed pneumothorax. The reason may be that we did not dissect the first rib from the parietal pleura because we did not perform FRR in the first place. On the other hand, postoperative hemothorax may occur following subclavian vascular injury, accounting for 1.4% of cases. Chylothorax usually occurs after left-sided thoracic outlet decompression as a result of thoracic duct injury, occurring in 2–3% of all cases [2, 17].

It is well known that TOD yields significant postoperative morbidity. Compared to previous literature, Degeorges et al. reported the surgical outcomes of 38 patients with aTOS who underwent either trans-axillary or supraclavicular TOD, depending on the presence of cervical ribs or the need for arterial reconstruction [12]. According to Derkash’s classification, 61% had an excellent outcome, 26% had a good outcome, 3% had a fair outcome, and 11% had a poor outcome [12]. Another study described surgical outcomes of 5 aTOS patients treated with CRR. According to Derkash’s classification, 4 patients had an excellent outcome and 1 patient had a fair outcome, with 1 pneumothorax and 1 brachial plexus injury [11]. However, this study lacked detailed data on complications and follow-up. Taking these results into consideration, the surgical technique was modified with the aim of minimizing surgical trauma, procedure-related complications as well as morbidity. Given that the compressive pathology of postoperative morbidity was clearly attributable to the CRR, we hypothesized that sparing FRR and sympathectomy could achieve adequate decompression with lower morbidity.

Regarding our results, none of our patients experienced recurrent symptoms or any of the above-mentioned complications, yielding an excellent outcome according to Derkash’s classification over a 2-year follow-up. Thus, while no surgical approach is risk-free, we believe we were able to reduce the incidence of postoperative neurological deficits in our patients.

The modified surgical approach may offer cost benefits due to its reduced invasiveness and shorter hospital stay. By eliminating the need for extensive rib resections and sympathectomy, this technique could lower the overall surgical costs, as well as reduce the economic burden associated with postoperative complications and prolonged recovery periods.

On the other hand, this study has several limitations, most notably the small sample size of 10 patients, which restricts the generalizability of our findings. The absence of a control group further limits the ability to compare outcomes directly with traditional surgical techniques. Due to the rarity of this pathology, the limited number of cases, and the retrospective nature of the earlier experience, a meaningful comparative analysis was not feasible and would have yeald to significant reporting bias. Larger, multicenter studies with extended follow-up are needed to validate these promising results.

Conclusion

This modified supraclavicular approach, involving cervical rib resection and scalenotomy without first rib resection or sympathectomy, appears to be a safe and effective option for selected patients with arterial thoracic outlet syndrome. It minimizes surgical trauma, reduces complications, shortens hospitalization, and provides excellent functional outcomes. Although limited by small sample size and lack of control group, the consistent 2-year results are promising and warrant further multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up.
