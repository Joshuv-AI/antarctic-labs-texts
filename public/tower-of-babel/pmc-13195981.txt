Comparative plastid genome analysis of four different variants of Gastrodia elata and development of molecular markers based on plastid DNA sequences
Authors: Suyin Wang, Qingqing Hu, Xu Zeng, Shunxing Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastrodia elata is an important medicinal plant, generally considered to have five distinct variants. These variants differ in quality and medicinal value. However, accurately identifying them has remained a significant challenge in both production and research due to their confusingly similar morphology.ResultsThis study describes the morphological characteristics of both the underground tubers and the aerial parts of plants from four variants of G. elata collected from major producing areas in China. Subsequently, we performed plastid genome sequencing on 11 of these samples, assembling and annotating their complete plastid genomes. The 11 plastid genomes range in size from 35,275 to 35,328 base pairs, and all encode 20 protein-coding genes, 3 rRNA genes, and 5 tRNA genes, demonstrating highly conserved genome size and gene content. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the four G. elata variants can be classified into three clades: G. elata f. elata and G. elata f. glauca, each formed distinct monophyletic clade, while G. elata f. flavida and G. elata f. viridis clustered together. Based on comparative analysis of the plastid genomes, we identified two insertion-deletion (InDel) loci and successfully developed the corresponding InDel markers, designated as GEpt11 and GEpt13. These markers were validated via PCR amplification experiments on 22 samples.ConclusionsHere, we report the first complete plastome of Gastrodia elata f. flavida; and development of practical InDel markers GEpt11 and GEpt13 distinguishing G. elata variants. These results providing molecular tools for germplasm identification, breeding, and quality control of G. elata.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08695-4.Keywords: Gastrodia elata, Plastid genome, Molecular markers, Morphological characteristics, Phylogenetic analysis

Background

Gastrodia elata is a non-photosynthetic saprophytic plant belonging to the Orchidaceae family, distributed in forests and shrublands at elevations of 400–3,200 m in China, Nepal, Bhutan, India, Japan, the Korean Peninsula, and Siberia [1]. Based on inflorescence color and tuber morphology, the species is classified into five variants: G. elata f. elata, G. elata f. viridis, G. elata f. glauca, G. elata f. flavida, and G. elata f. alba [2].The tuber of G. elata is a traditional Chinese medicinal material, first documented in Shennong’s Classic of Materia Medica (compiled during the Warring States to Qin-Han periods of China) and classified as a “superior-grade” herb [3, 4]. With a history of thousands of years of use, it is primarily indicated for treating dizziness, blurred vision, limb numbness, and similar conditions [4, 5]. Modern pharmacological studies have revealed its broad bioactivities, including neuroprotective effects [6], improvement of learning and memory, cardioprotection, vascular regulation, antidepressant properties, and anticancer potential [7].

Historically, all G. elata used was wild-harvested, resulting in unpredictable species and quality. Since the early 1970s, artificial cultivation of G. elata has been successfully implemented in China. The main cultivated variants are G. elata f. elata and G. elata f. glauca, while G. elata f. viridis and G. elata f. flavida are occasionally cultivated in southwestern regions of China. G. elata f. alba has not been introduced into cultivation due to its low dry matter ratio. Although the cultivated variants of G. elata share a similar profile of chemical components, they exhibit differences in the relative abundance of these constituents, leading to distinct medicinal value. Specifically, levels of parishins, vanilloloside, and gastrodin A/B were markedly higher in G. elata f. glauca, whereas G. elata f. elata (red form) and G. elata f. viridis (green form) contained higher concentrations of gastrodin, gastrol, and syringic acid. These metabolites have demonstrated distinct pharmacological values in various assays [8]. Due to these differences in metabolite composition, each variant may have specific advantages in different medicinal applications. The polysaccharide content also differs notably among the variants. Studies focusing on polysaccharide activity have suggested that G. elata f. elata (red form) exhibits the most potent biological effects, showing relatively high antioxidant and hypoglycemic activities [9]. According to Wang’s research, more than 345 metabolites were detected, revealing that components such as parishins and vanilloloside were higher in the black‑stemmed variety, while the red‑stemmed and green‑stemmed varieties were richer in gastrodin, syringic acid, and related compounds [10]. Research by Wang et al. indicated that the phenolic compound content is generally higher in G. elata f. glauca (black form) compared to other variants [11]. They suggested that for the medicinal market and scientific research, the traditional red and black variants, owing to their higher levels of bioactive compounds, are more aligned with the demands of the authentic herb market and the extraction of components for specific applications. Furthermore, G. elata f. glauca typically has a longer growth cycle, lower yield, and superior quality, which collectively contribute to its market value being up to five times higher than that of the red and green variants [12]. In conclusion, these studies indicate that while the various variants of G. elata share a similar profile of active chemical substances, these components differ in their relative abundance, leading to variations in quality and medicinal value [8, 9]. Therefore, it is essential to identify these variants.

However, issues related to germplasm confusion and unclear variant identification persist widely in current production and marketing, because no standardized and effective system established. In practice, traditional morphological identification methods, such as observing tuber shape, length-to-width ratio, and number of annular rings, are commonly used. However, tuber morphology exhibits considerable variation (Fig. 1), making it difficult to accurately define boundaries between different variants, as it is easily influenced by environmental factors. Thus, this method can only serve as a rough assessment. In breeding processes, to ensure germplasm purity, a more accurate approach involves planting overwintered tubers and allowing them to bolt and flower, enabling observation of stem and inflorescence colors. Since these colors differ among each variant (Fig. 2), this method is more reliable than tuber morphology. However, it requires over a quarter from tuber vernalization to flowering, resulting in low timeliness for practical application. Some researchers have proposed identifying variants based on characteristic chemical component contents [13]. Moreover, such methods often involve complex preprocessing or require specialized equipment and expertise [12, 14, 15], limiting their feasibility for basic research institutions and production areas. Currently, there is a lack of reliable methods for accurately identifying G. elata variants solely based on morphology or chemistry.

In plant cells, the plastid genome (plastome) is a circular DNA molecule that exists separately from the nuclear genome. It serves as a powerful molecular marker for species identification and phylogenetic studies [16–19]. This is largely due to several inherent advantages: a generally maternal inheritance pattern, high copy number, and highly conserved gene content and structure. For example, plastome sequencing of 62 African timber species enabled the discovery of novel genetic markers for discriminating closely related taxa, supporting timber trade regulation [20]. Furthermore, research on Quercus successfully identified four mutation hotspots (e.g., matK-trnK-rps16, ndhF) that outperformed standard barcodes in species delimitation [21]. In medicinal plants, the development of molecular markers based on the variation of plastid genomes for species identification has also become very common. For example, the plastome studies in Amomum identified hypervariable markers (e.g., ccsA gene region) with high species resolution and revealed natural hybridization events [22]. In Iris, plastome data from 14 Korean species helped resolve taxonomic ambiguities and identified key regions for species discrimination [23]. Similarly, in Styrax [24], comparison of 34 plastomes led to the development of genus-specific barcodes (e.g., ycf1b, trnT-trnL) with higher discriminatory power than universal barcodes. Furthermore, a 60 bp InDel was found in the intron of rpl16, which can serve as a molecular marker to effectively distinguish between the two Pueraria Species [25]. These cases demonstrate that plastome sequencing, provides substantially more informative sites than conventional barcodes, improving resolution for closely related and taxonomically challenging species.

The plastomes of G. elata have been reported previously. For instance, as early as 2020, a research team from South Korea reported the complete plastome sequence of G. elata [26, 27]. However, detailed information on its specific variants was not available. Our team previously investigated the plastomes of three different variants: G. elata f. elata, G. elata f. viridis, and G. elata f. glauca [12], but due to limitations in sample collection at that time, we were unable to include all four major cultivated variants. In the present study, we conducted renewed sampling. Firstly, we supplemented the previously missing variants, G. elata f. flavida, marking the first report of the complete plastome for this variant. Secondly, we increased the number of samples from different geographic regions to enhance representativeness. Finally, we placed particular emphasis on incorporating detailed morphological identification and description. To our knowledge, this study represents the most detailed intraspecific investigation of the plastome within G. elata to date.

Materials and methods

Sampling and voucher specimens’ deposition

We collected 33 G. elata samples representing different variants from several provinces in China, including Yunnan, Guizhou, Sichuan, Shaanxi, and Hubei. Since the successful achievement of artificial cultivation of G. elata in the 1970s, these regions have gradually begun to widely cultivate this medicinal plant. All samples were obtained from farms in the major production bases of G. elata, and no wild plants were involved in this study. All plant materials were morphologically identified by Professor Shunxing Guo at the Institute of Medicinal Plant Development, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College, based on characteristics described in the Flora of China. Voucher specimens were prepared following standard herbarium procedures and deposited in the herbarium of the Institute of Medicinal Plant Development.

Each specimen was assigned a deposition number. For whole plastid genome sequencing, 11 samples encompassing all four variants were selected from Yunnan and Sichuan, included three samples of G. elata f. glauca (YN-g1, YN-g2, SC-g3), four of G. elata f. elata (YN-e1, YN-e2, SC-e1, SC-e2), two of G. elata f. flavida (YN-f1, YN-f2), and two of G. elata f. viridis (YN-v1, SC-v1). The remaining samples were used for PCR validation experiments, comprising ten G. elata f. glauca (Geg, 01–10), five G. elata f. elata (Gee, 11–15), three G. elata f. flavida (GEf, 16–18), and four G. elata f. viridis (GEv, 19–22) (Table 1). The curator responsible for the management of these voucher specimens at the herbarium is Dr. Bing Li (zudengtianxia@126.com).

DNA extraction and sequencing

For whole-genome sequencing, total genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method [28]. DNA libraries with an insert size of 350 bp were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA) [29] and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform with PE150 reads at BioMarker (Beijing, China). Approximately 5 Gb of raw data were generated per sample. Raw sequencing reads were processed with Trimmomatic [30] to obtain clean reads by removing low-quality sequences. The filtering criteria were as follows: reads with more than 50% of bases having a Phred quality score < 19, or containing over 5% N bases, were discarded.

Plastome assembly and annotation

The plastome was assembled using the GetOrganelle pipeline (v1.7.3) [31] with default parameters: “-R 15 -k 21,45,65,85,105 -F embplant_pt”. Circular plastome sequences were successfully obtained. As this species lacks typical inverted repeat regions, we adjusted the starting point and orientation of the genome with reference to a previously published sequence (NC_037409.1) to ensure consistent genome structure. Annotation was performed using the GeSeq [32] tool (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html) with NC_037409.1 as the reference. The circular genome map was drawn using OGDRAW [33], and font sizes were adjusted during image editing.

Repeat element analysis

We identified three types of repeat elements in the plastome. Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were detected using the MISA web server (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/). The identified repeat units included mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotides with minimum repeat thresholds of 10, 5, 4, 3, 3, and 3, respectively [34]. Dispersed repeats were identified using REPuter (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/) with the following parameters: Hamming distance of 3, minimal repeat size of 30 bp [35], and e-value < 1e-05. Tandem repeats were detected using the Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) web server (https://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.html) with default parameters under the Advanced module [36].

Genome comparison

To compare sequence similarity among the 11 plastomes, we employed two methods. First, nucleotide diversity (Pi) was calculated using DnaSP v6.0 [37] with a window size of 100 bp and a step size of 25 bp. Second, we used mVISTA (http://genome.lbl.gov/vista/mvista/submit.shtml) in shuffle-LAGAN mode to align and compare the 11 plastome sequences [38]. The annotation file of G. elata f. elata (PX233521) was used as a reference.

Collinearity analysis was performed by identifying homologous regions using the BLASTn program with the parameters: “-word_size 7 -evalue 1e-10 -outfmt 6”. The diagram was then generated using LINKVIEW2 (https://github.com/YangJianshun/LINKVIEW2) with the parameters: “--style simple --bezier --chro_axis --chro_axis_unit 2 kb --chro_thickness 18”.

The calculation of synonymous substitution rate and non-synonymous substitution rate

Using PhyloSuite [39], we extracted annotated genes from GenBank files, identifying 19 conserved protein-coding genes (PCGs): rpl14, rpl16, rpl2, rpl20, rpl36, rps11, rps12, rps14, rps18, rps19, rps2, rps3, rps4, rps7, rps8, clpP, accD, ycf1, ycf2. The matK gene is only present in G. elata and G. angusta (NC_066060.1), and therefore is not included in the subsequent analysis. The plastid gene sequences were aligned using MAFFT (v7.525) with default parameters [40]. Subsequently, the nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates and their ratio (Ka/Ks) were calculated using DnaSP v6.0 [37].

The calculation of relative synonymous codon usage

Protein-coding gene sequences were extracted from the annotated GenBank files, and the Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) values were calculated in MEGA v7.0 [41].

Phylogenetic analysis

Two closely related species, G. angusta (NC_066060.1) and G. javanica (NC_066063.1), were used as outgroups to infer the phylogenetic relationships within G. elata based on the 11 sequenced samples. The complete plastome sequences were aligned using MAFFT (v7.525) with default parameters [40]. The aligned sequences were used as input for IQ-TREE2 (v2.2.5) [42] with combined ModelFinder [43], tree search, ultrafast bootstrap (1000 replicates), and SH-aLRT test (1000 replicates) under the parameters “--alrt 1000 -B 1000”. The best-fit model is K3Pu + F+I, and it was chosen according to Bayesian Information Criterion. We also performed a phylogenetic analysis on the same dataset using Bayesian inference (BI). The BI tree was conducted using MrBayes (v3.2.6) [44] with the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method for 200,000 generations and sampling trees every 100 generations. The first 20% of trees were discarded as burn-in, with the remaining trees being used for generating a consensus tree.

PCR amplification and sanger sequencing

Based on the plastome sequences, two pairs of specific primers were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) with default parameters. In addition, we also designed a pair of primers for amplifying the ITS region. Genomic DNA was extracted as described above. PCR was performed in a 50 µL reaction mixture containing 2 µL template DNA, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 20 µL ddH₂O, and 26 µL Tiangen 2× Taq PCR MasterMix. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. For plastome sequence amplification, PCR products were separated by using 2.2% agarose gel, the electrophoresis conditions were 60 V for 150 min. For ITS sequence amplification, PCR products were separated by using 1% agarose gel, the electrophoresis conditions were 120 V for 30 min. Those with expected band sizes were sent for Sanger sequencing.

Results

Limitations of tuber morphology in different G. elata variants identification

According to the Flora of China [45], the tubers of G. elata f. glauca are described as elliptical to oval-elliptical; those of G. elata f. elata are generally larger and often dumbbell-shaped; G. elata f. viridis tubers are long-elliptical or obconical; and G. elata f. flavida tubers are ovoid-oblong. These descriptions have traditionally served as criteria for germplasm identification and classification in both research and production. However, our practical observations reveal considerable variation in tuber morphology within each variant. For example, as shown in (Fig. 1), tubers of G. elata f. glauca can be elliptical (in the shape of an ellipse, or a narrow oval; broadest at the middle and narrower at the two equal ends, e.g., YN1), oval-elliptical (broadly elliptic, the width over one-half the length, e.g., GZ1), obovate-elliptical (Almost ovate-elliptical, but with the distal end somewhat larger than the proximal end, e.g., HB1), obconical (tapering toward the base, resembling an inverted cone, e.g., SX2), or nearly cylindrical (relatively uniform thickness, e.g., SC2). Tubers of G. elata f. elata can be dumbbell-shaped (thinner in the middle and thicker at both ends, e.g., YN4), obconical (e.g., YN3, GZ3), obovate-elliptical (e.g., CQ3), cylindrical (e.g., SC4), or long-cylindrical (e.g., GZ4). Tubers of G. elata f. viridis can be elliptical (e.g., HB9), obovate-elliptical (e.g., YN10, HB10), dumbbell-shaped (e.g., YN7), obconical (e.g., YN8), fusiform (spindle-shaped; broadest near the middle and tapering toward both ends, e.g., HB6), or long-elliptical (e.g., YN6, HB5). Tubers of G. elata f. flavida can be obovate-elliptical (e.g., YN13), long-elliptical (e.g., YN12), or cylindrical (e.g., YN14). This indicates that classification based solely on tuber morphology is insufficient for accurate identification of different G. elata variants. Furthermore, in recent years, cultivated varieties have been developed whose tuber morphology differs slightly from the original variants, increasing the potential for misidentification. For instance, G. elata f. elata ‘XueHong’ (Fig. 1E), a variety selected from Guizhou, exhibits tuber shapes including elliptical (GZ6), oval-elliptical (GZ8, GZ9), and obovate-elliptical (GZ5), which differ from the typical tubers of G. elata f. elata from Guizhou (obconical and long-cylindrical, Fig. 1B, GZ3, GZ4). These observations highlight that tuber morphology is highly variable and unstable, easily influenced by environmental factors such as locality and climate, making it an unreliable and inaccurate identification method.

Comparative analysis of above-ground parts across geographical origins

During our research, we found the descriptions in the Flora to be limited in detail and covering fewer structures. Even within the five defined variants, finer distinctions exist. G. elata from the Qinba Mountains (the Qinling-Daba Mountains and adjacent areas) and the Wumeng Mountains (the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau and parts of Sichuan) exhibited different morphological characteristics. To investigate further, we collected overwintered G. elata tubers in April 2025 from the Wumeng Mountains in Yunnan and the Qinba Mountains in Sichuan (collecting four variants from Zhaotong, Yunnan, and three variants from Qingchuan, Sichuan) for morphological study and detailed dissection. The results are shown in (Fig. 2).

Notably, red and green variants from Sichuan and Yunnan showed different morphological characteristics, primarily in perianth tube color, ovary morphology, labellum apex color, and fruit morphology. For Sichuan G. elata f. elata: The perianth tube is yellow, tinged with orange-red at the apical lobes (Fig. 2A, red arrow). The ovary is dark red, elliptical, with slightly darker coloration near the apex adjacent to the perianth tube and along the ventral and dorsal sutures (Fig. 2A, yellow arrow). The pedicel is covered by narrow-lanceolate bracts, which are often withered at flowering (Fig. < link rid="fig2”>2A, 1 and 2, green arrow). The labellum is narrow and long (high length-to-width ratio), exhibits a strong curvature in lateral view, has bright yellow fringed at the apex, an orange center at the top of the labellum (Figs. 2A and 3, white arrow), and two slightly bluish or greenish calli at the base (Figs. 2A and 3, gray arrow). The capsule is dark red, obovate-elliptical, relatively uniform in color with a slightly darker apex and faint dark red stripes (Figs. 2A and 4, blue arrow).

For Sichuan G. elata f. viridis: The rachis (Figs. 1 and 2C) and perianth tube are yellow-green, perianth tube with a slightly deeper green color at the cleft where the two lateral tepals are fused at the apex (Fig. 2C, red arrow). The bracts are narrow-lanceolate and often withered at flowering (Figs. 2C, 1 and 2, green arrow). The labellum is narrow and long (high length-to-width ratio), exhibits a strong curvature in lateral view, has bright yellow fringed at the apex (Figs. 2C and 3, white arrow), and slightly bluish-green calli at the base (Figs. 2C and 3, gray arrow). For Yunnan G. elata f. viridis: The rachis (Figs. 1 and 2D) and perianth tube are blue-green, tinged with a slightly deeper blue-green at the apical lobes (Fig. 2D, red arrow). Genomic characteristics and gene content

Considering the morphological instability, we conducted a study on the plastomes of 11 samples of G. elata from different regions, aiming to identify the genetic differences between the four variants from a genetic perspective. The complete plastomes of the 11 G. elata samples sequenced in this study ranged in size from 35,275 bp to 35,328 bp (Table 2), with a variation of only 53 bp, indicating high conservation in plastome size even among different variants. The GC content of these plastomes ranged from 26.75% to 26.79%. We observed that the genomes of the four individuals of G. elata f. elata were relatively small (all below 35,300 bp), while those of the other variants exceeded 35,300 bp. In contrast, G. elata f. elata showed the highest GC content (26.78%–26.79%) among all variants, whereas the others ranged from 26.75% to 26.77%. These results suggest minor but consistent differences between G. elata f. elata and the other variants. As a highly heterotrophic plant, G. elata exhibits plastome reduction, with genome sizes much smaller than those of photosynthetic plants (typically 150–160 kb) [46].

Comparison of repeat elements among plastomes

For SSRs, the total number ranged from 42 to 45 across the 11 individuals of the four variants, with mononucleotide repeats being the most abundant (Fig. 4A, Table S1). The four individuals of G. elata f. elata each contained 42 SSRs, fewer than the other variants. This difference was mainly due to mononucleotide repeats (24 in G. elata f. elata vs. 25 in the other three variants) and tetranucleotide repeats (9 in G. elata f. elata vs. 10 in the others). These differences in SSR numbers may serve as potential markers for distinguishing G. elata f. elata from the other three variants. For dispersed repeats, only two types (namely palindromic and forward repeats) were detected. Both types were conserved across all variants and individuals, with four palindromic and one forward repeat identified in each plastome (Fig. 4B, Table S2). All dispersed repeats were shorter than 60 bp, indicating the absence of large dispersed repeats. Tandem repeats were also relatively scarce, ranging from 9 to 11 per genome. G. elata f. elata had fewer tandem repeats (9 in three individuals and 10 in one) compared to the other variants (10–11), though the difference was not substantial (Fig. 4B, Table S3).

Sequence similarity and collinearity comparison of plastomes

Given the conservation in genome size, gene content, and repeat elements, we further analyzed sequence similarity among the plastomes. Nucleotide diversity (Pi) calculated using DnaSP showed that most regions had a Pi value of 0 (Table S4), indicating complete sequence identity in these regions and reflecting high sequence conservation (Fig. 5A). Two regions with relatively higher diversity were located at positions 7,117–7,291 and 30,738–30,912, but the maximum Pi value was only 0.01018. Similarly, mVISTA analysis revealed 100% sequence similarity across most regions, with minor variations confined to a few intergenic spacers, such as the region between ycf2 and rpl2, where four individuals of G. elata f. viridis and G. elata f. flavida differed from the other samples (Fig. 5B). Overall, the 11 plastomes exhibited high nucleotide sequence conservation, with limited divergence occurring primarily in non-coding regions.

We conducted a collinearity comparison of the plastid genomes from a total of 12 samples, comprising the 11 newly sequenced G. elata samples in this study and one previously published sample (NC_037409.1). The results indicated that these 12 plastid genomes share a consistent genomic structure, with no observed genomic rearrangements or inversions (Figure S2). We also compared the collinearity of the plastid genomes between G. elata and ten other Gastrodia species. Given the high consistency within G. elata, only G. elata f. glauca (PX233513) was used as its representative. The results revealed considerable variation in the plastid genomes among different Gastrodia species. For instance, only partial sequences showed homology between G. elata f. glauca and G. longistyla (NC_063317.1), with numerous gaps exhibiting no homology (Figure S3), indicating the presence of unique sequences in different Gastrodia species. These findings demonstrate that, although sequence divergence is substantial among different Gastrodia species, the genomic structure is highly conserved among different varieties within G. elata.

Preference of codon usage of plastidial genes

Owing to the degeneracy of the genetic code, a single amino acid may be encoded by multiple synonymous codons. The Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) value for each codon of a given amino acid was calculated to assess whether a usage bias exists. Typically, an RSCU value greater than 1 indicates a preference for that codon. We analyzed the codon usage of the protein-coding genes in the 11 newly sequenced plastid genomes. The results showed that the 11 samples exhibited little variation and shared a highly similar codon usage bias pattern (Figure S4). Specifically, except for Met and Trp which are each encoded by only one codon, all other amino acids exhibited a clear preference for a specific codon. The preferred codons had RSCU values substantially greater than 1. Notably, the RSCU value for the UUA codon of Leu (Leucine) was strikingly high, exceeding 3 (Table S5). The RSCU value for the AGA codon of Arg (Arginine) also exceeded 2.7. Together, these findings indicate that the majority of amino acids exhibit codon usage bias.

A chi-square test performed on the codon usage frequencies further supported that the codon preferences were extremely significant, with only the preference for the stop codon being statistically significant (Table S6).

Analysis of Ka, Ks and Ka/Ks of plastid-encoded genes in the genus Gastrodia

Among the 19 plastidial PCGs analyzed, ycf2 exhibited the highest Ka value (Fig. 6 and Table S7), with a maximum value of 0.2824 and the highest average Ka value (0.1397). In contrast, clpP and rps2 showed the lowest mean Ka values. The Ks value was highest in rpl36, with a maximum of 0.7548, while the mean and median values of Ks for rpl20 are the lowest. The highest Ka/Ks ratio was observed in rpl20, reaching a maximum of 4.8046. The mean Ka/Ks value for this gene was 1.295, indicating that the rpl20 gene may have been under positive selection in the studied samples. Overall, the accumulation rate of nonsynonymous substitutions in this gene was higher than that of synonymous substitutions. In contrast, the average Ka/Ks values for all other genes were less than 1, a pattern typically associated with purifying selection.

Phylogenetic analysis

We performed a phylogenetic analysis based on complete plastome sequences using two related species, G. angusta (NC_066060.1) and G. javanica (NC_066063.1), as outgroups, along with our 11 sequenced samples. The phylogenetic trees constructed using maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference methods shared identical topologies. The results showed that it can be classified into three clades of these G. elata samples. The first clade contained the four individuals of G. elata f. elata, the second clade contained the three individuals of G. elata f. glauca, with the two clades being sister groups (Fig. 7). In contrast, the two individuals of G. elata f. flavida and the two of G. elata f. viridis formed a closely related clade based on cpDNA data. The sharing of identical or highly similar plastid haplotypes between these two different forms could be attributed to their very recent divergence, wherein insufficient time has elapsed for cpDNA mutations to accumulate. However, given the maternal, uniparental inheritance of cpDNA, an alternative and compelling explanation is cytoplasmic introgression through historical hybridization and backcrossing between these taxa in sympatry.

Development of molecular markers based on plastid DNA sequences

By comparing the differences among the 11 sequenced plastomes. Our results indicate that the sequences between G. elata f. viridis and G. elata f. flavida are nearly identical. Specifically, the two samples of G. elata f. viridis (PX233515 and PX233518) and one sample of G. elata f. flavida (PX233516) are completely identical in sequence, with no variations observed. The only difference detected was a 1 bp deletion (a missing ‘T’ at position 12,421) in another sample of G. elata f. flavida (PX233520) compared to the other three plastomes. The sole identified variation was not mutually confirmed across the two individuals and is therefore likely an unstable mutation in G. elata f. flavida. Consequently, we conclude that there are no differences at the plastome level between these two variants. This result is consistent with their clustering together to form the third clade in the phylogenetic tree.

However, from the sequence similarity analysis, we identified two InDel sites that showed consistent differences among the three clades (describe in the phylogenetic analysis section): an 11-bp (near the 1,500 bp region of the plastome) InDel between gene trnC-GCA and trnE-UUC, and a 13-bp (near the 31,330 bp region of the plastome) InDel in rpl2 intron region. These loci, which were not detected in the repeat element analysis, were used to design specific primers (Table S8).

To validate these markers, we included an additional 22 samples from five provinces in China (Table 1), representing the same four variants but distinct from the original 11 sequenced individuals. PCR amplification using the two primer pairs was successful in all 22 samples, producing bands of the expected sizes (Figs. 8 and 9). Amplification with primer P1 revealed an 11-bp band difference in the 10 samples of G. elata f. glauca (Fig. 8A), it can be used to distinguish the species in clade 2. The primer P2 amplified a 13-bp difference in the 7 samples of G. elata f. flavida and G. elata f. viridis (Fig. 8B), it can be used to distinguish the species in clade 3. The original gel images are shown in Figure S5. Sanger sequencing also confirmed these differences: the 11-bp InDel (GEpt11) was caused by a repeat of the motif “TTTGAACCAAA” (Fig. 8A), and the 13-bp InDel (GEpt13) resulted from a repeat of “TAATATATAATAT” (Fig. 8B). These two small InDels can serve as effective molecular markers for distinguishing the three clades of G. elata. However, no plastome-level markers were found to distinguish G. elata f. flavida from G. elata f. viridis (both in clade 3 of the phylogenetic tree), due to the fact that the sequences are almost completely identical.

We further amplified the ITS region of these 22 samples using the primers listed in Table S8 (ITS-F/ITS-R). The electrophoresis results clearly showed amplified products of approximately 800 bp, corresponding to the ITS region (Figure S6). Sequence alignment revealed that all samples possessed completely identical ITS sequences (Figure S7). Although the ITS sequence is the most commonly used DNA barcode for distinguishing species, its resolution appears insufficient for discriminating infraspecific samples, such as the varieties in this study. This may be because the divergence time of the infraspecific groups is relatively recent, and the ITS sequences may not have accumulated sufficient mutations. Alternatively, in groups that underwent rapid recent diversification, ancestral gene polymorphisms may not have been completely sorted into different lineages, resulting in identical or highly similar ITS sequences among distinct groups.

We further performed phylogenetic analysis on the two amplified plastid sequences, namely, the trnC-GCA–trnE-UUC intergenic spacer and the rpl2 intron regions, which contain these two indel markers. The results are shown in Figure S8. The phylogenetic results also support that these markers divide the samples into distinct clades, enabling rapid taxonomic identification of the samples.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted morphological observation and identification of four primary G. elata variants (G. elata f. elata, G. elata f. glauca, G. elata f. viridis, and G. elata f. flavida). Initial findings revealed considerable diversity in tuber morphology, which appears influenced by environmental factors. For instance, G. elata f. glauca exhibited a range of tuber shapes, including elliptical, ovoid, and obconical, challenging the conventional view that tuber morphology alone is a reliable taxonomic character. In contrast to the plastic nature of tubers, characteristics of the flowering parts, such as stem and inflorescence color, floral structure, and capsule morphology, are proved more consistent, serving as more stable keys for variants discrimination. Notably, morphological divergence was observed within the same variants across different geographical regions. G. elata f. elata from Sichuan displayed a yellow perianth tube with orange-red tinges at the apical lobes, a dark red elliptical ovary, a narrow and strongly curved labellum with bright yellow fringed, and dark red obovate-elliptical capsules. In contrast, the Yunnan population of the same variants featured a pink-white perianth tube, a light red, elongated-triangular ovary, a broad, slightly curved labellum with whitish fringed, and spathulate capsules with orange-red stripes. This indicates that floral morphology in G. elata is also subject to modifying factors, potentially including regional environmental conditions or genetic drift.

Plastome analysis demonstrated high conservation across all four variants, with sizes ranging narrowly from 35,275 bp to 35,328 bp, identical gene content (20 protein-coding genes, 5 tRNA genes, 3 rRNA genes), and minimal GC content variation (26.75%–26.79%). This genomic stability aligns with the general trend of plastome reduction in heterotrophic plants. For example, research on the mitochondrial genomes of G. elata has indicated that a heterotrophic lifestyle leads to functional degradation of plastids and consequent genomic streamlining [47]. However, this high degree of conservation complicates discrimination at the variants level; despite marked differences in morphology (e.g., tuber shape, flower color) and medicinal compound profiles [8, 48–50], the plastomes lack sufficient specific mutations to unequivocally distinguish all variants. Nucleotide diversity (Pi) analysis revealed Pi values of zero across most regions, with only two minor loci (positions 7,117–7,291 bp and 30,738–30,912 bp) showing low-level variation (maximum Pi = 0.01018). Consequently, relying solely on the plastome as a “super-barcode” may be insufficient for resolving closely related variants, particularly G. elata f. flavida and G. elata f. viridis.

Despite overall conservation, our comparison identified subtle yet potentially useful discriminatory markers. For instance, consistent minor differences were noted in genome size and GC content: plastomes of G. elata f. elata were generally smaller (< 35,300 bp) with slightly higher GC content (26.78%–26.79%), while other variants typically exceeded 35,300 bp. SSR analysis revealed that G. elata f. elata possessed fewer SSRs (42) compared to other variants (43–45), primarily due to reductions in mono- and tetranucleotide repeats. Furthermore, specific InDel markers (GEpt11 and GEpt13t) displayed stability in G. elata f. glauca, G. elata f. flavida, and G. elata f. viridis. Primers designed for these loci successfully amplified targets in all 22 validation samples, indicating that PCR-based detection of these InDel offers a cost-effective and efficient auxiliary identification method. Based on phylogenetic analysis of these amplified sequences containing the Indel markers can effectively distinguish between the different varieties of G. elata. Previously, plastid DNA-based InDel markers have been widely applied for species identification and phylogenetic analysis across various plant genera. For example, In Polygala, comparative analysis of plastomes of three species (P. tenuifolia, P. japonica, and P. sibirica) revealed locally divergent regions, leading to the development of six molecular markers that successfully authenticated commercial products and breeding lines, confirming their authenticity [51]. Similarly, in Ipomoea, plastome sequencing of six species identified highly variable regions, enabling the development of InDel markers (IPOTY and IPOYCF) to distinguish medically important species like I. nil and I. purpurea [52]. In maize, a Varietal Chloroplast Panel (VCP) comprising 11 InDel markers was developed to characterize maternal inheritance, aiding in the identification of cytoplasmic male sterility and reciprocal cross hybrids [53]. For Angelica polymorpha, a species-specific InDel marker (LYCE) targeting a 418-bp deletion in the ycf4-cemA intergenic region effectively differentiated it from Ligusticum officinale [54]. These studies demonstrate the utility of plastid-based InDel markers as reliable tools for taxonomic clarification, authentication of medicinal plants, and tracing of maternal lineages in crops.

However, the plastome failed to reliably distinguish G. elata f. flavida from G. elata f. viridis, consistent with their clustering in a single clade (clade 3) in phylogenetic analysis, possibly due to recent divergence or gene flow preventing the accumulation of sufficient plastid divergence [55]. This close relationship, despite their recognized taxonomic distinction, can be explained by two non-mutually exclusive hypotheses: (1) Recent Divergence: The two forms diverged so recently that their maternally inherited cpDNA has not had sufficient time to coalesce or accumulate diagnostic mutations. (2) Cytoplasmic Introgression: The sharing of identical plastid haplotypes is the result of historical hybridization and subsequent backcrossing (cytoplasmic introgression), where the cpDNA of one variety has introgressed into the nuclear background of the other. Given the uniparental inheritance of cpDNA, hypothesis (2) is particularly plausible if the varieties occur in sympatry, as it explains the complete lack of cpDNA differentiation without invoking an exceedingly recent and simultaneous origin of both morphological varieties. This introgression event, if confirmed, is itself a noteworthy evolutionary phenomenon.

The challenge of distinguishing closely related variants like G. elata f. flavida (Yellow-type) and G. elata f. viridis (Green-type) necessitates an integrative approach combining plastid genomics and morphology. While plastid sequences show high similarity, hindering development of specific DNA barcodes, observable morphological differences exist. For example, the Green-type typically has taller stems, bluish-green flowers, while the Yellow-type exhibits yellowish stems and flowers at maturity (Figure S9). These stable morphological traits provide preliminary identification cues. Therefore, establishing a combined identification strategy is essential. In practice, key morphological characters like stem and flower color can be used for initial screening. For specimens with ambiguous morphology or suspected hybridization, genotyping using plastid-derived markers (e.g., GEpt11, GEpt13) can provide verification. Furthermore, a variety of chemical methods, such as three-dimensional synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy [56], near-infrared spectroscopy [57] and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry [58], can effectively distinguish different varieties and origins of G. elata by analyzing its chemical components. The combination of these multi-evidence approach can reduce misidentification due to phenotypic plasticity or complex genetic backgrounds, and offers a more reliable foundation for germplasm resource management, breeding, and conservation of G. elata.

This study has several limitations. The sample size was relatively limited (11 samples for sequencing, 22 for PCR validation), potentially not capturing the full range of intraspecific variation. Geographical representation was also restricted to five provinces in China, and one variant, G. elata f. alba, was not included, which may affect the generalizability of the conclusions. Furthermore, the maternally inherited plastome cannot reflect hybridization events or nuclear gene flow within the species, as might be the case for the close relationship between G. elata f. flavida and G. elata f. viridis. Future research should expand sampling to include a wider geographical range and additional variant (especially G. elata f. alba and wild populations), and incorporate nuclear genomic data (e.g., ITS sequences or genome-wide SNPs) to resolve relationships not discernible via plastomes alone.

In summary, this study systematically compared the plastomes of four G. elata variants and identified two reliable molecular markers at the plastid DNA level. Our results demonstrate the potential of the plastome as a “super-barcode” for application in germplasm resource management, breeding, and quality control of G. elata, while underscoring the necessity of integrating morphological data for accurate discrimination among closely related variants.

Conclusions

In this study, complete plastome sequences were newly obtained for 11 individuals representing four different variants of G. elata. Comparative analysis revealed that these plastomes exhibit an extremely high degree of conservation in terms of genome size, gene content, repetitive sequence elements, and nucleotide sequence similarity. We identified two key InDel sites (GEpt11 and GEpt13), consisting of 11-bp and 13-bp motifs, which can serve as potential molecular markers for distinguishing the three clades of G. elata variants. However, the variants G. elata f. flavida and G. elata f. viridis did not show sufficient divergence at the plastome level. Furthermore, in the phylogenetic analysis, they clustered together into a single clade, indicating a very close phylogenetic relationship between them. Our study provides rich genetic resources for G. elata, and the newly developed molecular markers can facilitate the species identification of this species.
