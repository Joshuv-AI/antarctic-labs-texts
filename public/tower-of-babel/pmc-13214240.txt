Laparoscopic Suitcase Technique for lateral incisional hernias enables fully laparoscopic fascial closure: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Kryspin Mitura, Krystian Kisielewski, Michal Romanczuk, Mitura Bernard, Malgorzata Sawicka, Laura Kacprzak, Piotr Niecikowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLateral incisional hernias represent a distinct subset of abdominal wall defects and pose specific challenges in minimally invasive surgery due to unfavorable force vectors and the absence of a central anchoring structure. Hybrid approaches combining laparoscopic dissection with open fascial closure can facilitate repair but partially negate the benefits of minimally invasive surgery. This study evaluated the safety and effectiveness of a fully laparoscopic method for fascial closure—the Laparoscopic Suitcase Technique (LST). While central traction techniques have been previously described, their standardized laparoscopic application in lateral incisional hernias has not been clearly defined.MethodsA retrospective comparative cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center. Consecutive adult patients undergoing elective minimally invasive preperitoneal repair of lateral incisional hernias (EHS L1–L4, defect width ≥ 4 cm) were included. Patients treated with a hybrid approach between January 2019 and February 2020 were compared with patients treated exclusively using LST between July 2023 and January 2025. Surgical technique, mesh type, fixation, and overlap were standardized, with the method of fascial closure as the only procedural difference. Primary outcomes were operative time and 30-day postoperative complications. Secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay, mesh area, and hernia recurrence at 12 months.ResultsThirty-three patients were included (hybrid n = 16; LST n = 17). Overall 30-day complication rates were similar and limited to Clavien–Dindo grade I–II events. Length of hospital stay was significantly reduced with LST (3.0 vs. 4.5 days, p < 0.001). No hernia recurrences were observed at 12-month follow-up in either group.ConclusionsThe Laparoscopic Suitcase Technique is a safe and feasible approach that may facilitate fully laparoscopic fascial closure in selected patients. Introduction

Lateral incisional hernias constitute a distinct subgroup of abdominal wall defects and pose specific technical challenges during minimally invasive surgery (MIS) [1–3]. Unlike midline hernias, lateral defects lack a central linea alba anchor point and are exposed to unfavorable force vectors during fascial approximation [2]. Under pneumoperitoneum, excessive tension develops at the wound margins, hindering their approximation; in contrast to open surgery, direct manual or instrumental traction of the fascial edges is not feasible in a minimally invasive setting [4]. Consequently, progressive suture placement during approximation of the myofascial layers may generate substantial tension within both the suture material and the surrounding tissues. This may result in suture loosening or inadequate tissue apposition, which is essential for effective tissue integration and healing [5]. In extreme cases, excessive tension may lead to tissue tearing during suture tightening or even rupture of the suture itself [6].

The use of barbed sutures may partially mitigate these challenges, as their unidirectional barbs prevent backward sliding and suture loosening, allowing for gradual approximation of the fascial edges [7]. With sustained tension applied over time, the surgeon can progressively tighten the suture in a stepwise manner, commonly referred to as the shoelacing technique [8]. However, in lateral incisional hernias, where defect width frequently exceeds 6 cm, this approach is often limited by the fixed length of commercially available barbed sutures, which may not allow for multiple passes using a single strand. This limitation is particularly relevant in laparoscopy, where suturing is technically more demanding than in robotic-assisted techniques [9].

Another approach to address these challenges involves hybrid techniques that combine laparoscopic creation of the preperitoneal or retromuscular space with open fascial closure [10]. While effective, hybrid approaches require an additional incision and partially negate the advantages of minimally invasive surgery, particularly with regard to wound healing, postoperative pain, and recovery time [11].

Based on prior experience with the Suitcase Technique in open abdominal wall reconstruction, we developed a fully laparoscopic adaptation—the Laparoscopic Suitcase Technique (LST). The Suitcase Technique is a horizontal traction suture method originally designed to facilitate gradual myofascial approximation under excessive tension by redistributing forces through a centrally placed Z-shaped suture. This configuration reduces the angle between fascial edges and enables progressive closure without focal tissue overload. The term “suitcase” derives from the mechanical analogy of closing an overfilled suitcase, in which central compression aligns the edges and allows controlled closure without damage to the fastening mechanism.

While the principle of central traction sutures is known, its standardized application in laparoscopic repair of lateral incisional hernias has not been clearly described, particularly in relation to clinical outcomes and avoidance of hybrid approaches. The present study compares outcomes between a historical hybrid cohort and a contemporary cohort treated exclusively using LST.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective comparative cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center for abdominal wall reconstruction. The study compared outcomes before and after a protocolized change in the method of fascial closure during minimally invasive repair of lateral incisional hernias. All procedures were performed by a single surgeon experienced in laparoscopic abdominal wall reconstruction.

Patient selection

All consecutive adult patients undergoing elective preperitoneal minimally invasive repair of lateral incisional hernias classified as EHS L1–L4 were included [12]. All procedures were performed using a transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) approach, with dissection carried out in the preperitoneal plane and extended retromuscularly when necessary. The minimum hernia defect width was 4 cm on preoperative computed tomography, corresponding to EHS width category ≥ W2. Patients treated between January 2019 and February 2020 constituted the hybrid group (HG), while those treated between July 2023 and January 2025 formed the LST group. Emergency procedures were excluded.

Surgical technique

In both groups, surgery began with laparoscopic creation of the preperitoneal and/or retromuscular space. Dissection planes, extent of exposure, mesh type, fixation strategy, and intended overlap were standardized. In the hybrid group (HG), following completion of laparoscopic dissection, fascial closure was performed through an open incision with excision of the previous surgical scar. The fascia was closed using a small-bites technique with a long-term absorbable suture (USP 0) [13]. After closure of the fascia and skin, pneumoperitoneum was re-established, and the procedure was completed laparoscopically with intraperitoneal mesh placement and subsequent peritoneal closure.

In the LST group, fascial closure was performed entirely laparoscopically. A centrally placed non-absorbable barbed suture (USP 0) was introduced in a Z-shaped configuration at the widest portion of the defect and progressively tightened to allow gradual redistribution of tension (Fig. 1A). At this stage, intra-abdominal pressure was temporarily reduced from 12 mmHg to 6 mmHg to facilitate approximation of the fascial edges. Final locking of the barbed suture was performed under direct laparoscopic visualization to avoid inadvertent entrapment of intra-abdominal viscera within the suture line (Fig. 1B). If adequate approximation of the fascial edges could not be achieved at this stage, one or two additional central suture passes were placed, followed by progressive tightening of the suture in a shoelacing manner to facilitate further gradual closure. Following successful central approximation of the defect and reinsufflation of the peritoneal cavity, definitive fascial closure was performed bilaterally from both ends of the defect using a long-term absorbable barbed suture (USP 0) (Fig. 1C). Mesh placement and peritoneal closure were then completed laparoscopically in the same manner as in the hybrid group.

Schematic representation of the Laparoscopic Suitcase Technique (LST). A wide lateral fascial defect with an unfavorable initial angle (α) and a centrally placed Z-shaped traction suture at the widest part of the defect (A). Progressive tightening of the traction suture with temporary reduction of intra-abdominal pressure, resulting in central tension redistribution and a reduced angle (β < α) (B). After approximation of the fascial edges, definitive closure is performed in a conventional manner from both ends of the defect (C)

The method of fascial closure was the only procedural difference between groups.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

Primary outcomes were operative time and 30-day postoperative complications graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification [14]. Secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay, mesh area (calculated as mesh width × length), and hernia recurrence at 12 months. Hernia recurrence was assessed by structured telephone interview at 12 months postoperatively, with patients reporting any symptoms or signs suggestive of recurrence. The structured interview was developed specifically for this study and is provided as Supplementary Material 1. Routine imaging was not performed in asymptomatic patients, in line with standard clinical practice. Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using Fisher’s exact test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

The study protocol was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of the University of Siedlce, Poland. Due to the retrospective design of the study and the use of anonymized routinely collected clinical data, the requirement for formal ethical approval and informed consent was waived by the committee in accordance with national regulations. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Baseline and anatomical characteristics

Baseline demographic, anatomical, and hernia-related characteristics were comparable between groups (Table 1). There were no significant differences with regard to sex distribution, age, body mass index, defect width on preoperative CT, or rate of recurrent hernias. The distribution of hernia localization according to the EHS classification (L1–L4) did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.65). Mesh area was comparable between the hybrid and LST groups, suggesting a similar extent of reconstruction and intended mesh overlap.

Perioperative and postoperative outcomes

Median operative time was significantly shorter in the LST group compared with the hybrid approach (130.0 vs. 155.5 min, p = 0.02) (Table 2). Overall 30-day complication rates were similar between groups, and all complications were minor (Clavien–Dindo I–II). No Clavien–Dindo grade III or higher complications occurred. There were no reoperations during the 30-day postoperative period, and readmission rates were low and comparable between groups. Length of hospital stay was significantly shorter in the LST group, with a median of 3.0 days compared with 4.5 days in the hybrid group (p < 0.001). All patients completed 12-month follow-up, and no hernia recurrences were observed in either group.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that fully laparoscopic fascial closure using the Laparoscopic Suitcase Technique is safe and feasible for lateral incisional hernias. Compared with a hybrid approach, LST significantly reduces operative time while maintaining comparable short-term outcomes. Length of hospital stay was also significantly shorter in the LST group, which may be related to preservation of a fully minimally invasive approach and avoidance of additional access-related trauma [15]. The present study does not aim to introduce a fundamentally new suturing concept, but rather to provide a structured and reproducible laparoscopic implementation of central force redistribution in a setting where conventional techniques are often limited.

Although the concept of using central traction to facilitate fascial approximation may be intuitively applied by experienced abdominal wall surgeons, it has not been formally standardized or systematically described in the context of laparoscopic repair of lateral incisional hernias. Moreover, its implementation in minimally invasive surgery remains variable, and its clinical impact has not been previously evaluated in a comparative setting.

Importantly, the observed reduction in operative time is likely attributable to preservation of a continuous laparoscopic workflow. Hybrid procedures require conversion to open access, including deflation of the pneumoperitoneum, incision through prior scars, dissection of the hernia sac within subcutaneous tissues, identification and open suturing of the fascial edges, closure of subcutaneous tissue and skin, and subsequent re-establishment of laparoscopy [10, 11]. Each of these steps increases operative time and tissue trauma, all of which are avoided with a fully laparoscopic LST [16].

Beyond its perioperative advantages, the Laparoscopic Suitcase Technique may represent a conceptual refinement in the strategy of fascial closure for lateral incisional hernias. Rather than attempting definitive edge-to-edge approximation under maximal tension, LST prioritizes early central force redistribution, effectively modifying the geometry of the defect before completion of closure [17]. By reducing both the distance and the angle between fascial edges at the point of greatest tension, subsequent bilateral closure becomes technically simpler and biomechanically more controlled [18, 19].

In this context, LST should not be viewed merely as an alternative suturing method, but rather as an enabling technique that transforms a high-tension maneuver into a stepwise, reproducible laparoscopic process. This distinction is particularly relevant in lateral abdominal wall defects, where unfavorable force vectors and the absence of a central anchoring structure frequently limit the effectiveness of conventional closure strategies [20].

Beyond time savings, eliminating the open access component preserves the full benefits of minimally invasive surgery [21]. Avoidance of an additional incision reduces access-related morbidity, including wound complications, postoperative pain, and delayed recovery [22]. This advantage is particularly relevant in lateral incisional hernias, where access-related trauma may be disproportionate to defect size [23, 24].

An additional and potentially underappreciated benefit of laparoscopic fascial closure relates to tissue tension and layer identification. In the lateral abdominal wall, particularly lateral to the semilunar line, the abdominal wall consists of three distinct myofascial layers. Under pneumoperitoneum, laparoscopic visualization allows clear identification of the true myofascial edge and enables full-thickness suture placement encompassing all layers with a standardized bite margin. In contrast, during open access these tissues are often lax and poorly defined, and the true fascial edge may be difficult to distinguish from surrounding scar tissue within the subcutaneous plane, potentially compromising adequate suture placement and tension distribution.

Apart from the method of fascial closure, the two groups did not differ with respect to the applied surgical technique. By adhering to standard principles of minimally invasive hernia repair, including adequate dissection and mesh overlap in both approaches, the overall effectiveness of the two methods would be expected to be comparable [20]. Analysis of mesh area demonstrated no reduction in mesh dimensions in the LST group, confirming that fully laparoscopic fascial closure does not compromise available space or intended overlap.

Although this study focuses on laparoscopic application, the conceptual principles of the Suitcase Technique may also be applicable to robotic-assisted repair. Early central force redistribution may improve access to a distant or unfavorably oriented fascial edge, enhancing suturing ergonomics in the robotic setting. Moreover, in the absence of haptic feedback, modification of defect geometry prior to definitive closure may reduce edge-to-edge tension and lower the risk of suture overload or tissue tearing in the absence of haptic feedback during robotic fascial closure [23, 24].

Collectively, these findings suggest that the Laparoscopic Suitcase Technique shifts fascial closure in lateral incisional hernias from a tension-limited maneuver to a controlled, geometry-driven laparoscopic process.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the surgical technique, dissection planes, mesh type, fixation strategy, and intended overlap were standardized across both groups, ensuring a high degree of procedural consistency. Second, all operations were performed by a single surgeon with extensive experience in laparoscopic abdominal wall reconstruction, minimizing inter-operator variability and allowing a focused assessment of the impact of the fascial closure technique itself. Third, the comparative analysis included objective perioperative outcomes as well as 12-month follow-up for hernia recurrence, with complete follow-up achieved in all patients.

The retrospective design and limited sample size represent acknowledged limitations of this study. In particular, comparison with a historical cohort predating the routine implementation of the modified fascial closure technique in TAPP repair of lateral incisional hernias may introduce temporal bias. The retrospective design, small sample size, use of a historical control group, and absence of routine imaging follow-up should be considered when interpreting the results. The difference in data collection periods was largely attributable to the interruption of elective surgical activity during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although mesh area was analyzed post hoc, the extent of dissection and intended mesh overlap were standardized across both groups, mitigating potential bias related to reconstruction strategy. Although the retrospective design limits causal inference, the standardized surgical technique and consistency of perioperative management strengthen the internal validity of the comparison. While the use of a single experienced surgeon represents a methodological strength, it may also limit the generalizability of the results. Future studies should investigate whether laparoscopic fascial closure assisted by the Suitcase Technique influences suture density compared with the standard open small-bites technique, and whether such differences translate into long-term clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

The Laparoscopic Suitcase Technique represents a structured and reproducible approach to laparoscopic fascial closure in lateral incisional hernias. By enabling early central tension redistribution, it may facilitate controlled closure in anatomically challenging defects while preserving a fully laparoscopic workflow. Further studies are required to confirm these findings.
