Expanding evidence-based care for opioid-exposed mother-infant dyads: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Rachel M. Mayo, Jordyn C. Sutton, Madison Edgar, Lori Dickes, Windsor W. Sherrill, Leah A. Holcomb, Elizabeth Charron, Bonnie Treado, Katie Howle, Jessica Boyd, Jennifer Hudson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrenatal opioid use continues to contribute to rising rates of neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) and adverse maternal-infant outcomes in the United States. The Managing Abstinence in Newborns (MAiN) 2.0 model was implemented to expand evidence-based, family-centered NOWS care practices across hospitals in South Carolina (SC). This study evaluated the midpoint implementation of MAiN 2.0 by examining maternal and infant outcomes alongside healthcare provider perspectives on implementation facilitators and barriers.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods design was used. Quantitative and qualitative data were collected from opioid-exposed mother-infant dyads and healthcare providers at four rural SC hospitals participating in MAiN 2.0. Quantitative data were used to compare maternal and infant outcomes between the baseline and midpoint implementation periods using independent-samples t-tests and chi-square tests. Outcomes included NOWS severity, length of stay, discharge disposition, prenatal care utilization, rooming-in, and use of supportive services. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 23 key informants in clinical and healthcare administrative hospital roles. Qualitative data were analyzed using deductive thematic analysis guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). Data were triangulated to contextualize findings.ResultsSignificant improvements were observed at the midpoint of implementation. Peak NOWS scores decreased significantly from baseline to midpoint (12.3 vs. 8.4, p <.001). Infants were more likely to be discharged to their mothers (75.9% vs. 17.2%, p <.001) and to be monitored throughout hospitalization (70.8% vs. 24.0%, p=.002) at the implementation midpoint than at baseline. Maternal prenatal visits increased significantly (5.5 vs. 9.4 visits, p=.002), as did rooming-in duration (2.7 vs. 5.5 days, p<.001) and lactation consultation completion (14.8% vs. 60.8%, p=.001). Qualitative findings identified training, protocol standardization, organizational support, and reduced stigma as key facilitators of implementation. Persistent barriers included staffing shortages, workflow inconsistencies, pharmacy-related challenges, and broader social and structural constraints. Integrated findings suggested that improvements in outcomes were associated with increased adoption of standardized, family-centered care practices despite ongoing implementation challenges.ConclusionsImplementation of the MAiN 2.0 model was associated with improvements in NOWS severity, maternal engagement, and family-centered care processes across expansion hospitals. Continued investment in provider training, interdisciplinary coordination, and structural support may strengthen sustained implementation and improve outcomes for opioid-exposed mother-infant dyads.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Prenatal opioid use has become a maternal and neonatal health concern in the United States (US), contributing to increasing rates of neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) and related adverse maternal-infant health outcomes, including maternal morbidity and preterm labor [1, 2]. Infants exposed to opioids may also experience feeding difficulties, prolonged hospital stays, and complications requiring specialized treatment [3]. Mothers of infants with NOWS often experience social and structural challenges, including stigma, a lack of accessible and trauma-informed prenatal care, mental health concerns and barriers to treatment engagement, which can further impact adverse health outcomes [4, 5]. In response, hospitals have adopted family-centered and nonpharmacologic approaches to care, but implementation and delivery vary [6, 7].

Evidence-based approaches for the management of infants with prenatal opioid exposure emphasize standardized assessment protocols, nonpharmacologic interventions, maternal engagement and coordinated interdisciplinary care [8–10]. Practices such as rooming-in, breastfeeding support, caregiver education and consistent monitoring have been associated with reductions in NOWS severity, decreased pharmacological treatment, and shorter hospital stays [11–14]. Despite growing evidence supporting these approaches, hospitals frequently encounter staffing limitations, variability in provider training, challenges with workflow integration, barriers to pharmacy coordination and limited organizational resources [15, 16]. These challenges highlight the need for scalable care models that support both clinical standardization and implementation feasibility across diverse maternal-neonatal settings.

In response to a 16.5% increase in the rate of NOWS cases in South Carolina (SC) from 2016 to 2020, the Managing Abstinence in Newborns (MAiN) model was developed to improve the care of opioid-exposed mother-infant dyads [1, 2, 17]. The model incorporates standardized clinical protocols, staff education, maternal engagement strategies, interdisciplinary coordination and nonpharmacologic care practices intended to reduce variability in care delivery and improve maternal and infant health outcomes [17]. Given its comprehensive and cost-effective approach to NOWS management, the MAiN model has been replicated through implementation efforts in 10 expansion hospitals in SC, otherwise known as MAiN 2.0 [18, 19].

Prior to MAiN 2.0 implementation, previous research analyzed baseline barriers and facilitators to adoption among expansion hospitals using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [16, 20]. These findings guided the formation of collaborative partnerships among regional stakeholders, but limited information is known about the perceived barriers and facilitators of the MAiN 2.0 model at the midpoint [16]. In tandem, maternal and infant outcomes at expansion hospitals have not been examined. This study aims to evaluate the midpoint implementation of the MAiN 2.0 model across expansion hospitals by examining maternal and infant outcomes between the baseline and midpoint time periods, and eliciting stakeholder perspectives on implementation facilitators and barriers.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was part of a multi-site, mixed-methods evaluation of the MAiN 2.0 program. The evaluation used a convergent mixed methods design to assess implementation processes and clinical outcomes over time. The present analysis focuses on qualitative findings from the evaluation midpoint and incorporates baseline and midpoint quantitative outcomes to contextualize implementation progress. Baseline data were collected retrospectively over the 12 months preceding implementation, and midpoint data were collected after expansion hospitals initiated the MAiN model and reached an early implementation threshold of at least 6 infants treated under the protocol. Baseline and midpoint data were collected during different calendars periods as part of the implementation evaluation.

Participant recruitment and sampling

Expansion hospitals were recruited through a multi-pronged approach. The recruitment process started with the MAiN team identifying hospitals serving communities with a high burden of NOWS and Opioid Use Disorder (OUD), based on county and statewide data. Hospitals were recruited based on high NOWS and OUD burden and some hospitals were recruited through the SC Birth Outcomes Initiative (BOI), a collaborative that bridges together Medical Managed Care Organizations, hospitals, health systems and public health agencies to improve maternal and infant health outcomes in South Carolina [21]. The identified hospitals were then invited to learn more about the program. Recruitment processes included direct outreach to hospital leadership, presentations describing the MAiN model and its potential benefits, presentations at SC BOI meetings, and on-site open house events for hospital teams interested in implementation. Hospitals then self-selected to participate in the MAiN program by submitting an electronic application describing their NOWS population and current treatment protocols. While recruitment efforts focused on hospitals with the greatest potential need, participation was voluntary and all participating hospitals elected to apply and join the MAiN program. At each site, a designated hospital champion identified key stakeholders involved in the implementation and care delivery of substance-exposed newborns. A purposive sampling approach was used to sample participants with diverse perspectives and roles within the hospital institutions. A total of 23 key informants participated in midpoint interviews across 4 hospitals.

Although MAiN 2.0 expansion analyses included 10 expansion hospitals, quantitative analyses were conducted on opioid-exposed mother-infant dyads from 4 hospitals due to variations in outcome variables, reporting time periods, and consistency amongst health outcomes measures. The 4 expansion hospitals were selected based on data availability at both the baseline and midpoint implementation periods and the comparability of the measured outcomes. Inclusion of these 4 hospitals was determined by data availability rather than hospital performance or implementation success. All available data from all opioid-exposed mother-infant dyads from the 4 expansion hospitals were included in baseline (N = 39) and midpoint (N = 29) quantitative analyses.

Data collection

Trained members of the evaluation team conducted semi-structured key informant interviews. Interviews were conducted individually, either in person during site visits or virtually when necessary and lasted approximately 15–30 min. The original CFIR constructs informed the interview guide to maintain consistency with prior baseline findings, with an emphasis on understanding implementation experiences over time, including perceived facilitators, barriers, adaptations, and organizational context [20]. Questions explore domains such as training experiences, workflow integration, interprofessional communication, resource availability, and patient care practices. All interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim. Participants were provided with an overview of the study, and verbal informed consent was obtained before participation. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at Prisma Health (IRB #2070994).

Quantitative data were collected from expansion hospitals on maternal and infant clinical and social outcomes associated with NOWS care. Expansion hospitals submitted case-level data via secure transmission to the evaluation team for all eligible mother-infant dyads. Infant outcomes included birth weight, gestational age, inpatient length of stay, percent of peak weight loss, the day of peak weight loss, peak neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) score, and the day of life (DoL) at which peak NAS score was observed. NAS scores, which were utilized to measure NOWS severity, were measured using the modified Finnegan Neonatal Abstinence Scoring (FNAS) Tool [22, 23]. Hospitals also provided information on the infants’ gender, urine and meconium results (if collected), and discharge and monitoring practices. Maternal outcomes included daily opioid dose, number of prenatal visits, rooming-in stay length, prenatal and MAiN consult completion, urine screening results, smoking status during the third trimester, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) status at time of delivery, type of medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), and breastfeeding status during hospital stays. Hospitals also provided information on whether the mother-infant dyad experienced an unsafe sleep event, had an emergency department (ED) visit within 60 days of delivery, completed safe sleep education, and attended consultations for social work, lactation and physical therapy/occupational therapy/speech therapy (PT/OT/ST) services.

Baseline data included outcomes from the 12 months before MAiN implementation, and midpoint data were collected prospectively once hospitals began implementing the program and reached the predefined midpoint threshold (at least 6 infants treated). For this study, only hospitals with both baseline and midpoint data were included to ensure consistency across time points.

Data analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis, incorporating both deductive and inductive approaches [24, 25]. A preliminary codebook was developed based on CFIR domains and constructs identified in prior baseline analyses [20]. Two independent analysts (LH and EC) coded all transcripts. Coding discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus, and the codebook was iteratively refined throughout the analytic process. Inter-rater reliability was not formally calculated. Themes were then organized to reflect key facilitators and barriers to implementation, as these CFIR constructs were used in the baseline implementation analysis.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize maternal and infant characteristics at baseline and at the midpoint. Primary analyses were conducted to identify changes in clinical outcomes potentially associated with MAiN implementation, including length of stay, NOWS severity, prenatal care utilization and service engagement. Continuous variables were compared between baseline and midpoint using the Mann-Whitney U test, given that several outcomes had relatively small sample sizes. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests when expected cell counts were small. Statistical significance was defined as p < .05.

Qualitative and quantitative findings were integrated during analysis to provide a comprehensive understanding of MAiN implementation. Quantitative outcomes were used to contextualize reported experiences, while qualitative findings helped explain variability in observed outcomes across sites.

Results

Quantitative maternal and infant characteristics

Quantitative analyses included mother-infant dyads from hospitals (n = 4) that contributed data at both baseline and midpoint to ensure comparability. A total of 68 mother-infant dyads were included in the analysis, including 39 during the baseline period (2016–2020) and 29 during the midpoint period (2020–2024). During the baseline period, participants were recruited from 4 hospitals, with the largest proportion enrolled at Hospital 1 (46.2%), followed by Hospital 2 (23.1%), Hospital 3 (23.1%), and Hospital 4 (7.7%). Infant birth weight and gestational age did not differ significantly between the baseline and midpoint cohorts. The median birth weight was 3005 g (Q1;Q3: 2684,3310) during the baseline period and 3090 g (Q1,Q3: 2920, 3290) during the midpoint (p = .519). The median peak NAS scores at midpoint were 8.0 (5.0–10) and the median peak NAS scores at baseline were 13.0(10.0–15.0), indicating reduced symptom severity (p=.0003). Infants at the midpoint were also significantly more likely to be discharged to their mothers (75.9% vs. 17.2%, p < .0001) and to be monitored throughout their hospital stay (70.9% vs. 24.0%, p = .0016). There were no statistically significant differences in length of stay, percent weight loss, or timing of peak NOWS symptoms. Toxicology screening results and infant sex distribution remained consistent across time points.

Rooming-in duration increased significantly, as the median rooming in length of stay was 2.0 days (1.0–4.0) at baseline and 5.0 days (5.0–7.0) at the midpoint (p < .0001), reflecting greater integration of family-centered care practices. Significant increases were also observed in the receipt of lactation consultations (14.8% vs. 60.8%, p = .0011). However, PT/OT/ST consultations decreased at the midpoint (100% vs. 73.9%, p < .0001), potentially reflecting changes in clinical need or practice patterns. Feeding practices also changed significantly over time: mothers at the midpoint were more likely to exclusively breastfeed (13.8% vs. 2.6%) or use mixed breast/formula feeding (44.8% vs. 20.5%), while formula-only feeding decreased from 76.9% to 41.4% (p=.0070).

Facilitators of implementation

The facilitators of implementation at the midpoint included training and knowledge development, access to resources and ongoing support, organizational support and culture and improvements to care delivery. Training was the most consistently cited facilitator, with participants reporting increased knowledge, confidence, and standardization of NOWS care compared with baseline. Ongoing access to training materials and real-time support from the MAiN team further reinforced implementation. Organizational support, including engaged leadership and the presence of site champions, contributed to improved staff buy-in and sustained program momentum. Participants also described shifts in care delivery practices, including greater adoption of rooming-in and standardized protocols, which were perceived to improve both patient care and staff experience.

Barriers to implementation

The implementation barriers at the midpoint included workforce constraints and training gaps; social and structural changes; pharmacy and medical processes; workflow and departmental tensions; parent education and care; and policy consistency. Staffing constraints, including shortages and turnover, continued to limit implementation capacity and consistency of training. Social and structural challenges, such as limited social work resources and difficulties engaging mothers in care due to a lack of parental education on care approaches, remained significant. Pharmacy-related barriers, including medication dispensing processes and the lack of standardized protocols, were frequently cited as ongoing challenges. Some resistance to workflow changes and gaps in communication across departments and providers affected implementation as some participants described variations in provider referrals and policies.

Integration of findings

Improvements in infant outcomes, such as reduced NOWS severity and increased maternal discharge rates, aligned with reported increases in staff training, protocol adoption and organizational support. Increases in prenatal visits and rooming-in duration were consistent with qualitative reports of enhanced care coordination on family-centered care practices.

In tandem, barriers related to staffing, pharmacy processes, and social determinants helped explain why some clinical outcomes did not change significantly. Together, these findings suggest that while early implementation of MAiN 2.0 improved key aspects of care delivery, structural and resource-related challenges continue to influence program effectiveness across sites. Discussion

This mixed-methods study found that early implementation of the MAiN 2.0 model across diverse expansion hospitals was associated with improvements in infant outcomes and care delivery processes. Reductions in NOWS severity, increases in infant discharge to mothers, and continuous monitoring suggest that the model can be effectively adapted beyond its original setting while maintaining core clinical benefits. Concurrent improvements in prenatal care utilization, rooming-in, and feeding practices further indicate greater engagement with family-centered practices.

Comparison of qualitative and quantitative findings highlights the mechanisms driving these changes. As found in previous research, training and protocol standardization emerged as significant facilitators, improving provider confidence and reducing variability in perinatal and neonatal care [26–28]. Organizational support through the utilization of leadership engagement and local champions reinforced adoption and contributed to shifts in staff attitudes, reducing stigma towards mothers with substance use [29, 30]. These changes supported greater uptake of family-centered practices, which align with observed improvements in maternal-infant outcomes.

Continuous structural barriers help explain why some outcomes may remain unchanged. Staffing constraints, turnover, and uneven training uptake limited implementation consistency, while pharmacy-related challenges and gaps in care coordination created workflow inefficiencies. As noted in other NOWS protocol research, social and contextual factors, including limited community resources and barriers to patient engagement, further constrained implementation [31–33]. These findings suggest that while standardized clinical models can improve care, their effectiveness may be contingent on local capacity and system-level support.

Strengths & limitations

This study has several strengths, including its multi-site, mixed-methods design, which explains not only whether outcomes change but also the potential underlying reasons for these changes. However, findings should be interpreted considering limitations, including small and variable sample sizes, incomplete data, and a focus on early implementation rather than long-term program sustainability.

In addition, the study’s results have limited generalizability, as participating hospitals were located within a single state and voluntarily joined the program, which may limit the applicability of the findings to other settings or regions. Even given the varied types of hospitals and locations within the state, the generalizability of results should be treated with caution. Further, only 4 of the 10 expansion hospitals were analyzed in the quantitative analyses given limitations in data availability, which may introduce selection bias. Given that the remaining 6 hospitals were not included, it is unknown whether the included hospitals differed systematically from those excluded. These findings should be interpreted with caution and may not fully represent outcomes across all MAiN 2.0 hospitals.

This study did not fully examine implementation practices and differences across hospitals. Differences in staffing, resources, leadership support, and other implementation practices across hospitals may have affected outcomes. Related to this, the study did not include a separate comparative control group that did not implement MAiN 2.0, which makes it difficult to clearly determine whether observed improvements were directly attributable to MAiN 2.0 rather than other external factors. Baseline data (2016–2020) and midpoint data (2020–2024) were collected at different times, and broader changes in NOWS care practices or healthcare delivery may have influenced outcomes. Further, qualitative findings relied on stakeholder perceptions, which may have been influenced by recall or social desirability bias.

Conclusions

This study found that implementation of the MAiN 2.0 model was associated with measurable improvements in care practices and selected maternal and infant outcomes among opioid-exposed mother-infant dyads. Findings indicate that expansion hospitals adopted core elements of the model while improving prenatal engagement, increasing family-centered care practices, and supporting safer discharge outcomes for infants. Importantly, improvements in NOWS severity and maternal-infant care processes were observed despite relatively stable maternal clinical risk profiles across time points, suggesting that changes in care delivery may contribute to improved outcomes.

These findings are relevant to ongoing national efforts to improve care for families affected by prenatal opioid exposure and NOWS. The results support continued investment in family-centered approaches that prioritize care coordination, provider education, and maternal engagement throughout the perinatal period. Future research should examine the long-term sustainability of MAiN implementation, evaluate outcomes in larger, more diverse populations and explore strategies to address persistent structural barriers that may limit equitable implementation across healthcare systems.
