Corvis ST parameters in eyes with primary angle-closure glaucoma: a retrospective cross-sectional study using propensity score matching
Authors: Yuta Nakaniida, Kana Tokumo, Shunsuke Nakakura, Ryo Asaoka, Yoshiaki Kiuchi, Hirokazu Sakaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate differences in Corvis ST tonometry (CST) parameters between healthy eyes and eyes with primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed CST parameters of 24 healthy eyes and 24 eyes with PACG after 1:1 propensity score matching based on age, sex, axial length, intraocular pressure, and central corneal thickness. Differences in 12 CST parameters were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models, with group as a fixed effect and subject ID as a random intercept to account for inter-eye correlation. The models were adjusted for age, sex, axial length, intraocular pressure, and central corneal thickness. Multiple comparisons across parameters were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.ResultsCompared with healthy eyes, eyes with PACG exhibited greater A1 deformation amplitude and a shorter A2 time.ConclusionsCompared with healthy eyes, eyes with PACG showed differences in selected CST parameters, specifically greater A1 deformation amplitude and shorter A2 time. These findings may indicate altered corneal dynamic response behavior in PACG.Keywords: Corvis ST tonometry, Primary angle-closure glaucoma, Glaucoma, Cornea

Background

Glaucoma is one of the leading causes of irreversible visual impairment, affecting an estimated 60 million individuals worldwide [1]. The disease is characterized by progressive loss of retinal ganglion cells, ultimately resulting in permanent visual field defects. Although elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) remains the most well-established risk factor, growing evidence suggests that corneal biomechanical properties also influence glaucoma development and progression [2].

Corneal visualization Scheimpflug technology (Corvis ST tonometry [CST]; Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany) employs an ultrafast Scheimpflug camera to quantitatively assess corneal biomechanical responses during air-puff–induced deformation [3]. Most previous studies evaluating CST parameters have focused on primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) [4–7], whereas data specific to primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) remain limited. Because PACG is characterized by anatomical features distinct from those of POAG, including a shallower anterior chamber and shorter axial length [8, 9], its corneal dynamic response behavior may also differ. Therefore, characterizing CST parameters in PACG may provide additional insight into disease-related ocular biomechanics and may contribute to improved understanding and management of disease progression.

This study aimed to evaluate differences in CST parameters between healthy eyes and eyes with PACG.

Methods

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee for Epidemiology of Hiroshima University (permission No. Epd-826-4). All participants provided written informed consent allowing their clinical data to be stored in the hospital database and used for research purposes. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

This study did not involve human eye tissues or cells (e.g., postmortem donor tissue or cultured cells); all data were obtained from in vivo clinical measurements in living participants. Therefore, donor-related information (e.g., cause of death, death-to-preservation interval, and time on ventilator) is not applicable.

Healthy eyes and eyes with PACG

This retrospective cross-sectional study included 24 healthy eyes and 24 eyes with PACG. Data were collected at Hiroshima University Hospital, Saneikai Tsukazaki Hospital, and Seirei Hamamatsu General Hospital between August 1, 2019 and December 25, 2021.

PACG was diagnosed according to the Japan Glaucoma Society Guidelines for Glaucoma (5th edition) [10]. Eyes were classified as having PACG when both of the following criteria were satisfied: (1) gonioscopic angle closure, defined as an inability to visualize the posterior pigmented trabecular meshwork over a visual field of ≥ 180° in primary gaze without indentation, and (2) glaucomatous optic neuropathy, indicated by neuroretinal rim thinning with a vertical cup-to-disc ratio > 0.7, inter-eye vertical cup-to-disc ratio asymmetry > 0.2, and/or focal rim notching, accompanied by a corresponding glaucomatous visual field defect. Eyes with PACG were referred from other hospitals for laser or surgical treatment following drug therapy, and all examinations were performed before these interventions.

Eligibility criteria for healthy eyes were as follows: (1) age ≥ 20 years; (2) an open angle on gonioscopy, defined as the absence of angle closure ≥ 180° in primary gaze without indentation (i.e., the posterior pigmented trabecular meshwork visible for ≥ 180°); (3) no glaucomatous optic disc changes; and (4) no history of ocular disease other than mild refractive error. Exclusion criteria for healthy eyes were as follows: (1) IOP > 25 mmHg; (2) history of an acute glaucoma attack, prior laser treatment, or previous ocular surgery, including glaucoma or cataract surgery; and (3) corneal pathology, such as keratoconus or corneal scarring.

If both eyes met the eligibility criteria, data from both eyes were included in the analysis.

CST measurement

The operating principle of CST has been described previously [3]. Briefly, the system uses a high-speed Scheimpflug camera to capture a sequence of corneal images during its dynamic response to an air-puff stimulus. Images are acquired at 4,330 frames per second, and the recorded data are processed to derive parameters such as central corneal thickness (CCT), deformation amplitude, length of the deformed corneal region, and corneal motion velocity.

Definitions of CST parameters were as follows. A1 time was defined as the interval from air-puff onset to the first applanation, representing the initial corneal flattening during inward deformation. In noncontact tonometry, IOP is estimated by converting this first applanation time (A1 time) into an IOP value [11, 12]. A2 time corresponds to the second applanation, which occurs as the cornea rebounds outward toward its baseline configuration after initial inward deformation induced by the air puff; following the first applanation, the cornea continues to move posteriorly before this recovery phase begins. The velocity of the corneal apex at A1 and A2 times was defined as A1 and A2 velocity, respectively. The length of the flattened cornea at A1 and A2 times was defined as A1 and A2 length, respectively. The magnitude of corneal deformation at A1 and A2 times was defined as A1 and A2 deformation amplitude, respectively. At the highest concavity (HC), deformation amplitude represents the maximum deformation at the moment. Peak distance was defined as the separation between the two peripheral deformation peaks observed at the HC. Radius represents the radius of curvature of the central cornea at HC. Whole eye movement was defined as the maximum posterior displacement of the entire eye induced by the air-puff stimulus.

Corvis ST examinations (software v1.6r2031) were performed three times per eye on the same day, with a minimum 1-min interval between measurements. The mean of the three readings was used for each parameter in each eye, and only acquisitions that passed the instrument’s on-screen quality check (quality index: “OK”) were included in the analysis.

Other measurements

Axial length (AL) was measured using IOLMaster 700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA), IOP was determined using Goldmann applanation tonometry, and CCT was measured using CST.

Statistical analysis

To reduce imbalance in the baseline characteristics between the groups, we performed propensity score matching [13, 14]. The propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model including age, sex, IOP, AL, and CCT as covariates. We then conducted 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement on the logit of the propensity score with a caliper of 0.2 [14]. For reproducibility, the random seed was set to 20,260,101. Among 180 potentially eligible healthy eyes, 24 were matched 1:1 to 24 eyes selected from 92 eyes with PACG, resulting in 24 eyes per group.

Standardized mean difference (SMD) is a sample size–independent measure of the magnitude of between-group differences in each covariate. Covariate balance before and after propensity score matching was assessed using SMDs. An absolute SMD of less than 0.1 was considered to indicate good covariate balance, whereas larger values suggested residual imbalance. For continuous variables, SMDs were calculated as the difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation, whereas for categorical variables, they were calculated using the difference in proportions standardized by the pooled binomial variance.

A Love plot is a graphical tool for visualizing covariate balance before and after propensity score matching. It displays the absolute SMDs for each covariate, with values closer to zero indicating better balance between groups. The term “distance” refers to the propensity score distance derived from the estimated propensity score used for matching.

Sensitivity analyses are additional analyses performed to evaluate whether the main findings remain consistent under different analytical assumptions or modeling strategies. Although propensity score matching improved covariate balance, some residual imbalance may still have remained. Therefore, we fitted covariate-adjusted LMMs to account for possible residual imbalance and to improve precision. We also performed sensitivity analyses comparing the matched analysis without further covariate adjustment (PSM only) with the covariate-adjusted matched analysis (PSM + LMM).

Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation) [minimum–maximum] and categorical variables as counts and percentages. At the subject level, age differences between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test [15], and sex distribution was compared using Fisher’s exact test [16].

AL, IOP, CCT, and 12 CST parameters were compared using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) at the eye level, with group as a fixed effect and subject ID as a random intercept (1|ID) to account for inter-eye correlation. LMMs extend ordinary linear models by incorporating both fixed and random effects, allowing valid inference in the presence of clustered or repeated observations [17, 18]. Accordingly, the 12 CST parameters were compared between healthy eyes and eyes with PACG using LMMs, with group (healthy eyes or eyes with PACG) as a fixed effect and subject ID as a random intercept to account for inter-eye correlation. In ophthalmic studies where both eyes from the same subject are included, eye-level observations are correlated; these observations violate the independence assumption of ordinary linear regression. For multiple comparisons across demographic variables and CST parameters, p-values for the group effect were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR) [24].

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant; for analyses involving multiple testing, FDR-adjusted q-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for age, sex, AL, IOP, or CCT in the matched sample, although residual imbalance remained for sex, AL, and CCT based on SMDs.

Among the 12 CST parameters, two showed significant differences between the two groups: eyes with PACG exhibited greater A1 deformation amplitude and shorter A2 time. This suggests that a greater A1 deformation amplitude may reflect deeper corneal indentation during the initial deformation phase, whereas a shorter A2 time may reflect a faster recovery response after maximal deformation in eyes with PACG. Thus, compared with healthy eyes, eyes with PACG showed significant differences in selected CST dynamic response parameters, namely greater A1 deformation amplitude and shorter A2 time. These findings may suggest altered corneal dynamic response behavior rather than a generalized increase in corneal deformability.

Previous studies have reported that compared with healthy eyes, eyes with PACG exhibited a higher Corvis Biomechanical Index (CBI) and a lower Corvis Biomechanical Factor (CBiF), both of which are CST-derived parameters reflecting corneal biomechanical stability [25]. Corneal biomechanical stability refers to the cornea’s resistance to excessive deformation in response to external forces (e.g., an air puff or IOP fluctuations) and its ability to rapidly recover its original shape; this combination of “deformation resistance” and “recovery strength” is represented by CBI and CBiF [25–27]. CBI is calculated using a logistic regression algorithm that integrates multiple dynamic corneal response parameters obtained using CST and serves as a nonlinear index for assessing corneal biomechanical stability [26]. Alternatively, CBiF provides a linear measure of overall corneal biomechanical stability, enabling a standardized assessment of corneal biomechanics [27]. A higher CBI and lower CBiF indicate reduced corneal biomechanical stability and have been associated with faster, greater corneal deformation in response to an air puff.

A previous study comparing 35 eyes with POAG and 35 age- and IOP-matched healthy eyes reported that eyes with POAG exhibit shorter A1 and A2 times, longer peak distance, smaller radius, and a shorter whole eye movement [4]. These findings suggest that, relative to healthy eyes, eyes with POAG display altered corneal dynamic behavior. In this study, eyes with PACG showed greater A1 deformation amplitude and shorter A2 time than healthy eyes. Although these findings may also reflect altered corneal dynamic response behavior, the current results should be interpreted specifically in the context of PACG, and direct comparison with POAG should be made with caution.

A previous study comparing CST parameters between eyes with POAG and PACG reported that eyes with PACG exhibit a longer A2 time, greater A2 deformation amplitude, shorter peak distance, and longer whole eye movement [28]. However, in the previous study, eyes with PACG had a higher proportion of women and a shorter AL than eyes with POAG. Because these CST parameters are significantly correlated with AL, the observed differences may be partly attributable to PACG-specific characteristics, such as a shorter AL, rather than the glaucoma type.

Corneal biomechanical behavior in glaucoma may be influenced not only by disease subtype but also by chronic IOP-related tissue changes and treatment exposure. In this study, most eyes with PACG were treated with topical antiglaucoma medications, and these factors may have affected CST measurements. Therefore, the observed between-group differences should be interpreted with this possibility in mind.

Although propensity score matching improved balance for some covariates, residual imbalance remained for sex, AL, and CCT. For this reason, we additionally used covariate-adjusted LMMs after matching to account for residual imbalance and improve precision. Importantly, sensitivity analyses showed that the significant group differences in A1 deformation amplitude and A2 time were consistent in both direction and statistical significance between the PSM only and PSM + LMM analyses, suggesting that the main findings were broadly consistent across the two analytic approaches, despite the residual imbalance.

This study has several limitations. First, because the final matched sample was relatively small (24 eyes per group), this study should be considered exploratory. The limited sample size may have reduced statistical power, meaning that some non-significant findings may reflect insufficient power rather than true equivalence between groups. Likewise, even statistically significant results should be interpreted with caution given the limited precision achievable in a small matched sample, which may also limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, because this was a retrospective cross-sectional study, causal inference cannot be established. Third, both eyes were included when eligible; although inter-eye correlation was accounted for using linear mixed-effects models, inclusion of both eyes remains a methodological consideration that may affect generalizability. Fourth, anterior chamber depth and anterior chamber volume were not consistently available in this retrospective dataset and therefore could not be included in the current analysis. Because these parameters are highly relevant to PACG, future prospective studies should evaluate their relationship with CST parameters. Fifth, this study focused on between-group differences in CST parameters and did not evaluate the relationship of these biomechanical indices with functional outcomes (e.g., visual field) or structural changes assessed via optical coherence tomography. Therefore, the clinical significance of these findings remains unclear. Sixth, most eyes with PACG were treated with topical antiglaucoma medications, and the potential effects of these medications on corneal biomechanical behavior could not be fully accounted for [29, 30].

Conclusions

Compared with healthy eyes, eyes with PACG showed differences in selected CST dynamic response parameters, specifically greater A1 deformation amplitude and shorter A2 time. These findings suggest altered corneal dynamic response behavior in PACG; however, broader biomechanical interpretations require further investigation.
