Longitudinal 3D quantification of joint space from CT images: Semi-automatic segmentation and feasibility of nnU-Net automation
Authors: Jean Baptiste Deloges, Stéphanie Uk, Jordan Cohen, Marianne Bourguignon, Jean Denis Laredo, Klaus Engelke, Christine Chappard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe most commonly imaging techniques used in knee osteoarthritis (OA) of the are Xrays and MRI. Computed tomography (CT) however, has his own advantages, especially three-dimensional high-resolution representation of subchondral bone and epiphyseal bone structure. Addition of a method allowing 3D joint space (JS) quantification would improve the clinical value of CT in knee OA follow-up. We here propose a method for quantifying the 3D JS from CT images using a semi-automatic and automatic segmentation based on deep learning method.DesignFifty-four subjects (6 men, 48 women; mean age: 62.8 ± 8.4 years), from a multicenter longitudinal study, with medial compartment OA (Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2 -3) who underwent high-resolution non-weightbearing CT scans performed 36 months apart (M00 and M36). A semi-automatic segmentation that could be manually corrected was used to segment the JS and to train a nnUNet algorithm. JS thickness mapping was obtained using a 3D sphere method both for the medial (MED) and lateral (LAT) compartments at times M00 and M36. Parameters measured included mean thickness (JS_mTh), standard deviation of thickness (JS-SDTh), minimum (JS_min), maximum (JS_max) and maximum/minimum ratio (JS_max/min).ResultsFor the MED and LAT compartments, JS_mTh presented the best root mean square coefficient of variation (RMSCV%) about 2.7% and 2.8%, standard deviation (RMSSDmm) about 0.137 mm and 0.158 mm. JS_mTh, JS_SDTh and JS_max were significantly different for the MED compartment between M00 and M36, there is no differences for the LAT compartment, with 22% of the subjects with JS_mTh reduction beyond 0.5 mm for the MED compartment and 4% for the LAT compartment. The mean biases between semi-automatic and nn-Unet JS_mTh measurements were - 0.16mm±0.38 and -0.04±0.59 for MED and LAT compartments, respectively.ConclusionThis study shows that JS_mTh measured on non-weight bearing CT scans in patients with medial compartment OA had a satisfactory reproducibility and is able to measure significant variations between two CT-scans taken 36 months apart. Automatic segmentation based on nnUnet has the potential to replace semi-automatic segmentation.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis the most common musculoskeletal disorder in older adults, is a joint disease with articular cartilage degeneration. Cartilage damage typically begins with fissures in the superficial layers, gradually extends to deeper zones, and eventually results in complete cartilage loss [1]. OA is a highly heterogeneous disease, however, with no single clinical, laboratory, pathological or radiologic feature serving as a gold standard for its diagnosis and/or classification [2]. The knee is the most commonly affected weight‐bearing joint. The prevalence of knee OA has doubled since the mid 20th century [3].

The most commonly used imaging techniques to monitor cartilage deterioration in clinical trials of knee OA are conventional radiography and MRI [4]. Measurements of joint space width on conventional anteroposterior weight-bearing X-rays, performed according to a standardized methodology, remain an acceptable primary endpoint for assessing structural damage and demonstrating the efficacy of disease-modifying drugs in clinical trials [5]. Radiographic measurement of joint space width is a two-dimensional (2D) projection of the distance between the bones, which to some extent reflects the thickness of the articular cartilage. There are semi-automated algorithms for measuring knee joint space in the medial and lateral compartments, namely the knee osteoarthritis computer-aided diagnosis (KOACAD) [6], which was recently used in a Japanese cohort of 247 subjects [7], and the Knee Image Digital Analysis (KIDA) method [8], used in European cohorts [9,10]. The main drawback of radiographic joint space measurements is their lack of sensitivity in the early stages of the disease [11]. Since conventional X-rays capture only a 2D projection of a 3D structure, the reliability of 2D measurements depends heavily on X-ray acquisition conditions, such as knee positioning, flexion angle, and alignment of the X-ray beam with the tibial plateaus [12]. Even under optimal imaging conditions, conventional X-rays may still prove to be insensitive and imprecise in cases of focal cartilage degeneration or meniscal lesions [12].

MRI is commonly used in clinical practice to visualize cartilage, joint effusions, ligaments, tendons, menisci, osteophytes, and bone marrow lesions. In research, cartilage mapping can be performed using MRI sequences such as T2, T2*, and T1ρ or 3D gradient echo [13]. For this reason, it is strongly recommended to supplement X-rays with MRI in clinical studies [12]. Computed tomography (CT) provides good visualization of bone, osteophytes and calcium deposits [14]. It is used for surgical planning [15], and can be also used to assess subchondral bone density [16]. CT arthrography with intra-articular injection has been proposed for the evaluation of the cartilage surface [17]. Standing cone-beam computed tomography, an emerging technology over the past decade, has also been proposed for measuring the intertibial space thickness in 3D using various methods. These methods include calculation of the Euclidean distance between the femur and tibia from a 3D triangulated surface mesh [[18], [19], [20]], analysis of the minimum distance between any two points in 3D [21], and direct measurement of the knee joint thickness at each point along a normal to the tibial surface using a shape model derived from conventional neural network machine learning [22].

A recent review highlighted the renewed interest in CT for the assessment of knee osteoarthritis [23]. It has also been suggested that new imaging biomarkers capable of supporting the refinement of the conventional classification will enrich studies on patients at high risk of disease progression [2] and that imaging protocols need to be reconsidered [3].

Deep learning tools have been used to improve human interpretation of imaging studies, to determine the severity of the condition, and to predict disease progression. They can also be used for segmentation or quantitative measurements. Various convolutional neural network architectures have been used to assess the severity of osteoarthritis based on large sets of radiographic data [24,25], and to predict the need for joint replacement based on X-rays [26] or on MRI images [27]. A U-Net-based convolutional neural network architecture has been used to measure the minimum joint space width on X-rays [28], and to segment cartilage on MRI images [[29], [30], [31]].

The objective of this study is to evaluate the capability and performance of a semi-automated algorithm applied to CT images to generate a 3D map of the knee joint space using statistics from local thickness measurements, and to compare these data at two time points, 36 months apart, with sufficient accuracy. In a second step, the joint space database is used to train an automatic segmentation system based on convolutional neural networks, whose preliminary validation results are compared to those of the semi-automatic algorithm.

Patients and methods

This study is a retrospective analysis of a prospective cohort, utilizing CT scans of a longitudinal multicenter study on efficacy and safety of strontium ranelate versus placebo in the treatment of knee OA (www.controlled-trials. com; number ISRCTN41323372 [32,33]. Fifty-four subjects including 6 men (mean age=66.6 ± 11.7 years) and 48 women (mean age=62.3 ± 7.9 years) participated in a CT substudy. Primary inclusion criteria were the presence of medial femorotibial knee OA (Kellgren Lawrence grade 2 or 3) and knee pain experienced on most days in the month prior to the CT investigation with a joint space width between 2.5 mm and 5 mm on baseline radiographs [32]. Exclusion criteria included predominant lateral compartment OA, secondary knee OA, clinically significant hip OA, previous hip prostheses (<1year), recent intra-articular injections (<3 months) and clinically significant deformities of the lower limbs (varus or valgus). Ethical approval was obtained from the Comité de protection des personnes (CPP) d'Ile de France (Hôpital Saint Antoine, Paris APHP, France). All measurements and evaluations were performed on anonymized data sets.

CT scans were obtained from three different (Volume Zoom, Sensation 4, Sensation 64) Siemens scanners (Siemens Healthcare GmbH, Erlangen, Germany) operated in a supine position with the knee in extension at three different imaging locations (Russia, Belgium and France). The knee CT scan protocol was identical (120 kV, 170 mAs, collimation 4 × 0.5 mm, 1 s rotation, U40u kernel, 0.5 mm reconstructed slice thickness and 0.3 mm reconstruction increment). A field of view of 130 mm (512 × 512) with 434 slices was used for segmentation and analysis. The voxel size is 0.25 × 0.25 × 0.30 mm. CTDIw ranged from 7.2 to 8.46 mGy [34]. CT scans were performed twice: once at inclusion and again at 36 months.

Computed tomography scan analyses

All CT were reoriented using Image J. In the axial plane, the knee was aligned to bring the bicondylar line parallel to the horizontal line; in the coronal plane, lateral and medial plateaus were aligned horizontally. All images were converted to Analyse format.

Semi-automatic segmentation

For joint space segmentation, the open source ITK-SNAP 3.8.0 (Penn Image Computing and Science laboratory (http://itksnap.org), a medical imaging software was used. It was developed for analyzing the volumes of anatomical regions of interest, originally for brain MRI. The software offers interactive tools for image visualization, manual segmentation, semi-automatic segmentation based on geodesic active contour and region competition methods [35].

The CT datasets were imported into ITK-SNAP where sagittal, coronal, and axial reformations were displayed. A rectangular region of interest was manually defined as follows: in the coronal plane, vertical lines were positioned at the plumb line of the inner and outer edges of the medial and lateral condyles. In the sagittal plane, the anterior/posterior lines were positioned at the intersection between the tangent to the anterior/posterior border of the condyles and a horizontal line passing through the tibial epiphyseal border. The same processes were performed for the lateral compartment.

The segmentation process per se included a threshold to highlight the cortical bone without reducing the joint space. A bubble was then placed in the joint space and expanded using a region-growing method. The expansion was constrained by the cortical bone and by the limit of the volume of interest. If corrections were needed, a paintbrush tool could be used to adjust undesirable pixels. Minimum and maximum joint space were measured. Mean thickness and standard deviation of thickness were based on the 3D sphere method [36]. This process utilized Bone j after applying a median filter (pixel=1) [37]. The process is illustrated in Figure 1S in the Supplement.

Segmentation and 3D mapping for the medial and lateral compartments in the entire data set at month zero (M00) and at month 36 (M36) were performed once by a rheumatologist (CC) with 20 years of expertise in quantitative image analysis for musculoskeletal diseases. To assess the intra-observer and inter-observer reproducibility, the semi-automatic segmentation was repeated in a subgroup (n = 23) by the expert (CC) and performed once by two unskilled observers after one hour training (MG and JC). The entire process per compartment took between 15 min to 30 min. Deep learning segmentation

The database was divided into two groups: the first one from Russia for training (n = 65) and the second from France and Belgium for validation (n = 37). Following preprocessing, we trained the nnU-Net in its 3D full-resolution configuration [38]. The network was trained for 1000 epochs with a batch size of two, utilizing a learning rate that started at 0.010 and decreased by 0.0001 at each step. We employed the optimizer and loss function of nnU-Net, which utilizes the Stochastic Gradient Descent optimizer and a combination of Dice loss and cross-entropy loss. Segmentation quality analysis

The Dice similarity coefficient and maximum Hausdorff distance were used to evaluate segmentation results of the joint space (n = 23) from the M00 dataset analyzed by the expert, and the two unskilled observers. Dice and Hausdorff values were also calculated (n = 19) from the validation dataset M00 analyzed by the expert and nnU-net. This coefficient measures the relative overlap between segmentations provided by different users on the same volume. The Dice values range from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates complete overlap, 0–1, indicates partial overlap, and 0, no overlap. A Dice value > 0.7 is considered good [39]. The maximum Hausdorff distance was also calculated to assess boundary-based loss, and indicated the largest segmentation error [40].

Reliability

The reliability of parameters measured from joint space segmentations was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(1,1)) by Shrout and Fleiss [41], root mean square standard deviation (RMSSD; mm³ for volume measurements and mm for distance measurements), and root mean square coefficient of variation (RMSCV) [42]. Intra-observer reliability was calculated from the expert’s first and second analyses (Exp1 vs Exp2). Inter-observer reliability was calculated by comparing the expert with the two unskilled observers (Exp1 vs Obs1, Exp1 vs Obs2) and nnU-Net (Exp1 vs nnU-Net).

From the semi-automatic results from the expert, a T test was used to compare measurements at M00 and M36; in case of non-normality, a Wilcoxon rank test was applied. Statistical calculations were conducted using NCSS 2004 (Kaysville, UT). A Mann Whitney test was used to test the influence of sex on values. The effect size was calculated using Cohen’s d, where values of 0.2, 0.5, ≥ 0.8 correspond to small, medium and large effects.

Bland and Altman plots of the entire dataset for both medial and lateral compartments (M00, n = 19; M36, n = 18) were performed by comparing semi-automatic segmentations performed by the expert with those from the validation dataset of nnU-net (n = 37).

Discussion

Using a semi-automatic procedure, the most interesting parameter is the mean thickness of the joint space, measured with the sphere method, which showed good reliability, although it requires a high level of expertise. Measurements of mean thickness, as well as the standard deviation of thickness and the maximum thickness showed significant differences between M00 and M36. Conversely, the minimum joint space, which is traditionally observed on radiographs, appears to be the least relevant parameter with no significant differences and poor reliability. We also demonstrated that an automatic procedure on 3D full resolution UNet using the nnU-Net pipeline performs nearly as well as semi-automatic segmentation of the joint space performed by an expert. The nnU-Net segmentation could potentially replace semi-automatic segmentation made by an expert.

This process can also identify different progression profiles between M00 and M36 in patients with medial compartment OA (Kellgren Lawrence grade 2 or 3) with no predominant lateral compartment OA. Of the participants, 36 of 49 (73%) showed no change in the medial compartment.

Mean medial compartment thickness was 4.9 mm, similar to the 5.1 mm measured in non-weight-bearing CT scans in a population consisting of 65% participants with knee osteoarthritis, 15% with avascular necrosis and 13% with anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction [21]. Mean lateral compartment thickness was 5.5 mm, slightly different than those of non-weight-bearing CT scans (6.8 mm) [21]. Such differences could be explained by differences in positioning related to varying degrees of rotation in device positioning, but also joint effusion and antalgic flexion contracture. CT scans are usually performed in the supine position with the knee fully extended [21].

For the semi-automatic segmentation, the software used was the open source ITK-SNAP, in which manual refinement of the contour layer by layer is possible. This process has limitations. When the cortical bone is thin, region growing may extend beyond the cortical bone into the trabecular bone, requiring manual removal of additional pixels, which can be time-consuming.

In previous studies, the segmentation was fully automated [33] or required a manual step [[20], [21], [22]]. For example, the mineralized cortical bone surface of the medial and lateral tibial plateaus must be manually segmented [21] and the tibial and femoral triangulated meshes obtained through CT segmentation can be corrected manually by the operator [20]. Statistical shape modeling of bone contours refined by a convolutional neural network were used to secondly measured the JSW [22].

We used the 3D direct sphere method to measure the joint space, a robust method for assessing trabecular thickness at high resolution [44]. The largest spheres, centered on the medial axis and measured voxel by voxel, provide accurate, orientation-independent geometric thickness measurements including mean, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation. Typically, measurements are obtained as the minimum Euclidean distance between points on the femoral and tibial cortical bone surfaces, [21] along tibia-normal vectors on the medial condyle, [20] or using an iterative closest-point algorithm applied to the surface meshes of the central region [22].

The mean RMSCVs were below 3% for Exp1 vs Exp2 for mean thickness, and ranged from 4.7% to 7.7% for the standard deviation of thickness, and joint space maximum with no major differences between medial and lateral compartments. As noted in a previous study, there were negligible differences in measurements between the medial and lateral compartment reliability [19]. The minimum joint space RMSCV was much higher than other parameters, affecting the reliability of the maximum-to-minimum ratio. The minimum joint space is probably more relevant in weight-bearing positions. However, a previous study comparing standing cone beam CT and non-weight-bearing conventional CT measurements found no significant differences in minimum joint space, except in case of genu varum and genu valgum [21] which were excluded from this study.

For most parameters, reliability was about 2-fold higher for Exp1 vs Exp2 than for Exp1 vs Obs1 and Exp1 vs Obs2. The minimum to maximum ratio was the exception, however, with reliability only about 50% higher. Similar tendencies were found for the RMSSD results, except for the standard deviation in Exp1 vs Obs1 and Exp1 vs Obs2 which were lower than Exp1 vs Exp2, likely due to less detailed segmentation requiring fewer corrections.

In the present study, all ICCs were greater than 0.70 when comparing Exp1 vs Exp2, Exp1 vs Obs1, and Exp1 vs Obs2, which is considered good [41]. The ICC is an acceptable summary measure for agreement between continuous data, but unlike RMSCV, it does not provide information about data variability relative to the magnitude of measurements [45]. Additionally, RMSSD results are presented in the unit of measurement, making them more relevant for practical use and easy to understand for practitioners.

The primary source of variation between observers is the positioning of the volume of interest, which can be difficult to define without an in-depth understanding of knee anatomy. Other sources of variation include the threshold used to differentiate the joint space from the cortical bone, and the lack of refinement in the contour, especially when the cortical bone is thin. It is worth noting that the unskilled observers had no background in anatomy, and only one hour for training. The main advantage of CT is good contrast between bone and soft tissue. The joint space thickness is measured between the superior and inferior borders where the contrast between cortical bone and joint space is high, which may explain the high reliability of mean thickness. Indeed, contour error illustrated by high maximal Hausdorff values are encountered at the medial-lateral and antero-posterior borders.

Performing 3D segmentation from CT data is time consuming. Over the past decade, deep learning methods for 3D medical image segmentation have seen extensive development. Therefore, development of 3D biomarkers requires automated segmentation tools that allow practitioners to control and adjust the segmentation, expediting data analysis. Based on the segmentation performed by the expert (CC), we trained a 3D full-resolution UNet using the nnU-Net pipeline. We chose nnU-Net due to the small amount of available data, only 54 patients and 102 CT scans. We opted to train a convolutional neural network which requires less data than a Vision Transformer, as the latter requires a larger data set for comparable performance. To evaluate all volumes using the neural network, we automatically created 5 folds via nnU-Net, with 80% allocated for training and 20% for validation. Consequently, each data point is present in one of the folds as validation data, and the validation score obtained from this process can be retained to assess the network. To avoid overestimating clinical performance, we tested performance with data from France and Belgium which can be considered as external datasets compared to the Russian database. The obtained Dice coefficients were 0.85 for both medial and lateral compartments. These results are perfectly coherent with previous ones obtained on a subsample of scans segmented by different observers and the deep learning algorithm. Geographic validation is interesting because it takes into account the radiologic imaging variation across centers with slightly different technical parameters [46]. The preprocessing of the nnU-Net automatically adapts to image properties and identifies the optimal parameter configuration for the U-Net, which has consistently outperformed other U-Net architectures for medical image segmentation [38].

One of the main drawbacks of 2D knee joint assessment from radiographs is poor reliability due to variations in knee positioning. The smallest detectable difference, calculated as 1.96 times the standard deviation of the two readings using the KIDA software, was 2.02 mm for the lateral compartment and 0.76 mm for the medial compartment [10]. The mean coefficient of variation was high, at 13.4% and 14% for the mean joint space width of the medial and lateral compartments, respectively, and 45.5% for the minimum joint space. The main explanation is that improper beam angles may require repeating radiographs at different beam angles or with fluoroscopic assistance. Even with repeated radiation exposures and beam angle calibration, incorrect angulation or transverse rotation negatively affects reliability due to the variability in patients and technologists [11]. Since disease progression is generally slow and variable within and between subjects, using joint space width as an endpoint requires long-term treatment periods (over one year) and the inclusion of large patient populations [46]. In this study, the 3D CT measurements demonstrated better reliability than the 2D X-ray measurements. However, the variations in joint space provided by CT scans in supine position must be interpreted in the context of clinical symptoms and disease progression.

Joint space segmentation was semi-automatic and manually corrected by the operator to align as closely as possible with the contour. In Brett’s study, bone shapes of the tibia and femur were produced automatically with an error of 0.12 mm compared to manual segmentation. Similarly, we found a RMSSD of about 0.15 mm between the two expert analyses. Unlike Brett’s study, which measured a central zone, we measured the joint space from the periphery to the tibial spines to capture as much as data as possible, accounting for the non-uniformity of the space across the joint surface [22].

Like radiographs, CT provides a clear view of the bone-to-bone distance with good contrast, avoiding superimposition of other knee structures. One advantage of CT is the ability to provide a submillimetric 3D quasi-isotropic image, a good visualization of cortical bone surface which allows quantitative analysis [33]. In this study, the plane resolution was 0.25 mm with a slice thickness of about 0.3 mm allowing undistorted evaluation of 3D geometry [4].

For distance measurements, CT slightly outperforms MRI regarding intraobserver reliability [47]. Another advantage of CT is the possibility to geometrically calibrate devices [48] in multi center studies, which is difficult with MRI due to the complexity of acquisition settings and potential inhomogeneities in the magnetic field, leading to geometric distortion [22]. On the other hand, CT measurements, unlike MRI, can include joint fluid and meniscal components which may also result in variations.

Unlike radiography, CT and MRI images are taken in a non-weight-bearing position, so cartilage deformation is not an issue [10]. It is now possible to obtain 3D images with isotropic voxels, especially using standing CT scans, within about one minute, with costs and radiation dose less than conventional CT, although this technology is not widely available [49]. The joint space measurements developed here may perhaps be applied on standing CT scans. The weight bearing position will probably also limit the variability of joint space measurements in the lateral compartment, which could be due to rotation during acquisition in a supine position.

To test the precision of the entire chain of measurements, two image acquisitions must be performed at different times. However, for ethical reasons repositioning was not tested in the present study but only image analysis and segmentation. We do, however, have indirect information from two acquisitions taken 36 months apart. While the reliability of the medial and lateral compartments was comparable, most parameters showed significant variations in the medial compartment but not in the lateral compartment. Moreover, this finding is consistent with a cohort of patients selected for dominant medial compartment OA with little or no impairment in the lateral compartment. In this study, we had an imbalance between men (n = 6) and women (n = 48) which could affect interpretation of results between M00 and M36.

This study presents a tool with sufficient reliability to track joint space variations induced by OA. It can be useful in patients with moderate knee OA (Kellgren Lawrence grades 2–3), a disease stage where pharmacological intervention may be more effective. It is crucial to measure various continuous variables objectively and quantitatively to improve disease characterization. CT scans can provide new insights into the pathophysiology of OA and aid in the development of new treatment strategies and can also be potentially useful to identify rapid progressors, who could be prioritized for clinical group trial recruitment and cohort studies. nnU-Net is a good candidate for automating the process on CT scans but needs an external testing group for clinical validation.
