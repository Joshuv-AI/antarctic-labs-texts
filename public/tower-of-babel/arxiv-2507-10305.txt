A Grounded Theory on the Teacher and Student Roles in Pair Programming
Linus Ververs, Trang Linh Lam, Janina Berger, Lutz Prechelt

Abstract
Context: Pair programming is an established (agile) practice and is practiced throughout the industry. Objective: Understand under what circumstances knowledge transfer can harm a pair programming session. Method: Grounded Theory Methodology based on 17 recorded pair programming sessions with 18 developers from 5 German software companies accompanied, by 6 interviews with different developers from 4 other German companies. Results: We define the student and teacher roles to help developers deal with a one-sided knowledge gap. We describe pitfalls to avoid and develop a grounded theory centered around the Power Gap in pair programming. Conclusions: Knowledge transfer can be harmful when developers don’t pay attention to their partners needs and desires. If developers don’t pay attention to the Power Gap and keep it in check, Defensive Behavior may arise that leads to a vicious cycle impacting the knowledge transfer, the Togetherness and the code quality in a negative way.

“In pair programming, two programmers jointly produce one artifact (design, algorithm, code)”

I-A

Research Question

As we follow the Grounded Theory Methodology guidelines by Strauss & Corbin, we didn’t start with a precise research question but rather a broad objective
[
13
]
: Try to understand pair programming as it happens in the industry better, identify positive or negative phenomena that influence the pair programming session, describe and analyze them to give scientifically backed-up advice to practitioners on how to better do pair programming.
While doing so, we noticed one phenomena that sparked our interest because it was so counter-intuitive: in some cases, knowledge transfer seemed to be harmful to the pair programming process. This is when our concrete research questions arose that we investigated further using grounded theory methodology and that culminated in the present article:

RQ1

Under which circumstances can knowledge transfer harm a pair programming session? What are harmful behavioral patterns?

RQ2

What kinds of effects does this particular knowledge transfer behavior have on the pair programming session and on the developers?

RQ3

What strategies can developers incorporate to avoid this behavior or to mitigate its harmful effects?

I-B

Research Contributions

Our work makes two contributions:

First, based on our analysis we define the Teacher and Student roles in pair programming. Similar to design patterns, these roles serve as an abstraction providing solutions for recurring pair programming contexts. By defining a conceptual framework that is both generalizable and practically grounded, our role definitions offer a useful tool for practitioners to improve pair programming sessions where one partner possesses a lot more crucial knowledge than her partner. As we only define two roles here, this can only be seen as a first step towards a comprehensive catalog of different roles for different contexts and goals in pair programming.

Second, we identify the
Power Gap
as a risk factor which is fueled by
Hierarchical Behavior
. A big
Power Gap
may lead to
Defensive Behavior
that hinders knowledge transfer. We explain how and why it develops, how partners can manage it, and by doing so protect their pair dynamic.

II
Related Work

II-A

Qualitative Research on Pair Programming in the Industry

Zieris, as part of his qualitative analysis of knowledge transfer in pair programming coined the concepts of
Togetherness
and
Fluency
[
3
]
. He applied grounded theory methodology to recorded pair programming sessions from the industry (as we do here). In the later stages of our own research we opted to apply some of his concepts as part of our own code system (see Section
III
).

In two earlier publication with Prechelt on knowledge transfer in pair programming
[
14
,
15
]
they find among other things that “there is usually no pair member who is more knowledgeable in all relevant areas”
[
15
]
.

Zieris and Prechelt
[
16
]
worked with the same data set as we do here (which is described in
[
17
]
). In their publication “Two Elements of Pair Programming Skill” they discuss one example from session PA3 that we also discuss (Example
IV-C1
). They use it to illustrate the anti-pattern “Drowning the Partner” that is characterized by “too many explanations that (a) go far beyond the task and are hence not expedient and (b) also threaten the pair’s
Togetherness
”.

Chong and Hurlbutt conducted an ethnographic observation study in two teams where they observed different pair programming sessions over the course of multiple weeks
[
18
]
. They also recorded and transcribed the pair’s dialog where possible. They found that “[a]side from the task of typing, we found no consistent division of labor between the ‘driver’ and the ‘navigator’”. In addition, they observed that “[t]he gaps in expertise between the programmers on Team B clearly influenced pair programming interactions on the team. On Team B, the member of the pair with greater expertise drove the bulk of the programming discussions”.

Salinger et al. note that “[pair programming] participants fulfill more different, conceptually separate functions (the Facets) than only the commonly described and hopelessly overloaded driver and observer (or navigator) roles”
[
19
]
. They define a meta-model for defining roles in pair programming. A role is defined by facets, which are observable actions taken by one developer during a session. A role can be defined by multiple facets, but “[t]here is no
fixed rule as to which or how many facets must be observed to conclude the respective pair member has assumed the Role”
[
19
]
. A role is also associated with a mission (the reason why a developer takes on a role). This mission isn’t observable and becomes only clear when talking to developers. We built upon their framework for role definition in this article.

Begel and Nagappan surveyed 487 developers at Microsoft
[
20
]
in 2005. They focused on benefits and problems of pair programming. “Personality clash”, “disagreements”, and “skill differences” are part of the top 5 problems given by participants. As attributes of good pair programming teams the participants listed among others: “good communications”, “compatible personalities”, and “no ego”. Participants wrote about good programming teams: “personality types which aren’t domineering” and “[a] team should exhibit a ‘willingness to co-excel, as opposed to compete directly with their partner’”.

Salinger and Prechelt created the “Base Layer” which is a collection of codes that serve as “a foundation for qualitative research into pair programming […] that aims at explaining the pair programming process”
[
21
]
. They do so by analyzing recorded industrial pair programming sessions and focusing on the dialog of a pair.

II-B

Other Relevant Research on Pair Programming

Bowman et al. examined 587 students working in different pair constellations
[
22
]
. They found that “students who worked with a more experienced partner actually had poorer outcomes, including lower effort exerted on the assignment, perceptions that their partner gave more effort than they did, less time in the driving role (i.e., typing out the assignment), lower understanding of concepts from lab, and less interest in computer science overall”.
This serves as evidence for two things we will discuss here: First, taking up the
Teacher
role is difficult and can even be dysfunctional if not done properly. Second, a
One-sided Knowledge Gap
can lead to a
Power Gap
(see Section
IV-E
).

Domino et al. studied 14 pairs of students in a quasi-experimental setting, where the students were asked to work on different tasks over the course of one 4-hour session
[
23
]
. They measured and ranked the performance, cognitive ability, amount of conflict and faithfulness to the method (equal collaboration vs dominating) of the pairs. Because of the low number of participants, the authors weren’t able to find any statistically significant relationships. But when comparing, based on performance, the high-ranking pair (G) with a low-ranking pair (C), they note:
“Pair C, however, had low faithfulness to the collaborative method with one subject progressively dominating the other partner throughout the experiment. This work pattern, combined with a high conflict avoidance score of the dominated partner led to high task conflict, resolved by escalated withdrawal of this subject. As a result the performance outcomes suffered. Additionally, the participant with the high avoidance score reported low satisfaction ratings for the pair programming exercises”.

In their meta-analysis of 18 experiments comparing pair programming to solo programming, Hannay et al. come to the conclusion that pair programming leads to a significant but small positive effect on quality, a significant medium positive effect on duration, while impacting effort negatively
[
8
]
. As the individual experiments were conducted in a controlled laboratory environment, the tasks were small, and most of the experiments were conducted with students not with practitioners. Aspects that play a role in pair programming happening in the industry like transfer of software specific knowledge on a preexisting code base and the impact of knowledge transfer not on the session but on the project level aren’t studied which limits practical implications.

Vanhanen and Lassenius studied five student teams, each consisting of four developers, to compare pair programming as a software development practice applied at the team level to solo programming
[
7
]
.
Three teams practiced pair programming exclusively, while two teams followed solo programming with only minimal collaboration.
Initially, the pair programming teams were less productive, mainly due to longer onboarding times (i.e., getting familiar with the other developers).
However, later in the project, they outperformed the solo programming teams in terms of productivity.
The pair programming teams also produced fewer defects before classifying their code as ready.
Yet, they seemed to perform less rigorous testing afterwards, which ultimately resulted in more defects in the final product.
Pair programming further led to greater knowledge transfer and a slight increase in overall developer satisfaction.

II-C

Research on Pair Work in Other Fields

Young and Perrewe studied the perception of behaviors in mentor-protégé relationships of PhD students and professors in Management.
[
24
]
They surveyed 323 people and found that “mentors value career-related behaviors exhibited by proteges over social behaviors, whereas protégés place more emphasis on social behaviors exhibited by mentors”.

Zeijen et al. surveyed police officers working in pairs (dyads) two times during one workday
[
25
]
by applying experience sampling methodology. They measured their work engagement, social support, autonomously motivated support, psychological need fulfilment, and emotional demands. They conclude that “providing support based on an autonomous motivation relates positively with the provider’s work engagement through satisfying the provider’s needs”. In addition, they found out “that it matters for the support provider’s need satisfaction and work engagement […] whether the receiver is concerned with proving him/herself”.

Methods and Data

We use Grounded Theory Methodology mostly based on Strauss & Corbin’s interpretation
[
13
]
, but also incorporate Charmaz’s remarks
[
26
,
27
]
especially her stance on the biases of the researchers on how they will always influence the emerging theory:

“Researchers are part of what they study, not separate from it.”
[
26
]

For our research, we rely on a preexisting data set of approx. 100 hours of recorded pair programming sessions from 13 companies which were recorded between 2006 and 2018
[
17
]
. 57 developers participated. They worked on the company’s software and
real
tasks. The videos we analyzed consisted of a screen recording of the IDE and video with audio of the developers.

Our qualitative research process, which we describe here, started when we noticed how in one of the recorded sessions the pair dynamic seemed to be off and how it was further negatively influenced by knowledge transfer. As knowledge transfer is normally viewed as a positive outcome from pair programming we decided to investigate this further. The result of this investigation is the present article.

Open Coding & Code Development

Here, we aim to create transparency - and thus credibility - by explaining how the coding process evolved and which codes emerged when.

III-C
1
First Generation of Codes: Capturing the problematic behavior

When we started looking at PA3, we quickly realized that something was off.
We therefore started our Open Coding with the goal to describe and explain what we felt was off - and why.
We noticed that most of the dysfunctional behavior of the pair was centered around knowledge transfer from P1 to P3.
This is when we decided, after we had defined our first codes to build upon previous qualitative research on pair programming and codes defined there.
We used Salinger & Prechelt’s Base Layer
[
21
]
to structure the dialog of the pairs and to highlight knowledge transfer activities.
See an excerpt of the codes defined in the Base Layer and applied for our analysis
2
2

2

Please refer to
[
21
]
for the code definitions.
:

•

{ask/explain/agree/challenge/disagree}_knowl-edge: for declarative knowledge transfer activities

•

{explain/ask}_standard of knowledge: for ex-changes regarding the standard of knowledge of the partners

•

{propose/agree/challenge/disagree/amend}_hy-pothesis: to discuss hypotheses or conjectures

•

{explain/agree/challenge/disagree/amend}_find-ing: to verbalize new insights

•

{ask/propose/agree/challenge/disagree/amend/ decide}_{design/strategy/step}: for decision making regarding the design of the product (design) or the process (strategy/step).

We did this only as a first step towards our own codes and goals - mainly to avoid pitfalls when differentiating knowledge transfer activities from decision making, and still applied Constant Comparison to validate if these codes really fit our purpose.

To investigate the pair dynamics and for the same reasons as described above we also opted to apply Zieris’ codes on process fluency, where utterances are classified into five different base activities that model their “conversational role” (only the first 4)
[
3
]
:

1.

initial activity: “Initiatives such as proposals, questions, or explanations; may be expecting a reaction or non-expecting”

2.

pair-referential activity: “A reaction to a[n] initial activity that both partners are involved in; may be prompt or delayed, evaluative or non-evaluative, appropriate or misled”

3.

self-referential activity: “Referring to a[n] initial activity in which the partner is not yet or not anymore involved”

4.

corrective activity: “Attempting to clear up a misunderstanding”

5.

conversational defect: “A missed connection in the dialog, e.g., not referring to an expecting initial activity; can be a non-action”

Franz Zieris uses these codes to draw conclusions about the process fluency, i.e., to identify focus phases and breakdowns which are the two poles of the process fluency spectrum.
This can be used to infer the Togetherness of the pair, which is Zieris’ key concept and fundamental for a successful pair programming session:

“[Togetherness] is the degree to which the pair members are able to fully understand each other’s activities, including all intentions and meanings associated by the respective speaker or actor.”
[
3
]

Here, we realized that Zieris’ Conversational Defect highlighted a lot of the instances where something went wrong during the session and decided to differentiate different kinds of Conversational Defects.

Broadly speaking, Zieris’ Conversational Defects highlight the parts in the session where something went wrong regarding the pair’s Togetherness.
That doesn’t mean that these parts are problematic per se but most of the problematic ones - in our case - were among them.
That’s why we decided to further differentiate the Conversational Defects by analyzing the accountability:

Situational Conversational Defect
(SCD):
These are Conversational Defects that are caused by the evolving situation, e.g., a misunderstanding or when one partner is missing crucial knowledge.
No one is directly responsible (although one could argue that maybe a good opportunity for knowledge transfer was previously missed).
These happen in most pair programming sessions and aren’t problematic on their own.
(If they reoccur, they are a sign that the Togetherness is low.)

Intentional Conversational Defect
(ICD):
These are Conversational Defects that are directly caused by one partner’s unwillingness to cooperate or engage.
The root cause for this behavior likely lies elsewhere in the session but only looking at this moment there is nothing hindering the partner from reacting in a way that wouldn’t be a Conversational Defect.
We found a lot of these in our main session PA3.
Among them are cases where the partner is ignored completely (i.e. a non-reaction), the partner is ignored content-wise (i.e. a reaction that doesn’t take the partner’s utterance into account) or the partner is interrupted with something unrelated to the partner’s utterance.

Ambiguous Conversational Defect
(ACD):
In most cases, it isn’t possible to reconstruct everything necessary to determine whether a Conversational Defect is Situational or Intentional. ACD serves as a backup code for those cases.

III-C
2
Second Generation of Codes: Defining the Teacher and Student Role

When looking at the context of the ICDs, which were in their majority caused by one partner (P3),
we noticed again - now highlighted by the corresponding Base Layer codes - that knowledge transfer from P1 to P3 seemed to be the cause for this counterproductive behavior.
For us, it is clear that P1 tried to act as a
Teacher
in PA3,
which is further supported by the conversation between P1 and P3 after PA3 (see Section
III-A1
).
To better analyze his behavior and what went wrong,
we defined the
Teacher
role according to the framework for role definition in pair programming by Salinger et al.
[
19
]
(see Section
IV-A
).

We once again decided to employ codes from previous work to aid us in defining the facets of the
Teacher
role.
Zieris, who studied knowledge transfer in pair programming extensively, distinguishes four knowledge transfer modes
[
3
]
:

Push
,
Pull
,
Pioneering Production
, and
Co-Production
.
In this work, we only reuse the first two, which are also mentioned in an earlier publication by Zieris and Prechelt
[
15
]
.

•

Push
: “Propellor pursues an internal Knowledge Want by eliciting explanations from partner who understands Topic and delivers Target Content.”
[
3
]

•

Pull
: “Propellor pursues an external Knowledge Want by providing Explanations to her partner without being requested to do so.”
[
3
]

In addition, we also define the
Student
role to better understand P3’s behavior and to highlight where and why their dynamic was off (see Section
IV-B
).

III-C
3
Third Generation of Codes: Defining what’s invisible: The Power Gap

When we analyzed the knowledge transfer episodes through the lens of the
Teacher
role,
we noticed multiple things.

First, there was a role discrepancy between P1 and P3 in PA3,
as P1 acted — or tried to act — as a
Teacher
,
but P3 wasn’t adopting the corresponding
Student
role.
Second, we noticed that P1 tried to take the lead in the session and indirectly communicated this multiple times,
to which P3 didn’t react positively.
We therefore found that the growing
Power Gap
between the partners was the root cause for a suboptimal pair programming session.
After identifying the
Power Gap
as our core category,
we restructured our codes — mainly by creating two new concepts adjacent to the
Power Gap
:

Defensive Behavior
and
Hierarchical Behavior
(see Sections
IV-D3
and
IV-D4
).

This led us to the Grounded Theory we present here (see Section
IV-E
).
Because the
Power Gap
is by definition not visible through pure observation,
we decided — as part of Theoretical Sampling — to gather different kinds of data through semi-structured interviews with developers.
We talked to six developers (I1-I6) from four different companies.

We found that our new data was in line with our Grounded Theory,
except for one instance where the interview data led to the development of a new facet for the
Teacher
role.
We added quotes from the interviews, especially in those cases where our observational data is less clear.

IV
Results

IV-A

Defining the Teacher Role

Keep in mind that a role definition always comes with a normative component,
as it categorizes actions during a pair programming session that could also be categorized in a totally different way.

We define the
Teacher
role based on six facets in the following Sections
IV-A1
–
IV-A6
.

IV-A
1
Facet: Prioritizing Knowledge Transfer

This is the most important facet.
If it’s not visible, we don’t assume one has taken on the
Teacher
role.
But as you may notice, this facet is named after an intention.
So, how do you notice and annotate this facet in a given pair programming session without looking into the developers’ heads or speculating on their intentions?

We do so by differentiating knowledge transfer episodes initiated by the partner in possession of the knowledge (defined as
Push
by Zieris
[
3
]
) based on their purpose in the pair programming session.
This is done by answering the binary question:
Does the transferred knowledge directly impact the artifact the developers are working on?
If so, we call it
Mandatory Knowledge Transfer
.

Typically, this is transferred as part of a decision-making process.
See Examples
IV-A4
and
IV-D4
.
All other cases we call
Extended Knowledge Transfer
.
We further differentiate this into:

•

Supplementary Explanation
, where knowledge is transferred that is related to the content of the session,

•

Extra Explanation
, where the knowledge isn’t directly related to the content, and

•

Follow-up Explanation
, where knowledge is transferred that explains or gives more context to a past decision and could classify as
Mandatory Knowledge Transfer
if it had happened earlier in the session.

See Examples
IV-A1
and
IV-A1
.

Example 1: PA4 - Follow-up and Supplementary Explanation

P3 and P1 decide to rename an attribute.
P3 starts to do so manually.
He consequently also changes the get- and set-methods in the corresponding class.
This exchange happens after he is done.

P1:

“The alternative would have been (!!...!!)
3
3

3

(!!…!!) symbolizes an interruption by the partner.
”

<follow-up_expl>

P3:

“Refactor.”

P1:

“Exactly. To rename the member. Then the IDE would have done everything for you.”

<follow-up_expl>

P3:

“Does it even change the get- and set-methods?”

P1:

“Yes, it even goes so far that if you rename the class, it renames the name of the class and possibly even the namespaces to which it belongs.”

<suppl_expl>

P3:

“Nice.”

<acknowledge_helpful>

P1’s first
Push
is labeled as
Follow-up Explanation
because it happens after P3 is done.
P1’s second remark is a
Supplementary Explanation
.
Note that although P1 is initially answering P3’s question (
Pull
),
he continues to provide additional information, although a simple “yes” would have been sufficient.
It’s a
Pull
followed by a
Push
.

Example 2: PA3 - Extra Explanation

P3 renames a test class using the IDE inefficiently.

P1:

“If you use Tab, it completes it for you without you having to remove the test. So Tab always means ‘complete this’ and replaces what is underneath it”

<extra_expl>

P3:

“Ah, cool.”

<acknowledge_helpful>

P1’s remark isn’t concerned with the product they are working on, but it’s a general piece of advice on how to better use the IDE. That’s why it’s coded as
Extra Explanation
.

IV-A
2
Facet: Eliciting Knowledge Needs

Being a (good)
Teacher
requires knowledge of the standard of knowledge of your partner.
The Base Layer
[
21
]
provides the fitting annotation
ask_standard of knowledge
for direct questions about the partner’s level of knowledge.
But besides this direct approach, there are other observable exchanges that fall under this facet,
as they show that the partner pays attention to the standard of knowledge of their counterpart.

We annotate
Recognize Unnoticed Knowledge Gap
when one partner reacts to their counterpart doing something wrong or inefficient by providing and explaining a better alternative.
Usually, this is coded with
propose_design
followed by
explain_knowledge
, according to the Base Layer
[
21
]
.
Consider again Example
IV-A1
, where P1’s first remark also qualifies as
Recognize Unnoticed Knowledge Gap
.

Besides
Recognize Unnoticed Knowledge Gap
, we also use the concept of
Recognize Semi-Aware Knowledge Gap
.
Here, the partner expresses their uncertainty implicitly — by hesitant behavior, prolonged pauses, interrupting themselves, thinking aloud, or postponing an encountered problem.

IV-A
6
Facet: Creating Learning Opportunities

Similar to
Prioritizing Knowledge Transfer
(see Section
IV-A1
),
the overarching theme of this facet is that the teacher puts her partner’s learning
above the efficiency of the session (in the sense of achieving the best possible result
as quickly as possible).
In contrast to
Prioritizing Knowledge Transfer
, where this manifests in transferring knowledge
that isn’t mandatory, here this facet encapsulates all situations where one partner creates a situation
that leads her partner to gaining first-hand experience by sacrificing efficiency in the short term.
We only realized this facet exists after one developer mentioned this strategy proactively during our interviews:

“The
other thing is when someone starts doing something in one direction
and you realize it’s going to be a dead end.
You can - and I’ve done this a few times, sometimes it works very well -
you can just let them go and say: ‘Yes, go for it’.
Then he’ll see that it doesn’t work or he’ll know afterwards. […]
That’s a really good learning path.
If you have the time, that’s good.
And of course you have to take yourself back.”
(Q1:I2)

After realizing this could plausibly be another facet,
we looked in our data for such instances.
We found that
Letting mistakes happen
by
not intervening
,
as described by our interviewee, is the most extreme case we define as part of this facet.
It’s really hard to spot, as in most cases where one developer realizes he made a mistake,
we don’t know if his partner knew this in advance and did
not intervene
as part of a conscious decision.
See Example
IV-A4
.
Here, P1 presumably realizes early on that P3’s implementation of the for-loop is lacking
but doesn’t mention it until P3 is finished.
This is one of our stronger examples where we are pretty sure this is what happens.
Beyond these extreme cases, the unifying characteristic is to hand over a certain degree of control
over the decision-making process and, in doing so,
Create Learning Opportunities
.

These include instances where the partner who is more suitable for tackling the task
is consciously letting the other person drive, thus decreasing the speed in favor of her partner’s learning.

Throughout PA3, P3 is constantly in control of mouse and keyboard,
although P1 is the developer who could tackle the task more easily.
But relinquishing control over the IDE isn’t the only factor at play here,
because the observing partner could still be the one in control of the decision-making.

Letting mistakes happen
is one side of the spectrum, where the partner doesn’t get involved even if he sees a mistake.

Backseat-Driving
is at the other end.
Here, every bit of thinking is done by the observing party,
and the driver is merely following exact orders.
See Example
IV-A6
.

IV-B

Defining the Student Role

Corresponding to the
Teacher
role, we now define the
Student
role. The Student and the Teacher roles complement each other.
This doesn’t mean that if one partner is acting according to the Teacher role, the other partner will be taking up the Student role automatically.
But on the other hand we don’t assume someone is acting as a Student without facets of the Teacher role being present in their partner.
We make this distinction because the Student facets we define here are much more common in our data and also apply to episodes where we argue the partners are taking up other roles.
So if only looking at the Student facets, one could argue that the role is almost always fulfilled by one of the partners.

IV-C

Here we will focus on all behavioral aspects that went wrong in session PA3, analyzed through the lens of the Teacher and Student roles we previously defined.

What do we mean by behavioral aspects? We aren’t interested in mistakes or misunderstandings that happen during pair programming - those are normal and expected (within a certain scope).
Here, we are interested in parts of the session where one’s partner’s unwillingness to cooperate is visible and harms the session, i.e., where

IV-C
3
Teacher: Ineffective or Wrong Explanation

Explaining complex things is difficult. An
Ineffective Explanation
can make it hard for the partner to follow along, which can lead to frustration and even bad decisions. Example
IV-C2
shows all different kinds of
Ineffective Explanations
.

First,
Unstructured Explanation
is annotated if an explanation lacks a clear structure that emphasizes the important aspects for the partner. In Example
IV-C2
, the main argument for assertSame is that it takes the data types into account. A good explanation would mention this directly; here it gets lost in the weeds.

Second, an
Overcomplicated Explanation
is an explanation that is riddled with unimportant supplementary information, which deemphasizes the main point. P1 does so by talking about the order of the items, which he even acknowledges as “not necessarily that important”.

Third, this mixes with a
Lack of Focus
, as P1 gets sidetracked by P3’s random injections (for example, regarding JSON).

Fourth, P1’s explanations show a
Lack of Responsiveness
, as he ignores P3’s (non-)reactions that clearly signal that P3 either isn’t paying attention or doesn’t understand the explanation. A good explanation would take that into account.

Taken together, all these aspects taken together lead to an extremely ineffective explanation that even results in P3’s wrong decision of using assertEquals.
The case can only get worse if the explanations given are (partly) false, while the partner providing these explanations is unaware of his inadequate knowledge and presents them as facts. We briefly see this in Example
IV-C1
, where P1 talks about 3600 being the conversion factor for hours to minutes or minutes to seconds, while the actual conversion factor is 60. See also Example
IV-C3
, which is the only negative example without an
Intentional Conversational Defect
. Here, the

IV-D

Power Gap & Defensive Behavior

As Example
IV-C3
illustrates well, there are other factors at play that strongly influence the pair programming process besides
Togetherness
. One of these we call the
Power Gap
.

IV-D
1
Defining the Power Gap though Power Dynamics

The
Power Gap
defines the power difference the partners have over the pair programming session. Like
Togetherness
, it isn’t directly observable, but its effects are. And like
Togetherness
, it can change over the course of a session. We therefore define the concepts
High Power Dynamic
and
Low Power Dynamic
.

Episodes during a pair programming session in which one partner is in a leading position regarding product and process decisions, communicates this directly or indirectly, may even use a
Patronizing Communication Style
(see
IV-C4
), and isn’t challenged by his or her partner, we annotate with
High Power Dynamic
.
On the other side of the spectrum, we annotate episodes with
Low Power Dynamic
, where the partners make joint decisions, treat each other as equals, and proposals are challenged in a respectful way.

Note that the gap between
Low Power Dynamic
and
High Power Dynamic
episodes is huge, and there is a large middle ground where we annotate neither. See Example
IV-D1
.

.

If multiple episodes are annotated with
High Power Dynamic
without any

Power Gap
, and vice versa for a small
Power Gap
.
Applying this definition, only the extreme manifestations of the
Power Gap
are visible, although we assume that during most pair programming sessions there is a mild
Power Gap
with harder-to-spot effects.
In this article, we only focus on the extreme cases.

IV-D
2
A Good Teacher keeps the Power Gap in check

As knowledge is the key enabling factor to make decisions, a
Knowledge Gap
between the partners is predestined to lead to a
Power Gap
. We call it a
One-sided Knowledge Gap
if one of the partners clearly has more knowledge that helps with solving the particular task they set out to work on than the other.
One of our interviewees described a pair programming situation he witnessed second hand where this was the case and led to a massive
Power Gap

“One person fundamentally did not attribute any competence to the other. […] it often manifested itself in the fact that if he didn’t have the answer himself, he didn’t ask his [partner] or give them time to think, but then stood up and shouted ‘Hey, do you have an idea how to do that?’. Which is probably basically normal behavior,[…] just not taking into account these two minutes of thinking time for the other person. If you skip it, then it’s just that you’re losing the other person even more.”
(Q6:I1)

The same interviewee who earlier described his own pair programming experience linked the
Power Gap
directly to the
Knowledge Gap

“And the other person doesn’t know the database technology that well yet, doesn’t know the procedure that well, doesn’t know the language that well.
There is a massive imbalance in terms of decision-making power.”
(Q7:I1)

In another interview, one interviewee described a situation we would annotate as

because only one partner was in control of the process due to the

“And I wanted to bring in a lot of ideas, but admittedly I wasn’t as good at coding as he was. And he always explained to me why he did things the way he did them and why he left certain things out completely. And more or less shot down any of my suggestions. […] That’s why I remembered [the session] quite positively, although I more or less probably kept him from working during the session because he had to explain a lot to me.”
(Q8:I6)

As Zieris & Prechelt already observed, a

For the above reasons, being a

By doing a

If the Teacher isn’t

On the other hand through a respectful and appreciative attitude the Teacher can decrease the

IV-D

We discussed P1’s off-putting behavior as

IV-D
4
The Power Gap leads to Defensive Behavior

As session PA3 demonstrates, a big
Power Gap
can destroy the benefits of pair programming because it leads to behavior by the subordinate P3 that sabotages the session.
We call this
Defensive Behavior
.

Defensive Behavior
encapsulates actions and reactions during a pair programming session that come from the subordinate partner in a session or parts of a session as a reaction to a
Power Gap
that is usually manifested through
Hierarchical Behavior
.
These actions and reactions serve the purpose to stand his or her ground against the partner without engaging in a direct conflict regarding the partner’s
Hierarchical Behavior
.
We distinguish three different kinds of
Defensive Behavior
.

First,
Minimize the Communication
with the partner.
Intentional Conversational Defects
and
Not Providing a Back Channel
are the most prominent and easy to spot characteristics that we already discussed. This behavior is backed up by our interviews as one interviewee puts it while talking about a situation that happened during her time at university:

“And he was someone who already had a bit of previous experience […]. And for me the things were rather new and we then sat down to do pair programming, he programmed the things and I actually watched and tried to think along and give suggestions as to how it could be done and actually every time I made a suggestion, so to speak, I got ‘yes, no, we’d better do it this way and that way is better’ and I tried to ask why […]. Then somehow I didn’t really get any explanations, to be honest I was a bit frustrated at times and then at some point I didn’t even ask much more because I thought it wouldn’t help anyway […]. And whenever I said ‘Yes, what are you doing there?’ Then he gave me a very brief outline, […] rather a bit dismissive, a bit annoyed.”
(Q11:I4)

Second,
Hiding Knowledge Gaps
which is more severe but harder to spot, as it leads to
Not Pulling Important Knowledge
. Let’s revisit Example
IV-C2
.
Isn’t it odd that although P1 is arguing multiple times for
assertSame
, that P3 decides to use assertEquals anyway? This is the direct consequence of P3’s
Defensive Behavior
.
P3 doesn’t know how to implement P1’s proposal regarding assertSame, and that’s the reason he chooses assertEquals.
This becomes clear when we look at Example

where P1 once again proposes to use

This behavior was also described in one of the interviews we held:
“That’s why I often ask people, ‘Do you know how this and that works, do you know the technology, this working method or anything else?’ And funnily enough, it’s the supposed seniors who can’t admit that they don’t know this. They then start to get stressed and feel tested. You have to be careful, especially if people don’t know me well enough yet […].”
(Q12:I2)

V
Discussion

V-A

Answering our Research Questions

RQ1

Under which circumstances can knowledge transfer harm a pair programming session? What are harmful behavioral patterns?

Explained by our theory (see Section
IV-E
), knowledge transfer that ignores the partner’s
Knowledge Wants and Needs
can be seen as
Hierarchical Behavior
. This gets worse if combined with a
Patronizing Communication Style
.

RQ2

What kinds of effects does this particular knowledge transfer behavior have on the pair programming session and on the developers?

This behavior can lead to
Defensive Behavior
, where one becomes less cooperative and, by doing so, can harm the pair’s
Togetherness
and even the
Code Quality
.

RQ3

What strategies can developers incorporate to avoid this behavior or to mitigate its harmful effects?

In Section
IV-A
, we presented six facets of a Teacher in pair programming. By incorporating them—especially by
Eliciting Knowledge Needs and Wants
(see
IV-A2
and
IV-A3
)—one can avoid
Pushing Redundant and Unwanted Knowledge
.

Besides that, a Teacher needs to be aware of the
Power Gap
that manifests because of the
Knowledge Gap
and has to keep it in check.
Creating Learning Opportunities
(see
IV-A6
) can help, as it shifts decision-making power away from the Teacher and can reduce the
Power Gap
.

Moreover, the partners should avoid the anti-patterns of the Teacher and Student roles we defined in Section
IV-C
.

V-B

Advice for Practitioners

Be aware that there are more roles in pair programming than the typically described Driver and Navigator/Observer roles. The Teacher and Student roles (especially their facets and pitfalls) can serve as an orientation for your behavior in pair programming sessions that are characterized by a
One-sided Knowledge Gap
. Be aware of the
Power Gap
and how it may influence your or your partner’s behavior. Keep it in check.

Although
Defensive Behavior
may lead to a tendency to avoid the meta-discussion of the
Power Gap
and other process problems like
Hierarchical Behavior
, try to incorporate the meta-discussion to fix your problems and reduce a
Power Gap
that became too big during your session.

VI
Limitations

As this is a qualitative study, we will discuss two things here. First, we will discuss whether our results are trustworthy by explaining how we implemented the pillars of Grounded Theory Methodology that lead to grounded results. Second, we will discuss how relevant our results are for practitioners and where the limits of our theory lie.

VI-A

Theoretical Sampling and Theoretical Saturation

Theoretical Sampling is one cornerstone of Grounded Theory Methodology
[
13
]
. Data should not be gathered upfront. Data gathering should be an iterative process that is fueled by the current state of the analysis and the emerging research questions. Where are blind spots that need to be closed? Which kinds of codes did we expect but haven’t seen yet? These should be the questions that guide the gathering of additional data.

As part of our research process, we worked with a large dataset consisting of 67 industrial pair programming sessions that were, in fact, gathered upfront. But in contrast to interviews that are already fairly narrowed down depending on the questions asked, our data is observational. Each session is in itself a rich data source. Working with this kind of dataset can be compared to wandering through a large library. You walk through the aisles, looking at the book spines to decide whether the book will take you any further; sometimes you flick through the pages and then put the book down again. Other times you read the book down to the smallest detail. Although the library already existed, we applied theoretical sampling when deciding which session to analyze and which episodes in those sessions to analyze further. Without theoretical sampling, one would be overwhelmed by the sheer amount of data.

When we discovered the phenomenon of harmful knowledge transfers in the first place, we decided to analyze the sessions PA3 and PA4 in great detail to understand what happened. We then looked specifically for sessions that helped enrich our data by looking for other instances of
Hierarchical Behavior
and
Defensive Behavior
. Although CA1 contains a lot of
Hierarchical Behavior
and
Defensive Behavior
, we excluded it in the later stages of our analysis, as we didn’t see Teacher and Student roles during their session (although this session has a
One-sided Knowledge Gap
, C2 doesn’t
Prioritize Knowledge Transfer
at any point). DA2 was used to study
Defensive Behavior
, and CA5 to see an instance of Teacher and Student roles where they kept their
Power Gap
in check.

When we realized that observational data wasn’t enough because we mostly could only speculate on the developers’ intentions and emotions, we decided, in another Theoretical Sampling step, to conduct interviews.

Besides discovering
Creating Learning Opportunities
in our second interview, no new concepts emerged after that. We conclude that our theory reached theoretical saturation.

VI-B

Coding, Constant Comparison, and Theoretical Sensitivity

As our coding process described in section
III-C
illustrates, our codes emerged and changed constantly.
Guided by theoretical sampling, we first identified the Teacher role and later shifted our focus to our core concept, the
Power Gap
, which led to a complete rearrangement of our codes and concepts.
This was only possible by applying Constant Comparison and memo writing throughout the process.
We aim at creating transparency and thus trustworthiness by describing the different codes we employed, how our research interest shifted, and how some codes changed accordingly.

VI-C

Trustworthiness and Relevance of our Results

As we followed the grounded theory methodology process strictly, our results and the grounded theory we present here are grounded in our data. As our data is mostly observational and the interviews (with one exception) only served as an additional data source, our results are grounded in reality, i.e., real pair programming sessions that happened in the industry.

One could argue that, by being observed, the developers tried to present themselves in the best possible light. But as our analysis shows, they still acted in suboptimal ways. If this kind of behavior happens in recorded sessions, we believe it’s very likely that it also happens when developers aren’t observed—and maybe even in more severe forms.

Because of this reason and the naturalness of the data studied here, we argue that our results are not only trustworthy but also of high relevance for developers who do pair programming.

VII
Conclusions and Further Work

By analyzing 17 pair programming sessions and conducting six interviews with developers, we shed light on a phenomenon that was previously invisible: knowledge transfer that harms the pair programming session. While doing so, we defined the Teacher and Student roles in pair programming that developers can take up when dealing with a
One-sided Knowledge Gap
. We also categorized anti-patterns that developers should avoid when taking up those roles.

By developing our grounded theory on
The Vicious Cycle of Defensive Behavior
, we explain how these anti-patterns can manifest themselves and fuel each other throughout a session, harming the pair’s
Togetherness
and the
Code Quality
.

Our grounded theory puts the
Power Gap
at the center — a concept that plays a role in all pair programming sessions and must be actively managed by the partners.

There are two possible research routes one can take from here, and we are working on both of them.

First, we want to expand on our theory presented here, as we believe the
Power Gap
is relevant to all pair programming sessions — not only those where the developers take on the Teacher and Student roles.
Notably, here we focused on the negative dynamics surrounding the
Power Gap
and less on actions a partner could take to shrink it as explained in quote
IV-D2
.

Second, as we explained, we believe that by discovering and defining more roles in pair programming, we help developers adopt beneficial behavioral patterns for different challenges during pair programming. We want to expand on this by defining more roles and explaining their dynamics among them.

Besides that, we also believe that qualitative research is only the first step in a long research process. Now that we have discovered and explained the patterns at work here, it is time to quantify them by answering the following questions: How often is a
One-sided Knowledge Gap
present in pair programming? How often are developers unhappy with knowledge transfer activities? How common are sessions with a high
Power Gap
?
