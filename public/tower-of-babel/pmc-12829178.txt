Evaluation of the effectiveness of different supplemental cleaning techniques in the retreatment of roots with small simulated internal resorption cavities: an in vitro comparative study
Authors: Sine Güngör Us, Özgür Uzun, Nazlı Merve Güngör
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe irregular morphology of internal root resorption (IRR) cavities complicates non-surgical retreatment by limiting effective cleaning and complete removal of filling materials. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of three supplemental techniques—the EndoActivator System (EAS), passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI), and XP-Endo Finisher R (XPR)—for removing filling materials from simulated IRR cavities.MethodsOne hundred single-rooted maxillary central incisors were decoronated and split longitudinally. Standardized simulated IRR cavities (0.8 mm depth, 1.6 mm diameter) were prepared using a round diamond bur, followed by a nitric-acid demineralization protocol. Hemisections were reassembled with cyanoacrylate gel and sealed with wax. The canals were obturated with gutta-percha and AH Plus using lateral condensation apically and continuous heat vertical compaction in the IRR region. Five teeth served as negative controls and five as positive controls. After mechanical filling removal, the remaining specimens were randomly assigned to three supplementary cleaning techniques—PUI, EAS, and XPR (n = 30). All groups received the same NaOCl–saline–EDTA irrigation regimen. Residual filling material was assessed by two independent examiners under ×12.5 magnification and quantified using ImageJ software. The data were compared between groups using Kruskal–Wallis tests, and intra- and inter-examiner agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).ResultsNone of the supplemental techniques achieved complete removal of filling material from the simulated IRR cavities. Numerical differences among PUI, EAS, and XPR were small and not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Inter- and intra-examiner agreement was excellent (ICC > 0.95).ConclusionsAll three techniques reduced residual filling material in the simulated IRR cavities, but none achieved complete cleaning, and no statistically significant differences were found among groups.

Background

Internal root resorption (IRR) is characterized by the progressive loss of intraradicular dentin resulting from odontoclastic activity within the root canal system [1, 2]. While its precise etiology remains unclear, trauma and chronic pulpal inflammation are regarded as the primary etiological factors for IRR [2, 3]. Non-surgical root canal therapy is generally considered the primary method for arresting the resorptive process and preserving the tooth [4]. IRR generally has a favorable prognosis when detected and managed in the early stages through proper cleaning and obturation techniques [5, 6]. However, the irregular morphology of IRR cavities can compromise the success of conventional treatment [7, 8].

Similar to primary treatment planning, the decision to manage internal root resorption nonsurgically or surgically depends on factors such as the size, corono-apical extension, and perforation status of the defect [9, 10]. Clinical reports show that smaller, non-perforating IRR defects can be managed nonsurgically, whereas perforating or apically located lesions are more frequently treated surgically [11–14]. When a nonsurgical approach is selected as the more conservative option, its success largely depends on the effective removal of existing filling materials from the irregular and concave resorptive cavity. Although micro-CT studies specifically focused on IRR retreatment are limited, investigations on anatomically complex spaces—such as deep concavities, isthmuses, and irregular canal extensions—consistently demonstrate residual filling material after retreatment procedures [15–19]. Such remnants are known to retain bacteria and biofilm, hindering disinfection and increasing the risk of persistent or recurrent infection, which can negatively affect long-term outcomes [20, 21]. Therefore, improving cleaning efficiency in IRR cases is clinically significant, as it directly influences treatment outcomes and tooth survival.

A range of adjunctive systems has been proposed to enhance the removal of residual filling material during retreatment, particularly in anatomically complex regions where conventional instrumentation is insufficient. Among these, the EndoActivator System (EAS), passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI), and the XP-Endo Finisher R (XPR) have emerged as the most widely adopted sonic, ultrasonic, and mechanical approaches in contemporary endodontic practice [22–24]. The EndoActivator System (EAS) is a sonic device that enhances irrigation through intracanal agitation using flexible polymer tips at frequencies from 2,000 to 10,000 cycles per minute [25]. Passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) employs a non-cutting ultrasonic tip to activate irrigants within the canal [26]; in this study, PUI was performed using the Ultra X device, which operates at 45 kHz and generates acoustic streaming and cavitation [27]. Unlike sonic and ultrasonic methods, the XP-Endo Finisher R (XPR) is a mechanical instrument manufactured from a proprietary nickel-titanium alloy (MaxWire). At room temperature, it remains straight in the martensitic phase, but at body temperature, the apical 10 mm transforms into a spoon-like form, increasing contact with the canal walls and enhancing the removal of filling remnants [28, 29].

Previous studies have primarily focused on the removal of calcium hydroxide or organic debris from simulated IRR cavities (SIRCs) [30–34]. Only a few investigations have evaluated supplemental techniques for cleaning SIRCs, and these studies have generally examined sealer remnants rather than the retreatment of fully obturated canals—limiting their relevance to clinical retreatment scenarios [35]. Moreover, although some reports have compared PUI, EAS, or XPR individually or in pairs in other complex canal anatomies, such as oval or flattened canals [24, 36–39], none of these investigations were conducted in standardized SIRC retreatment models. Consequently, comparative evidence on the relative performance of EAS, PUI, and XPR in removing filling materials from SIRCs remains scarce. To date, no study has simultaneously assessed all three systems using a standardized simulated IRR model after complete canal obturation. The null hypothesis was that the techniques would not differ significantly in their ability to remove residual filling material from SIRCs.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and sample size estimation

This study received ethical approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry at Gazi University (approval no: GÜDHKAEK.2023.22/2, approval date: November 16, 2023). The required sample size was estimated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.2; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany), considering an effect size of 0.43, a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 0.95. The calculation indicated that a minimum of 29 specimens per experimental group was required; therefore, each experimental group included 30 teeth to ensure adequate power.

Specimen selection and biomechanical preparation

Following extraction, the teeth were kept in physiological saline at ambient temperature. Their root length was adjusted to 19 mm by partial decoronation using sterile diamond discs under continuous water cooling. A size 15 K-file (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was used to establish the working length (WL), positioned 1 mm short of the apical foramen.

Only maxillary central incisors with a single root and a single straight canal, verified by periapical radiographs, were selected to ensure anatomical consistency across specimens. Teeth with caries, resorption, multiple roots or canals, cracks, fractures, or restorations were excluded. Additionally, teeth in which the WL could not be established or that could not be adequately reassembled after splitting were discarded during the experiment. After applying all eligibility criteria and incorporating positive (n = 5) and negative (n = 5) controls, a total of 100 single-rooted maxillary central incisors were included in the study.

The root canal was then prepared up to the WL using the ProTaper GOLD rotary system (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) and an electric endomotor (EndoWisePlus; Endoart, Istanbul, Türkiye). Instrumentation followed the manufacturer-recommended sequence (S1, S2, F1, F2, and F3), with F3 serving as the final shaping file. Each instrumentation step was supplemented with 5 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl; Werax, Izmir, Türkiye) applied for 60 s, resulting in a total of 25 mL NaOCl delivered during the shaping sequence. After completion of the mechanical preparation, the canal was irrigated with 5 mL of saline followed by 5 mL of 17% EDTA (Werax, İzmir, Türkiye) for 60 s. A final rinse with 10 mL saline was performed, and the canals were dried with sterile paper points.

Creation of the SIRCs

Longitudinal grooves were created on the buccal and lingual root surfaces using a diamond disc with continuous water cooling. Each tooth was split along the long axis with a chisel. The distance from the cementoenamel junction to the apex was measured and divided into thirds to standardize the location of the simulated defect. A standardized simulated IRR cavity (1.6 mm diameter, 0.8 mm depth) was prepared at the midpoint of the coronal third using a round bur, based on previously validated models showing that defects of similar size reproduce small early-stage resorptive lesions in a reproducible manner [40]. The specimens were subjected to a nitric acid–sodium hypochlorite sequence that has been shown to selectively demineralize canal wall dentin and create resorption-like concavities while preserving the external root structure [41]. Accordingly, the specimens were then treated with 5% nitric acid for 12 h, followed by 5% NaOCl for 10 min, and then with 5% nitric acid again for 12 h, with rinsing in distilled water between steps. After cavity preparation and chemical conditioning, the two halves were realigned under a dental operating microscope (×12.5) to ensure precise repositioning. A thin layer of cyanoacrylate gel (Scotch Super Glue gel; 3 M, St Paul, MN, USA) was applied along the internal surfaces, and the canals dried with paper points. The segments were gently pressed together until full contact was achieved. Excess adhesive was removed, and the external root surface was sealed circumferentially with modeling wax to prevent leakage during instrumentation and irrigation (Fig. 1).

Experimental groups and root canal obturation

Five specimens were left unfilled as the negative control group. All other specimens underwent root canal obturation under ×12.5 magnification using a dental operating microscope.

Lateral condensation was performed apical to the SIRC, and gutta-percha cones were cut at the apical margin of the simulated cavity using the Fast Pack Pro device (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China). The SIRC and coronal segments were obturated using the continuous heat vertical compaction technique with the Fast Fill device (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China). An AH Plus sealer (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was used in all specimens. After sealing the cavities with Coltosol (Coltene, Altstätten, Switzerland), all specimens were kept at 37 °C and 100% humidity for two weeks.

After canal obturation, all specimens were radiographed in the mesiodistal direction using a standardized setup to confirm complete filling with gutta-percha. If voids were detected, the obturation was repeated until complete filling was achieved. Five specimens with intact, unremoved root canal fillings were used as the positive control group.

Removal of root canal filling

Root canal filling material was mechanically removed under ×12.5 magnification using the ProTaper Universal Retreatment system (Dentsply/Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) with D1, D2, and D3 files at 340 rpm and torque control (EndoWisePlus; Endoart, Istanbul, Türkiye). Gates-Glidden burs were used for the coronal segment. The files were applied with controlled pecking motions, and 5 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite was delivered between each instrument. Advancement toward the working length was performed progressively, following the natural canal path. Filling removal was considered complete when the working length was reached, the instrument flutes were free of visible remnants, and the final rinse demonstrated no detectable debris.

Supplemental cleaning procedures

After retreatment instrumentation, the specimens were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 30/group) according to the supplementary cleaning technique applied: PUI, EAS, and XPR.

PUI

Irrigation was performed with 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl followed by 5 mL of 17% EDTA. Each solution was activated for four 30-second cycles with a size 20/0.02 ultrasonic tip (Ultra-X; Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) placed 2 mm short of the WL. The canal was rinsed with 5 mL of saline between irrigants.

EAS

The same irrigants and sequence were applied as in the PUI group. Activation was performed with a size 15/0.02 sonic tip (EndoActivator; Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) operating at 10,000 cycles/minute, placed 2 mm short of the WL.

XPR

In accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended protocol, a drop of orange oil solvent was applied inside the canal for 30 s. The XP-Endo Finisher R (FKG, Switzerland; ISO 30/0.00) was then operated inside an irrigant-filled canal at 800 rpm and 1 N·cm torque, using slow 7–8 mm in-and-out strokes for 30 s. During use, the canal was filled sequentially with 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl for 30 s, followed by a 5 mL saline rinse, and 5 mL of 17% EDTA for 15 s.

A final irrigation with 10 mL saline was performed in all groups.

Photographic evaluation and image analysis

After completion of all experimental procedures, the specimens were split longitudinally and photographed under ×12.5 magnification using a dental operating microscope (f170; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) equipped with a digital camera (D5000; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Before image analysis, pixel–millimeter calibration was performed using a 1-cm reference scale captured under the same ×12.5 magnification and identical camera settings. Then, the digital images were independently assessed by two blinded examiners using ImageJ software (version 1.47 V; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each specimen, the total area of the SIRC and the area of any residual root canal filling material were delineated (Fig. 2). Boundary tracing followed a standardized protocol that included identical magnification and zoom level within ImageJ, fixed threshold settings, and a uniform tracing sequence for all specimens. The percentage of residual filling material was calculated as the proportion of the SIRC area still covered by filling material (Fig. 3).

All measurements were performed for all experimental and control groups. Each examiner repeated the measurements one month later to assess intra-examiner reliability. The mean of the two measurements was used in the statistical analyses. Additionally, representative images from the control groups were included to further validate the methodology. In the positive control group, stereomicroscopic images confirmed the complete obturation of the simulated SIRCs, whereas in the negative control group, the absence of filling material was clearly visible.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 20; IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA), with a p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were calculated for each group, including the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and the median (range). The normality of each variable’s distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The variables were compared between groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Intra- and inter-examiner agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Both intra- and inter-examiner agreement were significant (p < 0.001, Table 1). Intra- and inter-examiner reliability was excellent (ICC > 0.95), with both examiners showing similar trends across groups (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The success of retreatment in IRR cases, as in all endodontic retreatment procedures, depends on the effective removal of filling materials and the achievement of adequate canal cleanliness [42]. In the present study, three supplemental activation techniques—PUI, EAS, and XPR—were evaluated in a standardized simulated IRR model. In accordance with the null hypothesis, none of the techniques demonstrated a statistically significant advantage in reducing residual filling material. Although minor numerical variations were noted among the groups, these differences were examiner-dependent. The excellent intra- and inter-examiner reliability (ICC > 0.95) supports the interpretation that these small variations reflect normal measurement variability within this model rather than true differences in cleaning efficacy. Overall, all techniques left considerable residual material, underscoring the persistent challenge of adequately cleaning small internal resorption cavities during retreatment.

IRR is most frequently observed in maxillary anterior teeth and may occur in various regions of the root [2, 3]. Clinical reports indicate that coronal and mid-root defects, particularly when small and non-perforating, are generally more accessible and can be predictably managed nonsurgically, whereas apical or perforating lesions present greater technical limitations and may more often require surgical intervention [11–14]. In line with these clinical considerations, the present experimental model employed human maxillary central incisors with simulated IRR cavities positioned in the coronal third, reflecting an anatomical scenario in which nonsurgical retreatment is both feasible and clinically relevant.

Various techniques have been employed to replicate IRR cavities in vitro. Round burs have often been used to create standardized cavities with smooth margins [43–45]; however, this approach cannot mimic the natural irregularities of clinical IRR cavities. In contrast, methods involving acid demineralization, such as the one proposed by da Silveira et al. [41], produce more irregular surfaces but lack dimensional consistency. Building on this idea, Marques-da-Silva et al. created standardized cavities with burs and then applied nitric acid, achieving both dimensional consistency and clinically relevant surface irregularities [46]. A similar modified approach was adopted in the present model, where cavities measuring 0.8 mm in depth and 1.6 mm in diameter were prepared with a round bur and then conditioned with 5% nitric acid, providing standardization while closely replicating the morphology of small clinical IRR lesions.

The SIRCs and the coronal portions of the canals were obturated under an operating microscope using the continuous wave vertical compaction technique to achieve optimal three-dimensional filling. This thermoplasticized approach was chosen based on previous studies demonstrating its superior performance in adapting to irregular cavity walls and maximizing the total filling area [47–49].

Radiographs were taken after obturation to confirm complete filling of the SIRCs in the specimens, and images of the positive control specimens demonstrated complete canal obturation. The inclusion of both positive and negative control groups not only verified that the experimental design functioned appropriately but also ensured that the validity of the results was not compromised by procedural errors or equipment malfunction, thereby strengthening the reliability of our findings.

Our findings indicate that none of the supplemental activation techniques were able to achieve complete removal of filling material from the simulated IRR cavities. This is consistent with the broader literature, as direct in vivo evidence on filling material removal in IRR retreatment is lacking, and the only in vitro study examining SIRC cleaning evaluated the removal of bioceramic and resin-based sealers rather than the retreatment of fully obturated canals, similarly reporting that no technique achieved complete cleaning [35]. Although that study did not assess gutta-percha removal, its findings reinforce the notion that complete debridement of irregular resorptive defects remains a substantial challenge regardless of the method employed. To date, few studies have directly compared XPR, PUI, and EAS in the context of complex canal anatomy [23, 36, 37]. Baumeier et al. evaluated three adjunctive techniques for their ability to remove residual filling material in flattened root canals, showing that XPR outperformed PUI and EAS, although the differences were not significant [36]. The findings of Baumeier et al. are consistent with our observations. In a study on oval canals, Volponi et al. demonstrated that XPR was more effective in removing filling remnants than either ultrasonic-assisted irrigation or EAS, and they found no significant difference between ultrasonic-assisted irrigation and EAS [37]. However, the findings of Volponi et al. contradict our results. While Volponi et al. obturated the root canals with the single-cone technique, we filled the canals using vertical compaction, as this approach provides better adaptation in such anatomies. This difference may partly explain the discrepancy between our study and Volponi et al. Furthermore, while Baumeier et al. examined flattened canals and Volponi et al. focused on oval canals, our study investigated SIRCs, which represent a distinct clinical challenge. These anatomical variations are likely to influence the distribution of filling materials and the subsequent efficacy of irrigant activation methods.

All activation techniques were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions, with the same total volume of irrigants applied to ensure methodological consistency. In the XPR group, orange oil was applied beforehand in accordance with clinical recommendations. Previous investigations conducted under purely mechanical conditions have suggested that the XP-Endo Finisher R may offer enhanced cleaning potential in areas with complex anatomy compared with conventional ultrasonic activation [16, 29]. However, the use of solvents introduces additional variables; orange oil and similar agents can soften gutta-percha and promote smear formation or residue adherence to canal walls, which may theoretically influence the instrument’s behavior during activation [50–52]. Supporting this, an ex vivo study reported that supplementing XPR use with a solvent did not improve its cleaning ability compared with mechanical use alone in isthmus-containing canals [19]. Taken together, these observations suggest that the use of a solvent may alter the behavior of XPR by softening gutta-percha and promoting residue adherence, which could potentially influence its cleaning performance.

The evaluation method employed in our study, based on stereomicroscopic imaging and digital analysis with ImageJ software, enabled objective and reproducible quantification of the residual filling materials, with excellent intra- and inter-examiner agreement. However, one limitation is that two-dimensional image analysis may not fully capture the three-dimensional complexity of filling material remnants within resorption cavities. Moreover, despite being performed with caution, the root-splitting procedure might have displaced small amounts of filling material. Future studies employing micro-computed tomography, which allows volumetric assessment, could overcome these limitations.

Using extracted human teeth increased the clinical relevance of our model compared to artificial samples, as natural dentin structure and canal morphology were preserved. Standardizing cavities in the coronal third also reflects scenarios where non-surgical retreatment is most feasible. Nevertheless, the SIRCs were artificially created, and despite efforts to mimic natural irregularities through a combined bur-and-acid protocol, they cannot fully replicate the variability of clinical IRR cavities.

Finally, the in vitro design of our study does not reproduce the biological conditions present in vivo, such as periodontal support, blood circulation, and immune responses, which may influence treatment outcomes. Additionally, our study only examined one tooth type and one cavity dimension, limiting the generalizability of its findings to other clinical presentations. Future investigations incorporating volumetric imaging techniques, a broader range of anatomical presentations, and in vivo validation are needed to more accurately characterize residual filling material within IRR cavities and to refine retreatment protocols.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, none of the supplementary techniques resulted in the complete removal of root canal filling material from SIRCs. Furthermore, no statistically significant differences in cleaning efficacy were observed among the protocols tested.
