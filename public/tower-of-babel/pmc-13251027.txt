Fenced enclosure and increased rainfall facilitate recovery of degraded desert steppe by restructuring soil seed bank dynamics
Authors: Huayang Chen, Gaohua Fan, Carol C Baskin, Jerry M Baskin, Qingguo Cui, Houjia Liu, Zhenying Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil seed bank is pivotal for vegetation regeneration in degraded desert steppes. However, there is limited information on the dynamic changes of aboveground vegetation and soil seed banks across different degradation stages of desert steppe following removal of grazing. Influence of 2-year fenced enclosure treatment and precipitation on changes of soil seed banks and aboveground vegetation of different degraded grasslands in west Ordos of North China were studied.ResultsThe climax community of desert steppe was dominated by Stipa breviflora. As degree of degradation increased due to intensified grazing, the dominant species were S. breviflora → Artemisia scoparia → Eragrostis minor → Peganum harmala. In addition, as degree of degradation increased aboveground species richness and density decreased, belowground richness declined, but seed bank density increased. Species similarity first decreased and then increased. In the starting year of enclosure (2021), Sorensen similarity index of aboveground and belowground vegetation of moderately and intensely grazed areas was lower than that of the control. After 1–2 years, the similarity index increased, showing that enclosure (fencing) increased the number of species in both enclosures. After 1 to 2 years of fencing, soil seed bank density continued to increase while diversity did not change, driven by increased seed production of E. minor and that of P. harmala decreasing. Growing-season precipitation increased in the above-average year of 2022 and a slightly below-average year of 2023, and it was significantly associated with increasing seed bank species density and aboveground species richness. Thus, seed bank community structure was altered by a reduction in P. harmala and an increase in E. minor but without a change in richness.ConclusionsThe combined effects of 2-year short-term enclosure and increased natural precipitation enhanced aboveground species density and seed bank capacity and facilitated the early natural restoration of degraded grasslands.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09106-4.Keywords: Degraded desert steppe, Fenced enclosure, Grazing; soil seed bank, Vegetation recovery

Introduction

Soil seed banks comprise all viable seeds present on the soil surface or buried within the soil matrix [1], and constitute a critical stage in the life history of plant populations. They act as the foundational reservoir for plant colonization, survival, reproduction and dispersal, while their spatiotemporal distribution patterns are critical to vegetation regeneration and restoration, biodiversity conservation, plant successional dynamics, and seed dispersal [2–4]. Under adverse environmental conditions, soil seed banks conserve genetic, species and functional diversity [5, 6]. Specifically, dormant viable seeds stored in soil can buffer populations against demographic loss in complex and variable environmental conditions [7–9]. During natural recovery of vegetation, soil seed banks largely dictate the direction and rate of vegetation restoration [6, 10]. These attributes exhibit distinct spatiotemporal variability (including horizontal and vertical spatial partitioning and seasonal and interannual temporal dynamics), which are collectively regulated by climatic conditions, soil properties, and reproductive output of aboveground plant communities [1, 11–15].

Current research on soil seed banks primarily has focused on their species composition, density (size), spatiotemporal dynamics, spatial distribution patterns, associations with aboveground plant communities, and driving factors. Abiotic factors (e.g. topography, soil properties, and climatic conditions), biotic factors (e.g. above ground vegetation community characteristics and seed morphology), and a suite of natural and anthropogenic disturbances (e.g. fire, flooding, and grazing) jointly modulate soil seed bank properties [16]. Grassland degradation, defined as retrogressive succession and reduced primary productivity driven by irrational land use, leads to declines in vegetation height, coverage, biomass, and forage quality, accompanied by soil habitat deterioration and loss of ecosystem functions [17]. Accordingly, soil seed banks represent a critical potential resource for the ecological restoration of degraded grassland vegetation [15, 18]. In grassland ecosystems, soil seed banks serve as the primary genetic reservoir for post-disturbance vegetation regeneration and are pivotal to restoration and reconstruction of degraded grasslands, because they integrate plant population responses to environmental perturbations [19]. In grassland ecosystems, the dynamics of soil seed banks and aboveground vegetation are jointly shaped by multiple biotic and abiotic factors, and natural and anthropogenic disturbances—among which grazing, fenced enclosure, and precipitation are the most critical drivers, and their interactions further modulate grassland restoration processes.

Grazing is the most widespread land-use type in grasslands and a dominant driver of ecosystem structural and functional changes [20]. It modulates not only aboveground vegetation community composition and soil physicochemical properties, but also key attributes of soil seed banks (including species composition, density, richness, diversity, and compositional similarity to aboveground vegetation), thereby reshaping plant reproduction processes and vegetation succession trajectories [21]. Intense grazing directly reduces vegetation cover and initiates grassland degradation [22, 23]. Empirical studies have shown that grazing can directly or indirectly alter soil seed bank properties during key plant phenological stages of spring green-up [24], summer-autumn reproductive growth, flowering, and especially fruiting periods [25].

Selective foraging by livestock modulates aboveground plant community composition, and trampling and dung deposition alter soil physicochemical properties [26]. Overall, livestock activity facilitates seed dispersal [27]. However, empirical research on effects of grazing on soil seed banks has produced inconsistent results between soil seed bank size and grazing intensity [13, 28–30]. Specifically, the differentiation of ephemeral and persistent seed banks during grazing does not result in a one-way response in the total quantity on the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau. Thus, the transient seed bank significantly increases with intensity of grazing, while the persistent seed bank significantly decreases. An increase in the transient bank offsets a decrease in the persistent bank, and the total density of seeds in the soil has no clear correlation with the grazing intensity [13]. Additionally, soil seed bank seasonal dynamics of the alpine meadows on the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau are dominant, and the effect of grazing intensity is masked by the environment [28]. Also, the size of the soil seed bank in arid regions is dominated by precipitation, while the grazing effect depends on the water background [29]. In semi-arid savannas, grazing affects the vegetation structure rather than the total seed bank [30].

To mitigate grazing-induced degradation, fenced enclosure is a widely recognized effective method for ecological restoration of degraded grasslands, since it mitigates unsustainable anthropogenic disturbances (e.g., intensive grazing) and thereby drives key ecosystem-level improvements, namely vegetation recovery, soil amelioration, and reconstruction of edaphic ecosystem functions [31–33]. This practice capitalizes on the intrinsic self-recovery potential of grassland ecosystems, driving long-term ecological succession mediated by plant-soil-environment feedback loops. Empirical studies have demonstrated that enclosure can directly or indirectly alter aboveground plant community composition, enhance vegetation cover and primary productivity, increase species diversity, improve community structural stability, boost soil seed bank reserves, and ameliorate soil physicochemical properties [32, 34–36]. However, although short-term enclosure can markedly increase vegetation height and aboveground biomass, prolonged enclosure can constrain plant growth via resource limitation, which may subsequently reduce grassland community diversity and structural stability [33, 37]. Notably, the effectiveness of enclosure in promoting grassland restoration is closely associated with precipitation, a key abiotic factor regulating species composition and community structure of grassland ecosystems—especially in arid desert steppes.

Precipitation modulates the growth and reproduction of dominant grassland species and dynamics of soil seed banks. For example, as a constructive dominant species in desert steppes, Stipa breviflora benefits from moderate precipitation increases but suffers reduced interspecific competitiveness under excessive precipitation [38]. Extreme drought events markedly reduced primary productivity of Fabaceae and Poaceae species, whereas deep-rooted perennial species such as Stipa caucasica display enhanced drought tolerance [39, 40]. For soil seed banks, moderate increases in growing-season precipitation positively enhance species richness and abundance, whereas excessive precipitation may elevate seed mortality via microbial decomposition, reducing the density of persistent seed banks [41, 42]. Additionally, the compositional similarity index between soil seed banks and aboveground vegetation communities shifts along precipitation gradients, positioning soil seed banks as potential early warning indicators of ecosystem functional degradation [43]. Compared with aboveground vegetation, soil seed banks exhibit lower interannual fluctuation and higher temporal stability in species composition, making them critical resources for the restoration of degraded arid desert steppes [44].

Desert steppe plant communities are highly sensitive to climate change and anthropogenic disturbances, with structural and functional properties vulnerable to external interference [45–46]. These ecosystems are predominated by drought-tolerant perennial herbs (e.g., Stipa spp., Artemisia frigida), with scattered shrubs and semi-shrubs, and are characterized by low species diversity, simple community structure and vegetation coverage ranging from 30% to 60% [45, 47]. Despite extensive research on the effects of grazing, enclosure, and precipitation change on grassland restoration, most studies have separately explored the individual effects of these factors, resulting in a critical research gap. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of how natural precipitation variability modulates ecological restoration processes under fenced enclosure remains insufficient. This gap hinders development of targeted restoration strategies for degraded desert steppes.

To address this core issue, the present study was conducted in the Ordos Desert Steppe, using a 10-year fenced plot as the control and moderately and intensely grazed plots as experimental treatments, to explore the short-term ecological effects of fenced enclosure on desert steppe vegetation and soil seed banks. We hypothesized that (1) fenced enclosure can increase soil seed bank density in grazed areas, enhancing the recovery potential of degraded desert grasslands; (2) enclosure can increase aboveground vegetation species diversity, increasing plant-soil species similarity; (3) seed germination and population recruitment of annual herbs in soil seed banks are closely associated with increased growing-season precipitation, jointly facilitating the early-stage restoration of degraded desert steppes.

Materials and methods

Study area description

The study was conducted in Otog Banner, Inner Mongolia, China(39°37′16′′ N, 107°40′17′′ E; 1460 m a.s.l) [42]. This area is located on the western margin of the Ordos Plateau and is characterized by unique climatic, geological, and edaphic conditions [48]. The environment is extremely fragile, rendering it highly sensitive to climate change and external disturbances [48, 49]. Stipa breviflora is the dominant plant community in this region [50]. According to the Otog Banner Meteorological Station (Station No. 52539, located 14.6 km from the experimental site, data sourced from the China Meteorological Data Service Center), the mean annual temperature from 1954 to 2022 is 7.11 °C, with a July maximum of 23.65 °C and a January minimum of -8.85 °C. Mean annual precipitation is 272.1 mm, with approximately 70% of the total precipitation occurring in May to August [51].

Experimental design

Study quadrat design

This study employed a 10-year enclosed plot established by the Otog Banner Forestry and Grassland Bureau in 2011 as the control of mild degeneration (I), which was a degenerate grassland (50 m x 50 m) experiencing intense grazing (3 sheep unit / hm) before the enclosure (for grazing degree, see Table 1). Based on the Banner-wide livestock-vegetation balance standard and a 2021 vegetation growth survey, grasslands under moderate grazing (1.5 sheep unit / hm) as moderate degeneration (II) and intense grazing (3 sheep unit / hm) as severe degradation (III) were selected as experimental sites (see Figure S1). The dominant species, grazing history, and habitat conditions of these grasslands are detailed in Table 1. Enclosure measures were implemented on the moderate grazing and intense grazing grassland in 2021, and we investigated vegetation and seed bank in 2022 and 2023, with grazing prohibited year-round. The size of each plot was 50 m × 50 m. Ten quadrats (1 m x 1 m) in each plot were selected randomly to investigate how enclosure and natural precipitation variations influence soil seed bank dynamics and aboveground vegetation structure. It was difficult to find plots with exactly same degree of degradation; therefore, only one plot was selected for each degraded level.

Vegetation and soil seed bank characteristics of desert steppe grasslands under different grazing intensities

Vegetation

From 2021 to 2023, annual surveys of aboveground vegetation communities were conducted in August. Ten quadrats of 1 m × 1 m were established to quantify species composition, individual plant abundance, and coverage of aboveground vegetation for each plot. (see Figure S2). At the end of the growing season, the aboveground parts of plants were harvested to determine biomass.

Soil seed bank

From 2021 to 2023, sampling for soil seed bank species was conducted annually in April (before seed germination), May (after seed germination), early July (before seed dispersal), and late September (after seed dispersal). At each grazing intensity plot, ten 1 m × 1 m quadrats were established. Soil cores were collected from each quadrat using a 10 cm × 10 cm soil auger via the five-point sampling method. (see Figure S2). Soil core sampling was conducted across three soil depth layers: 0–2 cm, 2–5 cm, and 5–10 cm. Soil core samples from the same depth layer in each quadrat were pooled and subsequently stored in No. 10 self-sealing plastic bags. This sampling regime yielded a total of 1080 (3 years × 3 plots × 4 seasons × 3 soil depths ×10 replicates = 1080 samples) soil core samples for our study.

The plant species were identified by the station director Tian Yanjun, a botanist at the Forestry and Grassland Workstation of Otog Banner. We declare that the experimental research on plants and soil in this study complied with institutional, national, and international guidelines. We confirm that the Administrative Committee of Forestry and Grassland Workstation of Otog Banner granted permission for the collection of samples and experimental materials for this study and assisted in the material collection. We have acknowledged their contribution.

Species composition in soil seed bank

We used the germination method [52] to assess species composition and size of the soil seed bank. First, soil seed bank samples were naturally air-dried in a well-ventilated and dry environment to minimize seed decay and mold growth for 2 weeks. Subsequently, air-dried soil samples were passed through a 5-mm mesh sieve to remove debris, including plant litter, fine roots, and gravel. The soil was stored dry in a well-ventilated shed at natural temperatures during winter and dry environment for overwintering. For samples collected from the time of before germination, after seed germination and before seed dispersal, continuous germination was carried out from April to August of the same year. But for samples collected from the time of after seed dispersal, gemination was conducted in subsequent year because of cold temperature.

For germination trials, soil samples were evenly spread approximately 1–2 cm thick in germination trays. We sprayed water on the soil to keep it moist and monitored germination every 3 days from April to August until no new seedlings emerged. All germination experiments were conducted in the well-ventilated awning at the Ordos Grassland Ecosystem National Field Scientific Observation and Research Station, Inner Mongolia (39°29′37.6″ N, 110°11′29.4″ E, with an altitude of 1290 m). Mean annual temperature is 7.0℃ in this station. Soil seed bank species composition and size were identified based on the morphological characteristics of the emerged seedlings (see Figures S3 and S4).

Precipitation during the 2022 growing season reached 361.2 mm, which was much higher than the 68-year average, with rainfall mainly concentrated in July and August, accounting for approximately 85% of the total growing season precipitation. Statistical analyses

Analysis of variance (ANOVA), general linear regression models, and Spearman correlation analysis were used to analyze the effects of enclosure, precipitation, and degradation degree on vegetation characteristics and soil seed bank dynamics. In our models, the similarity, species diversity, density, and richness of vegetation and soil seed bank were considered separately as dependent variables. Grazing levels, enclosure periods, soil depth, and season were considered as independent variables. The Tukey HSD test was used to evaluate differences in soil seed bank species richness and density and aboveground vegetation species richness and density among different grazing intensities and under enclosure conditions for each grazing intensity.

Graphs were plotted using the ggplot2 package [56]. For graphs depicting soil seed bank density across different soil depths, the ggbreak package was used to split the axis scales for better visualization [57]. All statistical analyses were performed using R software [58].

In the initial year of enclosure implementation, species compositional similarity between aboveground vegetation and soil seed bank followed the order of 10-year enclosed control plots > intense grazing plot > moderate grazing plot. Following enclosure restoration, this similarity increased, and the ranking shifted to intense grazing plots > moderate grazing plots > 10-year enclosed control plots (Fig. 9).

Growing season precipitation in experimental plots was 81.0 mm in 2021 (year of enclosure initiation), 361.2 mm in 2022 (1 year after enclosure), and 171.0 mm in 2023 (2 years after enclosure), with significant interannual variations in precipitation. To further clarify the relationships between growing season precipitation, enclosure measures, and the vegetation restoration process of grasslands at different degradation stages, correlation analyses were conducted between growing season precipitation, enclosure duration, and vegetation characteristic indices, respectively. In the 10-year enclosed control plots (Fig. 10), growing season precipitation was strongly correlated with aboveground-soil seed bank species similarity, Simpson Index and Evenness Index (r > 0.69), while enclosure duration correlated only with species richness and density. (r > 0.6). In the moderate grazing plots, growing season precipitation also showed strong correlations with aboveground and belowground species similarity, Simpson index, and evenness index (r > 0.75), while enclosure duration was strongly correlated only with species richness and species density (r > 0.6). In the intense grazing plots, growing season precipitation was also strongly correlated with aboveground and belowground species similarity, Simpson index, and evenness index (r > 0.79), whereas enclosure duration was strongly correlated only with species richness and species density (r > 0.6).

Correlation between vegetation diversity, richness, and density on different grazing levels, rainfall during the growing season, and enclosure measures

Overall, across all grazing intensity regimes, growing season precipitation was relatively strongly correlated with soil seed bank and aboveground vegetation species diversity in intensive grazing plots. In contrast, the correlation between enclosure duration and both species density and diversity was largely consistent across different grazing intensity regimes. These results demonstrate that under enclosure restoration conditions, elevated growing season precipitation significantly associated with the increasing of the species diversity, density, and richness of aboveground vegetation, as well as the species compositional similarity between the aboveground vegetation and soil seed bank. Furthermore, the greater the degree of grassland degradation, the more pronounced the facilitative effect of elevated growing season precipitation on these community attributes.

Discussion

Overall, the plant communities of the Western Ordos Desert Steppe are dominated by Stipa breviflora as the primary dominant species, with other major associated species including Caragana stenophylla, Allium mongolicum, Lespedeza potaninii, Cleistogenes squarrosa, and Artemisia scoparia. Long-term overgrazing has caused severe vegetation degradation, leading to a shift in core constructive species toward Artemisia scoparia, Eragrostis minor, Tragus mongolorum, and Peganum harmala. The degradation succession sequence follows the pathway: Stipa breviflora → Artemisia scoparia → Eragrostis minor → Peganum harmala. Prior to implementation of fenced enclosure, in comparison with the 10-year enclosed control plots, vegetation species richness, density, and diversity gradually declined progressively with increasing degradation degree. After enclosure implementation, combined with above-average growing-season precipitation in 2022 and slightly below-average precipitation in 2023, vegetation richness, density, and diversity in the moderately and intensely degraded plots gradually increased with extension of enclosure duration.

Meanwhile, constructive species of degraded grasslands, such as Eragrostis minor, Tragus mongolorum, and Artemisia scoparia, gradually increased in these plots, with the most pronounced increase in density of Eragrostis minor in the intensely degraded plot. Qiu et al. (2019) [59] reported that in the early stage of degraded grassland restoration the annual herb Eragrostis minor exhibits rapid germination and establishment, and it is subsequently replaced by other plant species. Eragrostis minor is regarded as an opportunistic species capable of rapid reproduction via utilization of environmental gaps and available resources, and its population size increases sharply with elevated precipitation [60, 61]. As a rain-dependent r-strategist, it is highly adapted to unstable habitats and tends to become dominant in degraded or disturbed ecosystems [62]. Notably, the present study found that fenced enclosure led to a significant increase in the soil seed bank density of the annual herb Eragrostis minor, which further propagated extensively and subsequently formed substantial aboveground populations. It is reasonable to speculate that this phenomenon is primarily driven by increased precipitation under fenced enclosure, as the exclusion of grazing disturbance under such conditions creates a favorable microenvironment that facilitates seed germination and population expansion of Eragrostis minor. Accordingly, this study confirms that elevated growing season precipitation combined with enclosure facilitates early-stage restoration of degraded grasslands in this region.

Effects of enclosure on aboveground vegetation in desert steppe

Before enclosure was implemented in moderately and intensely degraded plots, species diversity, density and evenness in the 10-year enclosed control plots were significantly higher than those in the moderately and intensely degraded plots. The 10-year enclosed plot was dominated by Stipa breviflora and Caragana stenophylla, moderately degraded plot by Eragrostis minor and Stipa breviflora, and intensely degraded plot by Artemisia scoparia and Plantago minuta. After enclosure, species diversity, density, and evenness in both moderately and intensely degraded plots increased significantly. Eragrostis minor became the dominant community in the intensely degraded plot, while density of Peganum harmala decreased significantly in the moderately degraded plot, indicating a weakening trend of retrogressive succession.

After enclosure, density and richness of aboveground species in grasslands under different grazing intensities increased significantly, especially a marked increase in the annual grasses Eragrostis minor and Tragus mongolorum, which indicated an early-stage restoration trend [59]. In our study, Fenced enclosure removes grazing pressure, permitting plant communities to complete their life cycles and produce substantial seed rain that replenishes the belowground soil seed bank. When natural precipitation increases, annual opportunistic species stored in the seed bank germinate and reproduce vigorously, ultimately contributing to the recovery of aboveground vegetation. Collectively, the combination of fenced enclosure and increased natural precipitation has facilitated the early-stage restoration trend of these degraded desert grasslands, as evidenced by the significant increase in soil seed bank density of the annual herb Eragrostis minor under enclosure. This annual species further propagated extensively to form substantial aboveground populations, an observation primarily attributed to the favorable microenvironment from grazing exclusion under enclosure, coupled with increased precipitation that effectively promotes its seed germination and population expansion. Nevertheless, given the relatively short enclosure duration, however, it has not significantly altered the overall degraded state of the grasslands.

Effects of enclosure on soil seed banks in desert steppe

Interannual variation of soil seed banks

Following fencing enclosure, soil seed bank density in plots across all different degradation levels was significantly higher than that pre-enclosure levels. This was attributable to the elimination of grazing disturbance by fencing enclosure, which enabled aboveground vegetation to complete its full life cycle and produce viable seeds that replenished the soil seed bank [63]. In desert steppes, Eragrostis minor is one of the dominant species in the early stage of degraded grassland restoration, but it is gradually replaced by other species as restoration period increases. This may indicate that Eragrostis minor emerges in the initial restoration stage, but long-term and complete grassland restoration requires the colonization of other plant species [64]. Eragrostis minor exhibits rapid germination and establishment and is subsequently displaced by other plant species-a key characteristic indicative of the early restoration stage of degraded grasslands [59].

With the extension of enclosure duration, species density increased most significantly in the intensely degraded plots, and it was higher than that in the moderately degraded plots and 10-year enclosed control plots. This result was mainly due to the significant increase in density of Eragrostis minor and Tragus mongolorum in the intense and moderate grazing plots (see Figures S6-S8). Along with grassland restoration, abundance of Artemisia scoparia, Tragus mongolorum, Allium mongolicum, and Stipa breviflora gradually increased. The most substantial increase in soil seed bank density was in the intensely grazed plots, which was mainly attributed to massive seed production by Eragrostis minor with these seeds being input into the soil seed bank. We also observed a decrease in proportion of Peganum harmala, which is a well-recognized degradation indicator species. Across different degradation severity levels, the significant increase in density of annual grasses and reduction in proportion of degradation indicator species in the soil seed bank of intensely grazed plots indicated that enclosure facilitated early restoration of degraded desert grasslands.

Seasonal dynamics of soil seed banks

Differences in effects of fenced enclosure on soil seed bank density and diversity across different soil depths were mainly reflected in the 0–2 cm topsoil layer. This was attributable to the relatively short fenced enclosure duration, which led to an absence of significant vertical changes in the soil seed bank. For the seasonal dynamics, based on the data of seed bank in the 0–2 cm topsoil layer, the size of soil seed bank decreased from the stage of pre-germination to post-germination and pre-dispersal, then increased at the stage of post-dispersal. Fenced enclosure facilitated an increase in seed density during the post-dispersal stage, thus augmenting the storage capacity of the soil seed bank. These seeds could improve seed germination potential in the following year, thereby facilitating restoration of the vegetation.

Effects of enclosure on aboveground-belowground species similarity

In desert steppe ecosystems, the “initial increase” in species compositional similarity between the aboveground vegetation and soil seed bank is driven by fenced enclosure. This is one of the key indicators of an ecosystem shift from a disturbance-dominated regime to a natural successional regime [65]. It implies that the underground soil seed bank is being reshaped by aboveground dominant species, providing a seed source for long-term stability of the grassland ecosystem.

Species compositional similarity between the aboveground vegetation and soil seed bank (Sørensen index) increased progressively following enclosure, indicating that fencing enclosure facilitated an increase in number of shared species between these two ecosystem components. Specifically, this pattern was shown as an enhanced mutual seed input between the soil seed bank and aboveground vegetation. This ecological pattern can be explained by the following mechanism. Following fenced enclosure, seeds of annual herb species in the soil seed bank (e.g., Eragrostis minor and Tragus mongolorum) germinated successively, thereby replenishing the aboveground seedling bank. These seedlings subsequently completed growth and reproduced with elevated precipitation. Meanwhile, aboveground perennial plants species such as Artemisia scoparia and Cleistogenes squarrosa also completed their reproductive cycles under combined effects of fenced enclosure and elevated precipitation, generating viable seeds that replenished the soil seed bank.

Effects of enclosure and variation in precipitation on degraded grassland restoration

In arid and semi-arid steppes, precipitation is the most critical environmental factor regulating soil seed bank size. Combined direct and indirect effects of precipitation significantly increase the abundance of viable seeds of annual herbs in the soil seed bank [44]. Under fenced enclosure conditions, elevated growing season precipitation was one of the primary drivers of the rapid early-stage restoration of degraded desert steppe grasslands. This was attributable to elevated precipitation facilitating the rapid propagation and seed set of annual plant species. These viable seeds were subsequently added to the soil seed bank, enabling annual plants to attain a transient dominant position during the early restoration stage, thereby enhancing the storage capacity of early-successional species in the soil seed bank. Additionally, elevated precipitation enhanced species compositional similarity between the aboveground vegetation and soil seed bank, species density of both components in desert steppe ecosystems, and improved aboveground species richness, which was a pattern reflected by replenishment of aboveground vegetation communities by the soil seed bank.

In the absence of fenced enclosure, however, there is a risk of reduced soil seed bank storage capacity. Although elevated precipitation can facilitate growth and reproduction of perennial plants and stimulate germination of annual plant seeds in the soil seed bank to replenish aboveground vegetation communities, unregulated grazing in the absence of fenced enclosure is likely to result in intense herbivore pressure, which suppresses seed set in herbaceous plants. This indirectly leads to the depletion of the soil seed bank due to a lack of subsequent seed replenishment [66]. In our study, fenced enclosure enabled annual plants to complete their full life cycle, which substantially enlarged the total soil seed bank. Coupled with increased natural precipitation, rapid propagation of annual plants further enhanced aboveground population density. Therefore, fenced enclosure and elevated growing season precipitation collectively constitute two key factors that facilitate the rapid early-stage restoration of degraded desert steppe grasslands.

Conclusion

This study revealed distinct responses of aboveground vegetation and soil seed banks to short-term fenced enclosure across moderate and severe degradation stages in the Western Ordos Desert Steppe of China. Fenced enclosure effectively eliminated grazing disturbance, enabled plant communities to complete their life cycles, and substantially increased the density and species richness of soil seed banks, especially for annual opportunistic species such as Eragrostis minor and Tragus mongolorum. Increased growing-season precipitation further promoted seed germination, population expansion, and aboveground community recovery of these annual species.

Fenced enclosure combined with elevated precipitation significantly improved species diversity, density and evenness of aboveground vegetation, and it increase compositional similarity between the aboveground vegetation and soil seed banks across the degraded grassland plots. The degradation indicator species Peganum harmala decreased in abundance, while native dominant and companion species gradually recovered, indicating an obvious early restoration trend of degraded desert steppe.

Nevertheless, due to the relatively short enclosure duration, the overall degraded state and community structure of the desert steppe have not yet been fundamentally reversed. It is concluded that short-term enclosure coupled with favorable precipitation can only drive the early-stage restoration of moderately and severely degraded grasslands, and long-term continuous enclosure is still required to realize complete community restoration and ecological stability in this region.
