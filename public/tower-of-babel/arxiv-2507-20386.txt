The Augmented Mixing Method: Computing High-Accuracy Primal-Dual Solutions to Large-Scale SDPs via Column Updates
Daniel Brosch, Jan Schwiddessen, Angelika Wiegele

Abstract
The Burer–Monteiro factorization has become a powerful tool for solving large-scale semidefinite programs (SDPs), enabling recently developed low-rank solvers to tackle problems previously beyond reach. However, existing methods are typically designed to prioritize scalability over solution accuracy. We introduce the Augmented Mixing Method , a new algorithm that combines the Burer–Monteiro factorization with an inexact augmented Lagrangian framework and a block coordinate descent scheme. Our method emphasizes solving the resulting subproblems efficiently and to high precision. Inequality constraints are handled directly, without reformulation or introducing slack variables. A novel dynamic update strategy for the penalty parameter ensures that primal and dual feasibility progress remain balanced. This approach enables our method to compute highly accurate primal-dual solutions, even for large-scale SDPs with more than ten million inequality constraints. Despite lacking theoretical convergence guarantees, the Augmented Mixing Method shows strong practical performance with default parameters, across a wide range of SDP instances. It often produces more accurate primal-dual solutions than state-of-the-art interior-point methods and scales significantly better. Our open-source Julia implementation is memory-efficient, customizable, and supports arbitrary-precision arithmetic.



Keywords—
Semidefinite Programming, Augmented Lagrangian Method, Block Coordinate Descent, Low-rank Methods, Large-scale Optimization

Introduction

Problem setting.

In this paper, we consider linear semidefinite programs with arbitrary numbers of equality and inequality constraints, that is, optimization problems of the form

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{minimize}&\langle C,X\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&\mathcal{A}(X)=a,\\
&\mathcal{B}(X)\geq b,\\
&X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n},\end{array}

(SDP)

where
C\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
,
a\in\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{a}}
,
b\in\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{b}}
, and
m_{a},m_{b}\in\mdmathbb{N}_{0}
. We denote by
\langle A,B\rangle\coloneq\operatorname{trace}(B^{\top}A)
the trace inner product, and
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}
is the cone of
n\times n
symmetric positive semidefinite matrices. The linear operators
\mathcal{A}\colon\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{a}}
and
\mathcal{B}\colon\mathcal{S}^{n}\to\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{b}}
are defined componentwise by

\mathcal{A}(X)=\begin{pmatrix}\langle A_{1},X\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle A_{m_{a}},X\rangle\end{pmatrix},\hskip 18.49988pt\mathcal{B}(X)=\begin{pmatrix}\langle B_{1},X\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle B_{m_{b}},X\rangle\end{pmatrix},

for given matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m_{a}}
and
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m_{b}}
in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
.
Problem (
SDP
) can be extended to a semidefinite program involving several matrix variables. We will cover this generalization in Section
4.4
.

The dual problem corresponding to (
SDP
) is

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{maximize}&\langle a,y^{a}\rangle+\langle b,y^{b}\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&C-\mathcal{A}^{\top}(y^{a})-\mathcal{B}^{\top}(y^{b})=Z,\\
&y^{a}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{a}},\penalty\ y^{b}\in\mdmathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{b}},\\
&Z\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n},\end{array}

(DSDP)

where the adjoint operators
\mathcal{A}^{\top}\colon\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{a}}\to\mathcal{S}^{n}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\top}\colon\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{b}}\to\mathcal{S}^{n}
are given by

\mathcal{A}^{\top}(y^{a})=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{a}}y^{a}_{i}A_{i},\hskip 9.24994pt\mathcal{B}^{\top}(y^{b})=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{b}}y^{b}_{i}B_{i}.

Throughout the paper, we assume that the constraints in (SDP) are linearly independent and that both (SDP) and (DSDP) satisfy Slater’s condition, ensuring strong duality and attainment of the optimal value for both problems.

Motivation.

Semidefinite programming can be seen as a generalization of linear programming and is well-known as a powerful modeling technique. Although semidefinite programs are convex optimization problems, they can be challenging to solve in practice. Under suitable regularity conditions, well-posed SDPs can be solved in polynomial time to arbitrary precision using interior-point methods
deklerk2016on
. However, despite their favorable theoretical complexity, primal-dual interior-point methods are known to scale poorly in practice: their computational cost typically grows at least cubically in both the order of the matrix variable and the number of constraints; see, e.g.,
borchers2007implementation
.

To address scalability, a variety of first-order methods have been proposed in the literature, including eigenvalue optimization algorithms such as the spectral bundle method
helmberg2000spectral
, approaches based on the dual augmented Lagrangian
sun2020sdpnal
;
yang2015majorized
, related operator splitting methods like the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
povh2006boundary
;
wen2010alternating
, and pure penalty-based approaches for specially structured SDPs
malick2013on
, among others. These methods typically offer lower per-iteration costs and better scalability to large-scale problems compared to interior-point methods. However, they often involve a trade-off between computational efficiency and solution accuracy. First-order methods are well-suited for obtaining medium-precision solutions efficiently, but achieving high-precision solutions remains challenging. Moreover, many existing approaches are tailored to specifically structured SDPs and are primarily used in combinatorial optimization, where the SDP serves as a convex relaxation of an NP-hard problem. Extending these methods to broader classes of SDPs is nontrivial.

Another important class of methods seeks to exploit the low-rank structure that is often present in semidefinite programs. It has been shown in
barvinok1995problems
;
pataki1998rank
;
shapiro1982rank
that, for any SDP in standard form, there exists an optimal solution whose rank is bounded by the square root of twice the number of constraints. Motivated by this observation, some approaches avoid optimizing directly over the positive semidefinite matrix variable
X
and instead apply a change of variables via the factorization
X=V^{\top}V
, where
V\in\mdmathbb{R}^{k\times n}
with
k\ll n
. Optimization is then carried out with respect to
V
rather than
X
. This idea, commonly referred to as the Burer–Monteiro factorization approach (BM)
burer2003nonlinear
;
burer2005local
, significantly reduces the number of variables and enables more scalable algorithms. However, both the cost function and the affine constraints become quadratic in
V
, introducing nonconvexity into the problem.

For generic cost matrices, despite the nonconvexity of the formulation, it was shown in
boumal2020deterministic
that the BM approach successfully solves semidefinite programs with equality constraints and a chosen rank
k
of order
\sqrt{2m}
, where
m
is the number of constraints. This result was extended to arbitrary semidefinite programs with inequality constraints and multiple positive semidefinite matrix variables in
cifuentes2021burer
. However, the method can fail in nongeneric settings
ocarroll2022theburermonteiro
;
waldspurger2020rank
.

It was shown in
cifuentes2022polynomial
that the BM method admits polynomial-time guarantees after slight perturbation, and can solve SDPs to any desired accuracy.

Our contribution.

In this work, we propose a new method built on the Burer–Monteiro factorization. The method further decomposes the problem into smaller, lower-dimensional subproblems by applying a block coordinate descent scheme. These subproblems are small enough to be solved efficiently by gradient-based methods to any desired accuracy, exploiting warm starts.
The main contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce a new algorithm, called the
Augmented Mixing Method
, for solving SDPs with arbitrary numbers of equality and inequality constraints. The algorithm is particularly suited for problems with many inequality constraints, as these are handled directly without reformulation or the introduction of slack variables.

•

We develop a novel dynamic update strategy for the penalty parameter that reliably balances primal and dual progress. This enables the computation of highly accurate solutions on many SDP instances, often outperforming state-of-the-art interior-point methods in terms of precision.

•

The algorithm has low per-iteration cost, relying primarily on (sparse) matrix-vector multiplications. It avoids eigenvalue computations, projections onto the cone of positive semidefinite matrices, and matrix factorizations of the SDP data.

•

We present extensive numerical experiments across a variety of SDP types using the solver’s default parameters. These experiments demonstrate the algorithm’s effectiveness and scalability, especially on problems with many constraints. On large-scale instances, it often outperforms state-of-the-art interior-point solvers in both runtime and accuracy.

•

We provide an open-source Julia implementation. The solver is fully type-generic and supports arbitrary-precision arithmetic. Numerical experiments confirm that it remains efficient and accurate when using extended floating-point precision.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first solver based on the Burer–Monteiro factorization to combine these features in a unified and practical framework.

2
Relation to Other Methods

The
Augmented Mixing Method
combines and extends ideas from two prominent approaches for solving semidefinite programs: the Burer–Monteiro factorization framework
burer2003nonlinear
;
burer2005local
and its implementation
SDPLR
, as well as the
mixing method
introduced in
wang2018mixing
. While the mixing method is highly specialized and limited to diagonally constrained SDPs,
SDPLR
is designed to solve equality-constrained semidefinite programs in standard form. Both approaches share the idea of employing a low-rank factorization. In this section, we briefly recall the core ideas behind
SDPLR
and the mixing method.

2.1
The Burer–Monteiro approach and
SDPLR

Burer and Monteiro
burer2003nonlinear
;
burer2005local
proposed solving SDPs in standard form

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{minimize}&\langle C,X\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&\mathcal{A}(X)=a,\\
&X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n},\end{array}

(1)

by introducing a low-rank factorization
X=V^{\top}V
using a rectangular matrix
V\in\mdmathbb{R}^{k\times n}
. This leads to the reformulated problem

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{minimize}&\langle C,V^{\top}V\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&\mathcal{A}(V^{\top}V)=a,\\
&V\in\mdmathbb{R}^{k\times n}.\end{array}

(2)

It is well known from
barvinok1995problems
;
pataki1998rank
;
shapiro1982rank
that (
1
) and (
2
) are equivalent if
k\geq\lceil\sqrt{2m}\rceil
, where
m
is the number of constraints.

The approach of
burer2003nonlinear
;
burer2005local
combines two ideas: first, they apply a standard augmented Lagrangian method to (
2
), solving the subproblems using a limited-memory BFGS method. Second, they use a dynamic strategy for updating the rank
k
, starting with a small value and increasing it at runtime only if necessary. Together, these ideas form the basis of the solver
SDPLR
. It proved successful on some large-scale instances of (
1
) that were difficult for other solvers at the time.

However, a known limitation of
SDPLR
is that it typically yields only moderately accurate primal feasible solutions, and the associated dual solutions are sometimes far from optimal. This shortcoming arises because
SDPLR
uses a standard augmented Lagrangian approach that does not explicitly target finding an accurate primal-dual pair. In contrast, the Augmented Mixing Method addresses this issue by adopting and enhancing the augmented Lagrangian scheme. In this scheme, subproblems are solved efficiently to any desired accuracy using a block coordinate descent strategy. Paired with a novel dynamic update scheme for the penalty parameter, the Augmented Mixing Method is able to compute high-accuracy primal-dual solutions. However, unlike
SDPLR
, our method does not employ a dynamic rank update strategy for adjusting the rank
k
during runtime.

2.2
The Mixing Method

The
mixing method
was proposed in
wang2018mixing
to solve diagonally constrained SDPs of the form

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{maximize}&\langle C,X\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&X_{ii}=1,\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\dots,n,\\
&X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n},\end{array}

(3)

which includes the well-known Goemans–Williamson SDP relaxation for the Max-Cut problem. Applying the Burer–Monteiro factorization
X=V^{\top}V
with a column-wise stored matrix
V=(v_{1}\mid\cdots\mid v_{n})\in\mdmathbb{R}^{k\times n}
leads to the reformulated problem

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{maximize}&\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}C_{ij}v_{i}^{\top}v_{j}\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&\lVert v_{i}\rVert_{2}=1,\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\dots,n,\\
&v_{i}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{k}.\end{array}

(4)

The mixing method then applies a block coordinate ascent approach with respect to the columns
v_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Fixing all columns but
v_{i}
, Problem (
4
) reduces to

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{maximize}&g^{\top}v_{i}\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&\lVert v_{i}\rVert_{2}=1,\\
&v_{i}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{k},\end{array}

(5)

where
g=\sum_{j\neq i}C_{ij}v_{j}
. Although this subproblem remains nonconvex, it has a closed-form solution for
v_{i}
: specifically,
v_{i}^{\ast}=g/\lVert g\rVert_{2}
when
g\neq 0
.

Under a mild nondegeneracy assumption and for
k>\sqrt{2n}
, Wang, Chang, and Kolter
wang2018mixing
establish local linear convergence of a block coordinate ascent scheme using this closed-form update in a cyclic fashion and starting from a random feasible point. A key feature of the mixing method is that it always maintains primal feasibility and guarantees strict monotonic improvement of the objective value. In addition, approximate dual multipliers can be constructed from any primal iterate, and their sequence also converges to the optimum
wang2018mixing
. Extensions and refinements of the mixing method for very large-scale problems have been proposed in
chen2023burermonteiro
;
erdogdu2019convergence
;
kim2021momentuminspired
.

As in the mixing method, our proposed algorithm updates only a single column of
V
at a time. However, since no closed-form solution is available in our setting, we use gradient-based methods to perform these column updates. Nevertheless, the computational effort required for each column update remains low, allowing the subproblems to be solved to any desired precision.

3
The Augmented Mixing Method

In this section, we introduce our new algorithm, the
Augmented Mixing Method
.
The Augmented Mixing Method shares with the mixing method the use of a block coordinate descent scheme, resulting in small subproblems that can be efficiently solved to high accuracy. At the same time, it shares with
SDPLR
the use of an augmented Lagrangian framework, which provides direct access to the dual variables. However, the Augmented Mixing Method is specifically designed to compute high-accuracy primal-dual solutions and extends the applicability of low-rank approaches to semidefinite programs with general affine constraints, including a large number of inequality constraints.

3.1
Low-rank reformulation

We reformulate (
SDP
) via the Burer–Monteiro factorization, which allows us to eliminate the positive semidefiniteness constraint on the matrix variable
X
. Specifically, we introduce a rectangular matrix
V\in\mdmathbb{R}^{k\times n}
for some
k\in\mdmathbb{N}
and set
X=V^{\top}V
. For sufficiently large
k
, (
SDP
) is then equivalent to

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{minimize}&\langle C,V^{\top}V\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&\mathcal{A}(V^{\top}V)=a,\\
&\mathcal{B}(V^{\top}V)\geq b,\\
&V\in\mdmathbb{R}^{k\times n}.\end{array}

(BM-SDP)

While (
SDP
) is a convex optimization problem, the reformulated problem (
BM-SDP
) is generally nonconvex due to the quadratic nature of both the objective and the constraints. In accordance with the
Barvinok–Pataki bound

barvinok1995problems
;
pataki1998rank
, we choose

k=\min\left(n,\left\lceil\sqrt{2(m_{a}+m_{b})}\right\rceil\right)

(6)

throughout the Augmented Mixing Method.

3.2
Augmented Lagrangian and its gradient

We adopt the framework of the augmented Lagrangian method, originally developed for equality-constrained problems
hestenes1969multiplier
;
powell1969method
. To handle inequality constraints in (
BM-SDP
), we follow the extension proposed in
rockafellar1973multiplier
. This avoids the introduction of explicit slack variables and keeps the number of optimization variables manageable.

The
augmented Lagrangian
associated with (
BM-SDP
) is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(V,y^{a},y^{b};\mu)\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\langle C,V^{\top}V\rangle+\langle y^{a},a-\mathcal{A}(V^{\top}V)\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|a-\mathcal{A}(V^{\top}V)\|_{2}^{2}

(7)

\displaystyle+\langle y^{b},[b-\mathcal{B}(V^{\top}V)]_{+}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|[b-\mathcal{B}(V^{\top}V)]_{+}\|_{2}^{2}.

Here,
y^{a}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{a}}
and
y^{b}\in\mdmathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{b}}
are the dual variables associated with the equality and inequality constraints, respectively. The scalar
\mu>0
is the penalty parameter.

The augmented Lagrangian (
7
) can also be written in the equivalent form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(V,y^{a},y^{b};\mu)={}

\displaystyle\langle C,V^{\top}V\rangle+\langle y^{a},a-\mathcal{A}(V^{\top}V)\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|a-\mathcal{A}(V^{\top}V)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\mu}\left\lVert\left[y^{b}+\mu\left(b-\mathcal{B}(V^{\top}V)\right)\right]_{+}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}-\frac{1}{2\mu}\left\lVert y^{b}\right\rVert^{2}_{2},

see, e.g., Section 5.4.2 in
geiger2002theorie
. Moreover, note that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2\mu}\left\lVert\left[y^{b}+\mu\left(b-\mathcal{B}(V^{\top}V)\right)\right]_{+}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}-\frac{1}{2\mu}\left\lVert y^{b}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\\
&=\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{j\in I}(b_{j}-\langle B_{j},V^{\top}V\rangle)^{2}+\sum_{j\in I}y_{j}^{b}(b_{j}-\langle B_{j},V^{\top}V\rangle)-\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{j\in I^{c}}(y_{j}^{b})^{2},\end{split}

where the index sets
I
and
I^{c}
are defined as

I\coloneqq\left\{j\in[m_{b}]\colon y^{b}_{j}+\mu\left(b_{j}-\langle B_{j},V^{\top}V\rangle\right)>0\right\},\hskip 18.49988ptI^{c}\coloneqq[m_{b}]\setminus I.

Putting everything together, we obtain the expanded form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(V,y^{a},y^{b};\mu)={}

\displaystyle\langle C,V^{\top}V\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{j\in[m_{a}]}(a_{j}-\langle A_{j},V^{\top}V\rangle)^{2}+\sum_{j\in[m_{a}]}y_{j}^{a}(a_{j}-\langle A_{j},V^{\top}V\rangle)

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{j\in I}(b_{j}-\langle B_{j},V^{\top}V\rangle)^{2}+\sum_{j\in I}y_{j}^{b}(b_{j}-\langle B_{j},V^{\top}V\rangle)-\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{j\in I^{c}}(y_{j}^{b})^{2}.

The augmented Lagrangian is continuously differentiable, and its gradient with respect to
V
is given by

\nabla_{V}\mathcal{L}(V,y^{a},y^{b};\mu)=2V\left(C-\sum_{j\in[m_{a}]}\left[y^{a}_{j}+\mu\left(a_{j}-\langle A_{j},V^{\top}V\rangle\right)\right]A_{j}-\sum_{j\in I}\left[y^{b}_{j}+\mu\left(b_{j}-\langle B_{j},V^{\top}V\rangle\right)\right]B_{j}\right).

(8)

Note that not all inequality constraints are relevant for computing the gradient (
8
), only those in the index set
I
contribute.

Following the standard augmented Lagrangian approach, an approximate minimizer of (
BM-SDP
) can be computed by initializing
V
,
y^{a}
,
y^{b}
, and
\mu
, then minimizing the augmented Lagrangian for fixed
y^{a}
,
y^{b}
, and
\mu
using the gradient (
8
), and subsequently updating
y^{a}
,
y^{b}
, and
\mu
iteratively until a suitable stopping criterion is satisfied. However, using this approach would require solving a sequence of subproblems in
kn
variables, for which it is challenging to obtain high-accuracy solutions.

We employ a
block coordinate descent
strategy to reduce the effort of solving the subproblems at each step, described in the subsequent section.

3.3
Column updates

At each iteration of the Augmented Mixing Method, all columns except one are fixed, and the augmented Lagrangian is minimized with respect to the selected column, which is then updated accordingly. In this way, each subproblem involves only
k
variables.

To formalize this, we write
\overline{V}(v_{i})
for the
k\times n
matrix obtained by keeping all columns of
V
fixed except for the
i
th column, which is treated as a variable. That is,
\overline{V}(v_{i})=(v_{1}\mid\cdots\mid v_{i}\mid\cdots\mid v_{n})
. When updating column
v_{i}
of
V
, the new value is computed by solving

v_{i}\leftarrow\argmin_{v_{i}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{k}}\mathcal{L}(\overline{V}(v_{i}),y^{a},y^{b};\mu),

(9)

where the dual variables
y^{a}
,
y^{b}
, the penalty parameter
\mu
, and all other columns of
V
are held fixed.

Subproblem (
9
) is a nonlinear optimization problem in
k
variables with a continuously differentiable objective function. According to (
8
), the partial derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
v_{i}
is given by

\begin{split}\nabla_{v_{i}}\mathcal{L}(\overline{V}(v_{i}),y^{a},y^{b};\mu)=&2\overline{V}(v_{i})\Bigg(C_{(i)}-\sum_{j\in[m_{a}]}\left[y^{a}_{j}+\mu\left(a_{j}-\langle A_{j},\overline{V}(v_{i})^{\top}\overline{V}(v_{i})\rangle\right)\right](A_{j})_{(i)}\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt-\sum_{j\in I}\left[y^{b}_{j}+\mu\left(b_{j}-\langle B_{j},\overline{V}(v_{i})^{\top}\overline{V}(v_{i})\rangle\right)\right](B_{j})_{(i)}\Bigg).\end{split}

(10)

To approximately solve subproblem (
9
), we employ a limited-memory BFGS method, warm-started from the previous iterate
v_{i}^{\textrm{start}}
. In early iterations, when high accuracy is not yet critical, we adopt an inexact augmented Lagrangian approach that accepts approximate minimizers.
Specifically, we terminate the subproblem solver and accept the current
v_{i}
as an approximate solution to (
9
) as soon as the following stopping criterion is satisfied:

\lVert\nabla_{v_{i}}\mathcal{L}(\overline{V}(v_{i}),y^{a},y^{b};\mu)\rVert_{\infty}<\max\left\{\varepsilon,\,\delta\lVert\nabla_{v_{i}}\mathcal{L}(\overline{V}(v_{i}^{\textrm{start}}),y^{a},y^{b};\mu)\rVert_{\infty}\right\},

(11)

where
\varepsilon,\delta>0
are predetermined parameters. We use the default values
\varepsilon=\delta=0.01
.

3.4
Dual and penalty parameter update

After each column has been updated, the dual variables
y^{a}
and
y^{b}
are updated using the standard augmented Lagrangian rules

y^{a}\leftarrow y^{a}+p\mu(a-\mathcal{A}(V^{\top}V)),\hskip 9.24994pty^{b}\leftarrow\left[y^{b}+p\mu(b-\mathcal{B}(V^{\top}V))\right]_{+},

(12)

where we use a default value of
p=1
for the dual step size. Although this choice is used in all experiments in Section
5
, our implementation allows users to specify a different
p
if desired (see Section
4.7
).

While the update of the dual variables is straightforward, updating the penalty parameter
\mu
is crucial and has a strong impact on the performance of augmented Lagrangian methods.
In many implementations of augmented Lagrangian methods,
\mu
is either kept constant or increased monotonically, but rarely decreased. In contrast, we found that adjusting
\mu
in both directions, depending on the progress in primal feasibility, is essential to obtain high-accuracy primal-dual solutions. A different penalty parameter update rule that allows for reductions is used in
wang2025solving
.

After each full sweep over all columns of
V
, we define the active index set of the inequality constraints as

\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\left\{j\in[m_{b}]\;\colon\;b_{j}-\langle B_{j},V_{\mathrm{new}}^{\top}V_{\mathrm{new}}\rangle\geq 0\text{ or }y_{j}^{b}>0\right\},

and introduce the projection operator
P_{\mathcal{I}}\colon\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{b}}\to\mdmathbb{R}^{\mathcal{I}}
,
P_{\mathcal{I}}(u)\coloneqq(u_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
, which removes all inactive components.
We define the ratio

\texttt{ratio}\coloneqq\frac{\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}a-\mathcal{A}(X_{\text{new}})\\
P_{\mathcal{I}}\left(b-\mathcal{B}(X_{\text{new}})\right)\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}}{\mu\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{A}(X_{\text{new}}-X_{\text{old}})\\
P_{\mathcal{I}}\left(\mathcal{B}(X_{\text{new}}-X_{\text{old}})\right)\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{2}},

(13)

where
X_{\text{old}}=V_{\text{old}}^{\top}V_{\text{old}}
and
X_{\text{new}}=V_{\text{new}}^{\top}V_{\text{new}}
denote the primal iterate before and after the most recent primal update, respectively. We aim to keep
ratio
close to
1
throughout our algorithm.

Assuming that the iterates move directly toward feasibility, meaning that
\mathcal{A}(X_{\text{new}}-X_{\text{old}})
is a scalar multiple of
\mathcal{A}(X_{\text{new}})-a
(and analogously for inequalities), the primal infeasibility decreases geometrically when
ratio
is close to
1
. In such cases, each iteration reduces the primal residual by a factor of
1-\mu^{-1}
. This ensures that we progress toward primal feasibility quickly enough to keep the number of iterations reasonable, while at the same time avoiding convergence to primal feasibility too early. This is important because once the constraints
\mathcal{A}(X)=a
and
\mathcal{B}(X)\geq b
are nearly satisfied, the dual variables are barely updated, limiting further improvement in dual feasibility.

If
ratio
exceeds the prescribed upper threshold
rat_max
, we increase
\mu
by the factor
\tau
; if it falls below the lower threshold
rat_min
, we decrease
\mu
by the factor
1/\tau
. Otherwise,
\mu
remains unchanged. Our update rule is:

\mu\leftarrow\begin{cases}\tau\mu,&\text{if }\texttt{ratio}>\texttt{rat\_max},\\
\mu/\tau,&\text{if }\texttt{ratio}<\texttt{rat\_min},\\
\mu,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(14)

We use the default values
\texttt{rat\_min}=0.8
,
\texttt{rat\_max}=1.2
, and
\tau=1.03
.

4
Extensions, Implementation Details, and Software

This section outlines several extensions and implementation details of the Augmented Mixing Method. In particular, we discuss data scaling, update order strategies, stopping criteria, support for multi-block SDPs, high-precision computation via warm-starting, and configurable parameters available to the user.
We also showcase two tiny examples on which our method has difficulties in solving them due to getting stuck in a local maximum or exhibiting extremely slow convergence.

4.1
Scaling

In augmented Lagrangian methods, it is essential to scale the problem data to ensure that the algorithm balances progress between minimizing the objective and improving feasibility. We therefore apply the following automatic scaling procedure to the input data.

First, each constraint matrix, as well as the cost matrix
C
, is normalized by its Frobenius norm:

\tilde{A}_{i}=\frac{A_{i}}{\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{F}},\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\ldots,m_{a},\hskip 18.49988pt\tilde{B}_{i}=\frac{B_{i}}{\lVert B_{i}\rVert_{F}},\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\ldots,m_{b},\hskip 18.49988pt\tilde{C}=\frac{C}{\lVert C\rVert_{F}}.

Next, the right-hand side vectors are scaled accordingly:

\bar{a}_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{F}},\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\ldots,m_{a},\hskip 18.49988pt\bar{b}_{i}=\frac{b_{i}}{\lVert B_{i}\rVert_{F}},\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\ldots,m_{b}.

Finally, the resulting vectors are normalized to unit
\ell_{2}
-norm:

\tilde{a}=\frac{\bar{a}}{\lVert\bar{a}\rVert_{2}},\hskip 18.49988pt\tilde{b}=\frac{\bar{b}}{\lVert\bar{b}\rVert_{2}}.

The Augmented Mixing Method then solves the scaled problem defined by the cost matrix
\tilde{C}
, the constraint matrices
\tilde{A}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{A}_{m_{a}},\tilde{B}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{B}_{m_{b}}
, and the right-hand side vectors
\tilde{a},\tilde{b}
. The computed solution is subsequently rescaled to yield an approximate solution to the original problem.

4.2
Order of column updates

By default, the Augmented Mixing Method performs cyclic updates over the columns
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
of
V
, as described in Section
3.3
. However, this update order can be modified. For instance, the paper
erdogdu2019convergence
on block coordinate descent for the Max-Cut relaxation (
3
) explores several update strategies, including dynamic heuristics that select the next column at runtime based on recent progress.

In our setting, such approaches are less straightforward due to the augmented Lagrangian formulation, where the objective functions change over the course of the algorithm. Nevertheless, results from the literature on multi-block ADMM and block coordinate descent suggest that randomized update orders can improve convergence behavior, sometimes even ensuring convergence in expectation, whereas deterministic schemes may fail
cipolla2023alinear
;
mihic2021managing
;
sun2020ontheefficiency
. The paper
sun2020ontheefficiency
also discusses double-sweep strategies (i.e., forward and backward passes) which can also improve practical convergence in some cases.

Our implementation supports these variants through two boolean parameters:
shuffling
, which randomizes the column order in each outer iteration, and
double_sweep
, which enables forward and reverse sweeps.
In practice, we observe that a fixed cyclic order tends to yield the best performance in terms of iteration count and runtime for most problem instances.
Hence, both parameters are disabled by default and were not used in the numerical experiments reported in Section
5
.
However, our software allows users to activate these alternative strategies as needed.

4.3
Stopping criteria

In the following, we discuss the stopping criteria implemented in the Augmented Mixing Method. Note that the stopping criteria are applied to the scaled version of the SDP; see Section
4.1
. This means that the specific values of the error measures presented below will differ slightly from those on the user’s original (unscaled) SDP. To simplify notation, we write
C
instead of
\tilde{C}
and so on.

At any point during the Augmented Mixing Method, we have access to the current value of the primal matrix variable
X=V^{\top}V
, which is positive semidefinite by construction, as well as approximate dual variables
y^{a},y^{b}
with
y^{b}\geq 0
. An approximation of the dual slack matrix
Z
is not directly available and must be computed by projecting
C-\mathcal{A}^{\top}(y^{a})-\mathcal{B}^{\top}(y^{b})
onto the cone of positive semidefinite matrices:

Z=[C-\mathcal{A}^{\top}(y^{a})-\mathcal{B}^{\top}(y^{b})]_{+}.

(16)

Our stopping criteria are based on the KKT conditions for the primal-dual pair (
SDP
) and (
DSDP
). For given
X,Z\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}
,
y^{a}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{a}}
, and
y^{b}\in\mdmathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{b}}
, we define the error measures as

\displaystyle\texttt{pinf}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\max\left\{\lVert a-\mathcal{A}(X)\rVert_{\infty},\,\lVert[b-\mathcal{B}(X)]_{+}\rVert_{\infty}\right\}}{1+\max\left\{\lVert a\rVert_{\infty},\,\lVert b\rVert_{\infty}\right\}},

(17)

\displaystyle\texttt{gap}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\left|\langle C,X\rangle-(\langle a,y^{a}\rangle+\langle b,y^{b}\rangle)\right|}{1+\left|\langle C,X\rangle\right|+\left|\langle a,y^{a}\rangle+\langle b,y^{b}\rangle\right|},

\displaystyle\texttt{dinf}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\left\lVert C-\mathcal{A}^{\top}(y^{a})-\mathcal{B}^{\top}(y^{b})-Z\right\rVert_{F}}{1+\lVert C\rVert_{F}},

\displaystyle\texttt{compl}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\langle X,Z\rangle}{1+\left|\langle C,X\rangle\right|+\left|\langle a,y^{a}\rangle+\langle b,y^{b}\rangle\right|}.

The Augmented Mixing Method terminates if

\max\{\texttt{pinf},\,\texttt{gap},\,\texttt{dinf},\,\texttt{compl}\}<\texttt{tol}

(18)

holds, where
tol
is a user-specified parameter which defaults to
10^{-12}
.

Note, however, that the error measures (
17
) are only required for termination and are not needed during the execution of the algorithm. For example, the dynamic update strategy for the penalty parameter is based on a different quantity, namely the ratio defined in (
13
). Moreover, computing
Z
can be very time-consuming when using extended precision arithmetic. Therefore, we avoid computing the expensive projection in (
16
) too frequently and only compute
Z
when the solver appears close to convergence.

To implement this, the computation of
Z
is triggered only every
iters_Z
outer iterations of the Augmented Mixing Method and only if

\max\{\texttt{pinf},\,\texttt{gap},\,\texttt{compl}^{*}\}<\texttt{tol}

holds, where
\texttt{compl}^{*}
is an approximation of
compl
defined as

\texttt{compl}^{*}\coloneqq\frac{\lvert\langle X,C-\mathcal{A}^{\top}(y^{a})-\mathcal{B}^{\top}(y^{b})\rangle\rvert}{1+\left|\langle C,X\rangle\right|+\left|\langle a,y^{a}\rangle+\langle b,y^{b}\rangle\right|}.

The parameter
iters_Z
can be set by the user and has a default value of
50
. Moreover, users can also set a time limit or a maximum number of outer iterations to terminate the method early; see Section
4.7
for a full list of available parameters.

4.4
Handling multi-block SDPs

The Augmented Mixing Method also supports SDPs with multiple positive semidefinite matrix variables, i.e., problems of the form

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{minimize}&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}\langle C_{i},X_{i}\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mathcal{A}_{i}(X_{i})=a,\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mathcal{B}_{i}(X_{i})\geq b,\\
&X_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n_{i}},\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\ldots,q.\\
\end{array}

Here,
q\in\mdmathbb{N}
denotes the number of blocks, and
n_{1},\ldots,n_{q}\in\mdmathbb{N}
are the corresponding block sizes. For such SDPs, the Augmented Mixing Method maintains a factorization
X_{i}=V_{i}^{\top}V_{i}
for each block
i\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
, where each rectangular matrix
V_{i}
has dimensions
k_{i}\times n_{i}
. Similar to (
6
), the rank parameter
k_{i}
is chosen as

k_{i}=\min\left(n_{i},\left\lceil\sqrt{2(m_{a}+m_{b})}\right\rceil\right)

for all
i=1,\ldots,q
. Each outer iteration of the Augmented Mixing Method then consists of sequentially updating every column of every matrix
V_{i}
.

The initial penalty parameter is set to
\mu=\sqrt{\max\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{q}\}}
. Updating the penalty parameter and scaling the problem is handled analogously to the one-block case, and the error measures (
17
) are defined accordingly. Numerical results for multi-block SDPs are reported in Section
5.6
.

4.5
Simple examples where the method fails or stagnates

Our method does not successfully solve all semidefinite programs. Even in cases where Slater’s condition holds (i.e. both primal and dual problem are strictly feasible), convergence is not guaranteed. Below, we outline two primary obstacles we have identified.

Getting stuck in a local maximum.

Consider the following strictly feasible SDP involving two blocks of size
1\times 1
and its Burer–Monteiro factorization counterpart with
x_{i}=v_{i}^{2}
:

\begin{array}[]{rlcrl}\text{minimize}&x_{1}&=&\text{minimize}&v_{1}^{2}\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&x_{1}+x_{2}=2,&&\text{subject to}&v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}=2,\\
&x_{2}=1,&&&v_{2}^{2}=1,\\
&x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{1},&&&v_{1},v_{2}\in\mdmathbb{R}.\end{array}

(19)

The corresponding augmented Lagrangian is given by:

\mathcal{L}(v_{1},v_{2},y;\mu)=v_{1}^{2}+y_{1}(2-v_{1}^{2}-v_{2}^{2})+y_{2}(1-v_{2}^{2})+\frac{\mu}{2}(2-v_{1}^{2}-v_{2}^{2})^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{2}.

Suppose the current iterate consists of
v_{1}=0
,
v_{2}=\sqrt{1.5}
, and
y=(2,-2)^{\top}
. In this case, neither block update changes the values of
v_{1}
or
v_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(v_{1},\sqrt{1.5},y;\mu)

\displaystyle=v_{1}^{2}+y_{1}(0.5-v_{1}^{2})-0.5y_{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}(0.5-v_{1}^{2})^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}(0.5)^{2}

\displaystyle=2-v_{1}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}(0.5-v_{1}^{2})^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}(0.5)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(0,v_{2},y;\mu)

\displaystyle=y_{1}(2-v_{2}^{2})+y_{2}(1-v_{2}^{2})+\frac{\mu}{2}(2-v_{2}^{2})^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\mu}{2}(2-v_{2}^{2})^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{2}.

The first block remains at a local maximum of the augmented Lagrangian, and the second at a local minimum. The dual variable
y
keeps getting updated in the direction
(+1,-1)^{\top}
, and the penalty parameter
\mu>0
has no effect on this behavior. While this example is constructed to trigger failure, we observe that similar stagnation often occurs, though not always, when starting from random initializations. Notably, this issue is not caused by Problem (
19
) having multiple blocks: combining the two blocks into a single
2\times 2
matrix variable does not affect the outcome.

This type of stagnation, where iterates do not improve in feasibility, is frequently observed in SDPs whose objective depends only on a single
1\times 1
block. Such structures commonly arise in polynomial optimization relaxations, particularly in the moment–sums-of-squares hierarchy. A simple workaround is to randomly perturb the current iterate slightly before solving the inner problem. While such a random perturbation can prevent complete stalling on a given instance, we found that it did not enable the solver to succeed on any additional instances from our test set (see Section
5
) that it could not already solve.

Slow convergence.

The Augmented Mixing Method performs more than one million iterations to solve the following SDP to a relative accuracy of
10^{-8}
:

minimize

\displaystyle\left\langle X_{1},\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1&0\\
1&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right\rangle+X_{2}

subject to

\displaystyle\operatorname{trace}(X)+X_{2}=4,

\displaystyle\langle X_{1},J_{3}\rangle+X_{2}=4,

\displaystyle X_{1}\in\mathcal{S}^{3}_{+},\hskip 9.24994ptX_{2}\geq 0.

In this example, the primal iterates initially converge rapidly toward feasibility, but progress slows down significantly in later iterations. Our penalty parameter update strategy targets a geometric reduction of the primal residual by maintaining the value of
ratio
close to
1
. However, as primal feasibility improves, this strategy keeps increasing the penalty parameter
\mu
, even when such increases are no longer beneficial. As a result, the primal residual is driven even smaller than necessary, leading to extremely small dual updates.

4.6
Arbitrary-precision computation via warm-starting

Most semidefinite programming solvers rely on fixed-precision arithmetic, primarily because modern CPU architectures natively support certain floating-point types and because highly optimized BLAS and LAPACK routines are available for these types. Typically, double precision is used, which often limits the maximum achievable relative primal-dual accuracy of state-of-the-art interior-point methods to around
10^{-10}
. However, some applications require significantly higher accuracy, see e.g. the recent work
delaat2024optimalityuniquenessd4root
on the famous kissing number problem.

Our implementation of the Augmented Mixing Method is fully parametrized and can operate with any floating-point type supported by the Julia programming language. To efficiently compute arbitrary-precision solutions, we employ a two-stage warm-starting strategy:

1.

Initial solve using double precision.
We first solve the SDP using the
Float64
data type with the tolerance parameter
tol
set to
10^{-12}
.

2.

Refinement using extended precision.
We promote the solution to the extended precision type. The solver resumes the optimization process from this warm-start point until the user-specified tolerance is reached.

A key advantage of our implementation compared to other arbitrary-precision solvers is that the most computationally expensive operations, namely (sparse) matrix-vector multiplications, are relatively straightforward to optimize even for extended precision, unlike the more demanding computations required by interior-point methods.

In Section
5.7
, we present numerical results on randomly generated SDPs where the accuracy parameter
tol
in the refinement phase is set to
10^{-12}
.

4.7

Our implementation of the Augmented Mixing Method is open-source and written entirely in the Julia programming language. It is designed to be easily configurable and extensible, enabling users to apply the solver to a broad class of SDPs with varying structure and precision requirements.

Using the solver in practice requires constructing an object of type

specifies the index of the first inequality constraint. If the SDP has no inequality constraints, the argument
ineq_start
must be set to
length(b) + 1

Float64
). For multi-block SDPs, the lists
A

C

To solve the SDP with default parameters, call

5
Computational Experiments

In this section, we present extensive numerical results that demonstrate the performance of the Augmented Mixing Method using its default settings. We compare the Augmented Mixing Method with several state-of-the-art SDP solvers in terms of runtime and solution accuracy.

Computational setup

We conducted all experiments on a machine running Debian 12, equipped with an AMD EPYC Genoa processor clocked at 3.59 GHz and 1024 GB of RAM. In all experiments, the number of computation threads was limited to four. Our implementation of the Augmented Mixing Method is written in the Julia programming language
bezanson2017julia

To solve the subproblems (

of the Augmented Mixing Method to

after warm-starting. We provide
Hypatia
with the dual formulation of the SDP, as this is the solver’s preferred input form.

Format and interpretation of results.

For each class of SDP instances, we provide detailed tables summarizing the performance of the Augmented Mixing Method using the default tolerance
tol=1e-12
, including all four primal-dual error metrics from (
17
). If only one additional solver is included in an experiment, we also report that solver’s computation time and the maximum of its four primal-dual errors in the same table. In other cases, we compare solvers using performance profiles
dolan2002benchmarking
, comparing runtime and solution accuracy. These plots also include results of the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-6
, which are not shown in the tables.

All primal-dual errors are computed from the solutions returned by the respective solvers, using the error metrics defined in (
17
) and evaluated on the original, unscaled SDP. This ensures a fair comparison across solvers, independent of their internal stopping criteria or scaling strategies.

In the tables, the first four columns list the instance name, the matrix size
n
, and the number of equality and inequality constraints,
m_{a}
and
m_{b}
. The next three columns show the Augmented Mixing Method’s return status (
tol
if all primal-dual errors on the internally scaled problem fall below the specified tolerance
tol
;
iter
if the maximum number of outer iterations is reached;
time
if the time limit is exceeded), the elapsed wall-clock time in seconds, and the number of outer iterations performed. The final four columns report the primal-dual error metrics defined in (
17
). In some cases, two additional columns report the runtime and maximum error of another solver for comparison. For randomly generated SDP instances, the columns for
n
,
m_{a}
, and
m_{b}
are omitted, since these values are evident from the instance names.

The performance profile figures show two plots side by side. The left plot evaluates cumulative runtime performance: for each solver, it shows the fraction of instances for which the runtime is within a given factor (
x
-axis,
\log
scale) of the best runtime achieved on that instance. The right plot evaluates solution accuracy: it shows the fraction of instances where the maximum primal-dual error is within a given factor of the smallest such error across all solvers. In both cases, curves that rise more steeply and reach
1
more quickly indicate better performance. All performance profiles presented in this section were generated using the Julia package
BenchmarkProfiles.jl

orbanbenchmarkprofiles2023
.

5.2
Max-Cut problem

The Max-Cut problem in a simple undirected graph
G
with
n
vertices and weighted adjacency matrix
A
can be formulated as the quadratic unconstrained binary optimization

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{maximize}&\frac{1}{4}x^{\top}Lx\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&x\in\{-1,1\}^{n},\\
\end{array}

(20)

where
L=\operatorname{Diag}(Ae_{n})-A
denotes the Laplacian matrix of
G
. For
C=\frac{1}{4}L
, the basic SDP relaxation of (
20
) is given by (
3
). This relaxation can be solved very efficiently by specialized solvers using low-rank factorizations such as the mixing method
wang2018mixing
or its extensions in
chen2023burermonteiro
;
erdogdu2019convergence
;
kim2021momentuminspired
, and algorithms from the
Manopt
package
boumal2014manopt
. Since the Augmented Mixing Method is not competitive on this specific type of SDP, we omit computational results for the basic SDP relaxation of the Max-Cut problem.

However, it is well-known that the basic relaxation (
3
) can be strengthened by adding the following
4\binom{n}{3}
triangle inequalities

\begin{split}X_{ij}+X_{ik}+X_{jk}&\geq-1,\hskip 9.24994pt1\leq i<j<k\leq n,\\
X_{ij}-X_{ik}-X_{jk}&\geq-1,\hskip 9.24994pt1\leq i<j<k\leq n,\\
-X_{ij}+X_{ik}-X_{jk}&\geq-1,\hskip 9.24994pt1\leq i<j<k\leq n,\\
-X_{ij}-X_{ik}+X_{jk}&\geq-1,\hskip 9.24994pt1\leq i<j<k\leq n,\\
\end{split}

(21)

which are facet-defining for the Max-Cut polytope
deza1997geometry
. Table
2
shows numerical results of the Augmented Mixing Method on various instances of Max-Cut SDP relaxation with
n
between 60 and 250 in which all triangle inequalities (
21
) were included at once. These instances are either directly taken from the
Biq Mac Library

wiegele2007biqmaclibrary
or are generated in the same way using the graph generator
rudy

rinaldi1998rudy
. To enhance numerical stability and reduce the effect of scaling, we set the main diagonal in all SDPs to zero, as it contributes only a constant to the objective. We report results for the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-12
and the
SCS
solver in Table
2
. We exclude
MOSEK
and
SDPLR
here because neither is suited to handle the very large number of inequalities in these instances.

As the results in Table
2
demonstrate, the Augmented Mixing Method can handle SDPs with extremely large numbers of inequality constraints while still producing primal-dual solutions with high accuracy. It typically requires fewer than
2000
outer iterations, with most instances being solved in under 1000 iterations. On instances with
n\in\{60,80\}
, the
SCS
solver has a shorter runtime than the Augmented Mixing Method, but the opposite holds for larger instances. However,
SCS
generally returns solutions with maximum primal-dual errors between
10^{-6}
and
10^{-7}
. In contrast, the primal-dual errors of the Augmented Mixing Method are almost always below
10^{-9}
and often even smaller. Moreover, the method successfully terminates on two out of five instances with more than ten million inequality constraints. It reaches the time limit of two hours on the remaining three such large instances but still yields reasonably accurate solutions. On these large instances,
SCS
terminates only well after the time limit has already been exceeded and produces solutions with maximum primal-dual errors ranging roughly from
10^{-3}
to
10^{-6}
.

Nevertheless, the Augmented Mixing Method may fail to converge within the prescribed time limit and maximum number of iterations in rare cases. In particular, some smaller instances of type ‘pw09’ seem to pose difficulties, as the method reaches the time limit without producing even moderately accurate solutions. We suspect that this behavior is due to our scaling strategy.

5.3
Bounding the stability number of a graph

Given a simple undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with
\lvert V\rvert=n
, the
stability number
of
G
can be computed as the optimal value of

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{maximize}&e_{n}^{\top}x\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&x_{i}x_{j}=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\{i,j\}\in E,\\
&x\in\{0,1\}^{n}.\\
\end{array}

A well-known SDP relaxation to get an upper bound on the stability number is given by

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{maximize}&\langle J_{n},X\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&X_{ij}=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\{i,j\}\in E,\\
&\operatorname{trace}(X)=1,\\
&X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}.\\
\end{array}

(22)

The optimal value of this SDP relaxation is also known as the Lovász number (or Lovász theta function) of
G
. This SDP relaxation can be strengthened by adding nonnegativity constraints on entries of
X
, resulting in the doubly nonnegative (DNN) relaxation

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{maximize}&\langle J_{n},X\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&X_{ij}=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\{i,j\}\in E,\\
&X_{ij}\geq 0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\{i,j\}\notin E,\\
&\operatorname{trace}(X)=1,\\
&X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}.\\
\end{array}

(23)

Tables
3
and
4
show numerical results of the Augmented Mixing Method applied to the basic SDP relaxation (
22
) and the strengthened DNN relaxation (
23
) on selected instances from the second DIMACS implementation challenge
DIMACS1992
. For the DNN relaxation, we exclude instances with
n
larger than
300
.

With a few exceptions in Table
3
, the Augmented Mixing Method is able to compute high-accuracy primal-dual solutions of the basic SDP relaxation (
22
) in reasonable time. The primal-dual errors are almost always smaller than
10^{-10}
, and in some cases significantly smaller. Five instances are not solved by the method: the time limit is reached on three, and the maximum number of outer iterations on two.

For the DNN relaxation (
23
), shown in Table
4
, the primal-dual errors are again typically below
10^{-10}
and occasionally even below
10^{-12}
. As with the basic SDP relaxation, five instances are not solved by the Augmented Mixing Method, although on one of them the maximum primal-dual error is still smaller than
10^{-9}
. Interestingly, the solving times for the DNN relaxation (
23
) are not much higher than for the basic SDP relaxation (
22
), despite the presence of significantly more affine constraints. In some cases, the DNN relaxation is even solved faster and with higher accuracy than its basic counterpart.

The left plot in Figure
1
, corresponding to the basic SDP relaxation (
22
), shows that all solvers exhibit similar runtimes on the majority of instances. However, the version of the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-6
is the fastest solver on about 75% of all instances, while the version with
tol=1e-12
is consistently the slowest. Nonetheless, the right plot in Figure
1
reveals that the Augmented Mixing Method with the default setting
tol=1e-12
produces the most accurate solutions on over 75% of all instances. Only
MOSEK
achieves comparable accuracy and successfully solves all instances considered. In contrast, the accuracy of the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-6
is similar to
SCS
, and
SDPLR
clearly yields the least accurate solutions overall.

Regarding the DNN relaxation (
23
), the solution accuracy comparison in Figure
2
is nearly identical to that of the basic SDP relaxation (
22
) in Figure
1
. The runtime plot, however, reveals significant differences.
MOSEK
and
SDPLR
are by far the slowest solvers, often by orders of magnitude. On almost all instances, the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-6
is the fastest solver, only occasionally outperformed by
SCS
. Notably, the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-12
is substantially faster than
MOSEK
on the instances it solves, while producing solutions of similar or even higher accuracy. This highlights the method’s favorable trade-off between runtime and accuracy, as demanding higher precision leads to only a modest increase in runtime.

5.4
Single-row facility layout problem

The single-row facility layout problem (SRFLP) was first introduced in
simmons1969onedimensional
and asks to arrange
n
facilities of given lengths on a straight line so that a weighted sum of distances between all pairs of facilities is minimized. It belongs to the class of linear quadratic ordering problems and can be formulated as a binary quadratic problem with both a quadratic objective and quadratic equality constraints, first done in
anjos2005asemidefinite
. From this formulation and a suitable choice of the cost matrix
C\in\mathcal{S}^{\binom{n}{2}}
, the SDP relaxation

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{minimize}&\langle C,X\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&X_{ij,jk}-X_{ij,ik}-X_{ik,jk}=-1,\hskip 9.24994pt1\leq i<j<k\leq n,\\
&X_{ij,ij}=1,\hskip 9.24994pt1\leq i<j\leq n,\\
&X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{\binom{n}{2}},\\
\end{array}

(24)

was derived in
anjos2005asemidefinite
. Note that each row (and column) of
X
corresponds to an ordered pair
(i,j)
of facilities. Thus, for an SRFLP instance with
n
facilities, the SDP relaxation (
24
) involves a positive semidefinite matrix variable of order
\binom{n}{2}
and has
\binom{n}{3}+\binom{n}{2}
equality constraints. To strengthen the relaxation, it was proposed in
anjos2008computing
to add the triangle inequalities (
21
). Note that the number of triangle inequalities grows as
\mathcal{O}(n^{6})
in the SDP relaxation (
24
) and some of them are already implied by the equality constraints.

For the strengthened SDP relaxation with triangle inequalities, we compare the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-12
only to the
SCS
solver, as the number of inequality constraints exceeds the capacity of the other solvers. Consequently, performance profiles are provided only for the basic SDP relaxation (
24
), where multiple solvers could be applied.

Table
5
shows numerical results of the Augmented Mixing Method on the basic SDP relaxation (
24
), and Table
6
reports results on the strengthened relaxation including all nonredundant triangle inequalities. The ‘O’ instances in these tables are taken from
obata1979quadratic
, the ‘S’ instances from
sarker1989theamoebic
, the ‘Y’ instances from
yu2003directional
, the ‘ste’ instances from
anjos2009provably
, the ‘N40’ instances from
hungerlander2013semidefinite
, and the remaining ‘N’ instances from
nugent1968anexperimental
. For the strengthened relaxation, we consider only instances with up to
n=300
.

As shown in Table
5
, the Augmented Mixing Method successfully computes high-accuracy solutions on all instances of the basic SDP relaxation (
24
), with all primal-dual errors below
10^{-10}
on nearly all instances. Although the time limit is reached on some of the largest instances, the resulting solutions still exhibit small primal-dual errors (at most
10^{-7}
, typically much smaller). The number of outer iterations ranges from roughly
1000
on smaller instances to around
45\,000
on larger ones, generally increasing with problem size.

The performance profiles in Figure
3
indicate that
MOSEK
is the fastest solver on most instances of the basic relaxation, while the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-12
is the slowest. However, the version with
tol=1e-6
is the second fastest on the majority of instances. In terms of solution accuracy, the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-12
outperforms all other solvers, including
MOSEK
. All other solvers yield solutions of significantly lower accuracy. These results again demonstrate the flexibility of the Augmented Mixing Method to trade runtime for precision.

For the SDP relaxation with triangle inequalities, Table
6
shows that
SCS
has shorter runtime on smaller instances, but often exceeds the time limit on larger ones. The Augmented Mixing Method performs fewer than
2000
outer iterations in total and often far fewer, in particular fewer than for the basic relaxation. However, on the largest instances with
n=300
and nearly 18 million inequality constraints, only about 200 iterations are performed before hitting the time limit, leading to lower-accuracy solutions. The same behavior is observed for
SCS
. On all other instances, the Augmented Mixing Method achieves substantially better primal-dual accuracy than
SCS
, often by several orders of magnitude. Most notably, it solves an instance with
n=276
and nearly 14 million inequality constraints to a relative precision of almost
10^{-11}
.

5.5
Edge expansion problem

For a given simple, undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with
\lvert V\rvert=n\geq 3
, the edge expansion of
G
is defined as the optimal value of

\min_{\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq V}\frac{\lvert\{ij\in E\,\colon\,i\in S,\,j\in V\setminus S\}\rvert}{\min\{\lvert S\rvert,\lvert V\setminus S\rvert\}}.

An SDP relaxation for this problem is given by

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{minimize}&\langle L,X\rangle\\[4.64996pt]
\text{subject to}&\operatorname{trace}(X)=1,\\
&\operatorname{diag}(X)=x,\\
&\frac{1}{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\leq\rho\leq 1,\\
&\langle J_{n},X\rangle\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor,\\
&\begin{pmatrix}X&x\\
x^{\top}&\rho\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n+1},\hskip 9.24994pt\rho\in\mdmathbb{R},\\
\end{array}

(25)

and a strengthened relaxation is obtained by adding nonnegativity constraints on
x
and
X
, resulting in a DNN relaxation; see
hrga2024connectivity
.

All instances of the edge expansion problem considered here were previously used in
gupte2024edge
. These include graphs of grlex and grevlex polytopes from
gupte2018ondantzig
, instances from the tenth DIMACS implementation challenge
DIMACS1992
, and network graphs from the online network repository
peixoto2020thenetzschleuder
.

Tables
7
and
8
show that the Augmented Mixing Method successfully solves almost all instances of both the basic and the strengthened DNN relaxations for the edge expansion problem. Notably, the largest instance ‘celegans_metabolic’ with
n=454
and more than
100\,000
inequality constraints is solved in about
4552
seconds. The primal-dual errors are typically below
10^{-10}
; two exceptions from the DNN relaxation are solved with reduced accuracy of about
10^{-6}
due to time or iteration limits.

Figures
4
and
5
show the performance profiles comparing our selection of SDP solvers on both the basic SDP relaxation and the DNN relaxation. We note that
SDPLR
could not be run on the DNN relaxation of the instance ‘celegans_metabolic’ because the modeling language
JuMP
failed to pass the problem to
SDPLR
. For this instance, we conservatively set the runtime of
SDPLR
to the time limit of two hours and assigned all primal-dual errors a value of 1. Doing so does not influence the overall conclusions drawn from the performance profile in Figure
5
.

The plots for the basic SDP relaxation (
25
) in Figure
4
show that
MOSEK
is the fastest solver on most instances, closely followed by
SDPLR
. Both versions of the Augmented Mixing Method are the slowest solvers overall. However, when considering solution accuracy, the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-12
consistently produces the most accurate solutions, followed by
MOSEK
. The other solvers produce significantly less accurate solutions.

Regarding the DNN relaxation covered in Figure
5
,
SCS
is the fastest solver overall, followed by
MOSEK
and the Augmented Mixing Method with
tol=1e-6
. However, the version with
tol=1e-12
produces the most accurate solutions on more than 75% of all instances. Only
MOSEK
remains competitive in accuracy, but it was unable to solve the largest instance ‘celegans_metabolic’ due the time limit.

We also test the Augmented Mixing Method on randomly generated SDPs, including instances with multiple positive semidefinite matrix variables. For simplicity, we only consider SDPs with equality constraints. We generate these SDPs following the approach in

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented the
Augmented Mixing Method
, a novel SDP solver that combines an inexact augmented Lagrangian method with a block coordinate descent approach. The computational results in Section
5
demonstrate the strong practical performance of the Augmented Mixing Method using its default settings across various types of SDPs. It frequently yields solutions of significantly higher accuracy than those produced by state-of-the-art interior-point methods. Accurate solutions are even obtained for SDPs with numbers of affine constraints exceeding the capabilities of interior-point methods and most first-order methods.

A key feature of the Augmented Mixing Method is the direct handling of inequality constraints without introducing slack variables. Another important component for achieving high-accuracy primal-dual solutions is the dynamic penalty parameter update scheme described in Section
3.4
. Compared to other solvers based on the low-rank factorization framework, this update scheme makes the initial choice of the penalty parameter less critical and leads to balanced progress of both the primal and dual variables. In contrast, other solvers may produce approximate primal feasible solutions more quickly, but often yield dual solutions of only moderate accuracy. This advantage is reflected in the success of the Augmented Mixing Method in Section
5.7
when solving SDPs using extended floating-point arithmetic. Apart from the external L-BFGS solver used for subproblems, most of the computational time is spent performing (sparse) matrix-vector multiplications, making the method well-suited for use with extended precision arithmetic.

Future research.

Despite its strong practical performance, further investigation into the convergence theory of the Augmented Mixing Method is warranted. Section
4.5
shows that without additional assumptions convergence cannot be guaranteed in general. Moreover, we believe that a more sophisticated scaling strategy than the one presented in Section
4.1
could further improve practical performance. We have also refrained from implementing a dynamic update strategy for the maximum rank
k
in the Burer–Monteiro factorization, which is commonly used by other low-rank solvers to improve scalability, especially with respect to the matrix dimension
n
. In our experience, the Augmented Mixing Method often becomes trapped in local minima if the rank
k
is not chosen as in (
6
), even when the optimal solution of the SDP has significantly lower rank.
Finally, a potential enhancement would be to implement an active-set style strategy in the Augmented Mixing Method to ignore inactive inequality constraints during iterations and reconsider them only when they become relevant. This could be particularly useful in the context of cutting-plane approaches based on SDP relaxations for combinatorial optimization problems.
