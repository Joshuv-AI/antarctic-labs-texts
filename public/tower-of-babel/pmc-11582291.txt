Intravenous Versus Oral Omadacycline or Linezolid for Acute Bacterial Skin and Skin Infections: A post hoc Analysis of the OASIS Trials
Authors: George D Rodriguez, Nathan Warren, Roman Yashayev, Surya Chitra, Maria Amodio-Groton, Kelly Wright
Published: 2024

Introduction

The 2014 Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) guidelines for the management of skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs) recommend intravenous (IV) antibiotics if there is a concern regarding systemic infection and inflammatory response [1]. However, IV therapy impacts length of hospital stay and treatment costs, and hospital avoidance could lead certain patients to prefer oral (PO) antibiotic therapy, particularly when bioequivalent oral products are available, and for patients with few or no complications or comorbidities. Switching therapies is often a concern for both providers and patients, and the preference for IV versus PO therapy should be tailored to the patient and the properties of the antibiotic [1].

Omadacycline is an aminomethylcycline antibiotic designed to overcome the most common tetracycline resistance mechanisms, bacterial ribosomal protection proteins and tetracycline-specific efflux pumps [2], and has in vitro activity against a broad spectrum of pathogens that commonly cause acute bacterial skin and skin-structure infections (ABSSSI) [2–4]. It is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of adults with ABSSSI caused by select gram-positive and -negative pathogens and is available in an IV and as a bioequivalent PO formulation, both administered once daily [3–5]. Linezolid is an oxazolidinone antibiotic that is approved by the FDA for the treatment of complicated skin and skin structure infections caused by gram-positive bacteria and is also available in an IV and as a bioequivalent PO formulation, both administered twice daily [6, 7]. The 2014 IDSA guidelines recommend linezolid and tetracyclines as oral treatments for infections with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) as the causative agent [1, 8]. The 2014 IDSA guidelines predated the availability of omadacycline [1], but the more recent 2021 Surgical Society Guidelines recommend omadacycline as an option for purulent infections when MRSA is suspected or confirmed, citing in vitro activity of omadacycline exceeding 90% against the most common pathogens causing skin infections, i.e., Streptococcus species and S. aureus including MRSA [9]. Real-world experience suggests that failure of initial antibiotic therapy occurs in a considerable proportion of patients and is partly related to misdiagnosis and inappropriate therapeutic choice [10–16], although treatment failure definitions and rates vary between studies [17]. Guidelines recommend IV antibiotics for patients that have failed oral therapy, without any consideration as to the appropriateness of the prior oral therapy [1]. Appropriate oral therapy is a challenge, as current guideline-recommended oral treatments do not fully cover the most common skin pathogens [1]. Complex dosing based upon patient characteristics and comorbidities and lower bioavailability may also be contributing factors to the observed treatment failure [18].

Two previous phase 3 trials, OASIS-1 (NCT02378480) and OASIS-2 (NCT02877927), compared clinical efficacy and safety of omadacycline versus linezolid [19, 20]. In this paper, we report a post hoc analysis from OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 comparing clinical response rates between patients with ABSSSI who started IV therapy and switched to PO (OASIS-1) and patients who were treated exclusively with PO therapy (OASIS-2) using pooled data from the two trials.

Methods

Trial Overview

This is a post hoc analysis of the OASIS-1 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02378480) and OASIS-2 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02877927) phase 3 trials. Adult patients ≥ 18 years of age with ABSSSI were randomized 1:1 to omadacycline or linezolid for a total treatment duration of 7 to 14 days. In OASIS-1, investigators had the option to transition from IV to PO after Day 3. In OASIS-2, patients were randomized to oral-only therapy. The intent-to-treat (ITT) population included all randomized subjects, the safety population included randomized patients who received at least one dose of study medication, and the modified intention-to-treat population (mITT) population included patients in the ITT who did not have a sole gram-negative pathogen identified at baseline.

Eligibility criteria were similar per trial. For both trials, patients were eligible if they had a qualifying skin or skin structure infection (wound infection, cellulitis, erysipelas, or major abscess [≤ 30% of randomly assigned patients with major abscess]) of at least 75 cm2 in total contiguous surface area. The tissue also had to exhibit erythema, edema, induration, or a combination of these factors [19, 20]. Eligible patients were also required to have evidence of an inflammatory response or systemic response to infection (i.e., leukocytosis, leukopenia, bandemia, lymphangitis, lymphadenopathy, fever, or hypothermia). Patients with sole gram-negative pathogens at baseline were excluded from the mITT population because linezolid is only active against gram-positive bacteria. Patients taking selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors were excluded from the studies. All eligibility criteria (Supplementary Table S1) and primary objectives of OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 (Supplementary Table S2) are provided in the supplementary material.

Ethical Approval

The OASIS-1 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02378480) and OASIS-2 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02877927) trials included in this post hoc analysis were conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments. They were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) or Ethics Committee (EC) at each participating site (Supplementary Table S3); the IRB/EC at each participating site approved the protocol and amendments. Each patient provided written informed consent prior to study participation.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint in both trials was early clinical response (ECR) defined as survival and ≥ 20% reduction in lesion size at 48 to 72 h, as per FDA recommendations [21], without rescue antibacterial therapy and unplanned surgical procedures in the mITT. While patients were permitted to have planned surgical procedures no later than 48 h after the start of the study drug, any unplanned major surgical intervention (i.e., procedures that would not normally be performed at the bedside) for the infection under study was classed as clinical failure. Secondary endpoints in both trials were investigator-assessed clinical response at post-treatment evaluation (PTE) performed 7 to 14 days after the last dose of study drug in the mITT and clinically evaluable population. Clinical success was defined as survival with resolution or improvement in signs and symptoms of infection to the extent that further antibacterial therapy was unnecessary.

Post Hoc Analysis

In this post hoc analysis, data for omadacycline and linezolid treatment groups were pooled per trial as IV-start (OASIS-1) or PO-start (OASIS-2). The IV-start and PO-start groups included 645 and 735 patients, respectively, who were randomized and received at least one dose of trial drug (safety population) and 627 and 720 patients, respectively, who were included in the mITT population (randomized patients without solely gram-negative pathogens at baseline [efficacy population]). In OASIS-1, the decision to switch from IV to PO therapy was optional but most patients transitioned to oral therapy (88.5% [omadacycline] and 87.9% [linezolid]). Both treatment groups were stratified for infection type.

Success rates were compared for the full pooled cohort and stratified per drug by IV-start (omadacycline = 316; linezolid = 311) versus PO-start (omadacycline = 360; linezolid = 360). Success rates were also compared for subgroups at risk for clinical failure (large lesion size, liver disease, obesity, persons who inject drugs [PWID]) and those with an inflammatory response. The treatment risk differences and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) for early clinical response and clinical response at post-treatment were estimated using the Miettinen and Nurminen method [22].

Clinical Response

Clinical success rates at ECR and PTE were similar overall and per drug in IV-start and PO-start and when analyzed among subgroups by characteristics such as lesion size and select medical history (Figs. 1 and 2). For patients with fever or hypothermia, clinical success rate at ECR was in favor of the IV-start (Fig. 1A). This trend was driven by the outcomes for patients treated with linezolid (Fig. 1C), but the difference did not persist at PTE (Fig. 2A). In the subset of patients with MRSA (IV-start N = 119, PO-start N = 211), clinical success was similar for both IV-start and PO-start when measured at ECR (89.1% versus 91.0%, respectively; difference 1.9% [95% CI −4.5, 9.5]) and PTE (84.0% versus 82.5%, respectively; difference −1.6% [95% CI −9.6, 7.3]).

Safety

The incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (AEs) was similar between the IV-start and PO-start (Table 2), although there were expected differences between groups in the incidence of events associated with methods of administration (nausea/vomiting for patients receiving oral treatment, infusion site extravasation for patients receiving IV treatment). The most frequently observed AEs were nausea (11.2% for the IV-start versus 18.9% for the PO-start) and subcutaneous abscess (5.6% versus 1.9%, respectively). Discontinuation due to an AE was infrequent in both treatment groups (2% versus 1.2%, respectively).

Discussion

This post hoc analysis assessed clinical response rates between patients with ABSSSI who initiated treatment with IV therapy and switched to PO (OASIS-1) and patients who were treated exclusively with PO therapy (OASIS-2) through a post hoc analysis of pooled data from both trials. In the prespecified analyses of both OASIS-1 and OASIS-2, omadacycline demonstrated non-inferiority to linezolid, and both products demonstrate potent in vitro activity against the most common pathogens associated with ABSSSI as well as bioequivalent exposures when given orally [19, 20].

This post hoc analysis demonstrates that oral therapy is equally efficacious to IV therapy for ABSSSI when a potent and bioequivalent agent such as omadacycline or linezolid is the selected therapy. As expected, nausea/vomiting was more common for patients receiving oral treatment; however, these AEs were generally not treatment limiting and occurred during the first 2 days with higher loading doses, as reported previously for the individual and integrated analyses of phase 3 studies of omadacycline and linezolid, including OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 [19, 20, 23].

Alternative, effective oral therapies for the treatment of ABSSSI are critical for patients who have previously failed oral therapy, for whom guidelines suggest IV therapy. Importantly, oral therapies, such as omadacycline and linezolid, which both offer reliable activity for the most common pathogens causing ABSSSI, should be prioritized before IV therapy given the burden of disease and published failure rates of 8–34%, attributable to inappropriate initial therapy [10–15]. Oral therapy can reduce the need for hospitalization or the length of stay for patients who are admitted, which are major drivers in the high costs associated with treating skin infections [24, 25]. Furthermore, oral therapies can reduce the risks associated with IV therapy, (e.g., infections at the injection site, infiltration, hematoma, air embolism, blood clots) and also reduce additional costs associated with IV administration (e.g., staffing time, additional disposable materials, costs associated with hospital admission and lost work days) [24, 26]. Finally oral administration can improve patients’ quality of life and treatment access for patients in remote areas with fewer health care resources [24].

The strengths of this analysis reside in the fact that the pooled dataset generated a large study cohort (1390 patients in the total pooled population and > 300 patients in each arm). Pooling data was sensible owing to the similarities between OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 in terms of study design, patient population, and interventions/comparators. Moreover, OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 trials included patients who may be at increased risk of poor treatment outcomes, such as those with obesity, liver disease, large lesion size, and PWID and those with an inflammatory response. Particularly, in another post hoc analysis of the OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 trials, a numerically higher rate of response was observed for omadacycline versus linezolid in treating ABSSSI in patients with diabetes [27]. Although the sample size was small, it has been suggested that neutrophil dysfunction in patients with diabetes makes them more susceptible to infection with gram-negative pathogens and thereby likely to benefit from the broader-spectrum activity of omadacycline compared to linezolid, which has poor efficacy against gram-negative pathogens [28]. Real-world evidence for patients with skin infections treated with omadacycline suggests that access to omadacycline can be challenging, with 36% of patients having an unapproved claim, despite most patients having an approved claim for another antibiotic. However, patients with an approved claim were less likely to require hospital admission or visit an emergency room [29].

Study Limitations

This post hoc analysis has some limitations. Although the source trials were randomized, this did not apply to the post hoc analysis, and as a result, some imbalance of baseline characteristics (e.g., geographical location, microbiological species and incidence of fever) was observed. However, these differences in baseline characteristics have not previously been identified as a driver of difference between therapies [30, 31].

The data used in this analysis come from two studies that included two antibiotics, linezolid and omadacycline. Most patients were located in the US, and the majority were white, particularly in the OASIS-2 study, which had zero non-US patients. While the pooled analysis represented a mixed sample, including infections caused by both gram-positive and -negative pathogens, most patients had gram-positive infections. Therefore, the results might not be applicable to other types of antibiotics or infection types, such as gram-negative infections. To account for differences in microbiological species, the efficacy results were stratified for the type of infection (wound, abscess, cellulitis); thus, any imbalance in pathogen differences/infection type are accounted for in the model. The presentation of fever has been previously documented in non-PWID and therefore, in our opinion, was not associated with difference in severity of infection [31]. Subgroup analyses suggest that IV therapy may be more efficacious than oral therapy in patients experiencing fever or hypothermia, which was largely due to patients who received linezolid, but the difference did not persist at PTE. Therefore, clinical judgment should be exercised for patients demonstrating clinical instability. Duration of therapy, which was decided by the study investigator, was 7–14 days. This was not an included outcome and may exceed what is used in some practices. Duration of therapy decisions are often based upon clinical assessment of response for which ECR may serve as a surrogate. Lastly, patients with prior antibiotic failure were excluded, which may limit the applicability to this patient population; however, real-world studies suggest that omadacycline is often used after multiple antibiotics and is associated with reduced healthcare resource utilization [29].

Conclusions

Our post hoc findings and previously published predefined analyses of OASIS-1 and OASIS-2 [19, 20, 27] demonstrate that a wide range of adult patients with ABSSSI may benefit from oral omadacycline therapy, a broad-spectrum antibiotic. Together, these data strengthen the evidence for adding oral and IV omadacycline as therapeutic options for SSTIs in the treatment guidelines. Alternative and effective treatment options are critical for patients with SSTIs who prefer oral therapy, want to avoid potential hospitalization, and have experienced treatment failure due to limitations of other therapies.
