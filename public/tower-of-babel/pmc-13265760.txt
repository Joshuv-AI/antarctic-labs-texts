Amelioration of colitis through restored gut ecology using Christensenella intestinihominis AF73-05CM02 as a probiotic in mice
Authors: Zhinan Wu, Xiaofan Sun, Xiaofang Li, Haoyu Wang, Hewei Liang, Mengmeng Wang, Zizhen Yang, Kunyi Zhao, Ningning He, Liang Xiao, Lijuan Ren, Yang Sun, Shangyong Li, Yuanqiang Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is closely linked to gut microbial imbalance marked by loss of beneficial microbes and overgrowth of pathogens. Christensenella intestinihominis, a bacterium that associated with intestinal homeostasis, is depleted in patients with ulcerative colitis (UC), yet its therapeutic potential against this disease remains to be elucidated. Here we demonstrate that C. intestinihominis AF73-05CM02, a strain isolated from Chinese individual, alleviates colitis. In healthy human gut, C. intestinihominis co-occurs with beneficial microbes in strongly connected networks, while these interactions are disrupted in UC. We employ the dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced murine colitis model, a widely recognized preclinical model for investigating intestinal inflammation. In this model, oral gavage with AF73-05CM02 mitigates weight loss, ameliorates colonic injury, improves intestinal health markers, and reverses colon damage. It exerts these effects by reducing harmful bacteria such as Helicobacter species and increasing beneficial taxa like Akkermansia, while enhancing the intestinal epithelial barrier integrity and regulating immune responses. These findings indicate AF73-05CM02 may aid in the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease by restoring a healthy gut microbial community.Subject terms: Gastroenterology, Microbiology

Introduction

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), represents a complex and chronic inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract. Its globally rising incidence poses substantial challenges for clinical management1. Accumulating evidence from recent years underscores gut microbiota dysbiosis as a pivotal driver of IBD pathogenesis and progression2,3. Characteristic microbial alterations in IBD include reduced biodiversity, disrupted ecosystem structure, expansion of potentially pathogenic bacteria, and depletion of beneficial microorganisms4,5. Nevertheless, current research remains largely focused on describing community-wide structural shifts or the role of individual pathobionts, leaving a significant gap in understanding the functional roles of key beneficial species within microbial networks and the mechanisms underlying their collapse in IBD6,7.

Inflammatory bowel disease is characterized by the selective depletion of key commensal taxa, a hallmark of gut microbial dysbiosis that drives the progressive breakdown of intestinal epithelial barrier function and immune homeostasis8,9. Members of the genus Christensenella, most notably Christensenella minuta, have emerged as conserved biomarkers of a healthy gut microbiota10, with consistent reports of their reduced abundance in patients with inflammatory disorders—including IBD—that reflect the compositional alterations characteristic of this disease11,12. While C. minuta’s metabolic outputs (short-chain fatty acids, bile salt hydrolase) are directly linked to pathways central to IBD pathogenesis13,14 (epithelial integrity maintenance, inflammatory response modulation), its functional relevance in the context of IBD remains poorly delineated. Notably, consistent with this genus-wide depletion in inflammatory conditions, our preliminary analyses and existing clinical data confirm that the closely related species Christensenella intestinihominis is highly enriched in healthy individuals but markedly depleted in patients with UC and Crohn’s disease. Despite this striking correlation, the functional significance of C. intestinihominis loss in IBD pathogenesis and its potential as a targeted therapeutic agent remain entirely unexplored.

To address this gap, the present study employs an integrated approach combining metagenomics and microbial co-occurrence network analysis to delineate the structural disintegration of C. intestinihominis-associated functional modules in IBD patient cohorts15. Building on these insights, we selected a representative strain (AF73-05CM02) for therapeutic evaluation in a DSS-induced murine colitis model16. We comprehensively assessed its efficacy through multi-dimensional analyses, including epithelial barrier integrity17, immune response modulation18, microbial network reorganization19, and host transcriptome functional profiling20. Our work aims to move beyond the correlation-based paradigm that dominates current IBD microbiome research, providing mechanistic evidence and experimental support for developing ecology-based interventions targeting microecological functional restoration in UC patients.

Results

Taxon-Specific Network Disintegration in IBD: Role of Christensenella intestinihominis in Dysbiotic Functional Shifts

The gut microbiota operates as a complex macroecological system, within which Christensenella intestinihominis emerges as a potential keystone taxon that modulates microbial community structure and function21. First, we characterized the macroecological features of the gut microbiota across distinct human cohorts (healthy control, hereafter HC, UC, and CD) (Supplementary Data S1). Alpha diversity analysis revealed that the Shannon index22 was significantly lower in both the UC and CD cohorts compared with the healthy control (HC) group (Fig. 1A). At the beta diversity level, Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA)23,24 based on Bray-Curtis25 dissimilarity showed a clear separation in microbial community structure among the three cohorts26 (Fig. 1B); this was confirmed by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA)27,28, which confirmed a statistically significant difference.

Pairwise comparisons further clarified that the microbial structure of the HC cohort was significantly different from both IBD cohorts, whereas no significant difference was observed between the UC and CD cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Furthermore, intra-group distance comparison showed that the microbial communities in the HC cohort were significantly less dispersed than those in the IBD cohorts, indicating a more stable and homogeneous community structure in healthy individuals (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

To identify the key taxa driving these microbial compositional and structural disparities, we compared the taxonomic composition across cohorts. At the phylum level, the mean relative abundance of Pseudomonadota was significantly higher in the IBD cohorts compared with the HC group (HC: 3.8%, UC: 12.7%, CD: 43.1%) (Fig. 1C). The differences were more pronounced at the family level (Supplementary Fig. 1D): the HC microbiota was dominated by Bacteroidaceae, whereas the IBD microbiota exhibited marked compositional shifts where characterized by a transition in dominance to Enterobacteriaceae and Lachnospiraceae, accompanied by a drastic reduction in the relative abundance of Bacteroidaceae. The UC cohort presented an intermediate state, where Bacteroidaceae remained the main family, but the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae was notably higher compared to the HC group. These shifts highlight cohort-specific differences in taxonomic composition at the family level. Among the differentially abundant taxa, we focused on the health‑associated bacterium Christensenella intestinihominis AF73-05CM02, the only representative of this genus detected in our dataset. As this strain is the only sequenced isolate of C. intestinihominis in the GTDB database (mid-February 2026), all sequences annotated as Christensenella intestinihominis were automatically assigned to AF73-05CM02 in our analysis. Its relative abundance was significantly higher in the HC cohort than in the IBD cohorts (Fig. 1D). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe)29 further identified this bacterium as a key biomarker distinguishing the HC from the UC cohort (Supplementary Fig. 1E-F).

To investigate the ecological function of C. intestinihominis AF73-05CM02, we began by constructing co-occurrence networks30. Visualization of the global co-occurrence networks revealed a striking structural divergence between the healthy and IBD cohorts (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Data S2). The HC network exhibited a clear modular structure, with Pseudomonadota confined to the periphery. In contrast, this modular organization was disrupted in the IBD cohorts. To test network stability, we simulated random node removal (n = 100 Repetitions). The HC network maintained better connectivity (AUC = 0.779) compared to the UC (AUC = 0.682) and CD (AUC = 0.582) networks (P = 0.001), indicating that the healthy network is more resilient (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Concurrently, nodes from Pseudomonadota migrated towards the network core, forming dense clusters with nodes from Bacteroidota and Actinomycetota.

Based on these macro-level observations, we further analyzed the core interaction module and position of C. intestinihominis AF73-05CM02 in these co-occurrence networks. Network analysis showed that in the HC cohort, this bacterium formed a predominantly positive interaction module interaction module: it exhibited 28 positive edges (i.e., positive correlations in abundance) and only one antagonistic edge (i.e., negative correlation in abundance) among 18 level-1 and 12 level-2 neighbors. Its core interaction partners were taxonomically diverse and dominated by the families Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae (Supplementary Fig. 1C). This interaction structure in healthy patients was different in the IBD cohorts. In the UC cohort, the module size was reduced (13 level-1 and 8 level-2 neighbors), and its composition shifted, with key butyrate-producing species being interspersed with species of unknown function. In the CD cohort, the module was reorganized into a larger (20 level-1 and 19 level-2 neighbors) network with an equal distribution of positive and negative correlations (ratio of 1:1). Although Lachnospiraceae remained the dominant family, the emergence of frequent negative correlations among its members disrupted the formation of a cohesive, positive interaction module—a sharp departure from the strictly synergistic network observed in the HC cohort (Fig. 1E, F).

Global network robustness simulations under random node removal revealed a significant stability gradient across cohorts (AUC: HC > UC > CD; P = 0.001), identifying the CD network as the most inherently fragile ecosystem (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). Against this backdrop of global instability, targeted module deletion analysis31 revealed that removing the C. intestinihominis unit significantly compromised connectivity in HC and UC networks but surprisingly not in CD (Fig. 1G, H). This indicates that although the CD network is globally fragile, its architecture has reorganized to operate independently of this health-associated module, rendering the strain functionally redundant in the disease state.

Analyzing the relationship between node degree and betweenness revealed significant differences across cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 2C). While the HC cohort exhibited a robust power-law fit (R2 = 0.798; Fig. 1I), the goodness of fit significantly decreased in both IBD cohorts (P = 5.6 × 10−7, P = 8.5 × 10−11 vs. HC), indicating a comparable loss of topological hierarchy in the dysbiotic gut (R2 difference between UC and CD: P = 0.136; Supplementary Fig. 2C). However, despite this shared level of network disorder, the IBD cohorts diverged significantly in centralization (Slope: CD > UC > HC; Supplementary Fig. 2C). The significantly steeper slope in CD compared to UC implies that, while both disease states are equally disorganized, the CD network relies even more heavily on a few highly connected nodes to maintain connectivity.

Multidimensional quantification of the bacterium’s topological role revealed cohort-specific functional specialization. In the HC network, C. intestinihominis AF73-05CM02 functioned as an efficient and cohesive community core21, exhibiting the highest closeness centrality and clustering coefficient (Supplementary Fig. 2D–H). In contrast, within the dysbiotic microenvironment of CD, the strain transitioned into a prominent bridging node32 (Fig. 1J). As illustrated in the radar chart, this state is characterized by maximal degree, betweenness, and eigenvector centrality but the lowest clustering coefficient, indicating that the strain connects numerous disparate taxa without forming a stable, cooperative module.

In summary, the ecological role of C. intestinihominis AF73-05CM02 exhibits distinct topological fates across IBD subtypes. While functioning as a stabilizing ecological anchor in the healthy gut, the bacterium retains a predominantly positive interaction module and critically sustains network robustness in UC, highlighting a substantial potential for targeted therapeutic intervention. In the CD cohort, however, its transition into a structurally central bridging node implies a more complex ecological landscape, suggesting that intervention strategies may face greater structural challenges.

AF73-05CM02 mitigates intestinal epithelial barrier impairment in DSS-induced colitis mice

Building upon the observed microbial dysbiosis in IBD patients—particularly the significant enrichment of beneficial species such as Christensenella intestinihominis in the healthy control (HC) group (Fig. 1D, E), which was markedly depleted in UC patients—we selected AF73-05CM02 for therapeutic evaluation in a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis model33. On day 7, DSS-treated mice exhibited severe clinical and pathological manifestations of colitis, including a body weight loss exceeding 30% relative to the NC group (Fig. 2B, C), elevated Disease Activity Index (DAI) scores (Fig. 2D, E), significant colon shortening (Fig. 2G), and histopathological damage characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration and mucosal erosion (Fig. 2F). These findings collectively confirmed the successful establishment of the DSS-induced colitis model. Mice administered with AF73-05CM02 showed a visible alleviation of DSS-induced weight loss (Fig. 2C), as well as a marked reduction in the DSS-induced elevation of DAI scores and colon shortening (Fig. 2E, Fig. 2G).

Colon sections were stained with H&E and assessed histologically. Both AF73-05CM02 and 5-ASA significantly reduced DSS-induced tissue damage, including mucosal erosion and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 2F). In the DSS group, red arrows indicate severe mucosal destruction, loss of crypt architecture, and extensive inflammatory infiltration. In contrast, blue arrows in the AF73-05CM02 and 5-ASA groups highlight the restoration of mucosal integrity and reduced inflammation. AF73-05CM02 treatment specifically attenuated the infiltration of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and macrophages in the mucosa and submucosa, ameliorated ulceration, and preserved crypt structure (Fig. 2F).

Alcian blue staining further showed that AF73-05CM02 treatment increased intestinal mucus production compared with DSS-treated controls (Fig. 2I). Red arrows in the DSS group indicate marked goblet cell depletion, whereas blue arrows in the AF73-05CM02 group indicate recovery of goblet cell numbers and mucus secretion. These findings demonstrate that AF73-05CM02 effectively mitigates DSS-induced impairment of the mucus layer and loss of goblet cells.

AF73-05CM02 ameliorates DSS-induced colonic epithelial barrier dysfunction in murine models

By employing immunofluorescence staining to assess the expression levels of the intestinal epithelial tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1, and Mucin 2 (MUC2)34–38, we elucidated structural and functional alterations in intercellular tight junctions—findings critical for understanding epithelial barrier dysfunction in IBD. Our results demonstrate that AF73-05CM02 significantly attenuated DSS-induced downregulation of intestinal epithelial tight junction protein expression (ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1, and MUC2) (Fig. 3A–E), indicating its potent therapeutic effects on mitigating intestinal inflammation and preserving epithelial barrier integrity.

Consistent with previous reports describing the pathological hallmarks of DSS-induced colitis¹, this model exhibits a biphasic pathological profile characterized by localized intestinal mucosal damage and systemic inflammatory responses. To comprehensively evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of AF73-05CM02, we employed a dual-analytical strategy targeting both mucosal and systemic inflammation: (a) Mucosal anti-inflammatory effects: Quantification of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, Fig. 3F; TNF-α, Fig. 3G; IL-1β, Fig. 3H) and the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 3I) in colonic tissue samples; (b) Systemic inflammatory modulation: Measurement of the identical cytokine panel (IL-6, Fig. 3J; TNF-α, Fig. 3K; IL-1β, Fig. 3L; IL-10, Fig. 3M) in serum.

In colonic tissues, both AF73-05CM02 and 5-ASA treatment significantly suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), restoring their levels to those observed in healthy control mice. However, the modulation of colonic IL-10 (an anti-inflammatory cytokine) exhibited a context-dependent pattern: DSS-induced colitis markedly reduced colonic IL-10 levels relative to healthy control mice, and AF73-05CM02 treatment further reduced colonic IL-10 expression levels, though this reduction was not statistically significant compared with the DSS group. In serum, AF73-05CM02 effectively inhibited DSS-induced elevations of TNF-α and IL-1β, while exerting no significant effect on serum IL-6 levels. Notably, AF73-05CM02 treatment significantly upregulated serum IL-10 expression levels compared with the DSS group, with levels fully recovering to those observed in healthy control mice.

Both AF73-05CM02 and 5-ASA displayed comparable efficacy in suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines and modulating anti-inflammatory factors in colonic tissues and serum. Collectively, these data indicate that AF73-05CM02 ameliorates mucosal intestinal inflammation and normalizes systemic immune imbalance in DSS-induced colitis via the differential modulation of mucosal and systemic inflammatory responses.

AF73-05CM02 administration mitigated DSS-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis in mice

Metagenomic sequencing on fecal samples was performed to characterize the gut microbiota composition in DSS-induced colitis mice and assess the modulatory effect of AF73-05CM02 on the gut microbiome. At the species level, analysis of alpha diversity revealed a non-significant decrease in the Shannon index of the DSS group compared with the NC group. Consistent with the observed values, neither the AF73-05CM02 nor the 5-ASA treatment group exhibited evidence of a recovery trend in alpha diversity. For beta diversity, the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity values indicated that the microbial community structure of the DSS group showed a degree of separation from the NC group, but this separation was not statistically significant. Similarly, while the AF73-05CM02 and 5-ASA treatment groups displayed a trend toward community structure similar to the NC group, these changes did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Data S3). Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity revealed distinct clustering of the gut microbiota communities among the experimental groups (Fig. 4B). Additionally, the Adonis test confirmed a significant difference in the overall microbial community structure across groups (R2 = 0.25, P = 0.004).

At the phylum level, Bacteroidetes, Bacillota, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia were the dominant phyla across all experimental groups (Fig. 4C upper panel). The DSS group exhibited a noticeable decrease in the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and a concomitant increase in Proteobacteria relative to the NC group. AF73-05CM02 treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes compared with the DSS group (P = 0.0427), partially restoring its levels to those observed in the NC group (Fig. 4C).

At the genus level, Bacteroides, Phocaeicola, Akkermansia, and Helicobacter were identified as the most dominant genera (Fig. 4D). Distinct fluctuations were observed in the relative abundances of Helicobacter and Akkermansia, as well as in specific non-dominant genera such as Escherichia and Muribaculum. Specifically, the relative abundance of Muribaculum, which was abundant in the NC group, was markedly reduced in the DSS group, whereas Escherichia showed a noticeable expansion. Following AF73-05CM02 administration, the relative abundance of Helicobacter noticeably declined compared to the DSS group. Conversely, Akkermansia exhibited a distinct recovery, becoming the second most abundant genus in the AF73-05CM02 group. Spearman correlation analysis within the AF73-05CM02 group (Fig. 4E) revealed that Helicobacter exhibited a significant positive correlation with Escherichia. Furthermore, both genera displayed significant negative correlations with Duncaniella, Lachnoclostridium, and Muribaculum.

The abundances of specific opportunistic pathogenic genera and Helicobacter species were also quantified (Fig. 4F). The relative abundances of several pathogenic genera (including Escherichia and Pasteurella) were elevated in the DSS group relative to the NC group, and AF73-05CM02 treatment significantly mitigated this increase. Similarly, the abundances of several Helicobacter species (e.g., H.typhlonius and H. hepaticus) were higher in the DSS group relative to the NC group, and AF73-05CM02 treatment significantly attenuated this elevation.

AF73-05CM02 reverses dysbiosis by targeting a Helicobacter-centered pathogenic module

To characterize microbial interaction patterns, we constructed a co-occurrence network based on robust (Spearman’s ρ > 0.7) and statistically significant (P < 0.05) correlations, after filtering out species with a mean relative abundance lower than 0.01%. The network consisted of 10 distinct modules (M1-M10) exhibiting marked heterogeneity in species richness and composition (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Data S4). Modules M5 and M8 were notably complex, harboring 92 and 80 species, respectively. Within these species-diverse modules, the hub species was not necessarily the most abundant taxon. In contrast, several low-diversity modules were dominated by a single genus; specifically, M1 (hub: Desulfovibrio desulfuricans), M3 (hub: Alistipes senegalensis), M6 (hub: Helicobacter winghamensis), and M9 (hub: Bacteroides faecis) consisted almost exclusively of species from their respective hub genera. Module partitioning was performed using the Louvain algorithm39.

Mapping LEfSe-identified differentially abundant taxa onto the network revealed their distinct topological positions (Fig. 5B). These key taxa were not randomly distributed but were predominantly clustered within modules M7 and M8. Module M7 was primarily composed of a densely, interconnected, positively correlated cluster of species enriched in the DSS group relative to the NC group (e.g., Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus).

In contrast, Module M8 exhibited a robust antagonistic topological architecture (Fig. 5C). A key topological feature of this module was a distinct subcluster comprising multiple NC group-enriched species (including Muribaculum intestinale, Muribaculum gordoncarteri, Duncaniella dubosii, Bacteroides uniformis, and Sodaliphilus pleomorphus) connected by robust internal positive correlations. This health-associated subcluster exhibited robust negative correlations with key Helicobacter species within the same module. Furthermore, these Helicobacter species exhibited distinct enrichment patterns: species-level LEfSe analysis indicated that Helicobacter bilis was significantly enriched in the DSS group relative to the NC group (Supplementary Fig. 3A), whereas Helicobacter typhlonius was significantly enriched in the DSS group relative to the AF73-treated group (Supplementary Fig. 3B). In parallel, the relative abundance of the Helicobacter genus was significantly reduced in the AF73-treated group compared to the DSS group, consistent with the lower abundance of H. typhlonius (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. 3B). Notably, the robust negative correlations observed at the species level in this global network (n = 24, Fig. 5C) between Helicobacter bilis and these beneficial taxa (specifically M. intestinale, M. gordoncarteri, and D. dubosii) are consistent with the genus-level negative correlations between Helicobacter and both Muribaculum and Duncaniella observed separately within the AF73-05CM02 group (n = 6, Fig. 4E). Collectively, this network topology indicates that the internal synergistic interactions of the health-associated microbial community could be restored via targeted inhibition of Helicobacter.

AF73-05CM02 treatment correlated with normalized microbiome function and host gene regulation

Differential expression analysis of the constructed gene set revealed that the DSS group had 179 upregulated genes and 690 downregulated genes compared with the AF73-05CM02 group (log2FC > 2, P < 0.05) (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Data S5). In the DSS-treated group, several key genes (TLR13, HERC5_6, hlyA, GZMA, and LRRK2) were significantly upregulated, a pattern directly linked to enhanced host immune responses and altered cytotoxic activity (Fig. 6A). Specifically, TLR13, as an innate immune receptor that specifically recognizes bacterial 23S rRNA, was upregulated, indicating an increase in intestinal bacteria or their associated components, which in turn triggers innate immune responses40. hlyA (a virulence gene of pathogenic bacteria) encodes a protein product that disrupts cell membrane integrity and exacerbates intestinal inflammation41. GZMA is predominantly expressed by cytotoxic immune cells and exerts cytotoxic effects via the induction of target cell apoptosis42. Furthermore, LRRK2 not only exerts immune regulatory functions but is also closely linked to the development and progression of neuroinflammation in the context of intestinal colitis43,44.

To investigate the potential functional associations between the gut microbiota and these host transcriptomic alterations, we performed a correlation analysis between these key inflammatory/barrier genes and the differentially abundant taxa identified via LEfSe (Fig. 6B). Validating our hypothesis regarding the potential pathogenicity of Helicobacter, Helicobacter typhlonius exhibited significant positive correlations with multiple inflammation-associated genes (HERC5, hlyA, GZMA) while showing significant negative correlations with barrier function genes (MUC2, Claudin). This distinct pattern suggests that H. typhlonius may exacerbate colitis by simultaneously associating with activated inflammatory cascades and compromised intestinal barrier integrity.

To further characterize the broad ecological impact of the intervention, we analyzed the correlation between these host genes and the top 20 most abundant genera specifically within the AF73-treated group (Supplementary Fig. 3C). On the pathogenic side, the genus Helicobacter showed strong positive correlations with HERC5, hlyA, and GZMA, corroborating the species-level findings. Conversely, regarding protective interactions, Bifidobacterium exhibited a significant negative correlation with the inflammation-associated gene GZMA, while Akkermansia showed a significant positive correlation with the barrier function gene MUC2. Collectively, these findings suggest that AF73-05CM02 treatment restores intestinal homeostasis not only by suppressing the pro-inflammatory Helicobacter, but also potentially by enriching a functional guild of beneficial microbes that synergistically correlate with suppressed inflammation and enhanced mucosal barrier function.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)45 pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant biological differences between the AF73-treated group and the DSS-treated group (Fig. 6C). The AF73-treated group was enriched in metabolic pathways (e.g., protein and vitamin digestion and absorption, cytochrome P450-mediated xenobiotic metabolism) as well as immune and signaling pathways (e.g., endocytosis, mTOR/TGF-β signaling pathways). These findings highlight the regulatory role of AF73 in maintaining intestinal metabolic homeostasis and immune homeostasis. The DSS-treated group was enriched in core metabolic pathways (e.g., amino acid biosynthesis, purine metabolism, pentose phosphate pathway) and DNA/protein homeostasis-related pathways, all of which are linked to intestinal inflammatory damage. Specifically, DSS-induced colonic inflammation triggers extensive shedding of intestinal epithelial cells. To compensate for this damage, residual intestinal epithelial cells upregulate core metabolic pathways to supply precursors and energy for cellular proliferation, while activating DNA/protein repair pathways to mitigate inflammation-induced cellular damage—representing a stress compensatory response.

Discussion

This study integrates multi-cohort human metagenomic and microbial co-occurrence network analysis with experimental validation in a DSS-induced murine colitis model to characterize the ecological role of Christensenella intestinihominis AF73-05CM02 in IBD and assess its potential as a next-generation probiotic. This strain acts as a health-associated commensal with keystone properties: its depletion correlates with disrupted microbial network architecture in IBD, while exogenous administration restores gut homeostasis via restructuring microbial interactions, reinforcing the intestinal barrier, and modulating host immunity. This work links human observational data with preclinical mechanistic validation, providing an ecology-informed framework for targeted probiotic intervention in UC.9.

In the human gut ecosystem, our analysis reveals subtype-specific alterations in the ecological niche of C. intestinihominis and its associated mutualistic networks across IBD phenotypes. In healthy individuals, this bacterium is a core member of a butyrate-producing consortium dominated by Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae, which helps maintain homeostatic network architecture21. In UC, reduced abundance of this strain—a biomarker of gut dysbiosis—is accompanied by decreased betweenness centrality but preserved clustering coefficient (with connections dominated by positive correlations), supporting further investigation of its therapeutic potential via retained local synergistic features. In CD, C. intestinihominis exhibits a “bridging hub” topological profile with higher degree and betweenness centrality, yet targeted removal of this structurally central module does not impact overall network connectivity31, suggesting its ecological functional relevance is diminished in CD with the microbial network no longer dependent on this module. These subtype-specific shifts align with clinical distinctions between UC and CD, extending prior observations of Christensenella depletion in IBD⁹ by associating taxonomic loss with ecological network disruption—an understudied aspect relative to compositional alterations in IBD microbiome research.

Translating human ecological observations to the preclinical model, intervention with AF73-05CM02 in DSS-induced UC mice was associated with amelioration of core disease phenotypes, including colon shortening, weight loss, and elevated Disease Activity Index (DAI) scores. Body weight was not fully restored, consistent with the multifactorial regulation of weight during colitis, while the strain promoted mucosal repair and reduced inflammation46. At the tissue and molecular levels, AF73-05CM02 upregulated key tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin) and the mucus protein MUC2, reversing epithelial barrier impairment—a hallmark of IBD pathogenesis35—and suppressed overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) in colonic tissue and serum. These findings build on research into Christensenella minuta—the only other well-characterized species of the genus that has been shown to protect intestinal barrier function in colitis models47. The current study extends this work by investigating the association between C. intestinihominis’ barrier-protective and anti-inflammatory effects and microbial network remodeling, rather than isolated taxonomic or metabolic modulation.

We observed incomplete restoration of colonic IL-10 levels, likely reflecting slower recovery of homeostatic immune markers relative to rapid pro-inflammatory suppression in acute colitis. The DSS model mimics key features of UC but not its chronic relapsing course, which limits direct translational extrapolation33,48.

A key finding is that AF73-05CM02 restructures the gut microbial community via targeted ecological interactions rather than broad compositional shifts. Murine network analysis identified a Helicobacter-centered module (Module 8) linked to DSS-induced dysbiosis, which contains a health-associated subcluster enriched in Muribaculum and Duncaniella that is suppressed by pathogenic Helicobacter species49. AF73-05CM02 intervention reduced the abundance of the Helicobacter and its representative species, associated with relief of suppression on the health-associated subcluster and restoration of native microbial interaction architecture. This targeted remodeling differs from non-specific probiotic interventions for IBD that often fail to address underlying dysbiotic network structure9. Helicobacter species are well-established drivers of microbially induced colitis in murine models49, and the current work identifies an association between their pathogenicity and ecological displacement of beneficial commensal clusters—a mechanism that may inform future investigations of IBD therapeutic strategies targeting pathobiont modules rather than individual taxa.

Functional correlation analysis links these microbial ecological shifts to improved host physiological outcomes, identifying a potential microbe-host crosstalk axis associated with AF73-05CM02 intervention. Suppression of the Helicobacter-centered pathobiont module coincided with downregulation of host genes driving cytotoxic immune responses (GZMA, TLR13) and bacterial virulence-related pathways (hlyA, HERC5)41,42, while enrichment of beneficial genera including Akkermansia and Bifidobacterium correlated with upregulation of barrier function genes (MUC2, Claudin)36,50.

These multi-omics correlations support the translational value of a network-based approach, moving beyond simple correlation to identify ecological and molecular links between microbial dysbiosis and host pathology in IBD. Methodologically, integration of human cohort network analysis (SpiecEasi) with murine experimental validation (Spearman correlation networks and Louvain modularity) addresses a common limitation of prior studies focused on either human observational data or preclinical models in isolation30. This combined approach enables identification of ecologically relevant microbial features in human IBD, followed by experimental assessment of their potential therapeutic role and associated mechanisms in a preclinical model—an important step given human microbiome compositional data often lack functional validation and preclinical studies may not fully reflect human microbial ecology9.

While network-based approaches have been used previously to characterize gut microbial dysbiosis in IBD30, their application to investigate a candidate probiotic strain targeting a specific pathobiont-associated module represents a focused application of these methods, potentially scalable for discovery of next-generation microbiome-based therapeutics19. For the field, this combined human-preclinical network approach provides a structured way to translate human microbiome observations into testable preclinical hypotheses, reducing the gap between observational human data and functional experimental validation—a critical challenge in IBD microbiome research9.

This study has implications for translational IBD research, particularly for UC, a disease with limited targeted therapeutic options beyond anti-TNF agents and JAK inhibitors that are often associated with adverse effects or loss of response over time. AF73-05CM02 exhibited efficacy comparable to 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA), a first-line UC therapy, in the DSS model51, with a distinct mechanism centered on microbial network modulation rather than direct anti-inflammatory activity. This unique mode of action suggests AF73-05CM02 is a candidate for further investigation as a combination therapy with existing UC treatments, which may enhance therapeutic efficacy by addressing microbial dysbiosis contributing to disease persistence3.

A key strength of this study is its integrative human-preclinical design, linking observational network analysis in IBD patient cohorts to mechanistic experimental assessment in the murine model, ensuring the investigated microbial target is ecologically relevant to human disease. We also employed a multi-dimensional analytical approach (metagenomics, host transcriptomics, cytokine profiling, histological analysis) to characterize the strain’s effects across multiple levels, from microbial ecology to host tissue and molecular responses. Additionally, use of two complementary network inference methods addresses potential biases associated with single-method network analysis30.

Notwithstanding these strengths, the study has several limitations: human cohort analysis is cross-sectional, identifying associative trends rather than direct causal relationships, requiring longitudinal studies to track dynamics of C. intestinihominis-associated networks during disease onset and progression; there are taxonomic and ecological differences between human and murine gut ecosystems — the murine gut is dominated by Muribaculaceae and cannot fully recapitulate the Lachnospiraceae-centered interaction modules in the human gut, limiting the direct translational value of findings from murine microbial networks; the DSS model’s acute, chemical induction differs from chronic human IBD, necessitating validation in chronic models (e.g., repeated DSS cycles, IL-10 knockout mice)49; we focused on the strain’s effects in a UC model, with its relevance for CD therapy unclear given the diminished ecological role of C. intestinihominis in CD-associated networks11; and the study does not characterize metabolic mechanisms underlying the strain’s ecological and potential therapeutic effects (e.g., short-chain fatty acid production, bile salt metabolism), which future metabolomic and transcriptomic analysis will help address12.

This work identifies key unanswered questions for future research: the molecular mechanisms underlying AF73-05CM02-mediated Helicobacter reduction and beneficial microbial restoration remain unclear, requiring validation of pathways such as direct competition, metabolite production, or host immune modulation of pathobionts12; subtype-specific topological shifts of C. intestinihominis in UC and CD prompt exploration of whether other keystone taxa have disease-specific ecological roles and if strain-tailored probiotics can target distinct IBD dysbiotic networks19; the delayed colonic IL-10 recovery in the acute model also necessitates chronic model studies to assess long-term immune homeostasis restoration and synergy with immunomodulators. For translational research, critical steps include evaluating the strain’s stability, viability and function in human gut-mimetic systems (e.g., in vitro organoids)50, conducting preclinical safety assessments for subsequent clinical trials, extending the network analytical framework to other microbiome-linked chronic inflammatory diseases (e.g., irritable bowel syndrome, metabolic syndrome)8, and elucidating the metabolic pathways underlying the strain’s ecological effects to uncover novel strategies for enhancing its therapeutic potential12.

Method

Cohort analysis

We downloaded metagenomic sequencing data from gut samples of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and healthy individuals from the China National GeneBank DataBase (CNGBdb)52 with the accession number CNP0004022. We conducted annotation by building a database using the kraken2-build function in Kraken253 (version 2.1.2) software. The species in the database were 527 representative species from our previous culture genomic reference (CGR2) study54.

Human cohort network analysis

Global co-occurrence networks were constructed using the SpiecEasi55 (v1.1.2) R package to infer inter-species interactions. To prioritize persistent community members and minimize computational noise, taxonomic profiles were pre-filtered to retain only species present in at least 15% of the samples within each cohort. Network inference was performed using the Meinshausen-Buhlmann56 (mb) neighborhood selection method. The optimal model stability was determined via the Stability Approach to Regularization Selection (StARS), with the minimum lambda ratio set to 0.2 (nlambda = 20), ensuring that inferred associations were statistically robust57. Association weights were extracted from the refitted sparse inverse covariance matrix, representing the partial correlation strength between taxa.

Based on the inferred networks, five key topological metrics were quantified using the igraph package to characterize the ecological roles of specific taxa:

1.Degree (the total number of direct associations for a node, reflecting its local community influence);

2.Betweenness Centrality (the frequency with which a node acts as a bridge along the shortest paths between other nodes, identifying potential ‘gatekeeper’ or ‘broker’ taxa);

3.Closeness Centrality (the inverse of the average shortest path length to all other nodes, measuring the efficiency of a taxon in perceiving or spreading community-wide environmental shifts);

4.Eigenvector Centrality (a measure of a node’s influence based on the importance of its neighbors, distinguishing members within the network’s high-influence ‘core circles’);

5.Clustering Coefficient (the density of connections among a node’s neighbors, representing the local stability and functional redundancy of the microbial sub-community).

For the hierarchical sunburst visualization, Level 2 partners were identified based on an absolute association weight (|w| ) > 0.2 with Level 1 taxa. Additionally, network robustness was assessed via targeted module deletion analysis, quantifying the decline in Natural Connectivity.

Bacterial strain and culture

As previously reported in our study, C. intestinihominis AF73-05CM02 was isolated from the fecal sample of a healthy adult in Shenzhen, China58. C. intestinihominis AF73-05CM02 was cultured in an anaerobic workstation (SHELLAB, Bactron 600) using a gas mixture of 90% N2, 5% CO2, and 5% H2, in MPYG medium at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions for 36 h. After bacterial culture, the bacteria were washed three times with sterile anaerobic 1×PBS and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The final concentration for oral gavage of AF73-05CM02 was adjusted to 1 × 109 CFU/200 μL and thoroughly mixed with 20% glycerol at a 1:1 volume ratio.

Animal experiments

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (body weight: 17–19 g) were purchased from Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China). All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, 22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 5% humidity, with free access to standard chow and distilled water, in compliance with the national standard GB14924.1-2010 Laboratory Animal—Requirements of Environment and Housing Facilities. Prior to experimental modeling, a 7-day acclimatization period was provided to allow mice to adapt to the housing environment.

Subsequently, mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group) with the following treatment regimens:

NC group (negative control): Administered distilled water ad libitum from day 0 to day 14;

DSS group: Given distilled water ad libitum from day 0 to day 7, followed by 2.5% (w/v) dextran sulfate sodium (DSS; molecular weight: 36–50 kDa; MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA) dissolved in distilled water ad libitum from day 8 to day 14;

AF73-05CM02 group: Received distilled water ad libitum from day 0 to day 7, and AF73-05CM02 (1×10⁹ CFU/200 μL) via oral gavage daily from day 0 to day 14; from day 8 to day 14, 2.5% (w/v) DSS water was provided ad libitum;

5-ASA group: 5-Aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) was used as a positive control based on its well-established therapeutic efficacy in the DSS-induced colitis model51. Given distilled water ad libitum from day 0 to day 7; from day 8 to day 14, 2.5% (w/v) DSS water was provided ad libitum, and 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA; 400 mg/kg/day) was administered via oral gavage daily.

During the experimental period, mice were monitored daily for body weight changes. The Disease Activity Index (DAI) was evaluated daily in the final week (day 8 to day 14), with scoring based on three metrics: percentage of body weight loss, stool consistency (normal, loose, or watery), and presence of fecal bleeding (none, occult, or gross). Prior to euthanasia, fresh fecal samples were collected, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C for subsequent microbial and molecular analyses. The primary outcome measure was the Disease Activity Index (DAI) score. Secondary outcomes included body weight change, colon length, histological scores, and cytokine levels.

Mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of chloral hydrate (anesthesia dose: 350 mg/kg body weight) to minimize suffering. Blood was collected via orbital sinus puncture (a standard, minimally invasive technique), and serum was separated by centrifugation at 1100 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, then stored at −80 °C. The entire colon was excised, rinsed with ice-cold physiological saline to remove residual feces, measured for length (from the cecum to the anus), and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for long-term storage at −80 °C until further histological or biochemical analysis.

All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical College of Qingdao University (Approval No.: QDU-AEC-2022362). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal experiments. With adherence to predefined humane endpoints—specifically, weight loss exceeding 20%, signs of severe lethargy, or an inability to reach food or water—the mice were closely monitored at least twice per day throughout the DSS-induced colitis period, and efforts to mitigate pain and distress, including the supply of softened food and supplemental fluids, were implemented.

All mice that completed the experimental protocol were included in the analysis. No animals or data points were excluded. During the outcome assessment (including histological scoring, DAI evaluation, and data analysis), the investigators were blinded to the group allocation of the samples. A detailed experimental protocol was designed prior to the study but was not formally registered.

Histological assessment

After embedding in paraffin, the collected colon tissue fragments were fixed in 10% formalin and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Alcian blue staining was performed after dewaxing the paraffin sections. The stained tissue sections were observed with a microscope at 200 × magnification (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The histological score for H&E staining was estimated in a blinded fashion. Quantitative analysis of Alcian blue staining was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence analysis

As described in previous research59, after antigen retrieval by boiling in sodium citrate buffer, nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin. Subsequently, sections were incubated overnight with primary and secondary antibodies specific to target proteins such as MUC2, ZO-1, Claudin-1, and OCC. The sections were then stained with DAPI to label the nuclei and observed under a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) to assess the expression and localization of these proteins within the tissue.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay analysis

Serum samples were centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min to remove particulates. Colon tissues were homogenized in ice-cold PBS (1:5 w/v) and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected and total protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10) were quantified using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer-provided protocols. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader. A standard curve was generated for each plate using recombinant cytokines, and concentrations were normalized to total protein (mg/mL). All samples and standards were analyzed in duplicate.

DNA extraction and metagenomics sequencing

Before termination, fecal samples were collected and stored at −80 °C. Bacteria genomic DNA was extracted from each sample using the MagPure Stool DNA KF Kit B (Catalog No. MD5115, Magen, Guangzhou, China). Metagenomics sequencing of fecal samples was used on the BGI-DNQSEQ T5 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China).

Gut microbiota data analysis

Raw sequencing reads were first subjected to quality control using fastp60 (version 0.23.4) with the following parameters: removal of low-quality bases (Phred score < 20), trimming of adapter sequences, and filtering of reads shorter than 50 bp. Host-derived DNA was then removed by aligning reads to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using Bowtie261 (version 2.4.5), retaining only unmapped reads for downstream analysis. Taxonomic profiling was performed using Kraken2 (version 2.1.2) with the custom database described above. Alpha diversity (Shannon index) and beta diversity (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) were calculated to assess microbial community richness and inter-group heterogeneity, respectively. To identify differentially abundant bacterial taxa, we applied the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) method29, reporting only features with a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score > 2.0. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package (version 1.38.3)62 in R63 (version 4.2.3). All statistical visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.4) in the RStudio.

Animal model network analysis

To evaluate the community remodeling effects of AF73-05CM02 in DSS-induced colitis mice, a co-occurrence network was constructed by merging samples from all four treatment groups. Prior to construction, species with a mean relative abundance lower than 0.01% were excluded. The network was based on Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ > 0.7, P < 0.05). The resulting structure was partitioned into Louvain-identified modularity classes (e.g., M1, M2) to identify functional microbial clusters39.

Module background shading was scaled according to the mean intra-module edge density, with module numbering inversely correlated with internal association strength. Finally, differentially enriched taxa identified via LEfSe analysis (LDA thresholds specified above) were mapped onto the nodes to visualize the enrichment patterns of specific treatment groups within these functional modules64.

Pathway enrichment analysis of HUMAnN3

Functional annotation of the non-redundant gene catalog was conducted using EggNOG-mapper65 (version 2.4.5). Raw gene counts were normalized via the Transcripts Per Million (TPM)66 method; only genes with an average TPM value > 10 were retained for subsequent downstream analyses. To independently profile microbial metabolic pathways, raw sequencing reads were analyzed using HUMAnN367, with functional annotations referenced against the KEGG database (accessed in May 2023). Pathway abundances were normalized to copies per million (CPM), and pathways with an average CPM < 10 across all samples were excluded from further analysis. Differential analysis of metabolic pathways between groups was performed using the DESeq2 package (version 1.38.3) in R. Significance thresholds were set as follows: adjusted P-values < 0.05 (using the Benjamini–Hochberg method for false discovery rate correction) and |log₂ Fold Change | > 2. Only metabolically relevant pathways were reported in the results.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.3) and GraphPad Prism68 (version 9.3.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with a statistically significant difference defined as P-value < 0.05. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data that did not meet the assumption of normality, non-parametric tests (e.g., Wilcoxon rank-sum test) were employed as indicated.

Clinical parameters (including body weight and DAI) and cytokine levels were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. For gut microbiota data, pairwise comparisons of alpha diversity and bacterial abundance were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The overall microbial community structure (beta diversity) was evaluated via permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, Adonis test). Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was applied for microbial biomarker identification, while the DESeq2 package was used for host differential gene expression analysis.

A central component of the statistical methodology in this study involved two distinct microbial network analysis approaches,as follows:

Human Cohort Network Analysis (Fig. 1): The microbial co-occurrence network was constructed using the SpiecEasi55 R package to infer inter-species interactions. After network construction, topological analysis was performed to calculate key metrics—including degree, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, and clustering coefficient—for characterizing network structure and node (microbial species) importance.

Animal Model Network Analysis (Fig. 5): The microbial co-occurrence network was constructed based on Spearman’s rank correlation, with inclusion criteria set as correlation coefficient ρ > 0.7 and P < 0.05. The resulting network was then partitioned into functional modules using the Louvain algorithm to identify potential microbial community structures.
