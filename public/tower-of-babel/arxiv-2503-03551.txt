Ross Willard

Abstract
. This is the second of three papers motivated by the author’s desire to understand and explain “algebraically” one aspect of Dmitriy Zhuk’s proof of the CSP Dichotomy Theorem. In this paper we extend Zhuk’s “bridge” construction to arbitrary meet-irreducible congruences of finite algebras in locally finite varieties with a Taylor term. We then connect bridges to centrality and similarity. In particular, we prove that Zhuk’s bridges and our “proper bridges” (defined in our first paper) convey the same information in locally finite Taylor varieties.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
08A05, 08B99 (Primary), 08A70 (Secondary).

1.
Introduction

Arguably the most important result in universal algebra in the last ten years is the
positive resolution to the Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP) Dichotomy Conjecture,
announced independently in 2017 by Andrei Bulatov
[
2
]
and
Dmitriy Zhuk
[
17
,
18
]
.
One particular feature of Zhuk’s proof is his analysis of
“rectangular critical” subdirect products
R\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
of
finite algebras
{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
in certain locally finite idempotent Taylor varieties.
Zhuk showed that
such relations
R
induce derived relations, which he named “bridges,”
between certain meet-irreducible congruences of
{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}

which
R
determines.
Zhuk also established a number of useful properties of his bridges, and ultimately used them to tease
out implicit linear equations in CSP instances. In this and two companion
papers
[
16
,
15
]
, we aim to understand “algebraically”
Zhuk’s bridges and their
application to rectangular critical relations.

Our goal in this paper
is to establish precise connections between Zhuk’s bridges,
centrality, and a relation called “similarity” due to Freese

[
4
]
in the congruence modular setting and extended to varieties
with a weak difference term (including locally finite Taylor varieties)
in our first paper
[
16
]
.
In Section
2
we give the basic definitions and
tools needed in this paper. In Section
3
we
summarize the results about similarity from
[
16
]
which we will use
here.
With these preliminaries out of the way, we
address two technical
limitations of Zhuk’s original presentation:
Zhuk defined his bridges between pairs
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
and
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)

where (i)
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
are finite algebras in a very special kind of Taylor variety, and
(ii)
\rho
and
\sigma
are congruences satisfying an “irreducibility”
property stronger than meet-irreducibility.
In fact, Zhuk’s definitions, and all but one of Zhuk’s theorems about bridges
(see Theorem
6.4
),
work in arbitrary locally finite Taylor varieties, so
in Section
4
we present his definitions and basic results
avoiding limitation (i).
Then in Section
5
we use tame congruence theory to
show how Zhuk’s definitions and basic results can
extend to arbitrary meet-irreducible congruences, avoiding limitation (ii).
Finally, in Section
6

we align Zhuk’s bridges, in this broader context,
with the algebraic relations of centrality and similarity.
In particular, we show that Zhuk’s bridges and our “proper bridges”
defined in
[
16
]
(see Definition
3.7
) are equivalent.

2.
Definitions and helpful results

We assume that the reader is familiar with the fundamentals of universal algebra as given
in
[
3
]
,
[
13
]
or
[
1
]
.
Our notation generally follows that in
[
13
]
,
[
1
]
and
[
9
]
.
If
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra, then
\Con({\mathbf{A}})
denotes its congruence lattice.
The smallest and largest congruences of
{\mathbf{A}}
are the

diagonal

0_{A}:=\{(a,a)\,:\,\text{$a\in A$}\}
and the
full congruence

1_{A}:=A^{2}

respectively; they may be denoted
0
and
1
if no confusion arises.
If
\alpha,\beta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
with
\alpha\leq\beta
, then
\beta/\alpha

denotes the congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}/\alpha
corresponding to
\beta
via the
Correspondence Theorem (
[
13
, Theorem 4.12]
or
[
1
, Theorem 3.6]
).
If in addition
\gamma,\delta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
with
\gamma\leq\delta
, then we write

(\alpha,\beta)\nearrow(\gamma,\delta)
and say that
(\alpha,\beta)
is

perspective up to

(\gamma,\delta)
, or
transposes up to

(\gamma,\delta)
,
if
\beta\wedge\gamma=\alpha
and
\beta\vee\gamma=\delta
. The notation

(\gamma,\delta)\searrow(\alpha,\beta)
means the same thing.
We write
\alpha\prec\beta
and say that
\beta

covers

\alpha
,
or is an
upper cover of

\alpha
, if
\alpha<\beta
and there does not exist
a congruence
\gamma
satisfying
\alpha<\gamma<\beta
.
A congruence
\alpha
is
minimal
if it covers
0
, and is

completely meet-irreducible

if
\alpha\neq 1
and there exists
\alpha^{+}

with
\alpha\prec\alpha^{+}
and such that
\alpha<\beta\implies\alpha^{+}\leq\beta
for all
\beta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
.
When
{\mathbf{A}}
is finite, we use the phrase “
meet-irreducible
”
to mean the same thing. We say that
{\mathbf{A}}
is
subdirectly irreducible

if
0
is completely meet-irreducible, in which case
0^{+}
is
called the
monolith
of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
A subset
T\subseteq A
is a
transversal
for a congruence
\alpha
if
it contains exactly one element from each
\alpha
-class.

If
f:A\rightarrow B
is a function, then its
graph
is the set

\operatorname{graph}(f)=\{(a,f(a))\,:\,\text{$a\in A$}\}
.
If
n>0
, then
[n]
denotes
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
If
R\subseteq A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{n}
and
i,j\in[n]
, then

\operatorname{pr}_{i}(R)
denotes the projection of
R
onto its
i
-th coordinate,
and
\operatorname{pr}_{i,j}(R)
denotes
\{(a_{i},a_{j})\,:\,\text{$(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in R$}\}
.

We follow
[
9
]
and refer to a set of operation symbols with assigned
arities as a
signature
.
Every algebra comes equipped with a signature, which indexes the

basic operations
of the algebra.

Terms
are formal recipes for constructing new operations from the
basic operations
via composition and variable manipulations; see

[
13
]
or
[
1
]
or any textbook on first-order logic.
We will not distinguish between terms and the term operations they
define in an algebra, except when the distinction is crucial.
A
polynomial
of an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
is any operation on
A
having the form

t(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\mathbf{c})
where
t
is an
(n+k)
-ary term
in the signature of
{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mathbf{c}\in A^{k}
.
\Pol_{n}({\mathbf{A}})
is the set of
all
n
-ary polynomials of
{\mathbf{A}}
.

If
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra, a term
t(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
of
{\mathbf{A}}
is

idempotent
if
{\mathbf{A}}
satisfies the identity
t(x,\ldots,x)\approx x
,
and is a
Taylor term

if it is idempotent,
n>1
, and for each
i\in[n]
,
{\mathbf{A}}
satisfies an identity
of the form

t(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\approx t(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})

where each
u_{j}
and
v_{k}
is the variable
x
or
y
, and

\{u_{i},v_{i}\}=\{x,y\}
.
An algebra is said to be
Taylor
if it has a Taylor term.
A particularly important example of a Taylor term is a
weak near-unanimity term

(WNU),
which is an
n
-ary idempotent term
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
with
n>1
which
satisfies the identities

w(y,x,x,\ldots,x)\approx w(x,y,x,\ldots,x)\approx w(x,x,y,\ldots,x)\approx\cdots\approx w(x,\ldots,x,y).

Another important example of a Taylor term is a
Maltsev term
; this is a ternary
term
p(x,y,z)
satisfying the identities

(2.1)

p(x,x,y)\approx y\approx p(y,x,x).

The identities (
2.1
) are called the
Maltsev identities
. Any
ternary operation
(whether a term or not) satisfying them is called a
Maltsev operation
.

A
variety
is a class of algebras (in a common signature) which is closed under
subalgebras, homomorphic images, and direct products of arbitrary (including
infinite) families of algebras.
\mathsf{HSP}({\mathbf{A}})
denotes the smallest variety containing
{\mathbf{A}}
.
A term is a Taylor term, or a WNU term, for a variety if it is such for
every algebra in the variety. Because the definitions of Taylor terms and WNU terms
are given in terms of satisfied identities, a Taylor or WNU term for an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
is
automatically a Taylor or WNU term for the variety
\mathsf{HSP}({\mathbf{A}})
.

Before defining “weak difference term,” we recall the ternary centralizer relation on
congruences and the notion of
abelian congruences.
Given a non-empty set
A
, let
A^{2\times 2}
denote the set of all

2\times 2
matrics over
A
. If
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra, let

{\mathbf{A}}^{2\times 2}
denote the algebra with universe
A^{2\times 2}
which is
isomorphic to
{\mathbf{A}}^{4}
via the bijection

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{3}\\
a_{2}&a_{4}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}).

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra and
\theta,\varphi\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
.

M(\theta,\varphi)
is the subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2\times 2}
generated by the
set

X(\theta,\varphi):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}c&c\\
d&d\end{pmatrix}:(c,d)\in\theta\right\}\cup\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
a&b\end{pmatrix}:(a,b)\in\varphi\right\}.

The matrices in
M(\theta,\varphi)
are called
(\theta,\varphi)
-
matrices
.

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose
\theta,\varphi,\delta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
. We say that

\varphi

centralizes
\theta
modulo
\delta
, and write

C(\varphi,\theta;\delta)
,
if any of the following equivalent conditions holds:

(1)

For every matrix in
M(\varphi,\theta)
, if one row is in
\delta
, then so is the other row.

(2)

For every matrix in
M(\theta,\varphi)
, if one column is in
\delta
, then so is the
other column.

(3)

For every
(1+n)
-ary term
t(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
and all
(a,b)\in\varphi

and
(c_{j},d_{j})\in\theta
(
j\in[n]
),

if

t(a,\mathbf{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta}}{{\equiv}}t(a,\mathbf{d})
, then

t(b,\mathbf{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta}}{{\equiv}}t(b,\mathbf{d})
.

Let
\theta,\delta
be congruences of an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
.
The
centralizer
(or

annihilator
)
of
\theta
modulo
\delta
, denoted

(\delta:\theta)
, is the unique largest congruence

\varphi
for which
C(\varphi,\theta;\delta)
holds.
We say that
\theta
is
abelian
if
C(\theta,\theta;0)
holds; equivalently,
if
\theta\leq(0:\theta)
.
We say that
{\mathbf{A}}
is
abelian
if
1_{A}
is abelian.
More generally, if
\theta,\delta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
with

\delta\leq\theta
, then we say that
\theta
is
abelian modulo

\delta
if
any of the following equivalent conditions holds: (i)
\theta/\delta
is an abelian
congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}/\delta
; (ii)
C(\theta,\theta;\delta)
holds; (iii)

\theta\leq(\delta:\theta)
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a variety,
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{V}
, and
d(x,y,z)
a ternary term in the
signature of
\mathcal{V}
.

(1)

d
is a
weak difference term
for
{\mathbf{A}}
if
d
is idempotent and for
every pair
\delta,\theta
of congruences with
\delta\leq\theta
and
\theta/\delta

abelian, we have

(2.2)

d(a,a,b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta}}{{\equiv}}b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta}}{{\equiv}}d(b,a,a)

for all
(a,b)\in\theta
.

(2)

d
is a
weak difference term
for
\mathcal{V}
if it is a weak difference term for
every algebra in
\mathcal{V}
.

Note in particular that if
d
is a weak difference term for
{\mathbf{A}}
and
\theta
is
an abelian congruence,
then setting
\delta=0
in (

) gives that
the restriction of

to any
\theta
-class is a Maltsev operation on that class.
In fact,
d
induces an abelian group operation on each
\theta
-class in this case,
by the following result of Gumm
[
5
]
and Herrmann
[
7
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra having a weak difference term
d(x,y,z)
,
and
\theta
is an abelian congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
Given

, let
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
denote the algebra
(e/\theta,+,e)
whose
universe is the
\theta
-class containing

.

Lemma 2.5

(essentially
[
5
,
7
]
; cf.
[

.

Suppose

The next result is folklore.

Lemma 2.6

(cf.
[

)
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra and
\rho
is a reflexive subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
.
Suppose

has a ternary

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra having a weak difference term,

\mu\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is an abelian minimal congruence, and
S\leq{\mathbf{A}}
is a
subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
which is a transversal for
\mu
.
Then
S
is a maximal proper subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
.

Proof.

Clearly
S\neq A
as
\mu\neq 0_{A}
.
Let
\pi:{\mathbf{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{S}}
be the homomorphism given by letting
\pi(x)
be the
unique element

satisfying
(x,y)\in\mu
.
Fix

.
Let
\rho=\sg^{{\mathbf{A}}^{2}}(\{(a,\pi(a))\}\cup 0_{A})
.
\rho
is a reflexive
subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
satisfying

. Thus
by Lemma
2.6
and minimality of

, we get
\rho=\mu
.
Now let

be arbitrary.
Then

(b,\pi(b))\in\mu=\rho
, so we can pick a term

and a tuple
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
of elements of

such that

(b,\pi(b))=t^{{\mathbf{A}}^{2}}((a,\pi(a)),(c_{1},c_{1}),\ldots,(c_{n},c_{n}))=(t(a,\mathbf{c}),t(\pi(a),\mathbf{c})).

Let
\mathbf{u}
be the tuple in
S
obtained by applying
\pi
coordinatewise
to
\mathbf{c}
. Observe that

\pi(b)=\pi(t(a,\mathbf{c}))=t(\pi(a),\mathbf{u})
, so

As
\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}
are coordinatewise in
\mu
,

, and

is abelian, we can use Definition

(

) and
replace the underlined instances of

.
∎

Some proofs in Sections

use tame congruence theory, of which we will

Given a finite algebra

.

(

]
)
.

.

-minimal set.

(2)

Similarity in varieties with a weak difference term

In this section we list the definitions and results about similarity from

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra and
\theta,\alpha\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

with
\theta\leq\alpha
.

(1)

{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
denotes

(2)

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be an algebra and
\delta,\theta,\alpha\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

with
\theta\leq\alpha

(see e.g.

follows by

(

∎

D({\mathbf{A}})
is
defined as follows:

(1)

If
\mu
is nonabelian, then
D({\mathbf{A}})={\mathbf{A}}
.

(2)

If
\mu
is abelian, then
D({\mathbf{A}})=D({\mathbf{A}},\mu)
as defined in Definition
3.3
.

Definition 3.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term, and

{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{V}
are subdirectly irreducible. We say that
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
are

similar
, and write
{\mathbf{A}}\sim{\mathbf{B}}
, if
D({\mathbf{A}})\cong D({\mathbf{B}})
.

The following definition from
[
16
]
was motivated by Zhuk’s bridges

[
18
]
.

Definition 3.7
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}

are subdirectly irreducible algebras in a common signature with monoliths

\mu,\kappa
respectively. A
proper bridge
from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}

is a subuniverse
T\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
satisfying

(B1)

\operatorname{pr}_{1,2}(T)=\mu
and
\operatorname{pr}_{3,4}(T)=\kappa
.

(B2)

For all
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T
we have
a_{1}=a_{2}
if and only if
b_{1}=b_{2}
.

(B3)

For all
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T
we have
(a_{i},a_{i},b_{i},b_{i})\in T
for
i=1,2
.

Theorem 3.8

(
[
16
, Theorem 7.8]
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term, and

{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{V}
are subdirectly irreducible.
The following are equivalent:

(1)

{\mathbf{A}}\sim{\mathbf{B}}
.

(2)

There exist an algebra
{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathcal{V}
,
surjective homomorphisms
f_{1}:{\mathbf{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{A}}
and
f_{2}:{\mathbf{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
,
and congruences

\psi,\tau\in\Con({\mathbf{C}})
with
\psi<\tau
, such that, letting
\delta_{i}=\ker(f_{i})
and
letting
\delta^{+}_{i}

denote the unique upper cover of
\delta_{i}
in
\Con({\mathbf{C}})
for
i=1,2
, we have

(\delta_{1},\delta^{+}_{1})\searrow(\psi,\tau)\nearrow(\delta_{2},\delta^{+}_{2})
.

(3)

There exists a proper bridge from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}
.

Corollary 3.9

(
[
16
, Corollary 7.9]
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term, and

{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{V}
is subdirectly irreducible with abelian monolith
\mu
.
Setting
\alpha=(0:\mu)
and
\Delta=\Delta_{\mu,\alpha}
, the set

T_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{D}:=\{(a,b,(a,e)/\Delta,(b,e)/\Delta)\,:\,\text{$a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu}}{{\equiv}}b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu}}{{\equiv}}e$}\}

is a proper bridge from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
D({\mathbf{A}})
.

4.
Zhuk’s bridges

In his solution to the
Constraint Satisfaction Problem Dichotomy Conjecture
[
18
]
, D. Zhuk
defined and used to great effect certain devices which he called “bridges.”
In this section we present Zhuk’s bridges, in slightly greater generality
than Zhuk’s original setting.
To help clarify their development,
we introduce some new terminology and recast some of Zhuk’s basic proofs.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be an algebra and
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
.
A subuniverse
R\leq{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
is said to be
stable under

\rho

[
18
]
, or
\rho
-
saturated

[
11
]
,
or
\rho
-
closed

[
8
]
,
if
R=\rho\circ R\circ\rho
.

Definition 4.2
.

An
anchor
is a triple
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
where
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite algebra,

\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
,
R\leq{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
,

R
is stable under
\rho
, and
R
properly contains
\rho
.

Definition 4.3
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
are finite algebras in a common signature
and
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
,
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
are anchors.
A
bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
is
a subuniverse
T\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}\times{\mathbf{B}}

satisfying

(B0
∗
)

T
is “stable under
\rho
in its first two coordinates and stable
under
\sigma
in its last two coordinates.” That is,
for all
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}),(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime})\in A\times A\times B\times B

such that
(a_{i},a_{i}^{\prime})\in\rho
and
(b_{i},b_{i}^{\prime})\in\sigma
for
i=1,2
, we have

(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T\iff(a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime},b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime})\in T.

(B1
∗
)

\operatorname{pr}_{1,2}(T)=R
and
\operatorname{pr}_{3,4}(T)=S
.

(B2
∗
)

For all
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T
we have

(a_{1},a_{2})\in\rho\iff(b_{1},b_{2})\in\sigma
.

Condition
(B0∗)
, together with the requirement of
stability in the definition of anchors, allow for
“faithfully modding out by
\rho
and
\sigma
.” That is, if

({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
and
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
are general anchors and

T\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
with
T,\rho,\sigma
satisfying

(B0∗)
, then setting
\overline{{\mathbf{A}}}:={\mathbf{A}}/\rho
and

\overline{{\mathbf{B}}}:={\mathbf{B}}/\sigma
, we can form quotients

\overline{R}\leq(\overline{{\mathbf{A}}})^{2}
,

\overline{S}\leq(\overline{{\mathbf{B}}})^{2}
, and

\overline{T}\leq\overline{{\mathbf{A}}}\times\overline{{\mathbf{A}}}\times\overline{{\mathbf{B}}}\times\overline{{\mathbf{B}}}
in the obvious way, namely,

\displaystyle\overline{R}

\displaystyle=\{(a/\rho,\,a^{\prime}/\rho)\,:\,\text{$(a,a^{\prime})\in R$}\}

\displaystyle\overline{S}

\displaystyle=\{(b/\sigma,\,b^{\prime}/\sigma)\,:\,\text{$(b,b^{\prime})\in S$}\}

\displaystyle\overline{T}

\displaystyle=\{(a/\rho,\,a^{\prime}/\rho,\,b/\sigma,\,b^{\prime}/\sigma)\,:\,\text{$(a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime})\in T$}\}.

Then

(\overline{{\mathbf{A}}},0_{\overline{A}},\overline{R})
and

(\overline{{\mathbf{B}}},0_{\overline{B}},\overline{S})
are anchors,
and

T
is a bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
if and only
if
\overline{T}
is a bridge from
(\overline{{\mathbf{A}}},0_{\overline{A}},\overline{R})

to
(\overline{{\mathbf{B}}},0_{\overline{B}},\overline{S})
.
Moreover,
R
,
S
and
T
are recoverable from
\overline{R}
,
\overline{S}
and

\overline{T}
respectively, as the pre-images under the natural maps

{\mathbf{A}}^{2}\rightarrow(\overline{{\mathbf{A}}})^{2}
,
{\mathbf{B}}^{2}\rightarrow(\overline{{\mathbf{B}}})^{2}
, and

{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}\times{\mathbf{B}}\rightarrow\overline{{\mathbf{A}}}\times\overline{{\mathbf{A}}}\times\overline{{\mathbf{B}}}\times\overline{{\mathbf{B}}}
of
\overline{R}
,
\overline{S}
and

\overline{T}
respectively.

As the next definition suggests,
we can view bridges as the arrows in a category whose objects are
the anchors. Moreover, every bridge has a natural “converse.”

Definition 4.4
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{C}}
are finite algebras in a common signature
and
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
,
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S),({\mathbf{C}},\upsilon,U)
are anchors.
Let
T
be a bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)

and let
T^{\prime}
be a bridge from
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
to
({\mathbf{C}},\upsilon,U)
.

(1)

The
identity
bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to itself is the
relation given by

I_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in A^{4}\,:\,\text{$(a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})\in R$
and $(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2})\in\rho$}\}.

(2)

The
converse
of
T
is the subuniverse
T^{\cup}\leq{\mathbf{B}}\times{\mathbf{B}}\times{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{A}}
given by

T^{\cup}=\{(b_{1},b_{2},a_{1},a_{2})\,:\,\text{$(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T$}\}.

(3)

The
composition

T\circ T^{\prime}
is the subuniverse

T\circ T^{\prime}\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{C}}\times{\mathbf{C}}
given by

T\circ T^{\prime}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},c_{1},c_{2})\,:\,\text{$\exists b_{1},b_{2}\in B$ with
$(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T$ and $(b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2})\in T^{\prime}$}\}.

It is easy to check that identity bridges are bridges, the converse
of a bridge is a bridge, and the composition of bridges is a
bridge.

An important invariant of a bridge is its “trace.”

Definition 4.5
.

Suppose
T
is a bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
.

(1)

The
trace
of
T
, denoted
\operatorname{tr}(T)
,
is the subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
defined by

\operatorname{tr}(T)=\{(a,b)\,:\,\text{$(a,a,b,b)\in T$}\}
.

(2)

When
{\mathbf{B}}={\mathbf{A}}
, we say that
T
is
reflexive
if

0_{A}\subseteq\operatorname{tr}(T)
.

Zhuk
[
18
]
denoted
\operatorname{tr}(T)
by
\widetilde{T}
(and did not
call it a “trace”).
One can check that

\operatorname{tr}(I_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)})=\rho
,
\operatorname{tr}(T^{\cup})=\operatorname{tr}(T)^{-1}
,
and
\operatorname{tr}(T\circ T^{\prime})=\operatorname{tr}(T)\circ\operatorname{tr}(T^{\prime})
.

Remark 4.6
.

In his original definition, Zhuk viewed
a bridge
T
as being “from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)
,” not
“from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
.” The relations
R
and
S
were
left implicit, though recoverable from
T
via the equations

R=\operatorname{pr}_{1,2}(T)
and
S=\operatorname{pr}_{3,4}(T)
.

Definition 4.7
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
are finite algebras in a common signature,
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
with
\rho\neq 1_{A}
, and
\sigma\in\Con({\mathbf{B}})
with
\sigma\neq 1_{B}
.
A (
Zhuk
)
bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to

({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)
is
any bridge
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
, for some subuniverses

R\leq{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
and
S\leq{\mathbf{B}}^{2}

for which
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
and
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
are anchors.

Zhuk’s bridges cannot be viewed as arrows in a category whose objects
are pairs
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
, as composition is then not always defined. Happily, Zhuk
only ever used his bridges between pairs

({\mathbf{A}},\rho),({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)
for which there are
canonical choices for the relations
R
and
S
.

Definition 4.8

(Zhuk
[
18
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite algebra and
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
.

(1)

\rho
is
irreducible
if
\rho\neq 1_{A}
and
\rho
cannot be written as the
intersection
of two
\rho
-saturated subuniverses of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
both properly containing
\rho
.

(2)

If
\rho
is irreducible, then
\rho^{\ast}
denotes the unique smallest

\rho
-saturated subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
properly containing
\rho
.

The following fact is easily proved.

Lemma 4.9
.

If
{\mathbf{A}}
is finite and
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is irreducible, then
\rho
is meet-irreducible
in
\Con({\mathbf{A}})
; its unique upper cover
\rho^{+}
in
\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is the
transitive closure of

\rho^{\ast}
.

The next lemma explains the tight connection between Zhuk’s bridges and bridges as we defined
them in Definition
4.3
, when the congruences are irreducible.

Lemma 4.10

(Zhuk
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
are finite algebras in a common signature,

\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
, and
\sigma\in\Con({\mathbf{B}})
.
If
\rho
and
\sigma
are irreducible, then every bridge
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)
contains a
bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{\ast})
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,\sigma^{\ast})
with the same trace.

Proof
(Zhuk
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose
T
is a bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
.
Define
T_{1}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T\,:\,\text{$(a_{1},a_{2})\in\rho^{\ast}$}\}
.
Let
S_{1}=\operatorname{pr}_{3,4}(T_{1})
.
As
\rho^{\ast}\subseteq R=\operatorname{pr}_{1,2}(T)
, we get

\rho^{\ast}=\operatorname{pr}_{1,2}(T_{1})
. In particular, there exists

(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T_{1}
with
(a_{1},a_{2})\not\in\rho
.
By property
(B2∗)
, this implies
(b_{1},b_{2})\not\in\sigma
.
Hence
S_{1}\neq\sigma
. On the other hand,
\sigma\subseteq S=\operatorname{pr}_{3,4}(T)
and
(B2∗)
imply
\sigma\subseteq S_{1}
, and
(B0∗)
implies
S_{1}

is stable under
\sigma
. As
\sigma
is irreducible, these facts
imply

\sigma^{\ast}\subseteq S_{1}
. It can be checked that

T_{1}
is a bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{\ast})
to

({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S_{1})
with
\operatorname{tr}(T_{1})=\operatorname{tr}(T)
.

Next let
T_{2}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T_{1}\,:\,\text{$(b_{1},b_{2})\in\sigma^{\ast}$}\}
.
A similar argument shows

T_{2}
is a bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R_{2})
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,\sigma^{\ast})

with
\operatorname{tr}(T_{2})=\operatorname{tr}(T_{1})
, where
R_{2}=\operatorname{pr}_{1,2}(T_{2})
.
But clearly
\rho^{\ast}\subseteq R_{2}\subseteq\operatorname{pr}_{1,2}(T_{1})=\rho^{\ast}
,
i.e.,
R_{2}=\rho^{\ast}
as required.
∎

Definition 4.11
.

Given an anchor
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
, we let
\operatorname{ref\hskip-1.0pt.\hskip-2.0pttr}(\rho,R)
denote the set

\operatorname{ref\hskip-1.0pt.\hskip-2.0pttr}(\rho,R)=\{\operatorname{tr}(T)\,:\,\text{$T$ is a reflexive bridge from $({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)$ to
$({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)$}\}.

Note that each member of
\operatorname{ref\hskip-1.0pt.\hskip-2.0pttr}(\rho,R)
is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
containing
\rho
,
by
(B0∗)
.
In addition,
\operatorname{ref\hskip-1.0pt.\hskip-2.0pttr}(\rho,R)
is nonempty and closed under inversion and composition by the comments
following Definitions
4.4
and
4.5
.
It follows from this and finiteness that
\operatorname{ref\hskip-1.0pt.\hskip-2.0pttr}(\rho,R)
contains a unique maximal member;
and this unique maximal member is a congruence containing
\rho
.
The following definition and lemma record this observation.

Definition 4.12
.

If
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
is an anchor, then

\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,R)
denotes the unique maximal member of
\operatorname{ref\hskip-1.0pt.\hskip-2.0pttr}(\rho,R)
.

Lemma 4.13
.

For any anchor
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
we have
\rho\leq\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,R)\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
.

Definition 4.14

(Zhuk
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite algebra and
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is irreducible.

(1)

\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
denotes
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,\rho^{\ast})
.

(2)

A bridge
T
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
is

optimal
if
\operatorname{tr}(T)=\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
.

Zhuk defined
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
in a slightly different way: he let
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
be the
unique maximal member of the set

\{\operatorname{tr}(T)\,:\,\text{$T$ is a reflexive bridge from $({\mathbf{A}},\rho)$ to
$({\mathbf{A}},\rho)$}\}.

In fact, this set is identical to
\operatorname{ref\hskip-1.0pt.\hskip-2.0pttr}(\rho,\rho^{\ast})
by Lemma
4.10
, so
Zhuk’s and our definitions of
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
are equivalent.

The following fact can be extracted from Zhuk’s proof of
[
18
, Corollary 7.24.1]
.

Lemma 4.15
.

Suppose

({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
and
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
are anchors and
T
is a bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to

({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
. Let
\alpha=\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,R)
and
\beta=\operatorname{Opt}(\sigma,S)
.
Then
\operatorname{tr}(T)
induces an isomorphism
\gamma:{{\mathbf{A}}/\alpha}\cong{\mathbf{B}}/\beta
defined by

\gamma(a/\alpha)=b/\beta\iff(a,b)\in\alpha\circ\operatorname{tr}(T)\circ\beta.

Proof.

Suppose
(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in\operatorname{tr}(T)
. It suffices to show

(a,a^{\prime})\in\alpha\iff(b,b^{\prime})\in\beta
.
Assume
(b,b^{\prime})\in\beta
.
Let
T_{0}
be a reflexive bridge from
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,S)
to itself satisfying

\operatorname{tr}(T_{0})=\beta
.
Let
T_{1}=T\circ T_{0}
and
T_{2}=T_{1}\circ T_{1}^{\cup}
.
Then
(a,b^{\prime}),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in\operatorname{tr}(T)\circ\beta=\operatorname{tr}(T_{1})
and thus
(a,a^{\prime})\in\operatorname{tr}(T_{1})\circ\operatorname{tr}(T_{1})^{-1}=\operatorname{tr}(T_{2})
.
Since
T_{2}
is a reflexive bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)

to itself, we get
\operatorname{tr}(T_{2})\subseteq\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,R)=\alpha
,
so
(a,a^{\prime})\in\alpha
.
Thus we have proved
(b,b^{\prime})\in\beta
implies
(a,a^{\prime})\in\alpha
.
A similar proof shows the opposite implication.
∎

Lemma 4.16
.

Suppose
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
and
({\mathbf{A}},\sigma,S)
are anchors on the same algebra.
If there exists a reflexive bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to
({\mathbf{A}},\sigma,S)
,
then
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,R)=\operatorname{Opt}(\sigma,S)
.

Proof.

Let
T_{1}
be a reflexive bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to
({\mathbf{A}},\sigma,S)
.
Let
T_{2}
be a reflexive bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,R)
to itself satisfying
\operatorname{tr}(T_{2})=\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,R)
.
Let
T_{3}=T_{1}^{\cup}\circ T_{2}\circ T_{1}
. We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,R)

\displaystyle=0_{A}\circ\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,R)\circ 0_{A}

\displaystyle\subseteq\operatorname{tr}(T_{1}^{\cup})\circ\operatorname{tr}(T_{2})\circ\operatorname{tr}(T_{1})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(T_{3})

\displaystyle\subseteq\operatorname{Opt}(\sigma,S)

where the last inclusion is because
T_{3}
is a reflexive bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\sigma,S)

to itself. A symmetric argument shows
\operatorname{Opt}(\sigma,S)\subseteq\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,R)
.
∎

Definition 4.17

(cf. Zhuk
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite algebra and
\rho,\sigma\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
are irreducible.
We say that
\rho
and
\sigma
are
adjacent
if there exists a reflexive
bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to
({\mathbf{A}},\sigma)
.

Corollary 4.18

(Zhuk
[
18
, Lemma 6.4]
)
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite algebra and
\rho,\sigma\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
are
irreducible and adjacent. Then
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)=\operatorname{Opt}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

Let
T
be a reflexive bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to
({\mathbf{A}},\sigma)
.
By Lemma
4.10
, we may assume that
T
is from

({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{\ast})
to
({\mathbf{A}},\sigma,\sigma^{\ast})
.
Now apply Lemma
4.16
.
∎

This completes our development of the basic terminology and results concerning bridges
from
[
18
]
.

5.
Rooted bridges in locally finite Taylor varieties

In this short section we use tame congruence theory, first to characterize
irreducible congruences in finite Taylor algebras, and secondly to extend the
material from the previous section to
(certain) bridges, which we call
“rooted bridges,” between arbitrary meet-irreducible congruences of
finite Taylor algebras.

Definition 5.1
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite algebra,
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is meet-irreducible, and

\rho^{+}
is its unique upper cover.

\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)
denotes the set of minimal (under inclusion)
\rho
-saturated subuniverses

\tau
of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
satisfying
\rho\subset\tau\subseteq\rho^{+}
.

If
\rho
is irreducible, then clearly
\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)=\{\rho^{\ast}\}
.
However, it can happen that
\rho
is meet-irreducible and
|\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)|=1
,
yet
\rho
is not irreducible; see Lemma
5.5
.

Definition 5.2
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
are finite algebras in a common signature,

\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
,
\sigma\in\Con({\mathbf{B}})
,
and
\rho
and
\sigma
are meet-irreducible. A bridge
T
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)

to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)
is
rooted
if there exist
\tau\in\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)

and
\tau^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Cov}(\sigma)
such that
T
contains a bridge
T^{\prime}
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\tau)
to

({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,\tau^{\prime})
with
\operatorname{tr}(T^{\prime})=\operatorname{tr}(T)
.

Equivalently, a bridge
T
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)

is rooted if and only if the set

T_{0}:=T\cap\{(a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime})\,:\,\text{$(a,a^{\prime})\in\rho^{+}$ and
$(b,b^{\prime})\in\sigma^{+}$}\}

satisfies
\operatorname{pr}_{1,2}(T_{0})\neq\rho
,
where
\rho^{+},\sigma^{+}
are the unique upper covers of
\rho,\sigma

respectively.

Observe that if
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
and
\sigma\in\Con({\mathbf{B}})
are irreducible,
then every bridge
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)
is rooted by Lemma
4.10
.
In extending Zhuk’s theory to meet-irreducible congruences, we will only ever
consider rooted bridges.

Next, we introduce some notation and record two facts about

\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)
given by tame congruence theory.

Definition 5.3
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite algebra,
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is meet-irreducible, and

\rho^{+}
is its unique upper cover.
Let
\overline{\rho}
denote the following subset of
\rho^{+}
:

\overline{\rho}=\rho\circ\left(0_{A}\cup\bigcup\{N^{2}\,:\,\text{$N$ is a $(\rho,\rho^{+})$-trace}\}\right)\circ\rho.

Proposition 5.4

(
[
8
, Lemma 5.24]
)
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite algebra,
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is meet-irreducible, and

\rho^{+}
is its unique upper cover.

(1)

If
\operatorname{typ}(\rho,\rho^{+})\in\{\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
, then
\overline{\rho}\leq{\mathbf{A}}^{2}

and
\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)=\{\overline{\rho}\}
.

(2)

If
\operatorname{typ}(\rho,\rho^{+})\in\{\mathbf{4},\mathbf{5}\}
, then

|\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)|=2
, say
\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)=\{\tau_{0},\tau_{1}\}
. Moreover,

\tau_{1}=\tau_{0}^{-1}
,
\tau_{0}\cap\tau_{1}=\rho
, and
\tau_{0}\cup\tau_{1}=\overline{\rho}
.

Now we can characterize irreducible congruences in finite Taylor algebras.

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite Taylor algebra,

\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

is meet-irreducible, and
\rho^{+}
is its unique upper cover.

(1)

The following are equivalent:

(a)

\rho
is irreducible.

(b)

\operatorname{typ}(\rho,\rho^{+})\in\{\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
, and for all
(a,b)\in A^{2}\setminus\rho^{+}
there exists a unary polynomial
f(x)\in\Pol_{1}({\mathbf{A}})
with

(f(a),f(b))\in\rho^{+}\setminus\rho
.

(2)

If
\rho
is irreducible, then
\rho^{\ast}=\overline{\rho}
.

Proof.

Assume
\rho
is irreducible.
We have
\operatorname{typ}(\rho,\rho^{+})\neq\mathbf{1}
by Theorem
2.9
.
Irreducibility of
\rho
forces
|\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)|=1
, so

\operatorname{typ}(\rho,\rho^{+})\in\{\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
by
Proposition
5.4
(
2
).
Assume next that there exists
(a,b)\in A^{2}\setminus\rho^{+}
such that

(f(a),f(b))\not\in\rho^{+}\setminus\rho
for all
f\in\Pol_{1}({\mathbf{B}})
.
Let
\sigma=\sg^{{\mathbf{A}}^{2}}(\{(a,b)\}\cup 0_{A})
.
The assumption implies
\sigma\cap\rho^{+}\subseteq\rho
, so
(\rho\circ\sigma\circ\rho)\cap\rho^{+}=\rho
, which
would contradict irreducibility of
\rho
. Thus if
\rho
is irreducible,
then the conditions in item (
1b
) hold.

Conversely, assume that the conditions in item (
1b
) hold.
Since
\operatorname{typ}(\rho,\rho^{+})\in\{\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
, we have
\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)=\{\overline{\rho}\}

by Proposition
5.4
(
1
).
We will show that
\rho
is irreducible with
\rho^{\ast}=\overline{\rho}
, which will
also establish item (
2
).
Let
R
be a
\rho
-saturated subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}

which properly contains
\rho
; we must show
\overline{\rho}\subseteq R
.
It will suffice to prove
R\cap\rho^{+}\neq\rho
, as then
R\cap\rho^{+}
will be
a
\rho
-saturated subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
satisfying
\rho\subset R\cap\rho^{+}\subseteq\rho^{+}
, so
\overline{\rho}\subseteq R\cap\rho^{+}
as
\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)=\{\overline{\rho}\}

by Proposition
5.4
(
1
).
To prove
R\cap\rho^{+}\neq\rho
, pick
(a,b)\in R\setminus\rho
. If
(a,b)\in\rho^{+}
then
we are done, so assume
(a,b)\not\in\rho^{+}
.
By condition (
1b
),
there exists
f\in\Pol_{1}({\mathbf{A}})
with
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}):=(f(a),f(b))\in\rho^{+}\setminus\rho
.
Then
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in\sg^{{\mathbf{A}}^{2}}(\{(a,b)\}\cup 0_{A})\subseteq R
,
so
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
witnesses
R\cap\rho^{+}\neq\rho
.
∎

The following easy lemma will help us extend the notions of
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
and adjacency from
irreducible congruences to meet-irreducible congruences.

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite Taylor algebra,

\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is meet-irreducible, and

\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)
. Then there exists a reflexive bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\tau)

to
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\tau^{\prime})
.
Hence
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,\tau)=\operatorname{Opt}(\rho,\tau^{\prime})
.

Proof.

If
\tau^{\prime}=\tau
, then we can use the identity bridge
I_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\tau)}
. Otherwise,
by Proposition
5.4
we must have
\operatorname{typ}(\rho,\rho^{+})\in\{\mathbf{4},\mathbf{5}\}

and
\tau^{\prime}=\tau^{-1}
.
Then the set
T=\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\,:\,\text{$(a_{1},a_{2},b_{2},b_{1})\in I_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\tau)}$}\}

is the required reflexive bridge. The last claim follows by Lemma
4.16
.
∎

Proposition
5.4
(
1
) and
Lemmas

(

) and
5.6
justify the

\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
from irreducible congruences to

.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite Taylor algebra

\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

(1)

\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
denotes the (unique) congruence

where

.

(2)

A bridge

from

to

is

\operatorname{tr}(T)=\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
.

We also extend the adjacency relation to meet-irreducible congruences, as follows.

.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite Taylor algebra
and
\rho,\sigma\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

Say that

and

.

Our definition of adjacency in the above context differs from Zhuk’s definition in

]
;

Corollary

.

Suppose

.

6.
Connecting bridges to centrality and similarity

In this final section we give our main results. We prove that,
in the context developed in the previous section, the
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
construction
is simply the centralizer
(\rho:\rho^{+})
(Lemma
6.2
);
we apply this and results from
[
16
]
to extend two important
results from
[
18
]
(Theorem
6.4
and Lemma
6.7
);
and we prove that the “there exists a rooted bridge”
relation between pairs
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
and
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)

is exactly the similarity relation between the respective
quotient algebras
{\mathbf{A}}/\rho
and
{\mathbf{B}}/\sigma
(Corollary
6.11
).

Definition 6.1
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite Taylor algebra
and
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is meet-irreducible with unique upper cover
\rho^{+}
.
If
\rho^{+}/\rho
is abelian, let
\alpha=(\rho:\rho^{+})
, define

\Delta^{\flat}_{\rho^{+},\alpha}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in A^{4}\,:\,\text{$((a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2}))\in\Delta_{\rho^{+},\alpha}$}\},

and set
T^{\,\mathsf{opt}}_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho)}=I_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{+})}\circ\Delta_{\rho^{+},\alpha}^{\flat}\circ I_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{+})}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite Taylor algebra
and
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is meet-irreducible with unique upper cover

\rho^{+}
.

(1)

\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)=(\rho:\rho^{+})
.

(2)

Hence
\rho^{+}/\rho
is
abelian if and only if
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)>\rho
.

(3)

If
\rho^{+}/\rho
is abelian, then
\overline{\rho}=\rho^{+}
, so
\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)=\{\rho^{+}\}
, and

T^{\,\mathsf{opt}}_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho)}
is an optimal bridge
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to itself.

Proof.

We first show if
\rho^{+}/\rho
is abelian, i.e.,
\operatorname{typ}(\rho,\rho^{+})=\mathbf{2}
,
then
\overline{\rho}=\rho^{+}
.
Note that
\overline{\rho}/\rho
is a reflexive subuniverse of
({\mathbf{A}}/\rho)^{2}
contained in the abelian
minimal congruence
\rho^{+}/\rho
and properly containing
0_{A/\rho}
.

{\mathbf{A}}/\rho
has a weak difference term by Theorem
2.9
, which is a Maltsev
operation when restricted to each block of
\rho^{+}/\rho
.
Hence
\overline{\rho}/\rho=\rho^{+}/\rho
by Lemma
2.6
.
Since
\overline{\rho}
is
\rho
-saturated, it follows that
\overline{\rho}=\rho^{+}
.
Hence
\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)=\{\rho^{+}\}
by Proposition
5.4
(
1
).

Next we show that if
\rho^{+}/\rho
is abelian, then
T^{\,\mathsf{opt}}_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho)}
is a
bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{+})
to itself with trace
\alpha:=(\rho:\rho^{+})
.
Note that
\Delta^{\flat}_{\rho^{+},\alpha}
is not necessarily a bridge
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{+})
to itself,
because it may fail to satisfy
(B0∗)
.
However it does satisfy
(B1∗)
and
(B2∗)
,
the latter by Lemma
3.2
using
C(\alpha,\rho^{+};\rho)
.
Pre- and post-composing
\Delta^{\flat}_{\rho^{+},\alpha}

with
I_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{+})}
preserves

(B1∗)
and
(B2∗)
and also guarantees
(B0∗)
.
Thus
T^{\,\mathsf{opt}}_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho)}
is indeed a bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{+})
to itself.
Finally,

\operatorname{tr}(T^{\,\mathsf{opt}}_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho)})=\operatorname{tr}(I_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{+})})\circ\operatorname{tr}(\Delta^{\flat}_{\rho^{+},\alpha})\circ\operatorname{tr}(I_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{+})})=\rho\circ\alpha\circ\rho=\alpha

as required. Clearly
T^{\,\mathsf{opt}}_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho)}
is rooted.

Next, we will prove (
1
).
Assume first that
\rho^{+}/\rho
is nonabelian. Because
\rho
is
meet-irreducible and
\rho^{+}
is its unique upper cover, we get

(\rho:\rho^{+})=\rho
.
As
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)\geq\rho
by Lemma
4.13
, it will be enough in this case to
show that
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)\ngeq\rho^{+}
.
Assume instead that
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)\geq\rho^{+}
.
Choose
\tau\in\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)
and let

T_{0}
be an optimal bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\tau)
to itself.
Let
T=T_{0}\circ T_{0}
; then
T
is also an optimal bridge from

({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\tau)
to itself. Hence
\operatorname{tr}(T)=\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
, and moreover

(a,b,a,b)\in T
for all
(a,b)\in\tau
.

Pick a
(\rho,\rho^{+})
-trace
N
.
By tame congruence theory,
i.e., Proposition
2.8
,
there exists
(0,1)\in N^{2}\setminus\rho

and a binary polynomial

p(x,y)\in\Pol_{2}({\mathbf{A}})
such that
p(0,0)=p(0,1)=p(1,0)=0
and
p(1,1)=1
.
Then
(0,1)\in\overline{\rho}
(see Definition
5.3
), and since
\overline{\rho}=\tau\cup\tau^{-1}

by Proposition
5.4
, we have either
(0,1)\in\tau
or
(1,0)\in\tau
.
Assume with no loss of generality that
(0,1)\in\tau
.

Choose a

(2+n)
-ary term and a tuple
\mathbf{c}\in A^{n}
so that
p(x,y)=t(x,y,\mathbf{c})
.
Then we have the following tuples in
T
:

\begin{array}[]{ccl}(~0,~1,~0,~1\,)\in T&\rule{36.135pt}{0.0pt}&\mbox{as $(0,1)\in\tau$}\\[3.61371pt]
(~0,~0,~1,~1\,)\in T&&\mbox{as $(0,1)\in\rho^{+}\leq\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)=\operatorname{tr}(T)$}\\[3.61371pt]
\begin{array}[]{c}(c_{1},c_{1},c_{1},c_{1})\in T\\
\vdots\\
(c_{n},c_{n},c_{n},c_{n})\in T\end{array}&\left.\rule{0.0pt}{25.29494pt}\right\}&\mbox{as $T$ is reflexive}\end{array}

Applying
t
coordinate-wise to these tuples gives
(0,0,0,1)\in T
,
contradicting
(B2∗)
since
(0,1)\not\in\rho
. This contradiction
proves
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)\ngeq\rho^{+}
and hence
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)=\rho
when
\rho^{+}/\rho
is
nonabelian.

Assume next that
\rho^{+}/\rho
is abelian.
We will first show
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)\leq(\rho:\rho^{+})
.
As shown earlier,
\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)=\{\rho^{+}\}
.
Fix an optimal bridge
T
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\rho^{+})
to itself.
By replacing
T
with
T\circ T
, we may assume that
(a,b,a,b)\in T
for all
(a,b)\in\rho^{+}
. To prove
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)\leq(\rho:\rho^{+})
, we will simply show that

C(\operatorname{tr}(T),\rho^{+};\rho)
holds by verifying the condition in
Definition
2.2
(
3
).

Let
t(x,\mathbf{y})
be a
(1+n)
-ary term, let
(a,b)\in\operatorname{tr}(T)
, and let

(c_{i},d_{i})\in\rho^{+}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Then we have the following tuples in
T
:

\displaystyle(~a,~a,~b,~b~)\in T

\displaystyle(c_{1},d_{1},c_{1},d_{1})\in T

\displaystyle(c_{n},d_{n},c_{n},d_{n})\in T

Applying
t
coordinatewise gives

(t(a,\mathbf{c}),t(a,\mathbf{d}),t(b,\mathbf{c}),t(b,\mathbf{d}))\in T.

Then by
(B2∗)
,

t(a,\mathbf{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho}}{{\equiv}}t(a,\mathbf{d})\iff t(b,\mathbf{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho}}{{\equiv}}t(b,\mathbf{d}).

This proves that
C(\operatorname{tr}(T),\rho^{+};\rho)
holds
and hence
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)=\operatorname{tr}(T)\leq(\rho:\rho^{+})
.

On the other hand, we have already shown that
T^{\,\mathsf{opt}}_{({\mathbf{A}},\rho)}
is a rooted bridge
from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to itself with trace
(\rho:\rho^{+})
.
Hence
(\rho:\rho^{+})\leq\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)
, which proves
\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)=(\rho:\rho^{+})
in the
abelian case and completes
the proof of (
1
) and (
3
). (
2
) follows
from (
1
).
∎

Using our results about similarity,
we can now easily obtain (and generalize) one of the

.

Definition 6.3
.

An algebra is
affine
if it is abelian and has a Maltsev term.

Theorem 6.4

(Cf. Zhuk
[

.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}

\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

\operatorname{Opt}(\rho)=1_{A}

Then there exists a simple affine algebra
{\mathbf{Z}}\in\mathsf{HS}({\mathbf{A}}^{2})

\rho^{+}

Next we establish a simple invariant of rooted bridges.

Theorem 6.6
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
are finite algebras in a locally finite Taylor variety,
and

\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
,
\sigma\in\Con({\mathbf{B}})
, where
\rho
and
\sigma

are meet-irreducible. Let
\rho^{+},\sigma^{+}
be the respective unique upper

\rho,\sigma
.
Assume that there exists a rooted bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)
.
Then

\rho^{+}/\rho
is abelian if and only if
\sigma^{+}/\sigma
is abelian.

Proof.

Let
T
be a rooted bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)
.
We may assume that
T
is from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho,\tau)
to
({\mathbf{B}},\sigma,\tau^{\prime})

where
\tau\in\operatorname{Cov}(\rho)
and
\tau^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Cov}(\sigma)

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\rho^{+}/\rho
is abelian while

\sigma^{+}/\sigma

\operatorname{typ}(\rho,\rho^{+})=\mathbf{2}
while
\operatorname{typ}(\sigma,\sigma^{+})\in\{\mathbf{3},\mathbf{4},\mathbf{5}\}

\tau=\rho^{+}
by Lemma
6.2

and
\tau^{\prime}\cup(\tau^{\prime})^{-1}=\overline{\sigma}
by Proposition
5.4
.

By passing to
\overline{{\mathbf{A}}}:={\mathbf{A}}/\rho
and
\overline{{\mathbf{B}}}:={\mathbf{B}}/\sigma
,
we may assume that
\rho=0_{A}
and
\sigma=0_{B}
.
Rename
\rho^{+}
and
\sigma^{+}
as
\mu_{{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\mu_{{\mathbf{B}}}
respectively.
Let
\alpha=(0_{A}:\mu_{{\mathbf{A}}})
and observe that
(0_{B}:\mu_{{\mathbf{B}}})=0_{B}
. Replace
T

with
T^{\,\mathsf{opt}}_{({\mathbf{A}},0)}\circ T
;
then by Lemma
4.15
, the rule

h(a)=b\iff(a,b)\in\operatorname{tr}(T)

defines a surjective homomorphism
h:{\mathbf{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
with kernel
\alpha
.

Claim 6.6.1
.

For all
f\in\Pol_{k}({\mathbf{B}})
there exists

such that

(1)

h(f_{{\mathbf{A}}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}))=f(h(x_{1}),\ldots,h(x_{k}))

x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in A
.

(2)

For all
(x_{1},y_{1},u_{1},v_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},y_{k},u_{k},v_{k})\in T
we have

(f_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\mathbf{x}),f_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\mathbf{y}),f(\mathbf{u}),f(\mathbf{v}))\in T.

Proof of Claim

.

Indeed, if we select a term
t(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
and
\mathbf{b}\in B^{n}

so that
f(\mathbf{x})=t^{{\mathbf{B}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b})
, then we simply need to select

\mathbf{a}\in A^{n}
with
h(a_{i})=b_{i}
for each

and then define

f_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\mathbf{x})=t^{{\mathbf{A}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})
. Item (1) then follows immediately,
and item (2) follows from the fact that
(a_{i},a_{i},b_{i},b_{i})\in T
for all
i\in[n]
.
∎

By tame congruence theory, i.e., Proposition
2.8
,
there exists a
(0_{B},\mu_{{\mathbf{B}}})
-minimal set

with unique

(0_{B},\mu_{{\mathbf{B}}})
-trace

,

(1)

and

for all

.

(2)

encode similarity between the respective subdirectly irreducible quotients.

Suppose

\overline{{\mathbf{B}}}={\mathbf{B}}/\sigma

Let
\mu

\kappa
be the monoliths of
\overline{{\mathbf{A}}}

By the discussion following Definition
4.3
,
(
2
) is equivalent to

(2
′
)

to
\overline{{\mathbf{B}}}

Thus (

\Leftrightarrow
(

7.
Conclusion

This paper is the second of three which aim to understand Zhuk’s bridges
and their application in algebraic terms. In the first paper
[
16
]
,
we developed the similarity relation between subdirectly irreducible algebras
in varieties with a weak difference term.
We showed in
[
16
]
that, when the
monoliths are abelian, the monolith blocks of
similar finite subdirectly irreducible algebras naturally inherit the structure
of vector spaces over a common finite field.
In the current paper, we have connected
Zhuk’s bridges to similarity. In particular, if
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}

are finite algebras in a Taylor variety and
\rho,\sigma
are meet-irreducible
congruences of
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
respectively, then
the existence of a rooted bridge from
({\mathbf{A}},\rho)
to

({\mathbf{B}},\sigma)
is equivalent to
{\mathbf{A}}/\rho
and
{\mathbf{B}}/\sigma
being similar.

The remaining task in our project is to apply the tools of similarity
developed in
[
16
]
to the bridges Zhuk
[
18
]

showed arise from rectangular critical relations
(among finite algebras in locally finite idempotent Taylor varieties).
In a third paper
[
15
]
, we will do just that, harmonizing Zhuk’s
analysis with
the corresponding analysis of Kearnes and Szendrei

[
11
]
in the congruence modular case.
In particular, we will show that every finite rectangular critical subdirect
product
R\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
in a Taylor variety
is described locally by a single linear equation over the finite field
arising from the similarity relation.
