Cool baby oil for pruritus and sleep quality among uremic patients: a nurse-led randomized controlled trial
Authors: Maha Gamal Ramadan Asal, Hoda Abdou Abd El-Monem El-Deeb, Mona Abd Elmaksoud Amer, Mervat Abdel Hamid Hawash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUremic pruritus (UP) is a prevalent and debilitating condition among patients undergoing hemodialysis that significantly impairs sleep quality and overall well-being. Despite its complex, multifactorial pathophysiology, effective management of pruritus remains challenging. Baby oil, known for its moisturizing properties, has been suggested as a potential intervention for alleviating pruritus. However, existing studies have focused primarily on its short-term effects, leaving a gap in understanding its sustained impact. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of cool paraffin-based baby oil massage on pruritus severity and sleep quality in hemodialysis patients over four and twelve weeks compared with those of a placebo.MethodsThis parallel-group, randomized, double-blind (participant and outcome evaluators), placebo-controlled trial involved 82 adults, randomized to either the baby oil group (n = 41), which received massage with commercial cool paraffin-based baby oil, or the placebo group (n = 41), which received massage with 10 cc of cool distilled water mixed with two drops of oil. The trial was conducted in two hemodialysis centers over a 12-week treatment period beginning in October 2022. The primary outcomes included pruritus severity, which was assessed via the Itching Severity Scale (ISS) and Pruritus Visual Analogue Scale (PVAS), and sleep quality, which was measured via the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).ResultsA total of 80 participants (40 in each group) were analysed. Compared with the placebo group, the baby oil group presented significantly greater reductions in pruritus severity, with mean differences of 8.70 (p = 0.03, d = 1.32) at 4 weeks and 10.03 (p < 0.01, d = 2.58) at 12 weeks for the ISS. For the PVAS, the mean difference was 1.83 (p < 0.01, d = 1.06) at 4 weeks and 4.42 (p < 0.01, d = 3.57) at 12 weeks. Sleep quality also improved significantly, with mean differences of 2.57 (p < 0.01, d = 0.97) at 4 weeks and 5.75 (p < 0.01, d = 2.95) at 12 weeks.ConclusionParaffin-based cool baby oil massage is an effective intervention for reducing pruritus severity and improving sleep quality among hemodialysis patients with UP. This simple intervention can be easily incorporated into nursing care plans, offering a practical approach to enhancing patient comfort and quality of life. By alleviating pruritus and promoting better sleep, it has the potential to significantly improve the well-being of patients with end-stage renal disease undergoing hemodialysis.Trial registrationThis study was prospectively registered as a randomized controlled trial at ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration ID: NCT05555524) on September 22, 2022. The full trial details are available at https://clinicaltrials.gov/.Keywords: Cool baby oil, Pruritus, Sleep quality, Uremic patients, Nurse-led, Randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Uremic pruritus (UP) is a common and distressing symptom in dialysis-dependent end-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients and is defined as itching lasting at least three months [1]. A meta-analysis of 42 cross-sectional studies revealed a 55% prevalence of pruritus among hemodialysis (HD) patients, significantly impairing their quality of life (QoL) through persistent discomfort, sleep disturbances, and depressive symptoms, which collectively contribute to a worsened prognosis [2]. This persistent itching sensation often provokes excessive scratching, compromising the integrity of the skin as a protective barrier and exacerbating the severity of the condition [3].

The multifactorial pathogenesis of UP includes xerosis, neuropathy, inflammation, and immune dysfunction [4, 5], which complicate treatment. Despite its high prevalence, UP is often underrecognized in HD patients, leading to persistent symptoms. Its severity ranges from mild to severe, with nighttime pruritus affecting 9% to 76% of patients and causing sleep disturbances [6].

The European S2k Guideline on Chronic Pruritus recommends a stepwise, evidence-based management approach, starting with basic skin care as the first-line treatment, followed by symptomatic therapies and systemic options for refractory cases [7]. Basic skin care aims to restore hydration and reduce xerosis-induced itching through daily moisturization with emollients (e.g., urea, glycerol), mild non-alkaline cleansers, cool baths, breathable clothing, and irritant avoidance [8].

If symptoms persist, topical capsaicin, calcineurin inhibitors, and phototherapy may be used. Systemic treatments, such as gabapentinoids and antidepressants, are reserved for severe cases but are limited by side effects in ESRD patients. The recent FDA approval of difelikefalin, a selective kappa opioid receptor agonist, has led to significant advancements in moderate to severe UP treatment [8].

Within this basic skin care framework, baby oil has emerged as a promising first-line intervention for UP management. Given its moisturizing properties, baby oil is a practical, cost-effective, and well-tolerated option, particularly in the early stages. The liquid paraffin component forms an occlusive barrier that prevents water evaporation, maintaining skin moisture by reducing trans-epidermal water loss [9].

Cold temperatures constrict blood vessels, reduce cell metabolism and nerve conduction, disrupt nerve transmission, impair neural receptors, and provide localized anaesthesia [10]. In ESRD, skin dryness is a major contributor to pruritus; therefore, baby oil may help relieve symptoms by increasing skin hydration and reducing transepidermal water loss.

Earlier studies on the use of moisturizers for UP have shown promising results, suggesting that moisturizers can relieve or even treat UP effectively [11, 12]. Although short-term studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of baby oil in UP management, these studies have typically evaluated effects over four weeks and were limited by concerns about performance bias due to the lack of blinding [13–16].

The chronic nature of UP requires evaluating whether baby oil provides sustained benefits, yet evidence on its long-term effectiveness and potential side effects is limited. Although a 12-month study is the gold standard, such durations are challenging in HD settings due to patient retention issues. Most UP trials, particularly phase 3 studies, assess effects over 8–12 weeks [17, 18]. To balance feasibility with meaningful insights into medium-term effects and ensure comparability, a 12-week period was selected for this study.

Nurses play a key role in alleviating patient suffering through direct care and evidence-based interventions, with symptom management being central to their duties [19]. As a potential noninvasive solution, baby oil—widely used for skincare—may help manage UP symptoms because of its moisturizing and protective properties.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of cool paraffin-based baby oil massage over a placebo in improving sleep quality and reducing itching severity over time. By assessing its impact, this study seeks to provide evidence for a simple and accessible nursing intervention that could offer sustained symptom relief for patients with UP. Accordingly, we hypothesize that patients receiving cool paraffin-based baby oil massage will experience improved sleep quality and reduced itching severity at both four and twelve weeks of follow-up compared with those receiving the placebo.

Materials and methods

Study design

This parallel-group, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial aimed to evaluate the effects of cool paraffin-based baby oil massage compared with a placebo on pruritus severity and sleep quality among patients undergoing HD at 4 and 12 weeks of follow-up.

The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials 2010 (CONSORT) were followed in the execution and reporting of the study. To evaluate the intervention’s effectiveness, the following hypotheses were formulated:

H1

Compared with those receiving the placebo, those receiving baby oil massage will report better sleep quality at 4 weeks of follow-up.

H2

Compared with those receiving the placebo, those receiving baby oil massage will exhibit decreased itching severity at 4 weeks of follow-up.

H3

Compared with those receiving the placebo, those receiving baby oil massage will report better sleep quality at 12 weeks of follow-up.

H4

Compared with those receiving the placebo, those receiving baby oil massage will exhibit decreased itching severity at 12 weeks of follow-up.

Setting

The study was conducted at two HD centers affiliated with Alexandria University, Egypt. These centers have a total bed capacity of 100 beds and provide care to approximately 330 patients with ESRD each week, operating on two shifts per day.

Participants and sampling

The study included patients diagnosed with ESRD undergoing maintenance HD three times per week at the selected units. All participants had either grafts or fistulas as their vascular access. Participants were eligible if they (1) were 18 years or older, (2) self-reported pruritus was defined as at least three itching episodes lasting five minutes or longer within the past two weeks, which is consistent with standard clinical definitions of pruritus intensity and duration, (3) self-reported intermittent itching complaints over the past six months, and (4) greed to maintain a fixed dose of medical treatment throughout the study.

The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) open wounds, skin infections, or edema; (2) history of allergic reactions to the oil used; (3) liver or dermatological diseases; (4) use of treatments other than prescribed medical treatment within the two weeks prior to randomization or during the study; (5) prior use of baby oil for pruritus treatment; or (6) enrollment in another clinical trial.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated on the basis of the primary comparison of pruritus scores between the study groups. In accordance with an earlier study [13], a total of 68 participants (34 participants per group) were estimated with a type I error of 0.05 and a power of 95% via G*Power (version 3.1.9.7). To account for a 20% drop-out rate, the sample size was increased to 82.

Measures

Sociodemographic and clinical data assessment sheet

This sheet collected data on age, gender, dialysis vintage, comorbidities, duration of itching, use of antihistamines, and the period of most intense pruritus. Laboratory measures that may influence pruritus were obtained at baseline and at the end of the study. These included dialysis adequacy, assessed by Kt/V (urea clearance × dialysis time ÷ volume of distribution of urea) and the urea reduction ratio (URR) as well as parathyroid hormone, serum calcium, creatinine, and phosphate levels. These data were retrieved from patient interviews and medical records.

Pruritus Visual Analogue Scale (PVAS)

The primary outcome was measured by assessing the intensity of itching via the Pruritus Visual Analogue Scale (PVAS). This tool is validated for quantifying subjective pruritus severity over a specified recall period (last 7 days), facilitating standardized assessment of symptom fluctuations. Patients were asked to rate the severity of their itching over the last 7 days. The PVAS consists of a 10-centimeter-long line with a single question. The left endpoint represents “no itch” (score of 0), whereas the right endpoint represents “the worst imaginable itch” (score of 10).

The PVAS scores were categorized as follows: 0 for no pruritus, < 3 for mild pruritus, ≥ 3 to < 7 for moderate pruritus, ≥ 7 to < 9 for severe pruritus, and ≥ 9 for very severe pruritus [20]. The primary endpoint was the mean change in PVAS from baseline after 4 and 12 weeks, with the significant change defined by the minimal clinically important difference of a 2–3-point decrease in PVAS, as highlighted in the study by Reich et al. [21].

Itching Severity Scale (ISS)

The itching severity was further measured via the Modified Itching Severity Scale (ISS), which is a standardized, valid, and reliable self-report instrument measuring pruritus severity. The ISS includes three subscales; the severity subscale ranges from 1 to 5 points, the distribution subscale ranges from 1 to 3 points, and the frequency of pruritus-related sleep disturbance subscale ranges from 1 to 2 points.

The total ISS ranges from 3 to 45. A high score indicates high severity and intensity of itching [22]. Using the ISS alongside the PVAS provides a multidimensional evaluation of pruritus, incorporating distribution and sleep disturbance components, which is essential for comprehensive assessment.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

Sleep quality was assessed via the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The PSQI, a widely used self-report questionnaire, is regarded as a generic instrument for assessing sleep quality in a variety of populations. It includes seven components: subjective sleep qualities, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction.

These components are assessed by 21 questions. The overall score was calculated by summing the scores of these components, which were rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3. The PSQI total score ranges from 0 to 21. A score of 5 indicates good sleep, whereas a score of more than 5 indicates poor or difficult sleep [23].

The tools were culturally adapted by translating them into Arabic, followed by back translation into English by two independent translators. A panel of five experts in medical-surgical nursing assessed the content validity of the tools. They used a four-point scale to rate the relevance of items, with scores of 1 and 2 (not relevant and somewhat relevant) assigned 0 and scores of 3 and 4 (quite relevant and highly relevant) assigned 1.

Content validity was determined via the item content validity index (I-CVI) for individual items and the scale content validity index (S-CVI) for the overall scale. All the items achieved an I-CVI greater than 0.83 and an S-CVI over 0.90, confirming scale validity. A pilot study with 9 participants was conducted to test clarity and applicability, revealing no significant issues. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.781 for the ISS and 0.754 for the PSQI, indicating acceptable reliability.

Data collection

Eligibility was verified through a structured interview conducted by the researchers. The participants were asked about the frequency and duration of itching. The information provided during the interview was cross-checked with the patients’ clinical history and records when available. A total of 151 patients were assessed, of whom 62 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 7 declined. Before randomization, sociodemographic and clinical data were assessed. Study outcomes were assessed at baseline (T0) after one month (T1) and three months (T2) via the ISS, PVAS, and PSQI. Data collection started in October 2022.

Randomization and blindness

Randomization was performed via simple 1:1 allocation, with 41 patients assigned to each group: the baby oil group or the placebo group. A computer-generated sequence ensured unbiased allocation [24]. Allocation information was kept confidential in sealed opaque envelopes, accessible only to clinical researchers. After baseline assessments and informed consent, the principal investigator opened the envelopes sequentially and administered the assigned intervention.

To maintain blinding, both baby oil and placebo were placed in identical, unmarked bottles. The placebo consisted of 10 cc distilled water with two drops of the investigational oil, mimicking the odor, texture, and tactile properties of the baby oil. Both solutions were stored in indistinguishable containers without labels. Participants were treated in separate rooms to prevent contamination and preserve blinding. Each room was used consistently for its assigned intervention, ensuring physical separation of groups and maintaining the integrity of blinding.

Outcome assessor remained blind throughout the study. Group assignments were labelled ‘Group A’ and ‘Group B’ and kept in sealed envelopes accessible only to the intervention team. Assessors, independent of the intervention team, were unaware of group allocations and prohibited from interacting with them. They measured predefined clinical outcomes using validated tools. Additionally, the statistician conducting the data analysis was blinded to the group assignments, ensuring that the analysis remained independent of group allocation.

Interventions

Both groups followed identical preparation procedures. The participants wore hospital gowns and were comfortably seated on their beds. Standard skin care was applied to the affected area, ensuring that it was thoroughly dried before the intervention. The pruritic areas were identified, avoiding application to edematous or compromised skin. Both the baby oil and placebo were maintained at a temperature of 10 °C to 15 °C, as recommended in prior research on the use of baby oil for UP [13]. The temperature was verified with a thermometer before application.

In the baby oil group, participants received a 15–20-minute gentle massage with paraffin-based baby oil, whereas the placebo group received the same massage protocol using a placebo preparation. Sessions were conducted three times a week before each HD session for 12 weeks.

Pruritus around vascular access sites was monitored and managed according to standard care practices, including maintaining hygiene and dressing integrity. The participants were instructed to avoid scratching the access site and to report any discomfort or irritation. No significant pruritus issues related to vascular access sites were observed during the study. After each session, patients were dried and comforted. They were positioned comfortably. Patients were monitored for discomfort, such as chills or pain.

Compliance and adverse event monitoring

The participants were reminded to avoid using other topical treatments, and adherence to these treatments was reinforced after each treatment session. Researchers assess compliance before each intervention session. Any deviations from the protocol, such as the use of other topical ointments, the use of new treatments for pruritus or changes in the dose, were documented. A total of 2 participants discontinued the intervention: one withdrew consent, and one used another topical ointment, resulting in a withdrawal rate of 2.4%, leaving 80 cases for analysis (see Fig. 1).

Adverse events, including skin dryness, irritation, redness, and allergic reactions, were monitored via a standardized reporting form. The form captured details such as the nature, onset, duration, and severity of the event before each session. The participants were instructed to report any symptoms from the start of the intervention until two days after completion. No significant adverse events were reported in either group.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the requirements of the Helsinki Declaration. Ethical approval to conduct the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Nursing, Alexandria University, Egypt (Approval No: 2022-9-43) on August 14, 2022, and was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration No: NCT05555524) on September 22, 2022.

Approval to conduct the study was obtained from the hospital administrative authorities. Patients signed informed consent before participation in the study. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The researchers ensured the privacy of the patients, the confidentiality of the collected data and the participants’ right to withdraw at any time from the study.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis plan and power calculations were prespecified and focused on the primary outcomes of pruritus severity and sleep quality. The secondary outcomes, while analysed, were considered exploratory and were not included in the initial power calculations.

Statistical analysis was conducted via SPSS software version 26.0. The normality of continuous data was assessed via the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test, confirming that the data were normally distributed (p > 0.05). Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, were used to summarize sociodemographic and clinical data.

Baseline qualitative characteristics were compared between the study and control groups via Fisher’s exact test and chi-square tests. Student’s t test was used to compare the means between the two groups at different time points. Effect sizes were calculated via Cohen’s d to provide additional context for the magnitude of differences between groups, with values interpreted as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5), and large (d ≥ 0.8) [25]. For all the statistical tests, a p value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and confidence intervals were calculated to ensure precision and interpretability.

For pruritus severity measured by the ISS, the baby oil group showed a significant reduction at T1 (mean difference = 8.70, 95% CI: 6.75–10.65, p = 0.03, d = 1.32) and an even greater reduction at T2 (mean difference = 10.03, 95% CI: 8.12–11.94, p < 0.01, d = 2.58). Similar improvements were observed with PVAS scores. At T1, the baby oil group showed a significant decrease (mean difference = − 1.83, 95% CI: − 2.57 to − 1.09, p < 0.01, d = 1.06), which further increased at T2 (mean difference = − 4.42, 95% CI: − 5.16 to − 3.68, p < 0.01, d = 3.57).

Sleep quality also improved significantly in the baby oil group compared with placebo. At T1, the mean difference was − 2.57 (95% CI: − 3.40 to − 1.74, p < 0.01, d = 0.97), increasing to − 5.75 at T2 (95% CI: − 6.58 to − 4.92, p < 0.01, d = 2.95). Overall, baby oil produced sustained and clinically meaningful improvements in both pruritus severity and sleep quality compared with placebo.

Discussion

This study adds to the growing evidence supporting baby oil and other emollients in the management of UP among patients on HD. Previous research has shown that emollients such as glycerol and paraffin improve skin hydration and restore barrier integrity, making them key components of UP treatment. Baby oil, with its high liquid paraffin content, aligns with current clinical guidelines that recommend emollients as a first-line therapy for UP [26–28].

This study evaluated the effects of cool paraffin-based baby oil massage, applied three times weekly, on pruritus severity and sleep quality in HD patients. Follow-ups at one and three months showed that the baby oil group achieved significantly greater reductions in pruritus severity, as measured by the ISS and PVAS, and sustained improvements in sleep quality, as assessed by the PSQI, compared with the placebo group.

Our findings corroborate those of Mokhtarabadi et al. (2017), who demonstrated that baby oil applied at room temperature for one month significantly diminished itching severity, as measured by the VAS [14]. Similarly, Karadag et al. (2014) emphasized the effectiveness of cool baby oil administered thrice weekly in alleviating pruritus [13]. While these prior studies primarily documented short-term benefits, our research extends this evidence by confirming the sustained therapeutic effect over an extended duration, thereby supporting its incorporation into standard nursing care protocols as endorsed by current clinical guidelines.

Our findings further expand upon the work of Singh and Vinayadev (2021), who reported a significant reduction in pruritus following daily application of vitamin E and mineral oil-based baby oil over a ten-day period [16]. While their study highlights the versatility of various baby oil formulations, our research specifically investigated the sustained effects of paraffin-based cool baby oil applied over a longer duration, thereby reinforcing the evidence base through a rigorous placebo-controlled design.

Lin et al. (2012) investigated the effectiveness of chilled versus unchilled baby oil therapy for treating UP in HD patients. The study concluded that both forms of baby oil significantly reduced pruritus severity compared with routine care, but there was no significant difference between the chilled and unchilled groups. These findings suggest that the moisturizing properties of baby oil are primarily responsible for its antipruritic effects rather than its cooling sensation [15].

However, other studies have indicated that cooling can provide itch relief. For example, a study by Palkar et al. (2018) revealed that cooling inhibits both histaminergic and nonhistaminergic itch pathways, suggesting a potential role for cooling in itch relief [29]. These differing findings highlight a gap in current clinical understanding regarding the additive benefit of cooling in pruritus management and underscore the need for further research combining moisturization and cooling strategies to optimize symptom control.

Our findings align with research on other emollients, reinforcing the role of skin hydration in pruritus management. Urea-based moisturizers have been extensively studied for their ability to increase water-binding capacity, significantly improving skin hydration and reducing pruritus severity in dialysis patients [11, 28].

Glycerol-based emollients, known for attracting and retaining skin moisture, have also demonstrated effectiveness in reducing pruritus intensity [26, 27]. Their affordability and accessibility make them a viable option. Similarly, paraffin-based emollients, such as baby oil, create an oily film that minimizes trans-epidermal water loss, resulting in prolonged hydration [30, 31]. Given their simplicity and cost-effectiveness, these emollients are suitable for long-term use in resource-constrained settings.

Lipid-enriched emollients containing ceramides and fatty acids further strengthen the skin barrier by replenishing essential lipids. Studies highlight their efficacy in improving skin conditions and reducing pruritus severity, particularly in atopic dermatitis patients [32, 33]. However, their higher costs than those of glycerol- or paraffin-based alternatives may limit accessibility.

Moreover, our study provides valuable insights into the link between pruritus and sleep quality. At baseline, both groups experienced significant sleep disturbances, which is consistent with the findings of Lin et al. (2012), who reported that 55.91% of HD patients experienced sleep issues due to pruritus [15]. Compared with the placebo group, the baby oil group presented notable improvements in sleep quality, highlighting its potential as a nonpharmacological intervention aligned with holistic nursing care models that emphasize physical and emotional well-being [34]. This aligns with previous studies demonstrating that baby oil application improves sleep in HD patients with pruritus [13]. These findings reinforce the importance of holistic nursing care, emphasizing simple, accessible strategies to relieve both physical and emotional distress.

Study implications for clinical nursing practice

This study highlights important implications for nursing practice in managing UP among HD patients. Using cool baby oil as a complementary intervention offers a simple, accessible, and patient-centered approach. It is widely available, affordable, and easily integrated into routine nursing care without requiring specialized training or equipment.

From a cost-effectiveness perspective, this intervention may reduce reliance on expensive pharmacological treatments, which often have side effects or limited efficacy. While this study did not directly assess cost-effectiveness, its affordability suggests potential savings, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Integrating nonpharmacological interventions empowers nurses to proactively address patients’ physical discomfort, enhancing holistic care. Improved pruritus management can also enhance sleep quality and overall well-being, potentially reducing complications and hospital readmissions. Future research should explore the long-term cost-effectiveness of this approach to strengthen evidence for its broader clinical adoption.

Study implications for future nursing research

While this study highlights the medium-term effects of cool baby oil on pruritus and sleep quality in HD patients, longer follow-up periods (6–12 months) are needed to assess its sustained effectiveness and potential cumulative benefits. Future research should also explore the impact of baby oil on overall QoL, providing insights into its broader benefits and long-term outcomes. The incorporation of QoL assessments could enhance clinical decision-making and holistic patient care.

Additionally, comparative studies evaluating different emollients could help identify the most cost-effective and patient-preferred options in clinical practice, further refining nonpharmacological strategies for pruritus management.

Study limitations

Achieving complete blinding was a challenge, as participants may have distinguished the placebo from the baby oil despite efforts to match their odor, texture, and feel. While no breaches were reported, future studies should use a placebo with closely matched properties to strengthen the integrity of the blinding process.

Another limitation is the reliance on subjective scales, which may introduce bias due to the absence of objective validation. Future research should incorporate objective tools to enhance reliability. Finally, the lack of a standardized tool for assessing itching severity complicates comparisons across studies. The development of a validated, universally accepted measure would improve consistency in pruritus research.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that paraffin-based cool baby oil massage, as a basic skin care measure, is a viable and promising complementary intervention for managing UP. It significantly reduces itching severity and improves sleep quality, supporting a holistic approach to patient well-being. Its ability to alleviate symptoms highlights its potential for integration into routine clinical nursing practice. Moreover, as a simple, cost-effective intervention, it offers a sustainable strategy for long-term patient care. Future research should explore its long-term efficacy and broader applicability across diverse clinical settings.
