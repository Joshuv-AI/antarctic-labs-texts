Early antiseizure response to cenobamate (200 mg/day) in focal drug-resistant epilepsy: a retrospective single-center analysis
Authors: Ewa Czapińska-Ciepiela, Agnieszka Kondak, Monika Kowalczyk, Piotr Czapiński
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•One hundred drug-resistant epilepsy patients received 200 mg/day CNB for three months.•≥ 50 % seizure reduction was achieved in 63 % of patients, ≥ 75 % in 36 %.•18% of patients were seizure-free.•CNB efficacy was unaffected by age, sex, epilepsy duration, or number of prior ASMs.•CNB was well tolerated if doses of co-administered ASMs were reduced.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a dysfunction of the brain which is manifested as the tendency to generate spontaneous epileptic seizures [1]. Epilepsy is a common disease which affects approximately 70 millions of people worldwide [2]. Despite numerous antiseizure medications (ASMs) introduced in the past two decades, roughly 1/3 of the affected individuals demonstrate refractory epileptic seizures [3,4], while in order to achieve the desired therapeutic effect, another 20 % of the patients require polytherapy [5]. Cenobamate (CNB) is a third-generation ASM which has been proven in clinical studies to be highly effective in inhibiting epileptic seizures in patients with focal drug-resistant epilepsy [6,7]. Despite the fact that the mechanism of CNB effect has not been fully understood, the drug is known to exert an inhibitory effect on excessive neuron excitability through promoting the fast and slow sodium channel inactivation, thus preferentially inhibiting the persistent component of the sodium current; it also acts as a positive allosteric modulator of the type A gamma aminobutyric acid receptor (GABA-A) at the non-benzodiazepine site [8]. The questions whether CNB will prove to be a true hope for patients with refractory epilepsy and on what factors the prognosis of its effectiveness in a given patient is dependent may be answered by information gathered in real-life clinical practice.

In our center which specializes in treating epilepsy the patients have been treated with CNB since the time the medication was registered in 2022; the number of such individuals has increased since the time the drug started to be reimbursed by the Polish National Health Fund in 2023. Many of these subjects have suffered from epilepsy for many years and in the past used numerous ASMs without any success. Thus, they mainly constitute a group of highly drug-resistant subjects with focal epilepsy. We have decided to perform a retrospective evaluation of the effectiveness, tolerance and safety of the add-on therapy with CNB in this group of patients and to define the factors which determine the early response to CNB defined as the reduction of seizures in response to introducing CNB up to the target dose of 200 mg/day which is considered to be an effective dose of the medication in this population.

Methods

Participants in the study

We performed a retrospective analysis of subsequent 100 patients treated with CNB who were under the care of the private health center by the name of Epilepsy and Migraine Treatment Center. The subjects included in the study were patients over 18 years of age or older who suffered from drug-resistant focal epilepsy. Patients with mild to moderate intellectual disability were eligible for inclusion in the study, provided that accurate recording of seizure frequency was feasible with the assistance of their caregivers. Those with severe intellectual disability were excluded from participation, as reliable seizure documentation was deemed unlikely in this group. The patients were prescribed CNB therapy and subsequently were under our care for at least 6 consecutive months.

The constant dosage protocol was employed in all the subjects in the initial 12 weeks of treatment. In keeping with the recommendations of the Summary of the Product Characteristic (SmPC), the CNB therapy started from the dose of 12.5 mg/day which was increased at two-week intervals to 25 mg/day, 50 mg/day, 100 mg/day, 150 mg/day and 200 mg/day. The physician-in-charge made the decision on further modifications of the dosage based on the effectiveness and tolerance of the treatment as well as on modification of doses of concomitant medications should the patient develop adverse effects (AEs). In case the dose of CNB reached 200 mg/day the therapeutic effect was evaluated for 3 months. The investigators had to exclude 5 patients from the final analysis as it was not possible to add CNB to their therapy, hence the statistical results are presented in two variants, i.e. per 100 patients prior to the said exclusion and per 95 patients following the exclusion data pertaining to 5 subjects from the study group.

Clinical data collection

All the demographic and clinical data originated from electronic patient records as well as from paper medical records. The analysis included the data of patients who were treated with CNB between March 1, 2022 and March 31, 2025. In each instance, the planned control medical consultation was held three months after the onset of treatment with CNB and subsequently after the next three months of treatment. Nevertheless, the patients and their caregivers were offered the option of calling the center between the scheduled visits in case of developing adverse effects of the treatment.

The collected data addressed age, sex, duration of the disease, concomitant intellectual disability, etiology of epilepsy (symptomatic vs. cryptogenic), the number of ASMs and the number of drug combinations employed before and during CNB introduction. The initial monthly frequency of seizures was retrospectively assessed based on the medical records covering preceding 6 months. The effectiveness of CNB at the dose of 200 mg was determined based on the number of seizures occurring in the period of 3 months in the patients receiving the above dose of the medication and the degree of seizure number reduction achieved as the effect of the treatment. The analysis also included AEs occurring in the course of the therapy, adjustment of the doses of concomitant medications and the reasons of CNB treatment discontinuation.

The diagnosis of focal epilepsy was based on the criteria formulated by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) [9,10], while refractory epilepsy was defined as the absence of seizure control as the effect of the therapy consisting in using two antiseizure medications employed subsequently, well-selected, well-tolerated and administered in appropriate doses as per the definition formulated by ILAE [11].

Objectives of the study

The primary endpoint was the change in the monthly number of seizures as the effect of treatment with 200 mg/day of CNB as compared to the initial number of seizures. The effectiveness of CNB treatment at the dose of 200 mg was determined as the percentage of reduction of the average number of seizures per month within 3 months following the achievement of the target dose as compared to the average number of seizures per month within 6 months preceding the introduction of the medication. The subjects responding to the treatment were defined as the individuals in whom the frequency of seizures was reduced by ≥ 50 %. Moreover, the authors determined the number of patients who achieved a ≥ 75 % and 100 % reduction of the number of seizures. The investigators also ascertained whether the early response to CNB treatment (defined as the response to the dose of 200 mg/day) was affected by factors associated with the previous course of the disease and treatment, such as:•Etiology of epilepsy: symptomatic/cryptogenic•Concomitant intellectual disability•Age, sex, duration of the disease•Number of seizures per month•Number of ASMs employed•Type of the employed ASMs•Number of the employed combinations of ASMs

The objective of the study was also determining the frequency and type of AEs associated with CNB introduction, the number of patients in whom it was possible to successfully introduce CNB (retention rate), as well as determining the degree of the reduction of the doses of concomitant ASMs.

The study was a retrospective analysis of the available clinical data, hence the acceptance credited by the ethics committee and informed consent of the patient to participate in the investigation were not required. The data were descriptively summarized.

Statistical analysis

The authors employed statistical tests (the two sample test and the chi-square independence test); the tests verified the hypotheses pointing to the existence of significant differences in the study groups. The level of statistical significance was accepted to be p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic data and clinical cohorts

The group of consecutive patients in whom CNB was added to their therapies consisted of 100 subjects. The group included 46 females and 54 males aged 19–58 years of life. The average age of the studied individuals was 36.2 years (standard deviation = 9.3).

The majority of the subjects suffered from focal epilepsy for many years (min = 2, max = 46 years). The average duration of epilepsy at the time of the study was 23.7 years (standard deviation = 11.6).

The majority of the patients demonstrated two or three types of epileptic seizures: focal motor seizures without loss of consciousness (40/100, 40 %), focal seizures with loss of consciousness (94/100, 94 %), focal seizures progressing to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures (91/100, 91 %). The median number of seizures per month was 4.6 (IQR = 14.5). Focal non-motor seizures without loss of consciousness were excluded from analysis due to difficulties in accurately quantifying their occurrence.

Some subjects (23/100, 23 %) showed concomitant intellectual disability. The etiology of epilepsy was symptomatic in 59/100 patients (59 %) and cryptogenic in 41/100 (41 %) subjects.

Prior to the introduction of CNB, the patients used numerous and diversified ASM (min. = 3, max. = 15). On the average, a patient used 7.9 ASMs (standard deviation = 2,9). The most commonly employed medications included levetiracetam (LEV) (89/100, 89 %), as well as lacosamide (LCM) (85/100, 85 %) and lamotrigine (LTG) (85/100, 85 %). Introduction of cenobamate

Addition of CNB to the currently used ASMs was started in all the patients; the plan was to obtain the dose of 200 mg per day within 12 weeks in keeping with the summary of product characteristics and generally accepted recommendations [12].

In the case of 5 patients the decision was made to discontinue the medication before the target dose of 200 mg/day was achieved. The reasons of such a decision were as follows: an increased number of seizures (3/100), allergy to the medication manifested as fever, lymphadenopathy, skin rash and itching (1/100), the patient’s choice to discontinue the treatment due to markedly intensified AEs consisting in dizziness and balance disorders, weakness, nausea and vomiting (1/100).

Adverse effects

During the introduction of CNB some patients manifested AEs, such as dizziness and balance disorders (47/95, 49 %), somnolence (36/95, 38 %), diplopia (16/95, 17 %), weakness (9/95, 10 %), concentration and memory impairment (10/95, 10 %), fluency disorders (6/95, 6 %), tremor of the hands (5/95, 5 %), nausea and/or vomiting (4/95, 4 %), headaches (3/95, 3 %), anorexia and weight loss (3/95, 3 %), elevated liver enzymes (2/95, 2 %), irritability (2/95, 2 %), weight gain (2/95, 2 %). Moreover, there were single cases of diarrhea, stomachache, back pain, dysphagia, insomnia, swelling of the lower limbs, decreased libido, tinnitus and photophobia.

The presence of the most common AEs was related to the addition of CNB to given ASMs. Dizziness and balance disorders were significantly more often seen in patients in whom CNB was added to LCM (p = 0.006). This may be attributed to the relatively high doses of LCM administered in our cohort, with a mean daily dose of 488 mg/day (median: 500 mg/day; range: 200–750 mg/day). No such relationship was demonstrated for somnolence (p = 0.44) or diplopia (p = 1). The majority of AEs were controlled by reducing the doses of concomitant ASMs.

Therapeutic effects

In the group of 95 study subjects, the reduction in the number of epileptic seizures when the dose of CNB was 200 mg/day during 3 months was seen in 84/95 (88 %) patients; at the same time in as many as 60/95 (63 %) cases the said reduction was ≥ 50 %, while in 34/95 (36 %) the reduction amounted to ≥ 75 %. Freedom from seizures (100 % of seizure reduction) was achieved by 17/95 (18 %) of the study subjects.

In 6/95 (6 %) cases the authors noted an increase in the average number of seizures. In 5/95 (5 %) of the subjects the number of seizures in the course of their therapy did not change, while in 5/95 (5 %) of the patient the said number decreased only slightly, i.e. by less than 25 %.

Conditions influencing the therapeutic effect

The effect of CNB therapy affecting the seizure number reduction was not related to the age of the patients (p = 0.18), their sex (p = 0.51), etiology of epilepsy (p = 0.48), duration of the disease (p = 0.07), concomitant intellectual disability (p = 0.78) or the number of seizures prior to the treatment (p = 0.12).

The effectieness of CNB therapy was also not affected by factors associated with the previous treatment, such as the number of ASMs used so far (p = 0.08), the type of the above-mentioned drugs: barbiturates (PB and PRM), p = 0.63, gabapentinoids (GBP and PGB), p = 0.13, medications affecting the SV2A molecule (LEV and BRV), p = 0.19, drugs characterized by multiple mechanisms of action (VPA, TPM and ZNS) p = 0.71, gabaergic medications (VGB and TGB), p = 0.50, or the number of their combinations (p = 0.92). Also AEs associated with introduction of CNB did not affect its effectiveness (p = 1.00).

Reduction of co-medications

During the course of CNB introduction extending until the medication reached the dose of 200 mg/day, reduction of the doses of co-medications included 69/95 (73 %) of the patients; the reduction was the result of AEs appearing during the period of introducing CNB. Most commonly, the reduction was necessary in the case of such ASMs as LCM in 44/95 of the patients by the mean value of 37.1 % (median value = 25 %, standard deviation 27.4 %), LTG in 12/95 of the subjects by the mean value of 49.6 % (median value = 26.8 %, standard deviation 38.4 %), TPM in 6/95 of the individuals by the mean value of 55.8 % (median value = 50 %, standard deviation 37.5 %), OXC in 5/85 of the patients by the mean value of 48.2 % (median value = 40 %, standard deviation 31.8 %).

Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive and detailed analysis of the early response to antiseizure therapy with CNB at a dose of 200 mg/day, specifically examining how this response relates to various factors associated with the prior course and management of epilepsy. By thoroughly exploring these relationships, the study results will help to identify the patients who are most likely to achieve optimal benefit from the introduction of CNB to their regimen.

Adverse effects and reduction of doses of co-medications

During the treatment with CNB, in many patients it was necessary to reduce the doses of co-medications in view of the reported AEs involving the central nervous system; the said AEs were predominantly dizziness and balance disorders, somnolence and diplopia. In the case of our patients, the most commonly employed ASM in the therapy to which CNB was introduced was LCM – its presence was significantly more often the cause of the patient developing AEs such as dizziness and balance disorders which typically occur following combination therapy where sodium channel modulations are used [13]. At the same time, LCM was an ASM that demonstrated partial effectiveness in some of our patients, therefore in most cases our approach was to decrease the LCM dosage in a reactive manner upon the emergence of AEs.

Due to the common occurrence of adverse reactions as the effect of CNB being added to other medications, the authors of Spanish reccommendations propose a proactive reduction of doses of co-medications characterized by the mechanism of action that includes sodium channels blocking. Yet, the above authors emphasize the fact that the tolerance of various ASMs may significantly differ in different subjects and depends on individual characteristics of the patient [12]. On the other hand, American experts recommend proactive approach to reducing the doses of concomitant ASMs such as clobazam, phenytoin, phenobarbital, and lacosamide when administered at doses exceeding 400 mg per day. Additionally, they recommend implementing reactive dose reductions in response to the emergence of AEs associated with polytherapy, as well as further decreasing the doses of other medications when CNB reaches 150–200 mg per day [14].

In view of the need for individualized treatment with CNB, the subjects treated in our outpatient clinic were offered the opportunity of contacting us by calling the nurse and reporting AEs, while the doctors were able to reactively adjust the dose of concomitant ASMs in order to reduce the AEs. Such adjustment was employed in 69/95 of the patients (73 % of the study group); the said percentage was similar to values reported in other studies [15,16] The employed dose reduction was the most frequently required in the modulators of sodium canals which were used in our cohort of patients, such as LCM (by the mean value of 37.1 % of the dose), LTG (by the mean value of 49.6 % of the dose) and OXC (by the mean value of 48.2 % of the dose), but it was also needed in case of medications belonging to other groups, such as TPM (by the mean value of 55.8 % of the dose). In the majority of the subjects, the reductions of the doses of co-medications led to total subsidence of the AEs and allowed for introducing CNB to the target therapy at the dose of 200 mg/day. Nevertheless, in one patient (1/100), despite the changes introduced to the treatment regimen, AEs continued to persist and for this reason the subject decided to abandon the CNB therapy before it was possible to again reduce the doses of the co-medications. In patients who used PHT (1 subject) or PRM (1 subject), empirical reduction of the doses of the above ASM resulted in a fast resolution of AEs associated with the introduction of CNB; this observation is significant since the presence of such ASMs in the therapy fairy quickly results in the appearance of AEs involving the central nervous system during the process of CNB inclusion due to pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions [12]. None of our patients was treated with BZD, CLB or PB, thus no changes in the therapy with these drugs were necessary. The dose reduction affecting the co-medications and employed in the course of CNB introduction seems to be necessary in view of the patients developing AEs in order to allow for a better tolerance of the drug [17,18], but also in order to possibly attain the improvement of the cognitive functions of the patient. As it was demonstrated in the study of Serrano-Castro et al., the introduction of CNB up to the mean dose of 192 mg/day resulted in a significant improvement of cognitive functions in patients with multi-drug-resistant epilepsy; such an effect might have been associated both with the reduction of seizures (70 % of their patients showed a minimum 50 % seizure reduction), as well as with the reduction of the number and doses of co-medications (total drug load) [19]. Other authors also demonstrated that the introduction of CNB to the therapeutic regimen was associated with a decreased intensity of the coexisting anxiety disorder [20]. Nevertheless, in our therapeutic decisions we took under consideration the fact of partial effectiveness of some ASMs employed in the earlier therapy of our patients and at the stage of CNB introduction to the therapy, and thus we consciously reduced their doses. As it is demonstrated by the results of the open clinical studies, reduction of the doses of co-medications usually occurs gradually, even extending for as long as for 2 years following CNB introduction without affecting its effectiveness [21]. In the majority of patients, reduction of polytherapy is possible and in some subjects it may be even possible to convert the polytherapy to CNB monotherapy [15].

The addition of CNB to the therapies of 9/100 patients resulted in an increase in the number of epileptic seizures and for this reason in 3 of them (3/100) the medication was discontinued prior to achieving the dose of 200/day and they were excluded from the present analysis. In view of the small percentage of the increase in the number of seizures, the remaining 6 subjects continued to be administered CNB in higher doses and then further evaluation of the effectiveness of the medication was made.

Following the introduction of CNB 1 patient (1/100) developed allergic symptoms when the dose of 50 mg/day was administered; the symptoms included fever, lymphadenopathy, skin rash of the legs and back, and itching. In this case the authors decided to immediately discontinue the treatment with CNB in view of the risk of the subject developing the DRESS (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms) syndrome and thus obtained total resolution of the symptoms in the affected individual. DRESS is a rare but potentially mortal complication of treatment with CNB which seems to be successfully prevented by the significantly prolonged in time schedule of CNB introduction [22,23].

The effect of cenobamate therapy

In the group of 95 analyzed patients, the reduction in the number of seizures while receiving CNB in the dose of 200 mg/day for 3 months was noted in 88 % of the subjects, the response to CNB understood as the reduction of ≥ 50 % seizures was seen in 63 % of the patients, while in 36 % of them the reduction was ≥ 75 %. Freedom from seizures was achieved by 18 % of the study subjects.

Our results describing the effectiveness of CNB do not significantly differ from the results obtained in clinical studies and clinical practice investigations. In the drug registration trial, the response to CNB (≥50 % seizure reduction) at the dose of 200 mg/day was achieved in 56 % of subjects [6]. In the study of Winter et al. in the group of patients receiving CNB at the dose of 200 mg/day on the average, the reduction amounting to ≥ 50 % was achieved by 71 % of the patients, while the freedom from seizures was characteristic of 20 % of the subjects; this was, however, a cohort of patients who early received CNB as the third therapy in epilepsy treatment. At the same time CNB was the most effective ASM in add-on therapy as compared to the controls who received one of other ASMs [24]. A similar percentage of patients with seizure reduction of ≥ 50 % (55 % of the group) and free from seizures (15 %) was seen in the early results of the Italian multicenter BLESS study (Cenobamate in Adults with Focal-Onset Seizures) [18] and in its subsequent phase (59 % and 11 %, respectively) [25]. In their study of the early response to CNB in clinical practice Lauxmann et al. noted that the percentage of responders with ≥ 50 % reduction was 50 %, while the percentage of subjects who achieved freedom from seizures was 18 %. In the study of the early accessiblity of CNB (Spanish Expanded Access Programme- EAP), the percentage of free from seizures patients was 13 %, the percentage of ≥ 50 % seizure number reduction was 63 % and ≥ 75 % reduction was 46 % [26]. Similarly, in the recent work of our Polish colleagues, the response to cenobamate (≥50 % seizure number reduction) was seen in 61,9% of patients and 16.5 % achieved seizure freedom [27]. The metaanalysis performed by Makridis and Kaindl which included 7 studies and the total of 229 patients showed the ≥ 50 % response to CNB in 68 % of the study subjects and freedom from seizures in 16 % of them [28]. Surprisingly, in some studies the percentage of free from seizures patients following the introduction of CNB was very high amounting to approximately 50 % [15,29], while in other investigations it was very low (5 %) [16]. The metaanalysis of Zhang points to the fact that the response to CNB and the percentage of patients who are free from seizures as the effect of the treatment is higher when compared to the effects of other ASMs [30]. Similar conclusions are presented in the results of the observation study KORK which compared the percentage of patients responding to cenobamate as compared to historical cohorts of subjects treated with the use of other III generation antiseizure medications [31].

The age and sex of the patients, duration of the illness, number of seizures or number of ASMs and drug combinations did not affect the response to treatment with CNB in our patients. This is a significant result of our study since our patients were in the past on 3 to as many as 15 ASMs (7.9 on the average), the majority of our subjects (99/100) also used at least one two-drug and three-drug combinations (95/100), while the range of duration of the illness was between 2 to 46 years. As it follows from the hitherto performed studies on the effectiveness of ASMs, the chance for successful overcoming epileptic seizures decreases with each subsequent introduced medication [32]. Yet CNB seems to demonstrate a higher effectiveness as compared to the majority of ASMs used so far, both when it is used in the early add-on therapy and in highly drug-resistant patients. Similar results were also achieved by other authors: the disease duration or the initial number of seizures did not affect the effect of CNB introduction in the post-hoc analyses of CNB introduction in clinical studies [21,33]; the number of ASMs previously administered and administered during the process of CNB introduction did not affect its effect in the study of Villanueva et al. [26] Nevertheless, the authors of the BLESS study emphasize the fact that CNB proved to be more effective in patients who – prior to its introduction – used no more than 2–3 ASMs [25].

The effect of CNB therapy was also not affected by the etiology of epilepsy (symptomatic/cryptogenic) as well as by concomitant intellectual disability what indicates that CNB may be also effective in the group of patients characterized by the risk of severe and drug-resistant course of epilepsy. This is confirmed by the results of medical practice studies on the high effectiveness of CNB also in individuals with coexisting intellectual disability [29] and the promising – when compared to other medications – effectiveness in patients with epileptic encephalopathies [[34], [35], [36], [37]].

The strengths and weaknesses of our study

Our study is retrospective and its results are based on the analysis of medical records which diminishes its value in comparison to the prospective study model. However, contrary to numerous other retrospective studies analyzing data obtained in the therapeutic process, during the process of CNB introduction our patients were capable of contacting us more often than only on the occasion of planned appointments with the doctor. This solutions allowed us to behave reactively with respect to adjusting the doses of co-medications and for defining precisely the degree of necessity of reducing the said doses which would allow for introducing CNB up to the dose of 200 mg/day.

Our study determines the effectiveness of CNB at the dose of 200 mg/day, yet some patients may need higher doses in order to demonstrate the therapeutic effectiveness understood as the reduction of seizures or freedom from seizures [12]. This is why the effectiveness of CNB and the possibility of the reduction of the dose of co-medications will be in the future analyzed in our patients at the moment when they reach the individual target dose and the results will be published in subsequent articles.

Conclusions

CNB showed its high effectiveness at the dose of 200 mg/day in our multi-drug resistant patients with focal epilepsy. The effect of the activity of the medication did not depend on demographic factors or on factors associated with the disease itself, such as its duration or hitherto employed therapy and the number of seizures, as well as coexisting intellectual disability or the symptomatic cause of epilepsy. In the course of CNB introduction, the majority of patients developed AEs, which subsided following the reduction of co-medication doses. CNB was discontinued in a small percentage (5 %) of the patients. It is necessary to conduct further observation of patients and the therapeutic effects they achieved when treated with CNB at doses higher than 200 mg/day.
