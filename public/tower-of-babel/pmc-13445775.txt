Artificial intelligence in prehospital assessment of acute coronary syndrome: a scoping review
Authors: Kimia Khonakdar, Reyhaneh Sadat Faghani, Fatemeh Keyhanian, Sogand Ramezanian, Hosna Ziabakhsh Ganji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute coronary syndrome (ACS) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, where timely diagnosis is critical. Prehospital assessment is challenging due to limited diagnostic resources and high clinical uncertainty. Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a potential tool to support early diagnosis, risk stratification, and triage. This scoping review aimed to map the current evidence on AI applications in the prehospital assessment of suspected ACS and to identify existing knowledge gaps.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines. A comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase was performed up to May 2026. Studies evaluating AI-based models using prehospital data for ACS diagnosis, prediction, or triage were included.Results19 studies involving 319,709 patients were included. AI-based models, particularly ECG-based deep learning and multimodal approaches, demonstrated promising diagnostic performance, with AUC values generally ranging from approximately 0.81 to 0.99, sensitivity from 73% to 94%, and specificity from 56% to 99%. These models improved sensitivity, reduced diagnostic variability, and enhanced triage efficiency in some settings. Risk prediction models showed moderate to good performance (AUC ~ 0.71–0.95) but were more variable. Emerging applications extended beyond diagnosis to risk stratification and decision support, including prediction of cardiogenic shock and need for revascularization.ConclusionsAI shows considerable potential to improve prehospital assessment of suspected ACS, particularly through enhanced ECG interpretation and multimodal data integration. However, current evidence remains limited by methodological constraints. Future prospective, multicenter studies with standardized approaches are essential to support clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01636-2.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Acute coronary syndrome, Emergency medical services, Electrocardiography, Triage

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) remains a major cause of death and long-term disability worldwide [1–3]. Each year, more than seven million people are diagnosed with ACS globally [4]. Ischemic heart disease, which includes ACS, accounts for approximately 22% of all global deaths [5], underscoring its clinical impact. Early detection is vital; timely reperfusion, such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) within two hours, can significantly reduce mortality [4].

Prehospital triage of chest pain is inherently challenging. Although chest pain is one of the most common reasons for emergency medical services activation, the majority of these patients have non-ACS etiologies [5, 6]. In a prehospital setting, clinicians must rely solely on symptoms and a single on-scene electrocardiogram (ECG), without access to confirmatory laboratory testing [5], resulting in substantial diagnostic uncertainty. For instance, one study found that approximately 38% of ambulance-suspected ACS cases were confirmed in hospital, and that prehospital diagnostic accuracy for ACS ranged widely (46–80%) [5].

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools are now being developed to address these limitations in prehospital triage. Evidence from recent studies indicates that AI-assisted ECG interpretation can deliver high diagnostic accuracy while substantially shortening analysis times; for example, a deep-learning ECG algorithm correctly identified ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) with almost 99% accuracy and significantly faster turnaround than physicians [7]. Models using machine learning (ML) techniques and prehospital data (symptoms, clinical history, ECG) have also been reported to enhance ACS prediction, although the magnitude of improvement reported varies considerably across studies. For instance, one ML model markedly increased triage specificity while maintaining high sensitivity [6]. Large-scale performance evaluations further indicate that AI-driven ECG systems substantially decrease false activation rates and achieve higher sensitivity for STEMI recognition [8]. A 2025 systematic review reported comparable findings, noting that AI-based analyses of 12-lead ECGs outperformed clinicians in identifying ACS, with sensitivities reaching 98–99% [9].

Even with these encouraging findings, most available studies are retrospective and highly heterogeneous, demonstrate considerable variation in model performance, and provide limited real-time assessment [9]. Thus, a scoping review is essential to systematically characterize the range of AI methods used in the prehospital assessment of ACS, assess the strength of current evidence, and clarify unresolved gaps for future research.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [10] and guidance from the general framework outlined by Arksey & O’Malley [11]. This scoping review did not meet the criteria for PROSPERO registration based on existing guidelines [10]. A scoping review methodology was chosen because the review aimed to broadly map the range of AI applications, study designs, input data types, and outcome measures related to prehospital ACS assessment, as well as to identify unresolved knowledge gaps in this evolving field. The review proceeded through five stages: [1] defining research questions; [2] identifying relevant studies; [3] study selection; [4] data charting; and [5] collating, summarizing, and reporting the results. An iterative approach was employed to ensure comprehensive mapping of the available evidence.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across four electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase. All searches were performed on 8 May 2026, with no restrictions on publication date. The search strategy focused on three core concepts: artificial intelligence, ACS, and prehospital/EMS settings. Boolean operators were applied to integrate synonyms both within and across these concepts, and search strings were customized for each database. The complete database-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Study selection

All records retrieved from the database searches were imported into EndNote 21, where duplicate entries were removed prior to screening. Study selection followed a two-stage procedure. First, two independent authors (KK and RF) screened the titles and abstracts according to the predefined eligibility criteria. During the second stage, the same reviewers performed a full-text evaluation of all potentially eligible articles. Any disagreements at either stage were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, through the involvement of a third author (FK) to reach a consensus.

Data charting process

The data charting process was carried out using a structured form that was developed specifically for this review to ensure consistent extraction of study characteristics. Two authors (KK and RF) independently charted the data from all included studies. Extracted variables covered information related to study identification, population characteristics, prehospital components, AI model characteristics, and reported outcomes. Reviewer discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, with the involvement of a third author (FK) when needed. The complete data charting form used in this review is provided in Supplementary File 2.

Collating, summarizing, and reporting the results

Data extracted from the studies were compiled and summarized using tabular and descriptive formats to map key study characteristics and outcomes. A narrative synthesis was conducted by three authors (HG, RF, and KK) in accordance with the predefined research questions to describe the scope, distribution, and main findings of the included studies. Consistent with the aims and methodological approach of scoping reviews, a formal methodological quality or risk of bias assessment of the included studies was not carried out [10].

Use of artificial intelligence tools

A large language model (ChatGPT, OpenAI) was used to support English language editing, grammar correction, paraphrasing, and overall readability enhancement during manuscript preparation. All outputs were critically reviewed and revised by the authors to ensure scientific accuracy, clarity, and compliance with academic standards.

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

A total of 648 records were retrieved from the database searches. Following the removal of 228 duplicates, 420 records underwent title and abstract screening. After full-text evaluation of 75 potentially relevant articles, 19 studies were found to meet the predefined eligibility criteria and were therefore included in this scoping review. Geographical context

The 19 included studies were carried out in nine countries, reflecting a predominantly high-income research landscape. The United States contributed the largest share of publications (6/19; 31%) [12–17], followed by the Netherlands (4/19; 21%) [6, 18–20], and South Korea (2/19; 10%) [21, 22]. The remaining studies originated from Japan [23], Taiwan [7], Argentina [24], Sweden [25], Romania [26], Spain [27] and Germany [28], each contributing one study (Fig. 2). All included articles were published between 2020 and 2026 (Fig. 3).

Participant characteristics

In the 19 included studies, a total of 319,709 prehospital patients were assessed for chest pain or ACS syndromes, sample sizes varied from 53 to 184,577. age of participants generally fell between 57 and 74 years. While sex distribution was inconsistently reported, a male predominance was commonly observed, with males accounting for 48–79% of participants. In general, demographic reporting was limited and heterogeneous across studies.

Study design

Most of the included studies were observational or retrospective cohort in design (n = 11), followed by prospective observational studies (n = 7). One study used a two-phase hybrid design, integrating a retrospective model-development cohort with prospective external validation [18].

Prehospital component

The included studies implemented AI systems to support prehospital management of suspected ACS using data available to EMS at the scene, during ambulance transport, or at the dispatch level. The main inputs analyzed in AI applications were usually prehospital ECG recordings (12-lead or 3-lead), and often incorporated EMS documentation, structured paramedic assessments, or free-text reports processed with natural language processing. Additionally, some studies included geospatial information, social determinants of health, or focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS) conducted under tele-guidance or by paramedics. Most AI applications operated on real-time prehospital data, while a few combining these inputs with information obtained after hospital arrival.

AI model characteristics

The reviewed studies identified four main AI model categories (Table 3). ECG-based deep learning models were the most common AI approach, accounting for 36.8% of the included studies. These models utilized 12-lead or 3-lead ECG signals, serial features, or ECG images for early detection of ACS, occlusion myocardial infarction (OMI), or STEMI [7, 12–14, 17, 20]. One study specifically used telecardiology-enabled ECG image analysis with the QCG™ Analyzer in EMS-suspected STEMI patients [22]. Multimodal or hybrid AI models accounted for 26.3% of the studies that integrated ECG data with clinical variables, biomarkers, or risk factors beyond ECG-only interpretation [18, 21, 23, 25, 28]. 31.5% of the included studies used machine learning models based exclusively on clinical and EMS data. These approaches utilized unstructured prehospital inputs, including vital signs, symptoms, paramedic documentation, social determinants of health, or geospatial data to support dispatch-level triage, risk stratification, and early outcome prediction [6, 15, 24, 26]. A single study (5.2%) explored AI-assisted cardiac imaging in the prehospital setting, applying AI-guided FoCUS to evaluate cardiac function parameters such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) in patients with suspected ACS [19].

Outcomes

Diagnostic performance

AI–based models demonstrated promising diagnostic performance for the detection of ACS across prehospital settings. 10 studies evaluated AI-driven diagnostic approaches, with AUC values generally ranging from approximately 0.81 to 0.99, alongside, sensitivity values ranged from 73% to 94%, whereas specificity ranged from 56% to 99%, demonstrating consistently high diagnostic accuracy among AI-based models for prehospital ACS detection [7, 12–14, 17–20, 22, 25].

Four studies using standard 12-lead ECG data showed that ML models achieved comparable or superior performance to clinicians, improving sensitivity and reducing diagnostic variability; while also decreasing ECG interpretation time and facilitating faster prehospital triage [7, 12, 14, 22]. More advanced approaches, including serial ECG analysis and multimodal models integrating clinical and demographic data, further improved diagnostic accuracy [14, 20, 25]. In addition, deep learning–based models demonstrated high accuracy in detecting OMI, while explainability tools enhanced clinician confidence without substantially affecting diagnostic performance [17]. Notably, Baker et al. found that AI applications improved diagnostic decision-making by reducing false-positive catheterization laboratory activations without missing true cases [13].

Risk stratification and prediction

AI-based models showed moderate to good performance in the prehospital prediction and risk stratification of acute coronary syndromes. Six studies evaluated prediction models in this subtheme, with AUC values generally ranging from approximately 0.71 to 0.95. Sensitivity was reported between 69% and 99.5%, while specificity varied more widely between 11% and 98% [6, 15, 16, 21, 23, 24], particularly in studies prioritizing high sensitivity for prehospital triage and rule-out purposes [6].

Three studies used routinely available prehospital data, including vital signs, symptoms, and ECG findings [6, 21, 23]. Another study showed that integrating ECG findings with clinical variables using gradient boosted decision trees (GBDT) and selective classification (SC) enhanced prehospital ACS/NSTE-ACS prediction while increasing diagnostic confidence by excluding cases with uncertainty [16]. Models developed from large EMS datasets also demonstrated better predictive performance than conventional statistical approaches, supporting the value of real-world registry-based data integration [21]. In addition, one study showed that ACS risk prediction could be extended beyond immediate diagnosis to short-term follow-up, with high accuracy for 30-day ACS events [24]. Notably, Faramand et al. reported that non-physiological variables, including social and geographic determinants, also contributed to prediction performance [15].

Prognostic and clinical decision-support

AI-based models also demonstrated promising performance in prognostic assessment and clinical decision-making in patients with ACS. Three studies specifically evaluated outcomes beyond diagnosis, focusing on early risk prediction and treatment decision support [26–28]. Sensitivity and specificity were not reported, and model performance was primarily characterized by AUROC values ranging from 0.76 to 0.98 with several models reporting accuracy above 90% [26, 27].

Stamate et al. reported that ML models achieved high accuracy in predicting the development of cardiogenic shock in patients with STEMI, particularly in the prehospital phase, supporting their potential role in early risk stratification and triage [26]. Similarly, Sigle et al. found that ML models effectively predicted the need for coronary revascularization, with performance improving as additional in-hospital data were incorporated [28]. Ramos-Rodríguez et al. employed unsupervised ML to identify three distinct STEMI phenotypes with substantially varying 30-day mortality risks, supporting early prehospital risk stratification and triage optimization [27].

Discussion

This scoping review presents an overview of the existing literature on the role of AI in the prehospital assessment of patients with suspected ACS. Overall, the findings suggest that the utilization of AI systems may have the potential to support early diagnosis, risk stratification, and clinical decision-making in this setting.

The findings of this review indicate the acceptable diagnostic performance of AI-based models in detecting ACS and may support early identification of high-risk patients in prehospital settings. This was particularly noticeable in ECG-based models, which in some cases improved sensitivity and reduced diagnostic variability, achieving results comparable to or exceeding conventional clinical assessment.

These improvements likely reflect the ability of AI models to capture complex, non-linear ECG patterns and dynamic ischemic changes. In this context, approaches incorporating serial ECG analysis or integrating ECG with clinical data further improve diagnostic accuracy, emphasizing the value of both temporal information and multimodal inputs.

Moreover, several models showed the capacity to detect subtle ischemic patterns, such as OMI without prominent ST-segment elevation, which could be difficult to detect during standard analysis. In addition, AI-based diagnostic support tools were shown to increase triage efficiency by reducing false-positive activations without increasing missed cases [13, 17]. Collectively, these findings imply that AI can be used as an effective complementary tool to improve the efficiency and accuracy of early ACS diagnosis in prehospital care.

These findings are consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy of AI-based ECG analysis for ACS and, in some settings, may outperform clinician interpretation or rule-based algorithms [9, 29, 30]. This advantage may be explained by the ability of deep learning models to capture complex, non-linear ECG patterns and subtle ischemic signatures that are often difficult for human readers or conventional algorithms to detect [31]. Consistent with this, previous studies have found that AI models can enhance sensitivity and positive predictive value for MI detection, particularly in retrospective and multicenter evaluations [9, 31]. Moreover, integrating demographic and clinical variables with ECG signals has been shown to further improve classification performance, highlighting the added value of multimodal data in ACS diagnosis [32]. This reflects the multidimensional nature of ACS assessment in real-world clinical practice, where diagnosis is not based solely on ECG findings but also incorporates symptoms, risk factors, and, when available, biomarkers [33–35]. Furthermore, emerging concepts such as the OMI paradigm highlight the limitations of strict ST-elevation criteria and support the development of AI models capable of identifying ischemic patterns beyond traditional diagnostic thresholds [36]. Together, these findings demonstrate that AI-based ECG analysis is not only improving diagnostic accuracy but also transforming the diagnostic approach in ACS shifting from rigid rule-based criteria toward pattern recognition–driven decision-making.

According to the findings of this review, AI-based models demonstrate moderate to good performance in prehospital risk stratification of ACS; however, they are more inconsistent compared to diagnostic models, reflecting the inherently multifactorial and probabilistic nature of risk prediction.

A key observation is the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity across models. Some models achieved very high sensitivity with low specificity, making them more suitable for rule-out strategies rather than definitive diagnosis [6], which may help reduce unnecessary emergency department use in prehospital triage.

In addition, the model performance was strongly dependent on the type of input data. Models developed using large EMS registries showed improved predictive performance than conventional statistical approaches, emphasizing the importance of real-world, high-dimensional data in enhancing generalizability [21]. This finding aligns with prior studies suggesting that AI models utilizing multiple data sources outperform conventional scoring systems in prehospital settings [37]. Notably, a shift from physiological-only to context-aware prediction was observed. In addition to clinical variables such as age and prior revascularization, social and geographic factors such as income level and distance from healthcare facilities, contributed to prediction performance [15], reflecting the broader determinants of health in ACS outcomes. This is consistent with emerging evidence indicating that incorporating social determinants of health into AI models can significantly enhance risk prediction across cardiovascular conditions [38]. Moreover, incorporating temporal information suggests that AI algorithms may provide not only immediate triage but also short-term risk prediction [24]. Overall, while AI-based prediction models are more complex and variable than diagnostic models, they hold substantial potential to improve prehospital triage through more personalized and context-aware risk assessment.

The findings of this review suggest that the application of AI in ACS is extending beyond early diagnosis toward prognostic assessment and clinical decision-making. Recent studies have demonstrated that machine learning has the capacity to predict critical events such as cardiogenic shock in the early stages, thereby supporting timely triage and prioritization of high-risk patients [26]. Moreover, some models have demonstrated the ability to predict the need for therapeutic interventions, such as coronary revascularization, with acceptable accuracy, particularly when both prehospital and in-hospital data are used [28]. This highlights the value of multimodal data integration, including ECG, clinical features, and laboratory findings, in enhancing decision-making and optimizing resource utilization.

Moreover, studies indicate that AI algorithms can improve not only clinical prediction but also the operational aspects of prehospital care. AI-driven triage models have been shown to assist paramedic prioritization and reduce unnecessary emergency department transports, while real-time ECG analysis can facilitate faster diagnosis and treatment initiation [7, 37]. Similarly, Predictive models have enabled early identification of critical care requirements and informed transport decisions [39]. In addition, AI systems integrating symptoms, vital signs, and ECG data have improved early risk stratification and communication with receiving hospitals [40, 41].

These findings reflect a shift from AI applications focused solely on diagnosis toward more comprehensive clinical and system-level decision support. By providing timely and actionable predictions, AI has the potential to improve both patient outcomes and healthcare efficiency. However, translating these promising findings into routine clinical practice will require robust validation across diverse populations and real-world settings [42].

Beyond conventional validation studies, future progress in AI-assisted prehospital ACS assessment will likely depend on implementation-oriented and adaptive AI systems. Emerging multimodal methods that integrate ECG signals with clinical, biomarker, imaging, and contextual EMS data have the potential further improve diagnostic precision and individualized risk assessment [43]. In addition, explainable AI techniques, edge-based real-time deployment on EMS devices, and seamless integration into EMS workflows may improve clinician trust and facilitate broader clinical adoption, particularly in prehospital environments where network connectivity may be limited or unreliable. AI technologies may also evolve from passive diagnostic classifiers toward workflow-focused decision-support tools capable of assisting dynamic triage and resource allocation. With the rapid evolution of AI technologies, strategies supporting continuous learning and scalable deployment across diverse healthcare settings may become increasingly important for translating proof-of-concept models into routine clinical practice.

Limitations

Despite these promising findings, this review highlights several important limitations. Most included studies were retrospective, single-center, and based on relatively small sample sizes, limiting generalizability. Substantial heterogeneity was identified among studies in patient populations, healthcare settings, AI methodologies (including ML, DL, clustering algorithms, and multimodal models), input data types (e.g., 3-lead vs. 12-lead ECGs, clinical variables, biomarkers, and imaging data), target outcomes, validation strategies, and reported performance metrics. In particular, the included AI models represented fundamentally different methodological paradigms with varying levels of complexity, interpretability, data requirements, and validation needs, further complicating direct comparison and standardization across studies. In addition, several studies were exploratory or proof-of-concept investigations with inconsistent methodological reporting, further limiting direct comparability and the feasibility of a more focused quantitative synthesis. Moreover, most models were not externally evaluated in real-world settings, raising concerns about their practical applicability. The use of complex AI models also raises challenges related to interpretability, which may limit clinical trust and adoption. Although explainability techniques including saliency mapping can improve model transparency, their practical sufficiency for supporting physician trust and decision-making in high-stakes, time-sensitive prehospital environments remains uncertain. In addition, incorporating non-physiological variables, such as social and geographic factors, may introduce biases related to population characteristics and healthcare access. Most studies were conducted in high-income countries, such as the United States and the Netherlands, which may further limit generalizability. Finally, although evidence supporting AI in clinical decision-making is growing, most models remain at the developmental stage, with limited real-world implementation. Future prospective, multicenter studies with standardized methodologies and external validation are needed to establish clinical utility.

Conclusion

Taken together, the findings from the current scoping review highlight the growing potential of AI, particularly ECG-based and multimodal models, to improve prehospital assessment of suspected ACS. By capturing complex data patterns beyond conventional interpretation, AI algorithms demonstrated improved identification of high-risk presentations and may enhance triage efficiency. In addition to diagnostic support, emerging evidence suggests that AI is evolving toward supporting risk stratification and clinical decision-making in prehospital care. However, the field remains in an early stage, with notable methodological limitations, including retrospective design, small and homogeneous datasets, and lack of external validation. To move beyond proof-of-concept and into clinical integration, future research must prioritize prospective, multicenter validation across diverse populations and system contexts. Such efforts are essential to ensure the safe and effective integration of AI-based tools into real-world prehospital care, with the aim of improving early diagnosis, risk stratification, and resource utilization.
