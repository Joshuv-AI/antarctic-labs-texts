Development and validation of a nomogram model for predicting bone cement leakage during vertebroplasty
Authors: Tangfei Ran, Guiyong Fan, Zhen Wang, Sen Yang, Yijie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and validate a nomogram model for predicting the risk of bone cement leakage (BCL) during vertebroplasty, providing a visual tool to assist preoperative risk assessment and clinical decision-making.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients who underwent vertebroplasty at our hospital between January 2022 and June 2025. Patients were divided into two groups based on the occurrence of BCL. Univariate and multivariate Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors, which were then used to construct a combined predictive model. Based on the regression results, a nomogram was established. The discriminative ability of the model was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Calibration was assessed using a Bootstrap resampling calibration curve, and clinical utility was examined through decision curve analysis.ResultsUnivariate analysis indicated significant differences between the two groups in bone mineral density, operative time, surgical type, fracture vertebral segment, presence of multiple fractures, cortical disruption of the vertebra, degree of vertebral compression, bone cement injection volume, cement viscosity during injection, postoperative vertebral height restoration rate, and hypertension (all P < 0.05). Multivariate Logistic regression identified bone mineral density, multiple fractures, vertebral cortical disruption, degree of vertebral compression, and bone cement injection volume as independent predictors of cement leakage. Validation of the nomogram demonstrated good discrimination and excellent calibration.ConclusionThe nomogram model exhibits robust predictive performance and clinical applicability. It may offer a visual tool for individualized preoperative risk assessment in vertebroplasty.Keywords: Vertebroplasty, Bone cement leakage, Nomogram model, Risk factors, Risk prediction

Introduction

Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and its derivative technique, percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP), have become minimally invasive standard procedures for treating osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, vertebral hemangiomas, and selected vertebral metastatic tumors [1, 2]. These techniques involve the injection of bone cement into the affected vertebral body to stabilize the vertebra, relieve pain, and partially restore vertebral height, offering advantages such as minimal trauma, rapid effect, and shortened hospital stay [3]. However, with the widespread application of these procedures, surgery-related complications have increasingly emerged, among which BCL represents one of the most common and potentially serious complications [4]. Reported incidence ranges from 16% to 55%, with variability largely attributed to differences in surgical technique, bone cement properties, vertebral conditions of patients, and diagnostic criteria [5–7].

Bone cement leakage (BCL) can be classified according to its location into paravertebral venous plexus leakage, intervertebral disc leakage, spinal canal leakage, and puncture tract leakage [8]. Of these, spinal canal leakage can directly compress the spinal cord or nerve roots, leading to neurological deficits or even paralysis [9], whereas leakage into the venous plexus may result in pulmonary embolism, posing a life-threatening risk [10]. Furthermore, even asymptomatic leakage may increase the risk of subsequent fractures in adjacent vertebrae [11]. Therefore, effectively predicting and preventing BCL is a core challenge for improving the safety and efficacy of PVP/PKP procedures.

Currently, clinical risk assessment for BCL primarily relies on the surgeon’s experience and qualitative evaluation of preoperative imaging. Commonly considered factors include posterior vertebral wall integrity, degree of vertebral compression, and fracture type. However, this experience-based assessment lacks standardized quantitative criteria, is highly subjective, and often overlooks the comprehensive impact of patients’ overall health, bone metabolic status, and intraoperative parameters. Although numerous studies have explored potential risk factors associated with BCL [12–14], such as low bone mineral density, vertebral cortical disruption, large cement injection volume, and inappropriate injection timing (e.g., low cement viscosity), their conclusions are inconsistent. Most studies focus on single or a limited number of factors and fail to establish a systematic predictive framework integrating clinical, imaging, and intraoperative information. This limitation makes precise individualized risk stratification difficult for clinicians preoperatively, hindering targeted surgical planning and adequate informed consent for high-risk patients.

In view of this, the present study aims to retrospectively analyze comprehensive clinical data from a single center of patients undergoing PVP/PKP to systematically identify independent predictive factors significantly associated with BCL. Based on these findings, a multivariate Logistic regression analysis will be performed to construct a nomogram prediction model, with internal validation assessing its discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. The ultimate goal is to establish a reliable and practical individualized risk prediction tool, providing evidence-based support for preoperative precision assessment, surgical planning optimization, and complication prevention.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study. Based on previous studies, the reported incidence of BCL after vertebroplasty ranges from 16% to 55% [5–7]. To estimate the required sample size, we used the formula for prevalence-based sample size calculation: . In our study, we assumed p = 0.3 based on prior reports and set d = 0.07, corresponding to a 7% margin of error at a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96). Using these values, the calculated sample size was approximately 177. From January 2022 to June 2025, all patients who met the inclusion criteria and received vertebroplasty treatment in our hospital’s spinal surgery department were retrospectively and consecutively included in the study. A total of 200 patients who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively included in the study. (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of patient selection and inclusion

Inclusion criteria

Age ≥ 50 years; Underwent PVP or PKP; Complete clinical data, including preoperative and postoperative imaging and clinical records.

Exclusion criteria

Severe neurological deficits (American Spinal Injury Association, ASIA grade C or below) requiring open decompression surgery; Severe cardiopulmonary, hepatic, or renal insufficiency, or coagulation disorders precluding surgery; History of prior surgery on the target vertebra; Incomplete follow-up data or poor-quality postoperative computed tomography (CT) images affecting leakage assessment.

The study protocol was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of Suzhou Kowloon Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Ethics Approval Number: HG-2026-002) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [15]. As this was a retrospective analysis using anonymized clinical data, the ethics committee waived the requirement for patient informed consent.

Data collection

Clinical data for all enrolled patients were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. The study team standardized data extraction and used double-entry and cross-verification to ensure accuracy. Since all patients were required to provide complete preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative clinical data to meet the inclusion criteria, there were no missing values for any of the collected variables. Collected data included: (1) Demographic information: sex, age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes). (2) Imaging parameters: location of fractured vertebrae (thoracic, lumbar), vertebral cortical integrity, fracture type (single vs. multiple fractures), degree of vertebral compression [16] (classified based on the ratio of the most compressed vertebral height to the height of the same or adjacent vertebra: mild 20%-25%, moderate 25%-40%, severe > 40%), postoperative vertebral height restoration rate [17], calculated as Vertebral Height Restoration Rate=[(Postoperative anterior height - Preoperative anterior height)/Preoperative anterior height]×100%. (3) Laboratory parameters: bone mineral density (BMD) T-score. (4) Surgical parameters: operative time, surgical approach (PVP/PKP), puncture path (unilateral/bilateral), bone cement injection volume, and cement viscosity during injection. High-viscosity cement is thick and pasty, requiring significant pressure to inject and maintaining its shape without rapid flow, whereas low-viscosity cement flows easily from the syringe and fills the vertebral body rapidly.

Outcome assessment

BCL was defined as the presence of cement outside the vertebral cortical margin or the pedicle puncture tract. Diagnostic criteria [18] included intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy or postoperative 3-day CT showing cement extending beyond the affected vertebra, including paravertebral leakage, intervertebral foramen leakage, epidural leakage, and venous plexus leakage. Any detected leakage was classified into the “leakage group.”

Outcome determination was performed by a panel of two associate chief radiologists and one associate chief spine surgeon. All reviewers independently evaluated both intraoperative fluoroscopy and postoperative CT images. To minimize potential detection bias, the reviewers were blinded to the patients’ clinical information and surgical outcomes during image assessment. Consensus among the three reviewers established the outcome; in cases of disagreement, joint review combined with surgical records and patient symptoms was used to reach a final decision.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent-samples t-tests; non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square (χ²) test. Prior to multivariate model construction, multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF < 5 considered indicative of no significant collinearity. Multivariate binary Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent risk factors for BCL. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the predictive value of individual variables. A nomogram model for predicting the risk of BCL after vertebroplasty was constructed using R version 4.5.1. Internal validation was performed using Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations). Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), calibration was evaluated via calibration curves, and clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA). Graphs were generated using the ggplot2 package in R. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

A total of 200 eligible patients were included, of whom 147 did not experience BCL and 53 experienced BCL, yielding an incidence of 26.5% during vertebroplasty. Comparison between groups showed significant differences in BMD, operative time, surgical approach, location of fractured vertebrae, presence of multiple fractures, vertebral cortical disruption, degree of vertebral compression, bone cement injection volume, cement viscosity during injection, postoperative vertebral height restoration rate, and hypertension (all P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between groups regarding age, sex, height, weight, BMI, puncture path, or history of diabetes (all P > 0.05) (Tables 1 and 2).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Higher BMD was a protective factor (OR < 1), whereas multiple fractures, cortical disruption, higher degree of compression, and larger cement injection volume were risk factors (OR > 1). Other variables, including surgical type, operative time, cement viscosity during injection, postoperative vertebral height restoration, and hypertension, were not statistically significant after adjustment for other factors (P > 0.05). VIF values for all variables were close to 1, indicating no multicollinearity issues in the model.

In addition, we employed internal validation based on bootstrap sampling (B = 1000). The AUC calculated through bootstrap correction was 0.887 (95% CI: 0.832–0.951), and the average absolute error of the calibration curve was 0.041, indicating that the estimated values of the multivariate logistic regression analysis model were stable and the prediction performance was reliable.

Predictive performance of clinical indicators for BCL

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of individual clinical indicators for BCL after vertebroplasty. Among single predictors, bone cement injection volume demonstrated the highest predictive performance (AUC = 0.754), followed by BMD (AUC = 0.735), multiple vertebral fractures (AUC = 0.682), vertebral cortical disruption (AUC = 0.668), and degree of vertebral compression (AUC = 0.655). Integration of all five factors into a combined predictive model demonstrated superior discriminative ability compared with any individual factor, achieving an AUC of 0.909 (95% CI: 0.858–0.961), indicating that the combined model is an effective tool for predicting BCL after vertebroplasty (Table 5; Fig. 2).

Nomogram model construction and validation

Based on the multivariate analysis results, a nomogram was constructed to predict the risk of BCL after PVP/PKP (Fig. 3A). The model integrated five independent predictors: BMD, presence of multiple fractures, cortical disruption, degree of vertebral compression, and bone cement injection volume. Individual values for each predictor were converted into single scores, which were summed to yield a patient-specific predicted probability of BCL, providing a visual translation from clinical indicators to risk probability. Total score correlated positively with BCL risk. Model discrimination evaluated by ROC curve showed an AUC of 0.893 (95% CI: 0.845–0.963) (Fig. 3B), indicating good discriminative ability. The calibration curve, based on Bootstrap resampling (B = 1000), closely approximated the ideal 45° line (perfect prediction) (Fig. 3C). The model demonstrated good calibration across the entire probability range (0.0–1.0), with a mean absolute error of 0.039 (n = 200), indicating accurate prediction without significant overestimation or underestimation, confirming the reliability and clinical accuracy of the model. DCA (Fig. 3D) showed that the model provided higher net benefit than “intervene in all patients” (All) or “intervene in no patients” (None) within a wide threshold probability range of 5%–40%, demonstrating clear clinical utility. This allows for precise identification of high-risk patients requiring intervention while avoiding unnecessary treatment for low-risk patients.

Discussion

This study successfully developed and validated a nomogram model for predicting the risk of BCL during vertebroplasty. Multivariate analysis identified five independent predictors: BMD, presence of multiple fractures, vertebral cortical disruption, degree of vertebral compression, and bone cement injection volume. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination and good calibration (mean absolute error = 0.039). DCA further confirmed that within a clinical decision threshold range of 5%–40%, risk stratification using this model provides clear clinical net benefit.

Firstly, multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that BMD, multiple fractures, cortical disruption, degree of compression, and cement injection volume are independent predictors of BCL, consistent with previous studies and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms [19, 20]. BMD, reflecting the integrity of bone microarchitecture and overall mechanical strength, is highlighted as a key protective factor in the model. From a pathophysiological perspective, low BMD indicates sparse trabeculae, widened intertrabecular spaces, and cortical thinning. These microstructural changes not only weaken vertebral containment and anchoring of cement but also create numerous low-resistance channels under injection pressure [21]. Studies have shown that the volume of venous sinuses in osteoporotic vertebrae is relatively increased, facilitating cement flow along these physiological or pathological cavities, significantly raising the risk of paravertebral venous plexus or epidural leakage [14, 22]. Multiple fractures and higher degrees of compression generally indicate more severe trauma and unstable vertebral structures, making cement distribution more difficult to control. Fracture clefts may directly communicate with paravertebral venous sinuses or the spinal canal, further increasing leakage risk. Vertebral cortical integrity, particularly of the posterior wall, is a critical anatomical barrier preventing cement from entering the spinal canal, and its disruption is widely recognized as a strong risk factor for BCL [23]. Cement injection volume is a key controllable surgical factor: excessive cement can increase intravertebral pressure, forcing extrusion through microcracks, and may overcorrect vertebral height, causing endplate fracture and creating new leakage pathways. By integrating these multidimensional and heterogeneous predictors into a single model, the predictive performance significantly exceeded that of any individual factor, emphasizing the advantage of comprehensive assessment.

In addition to statistical performance, our study provides practical implications for clinical application. Nomogram models have been increasingly used as intuitive risk prediction tools in various surgical and medical contexts, translating complex multivariable regression analyses into user-friendly graphical formats. They allow clinicians to estimate individualized risk probabilities without complex calculations, facilitating evidence-based decision-making. In this study, the developed nomogram can be used preoperatively to estimate each patient’s risk of BCL during vertebroplasty. By inputting readily available variables—BMD, presence of multiple fractures, vertebral cortical integrity, degree of compression, and planned cement injection volume—clinicians can identify high-risk patients and tailor preventive strategies accordingly. For example, patients with predicted high risk may benefit from more careful cement injection techniques, consideration of alternative cement viscosity, or closer intraoperative imaging surveillance. This practical tool bridges the gap between quantitative risk assessment and individualized surgical planning, potentially optimizing patient safety and resource allocation in minimally invasive spine surgery. Meanwhile, DCA provides further evidence of the model’s clinical value. Results indicate that when clinicians consider a leakage risk above 5% (i.e., willing to take additional preventive measures), using the model to identify high-risk patients for targeted intervention yields higher net benefit than blanket strategies of intervening in all patients or none. This demonstrates that the model has not only statistical significance but also practical clinical decision-making value, optimizing resource allocation and avoiding overtreatment of low-risk patients and undertreatment of high-risk patients.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center, retrospective study, data collection may be subject to selection and information bias, and the generalizability of the model to other populations or institutions is limited. External validation in diverse patient cohorts and surgical settings is required before widespread application. Second, although key clinical and imaging variables were included, some technical details potentially affecting leakage (e.g., surgeon experience, specific cement brand, injection rate, precise needle placement) and finer radiomic features were not analyzed. These unmeasured factors could have introduced residual confounding and may bias the observed associations. Third, the number of BCL events relative to the number of candidate predictors was limited, resulting in a relatively low events-per-variable (EPV) ratio, which raises potential concerns regarding overfitting and the stability of regression coefficients. Although internal validation using bootstrap resampling (B = 1000) demonstrated stable estimates, good discrimination, and calibration, caution should be exercised when interpreting the model, and its generalizability should be confirmed in larger and independent external cohorts. Compared with previously reported predictive models, which often focus on single factors or simpler scoring systems, our nomogram integrates multiple independent predictors and provides a visual, user-friendly tool for individualized risk assessment, potentially enhancing clinical applicability. Fourth, due to the retrospective and observational nature of this study, the identified associations between predictors and BCL should be interpreted as correlations rather than causal relationships; the design precludes definitive causal inference. Fifth, bone cement leakage was analyzed only as a binary outcome (leakage versus no leakage). Different leakage patterns, including intradiscal, venous, paravertebral, or spinal canal leakage, may have distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical consequences. Subgroup analysis by leakage type could provide more detailed insights into the associated risk factors and imaging characteristics. However, the relatively limited number of leakage events in our cohort restricted the statistical power for such stratified analyses. Future studies with larger sample sizes should consider subclassifying leakage types to explore their specific risk factors and clinical outcomes. Future studies could incorporate multidimensional data to improve predictive accuracy. Additionally, the model predicts the risk of BCL occurrence, not its clinical consequences. Future work could combine risk stratification with specific clinical outcomes to develop a more clinically oriented predictive system.

In conclusion, this study established and preliminarily validated a well-performing nomogram for individualized prediction of BCL risk during vertebroplasty. The tool demonstrates good accuracy, calibration, and clinical utility, offering clinicians a practical means for preoperative risk assessment and patient communication, optimizing surgical planning and perioperative management, thereby enhancing safety and advancing precision medicine in minimally invasive spine surgery.
