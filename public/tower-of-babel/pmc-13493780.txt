Atieh Schizophrenia EEG, a novel high-quality dataset designed to advance biomarker and machine learning research
Authors: Sara Bagherzadeh, Mohammadreza Norouzi, Fatemeh Farokhshad, Pouya Tolou Kouroshi, Amirhesam Ghasri, Reza Kazemi, Reza Rostami, Ahmad Shalbaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective, quantifiable electroencephalogram (EEG) biomarkers and machine learning systems for schizophrenia (SZ) research are limited by the scarcity of large, diverse, and high-quality public datasets. To address this gap, we present ASEEG (Atieh Schizophrenia EEG), a resting-state EEG dataset designed to support methodological development and benchmarking in SZ studies. ASEEG comprises 198 recordings from 101 subjects, including 51 individuals diagnosed with SZ according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria and 50 demographically matched healthy controls. Data were collected at a clinical setting using a standardized 19-channel EEG montage (international 10–20 system) with a sampling rate of 500 Hz. For each subject, both eyes-open and eyes-closed resting-state conditions were recorded for five minutes each, enabling analysis of complementary neural dynamics. The dataset spans a wide age range (15–65 years) and provides balanced diagnostic groups and extended recording durations. ASEEG is intended for use in biomarker extraction, machine learning model development, validation, and comparative evaluation of EEG-based approaches for SZ research.

Background & Summary

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a severe, chronic psychiatric disorder characterized by profound disruptions in thought, perception, emotion, and behavior, including hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking, and cognitive impairments1,2. Affecting approximately 1 in 300 individuals worldwide—equating to over 20 million adults globally—it imposes a substantial burden on patients, families, and healthcare systems, with lifetime morbidity risks approaching 1% in the general population1. Traditional diagnostic approaches, primarily reliant on clinical interviews and phenomenological criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), suffer from inherent subjectivity, inter-rater variability, and delayed identification, often leading to suboptimal treatment outcomes and prolonged untreated psychosis. The absence of reliable, objective biomarkers exacerbates these challenges, underscoring the urgent need for innovative, quantifiable diagnostic tools that can facilitate early intervention and personalized therapeutic strategies2,3.

Electroencephalography (EEG) has emerged as a promising non-invasive modality for probing neurophysiological underpinnings of SZ, offering high temporal resolution to capture aberrant brain dynamics in real time4–6. Resting-state and task-evoked EEG signatures in SZ patients frequently reveal deviations from healthy controls (HCs), such as diminished gamma-band oscillations (indicative of impaired neural synchrony), elevated theta/delta power (reflecting cortical hypoexcitability), and deficits in sensory gating (e.g., P50 suppression) and mismatch negativity (MMN) responses—hallmarks of disrupted predictive coding and glutamatergic dysfunction. These electrophysiological biomarkers not only correlate with core SZ symptomatology but also hold diagnostic potential, with studies demonstrating classification accuracies exceeding 80% in controlled settings. However, the translational utility of EEG remains constrained by signal artifacts, inter-individual variability, and the complexity of feature extraction from high-dimensional multichannel data4–6.

Two public resting-state EEG databases for schizophrenia are currently available: the Warsaw SZ database7 (28 subjects) and the Moscow SZ database8 (84 adolescents). In contrast, our newly introduced database includes higher sample size i.e., 101 subjects and offers two recording conditions for Eyes-Open (EO) and Eyes-Close (EC). The Warsaw SZ EEG dataset, hosted on the RepOD repository under 10.18150/repod.0107441 and created in 2017 by Elzbieta Olejarczyk and Wojciech Jernajczyk from the Nalecz Institute of Biocybernetics and Biomedical Engineering (IBIB PAN) and the Institute of Psychiatry and Neurology in Warsaw, Poland, respectively, comprises EEG recordings from 14 patients diagnosed with paranoid SZ and 14 age- and gender-matched HC subjects, ensuring balanced demographics across groups for comparative analysis7. Data were collected using a standard 19-channel EEG setup based on the international 10–20 system, with a sampling frequency of 250 Hz, capturing resting-state eyes-closed (EC) conditions over approximately 1–2 minutes per subject to assess brain connectivity and spectral features indicative of schizophrenic alterations. The dataset includes raw EEG signals in EDF format files, totaling around 28 individual subject files with no extensive preprocessing applied beyond basic artifact removal, enabling downstream applications like phase-locking value (PLV), phase-lag index (PLI), and directed transfer function (DTF) computations for graph-based connectivity studies; it is publicly accessible without restrictions, supporting machine learning classifications and neuroscientific research into SZ biomarkers, with a modest total size suitable for academic replication.

The Moscow SZ EEG database, maintained by the Faculty of Biology at Lomonosov Moscow State University and preliminarily prepared by Prof. N.N. Gorbachevskaya from the Mental Health Research Center and Ph.D. S.V. Borisov, features resting-state EEG recordings from 45 adolescent subjects exhibiting symptoms of SZ and 39 screened healthy adolescent controls, forming a cohort of 84 participants focused on early-onset psychiatric evaluation without specified age ranges or gender distributions to emphasize diagnostic screening over demographic stratification8. Recordings were acquired using a 16-channel montage aligned with the international 10–20 system—encompassing electrodes F7, F3, F4, F8, T3, C3, Cz, C4, T4, T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, and O2—at a sampling rate of 128 Hz, yielding 1-minute epochs (7680 samples per channel) of EC resting activity in microvolts (µV), captured under standard clinical conditions to probe spectral and temporal anomalies in adolescent brain function. Each subject’s data is stored in a single-column TXT file with sequentially concatenated channel samples, comprising two downloadable archives (one per group) devoid of advanced preprocessing, ideal for raw signal analysis in connectivity mapping or deep learning models; freely available via direct links, the dataset underscores collaborative Russian efforts in pediatric neuropsychiatry8.

Collectively, these attributes establish ASEEG as a high-quality, robust resource poised to serve as a superior benchmark for future machine learning and biomarker discovery in neuropsychiatric research.

Machine learning (ML) approaches for EEG-based schizophrenia (SZ) detection have shown promise, but their generalizability is severely limited by small, heterogeneous public datasets (often <100 subjects) and insufficient control of demographic or medication confounders9–13. To address these gaps, the present study introduces a new resting-state EEG database comprising 50 demographically matched healthy controls and 51 individuals diagnosed with SZ (DSM-5 criteria). Collected at Atieh Clinic, Tehran, Iran, under standardized protocols, this 19-channel, 500 Hz dataset features subjects aged 18–65 years, detailed clinical phenotyping (including Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale scores (PANSS)), and minimal medication effects at baseline. This resource is designed to support robust ML benchmarking and biomarker discovery while overcoming key limitations of prior public databases.

Methods

Ethics statement

The ethics review board at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences has approved this study as part of the defined diagnostic research project. All studies and activities are conducted under the ethics code number IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1404.441 and adhere to the principles of ethical research. Patient privacy has been prioritized throughout the process. The data repository for this study was organized based on established ethical guidelines and board standards. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s)/ parents. Participants were assured that the research would be conducted in a manner that protects their anonymity and provides a safe, secure environment to safeguard their rights and comfort.

In accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and local regulations, specific informed consent was obtained for the EEG recording procedure and for the subsequent sharing of de-identified data. For all participants under the age of 18, or for those with diminished capacity to provide independent consent, a parent, legal guardian, or other directly responsible adult provided written authorization on a dedicated consent form. This form detailed the nature and duration of the EEG recording, the management of personal information, and the scope of data sharing for research purposes. Participants (and their guardians) were explicitly informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence, and that all data would be anonymized prior to analysis and publication to ensure complete confidentiality.

Participants

The participant group was sourced from the Atieh Clinic, a private facility located in Tehran, Iran. This dataset includes EEG recordings from 51 patients diagnosed with SZ (average age: 31.29 ± 13.63 years; age range: 15–65 years; 15 females, representing 29.41%) alongside 50 healthy volunteers free of neurological or psychiatric conditions (average age: 29.4 ± 8.3 years; age range: 18–52 years; 33 females, representing 66%). HC participants underwent a structured clinical interview conducted by a licensed psychiatrist (R.R., >15 years of experience) to rule out current or past psychiatric disorders (including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and anxiety disorders). Neurological conditions (e.g., epilepsy, traumatic brain injury, neurodegenerative disorders) were excluded via medical history review and, when available, neurological examination. Participants with a first-degree relative diagnosed with a psychotic disorder were also excluded. All screening procedures were documented in the clinic’s medical records. No formal neuropsychological testing was administered; exclusion was based on clinical judgment and self-report corroborated by medical history. Distribution of age and gender for (a) and (b) HC and (c) and (d) SZ participants.

Gender differences in EEG spectral power and connectivity have been documented in the literature, particularly in alpha and beta bands12. Therefore, while the primary comparisons in this study focus on disease-related effects, we acknowledge that the observed group differences in EEG features could be partially influenced by gender imbalance. In the baseline machine learning classification reported in this paper, we did not apply explicit gender balancing; however, the dataset is made publicly available to allow future studies to examine gender-specific effects or apply covariate adjustment techniques (e.g., propensity score matching, stratified sampling). Age, being well-matched between groups, is unlikely to confound the main findings.

Note: The significant gender imbalance between SZ and HC groups, despite age matching, represents a limitation. While our primary goal was to describe and release the dataset rather than claim generalizable diagnostic accuracy, future users of this resource should consider gender as a potential covariate in their analyses.

Selection and preparation of the samples

The ASEEG dataset was compiled at the Atieh Clinic (located in Tehran, Iran) under the oversight of the Department of Biomedical Engineering and Medical Physics, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. The data collection took place during the 2020–2025 period. In total, the dataset comprises 198 EEG samples obtained from patients and healthy group, accompanied by psychologist’ diagnoses, mental and behavioral tests and DSM-5.

Expert labels

Psychiatrist (R.R) with more than 15 years of expertise and psychologist (R.K.) with more than 8 years of expertise collected EEG data, which were then evaluated and approved labels. Out of the 101 patients under investigation, 51 were categorized as SZ and 50 as HC.

Record EEG protocol

EEG signals were obtained retrospectively from the clinic’s medical records. All recordings took place in a controlled clinical environment, conducted by a qualified technician with a 21-channel Mitsar EEG device (Mitsar Co. Ltd., Russia). Nineteen scalp electrodes, arranged in a configuration akin to that of the Warsaw dataset, followed the standard 10–20 system. Data were captured at a sampling rate of 500 Hz, with A1 and A2 electrodes positioned on the ears to serve as reference and ground leads, respectively. Protocols focused on resting-state conditions involving both EO and EC segments, while sleep-stage recordings were omitted to minimize potential influences from varying arousal levels.

Recording conditions and quality assurance

All EEG recordings were conducted in a sound-attenuated, electrically shielded room at Atieh Clinic, with ambient temperature maintained at 22–24 °C and lighting dimmed to a constant low level (~50 lux) to minimize visual evoked potentials and promote stable vigilance. Electrode impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ for all 19 scalp electrodes prior to and during each recording session, verified using the Mitsar device’s built-in impedance check function. Recordings with any electrode exceeding 10 kΩ were repeated or excluded.

Prior to each recording, participants were instructed as follows: (1) sit comfortably in a reclining chair with head supported; (2) remain still with eyes closed (EC condition) or fixated on a centrally presented cross (EO condition); (3) avoid deliberate eye movements, jaw clenching, or swallowing unless necessary; (4) stay awake and refrain from falling asleep. For the EC condition, participants were monitored via real-time video and EEG waveform to detect signs of drowsiness (e.g., increased theta activity, slow rolling eye movements). If drowsiness was observed, the recording was paused, and the participant was gently re-alerted before resuming.

For the EO condition, a trained technician visually confirmed fixation every 30 seconds. Each condition lasted 5 continuous minutes, with a 2-minute break between EC and EO blocks to reduce fatigue. Only artifact-free segments confirmed by both the recording technician and an experienced clinical neurophysiologist (R.R.) were included in the final dataset.

Diagnoses of SZ were confirmed separately by two experienced psychiatrist and psychologist using DSM-5 guidelines. HCs consisted of individuals undergoing standard EEG evaluations for reasons not linked to mental health or neurological issues. Exclusion criteria for controls encompassed prior diagnoses of bipolar disorder, substance use disorders, any psychiatric or somatic illnesses necessitating inpatient care, seizures or antiseizure medication use, history of electroconvulsive treatment, vagus nerve stimulation, or repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation. Further disqualifiers involved head trauma or radiological findings suggestive of vascular brain pathology (either ischemic or hemorrhagic types). Data Record

The dataset is publicly available at https://zenodo.org/uploads/1802953614. Raw EEG recordings were initially acquired in native.eeg format at 500 Hz sampling rate using the Mitsar system. The original dataset comprised 53 individuals with schizophrenia; however, recordings from two subjects exhibited severe technical artifacts and were excluded following quality control review, resulting in 51 valid SZ participants for the EC condition. For the EO condition, an additional four SZ recordings (subjects sz4, sz15, sz18, sz19) were excluded due to excessive movement artifacts or incomplete sessions, yielding 47 usable EO SZ recordings. All 50 healthy control participants provided artifact-free recordings for both conditions, with each session comprising 5 minutes of continuous recording per condition. For public dissemination and to ensure participant confidentiality, raw signals underwent standardized preprocessing prior to release. The preprocessing pipeline is described in next section as “Preprocessing pipeline”. Preprocessed recordings were then converted to .mat format (Matlab format) and organized into a hierarchical directory structure. The ASEEG dataset is divided into two main archives based on recording conditions: EC and EO. Each archive contains two subdirectories: normal/ for healthy controls and sz/ for schizophrenia patients. To preserve anonymity, subjects are identified by sequential numerical codes (n1-n50 for controls, sz1-sz51 for patients) rather than original clinic identifiers.

The EC archive contains 101 preprocessed recordings:

- sz/: 51 files (sz1 .mat through sz51 .mat)

Each .mat file contains a 19 × 38,400 matrix of preprocessed EEG amplitudes (channels × samples, representing 300 seconds at 128 Hz).

Subject numbering is consistent across conditions where available (e.g., n1 .mat in both archives represents the same individual).

Preprocessing pipeline

All raw EEG recordings (native.eeg format, 500 Hz) underwent minimal preprocessing using EEGLAB (v2025.0) in MATLAB R2025a. No Artifact Subspace Reconstruction (ASR), no independent component analysis (ICA), and no automated bad channel removal or interpolation were applied. The goal of this minimal approach is to preserve the natural signal characteristics, leaving all artifact rejection, channel interpolation, and epoch selection decisions to end users based on their specific research questions. The preprocessing workflow proceeded as follows:

Step 1: Band-pass filtering

Continuous data were filtered using a 4th-order Butterworth filter with a passband of 1–45 Hz to remove slow drifts and high-frequency noise (e.g., muscle activity above 45 Hz).

Step 2: Notch filtering

A 50 Hz notch filter was applied to remove line noise.

Step 3: Downsampling

Data was downsampled from 500 Hz to 128 Hz to reduce file size and computational load while preserving spectral content up to the Nyquist frequency (64 Hz), well above the 45 Hz filter upper bound.

Note: No bad channel removal or interpolation was performed; all 19 channels were retained for every subject to allow researchers to apply their own rejection criteria. Neither ASR nor ICA was applied, meaning transient artifacts (e.g., eye blinks, muscle activity, electrode pops) remain in the data by design. Continuous data are provided in their entirety (300 seconds per condition per subject), with no fixed-length epochs created and no segments deleted based on amplitude thresholds, giving users full flexibility to define their own epoch boundaries and artifact rejection strategies.

Step 4: Final dataset size

After preprocessing, the dataset comprises 198 recordings (101 EC files: 50 HC + 51 SZ; 97 EO files: 50 HC + 47 SZ) as described in the Data Record section. Each preprocessed recording is stored as a 19 × 38,400 matrix (channels × samples at 128 Hz for 300 seconds). No further processing was performed, ensuring that users receive continuous, minimally preprocessed data suitable for flexible downstream applications (e.g., epoch-based analysis, connectivity estimation, ICA, or machine learning).

Technical Validation

Alpha attenuation test

To check the validation of signals, first we go through the alpha attenuation test. In this test, PSD should be higher in eyes closed condition than eyes open. First, we preprocess signals then calculate PSD. Preprocessing was performed in MATLAB R2025a using EEGLAB. Continuous data were band-pass filtered between 1 and 45 Hz with 4th order finite-impulse-response (FIR) Butterworth-characteristic filters to suppress slow drifts and high-frequency noise before downstream analysis. Then, signals were downsampled to 128 Hz due to simplified processing and reduce computational costs.

Quantitative validation of alpha attenuation and group comparison

To quantitatively validate data quality, we performed a paired t-test comparing alpha band (8–13 Hz) power between EC and EO conditions across all healthy control subjects with both recording conditions. Results confirmed a statistically significant alpha attenuation effect (EC: 6.65 ± 3.22 dB/Hz; EO: 3.71 ± 2.94 dB/Hz; t(49) = 11.70, p < 0.001), consistent with expected physiological responses and confirming data quality. Also, for SZ patients results confirmed a statistically significant alpha attenuation effect (EC: 5.21 ± 3.27 dB/Hz; EO: 3.38 ± 2.55 dB/Hz; t(46) = 5.04, p < 0.001), consistent with expected physiological responses and confirming data quality.
