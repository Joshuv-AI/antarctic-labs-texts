Stochastic Languages at Sub-stochastic Cost
Smayan Agarwal, Aalok Thakkar

Abstract
When does a deterministic computational model define a probability distribution? What are its properties? This work formalises and settles this stochasticity problem for weighted automata, and its generalisation cost register automata (CRA). We show that checking stochasticity is undecidable for CRAs in general. This motivates the study of the fully linear fragment, where a complete and tractable theory is established. For this class, stochasticity becomes decidable in polynomial time via spectral methods, and every stochastic linear CRA admits an equivalent model with locally sub-stochastic update functions. This provides a local syntactic characterisation of the semantics of the quantitative model. This local characterisation allows us to provide an algebraic Kleene-Schützenberger characterisation for stochastic languages. The class of rational stochastic languages is the smallest class containing finite support distributions, which is closed under convex combination, Cauchy product, and discounted Kleene star. We also introduce Stochastic Regular Expressions as a complete and composable grammar for this class. Our framework provides the foundations for a formal theory of probabilistic computation, with immediate consequences for approximation, sampling, and distribution testing.



1
Introduction

A fundamental question in formal language theory asks:
Which functions on strings give rise to probability distributions?
More precisely, given a quantitative automaton that assigns real-valued weights to words through algebraic computations, when does this function, after normalization, define a valid probability measure over
\Sigma^{*}
? This stochasticity problem sits at the confluence of automata theory, linear algebra, and probability theory, connecting classical questions about regularity with modern challenges in probabilistic modelling.

The question is deceptively simple. Unlike classical probabilistic automata
[
30
,
29
]
, which embed randomness directly into their operational semantics, weighted automata
[
23
]
and cost register automata (CRAs)
[
1
]
compute weights by accumulating numeric values through matrix multiplication or register updates, producing a function
f:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
without internal probabilistic choices. The stochasticity question asks whether this deterministically computed function satisfies two global conditions: non-negativity (
f(w)\geq 0
for all
w
) and normalization (
\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{*}}f(w)=1
). Answering this requires reasoning about infinite summations over all possible strings–a task that, as we show, is undecidable in general.

Motivation.

Probability distributions over strings pervade computer science. In natural language processing, stochastic grammars model syntactic structure
[
16
,
28
]
, while neural language models implicitly define distributions over token sequences
[
12
]
. Biological sequence analysis relies on probabilistic models of DNA and protein strings, where hidden Markov models capture evolutionary dynamics
[
25
]
. Formal verification employs probabilistic specifications to reason about quantitative properties
[
4
,
7
]
. Information theory studies optimal compression via string distributions
[
19
]
.
Quantitative automata theory offers formal models for their study. When they define stochastic languages, they provide interpretable, verifiable probability models with decidable properties and compositional structure. Understanding
when
they are stochastic, and
why
, is thus both theoretically natural and practically consequential.

Results and Contributions.

Our focus on deterministically computed distributions provides a unifying framework for these domains. We study cost register automata as a model of computing probability distributions over strings. Our main contributions are:

1.

We prove that determining whether a CRA defines a stochastic language is undecidable in general (Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
). To recover decidability, we identify and analyse the fragment of CRAs with affine updates. We establish that stochasticity decision problem decidable for this fragment (Theorem
4.4
).

2.

Our second main contribution proves that every linear CRA defining a stochastic language admits a semantically equivalent representation in which stochasticity becomes local and can be checked by computing the sum of transition weights of each register (Theorem
5.1
).

3.

We provide a Kleene-Schützenberger characterization for rational stochastic languages (Theorem
5.2
), and introduce stochastic regular expressions as a grammar for rational stochastic languages.

Our results chart a precise boundary between decidable and undecidable semantic properties of quantitative automata and provide sub-stochastic and local characterisation for CRA with linear updates.
We also present three open questions on

stochasticity decision problem
for general quantitative models,
minimal approximation methods
, and
distribution testing
for stochastic languages.
These results and problems bridge automata theory and statistical inference,
opening new directions for both theoretical investigation, modelling of stochastic sequences, and validating probabilistic generative models against formal distributional specifications.

Related Work

Stochastic languages formalize probability distributions over words, with canonical representations via weighted automata
[
23
]
and probabilistic grammars
[
22
]
. These frameworks connect formal language theory with probabilistic modeling, supporting applications in grammar induction
[
17
]
, and natural language processing
[
18
]
. Despite this extensive development, there is no established syntactic characterization that delineates exactly the class of stochastic languages definable by such models.

Early probabilistic automata, introduced by Rabin and Paz, localize randomness in the transition relation: outgoing transitions from each state form a stochastic distribution, and the probability of accepting a word is obtained by summing over all accepting runs labeled by that word
[
30
,
29
]
. This yields a probabilistic acceptance semantics governed by cutpoint conditions. For
\omega
-languages, probabilistic Büchi automata assign Markov chains to runs and classify behaviors by almost-sure, positive, or threshold probability acceptance
[
3
]
. Chatterjee, Doyen, and Henzinger later generalized these models to probabilistic weighted automata, where transitions are probabilistic but evaluations are quantitative, defining random variables over runs rather than word-level distributions
[
15
,
9
]
. In concurrency theory, Segala and Lynch formalized probabilistic automata as systems with randomized transitions, introducing semantic notions of probabilistic bisimulation
[
32
]
. Across these variants, randomness arises operationally along runs rather than as a distribution over the word space.

On the other hand, Cost Register Automata (CRA), introduced by Alur et al.
[
1
]
, provide a deterministic, register-based model for quantitative functions in which a finite set of registers is updated along a run using algebraic operations. The model generalizes classical weighted automata, while retaining explicit operational semantics. More recently, Benalioua, Lhote, and Reynier analyzed CRA with linear and affine updates over fields, demonstrating equivalence in expressive power with weighted automata and developing minimization algorithms with matching complexity bounds
[
8
]
. Together, these lines of work position CRA as a deterministic and compositional alternative to path-summing weighted models, offering a good fit for extending quantitative automata theory to probabilistic or normalized semantics.

A distinct line of work, initiated by Denis and Esposito
[
21
,
22
]
, studied rational stochastic languages through multiplicity automata. While such models provide an algebraic vehicle for representing stochastic languages, the literature still lacks a clear syntactic or compositional characterization analogous to the Kleene–Schützenberger theorem for rational stochastic languages. In particular, it remains unclear whether stochastic languages admit a closed algebra of regular-like expressions corresponding precisely to the distributions generated by these automata.

Our work addresses this gap by developing a cost register automata based framework for stochastic languages, proving a locally sub-stochastic characterisation, and introducing Stochastic Regular Expressions that give an equivalence between algebraic and automata-theoretic descriptions of stochastic languages.

Quantitative Languages

Let
\Sigma

Definition 1
(Mass)

Let

\mathrm{mass}\left(f\right)_{L}=\sum_{w\in L}f(w),

A quantitative language

is

inite-mass if

Example 1

Let

Definition 2
(Stochastic Language)

A quantitative language

\mathrm{mass}\left(f\right)_{\Sigma^{*}}=1
. The set
\mathcal{S}(\Sigma^{*})
denotes the set of all stochastic languages over
\Sigma^{*}

[
22
,
24
]
.

Example 2
(Dirac Distribution)

For a fixed string
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, the
Dirac distribution

\delta_{w}
is a stochastic language over
\Sigma^{*}
defined for all

as:

\delta_{w}(u)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }u=w,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Example 3

Consider the function
f
in Example
1
. The function
\overline{f}:w\mapsto\frac{6f(w)}{\pi^{2}}
is a stochastic language. In general, if
f
is a finite-mass language such that for all

,
f(w)\geq 0
, and
\mathrm{mass}\left(f\right)_{\Sigma^{*}}\neq 0
, the

function

\overline{f}:w\mapsto f(w)/\mathrm{mass}\left(f\right)_{\Sigma^{*}}
is a stochastic language.

Definition 3

We can now define some operations over stochastic languages. Let
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
be two stochastic languages, and let
\lambda\in(0,1)
.

1.

Their convex combination parametrised by
\lambda
is:

\lambda f_{1}+(1-\lambda)f_{2}:w\mapsto\lambda f_{1}(w)+(1-\lambda)f_{2}(w)

2.

Their Cauchy product is:

f_{1}\cdot f_{2}:w\mapsto\sum_{w=uv}f_{1}(u)\cdot f_{2}(v)

3.

The
d

parameterised by
\lambda

(f_{1})^{*}_{\alpha}:w\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\in\Sigma^{+}\\
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{k}\end{subarray}}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{k-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{1}(w_{i}).

Intuitively,

defines a distribution over strings obtained by concatenating

. This definition implicitly sets

. This is in accordance with standard definitions
[
10

. It is immediate that stochastic languages are closed under the above three operators.

A real-valued CRA is a tuple

{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)=\mu(q_{n},x_{n}),

3.3
Matrix Properties and Convergence

We recall standard results from non-negative matrix theory
(see
[
27
]
) that will be used to analyse convergence of weighted computations. The results discussed here are for real valued square matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

1.

A non-negative square matrix
A
is

irreducible
if for all
i,j
there exists
k\geq 1
such that

(A^{k})_{ij}>0
.

2.

The
spectral radius

\rho(\cdot)
of a square matrix is the maximum of the absolute values of its eigenvalues. The spectral radius of a bounded operator is the supremum of the absolute values of the elements of its spectrum.

3.

A non-negative square matrix A is
row sub-stochastic
if for every row
i
,
\sum_{j}A_{ij}\leq 1
. It is strictly sub-stochastic if for at least one row, the sum is stictly less than
1
.

4.

(Perron-Frobenius Theorem) If a non negative matrix
A
is irreducible, then:

\min_{i}\sum_{j}A_{ij}\leq\rho(A)\leq\max_{i}\sum_{j}A_{ij}.

In particular, if it is strictly sub-stochastic, then
\rho(A)<1
.

5.

(Neumann Series) For a square matrix
A
, the series
I+A+A^{2}+A^{3}\ldots
converges if and only if
\rho(A)<1
. In this case, it converges to
(I-A)^{-1}
.

\rho(A)
or
(I-A)^{-1}
can be computed in
O(n^{3})
time.

4
Problem Formulation and Decidability Results

This work is motivated by a central semantic question:

Given a cost register automaton (CRA), when does it define a stochastic language?

This problem connects the operational view of quantitative automata with the analytic notion of probability distributions over strings.

Problem 1
(Stochasticity Decision Problem)

Given a CRA
\mathcal{A}
, determine whether its semantics satisfies:

(i)

Non-negativity:
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)\geq 0
for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and

(ii)

Normalization: the total mass equals one,

\mathrm{mass}\left({\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}\right)_{\Sigma^{*}}=\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{*}}{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)=1.

Intuitively, a CRA represents a stochastic language when its register updates behave like probability-preserving transitions and the total accumulated weight over all strings equals 1.
In what follows, we chart the boundary between decidable and undecidable instances of this problem.

4.2
Linear Cost Register Automata

To recover decidability, we focus on a restricted and well-behaved fragment of cost register automata.
First, to guarantee non-negativity of register valuations, we define the automaton over the positive semiring

(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},+,\times,0,1)
only, ensuring that all intermediate and output values remain non-negative.
Second, we constrain both register updates and output expressions to be
affine
, that is, linear transformations combined with additive constants.
The analysis of affine CRAs can be further simplified using a standard linearization technique:

Lemma 1

The class of functions computable by CRAs with linear updates coincide with the class of functions computable by CRAs with affine updates.

Proof

(Sketch)

Let
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,X,\delta,\rho,q_{0},\mu)
be a CRA with affine updates of the form
x\mapsto A_{q,\sigma}x+b_{q,\sigma}
.
We add a new register
x_{0}
that always holds
1
.
Replacing each affine update by the linear map

(x,x_{0})\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}A_{q,\sigma}&b_{q,\sigma}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}(x,x_{0})

and output by
\mu^{\prime}(x,x_{0})=[c_{q}^{\top}\;d_{q}](x,x_{0})

preserves semantics. ∎

These restrictions yield the class of
linear CRAs
, which is placed within a well-studied computational model whose dynamics can be represented by systems of linear transformations over the non-negative reals. Linear CRAs admit an exact correspondence with weighted automata:

Theorem 4.3
(Alur et al.
[
1
]
)

The class of functions computed by CRAs with linear updates
coincides with those computed by weighted automata.

The key idea is that a linear CRA with
k
registers and
m
states can be converted to an equivalent weighted automaton with
m\cdot k
states by treating each register-value pair as a state, with transitions derived from the linear update functions. This equivalence immediately yields numerous closure properties and normal forms for linear CRAs.
We can now prove our first main result:

Theorem 4.4

Given a CRA
\mathcal{A}
over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with affine register updates and affine output function, the total mass
\mathrm{mass}\left({\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}\right)_{\Sigma^{*}}=\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{*}}{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)

By Lemma
1
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{A}
has linear updates. By Theorem

\mathcal{A}
is equivalent to a weighted automaton. Let
\mathcal{W}
be its

version (containing only states that are both reachable from an initial state and co-reachable from a final state), with
n

Q=\{1,\ldots,n\}

Let
M_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times n}
be the transition matrix for symbol
\sigma\in\Sigma

(M_{\sigma})_{i,j}
is the weight of transitioning from state
i
to state
j

\sigma
. Let
\iota\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
be the initial weight vector and
c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}

Define the transition matrix
M=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}M_{\sigma}
. The trimmed automaton
\mathcal{W}
is strongly connected by construction, and hence

a nonnegative irreducible matrix. The total weight of all strings of length

is given by
c^{\top}M^{k}\iota
. Therefore, the total mass is:

\mathrm{mass}\left({\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}\right)_{\Sigma^{*}}=\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{*}}{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)={c}^{\top}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M^{k}\right){\iota}.

This Neumann series converges if and only if the spectral radius
\rho(M)<1

[

.

•

If

\mathrm{mass}\left({\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}\right)_{\Sigma^{*}}
diverges.

•

If
\rho(M)<1
, then
\mathrm{mass}\left({\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}\right)_{\Sigma^{*}}
converges to
{c}^{\top}(I-M)^{-1}{\iota}
.

The computation involves computing
\rho(M)

O((|Q|\cdot|X|)^{3})
time, and if the spectral radius
\rho(B)<1

(I-M)x=\iota
via Gaussian elimination in
O((|Q|\cdot|X|)^{3})
time. Thus, the total mass is computable in polynomial time in the size of the input CRA
\mathcal{A}
. ∎

5
Localizable Stochasticity and Sub-stochastic Cost

The decidability result in Theorem
4.4
establishes that the total mass
of a linear CRA is computable whenever the combined transition
matrix
M=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}M_{\sigma}
is
irreducible and has spectral radius
\rho(M)<1
.

In contrast, classical probabilistic models enforce local properties: each
state’s outgoing weights sum to
1
, guaranteeing that the semantics forms
a probability distribution without the need for a spectral analysis
[
10
]
.

In this section, we show that every finite-mass weighted automaton
(and hence every CRA with affine updates defining a stochastic language)
admits an equivalent representation in which stochasticity is
localizable
.

5.1
Locally Sub-stochastic Weighted Automata

Formally, we show that any WFA with only reachable and co-reachable states and
\rho(M)<1

can be normalized so that the outgoing weights from each state sum to at most
1
.
We call this property
local sub-stochasticity
.
Such normalization can be achieved by a similarity transform
based on the Perron–Frobenius eigenvector,
and it preserves the global semantics of the automaton.

Proposition 1
(Perron–Frobenius normalization)

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times n}
be an irreducible matrix with spectral radius
\rho(A)<1
.
Then there exists a positive diagonal matrix
D=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
with

d_{i}>0
such that
B=DAD^{-1}
is strictly row sub-stochastic.

Proof
(Sketch)

By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, there exists a unique (up to scaling) positive eigenvector

v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
Av=\rho(A)v
. Since
\rho(A)<1
, we have
Av<v
component-wise.
Let
D=\mathrm{diag}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
and define
B=DAD^{-1}
. Then for each row
i
,

\sum_{j}B_{ij}=\sum_{j}\frac{v_{i}}{v_{j}}A_{ij}=\frac{(Av)_{i}}{v_{i}}\leq 1,

as required. As
\rho(A)<1
, for some row
i
,
\sum_{j}B_{ij}<1
. ∎

Given a WFA
\mathcal{W}=(\Sigma,Q,\lambda,\{M_{\sigma}\},\mu)
, define

M=\sum_{\sigma}M_{\sigma}
. Applying Theorem
1
to
M
yields a
diagonal matrix
D
such that the similarity transform
M^{\prime}=DMD^{-1}
is row-substochastic.
We then define a
normalised automaton

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=(\Sigma,Q,\lambda^{\prime},\{M^{\prime}_{\sigma}\},\mu^{\prime})
with

M^{\prime}_{\sigma}=DM_{\sigma}D^{-1},\qquad\lambda^{\prime}=D^{-1}\lambda,\qquad\mu^{\prime}=D\mu.

The semantics of
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
satisfies

\llbracket\mathcal{W}^{\prime}\rrbracket(w)=\llbracket\mathcal{W}\rrbracket(w)
for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
,
since each path weight is invariant under this rescaling.
Moreover, every row of
M^{\prime}
now sums to at most
1
, hence the automaton is

locally sub-stochastic
.

4
illustrates this normalization for the running example.

Theorem 5.1
(Local Sub-stochastic Characterisation)

A quantitative language
f
belongs to the class of stochastic rational languages

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}^{rat}(\Sigma^{*})
if and only if there exists a
locally sub-stochastic
weighted automaton

\mathcal{W}
such that
{\llbracket\mathcal{W}\rrbracket}=f
.

The notion of rational stochastic languages has been investigated in several equivalent frameworks,
including probabilistic automata and rational series over the non-negative reals
(
[
10
,
22
]
).
The local sub-stochastic normalization bridges these perspectives,
revealing that the analytic convergence condition
\rho(M)<1

is equivalent to the existence of a locally normalizable form.

5.2
Stochastic Regular Expressions

This local sub-stochasticity enables a Kleene-Schützenberger
characterization of rational stochastic languages.
We introduce
stochastic regular expressions
(SREs), which extend ordinary regular
expressions with
a probabilistic choice and discounted iteration operators.
SREs capture exactly the class of quantitative languages definable by a sub-stochastic weighted automata.

Definition 6
(Syntax of Stochastic Regular Expressions)

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet. The grammar of SREs is

r::=\delta_{\sigma}\mid\alpha r_{1}+(1-\alpha)r_{2}\mid r_{1}\cdot r_{2}\mid r^{*}_{\alpha}

where
\sigma\in\Sigma
,
r_{1},r_{2}
are SREs, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Here
\delta_{\sigma}
denotes the Dirac distribution concentrated on
\sigma
(see Example
2
), the operator

\alpha r_{1}+(1-\alpha)r_{2}
represents convex combination,
r_{1}\cdot r_{2}
denotes the
Cauchy product (probabilistic concatenation), and
r^{*}_{\alpha}
denotes a
discounted Kleene star
, where the number of repetitions follows a
shifted geometric distribution with parameter
\alpha
(see Definition
3
). We can now prove our characterisation theorem:

Theorem 5.2
(Kleene-Schützenberger Characterisation of Stochastic Regular Languages)

The class of stochastic regular languages, denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}^{rat}(\Sigma^{*})
, is the smallest class of
quantitative languages over
\Sigma
that contains all Dirac distributions

\{\delta_{\sigma}\mid\sigma\in\Sigma\}
and is closed under convex combinations, Cauchy products, and discounted Kleene star.

The proof follows by constructing equivalences between locally sub-stochastic weighted automata and stochastic regular expressions (SREs). It parallels the construction between nondeterministic automata and regular expressions.

Lemma 2

Every language defined by a
locally sub-stochastic weighted automaton can be represented by a Stochastic Regular
Expression (SRE).

Proof
(Sketch)

We adapt the standard state-elimination construction, exploiting that local sub-stochasticity ensures all geometric
series that appear during elimination converge.

Given a normalized weighted automaton

\mathcal{W}=(\Sigma,Q,\lambda,T,\mu)
, augment it with a new start state
q_{s}
and final
state
q_{f}
, connected by weighted
\varepsilon
-transitions according to
\lambda
and
\mu
.
Then iteratively eliminate all intermediate states
q_{k}\in Q
, replacing paths

q_{i}\to q_{j}
that pass through
q_{k}
by a single edge labelled with the expression

R_{ij}=T(q_{i},\sigma,q_{k})\,\delta_{\sigma}\cdot\Bigl(\sum_{\sigma}\tfrac{T(q_{k},\sigma,q_{k})}{W_{k}}\delta_{\sigma}\Bigr)^{*}_{1-T(q_{k},\Sigma,q_{k})}\cdot R_{kj},

where
W_{k}
is the total outgoing weight from
q_{k}
.
Because
T(q_{k},\Sigma,q_{k})<1
, every such geometric expansion is well defined.
After all eliminations, the remaining edge
q_{s}\to q_{f}
is labeled with an SRE
r

satisfying
\llbracket r\rrbracket=\llbracket\mathcal{W}\rrbracket
. ∎

Note that the size of the SRE can be exponential in the size of the weighted automaton
[
24
]
. Conversely:

Lemma 3

Every SRE
r
defines a stochastic language that can be realized by a
locally
stochastic weighted automaton
, that is, for every state, the sum of weights of outgoing transitions is exactly
1
. ∎

Proof

The proof proceeds by structural induction on
r
.
For
r=\delta_{\sigma}
, define the two-state automaton

\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}=(\Sigma,\{q_{i},q_{f}\},\lambda,T,\mu)
with

\lambda(q_{i})=1
,
T(q_{i},\sigma,q_{f})=1
, and
\mu(q_{f})=1
.
Clearly
\sum_{w}\llbracket\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}\rrbracket(w)=1
. For the operators:

1.

(Convex Combination) For
r=\alpha r_{1}+(1-\alpha)r_{2}
, given automata
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}
for
r_{1},r_{2}
on disjoint
state sets, scale the initial weights of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
by
\alpha
and
(1-\alpha)
,
respectively. Linearity of semantics yields

\sum_{w}\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket(w)=1
.

2.

(Cauchy Product) For
r=r_{1}\cdot r_{2}
, connect every final state of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
to every initial state of

\mathcal{A}_{2}
by
\varepsilon
-transitions weighted by
\mu_{1}(p)\lambda_{2}(q)
.
This realizes the Cauchy product of distributions and preserves total mass.

3.

(Discounted Kleene Star)
For
r=(r_{1})^{*}_{\alpha}
, introduce
discount factor
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{1}
realize
r_{1}
.
Each repetition contributes a multiplicative
(1-\alpha)
factor, and termination
contributes
\alpha
. Thus

\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{+}}\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket(w)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{k-1}\Bigl(\sum_{w}\llbracket\mathcal{A}_{1}\rrbracket(w)\Bigr)^{k}=1.

By construction, the total mass of the automaton is exactly
1
and for every state, the sum of weights of outgoing transitions is exactly
1
. ∎

Together, these two lemmas complete the proof for Theorem
5.2
. Intuitively, the nested discounted stars correspond to loops in the automaton whose expected number of traversals follows a geometric law. For our running example, the stochastic regular expression is:

\left(\left(\frac{1}{4}a+\frac{1}{4}b+\frac{1}{4}c\left(\frac{2}{3}a+\frac{1}{3}b\right)^{*}_{\frac{1}{2}}c\right)^{*}_{\frac{2}{3}}(a)^{*}_{\frac{3}{4}}a\right)c\left(\frac{2}{3}a+\frac{1}{3}b\right)^{*}_{\frac{1}{2}}

In summary, Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
provide a local characterisation of probabilistic behaviour while preserving global convergence semantics.

6
Approximation and Statistical Inference for Stochastic Languages

In this section, we move beyond exact
characterization to the
quantitative
analysis of stochastic languages:
their approximation, sampling, and learnability. We also introduce a minimal
testing framework that connects these ideas to classical distribution testing
in statistics.

Universal Approximation.

We begin by showing that stochastic regular languages (
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}^{rat}(\Sigma^{*})
) are dense
in the space of all stochastic languages (
\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
) under the
\ell_{1}
(total variation) metric. This follows
from the simple observation that any distribution over
\Sigma^{+}
can be
approximated arbitrarily well by truncating its support.

Theorem 6.1
(Universal Approximation)

For every
f\in\mathcal{S}(\Sigma^{*})
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

r\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}^{rat}(\Sigma^{*})
such that
\|f-r\|_{1}<\varepsilon
.

Proof

Since
f
is a probability distribution over the countable set
\Sigma^{*}
,
choose a finite
S\subseteq\Sigma^{+}
such that
\sum_{w\in S}f(w)>1-\varepsilon/2
. Define
r_{S}
as the normalized restriction of
f
to
S
:

r_{S}(w)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{f(w)}{f(S)}&\text{if }w\in S,\\[4.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
r_{S}
is a stochastic language with finite support and therefore

r_{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}^{rat}(\Sigma^{*})
. Moreover,

\|f-r_{S}\|_{1}=2(1-f(S))<\varepsilon
. ∎

Hence
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}^{rat}(\Sigma^{*})
is dense in
\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
under
\ell_{1}
(and therefore also
under
\ell_{\infty}
). Intuitively, the convex hull of Dirac distributions

\{\delta_{w}\mid w\in\Sigma^{+}\}
suffices to approximate any stochastic
language arbitrarily closely. This motivates a quantitative analogue of
classical automata minimization.

Problem 2
(Minimal-State
\varepsilon
-Approximation)

Given
r\in\mathcal{S}(\Sigma^{*})
and
\varepsilon>0
, determine the minimal

k\in\mathbb{N}
for which there exists a
k
-state weighted automaton

\mathcal{A}
satisfying

\|r-\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon
.

When
\varepsilon=0
and
r
is rational, the minimal
k
coincides with the
Hankel rank of
r
, computable via standard spectral methods
(e.g.
[
14
,
31
,
21
]
).
For
\varepsilon>0
, Problem
2
becomes that of computing an

approximate Hankel rank
, a continuous relaxation of minimality that
remains open in general.

Sampling from Stochastic Regular Expressions.

Stochastic regular expressions (SREs) admit exact generative sampling
procedures aligned with their semantics. Sampling proceeds recursively as
follows:

(a)

Atomic symbol: For
r=\delta_{\sigma}
, output
\sigma
.

(b)

Convex combination: For

r=\alpha r_{1}+(1-\alpha)r_{2}
, sample

b\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\alpha)
and recurse into
r_{b}
.

(c)

Concatenation: For
r=r_{1}\cdot r_{2}
, sample

w_{1}\sim r_{1}
and
w_{2}\sim r_{2}
, and output
w_{1}w_{2}
.

(d)

Discounted star: For
r=r^{*}_{\alpha}
, draw

k\sim\mathrm{Geom}(\alpha)
and concatenate

w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
sampled independently from
r
.

The expected length of a sample from
r^{*}_{\alpha}
is
(1-\alpha)/\alpha
, hence
finite for
\alpha>0
. Each operator preserves independence and normalization,
ensuring that the sampling process exactly realizes

\llbracket r\rrbracket
.

Learnability.

The density result of Theorem
6.1
implies that
stochastic languages can be approximated by finite-parameter models, suggesting
learnability from data. Two settings naturally arise:

1.

Given i.i.d. samples from an unknown
r\in\mathcal{S}(\Sigma^{*})
, one can fit a weighted automaton by empirical estimation of Hankel moments, following
the spectral learning paradigm for rational series

[
5
,
21
]
.
Sample complexity bounds follow from standard concentration inequalities for
low-rank moment matrices.

2.

If an oracle provides evaluation access to
\llbracket r\rrbracket(w)
for
queries
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, the parameters of a linear CRA or weighted automaton
can be recovered by solving a finite system of linear equations
[
2
]
. Under regularity assumptions, this system is solvable in
polynomial time.

Distribution Testing.

Finally, we consider the problem of distribution testing:

Problem 3
(Distribution Testing)

Given
Q\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}^{rat}(\Sigma^{*})
and sample access to
P\in\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
, design a
randomized algorithm that, for tolerance
\varepsilon>0
and confidence

\delta\in(0,1)
, satisfies:

\begin{split}\text{Accept},\quad&\|P-Q\|_{1}<\varepsilon,\\
\text{Reject},\quad&\|P-Q\|_{1}>\varepsilon,\end{split}

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

This extends the classical problem on finite domains
[
6
,
13
]
. A naive solution proceeds by a transforming it to the finite-support case by
truncating the distribution tail. Let
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest
length such that
\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{\leq\theta}}Q(w)>1-\varepsilon/3
.
Construct the restricted distributions
P_{\leq\theta}
and
Q_{\leq\theta}
on

\Sigma^{\leq\theta}
and apply any finite-domain tolerant tester
(e.g.
[
13
]
) with thresholds
(\varepsilon/3,\varepsilon)
.
This yields correctness guarantees identical to the finite setting, with
sample complexity
\widetilde{\Theta}(|\Sigma|^{\frac{\theta+1}{2}}/\varepsilon^{2})
.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we advance the theory of stochastic languages through the lens of sub-stochastic weighted automata, establishing fundamental computational boundaries for quantitative automata models. Our main contributions are:

1.

We show that determining whether a cost register automaton defines a stochastic language is undecidable in general, even for affine register updates (Theorem
4.1
) and the fully polynomial fragment (Theorem
4.2
). This holds for finite mass, non-negativity, and exact normalization.

2.

By restricting to cost register automata over non-negative reals with affine updates, we recover decidability and efficient computation. For this fragment, expressively equivalent to weighted finite automata, the total mass is computable in polynomial time via spectral analysis (Theorem
4.4
).

3.

We establish that every stochastic weighted automaton admits a semantically equivalent
sub-stochastic
representation via
Perron-Frobenius normalization
(Theorem
5.1
). This local characterization mirrors classical probabilistic automata, enabling convergence verification syntactically, without global spectral analysis.

4.

We introduce
stochastic regular expressions
(SREs) as a compositional, probabilistic analogue of regular expressions and provide a Kleene-Schützenberger characterization for rational stochastic languages (Theorem
5.2
).

Open Problems and Future Directions

Despite these advances, several fundamental questions remain open. We highlight three directions where sub-stochastic representations could play a decisive role:

1.

Decidability Beyond Linear Updates. (Problem
1
)
Can syntactic conditions on non-linear cost register automata ensure stochasticity decidability? One can look at functions
f
that satisfy
\|f(x)-f(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|
with
L<1
. Banach’s fixed-point theorem guarantees convergence to a unique fixed point. For CRAs with contracting updates, we can explore if stochasticity is decidable and if spectral methods extend via linearization around fixed points? More generally, polynomial dynamical systems with well-understood asymptotics may admit decidable fragments. Investigating
semantic classes
of well-behaved CRAs could significantly expand the applicability of sub-stochastic methods. Approximate notions of stochasticity, e.g.,
\varepsilon
-closeness, may suffice for practical purposes and connect to numerical optimization.

2.

Complexity of Minimal-State
\varepsilon
-Approximation (Problem
2
).
Determining the minimal number of states to
\varepsilon
-approximate a stochastic language is a central open question. This connects to low-rank matrix approximation and non-negative factorization. Approximate minimal-state representations determine sample complexity in learning stochastic languages and illuminate the intrinsic complexity of probabilistic sequential models.

3.

Distribution Testing (Problem
3
).
Efficient, instance-optimal testing algorithms are needed for stochastic languages, particularly for validating large language models. Some important questions are: Can we exploit local sub-stochastic properties of distributions for efficient testing? Can we use these methods to perform efficient black-box testing for language models? This problem bridges theory and practice, providing foundations for
trustworthy AI
and enabling rigorous, sample-efficient validation of probabilistic generative models.

In conclusion, our work establishes the connection between stochastic languages and their sub-stochastic representations. As probabilistic models become central to computing, formal methods to specify, analyse, and verify their distributional behaviour are increasingly urgent. Viewing stochastic languages through sub-stochastic models offers a principled foundation, laying the groundwork for a formal
theory of probabilistic computation
.
