Pre-harvest bagging modulates fruit traits, phytochemical profiles and disease incidence in mango (Mangifera indica L.) cv. Mallika
Authors: Ishu Kumari, Ashok Kumar Singh, Dinesh Chandra Dimri, Krishna Pratap Singh, Viveka Nand, Jitendra Singh Shivran, Kumar Gaurav, Divya, Saurabh Gangola, Debasis Mitra, Sourav Chattaraj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluates the impact of pre-harvest bagging on yield, quality, shelf life, disease incidence, and economic feasibility in the mango (Mangifera indica L.) cultivar Mallika conducted at the Horticulture Research Center, Pantnagar. The experiment assessed six treatments using various bagging materials (brown paper, white paper, ordinary brown paper, butter paper, newspaper, and control). Pre-harvest bagging significantly enhanced fruit attributes, with organic/UV/water-resistant brown paper yielding the highest fruit weight (630.08 g), pulp weight (530.89 g), and TSS (22.67 °Brix). It also improved ascorbic acid content (41.53 mg/100 g) and total sugars (14.64%). Phytochemical composition analysis revealed a notable increase in carotenoids, flavonoids, and phenolic content with brown paper bag (Organic/ UV/ water resistant) and white paper bag (Organic/ UV/ water resistant), indicating improved nutritional quality. Fruits bagged with brown paper extended shelf life (11.67 days) and reduced incidence of major post-harvest diseases such as anthracnose, stem-end rot, and sooty mould. Economic analysis revealed a 53.18% higher net return and improved benefit-cost ratio with bagging compared to unbagged fruits. Correlation studies highlighted positive relationships between yield, fruit weight, and quality traits, while disease resistance was negatively correlated with fruit quality attributes revealed the effectiveness of this eco-friendly practice. PCA revealed that bagging techniques enhance mango fruit quality and reduce post-harvest diseases, with PC1 explaining most of the variation. This research demonstrates that pre-harvest bagging, particularly with organic/UV/water-resistant brown paper, offers a sustainable and profitable solution for enhancing mango quality, reducing pesticide usage, and meeting global market demands for high-quality, residue-free fruits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27233-0.Keywords: Disease incidence, Mangifera, Phytochemical, Pre-harvest bagging, Shelf life, YieldSubject terms: Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Mango (Mangifera indica L.), often celebrated as the “King of Fruits,” holds significant nutritional and economic importance across tropical and subtropical regions1. The mango fruit is a vital source of micronutrients, vitamins, dietary fiber, and bioactive compounds essential for human health2. India is the largest mango producer globally and contributes significantly to the country’s horticultural economy. The crop is cultivated on over 2.40 million hectares, yielding approximately 22.40 million MT annually, with an average productivity of 9.35 t/ha. In Uttarakhand, mango cultivation is primarily restricted to subtropical zones, covering an area of 21.14 thousand hectares and producing 112.09 thousand MT of fruit annually3. Between April and August in the fiscal year 2023–24, India exported 27.33 thousand MT of mangoes, earning 47.98 million USD, reflecting a 19% increase over the previous year4. Mango exports are primarily categorized into three types: fresh mangoes, mango pulp, and mango slices5. To strengthen India’s global market presence under the WTO framework, there is an urgent need to enhance both the quality and productivity of mangoes. Despite its economic significance, mango production faces several challenges. Abiotic stresses negatively impact fruit development and quality, including sudden temperature fluctuations, irregular rainfall, and hailstorms. Biotic stresses, such as mango hoppers, fruit flies, and borers infestations, as well as diseases like anthracnose, stem-end rot, and sooty mould, further reduce yield, quality, and marketability6. These issues compel farmers to rely heavily on chemical pesticides, often exceeding recommended application rates, raising concerns about environmental sustainability and human health7. To address these challenges, sustainable horticultural practices have gained prominence8. However, these solutions are not always cost-effective and come with certain limitations. To mitigate the losses caused by biotic and abiotic factors, the adoption of good agricultural practices is gaining traction worldwide. This shift is driven by increasing consumer concerns regarding synthetic agrochemicals and heightened environmental awareness. Consequently, the development of alternative approaches to improve fruit quality and appearance while simultaneously reducing disease and insect infestations has become imperative9.

Pre-harvest bagging has proven to be an effective technique for improving fruit quality while minimizing pesticide residues. Pre-harvest bagging acts as a mechanical barrier that reduces direct contact between the developing fruit and pests such as mango fruit flies, hoppers, and sooty mould vectors. This barrier significantly lowers pest incidence and disease transmission, minimizing the need for chemical sprays. Additionally, by modifying the microenvironment (light, temperature, and humidity), bagging deters both oviposition and fungal spore germination, offering a pesticide-free alternative for fruit protection. The protective mechanism of bagging involves microenvironment modification through light filtration, temperature regulation, and moisture retention, which influence fruit ripening, sugar accumulation, and disease suppression. Bagging materials, such as UV-resistant paper, allow optimal sunlight transmission for carotenoid and anthocyanin biosynthesis, while restricting excessive radiation and fungal colonization10–12. The present study focuses on assessing the impact of pre-harvest bagging on the yield, quality, shelf life, and disease resistance of mango cv. Mallika. By examining the effects of different bagging material, the study aims to provide valuable insights into sustainable agricultural practices that can improve mango production, profitability, and environmental sustainability.

Materials and methods

Experimental site description and soil type

The field experiment was carried out during 2022–23 at Horticulture Research Center, Patharchatta, Govind Ballabh Pant University of Agriculture and Technology, Pantnagar, Uttarakhand, India. The experimental site is situated at the foothills of the Shivalik range of the Himalayas in Pantnagar, Udham Singh Nagar, Uttarakhand. Pantnagar is positioned geographically at 29.0º North latitude and 79.30º East longitude, with an elevation of about 243.84 m above mean sea level (Fig. 1). Climatic parameters during the experimental period (May–June 2022), including temperature, relative humidity, rainfall, rainy days, sunshine hours, wind velocity, and evaporation, were recorded at the meteorological observatory of the Norman E. Borlaug Crop Research Centre, Pantnagar. The instruments used were: a Thermometer (Mercury Thermometer, Zeal International, India) for air temperature, a Hygrometer (Digital Hygrometer, Vaisala, Finland) for relative humidity, a Rain Gauge/Pluviometer (Symons Rain Gauge, IMD (India Meteorological Department), India) for rainfall, an Anemometer (Cup Anemometer, Vector Instruments, United Kingdom) for wind speed, a Sunshine Recorder (Campbell–Stokes, Casella London, United Kingdom) for sunshine duration, and a Class A Pan Evaporimeter (Class A Pan (120 cm), ISI Standard/IMD Supply, India) for evaporation. All instruments are routinely maintained and calibrated by the institute. With little rainfall in the experimental year, the mean fortnightly minimum and maximum temperatures were reported as 23.15 °C and 39.10 °C, respectively (Table S1). The soil at the experimental site belongs to the Patharchatta sandy loam series (series VI), classified under the Mollisol order13 and the physical and chemical characteristics of the soil are presented in Table S2.

Experimental setup

During the investigation, healthy and uniform-sized fruits (60 bags per treatment) of 15-year-old mango cv. Mallika trees were covered with organic and biodegradable bags. Fruits were bagged 45 days before the anticipated harvest date (at the mature green stage) as it is optimal for enhancing fruit development and reducing susceptibility to diseases and pests during the critical maturation stage14. A randomized block design with six treatments and four replications was employed to account for spatial variability withinthe orchard. Treatment T6 includes unbagged fruit (i.e., control), while T1 to T5 represent different bagging materials (Fig. 2). The organic/UV/water-resistant paper bags were double-layered, with the outer layer made of breathable paper and an inner black film composed of biodegradable bio-polymer coating (not polyethylene), thus ensuring compatibility with organic farming standards. These were procured from Diyaan Agritech, a certified vendor for organic packaging solutions (www.diyaanagritech.com), whereas ordinary brown paper bag, butter paper bag and newspaper bag were purchased from the local shops.

Study of parameters

Physical attributes and yield

The fresh weight and pulp weight (g) of the fruits were determined immediately after harvesting using an electronic balance (Model BL-220 H, Shimadzu, Japan). Fruit length and diameter (cm) were measured for each treatment using a digital Vernier caliper (Model CD-15CPX, Mitutoyo, Japan). The pulp-to-stone ratio was determined by dividing the mango pulp weight by the corresponding stone weight. Fruit volume (mL) was determined by the water displacement method using a volumetric flask, and specific gravity was calculated as the ratio of fruit weight in air to its volume (15,16).The yield per hectare (t/ha) was calculated by multiplying the fruit yield per tree by the total number of trees per hectare.

Biochemical properties

All biochemical parameters were assessed after ripening during postharvest storage to reflect the fruit quality at consumer stage, rather than at harvest. Total soluble solids (TSS, ºBrix) were determined using Erma Hand Refractometer, Model N-1E, Erma Optical, Japan (0–32 ºB range), works based on the principle of total refraction17. Titratable acidity, expressed as a percentage (%) of citric acid18,was calculated using the method proposed by17. For this, 10 mL of juice was transferred into a 100 mL volumetric flask, to which 2–3 drops of phenolphthalein indicator were added. The sample was subsequently titrated with 0.1 N NaOH until a light pink endpoint was achieved19. Ascorbic acid (AA) content was quantified using the 2,6-dichloroindophenol titration method20, and the results were expressed as grams of AA per 100 g of fresh weight (mg/100 g FW)21. The total and reducing sugars (%) were analyzed using the22method as described by17. For reducing sugars, pulp extract was prepared by homogenization, dilution, neutralization, clarification with lead acetate and potassium oxalate, and subsequent filtration. For total sugars, an aliquot of the same extract was hydrolyzed with HCl, incubated at room temperature followed by heating at 70–80°Cusing heating water bath (Model WB-22, REMI Instruments, India), neutralized with sodium carbonate, and diluted to volume prior to analysis23.

Phytochemical composition

All phytochemical quality attributes were evaluated after the fruits had ripened during postharvest storage, in order to represent quality at the consumer stage rather than at harvest. Total carotenoids (mg/100 g FW) were measured using a modified method described by17. Flavonoid content was measured in fresh fruit using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method24, with absorbance at 510 nm, and expressed as mg quercetin equivalents (QE) per 100 g FW25. Anthocyanin concentration (mg/100 g FW) was determined using a calorimetric method outlined by17. Although mango cv. Mallika is not typically red-colored but anthocyanin accumulation may occur under light stress or UV exposure in the peel, particularly in bagged fruits with partial translucency. These pigments may contribute to antioxidant potential and are thus measured for nutritional evaluation. Total phenolic content in the fruits was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent method as outlined by26. A standard calibration curve was constructed with gallic acid, and the results were expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (mg GAE) per 100 g of pulp fresh weight (FW)27. The carbohydrate content (%) in the fruit was estimated using the anthrone method described by28. The total protein content of mango fruit was quantified following the method of29, employing bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard, and expressed as mg per 100 g of fresh weight (FW)30. A UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Model UV-1900i, Shimadzu, Japan; wavelength range 190–1100 nm) was used for the quantification of phytochemical composition of attributes.

Fruit colour and shelf life

Fruit color is typically assessed by comparing the fruit to the Royal Horticultural Society (RHS) Colour Chart cards. Fruits were stored under ambient room conditions (28–32 °C temperature and 60–75% RH) without use of refrigeration. No secondary packaging or chemical treatment was applied. Four separatebatches (each containing5 fruits) were preparedto observe shelf life and diseases parameters. The shelf life (in days) refers to the duration from harvest until the fruit is no longer edible or saleable. Shelf life was evaluated based on marketable quality retention until visible signs of decay or shriveling. Disease incidence was recorded as a separate biotic parameter, extending observations beyond the end of marketable life to capture delayed disease expression.

Disease incidence and indexing

The disease occurrence and severity of anthracnose, stem end rot and sooty mould were recorded 9, 12, 15 and 18 days after harvest. The occurrence of diseases such as sooty mould was documented on 9th day, while anthracnose and stem end rot were recorded at 12th days after harvest. Visual assessments were performed to identify disease symptoms, and the incidence percentage was determined by counting the number of affected fruits.

The disease severity was documented at 9th day for anthracnose and 12th day for stem end rot and sooty mould after harvest. The disease index, representing the extent of disease based on the affected area or volume of fruit tissue, was calculated following the formula described by31.

Scoring Mango diseases

Disease rating for major postharvest diseases of mango were observed using following rating scale32. A rating scale was used to assess the severity of diseases on mango fruits based on the percentage of the fruit area affected. The scale ranged from 0 to 5, where grade 0 indicated no infection. Grade 1 was assigned to fruits with less than 5% of the area affected, while grade 2 corresponded to 6–10% infection. Fruits with 11–20% of the area affected were classified as grade 3, whereas those with 21–50% infection were given grade 4. Finally, grade 5 was designated for fruits with more than 50% of the area affected.

Economics

The cost of cultivation for each treatment was determined by accounting the cost incurred on horticultural operations along with the total cost of bags. The cost per bag was ₹ 2.6 for brown paper bag (Organic/UV/water resistant) and white paper bag (Organic/UV/water resistant), ₹ 1.0 per for ordinary brown paper bag, ₹ 1.2 for butter paper bag and ₹ 0.8 for newspaper bag.

The yield from each bagging treatment was converted into gross returns (₹) based on the current local market prices. The selling price of fruits was ₹ 50 in brown paper bag (Organic/UV/water resistant), ₹ 45 in white paper bag (Organic/UV/Water resistant), ₹ 40 in ordinary brown paper bag and butter paper bag, ₹ 30 in newspaper bag and ₹ 25 in case of control. The net return for each treatment was calculated by subtracting the cost of cultivation from the gross return. The benefit-cost ratio for each treatment was then determined by dividing the net return by the total cost of cultivation.

Statistical analysis

The significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05, ensuring results reflect statistically meaningful differences. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at p ≤ 0.0534. The Pearson correlation coefficient matrix analysis of various variables was performed using Origin Pro 8.1 to evaluate the relationships between different parameters35. Principalcomponent analysis was determined using the ‘R’ Statistical Software36.

Results and discussion

Effect of different bagging materials on the fruit physical attributes and yield

The application of pre-harvest bagging significantly improved the physical attributes of mango fruits. Among the various treatments, mango fruits enclosed in brown paper (organic/UV/water-resistant) bags exhibited the highest fruit weight (630.08 g), pulp weight (530.89 g), and fruit volume (591.83 ml), outperforming the control fruits, which recorded 572.14 g, 473.45 g, and 521.17 ml, respectively (Table 1).Additionally, bagged fruits displayed superior dimensions, achieving maximum fruit length and diameter of 16.34 cm and 8.35 cm, respectively, compared to 14.34 cm and 7.45 cm observed in non-bagged fruits (Fig. 3). While parameters such as the pulp-to-stone ratio and specific gravity remained unaffected, the yield of fruits bagged with brown paper (organic/UV/water-resistant) was notably higher (14.70 t ha⁻¹) than the control (13.51 t ha⁻¹).

The enhancement in physical attributes can be ascribed to the regulated microenvironment established by brown paper bags (organic/UV/water-resistant or ordinary), which remained intact until harvest. These bags shielded the fruits from environmental stresses while moderating light intensity, reducing UV exposure, and maintaining favorable temperature and humidity conditions. This favorable microclimate enhances photosynthate allocation to the fruits, promoting better pulp development and fruit growth. These findings corroborate earlier studies by37 in mango, as well as38 in banana and39in litchi. The correlation between pulp weight and overall fruit dimensions underscores the pivotal role of bagging in improving mango fruit development. Enhanced fruit development and overall yield through bagging were also supported by studies from40in pomegranate and41in guava.

Bagged fruits displayed a notable increase in TSS compared to non-bagged fruits. Fruits enclosed in brown paper bags (organic/UV/water-resistant) showed the highest TSS (22.67 °Brix), representing a 19.14% increase over the control fruits (18.33 °Brix). Bagging also led to a significant reduction in titratable acidity, with the greatest decrease (23.53%) observed in fruits bagged with brown paper bags (organic/UV/water-resistant). Similar trends were recorded for sugar content, with a rise of 11.61% in total sugars and 24.24% in reducing sugars in fruits treated with brown paper bags (organic/UV/water-resistant) compared to the control.Additionally, ascorbic acid content was significantly higher in bagged fruits, with an increase of 12.38% recorded in fruits bagged with brown paper bags (organic/UV/water-resistant) compared to the control.

Brown paper bags typically absorb more heat and maintain a slightly warmer microclimate compared to white bags, which reflect more solar radiation and keep the fruit relatively cooler. This temperature modulation affects metabolic processes such as respiration, sugar accumulation, acid degradation, and pigment synthesis, thereby influencing overall fruit quality and ripening dynamics. The favorable microenvironment generated by bagging enhances enzymatic activity, facilitating the conversion of starches and polysaccharides into sugars. Concurrently, the decline in acidity can be attributed to the metabolic transformation of organic acids into sugars and improved carbohydrate translocation. These outcomes are consistent with earlier reports in different fruit crops14,39,42,43. Moreover, optimized temperature and humidity inside the bags support enzymatic reactions in the L-galactose pathway, a critical route for ascorbic acid biosynthesis, while the barrier provided by the bag reduces excessive light exposure and limits ascorbic acid degradation. Similar trends have been documented in mango by14,21,44.

Effect of different bagging treatments on the phytochemical composition of fruits

Phytochemical attributes such as carotenoids, flavonoids, phenolic content, and anthocyanin were significantly influenced by bagging and the type of bagging material used (Table 3). Bagged fruits demonstrated a marked increase in carotenoid content compared to non-bagged fruits. Fruits bagged with brown paper bags (organic/UV/water-resistant) exhibited the highest carotenoid (8.60 mg 100 g⁻¹) content, significantly surpassing the control by 21.63%. This enhancement is attributed to the ability of bagging to regulate internal temperature and allow optimal light filtration which stimulates pigment biosynthesisand prevent UV-induced degradation. These results align with findings by45–47 in mangoes. Interestingly, the white paper bag treatment recorded the highest flavonoid (21.98 mg QE/100 g), anthocyanin (0.047 mg/100 g) and phenolic content (103.20 mg GAE/100 g) compared to the control (17.56 mg QE/100 g, 0.040 mg 100 g⁻¹ and 90.71 mg GAE 100 g⁻¹, respectively). White paper bags promote higher accumulation of anthocyanins, phenolics, and flavonoids in mango by reflecting greater amounts of incident light and UV radiation onto the fruit surface. This enhanced light exposure stimulates the phenylpropanoid pathway through the activation of key enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), chalcone synthase (CHS), and UDP-glucose: flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT), which play central roles in anthocyanin, flavonoid, and phenolic biosynthesis. Moreover, the UV reflectance of white paper acts as a mild abiotic stress, eliciting a defense response that further elevates phenolic compound production. These findings are supported by48–50.

Effect of different bagging treatments on the colour, shelf life and, disease incidence and severity of fruits

Fruit quality attributes such as colour development and shelf life were significantly influenced by bagging and the type of bagging material used (Table 4). Fruits enclosed in brown paper bag (Organic/UV/water resistant) developed a uniform yellow coloration at physiological maturity.In contrast, fruits subjected to other treatments developed only a slight yellow hue, while those in the control group remained green (with blemishes and bruises). Upon ripening, fruits bagged with brown paper bags (organic/UV/water-resistant) exhibited an attractive and uniform dark yellow color (15 A dark yellow), free from blemishes and bruises, while non-bagged fruits displayed a medium yellow color with numerous black spots and poor visual appeal. Bagged fruits developed enhanced colour and uniform ripeness during maturation. This improvement was due to microclimatic modification inside the bags. Bagging also modulates the ripening process by regulating microclimatic factors such as temperature and humidity, which influence enzymatic activities involved in pigment biosynthesis and cell wall softening. The filtered light exposure promotes carotenoid accumulation while reducing chlorophyll retention, contributing to enhanced peel coloration. Additionally, reduced biotic and abiotic stress in bagged fruits leads to more uniform ethylene synthesis and climacteric behavior, resulting in synchronized ripening and consistent internal quality. These mechanisms are consistent with previous physiological findings in mango and other fruit crops11,14. Bagging also extended the shelf life of the fruits, with those enclosed in brown paper bags (organic/UV/water-resistant) remaining in marketable condition for up to 11.67 days under ambient conditions, compared to 7.67 days for non-bagged fruits. The enhanced color development in bagged fruits can be attributed to the ability of the bagging material to regulate light exposure, reducing chlorophyll content and promoting the transition to yellow hues during ripening. Additionally, bagging protects fruits from mechanical damage and pest attacks, thereby minimizing bruising and disease, which contributes to improved shelf life44. These findings are in line with previous reports by52,40, highlighting the role of bagging in reducing decay and maintaining fruit quality.

The incidence, severity, and AUDPC (Area Under Disease Progress Curve) for anthracnose, stem-end rot, and sooty mould in mango cv. Mallika, with varying physical and physiological properties, were evaluated by collecting data 12 days after harvest for anthracnose and stem-end rot, and 9 days after harvest for sooty mould (Figs. 4 and 5). The recorded data revealed marked variations in mango under the given biotic parameters (Table 5). Fruits bagged with brown paper bags (Organic/UV/water resistant) had the lowest incidence, severity, and AUDPC for anthracnose at 12 days post-harvest (13.33%, 8.89%, and 19.99 units, respectively), while the highest values were observed in non-bagged fruits (85.33%, 72.22%, and 198.32 units, respectively). Similarly, the minimum incidence, severity, and AUDPC for stem-end rot were recorded in fruits bagged with brown paper bags (Organic/UV/water resistant) at 12 days after harvest (5.33%, 7.77%, and 14.99 units, respectively), with the maximum values in non-bagged fruits (54.67%, 59.99%, and 168.32 units, respectively). For sooty mould, bagged fruits demonstrated the lowest incidence (5.33%) and severity (5.55%), while non-bagged fruits reported the highest incidence (69.33%) and severity (66.66%). Fruit bagging effectively isolates the fruit from potential sources of infections like fungi, bacteria, and pests, reducing the risk of disease transmission. Additionally, bagging creates a controlled microclimate around the fruit, which can be adjusted to optimize growth conditions and discourage the onset of diseases. Bagging treatments significantly reduced bacterial disease oily spot in pomegranate cv. PhuleBhagwa Super40. Similarly52, documented an 86.7% reduction in decay caused by stem-end rot and anthracnose in pre-harvest bagged mangoes combined with improved postharvest management compared to traditional non-bagged practices.

Effect of different bagging treatments on economics feasibility of fruits

Economics plays a crucial role in research by providing guidance on how resources should be allocated, conducting analyses of costs-benefit analyses, evaluating impacts, and aiding decision-making. From the perspective of farmers, the ultimate objective is to achieve a net profit from their endeavors. Net returns and the benefit-cost (B: C) ratio under different treatments are presented in Fig. 6.

Impact of pre-harvest bagging on the net economic returns from mango cv. ‘Mallika’.Note: Means with different letter within a column shows significant differences (at p ≤ 0.05 of net returns:67527.48, and B: C ratio: 1.25) as per Duncan’s multiple-range test. Error bars represent the standard error.

Fruits bagged with brown paper (Organic/UV/water resistant) bags yielded the highest net return, reaching ₹629,684.30 ha⁻¹. This represented a 53.18% increase compared to non-bagged fruits, which had the lowest net return of ₹294,824.20 ha⁻¹. Higher yield, improved fruit quality and appearance, and reduced incidence of diseases and pests resulted in higher net returns with the brown paper bag (Organic/UV/water resistant). Beyond profitability, the adoption of biodegradable, organic/UV/water-resistant paper bags offer added value for growers aiming to access high-value export markets. These bags comply with organic certification standards, meeting the increasing international demand for residue-free and eco-labeled fruits. Similarly, the B: C ratio was higher in all bagging treatments. The highest B: C ratio (7.72) was observed in fruits treated with ordinary brown paper bags, while the lowest (5.08) was recorded in fruits enclosed in white paper (Organic/UV/water resistant) bags. Compared to non-bagged fruits, the ordinary brown paper bag showed an 11.14% increase in the overall benefit-cost ratio. Considerable variation in the benefit–cost (B: C) ratio was observed across treatments, primarily influenced by differences in cultivation costs arising from the price of bagging materials, while the absence of bagging in the control reduced the overall cultivation expenditure. Variations in yield, market price, gross return, and net return further contributed to these differences. Although the ordinary brown paper bag treatment exhibited the highest B: C ratio, the brown paper (Organic/UV/water resistant) bag demonstrated superior performance in terms of net return, fruit quality, shelf-life extension, and reduction in disease incidence. In addition, it was more resilient under field conditions, withstanding rainfall and remaining intact on fruits until harvest.

Correlation and multivariate analysis of observed physical and chemical attributes of fruits

The relationships among the observed physico-chemical attributes of mango fruits arepresentedin Fig. 7. Several traits exhibited strong positive correlations (indicated in green), indicating that an increase in one trait is associated with an increase in another. Notable positive correlations include fruit weight (FW) and fruit length (FL) (r = 0.994), indicating a very strong relationship. Additionally, higher fruit weight correlates significantly with higher yield (r = 0.991), and FW showed a near-perfect positive correlation with total protein (r = 0.999). Ascorbic acid is also highly correlated with total sugars (TS) (r = 0.995). Higher total carotenoids arepositively associate with higher yield (r = 0.993). Some traits exhibited weak or neutral correlations (indicated in white), indicating minimal or no significant relationship. For example, anthocyanin (A) and ascorbic acid (AA) (r = 0.009) show a very weak positive correlation. Similarly, total sugar (TS) and Anthocyanin (A) (r = 0.007) showed an essentially neutral correlation. A low positive correlation was observed between anthocyanin (A) and total carbohydrate (TCarb) (r = 0.135). Strong negative correlations (shown in red) suggest that an increase in one trait is associated with a decrease in the other. A significant negative correlation was observed between titratable acidity (TA) and total sugars (TS) (r = −0.964) indicating that higher titratable acidity corresponds with lower total sugar levels. Additionally, fruit weight and anthracnose disease incidence (ADI) are negatively correlated (r = −0.913), as are shelf life (SL) and sooty mould disease incidence (SMDI) (r = −0.990), meaning that longer shelf life is associated with lower incidences of sooty mould. A negative correlation was also observed between yield and titratable acidity (r = −0.981).

The correlation analysis revealed significant relationships among various traits, indicating both positive and negative associations that offer insight into trait interactions and potential breeding targets. Key findings include strong positive correlations between fruit weight and fruit length, as well as yield, highlighting that larger fruit dimensions are typically associated with higher yields. The near-perfect positive relationship observed between fruit weight and total protein suggests that heavier fruits tend to have higher protein content, which may be advantageous for nutritional quality. Additionally, the significant correlation between ascorbic acid and total sugar reflects a potential linkage between sweetness and vitamin C content, a desirable quality in fruit breeding. Total carotenoids showed a positive association with yield, suggesting that increased pigment concentration might enhance productivity, possibly due to improved fruit quality. Traits with weak or neutral correlations, such as anthocyanin with ascorbic acid and total sugar, suggestthat pigment levels in anthocyanins may not substantially affect these other quality traits. A low positive correlation between anthocyanin and total carbohydrate indicates only a minimal association, implying that carbohydrate levels might not strongly impact anthocyanin content.

Negative correlation between titratable acidity and total sugar, indicates that higher acidity generally corresponds with lower sweetness, a critical factor in developing the flavor profile. The inverse relationship between fruit weight and anthracnose disease incidence, as well as between shelf life and sooty mould disease incidence, suggests that robust fruit characteristics may confer some natural resistance to disease. Similarly, yield’s negative correlation with titratable acidity implies that higher yield might come at the cost of increased acidity, which could affect fruit flavor. Moreover, disease resistance traitsgenerally show negative correlationswith yield and quality attributes such as fruit weight and total soluble solids, suggesting that enhanced fruit quality may reduce susceptibility to diseases. Nutritional traits including ascorbic acid, total sugar, total carotenoids, and total protein exhibitedstrong inter-correlations, suggesting a cluster of nutritionally valuable traits that could be co-targeted in breeding to improve the fruit’s nutritional profile without compromising yield or disease resistance.

The principal component analysis (PCA) biplot (Fig. 8) effectively illustrates the variation in mango fruit quality and disease incidence/severity parameters under various bagging treatments. The first principal component (PC1), which explains 71.1% of the total variation, is primarily driven by fruit quality attributes such as fruit weight (FW), fruit length (FL), total soluble solids (TSS), total sugar (TS), ascorbic acid (AA), and total carotenoids (TCro). These variables are strongly aligned with PC1, indicating their significant contribution to the differentiation of treatments. In contrast, the second principal component (PC2), accounting for 13.4% of the total variation, highlights moderate contributions from variables such as anthocyanin (A) and total phenol (TP), suggesting a secondary axis of variation related to phenolic and pigment-related traits. The treatments display clear separation along PC1, with the control-T6 (without bagging) showing a distinct position along the positive PC1 axis, indicative of its divergence from the bagging treatments. This positioning reflects the substantial impact of bagging techniques on fruit quality, as T6 lacks the protective influence of bagging. Among the disease-related parameters, anthracnose disease incidence (ADI), anthracnose disease severity (ADS), stem end rot disease incidence (SERDI), and sooty mould disease severity (SMDS) contribute moderately to PC1 and PC2, indicating their role in influencing fruit quality and treatment differentiation. This analysis confirms the pivotal role of bagging techniques in improving mango fruit quality parameters and reducing post-harvest disease incidences, with PC1 serving as a critical axis for evaluating treatment efficacy.

Conclusions

This study confirms and extends previous findings by demonstrating that pre-harvest bagging significantly improves the physico-chemical quality, yield, shelf life, and marketability of mango cv. ‘Mallika’. The results provide strong practical relevance for growers, particularly in terms of enhanced economic returns. For green/yellow cultivars, bagging with organic/UV/water-resistant brown paper is recommended as a cost-effective practice to enhance fruit value with minimal additional inputs, with ordinary brown paper as a suitable alternative. The use of biodegradable and organically compliant bags also supports India’s export ambitions by aligning with international standards for residue-free, environmentally sustainable produce. This practice reduces the need for chemical pesticides, improves shelf life, and lowers post-harvest disease incidence, making it highly suitable for environmentally conscious consumers and export markets. Therefore, pre-harvest bagging is not only an effective horticultural technique for improving mango fruit quality but also a strategic intervention for sustainable, profitable, and market-driven mango cultivation. However, the study was limited to a single cultivar and location, restricting broader applicability. Future research should evaluate diverse cultivars, climatic zones, and long-term environmental impacts. Comparative analyses with alternative bagging materials and integration with other eco-friendly practices could further optimize quality, profitability, and sustainability in mango production.
