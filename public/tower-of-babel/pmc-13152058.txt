INTRODUCTION

In 1963, Hardy et al. performed the first human lung transplant on a 58-year-old man who survived only 18 days [1]. Meaningful progress was achieved in the 1980s, and the first successful pediatric lung transplant was performed in 1987. Over time, pediatric lung transplantation has advanced substantially to become a viable, life-saving treatment for children with end-stage lung failure. Improvements in surgical techniques, enhanced organ preservation strategies, refinement of immunosuppressive protocols, and advances in peri- and postoperative management strategies have collectively led to improved survival for pediatric lung transplant recipients. The development of specialized pediatric specific transplant centers with multidisciplinary care teams has led to expanded access, accumulation of institutional experience, and establishment of lung transplantation as a standard therapy for pediatric end-stage lung disease [2,3,4▪▪,5].

Although short- and intermediate-term survival after pediatric lung transplantation has improved over time, rates of chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) and late mortality have remained largely unchanged. Despite advances in immunosuppression, surveillance, and supportive care, there are no well established therapies that reliably prevent, halt, or reverse CLAD [6]. Significant challenges remain in the early detection of allograft injury, particularly in younger children who are unable to reliably perform spirometry, limiting timely identification of subclinical disease and potentially contributing to delayed intervention [7]. Additional barriers to improving long-term outcomes include limited pediatric-specific evidence, immunologic complexity, and persistent inequities in access to specialized multidisciplinary care, challenges that are amplified during the transition to adult transplant programs.

EPIDEMIOLOGY AND CURRENT LANDSCAPE

The number of children undergoing lung transplantation has changed dramatically over the past several decades. Following the first pediatric lung transplant in 1987, annual pediatric lung transplant volumes rose, peaking in the early 2000s at approximately 120 transplants globally per year [8]. During this time, cystic fibrosis (CF)-related advanced lung disease remained the predominant indication, particularly in adolescents. With the advent of highly effective modulator therapy (HEMT) for CF, the landscape of pediatric lung transplantation shifted, with a decline in annual transplant volumes since the late 2010s as seen in Fig. 1. By the early 2020s, global pediatric lung transplantation rates decreased to approximately 40 cases annually [8]. In parallel, the primary indications have transitioned to interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary vascular disease (PVD), or pulmonary hypertersion, with increasing proportions of both as seen in Fig. 2 [9,10▪,11].

Changes in annual volume of cases and disease epidemiology have coincided with an increase in illness severity of pediatric lung transplant candidates at the time of listing. Compared to prior, children listed in the current era are more likely to require noninvasive or invasive mechanical ventilation, and, in some cases, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) as a bridge to transplantation. This reflects both delayed progression among historically lung transplant eligible populations, such as CF, and a growing proportion of candidates with rapidly progressive diseases [2,4▪▪].

Pediatric lung transplantation remains highly centralized, with relatively few centers worldwide, and most centers performing a small number of transplants annually. The scarcity of high-volume pediatric centers can influence access and referral patterns, particularly for adolescents, who may be transplanted at adult lung transplant centers rather than pediatric. Importantly, despite higher overall volume at adult centers, pediatric centers achieve better graft survival in children and adolescents suggesting that pediatric-specific experience impacts overall pediatric lung transplant outcomes [5].

CANDIDATE SELECTION

Lung transplantation should be considered for children with end-stage, progressive lung or pulmonary vascular disease with a high risk of mortality despite maximal medical therapy. Additionally, transplantation should offer the potential for improved quality of life [4▪▪,10▪]. A structured and systematic process for referral, evaluation and listing is crucial for this vulnerable population. In 2025, the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) provided the first comprehensive pediatric-specific consensus document providing a framework for the referral and selection of pediatric lung transplant candidates. Early referral, ideally before clinical decompensation or the need for urgent transplantation, maximizes the likelihood of successful transplantation and reduces waitlist mortality. Evaluation should be multidisciplinary and include assessment of disease severity, thoracic anatomy, surgical candidacy, frailty, growth and nutritional status, and psychosocial factors [4▪▪]. Listing decisions are increasingly complex, underscoring the need for clear, disease-specific listing criteria.

There are several unique considerations in the pediatric lung transplantation evaluation process that warrant emphasis (Fig. 3). Psychosocial assessment differs fundamentally from adult assessment, as it must account for the broader context in which a child exists and relies heavily on caregiver report to assess child functioning [4▪▪,12▪]. Decision-making and treatment responsibility predominantly rest with caregivers, introducing complex considerations around caregiver capacity, family dynamics, medication adherence, developmental and neurocognitive status, and the ethical nuances of assent and consent. Caregiver capability and commitment is essential to transplant success, yet caregivers face increased mental health burden following transplantation, underscoring the importance of evaluating family strengths, coping strategies, and the quality of a family's relationship with the transplant team [13,14]. Nutritional status and frailty represent key biologic vulnerabilities in children undergoing transplant evaluation. Nutrition status is known to significantly influence allograft and overall survival, yet optimal assessment in children remains debated. BMI percentile (BMI%) is commonly used as a surrogate for growth and nutrition with severely low BMI% at listing associated with poorer outcomes [15]. Frailty is an increasingly recognized consideration in candidate selection. Conceptually, frailty is characterized by decreased physiologic reserve, arising from the cumulative effects of chronic illness, impaired physical function, and altered body composition [16,17]. Although adult studies have validated frailty and nutritional assessment tools that predict waitlist and posttransplant outcomes, comparable tools and outcome-based analyses remain limited in pediatric cohorts, underscoring the need for more refined, child-specific tools and related measures [18].

CONTRAINDICATIONS

Absolute contraindications in pediatric lung transplantation are dynamic and center dependent. Advances in medical care, surgical techniques, infection management, and multidisciplinary support have shifted prior risk factors, such as ECMO, from absolute to relative contraindications, increasing access for high-risk children [4▪▪,19,20]. Infection with certain microorganisms such as Mycobacterium abscessus may be treated as an absolute contraindication at some centers but acceptable at others depending on institutional experience and risk tolerance. Most centers consider infection with Burkholderia cenocepacia an absolute contraindication given its unfavorable posttransplant outcome, while infection with other Burkholderia species, such as multivorans, remains a relative contraindication. Optimization of comorbid conditions, such as renal insufficiency, diabetes, chronic airway colonization, osteoporosis, malnutrition is important to minimizing the impact of relative contraindications as well, particularly since the additive impact of multiple relative contraindications can ultimately lead to a candidate that is too high risk. Surgical contraindications additionally exist on a spectrum, with the degree and severity of chest wall abnormalities, number of sternotomies, and presence of thoracic collateral vasculature burden all playing a role into what is considered high risk [4▪▪,10▪].

IMPROVED SURVIVAL ON WAITLIST

Waitlist survival for pediatric lung transplant candidates has improved significantly over the past two decades. In the United States (US), implementation of the lung allocation score (LAS) in 2005 prioritized candidates based on medical urgency and posttransplant survival, decreasing median waiting time and waitlist mortality [20]. To further promote equitable organ allocation, the composite allocation score (CAS) replaced the LAS in 2023, incorporating additional biologic and patient access factors into a continuous framework with individual scores for every donor-recipient offer. In this system, pediatric candidates receive significant point advantage over adult candidates, taking into account the limited pediatric donor pool and allowing for prioritization of adult donors lungs for pediatric recipients if appropriately sized [21,22]. Within the current allocation system, most pediatric candidates receive viable organs within 90 days, although a minority wait upwards of a year, with smaller candidates waiting longer [23].

Bridging strategies continue to play a critical role in maintaining transplant candidacy, including noninvasive or invasive mechanical ventilation (MV) and when these approaches are insufficient, extracorporeal life support (ECLS) options such as ECMO or paracorporeal lung assist devices (PLAD) can be utilized [4▪▪,19]. There are limited reports regarding use of a tracheostomy tube prior to lung transplantation with some centers preferring early tracheostomy to enable ambulation and rehabilitation if long term MV is needed [24].

Over the past decade, ECMO use has increased substantially and is now the preferred bridging modality for critically ill pediatric candidates. Ambulatory ECMO is increasingly used as a bridging strategy to allow full patient participation in physical rehabilitation to better preserve transplant candidacy. Although ECMO is associated with risks of bleeding, infection, and thromboembolic events, studies suggest that long-term outcomes, including one-year posttransplant mortality and re-transplantation rates, are comparable among patients bridged with ECMO, MV, or nonbridged groups [19,20]. This has been shown with favorable outcomes even in infants/young children [25]. PLAD represents a newer bridging approach where the patient's own cardiac output drives blood through a passive circuit to provide respiratory support without the hemodynamic consequences of traditional ECMO [26]. Experience remains limited, however, with anecdotal evidence of high thromboembolic events leading to infrequent use. Many of these bridge-to-transplant practices have evolved from absolute to relative contraindications to now accepted strategies, particularly at high-volume centers with multidisciplinary expertise capable of managing the associated technical and clinical complexity.

INCREASING THE DONOR POOL

Donor availability remains a critical barrier in pediatric lung transplantation. In the United States, policy changes, including broader geographic sharing of pediatric donor lungs following the removal of the donation service area-based allocation system in 2017 have reduced regional disparities in donor availability and narrowed differences in waitlist outcomes between organ-poor and organ-rich lung transplant centers [27,28]. Surgical approaches, such as volume reduction, are effective options for addressing donor recipient size mismatch and are performed at experienced, high-volume centers [29,30]. While no longer a common practice in the US, living donor lobar lung transplantation can be utilized for the sickest patients, eliminating the wait time for a suitable decreased donor [31].

The practice of ABO-incompatible (ABOi) lung transplantation is possible owing to the relative immaturity of the infant immune system and lower levels of preformed ABO antibodies, expanding the donor pool for the youngest patients with the highest waitlist mortality. ABOi transplantation has additionally been demonstrated to be successful in cardiac transplantation beyond the first year, holding promise for lung transplantation in the future [4▪▪].

Donation after circulatory death (DCD) remains underutilized in lung transplantation, with fewer than 5% of eligible DCD lungs transplanted, despite lungs being used from only 15–20% of eligible multiorgan donors overall [32]. Given the increasing contribution of DCD to lung transplantation worldwide, expanding its use in pediatric populations represents an important opportunity to further increase access to donor organs.

Advances in organ procurement and preservation have further expanded donor availability. Ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) allows for functional evaluation of donor lungs outside of the body, allowing otherwise marginal donors to be reconditioned for transplantation. Studies have demonstrated survival and posttransplant outcomes comparable to conventional transplantation. Portable EVLP using the Organ Care System (OCS) allows further perfusion and ventilation assessment from the time of donor retrieval until implantation, with similar outcomes when compared with conventional transplant. This technology is limited with minimal reported use in pediatric lung transplantation [30,33]. Donor lung preservation systems such as LUNGguard and BAROguard have become more common practice in recent years, even at pediatric programs. By more precisely maintaining temperature conditions at 10°C (LUNGguard, BAROguard) and regulating airway pressures (BAROguard) during transport, these devices have minimized the effects of cold ischemia on graft function. Their use has extended acceptable procurement time outside the traditional 6-h window and facilitated broader geographic sharing of pediatric donor lungs [34▪].

IMMUNOSUPPRESSION

All pediatric lung transplant recipients require lifelong immunosuppression to prevent rejection. Induction therapy is standard, with recent practice shifting from antithymocyte globulin (ATG) to basiliximab, due to its favorable efficacy and safety profile and minimization of ATG-associated cytopenias and nephrotoxicity [23,35–37].

Maintenance immunosuppression consists of triple-drug therapy with tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and corticosteroids used in 95% of pediatric programs, though significant international practice variation exists with limited pediatric-specific evidence-based guidelines [31]. Given the high immunogenicity of the lung allograft relative to other organs, higher doses of immunosuppressants are required, placing recipients at increased risk for drug-induced toxicities, including posttransplant diabetes and progressive renal dysfunction [23,38,39].

LONG TERM SURVIVAL

Short term survival for pediatric lung transplant recipients has substantially improved over time, with recent estimates of one-year post transplant survival nearing 90% [40▪]. Long term survival, while improved, continues to be less than for other pediatric solid organ transplant recipients with median survival hovering around 6 years [41]. The primary limitation to long term survival remains chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD), accounting for greater than 50% of deaths beyond the first-year posttransplantation [6,35]. CLAD is defined clinically as a persistent decline of at least 20% in FEV1 from baseline, and may manifest as an obstructive, restrictive, or mixed phenotype. The most common presentation is bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS), characterized by progressive small airway obliteration and airway obstruction. Less common, but with worse outcomes, is restrictive allograft syndrome (RAS), characterized by peripheral and interstitial fibrosis and parenchymal opacities [6,42,43]. The multifactorial pathogenesis of CLAD likely underlies the limited effectiveness of current therapies and the absence of proven preventive strategies. Current pediatric treatment strategies are largely extrapolated from adult studies and focus on mitigating ongoing injury, including augmented immunosuppression with T-cell–directed agents like ATG and therapies to remove T cells such as extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP) and total lymphoid irradiation (TLI). ECP has demonstrated both prophylactic and therapeutic benefit, with reduction in acute rejection, cytomegalovirus infection, and CLAD incidence when used prophylactically, and stabilization in established CLAD [44–47]. TLI represents an alternative strategy with more limited evidence but has nonetheless been associated with a significant attenuation of FEV1 decline across multiple CLAD phenotypes [46,48]. Emerging therapies, such as ruxolitinib and belumosudil, have shown efficacy in pulmonary chronic graft-versus-host disease after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation which shares histopathologic features with BOS. These similarities suggest a potential rationale for its use in lung transplant recipients, with the additional advantage of oral administration [49,50]. Given that dysregulated repair and fibrosis represent a final common pathway in CLAD, antifibrotic strategies may also complement immunomodulatory approaches in future treatment paradigms. For advanced or progressive CLAD, re-transplantation may represent the only definitive therapeutic option in carefully selected candidates.

Antibody mediated rejection (AMR) remains a concern posttransplant with data showing association to early CLAD development [51,52]. Treatment has evolved to include targeted B cell depletion and antibody removal, with newer classes of proteasome inhibitors, complement inhibitors, and monoclonal antibodies being used in refractory cases. While efforts to create a consensus guideline for AMR exists, current treatment practices lack uniformity and are largely center specific. The presence of high HLA allosensitization pretransplant also presents a conundrum, increasing the risk of a positive crossmatch, primary graft dysfunction, and early CLAD. While many pediatric centers embark on desensitization to increase the donor pool, strategy and timing remain center specific.

SURVEILLANCE

Longitudinal assessment of lung function is central to surveillance after pediatric lung transplantation (Fig. 4). Although spirometry remains the gold standard, it has limited sensitivity for detecting early small-airway pathology and is highly effort dependent. Accurate and reliable measurements are particularly challenging in the pediatric population, especially in children younger than six years of age [6,7]. Alternative modalities, such as nitrogen multiple-breath washout (N2-MBW) to calculate the lung clearance index (LCI) as a measure of ventilation inhomogeneity or impulse oscillometry to assess airway impedance and reactance offer the advantage of requiring only tidal breathing in an effort independent manner without forced expiratory maneuvers. Despite this benefit, these techniques are not broadly used due to the need for specialized equipment and expertise, and remain limited by a lack of standardization in protocols, calibration, and interpretation [53–55].

Surveillance monitoring modalities in the posttransplant period.

Imaging modalities play an important adjunctive role in CLAD surveillance. Noncontrast chest computed tomography should be obtained with inspiratory and expiratory views at routine intervals and repeated when CLAD is suspected to assess for air trapping and parenchymal abnormalities. Lung magnetic resonance imaging represents a novel, radiation-free alternative, though its role in lung transplant surveillance continues to evolve [6,56,57]. Invasive surveillance with transbronchial biopsy with bronchoalveolar lavage is central to identifying treatable causes of lung function decline prior to a diagnosis of definite CLAD and is commonly performed at scheduled intervals during the first one to two years posttransplant, with substantial variation in practice thereafter [6]. Emerging approaches such as transbronchial cryobiopsy offer larger tissue samples and improved diagnostic yield but may carry increased bleeding risk [58–60]. Finally, noninvasive biomarkers such as donor-derived cell-free DNA (cfDNA) are being explored as adjunctive surveillance tools. Studies suggest that trends in cfDNA may correlate with severe allograft injury, allograft failure, and mortality, although their role in pediatric lung transplant surveillance remains to be defined [61].

OUTCOMES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Children who undergo lung transplantation face lifelong medical complexity, with significant physical, neurodevelopmental, and psychological sequelae, including trauma related to chronic illness, and prolonged hospitalizations [62,63▪,64]. Despite these challenges, advances in candidate selection, perioperative care, donor utilization strategies, immunosuppression, and posttransplant surveillance have contributed to improved short- and intermediate-term survival, particularly in younger children and those bridged to transplantation with mechanical ventilation or ECMO [3,40▪,65,66].

As survival improves, emphasis should be placed on optimizing long-term outcomes and quality of life through comprehensive, multidisciplinary care [3,67]. Sustained improvements in long-term outcomes depend on coordinated, integrated inpatient and outpatient teams working in concert. Ensuring equitable access to transplantation and posttransplant care remains a critical challenge, with disparities evident across geographic regions, socioeconomic strata, and healthcare systems worldwide. Global collaboration, standardized outcome reporting, and a renewed focus on patient-centered outcomes will be essential to defining the next era of pediatric lung transplantation [68▪,69▪].

Concurrently, the decline in pediatric lung transplant volumes and concentration of procedures at few select centers raise important questions regarding candidate selection, maintenance of expertise, and optimization of outcomes in smaller, higher-risk patient populations. Future directions in pediatric lung transplantation may include expansion into traditionally high-risk or underrepresented groups, such as patients with complex congenital heart disease [2,4▪▪,70▪]. Innovative immunomodulatory strategies, such as combined lung and stem cell transplantation aimed at inducing immune tolerance, may represent a potential avenue to reduce CLAD and improve long term outcomes [71–73]. More novel approaches, including xenotransplantation and bioengineered organs, may ultimately help address the persistent donor shortage, though substantial immunologic, ethical, and safety challenges exist [74,75].

CONCLUSION

Meaningful advancements have been made in pediatric lung transplantation over the last decade with improvements in medical care, surgical techniques, and multidisciplinary support increasing access for high-risk children. Bridge-to-transplant practices, such as use of ECMO, have evolved from contraindications to now accepted strategies, improving waitlist survival and allowing transplantation in critically ill children. Despite ongoing high demand for donor lungs, availability has improved with innovative surgical and immunologic strategies and emerging technologies such as EVLP offering further promise. Despite these many strides, rates of CLAD and long-term mortality remain unchanged. Developing a better understanding of pediatric-specific risk factors, optimizing immunosuppressive strategies, and developing interventions to prevent, halt, or reverse CLAD may offer promise in improving the survival of children undergoing lung transplantation.
