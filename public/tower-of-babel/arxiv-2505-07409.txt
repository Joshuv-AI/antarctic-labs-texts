Abstract
. Democratic societies need reliable information. Misinformation in popular media such as news articles or videos threatens to impair civic discourse. Citizens are, unfortunately, not equipped to verify this content flood consumed daily at increasing rates. This work aims to semi-automatically quantify scientific accuracy of online media. By semantifying media of unknown veracity, their statements can be compared against equally processed trusted sources. We implemented a workflow using LLM-based statement extraction and knowledge graph analysis. Our neurosymbolic system was able to evidently streamline state-of-the-art veracity quantification. Evaluated via expert interviews and a user survey, the tool provides a beneficial veracity indication. This indicator, however, is unable to annotate public media at the required granularity and scale. Further work towards a FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) ground truth and complementary metrics are required to scientifically support civic discourse.

Our goal is to improve the quality of public information exchange using a scientific accuracy score, by computationally evaluating statements in media reports based on expert research.
As motivated in
section 2
, this requires a solid knowledge base built on statements from peer-reviewed scientific literature and structured in a knowledge graph.
This knowledge is structured using the workflow presented in
Figure 1
:
Trusted source media is processed, transformed into semantic statements (unless already available), aligned with a cohesive data model, and ingested into a knowledge graph.
In our case, the ground truth utilized consisted of headline statements of the IPCC AR6
(
Calvin et al. 2023
)
, the highly synthesized consensus of climate science.

Now we can process media of unknown veracity, such as media of significant public interest (e.g. viral posts or videos), and subsequently align and compare their statements with our ground truth knowledge graph.
The following sections describe the process through which any media will transition.

3.1.
Process media

The type of media defines the required further processing.
We extract text from web documents using the Python library Beautiful Soup.
Audio and video files in multiple languages are transcribed using OpenAI Whisper.
Text from PDF files is extracted using the Python library PDFMiner.
Text extracted this way has the downside of losing structural context information and being distorted with noise not belonging to the text body, such as headings and editorial information.
All processed media documents are assigned a unique ID.
If the media is not already structured as triple statements (e.g. RDF dump), triples are extracted from the media in the next step.

Statement extraction

While investigating existing techniques to extract triple statements, we did not find a general-purpose approach that clearly suits our needs; hence, we tested three different approaches.
First, semantic role labeling (SRL)
(
He et al. 2017
)
was used to create Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR)
(
Banarescu et al. 2013
)
graphs.
Disambiguating unrestricted AMR graphs and using them for semantically consistent and reliable fact-checking was highly time-intensive.
Once set up, these graphs work consistently, but their setup does not scale well to the complexity and number of statements required.
Second, we explored triple extraction using a domain-specific Named Entity Recognition (NER) model.
Training a domain-specific model combined with an ontology could provide better results, but is cost-intensive, time-consuming, and not very suitable to the heterogeneous domains of public discourse.
Our third approach utilized Large Language Models (LLM) to extract and align triples.
While LLMs are currently not reliable either, human-in-the-loop supervision promised faster, sufficiently reliable results.
After iterative testing, these initial assessments were reviewed by NLP and structured knowledge experts.
The findings were substantiated and the LLM-based approach was deemed the best fit for the scope, potentially to be complimented with a NER-aided ontology-based mediator.

Alignment

Predicate normalization and semantic alignment, such as synonym detection, concluded the triple processing.
Each triple is assigned a media ID array, allowing the statement origin to be traceable through multiple sources.

3.2.
Veracity Checking

The veracity check involves knowledge graph analysis against the ground truth, initially searching for an exact match.
If no match is found, a path check according to
Ciampaglia et al. 2015

(
Ciampaglia et al. 2015
)
can approximate the veracity.
This approximation should only be interpreted as an indication, not a sufficient check.
Among the implementations testing these concepts, only match-based veracity scoring was deemed sufficiently reliable.

Quantify additional metrics

In addition to veracity, the conceptual score may consist of other criteria, such as temporal relevance, confidence, clearness, transparency, information depth, objectivity, and rationality.
Neglecting these factors can negatively impact the annotation quality.
The overall accuracy score
s_{acc}\in[0,1]
is calculated using the weighted individual metrics:
s_{acc}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\cdot w_{i}
, where
s_{i}\in[0,1]
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=1
.
According to our investigation of computational fact-checking, accuracy consists mostly of veracity:
w_{ver}\geqslant 0.5\geqslant\sum_{i=1,\ i\neq ver}^{n}w_{i}
.
Since no satisfactory computational quantifications could be identified for the additional metrics, our accuracy scoring is currently limited to veracity (
w_{ver}=1
).

4.
Evaluation

To ensure that the methods and tools being used are up-to-date and properly contextualized, the approach and its implementation were presented to a number of knowledge graph experts from the ORKG team.
The first presentation verified that the approach was timely and needed.
For further refinement of the approach, 10 experts provided detailed responses in individual semi-structured interviews.
In sessions averaging 28 min (total: 4:42:00, max: 0:43:55, min: 0:13:39,
\sigma
: 0:10:40), the following insights were gathered:

•

The tool suits as a personal assistant, but interoperability of tools ranging from trusted graphs over browsers to PDF readers is important.

•

Knowledge graphs are state-of-the-art interoperable knowledge representations. When no exact match is found, embeddings or graph walks should be utilized, particularly with increased weighting of incoming edges.

•

While LLMs are state-of-the-art for scalable statement extraction, they are not reliable for semantic parsing and require identifying non-reproducible or hallucinated triples.

•

A NER mediator could complement the LLM to ensure consistency in moderating statements, instances, and classes.

Using these insights, the approach and tool were refined in a second iteration, concluding in a final expert presentation substantiating the lessons learned and a public user survey diversifying the evaluator scope.
The results from the 43 participants verified the necessity and usability of the tool, as depicted in

5.
Discussion and Future Work

We demonstrated a workflow to semantically align a ground truth alongside media statements to facilitate computational fact-checking.
While promising in addressing misinformation, this approach revealed several issues not currently solved by state-of-the-art computational fact-checking:

Fact-checking is always only as good as its facts, but the availability of high-quality FAIR
(
Wilkinson et al. 2016
)
ground truth knowledge graphs is limited.
Especially for urgent and cohesive domains such as climate change, larger-scale semantification collaboration is required before a comprehensive corpus can be built.
Citizens distrusting scientists will not accept a ground truth built upon science and hence reject this fact-checking method.
Extensive manual fact-checking resources exist, yet reaching their intended audience remains an equally challenging issue in the attention economy.

Hyperfocusing factuality as a parameter could create other blind spots, such as context (e.g. sarcastic), clearness (e.g. convoluted), authenticity (e.g. cited incorrectly), subjectivity (e.g. “I feel like”), or confidence (e.g. “likely”).
Even if a statement cannot be proven factually wrong (such as any subjective statement starting with “I feel like …”), it can strongly influence a discussion.
Similarly, a factually correct statement taken out of context can misrepresent a complex topic.

Evidently, computational fact-checking to alleviate misinformation requires significant future work, including on:
A knowledge community that crowd-sources ground truth synthesis using FAIR digital infrastructure, extensive popular media annotation, and effective result dissemination alongside resources for science skeptics.

6.
Conclusion

This work illustrated the problem of computational fact-checking and demonstrated a usable tool to assist this effort against misinformation.
While plenty of fact-checking and ground truth resources exist, too few are as machine-readable as recommended by the literature.
Our workflow contributes to solving this issue, while a diverse set of tasks still needs to be addressed outside the scope of this work to adequately check misinformation.
