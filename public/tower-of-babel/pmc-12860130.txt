Photo coding: a pragmatic approach to measuring intervention fidelity in a pharmacy system re-design
Authors: Stephanie M Resendiz, Shiying Mai, Aaron M Gilson, Jamie A Stone, Emily L Hoffins, Jason S Chladek, Taylor L Watterson, Katherine G Moore, Michelle A Chui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeasuring intervention fidelity can help justify the transition of evidence-based practices into everyday community-based practices by helping identify components of the intervention supporting the intervention’s uptake. Current methods to measure fidelity include self-reported data, observational data using audio or video recordings, and in-vivo observations, but these are limited due to cost-ineffectiveness, time inefficiency, and external validity threats. Photo coding is proposed as a new, pragmatic method of observational data to measure intervention fidelity, as was used in a pharmacy system re-design intervention (Senior Safe™) to improve over-the-counter (OTC) medication safety for older adults.MethodsGuided by human factors engineering principles, Senior Safe re-shelved OTC medications based on safety level, and signage was added to designate safer and high-risk products. Senior Safe was implemented by pharmacy leadership and maintained by pharmacy staff. Pharmacy leadership and researchers collaborated to take photos of Senior Safe two times (Time 1 and Time 2), at least three months apart, to examine intervention layout and medication categorization fidelity. A Layout Codebook was constructed to evaluate the conformity of signage to ergonomic principles. A Medication Categorization Codebook was designed to assess whether the signage was properly allocated to the designated products. Two research assistants and two PharmD students coded the photos. A fidelity standard of ≥ 80% concordance with intervention guidelines was used to signify high-fidelity.ResultsFidelity was assessed within 67 pharmacy sites implementing Senior Safe. All sites achieved ≥ 80% fidelity concordance between Time 1 and Time 2 for the Layout Codebook. Alternatively, for the Medication Categorization Codebook, 97% of sites met high-fidelity standards during Time 1, compared to 85% of sites at Time 2, indicating statistically significant negative drift. Overall, over half of all Time 1 and Time 2 concordance rates involving the Layout and Medication Categorization Codebooks exceeded the ≥ 80% high-fidelity standard.ConclusionMeasuring intervention fidelity is useful in determining whether interventions are sustained and successfully transitioned into community-based practice. Photo coding is an innovative approach to measure intervention fidelity and allows researchers to identify multiple layers of concordance and discordance to intervention guidelines. Organizations are encouraged to investigate intervention fidelity, including identifying discordance and adaptation needs.Keywords: Intervention fidelity, Adaptation, Community pharmacy, Medication safety

Background

Implementation science aims to identify factors that contribute to the uptake of evidence-based practices (EBPs) and evidence-based interventions (EBI) into real-world settings [1] such as translations to community-based settings. Identifying factors that support translation of EBPs into routine clinical practices could directly contribute to reducing health research expenditures by $85 billion, globally, which would otherwise be considered as wasted costs [2–5]. Measuring intervention fidelity is one way to identify components of an intervention that support EBP translation into practice and components that may hinder intervention uptake. Intervention fidelity is described as the extent that an intervention was delivered as originally intended [6–9]. Measuring intervention fidelity can help ensure a successful transition of EBPs into sustainable everyday community-based practices [8].

Historically, numerous terms have been used interchangeably to describe intervention fidelity, such as, implementation fidelity, fidelity of implementation, fidelity, treatment fidelity, treatment integrity [8]. Although, the listed terms have been defined similarly, most recent research in implementation science has distinguished intervention fidelity as the most precise term when examining concordance to intervention guidelines. Additionally, in parallel to intervention fidelity is implementation strategy fidelity, which has been defined as carrying out implementation strategies as intended [7]. Implementation strategies are deliberate and intentional efforts made to improve the uptake of the implementation of an intervention [7]. This paper, however, will focus solely on intervention fidelity as was measured in a pharmacy system re-design.

Current research suggests that EBIs with higher rates of fidelity often yield more positive implementation outcomes, correlating into a positive relationship with clinical outcomes [7, 10–12]. Additionally, measuring intervention fidelity can assist in providing a baseline indicator for evaluating fidelity within a sustainability timeframe. However, there is limited research examining fidelity measures that promote intervention sustainment after implementation in community-based settings.

Although there are two aspects of intervention fidelity, adherence and competence [8, 9, 13–15], this study focuses exclusively on adherence. Adherence describes whether the intervention has been implemented according to guidelines [8, 16]. Current methods to measure intervention fidelity include self-reported data and observational data [8, 17]. Self-reported data includes data collected directly from the intervention’s participants often using checklists and/or verbal reporting [8, 17, 18]. The self-reported method focus on the adherence of participants to an intervention’s guidelines [8]. Observational data such as audio [19, 20] or video recordings [21, 22] (recordings captured during the intervention session), and in-vivo observations [12, 23] (observer present during the intervention session) are methods used to identify participant adherence to the intervention guidelines [8, 17]. However, both of these methods are limited due to cost-ineffectiveness, time inefficiency, and external validity threats [8]. To circumvent these limitations, photo coding is proposed as a new, pragmatic method of observational data to measure intervention fidelity. Photo coding was used in a pharmacy system re-design intervention (Senior Safe™) to improve over-the-counter (OTC) medication safety for older adults (age 65 or older) by designating both safer and higher-risk OTC medications [24–29].

Pharmacy-based intervention: Senior Safe

Guided by human factors engineering principles, Senior Safe [24, 25] designated OTC medications based on safety level, and signage was added to designate safer and higher-risk products for older adults. Using the Beers Criteria [30], OTC medications were categorized as either Green, Yellow, Red, or Behind-the-Counter (BTC), representing their level of perceived safety for older adults. The OTC medications classified as Green were those that were considered safe for use by older adults [24–29]. The Yellow products were considered to be neutral, neither the safest nor highest risk for the OTC medications. The Red products were single-ingredient first-generation antihistamine Cough, Cold, and Allergy products that were considered generally unsafe for older adults [24–29]. BTC products were PM Pain and Sleep OTCs with first-generation antihistamines and combination OTC with antihistamines that were designated to be unsafe [24–29]. Based on these OTC medication classifications, medications were re-shelved within one of three categories: Green Banner, Stop Signs, or BTC products. Senior Safe was designed to seamlessly integrate itself within a pharmacy work system without increasing pharmacy staff burden [24–29].

Senior Safe re-shelving and guiding principles

The “Green” OTC medications were re-shelved in concordance to ergonomic principles, such as being easily visible and comfortably within reach, to designated areas which were distinctively characterized by a Green Banner. The Green OTCs were placed between shoulder (42 inches) and knee height (18 inches) with the green banner hung on the bottoms of shelving, just below the product price and descriptive labels [31–33]. Stop Signs were designed to act as soft barriers to OTC medications that were considered as a higher risk “Red” product for older adults [34]. The Stop Signs were placed directly on the shelving in front of the OTC product, this was done to elicit a physical soft barrier for older adults attempting to select higher risk products and redirect the selection to a safer product. Finally, the re-design incorporated the use of BTC signs in the OTC section and the movement of the highest risk products behind the counter, inaccessible to patients. The BTC signs are used as a hard barrier to encourage medication counseling and redirection of older adult selection to a safer product selection [34]. However, all BTC-designated products were available for patients if, even after discussing their selection with pharmacy staff, they still chose to purchase the product. In addition, a Senior Safe poster was placed in both the OTC section and at the front counter (the point of sale) to provide a brief conceptualization of the intervention. Re-design components and guidelines were collectively incorporated as guiding principles for Senior Safe. An example of all re-design components, including the signage, can be seen in Fig. 1.

The Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework was selected and used to conceptualize and develop Senior Safe [25, 35, 36]. In the Exploration phase hypothetical health situations were used to elicit older adult OTC selections to treat symptoms of a cough/cold/allergy, pain, or a sleep situation [25, 27]. A research advisory team was created during the Preparation phase. This group was comprised of experts from a multitude of fields who met monthly to be updated on progress, provide feedback on intervention development, and help brainstorm strategies for potential barriers [25]. EPIS accounts for the inner and outer contexts of an organization, and these contextual factors are vital to the preparation of an intervention leading up to the implementation process [26]. Importantly, the goals of Senior Safe were conceptualized to align with EPIS’s emphasis on intervention implementation and sustainment. That is, numerous processes and outcomes were delineated to measure various aspects of the intervention’s implementation and sustainment, including intervention fidelity. Consequently, using EPIS as the conceptual framework ensured that each stage leading up to the implementation of Senior Safe was well-considered and anticipated from a sustainability standpoint [25].

Objective

The purpose of this study was to operationalize and measure intervention fidelity within a pharmacy system re-design using an innovative Photo Coding data collection method.

Methods

Implementation of Senior Safe began in June 2023 in a large mid-western integrated health system throughout Wisconsin by the organization’s pharmacy leadership in 67 community pharmacy sites—pharmacy leadership was responsible for moving and reshelving products based on safety indications, and placing color-coordinated signage on appropriate products. The research team designed and printed all intervention components and provided a document outlining all guiding principles and their justifications to ensure the most accurate implementation of Senior Safe. Additionally, pharmacy sites received a comprehensive list of all OTC medications, sold within their pharmacies, with their given safety designations. Researchers maintained consistent communication with pharmacy leadership throughout intervention implementation to offer support and any additional guidance that was required. After implementation, Senior Safe was independently maintained by pharmacy staff and supervised by pharmacy leadership. All 67 community pharmacy sites implemented Senior Safe by January 2024.

To assess the intervention fidelity of Senior Safe at each pharmacy site, the research team used the guiding principles to create a Layout Codebook and a Medication Categorization Codebook. The Layout Codebook was constructed to evaluate the conformity of signage to ergonomic principles. Separately, the Medication Categorization Codebook was designed to assess whether the signage properly designated the products to which they should be allocated (e.g. designated safe products allocated with a Green Banner). Both codebooks were designed to measure adherence to intervention guidelines for each component of Senior Safe during a short-term (three month) sustainability timeframe. Features of Senior Safe were incorporated as questions in the codebooks, allowing for a complete and standardized analysis of each component of the Senior Safe intervention (see Table 1).

Data collection

Pharmacy leadership and researchers collaborated to take photos of the Senior Safe intervention two times (Time 1 (implementation) and Time 2 (short- term sustainability)), at least three months apart, to examine the intervention’s layout and medication categorization fidelity during both phases. Photos were taken during the regular hours of operation of the pharmacy, at a time convenient for pharmacy leadership and researchers. Visits to capture photos in Time 2 were completed unannounced to pharmacy staff; however, pharmacy leadership were notified when photos for Time 2 were being initiated. Pharmacy areas where Senior Safe was present were photographed, including Cough/Cold/Allergy, Pain, Sleep, and front counter areas. Additional miscellaneous photos were taken around the pharmacy to help researchers gauge shelving height. During this process, no pharmacy customers or staff members were photographed. Coding dyads were assigned each codebook. The photos were coded according to the layout codebook by a dyad of research assistants (SR & SM). Simultaneously, the photos were coded according to the medication categorization codebook by a dyad of third year PharmD students (YC & JK), both who had previously completed OTC coursework. The Layout and Medication Categorization Codebooks were initially entered by one member of the dyad then verified by the partnering dyad, roles were alternated between Time 1 and Time 2 (see Table 2). In the case of disagreement among coders, the pharmacy sites were brought to the research team for further discussion on coding and eventual consensus.

Data analysis

There is limited research establishing a minimum competency level while measuring adherence to an intervention; existing literature has identified an adherence standard of ≥ 80% of intervention components to qualify as high fidelity [37]. Alternatively, fidelity measures of ≤ 50% representing low fidelity [37]. To evaluate Senior Safe fidelity, the ≥ 80% concordance standard was used. Proportional statistics and paired-samples proportion analysis using McNemar tests were calculated using IBM SPSS 29.0.2.0 [38] to compare Time 1 and Time 2 to identify the extent of three-month intervention’s implementation drift. The term, “drift” is used define incremental changes between the control conditions of an intervention and the interventions implementation [39]. Paired-samples proportion tests were run at a 95% confidence interval and were significant at the p < 0.05 level. Significant drift was identified as either positive or negative, determined by directionality of proportional differences between Time 1 and Time 2.

Results

Layout codebook

Comprehensively, between Time 1 and Time 2, 100% of sites met the high-fidelity standards of ≥ 80% concordance to intervention guidelines according to the Layout Codebook.

Raw proportions between all Layout Codebook variables from Time 1 and Time 2, as well as paired-samples proportion test results, can be found in Table 3. The Green Banner Sleep category showed a proportional fidelity increase representing statistically significant positive drift (Z = −2.449, p = 0.014) from Time 1 to Time 2, while changes for the Cough/Cold/Allergy and Pain categories were not statistically significant. For BTC signage, no statistically significant drift was identified for the broad Cough/Cold/Allergy, Pain, and Sleep categories, as well as for the individual products within each medication class (specific sleep products had insufficient data for analyses). Relating to the use of Stop Signs only for Cough/Cold/Allergy products, fidelity reduced from 91 to 84%, representing significant negative drift (Z = 2.121, p = 0.034) between Time 1 and Time 2. During our analysis we discovered that 88% of sites in Time 1 and 85% in Time 2 added additional signage to BTC products using intervention’s stop signs.

At Time 1, the Senior Safe poster was present at the front counter in 99% of occurrences but significantly reduced to 87% at Time 2 (Z = 3.000, p = 0.003). Alternatively, the Senior Safe poster location in the OTC sales floor showed no significant placement changes between the two assessment times.

Medication categorization codebook

The Medication Categorization Codebook at Time 1 showed a 97% of sites with high fidelity concordance, compared to 85% of sites in Time 2 (Z = 3.00, p = 0.003). Although the drift between Time 1 and Time 2 resulted in statistically significant negative drift for the Medication Categorization Codebook’s overall fidelity, the ≥ 80% high-fidelity standard was maintained at both time points.

The placement of Green Banners for Cough/Cold/Allergy category reduced in fidelity over time, from 61% at Time 1 and 31% at Time 2 (Z = 4.082, p < 0.001), while Sleep signage fidelity decreased from 84% in Time 1 to 76% in Time 2 (Z = 1.732, p = 0.042). As a result, both medication categories demonstrated statistically significant negative drift. BTC signage placement for Pain and Sleep products showed no significant drift. Although signage fidelity for Cough/Cold/Allergy products showed significant negative drift, proportional frequencies still ranged from 99% in Time 1 to 88% in Time 2 (Z = −2.646, p < 0.008), both values within the recognized standard of high-fidelity. There also was significantly reduced fidelity over time relating to Stop Sign placement for Cough/Cold/Allergy products, from 96% in Time 1 to 82% in Time 2 (Z = 2.496, p < 0.013). Discussion

As the results suggest, Senior Safe was successful in maintaining short-term sustainability between Time 1 and Time 2. For both the Layout and Medication Categorization Codebooks the high-fidelity standard of ≥ 80% was exceeded. Additionally, using photo coding allowed flexibility for the research team to compare fidelity measurements for each component of the Senior Safe pharmacy re-design. As indicated in Tables 3 and 4, some of the individual component comparisons between Time 1 and Time 2 showed drift. However, often this drift was not found to be statistically significant. By comparing individual components of Senior Safe, several components of the re-design may have caused greater challenges for pharmacy staff to sustain, which could be used to help inform any future iterations of Senior Safe.

The data analysis conducted for the Senior Safe intervention focuses on intervention fidelity. When identifying components where fidelity was lower, it was apparent that strict classifications of concordance and discordance may not encompass the full scope of the interventions being translated into practice. Discordance with intervention guidelines may be interpreted as a negative outcome, but there is potential that at least some discordance is a result of community partners’ adapting components of an intervention to better fit and represent the setting. Future research should help further understand the phenomenon of adaptations and identify implications to measuring intervention fidelity, including the use of pharmacy staff interviews to understand reasons for various discordances.

Adaptiveness

Adaptations are intentional modifications to the design and/or delivery of an intervention, done with the intent of improving the intervention’s fit or effectiveness [40]. Adaptations to interventions are commonly in tension with high-fidelity of that intervention. When the EBI is aligned to the setting or system where implementation will occur, it is likely only minimal adaptations could surface [41]. When considering acceptable adaptations, it is vital to ensure that the adaptations remain in alignment with the projects outcomes [41].

Community pharmacies can greatly vary in size and sales floor space, even those within the same pharmacy organization. The Senior Safe intervention aimed to re-design these spaces and the structures within the pharmacy setting to improve older adult medication safety. When conceptualizing the photo coding data analysis, efforts focused on measuring the degree of concordance between the Senior Safe intervention’s layout and medication categorization re-design guidelines, which indicated high intervention fidelity. Measuring intervention fidelity was an unambiguous process, using strict concordance and discordance guidelines. By only measuring intervention fidelity we are assuming that standardization across community-based settings, like pharmacies, would be feasible. However, as learned with the Senior Safe intervention, when working with complex work systems, one standardized method may not be completely representative.

The Senior Safe intervention was designed to acknowledge the variance of community pharmacy spaces. Given an intervention fidelity standard of ≥ 80% concordance with intervention guidelines to demonstrate high-fidelity for Senior Safe, pharmacy sites that did not meet this high-fidelity standard were reviewed for adaptiveness traits. Size constraints often were a result of the sales floor space being too small for the number of products sold in the given pharmacy, this constraint made it difficult for sites to conform to a complete re-design of their shelves (Fig. 2). Another adaptation was noticed within how sleep products are commonly shelved in the pharmacies. Most of the pharmacy sites included Melatonin with the vitamin products, the vitamin sections were oftentimes shelved alphabetical for ease of locating products. However, Fig. 3 demonstrates that this situation had the potential to cause the sleep products to be located below average knee height, against the intervention’s layout guidelines.

A final common adaptation represented pharmacy staff adding additional intervention signage to BTC products. According to intervention guidelines, the higher-risk OTC products were moved to a designated area BTC, an area only accessible to pharmacy staff. As mentioned in the results, we uncovered that 88% of sites in Time 1 and 85% in Time 2 added additional signage in the form of the intervention’s stop signs to BTC products (Fig. 4). Our study did not entail gathering information from pharmacy staff to understand why this was done. However, we hypothesize that such adaptations were an attempt to improve effectiveness of the intervention by including additional reminder signals to pharmacy staff when selling higher-risk products.

As researchers, it may not always be easy to consider adaptations to evidence-based interventions. However, as learned with Senior Safe, some adaptations could support the fitting of the intervention into the setting. While working with complex work systems and design layouts that are variable, adaptiveness could help support the integration of interventions. Additionally, improving the fit and effectiveness of an intervention could contribute to supporting the sustainability of interventions in community-based settings, like in community pharmacies.

Innovation

Photo Coding was used as a data-generation method because it was a cost-effective way to measure intervention fidelity in work system re-designs. Recommendations and lessons learned by our research team during our photo coding experience from conceptualization to data analysis has been outlined below.

Analytic approach for codebook design

When conceptualizing a codebook, it is important to consider all different facets of interests and how those components may be analyzed later. To analyze intervention fidelity of Senior Safe there was a development of two codebooks, for both layout and medications. This was done to answer the different questions our research team envisioned for analysis. The layout codebook focused solely on the physical layout and signage of the intervention. Alternatively, the medication codebook focused solely on the re-shelving of the OTC products, ensuring that their re-shelving was in concordance with intervention guidelines.

Photo quality control

It is imperative that the photos taken for different time periods are mirror images of each other. The photos taken at Time 2 in the pharmacies were intentionally done to replicate the images taken during Time 1. Utilizing this process assisted the coders during the data analysis portion of our project by providing clarity and consistently.

Photo resolution

Using a camera that can take high resolution photos will facilitate coding ease. Durning our analysis, however, our group noticed a few photos where the resolution resulted in lack of clarity. These few occurrences resulted in the coders having difficultly coding those images.

Measured variables not facing camera

Ensuring that an analysis approach is considered for cases when variables cannot be measured from the photos. In the community pharmacy environment, there were times when OTC products were not legible based on their placement on the shelves (e.g. box tipped over on its side). Our codebook included a place for coders to document these few occurrences.

Time commitment

Anticipating and communicating that the photo coding process can be time consuming is essential for efficient planning. There is a time commitment that should be expected, from the conceptualization and development of the codebook(s) to the completion of the data analysis.

Coders

Multiple coders are encouraged to help resolve coding disagreements and mitigating coding time commitments. Our coding team consisted of four graduate students, who met regularly to resolve any coding disagreements. In few cases, disagreements left unresolved were brought to the larger research team for discussion.

Limitations

There are potential limitations to this study that must be acknowledged. This study only evaluated one dimension of intervention fidelity, adherence. Next, photos were taken at two time points – during intervention implementation and three months after the intervention – capturing relatively short-term sustainment. During the coding process, shelving height was not systematically measured but instead was assumed by the coders and verified by using interrater reliability based on the pharmacy proportions interpreted from the photos. At Time 2 there were some instances where signage durability impacted the extent in which pharmacy staff was able to maintain the intervention, which can be rectified in the future. Utilizing photo coding allowed evaluation for organizational adherence to the physical components Senior Safe, however, this analysis does not intend to infer causality to organizational usage of the intervention. Finally, although our study collaborated with 67 different community pharmacy sites, all pharmacy sites were a part of one large Midwest health system and these findings may not be generalizable to pharmacies outside of the system.

Conclusion

Measuring intervention fidelity is useful in determining whether interventions are sustained and successfully transitioned into community-based practice. Photo coding is an innovative, cost-effective, and low-disruptive method to measure intervention fidelity for interventions representing system re-designs. Future research should further investigate a complementary relationship between fidelity and adaptation, and how to support organizations with fidelity discordance when adaptations are not a sufficient option. Additionally, future work should focus on durability of intervention signage in community-based settings to help support organizational sustainment.
