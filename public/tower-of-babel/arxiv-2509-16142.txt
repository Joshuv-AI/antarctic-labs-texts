Shanks bias in function fields
Seewoo Lee

Abstract
We study the function field analogue of Shanks bias. For Liouville function λ ⁡ ( f ) \lambda(f) , we compare the number of monic polynomials f f with λ ⁡ ( f ) ​ χ m ​ ( f ) = 1 \lambda(f)\chi_{m}(f)=1 and λ ⁡ ( f ) ​ χ m ​ ( f ) = − 1 \lambda(f)\chi_{m}(f)=-1 for a nontrivial quadratic character χ m \chi_{m} modulo a monic square-free polynomial m m over a finite field. Under Grand Simplicity Hypothesis (GSH) for L L -functions, we prove that λ ⋅ χ m \lambda\cdot\chi_{m} is biased towards + 1 +1 . We also give some examples where GSH is violated.



1   Introduction

Chebyshev’s bias
[
8
]
is a phenomenon in analytic number theory, referring to the observed preference for certain residue classes of primes in arithmetic progressions.
Specifically, it refers to the observed tendency for there to be more primes of the form
4n+3
than of the form
4n+1
among the primes up to some bound.
For general moduli, it is conjectured that non-quadratic residues are favored over quadratic residues.
Rubinstein and Sarnak were the first to prove this under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) and the Grand Simplicity Hypothesis (GSH) for Dirichlet
L
-functions.
Shanks
[
15
]
approached the bias from a different angle, examining higher-order effects.
Based on numerical experiments, he conjectured that there are more positive integers
n
such that
\lambda(n)\chi_{-4}(n)=+1
than those with
\lambda(n)\chi_{-4}(n)=-1
, where
\lambda
is the Liouville function and
\chi_{-4}
is the nontrivial quadratic character modulo
4
(See Section
2.1
for details).

Cha
[
4
]
studied a function field analogue of Chebyshev’s bias, closely following Rubinstein–Sarnak’s argument
[
14
]
, and proved the existence of bias under similar assumptions for
L
-functions.
He also exhibited examples with biases in unexpected directions when
L
-functions do not satisfy GSH.
Later works have extended these ideas to Galois extension of function fields
[
6
]
, explicity formula for the limiting distributions of biases
[
7
]
, multiple prime factors
[
9
]
, and elliptic curves
[
5
]
.

In this paper, we study the function field analogue of Shanks conjecture.
Let
p>2
be a prime and
A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
be the polynomial ring over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of size
q=p^{k}
.
Let
m\in A
be a monic square-free polynomial and
\chi_{m}
be the unique nontrivial quadratic character modulo
m
.
Writing the Liouville function on
A
as
\lambda:A\to\{\pm 1\}
, we prove that
\lambda\cdot\chi_{m}
is biased towards
+1
under GSH.
This gives an affirmative answer to a function field analogue of Problem 22 in the Comparative Prime Number Theory Problem List
[
10
]
.

Theorem

(Corollary
4.5
and
4.7
)
.

Let
A_{\pm}^{\lambda}(n;m)
be the number of non-constant monic polynomials
f\in A
of degree at most
n
with
\lambda(f)\chi_{m}(f)=\pm 1
.
Assume that GSH holds for the Dirichlet
L
-function
L(s,\chi_{m})
.
Then we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq k\leq n:A_{+}^{\lambda}(k;m)>A_{-}^{\lambda}(k;m)\}}{n}>\frac{1}{2}

where a closed formula for the density is obtained.
When
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, the density can be made arbitrarily close to
\frac{1}{2}
by choosing
m
appropriately with large enough degree.

We also study several variants (e.g. non-cumulative densities and replacing
\lambda
by the Möbius function
\mu
). In particular, we show there is no bias when restricting to square-free polynomials (Theorem
4.3
).
Example calculations of the densities are given in Section
5
.

To prove our main theorem, we first exhibit
L
-functions attached to these biases (Proposition
3.1
). We then apply the Kronecker–Weyl equidistribution theorem and Portmanteau’s theorem to derive explicit density formulas via elementary trigonometric-series calculations. In particular, one expresses the bias as a linear combination of cosines with
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent periods; equidistribution reduces the question to evaluating a constant term, and the final inequality follows from comparing symmetric polynomials in those cosines (Theorem
4.4
).
We also gave several numerical examples to illustrate our results (Section
5
).
Supplementary SageMath codes can be found in the GitHub repository
https://github.com/seewoo5/sage-function-field
.

2   Preliminaries

2.1   Chebyshev’s bias and its variants

Since Chebyshev first observed this bias in 1853
[
8
]
, it has been widely studied by several authors.
Littlewood
[
12
]
proved that
\pi_{+}(n)>\pi_{-}(n)
happens infinitely often, where
\pi_{\pm}(n)
is the number of primes
p\leq n
such that
p\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}
.
Rubinstein and Sarnak
[
14
]
gave more quantitative results.
Assuming Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) and Grand Simplicity Hypothesis (GSH), they proved that the
logarithmic density
of the set of
n
such that
\pi_{-}(n)>\pi_{+}(n)
is about
0.9959
, which is very close to
1
but not exactly.
In fact, they considered general “prime race” scenarios with general modulus where more than two congruence classes may exist, and computed the limiting distribution of the normalized bias.

Shanks
[
15
]
studied various aspects of Chebyshev’s bias.
He considered the normalized bias

\tau(n)=\frac{\pi_{-}(n)-\pi_{+}(n)}{\pi(n)}\sqrt{n}

and conjectured that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tau(n)=1

supported by numerical evidence.
In particular, he considered the higher-order effects and conjectured that, if
\pi_{\pm}^{(a)}(n)
counts the number of positive integers of the form
4k\pm 1
that are product of
a
primes (not necessarily distinct), then the average of the order
a
normalized bias

\tau^{(a)}(n)=\frac{\pi_{-}^{(a)}(n)-\pi_{+}^{(a)}(n)}{\pi_{-}^{(a)}(n)+\pi_{+}^{(a)}(n)}\sqrt{n}

is
(-1)^{a}

[
15
, Section 9]
. In other words, there are more numbers of the form
4k-1
(resp.
4k+1
) with odd (resp. even) number of prime factors than with even (resp. odd) number of prime factors.
Specifically, he claimed that the number of
n
with
\lambda(n)\chi_{-4}(n)=+1
exceeds that of
\lambda(n)\chi_{-4}(n)=-1
, where
\lambda
is the Liouville function

\lambda(n)=(-1)^{e_{1}+e_{2}+\cdots+e_{k}}\quad\text{when}\quad n=p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}

and
\chi_{-4}=\left(\frac{\cdot}{-4}\right)
is the nontrivial quadratic Dirichlet character modulo
4
. Note that the
L
-function for
\lambda(n)\chi_{-4}(n)
is simply given by

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\lambda(n)\chi_{-4}(n)}{n^{s}}=\frac{(1-2^{-2s})\zeta(s)}{L(s,\chi_{-4})}

(1)

and the bias might be related to the analytic properties of this
L
-function (
1
).
The Problem 22 in the comparative prime number theory problem list
[
10
]
asks to compute logarithmic density of the corresponding set (or replacing
\chi_{-4}
with other quadratic Dirichlet characters), assuming GRH and GSH for the relevant
L
-functions.

2.2   Function fields,
L
-functions, and Chebyshev’s bias

We fix an odd prime
p
.
Let
A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
be the polynomial ring over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of size
q=p^{k}
.
Let
A^{\circ}\subset A
be the subset of monic polynomials.
For a monic square-free polynomial
m\in A^{\circ}
, let
\chi_{m}(f):=\left(\frac{f}{m}\right)
be the unique nontrivial quadratic character modulo
m
.
Möbius function
\mu
and Liouville function
\lambda
are defined on
A
as

\displaystyle\mu(f)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k}&f=g_{1}\cdots g_{k},\text{ $f$ is square-free and $g_{1},\dots,g_{k}$ are distinct irreducible factors}\\
0&f\text{ is not square-free}\end{cases}

(2)

\displaystyle\lambda(f)

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{e_{1}+\cdots+e_{k}},\,\text{if }f\text{ factors as }g_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots g_{k}^{e_{k}}.

(3)

Especially, they agree on square-free polynomials.

Riemann zeta function for
A
is defined as

\zeta(s)=\sum_{f\in A^{\circ}}\frac{1}{|f|^{s}}=\frac{1}{1-q^{1-s}}

where
|f|:=q^{\deg f}
for
f\in A
.
If
\chi
is a Dirichlet character on
A
with modulus
m\in A
, then the Dirichlet
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
is similarly defined as

L(s,\chi)=\sum_{f\in A^{\circ}}\frac{\chi(f)}{|f|^{s}}.

We often write
L
-functions as a function in
u=q^{-s}
and write as
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)
.
Both functions admit Euler product over monic irreducible polynomials as

\displaystyle\zeta(s)

\displaystyle=\prod_{P}(1-|P|^{-s})^{-1},

\displaystyle L(s,\chi)

\displaystyle=\prod_{P}(1-\chi(P)|P|^{-s})^{-1}.

When
\chi
is a nontrivial character, it is known that the Dirichlet
L
-function becomes a polynomial in
u
of degree at most
\deg m-1

[
13
, Proposition 4.3]
.
By the Riemann Hypothesis for function fields, the zeros of
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)
are either
u=1
(if this happens, it is always a simple zero) or have norm
q^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
For quadratic characters, we have:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
4
, Proposition 6.4]
)
.

Let
\chi
be a quadratic Dirichlet character of modulus
m
and let
M=\deg m
.

1.

If
M
is odd, then
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)\in\mathbb{Z}[u]
is a polynomial of degree
M-1
where all zeros have norm
q^{\frac{1}{2}}
and can be written as

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=\prod_{j=1}^{M^{\prime}}(1-\sqrt{q}e^{i\theta_{j}}u)(1-\sqrt{q}e^{-i\theta_{j}}u)=\prod_{j=1}^{M^{\prime}}(1-2\sqrt{q}\cos\theta_{j}u+qu^{2})

for
M^{\prime}=(M-1)/2
and
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.

If
M
is even, then
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)\in\mathbb{Z}[u]
is a polynomial of degree
M-1
where it has a simple zero at
u=1
and all the other zeros have norm
q^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
It can be written as

\mathcal{L}(u,\chi)=(1-u)\prod_{j=1}^{M^{\prime}}(1-\sqrt{q}e^{i\theta_{j}}u)(1-\sqrt{q}e^{-i\theta_{j}}u)=(1-u)\prod_{j=1}^{M^{\prime}}(1-2\sqrt{q}\cos\theta_{j}u+qu^{2})

for
M^{\prime}=(M-2)/2
and
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}
.

In
[
4
]
, Cha proved a version of Chebyshev’s bias in function fields of odd characteristic, by following the proof of Rubinstein–Sarnak
[
14
]
in the number field case.
As in
loc. cit.
, he defined a function field version of Grand Simplicity Hypothesis (GSH), which is on
\mathbb{Q}
-linear independence of arguments of inverse zeros of
L
-functions.
Under GSH, he showed that quadratic non-residues are preferred over quadratic residues modulo irreducible polynomials.
One noticeable difference from the number field case is that, in the function field case, there are examples where GSH does not hold.
In such case, the bias could happen in both expected and unexpected directions
[
4
, Section 5]
.

2.3   Kronecker–Weyl Equidistribution theorem

Kronecker–Weyl theorem is a following multidimensional generalization of the Weyl’s equidistribution theorem.

Theorem 2.2

(Kronecker–Weyl)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function on the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}=(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{d}
.
Let
\pi,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
be
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent real numbers.
Then
\{(n\theta_{1},\dots,n\theta_{d}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
is equidistributed and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(n\theta_{1},\dots,n\theta_{d})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g(\phi)\mathrm{d}\phi

(4)

where
\mathrm{d}\phi
is the normalized Haar measure on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\phi=1
.

Proof can be found in the Appendix of
[
11
]
.
In such case, the counting measure

\mathrm{d}\phi_{n}(S):=\frac{1}{n}\#\{1\leq k\leq n:(k\theta_{1},\dots,k\theta_{d})\in S\}

weakly converges to
\mathrm{d}\phi
, so by Portmanteau’s theorem
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{d}\phi_{n}(S)=\mathrm{d}\phi(S)

for any Borel set
S\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
From this, we can deduce the following corollary, which will be used later to compute various densities.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
be real numbers where
\{\pi,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{d}\}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent.
Let
\alpha,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d},\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
.
For any sequence
x_{n}\to 0
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\#\left\{1\leq k\leq n:\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_{j}\sin(n\theta_{j}+\omega_{j})>x_{n}\right\}=\mathrm{d}\phi\left(\left\{\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_{j}\sin(y_{j})>0\right\}\right)

(5)

Proof.

When
x_{n}
is identically
0
, this follows from the previous discussion applied to the Borel set

S=g^{-1}((0,\infty)),\quad g(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})=\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_{j}\sin(y_{j})

where
g
is continuous on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
For general
x_{n}
, fix
\epsilon>0
and let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
|x_{n}|<\epsilon
for all
n\geq N
.
Then
g(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})>\epsilon
implies
g(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})>x_{n}
and the limit (
5
) is bounded above by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\#\left\{1\leq k\leq n:\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_{j}\sin(n\theta_{j}+\omega_{j})>\epsilon\right\}=\mathrm{d}\phi\left(\left\{\alpha-\epsilon+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_{j}\sin(y_{j})>0\right\}\right).

Similarly, we obtain a lower bound

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\#\left\{1\leq k\leq n:\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_{j}\sin(n\theta_{j}+\omega_{j})>-\epsilon\right\}=\mathrm{d}\phi\left(\left\{\alpha+\epsilon+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_{j}\sin(y_{j})>0\right\}\right).

Since
\epsilon
is arbitrary, we can take
\epsilon\to 0
and obtain the desired result.
∎

3
L
-functions

To study Shanks bias, we consider the counts

\displaystyle a_{\pm}^{\mu}(n;m)

\displaystyle:=\#\{f\in A^{\circ}:\deg f=n,\,\mu(f)\chi_{m}(f)=\pm 1\},

(6)

\displaystyle a_{\pm}^{\lambda}(n;m)

\displaystyle:=\#\{f\in A^{\circ}:\deg f=n,\,\lambda(f)\chi_{m}(f)=\pm 1\},

(7)

for each
n\geq 1
.
Define the
cumulative
counts

A_{\pm}^{\bullet}(n;m):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{\pm}^{\bullet}(k;m)

(8)

for
\bullet\in\{\mu,\lambda\}
.
Then our goal is to analyze the biases

\displaystyle b^{\bullet}(n;m)

\displaystyle:=a_{+}^{\bullet}(n;m)-a_{-}^{\bullet}(n;m)

(9)

\displaystyle B^{\bullet}(n;m)

\displaystyle:=A_{+}^{\bullet}(n;m)-A_{-}^{\bullet}(n;m),

(10)

i.e. how often do we have
b^{\bullet}(n;m)>0
or
B^{\bullet}(n;m)>0
.

The following proposition shows that the biases have nice
L
-functions.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

\displaystyle L^{\lambda}(s;m)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{f\in A^{\circ}}\frac{\lambda(f)\chi_{m}(f)}{|f|^{s}},

(11)

\displaystyle L^{\mu}(s;m)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{f\in A^{\circ}}\frac{\mu(f)\chi_{m}(f)}{|f|^{s}}.

(12)

and define
\mathcal{L}^{\bullet}(u;m):=L^{\bullet}(s;m)
for
u=q^{-s}
and
\bullet\in\{\lambda,\mu\}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}(u;m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}b^{\lambda}(n;m)u^{n}

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mu}(u;m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}b^{\mu}(n;m)u^{n}

(14)

and they admit Euler factorizations

\displaystyle L^{\lambda}(s;m)

\displaystyle=\prod_{P}\left(1+\frac{\chi_{m}(P)}{|P|^{s}}\right)^{-1}

(15)

\displaystyle L^{\mu}(s;m)

\displaystyle=\prod_{P}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{m}(P)}{|P|^{s}}\right)=\frac{1}{L(s,\chi_{m})}.

(16)

Proof.

We have

\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}(u;m)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in A^{\circ}\\
\deg f=n\end{subarray}}\lambda(f)\chi_{m}(f)\right)u^{n}

and the
n
-th coefficient of
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}(u;m)
is
a_{+}^{\lambda}(n;m)-a_{-}^{\lambda}(n;m)=b^{\lambda}(n;m)
by definition.
Moreover, since
\lambda
and
\chi_{m}
are multiplicative,
L^{\lambda}(s;m)
admits an Euler factorization

L^{\lambda}(s;m)=\prod_{P}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{m}(P)}{|P|^{s}}+\frac{\chi_{m}(P^{2})}{|P|^{2s}}-\cdots\right)=\prod_{P}\left(1+\frac{\chi_{m}(P)}{|P|^{s}}\right)^{-1}.

The identity (
14
) for
\mathcal{L}^{\mu}(u;m)
follows similarly, while the Euler factorization (
16
) comes from the multiplicativity of
\mu,\chi_{m}
and
\mu(P^{2})=0
.
∎

As a corollary, we obtain a simple expression for
L^{\lambda}(s;m)
analogous to (
1
).

Corollary 3.2
.

Write
m=m_{1}\cdots m_{r}
as a product of distinct irreducible monic polynomials
m_{i}
in
A
and let
M_{i}:=\deg m_{i}
.
Then

\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}(u;m)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1-u^{2M_{i}})}{1-qu^{2}}\,\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{m})}.

(17)

In particular, this shows that
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}(u;m)
is a rational function in
u
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{L^{\lambda}(s;m)}{L^{\mu}(s;m)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{P}\left(1-\frac{\chi_{m}(P)^{2}}{|P|^{2s}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{P\nmid m}\left(1-\frac{1}{|P|^{2s}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\zeta(2s)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1-|m_{i}|^{-2s})

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1-u^{2M_{i}})}{1-qu^{2}}

and the results follows from (
16
).
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

The generating functions for the cumulative biases are given by

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}B^{\bullet}(n;m)u^{n}=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\bullet}(u;m)-1}{1-u},\quad\bullet\in\{\mu,\lambda\}.

(18)

Proof.

Both follow from the following identity

\frac{\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}u^{n}}{1-u}=\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}u^{n}\right)(1+u+u^{2}+\cdots)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}a_{k}\right)u^{n}.

∎

4   Density and biases

The natural densities for the degree of polynomials are defined as

\displaystyle\delta_{+}^{\bullet}(m):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq k\leq n:A_{+}^{\bullet}(k;m)-A_{-}^{\bullet}(k;m)>0\}}{n},

\displaystyle\delta_{0}^{\bullet}(m):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq k\leq n:A_{+}^{\bullet}(k;m)-A_{-}^{\bullet}(k;m)=0\}}{n},

\displaystyle\delta_{-}^{\bullet}(m):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq k\leq n:A_{+}^{\bullet}(k;m)-A_{-}^{\bullet}(k;m)<0\}}{n}.

for
\bullet\in\{\mu,\lambda\}
.
We also consider the
non-cumulative
version of the densities as

\displaystyle\delta_{+}^{\bullet,\mathsf{nc}}(m):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq k\leq n:a_{+}^{\bullet}(k;m)-a_{-}^{\bullet}(k;m)>0\}}{n},

\displaystyle\delta_{0}^{\bullet,\mathsf{nc}}(m):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq k\leq n:a_{+}^{\bullet}(k;m)-a_{-}^{\bullet}(k;m)=0\}}{n},

\displaystyle\delta_{-}^{\bullet,\mathsf{nc}}(m):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq k\leq n:a_{+}^{\bullet}(k;m)-a_{-}^{\bullet}(k;m)<0\}}{n}.

The following lemma is helpful for the computations that follow.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
a,\theta,\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{k}\sin(k\theta+\omega)=\frac{a\sin(\theta+\omega)-a^{2}\sin\omega-a^{n+1}\sin((n+1)\theta+\omega)+a^{n+2}\sin(n\theta+\omega)}{1-2a\cos\theta+a^{2}}.

(19)

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\sin(2\theta)+\sin(4\theta)+\cdots+\sin(2k\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos\theta-\cos((2k+1)\theta)}{2\sin\theta}

(20)

\displaystyle\sin\theta+\sin(3\theta)+\cdots+\sin((2k+1)\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\cos((2k+2)\theta)}{2\sin\theta}

(21)

Proof.

Use
a^{k}\sin(k\theta+\omega)=a^{k}\Im(e^{i(k\theta+\omega)})=\Im((ae^{i\theta})^{k}\cdot e^{i\omega})
and the geometric series formula.
The last two equations easily follow from (
19
).
∎

Let us consider the
\mu
-bias first.
Since the
L
-functions for
b^{\lambda}(n;m)
and
B^{\lambda}(n;m)
are rational functions, they satisfy (
M
-term) linear recurrence relations.
Thus, one can express
b^{\lambda}(n;m)
and
B^{\lambda}(n;m)
as linear combinations of trigonometric functions.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
m\in A
be a monic square-free polynomial of degree
M
and
\chi_{m}
be the unique nontrivial quadratic character modulo
m
.
Define
M^{\prime}
and
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M^{\prime}}
as in Theorem
2.1
.
Then we have

b^{\mu}(n;m)=q^{\frac{n}{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{M^{\prime}}\gamma_{j}\sin(n\theta_{j}+\phi_{j})+\begin{cases}\delta&\text{if }2\mid M\\
0&\text{if }2\nmid M\end{cases}

(22)

and

\displaystyle B^{\mu}(n;m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{M-1}{2}}\gamma_{j}\frac{q^{\frac{1}{2}}\sin(\theta_{j}+\phi_{j})-q\sin\phi_{j}-q^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\sin((n+1)\theta_{j}+\phi_{j})+q^{\frac{n+2}{2}}\sin(n\theta_{j}+\phi_{j})}{1-2q^{\frac{1}{2}}\cos\theta_{j}+q}

(23)

\displaystyle+\begin{cases}n\delta&\text{if }2\mid M\\
0&\text{if }2\nmid M\end{cases}

for some
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{M^{\prime}},\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{M^{\prime}},\delta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

If
M
is odd, then by Theorem
2.1
,
b^{\mu}(n;m)
satisfies the homogenous linear recurrence relation

\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}a_{k}b^{\mu}(n-k;m)=0

when
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{m})=\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}a_{k}u^{k}
. The characteristic polynomial of this recurrence relation is the reciprocal polynomial of
\mathcal{L}(u,\chi_{m})
, which has roots
q^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{i\theta_{j}}
and
q^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-i\theta_{j}}
for
j=1,\dots,M^{\prime}
.
Hence
b^{\mu}(n;m)
can be expressed as a linear combination of
q^{\frac{n}{2}}e^{in\theta_{j}}
and
q^{\frac{n}{2}}e^{-in\theta_{j}}
, and since they are all real, we can write it as a linear combination of
q^{\frac{n}{2}}\sin(n\theta_{j}+\phi_{j})
for some
