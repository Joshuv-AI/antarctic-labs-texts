Impact of PharmaTE trial on glycemic control, diabetes knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life in type 2 diabetes patients: a mixed-methods study protocol
Authors: Nour Aymn Ahmad, Rabia Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a significant public health issue that requires lifelong management. Effective management requires a comprehensive approach integrating technology and clinical pharmacists into the healthcare team to improve glycemic control, patient knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life. This study aims to evaluate the impact of a clinical pharmacist-led tele-educational intervention (PharmaTE trial) on glycemic control and patients’ outcomes among patients with type 2 diabetes at a secondary care hospital in Ras Al Khaimah, United Arab Emirates (UAE).Methods:A mixed-methods embedded design will be employed, consisting of a randomised controlled trial (RCT) as the quantitative phase and a prospective descriptive qualitative study, a total of 154 adult patients diagnosed with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes, will be recruited and randomly assigned to either the (PharmaTE trial) intervention group (IG), which will receive five tele-education sessions led by a clinical pharmacist, or a control group (CG) receiving standard care. The primary outcome will be improved glycemic control, specifically achieving a target HbA1c level < 6.5% for individuals aged 18–65. Secondary outcomes will include improved diabetes knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life, assessed using a validated Arabic version of the questionnaires: the Michigan Diabetes Knowledge Tool (MDKT), Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS), and Diabetes Quality of Life (DQOL). Moreover, the glycemic control will be evaluated by measuring HbA1c levels.Discussion:Compared to the control group, participants with uncontrolled glycemic levels are anticipated to show improvements in glycemic control, disease knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life upon completion of the PharmaTE trial. These expected outcomes highlight the importance of adopting innovative, technology-based strategies and integrating clinical pharmacists into multidisciplinary care teams to enhance diabetes management. The PharmaTE intervention may offer an effective model for improving patient engagement, optimising treatment adherence, and ultimately advancing overall diabetes care and outcomes among patients with type 2 diabetes. The study protocol was registered as a clinical trial on ClinicalTrials.gov with the identifier NCT07043816 on June 20, 2025, and it was registered prospectively.Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT07043816.KEYWORDS: Clinical pharmacist, glycemic control, diabetes knowledge, medication adherence, quality of life, type 2 diabetes

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a significant noncommunicable disease and represents a major emerging challenge in clinical and public health contexts (Al-Lawati, 2017). It accounts for 5.2% of all deaths worldwide, highlighting its substantial impact on health systems and populations worldwide (Tripathy et al., 2017). The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) predicts that approximately 537 million adults are living with diabetes (Cho et al., 2015). This number is expected to rise to 643 million by 2030, underscoring the urgent need for effective interventions (Al-Busaidi et al., 2018; Mohamed et al., 2017).

The Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) comprises six member countries: the United Arab Emirates (UAE), the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman, and Qatar. As of 2023, the total population of the region is approximately 57.6 million (WAM. WAM News, 2025). These nations are characterised by significant rapid urbanisation and economic growth. Nevertheless, they face considerable public health challenges, particularly regarding DM (Aljulifi, 2021). The GCC regions have some of the highest global age-adjusted DM prevalence rates among adults, ranging from 14.8% in Oman to 20% in each of Kuwait, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia (Al-Busaidi et al., 2018). Moreover, the GCC countries’ healthcare systems are inadequately structured to face the growing DM burden, resulting in suboptimal care (Al-Busaidi et al., 2018). The IDF estimates that in 2021, the total adult population in the UAE was 8,057,100, with a diabetes prevalence of 12.3%, resulting in approximately 990,900 cases of diabetes among adults, and this is estimated to rise to 21.6% in 2030 (Shieb et al., 2019; Sulaiman et al., 2018). Around 40% of individuals aged over 60 years suffer from diabetes, primarily type 2 diabetes (Al Awadi et al., 2020).

Poorly controlled DM is a significant public health concern. It increases the risk of short and long-term complications due to prolonged exposure to high glucose levels over time, which significantly affects the quality of life (Farmaki et al., 2021). Therefore, reasonable glycemic control prevents further complications and organ damage (Zakir et al., 2023). The HbA1c is an essential marker for determining the control of diabetes and helps in predicting complications (Alam et al., 2021; Zakir et al., 2023).

Diabetic patients face significant challenges in managing their diabetes, which can negatively affect their overall health (Nikpour et al., 2022). Effective disease management can reduce symptoms and complications, improve glycemic control, and lower mortality risk and hospital readmission rates (Nikpour et al., 2022). Evidence has shown that diabetes management faces several significant challenges, including poor treatment adherence, limited health literacy, and limited patient engagement in their care (Pourhabibi et al., 2022). Patient education about diabetes and its management is an essential and often overlooked component of the diabetes management process (Phillips et al., 2018). One of the most critical factors in controlling chronic disease is patient participation in disease management (Chamoun et al., 2024). Limited patient engagement in diabetes management is a significant challenge that affects treatment outcomes and overall health (Chamoun et al., 2024). Several studies emphasise the complex nature of this issue, determining barriers such as a lack of customised information, emotional burden, and limited involvement in care decisions (Abdulhadi et al., 2013; Kirk et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Pooley et al., 2001). Understanding these factors is essential for developing effective strategies to improve patient engagement (Abdulhadi et al., 2013; Kirk et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Pooley et al., 2001; Sugandh et al., 2023).

The rapid progress of technologies, particularly the rise of telehealth, offers a promising future for chronic disease management, including diabetes (Barbosa & Silva, 2017). Telehealth utilises digital information and communication technologies to access health care services and manage health care remotely, including medical care, provider, and patient education (Barbosa & Silva, 2017). Studies revealed the efficacy of the telehealth approach on overall healthcare quality and that it can be an alternative to conventional monitoring of diabetic patients (El-Deyarbi et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2024). Telehealth is applicable in ensuring medication adherence and reducing type 2 diabetes complications, along with their associated financial burden (Whitehouse et al., 2020). Significantly, technology through telehealth has revolutionized type 2 diabetes management and provided more contact time with healthcare providers, leading to improved clinical outcomes (Whitehouse et al., 2020). A study indicated that the transition to telehealth has shown comparable outcomes to in-person visits, for instance, 69% of patients achieved an HbA1c reduction post-COVID (Li et al., 2024). Telehealth interventions have significantly improved glycemic control, patient knowledge, medication adherence, and overall quality of life among diabetes patients (Adesokun et al., 2024). Several studies indicate that telehealth, such as mobile and telephonic support, can improve health outcomes, particularly in managing type 1 and type 2 diabetes (Adesokun et al., 2024; Kaur et al., 2015; O’Connor et al., 2014; Van et al., 2024).

Telehealth, through technology utilisation, allows pharmacists to play a crucial role in providing services such as medication management, counselling, and educational support (Badil, 2023). This enhances patient access to care and improves health outcomes, bridging healthcare delivery gaps, particularly for underserved populations (Badil, 2023). Pharmacists’ role in telehealth for diabetes management has gained importance, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic (Ahmed et al., 2023). Telehealth improves patient care by enabling effective medication management, improving patients’ knowledge, improving glycemic control, and addressing the burden of healthcare access (Curran et al., 2023). This transition to telehealth has been witnessed in various studies, highlighting the pharmacist's significant role in managing chronic disease (Ahmed et al., 2023; Curran et al., 2023; Mukherjee et al., 2023).

Pharmacists provide comprehensive medication management through telehealth, allowing for timely interventions and drug interaction monitoring, essential for diabetic patients on polypharmacy (Lisni et al., 2024). A study indicated that patients receiving telehealth education led by pharmacists had a 3.653 times higher probability of achieving reasonable glycemic control than those who did not receive a telehealth consultation (Lisni et al., 2024). In another study, patients’ HbA1c levels decreased from 10.2% to 9.2% during five months, demonstrating the effectiveness of pharmacist-led telehealth interventions (Mukherjee et al., 2023).

Rationale for study

Patient education is essential for effective disease management, particularly in diabetes care (AlHaqwi et al., 2023). Pharmacist-led educational interventions can significantly improve patients’ understanding of their condition, improving lifestyle modifications and treatment regimen adherence (Bukhsh et al., 2022).

A systematic review identified only six relevant studies across four Arab countries, two in Jordan and the UAE, and one each in Sudan and Iraq. These studies demonstrate that pharmacist-led patient care or education interventions in diabetes management can lead to positive outcomes, including improved glycemic control, patient knowledge, and medication adherence (Mikhael et al., 2019). However, studies remain absent in certain areas, such as Ras Al Khaimah (RAK), and there remains a general need for more comprehensive and robust research across various Arab countries.

Despite growing evidence that tele-education interventions, such as short message service (SMS) reminders, video calls, interactive voice response (IVR), and software-based tools, can significantly enhance medication adherence and patient knowledge in diabetes management (Belete et al., 2023; Conway & Kelechi, 2017). Further research used to determine which digital health interventions are most effective for diabetes patients (Conway & Kelechi, 2017; Lindenmeyer et al., 2006). While some studies indicate that virtual education contributes positively to medication adherence, its direct influence on patients’ quality of life is still being explored (Anmaria et al., 2023). Moreover, although improved adherence is generally associated with better clinical outcomes, comprehensive evaluations of these interventions’ long-term impact are limited (Anmaria et al., 2023). Given the escalating burden of diabetes and its strain on healthcare systems, there is an urgent need for innovative, evidence-based strategies. One such promising approach is a clinical pharmacist-led tele-education model, as proposed in the PharmaTE trial. This study aims to address a critical gap in current research and offer a practical solution to enhance both patient care and medication adherence in individuals with type 2 diabetes.

Objectives and hypothesis

The main objective of the study is to evaluate the impact of the intervention (PharmaTE trial) on glycemic control, patient knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life among type 2 diabetes patients.

The specific objectives of the RCT study are:

To assess the impact of the PharmaTE trial on glycemic control, specifically measuring changes in HbA1C levels among patients with type 2 diabetes.

To assess the impact of the PharmaTE trial on glycemic control, specifically measuring changes in HbA1C levels among patients with type 2 diabetes.

To evaluate the change in the level of knowledge regarding diabetes among type 2 diabetes patients after completing the PharmaTE trial.

To evaluate the change in the level of knowledge regarding diabetes among type 2 diabetes patients after completing the PharmaTE trial.

To evaluate the impact of the PharmaTE trial on medication adherence among type 2 diabetes patients.

To evaluate the impact of the PharmaTE trial on medication adherence among type 2 diabetes patients.

To measure the improvement of type 2 diabetes patients’ quality of life after the completion of the PharmaTE trial.

To measure the improvement of type 2 diabetes patients’ quality of life after the completion of the PharmaTE trial.

To evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of the PharmaTE trial intervention following its implementation in a secondary care hospital setting.

To evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of the PharmaTE trial intervention following its implementation in a secondary care hospital setting.

We hypothesise that the PharmaTE trial, involving the interventional group (IG), will have a significant impact on glycemic control, patient knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life at the end of the study compared to those assigned to the control group (CG). Furthermore, we hypothesise that the PharmaTE Trial will be perceived as both feasible and acceptable by patients randomised to the intervention group (IG).

Methods

Study design

A mixed-methods embedded study design, consisting of a randomised controlled trial (RCT) as the quantitative phase and a prospective descriptive qualitative study, will be used to evaluate both the effectiveness and the contextual understanding of the intervention.

Quantitative phase

A prospective, two-arm, randomised controlled trial (RCT) designed as a superiority trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The intervention consists of five tele-educational sessions delivered at 2-month intervals (months 0, 2, 4, 6, 8), with outcomes assessed at baseline and 11 months (3 months after the final session). The two arms will include (1) an interventional arm where patients diagnosed with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes will receive the PharmaTE trial, and (2) a control arm where patients diagnosed with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes will receive usual standard care without any intervention from a clinical pharmacist.

The prospective nature of the study allows participants to be followed forward in time from the point of recruitment, facilitating the collection of real-time data on outcomes as they occur. The RCT design aims to provide robust, reliable evidence on the effectiveness of the intervention, ultimately contributing to improved health management strategies for diabetes patients. The study will compare the tele-education intervention with standard care in patients with type 2 diabetes at a secondary care hospital in Ras Al-Khaimah.

Due to the nature of the intervention study, the clinical pharmacist conducting the PharmaTE trial will be aware of whether the patient will be assigned to the PharmaTE trial interventional group (IG) or control group (non-blinded). However, the investigators who will collect the data from each patient before and after the intervention will be blinded.

Qualitative phase

A prospective qualitative descriptive design will be employed to explore participants’ experiences and perceptions regarding the PharmaTE trial. Data will be collected through semi-structured interviews conducted three months after the intervention. Interviews will be carried out via virtual meetings or phone calls, depending on participants’ preferences. This approach will allow an in-depth understanding of the acceptability, feasibility, and perceived impact of the PharmaTE trial from the patients’ perspective.

The flow of participants through the study, from enrolment to final analysis, is summarised in the CONSORT flow diagram (Figure 1). Reporting of the study will adhere to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) template (Chan et al., 2013). Reporting has also been guided by the CONSORT extension for randomised controlled trials conducted using cohorts and routinely collected data (CONSORT-ROUTINE): checklist with explanation and elaboration (Kwakkenbos et al., 2021).

Study setting and site

The pharmacist intervention, quantitative data collection, and the qualitative phase will take place in a private counselling room at the Diabetic Excellence Center in IBOH, the secondary care hospital of Ras Al-Khaimah (RAK). RAK is the northernmost of the seven emirates in the UAE and its fourth-largest city. In 2023, its population reached 400,000, making it the sixth-most populous city in the UAE (Lydia et al., 2024). Ibrahim Bin Hamad Obaidullah Hospital (IBOH) is a secondary care public hospital located in RAK. Among more than ten hospitals in the region, IBOH stands out for its comprehensive services and specialised centres, including a kidney dialysis unit, a thalassemia centre, and a diabetic excellence centre. The hospital holds international accreditations from the Joint Commission International (JCI) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and is recognised by the American Heart Association as a Primary Stroke Center. With around 200 beds, IBOH serves 40,000–50,000 patients annually across various specialties, including endocrinology, cardiology, nephrology, and internal medicine (Emirates Health Services, 2024). The Diabetic Excellence Centre provides multidisciplinary care, such as diabetes, nutrition, psychiatry, ophthalmology, and laboratory. Every Tuesday and Wednesday, it is held with either a new or follow-up appointment, accommodating 20–50 patients per clinic day.

Participants

Quantitative phase

Patients diagnosed with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes within the past 12 months and seeking diabetes management at the study site will be invited to participate in this study. A study team member will screen interested patients and provide them with written and verbal information. To support recruitment, an informational poster will also be displayed in the reception area to inform and motivate potential participants.

Qualitative phase

For the qualitative component, a purposive sample of patients from the PharmaTE trial IG will be invited to participate in semi-structured interviews. The sampling strategy will aim to capture maximum variation in terms of age, gender, diabetes duration, and treatment experiences until data saturation is achieved. Participants must be willing and able to discuss their experiences with pharmacist-led education.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Quantitative phase (RCT)

Patients diagnosed with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes (defined as glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c] > 7%) (Korcegez et al., 2017), who are treated in outpatient treatment, will be included if they are: adults (18–65 years) of either gender, prescribed at least one antidiabetic medication, the record of the blood tests (HbA1C) of 7% or more within the previous 12 months, have access to either a telephone or a mobile phone, treated in IBOH in RAK, Arabic-speaking patients, and patients who understand study information and are able to give written informed consent.

Patients will be excluded if they are less than 18 years old, pregnant ladies and patients in the emergency department, having severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, diagnosed with gestational diabetes, patients with vision or hearing impairments and psychological problems, immunocompromised patients, such as organ transplants, AIDS, cancer patients, and patients on immunosuppressant therapy, lacking access to either a telephone or mobile phone, as well as those without reliable internet access, non-Arabic-speaking patients, and unwilling to participate.

Qualitative phase

Patients will be drawn from the PharmaTE trial IG. Patients will be included if they complete at least 4 tele-educational sessions in the PharmaTE trial and are willing to share personal experiences in an interview. Exclusion will apply to those who decline participation or are unable to engage in an interview due to health or communication limitations.

Recruitment

A recruitment visit appointment will be organised for the quantitative phase. At this appointment, the investigator, who will be blinded to the patient group allocations, will obtain written informed consent and conduct a baseline assessment. Eligible patients will be enrolled in the trial and randomly assigned to either the PharmaTE trial IG or a CG. All participants in the IG will receive tele-educational intervention from the clinical pharmacist (PharmaTE). Patients in the CG will receive standard care from the clinical pharmacist during routine medication dispensing. They will continue to visit the hospital pharmacy to fill their prescriptions. However, they will not be restricted from contacting the clinical pharmacist for advice.

For the qualitative phase, potential participants will be approached by the investigator after completion of the PharmaTE trial. The investigator will explain the purpose of the interviews, and consent will be obtained.

Sample size

Quantitative phase (RCT)

The sample size is calculated according to the guidelines provided by Kelly and Rodgers (2000), using a special formula for clinical trials for continuous outcome variables and superiority significance (Kelly & Rodgers, 2000). The parameters used are as follows: δ = minimum clinically important difference, S = Polled standard deviation of both comparison groups, α = Significance level, and β = z-score for the desired power. Based on a previously conducted study, the following values are considered: δ = 1, S = 2.1, α = 0.05, and β = 0.2 (Korcegez et al., 2017). Consequently, the estimated sample size for each arm will be 67 participants. After considering the dropout or missing data, an additional 15% is added to the sample, resulting in 77 participants for each arm to be recruited. This calculation has been validated through consultation with a statistician, confirming that 154 participants will be enrolled in the study.

Qualitative phase

The sample size is guided by data saturation for qualitative research, whereby no new themes appear from the data (Glaser & Strauss, 2017) It is anticipated that 15–20 semi-structured interviews with participants from the PharmaTE trial IG will be sufficient to achieve data saturation. (Glaser & Strauss, 2017).

Sample recruitment procedures

Eligible patients must sign the written informed consent forms before any study-related procedure. Support will be provided to patients with limited literacy or those who request it. The study protocol will be explained verbally. Additionally, hospital medical records of potentially eligible patients will be cross-checked for verification to ensure that the included patients meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

During the first interaction, eligible patients will be informed about the purpose of the study, and the investigator will obtain the written informed consent. The baseline assessment for the dependent variables will be conducted during the same visit by an investigator. Patients whose HbA1c has been measured within the last two weeks will not be required to undergo repeat testing. Participants in the quantitative phase will receive the questionnaires either in hard copy or via a Google Form link, which will be scanned through a QR code, while participants in the qualitative phase will be invited to one-on-one semi-structured interviews after three months of completing the PharmaTE trial.

Randomisation and blinding

A simple random sampling method will utilise a list of eligible patients’ random numbers generated through Microsoft Excel® (version 365). This technique ensures that every individual has an equal opportunity to be chosen (Thomas, 2023).

Due to the structure of the intervention, the clinical pharmacist delivering the tele-educational intervention will know if a patient is in the intervention or control group (non-blinded). However, group assignments will be revealed to the clinical pharmacist delivering the intervention only after each patient has been enrolled to ensure allocation concealment.

The investigators who collect data from each patient before and after the intervention will remain blinded. Additionally, the same investigator will conduct the semi-structured interviews for the qualitative phase, ensuring consistency in data collection and maintaining methodological rigour.

Control group

Patients randomised to the control group will receive standard care provided in a diabetes excellence centre, including nurse assessment, physician assessment, and care provision by a multidisciplinary team. They will also visit the hospital pharmacy to fill their prescriptions without further clinical pharmacist intervention. However, there will be no restriction on contacting the clinical pharmacist for advice.

Interventional group (PharmaTE trial)

In addition to standard care, patients in the PharmaTE trial interventional group will receive tele-education led by a clinical pharmacist who has completed a training course and is certified as a diabetes educator. The tele-education programme is comprised of five individualised educational sessions, each lasting approximately 20–30 min and held at two-month intervals. These sessions are conducted via Zoom or by telephone, based on the patient's preference. Before the beginning of each session, participants will receive a reminder message along with the Zoom meeting invitation link about the session, covering various educational topics systematically related to diabetes management. Additionally, after each session, patients will be provided with a brochure outlining the main topics covered.

Furthermore, enrolment in other structured diabetes education programmes or additional interventions outside the study will be discouraged for both control group participants and those in the PharmaTE trial interventional group during the trial period. This is to avoid confounding effects on the study outcomes.

Educational module development, translation, and validation

Phase I. Development of the educational module

The educational module will be developed after a comprehensive revision of guidelines from the UAE Ministry of Health and Prevention, the World Health Organization, the American Diabetes Association (ADA), the European Association for the Study of Diabetes, and the IDF, combined with findings from previous interventional studies (Besemah et al., 2020; Bukhsh et al., 2018; Jörgens & Grüsser, 2010; Mirshahi et al., 2024; Powers et al., 2015).

The content of the educational module will integrate different core aspects of diabetes self-management.

Phase II. Translation of the educational module

The educational module will be translated in accordance with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommended guidelines (Granat et al., 2022). This process will involve a forward–backward translation procedure, ensuring accuracy, cultural appropriateness, and conceptual equivalence with the original English version (World Health Organizationo, 2025).

Phase III. Content validation and face validation of the educational module

A panel of experts will evaluate the content of the completed educational module for appropriateness and relevance. It has been recommended that the panel of experts should be comprised of at least five members from different disciplines to avoid chance agreement (Zamanzadeh et al., 2015). The interdisciplinary panel will comprise two clinical pharmacists, an endocrinologist, a dietitian, and a health communication researcher with an educational background to facilitate the exchange of ideas. Patient or public involvement was not included in the design or validation of the educational module.

Both content and face validation will be conducted using Content Validity Index (CVI) will be used to quantitatively assess the relevance and clarity of the module’s content (Jodalli et al., 2025). Content and face validation will be conducted using the Content Validity Index (CVI), with a threshold of ≥0.80 indicating acceptable content validity (Jodalli et al., 2025).

Following the CVI calculation, the module will undergo pilot testing with a small group of type-2 diabetes patients. Their understanding, engagement, and feedback will be closely monitored, and any identified issues will be documented (Jodalli et al., 2025). Based on expert feedback and pilot testing, final modifications will be made to the module to enhance clarity, accuracy, and cultural appropriateness before its implementation.

Plan of contact with the participant in the interventional group

Before each session, participants will receive a reminder message along with the Zoom meeting invitation link. Participants who do not respond will be contacted up to three times by phone. If there's still no response, such participants will remain included in the primary intention-to-treat analysis according to their randomised allocation. Their missing outcome data will be handled using the methods described in the Statistical Analysis section.

Discontinuation, withdrawal, and management of adverse events

Discontinuation or withdrawal criteria

Participants may discontinue or modify their involvement in the PharmaTE trial if they choose to withdraw, experience health concerns, or are unable to attend scheduled sessions despite repeated contact attempts. Any such changes will be documented, including the reason and date, to ensure transparency and adherence to the study protocol.

Harms and adverse event management

The PharmaTE trial is considered low-risk, and no physical or medical harms are anticipated. Participants will be encouraged to report any health concerns during the study, and any unexpected events will be documented and addressed according to standard clinical care.

Outcomes

Glycemic control assessed by measuring (HbA1C level) is the primary clinical outcome measure, and additional outcomes include patients’ knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life.

All outcomes will be collected at baseline (before the intervention) and three months after the intervention (post-intervention) in both the interventional and control groups.

Quantitative outcomes

Primary clinical outcome

Glycemic control

The primary outcome is improved glycemic control compared to the baseline. The glycemic level will be assessed by evaluating the HbA1c level at baseline and after completion of the intervention, in both the interventional and control groups.

Glycemic levels are considered normal when HbA1c levels are within predefined ranges, as suggested by the ADA, which recommends that adults with type 2 diabetes maintain an HbA1c of less than 6.5% (ElSayed et al., 2024; ElSayed et al., 2025). Both groups will assess these levels at baseline and after the end of the intervention.

Patients whose HbA1c has been measured within the last two weeks will not be required to undergo repeat testing.

Secondary outcomes

Diabetes knowledge

Patient knowledge about diabetes will be assessed using the Michigan Diabetes Knowledge Test (MDKT), which is a validated tool and has been utilised in several settings to evaluate diabetic patients’ knowledge. The Michigan survey instruments have been translated into several languages, including Arabic (Collins et al., 2011; Fitzgerald et al., 2016).

The Michigan Diabetic Knowledge Test (MDKT) is comprised of 14 multiple-choice questions. The knowledge test scores ranged from 0 to 14, with 14 representing the highest level of knowledge of diabetes and zero representing the lowest. We considered that a knowledge score of <7 constituted poor knowledge, 7–11 moderate knowledge, and 12–14 good knowledge (Al-Aboudi et al., 2016).

The Arabic version of the MDKT will be utilised, and permission for its use has already been obtained from the tool developer (Alhaiti et al., 2016).

Medication adherence

Medication Adherence will be assessed to evaluate the patient's compliance with their treatment regimen using the Arabic version of Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8),

The MMAS-8, created by Morisky et al. (1986) is a reliable and validated tool for evaluating medication adherence (Morisky et al., 1986). MMAS-8 has good validity and reliability, has also been proven to be a useful screening technique for chronic disease medications, and has been validated on other Arabic patients suffering from chronic diseases (Lee et al., 2013; Sweileh et al., 2014).

It has been verified and substantiated by numerous studies on a global scale, with over 110 versions and over 80 translations (Kishor et al., 2024). It consists of eight questions with a yes or no answer. Questions 1–7 offer ‘yes’ or ‘no’ answers, with ‘0’ points will be given for every ‘Yes’ response and a ‘1’ point will be given for every ‘No’ response. Question 8 will be presented with a 5-point Likert scale, and a score of ‘0’ will be assigned if response ‘0’ is chosen, and ‘1’ will be assigned if response ‘4’ is chosen. The responses ‘1, 2, 3’ will be graded as ‘0.25, 0.50, and 0.75,’ respectively. The total score ranged from 0 to 8, with higher scores representing better adherence. Scores < 6 are considered poor adherence, and scores ≥ 6 are considered high adherence (Kishor et al., 2024; Morisky et al., 1986)

Diabetic quality of life

The Diabetes Quality of Life (DQoL) questionnaire will assess diabetes quality of life (Al-Qerem et al., 2021). The questionnaire consists of 29 items divided into three factors: Satisfaction (14 items), Impact (11 items), and Worries (4 items). The satisfaction and impact questions include a 5-point Likert scale [very satisfied (1 point), quite satisfied (2 points), satisfied (3 points), a little satisfied (4 points), and very dissatisfied (5 points)].

The DQOL questionnaire has been previously field-tested and validated for its administration in type 2 diabetes patients and shown to be internally consistent, reproducible, and responsive (Al-Qerem et al., 2021). It is commonly used and translated into several languages, including Arabic. The Arabic version of the DQOL questionnaire has previously been validated. The permission to use the Arabic version of DQoL has already been obtained from its developers (Al-Qerem et al., 2021).

Qualitative outcome

Feasibility and acceptability of technology-assisted healthcare delivery

After completing the quantitative assessments, a semi-structured interview will be conducted at month 11 with a subset of patients from the IG, three months after completing the PharmaTE trial. The interview will include questions regarding participants’ age, gender, educational level, and duration of disease. The interviews will then focus on participants’ feedback, the challenges they faced, and their perceptions of the intervention's feasibility and acceptability. Semi-structured interviews will be conducted using an interview guide, which will be developed through a comprehensive literature review and expert input from a diabetes specialist, academicians, and clinical pharmacists. The topic guide will address key areas related to the clinical pharmacist-led tele-educational intervention, including participants’ expectations, perceived efficacy, and overall satisfaction, particularly within the context of technology-assisted healthcare delivery. The guide will be pilot-tested with two diabetes patients, who will be excluded from the final analysis.

The investigator will conduct the interviews via virtual meetings or phone calls. With participants’ consent, interviews will be audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic analysis supported by NVivo software. The interviewer will maintain reflexive notes throughout data collection.

Additional measures

A data collection form will be designed after a comprehensive literature review of similar studies conducted. These additional forms will be administered to obtain (1) socio-demographic information, (2) the duration of diabetes, the medicine used for diabetes treatment, other comorbidities, and family history. Additionally, the investigator will also collect laboratory data from the patients’ laboratory profiles.

Statistical analysis

Phase 1: quantitative analysis

The collected data will be entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. We will conduct descriptive statistical analyses to analyze continuous variables using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS) version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A P value less than 0.05 will indicate statistical significance, and data will be presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Primary analysis

The primary analysis will follow intention-to-treat (ITT) principles, including all randomised participants analyzed according to their original group allocation regardless of protocol adherence, intervention completion, or loss to follow-up. Continuous data will be assessed for normality; if normally distributed, data will be summarised by presenting the mean and its standard deviation (mean ± SD). The median and interquartile range will be reported if not normally distributed, while categorical data will be expressed as percentages (%) and frequencies. A Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test will be performed to evaluate the associations between categorical variables based on the expected count of cells. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality test will be applied, and the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney test will be performed based on normality. The One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) test or Kruskal–Wallis test will also be performed when applicable, based on normality. The Bonferroni Post hoc test will be applied for multiple group comparisons, and the results will be evaluated accordingly. The study’s findings will be conveyed through text, tables, figures, and graphs.

Missing data handling

Missing data patterns and extent will be reported. If missingness is ≥5% and data are missing at random (assessed via Little’s MCAR test), multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) will be used, incorporating baseline covariates (age, sex, diabetes duration), treatment group, and auxiliary variables. Sensitivity analyses will test alternative assumptions (best/worst-case scenarios). Subgroup or sensitivity analyses are not planned.

Phase 2: qualitative analysis

For qualitative data, thematic analysis will be utilised (Glaser & Strauss, 2017). Each interview will be transcribed verbatim. Following this, each transcript will be coded by using NVivo line by line. To enhance rigour, two researchers will independently code the transcripts, and discrepancies will be resolved by discussion. The initial coding framework will be checked for accuracy and completeness by reviewing two coded interview transcripts. Once all the interviews are coded, a list of all the codes will be generated after removing duplicates, and different codes will be sorted into potential themes. The relevant data extracts will be collated within potential themes. As new themes emerge, existing ones will be revisited and, where appropriate, revised or renamed to reflect evolving insights. The process will continue until no new themes are generated.

For incomplete interviews, available data will be included in the thematic analysis, with missing portions noted during coding.

Trial monitoring

Due to the low-risk nature of the trial, no independent Data Monitoring Committee is required. Additionally, the trial will be supervised monthly by the principal investigator, the study supervisor, the clinical pharmacist delivering the intervention, and the research team to ensure protocol adherence and data quality. Any issues or deviations will be addressed promptly. No interim analyses are planned; early termination will only be considered by the principal investigator in consultation with the study supervisor in case of unexpected safety or ethical concerns.

Any protocol modifications will be submitted to the ethical committee for approval, and the ClinicalTrials.gov registry (Identifier: NCT07043816) will be updated to ensure transparency.

Discussion

This study aims to explore the impact of the PharmaTE trial on glycemic control, diabetes knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life among type 2 diabetes patients. As one of the first RCTs conducted in the United Arab Emirates focused on this demographic, the findings will provide critical insights into the effectiveness of tele-education led by clinical pharmacists in managing diabetes.

The PharmaTE trial presents an innovative approach to diabetes care, integrating clinical pharmacists as essential members of the multidisciplinary team. By evaluating the feasibility and acceptability of such an intervention, this study will contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the role of clinical pharmacists in chronic disease management. The patient-centered nature of the tele-educational intervention is designed to enhance patients’ understanding of their condition, ultimately leading to improved self-management and health outcomes. Moreover, the trial will clarify the relationship between pharmacist-led tele-education and key diabetes-related outcomes, such as changes in HbA1c levels, enhanced diabetes knowledge, increased medication adherence, and improved patients’ quality of life. These findings could inform future healthcare practices, emphasising the importance of integrating clinical pharmacists into diabetes care, particularly in resource-limited settings.

The PharmaTE trial is poised to generate robust data supporting the development of larger, definitive RCTs. If successful, the RCT study could lead to widespread implementation of similar tele-education programmes in other healthcare settings, promoting collaborative care models that leverage the expertise of clinical pharmacists.

This study will emphasise the value of integrating clinical pharmacists into a multidisciplinary team in a diabetes excellence centre to improve patients’ clinical outcomes and overall health. Besides, the study explores a novel area of technology-assisted healthcare delivery by capturing patient experiences with a tele-educational intervention led by a clinical pharmacist, an approach that enhances accessibility, supports patient engagement, and promotes continuity of care.

Implications of the trial

PharmaTE trial seeks to provide robust evidence for the effectiveness of tele-education in enhancing diabetes management. This could potentially improve health outcomes for type 2 diabetes patients and contribute to better healthcare strategies across the region.

Strengths of the trial

The PharmaTE trial is the first RCT to be conducted in Ras Al-Khaimah, UAE, to evaluate the impact of a clinical pharmacist-led tele-educational intervention on glycemic control, diabetes knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life among type 2 diabetes patients. This study will emphasise the value of integrating clinical pharmacists into a multidisciplinary team in a diabetes excellence centre to improve patients’ clinical outcomes and overall health. Besides, the study explores a novel area of technology-assisted healthcare delivery by capturing patient experiences with a tele-educational intervention led by a clinical pharmacist, an approach that enhances accessibility, supports patient engagement, and promotes continuity of care.

Limitations and barriers to the trial

A potential limitation of the study, as noted in similar research studies, may include the short duration of the intervention, which may not be sufficient to observe a sustained change in glycemic control. A more extended study period allows for the sustained impact of the tele-educational programme (Bukhsh et al., 2018). Additionally, the study will be conducted at a single centre with a small number of participants and over one year, so the patients may not be entirely representative of the general diabetic population (Korcegez et al., 2017). Furthermore, participants may face challenges such as limited digital literacy or internet access, and those who choose to participate may be more motivated or technologically proficient than the general population, thus introducing selection bias.

Conclusion

The PharmaTE trial is the first RCT to be conducted in RAK, UAE, to evaluate the impact of a clinical pharmacist-led tele-educational intervention (PharmaTE trial) on glycemic control, diabetes knowledge, medication adherence, and quality of life among type 2 diabetes patients. The findings are expected to support the integration of telehealth and pharmacist-led education into diabetes care, offering a promising approach to enhance patient outcomes and chronic disease management.
