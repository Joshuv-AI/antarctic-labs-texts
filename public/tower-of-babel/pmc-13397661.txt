Linking fruit and stone morphology with germination and polyembryony in two mango genotypes, Olour and Kurukkan
Authors: Shikha Jain, Jai Prakash, S. K. Singh, Chavlesh Kumar, Kanhaiya Singh, Renu Pandey, Sandhya Sharma, Anshuman Singh, Ishu Kumari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMango (Mangifera indica L.) productivity improvement depends partly on the identification and characterization of suitable polyembryonic genotypes used in propagation. Understanding the relationship between fruit and stone morphology and early reproductive traits such as germination and polyembryony is essential for effective germplasm utilization. The present study aimed to evaluate morphological variability, germination behavior, and polyembryony in two polyembryonic mango genotypes, ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’, grown at the ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute, New Delhi.ResultsA total of 260 fruits were analyzed for fruit weight, fruit length, fruit width, stone weight, stone length, and stone width, along with germination percentage, number of seedlings per stone, and polyembryony. Descriptive statistics indicated greater variability in mass-based traits compared to linear dimensions. Linear models revealed strong varietal differences in fruit weight, fruit width, stone weight, and stone width, whereas stone length exhibited relatively weak differentiation between genotypes. Correlation analysis showed strong positive associations among most fruit and stone traits, suggesting coordinated fruit-stone development. Allometric analysis demonstrated consistent sub-isometric scaling relationships, indicating conserved developmental allocation patterns across genotypes. Logistic and Poisson generalized linear models indicated that fruit and stone morphological traits had negligible influence on germination, seedling number per stone, and polyembryony. Deviance-based variance partitioning further revealed that varietal effects explained less than 1% of the variation in these early reproductive traits.ConclusionsThe findings indicate strong developmental canalization of germination and polyembryony in polyembryonic mango genotypes. Despite clear morphological differences between ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’, early reproductive responses remain largely independent of fruit and stone morphology. These results provide important insights for germplasm characterization and support the use of polyembryonic genotypes in propagation-oriented mango improvement programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08781-7.

Introduction

Mango (Mangifera indica L.) holds significant socio-cultural and economic importance in India and in several other countries. It is often referred to as the “King of Fruits” for its attractive appearance, unique flavor, delicious taste, substantial nutritional value, and pleasant eating experience [1–3]. In India, the leading mango-producing country, mangoes are grown on approximately 2.39 million hectares, with a total annual production of around 22.39 million tons and an average productivity of ~ 9.36 metric tons per hectare [4]. While mango production has increased in India over the years, productivity has largely remained stagnant and, in some years, even declined [5]. Widely spaced orchards, large tree sizes, poor crop management, and the prevalence of biotic and abiotic stresses are major factors contributing to poor mango productivity in India [6–8]. The use of non-descript rootstocks of unknown parentage in grafting is another major bottleneck that impedes mango productivity growth [9, 10]. In India, commercial mango cultivars are usually grafted onto nondescript seedling rootstocks, which often results in large, unmanageable trees, labor-intensive harvesting, and lower-than-expected fruit yields [11].

Numerous studies have shown that rootstocks have a significant impact on mango scion cultivars, including the tree physiological relations and photosynthetic performance [12–14], leaf nutrient composition [15], root growth and tree size [16], regularity of bearing [14], and tree growth, flowering, fruit yield and quality [17, 18]. Such relationships are critical because they serve as the basis for selecting the best possible graft combination(s) for higher fruit yield and better fruit quality under specific environmental conditions [19]. However, studies on potential rootstocks and their effects on commercial mango scion cultivars remain largely unexplored [9].

Typically, mango grafting research has shown that rootstocks affect several physiological traits of the scion [20]. Grafting is the ideal method for commercial use because it maintains the genetic purity of the propagated variety. The production of zygotic and nucellar plantlets by polyembryonic rootstocks makes them essential for producing grafted mangoes. Nucellar seedlings maintain the genetic characteristics of the mother plant. Nucellar seedlings provide uniformity to the orchard, making them preferable for grafting [21]. Polyembryonic rootstocks, with more than 80% polyembryony, increase the likelihood of obtaining nucellar plants [22]. The process of mango rootstock breeding is complex and time-consuming, making it challenging to select suitable rootstocks for specific traits and conditions. Mango exhibits a relatively low effective fruit set of 0.1% and excessive fruit drop at various stages of fruit development, resulting in limited availability of hybrid stones for rootstock breeding [23]. These challenges underscore the need for precise methods to identify and select suitable hybrid stones for rootstock breeding, thereby ensuring the production of robust, uniform mango orchards. Morphological characterization remains one of the simplest and most widely used approaches for germplasm evaluation, especially in field conditions, where traits can be easily recorded and compared [24]. The stone refers to the hardened endocarp enclosing the seed in drupaceous fruits. However, variation in fruit and stone traits even within the same cultivar is common, often resulting from natural mutations, outcrossing, or ecological adaptations [21]. Such diversity underscores the need to assess germplasm using multivariate analysis, which enables simultaneous evaluation of multiple morphological descriptors and provides deeper insights into the patterns of diversity among polyembryonic rootstocks [25]. Morphological characterization is the first step in describing and classifying germplasm. Statistical techniques such as principal component analysis [26] and cluster analysis [27] are beneficial for evaluating collected accessions. Multivariate analysis, in particular, has proven to be a powerful tool for germplasm characterization and has been effectively applied in several fruit species, including tamarind [28], bael [29], and avocado [30].

Despite these advances, limited information is available on how fruit and stone morphology relate to germination dynamics and polyembryony in polyembryonic mango rootstocks.

Understanding these relationships is particularly important because early reproductive traits such as germination, seedling emergence, and polyembryony directly influence the efficiency of nursery production and the selection of uniform planting material. While previous studies have documented variability in morphological traits and polyembryony across mango genotypes [31, 32], the extent to which morphological variation contributes to these early developmental processes remains unclear.

In this context, the present study focuses on two widely used polyembryonic mango genotypes, ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’, which are known for their contrasting horticultural traits and rootstock potential. We hypothesized that these genotypes may differ significantly in fruit and stone morphology, germination behavior, and extent of polyembryony, and that morphological traits may influence early reproductive responses. Accordingly, the objectives of this study were to: (i) characterize variability in fruit and stone morphological traits, (ii) quantify germination percentage, seedling number per stone, and polyembryony, (iii) analyze relationships among traits using correlation and allometric approaches, and (iv) evaluate the extent to which morphological traits explain variation in germination and polyembryonic behavior. By integrating descriptive statistics, allometric modeling, and generalized linear modeling, this study aims to provide a statistically robust framework for identifying morphologically distinct yet developmentally stable mango rootstocks suitable for sustainable nursery production and orchard establishment.

Materials and methods

Study site

The experiment was conducted between December 2022 and August 2023 at the Main Experimental Farm of the Division of Fruits and Horticultural Technology, ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute, New Delhi (28º40’N 77°12’E, 228.6 m above mean sea level). The study area has a subtropical semi-arid climate with distinct summer, rainfall, and winter seasons. The soil nutrient analysis of the experimental main orchard revealed a well-balanced composition of essential macro and micronutrients, indicating its suitability for mango cultivation. The soil contained 2.780% nitrogen, 1.004% phosphorus, 1.752% potassium, and 3.858% calcium, providing adequate nutrient availability for plant growth. Additionally, magnesium was recorded at 0.459%, ensuring proper enzyme activation and physiological functions in plants. Among the micronutrients, zinc and copper were measured at 369.742 ppm and 300.981 ppm, respectively, which are crucial for enzyme activation and reproductive growth. The overall soil nutrient profile provides a conducive environment for rootstock development and sustainable mango production, supporting optimal tree vigor and productivity.

Experimental material

Although polyembryonic mango rootstocks play a crucial role in ensuring genetic uniformity and facilitating grafting, their systematic evaluation remains limited due to breeding challenges. High morphological variability and the absence of standardized assessment methods further hinder their effective use. Therefore, adopting robust multivariate techniques is vital for accurately characterizing and selecting elite rootstocks to support sustainable mango improvement. The selection of ‘Olour’ as a rootstock for this study is based on its well-documented salt tolerance, polyembryonic nature, dwarfing ability, and genetic uniformity, making it a valuable choice for improving commercial mango cultivars under diverse environmental conditions. Additionally, ‘Olour’ is compatible with most improved mango cultivars, including Langra, Himsagar, and Alphonso, providing greater flexibility in grafting combinations. Studies by Palaniappan [33] and Dubey et al. [34] confirm that ‘Olour’ exhibits superior salinity tolerance compared to other rootstocks such as ‘Kurukkan’, making it particularly suitable for cultivation in regions with problematic soils.

Nevertheless, ‘Kurukkan’ was included in the present study because, despite its relatively lower salinity tolerance than ‘Olour’, it possesses other desirable attributes such as dwarfing ability. ‘Kurukkan’ was identified as a salt-tolerant rootstock [35]. Its polyembryonic nature, which produces 2–6 seedlings per stone, ensures high genetic uniformity, crucial for maintaining consistent orchard performance.

Germination, polyembryony, and morphological measurements

The experiment consisted of two treatments (mango rootstocks - ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’). The Plant materials in the form of ripe fruits (n = 170 (‘Olour’) and n = 90 (‘Kurukkan’)) of mango polyembryonic genotypes ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’ were collected from the Mango Germplasm Block of the Division of Fruits and Horticultural Technology, ICAR- Indian Agricultural Research Institute, New Delhi. A total of 260 fruits were evaluated, comprising 170 from ‘Olour’ and 90 from ‘Kurukkan’. This sample size is adequate to ensure reliable estimates of variability and means, and to draw statistically valid inferences for comparisons between the two rootstocks. Fruit weight was recorded using a digital scale (± 0.01 g). In contrast, fruit length, fruit width, stone length, and stone width were measured using a Vernier caliper (± 0.01 mm). Stone weight was determined after pulp removal and drying. To prevent the fungus Sclerotium rolfsii from causing collar rot, the stones were washed, made pulp-free, and treated with Carbendazim 50% WP at 2 g/L of water before sowing. Carbendazim 50% WP is a systemic benzimidazole fungicide (FRAC 1) that controls soil-borne pathogens by inhibiting fungal mitosis and is commonly used for seed and nursery treatments. The treated stones were then marked and sown in the beds at 45 cm between rows and 25 cm between seedlings. Seeds were sown at a depth of 10–15 cm in a seedling bed with a depth of 25 cm of soil substrate. Recommended practices, including watering and weeding, were followed to ensure proper germination and seedling emergence. The germination percentage was recorded 30 days after sowing. The germination percentage was calculated by dividing the number of germinated stones by the total number of stones sown, multiplied by 100. The extent of polyembryony was determined by dividing the number of stones with more than one seedling by the total number of germinated stones, multiplied by 100. Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in the R environment (v. 4.5.1) [36]. Descriptive statistics for fruit and stone traits, including mean, standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), and range (min-max), were calculated. The distributional shape of the data was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis, and normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, recognizing its sensitivity to minor deviations at large sample sizes (n = 260).

Linear mixed-effects models were initially fitted for each trait, with tree identity specified as a random intercept and variety as a fixed effect. For most traits, models produced a single fit with zero tree-level variance. The random intercept (tree identity) was thus removed and subsequent analyses were conducted using ordinary linear models. Model assumptions were evaluated graphically using residuals-versus-fitted plots to assess linearity, Q-Q plots to examine residual normality, scale-location plots to evaluate homogeneity of variance, and leverage diagnostics with Cook’s distance to identify influential observations (Fig. 4). Raw-scale models exhibited clear heteroscedasticity, whereas residuals remained approximately centered and symmetric. Accordingly, traits were log-transformed prior to final model fitting to satisfy constant-variance assumptions. Pairwise associations among traits were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. Principal component analysis was performed to summarize the multivariate structure. Components were orthogonally rotated using varimax rotation to improve interpretability. Trait loadings were used to identify underlying biological dimensions.

Fig. 4Residual Diagnostic Plots for Raw-Scale Linear Models

Residual Diagnostic Plots for Raw-Scale Linear Models

Allometric relationships between fruit and stone traits were examined using both raw-scale linear models and log-log allometric models for corresponding trait pairs (stone weight-fruit weight, stone length-fruit length, and stone width-fruit width). Random effects were excluded because the tree-level variance was consistently negligible. Model fit was quantified using the coefficient of determination (R²). Germination and polyembryony were analyzed as binary responses using logistic regression fitted at the individual fruit level. In contrast, seedling emergence per stone (SS) was analyzed as a count response using Poisson generalized linear models restricted to germinated fruits. Variety was included as a fixed categorical effect in all models. Morphological traits were first evaluated individually as covariates; owing to strong intercorrelations among traits, additional pairwise models were fitted as sensitivity analyses rather than for primary inference. Model adequacy was assessed using residual deviance and Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), with nested models compared based on changes in deviance.

To quantify varietal structuring of early reproductive outcomes, deviance-based variance partitioning was performed for germination, SS, and polyembryony. For each response, an intercept-only model represented total variation, and a variety-only model estimated genetic contribution. The proportion of deviance explained by variety was calculated as the reduction in residual deviance relative to the null model, providing an effect-size measure independent of morphological covariates. Analyses of SS and polyembryony were restricted to germinated fruits, with missing SS values treated as structural.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics reveal pronounced heterogeneity among mass-based traits relative to linear dimensions (Table 1). Fruit weight (FWt) and stone weight (SWt) exhibited the highest dispersion (CV ~ 27.0%), with ranges spanning more than threefold, whereas fruit length (FL) and stone length (SL) were markedly constrained (CV ≤ 8.8%; range ≤ 1.6-fold). Intermediate variability was observed for fruit and stone widths (FW, SW; CV = 13.6–17.0%). All traits displayed small negative skewness (− 0.48 to − 0.06) and uniformly negative kurtosis (− 0.49 to − 1.22), indicating broadly symmetric, platykurtic distributions and the absence of extreme values. Shapiro–Wilk tests detected departures from normality for most traits; however, given the modest skewness and kurtosis and the large sample size (n = 260), these deviations are statistically detectable but biologically minor. Overall, these results indicate substantial yet structured phenotypic variation, with mass traits showing greater dispersion than linear dimensions.

Varietal differences in fruit and stone traits

Varietal effects on fruit and stone traits were strong (p < 0.001) but trait-specific (Table 2). The largest difference was observed for fruit weight (FWt), with Olour exceeding Kurukkan by approximately 59.0 g (t = 34.34, R² = 0.821). Fruit length (FL) showed only modest differentiation (t = 8.78, R² = 0.23), whereas fruit diameter (FD) differed markedly between varieties (t = 23.6, R² = 0.68). Stone weight (SWt) (t = 23.0, R² = 0.67) and stone diameter (SD) (t = 34.6, R² = 0.82) also showed strong differences between varieties. In contrast, varietal effects were weak for stone length (SL) (t = 4.98, R² = 0.09).

Allometric scaling between fruit and stone traits

Strong and consistent allometric relationships were detected between fruit and stone traits across all three trait pairs examined (Table 3). Raw-scale analyses quantified absolute changes in stone traits with increasing fruit size, whereas complementary log–log models characterized proportional scaling and enabled inference on allometric exponents. Stone weight increased strongly with fruit weight in raw-scale analyses (β = 0.074 ± 0.020, p < 0.001), accounting for approximately 72% of the total variation in stone mass. Log–log regression further revealed a highly significant allometric exponent (β = 0.47, p < 0.001), indicating sub-isometric scaling, whereby proportional investment in stone mass declined with increasing fruit size. The interaction between fruit weight and variety was not significant in either the raw-scale or log–log models (p > 0.9), indicating that mass-allocation rules were conserved between Olour and Kur. Stone length scaled positively with fruit length on the raw scale (β = 0.32 ± 0.10, p = 0.002), although the explanatory power was lower (R² ≈ = 0.21), indicating greater flexibility in longitudinal growth relative to mass-based traits.

Log-log analysis confirmed significant but weaker sub-isometric scaling of stone length with fruit length (β = 0.40, p = 0.002), and no evidence of varietal divergence in scaling slopes was detected (p = 0.45). Stone width exhibited particularly strong coupling with fruit width in raw-scale analyses (β = 0.17 ± 0.08, p = 0.025), explaining over 80% of the observed variation, and log–log regression yielded a significant sub-isometric exponent (β = 0.31, p = 0.003) with the highest explanatory power among trait pairs (R² ≈ 0.85), indicating tight developmental integration of transverse growth (Table 3). Although the interaction term for stone width–fruit width scaling approached significance (p = 0.058), it did not meet conventional thresholds, and scaling slopes were therefore interpreted as statistically conserved across varieties. Collectively, allometric exponents across mass, length, and width dimensions were consistently less than unity, indicating pervasive sub-isometric scaling of stone traits with increasing fruit size. The absence of significant fruit trait × variety interactions across all models demonstrates that fruit–stone allocation rules are conserved between Olour and Kur, despite substantial variation in absolute fruit size, supporting strong developmental canalization of reproductive allocation in mango (Table 3).

Germination response

The results for germination response, assessed using single or paired covariates with variety as a fixed effect, are given in Table 4. Across all models, germination probability showed no meaningful association with fruit or stone traits. Single-trait models produced only trivial reductions in residual deviance (ΔDeviance ≤ 2.74) and no improvement in AIC relative to the baseline model (AIC = 359.74), indicating negligible explanatory power of individual traits. Among paired-trait models, the best combinations (e.g., SL + SW, FW + SL) yielded slightly larger deviance reductions (ΔDeviance ≈ 3.1–3.3); however, these gains translated into AIC values (≈ 360.4–360.7) that were comparable to or worse than the baseline and best single-trait models, and well below thresholds indicative of substantive model improvement. Estimated covariate effects (β) were uniformly small and inconsistent across trait combinations, reflecting shared size structure rather than stable biological effects. Overall, neither individual nor combined morphological traits provided meaningful explanatory power for germination, demonstrating that germination is robustly independent of fruit and stone morphology within the observed range of variation.

Seedlings per stone

The variety-only baseline model explained little variation in SS, indicating weak varietal structuring of seedling number per fruit. Including individual fruit or stone traits yielded negligible improvements in model fit, with changes in residual deviance ≤ 0.48 and no reduction in AIC relative to the baseline model (Table 5). Among single-trait models, stone width (SW) produced the largest improvement in deviance (ΔDeviance = 0.48), but this was insufficient to support a meaningful effect. Paired-trait models likewise failed to improve model performance, with ΔDeviance values remaining < 1 across all trait combinations and AIC consistently exceeding that of the baseline model. These results indicate that SS is largely independent of fruit and stone traits once the varietal effect is accounted for, consistent with strong developmental canalization of seedlings emerging per stone within the observed phenotypic range.

Polyembryony showed no meaningful association with fruit or stone morphology (Table 6). Overall, polyembryony appears largely independent of fruit and stone traits within the range of variation examined.

Discussion

In the present study, morphological variation in polyembryonic mango genotypes collected from the Main Experimental Farm of the Division of Fruits and Horticultural Technology, ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute, New Delhi, was evaluated using multivariate analysis. The use of morphological characteristics as reliable markers for the characterization and assessment of fruit crops has been well documented in species such as mulberry [37], date palm [38], and ber [39]. In polyembryonic mango genotypes, one sexual embryo and multiple nucellar embryos are typically present, with the nucellar embryos possessing the same genetic makeup as the mother plant [31]. These adventitious embryos arise directly from the maternal nucellar tissue surrounding the embryo sac, which contains the developing zygotic embryo. The present study provides comprehensive insights into the morphological variability of fruit and stone traits in two polyembryonic mango rootstocks, ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’, and their relationship with polyembryony and germination. In the present study, ‘Kurukkan’ exhibited higher germination rates (61.11%), while ‘Olour’ displayed a marginally higher percentage of polyembryony (46.59%). Such differences in seed behavior are consistent with previous reports that germination and polyembryony rates are genotype-dependent and influenced by environmental conditions [31]. These contrasting behaviors highlight the potential complementary role of ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’ in mango improvement programs. While ‘Kurukkan’ ensures vigorous germination and establishment, ‘Olour’ provides higher clonal uniformity through nucellar embryos. Reports from various regions have documented the extent of polyembryony in polyembryonic mango genotypes. Sane et al. [40] reported the highest polyembryony rate in ‘Olour’ (84.4%), followed by ‘Moreh’ mango (75.5%). Rao and Reddy [41] found the highest polyembryony percentages in ‘Peach’ (338%) and ‘EC 959862’ cv. (296%) of mango, with the lowest recorded in ‘‘Kurukkan’’ (138%). Earlier studies by Srivastava et al. [42] reported the highest number of seedlings per stone in Vellaikolamban and Moovandan mango rootstocks (1.7), and the lowest in the Mylepelian rootstock (1.3). Ochoa et al. [43] recorded polyembryony rates of 97% and 95% in ‘Manila’ and ‘Ataulfo’ mango cultivars, respectively, with an average of 3.4 and 3.2 embryos per seed and over 80% of seeds containing 2–4 embryos.

Fruit weight is one of the most variable traits across mango germplasm. In the current study, fruit weights in ‘Olour’ ranged from 93.5 to 168.5 g, while those in ‘Kurukkan’ varied from 50.5 to 121.5 g. These values are relatively lower than those reported in most commercial table cultivars. For instance, Bhuyan and Islam [44] documented fruit weights ranging from 202.8 g to 1014.4 g, with the largest in ‘Fazli’. Yadaw et al. [45] reported fruit weights of 185.5 g in ‘Dashehari’ and 240.0 g in ‘Langra’. Similarly, Prasad and Nalini [46] observed 315.0 g in ‘Bangalora’, and Reddy and Singh [47] noted a wide range among polyembryonic types (113.0 g in ‘Mylepelian’ to 411.3 g in ‘Kensington’). Ahmed et al. [48] further reported a maximum fruit weight of 214.8 g in ‘Kalia’. When compared to these cultivars, ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’ clearly represent the small-fruited end of the spectrum, which is consistent with their role as polyembryonic rootstocks rather than fruit types selected for table use.

Fruit length and width also showed moderate variation in both ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’. In the present study, ‘Olour’ fruits measured 61.29–80.42 mm in length, while ‘Kurukkan’ fruits measured 54.38–77.45 mm. Earlier studies across diverse germplasm have reported much broader ranges: Sadhu and Bose [49] documented fruit lengths ranging from 7.3 to 12.6 cm, while Awasthi and Pandey [50] recorded fruit lengths from 5.80 cm (‘Gilas’) to 10.44 cm (‘Sukul’). Limaye et al. [51] reported averages of 10.69 cm × 8.36 cm in ‘Ratna’, and Ghosh et al. [52] observed lengths of 7.1–10.7 cm and diameters of 5.3–8.7 cm. Reddy and Singh [47] reported lengths of 6.79–10.89 cm and diameters of 5.81–9.17 cm in polyembryonic cultivars. Larger fruits have also been noted, with Badiyala and Awasthi [53] recording the maximum length in ‘Fazli’ (14.56 cm) and diameter in ‘Mallika’ (8.06 cm). Similarly, Jadhao et al. [54] reported 15.61 cm in ‘Mallika’ and only 6.58 cm in ‘Dudhpedha’. The relatively smaller fruit size of ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’ is therefore consistent with their categorization as seedling rootstocks.

Stone traits also revealed meaningful variation. In the present study, ‘Olour’ stones ranged from 40.29 to 64.02 mm in length, 31.25–45.84 mm in width, and 11.5–27.0 g in weight, while ‘Kurukkan’ stones ranged from 43.65 to 62.99 mm in length, 21.15–30.45 mm in width, and 8.0–17.0 g in weight. Earlier literature provides evidence of comparable or larger ranges in commercial cultivars. Sadhu and Bose [49] recorded stone lengths between 5.9 and 10.2 cm, with diameters ranging from 2.3 to 4.8 cm (major) and 1.6 to 3.0 cm (minor). Ghosh et al. [52] observed stones from 5.2 cm (‘Sardamani Bhog’) to 8.0 cm (‘Bombay Green’), while Naik [55] reported stone lengths of 7.88 cm (‘Alphonso’) and 7.80 cm (‘Ratna’). Patil [56] documented variation from 7.29 cm in ‘Pairi’ to 11.40 cm in ‘Kesar’. Similarly, Badiyala and Awasthi [53] found stone weights ranging from 10.50 g in ‘S.B. Chausa’ to 20.90 g in ‘S.B. Rampur’. Recent studies consistently demonstrate that fruit and stone-related morphological traits are the major contributors to variability in mango germplasm and are highly effective for discrimination through multivariate approaches. Principal component and cluster analyses have shown that fruit weight, fruit dimensions, stone size, and seed traits account for most of the observed variation, underscoring their importance for characterization and selection [57, 58]. Similar findings in Indian and South Asian mango germplasm further confirm that polyembryonic and rootstock genotypes tend to possess smaller but highly variable fruit and stone traits, reflecting their adaptive and genetic diversity rather than table-fruit selection [59, 60]. These results support the present study’s observation that ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’ are morphologically distinct and well-suited for complementary roles in mango rootstock improvement programs. The relatively small fruit size observed in ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’, when compared with commercial table cultivars, is consistent with their classification as polyembryonic rootstocks rather than fruit types selected for consumption. While fruit weight, length, and breadth are often emphasized in table mango improvement, their significance in rootstock genotypes lies primarily in their association with seed and stone development, which directly influences seed physiology and seedling performance. In the present study, the smaller fruits of both rootstocks were accompanied by proportionally larger stones, indicating a preferential allocation of resources to seed tissues, a characteristic advantage for seed propagation. Stone traits play a critical role in determining germination capacity, embryo development, and early seedling emergence, particularly in polyembryonic mango rootstocks. Variations in stone size and weight observed between ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’ may influence the number of viable embryos per stone as well as the mobilization of stored reserves during germination. The observed correlations among fruit and stone traits suggest that morphological parameters may serve as indirect indicators of seed physiological performance, aiding in the selection of superior rootstock material for nursery use.

Developmental canalization refers to the tendency of developmental processes to produce consistent phenotypes despite genetic or environmental perturbations [61]. In plants, canalization has traditionally been examined in vegetative and morphological traits, yet increasing evidence suggests that early reproductive traits, including germination success, number of seedlings per seed, and polyembryony, are also subject to developmental buffering and constraint.

Theoretical and experimental work on canalization establishes that phenotypic variance can be reduced through genetic, epigenetic, and physiological mechanisms, resulting in robust developmental outcomes [62, 63]. Empirical studies of polyembryony provide a particularly useful system for examining canalization in early reproductive traits. Polyembryony, the production of multiple embryos within a single seed, introduces intrinsic developmental variation at the earliest life stages. Despite this, many polyembryonic species exhibit relatively consistent germination and seedling establishment patterns, suggesting strong developmental constraints. For example, studies in Eriotheca pubescens demonstrate that while polyembryony increases embryo number, germination percentage and early seedling performance remain relatively predictable, with trade-offs expressed primarily through reduced individual seedling size rather than highly variable germination outcomes [64]. This pattern is consistent with canalized allocation strategies during early development.

Similar results have been reported in apomictic Citrus species, where seeds frequently contain multiple embryos of both sexual and nucellar origin. Despite wide variation in embryo number, germination timing, and seedling emergence rates, they show limited dispersion, indicating developmental regulation of early establishment traits [32]. These findings suggest that while embryo initiation may be developmentally flexible, downstream processes such as germination and emergence are more tightly canalized.

Research on tropical tree species such as Handroanthus chrysotrichus further supports this view. Studies examining the seed-seedling transition reveal that increasing embryo number leads to predictable reductions in seedling biomass and establishment efficiency, but not to uncontrolled developmental variability [65]. Such results imply that developmental systems respond to embryo crowding through regulated trade-offs rather than stochastic outcomes, consistent with canalized developmental pathways.

At the genetic and epigenetic level, studies in Arabidopsis thaliana show that early reproductive development, including female gametophyte precursor specification, can vary among genotypes. Yet, this variation is constrained within narrow developmental bounds [66]. These constraints likely contribute to stabilizing downstream traits, such as embryo viability and germination success, reinforcing the idea that canalization operates across multiple stages of reproductive development.

These studies indicate that a balance between developmental flexibility and canalization shapes early reproductive traits. Polyembryony and variation in embryo number can introduce potential sources of phenotypic diversity, yet germination success, seedling emergence, and early growth often remain constrained. This pattern supports the hypothesis that canalization plays a key role in stabilizing early-life-history traits critical for fitness, ensuring reliable establishment despite underlying developmental or genetic variation.

The complementary characteristics of ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’, particularly with respect to polyembryony and germination behaviour, clearly indicate their potential utility in mango rootstock improvement programs. Integrating morphological evaluation with an understanding of seed physiology provides a more comprehensive basis for rootstock selection. These findings emphasize that, in rootstock-oriented mango genotypes, fruit and stone traits should be interpreted not in terms of market value but in relation to their contribution to successful propagation, nursery performance, and long-term orchard productivity.

Correlation analysis provided further insights into the relationships between fruit and stone traits. Correlation analysis revealed strong positive associations among most fruit and stone traits. Fruit weight showed high correlations with fruit width (r ≈ 0.83), stone weight (r ≈ 0.83), and stone width (r ≈ 0.86), indicating coordinated growth between fruit weight and stone development. In contrast, stone length exhibited comparatively weaker correlations with other traits (r ≈ 0.33–0.49), suggesting greater independence in longitudinal stone growth relative to weight and width-related traits.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated substantial morphological variation in fruit and stone traits between the polyembryonic mango genotypes ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’, particularly in weight and width-related attributes. Strong correlations and consistent sub-isometric allometric relationships between fruit and stone traits indicate coordinated developmental allocation patterns that are conserved across the two genotypes. However, germination percentage, number of seedlings per stone, and polyembryony were largely independent of fruit and stone morphology, and varietal effects explained only a negligible proportion of the variation in these early reproductive traits. These findings suggest strong developmental canalization of germination and polyembryony in polyembryonic mango genotypes. The study demonstrates that while ‘Olour’ and ‘Kurukkan’ are morphologically distinct, early reproductive responses remain largely independent of fruit and stone morphology, providing useful insights for germplasm characterization and propagation-oriented mango improvement programs.
