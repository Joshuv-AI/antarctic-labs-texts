No Universal Purification in Quantum Mechanics
Zhenhuan Liu, Zhenyu Du, Zhenyu Cai, Zi-Wen Liu

Abstract
We prove that the linearity and positivity of quantum mechanics impose general restrictions on quantum purification, unveiling a new fundamental limitation of quantum information processing. In particular, no quantum operation can transform a finite number of copies of an unknown quantum state or channel into a pure state or channel that depends on the input, thereby ruling out an important form of universal purification in both static and dynamical settings. Relaxing the requirement of exact pure output, we further extend our result to establish quantitative sample complexity bounds for approximate purification, independent of any task details or operational constraints. To illustrate the practical consequences of this principle, we examine the task of approximately preparing pure dilation and, for the first time, prove an exponential lower bound on the required sample complexity.



I
Introduction

The fusion of quantum mechanics and information theory has unlocked unprecedented possibilities for information processing, enabling tasks that are impossible in the classical world and holding the potential for a revolutionary impact.
Yet, the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics impose strict boundaries on its capabilities.
Prominent examples include quantum cloning and deleting, tasks trivial in classical physics but forbidden by the linearity of quantum mechanics
Wootters and Zurek 1982
;
Kumar Pati and Braunstein 2000
, and quantum broadcasting, which is further limited by the positivity of quantum mechanics
Barnum et al. 1996
;
Parzygnat et al. 2024
.
Identifying such fundamental principles and the limitations they impose on operational tasks marks an essential route for shaping the understanding of the very nature of quantum mechanics as well as its technological potential.

The purification of noisy quantum systems, aimed at reducing quantum entropy and preparing certain target pure systems, is of fundamental and ubiquitous importance, lying at the core of many quantum information processing tasks including quantum error correction and mitigation
Terhal 2015
;
Cai et al. 2023
, quantum cooling
Cotler et al. 2019
;
Zeng et al. 2021
;
An et al. 2023
, and a broad spectrum of important resource distillation or concentration tasks
Bennett et al. 1996
;
Pan et al. 2001
;
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
.
Among general purification tasks, a prominent special case is the preparation of a pure dilation

Beyond its application in widespread practical tasks, including quantum error mitigation

Over the course of its extensive use and development, various forms of limitations of purification have been observed, typically obtained under specific tasks, operations, or noise models

It is both theoretically profound and practically pressing to understand whether these limitations are artifacts of those restricted settings or manifestations of a more fundamental principle of quantum mechanics.

In this work, we identify a general no-go principle on quantum purification, which rules out the possibility of purifying any finite copies of noisy states in an input-dependent manner.
With an intuitive proof, we elucidate that this impossibility originates from two fundamental mathematical features of quantum mechanics—
linearity

For instance, the result reveals new insights relevant to the third law of thermodynamics and can be naturally extended to the purification of quantum channels.
Beyond its theoretical significance, this limitation directly constrains the performance of practical quantum tasks.
By relaxing the requirement of producing an exactly pure state to allowing nearly pure outputs, we derive general lower bounds on the sample complexity of approximate purification.
As a concrete application, we establish for the first time an exponential sample complexity lower bound for the task of preparing approximate dilation states.
It should be stressed that all rules and results here are universal in the sense that no restrictions on the tasks, allowed operations, or noise models are assumed.

II
No Universal Purification

As shown in Fig.
1
, a universal quantum purification task involves finding a quantum process, represented by
\mathcal{M}
, that can map many copies of an arbitrary noisy input state
\rho
into a pure state depending on
\rho
.
The form of
\Psi_{\rho}
is determined by the specific task.
At the same time, the quantum process,
\mathcal{M}
, is independent of the input state.
A typical example trying to partially achieve this is purification-based error suppression protocols
Huggins et al. 2021
;
Koczor 2021
;
Liu et al. 2025
, which aim to extract the principal component of a noisy state or channel from many copies of the noisy ones.
Our main result can be summarized as the following mathematical observation:

Theorem 1
.

Let
t
be any finite integer, and let
\mathcal{M}
be a nonzero positive trace-non-increasing map. Suppose that for any state
\rho
supported on a subspace
\mathcal{H}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{d}
with
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{S})\geq 2
, the output
\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})
is proportional to a pure state. Then the output of
\mathcal{M}
must always be proportional to a fixed pure state,

\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})\propto\Psi.

(1)

Proof.

Given two arbitrary states
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
that are not the same, input
(x\rho_{0}+(1-x)\rho_{1})^{\otimes t}
into the map
\mathcal{M}
with
0\leq x\leq 1
.
By assumption,

\mathcal{M}\left[(\alpha\rho_{0}+\beta\rho_{1})^{\otimes t}\right]\propto\Psi_{x}.

(2)

Because the input state can be written as

\sum_{k=0}^{t}\tbinom{t}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{t-k}\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}\otimes\rho_{1}^{\otimes(t-k)}),

(3)

with
\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}
being the symmetrization channel, the output state can thus be written as

\sum_{k=0}^{t}\tbinom{t}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{t-k}\mathcal{M}\left[\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}\otimes\rho_{1}^{\otimes(t-k)})\right],

(4)

due to
linearity
of
\mathcal{M}
.
According to Eq. (
2
), we have

\sum_{k=0}^{t}\tbinom{t}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{t-k}X_{k}\propto\Psi_{x},

(5)

where
X_{k}=\mathcal{M}\left[\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}\otimes\rho_{1}^{\otimes(t-k)})\right]
is a semi-positive matrix as
\mathcal{M}
is a
positive
map.
Notice that the L.H.S. of the above equation is the linear combination of semi-positive matrices with positive coefficients, while the R.H.S. is a rank-1 matrix.
According to a textbook result, this means that each positive matrix should be proportional to the rank-1 matrix on the R.H.S., including the first and the last matrices corresponding to
k=0
and
k=t
.
We thus have

\mathcal{M}(\rho_{0}^{\otimes t})\propto\Psi_{x},\ \ \ \mathcal{M}(\rho_{1}^{\otimes t})\propto\Psi_{x},

(6)

for arbitrary
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, which concludes our proof.
∎

This simple proof relies solely on the linearity and positivity of
\mathcal{M}
, two bedrocks of quantum mechanics, and covers all purification tasks that aim to produce a pure output state.
As we do not require
\mathcal{M}
to be trace-preserving, our result covers all quantum operations, encompassing post-selection and adaptive strategies.
Theorem
1
can be understood as a variant of the third law of quantum thermodynamics
Vinjanampathy and Anders 2016
;
Taranto et al. 2023
.
Specifically, a direct corollary of this theorem is that there is no universal quantum cooling machine that can cool an arbitrary quantum system down to zero temperature with finite copies of the target system.
By cooling a quantum system, we refer to the task of increasing the value of
\beta
in the thermal state
\rho(\beta)=e^{-\beta H}/\Tr(e^{-\beta H})
to infinity given a fixed Hamiltonian
H
.

Extending beyond the state setting, the transformation of quantum channels is also a fundamental and active area of research
Liu and Yuan 2020
.
We can directly generalize Theorem
1
to conclude that the universal transformation from multiple quantum channels into a unitary channel is also impossible unless using the replacement super-channel.
The reason is that any super-map acting on an input quantum channel can be represented as a map acting on the Choi state of the input channel
Chiribella et al. 2008a
;
Chiribella et al. 2008b
;
Liu et al. 2024b
.
As is known, the Choi state of a unitary channel is pure, and the Choi state of a general quantum channel can be a mixed state.
Therefore, if there exists a super-channel that can map an arbitrary input channel
\mathcal{C}^{\otimes t}
to a unitary channel depending on
\mathcal{C}
, then there exists a quantum channel that can map some independent input mixed states into different pure states, which violates Theorem
1
.

III
Approximate purification

In the above, we addressed the fundamental restriction on perfect quantum purification, whose targets are pure states or unitaries.
Nonetheless, approximate pure systems are often already useful in many tasks, thus the characterization of an approximate version of the principle is crucial for practical purposes
Li et al. 2024
;
Childs et al. 2025
;
Yao et al. 2025
.
With full details in Appendix
A
, we analyzed the robustness of Theorem
1
, showing a clear trade-off relationship.
Specifically, for any positive trace-preserving map
\mathcal{M}
with a fixed
t
, if its output state
\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})
is close to a pure state, then the difference between its output states will be small.
When the purification task demands the output state to vary significantly according to the input state, then one requires a large number of input states to obtain high purity.
This trade-off relationship is encapsulated in the following theorem:

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a positive trace-preserving map. Suppose that there exists a parameter
0\leq\epsilon<1
such that for all
\rho
supported on a subspace
\mathcal{H}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{d}
with
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\geq 2
,

\Tr\bigl[\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})^{2}\bigr]\geq 1-\epsilon.

(7)

Then there exists a state
\sigma_{0}
such that, for all
\rho
,

D\bigl(\sigma_{0},\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})\bigr)\leq 3\sqrt{t^{3}\epsilon}.

(8)

Here
D(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the trace distance
D(\omega,\tau)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\omega-\tau\|_{1}
.

Here, we let
\mathcal{M}
be trace-preserving, since we are considering the sample complexity; thus,
\mathcal{M}
should output the target state with unit probability.
Furthermore, the dimension-independent lower bound is compatible with results derived in specific tasks
Li et al. 2024
and essentially optimal up to polynomial factors given only conditions in Theorem
2
.
Indeed, given
t
copies, one can prepare states of the form

\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+e^{i\hat{p}}\ket{1})
,
where
\hat{p}
is an estimate of the purity of
\rho
obtained from
\rho^{\otimes t}
. The uncertainty of
\hat{p}
scales inverse-polynomially with
t
, and hence so does the in-purity
1-\Tr\bigl[\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})^{2}\bigr]
of the prepared state.
This shows that the
\Omega(\epsilon^{-1/3})
dependence cannot be substantially improved beyond polynomial refinements.
In this sense, our result establishes a fundamental limit for approximate quantum purification, analogous to the Heisenberg limit in metrology.
By contrast, other practical tasks with more stringent requirements may require an exponential sample complexity, as shown in the next section.

IV
Approximate dilation preparation

We define the pure state
\Psi_{AB}
as the pure dilation of a mixed state
\rho
when
\Tr_{B}(\Psi_{AB})=\rho
.
Besides being a fundamental concept, the dilation state also carries broad practical significance in areas such as quantum algorithms, learning, and simulation.
In Ref.
Liu et al. 2024a
, the authors show that with purification, certain quantum non-linear properties can be efficiently estimated without a high requirement for quantum memory.
A prevalent quantum algorithmic framework, known as Quantum Singular Value Transformation
Low and Chuang 2019
;
Martyn et al. 2021
, is based on an algorithm that encodes the target matrix as a block of a unitary operation.
If one encodes the target density matrix into a unitary, one can efficiently estimate properties such as mixed-state fidelity or quantum entropy
Gilyén et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
.
These algorithms typically rely on an oracle to prepare the pure dilation of the target quantum state
Gilyén and Li 2019
.
Hence, analyzing the complexity in preparing a pure dilation state helps to understand the difficulty in realizing such an oracle.

By definition, this task aims to find a quantum operation,
\mathcal{M}
, that acts on
\rho^{\otimes t}
and the output state satisfies two requirements: (1) the output is a pure state; (2) the marginal of the output state is
\rho
.
Theorem
1
shows that it is impossible to simultaneously achieve these two requirements, but in practice, it is also valuable to understand if we can do so up to some approximation.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes t})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{AB})
be a positive trace-preserving map with
d\coloneqq\dim\mathcal{H}_{A}\geq 20
.
Suppose there exist parameters
\epsilon,\eta\geq 0
such that
\tfrac{1}{3}>\sqrt{\epsilon}+\epsilon+\eta>13\exp\left(-\frac{d}{8(\ln d)^{2}}\right)
and, for arbitrary
\rho
,

1.

(Near purity)
\Tr\left[\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})^{2}\right]\geq 1-\epsilon
;

2.

(Marginal accuracy)
D\left(\Tr_{B}[\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})],\rho\right)\leq\eta
.

Then

t\geq\frac{ed}{512\left(\sqrt{\epsilon}+\epsilon+\eta\right)^{2}}.

(9)

This theorem follows from a direct extension of the proof of Theorem
1
and builds on the linearity and positivity of the map
\mathcal{M}
, and the full proof is provided in Appendix
B
..
Following the same argument, if the output state of
\mathcal{M}(\rho^{\otimes t})
is close to the pure dilation of
\rho
, then, given any positive decomposition,
\rho^{\otimes t}=\sum_{i}p_{i}M_{i}
with
p_{i}\geq 0
and
M_{i}
being semi-positive matrix,
\mathcal{M}(M_{i})
should also be close to the dilation state of
\rho
.
Suppose the target state is given by some classical probability distribution
\textbf{p}=\{p_{i}\}_{i}
,
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}
,
\rho^{\otimes t}
can be decomposed into different orthogonal states with probability
\textbf{p}^{t}
.
Thus, if the map for preparing an approximate pure dilation state exists, it can map a single state sampled by
\textbf{p}^{t}
to the pure dilation of
\rho
and recover the whole probability distribution
p
by tracing a subsystem.
According to a well-known result in distribution estimation
Han et al. 2015
, we can establish the exponential lower bound for
t
.

V
Discussion and Outlook

Previously, the main technique for proving sample complexity lower bounds was based on the hardness of quantum learning tasks
Chen et al. 2022
;
Huang et al. 2022
.
Given a quantum algorithm, if it can efficiently achieve some quantum learning task, which is proven to have some sample complexity lower bound, then such a lower bound also limits the complexity of the target quantum algorithm.
However, it has already been shown that the pure dilation
\Psi_{AB}
provides no speedup in any learning task for extracting information of
\rho
compared with the target state
\rho
itself
Chen et al. 2024
.
This shows that the lower bound in Theorem
3
does not stem from the hardness of any specific learning task, but rather from the intrinsic properties of quantum mechanics itself.

We note that Theorem
1
does not rule out the possibility of certain distillation tasks, whose target output state is some fixed resourceful pure state.
In these contexts, the limitations imposed by quantum resource theory are more directly relevant
Liu et al. 2019
;
Fang and Liu 2020
;
Fang and Liu 2022
;
Regula and Takagi 2021
;
Zang et al. 2025
.
Interestingly, Theorem
2
and
3
show that even beyond the scope of quantum mechanics, as they only require positive (not completely positive) maps, purification tasks still exhibit fundamental sample complexity lower bounds.
The positivity of quantum mechanics can be circumvented using virtual operations, i.e., post-processing
Parzygnat et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2024
, at the cost of sampling overhead.
It is intriguing to explore whether virtual operations can overcome the limitations imposed by our no-go theorems.
Finding explicit protocols that reach our complexity lower bounds would also be a valuable future direction.

Approximate purification (proof of Theorem
2
)

Fix an integer
t
and two states
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{S}
.
For each
x\in[0,1]
, define the mixture
\rho_{x}\coloneqq(1-x)\rho_{0}+x\rho_{1}
.
Write
\sigma_{x}\coloneqq\mathcal{M}(\rho_{x}^{\otimes t})
as a mixture of states:

\sigma_{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\binom{t}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{t-k}\,\omega_{k}\eqqcolon\sum_{k=0}^{t}p_{k}\,\omega_{k},

(10)

where
\omega_{k}\coloneqq\mathcal{M}\bigl(\Pi_{\mathrm{sym}}(\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}\otimes\rho_{1}^{\otimes t-k})\bigr)
is a state and
p_{k}\coloneqq\binom{t}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{t-k}
.
From the near-purity condition,

\begin{split}\epsilon&\geq 1-\Tr(\sigma_x^2)\\
&=\sum_{k}p_{k}^{2}[1-\Tr(\omega_k^2)]+\sum_{k\neq l}p_{k}p_{l}[1-\Tr(\omega_k \omega_l)]\\
&\geq\sum_{k\neq l}p_{k}p_{l}[1-\Tr(\omega_k \omega_l)]\\
&\geq p_{k}p_{k+1}[1-\Tr(\omega_k \omega_{k+1})]\\
&\geq p_{k}p_{k+1}[1-F(\omega_{k},\omega_{k+1})].\end{split}

(11)

Choose
x=\frac{k+1}{t+1}
so that
p_{k}=p_{k+1}
, and set
a\coloneqq k+1
,
b\coloneqq t-k
. Then

\begin{split}p_{k}&=\binom{t}{k}\left(\frac{k+1}{t+1}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{t-k}{t+1}\right)^{t-k}\\
&=\binom{t+1}{a}\left(\frac{a}{t+1}\right)^{a}\left(\frac{b}{t+1}\right)^{b}.\end{split}

(12)

Using Stirling’s bounds, for any integer
N\geq 1
,

\sqrt{2\pi}\,N^{N+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-N}\leq N!\leq e\,N^{N+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-N},

(13)

which implies

\binom{t+1}{a}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{e^{2}}\,\frac{(t+1)^{t+\frac{3}{2}}}{a^{a+\frac{1}{2}}\,b^{b+\frac{1}{2}}}.

(14)

Therefore,

p_{k}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{e^{2}}\,\sqrt{\frac{t+1}{ab}}.

(15)

Since
ab\leq\bigl(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\bigr)^{2}=\bigl(\tfrac{t+1}{2}\bigr)^{2}
, we obtain

p_{k}\geq\frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}}{e^{2}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}.

(16)

Substituting into (
11
),

1-F(\omega_{k},\omega_{k+1})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{p_{k}^{2}}\leq\frac{e^{4}}{8\pi}\,\epsilon\,(t+1).

(17)

By the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality,

D(\omega_{k},\omega_{k+1})\leq\sqrt{1-F(\omega_{k},\omega_{k+1})}\leq\frac{e^{2}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\sqrt{\epsilon\,(t+1)}.

(18)

Applying the triangle inequality along the path
0,1,\ldots,t
,

\begin{split}D(\omega_{0},\omega_{t})&\leq\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}D(\omega_{j},\omega_{j+1})\\
&\leq\frac{e^{2}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\,t\,\sqrt{\epsilon\,(t+1)}\\
&\leq 3\,t^{3/2}\,\epsilon^{1/2}.\end{split}

(19)

Finally, since
\omega_{0}=\mathcal{M}(\rho_{0}^{\otimes t})
and
\omega_{t}=\mathcal{M}(\rho_{1}^{\otimes t})
, the proof is done.

Approximate pure dilation (proof of Theorem

First, the near-purity condition implies that every output is close to some pure state in trace distance.

.

If
\mathcal{M}

(21)

Proof of Theorem

.

Lemma
2
tells us that

\delta_{t}\coloneqq\sup_{p\in\Delta_{d}}\,\mathbb{E}_{z^{t}\sim p^{\otimes t}}\norm{q(z^t)-p}_{1}\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}+\epsilon+\eta\eqqcolon\xi.

(35)

For
d\geq 20
, we have
\frac{ed}{32}\geq\frac{3d}{4(\ln d)^{2}}
.
Assume
t>\frac{ed}{32}
. By Lemma
3
,

\delta_{t}\geq\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{\frac{ed}{2t}}-\exp\!\left(-\frac{t}{6}\right)-12\exp\!\left(-\frac{d}{8(\ln d)^{2}}\right).

(36)

When
t>\frac{ed}{32}
, we have
\exp(-t/6)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{d}{8(\ln d)^{2}}\right)
.
Therefore,

\delta_{t}\geq\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{\frac{ed}{2t}}-13\exp\!\left(-\frac{d}{8(\ln d)^{2}}\right).

(37)

Combining with
\delta_{t}\leq\xi
and
13\exp\left(-\frac{d}{8(\ln d)^{2}}\right)\leq\xi
, we obtain

2\xi\geq\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{\frac{ed}{2t}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad t\geq\frac{ed}{512\,\xi^{2}},

(38)

which yields the desired lower bound.

Next, note that
\delta_{t}\geq\delta_{m}
for
t\leq m
. Hence, for any
t\leq\frac{ed}{32}
,

\delta_{t}\geq\delta_{ed/32+1}\geq\frac{1}{2}-o(1).

(39)

Thus, for sufficiently large
d
and any
\xi\leq\frac{1}{3}
, one cannot have
\delta_{t}\leq\xi
when
t\leq\frac{ed}{32}
. This completes the proof.
∎
