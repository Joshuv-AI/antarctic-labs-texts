The Role of Psychoeducation in Addressing Physician Shortages
Authors: Rosechelle M Ruggiero, Hetal J Patel, Carol S North, Traci N Adams
Published: 2026

Introduction

Substantial physician shortages are expected in the United States in the next decade. Primary care will experience the greatest impact, with a shortage of 20,200–68,000 primary care physicians expected by 2036. 1,2 A survey by the Health Resources and Services Administration projects a total shortage of 187,130 physicians by 2037, 3 and a report by the Association of American Medical Colleges projects a shortage between 13,500 and 86,000 by 2036. 2 These expected shortages may have downstream consequences, including reduced access to care, longer wait times, and increased burnout among remaining health care providers. 4,5 Urgent action is needed to mitigate this expected shortage. 4,5

Physician mental health challenges, including burnout and psychopathology, are contributing to the physician shortage. The prevalence of mental health challenges among physicians is staggering, 6,7 and these are associated with intention to leave medical practice. 8 This commentary will review the association between physician mental health and intention to leave medical practice and then outline strategies for management of both burnout and psychopathology among physicians. Fortunately, recognition and treatment of burnout and psychopathology among physicians may help physicians to practice longer and alleviate physician shortages.

Prevalence of Physician Mental Health Challenges

Mental health challenges are prevalent among physicians and therefore can have a substantial impact on the physician workforce. Although the prevalence of burnout has decreased in the years following the COVID-19 pandemic, 8,9 45% of physicians in a recent survey by the American Medical Association endorsed burnout, 9 and another study reported that 37.9% acknowledged burnout and 32.6% indicated moderate or greater intention to leave current practice. 8 Subspecialties such as emergency medicine and family medicine experienced the highest burnout rates. 8,9 Factors contributing to burnout include high workloads, long work hours, lack of autonomy, and burdensome clerical tasks. 7,10 An international analysis of depression prevalence studies, largely measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, reported rates of 25% to 60% in larger physician cohorts. Fewer studies have directly examined anxiety; in one study, 24% of more than 12,000 physicians surveyed reported symptoms consistent with generalized anxiety disorder. 11 A cross-sectional study of 2641 physicians in China found that 19% experienced both depression and anxiety. 12 Overall, the prevalence of depression and suicidality appears to be higher among physicians who are women and from underrepresented ethnic and racial groups. 7,9 Depressive symptoms have been associated with poor sleep and lack of mental health resources. 12 Adding insult to injury, physicians are less likely to seek out treatment due to underlying stigma created by assuming that depression is a problem of the individual rather than the environment. 13,14

Association Between Physician Mental Health and Intention to Leave Medical Practice

Both psychopathology and burnout are associated with intent to leave medical practice and contribute to physician shortages. 8 In a nationwide survey of physicians, each 1-point increase (range 0–10) in burnout was directly associated with intention to leave their current institution. 8 In an online survey of 253 pediatric critical care physicians in the United States conducted prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, of the 49% of physicians with burnout, 90% considered leaving the practice. 15 Depression and anxiety disorders are the 2 most common forms of psychopathology among physicians and are associated with job turnover. 16,17 A systematic review found that individuals with depression are more likely to miss workdays, have lower productivity, or leave their jobs. 16 A positive screen for generalized anxiety disorder in physicians was further associated with intention to leave practice within 2 years. 17

Although the previously discussed studies evaluated the association between burnout and intention to leave their current institution, other studies have found that physicians are leaving medicine altogether. A 2021 survey by Jackson Physician Search found that 54% of physicians planned to make an employment change, and 36% of those planned to leave the practice of medicine entirely or retire early. 18 Another study estimated the cost of physician turnover, citing $260 million in excess health care expenditures for public and private payers annually attributed to primary care physician burnout alone. 19 Addressing physician burnout and psychopathology may help to alleviate physician attrition and workforce shortages.

Distinction Between Burnout and Psychopathology in Physicians

Although burnout and psychopathology both present barriers to physician retention, these entities are distinct and require different management strategies. Psychological distress, of which burnout is one type, is defined as any negative emotional response to a stressor. 12–24 Stressors are ubiquitous in medicine and can include challenging patients, time constraints, and, for many, the burden of documentation in the electronic health record (EHR). 22,24 Psychological distress may include feelings of frustration or sadness and is considered an expected response to stressors. Severe distress, however, can impair functioning to the point of disability. 22–24 Burnout, a subtype of distress, consists of lack of emotional attachment, emotional exhaustion, and reduced performance. 25 Distress and burnout are treated with supportive interventions as described in the following text.

In contrast to distress, diagnosable psychopathology such as major depressive disorder and generalized anxiety disorder is made by a trained mental health professional based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision. 23,24,26 Identification of psychopathology allows choice of the most effective treatment, such as evidence-based psychotherapy or medications, unlike burnout, which is managed with supportive interventions. 23,24 If both psychopathology and burnout are found on a psychiatric evaluation, special efforts are needed, including formal psychiatric treatment in addition to psychosocial interventions suggested for burnout. Many individuals with psychiatric illness can expect return of usual functioning. Inadequately treated psychopathology can be life-threatening: 1 physician suicide death occurs every day on average, amounting to the equivalent of losing 1 medical school class per year to death by suicide. 27

Evaluation by a mental health professional is required to distinguish between burnout and psychopathology. 23,24 Yet many physicians would far rather conclude they are experiencing burnout than consider the possibility of a psychiatric disorder or consult a psychiatrist for several reasons, including stigma associated with psychiatric illness and fear of loss of medical licensure. 27,28 The stigma around psychopathology among physicians may be due to traits of perfectionism, societal stereotypes, and health declarations for medical licensure that suggest that psychiatric illness is synonymous with an impairment in job performance. The consequences of failure to seek or even entertain the idea of a psychiatric evaluation are substantial; in one study, only 60% of physicians who died by suicide had received psychiatric medications in the last year, and only 52% of physicians who died by suicide had seen a psychiatrist. 29

Destigmatization and implementation of psychoeducation are essential ways to improve mental health and physician job retention. Research demonstrates that psychoeducation can improve help-seeking behavior, reduce stigma, and improve mental health literacy. 30–32 Every physician wellness program must include warning signs of psychiatric illness, especially intolerable symptoms, request for psychiatric evaluation, inability to care for self or dependents, indications of impending harm to self or others, disorientation or altered level of consciousness, and a person who simply worries you (Table). 19,22,29 Unfortunately, many physician wellness programs, including those of the American Medical Association and the call for action on physician burnout issued by the Critical Care Societies Collaborative, fail to inform physicians of the importance of distinguishing between burnout and psychiatric illness, or to address the stigma that keeps many physicians from seeking help for themselves. 25,31

Although the addition of new wellness programs or training modules may not be feasible for many institutions, existing wellness programs can ensure the inclusion of core psychoeducation principles and advocate for evaluation by a psychiatrist to determine whether psychiatric illness is present. Individuals who deliver psychoeducation should be experts in disaster mental health psychoeducation or at least mental health professionals with psychoeducation expertise. Confidentiality can be addressed in psychoeducation and group interventions by asking participants to respect others’ confidentiality and informing participants in the participant agreement of the unavoidable risk to their confidentiality if they choose to share in a group format.

Psychoeducation is intended to complement, not replace, organizational changes to support physician mental health; psychoeducation is not intended to shift the burden of supportive interventions from the health care system to individuals. 13 The addition of psychoeducation programs to organizational changes that address burnout may help to destigmatize seeking mental health evaluation and lead to more appropriate treatment, ultimately helping physicians to continue practicing medicine.

Management of Distress

In addition to psychoeducation, appropriate management of distress and burnout is essential to help distressed physicians remain effective and continue in the workforce. Distress and burnout are treated with supportive interventions, such as yoga, gratitude journals, professional coaching, and exercise. 23,33,34 Importantly, these interventions should include monitoring of the participants for signs of psychiatric illness and linkage of physicians to appropriate psychiatric evaluation and treatment. Getting physicians together can also alleviate distress, as can supportive counseling. Supportive psychological interventions are typically part of physician wellness programs at large medical practices and academic centers but are lacking in rural settings.

Burnout interventions that address the health care system have a greater impact on physician burnout than the individual-level interventions discussed previously in this commentary. 13 Successful system-wide interventions can be included in psychoeducation programs to avoid the perception that distress is an individual problem rather than systemic. Physician burnout has been shown to be tightly associated with workload 7,10 ; therefore, the most successful interventions have targeted decreasing workload. Efforts to reduce workload have been implemented in many forms, including offloading clerical tasks to nonphysician employees, such as medical assistants, nurses, and advanced practice practitioners, and by utilizing scribes to reduce EHR documentation. 13,35 In the future, wider implementation of language learning models to offload documentation in the EHR is expected, as this has shown promise but has yet to be fully realized. 36 From a leadership standpoint, wellness officers have been implemented in many institutions to address burnout and depression, but their overall effect has been difficult to measure. 13 Not surprisingly, burnout affects underrepresented populations and women disproportionately in parallel with their unequal pay, discrimination, and harassment, all of which need to be addressed at the institutional level. Struggles between work and family obligations continue to fall disproportionately on physicians who are female, and targeting family leave policies to provide accessible and quality childcare and offer flexible schedules have been shown to improve wellbeing. 37–40 Through implementation of these changes at both individual and system levels, treatment of burnout may improve physician retention.

Addressing Psychopathology

In addition to including psychoeducation in physician wellness programs, operationalizing psychiatric professionals to respond to the mental health needs of the physician workforce is essential to address physician shortages. 22 Mobilization of psychiatric resources is particularly important during times of increased stress, including pandemics. 22 An opt-in strategy of mental health screening, creation of tele–mental health appointments for employees, and provision of workplace coverage for individuals to attend psychiatry appointments have been effective in increasing access to psychiatric care. 41,42

Conclusion

Distress and psychopathology are common among physicians and associated with intention to leave practice. 6–8,16 Attention to these conditions may help physicians remain in practice. Psychoeducation for distress and burnout is a cornerstone of physician wellness programs but needs to include potential for psychopathology and its warning signs. Supportive interventions for distress at both the individual and system levels are necessary to reduce burnout, while mobilization of psychiatric resources is necessary for diagnosis and treatment of psychopathology. The current model of burnout education, which ignores the essentials of education about psychopathology and mobilization of psychiatric resources, is analogous to playing with fire, 28 and the future of health care may hang in the balance.
