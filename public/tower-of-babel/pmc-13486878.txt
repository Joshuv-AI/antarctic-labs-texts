Early targeting of the YAP/TEAD-PKC-NF-κB axis alleviates post-traumatic osteoarthritis
Authors: Quinn T Ehlen, Ying Wang, Xiaomin Cai, Sebastian Thompson-Ceccato, Thomas Jacob Jahn, Joshua H Wu, Adair Treadway, Aliya Mulati, Lee Kaplan, Kiran Kurmi, Xiaoqiong Wang, Thomas M Best, Chun-Yuh Huang, Zhipeng Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPost-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) is a progressive joint disease driven by mechanical injury, inflammation, and cartilage degradation. The Hippo signaling effector YAP, acting through an atypicalPKC (aPKC)-NF-κB axis, couples mechanical stress to inflammatory signaling in chondrocytes. Using a murine anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture model, we show that joint injury rapidly co-activates YAP and NF-κB p65 and elicits a staged transcriptional response, from early inflammatory and YAP-associated transcription programs to matrix remodeling. Pharmacologic disruption of Hippo signaling or inhibition of YAP-mediated gene transcription, by preventing YAP from interacting with its transcription partner TEAD, reduced cartilage degeneration and overall joint pathology and reversed injury-induced inflammatory and remodeling programs. These findings provide preclinical proof-of-concept that early pharmacologic intervention can alter the course of PTOA. The ongoing clinical development of TEAD-targeted agents in oncology will facilitate translation of this therapeutic strategy to joint disease.Keywords: PTOA, Hippo signaling, YAP, PKC, inflammation, TEAD

Introduction

Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) is a prevalent and debilitating joint disorder.1,2 Unlike primary osteoarthritis (OA), PTOA arises following inciting events, such as an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury, a meniscal tear and subsequent meniscectomy, or an intra-articular fracture.3 Despite advances in surgery and rehabilitation, many individuals with these injuries still develop PTOA, highlighting the need for effective countermeasures to its development and progression.3,4

Key mediators of PTOA pathogenesis are inflammation and the subsequent matrix degradation.3 Joint injury induces inflammation via upregulating TNF-α and IL-1β, promoting cartilage breakdown and subsequent disruption of homeostasis.2,5 Furthermore, abnormal mechanical loading, particularly prolonged or excessive forces, is considered a major contributor to the initiation of joint OA.2 When subjected to abnormal mechanical loading, cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM) undergoes extensive pathological remodeling, leading to biophysical dysfunction.2 Thus, identifying targets that modulate inflammation and protect articular cartilage early following joint trauma is critical.

The Hippo signaling pathway has emerged as a crucial regulator and effector of mechanotransduction, cell proliferation, and tissue homeostasis.6,7,8,9 This pathway, composed of a kinase cascade, regulates the activity of its downstream effectors, Yes-associated protein (YAP) and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ).6 When the Hippo pathway is activated, YAP/TAZ are phosphorylated, leading to their cytoplasmic retention and degradation. Conversely, when Hippo is inactivated, YAP/TAZ move to the nucleus and activate genes controlling growth, survival, and inflammation via transcription enhanced associate domain (TEAD) transcription factors.6 A previous study from our group5 demonstrated that disrupting the dynamics of mechano-regulation of Hippo signaling in three-dimensional (3D) cultured chondrocytes could alleviate inflammation and matrix degradation. Furthermore, genetic deletion of large tumor suppressor kinase 1 and 2 (LATS1/2) or YAP/TAZ decreased inflammatory and degradative gene expression in mechanically loaded chondrocytes.

However, the precise role of Hippo signaling in OA remains a subject of debate, with conflicting evidence suggesting both protective and detrimental effects.10,11,12,13,14,15,16 Studies indicate that YAP activation promotes OA by inducing the expression of catabolic enzymes and inflammatory mediators, while other groups suggest that YAP downregulation is detrimental, leading to chondrocyte apoptosis and cartilage degradation.10,11,12,13,14,15 These discrepancies emphasize the context-dependence of Hippo signaling and the need to clarify its role across the spectrum of OA severity.

Our recent study showed that mechanical loading activates a novel Hippo-PKCζ (atypical PKC)-NF-κB pathway in chondrocytes, resulting in the expression of NF-κB target genes associated with inflammation and matrix degradation.5 Given that inflammation and matrix destruction drive PTOA and the potential role of the Hippo signaling in this process, we hypothesized that early intervention targeting the Hippo pathway could alleviate PTOA. Accordingly, we aimed to: (1) characterize the temporal dynamics of Hippo-YAP and NF-κB signaling in the early phase of PTOA using histological and transcriptomic analysis; (2) determine whether pharmacological inhibition of YAP/TEAD or upstream Hippo kinases mitigates joint pathology after ACL injury; and (3) define the transcriptional programs downstream of YAP/TEAD in injured cartilage and assess the contribution of PKC signaling to these responses. By targeting this axis early in the disease process, we aimed to identify potential therapeutic strategies to prevent or mitigate the progression of PTOA.

Results

Dynamic regulation of Hippo-YAP and NF-κB signaling in PTOA

To investigate the temporal dynamics and interactions of NF-κB and Hippo-YAP signaling following mechanical injury, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of p65 and YAP/TAZ localization in articular cartilage at different time points after ACL rupture.3 As shown in Figure 1A, p65 exhibited a progressive increase in nuclear localization following ACL rupture. In control cartilage, p65 was predominantly cytoplasmic, indicating an inactive NF-κB pathway.17 Following ACL rupture, p65 accumulated in the nucleus, indicating rapid and sustained NF-κB activation within the injured joint (Figures 1A and 1B).

The roles of Hippo signaling and its effectors YAP and TAZ in OA appear to be context-dependent.10,11,12,13,14,15 Under normal conditions, an active Hippo pathway phosphorylates YAP and TAZ, leading to their cytoplasmic retention and degradation, thereby suppressing YAP/TAZ-mediated transcriptional programs. Upon mechanical stimuli or in the disease development, Hippo pathway inactivation allows YAP and TAZ to translocate into the nucleus, where they interact with the TEAD family of transcription factors to drive gene expression potentially linked to OA-related processes. To characterize YAP and TAZ activity in PTOA, we assessed their subcellular localization in the early phase after ACL injury (Figures 1C, 1D, and S1). In uninjured cartilage, YAP and TAZ were primarily cytoplasmic, consistent with active Hippo pathway suppression of their nuclear function. Following injury, YAP translocated to the nucleus and remained elevated through 24 h, indicating rapid and sustained Hippo pathway inactivation.

Together, these findings indicate a coordinated temporal regulation of NF-κB and Hippo signaling pathways following ACL rupture, which is confirmed by a Pearson’s correlation analysis (Figure 1E). NF-κB activation, marked by increased nuclear p65, occurs in parallel with YAP nuclear translocation. This interplay suggests that mechanical injury rapidly induces inflammatory and catabolic responses in cartilage, providing insight into how ACL rupture contributes to PTOA.

Early time-course RNA-seq identifies rapid activation of inflammatory and YAP/TAZ-associated transcriptional programs

To better define the early molecular response after ACL injury, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on whole joints from ACL-injured and control knees (Figure 1F). Interrogating the early molecular response is biologically important because this phase captures the primary injury-induced signaling events before secondary structural changes dominate, thereby providing the clearest window into disease initiation. We analyzed knee joint RNA-seq data at 7 h after injury relative to uninjured controls (Figure 2). The most prominently induced genes were chemokines and inflammatory-response genes, reflecting rapid activation of inflammatory signaling within hours of joint injury (Figure 2A).

Pathway and gene-set analyses showed that the 7 h response was dominated by inflammatory and cytokine-related programs, including IL-17 signaling, TNF signaling, NF-κB signaling, chemokine signaling, and JAK/STAT signaling (Figures 2B and 2C). Critically, the YAP/TAZ gene signature was also significantly enriched at this early time point alongside NF-κB signaling (Figures 2D–2F), and representative YAP/TAZ target genes and NF-κB/chemokine genes were consistently induced across individual biological replicates (Figures 2E and 2G).

We next integrated the 7 h data with 24 and 72 h RNA-seq comparisons, which revealed a staged transcriptional response to ACL injury (Figure 3A). The early response was dominated by inflammatory and YAP/TAZ-associated programs, detectable at 7 h and persisting through 24 h, consistent with coordinated activation of these signaling axes during the acute injury phase (Figures 3B and 3C). By 72 h, the transcriptional landscape shifted toward ECM organization, tissue remodeling, and collagen metabolic processes, reflecting a transition from acute inflammation to early structural remodeling (Figures 3C and 3D). NF-κB signaling and the YAP/TAZ gene signature remained strongly enriched at 24 h, with representative target genes consistently elevated in injured joints (Figures 3E–3G).

The 24 h ACL-injury RNA-seq dataset was further supported by principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering, which separated ACL-injured joints from control joints (Figure S2). Additional analyses confirmed enrichment of inflammatory, ECM, collagen, cytoskeletal, and cytokine-related programs in ACL-injured joints (Figures S3 and S4). At 72 h, the transcriptome was further remodeled toward ECM-receptor interaction, cytoskeletal remodeling, complement/coagulation, glycosaminoglycan biology, and integrin signaling (Figures S5A–S5C). Notably, the YAP/TAZ signature remained enriched at 72 h while NF-κB signaling was no longer prominent, consistent with a shift from acute inflammatory to sustained mechanosensing and remodeling programs (Figures S5D–S5H).

Pharmacologic inhibition of PKC, LATS1/2, or YAP/TEAD preserves joint integrity after ACL injury

We next confirmed that the ACL injury model produced structural joint pathology by 3 weeks after injury and used this endpoint to evaluate early pharmacologic intervention (Figure 4). Representative H&E and toluidine blue staining showed marked joint damage and loss of cartilage proteoglycan staining in ACL-injured knees compared with control knees (Figures 4A and S6). To address joint-level pathology more comprehensively, we quantified an expanded joint score that incorporated cartilage Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) score,18 osteophyte score, subchondral bone score, and synovitis score. ACL-injured mice showed significantly higher joint scores than control mice (Figure 4B).

We then tested whether pharmacologic inhibition of the proposed signaling axis could mitigate injury-induced joint pathology. ACL-injured mice were treated with saline, PKC inhibitor (PKCi), LATS1/2 inhibitor (LATSi), or YAP/TEAD inhibitor (YAP/TEADi) (Figures 4C and S7). Inhibitor-treated joints showed markedly improved histological appearance relative to saline-treated injured joints, and all three inhibitors, PKCi, LATSi, and YAP/TEADi, significantly reduced the composite joint pathology score (Figure 4D).

YAP/TEAD inhibition broadly reverses injury-induced inflammatory and matrix-remodeling transcriptional programs

Because YAP/TEAD inhibitors are therapeutically tractable and related agents are advancing clinically, we next focused on the transcriptional consequences of YAP/TEAD inhibition in ACL-injured joints (Figure 5). At 24 h after injury and treatment, YAP/TEAD inhibition broadly remodeled the ACL injury transcriptome, altering thousands of genes in both directions relative to saline-treated injured controls (Figure 5A).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed that YAP/TEAD inhibition suppressed multiple injury-associated inflammatory and remodeling pathways, including inflammatory response, TNF-α/NF-κB signaling, IL-6/JAK-STAT3 signaling, chemokine/cytokine signaling, TGFβ signaling, ECM degradation/remodeling, Wnt/β-catenin signaling, apical junction, angiogenesis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition programs (Figure 5B). In contrast, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, G2M checkpoint, and E2F target programs were increased after YAP/TEAD inhibition (Figure 5B).

Trajectory analysis identified two major gene clusters whose injury-induced expression was reversed by YAP/TEAD inhibition (Figure 5C). Genes upregulated by injury and suppressed by YAP/TEADi were enriched for ECM organization, collagen metabolism, and receptor-ligand activity, while injury-downregulated genes restored by YAP/TEADi were enriched for complementary matrix and signaling processes (Figure 5D). Heatmaps of representative genes confirmed that YAP/TEAD inhibition consistently reversed both the up- and downregulation patterns induced by injury across individual samples (Figure 5E).

Because LATS1/2 inhibition and YAP/TEAD inhibition both reduced ACL injury-induced joint pathology, we next asked whether these two treatments converged on similar injury-response transcriptional programs. When gene-expression effects were compared between YAP/TEADi versus saline and LATSi versus saline in ACL-injured joints, the two inhibitor responses were strongly correlated, with 1,136 genes regulated by both inhibitors in the same direction at false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 (Figure S8A). Among the injury-up genes suppressed by YAP/TEADi, 457 of 637 genes (71.7%) were also downregulated by LATSi; among the injury-down genes restored by YAP/TEADi, 64 of 190 genes (33.7%) were also upregulated by LATSi (Figure S8B). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the shared injury-up genes suppressed by both inhibitors highlighted ECM organization, collagen fibril organization, wound healing, chemotaxis, leukocyte migration, ECM structural constituent, integrin binding, and collagen-containing ECM terms (Figure S8C). These results suggest that the similar protective effects of LATSi and YAP/TEADi are associated with convergent suppression of ACL injury-induced matrix-remodeling and inflammatory-response programs.

PKC inhibition suppresses inflammatory transcriptional programs after ACL injury

We also analyzed the effect of PKC inhibition in ACL-injured joints (Figure 6). Differential-expression and GSEA analyses showed that PKC inhibition altered the ACL injury transcriptome and reduced inflammatory pathway activity. In particular, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, NF-κB signaling, IL-17 signaling, and related inflammatory gene programs were decreased after PKC inhibitor treatment (Figures 6A–6D). Heatmap visualization of cytokine and receptor genes further demonstrated reduced expression of multiple inflammatory mediators after PKC inhibition (Figure 6C), supporting the role of PKC signaling in sustaining inflammatory transcriptional programs after ACL injury. The PKCi RNA-seq dataset was further supported by PCA, hierarchical clustering, gene-trajectory, and heatmap analyses (Figures S9 and S10), which showed that PKC inhibitor-treated samples were transcriptionally distinct from saline-treated ACL-injured samples and that PKC inhibition modulated injury-responsive inflammatory and matrix-associated gene clusters.

To further examine pathway dependency after inhibitor injection, we analyzed 24 h RNA-seq data from ACL-injured joints treated with LATSi, YAP/TEADi, PKCi, or the corresponding co-inhibitor combinations (Figure 7). Because LATS1/2 acts upstream of YAP/TAZ, we first asked whether LATSi-regulated transcriptional changes persist when YAP/TEAD activity is inhibited. LATSi regulated 4,433 genes compared with saline-treated ACL-injured controls. In the LATSi + YAP/TEADi group, 2,789 genes (62.9%) showed no significant residual LATSi effect and 115 genes (2.6%) retained a same-direction residual effect, whereas 1,529 genes (34.5%) showed an opposite residual effect (Figure 7A). Direction-stratified analysis confirmed this pattern for both LATSi-upregulated and LATSi-downregulated genes (Figure 7B), indicating that the majority of LATSi-associated transcriptional effects depend on YAP/TEAD activity, although a small subset persists independently. We next evaluated the relationship between YAP/TEADi and PKCi using a direction-aware overlap analysis. YAP/TEADi regulated 5,606 genes, whereas PKCi regulated 799 genes and combined YAP/TEADi + PKCi regulated 1,799 genes (Figures 7C and 7D). Among PKCi-regulated gene changes, 419 of 799 (52.4%) overlapped with the YAP/TEADi response in the same direction, while only 419 of 5,606 (7.5%) YAP/TEADi-regulated changes overlapped with PKCi (Figure 7E). These data indicate that PKCi-regulated genes represent a smaller, functionally linked subset of the broader YAP/TEADi-responsive transcriptional program, consistent with coupling between YAP/TEAD activity and PKC/NF-κB-associated inflammatory signaling in injured cartilage.

YAP/TEAD inhibitor treatment reduces p65 immunostaining in ACL-injured joints

To determine whether early pharmacological intervention altered signaling activity before overt structural disease developed, we examined p65 and YAP/TAZ immunohistochemistry (IHC) in inhibitor-treated joints at 7 h after ACL injury (Figure 8). Representative H&E and IHC images showed the histological context and the tissue distribution of YAP and p65 staining across saline-, PKCi-, IKKi-, and YAP/TEADi-treated injured joints (Figure 8A). Quantification showed that p65-IHC scores were reduced by IKKi and YAP/TEADi compared with saline-treated injured joints, whereas PKCi produced a partial reduction that did not reach significance in this small scoring set (Figure 8B). In contrast, YAP/TAZ-IHC scores were comparable across treatment groups at 7 h (Figure 8B), indicating that p65/NF-κB activation is the more sensitive acute tissue-level readout of inhibitor action in vivo. In addition, we performed complementary perturbation experiments in C28I2 chondrocytes in vitro (Figure S11). In the cytokine-stimulation experiment, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-1α activated inflammatory signaling, as shown by increased p65 phosphorylation, and were accompanied by changes in Hippo-YAP/TAZ readouts; pharmacologic NF-κB inhibition with Bay11-7082 modified these responses (Figure S11A). In the PKCζ perturbation experiment, PKCζ knockdown altered YAP/TAZ-associated protein responses following TNF-α stimulation (Figure S11B). Together with the RNA-seq dependency analysis, these in vivo and cell-based data support cross talk between inflammatory NF-κB signaling and Hippo-YAP/TAZ signaling, with YAP/TEAD-dependent transcription and PKC signaling converging on the early p65/NF-κB inflammatory response after ACL injury.

At the later 3-week endpoint, PKC inhibition also reduced p65 and YAP/TAZ immunostaining compared with saline-treated injured joints, suggesting that early pathway inhibition can produce sustained suppression of inflammatory and YAP-associated signaling readouts (Figure S12).

Discussion

Using a validated murine ACL rupture model of PTOA,19,20 we examined YAP/TEAD-PKC-NF-κB signaling dynamics within hours to weeks of injury. Our findings complement previous studies19,20 and uncover a therapeutic window in which targeting the YAP/TEAD-PKC-NF-κB axis can mitigate early PTOA, providing novel mechanistic understanding and promising intervention targets.

Dynamic regulation of Hippo-YAP and NF-κB signaling in PTOA

Our results reveal time-dependent NF-κB activation and Hippo inactivation after ACL rupture, evidenced by increased nuclear p65 and YAP. These findings align with reports showing mechanical injury induces catabolic responses,4 driving the progression of PTOA. The observed YAP activation in injured cartilage suggests that Hippo pathway inactivation plays a role in matrix remodeling and inflammation, though the precise contributions of YAP/TAZ in PTOA remain controversial. Some studies have suggested that YAP activation promotes cartilage degradation, whereas others indicate that YAP inactivation leads to chondrocyte apoptosis and loss of tissue integrity.10,11,12,13,14,15,16 Our findings support the hypothesis that YAP functions in a context-dependent manner in response to mechanical stress, necessitating further investigation into its role in PTOA.

Therapeutic potential of YAP/TEAD-PKC axis inhibition in PTOA

We demonstrate that pharmacologic inhibition of PKC, LATS1/2, or YAP/TEAD each significantly reduces joint pathology following ACL rupture, as evidenced by lower expanded joint scores incorporating cartilage OARSI grade, osteophyte formation, subchondral bone changes, and synovitis. All three inhibitors conferred significant protection compared with saline-treated injured controls. These findings suggest that the YAP/TEAD-PKC-NF-κB axis drives catabolic and inflammatory responses in injured joints, and that targeting multiple nodes of this signaling cascade, particularly at the YAP/TEAD level, can mitigate early PTOA. Transcriptomic analysis further showed that YAP/TEAD inhibition broadly reversed injury-induced inflammatory and matrix-remodeling programs (Figure 5), while PKC inhibition suppressed key inflammatory pathways (Figure 6), providing complementary evidence for the therapeutic potential of targeting multiple nodes of this axis. Notably, the YAP/TEAD-PKC-NF-κB axis is a well-established oncogenic driver, and the TEAD inhibitors evaluated here belong to a class of agents currently advancing in oncology clinical trials; our results therefore extend the relevance of these cancer-directed therapeutics to a non-malignant, mechano-inflammatory disease setting.

Molecular mechanisms: PKC and NF-κB signaling downstream of YAP/TEAD

Our RNA-seq and bioinformatics analyses provide mechanistic insights into how PKC inhibition alters signaling networks in PTOA. AEB-071 treatment suppressed key inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB, IL-17, and TNF-α signaling, which are known drivers of OA progression. In addition, AEB-071 modulated pathways related to ECM remodeling, mechanotransduction, and cytoskeletal organization, suggesting that PKC inhibition exerts both anti-inflammatory and matrix-protective effects.

Context-dependent role of Hippo-YAP signaling in PTOA

The interplay between PKC, Hippo-YAP, and NF-κB signaling in chondrocytes is complex and remains an area of active investigation. Our results indicate that PKC inhibition attenuates YAP-associated transcriptional programs (Figure 6), and that a majority of PKCi-regulated gene changes (52.4%) overlap with YAP/TEADi-regulated changes in the same direction, consistent with functional coupling between the two pathways (Figure 7). These findings support a model in which YAP/TEAD transcriptional activity promotes PKC-mediated NF-κB signaling, positioning YAP/TEAD as an upstream regulator of the inflammatory cascade in injured cartilage.5 However, whether YAP activation is protective or detrimental in PTOA remains highly context-dependent, as its effects may vary depending on disease stage, mechanical stress, and chondrocyte differentiation status.

Consistent YAP upregulation after ACL tear (Figures 1C and 1D) implicates Hippo pathway inactivation in PTOA development, in line with studies showing that blocking YAP nuclear translocation prevents cartilage degradation.10,21,22,23 However, YAP can also suppress NF-κB signaling by sequestering TAK1 substrates,15 and enhancing YAP activation post-injury has been reported to mitigate OA progression.12,13,15 Additionally, cytoplasmic YAP/TAZ can regulate Wnt and TGFβ signaling in contexts relevant to mechanotransduction and repair.24,25,26 Our finding that LATS inhibition, expected to increase nuclear YAP/TAZ, nonetheless reduces cartilage damage may reflect several non-mutually-exclusive mechanisms: moderate nuclear YAP may favor pro-survival over catabolic programs, LATS1/2 have YAP-independent inflammatory substrates, and cytoplasmic YAP/TAZ redistribution could be cartilage-protective. The strong correlation between nuclear p65 and YAP scores (Figure 1E) further supports a bidirectional amplification loop between inflammation and YAP activation. Collectively, these data indicate that the influence of YAP on PTOA is context-dependent, varying with disease stage, mechanical environment, and whether YAP/TAZ activity is nuclear or cytoplasmic, and underscore the need for conditional genetic models to dissect these relationships.

Clinical implications and future directions

Our study identifies YAP/TEAD inhibition as a particularly promising therapeutic strategy for PTOA prevention. VT104, the YAP/TEAD inhibitor used in this study, is structurally related to VT3989, a first-in-class TEAD autopalmitoylation inhibitor currently in clinical development. VT3989 has received FDA fast track and orphan drug designations, and phase 1/2 data from NCT04665206 in mesothelioma have been reported to support a planned registrational phase 3 study.27 The emerging clinical safety and activity data in oncology, together with the broad transcriptional reversal of injury-induced programs demonstrated here, support further evaluation of TEAD inhibitors for musculoskeletal indications. Importantly, the current data support acute, local intra-articular pathway modulation immediately after joint injury rather than chronic systemic inhibition, and dedicated preclinical safety studies in musculoskeletal models will be required before clinical translation. PKC inhibition is presented here primarily as mechanistic support for the YAP/TEAD-PKC-NF-κB signaling axis rather than a first-line therapeutic strategy, although AEB-071 has undergone clinical testing for inflammatory conditions28 (NCT00572585) and PKC inhibition reduces bone resorption following surgical destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM).29 However, systemic PKC inhibition carries immunosuppressive risks given broad PKC isoform distribution, and even local delivery may interfere with normal chondrocyte mechanotransduction and cartilage repair. Future development should therefore prioritize isoform-selective targeting, optimized local delivery, and rigorous long-term safety evaluation.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that early intervention targeting the YAP/TEAD-PKC-NF-κB signaling axis attenuates inflammatory and catabolic responses in PTOA. The broad transcriptional reversal achieved by YAP/TEAD inhibition, combined with the clinical advancement of TEAD inhibitors, positions YAP/TEAD as the leading therapeutic entry point for PTOA. Future studies will focus on evaluating TEAD inhibitors in chronic PTOA models, optimizing treatment regimens, and exploring combination strategies with PKC inhibitors to enhance therapeutic outcomes.

Limitations of the study

While these findings support the therapeutic potential of targeting the YAP/TEAD-PKC-NF-κB axis in PTOA, several limitations merit consideration.

First, the study focused on the acute post-injury phase (1–24 h and 3 weeks), based on the premise that the earliest molecular events represent the primary therapeutic window. Pro-inflammatory and catabolic programs peak within the first 24 h, and clinical drug delivery—for example, intra-articular injection at ACL reconstruction—is confined to this period. Published studies support the acute inflammatory phase as the principal driver of PTOA progression, with later remodeling changes largely reflecting early signaling. Future studies with longer time points are needed to confirm whether early intervention provides durable, disease-modifying protection.

Second, AEB-071 (Sotrastaurin) is a broad-spectrum PKC inhibitor with activity against classical (α, β, γ) and novel (δ, ε, θ, η) isoforms. Although our prior work identified PKCζ, an atypical isoform, as the relevant effector in the Hippo-PKCζ-NF-κB axis in chondrocytes, AEB-071 does not selectively inhibit PKCζ. Off-target effects on other PKC isoforms that regulate cartilage homeostasis, including those with protective roles, cannot be excluded. Isoform-selective inhibitors or genetic approaches (e.g., chondrocyte-specific PKCζ knockout) will be needed to define isoform-specific roles and rule out confounding effects.

Third, the current study relies primarily on pharmacological inhibition rather than in vivo genetic validation. Our prior work5 showed that genetic deletion of LATS1/2 and YAP/TAZ in chondrocytes under controlled mechanical loading reduced inflammatory and matrix-degradative gene expression, providing mechanistic rationale for interrogating this pathway in PTOA. However, those experiments do not replace cartilage-specific in vivo genetic validation in the ACL injury context. The present findings should therefore be interpreted as pharmacologic pathway-modulation evidence rather than definitive genetic proof of cell-autonomous pathway directionality. Future studies using conditional YAP/TAZ, LATS1/2, and/or PKCζ mouse models after joint injury will be needed to define pathway directionality, isoform specificity, and long-term structural outcomes.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse model of post-traumatic osteoarthritis (ACL rupture)

Male C57BL/6J mice, aged 8–12 weeks, were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Mice were housed under standard conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2°C, food and water ad libitum), provided by the University of Miami’s Division of Veterinary Resources (DVR). All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Miami (protocol IPROTO202300000077, approved through 6/12/2029), and all experiments were performed in conformance with the relevant institutional and federal regulatory standards for the care and use of laboratory animals. No criteria were used for including or excluding animals from treatment groups prior to the experiment. Animals were randomly assigned to control and experimental groups via a random number generator. To minimize potential effects of animal transport, all experiments were performed three days after delivery of mice to the facility.

For histological and joint-score analyses, n = 3–5 mice per group were used (as indicated in the corresponding figure legends). For immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses, n = 3 mice per group were used. For RNA-seq analyses, the number of biological replicates per group is indicated in the respective figure legends. All animals were included in the analysis, except that for joint-level scoring, samples with inadequate paired toluidine blue and H&E staining quality were excluded.

C28/I2 human chondrocyte cell line

The C28/I2 immortalized human chondrocyte cell line (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#SCC043) was used for in vitro mechanistic studies. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) antibiotic-antimycotic solution in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were routinely passaged and used within a low passage range to maintain chondrocyte-like characteristics. The cell line was authenticated by the supplier (Sigma-Aldrich) at the time of purchase and used at low passage number. Cells were tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

ACL rupture procedure

ACL rupture was induced using a non-invasive mechanical compression device as described previously.20 Briefly, mice were anesthetized using isoflurane, and one hindlimb was held in the device at a knee flexion angle of 30°. Following the application of a preload force of 2 N, ACL rupture was induced by administering a compressive force of 12 N at a velocity of 1.4 mm/s. Mice were randomly divided into each treatment group. The number of samples required to detect the calculated effect at a significance level of 0.05 with a power level of 0.9 was based on previous experiments in the laboratory.

Drug treatment (intra-articular injection)

Immediately post-injury, experimental mice received 10 μL intra-articular injections of either saline (vehicle control) or one of four inhibitors. Mice also received subcutaneous injections of buprenorphine ER (3.25 mg/kg) for pain management. Medical-grade physiological saline was used as the control. All inhibitors were prepared according to the supplying vendors’ instructions.

•PKC inhibitor — AEB-071 (sotrastaurin; MedChemExpress Inc.), a broad-spectrum PKC inhibitor active against classical and novel PKC isoforms, was prepared in 40% polyethylene glycol/10% DMSO/5% Tween-80/45% saline.

•LATS1/2 inhibitor — Lats-IN-1 (MedChemExpress Inc.) was prepared in 5% DMSO/95% corn oil.

LATS1/2 inhibitor — Lats-IN-1 (MedChemExpress Inc.) was prepared in 5% DMSO/95% corn oil.

•YAP/TEAD inhibitor — VT104 (a gift from Vivace Therapeutics Inc.) was prepared in 40% polyethylene glycol/5% Tween-80/55% saline.

All compounds were diluted 1:1000 in saline prior to injection to achieve a final concentration of 10 μM, yielding negligible residual solvent concentrations (≤0.1% total volume). Because the final injection vehicle was >99.9% saline, physiological saline alone served as the vehicle control.

Histology, scoring, and immunohistochemistry

Tissue processing

Dissected knee joints were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 h and decalcified in 10% EDTA for 10 days. Samples were bifurcated into medial and lateral knee components, split in the middle of the femoral condyles, and submitted to the University of Miami Cancer Modeling Shared Resource for histological preparation.

H&E and toluidine blue staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in running tap water, and counterstained with eosin for 2 min. Separate sections were stained with 0.04% toluidine blue solution for 5 min to highlight cartilage proteoglycan content and structural damage. H&E-stained sections were used for synovitis assessment and general morphology evaluation.

PTOA joint scoring

Each knee joint was sectioned to include both the medial and lateral femorotibial compartments. Cartilage degeneration was scored using the OARSI histopathology scoring system adapted for mouse models.18 Scores from medial and lateral compartments were given equal weight, and multiple sections from each joint were graded by two independent, blinded observers. PTOA pathology was assessed using four parameters: (1) cartilage degeneration (OARSI grade, 0–6), (2) osteophyte formation (0–3), (3) subchondral bone remodeling (0–3), and (4) synovitis (0–3). For each knee, each subscore was averaged across all sections, and the four per-mouse subscore averages were summed to generate a total joint pathology score per mouse.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For IHC analysis, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against YAP (Cell Signaling Technology, #93622) and NF-κB p65 (Cell Signaling Technology, #8242), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualization with DAB substrate. YAP and p65 staining intensities were scored using scales established in the literature.30,31

•0 — complete absence of staining

0 — complete absence of staining

•1 — weak cytoplasmic staining

1 — weak cytoplasmic staining

•2 — <50% positive cells with weak cytoplasmic staining, or <10% positive cells with nuclear staining

2 — <50% positive cells with weak cytoplasmic staining, or <10% positive cells with nuclear staining

•3 — >50% positive cells with cytoplasmic staining and >10% positive cells with nuclear staining

3 — >50% positive cells with cytoplasmic staining and >10% positive cells with nuclear staining

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For immunofluorescence (IF) staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were blocked in normal serum, then incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody against YAP (Cell Signaling Technology, #14074). After washing, sections were incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody (e.g., Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. Filamentous actin was visualized with fluorescently conjugated phalloidin (Alexa Fluor 488; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Slides were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. YAP signal (red), phalloidin/actin (green), and DAPI/nuclei (blue) channels were acquired and merged for analysis. Nuclear YAP localization was assessed to evaluate Hippo pathway activity at the indicated time points after ACL injury.

RNA isolation, library preparation, and sequencing

The study consists of three sets of bulk RNA-seq data. The first set included three groups: sham control, ACL injury treated with vehicle control, and ACL injury treated with a PKC inhibitor. Control mice underwent no ACL injury, and tissues were harvested 24 h after the procedure. Vehicle control mice were subjected to ACL injury and treated with 0.9% saline, with tissues harvested 24 h post-injury. PKC inhibitor-treated mice were subjected to ACL injury and treated with a PKC inhibitor, with tissues harvested 24 h post-injury. The second set was designed to examine the early transcriptional response after ACL injury and included three time points: 0 h, 7 h, and 72 h. The 0-h group consisted of mice without ACL injury. The 7-h and 72-h groups consisted of mice harvested at 7 h or 72 h after ACL injury, respectively. The third set was designed to compare pharmacological targeting of the PKC-Hippo signaling pathway after ACL injury and included six treatment groups: Vehicle control, PKC inhibitor (PKCi), LATS inhibitor (LATSi), YAP/TEAD inhibitor (Y/Ti), PKC inhibitor + YAP/TEAD inhibitor (PKC+Y/Ti), and LATS inhibitor + YAP/TEAD inhibitor (LATS+Y/Ti). All mice underwent ACL injury and received the indicated inhibitor treatment (or vehicle control) for 24 h before tissue collection. Each experimental group consisted of three biological replicates.

For the first set, total RNA was extracted from mouse knee joint cartilage tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA libraries were prepared by Innomics Inc. and sequenced using the DNBSEQ platform (paired-end 100 bp).

For the second and third sets, total RNA was extracted from mouse knee joint tissues using a modified TRIzol/RNeasy hybrid protocol based on the TRIzol/RNeasy hybrid method described by Zhang et al.32 Knee tissues were cryogenically homogenized using stainless steel beads, lysed in TRIzol reagent, treated with Proteinase K, subjected to chloroform extraction, and purified using RNeasy spin columns (Qiagen). RNA libraries were prepared by Admera Health, LLC and sequenced on an Illumina platform (paired-end 150 bp).

Bioinformatics analysis

Upstream bioinformatics analysis was performed in a Linux (Ubuntu 22.04) environment. Raw FASTQ files were aligned to the Mus musculus GRCm38.p6 (mm10) reference genome (Ensembl) using HISAT2.33 Read counts were quantified with featureCounts using the corresponding GTF annotation of the mouse genome.34

Differential expression (DE) analysis was performed using DESeq2.35 Low-count genes were filtered by requiring at least 10 counts in at least three samples. Variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) was applied for principal-component and sample-distance quality-control analyses. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the following thresholds: FDR <0.05 (without fold-change cutoff) for time-course comparisons, and FDR <0.1 (without fold-change cutoff) for inhibitor treatment comparisons. A likelihood ratio test (LRT) in DESeq2 was used to identify gene expression changes across three experimental groups. The degPatterns function from the DEGreport R package was used to identify gene clusters with similar expression patterns across conditions.

For the direction-aware dependency and co-inhibitor overlap analyses, genes were included at nominal p < 0.05 without a fold-change cutoff to preserve directional information for residual-effect classification; other inhibitor DEG and pathway analyses used the FDR thresholds described above.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)36 was performed using KEGG pathways.37 Genes were ranked by the DESeq2 Wald statistic, and enrichment was evaluated against MSigDB Hallmark and KEGG gene sets as well as curated gene signatures. The YAP signature gene list is provided in Table S1.38,39 In addition to GSEA, Gene Ontology (GO) over-representation analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed for up- and down-regulated genes separately across the Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component categories using clusterProfiler with the org.Mm.eg.db annotation. The raw data from bulk RNA-seq have been deposited in SRA under PRJNA1249407, and processed files are available under GEO accession number GSE294777.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed with 1 x SDS PAGE Sample buffer. 10 μL protein samples were loaded and separated on 9% or 4%–20% gradient SDS PAGE gels and then were transferred to PVDF membranes and blotted with the antibodies previously described.40

RNA interference in chondrocytes

Duplex siRNA-mediated PKCζ gene knockdown was performed with Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNA sequences were provided previously.5

Quantification and statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10 software was used for all statistical analyses. Values are expressed as mean ± SEM. The p-value between two groups was calculated using the standard two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used. Ordinal immunohistochemistry and histological scores were compared using the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test. Correlations between markers were evaluated using Pearson (and, where indicated, Spearman) correlation coefficients. Exact p-values are reported directly in the figures and figure legends, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For RNA-seq analyses, statistical thresholds are described in the bioinformatics analysis section above. Sample sizes (n) for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.
