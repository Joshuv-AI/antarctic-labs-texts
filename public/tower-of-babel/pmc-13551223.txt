Enablers and Barriers Among Women Ophthalmologists: A National Cross-Sectional Survey in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Walaa Abdullah Aldairi, Hajar Mahmoud AlHajri, Abdulmalek W Alhithlool, Ossama M Zakaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the self-perceived enablers and barriers among female ophthalmologists practising in health centres nationwide across Saudi Arabia. It also reviews their work-life balance, psychological wellbeing, experience of workplace discrimination, maternity-related challenges, and the personal and institutional factors that facilitate or hinder their professional and maternal roles.Patients and MethodsA cross-sectional, face-to-face survey was conducted among female ophthalmologists practising in health centres nationwide across Saudi Arabia over an eight-month period (October 2023 - June 2024). Participants were interviewed using a structured, piloted, and back-translated questionnaire. Self-perceived work-life balance (WLB), psychological wellbeing, happiness at home and at the workplace, experience of workplace discrimination, maternity-related challenges, and personal and institutional facilitating and impeding factors were assessed. Associations between WLB and demographic, family-related, and workplace variables were explored using non-parametric statistical tests.ResultsFifty-five female ophthalmologists were enrolled in the study. Key enablers included strong family support (94.5%), spousal understanding (78.2%), and satisfactory WLB in 40 participants (72.7%). The predominant barriers were psychological unrest in 32 participants (58.2%), workplace discrimination in 27 (49.1%), and disproportionate childcare responsibility in 46 (83.6%). Median happiness scores were higher at home (9.0; IQR 8–9) than at the workplace (8.0; IQR 7–8). Most participants (61.8%) experienced their first birth during ophthalmology residency. The principal institutional barriers were workplace inflexibility (38.2%) and children’s demands on maternal time (83.6%). No statistically significant associations were identified between WLB and age, number of children, or spouse’s occupation (all P>0.05).ConclusionFemale ophthalmologists in health centres nationwide across Saudi Arabia navigate a dual reality of meaningful enablers – including strong family networks, high WLB satisfaction, and effective personal coping strategies – alongside significant barriers, most notably psychological unrest, workplace discrimination, and institutional inflexibility. Targeted institutional and policy-level interventions are urgently needed to strengthen the enablers while systematically reducing the barriers faced by this workforce.Keywords: ophthalmology, women physicians, enablers, barriers, work-life balance, burnout, gender equity, motherhood, workforce, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Females currently represent about half of all medical graduates globally. However, persistent gender disparities remain across surgical and procedural specialties.1 Female physicians have been shown to provide more patient-centred care, engage more in preventive medicine, and demonstrate stronger communication with patients compared with their male counterparts. Hence, they may constitute meaningful professional enablers with direct implications for quality of care and workforce diversity.2

Despite these recognised contributions, women account for approximately 44% of the medical workforce in industrialised nations, and surgical specialties – including ophthalmology – continue to be disproportionately male-dominated.1,3 In ophthalmology specifically, the male-to-female ratio among practitioners in Saudi Arabia stood at 2.3:1 in 2022, even as the female proportion of residency graduates has been rising.4 Within the broader surgical field, recent national data from Italy confirm that women constitute only 16.5% of academic surgeons and face an independent disadvantage in academic promotion even after adjusting for age, research productivity, and geographic region – a phenomenon described as the surgical glass ceiling.5

Motherhood represents a pivotal life event that frequently coincides with critical stages of professional training and early career development. This represents one of the most significant barriers faced by female physicians in procedural specialties. The intersection of childbearing and residency or fellowship training has been associated with feelings of professional inadequacy, reduced training opportunities, and increased burnout.6,7 Among ophthalmologists in North America, more than half of female practitioners reported that career considerations influenced their family planning decisions, and 61% experienced heightened burnout after returning from maternity leave.8 Comparable barriers have been documented in India, where family responsibilities and extended working hours were the predominant barriers among female ophthalmologists, with 68% perceiving the professional environment to be more challenging for women.9

In Saudi Arabia, approximately 2400 ophthalmologists are currently in practice; applying the prevailing gender distribution suggests that approximately one-third are women.4 Saudi labour legislation provides maternity-related protections for working women across healthcare institutions. In Saudi Arabia, structural enabler protocols were set to support their continued professional participation.10 However, broader societal trends – including delayed childbearing, earlier return to work after delivery, and the predominance of nuclear family structures in urban settings – may compound the barriers faced by female ophthalmologist mothers.11 Significant social and regulatory reforms have expanded opportunities for women in professional roles. Yet, the specific enablers and barriers experienced by female ophthalmologists with respect to WLB, psychological wellbeing, happiness, and workplace equality remain incompletely explored.12

The aim of this study is to evaluate the self-perceived enablers and barriers among female ophthalmologists practising in health centres nationwide across Saudi Arabia. The study encompasses their work-life balance, psychological wellbeing, happiness, experience of workplace discrimination, maternity-related challenges, and the personal and institutional factors that facilitate or hinder their professional and maternal roles.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Deanship of Scientific Research at King Faisal University (approval number: KFU REC-2024-JUN-ETHICS1832). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. Confidentiality was maintained throughout; personal identifiers were removed prior to data analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Design and Participants

A cross-sectional, face-to-face survey was conducted between October 2023 and June 2024. Eligible participants were female ophthalmologists practising in health centres nationwide across Saudi Arabia, aged 25–45 years, who were mothers of at least one child. Female ophthalmologists who provided written informed consent were included. Male ophthalmologists, female ophthalmologists practising outside the national health centre network in Saudi Arabia, and those without children were excluded.

Sample Size

The estimated population of eligible female ophthalmologists practising in health centres nationwide across Saudi Arabia was 800, based on approximately one-third of the 2400 registered ophthalmologists nationally.4 Assuming that 85% of participants would report satisfactory WLB, a minimum sample size of 47 was required to achieve a 95% confidence level with a 10% margin of error. Applying a design effect of 1.2 for potential geographic clustering yielded a target of 55 participants. Sample size estimation was performed using OpenEpi software.13

Data Collection and Instrument

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by two trained female ophthalmologists using a structured questionnaire administered via Google Forms on tablet devices. Interviewers provided clarification only when requested to minimise bias.

The questionnaire was developed based on previously published studies on physician motherhood and WLB,6,8 initially drafted in English, translated into Arabic, and back-translated to ensure semantic equivalence. It was pilot-tested among ten ophthalmologists, and minor modifications were made prior to final administration. Demographic, professional, family, workplace, and maternity-related data were collected. The full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Appendix.

Outcome Measures

The study assessed six domains of enablers and barriers. Self-perceived WLB and psychological wellbeing were the primary outcomes. Self-perceived WLB was assessed using a single dichotomous item (“Do you feel you have a satisfactory work-life balance?”) adapted from previously published surveys of physician mothers and female ophthalmologists.7,8 A validated multi-item WLB instrument was not employed, in order to minimise respondent burden within a comprehensive multi-domain questionnaire administered face-to-face; the limitations of this single-item approach are acknowledged in the Limitations section. Happiness at home and at the workplace was assessed using a self-rated numerical scale from 1 (lowest) to 10 (highest). Workplace discrimination was assessed as a self-reported binary experience. Maternity-related challenges – including career stage at first delivery, maternity leave experience, and perceived skill attrition – were characterised descriptively. Personal and institutional factors facilitating or impeding WLB (enablers and barriers) were identified from a pre-defined list derived from the published literature. Psychological unrest was operationalised as a composite of self-reported emotional exhaustion and professional inflexibility.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analysed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were non-normally distributed and were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and minimum-maximum values. Associations between perceived WLB and demographic, family-related, and workplace variables were assessed using the chi-squared test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Mann–Whitney U-test as appropriate. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated where applicable. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. All subgroup analyses were prespecified and exploratory in nature, given the modest sample size, and should be interpreted accordingly. Age groups were defined a priori using the same clinically meaningful five-year bands employed for descriptive reporting, and were not data-driven. Percentages in subgroup comparisons are column percentages, with denominators of n=40 for the satisfactory WLB group and n=15 for the WLB imbalance group. The specific statistical test applied to each variable is indicated in the corresponding table footnote.

Family and Motherhood Characteristics

Family-related data are presented in Table 2. The majority of participants had two or three children (76.4%). Over 60% had their first child between the ages of 25 and 30 years, and 61.8% were ophthalmology residents at the time of their first delivery – one of the most frequently reported maternity-related barriers. Disproportionate childcare responsibility relative to their spouse was reported by 46 participants (83.6%). Contraceptive methods were used by 37 participants (67.3%), and five (9.1%) had used assisted reproductive techniques, reflecting the impact of career demands on reproductive choices.

Professional Challenges and Workplace Discrimination

When compared with male ophthalmologist colleagues, 36 participants (65.5%) perceived greater professional challenges – constituting a prominent barrier – while 19 (34.5%) reported similar experiences across genders. Only seven participants (12.7%) had considered changing their ophthalmology subspecialty because of these challenges. The most frequently reported barriers were greater family responsibility (78.2%), the need to work harder to achieve professional excellence (52.7%), workplace discrimination (49.1%), and extended working hours (47.3%).

The cumulative impact of these barriers resulted in psychological unrest in 32 participants (58.2%) and WLB imbalance in 15 (27.3%). Conversely, satisfactory WLB was reported by 40 participants (72.7%), representing a key enabler. Median happiness scores were higher at home (9.0; IQR 8–9; range 6–10) than at the workplace (8.0; IQR 7–8; range 5–10), reflecting a domestic environment that participants experienced as more supportive than the professional one.

Barriers and Enablers of Work-Life Balance

The enablers and barriers reported by participants are presented in Table 3. The leading enablers were strong family support (94.5%), an understanding spouse (78.2%), and effective time-management skills (72.7%). The predominant barriers were children’s demands on maternal time (83.6%) and workplace inflexibility (38.2%).

Discussion

The current study was designed to simultaneously characterise the enablers and barriers experienced by female ophthalmologists practising in health centres nationwide across Saudi Arabia. The results revealed a dual reality. The majority reported satisfactory WLB, high happiness at home, and identified powerful personal and social enablers. On the other hand, more than half experienced psychological unrest, nearly half reported workplace discrimination, and most bore a disproportionate share of childcare responsibility. This dual pattern mirrors observations among female ophthalmologists in the United States and India, where high professional satisfaction coexisted with substantial burnout and perceived gender-related challenges.8,9

Enablers: Facilitating Factors and Satisfactory Wellbeing

The most prominent identified enabler was strong family and spousal support, endorsed by the overwhelming majority of participants. These findings are consistent with other studies, which identify family networks as the most critical protective factor for physician mothers, particularly in societies where extended family structures provide a meaningful contribution to childcare.2,14 Effective personal time management, endorsed by nearly three-quarters of participants, represents a second key enabler, and aligns with evidence from physician mothers in India where structured domestic role-sharing facilitated professional continuity.15

The high proportion of participants reporting satisfactory WLB – despite the barriers documented – underscores that personal coping resources and social capital can meaningfully offset institutional shortcomings. This pattern has been observed among female dermatologists, who maintained acceptable WLB through domestic support despite concurrent burnout.16 Importantly, the high happiness scores reported at home (median 9.0) suggest that the domestic environment constitutes a genuine source of support and restoration for this cohort.

Barriers: Psychological Unrest and Burnout

Despite the enablers described above, psychological unrest affected more than half of participants, representing the most prevalent barrier identified. This rate is consistent with US data showing that roughly three in five female ophthalmologists experienced increased burnout after returning from maternity leave,8 and with national US survey data using the validated Mini Z Burnout Survey, which confirmed female sex, academic employment, and limited professional autonomy as independent predictors of burnout among ophthalmologists.17 The concordance between our findings and these international benchmarks indicates that the barriers faced by female ophthalmologists transcend individual healthcare systems and reflect systemic global conditions.

It is notable that psychological unrest occurred frequently even among participants reporting satisfactory WLB – a dissociation that has been documented in other physician specialties.16,18 This suggests that perceived WLB is a subjective construct that does not fully capture the physiological and psychological toll of dual professional and caregiving demands, and that burnout may develop through mechanisms independent of self-assessed balance.

When mapped onto established behavioural frameworks such as the Capability-Opportunity-Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) model and the Theoretical Domains Framework, the enablers and barriers identified in this study coalesce into three actionable domains. Opportunity encompasses organisational flexibility, accessible childcare support, and anti-discrimination policies, directly addressing the workplace inflexibility and gender-based discrimination reported by participants. Capability encompasses time-management and resilience training, building on the effective time-management skills already endorsed by nearly three-quarters of this cohort. Motivation encompasses supportive leadership, structured mentoring, and career re-entry programmes, which counter the psychological unrest and burnout-related symptoms documented here. Grouping the demands by setting – domestic (children’s demands on maternal time) and workplace (inflexibility, discrimination, and extended hours) – clarifies the pathways through which these pressures culminate in psychological unrest, and identifies the levers most amenable to institutional intervention.

Barriers: Workplace Discrimination

Workplace discrimination was reported by nearly half of participants, constituting one of the most significant institutional barriers identified. Two-thirds of the cohort perceived greater professional challenges compared with male colleagues, consistent with large-scale international evidence from physician mothers in the United States and female surgeons across nine countries.19,20 The surgical glass ceiling – recently quantified in Italy, where men remain 7.9 times more likely to attain full professorship even after adjustment for research output and seniority5 – is likely to operate in analogous forms across diverse healthcare settings. Complementary literature further supports engaging male colleagues as partners and advocates as an effective strategy for promoting gender equity in the workplace.21 This considerable prevalence of perceived discrimination may be partly attributable to prevailing social and community perceptions, as some segments of the local community may not yet be fully confident in women’s abilities as physicians in general, and as surgeons in particular. Community awareness programmes are therefore needed that highlight the achievements of successful female experts in the medical field, presenting them as role models within the community. Future studies should employ validated discrimination instruments to facilitate cross-study comparisons.

Barriers: Maternity-Related Challenges

The finding that nearly two-thirds of participants delivered their first child during ophthalmology residency is among the most clinically significant barriers identified. The collision of childbearing with the most demanding stage of ophthalmology training has been associated with feelings of inadequacy, reduced operative exposure, and burnout.6,7 Among Canadian female ophthalmologists, over half reported a significant slowdown in career progression attributable to childbearing.22 Concerns regarding surgical skill attrition following maternity leave and limited access to retraining resources upon return represent a modifiable institutional barrier.23 The use of assisted reproductive techniques by roughly one in ten participants further reflects the downstream impact of career demands on reproductive autonomy.

Barriers and Policy Implications

Workplace inflexibility and children’s demands on maternal time were the principal institutional and domestic barriers. These findings are consistent with US data where urgent policy action has been recommended.24 A recent observational study found that female ophthalmologists bore a disproportionately greater increase in domestic and childcare responsibilities compared with their male counterparts during the COVID-19 pandemic, exposing the fragility of existing support structures.25 Evidence synthesised in a systematic review of work-life balance among women medical professionals in the healthcare sector indicates that flexible scheduling for new mothers, on-site creche facilities, and protected nursing breaks reduce psychological unrest and burnout-related symptoms, and contribute directly to the retention of women in the healthcare workforce.21 Structural interventions – including flexible working schedules, on-site or employer-partnered childcare facilities, and structured return-to-practice programmes – should be further standardised and expanded.

Saudi labour legislation provides maternity-related protections for working women in healthcare settings, constituting a foundational systemic enabler.10 Recent societal and regulatory reforms have expanded women’s professional autonomy and leadership opportunities.12 Greater representation of women in healthcare leadership may accelerate the development of mother-friendly workplace policies, thereby strengthening the enablers and reducing the barriers experienced by this cohort.26

Strengths and Limitations

This study offers the first national characterisation in Saudi Arabia of the dual landscape of enablers and barriers experienced by female ophthalmologists in health centres nationwide across Saudi Arabia, addressing a significant gap in the regional and international literature. The back-translated and piloted instrument and the geographic coverage across five regions contribute to the representativeness of the data. However, although face-to-face administration ensured completeness of responses, for sensitive topics such as workplace discrimination, motherhood, childcare-related challenges, and WLB, this mode of administration may have increased social desirability bias; an anonymous online survey completed privately by participants might have reduced this potential. The possibility that the mode of administration influenced participants’ responses is therefore acknowledged.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the sample size, while reflective of the true population of eligible participants, limits statistical power for subgroup analyses. Second, psychological wellbeing and burnout were assessed using single self-report items rather than validated psychometric instruments such as the Maslach Burnout Inventory or the Mini Z Burnout Survey; future studies should employ standardised tools to enable cross-study comparison.17 Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Fourth, self-report methodology introduces the possibility of social desirability bias, which may have been accentuated by the face-to-face mode of administration. Fifth, the happiness scale (1–10) has not been independently validated in this population. Sixth, WLB was measured using a single, non-validated dichotomous item adapted from previous physician-mother surveys;7,8 this may limit the reliability, validity, and interpretability of this outcome, and future studies should employ validated multi-item WLB instruments. Seventh, the forced-choice questionnaire format constrained the depth of the perception-based data; qualitative or mixed-methods designs using open-ended interviews framed by the Theoretical Domains Framework would enrich future work. Finally, the focus on public health centres in Saudi Arabia limits generalisability to private sector or academic ophthalmology settings.

Conclusion

Female ophthalmologists in health centres nationwide across Saudi Arabia experience a dual reality of meaningful enablers and significant barriers. The principal enablers – strong family and spousal support, high WLB satisfaction, and effective personal coping strategies – serve as critical buffers against the professional demands of this specialty. The predominant barriers – psychological unrest affecting more than half, workplace discrimination reported by nearly half, and the collision of childbearing with residency training – demand urgent institutional and policy responses. To overcome these barriers, we recommend: institutional adoption of flexible scheduling and part-time pathways for mothers of young children; provision of on-site or employer-partnered childcare facilities and protected nursing breaks; structured return-to-practice and surgical skills refreshment programmes following maternity leave; formal mentorship and sponsorship schemes; workplace policies that promote gender equity and an inclusive professional culture, supported by awareness initiatives that engage male colleagues as partners and advocates; community awareness programmes that highlight successful female experts in medicine as role models; and greater representation of women in departmental and institutional leadership. Multinational comparative studies are needed to establish whether the balance of enablers and barriers observed in this setting is generalisable across diverse healthcare systems.
