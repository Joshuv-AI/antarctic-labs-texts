Simultaneous photoacoustic detection of coal spontaneous combustion indicator gases using single laser and absorption feature selection-optimized partial least squares regression
Authors: Lingmin Zhang, Zongxuan Mou, Lixian Liu, Huiting Huan, Xueshi Zhang, Yangjiayu Wang, Weichuan Zhang, Yize Liang, Huailiang Xu, Yufei Ma, Andreas Mandelis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To overcome spectral interference in detecting coal spontaneous combustion gases (CH4, C2H6, C3H8), an absorption feature selection-optimized partial least squares regression (AFS-PLSR) algorithm was developed. Using a single interband cascade laser and a single designated differential photoacoustic cell, simultaneous monitoring was achieved. By targeting the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) absorption features within the ICL emission range of 2966.70–2970.70 cm−1, the algorithm reduced the data volume by half. Single-component detection limits (1σ) reached 9.1 ppb (CH4), 5.7 ppb (C2H6), and 3.0 ppb (C3H8) at a 1-second integration time, corresponding to the system normalized noise equivalent absorption coefficient were 3.14 × 10−9 W·cm−1/√Hz, 1.56 × 10−9 W·cm−1/√Hz, and 2.97 × 10−9 W·cm−1/√Hz, respectively. In multi-gas detection, the coefficients of determination (R2) for the algorithmically processed prediction results of each gas test set were 0.9980, 0.9998, and 0.9999, respectively. This work effectively overcomes the spectral interference limitations, providing a reliable triple-gas detection solution for coal mines.

Introduction

As a foundational energy resource, coal safety holds pivotal strategic planning in the economies of many nations [1], [2], [3] Statistical data reveal that over 50% of mines are confronted with the risk of Coal Spontaneous Combustion (CSC), with more than 85% of mine fires attributable to this phenomenon [4], [5]. CSC not only leads to substantial wastage of coal resources and degradation of coal quality but also generates copious smoke and toxic/harmful gases [6], [7], thereby imposing severe environmental pollution [8]. Consequently, the early prediction of CSC has become an essential prerequisite for safeguarding the safety of mining operations. The CSC process inherently involves complex chemical reactions between coal and oxygen, generating characteristic gases such as methane (CH4), carbon monoxide (CO), ethane (C2H6), propane (C3H8), acetylene (C2H2) and others [6], [9], [10]. Most studies on classifying CSC stages rely on single-indicator gases for identification [11], [12]. However, the detectors with such single indicators have limitations, they fail to fully reflect the complex oxidation mechanisms and are vulnerable to external factors like ventilation dilution, leading to false alarms in early warning systems. In contrast, composite indicator gases integrate characteristic gas information across different oxidation stages, significantly improving early warning accuracy [13], [14], [15]. Zeng et al. proposed using CH4, C2H6, and C3H8 as indicator gases to determine the development stage of CSC and comprehensively and accurately predict the different oxidation stages of CSC [16]. However, a critical challenge that remains is the significant overlap of absorption spectra among CH4, C2H6, and C3H8, which renders it difficult for traditional detection technologies to achieve real-time, high-precision simultaneous measurement of the three gases.

Current mainstream technologies for multi-component gas analysis encompass gas chromatography (GC) [17], electrochemical sensor techniques [18], infrared spectroscopy (IR) [19], Raman spectroscopy [20], laser-induced fluorescence spectroscopy [21] and photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) [22]. GC features high sensitivity but is constrained by cumbersome pretreatment, prolonged analysis cycles, and the absence of real-time monitoring. Electrochemical sensors offer rapid response yet suffer from poor selectivity/stability and high susceptibility to environmental interference. IR is easy to operate but exhibits low sensitivity and inadequate trace detection performance. Raman spectroscopy enables non-destructive analysis, though it is plagued by weak signals and fluorescence interference. In fluorescence spectroscopy the intensity of the fluorescence is a function of gas concentration with spectral shifts or decay times providing temperature, velocity, or species identification, yet the identification for multiple simultaneous gas species remains a challenge. In contrast, PAS detection, with ultrahigh sensitivity, zero-background response, and micro-volume compatibility, has become a research focus in multi-gas analysis [23]. In PAS-based multi-component gas detection, a typical configuration involves multiple lasers in one-to-one correspondence with multiple photoacoustic cells. While this approach enables multi-component gas detection, it results in increased equipment size and weight, along with a significant rise in system complexity. Thus, simplifying the system architecture and reducing overall complexity, while ensuring multi-component gas detection accuracy, remains critical to PAS gas detection methodologies. Fu et al. developed a multi-component detection system based on all-optical non-resonant photoacoustic cells and broadband infrared thermal radiation sources. Operating at 10 Hz, it sequentially detected six target gases (CO2, CO, CH4, C2H6, C2H4, C2H2), with 3σ limits of detection (LODs) in the sub-ppm to ppm range [24]. However, its gas sensitivity is limited by the per-wavelength radiant intensity of the broadband light source, restricting further improvement in detection accuracy. Qiu et al. used a single distributed feedback (DFB) diode laser and a Herriott cell for quantitative detection of CSC. At a 2.5 s sampling time, the LODs of CO and CH4 reached sub-ppm levels, which could be reduced to ppb levels with extended integration time [25]. Although laser PAS can detect spectrally resolvable multicomponent gases, it cannot perform detection when gas absorption peaks overlap significantly. Therefore, the development of single-laser multicomponent gas detection method represents a critical area requiring urgent breakthroughs. Against this backdrop, integrating multi-component gas detection with algorithms addresses the aforementioned challenges [26], [27], [28], [29]. Commonly employed algorithms encompass Optimized Time Division Multiplexing (OTDM) [30], Independent Component Analysis (ICA) [31], Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) [32], and Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR) [33]. In spectral applications, OTDM mitigates channel crosstalk via temporal separation, yet necessitates high-precision timing control and exhibits limited temporal resolution in dynamic scenarios. ICA requires no prior information and exhibits strong adaptability, but is susceptible to noise and necessitates an adequate number of samples. MLR possesses a simple and interpretable structure, yet faces challenges in avoiding multicollinearity and demonstrates weak anti-interference capability. In contrast, the PLSR algorithm has been widely applied in diverse fields such as biology, medicine, soil detection, and spectroscopy, exhibiting outstanding performance [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39]. Cantatore et al. reported the use of a laser coupled with QEPAS technology and PLSR algorithm for the component analysis of CH4, C2H6, and C3H8 in natural gas, achieving good predictive accuracy at percent-level concentrations and low-pressure conditions [40]. In spectral applications, PLSR enables accurate concentration inversion under conditions of spectral overlap, filters out high-frequency noise, and demonstrates higher robustness compared to ICA. Furthermore, PLSR requires a smaller number of samples and is applicable to dynamic scenarios, thus outperforming other algorithms significantly. Nevertheless, PLSR has inherent limitations in data input, as it retains all data points by default. When spectra contain numerous irrelevant absorption peaks, excessive redundant information entering the model leads to reduced interpretability [29].

Based on this, this study proposed an absorption feature selection-optimized partial least squares regression (AFS-PLSR) algorithm. By effectively extracting and utilizing spectral features, this algorithm overcame crosstalk issues within the spectral overlap regions of CH4, C2H6, and C3H8, enabling simultaneous detection of all three gas components using only a single interband cascaded laser. Experiments compared the accuracy of WM and AM modulation modes, calibrated the concentration linear response curve of the sensing system, and achieved excellent prediction accuracy using AFS-PLSR algorithm. This approach effectively resolved spectral interference in single-laser multi-component photoacoustic detection, providing a solution for advancing its practical application in early-stage safety warning systems for coal mine gas.

Principle analysis

Photoacoustic spectroscopy

Upon selective absorption of incident light at a specific wavelength by a gas sample, the excited molecules undergo non-radiative relaxation to their ground state, inducing localized heating. This heat is primarily generated by molecular vibrational-translational (V-T) and vibrational-vibrational (V-V) relaxation processes, whose rate and efficiency depend on the concentration and energy level relationships of gas molecules [41]. When the incident light is subjected to intensity modulation with a periodic profile, the resultant thermal energy drives periodic thermal expansion and contraction, yielding longitudinal sound waves at the modulation frequency that are detectable by a microphone [42]. At low modulation rates with sufficient energy accumulation time, the relaxation process becomes highly efficient, the PA signal Stotal(λ) is expressed by Eq. (1)[43].(1)Stotal(λ)=Sm⋅P0⋅F⋅Cg⋅∑k=1Qαk(λ)where Sm is the microphone sensitivity; P0 is the incident laser power; F is the photoacoustic cell constant which depends on the geometric structure; Cg is the gas concentration; ∑k=1Qαk(λ) is the linear algebraic sum of the absorption coefficients αk(λ) of each component in the gas mixture at a specific wavelength λ, where Q is the total number of absorbing components in the gas mixture, with the index k (ranging from 1 to Q) denoting the k-th component. The absorption coefficient αk(λ) for the k-th component is given by αk(λ)=σk(λ)⋅Nk, where σk(λ) is the molecular absorption cross-section at wavelength λ, and Nk is the number density of the molecules [44]. The total detected PA signal, Stotal(λ), constitutes a linear superposition of the signals Sk(λ) from all individual components: Stotal(λ)=∑k=1QSk(λ).

The limitations of linear superposition of PA signals are primarily manifested in weak correlation between signals and component concentrations. When spectral lines overlap, even with known absorption cross-sections σk(λ) for each component, the detection system typically yields only a single total PA signal Stotal(λ). If the absorption features are overlapped with each other, it is difficult to distinguish the individual concentrations of different components in the gas mixture.

AFS-PLSR algorithm

The AFS-PLSR algorithm is designed to analyze PA signals generated by gas mixtures for concentration quantification. The algorithm is developed based on photoacoustic spectroscopic experiments of N gas mixture samples, where N denotes the total number of gas mixture samples. Each sample contains three components: CH4, C2H6, and C3H8. For the i-th sample (i = 1, 2, …, N), the concentration vector is defined as bi=[bi1,bi2,bi3], where bi1, bi2 and bi3 represent the concentrations of CH4, C2H6, and C3H8, respectively. The PA signal from each sample is recorded at P discrete sampling points, forming a signal vector ai=[ai1,ai2,…,aiP], where P denotes the total number of discrete sampling points and ai1, ai2, …, aiP represent the PA signal amplitude at each sampling point in the i-th sample (i = 1, 2, …, N), respectively. The complete dataset is organized into two matrices: the signal matrix A0=[a1;a2;…;aN]T (size N × P) and the concentration matrix B0=[b1;b2;…;bN]T (size N × 3). To eliminate the effects of instrumentation and experimental conditions, and enhance data accuracy and reliability, via Eq. The FWHM serves as a critical parameter characterizing spectral properties, which quantitatively describes the degree of energy dispersion of spectral intensity distribution in the frequency or wavelength domain. Specifically, the FWHM is defined as the wavelength interval when spectral intensity decays to 50% of its peak value [46], as described in Eq. Experiment configuration

Fig. 2 depicts the PAS schematic for multiple gas simultaneous detection with a single laser. A DFB ICL (HealthyPhoton, model HPQCL-Q) was employed as the excitation light source for the PA signal. The laser’s wavenumber tuning exhibits a good linear relationship with its drive current and the laser’s wavelength was scanned from 2966.70 to 2970.70 cm−1 to encompass the absorption features of CH4, C2H6 and C3H8. At those respective absorption peaks, the laser operated at an injection current of 84.9 mA with 11.3 mW output for CH4, 63.4 mA with 8.6 mW for C2H6, and 93.6 mA with 12.2 mW for C3H8, respectively. A laser controller (HealthyPhoton, model QC750-Touch) was used to set the laser current and temperature as 7 mA and 15 °C, respectively. Through systematic calibration, the tuning coefficient of the laser drive current at the current experimental temperature was determined to be −0.0958 cm−1/mA. Concurrently, the corresponding voltage-to-current conversion coefficient of the laser controller is 0.1 mA/mV. The latter was scanned from 59.0 mA to 99.0 mA in 0.1 mA increments to cover the above spectral range.Fig. 2Schematic of the PAS-based sensor for the simultaneous detection of CH4, C2H6, and C3H8.

Schematic of the PAS-based sensor for the simultaneous detection of CH4, C2H6, and C3H8.

For gas line configuration, certified gas cylinders with concentrations of 500 ppm CH4/N2, 2000 ppm C2H6/N2, 20 ppm C3H8/N2, and pure N2 were used as gas samples. Gas sample mixtures were prepared using a mass flow controller (Yanchuangzhongcheng, YC-ZC200) with a total flow rate set at 300 standard cm3/min (sccm). To mitigate the effects induced by water absorption peaks and relaxation enhancement, the target gas was dried via an electronic cold trap (Shanghaiyiheng, BCT-D02), it was found that when the operating temperature was set to −30°C, the concentration of sample water filtered through the electronic cold trap remains at 500 ppm, and all three gases can pass through completely without any loss. A pressure controller (Alicat Science, YK9) was employed to maintain the atmospheric pressure in the differential cell. A buffer bag was positioned upstream of the differential photoacoustic cell to deliver a smooth, continuous airflow, thereby creating stable operating conditions for the pressure controller. All experiments were conducted at room temperature (20 °C) and a working pressure of 760 Torr.

To suppress background noise and enhance detection limit, a custom differential photoacoustic cell was employed. Its operation is based on the anti-phase pressure distribution (180° phase difference) in dual resonant cavities. This configuration enables differential detection, where common-mode noise is canceled out while the photoacoustic signal is constructively enhanced, leading to superior anti-interference performance. Constructed by aluminum alloy, the photoacoustic cell featured a geometric structure comprising two cylindrical resonant cavities and two cylindrical buffer cavities. The resonant cavities were 140 mm in axial length and 7 mm in diameter, while the buffer cavities were 30 mm in axial length with a diameter of 34 mm, symmetrically positioned at both ends of the resonant cavities. The inner surface of the photoacoustic cell was polished and equipped with two calcium fluoride (CaF2) optical windows for light incidence. Two electret microphones (Yinglear, BO9745T, sensitivity: 100 mV/Pa) were symmetrically positioned at the center of each resonator cavity. The differential PA signal was collected into a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research Systems, SR830) for signal demodulation at the corresponding resonant frequency. A 500 ppm CH4/N2 standard mixed gas was used as the target gas, under the control of a signal generator (Tektronix, model AFG3021C), and a differential photoacoustic cell frequency sweep test was performed ranging from 1110 Hz to 1240 Hz, with a frequency step of 1 Hz. As shown in Fig. 3, the normalized resonance response curve determined the actual optimal resonance frequency to be 1174 Hz and the Q factor was calculated to be 18.34. The lock-in amplifier was configured with a filter slope of 12 dB/oct and an integration time of 1-second, corresponding to a detection bandwidth of 0.125 Hz. To investigate and optimize the system sensitivity performance, two operating modes were incorporated: Wavelength Modulation (WM) and Amplitude Modulation (AM). In the WM mode, the modulating signal was provided by the signal generator and consisted of a superposition of a sine wave and a sawtooth wave. The sine wave frequency was set to half the resonant frequency (587 Hz) and served as the reference signal for the lock-in amplifier, while the sawtooth wave scanned the absorption spectrum with a 60-second period. In the AM mode, a chopper (Stanford Research Systems, SR540) was used to generate the modulation signal at the resonant frequency of 1174 Hz, which then served as the reference input for the lock-in amplifier.Fig. 3Normalized frequency response curve measured by the differential photoacoustic cell experiment. Results and discussion

Modulation mode optimization and single component detection performance

Comparative tests were conducted using the WM mode and AM modes with single component gas samples of 500 ppm CH4, 200 ppm C2H6, and 20 ppm C3H8 diluted in N2. For the WM mode, the PA signals achieve maximum values when the modulation depth reached 2.2 times the half width at half maximum (HWHM) [48]. As shown in Fig. 4(a)∼(c), the optimal modulation depths were: 53 mV for CH4, 115 mV for C2H6, and 200 mV for C3H8.Fig. 4The PA signal amplitudes vs. modulation depths. (a) CH4, (b) C2H6, (c) C3H8.

Under the same set of laser currents, the AM mode yielded higher signal maxima: 87.64 mV for CH4, 41.12 mV for C2H6, and 9.37 mV for C3H8. The noise levels for CH4, C2H6, and C3H8 shown in Fig. 6 were obtained by calculating the standard deviation (1σ) of the PA signal. CSC can generally be divided into four consecutive stages: the latent period, the self-heating period, the combustion period, and the extinction period. During the latent period of CSC, CH4 concentrations ranged from 0 to 300 ppm with trace amounts of C2H6[49]. When C3H8 began to form, it indicated that the CSC had entered the self-heating period [50]. To meet the testing requirements for predicting the concentrations of three characteristic gases during the early stages of CSC, measurement experiments were conducted for CH4 (0–500 ppm), C2H6 (0–200 ppm), and C3H8 (0–20 ppm). Fig. 7. presents the concentration-response curves for CH4, C2H6, and C3H8. Each gas exhibited a linear response, as evidenced by coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9998, 0.9998, and 0.9999 for CH4, C2H6, and C3H8, respectively. The response sensitivity S, defined as the PA signal increase per unit concentration, were 177.5 μV/ppm for CH4, 204.0 μV/ppm for C2H6, and 465.7 μV/ppm for C3H8. According to the formula MDL = σ / S, the detection limits (1σ) for CH4, C2H6, and C3H8 were calculated as 9.1 ppb, 5.7 ppb, and 3.0 ppb, respectively. The corresponding normalized noise-equivalent absorption coefficient (NNEA) were 3.14 × 10−9 W·cm−1/√Hz (CH4), 1.56 × 10−9 W·cm−1/√Hz (C2H6), and 2.97 × 10−9 W·cm−1/√Hz (C3H8).Fig. 7Concentration response curves for the respective CH4, C2H6, and C3H8 were acquired in single-component gas detection experiments. AFS-PLSR algorithm analysis

Fig. 8 presents the PA signal test results for six different concentration ratios of CH4, C2H6, and C3H8 mixed gases diluted in N2. As shown in Table 2, when the concentration of a specific target gas (CH4, C2H6, or C3H8) in the mixed gas remains constant while only the concentrations of the other components are altered, the PA signals measured at the characteristic absorption currents of the corresponding gases still exhibit significant differences. Specifically, at a CH4 concentration of 50 ppm and a current of 84.9 mA, the signal difference between the two mixed gas groups was 1.48 mV, equivalent to 30.5% of the pure 50 ppm CH4 signal. At a C2H6 concentration of 30 ppm and a current of 63.4 mA, the signal difference was 0.37 mV, representing 11.6% of the pure 30 ppm C2H6 signal. At a C3H8 concentration of 4 ppm and a current of 93.6 mA, the signal difference reached 0.81 mV, accounting for 70.7% of the pure 4 ppm C3H8 signal. Within the current spectral region, these differences were primarily caused by secondary absorption peaks and sidelobe interference of CH4 and C2H6 and the broad spectral line of C3H8. The strong overlapped feature of the three target gases result in a loss of high selectivity, posing a major challenge for the ICL laser in achieving their simultaneous detection. The AFS-PLSR algorithm process is illustrated in Fig. 9.Fig. 9Flowchart of the AFS-PLSR algorithm.

Flowchart of the AFS-PLSR algorithm.

Step one: A total of 90 sets of mixed gas samples with varying concentrations of CH4, C2H6, and C3H8 were prepared. Among these samples, 80 sets were randomly allocated to the training set for constructing and optimizing the AFS-PLSR algorithm model, and the remaining 10 sets served as an independent test set to assess the model’s generalization prediction capability on unknown samples.

Step two: Absorption feature selection was performed on the experimental data. To determine the FWHM current scan ranges corresponding to the target absorption peaks of CH4, C2H6, and C3H8, we performed single-component current scans for each of these three gases and established their respective FWHM current ranges based on Eq. (4). The FWHM current range for the target absorption peak of CH4, corresponding to a laser drive current of 84.9 mA, was 83.7–85.7 mA. For C2H6, with a laser drive current of 63.4 mA, the FWHM current range was 62.0–66.0 mA. Similarly, for C3H8 (laser drive current: 93.6 mA), the FWHM current range spanned from 82.0 to 99.0 mA. In summary, the selected current ranges were 62.0–66.0 mA and 82.0–99.0 mA, yielding the original spectral matrices A0 and B0. By filtering out non-primary absorption regions, this process reduced the volume of data processing, thereby enhancing the algorithm’s operational efficiency.

Step three: Data normalization was applied to A0 and B0 to eliminate the effects of instrumentation and experimental conditions, yielding the normalized matrices A1 and B1, according to Eq. (2).

Step four: Algorithmic operations were performed on the training set and optimized the algorithmic model. A 10-fold cross-validation (CV) algorithm and a one-standard-error (1-SE) rule were applied to determine the optimal number of components for AFS-PLSR analysis, with the proportion of variance explained (PVE) and root mean square error of cross-validation (RMSECV) evaluated for each component. The PVE value essentially represents the proportion of the total variance explained by a specific component, calculated as shown in Eq. The 1-SE rule is widely adopted for latent-variable-based multivariate linear models [51]. This rule selects the smallest number of components such that the RMSECV remains within the minimum RMSECV plus one standard error, thus effectively preventing model overfitting [52]. While increasing the number of components improves the cumulative PVE value, enhances explanatory power, and reduces RMSECV, it may also cause the model to fit random noise, raise overfitting risk, and weaken generalization ability. Therefore, the 1-SE rule was used in this work to determine the optimal number of components and integrated into the algorithmic model.

10-fold CV algorithm results demonstrate that the RMSECV plateaus when the number of components exceeds five, attaining a minimum of 0.0374 ppm at nine components, as shown in Fig. 10 and Table 3. The corresponding standard error (SE) at this minimum is 0.0025 ppm, yielding a 1-SE rule threshold of 0.0399 ppm. The RMSECV for five components is 0.0398 ppm, which represents the smallest number of components falling below this threshold and thus constitutes the optimal number of components. Fig. 11 depicts the first fifteen cumulative PVE for PA signal matrix A1 and concentration matrix B1, respectively. At five components, the cumulative PVE for both the PA signal matrix A1 and concentration matrix B1 exceeds 99.8%, indicating strong model explanatory power. After comprehensive consideration, five components were ultimately selected as the optimal parameter to achieve a balance between prediction accuracy and model complexity. The first three components correspond to the distinct spectral contributions of CH4, C2H6, and C3H8 to the combined PA spectrum, respectively. The fourth component serves as the primary correction term for spectral crosstalk among the three gas species, while the fifth component compensates for systematic baseline drift induced by laser current tuning. Fig. 12 demonstrates the algorithm’s predictive performance on the test set. For CH4, C2H6, and C3H8, the predicted concentrations in the test set exhibit excellent linear relationship with the actual concentrations. Quantitative analysis indicated that the R2 values for the test set were 0.9980, 0.9998, and 0.9999, respectively. The RMSE values for the CH4, C2H6, and C3H8 test sets were 1.281 ppm, 0.431 ppm, and 0.042 ppm, respectively, with AREP of 1.53%, 0.84%, and 1.10%. These results demonstrate that the algorithm can accurately predict gas concentrations in the test dataset while maintaining low prediction errors. The AFS-PLSR algorithm was found to enhance computational efficiency and enable rapid quantitative analysis of multi-component gas concentrations by selecting only spectral information within the FWHM region as input variables, which effectively eliminates peripheral redundant bands characterized by low intensity and high noise. As a result, it reduces the number of data points input to the algorithm from the original 401 × 90 to approximately 200 × 90 while maintaining the same model R2. Under identical hardware and software conditions, the computational time required to process data using the AFS-PLSR algorithm decreased from 35 s to 17 s, roughly half the time needed for full-spectrum data processing. Conclusion

By employing the AFS-PLSR algorithm, simultaneous detection of multiple gas components was achieved using a single ICL laser and a differential photoacoustic cell. Experiments revealed that SNRs of the AM mode PA signals for the three gases were 1.13 times (CH4), 1.38 times (C2H6), and 2.23 times (C3H8) higher than those of the WM mode signals, respectively. In single-component gas detection, the calculated detection limits were 9.1 ppb for CH4, 5.7 ppb for C2H6, and 3.0 ppb for C3H8 and the system’s NNEA were 3.14 × 10−9 W·cm−1/√Hz (CH4), 1.56 × 10−9 W·cm−1/√Hz (C2H6), and 2.97 × 10−9 W·cm−1/√Hz (C3H8). Experiments validated the gas detection ranges of 0–500 ppm for CH4, 0–200 ppm for C2H6, and 0–20 ppm for C3H8, corresponding to the characteristic ranges during the early stages of CSC. The algorithm restricts spectral data to the FWHM range of the target analyte, thereby reducing the data volume by half. This shortens algorithm runtime and enhances computational efficiency. The final test set predictions demonstrate the model’s outstanding performance across all three gases: for CH4, R2 was 0.9980, RMSE was 1.281 ppm, and AREP was 1.53%; for C2H6, R2 was 0.9998, RMSE was 0.431 ppm, and AREP was 0.84%; For C3H8, R2 was 0.9999, RMSE was 0.042 ppm, and AREP was 1.10%. This paper addressed spectral interference issues in traditional gas detection methods and provided a solution and analytical framework for the simultaneous quantitative analysis of multiple gases in multi-component scenarios, especially CSC. In the future, we will seek collaboration with coal mines to conduct underground field tests, collect real-world scenario data, further optimize the robustness of our algorithm models, and ultimately advance this technology toward practical online monitoring and early warning equipment for CSC.
