The effect of a diet based on vegetable and dairy protein on biochemical and functional indicators of sarcopenia in patients with liver cirrhosis: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mahdiyeh Taghizadeh, Banafshe Khalese-Ranjbar, Amirreza Ostevari, Mohammad Taher, Amir Ali Sohrabpour, Mir Saeed Yekaninejad, Mohammad Javad Hosseinzadeh-Attar, Somayyeh Asghari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia, a prevalent complication of cirrhosis, is strongly linked to adverse clinical outcomes including hepatic encephalopathy and increased mortality which underscoring the urgent need for effective prevention and treatment strategies. This study investigates the effects of a diet based on vegetable and dairy protein on sarcopenia-related parameters in patients with cirrhosis.MethodsThis randomized, controlled clinical trial was carried out on 42 patients with cirrhosis aged 30 to 60 years. Patients were randomly assigned to either the diet based on vegetable and dairy protein (n = 21) or the standard omnivorous isocaloric and isonitrogenous diet (n = 21). Fasting blood samples, anthropometric measurements, handgrip strength, five times sit to stand (5STS) test, four meters gate speed (4MGS) as well as dietary intake and physical activity data were collected for all patients at the baseline and after 12 weeks of the intervention.ResultsFollowing 12 weeks of intervention, functional indicators of sarcopenia including handgrip strength, 4MGS, and the 5STS improved in vegetable and dairy protein-based diet group; however, no significant differences were found between the intervention and control groups (P > 0.05). Blood ammonia and myostatin levels remained stable in the vegetable and dairy protein-based diet group, whereas these biomarkers significantly increased in the standard diet group (P = 0.03). Moreover, a significant between-group difference was observed in blood ammonia levels (P = 0.02). Anthropometric indices including weight, BMI, mid-arm circumference (MAC), triceps skinfold (TSF), and mid-arm muscle circumference (MAMC) did not show significant differences between two groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionsIn conclusion, a vegetable and dairy protein-based diet effectively inhibited significant elevations in ammonia levels compared to the standard diet in persons with liver cirrhosis; however, anthropometric parameters and muscle function did not differ between two groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01272-9.Keywords: Liver cirrhosis, Sarcopenia, Plant and dairy protein, Animal protein, Ammonia, Myostatin

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis is the end stage of chronic liver disease resulting from various etiologies like viral hepatitis, alcohol use, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, genetic and autoimmune disorders, biliary and vascular problems, or sometimes even cryptogenic [1]. The 2021 Global Burden of Disease report estimated 1.7 billion global cases of liver cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases, resulting in 1.43 million deaths worldwide [2]. Liver cirrhosis is characterized by fibrosis, regenerative nodule formation, and architectural disruption [3], leading to impaired hepatic function, portal hypertension, and subsequent complications such as hematologic abnormalities, ascites, hyponatremia, hypoalbuminemia, varices, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), hepatorenal syndrome, and hepatocellular carcinoma [4].

Malnutrition and sarcopenia are common (with prevalence of 40% to 70%) but often overlooked complications in cirrhotic patients [5]. Sarcopenia is associated with an increased risk of falls, fractures, reduced quality of life, acute-on-chronic liver failure, HE, higher mortality, and poor post-transplant outcomes [6]. The pathogenesis of sarcopenia in cirrhosis extends beyond malnutrition alone, encompassing multifactorial mechanisms such as metabolic dysregulation which is characterized by depleted glycogen stores, exacerbated protein catabolism, reduced circulating branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), systemic inflammation, and hyperammonemia [7, 8]. Notably, elevated myostatin levels have been identified as a key mediator, correlating with accelerated muscle wasting and decreased survival in these patients [9, 10].

Emerging evidence suggests that the source of dietary protein holds particular clinical relevance. Observational studies link higher intakes of dairy and vegetables proteins with reduced mortality [11, 12], and improvements in muscle strength and nutritional parameters of patients with cirrhosis [13]. Intervention studies further demonstrated that patients who consumed plant proteins experienced reduction in severity of HE, along with decreases in blood ammonia [14–16]. These benefits are likely due to their high BCAA content [17] and the fiber-mediated prebiotic and laxative effects that aid ammonia excretion [18, 19]. A combined dietary approach, integrating vegetable and dairy proteins, may therefore offer an optimal nutritional strategy by providing high-quality BCAAs while mitigating issues like early satiety associated with purely plant-based diets.

Existing studies investigating the effects of vegetable and dairy proteins in patients with cirrhosis have been limited and primarily focused on the management and prevention of HE [14, 16, 20]. Furthermore, the few studies targeting sarcopenia often lack standard control groups and do not specifically evaluate the comparative benefits of a diet based on these protein sources versus an isocaloric and isonitrogenous standard diet. Importantly, we hypothesized that diet based on vegetable and dairy proteins can improve blood ammonia and myostatin levels in patients with cirrhosis. Therefore, this randomized controlled clinical trial was designed to investigate the effects of a vegetable and dairy protein-based diet, compared to a standard diet, on biochemical and functional indicators of sarcopenia in patients with liver cirrhosis.

Methods

The ethics committee of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences approved this study (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1402.060) which was performed in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was registered at the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (Registration no. IRCT20220426054667N2) on 08/06/2023. This study follows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline. Prior to the study enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed using G-Power software. Assuming a significance level (alpha) of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and an anticipated effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.8 for blood ammonia variable, the required sample size was calculated to be 21 patients per group. To account for a potential 10% attrition rate, 23 patients recruited in each group.

Study population

Forty-six subjects with liver cirrhosis diagnosed by a physician for at least six months and aged 30 to 60 years old were included in this study. Patients were selected from specialized gastroenterology clinics in Tehran based on the study’s inclusion criteria from September 2023 to June 2024.

Patients were not included in the study in cases of Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score > 18 or Child-Pugh class C, active gastrointestinal bleeding; serum creatinine > 1.5 mg/dL in the past month; pregnancy; breastfeeding; alcohol or tobacco use in the past six months; comorbidities including diabetes, pulmonary disease, heart failure, psychological disorders, hepatocellular carcinoma or other cancers, acute viral hepatitis, cholestatic cirrhosis, autoimmune cirrhosis, other autoimmune diseases, hyperuricemia, and musculoskeletal disorders; as well as intake of medications including corticosteroids or immunosuppressives. Patients excluded from the study in cases of occurrence of serious adverse events requiring hospitalization; development of any new disease during the study; undergoing liver transplantation; and non-adherence to the study protocol (< 70% compliance).

Study design

This study was a randomized, controlled clinical trial in which 46 patients were randomly allocated into two intervention groups using a block randomization method stratified by age (30–45 and 46–60), sex (male or female), and malnutrition condition (adequately nourished, moderately malnourished or severely malnourished) determined by Royal Free Hospital global assessment (RFH-GA) [21]. A random sequence was generated using the random allocation software (RAS). Due to the nature of the dietary intervention, blinding of participants and research staff to group allocation was not feasible. The intervention group (n = 23) was prescribed a diet based on vegetable and dairy protein and the control group (n = 23) received an isocaloric and isonitrogenous standard diet for 12 weeks. The Harris-Benedict equation was used for the estimation of patients’ basal energy requirements individually and multiplied by 1.3 to achieve total energy expenditure. The prescribed diets contained 1.2 g/kg of body weight protein, 30% fat, and 50–55% carbohydrate. Required amounts from each food group were determined, and a meal plan consisting of three main meals and four snacks was provided. The protein requirement of the intervention group was provided through vegetable and dairy sources such as bread, grains, vegetables, legumes, soy, soy milk, nuts, and dairy. On the other hand, standard diet contained all protein sources included bread, grains, vegetables, legumes, types of meats, eggs, dairy, and nuts. We conducted individual interviews during follow-up visits every two weeks to assess adherence to the prescribed diet plan, discuss and resolve any barriers or concerns related to the diet and encourage compliance. In addition, food record questionnaires were administered on a single day during the third, sixth, and ninth weeks to assess adherence to the diet. Noncompliance was defined as follows: For both groups, if a participant’s energy intake was less than 70% of their total energy expenditure (TEE), they were considered noncompliant. Additionally, for the intervention group specifically, noncompliance also occurred if the ratio of protein intake from vegetable and dairy sources to total protein intake was less than 70%.

Study measurements

A trained member of the research team carried out anthropometric measurements for all patients at the beginning and the end of the study. Body weight was recorded in light clothing without shoes using a scale (Seca, Hamburg, Germany), to the nearest 0.5 kg. A barefoot measured height was determined using a mounting tape with a precision of 0.1 cm. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the squared height (m2). To measure Triceps skinfold (TSF), first, a vertical fold of skin and subcutaneous fat was grasped at the posterior-medial site of the left arm while standing, using the thumb and index finger to pull it away from the underlying muscle. The caliper jaws were placed at the point of contact with the two layers of skin, and after applying full pressure for 2–4 s, the skin thickness was measured in millimeters then converted to centimeters. For more accurate results, the average of three measurements taken with a minimum interval of 15 s was considered. MAC was measured using a tape measure at the midpoint of the left arm, between the elbow and the acromion, with a precision of 0.1 centimeters while the individual stood with their arm relaxed alongside the body. Finally, mid arm muscle circumference (MAMC) was calculated using the following formula: MAMC = MAC – (3.1416 × TSF). To ensure the quality of anthropometric measurements, all assessments were conducted by a trained researcher following standardized protocols. Repeated measurements were taken to minimize intra-observer variability, and equipment was regularly calibrated for accuracy.

Muscle function assessments were done for all patients at the beginning and the end of the study by a trained member of the research team. In the 4 m gate speed (4MGS) test, the patient was asked to walk through a 6-meter corridor at his usual speed. This 6-meter zone consisted of an initial 1-meter zone for increasing speed, a middle 4-meter zone for moving at a normal speed, and a final 1-meter zone for slowing down. From the beginning to the end of the middle 4-meter zone, the time was recorded using a stopwatch. The number 4 was divided by the measured time to obtain the individual’s movement speed. Handgrip strength was measured using a dynamometer, with the hands at a 90-degree angle and elbows by the sides. The patient pressed the handle with maximum force, and measurements were taken from both hands to calculate an average. In the five times sit to stand (5STS) test, the patient asked to sat and stood up from a chair five times consecutively, and the total time was recorded with a stopwatch.

Food record questionnaires for three days (two weekdays and one weekend day) were used for dietary assessment in the week just before the study and week 12, while single-day records in weeks 3, 6, and 9 were used to monitor dietary adherence. The analysis of dietary intakes was conducted using a modified Nutritionist 4 software (First Databank, Hearst Corp., San Bruno, CA, USA) to determine the intake of energy; carbohydrate; total fat; total protein; animal, plant, and dairy based protein; as well as fiber. Dietary fiber was measured to adjust its confounding effect on blood ammonia to purify the effect of plant protein.

The Physical Activity record was used for assessing physical activity levels for two days (including one weekend) in the week just before the study and week 12.

Twelve-hour fasting blood samples (10 cc) were collected at the beginning and end of the intervention period using Serum-Separating tubes for serum isolation, EDTA tubes for whole blood stabilization, and PT blood tubes for coagulation studies. Samples were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum and plasma. Serum samples were promptly kept at − 70 °C until carrying out the analysis. Whole blood samples immediately used for measuring ammonia levels by an enzymatic method in Noor Pathobiology and Genetic Laboratory, Tehran, Iran. The International Normalized Ratio (INR) was determined on the same day at same center. All other biochemical tests were performed at the Central Laboratory of School of Nutritional Sciences and Dietetics, Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. Serum creatinine and total bilirubin were measured by an enzymatic method (Biorex Fars Inc. kit, Shiraz, Iran) using an autoanalyzer set (BT 1500, Italy). Serum myostatin level was assessed by ELISA kit (Human Myostatin, MSTN ELISA Kit, ZellBio) with sensitivity of 9 ng/L.

Statistical analyses

SPSS software version 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to perform all statistical analyses. The Q-Q plot and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test were used to assess the normality of the distribution of variables. Independent sample t-tests (for normally distributed variables), Mann-Whitney (for non-normally distributed variables and ordinal variables), chi-square, or Fisher’s exact test (for nominal variables) were performed to compare variables between groups where appropriate. Within-group comparisons were conducted using paired samples t-tests (for normally distributed variables) and Wilcoxon (for non-normally distributed variables). Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was applied to determine the impact of the intervention when adjusted for baseline values for normally distributed variables and Quade Nonparametric ANCOVA for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were reported as frequency (percentage), numeric normal variables as mean (standard error of the mean [SE]) and numeric non-normal variables as median (Interquartile Range). In all analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

General characteristics of the patients

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the study patients in the vegetable and dairy protein-based diet group (n = 21) and the standard diet group (n = 21). The mean age was 55.2 years in the intervention group and 52.6 years in the control group, with no significant difference (p = 0.23). Both groups had an identical sex distribution, with 66.7% males and 33.3% females. Nutritional status classification showed that the majority of participants in the intervention group (85.7%) were adequately nourished versus 71.4% in the control group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.26). Other clinical parameters, including weight, BMI, and MELD score, as well as physical activity levels, were comparable between groups (all p-values > 0.05) (Table 1). Physical activity levels did not change significantly throughout the study neither within, nor between the two groups (p > 0.05).

Dietary intakes

Energy, macronutrients, and fiber intake did not differ between the two groups at baseline. As can be seen in the Table 2, the mean intakes of energy, carbohydrate, and protein before and after the study did not differ significantly between the groups. In the standard diet group, the mean dietary fiber intake before and after the study did not differ significantly (21.97 ± 1.72 g/day to 22.18 ± 1.78 g/day, p = 0.93), but in the vegetable and dairy protein-based diet group, the mean fiber intake before and after the intervention was significantly different (21.16 ± 1.82 g/day to 29.08 ± 2.28 g/day, p < 0.001).

As expected, the intervention group exhibited a significant change in mean intake of plant (39.41 ± 3.18 g/day to 58.54 ± 4.18 g/day), dairy (7.15 ± 1.15 g/day to 13.66 ± 1.28 g/day), and animal (38.06 ± 4.56 g/day to 15.45 ± 3.03 g/day) proteins before and after the study (all P-values < 0.001). In contrast, the standard diet group showed no significant change in mean protein intake from each of these sources over the same period (all P-values p > 0.05). Furthermore, the between-group differences in fiber, as well as plant and animal protein intakes, were statistically significant at the end of the study (p < 0.01) (Table 2).

Patients in the intervention group demonstrated relatively acceptable compliance with the prescribed diet plan, achieving an 83% adherence rate for the ratio of plant and dairy protein sources to total protein intake. The estimated adherence to prescribed energy intake was 89% in standard diet versus 86% in the plant and dairy protein-based diet.

No significant differences observed in handgrip strength, 4MGS, and 5STS between two groups at baseline. After the intervention, the average handgrip strength values ​​increased in both the standard diet group (27.35 ± 2.00 kg to 29.19 ± 2.01 kg) and the vegetable and dairy protein-based diet group (28.52 ± 1.94 kg to 30.86 ± 1.89 kg), but this increase was only significant in the vegetable and dairy protein-based diet group (p = 0.08 vs. p < 0.01). However, comparison of final values between the two groups with adjusting for confounders showed no significant difference. Likewise, regarding 4MGS, after the intervention, the standard diet group had a slight and non-significant increase (1.22 ± 0.07 m/s to 1.3 ± 0.08 m/s, p = 0.17) and the intervention group had a more significant increase (1.26 ± 0.1 m/s to 1.76 ± 0.16 m/s, p < 0.001). However, when comparing the final values ​​with adjustment for baseline values ​​and changes in fiber intake, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of 4MGS. After the intervention, both groups showed improvement in 5STS and these within-group changes were significant in both groups (12.77 ± 0.94 s to 8.88 ± 0.62 s in intervention group and 12.21 ± 0.68 s to 9.8 ± 0.43 s in control group, p < 0.001). Our results showed a borderline significant difference between the two groups regarding 5STS (p = 0.05). However, after adjusting the results for baseline values ​​and changes in fiber intake, the mean 5STS in the intervention group was not significantly different compared to the control group (p = 0.27).

At baseline, there were no significant differences in serum myostatin and blood ammonia levels between the study groups. Following the intervention, serum ammonia levels increased significantly in the standard diet group (36.82 ± 3.01 micmol/L to 43.57 ± 3.20 micmol/L, p-within = 0.03) compared to the intervention group (44.68 ± 5.3 micmol/L to 39.20 ± 3.15 micmol/L, p-within = 0.12) (p = 0.02). However, after adjusting for baseline values and changes in dietary fiber intake, this difference was no longer significant (p = 0.06). Serum myostatin levels showed a significant increase in the control group [304.03 (267.85–374.54.85.54) ng/L to 346 (262.13–364.86.13.86) ng/L, p = 0.03] and a non-significant decrease in the intervention group [274 (225.66–346.10) ng/L to 273 (196.73–319.07.73.07) ng/L, p = 0.28]; nevertheless, the between-group comparison did not reveal a statistically significant difference (p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effect of a diet based on plant and dairy protein in patients with liver cirrhosis. Although the benefits of this diet on encephalopathy are well documented [14, 16, 20], to our knowledge, this is the first interventional trial to evaluate its impact on biochemical and functional markers of sarcopenia in cirrhosis, compared to a standard diet. Although blood ammonia and myostatin levels significantly increased in the standard diet group after 12 weeks, a diet based on plant and dairy proteins effectively prevented these increases. The intervention group demonstrated notable improvements in muscular strength and physical performance relative to baseline; however, these improvements did not reach statistical significance compared to the control group at the end of the study. Additionally, no significant effects on anthropometric indices (weight, BMI, MAC, MAMC, and TSF) were observed.

Ammonia, a toxic byproduct of amino acid and urea metabolism, accumulates in cirrhosis due to impaired hepatic clearance and is widely recognized as a key factor in the development and progression of sarcopenia [8, 22]. In the present study the vegetable and dairy based protein diet was associated with lower blood ammonia levels in comparison to the standard diet; however, after adjusting for the confounding effect of changes in dietary fiber intake, this reduction was not statistically significant. This finding underscores the critical role that fiber plays in these dietary protein sources. Consistent with our results, several studies have reported that plant-based diets reduce blood ammonia levels in patients with liver cirrhosis [16, 20, 23]. However, some small and short-term studies did not find significant differences in ammonia levels between plant- and animal-based diets [24, 25]. According to existing studies, a plant and dairy protein-based diet influences ammonia levels through multiple mechanisms. Fermentation of dietary fiber by gut microbiota produces acids that acidify the large intestine, promoting increased ammonia excretion [18]. Colonic bacteria utilize nitrogen for growth, incorporating a significant amount of nitrogen into their biomass, which is then excreted in the feces. Additionally, fiber enhances colonic motility and reduces transit time, influencing nitrogen elimination and preventing ammonia absorption [26]. This diet may also alter the amino acid profile in favor of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) [17]. Metabolism of these amino acids promotes ammonia clearance by facilitating glutamate formation, which can then combine with free ammonia to produce glutamine [27, 28]. Moreover, elevated plasma arginine and citrulline concentrations observed in individuals consuming plant-based diets may further aid ammonia clearance via the urea cycle [18, 24, 29].

Myostatin, member of transforming growth factor beta family [30], negatively regulates satellite cell proliferation and differentiation, impairing skeletal muscle repair and regeneration [31]. This myokine plays a key role in the pathogenesis of sarcopenia by inhibiting protein synthesis pathways such as mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling and activating catabolic pathways such as the ubiquitin-proteasome system [32]. Elevated myostatin levels have been observed in cirrhotic patients and are associated with muscle loss [10], making myostatin inhibition a promising therapeutic strategy [33]. In our study, myostatin levels remained stable in the vegetable and dairy-protein based diet group, while they increased significantly in the standard diet group; however, differences between groups were not statistically significant. Limited research exists on dietary effects on myostatin, but previous trials of BCAA supplementation in cirrhotic patients showed no significant changes in serum myostatin levels [34] and myostatin expression [35]. In a study of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, exercise transiently reduced myostatin expression both with and without whey protein concentrate supplementation, but the levels tended to return to baseline over time [37]. Overall, myostatin remains a key target in sarcopenia management, but dietary interventions alone may have limited impact on its circulating levels.

The diet based on plant and dairy proteins may modulate myostatin levels through reducing ammonia levels. Since, hyperammonemia has been shown to increase the expression of muscle myostatin by an NF-κB–mediated mechanism and increase autophagy of nitrated skeletal muscle proteins [36] and decrease muscle mass [7, 10]. Accordingly, ammonia-lowering therapies like rifaximin and L-ornithine L-aspartate has been shown to decrease ammonia and myostatin levels, improving muscle mass and strength in animal models [22].

Regarding the functional parameters of sarcopenia, the intervention diet led to significant improvements within the group; however, these improvements were not sufficient to create a significant difference between the two groups after the intervention. With respect to anthropometric parameters, no significant changes were observed either within or between the two groups. Due to the progressive nature of sarcopenia in cirrhosis, which is driven by intricate metabolic disorders, sarcopenia indices may have limited response to short-term (three months) nutritional interventions [37]. Similar studies in this field have also yielded conflicting results. A cross-sectional study revealed that higher intakes of dairy and vegetable proteins were significantly associated with greater muscle strength in cirrhosis patients and positively correlated with body weight, visceral fat, and TSF, while no significant correlations were found with MAC, MAMC, or BMI [13]. In another study patients who received nutritional therapy incorporating plant-based sources and casein-based dairy products demonstrated a significant improvement in MAC and handgrip strength without any changes in TSF and body fat mass compared to those who did not receive any nutritional intervention [20]. It is noteworthy that the control group had significantly lower caloric and protein intake, which may have influenced the differences observed, whereas in our study, calorie intake remained stable before and after the intervention, with both groups consuming isocaloric and isonitrogenous diets. Similarly, BCAA supplementation combined with a high-protein, high-fiber diet in patients with cirrhosis over a six-month period showed a significant increase in MAMC and a decrease in TSF compared to the control group [38]. However, BCAA supplementation combined with a 30-minute daily exercise program and nutritional counseling had no significant effect on skeletal muscle mass index, handgrip strength, 6 m gate speed, or 6-min walk distance during six months in in patients with liver cirrhosis and sarcopenia [34].These seemingly contradictory findings may result from several metabolic effects associated with plant proteins. For instance, plant proteins may influence nutrient absorption, modulate the gut microbiome, and elicit distinct hormonal responses that differ from those triggered by animal proteins. Other contributing factors could include edema, which might distort body measurements, and the short duration of the intervention, which may limit observable nutritional and performance responses.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, the duration of the intervention may have been insufficient to detect significant changes in anthropometric measurements and functional parameters related to sarcopenia. Additionally, due to cost constraints, direct calorimetry was not utilized to accurately estimate energy requirements. Furthermore, more precise assessment of muscle mass using imaging techniques such as CT scan or MRI would strengthen the evaluation of sarcopenia in future studies. Finally, this study did not specifically focus on patients with liver cirrhosis with pre-existing sarcopenia, a group that may experience more pronounced metabolic and functional benefits from dietary interventions. Future research should consider enrolling patients diagnosed with sarcopenia at baseline to better assess the impact of vegetable- and dairy-based protein diets on this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, a diet based on vegetable and dairy protein over a 12-week period improved hyperammonemia in patients with cirrhosis. This dietary approach also offered potential benefits in inhibiting the elevation of myostatin and improving functional outcomes related to sarcopenia, including handgrip strength, 4MGS, and 5STS. However, these improvements did not reach statistical significance when compared to the standard diet group. Therefore, further long-term studies are needed to confirm these findings and to determine whether this dietary strategy can effectively enhance indicators of sarcopenia in patients with cirrhosis or if strict dietary restrictions are unnecessary.
