Development of a risk assessment model for elder abuse vulnerability among ethnic minority older adults in Hunan, China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Chunhong Shi, Yizhou Liu, Xin Long, Linling Chang, Mengyang Li, Chunyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElder abuse is a significant public health issue, particularly among those from ethnic minorities who often face unique cultural and social challenges that may exacerbate their vulnerability. However, research on elder abuse in this population remains limited. This study aimed to explore the prevalence of vulnerability to abuse among older ethnic minorities in China and to develop a risk assessment model to inform the development of prevention strategies in this population.MethodsFrom February to September 2024, a cross-sectional study was employed to survey 314 older adults from ethnic minorities in Chenzhou, Yongzhou, and Jishou, in Hunan Province of China. The questionnaire included the Vulnerability to Abuse Screening Scale (VASS), the UCLA Loneliness Scale-8 (ULS-8), and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Statistical analyses were performed using the chi-square test and logistic regression.ResultsThe overall prevalence of self-reported elder abuse risk among older ethnic minorities in China was 58.92%, with the main types being psychological abuse (34.08%), physical abuse (29.94%), neglect (28.34%), and financial exploitation (13.06%). Loneliness and depressive symptoms were identified as risk factors for elder abuse vulnerability (odds ratio [OR] = 1.098 and 1.101, respectively), whereas living in rural areas and receiving community support were identified as protective factors (OR = 0.565 and 0.520, respectively). The risk assessment model was developed as follows: logit(p) = − 1.154 − 0.571×living in rural areas − 0.653× community support services + 0.094×loneliness + 0.096×depressive symptoms. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the model was 0.744, with a sensitivity of 0.568 and specificity of 0.860. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test showed a P value of 0.383.ConclusionsThe prevalence of self-reported elder abuse risk among older ethnic minorities in China is relatively high and is associated with living in rural areas, receiving community support services, loneliness, and depressive symptoms. The prediction model developed in this study has satisfactory discriminative efficacy and holds certain social application value. These findings inform policymakers and service providers to promote community support services, integrate mental health interventions, and improve older adults’ rights protection networks in ethnic minorities to reduce the vulnerability to abuse.Contributions to the literature• The prevalence of self-reported elder abuse risk among ethnic minority older adults in China is relatively high, reaching an overall rate of 58.92%.• Loneliness, depressive symptoms, living in rural areas and receiving community support were associated with self-reported elder abuse risk.• Enhancing community support, especially in rural areas, alleviating loneliness, and reducing depressive symptoms may reduce vulnerability to abuse and improve well-being.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Globally, population ageing is accelerating. The global population aged ≥ 60 years is projected to surge from 1.1 billion in 2023 to 2.1 billion by 2050 [1]. China is currently experiencing a rapidly ageing population. As of 2024, the number of individuals aged ≥ 60 years had reached 310 million in China, constituting 22% of the total population [2]. China is a unified, multi-ethnic nation comprising 56 ethnic groups. The Han ethnic group forms the majority at 91.11%, with the remaining 8.89% comprising 55 ethnic minorities [3], which are also facing the challenges of an ageing population. Existing research [4] indicates that by 2020, the proportion of individuals aged ≥ 60 years within these groups had reached 13.10%, amounting to 16.4183 million older adults.

Elder abuse is a serious and underreported social issue that impacts millions of older adults worldwide [5]. It is defined as ‘a single or repeated act, or lack of appropriate action, occurring within any relationship where there is an expectation of trust, which causes harm or distress to an older person’ [6]. Elder abuse includes physical abuse, psychological/emotional abuse, sexual abuse, financial exploitation, and neglect [7]. The perpetrators can comprise relatives, caregivers, or other individuals expected to be trustworthy [8]. The World Health Organization reports that approximately one in six people aged 60 and above have experienced some form of abuse [9]. By 2050, the number of abused older adults is estimated to 320 million, prompting a call for global action to protect their rights and well-being [10]. Self-reported abuse among community-dwelling older adults ranges from 19.4% to 54.1% [11–13]. Besides, the prevalence of elder abuse was 78.33 per 1000 person-years in East Asian cultures [14], with higher rates among women [15]. Both witnessed and experienced elder abuse can negatively impact the mental and physical well-being of older adults [16, 17], potentially leading to physical harm, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairment, financial hardship, forced placement in nursing homes, and even premature death [18].

Elder abuse is associated with a range of factors including sociodemographic characteristics, psychological and socioeconomic factors, and health conditions. In terms of sociodemographic characteristics, older adults who are female, illiterate, and living in nuclear families are at higher risk [13]. Additionally, those with religious beliefs, more children, and those cohabiting with a spouse show increased rates of self-perceived abuse [19]. Notably, older adults with less wealth and education are also more vulnerable [20]. Regarding psychological factors, poor mental health [15] (e.g. depression [19, 21]) and a history of abuse [11] are significantly related to any type of abuse. Self-perceived health, rheumatism [21], multimorbidity, lower functional levels, aggressive behaviour [12], and severe pain [19] are health conditions related to self-perceived elder abuse among older adults. Furthermore, the frailty, illness, and the need for help related to dysfunctional environments are also associated with elder abuse [22]. In the realm of social relationships, social networking [12] and ageism [23] are linked to elder abuse. Additionally, studies reveal that distinct racial and ethnic populations exhibit different patterns of risk across various forms of elder abuse [24]. For instance, African-American seniors might be more susceptible to financial and psychological mistreatment compared to their Caucasian counterparts [25], whereas Hispanic elders appear to face lower neglect risk [26]. However, research on elder abuse among older minority subgroups remains insufficient [27].

Despite widespread consensus on the severity of elder abuse, existing research in China has focused on elder abuse among the Han ethnic group, with limited exploration among ethnic minorities. This study aimed to explore the prevalence of self-reported elder abuse risk among ethnic minority older adults in China, develop a risk assessment model to identify high-risk individuals susceptible to elder abuse and provide protective measures against elder abuse.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a cross-sectional approach to evaluate self-reported elder abuse risk and develop a risk assessment model for ethnic minority older adults in China, adhering to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study sample

Convenience sampling was used to select older adults from ethnic minorities in Chenzhou, Yongzhou, and Jishou, in the Hunan Province of China. Several ethnic groups, such as Yao, Miao, Zhuang, and Tujia minorities inhabit these areas. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals registered as ethnic minorities in their household registration booklets; (2) aged ≥ 60 years; (3) having good cognitive abilities; and (4) providing informed consent and voluntarily participating in this study. Following Kendall’s guidelines for multivariate analyses [28], a minimum of 5–10 observations per independent variable was required. With 22 predictors (20 demographic-sociological variables, loneliness, and depression) and a 20% sample dropout rate, the estimated sample size ranged from 138 to 275. Finally, 314 participants were included in the study, meeting the required sample size.

Data collection

The research team comprised two doctoral advisors and six undergraduate students, three of whom belonged to ethnic minority groups and were proficient in Yao, Tujia, and Miao languages. All investigators had received training related to field investigations, including survey techniques, application of investigation instruments, and quality control methods. Prior to the formal survey, a pilot study was conducted from January 22 to 29, 2024, involving 30 ethnic minority older adults in Hunan Province. After excluding 4 invalid responses, 26 questionnaires were retained for analysis. Based on the pilot findings, the initial questionnaire was revised, and the formal questionnaire added three relevant items: familiarity with laws and regulations related to elder abuse, receipt of community mental support, and self-rated life satisfaction. From February to September 2024, we conducted face-to-face surveys using paper questionnaires to collect data. Potential participants were initially screened for ethnic affiliation as registered in their household booklets. Questionnaires were distributed only to those identified as ethnic minorities. Participants completed the questionnaires independently whenever feasible; investigators provided assistance to those with reading or writing difficulties. Ultimately, 406 questionnaires were distributed and subsequently retrieved, yielding a retrieval rate of 100%. After excluding 92 questionnaires with ambiguous responses or incomplete data, 314 valid questionnaires were analysed, with an effective response rate of 77.34%.

Measurements

The sociodemographic questionnaire

Based on the ecological model [24], the questionnaire was self-designed and included 18 items, such as age, gender, whether or not having a spouse, education level, living arrangement, self-rated family relationships, self-rated health status, and major income source.

Vulnerability to abuse screening scale (VASS)

Originally developed by Schofield and Mishra [29], this scale was adapted for use in China by Hu et al. [30] to screen for vulnerability to abuse. This scale has been widely adopted for self-reported elder abuse risk in several countries and has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in older adults [21, 31–33], including in the Chinese older adults [15, 19, 34]. Notably, the study population in Li et al. [34] included a subset of ethnic minority older adults. Moreover, our pilot study showed the high reliability of the Chinese version of the VASS, with a KR-20 value of 0.927. The Chinese version of the VASS contains 14 items divided into four types of abuse: physical abuse (items 1, 12, 13, and 14), psychological abuse (items 2, 3, 4, 7, and 8), neglect (items 5, 6, and 9), and financial abuse (items 10 and 11). The response options for each item are ‘never’, ‘occasionally’, ‘sometimes’, and ‘often’. Screen-positive abuse is considered present if items 1–4 and 7–14 are answered with ‘sometimes’ or ‘often’, or if items 5 and 6 are answered with ‘never’ or ‘occasionally’. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.850.

The UCLA loneliness scale-8 (ULS-8)

Hays and DiMatteo [35] developed this scale in 1987 to assess loneliness in individuals. The Chinese version has exhibited satisfactory psychometric properties [36–39] and has been previously used within ethnic minority populations [40], demonstrating good cultural sensitivity. The pilot study showed that the scale had excellent reliability and was suitable for factor analysis, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.849, a KMO value of 0.817, and a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ² = 146.676, p < 0.001). The scale consists of eight items with a unidimensional structure. Items 1,2,4,5,7 and 8 are scored from 1 point (‘never’) to 4 points (‘always’). Items 3 and 6 are reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 8 to 32 points, with higher scores indicating a greater degree of loneliness. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.802.

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The scale is a self-assessment depression tool developed by Columbia University based on the nine symptom criteria for depression [41]. This scale was adapted for use in China by Xu et al. [42] and has demonstrated strong psychometric properties for identifying depression in older individuals from ethnic minority groups [43, 44]. Our pilot survey also indicated adequate reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.847, a KMO value of 0.706, and a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ² = 88.373, p < 0.001). The Chinese version of the PHQ-9 contains nine items divided into two dimensions: cognitive-affective symptoms (items 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, and 9), and somatic symptoms (items 3, 4, and 5) [15]. Each item is scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), with total score ranging from 0 to 27 points. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms [45]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.888.

Ethical considerations

This research was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee at Xiangnan University (Approval No.: 2023YXLL040). Data collection began only after obtaining written informed consent from all participants. During the survey, personal identifiers such as names or ID numbers were not collected. The study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (2024 Revision), which highlights respect for participant autonomy, confidentiality, and the principle of non-maleficence. All potential participants provided informed and voluntary participation in this study after reviewing detailed information about the study’s objectives, procedures, potential benefits, and risks. Participants could withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. As a token of appreciation for completing the questionnaire, each respondent received a small hygiene product (either laundry detergent or soap, valued at approximately 5 RMB). Data were stored on private, password-protected computers of the research team.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics for continuous data included means and standard deviations, whereas categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square (χ²) test was used to compare the prevalence of self-reported elder abuse risk across different demographic variables. Independent samples t-tests were used to compare age, loneliness, and depression between participants with and without screen-positive elder abuse. Binary logistic regression was applied for multivariate analysis to identify factors associated with self-reported elder abuse risk. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to determine the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and the Youden index. The model’s goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Furthermore, nomograms were constructed using the RMS package in RStudio to visually represent the model.

Results

Participants’ demographic profile

The 314 ethnic minority participants comprised 129 women (41.08%) and 185 men (58.92%), aged 60–98 years (mean: 67.72 ± 7.16). Most were married (78.03%), had a junior high school education or less (71.02%), lived in rural areas (57.01%), reported chronic diseases (54.78%), and were unfamiliar with laws and regulations related to elder abuse (87.26%). Their primary leisure activities included chatting with others (63.69%), watching TV or reading (42.36%), and doing housework (41.72%).

The prevalence of screen-positive abuse, depression, and loneliness among the participants

Among the 314 ethnic minority participants, 185 self-reported elder abuse risk, yielding a screen-positive abuse prevalence rate of 58.92%. Specifically, 94 (29.94%) participants self-reported physical abuse, 107 (34.08%) psychological abuse, 89 (28.34%) neglect, and 41 (13.06%) financial abuse. The mean loneliness score was 17.01 ± 4.83 out of 32 points, and the mean depressive symptoms score was 6.43 ± 5.36 out of 27 points, which included cognitive-affective symptoms (3.59 ± 3.59) and somatic symptoms (2.85 ± 2.21).

Relationship between demographic characteristics and screen-positive abuse prevalence among the participants

Univariate analysis showed that differences in gender, age, residence, living arrangements, self-rated family relationship, exercise frequency, receiving community mental support, self-rated life satisfaction, depression, and loneliness were statistically significant in terms of abuse prevalence (all P < 0.05). By contrast, statistically significant differences were not observed in the prevalence of screen-positive abuse with respect to having a spouse, occupation, education level, number of children, major income source, monthly income, presence of chronic diseases, self-rated health status, or familiarity with laws and regulations related to elder abuse (all P > 0.05), as shown in Table 1.

The risk assessment model for screen-positive abuse among ethnic minority participants

Based on the results of logistic regression analysis, a risk assessment model for screen-positive abuse was established as follows:

logit(p) = − 1.154 − 0.571×living in rural areas − 0.653×community support services + 0.094× loneliness + 0.096×depressive symptoms.

The ROC curve was constructed to evaluate the validity of the risk assessment model for self-reported elder abuse (Fig. 1). The results indicated that AUC of the risk assessment model was 0.744, with a 95% CI of 0.690 to 0.798. This AUC value exceeded the theoretically acceptable threshold of 0.700 [46], demonstrating that the model had satisfactory discriminatory power in identifying the risk of elder abuse vulnerability among participants. Based on the maximum Youden index (0.428), the optimal cut-off value was determined to be 0.675, at which, the sensitivity and specificity were 56.8% and 86.0%, respectively. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed χ² = 8.539, p = 0.383 (> 0.05). Fig. 2 illustrates a nomogram of the risk assessment model for elder abuse vulnerability. Bootstrap internal validation with 1,000 resamples was performed to assess model optimism. The optimism-corrected AUC was 0.717 (95% CI: 0.714–0.720), compared to the apparent AUC of 0.744, indicating a small degree of optimism (Δ = 0.027). The calibration plot (Fig. 3) demonstrated excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, with a calibration intercept of 0.000, a slope of 1.000, and a Brier score of 0.201. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test further supported adequate model fit (χ² = 12.510, p = 0.130).

Discussion

This study addresses a critical gap in the literature by exploring self-reported elder abuse risk among ethnic minority older adults in China and constructing a risk assessment model. This study found that self-reported elder abuse risk in this population was relatively prevalent and was associated with residence, community mental support, loneliness, and depression. These findings shed light on the factors associated with elder abuse vulnerability and provide essential evidence for designing population-specific interventions to promote the well-being of ethnic minority older adults in China and across similar contexts.

The present study found that the prevalence of self-reported elder abuse risk among ethnic minority older adults was 58.92% as measured by the VASS. This figure is notably higher than the pooled global prevalence of approximately 15.7% [9] -27.6% [47]. The significant discrepancy can be primarily attributed to fundamental differences in measurement methodology. The VASS is a reliable screening instrument designed to capture a broad spectrum of elder abuse-related vulnerability (e.g., emotional neglect, violations of autonomy and rights) [29], rather than to confirm objectively documented incidents of abuse. The self-report format provides a more accurate assessment of abuse risk because it mitigates shame and stigma that some older adults experience when disclosing abuse, particularly in resource-limited or rural settings [48]. Previous research also indicates that most older adult victims remain hidden from authorities and endure mistreatment due to stigma, familial pressure, or lack of awareness [49].Thus, prevalence rates derived from screening tools like the VASS may be higher than those from studies that employ stricter diagnostic criteria of abuse or that rely on official reports (e.g., from abuse hotlines) [50]. The prevalence rate in our study is consistent with rates reported in Indian studies that used similar screening methodologies and also found considerable proportions of elderly reporting vulnerability, coercion, and dependence [51]. This study’s findings may also suggest different risk trends for abuse among older adults of ethnic minorities. Race is identified as a social determinant of elder abuse in previous studies [25, 52]. Ethnic minority older adults may face challenges such as lack of social resources, language and cultural barriers, insufficient family emotional support [53], and lack of knowledge regarding abuse laws. Notably, 87.26% of the study participants reported being unfamiliar with laws related to elder abuse. However, the prevalence of self-reported abuse risk may exhibit considerable heterogeneity across these diverse ethnic groups, given that cultural norms, social support networks, and access to community resources may vary substantially within minority populations. Regarding abuse subtypes, this study found that self-reported psychological abuse risk was the most prevalent, followed by physical abuse and neglect. This is similar to the findings of Zhang et al. [54] but different from those of Du and Chen [55], which were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. These differences may be related to variations in measurement tools, criteria for identification, and survey contexts. Regardless of the form of elder abuse, older adults’ negative experiences affect both their physical and mental well-being [16]. These findings call for an urgent need to establish sensitive anti-abuse intervention strategies, such as cultural educational programs [56], to sustain well-being of older adults and promote equitable aging.

The current study finds that rural residence is associated with a lower likelihood of self-reported elder abuse risk among older ethnic minorities in China. This finding contrasts with most prior studies [20, 57–59] but aligns with research from other contexts (e.g., Canada [60], Iran [61], Nepal [62] and America [63]). It may be partly explained by stronger community cohesion and familial support in rural ethnic minority villages [64]. Despite internal migration trends [65], rural elders often reside in close-knit communities where traditional family values prevail and support from children, spouses, and neighbours is more readily available [66, 67]. Increased social cohesion and support could act as a protective factor against abuse [63, 68, 69]. However, cultural beliefs, such as Confucian values of familism and filial piety, likely influence older adults’ abuse reporting behaviours [70]. Specifically, the stigma encapsulated in notions like ‘a betrayal of trust’ and ‘family shame should not be publicized’ [71], fear of retaliatory assault, and abandonment may deter self-disclosure or help-seeking behaviours [72], particularly within rural communities. Previous studies indicate that rural and remote areas not only lack services and resources to address abuse [73] but also have limited access to support from adult protective services, criminal justice, and health agencies [74]. Such resource constraints likely hinder rural older adults from self-reporting abuse vulnerability. Furthermore, a potential lack of awareness of what constitutes abusive behaviours may contribute to underreporting among rural ethnic minority elders. Existing research indicates that older adults with lower levels of education and those residing in economically deprived communities often have limited awareness of elder abuse and lack knowledge about how to respond to it [75]. Thus, the result likely captures a true protective effect or underreporting due to a lack of awareness and cultural barriers, highlighting the need for nuanced interpretation and further geographic-specific investigation.

This study indicates that community mental support may reduce vulnerability to abuse, which is consistent with previous research findings [76]. Community mental health programs can provide emotional and psychological assistance to older adults and their families, effectively addressing their mental health needs [77, 78]. Existing studies have revealed that providing social support services, such as group educational support, can buffer the link between mental disorders and elder abuse [59, 68, 79]. Furthermore, interventions such as promoting the development of community support and mutual aid networks [80], and combining educational and support services [81] have shown positive results in addressing elder abuse. Establishing legal protection hotlines and emergency shelters for older adults in the community is also widely used and has proven to be an effective elder abuse intervention in many countries [82]. These findings suggest that increasing the accessibility and utilisation of community mental health services may help reduce ethnic minority older adults’ vulnerability to abuse.

Loneliness reflects the subjective feeling of unsatisfied social needs, often manifesting as feelings of isolation, lack of fulfilling relationships, and a sense of disconnection [83]. Such negative feelings could lead to physical and psychological problems, such as vulnerability to cardiovascular disease, mood disorders, and cognitive impairment [84]. This study’s results suggest that older ethnic minorities reporting higher degrees of loneliness are more vulnerable to abuse, aligning with the findings of Giraldo-Rodríguez et al. [85] and Mehra et al. [86]. Loneliness is often associated with perceived traits such as frailty, unpopularity, poor social skills, maladjustment, and incompetence [87]. This can exacerbate older adults’ vulnerability and dependency, thereby increasing their susceptibility to abuse [12]. Mohammadpour et al. [88] revealed a notable association between loneliness and most types of abuse. The severity of loneliness correlated with the severity of caregiving abuse behaviours [86]. Family member abuse can be viewed as a stigma; those subjected to abuse might consequently reduce their social engagement [22] and exhibit lower life satisfaction and autonomy, which, in turn, heightens their vulnerability to loneliness [89]. Thus, mitigating loneliness (such as community empowerment, social cognitive training) may be a crucial strategy for preventing and responding to elder abuse in this population [90].

As a mood disorder, depression manifests ongoing sadness and a diminished interest in activities, significantly impairing mental health and overall functioning [91]. This study found that depressive symptoms correlate with self-reported elder abuse risk, which is supported by existing research evidence [11, 68, 85, 92]. Notably, depression is not only a risk factor for abuse vulnerability but also a potential consequence of creating a complex, bidirectional relationship [93]. Depressive symptoms, including low mood, demotivation, and a sense of worthlessness, might heighten older adults’ psychological vulnerability, thereby increasing their susceptibility to abuse [94]. Moreover, individuals with depression may be sensitive to their environment and the behaviours of others [95], and may tend to perceive minor negative actions as abuse, potentially increasing the probability of self-reported abuse [43]. A longitudinal study of Burnes et al. [76] further supports a causal relationship between depression and elder abuse. Conversely, elder abuse, particularly emotional abuse, can trigger older adults’ negative feelings (e.g., hopelessness and anxiety), thereby exacerbating or inducing depression [92, 96, 97]. Older adults who experience abuse frequently exhibit lower life satisfaction and autonomy [98], which further intensify depressive symptoms. Additionally, the frequency and severity of abuse have been positively correlated with the prevalence of depressive symptoms [99]. Therefore, interventions addressing depressive symptoms and preventing abuse vulnerability—including early depression screening, psychological support, and interdisciplinary protection teams for vulnerable older adults [100]— can be implemented concurrently to reduce abuse risk among ethnic minority older adults.

The risk assessment model developed in this study serves as a valuable tool for identifying elder abuse risk among ethnic minority older adults and provides practical relevance for community health services. It demonstrates acceptable fit and satisfactory discriminative ability (AUC = 0.744) [46]. Unlike existing models designed for institutional settings [101] or dementia caregivers [102], this model is designed for ethnic minority elders in Hunan, China, integrating psychological factors (loneliness, depressive symptoms) and community -environmental factors (rural residence, community mental support). Notably, risk factors for abuse vulnerability may vary across different ethnic groups due to distinct cultural norms and social structures; therefore, the model’s predictors might not be equally applicable to all minority populations, and its generalizability requires further validation. While the small degree of optimism suggests that the model is relatively stable, its modest sensitivity (56.8%) limits its practical use. In practice, the model is best positioned as a triage instrument—to identify individuals warranting further assessment—rather than as a definitive diagnostic tool. Future iterations to enhance sensitivity would focus on adjusting the risk threshold or refining predictor measurements.

Limitations

This study still has some limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (N = 314) and limited to ethnic minority older adults in Hunan Province, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Future studies should recruit larger samples employing stratified random sampling across multiple provinces to enhance the national representativeness and improve the stability and applicability of the model. Second, the inclusion of only four variables in the risk assessment model may not fully capture the complexity of elder abuse risk. Future research should incorporate additional dimensions, such as physiological health, cognitive function, and cultural context, to enhance predictive accuracy. Third, this study utilised self-report questionnaires to assess elder abuse among ethnic minority older adults. Future studies could utilize other assessment methods such as interviews and observational studies. Fourth, the risk assessment tool developed in this study has limited sensitivity and lacks external validation, potentially reducing its detection rate for at-risk individuals. Future research should validate and refine the model across diverse ethnic and geographic populations using multi-center data. Finally, the cross-sectional study design cannot infer causal relationships between the independent variables and self-reported elder abuse. Future research employing longitudinal design may offer valuable insights into the risk and protective factors impacting on elder abuse, as well as track the temporal changes.

Conclusions

This study found a relatively high prevalence of self-reported elder abuse risk among ethnic minority older adults in China. Establishing culturally sensitive intervention programs through multi-sector collaboration is critical for safeguarding the well-being of this vulnerable population. Additionally, this study identified four significant factors of vulnerability to abuse among ethnic minorities: living in rural areas, receiving community support services, loneliness, and depressive symptoms. These findings indicate that future public health strategies and policies should prioritise interventions for vulnerable individuals residing in non-rural areas who experience loneliness and depression, and provide accessible and targeted community mental health services. The preliminary risk assessment model developed in this study demonstrated satisfactory predictive validity and holds promise for broader applications in ethnic minority older-adult populations. This model can serve as a valuable tool for community health service providers, allowing them to effectively identify abuse vulnerability groups and implement proactive interventions. The findings of this study can inform the development of culturally appropriate and sensitive anti-abuse policies.
