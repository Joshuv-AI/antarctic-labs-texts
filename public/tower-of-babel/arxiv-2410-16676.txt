CausalEval: Towards Better Causal Reasoning in Language Models
Longxuan Yu, Delin Chen, Siheng Xiong, Qingyang Wu, Qingzhen Liu, Dawei Li, Zhikai Chen, Xiaoze Liu, Liangming Pan

Abstract
Causal reasoning (CR) is a crucial aspect of intelligence, essential for problem-solving, decision-making, and understanding the world. While language models (LMs) can generate rationales for their outputs, their ability to reliably perform causal reasoning remains uncertain, often falling short in tasks requiring a deep understanding of causality. In this paper, we introduce CausalEval, a comprehensive review of research aimed at enhancing LMs for causal reasoning, coupled with an empirical evaluation of current models and methods. We categorize existing methods based on the role of LMs: either as reasoning engines or as helpers providing knowledge or data to traditional CR methods, followed by a detailed discussion of methodologies in each category. We then assess the performance of current LMs and various enhancement methods on a range of causal reasoning tasks, providing key findings and in-depth analysis. Finally, we present insights from current studies and highlight promising directions for future research. We aim for this work to serve as a comprehensive resource, fostering further advancements in causal reasoning with LMs. 1 1 1 Resources are available at https://github.com/chendl02/Awesome-LLM-causal-reasoning .

3
Towards Causal Reasoning in Large Language Models

We separate the roles of language models in CR into two categories. First, LLMs can serve as
causal reasoning engines
, employing methods such as fine-tuning, prompt engineering, external tool integration, and alternative approaches to directly draw causal inferences from input prompts.
Second, LLMs can act as
helpers
to traditional methods by extracting causal information and generating causality data to enhance causal analysis across various domains.

3.1
Serving as Causal Reasoning Engines

3.1.1
Injecting Causal Knowledge through Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning language models has been proven to be a simple and effective technique for transferring learned knowledge from a pre-trained model to new downstream tasks. Compared to training from scratch, it can significantly reduce training time, and improve performance on new tasks
Lester et al. 2021
. For example,
Li et al. 2021
used self-supervised learning strategies, such as causal pairs ranking and fine-tuning, to inject causal knowledge into pre-trained models. Additionally,
Zheng et al. 2023
proposed Causal Effect Tuning, which is based on causal inference, to learn new knowledge from target data while preserving old knowledge from pre-trained models, thereby building a causal path without storing the pre-training data.

Although scaling language models increases the effectiveness of their prior knowledge in addressing complex causal questions, most training corpora collected from online sources consist of unstructured textual data rather than structured causal annotations.
To address this,
Jiang et al. 2023
collected a custom dataset containing causal questions and interpreted intents in a structured format for supervised fine-tuning.
Building on this,
Chen et al. 2024
combined the structural causal model with instruction tuning
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Chung et al. 2024
to develop a meta Structural Causal Model, which unified different NLP tasks under a single causal structure and introduced the Structural Instruction Tuning method to learn task-specific causal representations that mimic the causal factors for each task.

Limitations

Despite these progresses, LLMs often lack clearly defined supervision for causality, as most ML datasets contain only observational data without interventions, making key causal quantities, such as treatment effects, unknown
Kıcıman et al. 2023b
. Furthermore, causality datasets often feature complex, heterogeneous relationships between variables
Huang et al. 2020
, which are more challenging to capture compared to linguistic or perceptual patterns.
Thus, developing methods to bridge this gap, such as integrating causal structure learning or leveraging synthetic data for interventions, remains an important area of research.

3.1.2
Embedding Causal Knowledge within Contexts

Prompt-based methods have become crucial in unlocking the internal reasoning capabilities of LLMs across various tasks. Compared with general reasoning, CR tasks, particularly multi-step ones, pose greater challenges due to factors such as the complexity of understanding cause-and-effect relationships, the need for reasoning about interventions and counterfactuals, and the presence of confounding variables
Gandhi et al. 2023
;
Tan et al. 2023
;
Ban et al. 2023
;
Abdali et al. 2023
.

To address these challenges, various innovative prompting strategies have been proposed
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Bagheri et al. 2024
. Building on Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting
Wei et al. 2023
, CausalCoT
Jin et al. 2023a
enables LLMs to reason step-by-step through causal relationships, significantly improving causal inference tasks involving multiple causes and effects. Recent advancements, such as Causal Contextualized Learning (CausalCL)
Wang et al. 2023b
, and
Zhang et al. 2023
further enhances CR by combining code-like prompting with chain-of-thought prompting, boosting the performance for multihop event reasoning.
Multi-modal Causal Reasoning (MCR)
Zang et al. 2023
extends CR to multi-modal settings, integrating visual and textual information for a comprehensive analysis. Counterfactual Prompting Learning (CPL)
He et al. 2022
utilizes counterfactual thinking to guide models in exploring alternative scenarios, thereby improving their understanding of causal mechanisms.

These advanced prompting techniques collectively enhance the ability of LLMs to understand causal relationships, thereby improving precision in CR tasks and enabling models to manage long-term dependencies more effectively
Lu et al. 2022
. Furthermore, they reduce biases from training data, enrich the model’s knowledge with external information and commonsense, and do so without substantial increases in computational costs
Yao et al. 2022
;
Jin et al. 2023a
.

Limitations

Causal prompt engineering depends on the model’s inherent causality knowledge, which is often insufficient due to the correlation-driven nature of its modeling approach
Zhang et al. 2024
. Crafting effective prompts is labor-intensive, requiring significant domain expertise. Furthermore, the sensitivity to prompt variations can lead to inconsistent outputs, reducing robustness
Perez et al. 2021
. Additionally, multi-step causal prompts can increase computational inefficiency, demanding more resources and time. These limitations highlight the necessity of complementary approaches, such as explicit causal modeling or specialized fine-tuning.

3.1.3
External Causal Tool Integration

The motivation for integrating external tools into LLMs is that effective causal reasoning requires more than just internal model representations. By accessing structured knowledge bases and performing specialized computations, external tools enhance LLMs’ capacity for complex causal reasoning, complementing their inherent capabilities. The ability to use these tools can be acquired through methods such as fine-tuning, in-context learning, and prompt manipulation. This integration establishes a crucial link between causal reasoning and language understanding, enabling LLMs to handle complex causal tasks more effectively.

For methods that incorporate tools in prompts, PLAN
Lu et al. 2023
introduced a causal framework for procedural planning that leverages external knowledge bases, such as ConceptNet
Speer et al. 2017
. It semantically parses tasks into an entity set to retrieve relevant sub-graphs for improved planning.
Additionally,
Pawlowski et al. 2023
compared context augmentation and tool augmentation methods: context augmentation uses language models for supplementary operations beyond the causal expert system, whereas tool augmentation incorporates basic Python tools to manipulate outputs from the expert system.
Their experiments showed that context-augmented LLMs were more prone to errors compared to tool-augmented ones. For methods involving supervised fine-tuning, dialogue agents such as those by
Shuster et al. 2022
;
Komeili 2021
are trained to effectively use search engines.

Limitations

While integrating external causal tools can enhance LLMs, it also introduces several challenges. The effectiveness of these tools depends heavily on the quality and completeness of external resources, which can lead to errors when inadequate. The alignment between the model and tools is often complex and domain-specific, limiting the model’s generalizability. Additionally, reliance on external tools may slow response times and increase the risk of errors, as observed with context-augmented models
Pawlowski et al. 2023
. These challenges indicate that external tools should be used in combination with other approaches to fully address complex CR tasks.

3.1.4
Alternative Approaches

Recent studies have explored various alternative approaches to enhance the causal reasoning capabilities of LLMs, including iterative improvement protocols, multi-agent systems, and rationale-based evaluation. For example, ALLURE
Hasanbeig et al. 2023
uses a systematic auditing and
iterative improvement protocol
with in-context learning examples to refine LLM evaluation. ECHO
Xie et al. 2023
introduces a visio-linguistic dataset based on real-world scenarios to evaluate event causality through human-centric reasoning.

Agentic frameworks
have also been employed for causal reasoning in LLMs.
CausalGPT
Tang et al. 2023
employs a multi-agent system comprising reasoners and evaluators, with reasoners generating answers and evaluators assessing factual accuracy and causal consistency.

Du et al. 2023
presents a debate framework where multiple instances of LLMs debate a causality-related query to reach a consensus.

Selection and inference-based techniques
are another approach.
Creswell et al. 2022
alternate between selection and inference steps using pre-trained LLMs to generate interpretable causal reasoning steps, while
Creswell and Shanahan 2022
chain reasoning steps to produce valid reasoning traces.

Rationale-based evaluation methods
are also receiving increased attention.
Atanasova et al. 2023
use rationale-based methods to conduct tests for evaluating the faithfulness of natural language explanations.
Abdali et al. 2023
generate a prior model to address domain knowledge gaps, serving as a heuristic for assessing the informativeness of user feedback. Additionally,
Yue et al. 2023
utilize a cascaded model incorporating CoT and Program-of-Thought (PoT) approaches, along with answer sampling and consistency checking, to enhance causal reasoning.

LLMs significantly underperform humans in many CR Tasks.

Our results reveal a notable performance gap between LLMs and humans across many causal reasoning tasks, as illustrated in the Radar chart (Figure
8
, Appendix
D
). Despite recent advancements, LLMs continue to face challenges in fully grasping and applying causal reasoning, often relying on surface-level patterns and struggling with multi-step reasoning. These limitations reduce their effectiveness in complex scenarios. Future work should focus on enhancing LLMs’ ability to handle intricate causal structures, potentially through hybrid methods that integrate symbolic reasoning with statistical approaches. Addressing these challenges will be critical for bridging the gap between LLMs and human performance in causal reasoning.

Performance disparity exists between discovery and inference tasks due to contextual and multi-variable reasoning demands.
Our results reveal distinct performance trends across causal tasks. All models achieve high accuracy in certain discovery tasks, such as COPA and e-CARE, which require identifying potential causal relationships from observational data. However, their performance drops significantly in inference tasks, particularly when assessing causal strengths from long contexts (CRAB). Discovery tasks involving complex multi-variable interactions, such as Corr2Cause, also present significant challenges. This disparity underscores a fundamental limitation: while models are proficient at identifying potential causal relation candidates, they struggle with evaluating causal strengths and navigating intricate variable dependencies.

Deep thinking models exhibit targeted gains in structured causal reasoning.

Our analysis reveals that deep thinking models (DeepSeek-R1, o1, o3-mini) demonstrate particularly strong performance in structured causal reasoning tasks, especially in CLADDER where they achieve remarkable accuracy (92.0% and 92.2% respectively). This suggests that extended thinking contexts may be particularly beneficial for formal causal inference and graph-based reasoning. However, this advantage does not consistently extend to other causal tasks - performance on benchmarks such as COPA, NPDS, and e-CARE remains comparable to or worse than that of standard models. This trend indicates that deep thinking approaches primarily enhance structured causal reasoning rather than general causal understanding.

4.3
Analysis and Discussion

We further analyze the impact of various factors on accuracy below.

Zero-shot or few-shot under CoT prompting.

Figure
5
shows that both zero-shot and few-shot models benefit from CoT prompting, with few-shot CoT achieving the highest overall performance. While few-shot consistently outperforms zero-shot across all tasks, the models struggle the most with inference tasks, where accuracy remains lower than in discovery and additional tasks. These findings highlight the effectiveness of combining example-based learning with structured reasoning but also underscore the need for further improvements, especially in inference tasks, to enhance model performance across complex causal scenarios.

Model size vs. Causal reasoning performance.

Figure
6
demonstrates that causal reasoning performance improves consistently with increasing model size across all evaluated models. Both LLaMA3 and LLaMA3.1 achieve significant gains, with Gemma also exhibiting strong improvements. In contrast, Mistral shows only modest improvements as model size increases, likely due to its Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture. These results underscore the importance of both model size and architecture in advancing causal reasoning capabilities.

Error analysis.

Through systematic analysis of model outputs, we identify four common types of errors in causal reasoning.

Logical errors
occur when the model makes flawed cause-effect conclusions, such as confusing correlation with causation or failing to distinguish between necessary and sufficient conditions. For example, when asked whether someone caused a car to start in a scenario with multiple people each bringing a working battery, the model incorrectly attributes causation based solely on temporal sequence rather than considering the true causal relationship.

Probabilistic errors
arise when the model incorrectly handles numerical reasoning about likelihood and chance, including mishandling of conditional probabilities and confounding variables. In probability comparison tasks, the model often makes computational errors or misinterprets statistical dependencies, leading to incorrect conclusions about relative likelihoods.

Statistical errors
manifest when the model fails to properly account for complex relationships between multiple factors, such as indirect effects and selection bias. For instance, in scenarios involving multiple causal paths, the model often overlooks intermediate factors or assumes direct relationships where more complex dependencies exist.

Contextual errors
occur when the model fails to incorporate relevant background information or subtle details that should affect its causal reasoning. This often results in oversimplified conclusions that ignore important contextual factors or conditions that would alter the causal relationship.

Our analysis (Figure
7
) shows that logical errors are the most prevalent type of failure. Potential approaches to address these challenges include enhanced representation of causal relationships in the model architecture, systematic consistency checks for reasoning processes, and diversified training data covering a broader range of causal scenarios. Detailed examples of each error type and analysis of these approaches are provided in Appendix
D
.

Comparison with enhancement methods.

We evaluated two methods for improving causal reasoning: CausalCoT
Jin et al. 2023a
and Function Call. CausalCoT provides structured steps for causal inference with domain-specific guidance, while Function Call leverages Python scripts for precise mathematical computations. Our results show that effectiveness varies by task type. For formal reasoning tasks involving causal graph analysis, CausalCoT outperforms other approaches due to its structured inference steps (75.0% on CLADDER). In contrast, Function Call excels in numerical tasks, particularly on CLADDER (GPT-4o: 76.0% vs 69.0% with vanilla CoT), and performs comparably well on other computation-heavy datasets. However, for commonsense causal reasoning tasks like COPA and e-CARE, both methods underperform: CausalCoT shows substantial drops (40.8% and 35% respectively for GPT-3.5-turbo), while Function Call provides minimal gains (COPA: 78.0% vs 95.2% with vanilla CoT), suggesting that vanilla CoT remains the most effective for these tasks. These findings highlight the importance of task-aware method selection: Function Call for quantitative analysis, CausalCoT for structured causal inference with graphs, and vanilla CoT for commonsense causal reasoning.

5
Findings and Reflections

LLMs demonstrate shallow causal reasoning skills.

Evaluation metrics often emphasize task-specific accuracy, leaving open questions about the depth of the model’s causal reasoning
Zečević et al. 2023
.
For instance,
Hong et al. 2023
investigated how LLMs process event B in a script-based story, which causally depends on a prior event A. While large models performed better than smaller ones in the
\neg\mathrm{A}\rightarrow\mathrm{B}
condition, all models (even fine-tuned with code and instructions) failed to replicate human behavior when the cause was omitted (
nil
), indicating that LLMs lack sufficient representation of commonsense CR.

Integration/Collaboration enhances the performance.

Tasks that challenge a single LLM can often be addressed by integration with traditional methods, external tools, or multiple LLMs
Tang et al. 2023
;
Du et al. 2023
.
Recent approaches, such as neuro-symbolic methods
Yang et al. 2023
and iterative processes
Ban et al. 2023
, integrate LLMs with statistical analysis and causal structure learning, improving the quality of causal inferences.
The integration with causal analysis tools increases LLMs’ access to causal knowledge across domains
Kıcıman et al. 2023b
. As intermediaries between specialized tools and natural language, LLMs make causal reasoning more intuitive and user-friendly.
This collaboration fosters tools for comprehensively exploring causal relationships in complex systems.

Diverse and standardized causal reasoning benchmarks are needed.

Comprehensive evaluation of the causal reasoning abilities of LLMs requires more diverse and standardized benchmarks. Existing benchmarks tend to be relatively simple and domain-specific. Future benchmarks should encompass a broader spectrum of causal reasoning challenges, including multi-step tasks with increasing complexity, while maintaining consistent causal structures. Such datasets would enable LLMs to explore a wider range of causal reasoning scenarios and better adapt to real-world applications
Yang et al. 2024
.

6
Future Directions

Deep understanding of existing theories.

Integrating causal reasoning capabilities into LLMs presents unique challenges, especially when requiring adherence to established theories. These theories, such as the potential outcomes framework, graph-based causal inference methods, and structural equation modeling, often rely on foundational assumptions, including the stable unit treatment value assumption, ignorability/unconfoundedness, and positivity
Liu et al. 2024b
. Considering these assumptions necessitates innovative strategies, such as incorporating specific post-training data and implementing filtering mechanisms to ensure the model’s alignment with the theories.

Data efficiency for end-to-end learning.

LLMs have the potential to perform end-to-end causal reasoning, but their effectiveness depends on substantial data to learn and infer causal relationships. A major challenge is the scarcity of high-quality causality data, particularly for counterfactual reasoning, where alternative scenarios are often unavailable or difficult to generate. Improving data efficiency is therefore a critical research direction. Techniques such as analogical reasoning, imaginative simulation, and inferential methods can maximize the utility of existing data by generating synthetic datasets, filling data gaps, or extrapolating from limited information. These approaches enhance the model’s capacity to learn causal relationships with constrained training data.

Causality-based internal mechanisms for LLMs.

Incorporating causal reasoning into LLMs internally is supposed to enhance their accuracy and depth in complex problem-solving scenarios
(
Kıcıman et al. 2023b
)
. Thus, it is crucial to find feasible implementation ways so that LLMs generate content grounded in causality. One strategy could be the introduction of a dual-network architecture, where one is similar to current model and the other focuses on learning causal relations. Both networks are jointly trained to perform various tasks. Another approach involves modifying attention mechanisms to prioritize causal relevance.
By embedding internal causal mechanisms, we believe LLMs can enhance their reasoning, address fairness and safety, and better handle multi-modal inputs through improved understanding of causal relations, leading to more accurate and transparent decisions and reduced biases.

7
Conclusion

LLMs equipped with these advanced technologies demonstrate strong potential in causal reasoning, but further research is needed to fully realize and refine their capabilities. Addressing challenges such as managing intricate causal structures is essential for continued development. Enhancing interpretability and transparency through causal reasoning is crucial for building trust and helping users rely on model inferences. Exploring innovative methods will be key to overcoming current limitations and unlocking the full potential of LLMs in complex, real-world scenarios. Advancing these areas will make LLMs more reliable and effective tools for causal analysis across domains.

Limitations

In this survey, we review advancements in causal reasoning with LLMs, though several limitations should be noted. Our focus is primarily on text-based causal reasoning, with limited exploration of multi-modal or visual tasks, which restricts the generalizability of our findings across modalities. Although we evaluate mainstream LLMs and methods on popular benchmarks, we do not cover all enhancement techniques, particularly those requiring post-training or tailored to specific tasks and domains. Additionally, understanding the fundamental principles of causal reasoning in LLMs is crucial for ensuring trust and accountability in real-world applications, yet this area remains challenging and underexplored. Further research is warranted to address these gaps.

involves adding data or interventions to a causal graph from causal discovery to test variable cause-effect relationships. It aims to determine the direct impact of a variable or intervention on another. The methods used for causal discovery may include constraint-based algorithms

Although there has been much research based on traditional methods, they have several limitations in data requirements, model scalability, and model robustness that LLMs can address more effectively.

(1) Data Quality:

Traditional Methods
require well-developed, high-quality datasets, making them vulnerable to missing data and measurement errors. They rely on assumptions like linearity, stationarity, and causal sufficiency, which may not hold in real-world scenarios.

LLMs
capture non-linear interactions and are suitable for various types of data. They can augment sparse or incomplete data through in-context learning, generating plausible hypotheses, and filling gaps.

(2) Scalability:

Traditional Methods
can be computationally intensive and less scalable with increasing variables, often requiring costly intervention data.

LLMs
efficiently process large datasets and automate causal discovery, making them more scalable and adaptable to complex datasets.

(3) Robustness:

Traditional Methods
simulate counterfactuals and predict intervention effects without explicit data, but their reliance on strict assumptions can limit robustness in practical applications.

LLMs
apply methods such as fine-tuning to dynamically integrate vast background knowledge, providing a richer context for causal reasoning and making them more robust to data quality issues.

Appendix B
Enhancing Traditional CR Methods

B.1
Causal Information Extraction

LLMs can directly support causal reasoning by serving as knowledge bases or extracting causal information. The effectiveness of problem-solving algorithms can be improved by adopting a modular approach that separates the logic and control components
Kowalski 1979
. The logic component represents the essential knowledge for solving the problem, while the control component manages the problem-solving strategy. Decoupling these components enhances flexibility, allowing updates to the control strategy without altering the underlying knowledge base.

Yang et al. 2023
proposed a neuro-symbolic approach with two components: an LLM-based symbolic representation generator that translates natural language into formal knowledge for symbolic inference, and an LLM-free symbolic inference engine. Similarly,
Abdali et al. 2023
leveraged LLMs’ reasoning capabilities to extract causal variables and event sequences, providing a heuristic for assessing feedback informativeness in the absence of domain knowledge. Additionally, RealTCD
Li et al. 2024
introduced LLM-guided meta-initialization to extract meta-knowledge from textual information, improving the discovery process in real industrial settings.

Another notable contribution is the iterative LLM-supervised causal structure learning (ILS-CSL) framework introduced by
Ban et al. 2023
, which iteratively refines causal Directed Acyclic Graphs through feedback from LLMs. This framework highlights the efficiency and robustness of LLMs in improving causal discovery.
More broadly, the ability of LLMs to extract causal information can reduce the dependency on purely data-driven methods and unlock new opportunities for hybrid CR approaches. As LLMs evolve, they are likely to play an increasingly important role in causal discovery and inference by connecting unstructured text with structured causal models, making CR more robust and adaptable across domains.

B.2
Causality Data Generation & Augmentation

LLMs store a vast amount of knowledge within their weights, and several works have explored how to extract causal-related knowledge to support traditional causal reasoning models. For instance,
Li et al. 2020
introduced a method where, given an input cause sentence, a causal subgraph is retrieved from an event causality network, and the most probable effect event is selected to guide the generation of an effect sentence.
As LLMs scale, their capacity to generate causal alternatives has improved. For example,
Staliūnaitė et al. 2021
used GPT-2 to generate alternatives and re-write filtered augmentation data for causal relations. Similarly,
Gat et al. 2023
showcased how LLMs can generate counterfactual approximations efficiently, focusing on model-agnostic explanations that enhance the interpretability and faithfulness of CR methods.

However, despite the impressive knowledge stored within LLMs, they remain prone to hallucinations, often generating incorrect or misleading facts
Wang et al. 2023a
. This issue is particularly problematic in causal reasoning, where precision and factual correctness are essential for drawing valid inferences and supporting decision-making processes. Hallucinated outputs can lead to incorrect causal links, undermining the reliability of LLM-generated causal insights. Moreover, the lack of transparency in how LLMs generate such outputs further complicates efforts to identify and correct errors, posing challenges to researchers and practitioners alike.

Addressing these challenges requires strategies to ensure more controllable and precise causal data generation. High-quality causal data is essential not only for improving LLM accuracy but also for fostering trust in their outputs, particularly in real-world applications like healthcare, economics, and public policy. Future research should focus on robust methods to mitigate hallucinations and enhance the controllability of LLM-generated causal information, bridging the gap between language model capabilities and practical causality needs.

Appendix D
Implementation Details

We begin by discussing the evaluation metrics used across different benchmarks. TRAM uses accuracy to assess causal reasoning tasks. NPDS evaluates performance with both F1 score and causal accuracy, while Corr2Cause employs F1 as the primary metric, alongside precision, recall, and accuracy. CRAB focuses on Pairwise and Graded Causality tasks, using Macro F1 for its 4-class Pairwise Causality Multiple Choice Question (MCQ) task. MOCA compares LLM responses with human participants using agreement (Agg), Area Under the Curve (AUC), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Cross-Entropy (CE), though we directly assess LLMs on causal tasks without relying on agreement metrics. CRASS uses top-1 accuracy, while e-CARE assesses MCQs with accuracy, average-BLEU (n=4), ROUGE-L, Perplexity, and Causal Explanation Quality (CEQ) for explanations. Finally, COPA uses accuracy as the primary metric.

Across these benchmarks, metrics such as F1, precision, recall, AUC, and BLEU assess different aspects of performance, including classification quality, alignment with human responses, and explanation quality. We select pass@1 accuracy as our primary metric because it is straightforward and widely used for evaluating multiple-choice and prediction-based causal tasks.

For evaluation, we randomly select 500 samples from each dataset’s test set using a random seed of 42, or the maximum available size if fewer samples exist. Due to cost and rate constraints, models like GPT-4o and Claude-3.5 are tested on 100 samples.
We utilize a unified prompt pattern (Figure
9
) comprising three parts: (1)
Identity Recognition
, such as "You are a highly intelligent question-answering bot with profound knowledge of causal inference"; (2)
Question-Context
, which describes the specific question to be addressed; and (3)
Answer Format Control
, which guides the model’s response. The latter instructs the model to either provide a direct answer with "Give me the answer directly" for Direct I/O or to begin with reasoning or evidence, concluding with the final result marked by "####" for CoT reasoning, ensuring the output is easy to parse.
In the few-shot learning setup, we use two fixed examples for most datasets and one for the CRAB dataset, which has a longer context. Direct I/O prompts request direct answers, while CoT prompts involve generating CoT examples with GPT-4o (Figure
10
), followed by manual validation for accuracy.

For our error analysis, we first categorize errors into four primary types.
Statistical errors
(SE) arise from issues in data collection, measurement, or analysis, distorting relationships between variables. Sub-classes include:
confounding
, where an extraneous variable influences both independent and dependent variables, creating misleading associations;
selection bias
, where the sample is unrepresentative of the target population, leading to biased estimates; and
measurement error
, where inaccurate data collection results in flawed assessments.
Logical errors
(LE) involve flaws in reasoning, such as
directionality errors
(misidentifying causal direction),
inconsistency
(contradictory reasoning), and
over-generalization
(drawing broad conclusions from specific cases without proper support).
Probabilistic errors
(PE) stem from incorrect handling of probabilities and statistical inferences, including
Bayesian inference errors
(misapplication of priors or updates) and
probability comparison errors
(misinterpreting conditional probabilities or neglecting base rates). Finally,
contextual errors
(CE) occur when subtle details, such as shifts in tone or minor actions, are overlooked, leading to flawed causal inferences.

Detailed Error Analysis

This appendix provides detailed examples and analysis of the error types discussed in Section
4.3
. For each type, we present representative cases that illustrate common failure modes.

- Logical Errors - Case Study

Context:
Billy and Suzy inherited an unusual type of hybrid car that has two special car batteries called Bartlett batteries. The car won’t start unless it has at least one Bartlett battery. Since all the car needed was at least one Bartlett battery, they were able to start the car.

Question:
Did Billy cause the car to start?

Model’s Answer:
A delightful question! Let’s break down the stages: Billy brought a battery, Suzy also brought a battery, and either battery alone would start the car. Therefore, Billy’s action was sufficient to cause the car to start. Answer: Yes

Error Analysis:
The model exhibits several reasoning flaws. First, it conflates sufficiency with causation - while Billy’s action was sufficient, it wasn’t necessary. Second, it overlooks redundant causation by failing to recognize that Suzy’s battery would have started the car regardless. Third, it relies on temporal sequence, implicitly assuming first action implies causation.

- Statistical Errors - Case Study

Context:
A study tracked 1000 patients’ recovery rates. In the data, 70% of patients who exercised regularly recovered. Among those who didn’t exercise, 60% still recovered. The exercise group had more younger patients (avg age 45) than non-exercise group (avg age 65).

Question:
Does exercise improve recovery rates?

Model’s Answer:
Let’s analyze the data: The exercise group shows a 70% recovery rate while the non-exercise group shows a 60% recovery rate. There’s a 10% improvement in the exercise group. Answer: Yes, exercise clearly improves recovery rates.

Error Analysis:
The model makes several statistical reasoning errors. It fails to account for selection bias in group composition and ignores age as a potential confounding variable. The model draws causal conclusions from raw correlations without controlling for population differences, missing the need for age-stratified analysis or matched comparisons.

- Probabilistic Errors - Case Study

Context:
Imagine a self-contained, hypothetical world with only the following probabilities. Is the chance of kwox smaller when observing yupt?

Model’s Answer:
Let’s calculate step by step: P(kwox|yupt) = 0.47 * 0.57 + 0.65 * 0.43 = 0.5141. Since P(kwox)
\approx
0.5141 < 0.5, Answer: No.

Error Analysis:
The model demonstrates several probabilistic reasoning failures. It incorrectly applies the conditional probability formula and shows confusion between joint and conditional probabilities. Most critically, it makes a comparison with the wrong baseline probability, leading to an invalid conclusion about relative likelihoods.

- Contextual Errors - Case Study

Context:
In a small company with high employee turnover, all employees who left in the last quarter had completed their major projects. The company conducted exit interviews where employees cited various reasons for leaving including better opportunities, relocation, and personal reasons.

Question:
Does project completion cause employee turnover?

Model’s Answer:
Looking at the data: All departing employees had completed their projects, and there is a perfect correlation between project completion and leaving. Answer: Yes, project completion appears to cause employee turnover.

Error Analysis:
The model shows several contextual understanding failures. It ignores the broader context of employee career paths and job market dynamics, while failing to consider natural project completion as part of the normal work cycle. The model overlooks explicitly stated alternative reasons for leaving in the exit interviews. Furthermore, it misses crucial temporal aspects, namely that employees typically complete projects before leaving, regardless of their reason for departure.
