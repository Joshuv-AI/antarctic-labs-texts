Predictive factors for late aortic events after central repair of acute DeBakey I dissection
Authors: Sho Takagi, Yoshihiro Goto, Masahiro Inagaki, Atsuki Imagawa, Junji Yanagisawa, Yui Ogihara, Yuichiro Fukumoto, Hideki Kitamura, Yasuhide Okawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn acute type A aortic dissection, reports recommend total arch replacement with or without frozen elephant trunk over hemiarch replacement to avoid reoperation attributable to dilation of the residual dissected aorta. However, the residual dissected aorta can still enlarge. This study investigated the long-term factors contributing to enlargement in DeBakey type I aortic dissections.MethodsWe analyzed 126 cases of central repair for false lumen–patent DeBakey type I aortic dissection. We compared preoperative computed tomography findings and characteristics, operative findings, and long-term outcomes between 51 cases experiencing aortic events (dilation of distal aorta >5 cm, new aortic dissections, distal aortic surgery, or distal aortic rupture) and 75 without events.ResultsPatient characteristics and operative findings did not significantly differ between groups, with significantly more dissections involving the brachiocephalic artery or the iliac artery and cases undergoing hemiarch replacement in the event group. Univariable analyses showed that dissections involving the brachiocephalic artery, bilateral or unilateral iliac artery involvement, and hemiarch replacement were significantly associated with a greater risk of aortic events than in patients without these features. Multivariable analysis showed that dissections involving the brachiocephalic artery, unilateral iliac artery involvement, and hemiarch replacement were independent predictors of aortic events.ConclusionsDissections involving the brachiocephalic artery or unilateral iliac artery on preoperative computed tomography were prognostic predictors of long-term aortic events. When dissection involves these vessels, large re-entry sites may form and contribute to late aortic events. Extensive aortic surgery and pre-emptive endovascular treatment may help suppress aortic events.

Factors predicting late aortic events after central repair of acute DeBakey I dissection.

Central Message

In acute type A aortic dissection, dissection involving the brachiocephalic or unilateral iliac artery and hemiarch replacement are significant predictors of distal aortic events.

Perspective

Patent false lumen is a risk factor for late aortic events after central repair for DeBakey type I dissection, but many determinants are unclear. Specific preoperative CTA features, including brachiocephalic or unilateral iliac artery involvement, may identify high-risk patients and support tailored strategies, such as extensive aortic surgery and pre-emptive endovascular treatment to improve outcomes.

Acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) remains a life-threatening condition requiring emergent surgical intervention.1,2 Although advances in surgical techniques, such as frozen elephant trunk (FET), have contributed to improved early outcomes, long-term prognosis continues to be compromised by late aortic events, including progressive enlargement of the residual dissected aorta, distal redissection, and rupture.3,4 These adverse outcomes may necessitate reintervention and significantly affect patient survival and quality of life.

Total arch replacement (TAR), especially when combined with FET, may promote aortic remodeling and reduce distal aortic events compared with hemiarch replacement (HAR).4 However, despite the theoretical advantages of extensive repair, some patients experience progressive dilation of the residual false lumen. Although the postoperative maximal aortic diameter is a significant predictor of late aortic dilation in patients with residual dissected aorta after central repair,5 the mechanisms underlying such adverse remodeling remain incompletely understood. If the risk of late aortic events can be anticipated preoperatively, it may help guide intraoperative and early postoperative decision-making.

To establish more precise risk stratification and guide the selection of surgical strategies or postoperative surveillance, identifying preoperative predictors associated with late aortic events is essential. This study retrospectively analyzed preoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) scans and clinical data in patients with DeBakey type I dissection who underwent central aortic repair to identify key anatomical and procedural factors contributing to long-term aortic complications.

Methods

Data Source

A total of 225 consecutive patients underwent central repair for ATAAD at the Toyohashi Heart Center between 2007 and 2021. We excluded individuals presenting with intramural hematoma (n = 42) and DeBakey type II dissection (n = 24), those who died during the initial hospitalization (n = 22), and those whose postoperative follow-up was conducted at outside institutions (n = 11), leaving 126 patients with residual dissection eligible for retrospective analysis (Figure 1). Patient confidentiality was strictly preserved, and the investigation adhered to the ethical standards set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Toyohashi Heart Center in Aichi Prefecture (approval No. 240313; approval date: May 28, 2024), and written consent for participation was obtained from all participants.Figure 1Patient flow diagram. Study Variables

Patients were categorized into 2 cohorts according to the presence or absence of late aortic complications: 51 patients in the event group and 75 in the nonevent group. For each patient, only the initial aortic event observed during follow-up was included in the analysis. We compared the groups with respect to preoperative CTA features, clinical status, baseline characteristics, intraoperative details, and midterm outcomes. Late aortic events were defined as enlargement of the downstream aorta exceeding 50 mm, new-onset dissection, reintervention involving the distal aorta (including open surgical and endovascular procedures), or death attributable to aortic disease. Progressive dilation of the residual dissected aorta is a recognized marker of adverse aortic remodeling and a predictor of future aortic complications after central repair. Furthermore, as the residual chronic dissection enlarges, endovascular treatment becomes increasingly technically challenging. Therefore, we used 50 mm as the cutoff value for aortic enlargement, consistent with our previous report.4 Patients were followed through outpatient visits and review of medical records. Patients without late aortic events were censored at the time of their last follow-up.

Image Analysis

All patients who underwent preoperative CTA were retrospectively reviewed. Because postoperative CT follow-up was sometimes noncontrast or varied in timing, this study focused on preoperative CTA findings to identify predictors of late aortic events. Preoperative CTA was performed using multidetector CT scanners at our institution or the referring hospitals. Assessed parameters included maximum aortic diameter; diameters at predefined anatomical levels (T7 vertebra and celiac artery [CA]) (Figure 2, A); number and location of intimal tears; extent of distal dissection; and involvement of major branch vessels. When dissection extends into branch vessels, tears are often located near these branches, although they are not always detectable on preoperative CTA. Aortic diameter was calculated from the cross-sectional area perpendicular to the centerline (Figure 2, B), and intimal tears were counted when clearly visible (Figure 2, C). Branch involvement was defined as extension of the false lumen into the branch on CTA. All image measurements were reviewed by experienced cardiovascular surgeons and radiologists who were blinded to clinical outcomes.Figure 2Preoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) measurements and anatomical evaluations. Statistical Analyses

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation when normally distributed and as median (25th-75th percentile) when non-normally distributed. Continuous variables were compared using the Student t test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher exact 2-tailed test. There were no missing data in the variables analyzed. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify potential predictors of late aortic events. Variables with a P value < .10 in the univariable analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariable Cox regression model to identify independent predictors. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated. Interobserver agreement for categorical variables was assessed using the Cohen kappa coefficient, and measurement reproducibility for continuous variables was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient.

Event-free survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Subanalyses were performed for each individual component of the composite endpoint, including residual aortic dilation and distal aortic reintervention.

Because late mortality constitutes a competing risk that may preclude distal aortic reintervention, cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) were estimated using the Aalen-Johansen estimator, and group differences in the CIFs were assessed using the Gray test. Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard regression was additionally performed to evaluate the independent associations of brachiocephalic artery (BCA) involvement, unilateral IA involvement, and HAR with the cumulative incidence of distal reintervention in the presence of the competing risk of death.

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22 (IBM Corp), except for the CIF analysis and Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard regression, which were performed using R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Preoperative CTA assessments included measurements of maximal aortic diameter, aortic diameter at the levels of the seventh thoracic vertebra (Th7) and the CA; the number of entry tears; the extent of dissection beyond the CA; and the origin of major aortic branches—such as the BCA, left common carotid artery, left subclavian artery, visceral arteries, and iliac arteries (IAs)—from the false lumen. When comparing patients who experienced aortic events and those who did not, we found that dissection involving the BCA and unilateral or bilateral IAs was significantly more frequent in the event group (P < .05). In contrast, maximal aortic diameter, aortic diameter at the Th7 and CA levels, number of entry tears, and presence of false lumen perfusion to the left common carotid artery, left subclavian artery, CA, superior mesenteric artery, and renal arteries showed no significant differences between the groups.

Late aortic complications occurring distal to the surgical repair site are presented in Table E1. Dilation of the residual aorta was diagnosed in 28 patients. Enlargement of the residual dissection may be attributed to persistent entry tears in the supra-aortic branches or to blood inflow from re-entry sites distal to the central repair. Enlargement of the residual dissection was caused by a persistent re-entry located in the BCA (Figure E1, A). Although FET was used, multiple re-entries remained in the distal aorta, leading to true lumen narrowing and false lumen expansion (Figure E1, B); however, other contributing factors may also have been involved. Only 1 patient experienced a new dissection or rupture in the distal aorta. Distal aortic procedures—including thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR)—were required in 25 individuals. Of these, distal stent graft-induced new entry (SINE) occurred in 2 patients, requiring TEVAR. Consequently, the overall rate of secondary intervention after central repair was 19.8%. Late aortic events occurred in 51 patients during the follow-up period (40.5%). As a subanalysis of the composite end point, the individual components were evaluated separately. Subanalyses for dilation of the residual aorta alone and distal aortic procedure alone are presented in Figures E2 and E3. Separate analyses for other events were not performed because these events were rare in our cohort. Results of the Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that patients with preoperative dissection involving the BCA had a significantly lower dilation-free rate than those without such involvement (5-year event-free rate: 83.1% vs 86.6%, log-rank P = .02). Results of the Kaplan-Meier analysis also demonstrated that patients with preoperative dissection involving the unilateral IA did not have a significantly different dilation-free survival compared with those without such involvement (5-year event-free rate: 83.1% vs 86.4%, log-rank P = .32). Patients who underwent HAR had a greater rate of dilation of the residual aorta than those who underwent TAR with or without FET (5-year event-free rate: 78.3% vs 96.8%, log-rank P = .005). Results of the Kaplan-Meier analysis also demonstrated that patients with preoperative dissection involving the BCA had a significantly lower distal aortic reintervention-free rate than those without such involvement (5-year event-free rate: 71.5% vs 87.3%, log-rank P = .03). Moreover, patients with preoperative dissection involving the unilateral IA had a significantly lower distal aortic reintervention-free rate than those without such involvement (5-year event-free rate: 68.9% vs 93.0%, log-rank P = .003). There was no significant difference in the rate of distal aortic reintervention between patients who underwent HAR and those who underwent TAR with or without FET (5-year event-free rate: 85.7% vs 76.7%, log-rank P = .93; Figure E3, C).

To account for the competing risk of late mortality, CIFs were estimated for distal aortic reintervention (Figure E4). The Gray test demonstrated that BCA involvement was significantly associated with a greater cumulative incidence of distal reintervention (5-year CIF: 23.8% vs 12.0%; P = .03; Figure E4, A), as was unilateral IA involvement (5-year CIF: 27.8% vs 6.3%; P = .005; Figure E4, B). No significant difference in the cumulative incidence of distal reintervention was observed between patients who underwent HAR and those who underwent TAR with or without FET (5-year CIF: 19.5% vs 14.1%; P = .66; Figure E4, C). In Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard regression, unilateral IA involvement remained a significant independent predictor of distal reintervention after accounting for the competing risk of death (subdistribution hazard ratio [SHR], 4.86; 95% CI, 1.39-17.08; P = .014). In contrast, BCA involvement (SHR, 1.97; 95% CI, 0.81-4.83; P = .14) and HAR (SHR, 1.38; 95% CI, 0.52-3.68; P = .52) were not independently significant.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify preoperative predictors of late aortic complications after central repair for acute DeBakey type I dissection. Although 10 to 20% of patients require reintervention within a decade,6 complete false lumen thrombosis is uncommon because distal re-entry sustains false lumen pressurization.7 A patent false lumen increases the risk of distal aortic events and reduces survival8,9; however, false lumen patency alone does not uniformly confer high risk. Our analysis revealed that surgery without TAR or TAR with FET and dissection involving the BCA or unilateral IA were independent predictors of late aortic events. These findings highlight the importance of detailed preoperative imaging evaluation in identifying patients at higher risk of adverse aortic remodeling.

TAR with or without FET promotes favorable aortic remodeling in TAAD.3,4,10,11 Although TAR was traditionally associated with longer operative times and greater risk,12 the FET technique has made its operative time and risk comparable with HAR,3 leading to wider adoption. At our institution, the surgical strategy for ATAAD is primarily based on a tear-oriented approach. Since the introduction of the FET technique, TAR with FET has generally been performed for acute DeBakey I dissection, as it is expected to promote more extensive aortic remodeling. However, HAR may be performed in selected patients when the primary entry tear is located in the ascending aorta, and no other major entry tears are identified on CTA, including cases without BCA or IA involvement. Detailed surgical technique and cerebral protection strategies have been described previously.4 In our study, HAR was associated with a significantly greater risk of late aortic events, consistent with previous reports. The FET technique has been associated with SINE, a well-recognized complication during long-term follow-up after treatment for ATAAD.13 In our cohort, 2 patients developed distal SINE and subsequently required additional intervention with TEVAR. The occurrence of distal SINE may increase with longer follow-up periods. Ogino14 emphasized the importance of placing the FET graft in a straight configuration within the descending aorta and avoiding excessive oversizing to minimize the risk of SINE.

False lumen patency affects late outcomes, even in patients who undergo TAR with or without FET.8,9 In particular, cases with dissected supra-aortic vessels, such as the BCA, may serve as proximal sources of persistent false lumen perfusion, so these cases may warrant TAR to mitigate the risk of future complications. Yasuda and colleagues15 reported that the presence of a re-entry tear at the BCA is associated with early postoperative patency of the false lumen in the aortic arch after HAR and contributes to subsequent enlargement of the arch. The potential collinearity between BCA involvement and the selection of HAR should be considered, because supra-aortic branch extension may influence surgical decision-making. However, both variables remained independently associated with outcomes in the multivariable analysis, supporting the robustness of our findings. The average diameter of the BCA is 16.0 mm, whereas that of the IA is 10.5 mm, making them the largest arterial branches directly arising from the aorta.16,17 In large-caliber vessels, intimal tears tend to be larger. Re-entry into the IA may sustain false lumen perfusion and contribute to late aortic events. Unilateral IA re-entry may result in more concentrated flow into the false lumen than bilateral involvement, potentially explaining its significance in the current follow-up; however, bilateral IA re-entry may become relevant with longer observation. In patients with DeBakey type I dissection, additional entry or re-entry tears are frequently found in the descending thoracic aorta or near the visceral branches. However, aside from cases with a large re-entry tear, these distal lesions have little impact on maintaining false lumen patency in the aortic arch. As previously reported, preoperative aortic diameter appears to be associated with the risk of late aortic events.5 However, this association was not observed in our study, possibly because of the relatively short follow-up; with longer observation, preoperative aortic diameter may emerge as a predictor of adverse aortic outcomes. In fact, several reports have noted progressive dilatation of the descending aorta over time, even after TAR.18

Preoperative CTA findings may help guide surgical strategy, including extending aortic replacement or planning early adjunctive endovascular intervention. At our institution, for favorable aortic remodeling, adjunctive endovascular interventions are considered before the chronic phase when true lumen narrowing persists, and progressive enlargement of the residual dissected aorta is observed after central repair. Selective pre-emptive TEVAR may facilitate this remodeling and reduce the need for future complex open reinterventions.19 In addition to TEVAR, the STABILISE technique may also be considered in selected patients.20

Paraplegia from spinal cord injury is a serious complication of FET, although the risk is lower in acute dissection than in atherosclerotic disease. To reduce neurologic risk, long stent coverage (≥15 cm) or extension to T8 or beyond should be avoided; accordingly, our institution uses a 6-cm FET for spinal cord protection.21, 22, 23, 24

Our findings support previous reports identifying persistent re-entry flow and incomplete false lumen exclusion as contributors to late aortic events. In addition to branch involvement, particularly the BCA and unilateral IA, HAR was identified as an independent preoperative risk factor. Although TAR with FET promotes aortic remodeling, it carries a risk of late complications such as SINE, highlighting the need for careful long-term follow-up.25

In the subanalysis of individual components of the composite end point, preoperative BCA dissection and HAR were associated with an increased risk of dilation of the residual aorta; whereas, unilateral IA dissection was not identified as a significant risk factor for dilation. One possible explanation is that isolated unilateral IA dissection may require a longer period to influence the dilation of the residual aorta. Given the relatively limited follow-up period in the present study, the impact of unilateral IA dissection on late aortic enlargement may have been underestimated. With longer follow-up, IA dissection could potentially act as a large re-entry site and become an independent predictor of downstream aortic dilation.

In contrast, HAR was not identified as an independent risk factor for distal aortic reintervention. This finding may reflect the recent development of endovascular treatment strategies and the increasing use of early pre-emptive TEVAR, which may mitigate the need for subsequent distal aortic procedures.

This study has some limitations. First, early in-hospital deaths were excluded because the present analysis focused on predictors of late aortic events among patients who survived the initial operation and had available follow-up data. However, this approach may introduce survivorship bias. Moreover, death represents a competing risk for distal aortic reintervention. To address this, cumulative incidence analysis and Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard regression were performed. These analyses confirmed that unilateral IA involvement remained a significant independent predictor of distal reintervention even after accounting for the competing risk of death. BCA involvement and HAR were not independently significant in the Fine-Gray model, which may reflect the increasing use of early pre-emptive TEVAR and the relatively limited sample size and number of events in this cohort. Therefore, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution when applying them to the overall population of patients with acute DeBakey type I aortic dissection. In addition, we focused solely on preoperative CTA; intraoperative findings and postoperative imaging were not evaluated in detail owing to data unavailability. Stratification according to the presence of branch vessel dissection may provide additional insights into the risk of late aortic events and warrants further investigation in future studies. Although our institution frequently uses the FET technique for acute DeBakey type I dissection, the present study was not designed to establish a treatment strategy, and further studies with larger cohorts are required to clarify the optimal indications for this approach.

Conclusions

Dissection involving the BCA or unilateral IA on preoperative CT and undergoing HAR were independent predictors of late aortic events in acute DeBakey type I aortic dissection, with HRs of 2.11, 2.50, and 3.16, respectively. Surgical planning should take these anatomical risk factors into account to improve long-term outcomes. In selected high-risk cases, early adjunctive endovascular intervention may also help promote favorable aortic remodeling and prevent future aortic events.
