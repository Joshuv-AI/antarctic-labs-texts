Algorithm to Select the Treatment Modality for the Incompetent Saphenous Vein
Authors: Jin Hyun Joh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVaricose vein procedures are common vascular surgeries. Over the last two decades, the management of saphenous vein reflux has evolved from traditional surgical stripping (SS) to less invasive endovenous therapies (EVTs), including thermal and non-thermal ablation. As a result, EVT is now the preferred approach for treating incompetent saphenous veins. Although all methods demonstrate comparable effectiveness and high closure rates, both SS and EVT have unique advantages and disadvantages. SS ensures the complete removal of refluxing truncal veins but carries the risk of complications related to invasiveness, such as groin infection, lymphatic issues, and nerve damage. EVT is minimally invasive and promotes a quicker recovery and return to work. However, thermal techniques can cause heat-related nerve or skin damage and necessitate tumescent injections, whereas non-thermal approaches may lead to thrombophlebitis or allergic reactions. Thus, the selection of optimal treatment should consider both the anatomical features of the refluxing vein and the specific characteristics of each procedure. This review aims to compare the clinical outcomes of these procedures and propose a suitable decision-making process for selecting the appropriate treatment for incompetent saphenous veins.Keywords: Varicose veins, Surgical stripping, Radiofrequency ablation, Non-thermal ablation, Algorithms

INTRODUCTION

Varicose veins are a prevalent vascular condition characterized by enlarged, twisted veins, typically in the legs. These veins are a common manifestation of chronic venous disease (CVD), which presents with a spectrum of signs including reticular and varicose veins, edema, eczema, pigmentation changes, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche, and stasis ulcers [1]. The distinction between spider, reticular, and varicose veins is based on their diameters, with varices defined as veins that are at least 3 mm wide [2].

Despite being underestimated by healthcare systems, CVD is a widespread condition [3]. This underestimation stems from a lack of recognition of the severity and impact, as well as an incomplete understanding of the diverse symptoms associated with primary and secondary venous disorders. Based on an analysis of 32 research articles, a systematic review incorporating pooled prevalence data indicated that CVD affects between 38.3% and 90.4% of patients across six continents [4]. The clinical component (C) is the most widely used part of the Clinical, Etiologic, Anatomic, and Pathophysiologic (CEAP) classification system, including C0 (no visible vein), C1 (telangiectasia or venulectasia), C2 (varicose vein), C3 (edema), C4 (skin changes), C5 (healed venous ulcer), and C6 (active venous ulcer). The pooled prevalence for each C stage was as follows: C0: 9%, C1: 26%, C2: 19%, C3: 8%, C4: 5%, C5: 1%, C6: 0.42%. The prevalence of C2 disease varies by geographical region: 21% in Europe, 17% in Asia, 5.5% in Africa, 23% in North America, 22% in South America, and 19% in the Pacific Islands. In Korea, the annual number of patients with CVD managed in the healthcare system increased from 143,108 in 2010 to 219,319 in 2020 (risk ratio, 1.53; P<0.001) [5].

Treatment approaches for incompetent saphenous veins rely heavily on the patient’s clinical presentation and thorough individual duplex ultrasound (DUS) findings. DUS provides detailed preoperative mapping of saphenous vein anatomy and hemodynamics, identifying the precise location and extent of venous reflux as well as any associated abnormalities, thereby enabling a tailored and effective treatment strategy. Venous reflux lasting longer than 0.5 seconds, as detected by DUS, is generally considered clinically significant and serves as an indication for intervention.

In terms of clinical significance, symptomatic, uncomplicated varicose veins resulting from superficial venous insufficiency represent the most common indication for treatment [6]. In patients with CVD who present with edema but lack clinically obvious varicose veins, the need for superficial venous incompetence treatment is uncertain because edema may arise from multiple non-venous causes and may not respond to venous interventions. Therefore, when planning treatment for venous incompetence, clinicians should first assess other possible causes of edema such as venous outflow obstruction, calf muscle dysfunction, lymphedema, and hepatic or renal diseases. Conversely, patients with symptomatic varicose veins accompanied by edema are more likely to experience improvement following treatment of superficial venous reflux, with edema reduction frequently noted after intervention. Severe forms of CVD—such as eczema, hyperpigmentation, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche, and stasis venous ulcers—represent clear indications for treating superficial venous insufficiency [7]. Treating the underlying reflux in venous ulcers not only allows the ulcer to heal but also reduces the likelihood of recurrence. Treatment options include a variety of procedures such as endovenous ablation, sclerotherapy, or surgical ligation of veins with pathologic reflux.

Treatment strategies for incompetent saphenous veins aims to eliminate truncal vein reflux to manage the retrograde hydrostatic pressure and remove branch varicosities. Branch varicosities can be addressed using techniques such as stab avulsion, powered phlebectomy, or sclerotherapy. Although methods for managing branch varicosities have remained largely unchanged, significant advancements have been made in controlling truncal vein reflux to minimize complications. Over the past two decades, management of truncal vein reflux has evolved from traditional surgical stripping (SS) to less invasive endovenous therapies (EVT), including thermal and non-thermal ablation. However, SS remains the preferred method in some countries because of reimbursement policies and the limited availability of endovenous devices. In healthcare settings where EVT options are available, most patients undergo minimally invasive treatment.

This review compares the clinical outcomes of the available treatment modalities and proposes a practical decision-making algorithm for selecting the optimal treatment for incompetent saphenous veins.

TREATMENT MODALITIES

1) Surgical stripping

Traditional SS for great saphenous vein (GSV) incompetence combines high ligation at the saphenofemoral junction (SFJ) with axial stripping (Fig. 1). The key procedure involves dividing the GSV at the SFJ, ligating it close to the common femoral vein, and individually dividing and ligating its tributaries. To complete the process, an endoluminal stripper is inserted near the knee toward the SFJ and pulled distally, achieving inversion stripping of the GSV trunk. The main advantage of the inversion stripping is its ability to minimize saphenous nerve injury and reduce branch avulsion by rolling the vein inside out during removal. However, careful dissection is required near the SFJ because small branches of the femoral artery in this region may be easily injured, potentially leading to significant bleeding.

For small saphenous vein (SSV) incompetence, the surgical procedure also begins with high ligation, specifically at the saphenopopliteal junction (SPJ), guided by preoperative DUS. Through a small incision distal to the SPJ, the SSV is divided and the proximal stump is secured, typically with double ligation. The primary disadvantage and crucial concern of SSV surgery is the risk of sural nerve injury, as this nerve frequently courses adjacent to the distal third of the SSV. Therefore, if surgery is required, only short-segment inversion stripping from the SPJ to the upper or mid-calf, or limited segmental excision, may be preferred. A key advantage of this localized surgical approach is its ability to achieve definitive high ligation with reduced risk of nerve injury through careful perivenous dissection, in contrast to the more extensive stripping technique.

① Indispensability of SS

SS becomes necessary when EVT is not accessible, most commonly because of reimbursement limitations or the unavailability of devices. In such situations, SS remains a practical and effective option for treating incompetent saphenous veins.

Postoperative inflammation, hyperpigmentation, and induration following EVT can be more pronounced if the saphenous trunk is located very close to the skin (≤5 mm), making SS a more suitable choice. A saphenous vein aneurysm is defined as a localized dilation at least three times the upper limit of the normal vein diameter, or as >20 mm near the SFJ and >15 mm near the SPJ [8]. When junctional aneurysms are present near the SFJ or SPJ, EVT carries a high risk of developing stump thrombosis. In such cases, SS is preferable because of its ability to completely remove aneurysms and perform flush ligation. In summary, SS is warranted when endovenous devices are unavailable or not reimbursed, when the saphenous vein lies superficially (<5 mm), or when junctional aneurysms are present.

2) Thermal ablation

Endovenous thermal ablation (ETA), which encompasses radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and laser ablation (LA), is a primary minimally invasive treatment for incompetent saphenous veins [9]. The shared mechanism of ETA involves the use of thermal energy to damage the venous endothelium, ultimately producing fibrotic occlusion [10]. Specifically, RFA uses an electrode catheter to directly heat the tissue, causing endothelial injury, whereas LA uses an optical fiber to deliver laser energy [11], which is absorbed by blood components to create steam bubbles that thermally damage the endothelium (Fig. 2).

Heat-induced ablation was then performed. (B) Laser ablation used bare-tipped or jacket-tipped fibers to deliver laser energy to a target vein.

Both RFA and LA are considered first-line treatments for GSV incompetence because of their minimally invasive nature and favorable clinical outcomes. However, a key procedural requirement—often regarded as a disadvantage—is the mandatory use of tumescent anesthesia. Tumescent injection compresses the vein for better energy transfer and provides perivenous anesthesia to mitigate pain while protecting surrounding tissues from thermal injury [7,12].

The standard procedural technique for both RFA and LA involves positioning the catheter tip approximately 2 cm distal to the SFJ for the GSV (or 2 cm distal to the termination point for the SSV), injecting tumescent anesthesia, and delivering energy while gradually withdrawing the device. For SSV ablation, the tumescent anesthesia is gently injected around the SSV trunk between the superficial and deep fascia. In the proximal segment, where the SSV is located below the deep fascia, the tumescent solution is injected around the SSV trunk to separate the adjacent muscles and nerves. Another important consideration for SSV thermal ablation is the choice of access site because of the potential for thermal injury to the sural nerve. Therefore, access is typically obtained above the mid-calf level. If the required ablation length is shorter than the heating element of a standard RFA catheter, a shorter 3 cm heating element catheter can be used.

3) Non-thermal and non-tumescent (NTNT) ablation

ETA has the inherent disadvantages related to heat delivery, including pain, skin burns, nerve damage, pigmentation changes, and the potential formation of an arteriovenous fistula [13,14]. Although tumescent injection is used to mitigate these risks and provide anesthesia, its administration can be uncomfortable for patients. The initial capital costs of thermal generator devices are another practical limitation [15]. As an alternative, NTNT procedures have emerged, which offer the distinct advantage of avoiding both thermal energy and tumescent anesthesia. These methods, including cyanoacrylate closure (CAC) and mechanochemical ablation (MOCA), rely on different non-thermal mechanisms to achieve vein occlusion.

CAC and MOCA each utilize specific mechanisms to abolish truncal reflux (Fig. 3). CAC uses a high-viscosity glue injected into the vein in short segments, relying on its rapid polymerization properties combined with external compression to immediately create a physical blockage. Its main advantage is swift physical occlusion without heat, requiring only local compression. MOCA utilizes a dual-action mechanism: a rapidly rotating wire inside an infusion catheter mechanically disrupts the endothelium, while a liquid sclerosant is infused simultaneously to chemically damage the vein. This combined approach of mechanical disruption and chemical sclerosis is performed continuously as the catheter is slowly withdrawn, offering the advantage of a potent, non-thermal treatment while avoiding the nerve injury risk associated with heat.

COMPARISON OF OUTCOMES

Effective CVD management is best achieved through collaborative decision-making. This process involves the clinician’s expertise, including knowledge of evidence-supported treatment options, a clear understanding of risks and benefits, awareness of resources available within the healthcare system, consideration of cost-effectiveness, and importantly, respect for the patient’s preferences, values, and individual circumstances. Research indicates that when patients actively participate in treatment decisions, they are more likely to adhere to the chosen regimen, experience better outcomes, and feel less regret about their choices.

1) Comparative studies of the treatment modalities

Several studies have compared the outcomes of variable treatment options available for superficial venous insufficiency. Traditionally, SS has served as the benchmark therapy, and early investigations typically evaluated emerging modalities in comparison with SS. More recent trials, however, have directly compared the outcomes of different EVTs. ① SS vs. thermal ablation

Several meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared the clinical outcomes of SS with those of thermal ablation techniques, including RFA and LA. Luebke et al. [16] conducted a meta-analysis of eight studies that compared RFA and SS. The results indicated no significant differences between RFA and SS in terms of recanalization, need for retreatment, occlusion, or reflux rates. However, patient quality-of-life (QoL) scores were significantly favored RFA, particularly with respect to the earlier return to normal activities and work.

A 2014 Cochrane review examined the outcomes of thermal ablation versus SS [17]. This review found no significant difference in early or late recanalization rates between ETA and SS. However, the technical failure rate was significantly lower in the LA group. In addition, neovascularization at the SFJ has been reported more frequently after SS than after ETA [10,17]. In a larger systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 RCTs, Vemulapalli et al. [18] reported that all clinical outcomes, including short-term bleeding rates, reflux recurrence within 1-2 years, long-term symptom scores, and QoL outcomes at 2 years, were comparable between thermal ablation and SS. More recently, Mohamed et al. [13] reported 10-year RCT outcomes after thermal ablation or SS, suggesting that both thermal ablation and SS are effective interventions for varicose veins, providing durable symptom relief, improved QoL, and very low rates of disease progression. Clinical and QoL outcomes, however, were superior in the thermal ablation group.

Although comparative studies are primarily limited by heterogeneity in thermal ablation techniques and the potential detection and performance bias in non-blinded trials, thermal ablation is recommended as the primary treatment for incompetent saphenous veins. This recommendation is supported by comparable long-term anatomical outcomes, alongside improved clinical and patient-centered results associated with thermal ablation [7,12].

② RFA vs. LA

Many RCTs have compared the clinical outcomes of RFA and LA. Overall, RFA is associated with less postoperative pain and bruising than LA, despite similar occlusion and recurrence rates. Recently, Jiang et al. [19] conducted a meta-analysis of 29 studies (16 RCTs and 13 cohort studies) that evaluated these two modalities. Their analysis showed that the occlusion rates at 1 month were 98.3% for RFA and 98.0% for LA, whereas at 1 year, the rates were 93.1% and 94.1%, respectively. Subgroup analyses indicated that since 2016, RFA has had higher 1-year occlusion rates than LA (93.2% vs 91.2%; odds ratio 1.35; 95% confidence interval, 1.0-1.83; P=0.05). The authors concluded that RFA and LA demostrate comparable effectiveness in both early and long-term GSV occlusion, as well as similar rates of thrombotic and phlebitis-related complications. However, these more recent data suggest that RFA may provide better GSV occlusion rates than LA. In addition, RFA has been linked to fewer instances of paresthesia, burns, bruising, and disease recurrence than LA [19].

③ Thermal ablation vs. NTNT

Shahzad et al. [20] performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs to compare the outcomes of thermal and non-thermal endovenous ablation in superficial venous incompetence. The analysis included eight RCTs and demonstrated that both approaches achieved similar rates of vein occlusion. During the immediate postoperative period, non-thermal techniques were associated with less patient discomfort and a lower incidence of nerve injury. Improvements in QoL were similar between the thermal and non-thermal ablation techniques [20]. However, the conclusions of this review are limited by the relatively small number of available RCTs. Although the findings suggest similar occlusion rates and QoL improvements, the evidence may not fully capture all comparative outcomes between the diverse thermal and non-thermal techniques currently in use.

④ CAC vs. MOCA

Belramman et al. [21] conducted an RCT directly comparing two non-tumescent ablation techniques: CAC and MOCA. The study demonstrated that both modalities resulted in similar improvements in clinical severity, as well as general and disease-specific QoL outcomes. Rates of complete vein occlusion were also similar between the two groups. In addition, periprocedural pain scores did not differ significantly between CAC and MOCA.

⑤ SS vs. NTNT

An RCT comparing SS with CAC suggested that both interventions achieved similar rates of complete occlusion of the targeted vein at 3 months. However, postoperative pain scores and the incidence of ecchymosis were significantly lower in the CAC group. The frequency at which complications occurred and overall nature of complications differed between the two interventions. The study results suggest that interventions using CAC may be highly successful and result in a relatively limited number of complications [22].

ALGORITHM

Choosing the optimal procedure should primarily be guided by available evidence, a thorough assessment of risks and benefits, the structure of the healthcare system, device availability, cost, and patient preferences. In most cases, minimally invasive endovenous procedures are recommended as the first-line approach for treating incompetent saphenous veins [7,12]. SS may be considered only when endovenous options are unavailable or not reimbursed, when a junctional aneurysm is present, or when the saphenous vein lies close to the skin surface (<5 mm). Thermal ablation may be a good choice if the patient prefers treatment as an inpatient or requires sedative anesthesia to manage the significant pain or discomfort associated with heat activation. Additionally, when treating moderate-to-severe varicose veins, phlebectomy may be needed concomitantly, and thermal ablation may be selected if pain control can be better achieved under general or sedative anesthesia. In these cases, thermal ablation may be the best approach.

For NTNT procedures, the complex hypersensitivity and irritation reaction (CHAIR) phenomena should be carefully considered [28]. MOCA may be a suitable option if a patient has risk factors for CHAIR. Within the NTNT category, patient age may be a factor in decision-making. CHAIR can be a problematic complication, particularly in younger individuals [29]. In the study by Gibson et al. [30], the mean age of patients with CHAIR was 50 years, with no significant age difference between those with and without CHAIR; notably, no hypersensitivity reactions occurred in patients older than 70 years. Therefore, we used 50 years as a representative cutoff age when assessing CHAIR risk during CAC. Isolated SSV reflux can be effectively treated using CAC because of the low risk of sural nerve injury and flexibility in choosing an access site. The catheter used for MOCA procedures should be advanced without the aid of a guidewire. For this reason, MOCA may be difficult to perform in patients with tortuous saphenous veins. Therefore, MOCA should be preferentially selected when the patient does not have a tortuous saphenous vein. CONCLUSION

In summary, endovenous treatment is increasingly favored as the initial approach for managing incompetent saphenous veins. However, SS remains necessary under specific circumstances. In addition, NTNT procedures present a unique set of potential complications. Therefore, a well-reasoned decision-making process is required to guide the selection of the most appropriate treatment for incompetent saphenous veins. The algorithm presented in this study offers a practical framework that can be applied in real-world clinical settings to achieve favorable patient outcomes.
