BlockRR: A Unified Framework of RR-type Algorithms for Label Differential Privacy
Haixia Liu, Yi Ding

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce BlockRR , a novel and unified randomized-response mechanism for label differential privacy. This framework generalizes existed RR-type mechanisms as special cases under specific parameter settings, which eliminates the need for separate, case-by-case analysis. Theoretically, we prove that BlockRR satisfies ϵ \epsilon -label DP. We also design a partition method for BlockRR based on a weight matrix derived from label prior information; the parallel composition principle ensures that the composition of two such mechanisms remains ϵ \epsilon -label DP. Empirically, we evaluate BlockRR on two variants of CIFAR-10 with varying degrees of class imbalance. Results show that in the high-privacy and moderate-privacy regimes ( ϵ ≤ 3.0 \epsilon\leq 3.0 ), our propsed method gets a better balance between test accuaracy and the average of per-class accuracy. In the low-privacy regime ( ϵ ≥ 4.0 \epsilon\geq 4.0 ), all methods reduce BlockRR to standard RR without additional performance loss.

In many real-world scenarios, such as ad-click prediction or survey analysis

The randomized response (RR) mechanism

Here, it functions as a label perturbation mechanism: each true label is mapped to a privatized output via a randomized process that enforces the privacy guarantee. Models are subsequently trained on these perturbed labels.

The pursuit of improved utility has led to numerous variants of the RR mechanism, tailored to different learning tasks. These include RRWithPrior
[

[

We address this gap by proposing BlockRR, a unified framework for Label DP that generalizes RR-type mechanisms. The core idea is to partition the label spaces (original and privatized) into blocks, applying tailored RR transformations between specific block pairs. This design subsumes existing methods—such as RRWithPrior and RRonBins—as special cases defined by specific hyper-parameter settings (i.e., particular block structures). Thus, BlockRR provides a common design space where algorithm comparison reduces to an analysis of hyper-parameter choices, enabling systematic benchmarking and selection. 3
Unified Framework of RR-type algorithms under Label DP

A number of algorithms have been designed to achieve label DP. While they may seem different on the surface, these methods all share a common underlying design principle. To maximize utility, a label is mapped to itself (or a close surrogate) with high probability, with the remaining probability distributed across other labels. In the following, we introduce a unified framework for RR-type algorithms under label differential privacy.

3.1
BlockRR

We propose
BlockRR
, a general Label DP framework designed to unify existing RR-type methods and enable more flexible randomization strategies. Its core mechanism partitions the true label set into disjoint subsets (determined in Subsection
3.2
). Formally, let
\mathcal{S}
be the original label set. We partition it into two disjoint subsets,
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{1}
typically contains majority or important labels and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
contains minority or less important labels. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
denote the privatized label set, and let
B:\mathcal{S}\to\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
be a randomized mapping function with
y\in B(y),\forall y\in\mathcal{S}
. This partitions the output set into
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}=\tilde{\mathcal{S}}\cap B(\mathcal{S}_{1})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{S}}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
. As illustrated in Figure
1
, the Cartesian product of the true and privatized label sets is thus divided into four regions. We proceed by constructing the overall randomized algorithm in a modular fashion, designing the specific perturbation rules for each region.

In practice, majority labels often dominate the dataset. To improve the performance of Label DP algorithms, we focus on leveraging the information-rich majority labels while appropriately mitigating the influence of minority labels, which may carry limited useful information. To this end, we introduce a subset
\Delta\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
with cardinality
l
, which is a subset of the most dominated labels.

We now define the transition probability (or probability density function) for each block. Beginning with the bottom-left block, corresponding to the pair
(\mathcal{S}_{2},\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1})
, we define

\displaystyle\Pr(\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y},\tilde{y}\in\Delta|Y=y,y\in\mathcal{S}_{2})=\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|},

\displaystyle\Pr(\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y},\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}\setminus\Delta|Y=y,y\in\mathcal{S}_{2})=\beta.

Similarly, for the bottom-right block, corresponding to the pair
(\mathcal{S}_{2},\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2})
, we define

\displaystyle\Pr(\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y},\tilde{y}\in B(y)|Y=y,y\in\mathcal{S}_{2})=e^{\epsilon}\gamma,

\displaystyle\Pr(\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y},\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}\setminus B(y)|Y=y,y\in\mathcal{S}_{2})=\gamma.

For the top-left block (corresponding to the pair
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
) and the top-right block (corresponding to the pair
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
), the function is specified as follows:

\displaystyle\Pr(\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y},\tilde{y}\in B(y)|Y=y,y\in\mathcal{S}_{1})=e^{\epsilon}\beta,

\displaystyle\Pr(\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y},\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}\setminus B(y)|Y=y,y\in\mathcal{S}_{1})=\beta,

\displaystyle\Pr(\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y},\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|Y=y,y\in\mathcal{S}_{1})=\gamma.

By the probabilistic property
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{y}}[\Pr(\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y}|Y=y)]=1,\ \forall y\in\mathcal{S}
, we have the following system of linear equations

\begin{cases}(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-|B(y)|)\cdot\beta+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|\cdot\gamma=1,\ \forall y\in\mathcal{S}_{1},\\
(|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-|\Delta|)\cdot\beta+(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|-|B(y)|)\cdot\gamma\\
\hskip 128.0374pt=1-\frac{|\Delta|}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|},\ \forall y\in\mathcal{S}_{2},\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
\lvert\cdot\rvert
is the cardinality of a set. Assuming
|B(y)|
is constant for all
y\in\mathcal{S}
and
|\Delta|=l
, solving (
3.1
) yields:

\beta=\dfrac{\beta_{1}}{\kappa},\ \gamma=\dfrac{\gamma_{1}}{\kappa},

(3.2)

where

\begin{split}\beta_{1}=&(e^{\epsilon}-1)|B(y)|+\frac{l\cdot|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|},\\
\gamma_{1}=&((e^{\epsilon}-1)|B(y)|+l)-\frac{l}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}\cdot((e^{\epsilon}-1)|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|),\\
\kappa=&((e^{\epsilon}-1)|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|)\cdot((e^{\epsilon}-1)|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|)\\
&-(|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-l)\cdot|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|.\end{split}

For these four mapping regions (Figure

\mathcal{S}_{1}\to\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}

4
Main Idea of BlockRR

Traditional Randomized Response applies a one-size-fits-all perturbation strategy to all labels, disregarding dataset-specific structures during randomization. To better account for data distribution characteristics, Ghazi et al.
[
13
]
introduced the RRWithPrior algorithm, which incorporates prior information to adjust the randomization mechanism. A key limitation of this method, however, is its discarding of minority labels, making it unsuitable for tasks where per-class accuracy matters. To address these shortcomings, we propose the BlockRR algorithm, which introduces configurable, block-wise randomization mechanisms tailored to different regions of the label space.

The design of a suitable randomized mechanism reduces to specifying the conditional probability values
p_{\tilde{y}\mid y}
. To encode dataset-specific structure, we introduce a weight for each such conditional probability. These weights are organized into a matrix, where the entry corresponding to
(y,\tilde{y})
is defined as follows:

w_{y\tilde{y}}=p_{\tilde{y}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathbb{I}(\tilde{y}\neq y)}{\sigma}\right).

When the weight matrix is diagonally dominant, a standard RR mechanism is a suitable choice. However, when the weight matrix is not diagonally dominant, we partition
\mathcal{S}
into two subsets:
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\{i:w_{ii}\geq w_{ij},\forall j\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}=\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{1}
. For labels in
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, the condition
w_{ii}\geq w_{ij},\forall j
does not hold. Yet we still aim to keep
p_{y\mid y}
as large as possible for all
y\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
to maintain training performance. To this end, we introduce a subset
\Delta\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
as the set of indices corresponding to the
l
largest values of
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}}
and impose the constraint
p_{\tilde{y}\mid y}=\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|},\forall y\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
when
\tilde{y}\in\Delta
.

We now formulate the following optimization problem:

\begin{split}\max&\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S},\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}}w_{y\tilde{y}}p_{\tilde{y}\mid y}\\

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce BlockRR, a novel and unified RR-type mechanism for label differential privacy. This framework generalizes existed RR-type mechanisms as special cases under specific parameter settings. Theoretically, we prove that BlockRR satisfies
\epsilon
-label DP. We also design a partition method for BlockRR based on a weight matrix derived from label prior information; the parallel composition principle ensures that the composition of two such mechanisms remains
\epsilon
-label DP. Empirically, we evaluate BlockRR on two variants of CIFAR-10 (
\text{CIFAR-10}_{1}
and
\text{CIFAR-10}_{2}
) with varying degrees of class imbalance. Results show that in the high-privacy regime (
\epsilon\leq 1.0
), our method significantly mitigates its class collapse and high variance issues. In the low-privacy regime (
\epsilon\geq 4.0
), all methods reduce BlockRR to standard RR (when
l=0
) without additional performance loss. In addition, our propsed method shows a better balance between test accuaracy and the average of per-class accuracy. While there is extensive theoretical work on label-flipping noise, a comprehensive understanding of block-wise flipping noise remains an important direction for future research.

Appendix A
Related Work

Over the past decade, the field of differential privacy has seen significant theoretical and practical advances. A cornerstone technique is RR
[
25
]
, introduced by Warner in 1965. Originally designed for anonymous surveys, its core idea is to allow participants to provide untruthful answers with a known probability when reporting sensitive data. This preserves individual privacy while still enabling accurate statistical analysis. With the formalization of the differential privacy framework, RR has become a crucial tool in DP research. Specifically, RR methods can be applied directly during data collection, preventing raw sensitive data from ever being exposed to a central server and thus providing a strong privacy guarantee
[
11
]
.

Additive noise mechanisms form another essential category of differential privacy methods. The Laplace Mechanism
[
8
]
achieves privacy by perturbing query outputs with Laplace-distributed noise. Similarly, the Gaussian Mechanism
[
9
]
adds noise from a Gaussian distribution. A distinct variant, the Staircase Mechanism
[
17
]
, ensures privacy by discretizing outputs into stepped values and injecting noise within this discrete structure.

Under the standard sample-level differential privacy (DP) framework, DP-ERM
[
6
]
enables the private training of regularized empirical risk minimization by the output perturbation or the objective perturbation. Initial approaches to private training focused on Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), privatizing each parameter update by injecting calibrated noise directly into the computed gradients
[
24
]
. This method evolved into the now-standard DP-SGD algorithm, which enforces privacy by clipping individual per-sample gradients and adding Gaussian noise to their aggregated batch averages
[
1
]
. Beyond these foundational techniques, research has extended privacy mechanisms into deep learning architectures. For instance, in Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), randomization layers have been successfully integrated to perturb internal feature representations directly
[
20
]
.

To further enhance privacy protection, Chaudhuri & Hsu
[
5
]
introduced the concept of Label Differential Privacy (Label DP), which specifically perturbs and protects only the data labels while keeping the features unchanged. Research into deep learning under this framework has advanced significantly. For instance, Malek Esmaeili et al
[
21
]
proposed the ALIBI method, which integrates Laplace noise addition with Bayesian denoising, and also adapted the PATE framework to Label DP by combining it with semi-supervised learning
[
23
]
. In addition, Ghazi et al.
[
13
]
built upon Randomized Response (RR) techniques. They enhanced the RRTop-
k
method by filtering out minority class samples and upsampling majority classes. Additionally, they introduced an RRWithPrior mechanism that leverages prior knowledge of the data distribution to mitigate the utility loss.

Label DP has also gained significant attention in regression tasks. Expanding on their previous work, Ghazi et al.
[
14
]
proposed RRonbins, a Label DP randomization mechanism based on global prior distributions. This method provides privacy by having labels “randomly respond” to discretized value intervals (bins), and employs an efficient algorithm to determine the optimal bin size. Furthermore, they combined an optimal unbiased randomization scheme, where the perturbed regression labels preserves statistical unbiasedness
[
2
]
.

attains its minimum and

gets its maximum at
l=0
, and
\max_{l}\gamma=\min_{l}\beta
.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of
\epsilon
-LabelDP

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

We only need to prove that for any
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
and any
\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y}\mid Y=y]\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y}\mid Y=y^{\prime}].

(C.1)

For any
\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
and any
y\in\mathcal{S}
,

\Pr[\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y}\mid Y=y]=\begin{cases}e^{\epsilon}\cdot\beta,&\text{for }y\in\mathcal{S}_{1},\tilde{y}\in B(y),\\
\beta,&\text{for }y\in\mathcal{S}_{1},\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}\setminus B(y)\text{ or }y\in\mathcal{S}_{2},\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}\setminus\Delta,\\
\dfrac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|},&\text{for }y\in\mathcal{S}_{2},\tilde{y}\in\Delta,\end{cases}

where
\beta=\dfrac{(e^{\epsilon}-1)|B(y)|+\frac{l|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}}{(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-|B(y)|)(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|-|B(y)|)-(|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-l)|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|}
.

We only need to verify that
\beta\leq\dfrac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}\leq e^{\epsilon}\beta
. We have

\displaystyle\quad\;\;\dfrac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}-\beta\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\dfrac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}-\dfrac{(e^{\epsilon}-1)|B(y)|+\frac{l|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}}{(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-|B(y)|)(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|-|B(y)|)-(|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-l)|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|}\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-|B(y)|)(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|-|B(y)|)-(|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-l)|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|\geq(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|-|B(y)|)|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|-l|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|-|B(y)|)^{2}\geq 0.

Next, we check the other inequality:

\displaystyle\quad\;\;e^{\epsilon}\beta-\dfrac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\dfrac{e^{\epsilon}\left((e^{\epsilon}-1)|B(y)|+\frac{l|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}\right)}{(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-|B(y)|)(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|-|B(y)|)-(|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-l)|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|}-\dfrac{1}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}\geq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow e^{\epsilon}\left((e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|-|B(y)|)+\frac{l|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}\right)|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|\geq

\displaystyle\quad\;\;(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-|B(y)|)(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|-|B(y)|)-(|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-l)|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(e^{\epsilon}-1)^{2}(|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|-|B(y)|)|B(y)|+(e^{\epsilon}-1)l|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|\geq 0,

where the last inequality is from
\epsilon>0
and the set
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
contains
B(y)
.

For any
\tilde{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
(assume
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}\neq\emptyset
) and
y\in\mathcal{S}
,

\Pr[\tilde{Y}=\tilde{y}\mid Y=y]=\begin{cases}e^{\epsilon}\cdot\gamma,&\text{for }y\in\mathcal{S}_{2},\tilde{y}\in B(y),\\
\gamma,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

where

\gamma=\dfrac{(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+l-|B(y)|)-\frac{l}{|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|}(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-|B(y)|)}{(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-|B(y)|)\cdot(e^{\epsilon}|B(y)|+|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|-|B(y)|)-(|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{1}|-l)|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{2}|},

which clearly satisfies (
C.1
). Thus, we conclude that
\mathrm{BlockRR}_{\epsilon}

\epsilon

Appendix F
Parallel Composition Theorem

In the following, we recall the parallel composition theorem in differential privacy.

Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a partition of a domain
U
, i.e., the
U_{i}
are pairwise disjoint and
\cup_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}=U
. For any dataset
D\subseteq U
, the sets
\{D\cap U_{i}\}^{k}_{i=1}
constitute a partition of
D
. Then, the parallel composition theorem can be stated as follows:

Lemma F.1

(Parallel Composition
[
22
]
)
.

Suppose that
\{M_{i}:U\rightarrow R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a family of randomized algorithms, each of which satisfies
\epsilon
-DP, and that
\{U_{i}\}^{k}_{i=1}
is a partition of the domain
U
. Then the combined mechanism
M(D):=\{M_{i}(D\cap U_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
still satisfies
\epsilon
-DP.

Proof.

For any adjacent datasets
D,D^{\prime}\subseteq U
, let
D\cap U_{i}=D_{i}
and
D^{\prime}\cap U_{i}=D^{\prime}_{i}
. For any
r_{i}\in R_{i}
, let
r=[r_{1}\ r_{2}\ \ldots\ r_{k}]^{\top}
. Then

\Pr[M(D)=r]=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Pr[M_{i}(D_{i})=r_{i}].

In particular, since
D
and
D^{\prime}
are adjacent datasets, there is exactly one pair in
\{(D_{i},D^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
that are adjacent (i.e., differ in exactly one record), while all the other pairs are equal. Hence,

\displaystyle\Pr[M(D)=r]=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Pr[M_{i}(D_{i})=r_{i}]\leq e^{\epsilon}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Pr[M_{i}(D^{\prime}_{i})=r_{i}]=e^{\epsilon}\cdot\Pr[M(D^{\prime})=r].

Therefore, the parallel composition satisfies
\epsilon
-DP.
∎
