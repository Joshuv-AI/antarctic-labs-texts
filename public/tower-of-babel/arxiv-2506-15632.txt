Heavy Ball and Nesterov Accelerations with Hessian-driven Damping for Nonconvex Optimization
N. Hadjisavvas, F. Lara, R.T. Marcavillaca, P.T. Vuong

Abstract
In this work, we investigate a second-order dynamical system with Hessian-driven damping tailored for a class of nonconvex functions called strongly quasiconvex. Building upon this continuous-time model, we derive two discrete-time gradient-based algorithms through time discretizations. The first is a Heavy Ball method with Hessian correction, incorporating curvature-dependent terms that arise from discretizing the Hessian damping component. The second is a Nesterov-type accelerated method with adaptive momentum, featuring correction terms that account for local curvature. Both algorithms aim to enhance stability and convergence performance, particularly by mitigating oscillations commonly observed in classical momentum methods. Furthermore, in both cases we establish linear convergence to the optimal solution for the iterates and functions values. Our approach highlights the rich interplay between continuous-time dynamics and discrete optimization algorithms in the setting of strongly quasiconvex objectives. Numerical experiments are presented to support obtained results. Keywords : Nonconvex optimization; Dynamical systems; Hessian driven damping; Heavy-ball acceleration; Nesterov acceleration; Linear convergence.



1
Introduction

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function. We are interested in the study of the minimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(x),

(1)

when the function
h
belongs to a class of quasiconvex functions known as
strongly quasiconvex functions

[
41
]
.

Quasiconvex functions play an important role in several areas of applied mathematics such as mathematical programming, mathematical finance, minimax theory and game theory
[
10
,
12
,
20
]
as well as in economic analysis, particularly in production and utility theory
[
9
,
14
]
. The significance of quasiconvexity lies in the natural
tendency to diversification
assumption on the consumers (see
[
14
]
) as well as in their ability to generalize convexity while preserving convex sublevel sets, making them especially suitable for modeling problems in other fields of mathematical applications which involve nonconvex geometries.

Strongly quasiconvex functions, introduced by Polyak in his seminal work
[
41
]
, belong to a class of quasiconvex functions that satisfies several key properties. These include the existence and uniqueness of global minimizers with a quadratic growth condition, gradient dominance, and bounded nonconvexity. Moreover, well-known algorithms such as proximal point and gradient based methods exhibit linear convergence to the global optimal solution for such functions (see
[
17
,
23
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
). Strongly quasiconvex functions include all strongly convex functions and also relevant nonconvex functions such as the square root of the Euclidean norm
[
28
]
, and certain function ratios in which a strongly convex numerator is divided by a positive concave denominator
[
23
]
. Applications of strongly quasiconvex functions arise in diverse areas such as the calculus of variations, where they model energy functionals with controlled nonconvexity
[
19
]
, Bayesian inference
[
43
]
and nonconvex minimax optimization
[
13
]
among others. For a comprehensive overview of the theory and recent developments in this field, we refer the reader to the survey
[
18
]
.

Gradient descent is a computationally efficient method for minimizing differentiable functions. Starting from a point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it follows the iteration:

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\beta_{k}\nabla h(x_{k}),~~\forall~k\geq 0,

(2)

where
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k}
denotes a sequence of step sizes. This method can be viewed as a discretization of the first-order differential equation

\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla h(x(t)).

Despite its simplicity, gradient descent often suffers from slow convergence and oscillatory (“zigzag”) behavior, particularly in ill-conditioned or nonconvex problems. To address these drawbacks, Polyak proposed an accelerated gradient scheme derived from the second-order dynamical system now known as the
Heavy Ball with Friction
(HBF) system
[
40
]
:

\ddot{x}(t)+\alpha\dot{x}(t)+\nabla h(x(t))=0,\quad t>0,\quad\alpha>0,

(3)

where the damping term
\alpha\dot{x}(t)
reduces oscillations and the inertial term
\ddot{x}(t)
accelerates convergence. Its discrete-time counterpart is precisely the well-known Heavy Ball Method given by

x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\alpha_{k}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})-\beta_{k}\nabla h(x^{k}),

(4)

where
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k}
is a momentum parameter sequence controlling the influence of previous iterates.

The Heavy Ball Method has been widely studied in both convex and nonconvex settings (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
16
,
29
]
). Under suitable conditions, HBF can accelerate convergence relative to gradient descent, especially in problems with high curvature or in the presence of saddle points and nonconvexity, common situations in machine learning
[
32
,
33
]
and its applications.

Recently, the study of optimization methods via discretization of dynamical systems has attracted considerable interest. In convex settings, such methods can achieve improved convergence rates and reduced oscillations. In nonconvex settings, HBF may still outperform basic gradient descent, even though it may exhibit oscillatory behavior in poorly conditioned regions. These insights have motivated the development of more refined acceleration techniques, including Nesterov’s method
[
35
]
, which offers faster convergence in the convex case
[
42
]
.

For strongly convex and strongly quasiconvex functions, the HBF method is known to yield exponential convergence of
h(x(t))
to the minimum value
\min_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
, but for general convex functions, the convergence rate of the HBF is limited to
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
in the worst case, matching that of gradient descent. Moreover, oscillations may persist when the function is ill-conditioned or has flat regions.

For dealing with these situations, the authors in
[
3
]
proposed an enhancement of HBF through a Hessian-driven damping term. This leads to the modified system:

\ddot{x}(t)+\alpha\dot{x}(t)+\beta\nabla^{2}h(x(t))\dot{x}(t)+\nabla h(x(t))=0,\quad t>0,

(5)

where the additional term
\nabla^{2}h(x(t))\dot{x}(t)
adapts the damping effect according to the local curvature of the function. This modification effectively suppresses oscillations that typically arise in HBF, especially in ill-conditioned problems such as the Rosenbrock function
[
3
]
. Their results demonstrate improved convergence rates compared to the classical HBF method, making this approach particularly valuable in nonconvex and large-scale optimization.

Motivated by these developments and related works
[
3
,
4
,
29
]
, in this paper we propose to study the evolution of the dynamical system (
5
) in the context of strongly quasiconvex functions. Furthermore, we also develop two discrete-time algorithms derived from tailored discretizations of the continuous model:

•

Heavy Ball Method with Hessian Correction
:

\begin{split}y_{k}&=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(x_{k}-x_{k-1})-\theta_{k}\bigl(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\bigr),\\[2.84526pt]
x_{k+1}&=y_{k}-\beta_{k}\nabla h(x_{k}),\end{split}

(6)

where the correction term approximates the Hessian-driven damping by finite differences.

•

Nesterov-type Method with Adaptive Momentum
:

\begin{split}y_{k}&=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(x_{k}-x_{k-1})-\theta_{k}\bigl(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\bigr),\\[2.84526pt]
x_{k+1}&=y_{k}-\beta_{k}\nabla h(y_{k}),\end{split}

(7)

which extends Nesterov’s method with curvature-aware updates.

Our analysis bridges continuous-time dynamics and discrete optimization algorithms, establishing exponential and linear convergence rates and illustrating how Hessian-based damping mitigates oscillations more effectively than traditional momentum methods. This work advances the understanding of second-order methods in the context of nonconvex functions by showing a class of generalized convex functions for which exponential and linear convergence rates can be obtained and also underscores the potential of combining geometric insights with algorithmic design.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the preliminary definitions and basic results used throughout the paper. In Section
3
, we present a detailed study of the dynamical behavior of second-order system (
5
); we discuss the assumptions required to establish convergence rates and their relationship with strongly quasiconvex functions. Furthermore, in Section
4
, we analyze the convergence rates of the discrete‐time algorithms derived from (
5
), namely the Heavy Ball method with Hessian correction (
6
) and the Nesterov‐type method with adaptive momentum (
7
), and compare these new results with the state of the art on this subject. Moreover, numerical experiments are given in Section
5
in order to illustrate our new contributions. Finally, future research lines are discussed in Section
5
.

2
Preliminaries

The inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the Euclidean norm are denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert
, respectively. The sets
[0,+\infty[
and
]0,+\infty[
are denoted by
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and by
\mathbb{R}_{++}
, respectively. Given any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, the following relation holds:

\displaystyle\lVert\beta x+(1-\beta)y\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\beta\lVert x\rVert^{2}+(1-\beta)\lVert y\rVert^{2}-\beta(1-\beta)\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}.

(8)

Given any extended-valued function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, the effective domain of
h
is defined by
{\rm dom}\,h:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(x)<+\infty\}
. It is said that
h
is proper if
{\rm dom}\,h
is nonempty and
h(x)>-\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The notion of properness is important when dealing with minimization problems.

We denote by
{\rm epi}\,h:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}:h(x)\leq t\}
the epigraph of
h
, by
S_{\lambda}(h):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(x)\leq\lambda\}
the sublevel set of
h
at the height
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and by
{\rm argmin}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
the set of all points of minimum of
h
. A function
h
is lower semicontinuous (lsc henceforth) at
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if for any sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
x_{k}\rightarrow\overline{x}
, we have
h(\overline{x})\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow+\infty}h(x_{k})
. Furthermore, the usual convention
\sup_{\emptyset}h:=-\infty
and
\inf_{\emptyset}h:=+\infty
is adopted.

A proper function
h
with convex domain is said to be:

(a)

convex if, given any
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}\,h
, then

h(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\leq\lambda h(x)+(1-\lambda)h(y),~\forall~\lambda\in[0,1],

(9)

(b)

strongly convex on
{\rm dom}\,h
with modulus
\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[
if for all
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}\,h
and all

\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have

h(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\lambda h(y)+(1-\lambda)h(x)-\lambda(1-\lambda)\frac{\gamma}{2}\lVert x-y\rVert^{2},

(10)

(c)

quasiconvex if, given any
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}\,h
, then

h(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\leq\max\{h(x),h(y)\},~\forall~\lambda\in[0,1],

(11)

(d)

strongly quasiconvex on
{\rm dom}\,h
with modulus

\gamma\in\,]0,+\infty[
if for all
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}\,h
and all

\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have

h(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)\leq\max\{h(y),h(x)\}-\lambda(1-\lambda)\frac{\gamma}{2}\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}.

(12)

It is said that
h
is strictly convex (resp. strictly quasiconvex)
if the inequality in (
9
) (resp. (
11
)) is strict
whenever
x\neq y
and
\lambda\in\,]0,1[
.

The relationship between all these notions is summarized below (we denote quasiconvex by qcx):

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}{\rm strongly~convex}&\Longrightarrow&{\rm strictly~convex}&\Longrightarrow&{\rm convex}\\
\Downarrow&&\Downarrow&&\Downarrow\\
{\rm strongly~qcx}&\Longrightarrow&{\rm strictly~qcx}&\Longrightarrow&{\rm qcx}.\end{array}

None of the reverse implications holds in general. For instance, the Euclidean norm
h_{1}(x)=\lVert x\rVert
is strongly quasiconvex without being strongly convex on any bounded convex set (see
[
25
, Theorem 2]
) and the function
h_{2}(x)=\frac{x}{1+\lvert x\rvert}
is strictly quasiconvex without being strongly quasiconvex on
\mathbb{R}
while the other counterexamples are well-known (see
[
12
,
20
]
).

We recall the following existence result for lsc strongly quasiconvex functions.

Lemma 1
.

(
[
28
, Corollary 3]
)

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed and convex set and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lsc, and
strongly quasiconvex function on
K\subseteq{\rm dom}\,h
with
modulus
\gamma>0
. Then,
{\rm argmin}_{K}h
is a singleton.

Furthermore, if
h
is lsc and strongly quasiconvex with modulus
\gamma>0
on a closed convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the unique minimizer
\overline{x}={\rm argmin}_{K}\,h
satisfies that (see
[
34
, Theorem 5.1]
)

h(\overline{x})+\frac{\gamma}{4}\|x-\overline{x}\|^{2}\leq h(x),~\forall~x\in K.

(13)

Before continuing, let us show some new examples of strongly quasiconvex functions which are not convex.

Remark 2
.

(i)

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be given by
h(x)=\lVert x\rVert^{\alpha}
, with
0<\alpha<1
. Clearly,
h
is nonconvex, but it is strongly quasiconvex on any convex and bounded set by
[
34
, Corollary 3.9]
.

(ii)

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be two symmetric matrices,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the function given by:

h(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\langle Ax,x\rangle+\langle a,x\rangle+\alpha}{\frac{1}{2}\langle Bx,x\rangle+\langle b,x\rangle+\beta}.

(14)

Take
0<m<M
and define:

K:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:~m\leq g(x)\leq M\}.

If
A
is a positive definite matrix and at least one of the following conditions holds:

(a)

B=0
(the null matrix),

(b)

h
is nonnegative on
K
and
B
is negative semidefinite,

(c)

h
is nonpositive on
K
and
B
is positive semidefinite,

then
h
is strongly quasiconvex on
K
with modulus
\gamma=\frac{\lambda_{\min}(A)}{M}
by
[
23
, Corollary 4.1]
, where
\lambda_{\min}(A)
is the minimum eigenvalue of
A
.

(iii)

Let
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a linear operator and
h
a strongly quasiconvex function with modulus
\gamma\geq 0
. Then
f:=h\circ A
is strongly quasiconvex with modulus
\gamma\sigma_{\min}(A)\geq 0
(see
[
18
, Proposition 11
(b)
]
), where
\sigma_{\min}(A)\geq 0
is the minimum singular value of
A
.

(iv)

Let
h_{1},h_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be two strongly quasiconvex functions with modulus
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
, respectively. Then
h:=\max\{h_{1},h_{2}\}
is
strongly quasiconvex with modulus
\gamma:=\min\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}>0
(see
[
44
]
).

A function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
its said to be

L
-smooth on
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
if it is differentiable on

K
and

\lVert\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(y)\rVert\leq L\lVert x-y\rVert,~\forall~x,y\in K.

(15)

For
L
-smooth functions, a fundamental result is the descent lemma

[
36
, Lemma 1.2.3]
, that is, if
h
is a
L
-smooth
function on a convex set
K
with value
L\geq 0
, then for every

x,y\in K
, we have

h(y)\leq h(x)+\langle\nabla h(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}.

(16)

In the strongly quasiconvex case, we have (see
[
24
]
).

Lemma 3
.

(
[
44
, Theorems 1 and 6]
)

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex open set and
h:K\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be differentiable function. Then
h
is strongly
quasiconvex if and only if there exists
\gamma>0
such that for every
x,y\in K
, we have

h(x)\leq h(y)~\Longrightarrow~\langle\nabla h(y),x-y\rangle\leq-\frac{\gamma}{2}\lVert y-x\rVert^{2}.

(17)

Following
[
31
,
39
]
, we also recall that a differentiable function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies the
Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL henceforth) property if there exists
\mu>0
such
that

\lVert\nabla h(x)\rVert^{2}\geq\mu(h(x)-h(\overline{x})),~\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(18)

where
\overline{x}\in{\rm argmin}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
. The (PL)
property is implied by strong convexity, but it allows for multiple minima and does not require any convexity assumption (see
[
26
]
for instance).

Lemma 4
.

(
[
27
, Theorem 2]
)

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a strongly quasiconvex with modulus
\gamma>0
and differentiable with
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient (
L>0
). Then the (PL) property holds with modulus
\mu:=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2L}>0
, that is,

\lVert\nabla h(x)\rVert^{2}\geq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2L}(h(x)-h(\overline{x})),~\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(19)

where
\overline{x}={\rm argmin}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
.

The function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
h(x)=x^{2}+2\sin^{2}x
is an example of a differentiable strongly quasiconvex function (with modulus
\gamma=1/2
) on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying the (PL) property and without being convex.

Now, let us recall the following class of functions (see
[
22
, Definition 1]
). Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function. Then
h
is said to be (strongly) quasar-convex with respect to
\overline{x}\in{\rm argmin}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
if there exists
\kappa\in\,]0,1]
and
\gamma\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle h(\overline{x})\geq h(x)+\frac{1}{\kappa}\langle\nabla h(x),\overline{x}-x\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\overline{x}-x\|^{2},~\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}.

(20)

If
\gamma>0
, we said that
h
is
(\kappa,\gamma)
-strongly quasar-convex while if
\gamma=0
we just said that

is

-quasar-convex.

Quasar-convex functions are a class of generalized convex functions which has been studied deeply during the last years in virtue of its applications in machine learning theory (see

,
22
,

]
and references therein). Furthermore, note that (strongly) quasar-convex functions include (strongly) convex functions and (strongly) star-convex functions (see
[
37
]
).

Finally, we recall that for every
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\|z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{m}\|^{2}\leq m(\|z_{1}\|^{2}+\|z_{2}\|^{2}+\ldots+\|z_{m}\|^{2}).

(21)

For a further study on generalized convexity and strongly quasiconvex functions we refer to

3
Second-Order Continuous Dynamical System with a Hessian-Driven Damping

In this section, our goal is to investigate the problem (
1
) using a dynamical system approach. To that end, we examine the inertial dynamical system with Hessian-driven damping (
5
), which we recall below:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ddot{x}(t)+\alpha\dot{x}(t)+\beta\nabla^{2}h(x(t))\dot{x}(t)+\nabla h(x(t))=0,\,t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
x(0)=x_{0},\quad\dot{x}(0)=v_{0},\end{array}\right.

where
\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
are parameters controlling the damping and the influence of the Hessian term. This system dynamics allows us to analyze the behavior of the solution trajectory in the context of the minimization problem (
1
).

In order to analyze the dynamical behavior of (
3
), we will assume that there exists
\kappa\in\,]0,+\infty[
such that for every trajectory
x(t)
of (
3
) we have

\langle\nabla h(x(t)),x(t)-\overline{x}\rangle\geq\kappa(h(x(t))-h(\overline{x})),

(24)

where
\overline{x}={\rm argmin}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. This assumption holds trivially with
\kappa=1
for convex and strongly convex functions, and can be considered as a generalized convexity assumption. Furthermore, assumption (
24
) was used in
[
11
]
, and later in
[
6
,
7
]
with a stronger requirement that
\kappa\geq 1
, while it coincides with
\kappa
-quasar-convex (see relation (
20
) with
\gamma=0
) when
\kappa\in\,]0,1]
.

We will analyze sufficient conditions for differentiable strongly quasiconvex functions to satisfy relation (
24
) in Subsection
3.2
.

3.1
Exponential Convergence

The following result establishes exponential convergence of every trajectory to the unique solution of the minimization problem for strongly quasiconvex functions.

Theorem 5
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a twice differentiable and strongly quasiconvex function with modulus
\gamma>0
,
\overline{x}={\rm argmin}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
,
h^{*}:=h(\overline{x})
and
x(t)
be a solution of (
3
). Suppose that assumption (
24
) holds. Then for every
\alpha\in\,\left]0,\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(\kappa+2)^{2}}{8\kappa}}\right]
and every

\beta\in\,]0,\frac{\kappa+2}{\kappa\alpha}]
, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{4}\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}\leq h(x(t))-h^{*}\leq\mathcal{C}e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t},

(25)

where
\lambda:=\frac{2\alpha}{\kappa+2}
and

\mathcal{C}:=h(x_{0})-h^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\|\lambda(x_{0}-\overline{x})+v_{0}+\beta\nabla h(x_{0})\|^{2}.

Consequently, the trajectory
x(t)
converges exponentially to the unique solution
\overline{x}
of problem (
1
) and the function values
h(x(t))
converge exponentially to the optimal value
h^{*}
.

Proof.

We define a Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}:[0,+\infty[\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t):=h(x(t))-h^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\|\lambda(x(t)-\overline{x})+\dot{x}(t)+\beta\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}.

(26)

Set

v(t):=\lambda(x(t)-\overline{x})+\dot{x}(t)+\beta\nabla h(x(t)).

Note that

\displaystyle\dot{v}(t)=\lambda\dot{x}(t)+\ddot{x}(t)+\beta\nabla^{2}\,h(x(t))\,\dot{x}(t)=(\lambda-\alpha)\dot{x}(t)-\nabla h(x(t)).

Differentiating the energy function in (
26
) and combining it
with the two above equations, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}(t)=

\displaystyle~\langle\nabla h(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle+\langle v(t),\dot{v}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\langle\nabla h(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle+\langle\lambda(x(t)-\overline{x})+\dot{x}(t)+\beta\nabla h(x(t)),(\lambda-\alpha)\dot{x}(t)-\nabla h(x(t))\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\lambda(\lambda-\alpha)\langle x(t)-\overline{x},\dot{x}(t)\rangle+(\lambda-\alpha)\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}-\beta\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle~+\beta(\lambda-\alpha)\langle\nabla h(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle-\lambda\langle x(t)-\overline{x},\nabla h(x(t))\rangle.

(27)

On the other hand, since for every
t>0
,
h(x(t))\geq h(\overline{x})
, it follows from Lemma
3
and Assumption (
24
) that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla h(x(t)),x(t)-\overline{x}\rangle\geq\frac{\gamma}{4}\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}(h(x(t))-h^{*}).

(28)

Hence, combining (
28
) with (
27
), and then using (
26
), we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}(t)\leq

\displaystyle~\lambda(\lambda-\alpha)\langle x(t)-\overline{x},\dot{x}(t)\rangle+(\lambda-\alpha)\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}-\beta\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta(\lambda-\alpha)\langle\nabla h(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle-\frac{\lambda\gamma}{4}\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}(h(x(t))-h^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\lambda(\lambda-\alpha)\langle x(t)-\overline{x},\dot{x}(t)\rangle+(\lambda-\alpha)\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}-\beta\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta(\lambda-\alpha)\langle\nabla h(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle-\frac{\lambda\,\gamma}{4}\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}(\mathcal{E}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\lVert v(t)\rVert^{2}),

that is,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}(t)+

\displaystyle\,\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}\mathcal{E}(t)\leq\lambda(\lambda-\alpha)\langle x(t)-\overline{x},\dot{x}(t)\rangle+(\lambda-\alpha)\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}-\beta\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta(\lambda-\alpha)\langle\nabla h(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle-\frac{\lambda\,\gamma}{4}\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda\kappa}{4}\lVert v(t)\rVert^{2}.

Replacing
v(t)
in the previous equation, after developing and grouping the similar terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}(t)+

\displaystyle\,\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}\mathcal{E}(t)\leq\left(\lambda(\lambda-\alpha)+\frac{\lambda^{2}\kappa}{2}\right)\langle x(t)-\overline{x},\dot{x}(t)\rangle+\left(\lambda-\alpha+\frac{\lambda\kappa}{4}\right)\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta\left(\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}+\lambda-\alpha\right)\langle\nabla h(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle+\left(\frac{\lambda\kappa\beta^{2}}{4}-\beta\right)\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{\lambda^{3}\kappa}{4}-\frac{\lambda\,\gamma}{4}\right)\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\kappa\beta}{2}\langle\nabla h(x(t)),x(t)-\overline{x}\rangle.

(29)

Now, since

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{2}\kappa\beta}{2}\langle\nabla h(x(t)),x(t)-\overline{x}\rangle\leq\frac{\lambda^{3}\kappa}{4}\lVert x(t)-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\lambda\kappa\beta^{2}}{4}\lVert\nabla h(x(t))\rVert^{2},

we obtain from (
29
)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}(t)+\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}\mathcal{E}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\left(\lambda-\alpha+\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}\right)\langle x(t)-\overline{x},\dot{x}(t)\rangle+\left(\lambda-\alpha+\frac{\lambda\kappa}{4}\right)\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\beta\left(\lambda-\alpha+\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}\right)\langle\nabla h(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\rangle+\beta\left(\frac{\lambda\kappa\beta}{2}-1\right)\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\lambda^{2}\kappa-\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

(30)

Since
\lambda=\frac{2\alpha}{\kappa+2}
, we have that
\lambda-\alpha+\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}=0
, and
\lambda-\alpha+\frac{\lambda\kappa}{4}=-\frac{\kappa\alpha}{2(\kappa+2)}<0
, hence the first three terms of (
30
) are bounded above by zero. Moreover, the assumptions on
\beta
and
\alpha
guarantee that the coefficients of the last two terms are negative, so their sum is also non-positive.

Therefore, from the above analysis we deduce

\dot{\mathcal{E}}(t)+\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}\mathcal{E}(t)\leq 0,

which immediately implies that

\mathcal{E}(t)\leq\mathcal{E}(0)\,e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t}.

(31)

Recalling the definition of
\mathcal{E}
, we deduce

\displaystyle h(x(t))-h(\overline{x})\leq\mathcal{E}(0)e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t},

which corresponds to the second inequality in (
25
). The first inequality follows directly from (
13
).

Finally, it follows from (
25
) that
x(t)
converges exponentially to the unique solution
\overline{x}
, and the function values
h(x(t))
also converge exponentially to the optimal value
h^{*}
.
∎

Remark 6
.

(i)

As far as we know, Theorem
5
is the first second-order differential equation with Hessian driven damping for studying differentiable strongly quasiconvex functions. However, in the particular case when
\alpha=\beta=0
(i.e., without viscous damping and Hessian terms), particular results may be found in
[
38
]
and
[
29
]
.

(ii)

The case where
\beta=0
(i.e., without the Hessian driven-damping term) falls in the Heavy Ball dynamical system considered in
[
29
]
. Our convergence rate obtained here is comparable to that in
[
29
]
on constant factors (see
[
29
, Theorem 23 and Remark 24]
).

As a consequence of Theorem
5
, we have the following convergence rates.

Corollary 7
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5
, the following asymptotic exponential convergence rate, as
t\to+\infty
, holds

\displaystyle h(x(t))-h^{*}=\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t}\right)~~\textit{and}~~\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t}\right).

(32)

Moreover

\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\|\nabla h(x(s))\|^{2}ds\leq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}

\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\|\dot{x}(s)\|^{2}ds\leq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}

\kappa\in(2,+\infty)

e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\|\nabla h(x(s))\|^{2}ds\leq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t}

e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\|\dot{x}(s)\|^{2}ds\leq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t}

\kappa\in[1,2)

e^{-\lambda\kappa t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda\kappa s}\|\nabla h(x(s))\|^{2}ds\leq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t}

e^{-\lambda\kappa t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda\kappa s}\|\dot{x}(s))\|^{2}ds\leq\tilde{\mathcal{C}}e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t}

\kappa\in(0,1)
,

where
\lambda:=\frac{2\alpha}{\kappa+2}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}>0
and
h^{*}=h(\overline{x})
.

Proof.

The assertion of (
32
) follows directly from (
25
).

From (
26
) and (
31
) we have that there exists
\mathcal{C}>0
such that

\|\lambda(x(t)-\overline{x})+\dot{x}(t)+\beta\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}\leq 2\mathcal{C}e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t}.

Developing the left-hand side term in the above inequality, we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}+2\beta\lambda\langle x(t)-\overline{x},\nabla h(x(t))\rangle

\displaystyle+2\langle\dot{x}(t),\beta\nabla h(x(t))+\lambda(x(t)-\overline{x})\rangle\leq 2\mathcal{C}e^{-\frac{\lambda\kappa}{2}t}.

(33)

Moreover, by (
24
) we have

\langle x(t)-\overline{x},\nabla h(x(t))\rangle\geq\kappa(h(x(t))-h(\overline{x})).

Also

\langle\dot{x}(t),\beta\nabla h(x(t))+\lambda(x(t)-\overline{x})\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\Big(\beta(h(x(t))-h(\overline{x}))+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}\Big).

Combining these two relations with (
3.1
) and using
\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda\Big(2\beta\kappa(h(x(t)-h(\overline{x}))+\lambda\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}\Big)+\beta^{2}\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{d}{dt}\Big(2\beta(h(x(t))-h(\overline{x}))+\lambda\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathcal{C}e^{-\frac{\lambda\,\kappa}{2}t}.

(34)

Define

\displaystyle\phi(t):=2\beta(h(x(t))-h(\overline{x}))+\lambda\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

We separate in two cases:

(i) Suppose that
0<\kappa<1
: For this regime, we observe that
\kappa\lambda\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}\leq\lambda\|x(t)-\overline{x}\|^{2}
. Combining this inequality with (
3.1
) and recalling the definition of
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\phi(t)+\kappa\lambda\phi(t)+\beta^{2}\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}\leq 2Ce^{-\frac{\lambda k}{2}t}.

Multiplying by
e^{\kappa\lambda t}
to both sides of the inequality, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(e^{\kappa\lambda t}\phi(t))+\beta^{2}e^{\kappa\lambda t}\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}\leq 2Ce^{\frac{\lambda k}{2}t}.

Integrating, we obtain

\displaystyle e^{\kappa\lambda t}\phi(t)-\phi(0)+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\kappa\lambda s}\|\nabla h(x(s))\|^{2}ds\leq\frac{4C}{\kappa\lambda}(e^{\frac{\lambda k}{2}t}-1).

Since
\phi(t)\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda\kappa t}\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda\kappa s}\|\nabla h(x(s))\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\mathcal{C}}{\kappa\lambda}e^{-\frac{\lambda k}{2}t}+\phi(0)e^{-\kappa\lambda t}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4\mathcal{C}}{\kappa\lambda}+\phi(0)e^{-\frac{\lambda k}{2}t}\right)e^{-\frac{\lambda k}{2}t}.

(ii) Suppose that
\kappa\geq 1
: Note that
\beta(h(x(t)-h(\overline{x}))\leq\kappa\beta(h(x(t)-h(\overline{x}))
. Then (
3.1
) reduces to,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\phi(t)+\lambda\phi(t)+\beta^{2}\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}\leq 2Ce^{-\frac{\lambda k}{2}t}.

Integrating,

e^{\lambda t}\phi(t)-\phi(0)+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\|\nabla h(x(s))\|^{2}ds\leq 2\mathcal{C}\int_{0}^{t}(e^{(1-\frac{\kappa}{2})\lambda s}ds=\frac{2\mathcal{C}}{1-\frac{\kappa}{2}}(e^{(1-\frac{\kappa}{2})\lambda t}-1).

If
\kappa\in[1,2)
, then

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda t}\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\|\nabla h(x(s))\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\mathcal{C}}{2-\kappa}e^{-\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2}t}+\phi(0)e^{-\lambda t}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4\mathcal{C}}{2-\kappa}+\phi(0)e^{-\frac{2-\kappa}{2}\lambda t}\right)e^{-\frac{\kappa\lambda}{2}t}.

If
\kappa>2
, then

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda t}\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\|\nabla h(x(s))\|^{2}ds\leq\left(\frac{4\mathcal{C}}{\kappa-2}+\phi(0)\right)e^{-\lambda t}.

To estimate
\|\dot{x}(t)\|
, we basically follow the same steps as before, but this time we drop the nonnegative term
\beta^{2}\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}
from (
3.1
).
∎

Remark 8
.

The relations in Corollary
7
indicate that
\|\nabla h(x(t))\|^{2}
and
\|\dot{x}(t)\|^{2}
decay to zero at an exponential rate on average. For strongly convex functions, a similar convergence rate was established in
[
4
, Theorem 7]
considering a specific damping term equal to
2\sqrt{\gamma}
, where
\gamma
is the strong convexity parameter. In contrast, Theorem
5
and Corollary
7
allow a free damping term, providing greater flexibility in its selection.

3.2
Sufficient Conditions

Theorem
5
requires differentiable strongly quasiconvex functions which satisfy relation (
24
) (
\kappa>0
), which holds in particular when the function is quasar-convex (
\kappa\in\,]0,1]
).

First, note that if the function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
differentiable with
L
-Lipschitz gradient and strongly quasiconvex with
modulus
\gamma>0
, then it follows from (
17
),
(
16
) and
\nabla h(\overline{x})=0
that

\langle\nabla h(x),x-\overline{x}\rangle\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\lVert x-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}\geq\frac{\gamma}{L}(h(x)-h(\overline{x})),

which implies (
24
) with
\kappa=\frac{\gamma}{L}
.

Second, and as noted in
[
30
]
, differentiable strongly quasi-convex
functions are quasar-convex on bounded convex sets by
[
30
, Theorem 5.2]
,
but this inclusion does not hold on unbounded sets even in one dimension, as
the following example shows.

Example 9
.

Take
h_{1},h_{2}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

h_{2}(x)=x^{2}
and by

h_{1}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}3ex^{2}-2e\left|x\right|^{3},&\mathrm{if}~~\lvert x\rvert<1,\\
e^{n}(1+(e-1)(3(\left|x\right|-n)^{2}-2(\lvert x\rvert-n)^{3})),&n\leq\lvert x\rvert<n+1,~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}.\end{array}\right.

Note that
h_{1}
is even,
h_{1}\left(n\right)=e^{n}
and
h_{1}^{\prime}\left(n\right)=0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h_{1}\left(0\right)=h_{1}^{\prime}\left(0\right)=0
, and
h_{1}
is increasing on

\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Then we define
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows:

h(x)=h_{1}(x)+h_{2}(x).

Since
h_{2}
is strongly convex (in particular, strongly quasiconvex) with
modulus
\gamma_{2}=2
(see (
10
)) and
h_{1}
is nondecreasing on

\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the function
h=h_{1}+h_{2}
is strongly quasiconvex with
modulus
\gamma_{2}=2
. Indeed, let
\left|x\right|\leq\left|y\right|
. Then we have
\left|(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y\right|\leq\left|y\right|
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
and thus

h_{1}((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)=h_{1}\left(\left|(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y\right|\right)\leq h_{1}(\left|y\right|)=h_{1}\left(y\right)
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Hence,

\displaystyle h((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)

\displaystyle=h_{1}((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)+h_{2}((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)

\displaystyle\leq h_{1}(y)+h_{2}(y)-\lambda(1-\lambda)\frac{2}{2}(y-x)^{2}

(35)

\displaystyle=h(y)-\lambda(1-\lambda)(y-x)^{2}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{h(x),h(y)\right\}-\lambda(1-\lambda)(y-x)^{2},~\forall~\lambda\in[0,1],

Therefore,
h
is strongly quasiconvex with modulus
\gamma_{2}=2
.

On the other hand, observe that
h
is not even quasar-convex for any choice
of the parameters
\kappa\in\,]0,1]
and
\gamma\geq 0
(see
[
22
]
).
Indeed, since
h
is differentiable,
h(0)=0
, it follows from
(
20
) that if
h
were (strongly) quasar-convex, we would
have (for
x^{\ast}=0\in\mathrm{argmin}_{\mathbb{R}}\,h
) that

\frac{1}{\kappa}h^{\prime}(x)\geq\frac{h(x)}{x}+\frac{\gamma}{2}x,~~\forall~x>0.

Taking
x=n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
h^{\prime}(n)=h_{1}^{\prime}(n)=2n
and

h(n)=e^{n}+n^{2}>e^{n}
, thus

\frac{1}{\kappa}2\geq\frac{e^{n}}{n^{2}}+\frac{\gamma}{2},~\forall~n\in\mathbb{N},

which is impossible even for
\gamma=0
. Therefore,
h
is not quasar-convex.

A sufficient condition for differentiable strongly quasiconvex functions for being quasar-convex on unbounded domains, i.e., for strongly quasiconvex functions to satisfy relation (
24
), is given below.

Proposition 10
.

Let
K
be a closed and convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
h
be a differentiable strongly quasiconvex function with modulus
\gamma>0
and
\overline{x}
its unique minimizer. If

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\in K,\,\lVert x\rVert\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{h(x)}{\lVert x\rVert^{2}}

\displaystyle<+\infty,

(36)

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\in K,\,\lVert x-\bar{x}\rVert\rightarrow 0}\frac{h(x)-h(\bar{x})}{\lVert x-\bar{x}\rVert^{2}}

\displaystyle<+\infty,

(37)

then there exists
\kappa\in\,]0,1]
such that

\kappa(h(y)-h(\overline{x}))\leq\langle\nabla h(y),y-\overline{x}\rangle,~\forall~y\in K,

(38)

that is,
h
is quasar-convex on
K
with modulus
\kappa\in\,]0,1]
(i.e., satisfies relation (
24
)).

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
h
does not satisfy (
38
). Then for every
\kappa\in\,]0,1]
, there exists
y_{\kappa}\in K
such that

\kappa(h(y_{\kappa})-h(\overline{x}))>\langle\nabla h(y_{\kappa}),y_{\kappa}-\overline{x}\rangle.

For every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
(
\ell\geq 2)
), we take
\kappa=\frac{2}{\ell}\in\,]0,1]
. Then there exists
y_{\ell}\in K
such that

\frac{2}{\ell}(h(y_{\ell})-h(\overline{x}))>\langle\nabla h(y_{\ell}),y_{\ell}-\overline{x}\rangle.

Since
h
is strongly quasiconvex with modulus
\gamma>0
, it follows from
Lemma
3
that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\ell}(h(y_{\ell})-h(\overline{x}))>\langle\nabla h(y_{\ell}),y_{\ell}-\overline{x}\rangle\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\lVert y_{\ell}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\,h(y_{\ell})-h(\overline{x})>\frac{\gamma\ell}{4}\lVert y_{\ell}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\,\frac{h(y_{\ell})-h(\overline{x})}{\lVert y_{\ell}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}}>\frac{\gamma\ell}{4},~\forall~\ell\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\,\lim_{\ell\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{h(y_{\ell})-h(\overline{x})}{\lVert y_{\ell}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}}=+\infty.

(39)

If
\lVert y_{\ell}\rVert\rightarrow+\infty
for some subsequence, then

h(y_{\ell})\rightarrow+\infty
, too, and relation (
39
)
contradicts assumption (
36
). Therefore,
\{y_{\ell}\}_{\ell}

is bounded and there exists a subsequence, that we denote again by
\{y_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
, such that
y_{\ell}\rightarrow\widehat{y}\in K
. Using
(
39
) and since
h
is continuous, we conclude that
\widehat{y}=\overline{x}
. But then (
39
) again contradicts
(
37
). Therefore, (
38
) holds for some

\kappa\in\,]0,1]
.
∎

Corollary 11
.

Let
K
be a closed and convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
h
be a
differentiable strongly quasiconvex function with modulus
\gamma>0
and

\overline{x}\in\mathrm{int}\,K
be its unique minimizer. If
h
is twice
differentiable at
\bar{x}
and (
36
) holds, then
h
is
quasar-convex (i.e., satisfies relation (
24
)).

Proof.

We only need to prove that relation (
37
) holds. Indeed, let

\beta:=\max\left\{\left\langle\nabla^{2}h\left(\bar{x}\right)v,v\right\rangle:\left\|v\right\|=1\right\}.

By Taylor’s formula, using
\nabla h\left(\bar{x}\right)=0
, we obtain for

y
close to
\bar{x}
that

h\left(y\right)-h\left(\bar{x}\right)=\left\langle\nabla^{2}h\left(\bar{x}\right)\left(y-\bar{x}\right),y-\bar{x}\right\rangle+o\left(\left\|y-\bar{x}\right\|^{2}\right)\leq\beta\left\|y-\bar{x}\right\|^{2}+o\left(\left\|y-\bar{x}\right\|^{2}\right)

where

\lim_{y\rightarrow\bar{x}}\frac{o\left(\left\|y-\bar{x}\right\|^{2}\right)}{\left\|y-\bar{x}\right\|^{2}}=0.

Hence,

\limsup_{y\rightarrow\bar{x}}\frac{h\left(y\right)-h\left(\bar{x}\right)}{\left\|y-\bar{x}\right\|^{2}}\leq\beta.

Then, (
37
) holds. Therefore, the result follows by applying
Proposition
10
.
∎

Proposition
10
is not true if assumption (
36
) does not hold (see Example
9
where
h
is twice differentiable at
\bar{x}=0
). It is also not true if the assumption that
h
is twice differentiable at
\bar{x}
does not hold, as the next example shows.

Example 12
.

We define first the function
g(x)=\frac{1-\cos(\pi x)}{2}
,
0\leq x\leq 1
.
This function is differentiable, increasing on
[0,1]
, and has the properties:

g(0)=0
,
g(1)=1
,
g^{\prime}(0)=g^{\prime}(1)=0
. Then, we define
the function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{cc}0&x=0.\\
\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{3}{2}}}-\frac{1}{\left(n+1\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)g\left(n\left(n+1\right)\left|x\right|-n\right)+\frac{1}{(n+1)^{\frac{3}{2}}}&\frac{1}{n+1}<\lvert x\rvert\leq\frac{1}{n},\\
1&1<\lvert x\rvert.\end{array}\right.

Note that
f
has the following properties: it is even, increasing on
[0,+\infty[
thus quasiconvex, differentiable,
f(\frac{1}{n})=\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}
,

f^{\prime}(\frac{1}{n})=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f^{\prime}(0)=0
. Then
we define
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

h(x)=x^{2}+f(x).

The function
h
is differentiable and satisfies (
36
). It
also strongly quasiconvex; indeed, since
f
is quasiconvex and
x^{2}
is
strongly quasiconvex with modulus
2
, for each
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
with, say,

\lvert x\rvert\leq\lvert y\rvert
and
z=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y
, we observe
that
f(z)\leq f(y)
and
z^{2}\leq y^{2}-\lambda(1-\lambda)\lVert y-x\rVert^{2}
, so
h(z)\leq h(y)-\lambda(1-\lambda)\lVert y-x\rVert^{2}
.

However,
h
does not satisfy (
38
) for any choice of
\kappa>0
. In fact, assuming that (
38
) holds for some
\kappa
leads
to

\displaystyle\forall~n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\kappa\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\right)\leq\frac{2}{n}\frac{1}{n}~\Longrightarrow~\kappa\left(1+n^{1/2}\right)\leq 2.

This clearly cannot hold for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore,
h
is not quasar-convex.

4
Hessian Driven Methods

In this section, we prove that temporal discretizations of (
3
)
yield accelerated gradient-type methods, namely the Heavy Ball and Nesterov
acceleration methods, where the exponential convergence of the continuous
system translates into linear convergence (in both cases).

4.1
Heavy Ball Acceleration

Let us consider the Heavy Ball acceleration method with Hessian correction (
6
) and constant step sizes, that is,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}~~~\,y_{k}=x_{k}+\alpha(x_{k}-x_{k-1})-\theta\left(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\right),\\[5.69054pt]
x_{k+1}=y_{k}-\beta\nabla h(x_{k}),\end{array}\right.

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
\theta\geq 0
, and
\beta>0
.

In the following theorem, we provide a linear convergence rate for the sequence generated by (
4.1
) to the optimal solution and the optimal value of problem (
1
).

Theorem 13
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable strongly quasiconvex function with modulus
\gamma>0
with
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient and
\overline{x}\in{\rm argmin}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
. Suppose that

\alpha\in\,\left[0,\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right[,~~\theta\in\,\left[0,\frac{\alpha}{L\sqrt{3}}\right[,~~\beta>\theta(\sqrt{2}-1),~~\theta+\beta\in\,\left]0,\frac{1-2\alpha^{2}}{L}\right].

(42)

Then the sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k}
, generated by the Heavy Ball method
(
4.1
), converges linearly to
\overline{x}
and the sequence
\{h(x_{k})\}_{k}
converges linearly to the optimal value
h^{*}=h(\overline{x})
.

Proof.

First note that (
4.1
) can be-rewriten as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}-x_{k}=\alpha(x_{k}-x_{k-1})-(\theta+\beta)\nabla h(x_{k})+\theta\nabla h(x_{k-1}).

(43)

Hence,

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\,\alpha^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}+(\theta+\beta)^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}+\theta^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle-2\alpha(\theta+\beta)\langle\nabla h(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rangle+2\alpha\theta\langle\nabla h(x_{k-1}),x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rangle

\displaystyle-2\theta(\theta+\beta)\langle\nabla h(x_{k}),\nabla h(x_{k-1})\rangle.

(44)

Since
\nabla h
is Lipschitz continuous, we have

\displaystyle h(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq h(x_{k})+\langle\nabla h(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(45)

Replacing (
43
) and (
4.1
) into
(
45
), we obtain

\displaystyle h(x_{k+1})\leq h(x_{k})+\frac{\alpha^{2}L}{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}-(\theta+\beta)\left(1-\frac{L}{2}(\theta+\beta)\right)\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\theta^{2}L}{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2}+\theta\left(1-L(\theta+\beta)\right)\langle\nabla h(x_{k}),\nabla h(x_{k-1})\rangle

\displaystyle+\alpha\left(1-(\theta+\beta)L\right)\langle\nabla h(x_{k}),x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rangle+L\alpha\theta\langle\nabla h(x_{k-1}),x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rangle.

(46)

Multiplying both sides of the equality (
4.1
) by
\frac{1-(\theta+\beta)L}{2(\theta+\beta)}
and then adding the results with (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle h(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle-h^{*}+\frac{1-L(\theta+\beta)}{2(\theta+\beta)}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\theta+\beta}{2}\lVert\nabla h(x_{k})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq h(x_{k})-h^{*}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2(\theta+\beta)}\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\theta^{2}}{2(\theta+\beta)}\lVert\nabla h(x_{k-1})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha\theta}{\theta+\beta}\langle\nabla h(x_{k-1}),x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rangle.

Since

2\frac{\alpha\theta}{\theta+\beta}\langle\nabla h(x_{k-1}),x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rangle\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\theta+\beta}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{\theta^{2}}{\theta+\beta}\|\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2},

the last inequality becomes

\displaystyle h(x_{k+1})-h^{*}\leq

\displaystyle~h(x_{k})-h^{*}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(\theta+\beta)}\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\theta^{2}}{(\theta+\beta)}\lVert\nabla h(x_{k-1})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1-L(\theta+\beta)}{2(\theta+\beta)}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{2}-\frac{\theta+\beta}{2}\lVert\nabla h(x_{k})\rVert^{2}.

(47)

Defining the energy function

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}:=h(x_{k})-h^{*}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(\theta+\beta)}\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\theta^{2}}{(\theta+\beta)}\lVert\nabla h(x_{k-1})\rVert^{2},

(48)

we can write the last inequality as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq-\left(\frac{\theta+\beta}{2}-\frac{\theta^{2}}{\theta+\beta}\right)\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}-\frac{\left(1-(\theta+\beta)L-2\alpha^{2}\right)}{2(\theta+\beta)}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\rho(\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}+\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}),

(49)

where

\rho:=\min\left\{\frac{\theta+\beta}{2}-\frac{\theta^{2}}{\theta+\beta},\frac{1}{2(\theta+\beta)}\left(1-(\theta+\beta)L-2\alpha^{2}\right)\right\},

(50)

and
\rho>0
, by (
42
).

It remains to bound
\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}+\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}
below by
\mathcal{E}_{k}
. Indeed, it follows from Lemma
4
that

h(x_{k})-h^{*}\leq\frac{2L}{\gamma^{2}}\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(51)

Using (
21
), (
43
) and the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla h
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}+\theta(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1}))+\beta\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+3\theta^{2}L^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}+3\beta^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2},

which implies

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}\leq\frac{3\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{2}-3\theta^{2}L^{2}}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{3\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}-3\theta^{2}L^{2}}\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(52)

Moreover, it follows from (
43
) that

\displaystyle\theta^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}-\alpha(x_{k}-x_{k-1})+(\theta+\beta)\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+3\alpha^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}+3(\theta+\beta)^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2},

which in turn, combined with (
52
) yields

\displaystyle\theta^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle~3\left(1+\frac{3\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{2}-3\theta^{2}L^{2}}\right)\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+3\left((\theta+\beta)^{2}+\frac{3\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}-3\theta^{2}L^{2}}\right)\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(53)

Hence, replacing (
51
), (
52
) and (
53
) into (
48
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle=h(x_{k})-h^{*}+\frac{1}{\theta+\beta}\left(\alpha^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}+\theta^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\theta+\beta}\left(3+\frac{12\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{2}-3\theta^{2}L^{2}}\right)\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~+\left(\frac{2L}{\gamma^{2}}+3(\theta+\beta)+\frac{12\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}}{(\alpha^{2}-3\theta^{2}L^{2})(\theta+\beta)}\right)\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma\left(\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|^{2}+\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\right),

where

\displaystyle\sigma:=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\theta+\beta}\left(3+\frac{12\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{2}-3\theta^{2}L^{2}}\right),\frac{2L}{\gamma^{2}}+3(\theta+\beta)+\frac{12\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}}{(\alpha^{2}-3\theta^{2}L^{2})(\theta+\beta)}\right\},

(54)

and
\sigma>0
by (
42
).

Finally, combining the last inequality with (
49
)
we deduce

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\sigma}\right)\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\sigma}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}_{1},

which means that the sequence
\{\mathcal{E}_{k}\}_{k}
converges linearly to
0
(because
\rho<\sigma
). As a consequence, the sequence
\{h(x_{k})\}_{k}
converge linearly to the optimal value
h^{*}
and the sequences
\{\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|\}_{k}
and
\{\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|\}_{k}
converge linearly to
0
.

Finally, since

\langle\nabla h(x_{k}),x_{k}-\overline{x}\rangle\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\lVert x_{k}-\overline{x}\rVert^{2}~\Longrightarrow~\|x_{k}-\overline{x}\|\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|,

we conclude that
\{x_{k}\}_{k}
converges linearly to
\overline{x}
.
∎

As a direct consequence of the above theorem, we have the following convergence rate result.

Corollary 14

(Convergence rate)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
13
, we have
the following convergence rate

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{4}\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}\leq h(x_{k+1})-h^{*}\leq\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\sigma}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}_{1},

(55)

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\theta+\beta}{\alpha^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\sigma}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}_{1}.

(56)

Moreover, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla h(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\sigma}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\frac{\sqrt{\theta+\beta}}{\theta}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{1}},

where
\mathcal{E}_{1}
,
\rho
and
\sigma
are defined in (
48
), (
50
) and (
54
), respectively.

4.2
Nesterov Acceleration

Let us consider now the Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method with Hessian correction (
7
) and constant step sizes, that is,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}~~~\,y_{k}=x_{k}+\alpha(x_{k}-x_{k-1})-\theta\left(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\right),\\[5.69054pt]
x_{k+1}=y_{k}-\beta\nabla h(y_{k}),\end{array}\right.

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
\theta\geq 0
, and
\beta>0
.

Before presenting the main convergence result, we prove an auxiliary result.

Proposition 16
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function with
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient and strongly quasiconvex with modulus
\gamma>0
,
\overline{x}={\rm argmin}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h
,
\eta>1

and
0<\beta<\frac{\gamma}{\eta L^{2}}
.Then the sequence generated by (
4.2
) satisfies:

\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\mu_{2}\|x_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}\leq\mu_{1}\|y_{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2},~\forall~k\in\mathbb{N},

(59)

where

\displaystyle\mu_{1}:=\frac{1}{1+\beta(\gamma-\eta\beta L^{2})}\in\,]0,1[~~{\rm and}~~\mu_{2}:=\frac{1-\frac{1}{\eta}}{1+\beta(\gamma-\beta\eta L^{2})}>0.

(60)

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\|y_{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}-\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}=\|y_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}+2\langle y_{k}-x_{k+1},x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\|y_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}+2\beta\langle\nabla\,h(y_{k})-\nabla\,h(x_{k+1}),x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\rangle+2\beta\langle\nabla\,h(x_{k+1}),x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\|y_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}-2\beta L\|y_{k}-x_{k+1}\|\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|+\beta\gamma\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|y_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\eta}\|y_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}-\eta(\beta L)^{2}\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\beta\gamma\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{\eta}\right)\|y_{k}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}+\beta\left(\gamma-\eta\beta L^{2}\right)\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}.

(61)

Here, we have used Lemma
3
, the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla\,h
and the property: For all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, it holds
2ab\leq\eta a^{2}+\frac{1}{\eta}b^{2}
for every
\eta>0
.

Then, it follows from (
61
) that

\left(1+\beta\left(\gamma-\eta\beta L^{2}\right)\right)\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{\eta}\right)\|x_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}\leq\|y_{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2},

which implies (
59
).
∎

In the following theorem, we provide a linear convergence rate for the sequence generated by (
4.2
) to the optimal solution and the optimal value of problem (
1
).

Theorem 17
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable with
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient and strongly quasiconvex function with modulus
\gamma>0
and
\overline{x}={\rm argmin}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,h
. Let
\eta>1
,
0<\beta<\frac{\gamma}{\eta L^{2}}
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be defined as in (
60
). Let
\epsilon\in\,\left]0,\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}-1\right[
and
\alpha
,
\theta
be satisfying

\alpha+\theta L\leq\frac{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}(1+\epsilon)}{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}(1+\epsilon)+\mu_{1}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{\epsilon}+\mu_{2}}.

(62)

Then the sequence

\{x_{k}\}_{k}
, generated by (
4.2
), converges linearly to

\overline{x}
and the sequence
\{h(x_{k})\}_{k}
converges linearly to the
optimal value
h^{*}=h(\overline{x})
.

Proof.

The main idea is try to bound
\|x_{k+1}-y_{k}\|
and
\|y_{k}-\overline{x}\|
, then replacing into (
59
) of Proposition
16
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}=\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}-\alpha(x_{k}-x_{k-1})+\theta(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}-2\alpha\langle x_{k+1}-x_{k},x_{k}-x_{k-1}\rangle

\displaystyle+2\theta\langle x_{k+1}-x_{k},\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\rangle-2\alpha\theta\langle x_{k}-x_{k-1},\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\rangle

\displaystyle+\theta^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}-\alpha\left(\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\theta L\left(\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}\right)-\left(\alpha^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}+\theta^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\theta^{2}\|\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1})\|^{2}.

This implies

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}\geq(1-(\alpha+\theta L))\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}-(\alpha+\theta L)\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}.

(63)

On the other hand, by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\|y^{k}-\overline{x}\|

\displaystyle=\|x_{k}+\alpha(x_{k}-x_{k-1})-\theta(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k-1}))-\overline{x}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{k}-\overline{x}\|+(\alpha+\theta L)\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|.

Hence, using Cauchy-Schwartz inequality we can deduce

\displaystyle\|y^{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\|x_{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)(\alpha+\theta L)^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}.

(64)

Combining (
63
) and (
64
) with (
59
), noting that
(\alpha+\theta L)\in[0,1[
we get

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\mu_{2}(1-(\alpha+\theta L))\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}-\mu_{2}(\alpha+\theta L)\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\|x_{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\mu_{1}\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)(\alpha+\theta L)^{2}\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\|x_{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\mu_{1}\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)(\alpha+\theta L)\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\mu_{2}(1-(\alpha+\theta L))\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\|x_{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\left(\mu_{1}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{\epsilon}+\mu_{2}\right)(\alpha+\theta L)\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}.

(65)

Denoting for all
k\geq 1
the Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}:=\|x_{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}+\mu_{2}(1-(\alpha+\theta L))\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}\geq 0,

(66)

we can deduce from the last inequality that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle-\left(\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\mu_{2}(1-(\alpha+\theta L))-\left(\mu_{1}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{\epsilon}+\mu_{2}\right)(\alpha+\theta L)\right)\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}.

Since
\alpha
and
\theta
satisfy condition (
62
)
we have

\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\mu_{2}(1-(\alpha+\theta L))-\left(\mu_{1}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{\epsilon}+\mu_{2}\right)(\alpha+\theta L)\geq 0.

Hence, it follows from the last inequality that

0\leq\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\mathcal{E}_{k}.

Since
\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\in(0,1)
, we conclude that the sequence
\{\mathcal{E}_{k}\}_{k}
converges linearly to
0
with a linear rate
q:=\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\in(0,1)
. As a result, the sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k}
converges linearly to the unique solution
\overline{x}
. The convergence of the value functions is guaranteed by the descent property (
16
).
∎

As a direct consequence of the above theorem, we have the following
convergence rate result for the Algorithm generated by (
4.2
).

Under the assumptions of Theorem
17

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-\overline{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\|x_{k}-x_{k-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)}{\mu_{2}(1-(\alpha+\theta L))}\right)^{k-1}\mathcal{E}_{1}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle h(x_{k})-h^{*}\leq\frac{L}{2}\left(\mu_{1}(1+\epsilon)\right)^{k-1}\mathcal{E}_{1},

(67)

where
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is defined in (
66
) while
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
in (
60
), respectively.

6
Conclusions

We have further advanced the study of strongly quasi-convex optimization by introducing a second-order dynamical system driven by the Hessian. The discretization of this proposed system naturally recovers two classical momentum-based algorithms with Hessian damping: the Heavy ball method and the Nesterov method. The incorporation of Hessian-driven damping not only accelerates convergence but also reduces the oscillations commonly observed in traditional momentum methods. Future research will explore the behavior of the proposed methods in settings beyond strong quasiconvexity.
