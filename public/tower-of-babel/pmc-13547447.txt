Patient Evaluation of Professional Skills of Optometry Students at an Academic Eye Clinic in Johannesburg, South Africa
Authors: Thokozile Ingrid Metsing, Sanele Buthelezi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePatient evaluations of optometry students provide an important real-world perspective on the quality of care, complementing conventional academic assessments. However, many institutions lack formal systems to systematically gather and analyse such feedback on students’ clinical professionalism. This study aimed to assess the professional skills of final-year optometry students as perceived by patients attending an academic clinic in Johannesburg, South Africa.MethodsA cross-sectional quantitative design was employed. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire that measured patients’ perceptions of students’ technical competence, communication skills, professionalism, and overall satisfaction. A purposive sample of 80 patients aged 18–70 years participated, with the sample size determined using Slovin’s formula based on an estimated population of 180 patients. Data analysis was conducted in Microsoft Excel using descriptive statistics and correlation analysis; analyses of demographic subgroups were exploratory, and inferential comparisons by race were not undertaken owing to very small subgroup sizes.ResultsFindings indicated consistently high ratings across all domains. Technical competence was rated as good to excellent by 99% of participants (n=79). Communication was perceived as extremely clear by 77.5% (n=62). Professionalism was also highly rated, with 98% (n=79) describing students as respectful and courteous, and 86% (n=69) noting punctuality. Overall satisfaction was very high, with 98% (n=78) expressing satisfaction and willingness to recommend the clinic. No significant differences (p>0.05) were observed across age or gender groups.ConclusionPatients attending the academic clinic reported high levels of satisfaction with the professional skills of final-year optometry students and were willing and able to evaluate these skills. These findings suggest that structured patient feedback is a feasible complementary source of information on student professionalism in optometry education. Further research is needed to establish how such feedback can be integrated into formal student assessment and whether it improves student learning and patient care.Keywords: patient evaluation, patient feedback, optometry education, patient satisfaction, clinical training, professionalism, communication skills, South Africa, academic clinic, student assessment

Introduction

The assessment of clinical skills in optometry students by patients is increasingly recognised as an important component of comprehensive clinical training evaluation.1 This evaluative approach offers a unique and essential perspective on the quality of care delivered, complementing traditional academic and clinical assessments. Patient feedback provides valuable insights into areas such as communication, professionalism, and the practical application of optometric knowledge — competencies that are vital for the holistic training of future optometrists.2

The global shift towards patient-centred care has increased recognition of patient satisfaction as a meaningful metric for evaluating healthcare quality.2 For optometry students, incorporating professionalism into clinical training is particularly important, as it prepares them for independent practice post-graduation. Understanding patients’ perspectives on care received from student practitioners can help refine educational programmes, improve student performance, and ultimately lead to better patient outcomes.

Professionalism in healthcare encompasses a broad spectrum of attributes, bringing together competence in communication, knowledge, technical skills, clinical reasoning, emotional intelligence, values, and reflective practice, for the benefit of the individual and the community served.3 A foundational principle of professionalism is the promotion of patient safety,4 with specific attributes including effective communication, respect, honesty, confidentiality, and accountability.3

Professional appearance is one dimension of professionalism that influences patient perceptions. In a cross-sectional study examining patients’, medical students’, and physicians’ perceptions of dress codes in Brazil, the use of professional garments — such as laboratory coats and formal attire — was found to be a satisfactory option for healthcare providers.5 The manner in which clinicians present themselves to patients is therefore considered an important element of professional conduct.

The time clinicians spend with patients is essential for providing high-quality, patient-centred care that addresses both medical and emotional needs, contributing to better health outcomes and higher patient satisfaction.6 A prospective survey study demonstrated that time spent with a physician was significantly associated with patient satisfaction.7 Adequate consultation time enables clinicians to build trusting relationships with patients,8 encourage openness about health concerns, and ensure the collection of thorough medical histories prior to diagnosis.

Patient education is another key dimension of clinical professionalism. Clinicians who take time to educate patients about their conditions, treatment options, and preventive measures empower patients to take an active role in their healthcare.9 Evidence suggests that careful examination, accurate diagnosis, and personalised treatment plans lead to better health outcomes and greater patient satisfaction, with effective communication playing a central role in understanding patient needs and preferences.10,11

Infection control practices form another component of professionalism in clinical settings. A study evaluating compliance with hygiene practices among healthcare workers at a tertiary hospital emergency centre in South Africa found that overall hand hygiene compliance was only 34.4%.12 This highlights the importance of hygiene measures in clinical settings, including optometry, where instruments are frequently in close proximity to patients’ eyes.

Professional trust is sustained through the consistent expression of attitudes and behaviours such as integrity, accountability, altruism, empathy, and commitment to lifelong learning.13 Such professionalism is fundamental to bridging the knowledge gap between trained clinicians and the public, ensuring that patients receive safe, ethical, and effective care.

Patient feedback is already well established in the education of other health professionals. In medical education, patient ratings form part of workplace-based assessment and multisource feedback programmes used to evaluate trainees’ communication, professionalism, and interpersonal skills.14 A systematic review of the impact of patient feedback on the performance of qualified doctors found that such feedback can support changes in practice, particularly when it is specific and credible and is accompanied by reflection or facilitated debriefing, although its effects on performance remain variable.15 Comparable approaches have been reported in nursing, dentistry, and allied health education, where patient perspectives complement assessments made by clinical educators.15

By contrast, patient evaluation remains underutilised in optometry education. Existing South African studies have examined patients’ expectations of professionalism in optometry practice2 and employers’ and mentors’ perceptions of graduate optometrists’ practice readiness,16 but few studies have asked patients themselves to evaluate the professional skills of optometry students during supervised clinical training, and none, to the authors’ knowledge, have done so in a South African academic optometry clinic.

The present study extends this body of work by systematically capturing patient evaluations of final-year optometry students’ technical competence, communication, and professionalism in an academic clinic. Beyond describing satisfaction, such feedback has potential educational value: it can reveal strengths and deficits that are not always visible to clinical supervisors, inform curriculum and clinical training priorities, and, if integrated into structured assessment and fed back to learners, may support students’ professional development.14,15

Despite the potential benefits of patient-based assessment of clinical skills, no systematic approach existed at the study institution for collecting and analysing patient feedback on optometry students’ professional competencies. This gap may lead to incomplete evaluation of students’ competencies and missed opportunities for educational enhancement. The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the professional skills of final-year optometry students as perceived by patients at an academic clinic in Johannesburg, South Africa.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a cross-sectional, quantitative design. Cross-sectional studies are observational and collect data from a population at a single point in time.17 This design was appropriate for the study as it enabled efficient data collection and allowed for the assessment of multiple professional skill domains simultaneously. The advantages of a cross-sectional approach include its relative speed and cost, with all variables collected at a single time point.18

Setting

Data were collected at the optometry academic clinic of a university in Johannesburg, South Africa. The clinic provides optometric eye care services to the surrounding communities and sees approximately 60 patients per month across the general, contact lens, and binocular vision clinics. Optometric eye care services are provided by final-year students in training under the supervision of qualified academic clinicians.

Study Population and Sampling Strategy

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants from the optometry clinic. Study participants were patients scheduled for an eye examination at the university clinic, aged 18–70 years, who were literate and able to understand English. Participants of any gender and from any ethnic group were eligible for inclusion. All eligible patients attending the clinic during the three-month data collection period were approached consecutively by the researchers on the day of their appointment and invited to participate. Patients were allocated to student optometrists through the clinic’s routine booking system, and no patients were selected on the basis of the student who examined them.

Based on an estimated clinic population of 180 patients over the data collection period and a precision level of 0.03, the required sample size was calculated as 124. However, 80 participants completed the questionnaire during the three-month data collection period. The shortfall relative to the calculated sample size reflected lower-than-anticipated patient volumes during the data collection period. The achieved sample nevertheless represents approximately 44% of the estimated patient population over this period. The smaller-than-planned sample reduces the precision of the estimates; the findings are therefore reported descriptively and interpreted with caution, and all subgroup analyses are treated as exploratory (see Limitations).

Student Optometrists Evaluated

During the data collection period, 52 final-year student optometrists provided care across the general, contact lens, and binocular vision clinics; participating patients were distributed across these settings as follows: general clinic n=48, contact lens clinic n=18, binocular vision clinic n=14. Because patients were recruited consecutively across all clinics, most students on clinical rotation during this period were evaluated by at least one patient, although the number of evaluations per student was not fixed. Students were aware that a patient satisfaction study was being conducted in the clinic; however, they did not know which of their patients completed the questionnaire, and completed questionnaires were anonymous and could not be linked to individual students. The evaluation was therefore not fully blinded, and the possibility that students modified their behaviour in the knowledge that feedback was being collected cannot be excluded.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 18–70 years, of any gender, and able to read and understand English. Participants were excluded if they were younger than 18 or older than 70 years, had a disability that precluded questionnaire completion (including visual or hearing impairment), or were illiterate. These exclusion criteria were informed by the self-administered nature of the written English questionnaire and the absence, during the study period, of validated translated versions or of trained personnel to assist with completion. The exclusion of patients with visual impairment or limited literacy, who frequently access optometric services and whose perspectives are valuable, is acknowledged as an important limitation of this study; reasonable accommodations, such as having the questionnaire read aloud by trained personnel or caregivers, were not implemented in this study and are recommended for future work (see Limitations and Implications and Recommendations).

Data Collection

A structured questionnaire (Annexure A) was used to collect data. The questionnaire comprised four sections assessing: (1) technical competence; (2) communication skills; (3) professionalism; and (4) overall patient satisfaction. An open-ended section for additional comments was also included.

Questionnaires were administered by researchers immediately after each participant’s eye examination. No personal identifying information was collected; code names were assigned to each participant. Participants completed the questionnaire privately in an unoccupied cubicle within the clinic and deposited completed questionnaires in an anonymous box at the reception desk. Data collection took place over a three-month period.

Questionnaire Development, Reliability and Validity

The questionnaire was developed by the research team on the basis of the professional skill domains identified in the literature and was reviewed by the supervising clinicians, who are experienced academic clinicians, for face and content validity. Reliability refers to the consistency with which a measurement procedure assigns the same score to individuals with equal values.19 The structured questionnaire used standardised response options across all items to ensure consistency of measurement. Validity refers to the degree to which a study accurately measures the concept it is intended to measure.20 Content validity was assessed by the supervising clinicians, who reviewed all questionnaire items to ensure they were appropriate and representative of the professional skill domains under investigation. Because the questionnaire was completed by patients with varying levels of educational attainment, items were phrased in plain, non-technical language.

No formal pilot testing with patients was undertaken, and no additional psychometric validation (for example, internal consistency testing) was performed; this is acknowledged as a limitation of the study. Both internal and external validity were considered in the study design; the cross-sectional approach allowed for a representative snapshot of patient perceptions, although generalisability beyond this clinic setting may be limited.

Data Analysis

Quantitative data was captured and processed using Microsoft Excel. Data were summarised using descriptive statistics, including measures of central tendency (mean, median, and mode) and measures of dispersion (standard deviation and range). Age was recorded and analysed as a continuous variable; for descriptive reporting, participants were additionally categorised into two groups (18–32 years and 33–70 years). Correlation coefficients were calculated to explore associations between demographic variables (age and gender) and ratings across the professional skill domains. Statistical significance was assessed using p-values, with p>0.05 indicating no statistically significant difference. Statistical comparisons by race were not undertaken because of the very small sizes of some racial subgroups (n = 1–3 of 80); the racial composition of the sample is reported descriptively only. All correlation analyses were exploratory, and the small magnitude of the observed coefficients (|r| < 0.30) indicates weak associations.

Questionnaire items used four-point response scales. For summary reporting, the two most favourable response categories (eg, “good” and “excellent”) were combined to represent overall positive perceptions for items with responses strongly concentrated in these categories; full response distributions are reported for items with a greater spread of responses. Combining these categories did not alter the pattern or interpretation of the findings, and full response distributions for all items are available from the corresponding author on request.

Responses to the open-ended section were analysed using descriptive content analysis: responses were read repeatedly, coded, and grouped into categories reflecting commendations and suggestions for improvement, which are summarised narratively in the Results.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Faculty of Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee at the University of Johannesburg (REC-3053-2024). Permission to conduct the study was also obtained from the departmental research committee, the clinic manager, and the Head of the Department of Optometry prior to commencement of data collection. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). Participants were provided with a written information letter and a verbal explanation of the study before providing written informed consent. Participants were informed of the voluntary nature of participation and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. All data were maintained anonymously using participant code names. Collected data were stored in a password-protected document accessible only to the researchers and will be archived for 5 years in accordance with institutional policies before secure destruction.

Results

Participant Demographics

Descriptive statistics were calculated from data collected from 80 patients attending the academic optometry clinic in Johannesburg. The mean age of participants was 34.58 ± 16.87 years, with ages ranging from 18 to 70 years (Table 1). The age distribution was right-skewed (skewness = 0.86), reflecting a higher proportion of younger participants. A platykurtic distribution (kurtosis = −0.74) was observed, indicating fewer extreme age values relative to a normal distribution. The majority of participants (61%, n=49) were between 18 and 32 years old, while the remaining 39% were older than 32 years.

Descriptive Statistics of Participant Age

The sample comprised 67.5% (n=54) females and 32.5% (n=26) males. The ethnic distribution was predominantly African descent, accounting for 92.5% (n=74) of participants, followed by White and Indian participants at 3.75% (n=3) each, and Coloured participants at 1.25% (n=1). The majority of participants (88.75%, n=71) attended the clinic for a routine eye examination, 8.75% (n=7) for a follow-up appointment, and 2.5% (n=2) sought treatment. In accordance with the analysis plan, inferential analyses by race were not conducted owing to the very small sizes of some racial subgroups (n = 1–3); the results reported below therefore relate to age and gender only.

Technical Competence

The questionnaire assessed students’ professional skills with a focus on thoroughness, confidence, accuracy, and diagnostic precision. Most participants (99%, n=79) rated the technical skills competence of final-year students in clinical training as good to excellent, with only 1% (n=1) rating their skills as poor. Similarly, 98% (n=78) of participants rated the thoroughness and accuracy of visual examination tests as good to excellent, while 2% (n=2) rated them as poor. Furthermore, 98% (n=78) of participants expressed confidence in the students’ ability to diagnose and explain their visual conditions.

No statistically significant difference (p>0.05) was found in how different age groups rated the quality of professional services, the accuracy of testing, and the precision of diagnosis and treatment by students in training. Correlations between age and these ratings were uniformly weak (r = −0.01, −0.14, and −0.13), as were correlations between gender and ratings of technical skills, the students’ ability to diagnose and explain visual conditions, and the accuracy of the visual examination tests (r = −0.07, −0.18, and 0.05). These exploratory associations were small in magnitude and should not be over-interpreted.

Communication Skills

Clarity of Explanation

Participants assessed optometry students’ ability to listen and communicate clearly. Of the 80 individuals who completed the questionnaire, 5% (n=4) rated the clarity of explanations as not clear, 17.5% (n=14) as clear, and 77.5% (n=62) as extremely clear.

No statistically significant difference was found between the age groups (p>0.05). Correlations between age and ratings of students’ communication were weak (r = −0.08 and −0.03), as were those between gender and ratings of students’ listening abilities (r = 0.20) and the clarity of explanations (r = −0.10).

Based on assessments across different genders and age groups regarding the use of professional language and the respect and courtesy shown to participants, no statistically significant differences were found (p>0.05). The correlation between age and ratings of the use of professional language was weak (r = 0.18) and not statistically significant.

Listening Skills

Of the 80 participants, 92.5% (n=74) believed that the students paid attention to their problems, 5% (n=4) indicated they were usually listened to, and 2.5% (n=2) disagreed that they were listened to effectively.

Professional Language

The majority of participants, 96% (n=77), rated the professional language used by optometry students in clinical training as always to usually used. Only 4% (n=3) of participants indicated that professional language was sometimes used.

Professionalism

Respect and Courtesy

The professionalism of optometry students was assessed across three dimensions: respect and courtesy, empathy and compassion, and punctuality. The majority of participants, 98% (n=79), rated students as respectful and courteous to very respectful and courteous, while 2% (n=1) rated them as somewhat respectful and courteous. Correlations between ratings of respect and courtesy and age (r = −0.11) and gender (r = −0.27) were weak and not statistically significant (p>0.05).

Punctuality

Regarding punctuality, 86% (n=69) of participants rated their examiner as always punctual, 9% (n=7) as usually punctual, 4% (n=3) as sometimes punctual, and 1% (n=1) as never punctual.

Empathy and Compassion

Of the 80 participants, 71% (n=57) rated their examiner as very empathetic and compassionate, 26% (n=21) as empathetic and compassionate, and 3% (n=2) as not empathetic and compassionate.

Based on the evaluations of punctuality, empathy, and compassion by participants of different ages and genders, no statistically significant differences were observed (p-value >0.05). Correlations were uniformly weak for punctuality (age: r = 0.15; gender: r = −0.04) and for empathy and compassion (age: r = −0.05; gender: r = −0.25).

Overall Experience

Participants’ overall experiences at the clinic were assessed in terms of satisfaction with care received and likelihood of recommending the clinic to others. The majority of participants (98%, n=78) reported satisfaction ratings of very satisfied to satisfied. One participant (1%) gave a neutral rating, and one participant (1%) expressed dissatisfaction.

The majority of participants (98%, n=78) indicated that they would definitely or probably recommend the clinic to others. Only 2% (n=2) indicated they would not recommend the services to others.

Regarding age and gender differences in overall experience and recommendation ratings, no statistically significant differences were found (p>0.05). Correlations between overall experience and recommendation ratings and age (r = −0.08 and −0.04) and gender (r = −0.11 and −0.24) were weak.

Open-Ended Responses

Content analysis of the open-ended responses identified three categories: overall commendations, dissatisfaction, and suggestions for improvement. Thirty-two participants expressed being very pleased with the services received and rated them as excellent. One participant was dissatisfied with the examiner’s communication and punctuality. Positive feedback highlighted students’ respect, kindness, and empathy. Suggestions from participants included consulting patients before making decisions on their behalf, extending payment options for unemployed individuals, and focusing on presenting symptoms to adequately address patients’ primary concerns.

Discussion

Key Findings

The findings of this study indicate that the majority of patients who attended the Optometry academic clinic in Johannesburg were highly satisfied with the professional skills demonstrated by final-year optometry students across all evaluated domains. High ratings were recorded for technical competence, communication clarity, professionalism, and overall patient experience. No statistically significant differences (p>0.05) were found across age or gender groups for any domain assessed, suggesting a consistently high standard of student performance perceived across the patient population; comparisons by race were not performed owing to very small subgroup sizes.

Discussion of Key Findings

Accuracy and Thoroughness

In the current study, 98% (n=78) of participants rated the thoroughness and accuracy of visual examination tests as good to excellent. This contrasts with Putter et al’s study,16 in which employers and mentors rated accuracy at 28.9%. This difference may be attributed to differences in the evaluators’ perspectives: the Putter et al16 sample consisted of experienced mentors and employers with high professional expectations of newly qualified optometrists, whereas the current study’s participants were patients without formal optometric knowledge. In addition, the present study assessed thoroughness and accuracy as a single combined item, whereas Putter et al reported accuracy in isolation; the two findings are therefore not directly comparable. The difference in sample size between the two studies (n=80 vs n=28) may also affect the precision of the findings.

Communication and Clarity of Explanation

In the current study, 95% of participants (n=76) rated students’ explanations as clear to extremely clear. This finding compares well with the study by Putter, van Staden, and Munsamy,16 which reported that 64.3% of their participants (n=28) rated communication as clear, suggesting similar positive perceptions of optometry student communication across different study contexts. Direct comparison should nevertheless be made cautiously, because the two studies used different data collection instruments and response scales and sampled different respondent groups. The higher percentage observed in the current study may reflect differences in sample characteristics, including participants’ educational backgrounds and professional expectations (patients in the present study versus employers and mentors of graduates).

Listening Skills

In the current study, 92.5% (n=74) of participants reported being listened to by student optometrists. This finding aligns with that reported in a previous study of nursing care,21 in which 66.7% (n=10) of patients reported that nurses listened to them. While the settings and professional groups differ, both studies support the importance of active listening in health professions and suggest that optometry students demonstrated satisfactory listening skills.

Respect and Courtesy

In the current study, 98% (n=79) of participants reported receiving courteous and respectful treatment from student optometrists. This is broadly similar to the findings of Sajjad, Rao, and Naveed (2024),22 in which 304 participants (76%) reported receiving dignified and respectful treatment in an ophthalmology setting. The high levels of respect and courtesy reported across both studies are encouraging. However, these behaviours cannot be attributed solely to professional training: respect and courtesy are also cultural norms shaped by upbringing and previous life experiences outside health professions education, and the present study cannot separate the contribution of training from these broader influences. The supervised academic clinic context, in which professional standards are actively monitored, may nevertheless have reinforced these behaviours.

Punctuality

In the current study, 86% of participants (n=69) reported that the examiner was always punctual. A related cross-sectional study conducted in Kenya found that 75.3% (n=709) of participants expressed satisfaction with waiting times.23 Punctuality and waiting time are related but distinct constructs — a practitioner may be present and punctual while patients nevertheless wait for their appointments — so these findings are complementary rather than directly comparable. Taken together, they suggest that timely service contributes to patient satisfaction in clinical settings.

Empathy and Compassion

The current study found that 97% (n=78) of participants rated their examiner as compassionate and empathetic. This contrasts with findings from Putter et al,16 in which only 42.9% of respondents agreed that compassionate, patient-centred care was provided. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the evaluators’ perspectives and the sample characteristics. The referenced study16 utilised experienced mentors and employers with specific professional expectations, whereas the present study relied on patient self-reports. Additionally, the smaller sample size in the comparison study (n=28) may have influenced the precision of its findings.

Overall Experience

In the current study, 98% (n=78) of participants were satisfied with the care provided. This aligns with findings from Yancy et al, who reported that 90% of participants were satisfied with their examiners — resident and attending physicians — in ambulatory care clinics.24 The high satisfaction rates observed in both studies support the view that patient satisfaction with student and trainee clinicians can be high when training environments are well-structured and supervised.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is its practical, clinic-based approach to capturing patient perspectives on optometry student professionalism, which provided real-world, contextually relevant feedback. The use of a structured questionnaire with standardised items ensured consistency across assessments. For summary reporting, the two most favourable response categories were combined for items with highly concentrated response distributions, while full distributions were reported for items with a greater spread; this reporting choice should be borne in mind when comparing across items.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. The achieved sample of 80 participants fell short of the calculated target of 124 and is insufficient to allow firm generalisations about the proficiency of optometry students as a whole. The sample was also demographically imbalanced: the ethnic distribution was predominantly African (92.5%), with some racial subgroups represented by only one to three participants — which precluded meaningful statistical comparison by race — and females comprised the majority of participants. The exclusion of patients with visual impairment or limited literacy, and of patients older than 70 years, limits both the representativeness of the sample and the applicability of the findings, since these groups frequently access optometric services; no accommodations, such as assisted questionnaire completion, were made in this study. The questionnaire itself was not pilot-tested with patients and relied largely on closed-response options, limiting the depth of feedback on individual professional attributes, and combining favourable response categories for some items reduces granularity.

Not all dimensions of professionalism were evaluated; for example, hygiene compliance and professional attire were not assessed. Because students knew that patient feedback was being collected in the clinic, the evaluation was not fully blinded, and students may have adjusted their behaviour accordingly. Participants completed the questionnaire after an examination lasting up to 90 minutes and may have experienced fatigue. Finally, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to social desirability, exaggeration, or recall bias; differences in patients’ cultural backgrounds, personal values, and prior healthcare experiences may have influenced responses, and language barriers may have affected some participants’ understanding of questionnaire items despite the English literacy inclusion criterion.

Implications and Recommendations

The findings of this study demonstrate that patients attending an academic optometry clinic are willing and able to provide structured feedback on students’ professional skills, and that such feedback can be collected feasibly within routine clinic operations. High satisfaction alone, however, does not establish patient feedback as an assessment tool. Before patient feedback can be formally integrated into student assessment, further work is needed to determine how feedback should be communicated to individual students, how it influences their reflection and clinical development, and how supervisors can incorporate it into training — for example, through structured feedback reports, facilitated debriefing, or alignment with competency frameworks such as entrustable professional activities.14,15,25 In the present study, results were not fed back to individual students during training; recommendations regarding the educational use of patient feedback are therefore presented as directions for future research and practice rather than as demonstrated outcomes.

Despite the predominantly positive findings, areas for improvement were identified. Supervisors and educators are encouraged to continue emphasising professional skills — particularly communication and punctuality — as part of annual clinical training programmes to maintain and improve service quality. Attention to patient-centred consultation practices, such as involving patients in decisions about their care, should be reinforced. Participants also raised the accessibility of payment for economically vulnerable patients as a relevant service delivery concern. Future studies should recruit larger and more diverse samples, include reasonable accommodations (such as having the questionnaire read aloud by trained personnel or caregivers) to enable the participation of patients with visual impairment or limited literacy, and evaluate whether structured patient feedback, when returned to students, improves learning and patient care.

Conclusion

The majority of patients attending the optometry academic clinic in Johannesburg reported high levels of satisfaction with the professional skills of final-year optometry students across all evaluated domains, including technical competence, communication, professionalism, and overall experience. No statistically significant differences in satisfaction ratings were found across age or gender groups. This study demonstrates that patients are willing and able to evaluate aspects of student professionalism in an academic optometry setting. It did not, however, evaluate whether patient feedback improves student learning, clinical performance, or patient care; the use of patient feedback as a structured component of student assessment is therefore proposed as a direction for future research rather than an established conclusion. Future studies should include larger and more diverse samples, make reasonable accommodations to include patients with visual impairment or limited literacy, and examine how patient feedback can be communicated to students and integrated into clinical training.
