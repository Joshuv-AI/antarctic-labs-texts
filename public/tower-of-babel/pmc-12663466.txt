Neuronal activity-induced GLUT3 plasma translocation supports energy demands for memory acquisition
Authors: Xin-Yue Wei, Ze-Ming Zou, Zhong-Xiao Yao, Shuai-Wen Teng, Xin-Yu Wei, Wen-Jie Tang, Xiao-Lin Chen, Yun-Zhang He, Zhe-Yu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glucose transporter 3 (GLUT3) is crucial for glucose uptake in neurons and rapidly translocates to the plasma membrane in response to neural activity. However, the precise molecular mechanisms and physiological roles of this translocation remain elusive, hindering our understanding of how glucose metabolism supports brain function. This study found that PKCε phosphorylates Thr232 and Ser246 of GLUT3 upon neuronal activation, enhancing its binding to KLC1 and promoting GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion. To investigate the function of GLUT3 plasma translocation, we developed a peptide, TAT-GLUT3(2D), which disrupts GLUT3-KLC1 binding and blocks activity-dependent translocation of GLUT3. By utilizing TAT-GLUT3(2D), we showed that blockage of activity-induced GLUT3 neuronal surface translocation leads to decreased glucose uptake and ATP production, impairing memory acquisition without affecting memory consolidation or retrieval in mice. Our results suggest that PKCε-mediated phosphorylation of GLUT3 is a key regulator of neuronal activity-induced GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion and memory acquisition, advancing our understanding of the energy supply needed for memory acquisition.

Introduction

The central nervous system is thought to consume 20% of the body’s energy, with glucose as its primary fuel source. Evolutionary studies suggest that the emergence of increased cognitive functions in humans is closely associated with increased glucose utilization devoted to neural processing1,2. How the brain responds to the metabolic challenge of activation has been controversial for many decades. A model known as the astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle (ANLS) suggests that lactate released by astrocytes serves as fuel for neighboring neurons3. Proponents of the ANLS have argued that neurons preferentially use this astrocyte-produced lactate rather than glucose, which is supported mostly by experiments conducted under cell culture conditions4. However, in vivo measurements using near-infrared 2-deoxyglucose analogs and metabolic NADH biosensors revealed that brain activity increases direct glucose consumption by neurons, providing a rapid response to increased energy demand2,5. However, the relative role of rapid glucose uptake in response to neuronal activity in cognitive functions remains unknown.

Glucose transporter 3 (GLUT3) is the canonical glucose transporter in neurons and is widely distributed in brain areas such as the neocortex, striatum, hippocampus, and cerebellum6,7. GLUT3 contains 12 transmembrane helices with intracellular N- and C-terminal ends8. Decreased GLUT3 expression is a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease9. CaMK2-mediated GLUT3 deletion in the adult male limbic system led to impaired spatial memory and motor abilities10. A previous study revealed that synaptic activity can increase the surface expression of GLUT3, elevating the intracellular glucose, which is mediated by the activation of cGMP-dependent protein kinase11. Moreover, AMPK activation and its regulation of cell surface GLUT3 expression are critical in mediating neuronal tolerance to excitotoxicity12. Thus, surface GLUT3 plasma translocation might represent a novel pathway for controlling the energy supply during neuronal activity. However, the detailed molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying neuronal activity-induced GLUT3 surface translocation remain unclear, hampering investigations into the functional significance of increased neuronal glucose uptake.

To address these questions, we employed activity-dependent neuronal surface GLUT3 measurements and in vivo glucose and ATP biosensor analyses combined with learning and memory behavior paradigms. We identified the key structural motif responsible for neuronal activity-dependent GLUT3 surface translocation, which is critical for glucose uptake and energy supply during memory acquisition.

Results

Ratiometric fluorescence assay for monitoring GLUT3 recruitment to the cell surface

We initially investigated GLUT3 subcellular distribution through immunofluorescence analysis in cultured rat hippocampal neurons, which revealed widespread GLUT3 distribution throughout the soma and dendrites with additional co-localization with the Golgi, endosomes, and postsynaptic sites (Supplementary Fig. 1A), consistent with previous observations13. Then we employed a surface ratiometric fluorescence assay described in our previous study to quantify plasma membrane GLUT3 levels14,15. We constructed an HA-GLUT3-GFP construct containing an HA epitope inserted into the first extracellular loop (extracellular) and a GFP tag fused to the C-terminal (intracellular) of GLUT3. By staining with an anti-HA antibody under nonpermeabilized conditions, surface GLUT3 was quantified as the 594/488 fluorescence intensity ratio (Fig. 1A). Considering the low transfection efficiency of neurons, PC12 cells are commonly used as a model for neurons. In subsequent experiments, we typically undertook large-scale screening in PC12 cells, with key results subsequently validated in neurons. We stained transfected PC12 cells under both permeabilized and nonpermeabilized conditions and observed complete colocalization of 594 nm and 488 nm fluorescence under permeabilized conditions. In contrast, only colocalization at the plasma membrane in the nonpermeabilized group supports the feasibility of our strategy (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Using this system, we monitored HA-GLUT3-GFP plasma membrane insertion at different time points following KCl stimulation in PC12 cells. We detected that GLUT3 rapidly entered the membrane, with a plateau phase beginning at approximately 30 minutes, with a t1/2 of approximately 11.4 minu6tes (Fig. 1B). GLUT1 is expressed at the highest levels in the endothelium of barrier tissues and is also present in astrocytes but not in neurons7,16. We firstly transfected PC12 cells with HA-GLUT3-GFP or HA-GLUT1-GFP constructs and observed that KCl stimulation increased plasma membrane GLUT3 (~1.75-fold), whereas GLUT1 levels remained constant (Supplementary Fig. 1C), and consistent results were obtained in cultured neurons (~2.47-fold) (Fig. 1C). Therefore, we utilized GLUT1 to serve as a negative control in following experiments. Dual-color immunofluorescence was also performed to examine the GLUT3 neuronal surface translocation at axon and postsynaptic regions. Enhanced HA signal was observed at postsynaptic sites (marked by PSD95 positivity), but no significant increase was detected in axonal compartments (marked by SMI312 positivity; p = 0.0950) (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C), consistent with previous observations17. Quantification of GLUT3 translocation in somatic regions also revealed a significant KCl-induced increase in plasma membrane-localized GLUT3 (Supplementary Fig. 2C). These results indicated neuronal activity induced GLUT3 plasma translocation preferential to the soma and dendritic areas.

To exclude effects of exogenous GLUT3 overexpression on trafficking, we also verified activity-dependent GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion in cultured hippocampal neurons via surface biotinylation. Neuronal depolarization induced by KCl led to a notable increase in PM GLUT3. In contrast, no alterations in total GLUT3 protein levels or GLUT3 gene transcription were detected (Fig. 1D-1F), aligning with previous studies11,18.

CFC training induces hippocampus GLUT3 plasma translocation

Then we used the contextual fear conditioning (CFC) paradigm to explore whether neuronal activity upregulates GLUT3 plasma membrane translocation during in vivo learning. Mice were euthanized at intervals post-CFC training (5 min, 15 min, 30 min, 1, 2, and 4 h), and dorsal hippocampus (DH) samples were collected, as DH is crucial and highly active during CFC19. The plasma membrane (PM)-to-total GLUT3 ratio peaked at 30 min and returned to naive levels at 2 h relative to untrained controls. Training had no significant effects on total GLUT3 expression (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, plasma membrane GLUT3 levels in the DH of mice subjected only to environmental cues without shock (C group) did not differ from the naive group, with no variance observed in the total GLUT3 protein levels across the groups (Fig. 2B). 232-256 aa of GLUT3 is necessary and sufficient for its activity-dependent plasma membrane insertion

Thereafter, we returned to the in vitro model with the objective of designing tools blocking activity-dependent GLUT3 translocation specifically to investigate its role in memory. To dissect the minimal GLUT3 domain responsible for its activity-dependent plasma translocation, we searched for the intracellular GLUT3 domains most divergent from GLUT1. The N-terminal, loop 6, and C-terminal regions of GLUT3 emerged as candidates. We subsequently engineered GLUT3-GLUT1 chimeric proteins with swapped domains to avoid unstable or misfolded mutations for further analysis (Fig. 3A). Following KCl stimulation, we observed that the substitution of loop 6 from GLUT1 to GLUT3 (GLUT3 (loop 6/GLUT1)) abolished the activity-dependent plasma membrane insertion. Replacing the N-terminal and C-terminal regions had no effect, indicating that loop 6 is essential for the activity-dependent plasma membrane insertion of GLUT3 (Fig. 3B, C). To validate the sufficiency, we replaced the GLUT1 N-terminal, loop 6, and C-terminal domains with their respective GLUT3 domains. Phosphorylation of GLUT3 at Thr232 and Ser246 regulate its activity-dependent plasma membrane insertion

Phosphorylation is essential for the plasma membrane trafficking of AMPARs and NMDARs20–22, and S-palmitoylation facilitates GLUT1 plasma trafficking in glioblastoma (GBM)23. We hypothesized that GLUT3 residues 232–256 modulate its activity-dependent plasma translocation, potentially via posttranslational modifications. To test this hypothesis, we first identified threonine 232 (Thr232), serine 246 (Ser246), and serine 250 (Ser250) as potential candidate sites for phosphorylation using mass spectrometry databases and online prediction tools, which are highly conserved across species (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Owing to the lack of specific antibodies, we examined GLUT3 phosphorylation through immunoprecipitation (IP) of exogenous GLUT3-Flag, followed by immunoblotting (IB) with a pan phospho-serine/threonine antibody. The results revealed a marked increase in serine and threonine phosphorylation of GLUT3 upon KCl stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 3B). To identify phosphorylation sites critical for GLUT3 neuronal activity-dependent plasma translocation, we constructed nonphosphorylation mutants: GLUT3-T232A, GLUT3-S246A, and GLUT3-S250A. GLUT3-T232A and GLUT3-S246A failed to exhibit plasma translocation upon KCl stimulation, whereas GLUT3-S250A retained the capacity to respond to KCl (Supplementary Fig. 3C). We subsequently constructed phosphomimetic mutants: GLUT3-T232D and GLUT3-S246D. Contrary to our expectation that phosphorylation mimetic mutants would simulate such plasma membrane insertion in the absence of stimulation, GLUT3-T232D and GLUT3-S246D single-site mutants exhibited behavior similar to wild-type GLUT3 under basal conditions and after KCl stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Thus, we speculated that these two sites might function coordinately, leading us to construct a dual phosphorylation mutant GLUT3-TS/2D. Under basal conditions, TS/2D mimicked neuronal activity-dependent plasma membrane insertion, yet no further increase was observed following KCl stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Finally, we validated this phenomenon in cultured neurons by transfecting GLUT3-TS/2 A or GLUT3-TS/2D, yielding concordant results with those observed in PC12 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3E). These data suggest a cooperative role for Thr232 and Ser246 in the modulation of GLUT3 neuronal activity-dependent plasma membrane insertion.

PKCε phosphorylates GLUT3 and regulates its plasma translocation

We next aimed to identify potential kinases for GLUT3 T232 and S246. Based on these results, we performed a GST pulldown assay coupled with mass spectrometry. Specifically, we engineered a fusion protein consisting of the 232–256 aa fragment of GLUT3 linked to GST, designated GST-GLUT3232-256, and a mutant version with nonphosphorylated T232 and S246, GST-GLUT3232-256(TS/2A). These recombinant proteins and the GST control were incubated with hippocampal tissue extracted 30 min after CFC. The proteins were subsequently subjected to a GST pull-down assay and mass spectrometry analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Comparative analysis between the GST-GLUT3232-256 group and the other groups revealed that several proteins are potentially involved in the modulation of GLUT3 plasma translocation (Supplementary Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table 1).

Both GLUT3 T232 and S246 were predicted as PKC phosphorylation sites using NetPhos24,25. PKCε, which is detected uniquely in the GST-GLUT3232-256 group by mass spectrometry, may phosphorylate these sites (Supplementary Table 1). Thus, we investigated time-dependent changes in PKCε phosphorylation after CFC, which revealed synchronous elevation of PKCε phosphorylation and GLUT3 membrane expression following CFC training, suggesting potential functional coordination between these events (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Subsequently, we compared phosphorylation of PKCε between the context (C) and CFC (CS) groups at 30 min. Elevated PKCε phosphorylation was observed exclusively in the CS group (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Finally, in vitro analyses also revealed increased PKCε phosphorylation upon KCl stimulation in cultured neurons (Supplementary Fig. 4E), which suggests PKCε activation in response to neuronal activity and consistent with previous reports26,27.

To validate the interaction between GLUT3 and PKCε, we overexpressed GLUT3-Flag, together with the PKCε-HA or PKCα-HA proteins, in HEK293 cells. CO-IP revealed that GLUT3 interacted with PKCε but not with PKCα (Supplementary Fig. 5A). We subsequently investigated whether Thr232 and Ser246 of GLUT3 are the phosphorylation sites for PKCε. PKCε-HA and GLUT3-Flag, along with their respective GLUT3 Thr232, Ser246 or Thr232 and Ser246 dual-nonphosphorylated mutations (GLUT3-TS/2 A), were overexpressed in HEK293 cells. GLUT3 phosphorylation was assessed via HA-IP followed by IB with a pan phospho-serine/threonine-antibody. The results indicated that PKCε could phosphorylate wild-type GLUT3, whereas the corresponding phosphorylation levels were reduced in GLUT3 T232 A, GLUT3 S246A, and GLUT3 TS/2A mutants (Fig. 5A). Additionally, we adopted an in vitro phosphorylation assay to assess the phosphorylation of bacterially expressed GST-GLUT3232-256 and GST-GLUT3232-256(TS/2A) by recombinant PKCε. We subsequently investigated whether PKCε is involved in neuronal activity-dependent GLUT3 plasma translocation. To this end, we transiently transfected HA-GLUT3-GFP into PC12 cells, applied the pan-PKC inhibitor chelerythrine chloride, and found that it could abolish the KCl-induced membrane insertion of GLUT3. According to the previous report, Gö 6850 could distinguish the functions of PKC isoforms via dose response: it inhibits all PKCs at 25 μM; whereas at 2 μM it inhibits novel and classical isozymes; and at 0.2 μM it inhibits only classical PKCs28. Using Gö 6850, we found that both 25 μM and 2 μM Gö 6850, but not 0.2 μM Gö 6850, could abrogate KCl-induced GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion, suggesting that nPKCs are involved in the modulation of GLUT3 neuronal activity-dependent plasma translocation, whereas cPKCs and aPKCs are not involved. Finally, the PKCε-selective inhibitor ε-V1-2 abrogated KCl-induced GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Conversely, we applied the PKCε-selective agonist DCP-LA, which induced GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion at the same level as KCl does (Supplementary Fig. 5C). In parallel, we co-expressed PKCα or PKCε with GLUT3 in PC12 cells and observed that overexpression of PKCε rather than PKCα could mimic GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Finally, we employed DCP-LA and ε-V1-2 to validate our findings in hippocampal neurons, yielding concordant outcomes with those observed in PC12 cells (Fig. 5C). Thus, our findings suggest that PKCε mediates GLUT3 neuronal plasma membrane insertion by phosphorylating GLUT3 at Thr232 and Ser246.

Phosphorylation of GLUT3 at T232 and S246 residues enhances its binding with KLC1

The mechanism underlying phosphorylation-dependent GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion is still unclear. Our GST pull-down-MS data revealed that the 232–256 aa of GLUT3 binds to various cytoskeletal and motor proteins, which is lost in the nonphosphorylated mutant (Supplementary Table 1). Kinesin light chain 1 (KLC1) is highly abundant among these proteins. Kinesin superfamily proteins (KIFs) participate in the intracellular trafficking and cytoplasmic transport of cellular components along the microtubule network. The kinesin light chain is primarily responsible for binding to the transported cargo proteins29,30. Therefore, we speculated that GLUT3 increases its binding to KLC1 through phosphorylation, thus facilitating GLUT3 trafficking to the plasma membrane. CO-IP experiments in HEK293 cells overexpressing GLUT3-HA and KLC1-Myc confirmed their interaction in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 6A). CO-IP experiments with GLUT3-specific antibodies revealed that GLUT3 and KLC1 interact in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 6B). We subsequently cotransfected GLUT3 mutants, including GLUT3-HA, GLUT3-TS/2A-HA or GLUT3-TS/2D-HA, umalong with KLC1-Myc in HEK293 cells. CO-IP and subsequent WB revealed that KLC1 did not bind to GLUT3-TS/2 A but exhibited enhanced binding to GLUT3-TS/2D (Fig. 5D). Assessment of the GLUT3–KLC1 interaction following KCl stimulation further revealed an activity-dependent increase in binding between GLUT3 and KLC1 in both neuronal cultures and PC12 cells (Fig. 5E). These results suggest that the phosphorylation of GLUT3 at Thr232 and Ser246 promotes its binding to KLC1.

TAT-GLUT3(2D) interference with training-induced hippocampus GLUT3 plasma translocation

Based on the previous results, we engineered a fusion protein, via the amino acid residues 232–256 of GLUT3, whose Thr232 and Ser246 sites were mutated to aspartic acid (TS/2D), combined with a biotin-binding domain and a cell-penetrating peptide (TAT) containing a series of arginine-rich sequences (TAT, GRTRRRRRRRRRR) (named TAT-GLUT3(2D)). Concurrently, an identical scramble sequence with amino acid conservation was fused with biotin, and the TAT peptide served as a control (named TAT-Ctr). We postulate that this peptide could competitively bind with KLC1, thereby interfering with the KLC1-GLUT3 interaction and neuronal activity-regulated GLUT3 membrane insertion. To test our hypothesis, we first evaluated the influence of TAT-Ctr and TAT-GLUT3(2D) on the protein interaction between GLUT3 and KLC1. HEK293 cells overexpressing GLUT3-HA and KLC1-Myc were treated with TAT-Ctr or TAT-GLUT3(2D) at a concentration of 5 μM for 30 minutes, followed by CO-IP analysis. TAT-GLUT3(2D) attenuated the interaction between GLUT3 and KLC1, whereas TAT-Ctr had no significant effect (Fig. 6A). Next, we pretreated cultured neurons overexpressing HA-GLUT3-GFP with TAT-Ctr or TAT-GLUT3(2D) for 30 min, followed by 50 mM KCl. IF statistical analysis revealed that TAT-GLUT3(2D) markedly abrogated KCl-induced GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion compared with TAT-Ctr (Fig. 6B). We further examined the impact of TAT-GLUT3(2D) on endogenous GLUT3 plasma translocation in neurons. Primary hippocampal neurons were pretreated with TAT-Ctr or TAT-GLUT3(2D), followed by stimulation with 50 mM KCl. Plasma membrane-associated proteins were isolated via a surface biotinylation assay and detected via WB. Compared with TAT-Ctr, TAT-GLUT3(2D) abolished endogenous GLUT3 neuronal activity-dependent plasma membrane insertion without altering the overall protein level of GLUT3 (Fig. 6C). TAT-GLUT3(2D) treatment failed to reduce surface GLUT3 levels under unstimulated conditions, despite observed GLUT3-KLC1 binding in this state. Besides, mass spectrometry result revealed persistent binding of the mutant GLUT3(233-256 TS/2A), a negative control simulating unstimulated-state GLUT3, to multiple motor proteins (myosins, dyneins, kinesins) in control group, indicating complicated regulation of constitutive GLUT3 trafficking pathways. We therefore propose that although the involvement of KLC1 in GLUT3 plasma localization under unstimulated states cannot be excluded, basal GLUT3 cell surface localization appears primarily governed by transport mechanisms other than of KLC1, thus exhibiting minimal susceptibility to TAT-GLUT3(2D) perturbation. Next, we evaluated the in vivo effectiveness of the TAT-GLUT3(2D) peptide. Constrained by the diffusion range of the peptide, we chose dentate gyrus (DG) as our target region31,32, and ensuring TAT peptide cellular uptake with streptavidin-Alexa Fluor 488 in the DG region of the mice (Fig. 6D). The mice received TAT-GLUT3(2D) or TAT-Ctr microinjection followed by CFC. At 30 min post-CFC, mice were euthanized for DH lysate coimmunoprecipitation (CO-IP) with anti-GLUT3 or anti-IgG antibodies. The results revealed an increased association of GLUT3 with KLC1 in the DH following CFC training. TAT-GLUT3(2D) injection also attenuated this increase (Fig. 6E). In alignment with this observation, TAT-GLUT3(2D) also abrogated the increased GLUT3 surface levels on DH membranes following CFC training compared with TAT-Ctr injection (Fig. 6F). These findings indicate that TAT-GLUT3(2D) could block CFC-induced GLUT3 plasma translocation in DH by competitively interfering with the interaction between KLC1 and GLUT3.

The NPR task was subsequently performed 10 min after training. Notably, TAT-GLUT3(2D)-injected mice presented a significant loss of preference for novel place compared with TAT-Ctr-injected mice (Fig. 7C). The CFC relies critically upon both the DG and the basolateral amygdala (BLA), whereas auditory fear conditioning (AFC) depends on the auditory cortex and amygdala35,36. When injected before FC training, TAT-GLUT3(2D) significantly reduced the freezing time in the CFC test but did not affect freezing in the AFC test compared to TAT-Ctr groups (Fig. 7D). These results suggest that TAT-GLUT3(2D) disrupted hippocampus-dependent memory acquisition. We subsequently investigated the role of TAT-GLUT3(2D) in different memory processes (including consolidation and retrieval) by altering the timing of microinjection. Immediate injection of TAT-GLUT3(2D) after fear conditioning did not affect CFC or AFC performance in the 24 h memory test (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, injecting TAT-GLUT3(2D) 30 min before the test did not affect the retrieval of CFC or AFC memories (Fig. 7F). Finally, to assess whether TAT-GLUT3(2D) disrupts hippocampal-dependent spatial learning, mice received daily injections of TAT-Ctr or TAT-GLUT3(2D) prior to water maze training (7 days; fixed-platform acquisition), spatial memory was then assessed in a probe test 1 day following post-training with the platform removed from the pool (Fig. 7G). TAT-GLUT3(2D)-treated mice exhibited impaired spatial memory, manifesting as prolonged escape latencies during acquisition, reduced target quadrant occupancy, decreased platform crossings, and increased latency to first crossing in the probe test compared to the TAT-Ctr group (Fig. 7H, I). Collectively, these findings indicate that GLUT3 neuronal activity-dependent insertion into the plasma membrane is necessary for hippocampus-dependent memory acquisition but not for consolidation and retrieval.

Neuronal energy supply deficit resulting from blocking activity-dependent GLUT3 plasma translocation

Given that GLUT3 predominantly facilitates glucose uptake into neurons, we investigated the potential of TAT-GLUT3(2D) to impede this process, consequently affecting memory acquisition. Initially, we employed 2-NBDG to visualize glucose uptake in cultured neurons upon KCl stimulation. KCl stimulation increased neuronal glucose uptake, whereas TAT-GLUT3(2D) attenuated this KCl-induced glucose uptake (Fig. 8A). We subsequently transduced the hippocampus with a genetically encoded glucose sensor pAAV.hSynap.(cyto)iGlucoSnFR-mRuby2, which detects intracellular glucose in living animals37. A guide cannula equipped with an optical fiber was then implanted to visualize the impact of our peptide on glucose uptake during CFC training in vivo. Previous reports have indicated that activity enhances neuronal glucose uptake, as evidenced by labeled 2-DG5,33; however, our findings revealed no effect of CFC training on neuronal intracellular glucose levels. We attributed this to the accumulation of 2-DG within cells, as it cannot be metabolized via glycolysis38, whereas iGlucoSnFR captures glucose fluctuations without disrupting its metabolic consumption during stimulation39. This response remained unaltered following TAT-Ctr administration, but the intraneuronal glucose concentration decreased after shock in the TAT-GLUT3(2D) group as the GLUT3 plasma translocation failed (Fig. 8B, C). Additionally, neither TAT-Ctr nor TAT-GLUT3(2D) affected basal glucose levels in hippocampal neurons. The oxidative metabolism of glucose is the primary source of ATP production in the brain. First, we measured ATP levels in cultured neurons and observed a modest, nonsignificant decrease in ATP levels following 30 min of KCl treatment, which was exacerbated by TAT-GLUT3(2D) treatment (Fig. 8D). To monitor neuronal ATP levels during CFC training in vivo, we expressed the genetically encoded ATP sensor AAV-hSyn-Cyto-mRuby3-iATPSnFR1.0, which allows imaging of cytosolic ATP in the hippocampus40. Only minor fluctuations in neuronal ATP levels were observed during shock trials postinjection of TAT-Ctr. We hypothesize that this stability in ATP levels is attributable to a balance between ATP synthesis and metabolic demands since metabolically intact neurons are quite robust to electrical activity with respect to the maintenance of ATP levels41,42. In contrast, TAT-GLUT3(2D) application significantly decreased ATP levels after shock, as it blocked glucose uptake and compromised ATP synthesis (Fig. 8E, F).

Direct glucose uptake, as the primary energy substrate for neuronal activity, remains a subject of debate. An alternative metabolic pathway—the lactate shuttle—has also been demonstrated to affect neuron firing. Still, the functional relationship and distinct contributions of these two types of energy supply remain uncertain1,3. In the brain, monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) facilitate the release of lactate from astrocytes and its subsequent uptake by neurons43. Therefore, we injected 4-cin, an MCT inhibitor, and monitored ATP levels during CFC training. No effects of 4-cin on ATP levels upon shock were observed, suggesting that hippocampal neurons predominantly adopt direct glucose uptake as an ATP source during memory encoding (Supplementary Fig. 8A). Administration of 4-cin 30 min before CFC training impaired long-term memory but did not affect short-term memory (Supplementary Fig. 8B), consistent with previous reports33,44–46. These findings corroborate the notion that neurons rely on elicited direct neuronal glucose uptake rather than lactate shuttling to satisfy their energetic demands during memory formation. In contrast, the lactate shuttle appears more pivotal than glucose uptake during memory consolidation, complementing the original brain energy metabolism theory.

Enhanced CFC memory acquisition via acute exercise-induced GLUT3 plasma Translocation

Having established that disruption of neuronal activity-induced GLUT3 plasma translocation leads to an energy supply deficit and impairs CFC memory, we next explored whether increased GLUT3 translocation enhances learning and memory. Exercise is known to ameliorate cognitive performance in both human and murine subjects47–50; however, the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. We hypothesized that exercise-induced GLUT3 plasma translocation may be a contributing factor. To test this, we subjected mice to treadmill training and assessed the impact of varying durations of acute exercise on memory. To prevent ceiling effects, we adopted a 0.4 mA electric shock51. Our findings indicate that a 20-minute duration was maximally beneficial. Discussion

Our data reveal a new and important principle of metabolic regulation in activated neurons, which rely on translocating the GLUT3 glucose transporter to the plasma membrane for energy supply. GLUT3 is the most widely expressed glucose transporter in neurons and is essential for the high energy demand of neurons54,55. Here, we demonstrate that PKCε mediates the neuronal activity-induced insertion of GLUT3 into the plasma membrane by phosphorylating GLUT3 and thus enhancing its interaction with KLC1. This neuronal activity-dependent GLUT3 plasma translocation is essential for increased energy needs during neuronal activation and memory acquisition.

Our results provide several new insights into the mechanisms and significance of neuronal activity-enhanced GLUT3 surface recruitment. Membrane protein expression is tightly regulated by a series of molecular biological processes, which can be broadly categorized into controlling intracellular protein insertion rates into the membrane and regulating endocytosis and recycling rates56. Our study primarily elucidates the molecular mechanisms of GLUT3 vesicle insertion into the membrane from within the cell without focusing on the contribution of neuronal activity to regulating GLUT3 endocytosis. We identified the structural motif responsible for the activity-induced GLUT3 neuronal plasma translocation. We found that KCl-induced GLUT3 surface recruitment is a rapid insertion process with a t1/2 of ~11 minutes. This agrees with the view that increased neuronal activity requires a readily available energy substrate in the form of glucose. Using a chimeric receptor swapping strategy, we found that the 232–256 aa segment in loop 6 of the GLUT3 intracellular domain, which has the lowest homology with GLUT1, is necessary and sufficient for neuronal activity-driven GLUT3 surface recruitment. The domain-swap technique has been demonstrated to be successful in identifying the translocation domain of GLUT4, the most extensively studied glucose transporter that targets the plasma membrane57,58. GLUT4 exhibits preferential expression in muscle and adipose tissues while GLUT3 is highly expressed in the nervous system while GLUT3 is highly expressed in the nervous system. In adipocytes, insulin stimulates GLUT4 trafficking to the plasma membrane via PI3K-Akt/PKC signaling cascades. This process is coordinated by two distinct structural motifs: the N-terminal FQQI motif regulates cycling between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and retention vesicles, while the C-terminal TELEY motif governs trafficking between the ER and specialized GLUT4 storage vesicles (GSVs)57. Additionally, the C-terminal FQQI and dileucine (LL) motifs collectively mediate intracellular GLUT4 retention57. In skeletal muscle cells, chloroquine stimulation activating the PI3K and PKC pathways, or exercise-induced depolarization elevating cytosolic Ca²⁺, promotes GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane59,60. In neurons, synaptic activity triggers AMP kinase (AMPK) activation, which mediates GLUT4 insertion into the axonal plasma membrane17. We conducted a sequence alignment of Loop 6 across all major class I GLUT isoforms, which revealed that GLUT4 and GLUT3 exhibit the lowest pairwise sequence identity within this domain. Besides, C terminal of GLUT4 and GLUT3 share low identity as well. These jointly suggest that GLUT4 and GLUT3 may employ distinct structural domains to respond to divergent regulatory signals for their membrane trafficking. In addition, we found that the phosphorylation of Thr232 and Ser246 in this domain is critical for neuronal activity-dependent GLUT3 plasma membrane insertion. The phosphorylation of AMPARs and NMDA receptors has been reported to play a crucial role in their neuronal activity-triggered surface insertion61,62. A previous study reported that synaptic NMDAR stimulation induces the cGMP/cGK-dependent signaling pathway that facilitates the translocation of GLUT3 to the plasma membrane11. However, the kinase that directly phosphorylates GLUT3 and mediates its plasma translocation upon neuronal activity is still not known. The identified domain and phosphorylation sites opened a venue for screening the key interacting molecules involved in activity-driven GLUT3 surface recruitment.

Second, we demonstrated that PKCε can phosphorylate GLUT3 at Thr232 and Ser246, regulating neuronal activity-dependent plasma translocation. The PKC family broadly regulates the surface distribution of AMPARs and NMDARs63,64. Moreover, PKCε is reportedly involved in the modulation of pendrin translocation65 and the promotion of GLUT4 translocation to the cell surface in fibroblasts66 and is implicated in vesicle delivery and focal exocytosis67. Using in vivo GST pull-down experiments and mass spectrometric analysis, we determined that PKCε rather than other PKC family members could interact with GLUT3. An in vitro phosphorylation assay further confirmed that PKCε can phosphorylate GLUT3 at Thr232 and Ser246. PKCε immunoreactivity and mRNA are concentrated in dentate granule cells and CA3 pyramidal cells, and PKCε activation is an essential component of long-term potentiation (LTP) in CA368,69. PKCε is associated with functions such as nociceptive sensitization70–72, neurogenesis73, and motor learning63. Inhibition or deletion of PKCε affects memory acquisition and extinction26,74, but the precise downstream targets remain obscure. Here, we found that PKCε can phosphorylate GLUT3, facilitating its plasma translocation. We further revealed that PKCε-dependent GLUT3 phosphorylation could enhance its interaction with KLC1. Kinesin-1 is the founding member of a superfamily of motor proteins that transport macromolecules along microtubules in an ATP-dependent manner. Kinesin-1 has been reported to be involved in neuronal surface trafficking of NMDA and GABA(A) receptors75,76and the axonal transport of cannabinoid type-1 and TrkB receptors77–79. Using the TAT peptide to block the interaction between GLUT3 and KLC1 abolished the neuronal activity-induced GLUT3 surface recruitment, suggesting that this process is mediated by kinesin-1 along microtubules. Additional mechanisms may also regulate the increased binding of cargo proteins to kinesin-1. Posttranslational modifications of KLC1 itself or adapter proteins such as JIP3 and JIP1 from the JNK-interacting protein (JIP) family also facilitate cargo binding to KLC180–82. Our mass spectrometry data also revealed a high abundance of JIP3 in the GLUT3232-256 group, implicating JIP3 as a potential adapter protein in the phosphorylation-mediated increase in GLUT3–KLC1 binding. Further studies are needed to identify additional effector proteins and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms involved in activity-dependent GLUT3 plasma translocation. Whether the PKCε-enhanced interaction between GLUT3 and KLC1 can also facilitate GLUT3 axonal transport upon neuronal activation also needs further investigation.

Third, we demonstrated that specifically blocking KLC1-mediated GLUT3 cell surface insertion might blunt the energy supply of neurons and impair hippocampus-dependent memory acquisition. We found that KLC1-mediated GLUT3 neuronal surface recruitment is essential for increased glucose uptake during neuronal activity both in vitro and in vivo, which is expected to match energetic needs through a mechanism involving PKCε. TAT-GLUT3(2D) microinjection attenuated the increase in GLUT3 phosphorylation levels and the enhanced interaction between GLUT3 and KLC1 upon CFC training, leading to defects in novel place recognition and contextual fear memory acquisition rather than in consolidation and retrieval. Given the critical role of glucose in brain function and energy supply, deficiency of GLUT3 in adult glutamatergic excitatory neurons leads to spatial memory deficits but heightened exploratory and social responses33,83. Neuronal activity-driven ATP synthesis is required for synaptic function41, which can be fueled by direct glucose uptake and glycolysis or by lactate uptake from glia via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle2,84. Two-photon imaging has shown that whisker stimulation in the barrel cortex increases glucose uptake in both astrocytes and neurons5,85, complicating this issue. Using metabolomics, it has been recently reported that neurons metabolize glucose to supply ATP through glycolysis in vivo39. Furthermore, action potential (AP) firing triggers GLUT4 insertion into the axonal plasma membrane, suggesting enhanced glucose uptake in neurons17. Our data, integrating behavioral and in vivo recordings, suggest that neuronal activity-driven GLUT3 surface recruitment provides a mechanism for improving neuronal glycolysis and the energy supply.

Interestingly, we found that blocking activity-driven GLUT3 neuronal surface recruitment impaired hippocampus-dependent memory acquisition but not consolidation and retrieval. It has been reported that the astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle is required for long-term memory formation but not short-term memory, as tested 1 h after training44. Together with our results, these findings suggest that astrocyte-to-neuron lactate shuttling may supply energy during memory consolidation but not acquisition. In contrast, a transient increase in neuronal glycolysis plays an essential role in memory acquisition.

Finally, we found that acute exercise could increase GLUT3 plasma translocation and improve memory acquisition. Accumulating evidence suggests that regular physical exercise may promote synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus and enhance learning and memory86,87. Acute physical exercise can increase the levels of endocannabinoids and BDNF in the hippocampus, facilitating long-term potentiation88,89. In addition, our data suggested that acute exercise could improve memory acquisition via increased GLUT3 plasma translocation and aerobic glycolysis. The levels of GLUT3 and its translocation are decreased in Alzheimer’s disease, and glucose hypometabolism and deficits in ATP production are observed9,90. The PKCε-specific activator DCP-LA effectively prevents synaptic loss and cognitive deficits in aged rats and 5XFAD transgenic mice91,92. Together, these data suggest that exercise provides a simple and practical approach to improve neuronal glucose metabolism and enhance memory.

In summary, the present study revealed that PKCε and kinesin-1 play essential roles in modulating neuronal activity-driven GLUT3 plasma translocation and promoting energy supply and hippocampal memory function. Additionally, these findings shed light on new molecular mechanisms of neuronal activity-dependent GLUT3 cell surface recruitment and its relevant contribution to cognition. Future investigations could translate these findings into a therapeutic approach for memory disorders.

Materials and Methods

Animals

We adopted male C57BL/6 mice, 8 weeks old and weighing 23–25 g, in all the experiments. Mice were housed at 22 °C ± 2 °C and on a 12 h light/dark cycle condition. Food and water were available freely. All animal procedures were conducted on the basis of the National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of Shandong University.

Neuronal cultures and transfection

Primary hippocampal cultures were prepared from timed pregnant Sprague Dawley rats as described previously93. Briefly, embryos were removed from rats at 18 d of gestation and then were placed into Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) without Ca2+ and Mg2+. Hippocampal tissues were dissociated and digested with 0.05% trypsin–EDTA for 5 min at 37 °C and isolated into mono cells by mechanical isolation with a sterile, fire-polished glass Pasteur pipette in DMEM/F-12 (Invitrogen) plus 10% fetal bovine serum. Six-well plates,10 cm dishes or coverslips were coated with 0.1 mg/ml poly-D-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich) ahead. Neurons were counted via cell counter (Halo counter HD-4 CellCounter, Hiscore Inc) and seeded suitable containers according to experiment design in Neurobasal medium (Invitrogen) which was supplemented with 2% B27 and 0.5 mm glutamine, and an incubator at 37 °C, with 5% CO2, and 95% humidity was used for the culture.

At 5–7 d in vitro (DIV), cultured hippocampal neurons were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (1–2 μl per well) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). After transfection, experiments were conducted at 9 DIV.

RNA extraction and Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cultured hippocampal neurons using RNApure Tissue&Cell Kit (CWBio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The first strand of cDNA was synthesized from 100 ng of total RNA (10 min at 70 °C followed by 2 min at 0 °C) using a high-capacity RNA to cDNA reverse transcription system (Vazyme). 2 ul of the resulting cDNA was amplified by quantitative PCR (qPCR) with an CFX Connect real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad). The PCR mix was composed of 2 ul of cDNA, 1 ul of forward and reverse primers, 25 μL of 2× UltraSYBR Mix (CWBio) and add DDW up to 50 ul.

Forward and revers sequences were as follows: β-actin (actin) (NM_007393): 5′-GCTTCTTTGCAGCTCCTTCGT-3′ and 5′-ATATCGTCATCCATGGCGAAC-3′; Slc2a3 (GLUT3) (NM_001401): 5′-CAGCCGCTTCTCATCTCCAT -3′ and 5′-CCTGCTCCAATCGTGGCATA -3′.

Plasma membrane separation and Western blot analysis

Cultured neurons’ membrane surface protein was separated by surface biotinylation as previously described15. Briefly, hippocampal neurons with or without 50 mM KCl stimulation were washed three times with ice-cold PBS buffer (pH 7.4, with 0.1 mM Ca2+ and 1 mM Mg2+), and then incubated in sulfo-NHS-biotin (0.3 mg/ml in cold PBS; Pierce) for 1 h at 4 °C to biotinylate surface proteins at the end of stimulation. Then the cells were washed twice for 10 min with ice-cold Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1 mM Ca2+, pH 7.4 to quench and remove unreacted biotin. Cells were then lysed in TNE buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% NP-40 with protease and phosphatase inhibitors). Then samples were centrifuged (14,000 × g for 15 min) and the supernatant was incubated with 30 µl of streptavidin-conjugated Sepharose beads (Pierce) overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. The beads were washed three times in TNE Buffer. Biotinylated proteins were eluted from beads by boiling in 4× SDS sample buffer (Invitrogen), and eluted proteins were analyzed by Western blot.

The hippocampus PM fraction was extracted by aqueous two-phase system kit (Bio vision) following manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, half of dorsal hippocampal was homogenized with 2 ml homogenization buffer, and subsequently centrifuged (7000 × g for 10 min). The supernatant was collected, and a portion was retained as a total protein sample. Next the supernatant was further centrifuged (10,000 × g for 30 min), after which the pellet, containing plasma membrane and organelle membrane proteins, was harvested. Then the PM fraction was separated from total membrane protein pellet using upper phase solution and lower phase solution several times. Finally, the upper phase was collected and diluted with 5 mL of sterile water and centrifuged at the maximum speed of the centrifuge at 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting pellet was deemed to be the purified plasma membrane protein fraction.

Then the samples with loading buffer were incubated in 50 °C for 40 min, then go through 10% SDS-PAGE gels electrophoresis followed by transfer to PVDF membranes (#IPVH00010, Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with indicted primary antibodies. After 3 times washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The detailed antibody information was list above All blots were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence (#WBKLS0500, Millipore), and quantified by Image J software (National Institutes of Health).

Coimmunoprecipitation assay

For exogenous coimmunoprecititaton (CO-IP), HEK293 cells were electroporated with the indicated constructs (Amaxa) 48 h ahead of experiment. The cellular extracts were prepared in TNE containing protease inhibitors (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 10% glycerol with protease inhibitors), followed by a centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at a temperature of 4 °C to pellet cellular debris. The supernatants were incubated with corresponding primary antibody (1–5 ug/ml) and magnetic beads (MCE) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TNE buffer five times, the beads were eluted by boiling in sample buffer and then subjected to separation on sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and immunobloted with the indicated antibodies.

For endogenous CO-IP, mouse dorsal hippocampus was extract and lysed in TNE containing protease inhibitors and then centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant’s concentration was quantified by bicinchoninic acid protein assay. About 50 milligrams of mouse brain lysates incubated with rabbit anti-GLUT3 antibodies for 4 h at 4 °C, followed by an additional incubation with magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C, and the same amount of lysates incubated with rabbit IgG was used as the negative control group. Then the samples were examined by WB.

Immunofluorescence staining and Microscopic quantitative analysis

For surface GLUTs immunofluorescence analysis, a surface fluorescence ratiometric assay was used as previously described15. Briefly, neurons or PC12 were transfected with GLUT3-HA-GFP or GLUT1-HA-GFP or other chimeras’ plasmids 24 h ahead of experiments, cells were treated with KCl or other agonists and inhibitors according to experiment design, and then fixed and stained against the HA epitope under permeabilized and non-permeabilized conditions using Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody. In permeabilized cells, HA staining represented total GLUT3, which was the same as GFP fluorescence, proved by the complete colocalization between the GFP and anti-HA signals. Under non-permeabilized conditions, HA staining represents surface GLUT3 level, and the GFP fluorescence represented the total GLUT3 amount. The surface GLUTs amount was calculated as 594(HA)/488(GFP) fluorescence intensity ratio and was normalized to untreated GLUT3-HA-GFP group across experiments.

For microscopic quantitative analysis, fluorescence images (16 bit) were acquired by a Nikon Eclipse TE 2000-U microscope equipped with a HQ2 cool CCD camera and a motorized Z drive. All the images were acquired through a 60×, 1.4 NA oil-immersion objective lens and processed by MetaMorph software. In each experiment, >25 cells were examined at random and consistent set of acquisition parameters was used for each set of images. The fluorescence intensities of different channels were measured by ImageJ software by selecting a region of interest. Confocal (16 bit) were acquired at 63× magnification for showing pictures, and their fluorescence intensity was examined to consistent with previous statisticians.

GST Pull-down assay and mass spectrometry

For the GST pulldown assay, GST, GST-GLUT3232-256, and GST-GLUT3232-256(TS/2A) fusion proteins were expressed and purified using the BL21(DE3) system (P2262, Beyotime), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein expression was verified by Coomassie blue staining. Mice were allocated to either the context fear conditioning (CFC) group or the naive group. Hippocampal lysates were prepared 30 minutes after the respective treatments, on ice. The lysates were initially precleared with GST beads and then incubated with the GST fusion proteins and GST beads overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded, while the pellet was retained for mass spectrometry (MS) analysis. Additionally, a portion of the lysate (1/20) was reserved as the input control for protein quantification.

For mass spectrometry detection, the samples were first subjected to a purification step to enrich the proteins, followed by analysis using the UltiMate 3000 liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) coupled to the timsTOF Pro 2, an ion-mobility spectrometry quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics). The instrument was operated in the DDA PASEF mode. Tandem mass spectra were processed by PEAKS Studio version 10.6 (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, Canada). The database was Mus_musculus (version2022, 21992 entries) sequence which download from uniprot. Trypsin was set as the digestion enzyme and Semi-specific was specified as the digest type. PEAKS DB were searched with a fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.02 Da and a parent ion tolerance of 10ppm.The max missed cleavages was 2. Carbamidomethyl on Cysteine was specified as the fixed modification. Oxidation on Methionine, Deamination on Asparagine, Acetylation on protein N-term, phosphorylation on Serine, Threonine and Tyrosine, Ubiquitin on cysteine, Lysine, Serine and Threonine were specified as the variable modifications. The peptides with 1%FDR and the proteins with 1%FDR and containing at least 1 unique peptide were filtered.

Behavioral assays

Fear Conditioning (FC)

Mice aged approximately 8 weeks are typically selected for fear memory training, specifically contextual fear conditioning (CFC) and auditory fear conditioning (AFC) training. One day prior to the training, the mice, along with their cages, are habituated in the room where the subsequent behavioral experiments will be conducted.

During training, mice are placed in Environment A (Context A). The training protocol consists of the following stages: (i) a 3 min acclimation period in the chamber without any stimulation, (ii) presentation of a 30 s tone (3 kHz, 75 dB) as the conditioned stimulus (CS), with the final second of each tone paired with a 0.7 mA, 1-second foot shock, administered at 1 min intervals, and (iii) after the last shock, mice are left in the chamber for an additional 2 min before being returned to their home cages. Subsequent to the training, mice are either euthanized for tissue collection or subjected to testing, as per the experimental requirements. Prior to each mouse being placed in the chamber, the experimental box is thoroughly cleaned with 50% alcohol to eliminate any olfactory cues left by the previous mouse.

Depending on the experimental design, the context fear conditioning (CFC) test is conducted 1 or 24 h following the fear training. During the test, mice are placed back into Context A for a duration of 5 min, during which their freezing time is recorded to assess the retention of fear memory. For the auditory fear conditioning test (AFC), mice are placed in Environment B (Context B), which consists of a white cylindrical fear conditioning chamber with a white flat rubber floor (Panlab). The mice are allowed to acclimate to the environment for 3 minutes before being exposed to a 30 s tone stimulus (3 kHz, 75 dB), with 30 s intervals between each stimulus, for a total of three tone presentations. Following the final stimulus, the mice remain in the chamber for an additional minute to conclude the test. The duration of freezing behavior during the tone presentations is recorded to evaluate the retention of fear memory.

Since the unconditioned stimulus (US) in auditory fear conditioning (AFC) training is coupled with both environmental information (CS1) and auditory information (CS2), the context fear conditioning (CFC) test and the AFC test can utilize the same AFC training session, also referred to as fear memory training (FC).

Open Field Test (OFT) and the Elevated Plus Maze (EPM)

Prior to the experiments, mice were acclimated to the testing room in advance. Following the completion of each mouse’s session, the apparatus was cleaned with 75% alcohol, and subsequent behavioral tests were conducted after the alcohol had completely evaporated.

Open Field Test (OFT)

The open field test was conducted in a box measuring 40 cm in length, 40 cm in width, and 35 cm in height. The movement of the mice within the central area (20 cm by 20 cm) during the 10 min test period was recorded and analyzed using an intelligent video tracking system (Smart IO). Statistical analysis was performed to determine the total distance traveled by the mice and the percentage of time spent in the central zone of the open field.

Elevated Plus-Maze (EPM)

The elevated plus-maze was constructed from black stainless steel and comprised four arms: two closed arms (30 cm in length, 5 cm in width, with 10 cm high walls) and two open arms (30 cm in length, 5 cm in width, and 0.5 cm in height without walls). The entire maze was elevated to a height of 50 cm. Mice were placed at the center of the elevated plus-maze, and their time spent in the open arms and their movement trajectories during the 10 min test period were recorded and analyzed using Smart IO.

Novel Place Recognition (NPR)

Prior to the start of the experiments, mice were habituated as previous described. During training, the novel location recognition test was conducted in an open field box with dimensions of 40 cm in length, 40 cm in width, and 35 cm in height. Two identical objects (A and B) were placed in opposite corners of the box. Mice were placed between the two objects and allowed to explore for 10 min, during which the time spent exploring each object was recorded. Following this exploration period, the mice were returned to their home cages. The test phase commenced 30 min after the training session. During the test, object B was moved to a new location in a different corner of the box, while object A remained in its original position. Mice were allowed to freely explore the box for 10 min, and the time spent exploring each object, T(A) and T(B), was recorded. Statistics and reproducibility

For the fluorescence detection experiment, cells were examined at random and subjected to blind analysis using ImageJ software, the background of each image was subtracted; >25 cells were examined in each experiment. Each experiment was repeated at least three times. Comparisons were made after each group normalized to naïve GLUT3-HA-GFP group.

For the time-lapse experiment in Fig. 1B, we calculate the t1/2 using modified one phase decay model:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y=-\exp (-K\times X)+{{\rm{Plateau}}}$$\end{document}Y=−exp(−K×X)+Plateau

X: Time, Y: Relative surface expression, Plateau: Same unit as Y, K: Rate constant, Half-Life = ln(2)/k, Time constant: Tau = k⁻¹, Span = Y₀ − Plateau

For CO-IP assay and WB analysis, the DH regions from mice were separated, and proteins were extracted for the endogenous CO-IP and WB analysis (n = 4/group). HEK293 cells were prepared for exogenous CO-IP assay and WB. CO-IP experiments were employed solely to assess the presence or absence of interactions, not for quantitative analysis. Comparisons were made after each group normalized to GAPDH protein level. Each experiment was repeated at least three times.

In all behavioral experiments, the animals were randomly divided into different groups, and observers were blinded to the treatments during the experiments.

HEK 293 was employed solely in overexpression and CO-IP experiments, and the critical results found in fluorescence detection experiment in PC12 were verified in cultured neurons.

GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to analyze the data with Student’s t test and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test or Sidak’s multiple comparisons test. Prior to ANOVA analysis, if the assumption of sphericity was violated, a Geisser-Greenhouse correction was applied.For single-factor experiments involving two or more than two groups, Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA was used. For experiments composed of multiple factors, a two-way ANOVA with test for interaction was used. All data were displayed as means ± SEM, and the significance was set at P < 0.05. Additional details for specific statistical tests are described in the Figure legends.
