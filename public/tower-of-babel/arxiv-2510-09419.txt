Magnetotactic bacterial populations studied with a Pound-Drever-Hall atomic magnetometer
María Hernández Ruiz, Christopher Kiehl, Vito Giovanni Lucivero, Morgan W. Mitchell

Abstract
We demonstrate an optically pumped magnetometer that monitors spin polarization using Pound Drever Hall (PDH) technique. The instrument exhibits a noise floor of 22.2 pT / 22.2\text{\,}\mathrm{pT}\text{/}\mathrm{} limited by optical photon shot noise, short-term instability of ( 30.8 pT / ) / τ ($30.8\text{\,}\mathrm{pT}\text{/}\mathrm{}$)/\sqrt{\tau} for averaging times τ ≤ 0.2 s \tau\leq$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$ , instability below 70 pT 70\text{\,}\mathrm{pT} for 0.2 s ≤ τ ≤ 20 s $0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$\leq\tau\leq$20\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$ and a minimum instability of 47 pT 47\text{\,}\mathrm{pT} at τ = 6 s \tau=$6\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$ . We apply the OPM to investigate the ability of magnetotactic bacteria (Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense, MSR-1) to orient in externally applied magnetic fields. Observing an opaque, concentrated suspension, we detect deviations from exponential relaxation dynamics on second time-scales, which give information about the dispersion of bacterial magnetic moment and rotational damping coefficient. These parameters are observed to evolve as the population further concentrates due to evaporation and settling. To our knowledge, this is the first time such magnetic inhomogeneities and long-term relaxation deviations have been directly observed. This study showcases both the sensitivity and stability of our OPM and its potential for probing biophysical processes.

Introduction

Optically-pumped magnetometers (OPMs)
Budker and Romalis 2007
;
Fabricant et al. 2023
offer high sensitivity and absolute accuracy for applications ranging from magnetoencephalography
Boto et al. 2017
;
Boto et al. 2018
;
Tierney et al. 2019
to space exploration
Arridge et al. 2016
;
Dougherty et al. 2006
to searches for physics beyond the standard model
Safronova et al. 2018
. For any given atomic species, the fundamental limits of OPM sensitivity scale inversely with the volume of vapor probed
Jiménez-Martínez and Knappe 2017
;
Mitchell and Palacios Alvarez 2020
. Consequently, most high-sensitivity OPMs use centimeter- or millimeter-dimension vapor cells, with corresponding limits on spatial resolution. There is, nonetheless, interest in pushing OPM imaging to sub-mm spatial resolutions
Yang et al. 2017
;
Knappe et al. 2024
;
Rasser et al. 2025
, i.e., into the domain of magnetic microscopy
Chemla et al. 1999
;
Le Sage et al. 2013
. This could open new applications in materials science
Fekete et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2020
, in fundamental physics, and in biomagnetism of tissues and small organisms. One challenge in developing OPMs with very small working sensing volumes is the consequent reduction in optical depth, which implies a less efficient readout of the atomic signal by, e.g., Faraday rotation probing
Hernández Ruiz et al. 2024
. An appealing solution to this challenge is to resonate the probe light within an optical cavity
Crepaz et al. 2015
;
Mazzinghi et al. 2021
, to enhance the optical extraction of signal from the atomic medium. Other techniques such as multipass probing
Li et al. 2011
can increase optical depth but face increasingly stringent fabrication and alignment requirements as the cell dimensions decrease. In addition, the number of effective passes in a multipass geometry is fundamentally constrained by the beam’s Rayleigh range for a given cell size, and achieving uniform illumination across the vapor cell volume remains a challenge
Kee et al. 2025
.

Here we describe the application of an OPM with cavity-based optical readout to measurement of magnetic relaxation signals from the magnetotactic bacteria (MTB)
Marmol et al. 2024

Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense
, strain MSR-1
Gandia et al. 2019
. These microorganisms biomineralize chains of iron oxide nanocrystals to form magnetosomes, which impart a permanent magnetic dipole moment to the bacterium and enable alignment with external magnetic fields
Barber-Zucker et al. 2016
. MTBs are of particular interest not only for their potential biomedical applications, such as targeted tumor therapy via magnetic hyperthermia
Gandia et al. 2019
, but also as model systems for exploring active matter
Petroff et al. 2025
;
Birjukovs et al. 2025
and magnetohydrodynamics
Théry et al. 2020
. Magnetometer techniques complement optical MTB measurement
Rosenblatt et al. 1982
;
Zhao et al. 2007
;
Lefevre et al. 2009
;
Welleweerd et al. 2022
, as they can study MTB in bulk and in opaque conditions (due, e.g., to a strongly-scattering environment, opaque container, or simply due to a high concentration of MTB themselves), and offer direct information of the magnetic response, independent of bacterial morphology
Lefevre et al. 2009
. Enhancing the spatial resolution and sensitivity of magnetometers could enable meaningful bulk measurements at lower MTB concentrations and even extend to single-cell resolution in a magnetic microscopy configuration, as has previously been demonstrated with superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs)
Chemla et al. 1999
.

The OPM we use here implements a free-induction decay (FID) scalar magnetic measurement, with resonantly-enhanced readout using a planar external optical cavity and Pound-Drever-Hall (PDH) detection
Hernández Ruiz et al. 2024
. Even with concentrated MTB samples (
\sim$1\text{\times}{10}^{11}\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{L}$
), the biomagnetic signal is of
\sim$\text{\,}\mathrm{nT}$
magnitude, and relaxes to a thermal-magnetic equilibrium on
\sim$10\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
time scales as the bacteria re-orient, in the presence of a
\sim$50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro T}$
Earth-field background
Marmol et al. 2024
. As we show, these slow, weak signals can nonetheless be measured with sub-
100\text{\,}\mathrm{pT}
precision by the PDH-OPM, due to its high sensitivity – comparable to earlier work with a dcSQUID microscope
Chemla et al. 1999
– and high stability in Earth field conditions. The PDH-OPM is built around a
\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
-scale microfabricated vapor cell (MEMS cell), showing compatibility with techniques necessary for OPM magnetic microscopy, and in an one-layered open shield, to allow rapid access to the biological samples. The OPM performance under these conditions shows that meaningful MTB relaxation signals can be recovered without the expense and inconvenience of strong magnetic isolation. Finally, we show the PDH-OPM can quantify small deviations from exponential relaxation that cause parts-per-million changes in the observed field strength. In MTB populations such deviations arise from dispersion of the magnetic moment and viscous damping coefficient. Using this capability we observe immobilization of a fraction of the MTB population as the solution is concentrated by evaporation.

5
Non-exponential magnetic relaxation in MTB

As shown in Figure
3
, relaxation of the mean bacterial magnetization toward equilibrium resembles an exponential approach to a steady-state value, and we have used an exponential model, Equation
7
, for the fits used to generate Figure
4
. Nonetheless, angular diffusion does not in general produce exponential relaxation. Most fundamentally, this is because the angular coordinates are not Cartesian, so that the diffusion in effect occurs on a curved surface
Brillinger 1997
;
Yosida 1949
;
Carlsson et al. 2010
. A more pedestrian reason is that a single population can contain a range of values for
m
and
\gamma_{r}
. Here we show that with sufficient measurement precision, the non-exponential character of MTB magnetic relaxation becomes evident, and that the departure from the exponential form can give information about the spread of
m
and
\gamma_{r}
in the MTB population.

As shown in Appendix
7
, a bacterium’s polar angle
\theta
, defined relative to the field direction, obeys the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{Bm}{\gamma_{r}}\sin\theta+\frac{k_{B}T}{\gamma_{r}}\cot\theta\right)dt+\sqrt{\frac{2k_{B}T}{\gamma_{r}}}dW,

(8)

where
dW
is the Wiener increment.
We first illustrate by numerical simulation that Eq.
8
produces non-exponential relaxation of
\langle\cos\theta\rangle
. We consider a bacterial population with monodisperse
m
and
\gamma_{r}
, and initial
\theta=\pi/2
such that
\langle\cos\theta\rangle=0
. This corresponds to the experimental situation immediately after the polarizing field
B_{\mathrm{pol}}
is turned off. We average
1\text{\times}{10}^{6}\text{\,}
trajectories of Equation
8
, integrated by the Milstein method in Mathematica. As shown in Figure
5
(upper left), the resulting
\langle\cos\theta\rangle
shows a few-percent departure from exponential behavior. The other panels of Figure
5
show similar traces obtained by simulating the same scenario for populations with dispersion in
m
,
\gamma_{r}
, or both. In particular, we write the mean polarization for the population as

\displaystyle\langle\cos\theta\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dm\,d\gamma_{r}f(m,\gamma_{r})\langle\cos\theta\rangle_{m,\gamma_{r}},

(9)

where
f(m,\gamma_{r})
is the probability density function of
m,\gamma_{r}
and
\langle\cos\theta\rangle_{m,\gamma_{r}}
is the mean of
\cos\theta
for given
m,\gamma_{r}
.

The observed dispersion-induced departure from exponential behavior is, for most times, of opposite sign to the departure seen with a monodisperse population. In this scenario, the most nearly exponential relaxation in fact requires some
m
and/or
\gamma_{r}
dispersion to balance the effect of the curvilinear coordinates.

6
Conclusions

We have demonstrated optically-pumped magnetometery with a
\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
-scale atomic vapor volume, using a Pound-Drever-Hall optical-cavity readout scheme described in
Hernández Ruiz et al. 2024
. Using an FID method, the PDH-OPM achieves, in fields up to
160\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro T}
, an instability below
60\text{\,}\mathrm{pT}
for measurement durations between
1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
and
20\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
with minimal shielding (a single-layer mu-metal shield, open to allow rapid sample access). This sub-ppm stability allows measurement of slow signals from weak sources. We apply the PDH-OPM to the measurement of relaxation in a population of the magnetotactic bacterium
Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense
, strain MSR-1, and quantify the evolution of the distribution of damping coefficients as a consequence of concentration during settling and evaporation. We leave for future work detailed studies of the mechanism behind this observation. To our knowledge, this is the first time such magnetic inhomogeneities and long-term relaxation deviations have been directly observed. These results highlight the capability of compact atomic magnetometers to image and quantify collective magnetic dynamics in biological magnetic systems, particularly in environments where optical techniques are hindered by scattering or opacity.

While the PDH-OPM operates at the photon–shot–noise limit, aside from technical noise peaks primarily at harmonics of the mains frequency, there remains scope for improving the measurement sensitivity by increasing the optical cavity finesse, raising the PDH modulation frequency, and achieving critical coupling during cavity readout. The current undercoupled cavity limits the efficiency of the optical readout. Beyond improving the PDH-OPM itself, implementing a gradiometric configuration in future designs would significantly enhance magnetic background noise rejection.

Simulating bacterial angular diffusion

Considering a single bacterium, the angular motion is described by the Langevin equation
Codutti et al. 2019

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{e}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{r}}[\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{ext}}+\sqrt{2k_{B}T\gamma_{r}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}]\times\mathbf{e},

(10)

where
\mathbf{e}
is a unit vector indicating the bacterial orientation,
\gamma_{r}
is the rotational friction coefficient, which for a cylindrical bacterium of diameter
d
and length
L
in a medium of viscosity
\eta
is
\gamma_{r}\approx\frac{\pi\eta L^{3}}{3}\left[\ln{\left(\frac{L}{d}\right)-0.662-0.92\frac{d}{L}}\right]^{-1}
,
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{ext}}=m\mathbf{e}\times\mathbf{B}
is the torque due to the magnetic field,
k_{B}T
is the mean thermal energy and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}
is a 3-vector of independent unit white noises. This last term describes an isotropic, delta-correlated torque of thermal origin.

The above SDE clearly preserves the length of
\mathbf{e}
. It describes biased diffusion on a unit sphere, which has been studied in a variety of contexts
Brillinger 1997
;
Yosida 1949
;
Carlsson et al. 2010
. We can recast Eq.
10
in the form

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{e}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{r}}\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{ext}}\times\mathbf{e}+\sigma(\mathbbm{1}-\mathbf{e}\mathbf{e}^{T})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r},

(11)

where
\mathbbm{1}
is the identity and
\sigma=\sqrt{2k_{B}T/\gamma_{r}}
is the diffusion coefficient. In polar coordinates
\theta,\phi
with
\theta=0
when
\mathbf{e}
is along the
\mathbf{B}
direction, the first term above contributes a
\theta
drift rate
Bm\gamma_{r}^{-1}\sin\theta
, while second term contributes an isotropic diffusion.

This problem is reviewed in detail in Brillinger
Brillinger 1997
. A useful result for us
(
Brillinger 1997
, Eq. 3.5)
is that for
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{ext}}=0
, the polar coordinates obey the Itô stochastic differential equations

\displaystyle d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\cot\theta dt+\sigma dW_{\theta}

(12)

\displaystyle d\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma\csc\theta dW_{\phi},

(13)

where
dW_{\theta}
and
dW_{\phi}
are independent Wiener increments. The
\theta
drift contribution
\cot\theta
is a result of the curvature of the spherical surface, and reflects the increased phase space density near the equator, where
\theta=\pi/2
.

Reintroducing the drift term, we find
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{ext}}
contributes a
\theta
drift rate
Bm\gamma_{r}^{-1}\sin\theta
, at which point we have the Itô stochastic differential equation for magnetically-biased angular diffusion

\displaystyle d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{Bm}{\gamma_{r}}\sin\theta+\frac{k_{B}T}{\gamma_{r}}\cot\theta\right)dt+\sqrt{\frac{2k_{B}T}{\gamma_{r}}}dW.\hskip 25.60747pt

(14)

Numerical calculation shows that in the long-time limit this reproduces the Langevin formula for the mean polarization

Marmol et al. 2024

\langle\cos\theta\rangle=\coth\frac{mB}{k_{B}T}-\frac{k_{B}T}{mB}.

(15)

We note that this formula derives from equilibrium thermodynamics and the geometry of the angular coordinates, and as such is an independent check on the dynamical model in Eq.
14
