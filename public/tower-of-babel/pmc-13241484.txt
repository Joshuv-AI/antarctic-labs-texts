Caregiver perceptions of online peer mentoring for youth with learning differences
Authors: Alecia Mercier, Brianna Paquette, Fumiko Hoeft, Caroline G Richter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractYouth with learning differences (e.g., specific learning disorder and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) encounter exacerbated psychological and behavioral challenges compared to typically developing peers. Peer mentoring has been shown to be beneficial in addressing such challenges, yet there is limited research on online peer mentorship programs for this population. This study investigates the perceived benefits of an online peer mentoring program on mentees’ mental health, well-being, and behavior across 10 outcomes: anxiety, depression, fear, cognitive challenges, behavioral concerns, executive function, sleep difficulties, self-care, interpersonal skills, and self-esteem/self-confidence. Caregivers retrospectively reported on their child’s mental health, well-being, and behavior prior to and since receiving online mentorship. Results indicated significant caregiver-reported improvements across all outcomes following online mentorship. Interpretation of the findings is limited by the study’s retrospective design and absence of a control group. Overall, findings indicate an association between participation in online peer mentorship programs and improved caregiver-perceived outcomes, suggesting its potential as a supportive tool for youth with learning differences.Keywords: Online mentoring, Peer mentoring, Specific learning disorder, Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, Mental healthSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

Youth with learning differences, such as specific learning disorder (SLD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) are more likely to experience academic challenges, poor mental health, and behavioral difficulties compared to typically developing children1–3. Many of these challenges are exacerbated by the societal and psychological pressures these children face due to stigmatization and the general need to fit into an ableist society4. Recent work has identified that adopting a strengths-based approach, rather than viewing learning differences as deficits that are inherently negative and need correction, may facilitate better educational, psychological, and behavioral outcomes5–8. One promising route for encouraging children’s strengths and promoting their development is through mentorship programs9.

Students with learning differences

SLD and ADHD are highly prevalent learning differences, with SLD occurring in around 5% to 15% percent of children10,11 and ADHD occurring in around 5% to 8% of children12,13. SLD is characterized by deficits in academic skills, facing significant challenges in acquiring skills in areas such as reading (dyslexia), math (dyscalculia), and writing (dysgraphia)14. These difficulties stem from neurological differences and are not related to their educational background or intellectual ability14. SLD is frequently comorbid with ADHD, a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by ongoing inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that disrupts functioning14,15. ASD is another neurodevelopmental condition marked by persistent challenges in social communication, restricted and repetitive behaviors, and sensory differences14. ASD affects approximately 1 in 31 children in the United States16 and shows substantial comorbidity with other learning differences, including ADHD and SLD17–19. Beyond academic challenges, individuals with SLD, ADHD, and ASD often face higher stress, lower emotional well-being, mental health concerns, and additional social and emotional concerns3,8,17. These individuals typically attain academic support through the school system, but their socio-emotional needs may largely remain unmet9.

Mentorship programs

Mentoring broadly refers to a relationship in which a mentor provides guidance to a mentee, typically through psychosocial and instrumental support, to facilitate the mentee’s personal, academic, or professional growth9,20. Mentoring relationships have been found to mitigate the challenges often experienced by individuals with learning differences, such as stress, social engagement, hyperactivity, and inattention21–23. Mentorship programs for students with SLD and ADHD have also been shown to support complex cognitive challenges such as executive function24,25.

There is further evidence for a specific type of mentoring, peer mentoring, which pairs mentees with mentors who share similar lived experiences20. Peer mentoring programs that match students with SLD or ADHD to mentors who also have SLD or ADHD have been shown to benefit mentees’ academic skills and social-emotional well-being9 as well as to decrease depression and improve self-esteem26. Additionally, recent studies of peer mentoring for students with ASD have demonstrated that participation in peer mentoring supports improvements in social connectedness, self-advocacy, well-being, and sense of belonging, especially in postsecondary and transition-age populations27–29.

Social cognitive theory suggests that individuals are more likely to adopt and maintain health behaviors when they observe similar and attainable peers modeling those behaviors30, particularly in the context of shared routines and self-care practices31. In peer mentoring relationships, mentors model adaptive daily routines such as sleep hygiene and self-care strategies, which mentees can observe, imitate, and practice strengthening self-efficacy through goal setting and accountability30,32. Complementing this framework, the social support buffering model posits that perceived social support attenuates stress and emotional arousal33,34, thereby reducing physiological stress responses that interfere with sleep and daily functioning35. Together, these models suggest that peer mentoring may improve sleep and self-care both by promoting routine-based behavioral learning and by buffering stress, creating psychological safety that supports the adoption and maintenance of health-promoting daily behaviors32.

In recent years, there has been a rise in mentorship programs administered virtually, often called electronic mentoring, virtual mentoring, or online mentoring20,36. While a growing body of literature shows some promise for online mentoring37, little is known about the success of these programs for individuals with learning differences. One systematic review found that online peer mentorship programs have demonstrated success at increasing social communication skills and increasing ability to engage in routines for children with neurodevelopmental disorders including ADHD9. However, more research is needed specifically for SLD.

Further, despite differences in symptomology based on sex assigned at birth in learning differences38,39, little research has examined the differences in perceived mentoring outcomes between sexes. Limited existing literature from 2020 on neurodevelopmental disorders suggests females may benefit more from online mentorship through greater responses and participation in online programs40.

Superpower Mentors

Superpower Mentors program is a novel mentorship program that connects, facilitates, and manages mentorship relationships between students or youth with learning differences (“the mentee”) and young adults (“the mentor”). Mentors and mentees are paired using a near peer-mentoring method, connecting individuals who have similar backgrounds (e.g., SLD, ADHD) and lived experiences. Mentees are also typically paired with mentors of the same gender. This method of pairing mentors has been shown to facilitate the most positive outcomes in mentor/mentee relationships9,26,41.

Consistent with prior research from 2020 highlighting that mentor commitment is key to successful mentorship programs42, the Superpower Mentors’ mentor selection and onboarding process is highly competitive and rigorous. Using a comprehensive framework, mentors are assessed on relatability, personal tools, drive, reliability, life story, and goals. Mentors go through a thorough vetting process with less than a 3% acceptance rate, including a resume screening, two behavioral interviews, a live-online certification course with the Superpower Mentor’s team, a criminal background check, and an interview with the prospective mentee’s caregivers.

The mentoring sessions are conducted online so that the mentees can attend their sessions wherever they feel most comfortable. As a result, geography and/or transportation are not barriers to the mentor-matching process. The sessions occur once a week, and last 30–60 min based at a cadence most comfortable for the mentee and their family. Consistent with a meta-analysis showing larger effect sizes for youth peer-mentoring programs with moderate to high adult oversight and supervision43, the Superpower Mentors program embeds monthly caregiver-mentor and team meetings plus dedicated caregiver strategy time, alongside the mentor-mentee interactions, to ensure such oversight. In addition, there is an important emphasis on adherence to routines and organization and scheduling strategies that support mentee’s ability to engage in routines. This is the first known study to date to evaluate the outcomes related to participating in the Superpower Mentors program.

Current study

A growing but limited body of research suggests peer mentorship programs are beneficial for individuals with learning differences9. However, much of the research has been conducted in school settings, and there is a lack of evidence regarding how online mentorship is perceived by families. The current study aims to address this gap by investigating the caregiver-reported associations between participation in an online mentorship program and changes in mentee well-being for youth and young adult mentees with learning differences. This online mentorship program targeted many domains related to mental health, well-being, and behavior, which are presented and defined in Table 1. It was hypothesized that these outcomes would be reported as improving between the periods before and since participation in the mentoring program. This study additionally explored whether outcomes differed by sex.

Method

Participants

Participants included 40 primary caregivers of youth enrolled in the Superpower Mentors Program. Mentees included 14 females (26 males) ranging from 10 to 23 years old (M = 14.1 years, SD = 3.35). Weeks in the program working with the mentor ranged from five to 104 weeks (M = 26.14, SD = 24.07). Of the 40 mentees, 10 had a single diagnosis, 28 had two diagnoses, and two had three diagnoses. Overall, 35 had SLD, 34 had ADHD, and 3 had ASD, reflecting extensive comorbidity between SLD and ADHD. See Table 2 for additional details on specific diagnoses, comorbidities, and SLD subtypes. Participants primarily resided in the United States (n = 36), but two resided in the United Kingdom, one in Norway, and one did not report their country of origin. Measures

Primary caregivers retrospectively reported on ten outcomes related to their child’s mental health, well-being, and ability: anxiety, depression, fear, cognitive challenges, behavioral concerns, executive function, sleep difficulties, self-care, interpersonal skills, and self-esteem/self-confidence. Further descriptions for each item can be seen in Table 1. This measure was developed by the Superpower Mentors team for program monitoring, and the current study analyzed these existing data. The descriptions were not created by the present authors but were supplied by the program as part of their assessment materials. As part of the measure, caregivers were provided with the item descriptions presented in Table 1 to ensure responses were based on a shared understanding of the constructs. Items were rated on a 4-point scale based on the severity their child experienced each difficulty one month prior to mentoring and since participating in the mentoring program: Not present (0), mild (1), moderate (2) and severe (3). Lower scores indicated fewer concerns or difficulties in that area of functioning. The measure has not been standardized or validated.

Procedures

The study was approved by the University of Alabama at Birmingham Institutional Review Board (IRB#300012459), and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants provided informed consent prior to completing the survey. The survey was virtually administered to parents or caregivers of the mentored children using Qualtrics. The survey was emailed to caregivers of 118 mentees between October 2021 and August 2022, 40 of whom completed the entire survey. This response rate of 34% is relatively consistent with voluntary online research studies, which have an average response rate of 44%50. Caregivers were asked to report demographic characteristics for themselves and their child and on their child’s functioning prior to beginning the program and since the program. At the time of the study, all mentees were living with the primary caregiver that completed the surveys. Data on child outcomes prior to mentoring and since receiving mentoring were collected retrospectively at the same timepoint.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 29. Descriptive statistics were evaluated for demographic characteristics and mentorship outcomes. Repeated measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were used to examine the effects of time, as measured by differences prior to and post-mentoring, and sex on the 10 mentorship outcomes variables (i.e., anxiety, depression, fear, cognitive challenges, behavioral concerns, executive function, sleep difficulties, self-care, interpersonal skills, and self-esteem/self-confidence). Effect size was measured by η2p. Significant interactions were followed up with simple effect analyses using repeated-measures t-tests with Holm-Bonferroni corrections.

Discussion

The present study examined the perceived benefits of an online mentorship program for youth or students with learning differences on mentees’ mental health, well-being, and behavior. Results supported the hypotheses and caregivers endorsed lower anxiety, depression, fear, cognitive challenges, behavioral concerns, and sleep difficulties, and higher levels of executive function, self-care, interpersonal skills, and self-esteem/self-confidence.

This study supports and builds upon prior findings indicating that mentorship programs may be associated with positive benefits for students with learning differences9,23,26. Uniquely, our findings provide evidence supporting the virtual delivery of a mentorship program for students with SLD, ADHD, and ASD as caregivers reported positive outcomes for their children following participation40. Administering the programs online may help reduce potential barriers to accessing these programs, such as transportation and the availability of local mentors.

Prior work has also shown that students with learning differences benefit from having in-person peer mentors who also have learning differences9,44,45. The current study builds upon these findings by demonstrating that peer mentorships, which tend to rely on building personal relationships between the mentor/mentee, can also be successful in an online format.

This study also explored sex differences in mentorship outcomes. Our findings suggest that sex assigned at birth was associated with differences in changes in caregiver-reported outcomes prior to and after participating in online mentorship. The significant interactions between time and sex aligns with previous literature suggesting that females with neurodevelopmental disorders may engage more actively and benefit more from online mentorship programs due to their greater responses and participation40.

Limitations and future directions

Due to the retrospective, single-group, pre-post design of the study, we were unable to determine a causal relationship between participation in the mentoring program and improvement in students’ mental health, well-being, and behavior. Retrospective reports of items may be imprecise, thus leading to potential bias in the reported outcomes. Without baseline data collected prior to participation, and without a control group (i.e. non-mentored group, or an alternative intervention), it is not possible to conclude the reported changes in outcomes are directly related to the mentoring program, rather than other confounding variables, such as maturation or placebo effects. Given this study was retrospective in nature, the research team did not have access to mentors’ specific data, limiting the ability to examine the impact of mentor demographics, such as sex, gender, and age, on changes in outcomes. Given the program’s gender-based matching process, mentees were the same gender as their mentors for the majority of participants. Future studies should investigate the impact of mentor demographics on mentees perceived progress in the program.

Given that females entered the program with higher baseline severity of caregiver-reported depression and sleep difficulties than males, the larger reductions in symptom severity reported may partially reflect greater room for improvement or regression to the mean rather than a true sex-specific response to the intervention. This pattern may also reflect a floor effect among males, whereby lower baseline scores constrained the magnitude of observable change. Consequently, observed post-intervention sex differences cannot be conclusively attributed to differential intervention effects and should be interpreted with caution.

Despite the limitations of our study, the effect sizes for the pre-post differences in anxiety, depression, self-esteem, and interpersonal skills were either larger or comparable to those reported in a previous 2019 study26, which examined a mentorship group against two different control groups. Based on a recent literature review9, only one previous study with individuals with SLD had a control group. Future studies should include prospective data collection and control groups to determine differences in the effects of mentoring programs, including mentor specific variables, on students with and without learning differences.

Outcomes for this study were measured using a survey developed specifically for the intervention program rather than a standardized or validated instrument. Because each domain was assessed with a single item encompassing multiple facets, we could not estimate reliability (e.g., internal consistency), and the constructs may not align directly with standard clinical scales of anxiety, depression, or executive function. Without evidence that the measure accurately and consistently captures the constructs of interest, it is difficult to determine whether the observed outcomes reflect true changes or are influenced by issues with the measurement tool itself. The lack of standardization reduces comparability with findings from other studies using established measures.

Outcomes were also assessed via caregiver reports, and primarily consisted of White, highly educated mothers, rather than through self-report from the mentees, or from multiple informants, (i.e. mothers and fathers, parents and teachers). Given that only one caregiver identified as male, results primarily reflect mothers’ perceptions. Although excluding a single case had minimal impact on group-level ANOVA results, it is important to note that these findings may not be generalizable to more diverse families or fathers and other caregivers. Regarding informants, prior studies have indicated discrepancies in self and proxy reports on well-being measures, often showing low to moderate inter-rater agreement46,47. For example, in one 2015 study parents tended to report more positive mental health and well-being outcomes for their child with a learning difference than the child reported for themselves48, while in another, parents reported more negative well-being outcomes49. Thus, the positive outcomes reported by caregivers in this study may not reflect the true outcomes experienced by the mentees themselves. Additionally, although comparable to the average observed response in online survey research50, given the low caregiver response rate (34%), it is possible that caregivers who chose to respond to the survey were those who had more positive experiences, introducing the potential for positive selection bias. Future research should include standardized measures completed by students, caregivers, and blinded observers, prior to the start of participation in the mentoring program and upon completion of the program to determine the true effectiveness of mentoring on the reported outcomes. Future efforts should also include strategies to improve response rates across all participant groups to increase participation and yield more well-rounded perspectives.

Conclusion

The present study yields promising preliminary findings for online peer mentorship experiences for students with learning differences. Overall, our findings suggest that participation in the Superpower Mentors program may be associated with lower caregiver-reported severity across targeted domains for mentees with learning differences. While these results suggest potential benefits of virtual peer mentorship models, the retrospective, cross-sectional design and reliance on proxy reports precludes causal inferences. Future research using prospective or controlled designs are needed to further clarify the programs impact and establish efficacy.
