Acute Extensor Mechanism Disruption Following Total Knee Arthroplasty: Biologic Augmentation of Direct Repair Utilizing a Semitendinosus and Gracilis Autograft
Authors: Brian J Panish, Evan R Simpson, Alisha Chen, Clark Dorman, Viraj Deshpande, Marshall Fairres, Steven Barnett, Travis Scudday, Michael Hunter, Robert S Gorab, Nader A Nassif, Matthew P Siljander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtensor mechanism disruption (EMD) is a devastating complication following total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Treatment options vary depending on the chronicity of the injury and integrity of the remaining tissue, with allograft and synthetic mesh reconstruction representing the most common surgical modalities utilized. In this study, we present the surgical technique and clinical outcomes of primary extensor mechanism repair with biologic augmentation utilizing a gracilis and semitendinosus autograft in the setting of acute EMD.MethodsInstitutional Review Board-approved data from our institutional registry spanning from 2013 to 2024 was queried for TKA patients who underwent an acute extensor mechanism repair. The described technique involves open reduction, internal fixation if a patella fracture is present, or direct repair of patellar tendon ruptures with augmentation utilizing a gracilis and semitendinosus autograft tunneled through the tibia, looped over the patella, and sutured into place.ResultsSeven primary TKA patients with acute EMD underwent the described technique. Six out of seven patients had an intact repair at final follow-up, with active extension present. One of these six had a small residual extensor lag of 3°. One patient developed a recurrent EMD in the setting of prosthetic joint infection, necessitating placement of a static spacer and was ultimately lost to follow-up.ConclusionsPrimary repair with biologic augmentation utilizing a gracilis and semitendinosus autograft is a viable method to restore extensor mechanism function in acute disruptions following TKA. This technique demonstrated successful maintenance of active extension without the need for synthetic mesh or allograft tissue.Keywords: Extensor mechanism disruption, Gracilis, Semitendinosus, Autograft, Revision

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains one of the most common procedures performed in the United States, with annual use rates projected to increase to over 1.5 million within the next decade [1]. TKA implant survivorship exceeds 90% [2]. Extensor mechanism rupture in the setting of TKA is a rare yet serious complication, with an incidence of 0.17% to 2.5% of cases [3]. These ruptures impair active knee extension and severely impact a patient’s ability to ambulate [4]. Disruption of the extensor mechanism can result iatrogenically, or as an intraoperative or postoperative complication and can involve a tibial tubercle avulsion, a patellar or quadriceps tendon rupture, or a patella fracture [5]. Conservative treatment can be utilized in cases of mild or incomplete disruption, while surgical intervention remains the standard of care for complete disruption [6].

A multitude of operative management modalities exist for the treatment of extensor mechanism disruption (EMD), including primary repair of the quadriceps and patellar tendons, soft tissue allograft reconstruction, open reduction, internal fixation (ORIF) of patella fractures, total allograft reconstructions, and Marlex mesh reconstruction. Isolated direct repair of the extensor mechanism alone has fallen out of favor given documented success rates of ˂10% [7]. Clinical outcomes of allograft and synthetic reconstruction have yielded mixed results [6]. Wood et al. [8] found that synthetic reconstruction was associated with greater postoperative extensor lag, higher graft failure rates, and increased revision surgery than allograft reconstruction. Meanwhile, Richardson et al. [9] found no difference in revision rates between cohorts at 5-year follow-up. Meta-analyses comparing reconstruction with allograft and synthetic mesh demonstrated similar results, with approximately 25% failure rate in both groups [10,11]. Infection remains the most significant complication in both allograft and synthetic mesh reconstruction. In a meta-analysis conducted by Shau et al. [10], a combined failure rate of 13% was found due to infection—12% in allograft reconstruction and 15% in the mesh reconstruction.

Extensor mechanism repair or reconstruction utilizing autografts represents another less commonly utilized treatment modality. The use of hamstring autograft for reconstruction of chronic patellar tendon disruptions of the native knee has proven a successful technique, with 1 study demonstrating 10 out of 11 patients with intact repairs and only 1 patient with an extensor lag at final follow-up [12]. This technique was also previously described in 1992 by Cadambi and Engh. Their technique utilized a semitendinosus tendon graft, and, if necessary, the gracilis tendon for additional augmentation [13]. While the use of hamstring autograft for extensor mechanism reconstruction in TKA patients remains limited, prior studies have reported successful outcomes. Järvelä et al. [14] reported high patient satisfaction 1 year after using the semitendinosus–gracilis graft with an interference screw, staple fixation, and immediate postoperative mobilization in 1 patient with an extensor mechanism rupture after TKA. Spoliti et al. [15] reported that, of nine patients who underwent patellar tendon rupture reconstruction using semitendinosus–gracilis grafts without attempted patellar tendon repair and no supplemental suture fixation, all were satisfied with the procedure and able to return to their preinjury level of activity by the 4-year postoperative follow-up.

At our institution, acute periprosthetic EMDs involving patellar fractures or patellar tendon ruptures are managed with ORIF or direct tendon repair, respectively, augmented with suture and hamstring autograft. Survival of hamstring autograft augmented acute periprosthetic extensor mechanism repair has not been previously reported. The aim of this study is to describe our surgical technique and clinical outcomes for acute periprosthetic extensor mechanism repair augmented with hamstring autograft.

Material and methods

Patient selection and data collection

Seven patients who underwent primary TKA for osteoarthritis and subsequent acute extensor mechanism repair with gracilis and semitendinosus autograft augmentation between February 2013 and January 2024 were identified from Institutional Review Board-approved registry data. Patient demographics, surgical details, and clinical data were collected. Mechanism of injury and time from primary TKA to both injury and subsequent extensor mechanism repair were noted. Final range of motion (ROM) was assessed. All patients underwent patellar resurfacing during their primary TKA procedure and experienced an acute injury resulting in EMD.

Surgical technique

The patient is positioned supine on a radiolucent table. A nonsterile tourniquet is applied to the proximal thigh. The operative extremity is prepped and draped in the standard sterile fashion with inflation of the tourniquet. The previous midline incision is marked along its prior length and carried further distally and medially to provide access for harvest of the semitendinosus and gracilis. The skin and subcutaneous layers are sharply incised. Full thickness medial and lateral flaps are created for exposure of the extensor mechanism and medial and lateral retinaculum.

Examination of the retinaculum and the extensor mechanism injury is performed. Fracture edges, if present, are cleaned and hematoma removed. Defects encountered in the patellar tendon, as well as any residual hematoma, are debrided. Retinacular tears are inspected for later repair. The knee is irrigated with a combination of dilute Betadine and saline. If a patella fracture is present, the patellar component is assessed for stability; if stable, it is retained, and if loose, it is removed. The femoral and tibial components are evaluated and retained if stable.

Next, attention is turned to the harvest of the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons. At the more distal and medial extent of the incision, the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are palpated at their bursa. With the use of Metzenbaum scissors, the sartorius fascia is elevated and the sheath over the tendons is opened. The semitendinosus and gracilis are freed from the sheath and any other soft tissue adhesions are released from the tendons. With the knee flexed to 90 degrees, a corkscrew stripper is applied to the semitendinosus and, with the tendon stripper, is passed as proximally as possible to maximize tendon graft length. This process is repeated with the gracilis. The semitendinosus and gracilis are left attached at their distal tibial insertion (Fig. 1). Any muscle remaining on the tendons is debrided. The tendons are wrapped in a saline-soaked lap. At our institution, graft harvesting is performed by our arthroplasty trained surgeons, with some cases involving the assistance of our sports medicine trained colleagues.

Harvest of the semitendinosus and gracilis.

Next, attention is turned to the EMD for preparation of repair. For a distal pole patella fracture or patellar tendon injury, two #2 Fiberwire (Arthrex- Naples, FL) sutures are placed through the tendon in a running locking fashion leaving four suture strands. A drill is used to create three vertical tunnels through the patella from distal to proximal, staying superficial to the patellar component.

In the case of a patella fracture, the patella is reduced with the use of pointed reduction clamps as well as Kirschner wires. Fluoroscopy and direct visualization are used to confirm reduction quality. The patella is fixed with appropriate hardware. Our standard osteosynthesis technique utilizes the Depuy Synthes (Raynham, MA) Variable Angle Locking Patella Plating System, colloquially known as the “star plate,” to provide multiple locking screw options. Any additional patellar fragments can be fixed with independent fragment-specific screws as needed.

Prior to passage of the gracilis and semitendinosus, osteosynthesis of the patella is completed. For soft tissue tendon repairs, the sutures are passed through the patella in standard fashion. The two central limbs are passed through the central drill tunnel, and the medial and lateral sutures limbs are passed through the medial and lateral patellar drill tunnels, respectively. With the extremity in full extension, these sutures are tied to repair the soft tissue tendon injury.

After completion of patellar ORIF or soft tissue tendon repair, attention is turned to the gracilis and semitendinosus augmentation. A 4.5-mm drill is passed from medial to lateral just distal and posterior to the tibial tubercle to create a bone tunnel to shuttle one of the tendons through (Fig. 2). A suture passer is used and the longer of the semitendinosus or gracilis tendons is passed through the bone tunnel from medial to lateral exiting on the lateral side of the tibia (Figs. 3 and 4). A supplemental cerclage Fiberwire can also be passed with the tendon through the tibial drill tunnel. The longer tendon is passed along the lateral side of the retinaculum and tunneled through the quadriceps tendon just proximal to its insertion on the patella or through a transverse drill hole in the proximal pole of the patella. The tendon is reflected down medially along the medial retinaculum (Fig. 5). The shorter of the two tendons which has remained medial is passed proximally, medially along the retinaculum, and passed medial to lateral through the quadriceps tendon or transverse patella tunnel if length allows.

With the knee in full extension multiple #2 Fiberwire sutures are used to sew the grafts to each other, creating a robust loop of two tendons overlying the patella to reinforce the repair (Fig. 6). Multiple #1 Ethibond (J&J MedTech, New Brunswick, NJ) sutures are used in multiple locations incorporating the semitendinosus and gracilis into the medial and lateral retinaculum repairs (Figs. 7 and 8). The sling of Arthrex (Naples, FL) FiberTape® previously passed along the same route can be tensioned as additional fixation.

The remainder of the retinaculum is repaired. Subcutaneous layers are closed with 2-0 Vicryl (J&J MedTech, New Brunswick, NJ) followed by closure of skin with staples. A sterile dressing is applied followed by a knee immobilizer or hinged knee brace (HKB) to maintain the knee in full extension.

Our standard postoperative protocol is weight-bearing as tolerated with immobilization in full knee extension for 4 to 6 weeks after which incremental reintroduction of flexion in an HKB is initiated followed by formal physical therapy for quadriceps strengthening.

Results

There were seven patients who underwent extensor mechanism repair with autograft augmentation with a mean age 73 years (range, 62-83 years) and mean Body Mass Index (BMI) 30.41 kg/m2 (range, 21.67-47 kg/m2). Six were female and 1 was male. Four patients had a preoperative diagnosis of periprosthetic patellar fracture, three had patellar tendon ruptures. The mechanism of injury was consistently a ground level fall with hyperflexion of the knee. Mean time from index operation to injury was 4 years (range, 5 days-14 years). At the time of preoperative exam, three patients were unable to perform a straight leg raise, while four patients had an extensor lag ranging from 50 degrees to 75 degrees. Mean time between date of injury and repair was 2 weeks (range, 1-3 weeks). Postoperatively, patients were followed for a mean of 9 months (range, 3-14.5 months). All patients were immobilized in full knee extension for 4 to 6 weeks, followed by incremental reintroduction of flexion in an HKB. At final follow-up exam, six patients had no extensor lag, and 1 had a 3-degree lag. Final ROM varied from 0 degrees of extension (range, 0-5 degrees) to 120 degrees of flexion (range, 105-120 degrees). One patient had a preoperative flexion contracture of 5 degrees which remained unchanged at final follow-up. One patient received a static spacer and was thus limited in postoperative ROM. One patient, patient #5, had a recurrent EMD and was ultimately lost to follow-up. Patient characteristics and outcomes are outlined in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Patient outcomes.

Patient #5 was a 62F with morbid obesity (BMI 47 kg/m2) with a past medical history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, diabetic neuropathy, hypertension, and atrial fibrillation. She underwent an uneventful primary TKA and 1 week postoperatively sustained a traumatic EMD and was thus indicated for extensor mechanism repair. Intraoperative findings were notable for poor tissue and bone quality, a midsubstance patellar tendon rupture, as well as a traumatic disruption of the capsular repair. Two weeks following the procedure, the patient presented with erythema and an effusion. Aspiration of the knee was performed which was consistent with PJI. Physical exam was concerning for concurrent re-rupture of the patellar tendon. The patient developed septic shock, necessitating explantation of the right TKA, at which time re-rupture of their patellar tendon was confirmed. A static antibiotic spacer was placed. After 6 months of clinical follow-up and confirmation of eradication of PJI, the patient was ultimately lost to follow-up despite multiple contact attempts.

Discussion

Historically, direct repair of patellar and quadriceps tendon ruptures in the setting of TKA has demonstrated poor clinical outcomes with 63% and 22 % failure rate, respectively [16]. A 40% failure rate on 50 consecutive complete extensor mechanism allograft reconstructions has also been reported [17]. A multicenter study from 2023 examining extensor mechanism reconstructions with either mesh or whole extensor mechanism allografts demonstrated only a 50% success rate at 3.2 years mean follow-up [18]. A single-center mesh reconstruction cohort demonstrated an 80% survival at short term follow-up. However, other centers have continued to demonstrate approximately 50% failure rates for both allograft and mesh reconstruction [19].

Variation in methodology and definition of failure result in significantly varied survivorship and failure rates reported in the literature. When failure is defined as an extensor lag >30 degrees, need for revision surgery, or poor functional outcomes such as a Knee Society Score <60, survivorship free from failure ranges from 54 to 56% at 5 years [17,19]. Higher survivorship rates of 75% at 3 years, however, are reported when considering only in situ survivorship of the reconstruction without accounting for functional failures [18]. It is also important to note that septic vs aseptic cases demonstrate vastly different survival, with 83% survivorship in aseptic cases vs 58% in septic revisions at 10 years [20].

Functional outcomes reliably improve following extensor mechanism reconstruction in the setting of TKA. Mean Knee Society Scores have been demonstrated to increase from 30s preoperatively to 70s-80s postoperatively [[20], [21], [22]]. Extensor lag has also been shown to improve by a mean of 26 degrees following reconstruction, with final postoperative lag ranging from 5 to 13 degrees [[21], [22], [23]]. Patients can expect to ambulate independently in most successful reconstructions, with 64% of successful cases meeting the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score, Joint Replacement score above the minimum patient-acceptable symptom state for TKA [18].

Complication rates remain high in extensor mechanism reconstruction in the setting of TKA. Infection represents a major cause of failure, with PJI occurring in 17-21% of cases at 2 years [11,19]. Of those who develop a PJI, up to 41% may require salvage procedures such as arthrodesis or amputation [19]. Up to 43% of cases require reoperation, with revision reconstruction required in 14% and arthrodesis or amputation in 11% [18].

To date, reconstruction techniques with allograft vs synthetic mesh have not been shown to significantly impact outcomes, with both methods demonstrating similar failure rates, infection rates, functional outcomes, and overall survivorship. Autografts, such as that used in our technique, have shown promising results but remain underrepresented in the literature [24].

Extensor mechanism repair and reconstruction techniques require prolonged rigid immobilization in extension postoperatively, followed by gradual mobilization. Depending on the surgical technique utilized, duration of immobilization typically ranges from 6 to 12 weeks. Either long leg casting or use of a knee immobilizer can be used successfully provided sufficient duration of use and patient compliance. In a series of 33 Marlex mesh reconstructions, 6-year survivorship was comparable whether patients were immobilized in a long leg casting (67%) or knee immobilizer (71%) [25]. A larger series of 77 Marlex reconstructions out of Mayo Clinic demonstrated an 86% 2-year survivorship free of mesh revision utilizing standard postoperative protocols [21]. While no specific rehabilitation protocol has demonstrated superior functional outcomes across different repair or reconstruction techniques, abbreviated immobilization protocols and early mobilization (prior to 6 weeks) have been associated with significantly higher adverse event rates than fixed immobilization for at least 6 weeks. A series of 29 EMDs treated with Achilles tendon allograft reconstruction and an abbreviated postoperative immobilization protocol of 3 weeks demonstrated a 37.9% failure rate [26]. Likewise, a 2017 meta-analysis of 27 studies with 709 patients demonstrated that early mobilization produced significantly higher adverse (P = .02) and total (P < .001) event rates than late mobilization [27].

Limitations of our study include its retrospective design, which may introduce bias. Second, our series is rather small, consisting of only seven patients. EMD following TKA is a relatively rare complication, however, making a larger sample size difficult to obtain. Additionally, our study only presents patients with acute patellar fractures or patellar tendon ruptures following TKA. While our technique can be applied to acute quadriceps tendon ruptures following TKA, no such cases are presented in our series and thus our results cannot be applied to this unique mechanism of acute EMD following TKA. Finally, there was a lack of a standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocol which makes comparison of differential outcomes and results among our patients difficult to interpret and less generalizable to other institutions.

Our extensor mechanism repair technique utilizing semitendinosus and gracilis autograft augmentation for acute EMD following TKA has demonstrated favorable short-term clinical outcomes. Six out of seven patients demonstrated an intact repair at short term follow-up. No further operative intervention was required, and active extension was maintained on final clinical examination. One patient demonstrated a minor residual extensor lag, which was only 3 degrees. Advantages of this technique include biologic fixation of the autograft within the quadriceps tendon and preservation of the native distal insertion of the semitendinosus and gracilis on the tibia. Our cohort maintained active extension, even in the setting of patellar fracture displacement. In two of the four cases with patella fractures, there was either subsequent hardware failure or noted displacement of the patella fracture, but no deficit in active extension nor extensor lag was found at final follow-up. We hypothesize that the robust biologic augmentation of our repair technique provides a functional extensor mechanism even with patellar displacement. The use of autograft also obviates the need for allograft or Marlex mesh for reconstruction, allowing for natural host integration and negating risk of disease transmission and foreign body reaction seen with use of allograft or synthetic reconstruction materials [4].

Conclusions

In this study, we present our outcomes utilizing a semitendinosus and gracilis autograft for biologic augmentation of extensor mechanism repair in the setting of acute.

In patients with EMD following TKA, our repair technique demonstrates maintenance of active extension without the use of synthetic mesh or allograft tissue at up to 15 months of follow-up. Future studies examining mid- and long-term survivorship, as well as patient-reported functional outcomes are necessary to further evaluate the clinical utility of this technique.
