Sustainable Synthesis of Diamide Solvents from Succinic Anhydride under Microwave Conditions for the Production of Graphene via Liquid-Phase Exfoliation
Authors: Suphatta Aintharabunya, Suwiwat Sangon, Yuvarat Ngernyen, Francesca M. Kerton, Christopher M. Kozak, James Sherwood, Nontipa Supanchaiyamat, Andrew J. Hunt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liquid-phase exfoliation (LPE) is a viable method for the large-scale production of two-dimensional (2D) graphene; however, toxic solvents must be replaced. The microwave synthesis of sustainable succindiamide solvents, namely N,N′-diethyl-N,N′-dibutylsuccindiamide (EBSA) and N,N,N′,N′-tetrabutylsuccindiamide (TBSA), was investigated and applied to the LPE of graphite to obtain graphene. The yield of EBSA was 99.4% from succinic anhydride with a K60 silica gel catalyst under microwave conditions (160 °C in 30 min), while TBSA presented an 88.8% yield at 180 °C. The green metric demonstrated that this route has a significantly lower Process Mass Intensity (PMI) and higher Reaction Mass Efficiency (RME) values compared to previously published routes. TBSA demonstrated a ∼150% increase in the graphene and graphene oxide exfoliation (thickness <7 nm) compared to N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP). TBSA outperformed Cyrene in the LPE of graphene oxide. Graphene and graphene oxide nanosheets were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Raman spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and atomic force microscopy (AFM). The solvent surface tension, density, viscosity, Hansen Solubility Parameters (HSPs), and Kamlet–Abboud–Taft parameters (KATs) were investigated to determine the key parameters for exfoliation. The high dipolarity (π*) and hydrogen bond-accepting ability (β) of TBSA promoted significant graphene exfoliation, while its greater viscosity produced high-quality graphene sheets, with I D/I G ratios of 0.005 ± 0.02 for graphene flakes of <1 μm. Higher-viscosity solvents demonstrated a limited increase in the graphene concentration with longer sonication, but retained high-quality graphene. TBSA is a potentially greener alternative to NMP for LPE, leading to opportunities for sustainable graphene production.

Introduction

Graphene is a two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterial consisting of sp2 carbons. , A broad range of applications, such as energy storage, − catalysis, , and use in electronic devices, , have been identified for this next generation of materials due to their extraordinary mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties. , Graphene can be prepared using two processes: “top-down” methods (such as chemical vapor deposition and thermal decomposition of silicon carbide), and “bottom-up” methods (mechanical exfoliation). Among these, the liquid-phase exfoliation (LPE) of graphite has been identified as a suitable, low-cost, high-quality, noncomplex, and both laboratory- and large-scale production method.

A key point of LPE for 2D materials is the combined application of sonication and an appropriate solvent to overcome the weak interactions between the layers. The method yields graphene sheets that are stabilized by strong interactions between the solvent and graphene nanosheets; however, the selection of suitable solvents remains challenging. The common solvents for graphene production from graphite exhibit a surface tension in the range of 40–50 mN/m and Hansen Solubility Parameters (HSP) close to those of graphite (δD = 18.0 MPa1/2, δP = 9.3 MPa1/2, δH = 7.7 MPa1/2). The densities (ρ) and surface tensions (γ) are directly related to the LPE and stabilization of graphite. Moreover, HSP and Kamlet–Abboud–Taft (KAT) parameters were utilized to predict the best solvents and describe the interactions between solvents and nanostructures during LPE processes.

Generally, the most effective solvents for producing graphene flakes from graphite are highly polar traditional amine-based solvents, such as N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) or N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). A solvent with moderately high polarity (e.g., NMP, DMF) can provide a balance between exfoliation, overcoming interlayer van der Waals forces in graphite to separate graphene layers, and stabilization through dipole–dipole or dipole-induced dipole interactions with the graphene surface, thus preventing restacking. However, these traditional solvents have been reported to be reproductive toxicants and may damage unborn children. , Replacing both NMP and DMF with various greener solvents for graphene exfoliation is challenging, and any potential alternatives would require physical properties similar to those of traditional solvents but without the associated negative health profiles.

Cyrene has been reported as a highly polar aprotic green solvent to exfoliate graphene dispersions from graphite. In addition, Polarclean is an amide-containing polar aprotic solvent with high polarity and no detectable toxicity, and it has been used for LPE. The search for other sustainable dipolar solvents that can successfully exfoliate graphene and graphene oxide is of significant interest.

Water is an environmentally friendly solvent; however, it results in very low production yields of graphene. − Currently, the addition of stabilizers or dispersants in water has been used to prevent the reaggregation of graphene in water, such as surfactants − and polymers. − The strong adsorption affinity of surfactants leads to undesirable modifications on the surface of graphene and challenges their removal after the LPE process, , which can be particularly problematic for catalysis and electrical applications and significantly reduce the 2D nanosheet purity. ,,

Succindiamide dipolar aprotic solvents, namely N,N,N′,N′-tetrabutylsuccindiamide (TBSA) and N,N′-diethyl-N,N′-dibutylsuccindiamide (EBSA), were synthesized by using green synthesis, including succinic acid with a reusable heterogeneous catalyst. EBSA and TBSA have been used in organic synthesis, such as the Heck reaction, metal–organic framework (MOF) or material synthesis, and polymer processing. However, to date, these have not been investigated as solvents for graphene exfoliation. The presence of two amide groups leads to a high dipolarity. These solvents are reported to be potentially 100% biobased, derived from succinic acid and alkylbutylamines, and synthesized from biobased alcohols. Crucially, in silico analysis and a panel of in vitro reporter gene assays covering a broad range of toxicological endpoints suggested no significant toxicity in these new solvents; however, additional toxicity testing is needed to confirm such claims before widespread use. Nevertheless, the synthetic methods for producing EBSA and TBSA from succinic acid suffer from low yields and long reaction times with conventional heating as compared to those using succinyl chloride as the starting reagent. In addition, the current literature method utilizes dichloromethane as a solvent, which is banned by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), as it is known to cause liver and lung cancer. Therefore, synthetic routes were developed in this study using microwave conditions to improve the yields and reduce the reaction times. Microwave synthesis has been reported for the amidation of carboxylic acids using amines. Moreover, K60-700 silica is considered a sustainable heterogeneous catalyst for amide synthesis in solventless systems due to its high performance, low cost, ease of separation, and reuse over multiple cycles compared to other catalysts.

Herein, this work focuses on the sustainable microwave synthesis of TBSA and EBSA, marking the first reported application of these alternative solvents for the LPE of graphene and graphene oxide. This research demonstrates that these solvents outperform traditional solvents known for their toxicity, namely, NMP and DMF, by generating significant yields of graphene flakes with only a few layers (thickness <10 nm). Moreover, this research considered the impact of solvents on health, safety, and the environment using the CHEM21 solvent guide criteria.

Experimental Section

Materials

Graphite powder (<20 μm, CAS number 7782-42-5), all other chemicals, and solvents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. Succindiamide solvents (EBSA and TBSA) were synthesized following a previous report. Moreover, this work demonstrates the green synthesis and high-yield production of EBSA and TBSA from succinic acid and succinic anhydride under microwave conditions.

Optimized methods for the synthesis of EBSA and TBSA are reported in the Supporting Information, but a typical synthesis is as follows: the reaction mixture of N-alkylbutylamine (0.03 mol), succinic acid or succinic anhydride (0.005 mol), and K60-700 silica catalyst (200 mg) was added to a 10 mL vessel and placed into a microwave reactor (CEM Discover 2.0 microwave synthesizer using standard control: all parameters are controlled by instrument defaults) and then heated up to the desired temperature (140–220 °C) for different time intervals. After completion of the reaction, the reactor tube was cooled to room temperature. The catalyst was recovered by filtration, washed with acetone several times to remove the impurities, and dried at 120 °C for 30 min prior to being used in the next cycle. The viscous reaction mixture was distilled, and the yellow product was analyzed by NMR spectroscopy.

Exfoliation of Graphite and Graphite Oxide

Graphite powder (<20 μm, CAS number 7782-42-5) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. The graphite oxide was obtained after the oxidation of graphite (3.0 g) with nitric acid (7 mol/L, 50 mL) under reflux conditions at 90 °C for 3 h. The resulting sample was washed with deionized water until neutral pH and then dried in an oven at 80 °C overnight. Hydrogen peroxide was also used to produce graphite oxide. Hydrogen peroxide solution (30%, 50 mL) was added to a round-bottom flask containing 3.0 g of graphite and heated at 60 °C for 2 h. Graphite oxide was filtered, washed with deionized (DI) water, and dried overnight in an oven (80 °C). The samples oxidized with nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide were labeled as G-HNO3 and G-H2O2, respectively.

A mixture of solvent (10 mL) and graphite powder or oxidized graphite (10 mg) was sonicated for 15 min using an Elmasonic S 30H ultrasonicator (power: 280 W, frequency: 50/60 Hz, voltage: 220–240 V), and samples were chilled using a circulating cooling bath at 5 °C to prevent heating of samples. After sonication, the mixtures were allowed to equilibrate for 3 days at room temperature. The upper layer of solvent containing graphene or graphene oxide was carefully collected into a clean vial for quantification of carbon materials in the dispersions (in mg/mL), as determined by UV–vis analysis at λ = 660 nm.

Solvent Removal Strategy

Following exfoliation, the solution containing the graphene dispersion was transferred to a clean vial and centrifuged at 13,500 rpm for 15 min, followed by decantation to remove the solvent from the graphene. The resulting graphene was washed with 5 mL of ethanol to remove any residual dipolar solvent and dried at 80 °C for 3 days.

Characterization

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

AFM was used to investigate the thickness of exfoliated nanosheets (Park Systems, Model: XE-120) with a noncontact cantilever (PPP-NCHR 10M). Samples were drop-cast onto coverslips (diameter 12 nm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) preheated at 120 °C to reduce reaggregation of nanostructures. Samples were scanned at 0.1 Hz. The heights of different nanostructure areas were calculated to obtain a distribution of their thickness using XEI data processing and an analysis program.

Raman Spectroscopy

Graphite and graphite oxide samples before exfoliation were mounted on a hole tray, whereas exfoliated samples were prepared as thin films. Thin films were prepared by dropping samples onto a glass slide (diameter 12 nm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the selected exfoliation solvent to give thin films and then dried in an oven at 80 °C overnight. Subsequently, for EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene, heating at 80 °C in a vacuum oven for 2 days was used to remove any residual solvent. Analyses were performed using a Confocal Raman Microscope (HORIBA, Model: XploRA plus) with a 532 nm wavelength laser. The sample was focused on with a 50× microscope objective lens with a 25% filter laser power for 10 s, and a grating of 600 gr/mm. Spectra were recorded in the range between 0 and 3000 cm–1. All spectral data was measured five times and processed with Renishaw WiRE 3.3 software, while peak areas and heights were calculated using a Gaussian fit in OriginPro 8.6 software. The deconvolution of the 2D band involved fitting multiple Lorentzians in OriginPro 8.6 software with a range of 2500–2850 cm–1. The crystallite size (L a), distance between the defect (L D), and defect density (n D) were calculated from the intensity ratio of the D and G bands (I D/I G) with a similar flake size of ∼1 μm in width following eqs S7–S9, as shown in Supporting Information.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

The characterization of graphite and graphite oxide before and after exfoliation in NMP, DMF, DI water, EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene was conducted using a bright-field TEM image (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Model: Talos F200X G2), high voltage (HV) 200 kV, and combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). High-angle annular dark field (HAADF) imaging in an aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM) was used for evaluation. A mixture of nonexfoliated samples in methanol and a dispersion of exfoliated samples were dropped onto a carbon film supported on a copper grid. The exfoliated samples in EBSA and TBSA were diluted with methanol and dropped onto a holey carbon nickel grid. All samples were dried at 80 °C in a vacuum oven overnight.

X-ray Diffractometer (XRD)

Powder X-ray diffraction was conducted using an X-ray diffractometer (Panalytical, Model: Empyrean) equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm) to investigate the crystallinity of the graphite and graphene. The exfoliated samples were dropped onto a glass slide and dried at 80 °C in a vacuum oven overnight.

Fourier-Transform Infrared (FT-IR) Spectroscopy

The surface functional groups of nonexfoliated graphite, G-H2O2, and G-HNO3 were analyzed using an FT-IR spectrometer (Bruker, Model: Tensor 27) in the wavenumber range of 4000–500 cm–1.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

The elemental composition and oxidation states were determined by XPS (Kratos Analytical, Model: AXIS Supra+). The photon energy was Al Kα (1486.6 eV). The samples were prepared as powders on a carbon tape. The spectra were processed with the ESCApe program.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FEI, Model: Helios NanoLab G3 CX), secondary electron mode (SE), high voltage (HV) 20.00 kV, current 1.4 nA, working distance (WD) 4 mm, combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), was performed to characterize the surface morphology of nonexfoliated graphite, G-H2O2, and G-HNO3. For exfoliated graphene and graphene oxide, the upper layer of the solution was diluted with methanol and coated with carbon tape. The samples were dried at 40 °C overnight and kept in a desiccator for 3 days.

Contact Angle Measurements

The contact angle of water on the surface was measured to determine the hydrophobic characteristics of nonexfoliated graphite, G-H2O2, and G-HNO3 using a static sessile drop method on an FTA 1000 Drop Shape instrument B Frame system at room temperature. The sample powder was coated on a glass slide, followed by dropping 5 μL of deionized water generated by the automatic dispenser of the contact angle system.

UV–Vis Analysis

The amount of dispersed graphene was measured using a double-beam spectrophotometer (Analytik Jena, model: SPECORD 200 PLUS) and analyzed with the dedicated WinASPECT PLUS version 4.2.0.0 software using quartz cuvettes. The amount of graphene dispersed in the samples was estimated by using the absorbance values at 660 nm.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TA Instruments Thermal Analyzers, Model: SDT-Q600) was performed in nitrogen gas (100 cm3/min). The measurements were carried out using an open alumina crucible from room temperature to 400 °C, at a heating rate of 10 °C/min.

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS)

EIS was performed using a three-electrode system of a Biologic SP-50e Potentiostat connected to EC-Lab software. A Pt plate and Ag/AgCl were used as the counter electrode and reference electrode in 1 M KOH, respectively. The working electrode was prepared from a dispersion of graphene in NMP, TBSA, or EBSA coated on Ni foam by adding 5% w/v poly­(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) in the dispersed solution (1 mL), followed by heating at 100 °C for 15 min to obtain a homogeneous solution. The mixture (100 μL) was dropped on Ni foam and dried at 120 °C overnight or 3 days in the case of TBSA and EBSA. The frequency range was 0.1 Hz to 100 kHz with an alternating current (AC) offset within the range of −10 to 10 V.

The cleaned Ni foam (about 1.5 cm × 1 cm) was prepared using a washing liquid solution and sonicated for 15 min, followed by further sonication in deionized water and then ethanol for 15 min each to ensure that the surface of the Ni foam was cleaned.

In an attempt to improve the metrics and yield of the succinic acid route, microwave synthesis was employed (Process C), resulting in an EBSA yield of 99.6% after 30 min at 180 °C under microwave conditions, while TBSA was successfully synthesized with a lower yield of 74.6% after 30 min at 220 °C. This method demonstrates a significant increase in yield, reduction in reaction time, and improved green credentials of the synthetic routes to these solvents. The synthesis of EBSA from succinic acid under microwave conditions demonstrated a lower PMI value and a higher RME value compared to those obtained using the previously reported method, indicating a potentially greener method for a safer and sustainable solvent (Table ). In addition, succinic anhydride was investigated under microwave conditions (Process D). The yield of EBSA was 99.4% at a temperature of 160 °C, while TBSA gives a higher yield of 88.8% from succinic anhydride in 30 min at 180 °C, also under microwave conditions. Succinic anhydride produced higher yields of EBSA and TBSA because it is more reactive than succinic acid, creating strong electrophilic carbonyl carbons, while the acid form requires stronger conditions. The green metric of Process D presents several distinct advantages, including a lower PMI and a higher RME than succinic acid (Process C). In addition, the reuse of K60-700 silica was investigated for the EBSA and TBSA syntheses under microwave conditions using succinic anhydride, showing an excellent yield over five cycles (Figure S11 of Supporting Information). In fact, the yield only dropped to 89.4 and 87.2% for EBSA and TBSA, respectively, after five cycles. K60-700 silica has been reported as a weakly acidic and hydrophobic catalyst that promotes amide formation, where silanol groups are not protonated in inactive salt amine.

Influence of Solvent Parameters in Graphite Exfoliation

The concentration of graphene in dispersions of NMP, DMF, DI water, EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene was determined by UV–vis spectrometry at 660 nm, where the spectrum was not influenced by the solvents. ,− The optical absorption coefficient (α660) was 823 L/g/m for graphene and was calculated from the absorbance of graphene in TBSA divided by the cell length (1 cm, A/l) as a function of concentration. The optical absorption coefficients (α660) for graphene oxides produced from graphite via the oxidation with hydrogen peroxide (G-H2O2) or nitric acid (G-HNO3) were 1664 and 1162 L/g/m, respectively. This was higher than that of graphene and demonstrated an increase in absorbance at a comparable mass to that of nonoxidized graphite in 1 mL of solvent.

The dispersed graphene (15 min sonication) after 3 days of stabilization in EBSA and TBSA demonstrated exfoliation concentrations of 0.534 and 0.680 mg/mL, respectively. These solvents outperformed NMP and DMF, which exhibited exfoliation concentrations of 0.538 and 0.243 mg/mL, respectively. TBSA demonstrates a 30% increase in exfoliation concentration as compared to NMP. EBSA exhibited a slightly lower exfoliation ability than TBSA, whereas DI water demonstrated the lowest performance in graphite exfoliation (Figure ). Water is a poor solvent for exfoliation unless surfactants are added because the highly polar nature of the solvent does not match the surface energy of graphene, leading to reaggregation. Graphite oxides were also evaluated for exfoliation under the same conditions. Interestingly, EBSA and TBSA were also successful in exfoliating graphene oxide through LPE, followed by NMP, DMF, Cyrene, and DI water. Previous studies have demonstrated that graphene oxide prepared from graphite with strong oxidizing reagents, namely Hummer’s modification, can be successfully dispersed and stabilized in water due to hydrogen bonding with hydroxyl or epoxyl groups on the surface of graphene oxide. , This observation is in contrast to the current work but can be explained in terms of the incomplete formation of bulk graphite oxide under the mild oxidation conditions employed in this study, as evidenced by FT-IR, XRD, SEM-EDX, and contact angle measurements (Supporting Information). In the case of Cyrene, graphene was obtained at 0.517 mg/mL as compared to EBSA and NMP, while giving a lower graphene oxide concentration (Figure and Table S3 in Supporting Information).

To develop a deeper understanding of solvent properties leading to the stable dispersion of graphene, the surface properties of graphene were evaluated against the solvents used in the process. TBSA has a surface tension of 30.0 mN/m, which is lower than that of NMP and other selected solvents (40–50 mN/m) reported for graphene exfoliation, which is the energy required to overcome the van der Waals forces between two graphene sheets corresponding to the minimization of the enthalpy of mixing per unit volume (ΔH/V). , Similar surface energy values of both the solvent and graphene typically lead to enhanced LPE. Therefore, solvents that minimize the enthalpy of mixing would lead to greater exfoliation, and such surface energies can be calculated from the surface tension. In contrast, the surface tension of graphite was reported to be significantly higher at 65.0 mN/m (Table ). ,

Lower-surface-tension solvents may wet graphite more effectively, allowing intercalation between the layers and facilitating exfoliation. The density of TBSA was 0.97 g/mL, which is similar to the reported values for NMP. EBSA shows a surface tension and density similar to those of TBSA (Table ) but a decreased ability to exfoliate and disperse both graphene and graphene oxide. Cyrene displays slightly higher surface tension and density of 33.6 mN/m and 1.25 g/mL, respectively, compared with EBSA and TBSA. These results demonstrate that other solvent properties may influence the concentration of graphene in sustainable solvents such as ESBA and TSBA; therefore, an investigation of the Hansen solubility parameters (HSPs) was undertaken.

HSPs were used as a tool for solvent selection in graphene production through LPE. , HSPs represent the strength of intermolecular dispersion forces (δD), dipolar intermolecular forces (δP), and hydrogen bonds (δH) between molecules. ,, HSP parameters are most appropriate for nonpolar systems and have been widely used to aid in the solvent selection for polymer applications. , HSPs of a solvent and the material surface should be comparable to achieve dissolution, or, in the case of this study, exfoliation and the formation of a stable suspension. Graphite has HSP values of δD ∼ 18.0 MPa1/2, δH ∼ 7.7 MPa1/2, and δP ∼ 9.3 MPa1/2. NMP has a dispersion solubility parameter (δD) comparable to graphite, while amides EBSA, TBSA, and DMF have slightly weaker intermolecular dispersion forces. The δP of TBSA is 9.0 MPa1/2, which is close to the value for graphite, thus presenting a higher dispersion concentration than EBSA. The hydrogen bonding (δH) values of NMP and DMF are higher than those of the succindiamide solvents. In the case of graphite oxide, EBSA and TBSA also successfully exfoliated and presented a strong interaction between the solvent and the hydroxyl group in the structure of graphite oxide.

The three-dimensional (3D) mapping of the HSPs of the solvents, graphene, and graphene oxide enables the calculation of the distance between the solvent and material being exfoliated within the Hansen space, as shown in Figure . The solvent with the smallest Hansen radius from graphene should demonstrate the greatest affinity. TBSA is a good solvent for graphene dispersion, even though its polarity match to graphene in the 3D Hansen space (radius of 5.4 MPa1/2) is lower than that of NMP, EBSA, and Cyrene ( with radii of 3.0, 4.5, and 3.6 MPa1/2, respectively). For graphene oxide, DMF presents the lowest Hansen radius (5.8 MPa1/2), followed by NMP, Cyrene, DI water, EBSA, and TBSA (9.0, 9.8, 11.2, 12.0, and 12.7 MPa1/2, respectively). These results clearly demonstrate that other solvent properties are relevant for the exfoliation of both graphene and graphene oxide nanosheets.

Recently, Kamlet–Abboud–Taft parameters (KATs) have been reported to describe solvent–solute systems. KATs can be described using the three parameters α, β, and π*. The hydrogen bond-donating ability of the solvent is represented by α. The β parameter is the hydrogen bond-accepting ability, and π* represents the solvent dipolarity and/or polarizability. Each scale presents normalized values between 0 and 1. The polarizability of a solvent determines how easily its electron cloud can be distorted in response to electric fields, such as the π-electrons in graphene. Higher polarizability enhances the London dispersion forces, which are critical for exfoliation and stabilization, preventing reaggregation. EBSA and TBSA are both amides, substituted with larger alkyl N-groups, as compared to DMF and NMP, which therefore creates larger β values by electron donation, but at the cost of lower π* values (Table ). However, the β parameter is critical for governing the hydrogen bond-mediated stabilization of GO in LPE, as shown in Figure .

Cyrene was also investigated for the exfoliation of graphite and graphite oxide (Table S3, Supporting Information). Importantly, Cyrene demonstrated a graphene exfoliation concentration of 0.517 mg/mL, which was comparable to that of EBSA (0.534 mg/mL) and NMP (0.538 mg/mL), but represented a significantly lower performance than that of TBSA (0.680 mg/mL). Solvents with high polarizability can therefore form stronger temporary dipoles, improving adhesion to graphene surfaces and thereby reducing interfacial energy. The use of Cyrene for the exfoliation of graphene oxide resulted in lower yields than those obtained using both EBSA and TBSA (Table S3, Supporting Information). This observation can be explained by the lower β value (0.61) of Cyrene as compared to those of EBSA and TBSA, which is an essential property required for effective exfoliation of graphene oxide. Interestingly, TBSA had a higher dispersion concentration for both graphene and graphene oxide than EBSA. Although the KAT properties of these two solvents are similar, the butyl chains of TBSA result in a more hindered diamide group and lower polarity than EBSA.

TBSA has a viscosity of 37.51 mPa·s, which is higher than that of EBSA (30.00 mPa·s). The greater viscosity of TBSA yielded a high-quality graphene and was consistent with previous work demonstrating that viscosity is an important parameter for producing higher-quality graphene. Similar to Cyrene, TBSA and EBSA are ranked as problematic solvents according to the CHEM21 solvent selection guide due to the high boiling points (bp) of these biobased solvents, but these are a significant improvement over traditional dipolar solvents, NMP and DMF (Table S7, Supporting Information).

Characterization of Exfoliated Graphite and Graphite Oxide

TBSA can produce a high yield of graphene due to the favorable properties of the solvent; however, it is also necessary to evaluate the quality of the dispersed graphene. To aid in the understanding of exfoliation, the resulting graphene or graphene oxides were investigated by Raman spectroscopy, TEM, XRD, AFM, XPS, and SEM.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Bright-field TEM images of graphite before and after exfoliation with different solvents are presented in the first and second columns of Figure , respectively. Graphene with a thin sheet and a smaller number of layers (<7 layers) was observed after exfoliation. Furthermore, a hexagonal diffraction pattern demonstrating the existence of the (01̅10) plane of the graphene nanosheet (selected-area electron diffraction patterns (SAED) data, Figure g,k) was observed after exfoliation with EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene. Raman Analysis

Raman spectroscopy is a high-resolution and nondestructive tool to investigate the lattice structure, electronic, and phonon properties of carbon materials from the positions, shapes, and intensity of absorbance bands. The Raman spectra of the graphene nanosheets exfoliated by EBSA and TBSA show D and G bands at around 1340 and 1570 cm–1, respectively (Figure ). The D-band peaks originate from aromatic ring vibrations near sp3 carbon centers or breathing modes of defects in 6-atom rings, whereas the G band is produced from the stretching vibration of sp2 carbons. Intense G bands indicate the presence of graphitic arrangements and provide data on crystallite structures, whereas the D band is related to disorder in carbon structures and is usually strong in amorphous carbons and disappears in perfect graphite. In recent years, a 2D band around 2685 cm–1 originating from the D-band overtone has also been investigated, which indicates the double resonance (DR) transition in few-layered carbon structures. Graphene and graphite are characterized by varying intensities of their three characteristic bands. Amorphous carbon usually presents a D peak, which is absent in perfect graphite. The overtone always appears even in the absence of the D peak due to the double resonance (DR) effect of the D peak. , The few-layered graphene after exfoliation has a significant decreasing intensity of the D peak and an increase of the 2D peak for all solvents, comparable to bulk graphite. The G peaks of exfoliated graphene are slightly lower than those of graphite, which present the characteristic profile of graphene.

Comparison of Raman spectra of graphite and graphite oxide, including G-H2O2 and G-HNO3 before exfoliation (a) and graphene obtained by LPE using the following solvents: NMP, DMF, EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene (b).

In the current work, the evaluation of the shape and intensity of 2D peak was used to distinguish between the exfoliated graphene layers and bulk graphite, as presented in Figure . The 2D peak in graphite consists of two peaks with lower intensities compared to the G peak, while the 2D peak in a single layer of graphene has only one component with quadruple the intensity of the G peak. However, the splitting of the electronic bands of the 2D peak into four components indicates bilayer graphene after exfoliation with DMF, EBSA, and TBSA. The deconvolution of the 2D peak of graphene after exfoliation with Cyrene and NMP both shows two components with the ratio of red and blue components of 1:2 and 1:3, respectively. This indicates the presence of multilayered graphene sheets, as shown in Figure . , The difference in the 2D shape in the electronic band structure of Cyrene- and NMP-exfoliated graphene is due to interlayer coupling, which is the splitting of the transition of π and π*. However, the determination of the layer thickness using Raman spectroscopy becomes difficult because the the 2D peaks of EBSA, Cyrene, DMF, and NMP are similar in shape to that of bulk graphite.

Deconvolution of the 2D Raman band to estimate the number of stacked graphene layers using a 532 nm excitation laser line.

In addition, the spectrum of graphite oxide after oxidation with hydrogen peroxide or nitric acid shows higher intensities of the D band and lower intensities of the G band compared to graphite due to the formation of defects during oxidation. The formation of functional groups causes disruption of the graphite structure, and the p orbitals in the sp2 bonds transform into sp3 bonds during oxidation. However, the 2D band of graphite oxides still appears to be a disordered graphene oxide structure, consistent with previously reported work. After exfoliation with EBSA and TBSA, the deconvolution of the Raman 2D band presents five and four components, respectively, as shown in Figures S22 and S23 (Supporting Information), which relates to greater disorder in graphene oxide than in graphene. The ratios of the intensities of the D and G bands (I D/I G) of G-H2O2 and G-HNO3 are 0.113 ± 0.01 and 0.142 ± 0.02, respectively, which are higher than that of graphite (I D/I G = 0.093 ± 0.05). Thus, the graphite powder was oxidized, as evidenced by the FT-IR results and the lower contact angle measurements for graphite oxide compared to graphite.

The observed I D/I G ratio and calculated L a, L D, and n D of graphite have higher L a, longer L D, and lower n D compared to graphite oxide, indicating a decrease in the average size of the sp2 domain and a smaller distance between defects in graphite oxide. The increasing defect density resulting from the oxidation process involves carbon–carbon bond cleavage to enable functional group formation. Moreover, graphene and graphene oxide have higher quality in terms of L a, L D, and n D through the LPE process compared to the original bulk materials. Interestingly, TBSA yielded the highest quality of both graphene and graphene oxide compared to both traditional solvents (NMP and DMF) and Cyrene, which had previously demonstrated significant potential as a greener exfoliation solvent. Importantly, such measurements were reproducible, with results being obtained as an average over five Raman analyses. Solvents with high viscosity have been reported to protect graphene sheets from damage during ultrasound treatment. TBSA has the highest viscosity of the solvents investigated (37.51 mPa·s), thus providing an excellent environment for exfoliation and yielding high-quality graphene or graphene oxide sheets compared to other solvents.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

The XRD patterns of graphite and exfoliated graphene obtained using different solvents are shown in Figure . Graphite patterns exhibited strong and sharp peaks at 2θ = 26.41° and other peaks with weak intensity at approximately 2θ = 54.49°, corresponding to the (002) and (004) reflection planes of natural graphite, respectively. In addition, the intensity of the characteristic peaks observed at 2θ = 26.41° decreased, and the peak at 2θ = 54.49° almost disappeared after exfoliation due to the disruption of the stacking interlayer spacing of the graphite structure. The intensity result (2θ = 26.41°) of exfoliated graphene with TBSA exhibited the lowest intensity, indicating a small layer of exfoliated graphene following EBSA, Cyrene, DMF, and NMP, respectively, which confirmed the successful production of a few layers of graphene during LPE. The interlayer spacings (d 002) of graphite, G-H2O2, and G-HNO3 were 3.3714, 3.3604, and 3.3694 Å, respectively (Figure S24 and Table S6, Supporting Information). The results showed that graphite has a greater interlayer spacing than an ideal graphite crystallite (0.3354 Å). The d-spacings of graphite and graphite oxide are very similar due to incomplete oxidation between all graphitic layers. Moreover, graphene oxide with TBSA (Figure b) shows a broad diffraction peak at 2θ = 7.49° with a d-spacing of 11.7931 Å between planes after exfoliation. The increase of d-spacing after oxidation is due to the presence of oxygen-functional groups in the carbon layer structure. However, small peaks remain at 2θ = 20.34°, which indicates that it is not perfect graphene oxide.

Sonication Time Influences the LPE Process

Previous studies have identified exfoliation duration as one of the most crucial factors in graphene production. Therefore, the influence of sonication time on the LPE of graphite was investigated for up to 2 h in NMP, DMF, EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene. The dispersion concentration of graphene increases with sonication time, as shown in Figure . The amount of graphene in NMP and DMF increased gradually with sonication time, around 45% from 0.431 and 0.448 to 1.050 and 0.916 mg/mL at 120 min, respectively. However, the concentration of graphene in EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene demonstrates a less pronounced increase from 15 to 120 min of exfoliation, as shown in Table S7.

Function of graphene concentration and exfoliation time in NMP, DMF, EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene (a), and the influence of exfoliation time on graphene quality measured by the I D/I G ratio using Raman spectra with a 532 nm laser wavelength (b).

Although a higher yield of dispersed graphene was observed in NMP and DMF with increasing sonication time, the quality of the exfoliated graphene decreased, as shown in Figure b and Table S8. In the case of EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene, no significant increase in the I D/I G ratio values or loss of quality was observed during longer sonication times. Solvents with lower viscosities gave high graphene dispersion concentrations with longer sonication times, but resulted in a decrease in the quality of the graphene sheets. Higher-viscosity solvents, such as TBSA, can protect graphene sheets from destruction.

The mechanism of exfoliation with TBSA is consistent with that of other polar aprotic solvents for the ultrasound-assisted LPE or graphite. ,,, TBSA has suitable π* properties for wetting and intercalation to overcome the strong interlayer π–π stacking interactions of graphite. , This leads to greater interlayer spacing and weakens the van der Waals forces holding the layers together. Ultrasound creates oscillating pressure fields that form microbubbles that violently collapse in TBSA. The asymmetric collapse of microbubbles at the surface of graphite generates high-speed microjets and intense local shear forces, thereby overcoming the bonding in graphite to yield graphene sheets. TBSA has moderate to high viscosity, which is vital for the asymmetric collapse that generates effective microjets. Graphene sheets have enormous specific surface areas and are thermodynamically driven to reaggregate to minimize the high-energy solid–liquid interface. TBSA has a high β value, which enables the stabilization of high-energy edge sites, thereby reducing aggregation, a primary pathway for restacking. TBSA’s high π* and β values, in combination with its moderate to high viscosity, make it an excellent sustainable solvent for the LPE of graphene from graphite.

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS)

Initial electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted to investigate the ion conductivity of graphene sheets exfoliated by EBSA and TBSA compared to that of NMP. This demonstrates the lower diffusive resistance and higher ion transfer of the G-TBSA electrode compared to the G-NMP electrode (Figures S33 and S34, Supporting Information). This further supports the evidence that TBSA-exfoliated graphene is of high quality; however, further work is needed to fully determine the electrical properties of these materials.

Green Solvent Selection Guides of EBSA and TBSA

A selection guide for conventional and green solvents based on CHEM21 is known and has been adopted to assess diamide solvents. EBSA and TBSA, as novel biobased solvents, were previously not reported in the CHEM21 solvent guide, but the evaluation methods have now been applied to generate a score for comparative purposes. Safety, health, and environmental impact were used to categorize the potential hazards of solvents, with each score ranging from 1 to 10 (Table ). EBSA and TBSA, as new exfoliation solvents, were compared to Cyrene, as this was previously reported as one of the best-performing sustainable solvents in the exfoliation of graphene. , Importantly, EBSA and TBSA demonstrate no hazardous physical properties and currently pose no “hazard statements” that could be extracted from previous reports, and SDS. , Common solvents, such as NMP and DMF, are ranked as hazardous, whereas EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene represent problematic rankings. Overall, these two diamide solvents have health, safety, and environmental scores similar to Cyrene, with higher flash points that represent nonflammable. However, they are ranked as problematic because of their high boiling point (red shading of the environmental score) and the significant energy requirements for their removal by evaporation. Water is recommended for ranking; however, it gave low yields of graphene and graphene oxide in this study.

A significant issue with the use of polar aprotic solvents in the LPE of graphene is their high boiling points (NMP has a bp of 202 °C, while the bp of EBSA and TBSA are >250 °C), which can lead to the presence of residual solvents that may adversely affect the performance of the resulting materials or devices in which they are utilized. TGA analysis of NMP-exfoliated graphene after vacuum drying at 80 °C for 2 days indicated that the solvent residue accounted for 65% of the total sample mass (Figure S27a, Supporting Information). Therefore, it is important to develop sustainable strategies for the removal of such solvent residues from graphene sheets. One such method that could be employed uses centrifugation followed by washing with a low-boiling-point sustainable solvent such as ethanol. This solvent removal strategy was performed on graphene samples exfoliated with NMP, EBSA, and TBSA. TGA analysis confirmed no significant mass loss after washing, thus indicating that the high boiling point solvents can be removed by the combination of centrifugation, decantation, washing with ethanol, and drying (Figure S32b, Supporting Information). However, the impact of such a washing strategy on the resulting material properties warrants further investigation to determine the full impact of this method.

Conclusion

Succindiamide solvents, namely EBSA and TBSA, were sustainably synthesized from succinic anhydride under microwave conditions in yields of 99.4 and 88.8%, respectively. These solvents are efficient and sustainable for the production of high-quality graphene and graphene oxide sheets through LPE. The amount of graphene dispersions that contained only N < 4 layer sheets was ∼130% higher in TBSA than in NMP, with a graphene sheet thickness of 0.940 nm for TBSA, while NMP yielded thicker products of 1.155 nm. The defect density within the graphene and graphene oxide flakes reduced after exfoliation, and TBSA exhibited the lowest defect density, followed by EBSA, Cyrene, NMP, and DMF, as evidenced by the I (D)/I (G) ratio in the Raman spectra. TBSA demonstrated a superior yield of graphene and graphene oxide compared to NMP, DMF, and Cyrene, due to the high hydrogen bond-accepting ability (β) of TBSA. KAT parameters and viscosity were identified as crucial properties for exfoliation, thus stabilizing graphene–solvent intermolecular interactions and yielding high-quality graphene or graphene oxides. The CHEM21 solvent selection guide was used to categorize solvent greenness, presenting problematic rankings for EBSA, TBSA, and Cyrene because their high boiling points are associated with higher energy requirements for recovery; however, such issues are less significant in applications such as LPE. The high exfoliation performance of TBSA makes this solvent a potential candidate for the sustainable large-scale production of 2D materials. However, further scale-up of synthesis methods and full toxicity testing of such solvents are essential for large-scale industrial exploitation. Future research should focus on further enhancing the LPE ability of succindiamide solvents with the addition of sustainable dispersants. The utilization of 2D carbons from succindiamide solvent LPE in energy storage applications or electronic devices could open new avenues of research for sustainable materials.
