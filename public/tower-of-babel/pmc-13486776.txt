Enhancing the pumpability of solid-waste-based cement mortar: understanding the coupled effects of ultrasonication on rheological properties via support vector machine modeling
Authors: Liyi Zhu, Yucheng Jin, Zhikai Wang, Peng Yang, Kun Wang, Zhengbin Li, Wensheng Lyu, Shenghua Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolid-waste-based cement mortar (SWCM), prepared with ultrafine tailings and cement as the main raw materials, is widely used in mining backfilling and construction engineering. However, SWCM tends to form dense flocculation networks, inducing ultra-high yield stress and plastic viscosity that severely limit its pumpability. The objective of this study was to quantify the coupled effects of ultrasonic frequency (20–40 kHz), power (25–100 W), solid mass fraction (70%-78%), and binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio (1:6–1:10) on the plastic viscosity and yield stress of SWCM and to establish a support vector machine (SVM)-based prediction framework for pumpability-oriented parameter optimization. The results showed that ultrasonic treatment effectively reduced both the plastic viscosity and yield stress of SWCM. Under the optimal parameters tested here (40 kHz, 100 W), these two parameters decreased by averages of 39.33% and 34.27%, respectively, relative to untreated samples. Moreover, the established SVM model showed good predictive performance, with correlation coefficients of 0.9013 and 0.9322 for plastic viscosity and yield stress, respectively. This work clarifies the rheological response of SWCM to representative ultrasonic conditions and provides a data-driven reference for mix design and pumping-process optimization of solid-waste-based cementitious materials.Keywords: Ultrafine aggregates, Solid-waste-based cement mortar, Ultrasonic treatment, Rheological properties, Support vector machine

Introduction

Every year, vast quantities of solid waste are generated globally, most of which is disposed of through landfilling or incineration, leaving many potentially reusable resources underutilized. With technological advancement and increasingly stringent environmental requirements, the resource recovery of solid waste has become a major research focus in the field of building materials [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. As resource constraints and environmental regulations continue to tighten, the development of cement-based material systems using industrial solid waste to replace traditional raw materials has emerged as a key pathway for promoting the green and sustainable development of building materials [6], [7], [8]. Tailings are one of the primary solid by-products generated during mining and mineral processing. With advances in mining and beneficiation technologies and increasing requirements for resource utilization, the particle size of ground ore has continuously decreased, and tailings particles have also become increasingly fine. Long-term stockpiling of large quantities of fine-grained tailings not only occupies large areas of land but also adversely affects the surrounding ecological environment through dust dispersion, leachate discharge, and heavy metal migration. Tailings ponds themselves are potential sources of pollution in mine environmental management [9]. In recent years, the resource utilization of tailings has gradually gained attention, with reported applications including construction mortar, soil improvement, and ceramic products [10], [11], [12].

Ultrafine tailings can be used as fine aggregates in the preparation of cement-based building materials. For such materials, mixing uniformity, pumpability, construction adaptability, and forming quality are all related to their rheological properties in the fresh state; therefore, their rheological behavior must be investigated [13], [14]. Ultrafine tailings have a wide particle size distribution and a large specific surface area. When used together with cement as fine-particle components to prepare solid-waste-based cement mortar (SWCM), this fine-particle system exhibits a high solid mass fraction and frequent particle-particle contact, readily forming a structured network characterized by particle agglomeration, interlocking, and bridging [15], [16], [17], [18]. Compared with conventional mortar systems, high-solid-content SWCM prepared with tailings and cement as the main fine-particle components typically exhibits stronger non-Newtonian flow characteristics, accompanied by higher plastic viscosity and yield stress [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]. Higher plastic viscosity increases the internal frictional resistance during slurry flow, while higher yield stress means that greater structural resistance must be overcome to initiate slurry flow, thereby adversely affecting mixing uniformity, pumpability, construction adaptability, and forming quality. Therefore, effectively regulating the rheological behavior of tailings-cement slurries while maintaining a high level of solid waste utilization is an urgent issue for the engineering application of SWCM.

As a green and controllable external physical-field method, ultrasonication can induce cavitation, acoustic streaming, and localized microjets in solid-liquid dispersion systems, thereby altering particle agglomeration states, interparticle interactions, and internal structural connections within the system [24], [25]. When ultrasonic waves act on fresh SWCM, acoustic-pressure fluctuations cause microbubbles to form, grow, and violently collapse. These microbubbles, also known as cavitation bubbles, release intense shock waves and high-speed microjets during this process, creating localized high-temperature and high-pressure environments and inducing secondary effects such as acoustic streaming [26], [27], [28], [29]. This high-intensity mechanical shearing action can disrupt the flocculation network structure between particles in high-solid-content slurry, disperse particle agglomerates, and alter particle surface properties [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]. Therefore, by leveraging the physical cavitation effects of ultrasonication, the yield stress and plastic viscosity of the slurry can be reduced [25], [34], [35]. Superplasticizers address the same flowability constraint through adsorption, electrostatic repulsion, and steric hindrance, but their performance depends on dosage, cement chemistry, and competitive adsorption with other dissolved species [36], [37]. Ultrasound instead changes particle organization through a physical field without altering the mix composition.

Previous studies have shown that ultrasonication can influence the dispersion, sedimentation, thickening, and flocculation behavior of fine-particle suspensions such as tailings [34], [38], [39], [40], [41]. Zhu et al. [34] found, through flocculation-sedimentation and thickening tests, that ultrasonication can influence the sedimentation process in tailings systems and improve thickening efficiency by altering the structure of floc chains and the aggregation state of particles. In kaolin systems, Shi et al. [38] demonstrated that ultrasonication can influence the structural reorganization and subsequent separation behavior of fine-particle systems by regulating floc evolution. Research on rheological phenomena in food and biological systems under ultrasonic treatment has also been extensive [42], [43], [44]. Jafari and Koocheki [44], by observing the structural evolution of starch dispersions before and after ultrasonic treatment, concluded that the strong shear forces induced by ultrasonic cavitation disrupted the morphology of starch particles and their macromolecular chains, confirming that ultrasonication has a universal physical regulatory effect capable of significantly reducing the apparent viscosity and flow resistance of complex multiphase slurries.

Although the use of ultrasonic technology to break up flocs via the cavitation effect—thereby reducing system viscosity—has been extensively studied and applied in conventional cement-based materials and particle suspension systems, this study focuses on SWCM systems containing ultrafine tailings. The particle characteristics and rheological behavior of this system differ significantly from those of ordinary cement-based materials and conventional suspension systems, and research on the ultrasonic rheology of this system remains relatively limited. Furthermore, in SWCMs, the coupling effects among the solid mass fraction, the mass ratio of binder to fine aggregate, ultrasonic frequency, and ultrasonic power have not yet been fully quantified [45], [46], [47]. Therefore, the innovation of this study lies in quantifying the rheological effects of ultrasonic treatment in SWCM systems and integrating the results into an SVM predictive framework.

This study focuses on SWCM composed of ultrafine tailings, cement, and water. The specific objectives were to: (i) evaluate how solid mass fraction and binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio affect the fresh-state rheological properties of SWCM under representative ultrasonic frequencies and power levels; (ii) quantify the reductions in plastic viscosity and yield stress induced by ultrasonic treatment within the tested parameter range; (iii) construct an SVM-based model to predict rheological parameters under coupled material and ultrasonic conditions; and (iv) discuss the possible physical mechanism and practical implications of ultrasonic regulation. By facilitating the resource-efficient use and pumpability-related design of SWCM containing ultrafine tailings, this study supports United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production).

Materials and methods

Raw materials

The industrial solid waste material used in this study was ultrafine tailings obtained from the Sanshandao Gold Mine of Shandong Gold Group, China. This material is characterized by fine particle size, a wide particle size distribution, and a large specific surface area. Mineral phase analysis was performed on tailings samples using a laboratory optical microscope, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was also conducted, as shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2. Optical microscopy and XRD analyses revealed that the main components of the tailings used in this study included biotite, quartz, calcite, and labradorite, along with a small amount of chalcocite. The basic parameters of the tailings are shown in Table 1. The particle size distribution was determined using a Mastersizer 3000 laser particle size analyzer manufactured by Malvern Instruments (UK). The median particle size (d50) was 12.468 μm, with the particle size distribution ranging from 0.5 to 100 μm, as shown in Fig. 3. The cementitious material selected was P.O. 42.5 ordinary Portland cement produced by Shandong Shanshui Cement Group, China, with a density of 3100 kg/m3 and a specific surface area of 360 m2/kg. Tap water with a pH of 7.2 was used in the tests, and the water temperature was maintained at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C).

Fresh SWCM was formulated by mixing ultrafine tailings, cement, and water at specific mix proportions. The binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratios were set at 1:6, 1:8, and 1:10, and the solid mass fractions of the slurry were set at 70%, 72%, 74%, 76%, and 78%, respectively. A JJ-5 mechanical mixer (Wuxi Jianyi Instrument Factory, China) was used in the test. The mixture was stirred at a low speed of 60 rpm for 5 min. After stirring, it was allowed to stand for 2 min to allow air bubbles introduced during mixing to escape naturally, after which rheological tests were performed.

Test setup and ultrasonic system

An integrated testing platform comprising a rheometer, an ultrasonic generator system, and a customized test vessel was established in this study.

Rheological parameters were tested using a Brookfield R/S-SST Soft Solids Tester (Brookfield Engineering Laboratories, USA). This instrument employs a rotating paddle rotor system capable of accurately measuring the rheological properties of non-Newtonian fluids at low rotational speeds. The instrument was equipped with a V3–60-30 four-blade paddle rotor, with a rotor diameter of 30 mm, a height of 60 mm, and a blade thickness of 2 mm. Ultrasonic treatment system comprised an ultrasonic generator and externally mounted transducers operating at 20 or 40 kHz, with a rated power of 100 W. These frequencies represent power-ultrasound conditions commonly used to induce cavitation and acoustic streaming in particle suspensions and cementitious systems [24], [28], [29], [34], [38], [39], [40], [41], [48], [49]. Zhu et al. [34] investigated 17–25 kHz ultrasound and identified an optimized frequency near 20.4 kHz for tailings flocculation-sedimentation and thickening. A separate study of unclassified-tailings slurry compared 20 and 40 kHz and reported greater rheological improvement at 40 kHz under high-power conditions [35]. Accordingly, 20 and 40 kHz were selected to compare two representative ultrasonic regimes rather than to establish a complete frequency-response curve. This scope is consistent with the frequency-dependent cavitation thresholds and multifrequency responses reported in sonochemical studies [29], [49]. The applied power levels were 25, 50, 75, and 100 W.

Rheological testing procedure

Test methods for rheological parameters

The ultrasonic rheological coupling testing system mainly consists of ultrasonic generators, ultrasonic transducers, rheometers, and other equipment, as shown in Fig. 5. This study employed a controlled shear rate (CSR) mode for rheological testing, with the test procedure comprising four stages: pre-shear, resting, acceleration, and deceleration. First, a constant shear rate of 80 s−1 was applied over 0–60 s for pre-shear to minimize the impact of variations in sample loading and initial structural state on the test results. Subsequently, the shear rate was reduced to 0 s−1 over 60–90 s, followed by a 30 s resting period to eliminate mechanical disturbances in the mixture. Then, the shear rate was linearly increased from 0 to 120 s−1 over 90–210 s, and then linearly decreased back to 0 s−1 over 210–330 s. Measurements were performed at 25 ± 1 °C. Three repeated measurements were performed under each test condition, and the average value was reported. The container and rotor were cleaned immediately after each measurement to prevent cross-contamination between tests.

Ultrasonic treatment protocol

The ultrasonic treatment experiment was conducted in two groups: one group served as a control group, receiving no ultrasonic treatment; the other group underwent ultrasonic treatment. Two ultrasonic frequencies were used, 20 kHz and 40 kHz, with ultrasonic power set at four levels: 25 W, 50 W, 75 W, and 100 W. Rheological testing began immediately after the ultrasonic generator was started. All data were collected during the ultrasonic treatment process. For each mixing ratio and ultrasonic parameter combination, the test was repeated three times, and the final result was the average of the three measurements.

Data processing and svm prediction models

Dataset and variables

After the data obtained from the aforementioned rheological tests were organized, the SVM method was employed to establish predictive models for the rheological parameters of SWCM under ultrasonic treatment. From the entire dataset, 120 complete sample sets were screened. These samples covered various binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratios (1:6, 1:8, 1:10), solid mass fractions (70%, 72%, 74%, 76%, 78%), ultrasonic frequencies (20 kHz and 40 kHz), and power levels (25, 50, 75, and 100 W), encompassing all possible parameter combinations. All samples were randomly divided into training and test sets, with 110 sets allocated to the training set for model building and 10 sets allocated to the test set for internal validation. Stratified sampling was used to group the samples, ensuring that the full range of parameter values was represented in both sets.

The input attributes (feature variables) selected for the model were binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio, solid mass fraction (%), ultrasonic frequency (kHz), and ultrasonic power (W); the output attributes (target variables) were plastic viscosity (Pa·s) and yield stress (Pa). Separate prediction models were developed for plastic viscosity and yield stress.

Model construction and parameter optimization

Since the input variables have different dimensions and substantially different numerical ranges (e.g., the binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio ranges from 0.1 to 0.167, the solid mass fraction ranges from 70% to 78%, and the power ranges from 25 to 100), the Min-Max normalization method was employed to map the values of each attribute to the interval [1]. There is a nonlinear relationship between the rheological parameters of SWCM and various influencing factors. In this study, the SVM regression method was selected to establish prediction models for plastic viscosity and yield stress. The radial basis function (RBF) was chosen as the kernel function because it exhibits excellent nonlinear fitting performance and can predict rheological parameters under the combined influence of multiple factors.

The grid search method, combined with cross-validation, was employed for parameter optimization to determine the optimal combination of the penalty parameter C and kernel parameter g. With minimization of the mean squared error (MSE) as the objective, separate prediction models were established for plastic viscosity and yield stress. Model performance was evaluated using MSE and the correlation coefficient (R) to assess predictive accuracy and generalization ability.

Results and discussion

Rheological properties of SWCM without ultrasonic treatment

In the absence of ultrasonic treatment, the rheological behavior of fresh SWCM depended strongly on the solid mass fraction and exhibited Bingham plastic fluid characteristics; therefore, the rheological curves were fitted using the Bingham model.

Fig. 6 shows the trends in the plastic viscosity and yield stress of fresh SWCM under different binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratios and solid mass fractions. As shown in Fig. 6, when the binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio of fresh SWCM remained constant, both plastic viscosity and yield stress increased synchronously as the solid mass fraction rose from 70% to 78%. Conversely, when the solid mass fraction of fresh SWCM remained constant, an increase in the binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio led to a rise in viscosity, accompanied by a corresponding increase in yield stress. Taking the data with a binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio of 1:6 as an example, as the solid mass fraction increased from 70% to 78%, the plastic viscosity rose from 0.4366 Pa·s to 0.6765 Pa·s, and the yield stress increased from 34.78 Pa to 57.33 Pa.

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the rheological properties of SWCM

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on plastic viscosity

The cavitation and acoustic streaming effects of ultrasonic treatment can disrupt the flocculation structure within fresh SWCM, thereby altering its plastic viscosity. Fig. 7 shows the variation in the plastic viscosity of fresh SWCM under different ultrasonic frequencies and power conditions when the binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio was 1:6. Overall, under most test conditions, the plastic viscosity of fresh SWCM after ultrasonic treatment was lower than that of untreated fresh SWCM. The extent of the reduction in plastic viscosity increased with increasing ultrasonic power and frequency.

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the plastic viscosity of SWCM: (a) 20 kHz, 1:6; (b) 20 kHz, 1:8; (c) 20 kHz, 1:10; (d) 40 kHz, 1:6; (e) 40 kHz, 1:8; (f) 40 kHz, 1:10.

Taking fresh SWCM with a binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio of 1:6 and a solid mass fraction of 74% as an example, changes in plastic viscosity were observed. Without ultrasonic treatment, the plastic viscosity of this fresh SWCM was 0.5887 Pa·s. At a fixed frequency of 20 kHz, increasing the power from 25 W to 100 W reduced the plastic viscosity from 0.6186 Pa·s to 0.4896 Pa·s, representing a decrease of 20.9%. At a frequency of 40 kHz, the corresponding plastic viscosity decreased from 0.4994 Pa·s to 0.4153 Pa·s, indicating that the higher frequency yielded a better viscosity-reducing effect. The results showed that, under these mix-proportion conditions, higher frequencies corresponded to lower plastic viscosity levels, suggesting that enhanced ultrasonic agitation helps improve particle dispersion and reduce the flow resistance of fresh SWCM.

From the overall statistical results, the effect of ultrasonic treatment on plastic viscosity was more pronounced under higher power conditions. The average trends across different frequency conditions suggest that, in most cases, 40 kHz exhibits a stronger viscosity-reducing effect than 20 kHz. This may be attributed to the stronger localized cavitation and microscale disturbances induced by the higher ultrasonic frequency, which can more effectively disrupt the flocculation structures within fresh SWCM and promote particle dispersion.

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on yield stress

The yield stress reflects the structural resistance that must be overcome for fresh SWCM to begin flowing. Fig. 8 shows the variation in the yield stress of fresh SWCM under different ultrasonic frequencies and power conditions. Overall, under most test conditions, ultrasonic treatment reduced the yield stress of SWCM. The extent of stress reduction increased with increasing ultrasonic power and frequency.

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the yield stress of SWCM: (a) 20 kHz, 1:6; (b) 20 kHz, 1:8; (c) 20 kHz, 1:10; (d) 40 kHz, 1:6; (e) 40 kHz, 1:8; (f) 40 kHz, 1:10.

The mechanism of action stems from the disruptive effects of ultrasonic cavitation, microjets, and local shear on particle agglomerates and flocculation-bridging structures, which weaken interparticle attraction, mechanical interlocking, and structural connections, thereby reducing the critical stress required to initiate flow in fresh SWCM. It should be noted that under certain operating conditions, the variation in yield stress did not exhibit a strictly monotonically decreasing trend, indicating that ultrasonic regulation of the static structure of fresh SWCM is significantly parameter-sensitive. Fresh SWCM with a binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio of 1:6 and a solid mass fraction of 74% was selected for analysis. Without ultrasonic treatment, the yield stress of fresh SWCM was 43.7187 Pa. At an ultrasonic frequency of 20 kHz, when the power was increased from 25 W to 100 W, the yield stress decreased from 40.8365 Pa to 35.9405 Pa; when the ultrasonic frequency was adjusted to 40 kHz within the same power range, the yield stress decreased from 29.7687 Pa to 28.0880 Pa.

Analysis of the overall trends revealed that, under most operating conditions, the yield stress exhibited a measurable response to ultrasonic agitation. Ultrasonic treatment altered the viscous resistance during flow and also affected the static structural stability of fresh SWCM. At higher frequencies and power levels, the localized impacts, microjets, and shear disturbances generated by the collapse of ultrasonic cavitation bubbles were more effective at weakening the structural bonds between particles, thereby facilitating the reduction of the yield stress of fresh SWCM.

Mechanism by which ultrasonic treatment modulates the rheological behavior of SWCM

The experimental data were analyzed to determine how ultrasonic treatment may affect the rheological properties of fresh SWCM. Fig. 12 presents a schematic interpretation of the possible structural evolution under ultrasonic treatment. Without ultrasonic treatment, electrostatic forces, van der Waals forces, and bridging effects from early hydration products may promote flocculation and aggregation among ultrafine tailings and cement particles. The resulting particle agglomerates can form a three-dimensional network structure, which increases interparticle friction and structural bonding (A → B). When fresh SWCM undergoes shear flow, this network must be disrupted before flow can occur, leading to high plastic viscosity and yield stress. This interpretation is consistent with the observed increase in rheological parameters as the solid mass fraction and binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio increase.

Schematic diagram of the proposed mechanism.

When ultrasound was applied through the externally mounted transducers, acoustic cavitation and acoustic streaming generated localized shear, shock waves, and microjets within the dense particle suspension (C). These effects may loosen particle agglomerates and weaken the connectivity of the flocculated network, which agrees with the measured decreases in plastic viscosity and yield stress. This mechanism is also consistent with microscopic observations reported for tailings systems under ultrasonic treatment. In unclassified tailings, E-SEM images showed that ultrasound-treated underflow developed holes and cracks, and floc-size measurements showed that d50 decreased from 74.8 μm during free flocculation to 52.2 μm after 17 kHz treatment and 42.3 μm after 20 kHz treatment [34]. Together with the rheological trends observed in this study, these results support a mechanism in which ultrasound weakens the particle network, improves particle dispersion, releases interstitial water, and thereby reduces the structural and viscous resistance of SWCM (D1, D3).

The above structural evolution can also be interpreted from the perspective of water film thickness (WFT). In dense cemented-paste and ultrafine-tailings systems, WFT is governed by the balance among water volume, solid content, packing state, and particle surface area, and it is closely related to lubrication, flowability, yield stress, and viscosity [35], [50], [51], [52]. When ultrasonic cavitation breaks agglomerates and weakens flocculated networks, part of the water originally trapped in particle clusters can participate more effectively in surface lubrication. The resulting increase in effective water (free water) availability reduces direct particle-particle friction and helps explain the simultaneous decrease in plastic viscosity and yield stress after ultrasonic treatment (D2, D4).

In ultrafine tailings cementitious slurries, polycarboxylate superplasticizers reduce yield stress and regulate viscosity or thixotropy by dispersing flocs and increasing effective water content. However, their performance varies with dosage, solid content, solution chemistry, and adsorption state [50], [53], [54]. The effects of ultrasonic treatment measured in this study also depended on specific conditions: higher power and frequency generally improved rheological properties, but some mixtures exhibited non-monotonic changes. Unlike superplasticizers that act through adsorption, electrostatic repulsion, and steric hindrance, ultrasound disrupts the particle network through cavitation, acoustic streaming, and localized mechanical agitation without altering the admixture dosage. These two approaches may be complementary, but this possibility needs to be verified through direct testing, as chemical admixtures may interact with cement hydration and dissolved substances [36], [37].

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of ultrasonic treatment on the fresh-state rheological properties of SWCM. The influences of ultrasonic frequency, ultrasonic power, solid mass fraction, and binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio on plastic viscosity and yield stress were analyzed, and an SVM-based prediction model was established. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1)When no ultrasonic treatment was applied, the rheological properties of fresh SWCM were primarily influenced by the solid mass fraction and binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio. When the binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio remained constant, increasing the solid mass fraction from 70% to 78% caused both plastic viscosity and yield stress to rise simultaneously; when the solid mass fraction remained constant, higher binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratios resulted in higher plastic viscosity and yield stress.

When no ultrasonic treatment was applied, the rheological properties of fresh SWCM were primarily influenced by the solid mass fraction and binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio. When the binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratio remained constant, increasing the solid mass fraction from 70% to 78% caused both plastic viscosity and yield stress to rise simultaneously; when the solid mass fraction remained constant, higher binder-to-fine aggregate mass ratios resulted in higher plastic viscosity and yield stress.

(2)Ultrasonic treatment reduced the plastic viscosity of SWCM, with the degree of reduction increasing as ultrasonic power and frequency increased. Generally, under the same frequency conditions, increasing ultrasonic power enhanced the rheological modification effect; under the same power conditions, 40 kHz exhibited a stronger capability to reduce plastic viscosity and yield stress than 20 kHz in most test conditions. However, the response to ultrasonic parameters was not entirely consistent across different mix proportions, and under specific test conditions, the rheological parameters did not exhibit a strictly monotonically decreasing trend.

(3)The observed reductions in plastic viscosity and yield stress are preliminarily attributed to the disruption of particle agglomerates and flocculated network structures caused by cavitation and acoustic streaming effects. By correlating the rheological results of this study with relevant microscopic observations from the literature, it appears that ultrasonic treatment may have weakened the connectivity of the network structure and increased the effective water content available for particle lubrication.

The results indicate that ultrasonic treatment can serve as a potential physical regulation method for improving the fresh-state rheological behavior of high-solid-content SWCM. The established predictive models can provide a reference for optimizing SWCM mix proportions, screening ultrasonic process parameters, and regulating rheological behavior during mixing and pumping.

Limitations and future work

Several aspects deserve further investigation. The present study compared two representative ultrasonic frequencies and four power levels under a fixed rheological testing duration. Future work should decouple ultrasonic power, treatment duration, and energy density to establish a more complete process window, because cavitation thresholds and cavitation yield are strongly affected by ultrasonic frequency and operating mode [29], [49]. In addition, microscopic characterization, including SEM observation, particle-size evolution, zeta potential, floc-size analysis, and WFT calculation, should be integrated with rheological testing to quantify particle-scale evolution more directly. Because this work focuses on fresh-state pumpability-related rheology, subsequent studies should also examine hydration, setting time, strength development, durability, and long-term performance. Previous studies have shown that ultrasound may influence hydration, microstructural development, and mechanical properties of cement-based materials [28], [48], [55], and these hardened-property responses should be evaluated before field-scale application of ultrasonic regulation.
