What drives Malaysian medical students’ specialty choices? Findings from the MMSCI study

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe training of clinical specialists in Malaysia on completion of the undergraduate medical programme is long and expansive. In recent years, the training process has been plagued with issues of funding, recognition, insufficient training opportunities, and lack of uniformity in the delivery of the training. However, there is a dire shortage of clinical specialists across all specialties in Malaysia, especially with the increase in demand for healthcare services. An analysis of the intentions of Malaysian medical students to specialise and the factors that influence their decisions is currently lacking. This study aims to provide insights into specialty training and may inform the planning of human resources for health in the country.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study involving 859 medical students from 21 medical schools in Malaysia. This study was part of the Malaysian Medical Students’ Career Intentions (MMSCI) project. Data were collected via a self-administered online questionnaire.ResultsMajority of medical students expressed an interest to specialise (85%). The most favoured specialties were General Surgery, Paediatrics, and Obstetrics and Gynaecology. Medical students from private universities had a greater intention to specialise compared to public university students (p < 0.001). Male medical students were more likely to select a pure surgical specialty as compared to medical-based specialties (p = 0.037). Female medical students rated “Working Environment & Conditions” and “Work–Life Balance” as important factors for the choice of specialty training (p = 0.044 and p = 0.032, respectively).ConclusionMajority of Malaysian medical students expressed intentions to specialise. The preferences in specialty choices in our study reflect the global trends. Passion and interest were the top reasons for medical students when indicating their specialty of choice. Further studies are needed to explore the changing trends in specialty choices and the influencing factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12960-025-01042-y.

Introduction

Upon completion of basic medical education, doctors may choose to pursue specialty and subspecialty training, while others may not. In general, specialty programmes can be categorised into medical-based, surgical-based, para-clinical and public health. Globally, gaining entry into specialty training and progressing within the programme are becoming increasingly difficult, driven by various challenges [1–3].

In Malaysia, upon graduation from medical school, medical graduates will then enter the public healthcare service and serve as house officers (HO) or junior doctors for two years prior to obtaining a full registration from the Malaysian Medical Council (MMC) [4]. Subsequently, they progress as medical officers (MO) for one to two years of compulsory service. Entering into specialty training can take several years for the acquisition of desired entry requirements, depending on the specialty field.

There are currently 29 specialty fields recognised by MMC and listed in the National Specialist Register (NSR) in Malaysia [5]. In recent years, gaining entry into a specialty training has become increasingly competitive, with candidates going through a stringent selection process which includes entrance examinations, portfolio reviews, interviews, and assessments [6]. The postgraduate specialty training pathways can be generally categorised into 2 main pathways which are the postgraduate Clinical Master programmes provided by 8 public universities and 1 private university, and the ‘parallel pathway’ through the Royal Colleges of the United Kingdom, Ireland, or Australia [7, 8]. Upon completion of a recognised specialty programme, the trainees will undergo a mandatory supervised working experience period under the supervision of a qualified specialist. Following this, they will then be eligible to apply for specialist registration with the NSR and practice as independent specialists.

In recent years, specialisation in Malaysia has faced numerous issues and challenges. Prior to 2022, the terms of employment for most medical officers were prohibitive for sponsored specialist training [9]. From 2022 onwards, this was rectified but was only available for a limited number of sponsored training seats. Another challenge is the recognition process of some training programmes by MMC, preventing trainees who have completed their training in Malaysia from registering as specialists with the NSR [10–13]. Additionally, national governance issues caused the lack of uniformity in specialty training as well as limiting the availability of training seats in the country [14]. To further exacerbate the situation, the number of specialists resigning from the MOH has increased significantly by 57% over the past five years, with 917 specialists leaving between 2019 and 2023, excluding 2021 [15]. Many changes and improvements have then been planned by the government to resolve the specialty recognition issue based on the Medical (Amendment) Bill 2024 [16]. Moreover, the National Postgraduate Medical Curriculum (NPMC) has also been established to deliver a unified and structured curriculum for specialty training across Malaysia [6].

With increasing demand for healthcare services, Malaysia, like many other countries globally, requires more clinical specialists [17]. Currently, the country produces approximately 800–900 specialists and 400 subspecialists per year, the majority of these through sponsored training [18]. Based on the latest reported data, Malaysia only has 44% of its required number of specialists in public service [19]. Therefore, the projected need of 28,000 specialists by 2030 appears unattainable [18].

Studies exploring the specialty intentions of medical students in Malaysia prior to this were mostly based on single-centre studies [20–22]. This limits their generalisability and representativeness of the Malaysian medical student population and does not take into account recent developments in addressing healthcare issues.

In this study, we aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis of Malaysian medical students’ specialisation intentions and the factors influencing their choices.

Methodology

Study design and setting

The data reported in this study were part of the Malaysian Medical Students’ Career Intentions (MMSCI) study, which was a national and multi-centre cross-sectional survey conducted online. The study involved medical students from medical schools in Malaysia that were recognised by the Malaysian Medical Council (MMC) under the Second Schedule Medical Act 1971. The study adopted a non-probability sampling method and was conducted from the 5th of May 2023 to the 15th of October 2023. Ethical approval was obtained from the Universiti Malaya Research Ethics Committee (Reference Number: UM.TNC2/UMREC_2486).

Participant eligibility and recruitment

The inclusion criteria for the study were (a) Malaysian, (b) medical student registered in a public or private medical school recognised by the MMC and operating in Malaysia, (c) undertaking either the entire medical programme or clinical years in Malaysia. To increase the reach of the study and achieve a representative sample, we established the “Malaysia MedEd Collaborative” in collaboration with a national medical student society, the Society of Malaysian Medical Association Medical Students (SMMAMS). Thirty collaborators were recruited through SMMAMS from 18 Malaysian medical schools and were given an online briefing session prior to the start of the study. The collaborators were then tasked with distributing the online questionnaire hosted on Google Forms via various distribution channels, which included social media, internal email communication, and word of mouth. Furthermore, the online questionnaire was promoted and distributed by SMMAMS via social media, internal newsletter and internal email communication to ensure we increase the reach to the wider Malaysian medical student population.

We used the OpenEpi software for sample size calculation. With an estimated student population of 20,000 (4000 medical students per year) in Malaysia, at least 377 responses were required to achieve a representative sample with a confidence level of 95% and a 0.05 margin of error. To account for the use of convenience sampling and online survey, we multiplied the sample size estimate by the predicted design effect of two and determined the required number to be 754.

Study instrument and data collection

We developed a self-administered, 51-item online questionnaire based on literature, and used the online platform Google Forms to host the questionnaire. The questionnaire was designed in English language, as all medical schools in Malaysia used English language as the medium of instruction. The questions were formulated using Likert scale matrices, multiple-choice questions, and open-text entries. The contents of the questionnaire were validated by experts in the field of medical education. Subsequently, the questionnaire was pilot tested amongst medical students to assess the feasibility and relevance of the questionnaire. Feedback received was used to improve the questionnaire. For the question of “Reasons for deciding which specialty to pursue” which consists of 14 items, responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “very important” to “not important at all”. The Cronbach’s α was 0.935.

There were four sections in the questionnaire: (A) Demographic Information, (B) Career Outlook in the Ministry of Health (MOH), Malaysia, (C) Career Intentions after Graduation and Housemanship; (D) Specialisation Intentions. This paper focuses on the presentation and analysis of data under Section (D). The full questionnaire is included in Supplementary File 1: MMSCI Questionnaire. The list of specialties included in this study were based on the recognised specialties by the MMC under the Medical Act 1971 (Amendment 2012) and the Medical Regulations 2017. (https://mmc.gov.my/list-of-specialties-and-fields-of-practice/).

All participants were informed of the purpose of this research and had provided informed consent. All collected data were stored in a password-secured cloud-based storage system service account specifically created for the purpose of this study and are only accessible by the core research team.

Data analysis

A total of 882 responses from 21 medical schools were collected. We excluded incomplete entries with missing data. Duplicated entries with the same name, IC or email address were removed, with the earliest entry retained for analysis. Entries without informed consent and from international medical students were removed. A total of 859 responses from 21 medical schools were included in the final analysis.

The IBM SPSS Statistics version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) was used for statistical analysis. For items with a 5-point Likert scale, the lowest 2 points (e.g. Not Important At All and Not Important) and highest 2 points (e.g. Very Important and Important) were combined and presented together with the Neutral Score. Specialty choices were re-categorised into “Medical” (Anaesthesiology, Emergency Medicine, Family Medicine, Internal Medicine, Nuclear Medicine, Rehabilitation Medicine, Sports Medicine, Oncology, Clinical Radiology, Paediatrics, Transfusion Medicine, Psychiatry), “Surgical” (Obstetrics & Gynaecology, General Surgery, Cardiothoracic Surgery, Neurosurgery, Paediatric Surgery, Plastic Surgery, Ophthalmology, Otorhinolaryngology, Orthopaedic Surgery, Urology) and “Para-Clinical and Public Health” (General Pathology, Anatomical Pathology, Chemical Pathology, Genetic Pathology, Medical Microbiology, Forensic Pathology, Haematology (Pathology), Public Health). Descriptive statistics were applied to characterise the study population and key variables. Frequencies and proportions were used for categorical data, while non-parametric measures such as medians and interquartile ranges were used for continuous variables, given the non-normal distribution of age. Inferential statistical analyses, primarily using Chi-square tests, were conducted to examine associations between demographic characteristics and specialisation intentions, as well as factors influencing specialty choice. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with a 95% confidence interval.

Results

859 students from 21 medical schools in Malaysia participated in this survey. The participants consisted of 64.4% female, 41.3% were of Chinese ethnicity, and 25.8% originated from the state of Selangor. 54.4% of participants were from public universities, while 53.9% of them were in their clinical years. The median age was 22 (IQR = 2). A detailed breakdown of the participant demographics is illustrated in Supplementary Table 1.

Specialising intentions

85% of our respondents want to specialise, with almost half of them (46.3%) being “Certain” or “Very Certain” to specialise. The most popular specialties chosen included General Surgery, Paediatrics, and Obstetrics & Gynaecology. About one-fifth chose pure surgical specialties (19.9%), while the majority want to specialise via the public university Postgraduate Programmes (Masters) (54.6%). Analysis between gender and reasons to specialise

Gender and reasons: a sub-analysis

Among the list of reasons to specialise, there was a significant difference between gender for Working Environment and Conditions and Work–Life Balance. There were significantly more female medical students who selected Working Environment & Conditions and Work–Life Balance as important reasons (p = 0.044 and p = 0.032, respectively). Other reasons had no significant differences between gender. Among those who chose Working Environment & Conditions and Work–Life Balance as important reasons, significantly more female medical students selected Paediatrics (p < 0.001) and Obstetrics & Gynaecology (p < 0.001).

Differences in specialising intentions: a sub-analysis

Upon sub-analysis, we found that medical students from private universities had greater intention to specialise compared to public university medical students (p < 0.001). The top three specialty choices among private university students were consistent with the overall population, including General Surgery (24.6%), Paediatrics (22.3%), and Obstetrics & Gynaecology (23.3%). There were significantly more private university medical students who chose Plastic Surgery (p = 0.047), while public university medical students were more likely to choose Public Health (p > 0.001), Clinical Radiology (p = 0.017), Sports Medicine (p = 0.021), and Rehabilitation Medicine (p = 0.019). Discussion

Global trends in specialty choices

General surgery, paediatrics, and obstetrics and gynaecology were identified as the top 3 preferred specialties by Malaysian medical students, while the least preferred specialties were anatomical pathology, chemical pathology, and genetic pathology. When compared to the global trend, there have been varied results when it comes to the most preferred specialties by medical students, with several studies from the Middle East and Africa describing surgical specialties as most preferred, while Al-Beitawi et al. reported medical specialties to be most preferred [23–26]. Generally, the interests to pursue pathology-related specialties (General, Anatomical, Chemical, and Genetic) were low in our cohort of Malaysian medical students, and this trend is not unique to Malaysia. Globally, the interest in pathology-related specialties has been dwindling among medical students and doctors, with shortages of pathologists being reported in the UK, Germany, and Cambodia [27–30]. However, Taiwan has contrastingly reported an increasing number of pathologists over recent years, highlighting the need to intervene to prevent an “oversupply” of pathologists [31].

The low interest in pathology-related specialties among medical students could be contributed to by the insufficient exposure of pathology in medical school compared to other specialties, leading to a lack of understanding of the specialty [32–34]. Negative perceptions and stereotypes of a career in pathology further contribute to the decline in interest among medical students, which resonates with the findings of the study [34]. Herrmann et al. further described the need to modernise the pathology curriculum in medical schools [35].

Rise of artificial intelligence and its impact

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has rapidly evolved. The rise of AI use in healthcare may affect the choice of specialty among medical students. There have been several debates on whether AI may one day replace the role of doctors. The role of AI in healthcare primarily revolves around improving diagnostic accuracy, optimising treatment planning, monitoring diseases, and improving patient outcomes [36]. It is used to complement the work of doctors in providing healthcare services to patients.

Though AI can be applied across specialties, several specialties with routine and data-driven tasks are most susceptible to replacement by AI as the technology is most mature in these areas currently [37]. These include radiology, pathology, ophthalmology, and dermatology, all of which involve image analysis and pattern recognition. This may explain our study findings where less than 10% of participants selected these fields. Globally, several studies also reported that AI lowered students’ preference for radiology [38–40]. However, there were also studies that have reported that the presence of AI may not impact employability and may in fact stimulate greater interest among medical students and trainees to pursue careers in these specialties, especially when their misperceptions were addressed [40–42]. Doctors who utilise AI in their clinical practice will be at an advantage compared to their colleagues who do not [43].

Influence of demographic background on specialty choice

Male medical students were found to be significantly more likely to choose surgical specialties. This finding remains consistent with several literature as described by Santucci et al., Levaillant et al., Khamees et al. and Asaad et al. [23, 44–46]. Males could likely be driven by technical challenges, salary, career, and prestige, while females likely relate their career to societal orientation [45]. Past studies highlighted that the gendered division of specialties holds the societal association of women with family [47]. Reasons for specialisation revealed working environment and conditions, which are significant amongst female medical students. This finding also correlates with the statistically significant aspects of work–life balance and barriers to achieving specialty indicated in this current study. Khan S. noted that women are found to be pursuing primary care specialties while remaining minorities in surgical specialties [47]. A single-centre cross-sectional study in Malaysia previously conducted by Chew et al. also found female medical students to be more likely to choose medical specialties [20]. However, according to Wang’s findings, women made up most specialists in general internal medicine, endocrinology, and geriatrics but not other procedural specialties [48]. In an Asian context, despite the awareness of diversity, equity, and inclusivity, females are expected to possess many diverse traits and behaviours, shouldering a disproportionate amount of housework and childcare while still demonstrating the traditionally feminine trait of emotionality [49]. This is reflected in the results where female medical students are more inclined towards medical specialties, possibly due to the perceived thoughts that these disciplines have better work–life balance and work environment and conditions.

Medical students from private medical schools are found to be more likely to specialise. This is postulated by the fact that they paid a high fee for completion of medical school and hence, are more driven to continue to earn a better pay. This concurs with the systematic review by Pisaniello et al. (2019) which reported that medical student debt was associated with the pursuit of higher paying specialties [50]. Among private medical school students in our study, plastic surgery was significantly more popular, while public health was selected by a significantly smaller group as compared to their counterparts in public medical schools. Surgery and surgical subspecialties have been described as higher paying specialties in comparison to public health [50, 51].

Striving a balance in specialty training

Medical specialisation is essential for improving healthcare quality as this process enables physicians to develop advanced expertise, leading to better diagnostic accuracy, treatment efficacy, and patient outcomes. As medical knowledge expands, general practitioners face challenges in keeping up with advancements across multiple fields, making specialisation necessary to maintain high standards of care [52]. Specialists also drive innovation in healthcare, researching and developing new treatments and technologies while contributing to interdisciplinary teams managing complex and chronic conditions [53]. However, the increasing trend toward specialisation poses challenges, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where balancing specialist training with equitable healthcare distribution is crucial [54]. Effective specialisation policies must consider workforce needs, resource constraints, and access to continuing medical education to ensure sustainability in the healthcare workforce [55].

Globally, the demand for medical specialists is rising due to ageing populations, non-communicable diseases, and technological advancements [56]. High-income countries are expanding sub-specialisation programmes, while LMICs struggle with balancing specialist training and the need for general practitioners in underserved areas [54]. Additionally, medical students’ specialisation choices are influenced by factors such as career aspirations, work–life balance, financial incentives, and gender dynamics [45, 57]. However, this may evolve constantly throughout one’s education and practice, influenced by perceived prestige of the specialty, income, family background, and mentors [45, 58]. Recent studies indicate a growing preference for technology-driven specialties like radiology and surgery, reflecting shifts in healthcare demands [59, 60]. These trends highlight the need for a balanced approach in medical workforce planning to ensure both specialist expertise and accessible primary care are available.

Importance and implications

This study, a component of the Malaysian Medical Students’ Career Intentions (MMSCI) study focusing on specialisation intentions and perceptions, represents a crucial piece of research into the career intentions of Malaysian medical students. Specialist training programmes in Malaysia have progressed tremendously in recent decades. Prior to 1973, specialists training for Malaysian doctors was largely reliant on foreign institutions and international qualifications [61]. However, since the introduction of local postgraduate medical training programmes by Malaysian public universities and parallel training programmes by the Ministry of Health, most Malaysian specialists are now trained locally, serving the needs of the local population while not being dependent on other countries [61]. The introduction of the National Postgraduate Medical Curriculum (NPMC) in August 2021 and the amendment of the Medical Act in July 2024 were among several measures to further streamline and standardise the training process in the country [61]. Despite these initiatives, the country continues to face hurdles in the training of specialists. This study provides valuable insights for Malaysia’s human resource for health planning in general and offers evidence-based input for various speciality colleges and societies to develop long-term strategies aimed at attracting and nurturing the next generation of doctors and specialists—who are medical students today—into their respective specialties. This study also contributes to the limited body of literature on this topic in this region. Further research is needed to explore the gap in choice of specialisation between different genders and their wellbeing.

Study limitations

There are a few limitations of this study. Firstly, as a cross-sectional study, it captures students' perceptions at only one time point, limiting the ability to assess changes longitudinally. Selection bias due to convenience sampling may also be present, as the study would have attracted students with a particular interest in the topic or greater awareness of Malaysian healthcare issues. Moreover, as our respondents were medical students, their perceptions may be influenced by limited clinical experience and could change as they advance in their careers and life stages. All in all, this study was to give the voice to the students as they think and reflect on their career option.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a notable portion of Malaysian medical students intend to pursue specialisation in the future. This is an encouraging sign for the Malaysian healthcare system that is currently facing a shortage of specialists amid growing healthcare demands. The most preferred specialties by students are General Surgery, Paediatrics, and Obstetrics and Gynaecology, with passion and interest stated as the top reasons when deciding which specialty to pursue. On the other hand, pathology-based specialties (Chemical Pathology, Anatomic Pathology, and Genetic Pathology) were ranked as the least preferred specialties. Various strategies, such as increasing exposure to the specialty during medical school, should be explored by the relevant stakeholders to increase students’ interest in their fields. This is essential to ensure that the nation can continuously produce enough specialists across various fields in accordance with the needs of its population.

Future research direction should include qualitative studies to further explore and provide in-depth analysis of the factors influencing specialisation intentions. Longitudinal studies could also be carried out to examine how intentions and perceptions change as medical students progress through their careers and life stages. Lastly, regular studies on medical students’ career intentions are also warranted to provide invaluable data for continuous improvement of specialty programmes, long-term healthcare workforce planning, and ongoing health reforms.
