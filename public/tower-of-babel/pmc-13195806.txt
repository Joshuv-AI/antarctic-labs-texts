On the Low Abundance of Antibiotic Resistance Genes in Bacteriophage Genomes and Their Random Acquisition via Specialized Transduction
Authors: Praveen Kant, Bent Petersen, Thomas Sicheritz-Pontén, Kiran Kondabagil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe role of bacteriophages in spreading antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) has been debated for over a decade. Several questions regarding the ARG dissemination potential of bacteriophages remain. For example, do phages frequently carry ARGs? Besides generalized transduction (GT), could specialized transduction (ST) play an essential role in the spread of ARGs? To address these questions, we thoroughly analyzed the available phage genomes, viromes, temperate phages, and prophage sequences for the presence of all known ARGs and their genomic context. Out of the 38,861 phage genome sequences we analyzed, 82 phages were found to possess 141 ARGs in their genomes. Interestingly, a few of the Streptococcus phages were found to carry an entire ARG cluster with four or more genes. An uncharacterized Caudoviricetes phage was found to possess the complete vancomycin operon. In literature, the role of ST in phage-based ARG dissemination is often overlooked. Based on the presence of lysogenic markers, the terminal location of ARGs on phage genomes, and ARG clusters transferred to phages, we suggest that ARGs are predominantly acquired from pathogenic hosts by temperate phages via ST. These findings indicate that, in addition to GT, ST can also play a crucial role in phage-based ARG dissemination. Our study also suggests that the acquisition of ARGs by phages is sporadic. Overall, we propose that phage-mediated gene transfer is governed by a complex interplay of gene transfer bottlenecks and microenvironmental parameters, such as microbial density, diversity, and external stress, in addition to phage properties.

Because of their diversity and abundance, bacteriophages have been implicated in antimicrobial resistance gene (ARG) dissemination within microbial communities. Some studies have even suggested phages as reservoirs of ARGs. However, comprehensive research to delineate the role of phages in ARG dissemination is lacking. This study strives to fill this gap in our understanding of the ARG dissemination potential of phages through a thorough analysis of the available phage genomes, viromes, temperate phages, and prophage sequences. We show that only a handful of phages harbor ARGs in their genomes. Among the classified phages, the majority are temperate phages that infect a small fraction of mostly pathogenic bacteria. Furthermore, a thorough analysis of the genomic context of the ARGs suggests specialized transduction as a potent mechanism of ARG transfer. However, phylogenetic barriers and microenvironment parameters like microbial density, diversity, and external stress severely limit the role of phages in ARG dissemination.

Introduction

Genomic and metagenomic studies have shown the pervasive presence of putative antibiotic resistance genes in the environment (Parsley et al. 2010; Modi et al. 2013; Shousha et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2020). Antibiotic resistance can develop through either de novo mutations or the acquisition of antibiotic resistance genes by bacteria from the environment via horizontal gene transfer (HGT). Due to the presence of small quantities of antimicrobial residues in the environment, the selection of bacteria containing antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) has become more prevalent, making the spread of ARGs via HGT more rampant than other genes (Larsson and Flach 2022). The role of bacteriophages (or phages, viruses that infect bacteria) in the dissemination of ARGs has been a subject of evolving perspectives. Due to their intricate interaction with their host bacteria, bacteriophages have been implicated as a major contributor to the spread of ARGs in the environment (Rolain et al. 2011; Modi et al. 2013; Subirats et al. 2016). However, a study suggested that the presence of ARGs in phages may have been misrepresented or overestimated due to the less stringent criteria used for their selection (Enault et al. 2017). Additionally, several transfer bottlenecks, such as the ecological connectivity of donor and recipient populations, fitness costs, and the pre-existence of resistance in bacterial species in an environment, are proposed to further restrict the spread of ARGs in microbial communities (Martínez 2012). Despite these limitations, subsequent studies have identified phages as reservoirs of ARGs, implicating them in the dissemination of ARGs (Colombo et al. 2017; Lekunberri et al. 2017; Calero-Cáceres et al. 2019; Debroas and Siguret 2019; Moon et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2022).

Phages mediate DNA transfer between bacteria either by generalized transduction (GT) or specialized transduction (ST). In GT, phages aberrantly package host DNA into their capsids instead of their own DNA during the assembly process (Zinder and Lederberg 1952). ST involves only temperate phages, which integrate their DNA with the host bacterial DNA. The prophage (phage DNA integrated with the host bacterial genome) can acquire a small portion of host DNA due to errors in excision (Touchon et al. 2017; Chiang et al. 2019). In the discourse around phage-based ARG transfer, GT has been considered the default mode (Kenzaka et al. 2010; Shousha et al. 2015; Enault et al. 2017; Touchon et al. 2017). However, the contribution of ST to phage-mediated ARG dissemination to bacteria is poorly understood (Fillol-Salom et al. 2019; Arya et al. 2020; Leclerc et al. 2022). While some studies highlight the role of ST in phage-based ARG transfer (Kenzaka et al. 2010; Debroas and Siguret 2019), a comprehensive evaluation of this process has not been conducted. Besides this, transposons can transfer host bacterial genes to both temperate and virulent phages (Brown-Jaque et al. 2015; Qi et al. 2023). A recent study suggests an integrated form of phage and plasmid, known as phage-plasmid (P-P), can also promote phage-based ARG transfer (Pfeifer et al. 2022). A phage, when it undergoes lysogeny with a plasmid form P-Ps, similar to a prophage, can revert to an assembled phage state and, in the process, can acquire genes from plasmids (Pfeifer et al. 2022).

In addition to the mechanisms of DNA acquisition, it has been proposed that phages can play an active role by selectively acquiring and transferring functionally beneficial genes, including ARGs, from the host under antibiotic stress, thereby protecting the microbiome and preserving its functional robustness (Modi et al. 2013).

Although phages have been shown to mobilize and transfer ARGs (Parsley et al. 2010; Colomer-Lluch et al. 2014; Billaud et al. 2021), they have yet to be studied comprehensively to gain insights into their potential in ARG dissemination in the environment and the possible mechanisms involved. Here, we report the findings from our detailed analysis of phage genomes, viromes (a collection of viruses in an environment), and prophage sequences for the presence of ARGs. We detect the presence of 141 ARGs in 82 phages (identity and coverage % ≥80) out of the 38,861 phage genomes. We found that most of the ARGs are acquired by phages from their bacterial hosts via ST, suggesting that, in addition to GT, ST also plays a crucial role in ARG dissemination. By correlating the abundance of ARGs in hosts with their phages and comparing the frequencies of horizontal transfer of genes other than ARGs, we suggest that the transfer of ARGs to phages is sporadic.

Results

Sporadic Presence of ARGs in Phage Genomes

We analyzed 38,861 phage genomes downloaded from the NCBI Virus (Hatcher et al. 2017) and GenBank (Benson et al. 2013) databases. We used the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (Alcock et al. 2020) (CARD—a rigorously curated and regularly updated database of resistance genes, their products, and associated phenotypes) to identify the presence of ARGs in phage genomes. Because mutations in core genes that may confer adaptive benefits against antibiotic selective pressure may also contribute to resistance, the transfer of such genes, in addition to specialized ARGs, was considered in this study (Martínez et al. 2015). While the presence of ARGs in phage genomes has been widely reported (Parsley et al. 2010; Modi et al. 2013; Shousha et al. 2015; Calero-Cáceres et al. 2019; Debroas and Siguret 2019; Zhao et al. 2022), a study suggested that their prevalence might have been overestimated due to less stringent selection criteria (Enault et al. 2017). Hence, we performed a nucleotide-based search (BLASTN) between the CARD database and the phage genome sequences. Although we used a BLASTN threshold of identity and coverage of ≥80% and ≥40%, all hits had an identity and coverage of 80% or higher. We identified 141 ARGs from 82 bacteriophages (NCBI panel, Fig. 1a). More than half of the ARGs were related to aminoglycoside, tetracycline, macrolide-streptogramin, phenicol, and macrolide classes of antibiotics (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, six of the ARGs provide resistance against multiple drug classes (ARGs providing resistance against six or more drug classes were considered multiple-drug resistant ARGs) (Fig. 1b).

Besides NCBI Virus and GenBank, we also searched RefSeq phage genomes (5,067 sequences) and the following environmental virus databases: IMG/VR (Camargo et al. 2023) (viral (meta) genome repository, 1,613,043 sequences), GPD (Camarillo-Guerrero et al. 2021) (gut phage database, 140,549 sequences), CHVD (Tisza and Buck 2021) (viruses from human metagenome associated with chronic diseases, 44,935 sequences), and MGV (Nayfach et al. 2021) (virus from human gut, 123,177 sequences) (Fig. 1a). The different environmental viral and temperate phage sequences were fetched from the PhageScope database (Wang et al. 2024). We found only two and one ARGs in the IGVD (Shah et al. 2023) (infant gut virus database, 9,738 sequences) and GOV2 (Gregory et al. 2019) (marine virome, 171,295 sequences) databases, respectively, while no ARGs were found in the STV database (soil virome, 4,018 sequences, data not shown) (Santos-Medellin et al. 2021). Additionally, we screened temperate phage and prophage sequences for ARGs. A total of 35,434 temperate phage sequences were identified using the TemPhD (Zhang et al. 2022) method, and 48,357 prophage sequences were extracted from the PhageClouds database (Rangel-Pineros et al. 2021) (Fig. 1a). The sequences count reported for all databases represents non-redundant datasets obtained using MMseqs2 (Pfeifer and Rocha 2024) with a minimum sequence identity of 95% (–min-seq-id 0.95) and a minimum alignment coverage of 95% (-c 0.95, –cov-mode 1). The total number of original raw sequences for each database is provided in the Methods section.

We estimated the frequency of occurrence of ARGs with respect to all genes [predicted using Prodigal (Hyatt et al. 2010)] in phages across different datasets (Table S1). Interestingly, maximum frequencies of 3.9 × 10−4 and 3.2 × 10−4 were found in the temperate and prophages sequences, respectively, in TemPhD and Phagecloud datasets, which are at least ∼6.8 to 5.7-fold more, respectively, when compared to all the phages examined in this study (Table S1). Although some databases had other viruses, in addition to phages, the frequency remains significantly higher in temperate and prophage sequences. While only two and one ARG were found in the infant's gut (IGVD) and the marine virome (GOV2) databases, respectively, no ARG was found in the soil virome (STV).

Our results showed a low abundance of ARGs in phages. However, a recent study estimated that P-Ps, a merged form of phages and plasmids, harbor 20 times higher ARG abundance than phages at ∼4.2% (using a threshold of identity >35% and coverage >50% for inferring gene exchange) (Pfeifer et al. 2022). A comparative analysis revealed that the fraction of genes undergoing exchange in phages, P-Ps, and plasmids was 4.7%, 27.1%, and 14.9% respectively (Pfeifer and Rocha 2024). Considering that only 4.2% of P-Ps carry ARGs, the maximum fraction of ARGs that phages can acquire is 0.19% (4.7% of 4.2%), similar to our estimate. Also, the maximum fraction of ARGs that P-Ps can transfer to either phages or plasmids is roughly 0.63% (14.9% of 4.2%). This clearly suggests that although the abundance of ARGs in P-Ps appears relatively higher, the actual transfer rate of these genes remains lower than expected.

Stochastic Acquisition of ARGs by Phages

The majority of the 82 ARG-carrying phages identified in this study belong to the genus Streptococcus, accounting for approximately 29%, while Escherichia makes up about 16% (Fig. 2a). About 42% of the hosts of ARG-carrying phages were from the class Bacilli (Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, Bacillus), 25% belonged to class Gammaproteobacteria (Escherichia, Enterococcus, Pseudomonas, Salmonella), and ∼1% to class Erysipelotrichia (Erysipelothrix) (Fig. 2a). The hosts of ∼34% of ARG-carrying phages are not known. We attempted host prediction using the iPHoP tool and CRISPR-spacer sequence analysis; however, this analysis could not resolve the host to the genus level.

The ARG-carrying phages were further analyzed to understand if there is any correspondence between the abundance of ARGs in phages (i) with the abundance of ARGs in their respective bacterial genus and (ii) the abundance of phages infecting a particular bacterial genus. We hypothesized that if the transfer of ARGs from bacteria to phages is random, the resulting ARG abundance in phages would be in proportion to the ARG and phage abundance in host bacteria.

For this analysis, we utilized the CARD “Prevalence, Genomes, & Variants” data, which comprises a total of 20,041 ARGs associated with 166 distinct bacterial genera. We considered the top 20 ARG-abundant bacterial genera that carry at least 1% of the ARGs corresponding to 205 or more ARGs present in the database (Fig. 2b and Table S2). Similarly, we considered bacterial genera with at least 1% of the total phages as phage-abundant bacteria (data obtained from RefSeq NCBI Virus database and metadata information from IMGVR v4 database). A total of 19 bacterial genera met this criterion (Fig. 2c, Table S3). Most phage-abundant bacteria belong to class Gammaproteobacteria (24%), Bacteroidia (11%), Bacilli (9%), Actinomycetes (1.7%), and Negativicutes (1.1%).

We found that hosts of ARG-carrying phages are also among the ARG-abundant (compare the boxed region in Fig. 2a with the boxed region in Fig. 2b), and phage-abundant bacteria (compare the boxed region in Fig. 2a with the boxed area in Fig. 2c). Our findings further indicate that Gammaproteobacteria and Bacilli possess a large number of ARGs and are hosts to a large number of phages.

Interestingly, many well-known human pathogens, including Klebsiella and Acinetobacter spp. carry a large number of ARGs (Fig. 2b); however, none of their phages were found to carry ARGs. Hence, they are outside the “boxed” region in Fig. 2b. On the other hand, many bacterial species, which are hosts to ARG-carrying phages (Fig. 2c, outside the dotted box), do not carry as many ARGs in their genomes as some of the human pathogens, such as Klebsiella and Acinetobacter spp. This suggests that, in addition to ARG and phage abundance in the host, several other factors could influence the acquisition of ARGs by phages, such as external stressor (Cochran et al. 1998; Goerke et al. 2006), lysogenic lifecycle (Brüssow et al. 2004; Chiang et al. 2019), capsid volume (Nurmemmedov et al. 2012), phage-host range (de Jonge et al. 2019), host defense (Hampton et al. 2020) and, microbial diversity and density of the environment (Knowles et al. 2016; Erez et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2019) (please see Discussion for more details). Overall, the relationship between ARG-carrying phages and bacteria abundant in ARGs and phages indicates that the transfer of ARGs from host bacteria to phages is likely a stochastic process.

A Comparison of the Frequency of Acquisition of ARGs with Respect to Other Host Genes

Our data suggest that Streptococcus and Escherichia are the most common hosts of the phages carrying ARGs (Fig. 2). To investigate whether ARGs are preferentially acquired by phages, as an adaptive process (Rolain et al. 2011; Modi et al. 2013; Subirats et al. 2016) or acquired randomly like other host genes (a non-specific process), we also compared the number of ARGs with those of other genes acquired horizontally by phages of these two genera (the dataset used for this analysis was obtained from the “reviewed” and “RefSeq” databases of UniProt and NCBI Virus, respectively; see the Methods section for details). Out of the total 72,098 and 29,447 proteins in Streptococcus and Escherichia genera, respectively, 187 (0.26%) and 600 (0.68%) annotated proteins have been transferred to their respective phages, and 63 and 24 are the ARGs transferred between these two host-phage pairs.

We categorized all the horizontally transferred non-ARGs as clusters of orthologous groups (COGs) (Fig. 3). Genes with similar functions are typically located adjacent to each other and are more likely to move together (Lawrence 1999; Lal et al. 2008). Most of the genes transferred to phages of the two bacterial genera are uncharacterized. In Streptococcus, although ARGs are one of the most abundant groups transferred (63 out of 187 genes, 34%), the number of ARGs acquired by their phages is comparable to that of the COGs labeled as L (replication and repair) (58 out of 187 genes, 31%) as well as G (carbohydrate metabolism and transport) (27 out of 187 genes, 14%), with respect to other COGs. Similarly, in Escherichia phages, COGs L, K (transcription), and M (Cell wall membrane/envelope biogenesis) were the most abundant genes acquired from their hosts with abundance 185 out of 600 genes i.e. 31%, 87 out of 600 genes i.e.15%, and 73 out of 600 genes i.e. 12%, respectively, while ARG abundance was 4 out of 600 gene i.e. 4% (Fig. 3). This result implies that in Streptococcus and Escherichia phages, ARGs were not disproportionately enriched relative to other COG categories, suggesting lack of specific bias in the ARG acquisition. The transfer of more genes from the Escherichia genus to their phages, when compared to Streptococcus, may reflect the larger genome size and greater number of genes in Escherichia (the Escherichia genome is more than twice the size of the Streptococcus genome).

Role of ST in ARG Acquisition in Phage

We also examined the genomes of phages for potential markers of lysogeny. Typically, site-specific recombination systems that employ diverse components facilitate lysogeny (Sadowski 1986; Suzuki et al. 2020). We included all of them in our study, namely, int (integrase)/recombinase (serine and tyrosine recombinase) (Groth and Calos 2004), xis (excisionase), cI (repressor—represses expression of all other phage genes), cII (activator—for cI and int), cIII (protects cII from degradation), and attP (attachment site in phage genome) (Nash 1981; Suzuki et al. 2020; Willey et al. 2020). Since phage integrase could be confused with bacterial integrase (involved in transposition), we identified phage integrase by examining for the presence of tyrosine/serine integrases and analyzing the region adjacent to the integrase gene. If this region contains annotated phage genes, we classified it as a phage integrase. If the genes in the flanking regions were hypothetical, we attempted to annotate them using the transposon database [TnCentral (Ross et al. 2021)], which helped assess the phage origin of the integrase gene. Since our observations suggest that integrase/recombinase is the most reliable marker of lysogeny, we considered the presence of one or more of these markers as a signature of lysogeny (Nash 1981; Emerson et al. 2012; Silveira et al. 2021). About 84% of the ARG-carrying phages (including those with partial genomes) turned out to be temperate (Table S4). This finding led us to hypothesize that these phages might have acquired ARGs via ST.

To further confirm this, we located the position/s of the ARGs in complete phage genomes. We found that about 45% (37) of the ARGs are present in the terminal regions of the genome (Fig. 4a). Similarly, 48% (382) of ARGs in TemPhD and ∼50% (200) of ARGs in PhageCloud were found toward the termini (Fig. 4b and c). Using the Chi2-test, we confirmed that this terminal abundance is statistically significant rather than a random distribution. The P-values for the three datasets were significant: 2 × 10−6 for NCBI complete phages, 3 × 10−7 for temperate phages (TemPhD), and 3 × 10−4 for prophages (PhageCloud). In general, lysogeny markers such as integrase, excisionase, and attP are located near the termini of the phage genome and facilitate phage genome integration and excision during lysogeny (Nash 1981; Warren et al. 2013). Also, the presence of ARGs in different regions of the genome suggests that other mechanisms, such as homologous recombination between similar sequences in phage and bacteria during lysogeny and non-homologous recombination via mobilomes like integrons and transposons (Brüssow et al. 2004; Qi et al. 2023), are also operative.

Location of ARGs in a) phages with complete genomes, b) temperate phage genomes (from TemPhD database), and c) prophage sequences (from PhageCloud database).

When we investigated the identity of the genes in the flanking regions (5 kb upstream and downstream) of the phage ARGs, we found colocalization of mobile genetic elements (MGEs) and ARGs in ∼34% of the ARG-carrying phages (Table S4). MGEs help in the translocation of genes within or between genomes. Phages would have acquired these MGEs along with ARGs. The MGEs found around phage ARGs include TnpV, TnpW, Tn916, Mu and Mos transposase, IS629, Tn5251, Tn2, Tn1270, IS1380, IS5, IS1, and IS1216 (Table S4). Their presence is considered to increase dissemination potential and the “risk factor” of ARGs (Martínez et al. 2015).

Touchon et al. (2016) reported that bacteria with fast doubling times tend to carry more prophages. We found that most phages carrying ARGs in our dataset were temperate phages (Table S4). The hosts of these temperate phages turned out to be fast-growing pathogenic bacteria when we compared our dataset with the dataset used by Touchon et al. (Table S5). This result suggests a prevalence of temperate phage-carrying ARGs in fast-growing pathogenic hosts, potentially reflecting opportunities for host gene capture via ST.

Multiple ARGs Present in Phages are Part of the Gene Cluster

If four or more ARGs are present in close proximity, they could confer resistance to a single or multiple antibiotics, and we consider them an ARG cluster. From our analysis, the proximity is approximately within 10% of the genome. We identified only two distinct ARG clusters: one in Streptococcus phages and the other in an unclassified Caudoviricetes phage (Fig. 5). The Streptococcus phage cluster (APH(3′)-IIIa, SAT-4, and (6), ErmB) was found in four Streptococcus phages out of the total 878 Streptococcus phages we analyzed. These clusters are identical and appear toward the end of the genomes, suggesting that the transfer may have occurred only once (Fig. 5). We also observed the presence of the same cluster in their probable hosts, S. suis, S. dysgalactiae, S. acidominimus, and S. oriscaviae (Table S6). A complete vancomycin resistance operon (Depardieu et al. 2015; McGuinness et al. 2017) was found in an unclassified Caudoviricetes phage (Fig. 5). To our knowledge, these two are the only examples of complete ARG operons acquired by phages.

Furthermore, to determine whether the ARG clusters identified in the phage were indigenous or acquired horizontally, we used tetranucleotide frequencies as a genomic feature to compare the genomic composition of ARGs and phage core genes (PCGs) (Noble et al. 1998; Becq et al. 2010). This approach leverages the idea that sequences from different organisms exhibit characteristic compositional differences (Kariin and Burge 1995). For this analysis, we used the terminase large and small subunits, the portal protein, and the major capsid protein as representatives of PCGs, as these genes are essential components of phage structure. The PCA revealed a distinct clustering pattern of visually separate clusters of ARGs and PCGs across five phages (P1-P5) (Fig. 6). The principal components (PC1 and PC2) captured 43.2% to 53.7% of the total variance across the five phages, indicating they captured major patterns in the two groups.

A comparison of the tetranucleotide frequency of ARGs and phage core genes (PCGs) . The differences between the two groups were studied using tetranucleotide frequencies and represented using a PCA plot for five phages (P1 to P5) carrying ARG clusters. Each panel shows the first two principal components, with shaded regions representing the convex hulls of each gene group and triangles indicating the group centroids. The dashed line connects ARG and PCG centroids. The PERMANOVA analysis used to compare the two gene groups suggests that they significantly differ from each other (the variation between the groups is three times the variance between the groups; F-value ∼3) with P-value < 0.01. Further, > 40% of the variance in 256 tetranucleotide frequency attributes was captured by the two principal components (P1 + P2).

We performed a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) test to validate the observed clustering pattern. The test suggested a statistically significant compositional distinction between the two gene groups, with an F-value ranging from 2.89 to 3.58 (the variation between groups is nearly three times the variance within groups), and a P-value < 0.01. R2 values ranged between 0.249 and 0.338, demonstrating that the gene type (ARG vs. PCG) explained 24.9% to 33.8% of the total variance in tetranucleotide composition.

The analysis revealed a distinct genomic composition between ARGs and PCGs in all five instances (Fig. 6), suggesting that the ARGs are acquired from the host. However, the lack of information regarding specific host species has prevented us from identifying the host bacterial source of the ARGs.

Discussion

The debate on whether ARGs are abundant or scarce in phage genomes and whether they contribute significantly to the dissemination of ARGs has been ongoing for over a decade. In this study, we carried out a comprehensive analysis of all phage, RefSeq, temperate, prophage, and virome sequences for the presence of ARGs. We identified the presence of only 141 ARGs in 82 phages (out of 38,861 phage genomes, i.e. 0.21%, Fig. 1) with an estimated ARG frequency of 5.7 × 10−5 (Table S1). The literature on phage-based ARG transfer suggests that GT is a prevalent mode of gene transfer (Kenzaka et al. 2010; Shousha et al. 2015; Enault et al. 2017; Touchon et al. 2017), while some studies also suggest a role for ST(Kenzaka et al. 2010; Debroas and Siguret 2019). Interestingly, the highest ARG frequency was found in the temperate phage database (3.9 × 10−4) (Cook et al. 2021). Based on the presence of lysogenic markers (Table S4) and the terminal distribution of ARGs (Fig. 4), we found that temperate phages have acquired a majority of ARGs via ST, highlighting it as a potent mechanism of ARG transfer. While site-specific recombination is the primary mechanism in ST, other recombination mechanisms, such as intergenic recombination (between two different phages) and host-phage recombination (P2), as well as intragenic recombination [in phage T4 (Petrov et al. 2010) and transposition, as observed in the case of phage Mu (Craigie and Mizuuchi 1985)], can also occur. However, this study was unable to evaluate the contribution of these mechanisms to the overall acquisition of genes by phages.

Genes involved in the same metabolic function or affecting the same trait are generally clustered together (Elizondo et al. 2009) and is a common feature of bacteria (M. Fischbach and Voigt 2010). Clustering also facilitates the co-transfer of genes horizontally, passing on complete functions during evolution (Lawrence 1999; Fischbach et al. 2008). In the case of ARGs, co-clustering confers resistance against a diverse range of antibiotics. We identified the presence of five ARG clusters, of two different types, and, interestingly, a complete vancomycin operon in one instance (Fig. 5). Furthermore, four out of five clusters were found in the terminal regions of temperate phages, suggesting that these clusters may have been transferred from the host via ST.

An earlier study on viromes suggested a direct correlation between antibiotic exposure and the enrichment of ARGs and other functionally beneficial genes (Modi et al. 2013). This enrichment in phages was thought to represent an adaptive capability of phages to aid the host under stress (Modi et al. 2013). We found a correlation between the abundance of ARGs in phages and the abundance of ARGs in host bacteria, as well as the number of phages that can infect those bacteria (Fig. 2). We also identified and compared bacterial genes other than ARGs that are transferred horizontally in Escherichia and Streptococcus genera to their respective phages, as these two genera were among the most abundant in ARGs and also hosts to most phages carrying ARGs (Fig. 3). When we compared all the horizontally acquired genes by phages (Fig. 3), we found no specific bias in ARG acquisition by phages of both genera. This promiscuity in acquiring host genes by phages can be explained by considering the known mechanisms of gene transfer, which are primarily based on homologous recombination, such as in ST, P-Ps, and transpositions, and are random and low-frequency events in nature. Therefore, while antibiotic pressure alone may not trigger the transfer of ARGs and other functionally beneficial genes from host to phage, selection may favor phages carrying beneficial genes such as ARGs acquired accidentally, if it helps the host to gain resistance. However, the very low frequency of ARG acquisition by phages, along with further bottlenecks in the phage-mediated gene transfer back to the host bacteria, as described below, strongly suggests that the selection of ARG-carrying phages is, at best, very weak.

Phage-mediated gene transfer between bacteria is influenced by both conducive pressures and restrictive bottlenecks within the microbial ecosystems. External stressors, such as antibiotics, high population density, and a lack of microbial diversity, are conducive to phage-mediated gene transfer (Fig. 7a). In a less diverse environment under stressful conditions and various selection pressures, bacteria with ARGs are more likely to be favored over others (Gullberg et al. 2011; Mallon et al. 2015). Any external stress like antibiotics and pollutants could trigger prophage induction, potentially increasing phage-mediated gene transfer within the bacterial community (Cochran et al. 1998; Goerke et al. 2006). A high-density environment can favor lysogeny by employing a quorum-sensing-like system called arbitrium (Knowles et al. 2016; Erez et al. 2017). Instead of eliminating the most abundant hosts, phages may adopt a piggyback-the-winner strategy, in which switching to lysogeny promotes long-term persistence, host fitness (beneficial genes such as virulence, ARGs, and metabolic genes), and protection against superinfection (Touchon et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2019). The eventual induction of a high number of prophages may accelerate phage-mediated gene transfer (Haaber et al. 2016).

Hypothetical illustration of Bacteriophage-mediated gene/ARG transfer. a) Comparison of ARG transfer in two different environmental conditions. Factors that are conducive to phage-mediated gene transfer include high microbial density, lack of diversity, and external stress (like antibiotics). These conditions trigger prophage induction, thereby increasing the probability of host gene acquisition and its subsequent dissemination. b) Major bottlenecks involved in phage-mediated gene transfer. (i) Phage–host abundance and host specificity: Only phages that encounter and infect compatible bacterial hosts have opportunities to acquire genes. (ii) Host defences against phages: Host restriction–modification systems and CRISPR–Cas immunity limit phage replication and reduce chances of mobilizing host DNA. (iii) Genome packaging constraints: Even after infection, only DNA fragments that meet packaging requirements (e.g. size and sequence features) can be incorporated into new phage particles. A limited capsid volume restricts the packaging of extra DNA. Together, these steps create bottlenecks that limit the efficiency and frequency of phage-mediated gene transfer.

Although prophage induction can increase the likelihood of host gene acquisition and subsequent dissemination of ARGs via phages, several ecological and mechanistic bottlenecks limit this process. Successful transfer depends on the effective interactions between phages and bacteria, which are strongly influenced by the population densities of both phage and bacteria (Muniesa et al. 2011; de Jonge et al. 2019). Although phages are generally believed to outnumber bacteria by about tenfold (Ashelford et al. 2003; Suttle 2007), their ability to disseminate ARGs is constrained. The narrow host specificity of phages and the low likelihood that temperate phages acquire ARGs or ARG clusters limit their spread (Fig. 7b). Additional barriers include bacterial defense mechanisms, such as receptor binding proteins, restriction–modification (RM) systems, and CRISPR-Cas (Hampton et al. 2020), as well as the compact nature of phage genomes and limited capsid volume, which leaves very little room for extra DNA (Fig. 7b) (Nurmemmedov et al. 2012).

Overall, our findings indicate that only a small fraction of bacteriophages harbor ARGs and that ST serves as a potent mechanism in mediating ARG transfer between bacteria. However, the spread of ARGs via phages is constrained by multiple factors. These include intrinsic bottlenecks in gene transfer, microenvironmental parameters such as microbial population density, community diversity, and exposure to external stressors, as well as biological properties of phages. Together, these determinants shape the extent and efficiency of phage-mediated ARG propagation.

Methods

Data Collection and ARG Identification

CARD database (v4.0.2) (Alcock et al. 2020) with 4,804 unique ARGs was used for this study. Bacteriophage nucleotide sequences were fetched from the NCBI Virus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/virus/vssi/#/) and NCBI GenBank (Nov 2023). Phage genome sequences were downloaded from the NCBI Virus database by selecting Search by Virus > All nucleotides > Location and Source > Host: “Bacteria (bacteria), taxid:2”.

This generated 38,890 phage sequences. Similarly, to fetch RefSeq data, along with the above host filter, we also selected Identifiers and Classification > GenBank/RefSeq, and downloaded the nucleotide sequence from the Nucleotide tab. This generated 5,067 RefSeq phage sequences. Phage sequences were also retrieved independently from the NCBI GenBank using the following keywords: (“Bacteriophage sp.”[Organism] OR bacteriophage[All Fields]) OR phage[All Fields] NOT prophage[All Fields] AND (viruses[filter] AND is_nuccore[filter] AND (“5,000”[SLEN] : “500,000”[SLEN])). Sequences from the two databases were merged and redundant sequences were removed based on the identical accession number. Only 24 additional phages were contributed from the GenBank. This resulted in a final dataset of 38,924 phage sequences.

Following phages, sequences from different environments were also analyzed: IMG/VR (Camargo et al. 2023) (5,576,198), GPD (142,809) (Camarillo-Guerrero et al. 2021), CHVD (Tisza and Buck 2021) (44,935), MGV (Nayfach et al. 2021) (189,680), IGVD (Shah et al. 2023) (10,021), GOV2 (Gregory et al. 2019) (195,699), and STV (Santos-Medellin et al. 2021) (4065). These were downloaded from PhageScope database (Wang et al. 2024). In addition, temperate phage and prophage sequences were also screened for ARGs. A total of 192,326 temperate phage sequences were identified using the TemPhD method (Zhang et al. 2022) fetched from the PhageScope database (Wang et al. 2024) and 49,254 prophage sequences extracted from the PhageClouds database (Rangel-Pineros et al. 2021). In all the above databases, to remove redundancy, sequences were clustered using MMseqs2 (Steinegger and Söding 2017) with a minimum sequence identity of 95% (–min-seq-id 0.95) and a minimum alignment coverage of 95% (-c 0.95, –cov-mode 1).

Traditionally, for identifying ARGs, a threshold of 25 amino acid coverage and 90% identity were considered (Tian et al. 2016; Jaya Divakaran et al. 2019). These thresholds were too lenient, resulting in false positives and over-estimation of ARGs (Enault et al. 2017). Hence, at least 40% coverage with 80% identity of nucleotide is proposed as the conserved threshold for ARG prediction (Zankari et al. 2012). We performed standalone BLASTN (Altschul et al. 1990) for ARG prediction in bacteriophage and employed conservative criteria of threshold of 80% for coverage and 40% for identity and e-value 10−3. Notably, the 141 ARGs identified had coverage and identity above 80%. Further, we checked the genome sequences of these ARG-carrying phages and identified PCGs like capsid, tail fiber, small terminase, large terminase, attachment site (attp), and base plate to confirm that they are true phage sequences.

HGT and the Correlation Study

We compared the transfer frequencies of other bacterial genes (referred to as non-ARGs in this study) with ARGs. Escherichia and Streptococcus were used for this study as most of the phages carrying ARGs infect these two bacterial genera. Proteomes of the two bacterial genera were fetched from the UniprotKB Proteome database (Bateman et al. 2021), and their respective phage RefSeq proteins were obtained from the NCBI Virus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/virus/vssi/#/) (Hatcher et al. 2017). We performed BLASTP between bacterial proteome and their respective phage proteins with 80% as the cutoff for both identity% and coverage%. Characterization of these sequences was done using EggNOG-mapper (Cantalapiedra et al. 2021).

To check the correlation between the abundance of ARGs in phages with the abundance of ARGs in their respective host bacterial genus, as well as phages infecting a particular bacterial genus, data for ARG abundance in all bacterial genera were downloaded from the “CARD prevalence, resistome and variants” database version 4.0.2. To know the number of phage species in different bacterial hosts, we downloaded host information of all RefSeq bacteriophage nucleotide sequences deposited in the NCBI Virus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/virus/vssi/#/) and also included host information from IMGVR v4 high confidence metadata.

Bacteria with 200 or more (top 1%) ARGs were considered ARG abundant, and bacteria with 54 or more phages infecting that genus (top 1%) were considered abundant in phages. The three parameters, namely, ARG-carrying phages, ARGs in bacteria, and phages in bacteria, were represented using barplots created using ggplot2 in R (Wickham 2011).

Identification of the Mechanism of Transfer

We used the following gene/DNA segments as markers for lysogeny: int (integrase)/recombinase (serine and tyrosine recombinase, xis (excisionase), cI (repressor—represses expression of all other phage genes), cII (activator—for cI and int), cIII (protects cII from degradation), and attP (attachment site in phage genome). We identified lysogeny markers manually by searching the phage genomes carrying ARGs. Also, we performed standalone BLASTN (Altschul et al. 1990) between the lysogeny markers as a query against the phage sequences with the following parameter threshold: identity%—80, query coverage%—80, and e-value—10−5. Also, since the integrase genes are also present in bacteria and phages can acquire them via homologous recombination, to rule out such cases, we checked the genes around the integrase gene, and those integrases that are flanked by phage genes were considered legitimate phage integrases.

To calculate the statistical significance of ARG enrichment at the phage terminus, we used the Chi2-test. We calculated the Chi2 value and P-values manually by comparing the observed ARG frequency with the expected at different regions of the genome. The null hypothesis assumes a uniform ARG distribution across the phage genome (Ross 2017).

To determine whether the ARGs represent indigenous phage genes or acquired genes, we used tetranucleotide frequency (Noble et al. 1998) to compare genomic composition using the R packages Biostrings and seqinr (Charif and Lobry 2007; Pagès et al. 2024). We used the terminase large and small subunits, the portal protein, and the major capsid protein as representatives of PCGs. PCA plots were constructed for dimensionality reduction of 256 tetranucleotide features using R packages: ggplot2, dplyr, FactoMineR (Lê et al. 2008; Ginestet 2011; Wickham et al. 2019). The statistical distinction between ARGs and PCGs was calculated using the PERMANOVA test, done using Euclidean distance matrices (with 9,999 permutations) using the vegan package (Oksanen et al. 2024) of R. The test assessed variation in multivariate space, where the F-value compares the between-group variation with within-group, the R2-value reflects the total variance explained by the gene types, and the P-value indicates the statistical significance of the test. R version 4.4.2 was used for complete analysis.
