Building a library of acute traumatic spinal cord injury images across Canada: a retrospective cohort study protocol

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMRI is increasingly recognised as a valuable tool for assessing prognosis and predicting outcomes following traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI). Several potential MRI biomarkers have been identified, but efforts are still needed to improve the accuracy and feasibility of these biomarkers in clinical practice. This study aims to build a national Canadian SCI imaging repository for storing and analysing imaging data for SCI, with the goal of improving SCI MRI biomarkers to predict outcomes and inform clinical management.Method and analysisAs a substudy of the Rick Hansen SCI Registry (RHSCIR), this retrospective multisite study includes individuals who sustained a traumatic cervical SCI between 2015 and 2021, were previously enrolled in RHSCIR, and had MRI scans acquired within 72 hours of injury and before any surgical intervention. Individuals with a penetrating trauma and/or with any prior spine surgery are excluded. The study principal investigator and research associates, experienced with data curation and with the standardised format and specifications of the Brain Imaging Data Structure standard, guide the site’s curator on the steps to perform image deidentification and curation to create standardised datasets across all sites. These datasets are transferred to a Digital Research Alliance of Canada (‘the Alliance’) server designated for this project and concatenated to form the national Canadian SCI imaging repository (Neurogitea). We are using a semiautomated processing pipeline to quantify lesion morphology, together with additional imaging measures that are manually extracted from the images (for instance, the relative maximal spinal cord compression and the maximum canal compromise). Through linkage to RHSCIR clinical and epidemiological data already available on eligible participants, regression analysis is planned to predict neurological outcomes at discharge, including the American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale grade, upper and lower extremity motor and sensory scores.Ethics and disseminationThis protocol has been submitted by the participating sites to obtain ethics and institutional approvals prior to the study initiation at each site. All 12 sites across Canada have now obtained ethics and institutional approvals. Study results will be disseminated at local, national and international conferences and by journal publications.Keywords: Spine, Magnetic resonance imaging, Registries, Retrospective Studies

Background

Traumatic spinal cord injury (TSCI) can lead to devastating neurological impairment, long-term secondary health conditions and reduced functional independence and quality of life.1 Currently, no studies on neuroprotective treatment in the context of TSCI have identified a successful therapy to significantly and reliably improve neurological outcomes.2 One of the reasons for the slow progress is the difficulty in not only recruiting patients during the initial traumatic acute phase for studies but also in classifying their baseline severity, which is crucial for determining clinical management and predicting outcomes. The current best practice to establish baseline injury severity is the International Standards for Neurological Classification of SCI (ISNCSCI) examination developed by the International Standards Committee of the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA). However, performing early neurological examinations during the acute phase can be difficult owing to issues such as concurrent injuries affecting consciousness and the urgent need to operate. In addition, the neurological examination does not consider different patterns of injury.

For example, spinal cord transection is associated with a different prognosis than spinal cord compression. The use of biomarkers such as cytokines in the cerebrospinal fluid to assess neurological severity is emerging as a way to objectively measure the injury severity.3 Imaging of the acute TSCI offers another objective assessment of the injury severity. Recent guidelines have been published recommending that MRI be performed in adult patients with acute SCI prior to surgical intervention, when feasible, to facilitate improved clinical decision-making.4

Furthermore, MRI-derived biomarkers of SCI have also been found to be predictive of clinical and functional outcomes. A 2017 systematic review reported that several quantitative measures, such as longer intramedullary haemorrhage, smaller spinal canal diameter at the location of maximal spinal cord compression (MSCC) and the presence of cord swelling, are associated with poor neurological recovery.5 In addition, lower MSCC was also found to be associated with worse functional outcomes as measured by the Functional Independence Measure. However, other imaging features were either inconsistent (presence of haemorrhage, maximal canal compromise and oedema length) or not associated with the outcomes. Importantly, most of the studies evaluated these imaging features manually, usually on a single sagittal slice, which is prone to inter-rater/intra-rater variability in addition to being time-consuming and thus not feasible within a clinical setting or high-volume multi-participant studies.6

Recent advances in automated spinal cord imaging analysis tools7 8 open a new era of imaging biomarkers, offering a more accurate and less biased method for analysing MRI data. McCoy et al6 and Mummaneni et al9 have applied automated segmentation tools on MRI scans of patients with acute TSCI and showed that measures of the injury derived from automated lesion segmentation correlated with measures of motor scores in the acute phase6 and at discharge.9 However, both studies are limited by small sample size and were done on a single-site cohort with all scans performed on a single MRI scanner using similar parameters.6

Further investigation into imaging biomarkers and the use of automated tools in multicentre settings is needed to validate their prognostic and predictive value. The Rick Hansen SCI Registry (RHSCIR) is poised for this undertaking with 18 acute trauma hospitals and 12 rehabilitation centres specialising in providing care for SCI across Canada in participating sites.10 In this current RHSCIR sub-study, we establish the first national Canadian SCI imaging repository (Neurogitea) for individuals participating in RHSCIR, to be used together with their clinical data already collected through RHSCIR. While other important initiatives are also underway in this field,11 this current initiative is distinct in leveraging retrospective imaging, making it cost-effective compared with prospective studies. The lessons learnt from this initiative could benefit future collaborations on retrospective and prospective studies for SCI imaging biomarkers.

Greater precise details about the injury and the prognosis from the imaging biomarkers can promote the development of successful neuroprotective treatment and assist in developing personalised clinical management to improve patient outcomes. This information can inform discussions on the prognosis and recovery expectations and be shared with patients and their families to facilitate shared decision-making.

Study goal and objectives

The goal of the study is to build a national Canadian SCI imaging repository to facilitate imaging analytics for SCI. This repository serves as a library of imaging datasets, such that batch analysis can be conducted along with relevant clinical data already collected through RHSCIR.

The first objective of this study is to generate imaging datasets in different sites across Canada, in the setting of acute TSCI. Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) compliant datasets from the different sites are uploaded to a national repository of acute SCI to create the Canadian SCI imaging repository (Neurogitea).

The second objective of this study is to use Neurogitea and perform semiautomatic spinal cord and lesion segmentation on T2-weighted MRI scans to obtain quantitative MRI biomarkers. Imaging data, combined with admission clinical data, are used to develop prediction algorithms for patient outcomes.

We attempt to answer questions such as ‘what is the probability of me/my patient improving one ASIA Impairment Scale (AIS) grade from admission to discharge?’ and ‘what features of the imaging or clinical data best predict improved AIS grade or motor score at discharge?’.

We focus on cervical TSCI because they are the most clinically severe and are often prioritised for imaging by clinicians and spine surgeons. This focus maximises the clinical relevance and research impact of the repository.

Training

Study investigators (DWC and MGP), with coordination assistance from Praxis (NRK, SH), maintain regular connections with each local data curator. Each local curator is coached through the steps from image collection to BIDS compliance and transfer to Neurogitea.

Image collection

Based on the list of eligible RHSCIR participants (see more information in the section Cohort selection) provided by the Praxis team, the study site determines which participants have acute preoperative MR images acquired as part of standard clinical care. The study site team then retrospectively requests MR images of these participants from the hospital-based Picture Archiving and Communication System according to institutional protocols. The data collection period was limited to 2021 to avoid incomplete or ongoing delayed clinical data entry in 2022 due to COVID-19. Although some sites obtained approvals for study initiation in 2023, selecting 2021 as the endpoint ensured that the data were more complete and consistent across all sites. This study does not recruit new participants and only includes existing data from previously enrolled RHSCIR participants who had either provided consent to participate in the RHSCIR study or were enrolled under a consent waiver and had a minimal dataset collected. We do not seek to reconsent these participants as data collection for this current study does not vary considerably from standard RHSCIR data collection, and there is no additional risk to the participants by collecting deidentified imaging data. Prior to the upload of the images, the RHSCIR team prepares a crosswalk document that links between the RHSCIR ID (a random number used to identify RHSCIR participants) and a new Imaging ID, a randomly selected, unique number, used as a method to identify the participant (rather than using personal identifiers), which is made available only to the curator, principal investigator (PI) (DWC) and the Praxis team. All Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) files for a single participant are placed in a folder labelled using the imaging study ID number, on a local computer (preferably with use of Terminal in Mac or Linux) as deemed secure by local protocols (eg, not a laptop that is taken off hospital property).

Image deidentification and inspection

DICOM data are evaluated and converted into the compressed Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NIfTI) format and deidentified using Imaging ID by a trained curator. After conversion, the original DICOM datasets (which may include personal ID) are deleted. Imaging files in NIfTI format are manually inspected by the local imaging curator to confirm data integrity.

BIDS-compliant dataset creation at the local site

As described above, the site’s data are stored on a local computer at each participating site. Local data curators work with a study investigator (DWC and/or MGP) to formulate BIDS datasets. Only the local PI and data curator have access to data via a password-protected computer.

Curation and BIDS compliance

Following manual inspection, files (in NIfTI format) are compiled into a BIDS standard for organising and describing MRI datasets12 using a data curation pipeline. The pipeline is used to manage the local data, including validation at the point of entry, and for automated generation of the BIDS-compliant dataset to ensure uniform practice across all the participating sites and semiautomate the process from local site data to Neurogitea. This also improves capacity at the site to collect imaging data on a go-forward basis. BIDS is used to organise imaging data into a specific directory schema, labelled following a precise naming convention. The result is an organised dataset that can be easily shared and understood by other researchers. The BIDS standard uses file formats compatible with existing software, unifies the majority of practices already common in the field and captures the metadata necessary for common data processing operations. Necessary clinical data (age, sex and handedness) may be saved within the BIDS folder as well, as needed. Nomenclature is consistent between all study-participating sites.

BIDS-compliant datasets upload to the Digital Research Alliance of Canada (the Alliance)

Local datasets in a BIDS-compliant format are transferred via a secure method (such as secure file transfer protocol) and uploaded to a secure Praxis-designated server at the Digital Research Alliance of Canada (the Alliance)13 and maintained by a study investigator (JCA) and his team. Once datasets are transferred to the designated server, additional review of the files is performed by one of the study investigators (JCA) and his team to validate the dataset formatting, and if needed, they contact the study site team for any required corrections or if there are any follow-up questions about the images and associated data entered.

BIDS-compliant datasets of deidentified images from different sites are automatically aggregated together to form Neurogitea, which is backed up (to another virtual disk), versioned and is used for analysis.

National Canadian SCI imaging repository (Neurogitea)

Neurogitea is hosted on the Alliance servers, which are physically located in the University of Victoria (Arbutus).14 For the server, the Git suite of programs is used to allow efficient version control of the data on the repository, the git-annex plugin is used to minimise the effect of large files on the performance and the Gitea hosting program is used to provide a web interface for the repository. This web interface allows JCA’s team to manage fine-grained access control to the data, so that each site has access to their part of the dataset, and the analysis team can review the entire dataset (see figure 1 for a screenshot of the image repository web interface). Moreover, the web interface allows for discussion threads between the different teams, for example, about data quality issues or requests for additional data.

Rick Hansen Spinal Cord Injury Registry

The sites participating in this current study are participating in RHSCIR, a national registry that collects information on the preadmission, acute, rehabilitation and community phases of care.10 Patients with acute TSCI who are admitted to a RHSCIR site are enrolled and data are collected, including personal information (eg, sociodemographic factors, comorbidities), injury data (eg, diagnoses of TSCI and spinal column injury, mechanism of injury and other related injuries), interventions (eg, surgical procedures) and long-term participant outcomes (eg, quality of life). Image analysis approach

Lesion and spinal cord segmentation

Image data processing is done using Spinal Cord Toolbox (SCT) V.6.5.8 17 We have chosen SCT over its alternatives (for instance, FSL or SPM)18 19 for two main reasons.7 First, to our knowledge, it offers the most comprehensive, user-friendly pipeline specifically designed for spinal cord research. Second, it is well documented and open source, allowing a straightforward integration into clinical and research workflows without major legal difficulties or financial burden, although it is designed to support research and not intended as medical advice.

A qualitative radiological assessment of each T2w sagittal and axial MRI scan is performed by a third-year, experienced in-training radiologist to ensure data quality from a clinical standpoint. Each T2w MRI sequence is examined to confirm the presence of spinal cord oedema, to exclude significant artefacts and to identify critical findings such as suspected epidural haematoma or spine surgery material. The best T2w sagittal and axial sequences for each participant are selected for the processing of further model training and MRI-based metrics extraction. Each participant to be excluded from the in-training radiologist assessment is reviewed by a neuroradiologist with more than 10 years of experience for confirmation.

Segmentation of intramedullary lesions (annotating the lesion versus the other contents of the image) appearing as a T2w hyperintense signal compared with the physiological spinal cord signal is done manually for all participants on suitable T2w images, using SlicerCART extension through 3D Slicer software7 (or equivalent) to serve as a reference standard against automated segmentation techniques. If a T2w hypointense intramedullary signal is found compared with the physiological spinal cord signal, haemorrhage is presumed according to the radiology resident evaluation and segmented as a separate class. Note that other sequences for which haemorrhage is usually confirmed (eg, T2* sequences) are not accessible to the radiology resident (MB).

Segmentation of the spinal cord and spinal canal masks is done semiautomatically. We use this approach as segmenting the spinal cord and spinal canal fully manually is extremely time-consuming. This semiautomatic approach was also applied in previous studies.20–22 First, the contrast-agnostic (V.2.5)22 23 and Canal_t2w (r20241126) models from SCT are applied to the T2w sagittal and axial images selected by the radiologist (see below) to obtain initial spinal cord and spinal canal segmentation masks. Then, the automatically generated segmentations are visually reviewed and corrected when necessary (JV) using the FSLeyes viewer (V.1.9.0) and the manual-correction module8 or using the SlicerCART extension through 3D Slicer software (MB).

Sample size estimation

There is very little published on how to determine the sample size for studies using deep learning.27 Methods described in Gibson et al28 rely on statistical estimations, which cannot be directly adapted to deep learning-based methods as they are empirical. Moreover, sample size determination studies inherently assume that the training and test distributions are similar, which cannot be assumed in our study, as data from multiple sites, each with its own distinct distributions, is aggregated together. However, based on the sample size from previous imaging studies where 275 samples were used for two deep learning tasks,29 and another used 166 samples across three datasets for T2-weighted prostate segmentation,30 the expected sample size of our study (around 330) is notably larger and should have sufficient data to successfully train models.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive univariate statistics will be performed to describe the study population demographics, spine-specific and general trauma metrics and segmentation measures. MRI metrics (see Metrics collected section above) are used as predictors/independent variables for prognostic predictions. Target outcomes are AIS grade, total motor score, UEMS, LEMS and total sensory scores. Predictions are made for outcomes at final rehabilitation discharge, change in the scores between initial admission assessment and final rehabilitation discharge, as well as for dichotomised change scores (improved versus not improved). Categorical variables will be expressed as absolute and relative frequencies, while continuous variables will be expressed as means, medians and IQRs.

The independent impact of lesion segmentation, MRI metrics and other non-imaging clinical measures on the outcome will be assessed by performing univariable and multivariable logistic regression (for binary outcomes), linear regression (for continuous outcomes) or ordinal regression where applicable (eg, AIS grade). Multiple metrics, including area under the ROC curve, sensitivity/specificity as applicable, accuracy and calibration plots, will be used together with internal validation using repeated train/test splits to assess the accuracy of the predictive distribution models.20 Further, binary logistic, linear and hierarchical regressions will be used to develop statistical models combining the imaging measures and other non-imaging factors (such as neurological level of injury, age, sex and mechanism of injury) to predict neurological outcomes at discharge and assess the added value of MRI metrics over clinical examination.

Subgroup analysis might be conducted for participants with AIS A, as it is unlikely they would show AIS grade conversion, so motor score change is used for this group. Similarly, participants with AIS D are rarely converted to AIS E at discharge, and their motor score change is also examined.

We anticipate low levels of missing data since the availability of key outcomes and predictors has been used in the inclusion and exclusion criteria. In the case of missing data of other non-key outcomes or other measures, if more than 5% is missing, the data will be removed and disclosed separately. For variables with less than 5% missing levels, no imputation is planned in order to enhance the robustness of the prediction model. This approach will be revisited once all data are collected during the analysis phase and will be modified if necessary.

Discussion

In this study, we are establishing a comprehensive Canadian imaging repository by collecting retrospectively, routinely acquired diagnostic images of individuals with TSCI to be linked with the rich clinical data that RHSCIR already collects. This dataset has great potential for its use in cutting-edge analytical tools to advance the research of imaging-related biomarkers for TSCI and research in the SCI field in general.

Moreover, this central SCI imaging repository infrastructure may open the door for future, nation-wide collaboration and large-scale prospective or retrospective studies, which are still needed for the development and validation of the predictive value of MRI biomarkers for outcomes of TSCI.

More precise details of the injury and the prognosis can tailor clinical management in a personalised approach, leading to improved patient outcomes. This information can also be used for communication with patients and their families about the prognosis and recovery expectations to facilitate shared decision-making for patient-centred care.

There are a few limitations to this project that are worth mentioning. The collection of data and the process of data curation are time-consuming and require programming skills that some sites may lack. Our team made substantial efforts to reduce the workload on the site’s end by providing close guidance and by creating a data curation pipeline to minimise the barriers for participation. However, despite these efforts, it could be that the lack of resources potentially prevented a few sites from participating. This may introduce selection bias as participating sites could potentially differ from non-participating sites in terms of their size, patients’ characteristics and available resources. Nonetheless, the 12 participating sites across the country and from 7 out of 10 provinces represent a broad geographical and organisational cohort, which enhances the diversity of the sample and mitigates some concerns about generalisability. Where possible, a comparison to national RHSCIR data will be performed to assess how our results are representative of the Canadian population.

Participants were excluded if they had no ISNCSCI assessment done within 72 hours of their injury to ensure consistent neurological characterisation and enable robust associations between MRI findings and clinical status. We recognise that this may introduce selection bias by excluding individuals who could not have been fully assessed within this timeframe. Sensitivity analysis will be considered to evaluate whether this has an impact on the study’s generalisability.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public are not involved in the formulation of research questions, measurement of outcomes or data interpretation.

Study duration

This study is expected to run for about 18 months from the time of ethics approval to analysis completion, but the total duration depends on the time it takes to retrieve the images and obtain the necessary approvals and data sharing agreements.

Risks and benefits for the participants

This is a retrospective observational study with an overarching goal to develop and validate the prognostic value of MRI biomarkers for TSCI and improve clinical management of individuals with SCI. The study site does not collect any new data as part of this study. While no direct benefit is anticipated for the participating individuals, their inclusion in the study has the potential to indirectly improve care of individuals with TSCI. No additional risks are anticipated from this study.

Anticipated issues

There may be long delays with respect to ethics review time, institutional approvals and time to set up required agreements (eg, services agreements, data sharing agreements). To avoid potential delays, the local team contacts the imaging and contracts departments of the site to set up timeline expectations for image requisition and document processing so a schedule can be created for all subsequent steps for all parties involved.

Informed consent

As this is a retrospective study using patient records where the participant either already signed a consent form for the use of their data or a minimal dataset was collected under a waiver of consent model, a new consent form is waived for this study. The addition of imaging data to the data already collected for RHSCIR is not deemed to be a material change from the language in the RHSCIR consent form or protocol, and there are no anticipated additional risks to the participants from adding this data, and overall involves no more than minimal risk to the participants. Additionally, the participants for whom records would be reviewed may no longer be followed and could be lost to follow-up. Also, the proportion of individuals likely to have relocated or died may be a significant percentage of the cohort population and the research results may not be meaningful and lose statistical power, such that the research could not be carried out practically without the waiver of consent. RHSCIR governance principles and processes are followed as detailed in Privacy Compliance and Governance.
