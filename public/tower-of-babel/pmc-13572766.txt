Application of different polysaccharides as fat replacers in low-fat emulsified sausages: Effects on physicochemical, textural and sensory properties
Authors: Jianwei Sun, Mingyuan Huang, Haibo Zheng, Shengming Zhao, Zongyuan Zhen, Chunhui Zhang, Xuan Xu, Jin Tao, Guoyuan Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of guar gum, tara gum, and linseed gum at different addition contents (0%, 0.8%, 1.2%, 1.6%, 2.0%, and 2.4%) on the quality of low-fat emulsified sausages. Polysaccharide addition significantly affected cooking loss, texture, water distribution, and sensory quality, while fat content in low-fat formulations was reduced from 28.35% to 9.83%–11.93%. Regarding physicochemical stability, tara gum at 1.6% showed the most favorable effect on reducing cooking loss, reaching the minimum value of 4.52%. Appropriate polysaccharide addition improved textural properties, with tara gum showing the greatest increase in hardness. LF-NMR analysis showed that polysaccharide addition altered water distribution. Sensory evaluation further indicated that appropriate addition contents improved texture, flavor, juiciness, and overall acceptability. Notably, linseed gum at 1.6% achieved the highest overall sensory score of 43.15. These findings provide technical support for the application of polysaccharides as fat replacers in low-fat meat products.

Introduction

With the growing emphasis on health-oriented dietary patterns, the development of low-fat meat products possessing both enhanced nutritional value and desirable eating quality has emerged as an important research priority in meat science and food processing (Choi et al., 2016; Rahman et al., 2019; Syan et al., 2024). Meat products play an important role in the human diet because they provide abundant quality proteins, lipids, vitamins, and minerals. Among them, emulsified meat products, particularly emulsified sausages, are extensively consumed because of their convenience, palatable flavor, broad consumer acceptance, and high content of industrial applicability, thereby conferring considerable research and practical significance on this product category (Rahman et al., 2019; Syan et al., 2024). However, traditional emulsified sausage formulations often contain relatively high content of animal fat, especially saturated fatty acids (Rahman et al., 2019; Syan et al., 2024). In emulsified meat products, saturated fat is important not only because it contributes to flavor, juiciness, and mouthfeel, but also because it helps maintain emulsion stability, supports gel matrix formation, and affects the development of product texture (Rahman et al., 2019; Syan et al., 2024). Meanwhile, excessive fat intake, particularly saturated fatty acids, may increase the probability of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and other chronic metabolic disorders (Choi et al., 2016). Thus, achieving fat reduction without compromising product quality and physicochemical stability has become a major concern in both academic research and industrial practice under current health-driven demands (Zhao et al., 2023).

Although reducing fat content helps enhance the nutritional value of emulsified meat products, direct fat reduction often disrupts the original balance of the system, thereby resulting in quality deterioration of emulsified sausages, such as weaker flavor, lower juiciness, poorer texture, reduced emulsion stability, and decreased overall acceptability (Zhao et al., 2023). These results suggest that saturated fat in emulsified sausages is not only a source of energy, but also serves a vital function in lubrication, filling, and maintaining system stability. Therefore, a key to developing low-fat formulations is not simply to reduce lipid content, but to adopt suitable replacement strategies that can compensate, as far as possible, for the structural and physicochemical functions of fat (Zhao et al., 2023). Among the available fat-replacement approaches, polysaccharide hydrocolloids have attracted wide attention because of their natural origin, relatively high safety, and good functional properties, including water-binding, thickening, gel-forming, and certain emulsion-supporting effects (Bourouis et al., 2023; Du et al., 2023; M. Huang et al., 2022; Paglarini et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2026). In low-fat meat systems, polysaccharides can reduce the negative effects caused by fat reduction to some extent by binding water, providing a filling effect, improving tissue structure, and strengthening the gel network, thereby improving textural properties, processing stability, and sensory quality (Bourouis et al., 2023; Du et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2022; Paglarini et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2026).

The functional performance of polysaccharides in emulsified meat products is not entirely uniform, and their regulatory effects are generally closely associated with their intrinsic structural characteristics. Variations in molecular chain conformation, spatial structure, charge properties, and source may all influence the physicochemical attributes and modes of action of polysaccharides, thereby resulting in distinct effects on water distribution, emulsion stability, gel network formation, textural development, and sensory quality (Çelebi & Erge, 2024; Jayarathna et al., 2022). Although progress has been made in recent years regarding the application of polysaccharides in low-fat meat products, the regulatory roles of polysaccharides with distinct structural features in low-fat emulsified systems still warrant further elucidation (Bao, Liu, et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023).

Guar gum, tara gum, and linseed gum are all commonly used polysaccharide ingredients in food and have shown considerable application potential in improving water-holding capacity, enhancing textural properties, and maintaining system stability (Huang et al., 2024; Jayarathna et al., 2022). Considering the structural diversity of these polysaccharides, their functional performance in food systems may be closely associated with their molecular architecture and physicochemical characteristics (Zhang, Liu, et al., 2025). Specifically, the highly branched structure of guar gum may contribute to water retention through enhanced hydration capacity, whereas the moderately branched structure of tara gum may provide greater potential for interactions with myofibrillar proteins and gel network reinforcement. Meanwhile, the anionic heteropolysaccharide nature of linseed gum may facilitate electrostatic interactions and contribute to interfacial stabilization during protein gelation. However, the comparative effects of these three polysaccharides in low-fat emulsified sausages have not been systematically investigated. As demonstrated by Zhang, Yang, et al. (2025) such comparative evaluations in real food matrices are important because they bridge the gap between empirical food formulation and targeted quality optimization in practice. Furthermore, this study aimed to comparatively analyze the different effects of these polysaccharides in the emulsified sausages, providing a theoretical basis for optimizing polysaccharide-based fat-replacement strategies in low-fat emulsified meat products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh chicken breast, pork fat, and salt were purchased from Wanda Plaza Supermarket (Chuzhou, China). Allspice was obtained from Meishijia Food Co., Ltd. (Taizhou, China); white pepper powder from Haikuo Tiancheng Catering Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China); compound phosphate (sodium tripolyphosphate, CAS: 7758-29-4; sodium hexametaphosphate, CAS: 10124–56-8; sodium pyrophosphate, CAS: 7722-88-5) from Jiangsu Hengshi Food Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China); and plastic casings from Kangtai Plastic Packaging Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).

Guar gum (food grade, >82% purity) was supplied by Beijing Guarrun Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), tara gum (food grade, >80% purity) by Guangrao Liuhe Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China), and linseed gum (food grade, >99% purity) by Xinjiang Lishide Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xinjiang, China). The chemical reagents used for compositional analysis and scanning electron microscopy, including petroleum ether (CAS: 8032-32-4), copper(II) sulfate (CAS: 7758-98-7), potassium sulfate (CAS: 7778-80-5), sulfuric acid (CAS: 7664-93-9), sodium hydroxide (CAS: 1310-73-2), glutaraldehyde (CAS: 111–30-8), osmium tetroxide (CAS: 20816–12-0), ethanol (CAS: 64–17-5), and isoamyl acetate (CAS: 123–92-2), were of analytical grade and obtained from commercial suppliers in China.

Preparation of emulsified sausages

The preparation method was modified from Li et al. (2023). Chicken breast and pork back fat were used at ratios of 7:3 (w/w) for the high-fat control and 7:1 (w/w) for the low-fat groups. Polysaccharides were added at 0%, 0.8%, 1.2%, 1.6%, 2.0%, and 2.4% of the total material mass. Ice water, salt, compound phosphates, allspice, and white pepper powder were added according to Table 1. The raw materials were accurately weighed using an LC-JA5103 Electronic Analytical Balance (Shanghai Lichen Bangxi Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and separately minced using an S30-LA598 Meat Grinder (Joyoung Co., Ltd., Jinan, China). The minced meat was then chopped with the ingredients and ice water—prepared by an XD-30 Automatic Snowflake Ice Maker (Huayu Brothers Ice Systems Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China)—in three steps at 3000 r/min for 90 s each, with the batter temperature continuously monitored using a TP688 digital probe thermometer (Wenzhou Mitir Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., Wenzhou, China) and maintained between 8 °C and 11 °C (strictly below 12 °C) throughout processing. The resulting batter was filled into 23-cm plastic casings, cooked in an 80 °C water bath for 30 min until the core temperature reached 72 °C. A total of 17 groups were established, including one high-fat control group, one low-fat control group without polysaccharides, and 15 low-fat groups supplemented with guar gum (G), tara gum (T), or linseed gum (L) at five addition contents (0.8%, 1.2%, 1.6%, 2.0%, and 2.4%).

Cooking loss

Cooking loss was determined by the method of Abbasi et al. (2019) with slight modifications. The prepared emulsified sausages were gently wiped dry and weighed prior to cooking (m₁). The samples were then cooked in an 80 °C thermostatic water bath for 30 min. After cooking, the sausages were cooled overnight at 4 °C, the surface moisture was removed, and the samples were reweighed (m₂). Cooking loss was determined by the following equation:(1)Cooking loss%=m1−m2m1×100%where m1 represents the sample weight before cooking (g) and m2 represents the sample weight after cooking (g).

Determination of moisture, fat, and protein content

Moisture was measured by the direct drying method in accordance with GB/T 5009.3—2016. Protein was analyzed following GB 5009.5—2025 with a K9860 Automatic Kjeldahl Analyzer (Hanon Future Technology Group Co., Ltd., Jinan, China). Fat was measured by Soxhlet extraction according to GB 5009.6—2016 using an SZF-06C Crude Fat Analyzer (Zhejiang Top Cloud-Agri Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China).

Color measurement

Emulsified sausage samples were sliced into 2 cm thick sections using a double-sided cutter. A colorimeter was calibrated with a standard white plate (L* = 96.86, a* = −0.15, b* = 1.87). Color parameters (L*, a*, and b*) were measured at the center of the cross-sectional surface under natural light conditions, and the values were recorded.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

Texture profile analysis was carried out by referring to the procedure of Ho and Bok (2023) with minor modifications. Prior to testing, all samples were equilibrated to room temperature (25 °C) for two hours. Each sausage was cut into a cylindrical sample of 2 cm height and 2.5 cm diameter before testing. A CT-3 Texture Analyzer (Brookfield Engineering Laboratories, Inc., Middleboro, MA, USA) was used to determine hardness (g), springiness (mm), cohesiveness, and chewiness (g × mm). Measurements were taken in TPA mode. The pre-test and post-test speeds were set at 2.00 and 5.00 mm/s, respectively, and the time gap between two compressions was 3.00 s. The analyzer was further set to automatic trigger mode with a 50% compression ratio, 5.0 g trigger force, P50 probe, and 200 × 10−6 s data collection interval.

pH

The pH of the samples was analyzed following GB 5009.237—2016, Determination of pH Value of Foods. Briefly, 2.0 g of crushed material was combined with 18 mL distilled water, mixed thoroughly with a glass rod, and kept at room temperature for 30 min. The mixture was then filtered, and the pH reading was obtained with an FE28-CN pH meter (Mettler-Toledo Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Electronic nose

Electronic nose analysis was carried out with minor modifications to the procedure reported by Yin et al. (2021). A 5 g portion of sample was transferred into a headspace vial and heated in a thermostatic water bath at 50 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the samples were brought back to room temperature (20–25 °C) before measurement. The instrument settings were as follows: sampling interval, 3 s; pre-sampling time, 5 s; sensor cleaning time, 70 s; and injection flow rate, 600 mL/min. Volatile compounds were monitored using a PEN3 Electronic Nose System (AIRSENSE Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany), and the resulting data were used to support the subsequent flavor evaluation.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analysis

LF-NMR measurement was performed based on a previously reported method (Q. Zhang et al., 2017), with minor modifications. Approximately 5.0 g of emulsified sausage was sealed in plastic film and placed in an NMR tube. Spin–spin relaxation time (T₂) was recorded using an NMI20-040H LF-NMR Spectrometer (Suzhou Niumag Analytical Instrument Corporation, Suzhou, China) with the Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence. The instrument was operated at a proton resonance frequency of 40 MHz and a measurement temperature of 32 °C, with 250 μs between the 90° and 180° pulses. A total of 16 scans were performed with a repetition time of 6.5 s, generating 12,000 echoes. Each sample was analyzed in five replicates.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM was used to observe the microstructure of the sausage samples, following the procedure of Maqsood et al. (2011) with slight modifications. Samples were cut into cubes of 5 × 5 × 5 mm and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight, after which the fixative was discarded. The specimens were rinsed three times in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for 15 min each, then post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h. The same phosphate buffer was used again for three additional 15 min washes. Dehydration was performed stepwise in ethanol solutions of 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 95%, with 15 min at each step, and then in absolute ethanol twice for 20 min each. Next, the samples were treated with ethanol and isoamyl acetate (v/v = 1:1) for 30 min, followed by pure isoamyl acetate for 1 h or overnight. Finally, the samples were critical-point dried, sputter-coated, and examined by SEM.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated three times, and every measurement included at least three parallel determinations. Statistical analysis was carried out with SPSS 25.0, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Analysis of variance was used to evaluate significance, with P < 0.05 taken as statistically significant. Origin 2021 was applied for data processing and figure preparation.

Results

Cooking loss

Cooking loss is closely related to the water- and oil-holding capacities of emulsified meat products, so it is widely used as a practical indicator of system stability. The results showed that adding different polysaccharides greatly reduced the cooking loss of low-fat emulsified sausages (P < 0.05, Fig. 1), and all polysaccharide-treated low-fat sausages showed lower cooking loss than the low-fat control. As the polysaccharide content increased, the cooking loss first decreased and then increased slightly at higher addition content. These results indicate that moderate amounts of polysaccharides help improve water retention and maintain the structure of the protein matrix (Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2025). However, excessive addition may reduce moisture retention. This may be because the network structure becomes too compact or because the intermolecular balance is disturbed. Among the three polysaccharides, tara gum gave the lowest cooking loss. At an addition content of 1.6%, the cooking loss reached the lowest value (4.52%). This was about 27.7% below the control. Further increases in addition content did not cause significant changes (P > 0.05). Guar gum also showed a good effect at 1.6%; however, the cooking loss increased slightly at higher addition content. In contrast, linseed gum showed the lowest cooking loss at 2.0%, and then it increased significantly at 2.4%, showing that it was more sensitive to the addition content.Fig. 1Effect of different polysaccharide additions on the cooking loss of emulsified sausages. These results show that appropriate addition of polysaccharides can effectively reduce cooking loss in emulsified sausages. Tara gum provided better water retention at lower addition contents, indicating good potential for practical application. The observed trends agree with previous reports by Cortez-Trejo et al. (2021) who also found that polysaccharide type and addition content affected cooking loss in meat products. The decrease in cooking loss may arise from interactions between the hydrophilic groups of polysaccharides and water molecules, and also to their interactions with myofibrillar proteins. These interactions may help form a more compact and stable gel network. Such a network helps retain water and oil during heating.

Determination of moisture, fat, and protein content

Moisture content reflects the amount of water retained in the final product. As shown in Table 3, the addition of guar gum, tara gum, or linseed gum at contents of 0.8% to 2.4% significantly increased moisture content compared with the control group (54.31%), indicating that all three polysaccharides had good water-binding ability (P < 0.05). Within this range, each gum showed an optimal addition content. Guar gum at 2.0% resulted in the highest moisture content overall (64.66%, 19.1% higher than the control). Tara gum and linseed gum reached their highest values at 2.0% (63.99%) and 1.6% (64.19%), respectively. Beyond this content, moisture content decreased slightly, and this trend was similar to that observed for cooking loss. In addition, at the same addition content, no marked differences were observed among the three gums in water-holding capacity (P > 0.05). Hou et al. (2024) found that adding 0.5%–1.5% guar gum to low-fat meat batters improved water retention by increasing bound water capacity and enhancing gel network structure. However, Sarraf et al. (2023) found that excessive polysaccharide addition, such as xanthan gum, disrupted protein gel network formation. As a result, this system became less capable of retaining water and oil droplets, and emulsion stability decreased. These findings suggest that, in emulsified sausage systems, polysaccharides improve water retention mainly through hydrophilic interactions and by promoting gel network formation, and that this effect depends on the amount added. In contrast to the high-fat control (28.35% fat), all low-fat emulsified sausages formulated with polysaccharides exhibited a pronounced reduction in fat content, with values consistently ranging from 9.83% to 11.93% (P < 0.05). Among the three gums tested, guar and tara gum at their maximum addition contents (2.4%) produced the most substantial decreases, yielding fat contents of 9.87% and 9.91%, respectively. Linseed gum, by comparison, showed a more moderate reduction across the entire addition range. Notably, no significant differences in fat content were detected among all various low-fat treatment groups, irrespective of gum type or addition contents (0.8%–2.4%). This indicates that the final fat content is governed primarily by the baseline formulation—which contributes approximately 10% fat—and that the polysaccharides exert only a marginal direct influence under the evaluated conditions (Nasonova & Tunieva, 2019).

Relative to the high-fat control, the low-fat formulations showed significantly higher protein contents (19.26%–21.45%) (P < 0.05), with no marked differences across the polysaccharide-treated groups regardless of gum type or addition contents. This increase was mainly due to the decrease in the fat-to-lean ratio from 3:7 to 1:7. Taken together, these findings indicate that protein content in emulsified sausages is determined mainly by the degree of fat substitution in the base formulation, whereas the addition of polysaccharides exerts, at most, a secondary role under the experimental conditions examined.

Color

The L* value reflects the lightness of the product surface and serves as a key index for assessing the color characteristics of emulsified meat products. As shown in Table 4, guar gum, tara gum, and linseed gum all significantly reduced the L* value of low-fat emulsified sausages (P < 0.05), but the change trends were different: guar gum significantly decreased the L* value at 0.8% and then stabilized; tara gum showed a continuous downward trend with increasing addition content; linseed gum showed a significant decrease at 2.0%. Overall, linseed gum had the greatest impact on brightness at higher addition amounts, followed by tara gum, and guar gum had a relatively smaller impact. A possible reason for the decreased brightness is the lower fat content. In addition, the a* value indicates the redness level of the product. Guar gum and tara gum only changed a* marginally at each addition. Linseed gum slightly reduced the a* value as the addition contents increased. At 2.4%, the a* value reached the minimum (1.55), which was much below the control (P < 0.05). This may be due to the nature of the polysaccharides. The b* values (yellowness) did not change much, and in all treatments, the b* values remained between 11.00 and 12.58 and were very close to the control, suggesting that polysaccharides mainly affected lightness and redness, while yellowness remained relatively stable in the products. Overall, all three polysaccharides changed the color of low-fat emulsified sausages to some extent. Texture profile analysis (TPA)

Hardness

Meat hardness directly affects the chewiness and overall acceptability of emulsified meat products. Fig. 2(a) shows how different polysaccharides affected the hardness of low-fat emulsified sausages. Compared to the polysaccharide-free control, guar gum, tara gum, and linseed gum each significantly increased sausage hardness (P < 0.05). Tara gum showed the greatest effect on hardness, followed by guar gum. Linseed gum showed the lowest hardness among the three polysaccharides, but its level remained still well above that of the control group (P < 0.05). This ranking shows that tara gum is the best option for improving hardness in the low-fat system. The greatest improvements were observed at addition content of 2.0% guar gum, 2.0% tara gum, and 1.6% linseed gum. These results were consistent with those reported by Zhang, Liu, et al. (2025). Springiness

As a key sensory-related parameter for emulsified sausages, springiness captures the capacity of the product to recover its structure following deformation and remains closely tied to juiciness and chewing perception. As illustrated in Fig. 2(b), compared with the polysaccharide-free control, an appropriate polysaccharide addition effectively counteracted the springiness loss induced by fat reduction in low-fat emulsified sausages, restoring springiness to a content comparable to that of the high-fat control. The benefit, however, was not monotonic. With further increases in polysaccharide dosage, springiness declined. Among the three polysaccharides tested, tara gum produced the most pronounced enhancement; at 1.6% addition, it delivered the strongest restoration, consistent with the pattern discussed above. Taken together, reducing fat content tended to depress springiness, whereas moderate polysaccharide supplementation compensated for this deficit; excessive addition reversed the gain and led to a hardness-like downturn, while the overall springiness range across the three polysaccharides did not differ significantly, as reported previously (Zhao et al., 2026).

Cohesiveness

Cohesiveness, one of the core indices describing the texture of emulsified sausage, reflects the internal bonding strength among components within the matrix; as such, it bears directly on water-holding capacity and textural stability. As illustrated in Fig. 2(c), introducing guar gum, tara gum, or linseed gum led to distinct cohesiveness responses relative to the control. Examined individually, guar gum produced no significant change in cohesiveness across the tested addition content (P > 0.05), whereas tara gum caused a significant decrease when the dosage reached 2.4% (P < 0.05); linseed gum, by contrast, maintained a comparatively stable cohesiveness as the addition content varied. The pattern was similar at 1.2% addition, where no significant differences were detected among the three polysaccharides (P > 0.05). At 2.4%, however, tara gum exhibited a weaker cohesiveness effect than guar gum and linseed gum (P < 0.05). Differences in the molecular structure and hydration capacity of polysaccharides may explain these results, as they can affect gel stability and interactions within the matrix (Liu et al., 2020). These findings suggest that maintaining cohesiveness is important for preserving the structural integrity of emulsified sausages, as a significant decrease in cohesiveness may indicate a weakened internal matrix, leading to a crumbly texture and poor sliceability (Hu et al., 2016).

Chewiness

Chewiness integrates the energy required to masticate emulsified sausage to a swallowable state and serves as a central indicator of sensory acceptability and textural quality, closely coupled to hardness, springiness, and cohesiveness. The effects of the three polysaccharides on chewiness are presented in Fig. 2(d). Relative to the polysaccharide-free control, guar gum, tara gum, and linseed gum each increased the chewiness of low-fat emulsified sausage to varying extents (P < 0.05), and the differences among treatments were also significant (P < 0.05). Tara gum produced the most pronounced improvement, consistent with the hardness pattern in Fig. 2(a). A clear ranking emerged. This enhancement likely reflects its stronger capacity to build a cohesive gel network—thereby elevating hardness, springiness, and cohesiveness in concert and yielding a more substantial chewing sensation. Guar gum, in contrast, appears to contribute mainly through thickening and water retention, offering moderate structural support and a corresponding mid-content improvement in chewiness; linseed gum, whose gelling ability is comparatively weaker, showed a limited strengthening effect. Consistent with this interpretation, prior work has shown that an appropriate content of carrageenan can improve sausage quality by increasing batter viscosity, strengthening intermolecular interactions among meat proteins, and promoting a denser protein–polysaccharide network, ultimately enhancing structural attributes (Cao et al., 2022). Overall, increases in chewiness were associated with the strength of the polysaccharide-derived three-dimensional network, its water-holding capacity, and its interactions with proteins, with tara gum showing the best performance in improving chewiness in low-fat emulsified sausages.

SEM

From a microstructural perspective, SEM provides a direct window into polysaccharide–protein interactions and the integrity of gel matrices in meat products (Zhang et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 5, reducing fat content yielded a comparatively loose protein-gel network relative to the control, characterized by a heterogeneous pore-size distribution, locally enlarged voids, and poorer surface continuity. The matrix appeared less compact. Such features are consistent with a structure that may facilitate water migration and, consequently, increase cooking loss. In contrast, all polysaccharide-treated groups exhibited microstructural improvements to varying degrees.Fig. 5Effects of different polysaccharide additions on the microstructure of emulsified sausages.Fig. 5

At the 1.6% addition content, all three treatments exhibited a more integrated and dense three-dimensional network than the control group, with an evident reduction in pore size. Among them, the T1.6 sample showed a relatively uniform and dense network with fewer voids; the G1.6 sample appeared smoother overall, although a small number of irregular gaps remained; the L1.6 sample developed partial lamellar features, accompanied by an improvement in network integrity. Collectively, these observations suggest that a moderate polysaccharide addition can fill pores within the protein network and increase matrix compactness.

At the 2.0% addition content, the network structure became further densified across treatments compared with both the control and the 0% group. The T2.0 sample retained a uniformly tight architecture. In contrast, the G2.0 sample displayed a flatter surface but showed localized aggregation. Across both addition content, T produced the most uniform and compact protein network, followed by G, while L appeared slightly less homogeneous at the higher dosage.

These results suggest that polysaccharides, through hydration effects and interactions with myofibrillar proteins, can facilitate the development of a more compact and stable gel network, thereby strengthening water confinement. This interpretation aligns with the study by Zhou et al. (2021), in which citrate was shown to enhance intermolecular protein interactions and improve network homogeneity. Along similar lines, Jiang, Yang, and Li (2024) noted that dietary fiber can reduce gel pore size via spatial filling and hydrophobic interactions, leading to a denser gel matrix. Taken together, the trends observed for cooking loss and water-holding capacity observed here corroborate this microstructural evidence: the more compact and homogeneous the network, the greater the product's capacity to retain water.

Tara gum likewise performed best at 1.6%. The more ordered gel framework formed at this dosage appeared to support springiness effectively, yielding an overall score close to that of the high-fat control; at higher content, however, the matrix became overly compact, leading to a sticky, hardened bite and a darker appearance, which reduced sensory acceptance. By comparison, linseed gum showed the most comprehensive optimization at 1.6%. Discussions

By systematically comparing low-fat emulsified sausages formulated with different polysaccharide types and dosages across multiple quality metrics—including cooking loss, textural attributes, color, water distribution, and sensory performance—we found that guar gum, tara gum, and linseed gum each improved product quality to varying extents. The magnitude and nature of the improvement, however, were strongly dependent on both polysaccharide identity and addition content. Importantly, this comparative analysis provides further mechanistic insight into how different polysaccharides contribute to fat-replacing functionality through distinct structural and physicochemical characteristics. Guar gum may be primarily associated with enhanced viscosity and water immobilization, whereas tara gum may contribute more to gel matrix stability through potential interactions with meat proteins. In contrast, linseed gum may play a more prominent role in emulsion stabilization, possibly owing to its anionic heteropolysaccharide structure (Nasonova & Tunieva, 2019). Guar gum, tara gum, and linseed gum act as fat replacers by improving water retention, emulsion stability, and matrix structure, thereby helping to maintain juiciness, lubrication, texture, and mouthfeel in low-fat emulsified sausages (Totaro et al., 2025).

The addition of guar gum, tara gum, and linseed gum reduced cooking loss and improved the water-holding capacity of low-fat emulsified sausage, with tara gum showing the most pronounced effect at 1.6% addition. This observation is consistent with Cortez-Trejo et al. (2021) who reported that both polysaccharide type and addition contents can significantly influence cooking loss in meat protein gels. Unlike traditional single-component carbohydrate fat replacers such as kappa-carrageenan or alginate, which predominantly form rigid, phase-separated crystalline domains within meat matrices (Cao et al., 2022), the parallel comparison evaluated here reveals highly divergent structural pathways. The reduction in cooking loss may be related to the changes in water distribution observed by LF-NMR. Specifically, polysaccharide addition restricted water mobility and increased the proportion of bound water, thereby contributing to improved water retention in low-fat emulsified sausages (Bao, Wang, et al., 2025). Such a matrix can better retain moisture (and associated lipids) during heating, thereby limiting exudation—an interpretation that aligns with the explanation provided by Alboofetileh et al. (2025) for alginate-based polysaccharides in beef sausages. With respect to color, the addition of the three polysaccharides decreased the lightness (L*) and redness (a*) of low-fat emulsified sausages to varying degrees, while exerting no significant effect on yellowness (b*). The reduction in L* is closely associated with the lower fat content, whereas the attenuation of a* may also reflect the intrinsic color of the polysaccharides. Turning to texture, all three polysaccharides significantly increased hardness and chewiness in the low-fat system, with tara gum producing the most pronounced enhancement; this agrees with Li et al. (2025), who found that polysaccharides enhance the strength of myofibrillar protein gels. The increases in hardness and chewiness are likely attributable to hydrogen bonding and related interactions by which polysaccharide chains cooperate with myofibrillar proteins to build a denser three-dimensional gel network. In contrast, springiness showed no marked overall change, and cohesiveness tended to decline across all low-fat treatments. A similar pattern was noted by Cao et al. (2022) and may arise from fat reduction altering the binding state and spatial organization of components within the emulsified matrix.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analysis indicated that adding the three polysaccharides significantly reshaped water distribution in the low-fat emulsified sausage, generally increasing the bound-water fraction (P2b) while reducing the free-water fraction (P22). This finding is consistent with Hou et al. (2024) who reported that composite hydrocolloids used as fat replacers can optimize water distribution in meat batters. The observed shift in water populations likely reflects polysaccharide–myofibrillar gel interactions. As noted by Han et al. (2014) proton relaxation behavior is closely linked to the microstructural characteristics of protein gels. In the present work, polysaccharides may have competitively associated with water via their hydrophilic groups and, through pore filling and network reinforcement, promoted a more compact three-dimensional gel matrix with enhanced water-holding capacity—thereby converting a portion of mobile water into more tightly associated, immobilized water. Such optimization of water status provides a mechanistic explanation for the previously observed reductions in cooking loss, improvements in water-holding capacity, and increases in hardness, further supporting the notion that polysaccharides can compensate for fat functionality by modulating water retention and mobility (Huang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). Crucially, a direct multi-scale correlation exists between this optimized water distribution and the gel microstructure. The development of a highly compact, homogeneous protein-polysaccharide network with reduced pore dimensions, as visualized by SEM, introduces heightened spatial confinement and enhanced capillary forces within the biopolymer matrix. This restricted physical environment directly limits the translational and rotational mobility of encapsulated water molecules (Hou et al., 2024). Consequently, this microstructural entrapment manifests as a distinct shift in the LF-NMR proton relaxation profiles toward shorter T₂ domains and a concomitant reduction in the mobile free-water fraction (P₂₂). Conversely, the loose, fragmented network of the low-fat control possesses large structural voids that fail to confine moisture, thereby allowing high proton mobility, prolonged T₂ relaxation, and excessive macroscopic exudation (Yang et al., 2021).

Sensory evaluation offers the most direct gauge of product acceptability. In the present study, an appropriate addition of guar gum, tara gum, or linseed gum improved the overall sensory quality of low-fat emulsified sausage. Within the 1.6%–2.0% range, all three polysaccharides significantly increased scores for texture, juiciness, and mouthfeel; notably, linseed gum at 1.6% achieved an overall sensory score of 43.15, numerically higher than the high-fat control (42.20). Nevertheless, it suggests that linseed gum can reproduce key fat-associated sensations—lubricity and perceived juiciness—in a manner acceptable to trained panelists. Electronic-nose measurements support this interpretation, as samples formulated within this dosage window exhibited flavor fingerprints most similar to the high-fat product; this pattern implies that polysaccharide addition not only improves texture by promoting a denser gel matrix but may also favor aroma retention and regulate release via physical entrapment as well as intermolecular interactions, thereby enhancing the overall flavor experience. Therefore, the selection of an appropriate polysaccharide type and addition contents is critical for achieving a balance between water retention, textural properties, and sensory acceptance in low-fat emulsified meat products. These findings provide practical guidance for the rational application of polysaccharides as functional fat replacers in the development of healthier meat products.

Conclusions

This study achieved its aim of systematically comparing the effects of guar gum, tara gum, and linseed gum on the quality of low-fat emulsified sausages under the same formulation and processing conditions. The results showed that all three polysaccharides improved cooking stability, water retention, textural properties, and sensory quality to varying degrees, but their effects were dependent on polysaccharide type and addition content. Tara gum was more effective in improving cooking stability and textural strength, whereas linseed gum provided a more balanced sensory performance, particularly in juiciness and mouthfeel. Guar gum also contributed to quality improvement, mainly through water retention and matrix stabilization. Overall, these polysaccharides should be regarded as functional ingredients for compensating for quality losses caused by fat reduction. Among the three polysaccharides, tara gum at 1.6% is recommended as the preferred overall fat replacer because it provided the best balance among cooking stability, textural enhancement, water retention, microstructural improvement, and acceptable sensory quality. However, the choice should depend on the primary product-development target: tara gum at 1.6% may be more suitable when texture and stability are prioritized, whereas linseed gum at 1.6% may be preferred when sensory optimization is the primary target.
