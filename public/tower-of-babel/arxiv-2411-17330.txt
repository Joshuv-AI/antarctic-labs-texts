Derandomizing Multivariate Polynomial Factoring for Low Degree Factors
Pranjal Dutta, Amit Sinhababu, Thomas Thierauf

Abstract
For a polynomial f f from a class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of polynomials, we show that the problem to compute all the constant degree irreducible factors of f f reduces in polynomial time to polynomial identity tests (PIT) for class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} and divisibility tests of f f by constant degree polynomials. We apply the result to several classes 𝒞 \mathcal{C} and obtain the constant degree factors in 1. polynomial time, for 𝒞 \mathcal{C} being polynomials that have only constant degree factors, 2. quasi-polynomial time, for 𝒞 \mathcal{C} being sparse polynomials, 3. subexponential time, for 𝒞 \mathcal{C} being polynomials that have constant-depth circuits. Result 2 and 3 were already shown by Kumar, Ramanathan, and Saptharishi with a different proof, and their time complexities necessarily depend on blackbox PITs for a related bigger class 𝒞 ′ \mathcal{C}^{\prime} . Our complexities vary on whether the input is given as a blackbox or whitebox. We also show that the problem to compute the sparse factors of polynomial from a class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} reduces in polynomial time to PIT for class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} , divisibility tests of f f by sparse polynomials, and irreducibility preserving bivariate projections for sparse polynomials. For 𝒞 \mathcal{C} being sparse polynomials, it follows that it suffices to derandomize irreducibility preserving bivariate projections for sparse polynomials in order to compute all the sparse irreducible factors efficiently. When we consider factors of sparse polynomials that are sums of univariate polynomials, a subclass of sparse polynomials, we obtain a polynomial time algorithm. This was already shown by Volkovich with a different proof.



1
Introduction

The problem of
multivariate polynomial factorization
asks to find the unique factorization of
a given polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
as a product of distinct irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{F}
.
The problem reduces to
univariate polynomial factorization
over the same field, for which a deterministic polynomial time algorithm is known over the field
\mathbb{Q}
.
The complexity of multivariate factorization depends
on the representation of input and output polynomials.
If we use
dense representation
(where all the coefficients are listed including the zero coefficients), deterministic polynomial time algorithms for multivariate factoring are known
[
19
]
.
If we use
sparse representation
(where only the nonzero coefficients are listed), only randomized polynomial time
algorithms are known
[
45
,
18
]
.
There are other standard representations like arithmetic circuits,
and blackbox models,
where the evaluations of the polynomial at any point, but the internal structure of the computation is hidden.
Randomized polynomial time factorization algorithms are known in these models due to the classic results of Kaltofen
[
22
]
and Kaltofen and Trager
[
18
]
.
Randomization is naturally required for these models, as the more basic question of
polynomial identity testing
(PIT) is not yet derandomized and multivariate circuit factorization algorithms use PIT.
However, for various restricted arithmetic circuits we know deterministic (quasi-) polynomial- or subexponential-time PIT algorithms.
Naturally, one can ask whether factorization can be derandomized for the corresponding circuit classes. In various problems such as special cases of arithmetic circuit reconstruction, we encounter multivariate factoring of structured or very restricted instances, where either the input polynomial or some or all factors of the input polynomial belong to a very restricted class of circuits. Towards derandomization of special cases of multivariate factoring, we focus on the following two goals.

Challenge 1

(Promise factoring
[
45
]
)
.

Let
f=\prod_{i=1}^{m}{g_{i}}^{e_{i}}
,
where each
g_{i}
is an irreducible polynomial with its sparsity at most
s
.
Design a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm to output
g_{i}
.

To our surprise, we do not know a deterministic
polynomial
-time algorithm even for the special case of Challenge
1
, when the given blackbox computes the product of just
two
irreducible sparse polynomials.

Note that the factors of a sparse polynomial
f
might be nonsparse.
Therefore, instead of finding
all
the irreducible factors,
we only focus on those factors that are sparse.

Challenge 2
.

Given a sparse polynomial
f
,
design a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm that outputs all the sparse irreducible factors of
f
.

Multivariate polynomial factoring has various applications, such as
low-degree testing

[
1
]
, constructions of pseudorandom generators for low-degree polynomials
[
7
,
9
]
, algorithmic questions in computational algebraic geometry
[
15
]
and many more. Blackbox multivariate polynomial factorization algorithms are extensively used in
arithmetic circuit reconstruction

[
40
,
41
]
, and polynomial equivalence testing
[
24
,
25
,
35
]
.
There is a series of papers
starting with
[
17
]

about
algebraic hardness vs. randomness
results
that crucially rely on the closure of various algebraic complexity classes under factorization.
For a general overview of multivariate factoring,
we refer to the recent survey of Bhargav, Dwivedi,
and Saxena
[
2
]
.

General framework of factoring.

The typical steps in factorization algorithms for a given polynomial
f\in\mathcal{C}
, from a class
\mathcal{C}

are as follows.

1.

Transform
f
to a polynomial
\widehat{f}
that is monic in some newly introduced variable, using PIT; for a detailed discussion see below.

2.

Project
\widehat{f}
to a bivariate polynomial
such that the factorization pattern of
\widehat{f}
is maintained (by Hilbert Irreducibility Theorem; see Theorem
2.11
).

3.

Use the known algorithms to factor the projected bivariate polynomial.

4.

Now often
Hensel lifting
is used to lift the projected factors back to the real factors.
In our setting, we avoid Hensel lifting and argue that we can use
interpolation
instead. This interpolation trick was first used in the randomized blackbox factoring algorithm of Kaltofen and Trager
[
18
]
.

5.

To make sure that the computed polynomials are indeed factors,
there is a divisibility test at the end.

We now discuss some of the crucial steps and their importance in factoring a restricted class of polynomials. In our restricted setting, we completely
bypass
the step of Hensel lifting.

PIT.

Given an arithmetic circuit
C
, polynomial identity testing (PIT) asks to test if
C
computes the zero polynomial. Randomization in factorization algorithms mostly stem from the fact that these algorithms use PIT as a subroutine. Further, Kopparty, Saraf and Shpilka
[
28
]
showed that derandomization of whitebox and blackbox multivariate circuit factoring reduces to derandomization of polynomial identity testing of arithmetic circuits in whitebox and blackbox settings respectively. However, we
do not
know if sparse factorization reduces to sparse PIT (the algorithms of
[
28
]
reduce to general arithmetic circuit PIT). Recently, there has been good progress on these questions by
[
30
,
29
,
5
,
4
]
. Earlier works of Volkovich
[
43
,
44
]
and Bhargava, Saraf and Volkovich
[
3
]
made progress on derandomization of several special cases of sparse multivariate factoring. Shpilka and Volkovich
[
39
]
proved whitebox/blackbox factoring of
multilinear
polynomials in a class

\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to whitebox/blackbox derandomization of PIT of class
\mathcal{C}
.
For a comprehensive overview of derandomization questions related to multivariate polynomial factoring, we refer to the survey of Forbes and Shpilka
[
11
]
.

Divisibility testing.

In a factorization algorithm, we may want to check if a candidate factor is truly a factor via
divisibility testing
.
It asks to test if a polynomial
g({\boldsymbol{z}})
divides a polynomial
f({\boldsymbol{z}})
. Forbes
[
8
]
showed that the divisibility testing question can be efficiently reduced to an instance of a PIT question of a model that relates to both
f
and
g
, see Lemma
2.8
. Currently, we do not know any deterministic polynomial time algorithm even when
g
and
f
are both sparse polynomials.
When
f
is a sparse polynomial and
g
is a linear polynomial, the problem reduces to polynomial identity testing of any-order read-once oblivious branching programs (ROABPs),
see Section
3.4
.
We do not know a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm,
even for testing if a quadratic polynomial
g
divides a sparse polynomial.

Irreducibility projection.

For testing irreducibility of multivariate polynomials, one can use some of the effective versions of Hilbert’s Irreducibility
Theorem
[
45
,
20
,
23
]
.
These results can be also seen as an effective version of a classical theorem of Bertini in algebraic geometry. A multivariate irreducible polynomial
f(x,z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})
may become reducible if we project the variables to make it univariate, but irreducibility is preserved if we project it in a way to make it a bivariate polynomial. Unfortunately, finding such a projection in deterministic polynomial time is hard. We do not know
how to find polynomial-sized irreducibility preserving projections even for sparse polynomials, though we know polynomial-sized hitting set generators for them. This remains an outstanding open question.

Special cases of multivariate factoring.

In many settings, one has
a priori
structural information about the factors of a given polynomial—
for instance, that certain (or even all) factors are linear, have bounded degree, or more generally, are sparse.
A classic source of such a structure is the theory of
U
-
resultants
due to Macaulay, where one encounters families of polynomials that decompose into linear forms.
As another example, consider the standard arithmetization of a
3
-CNF formula:
it naturally produces a polynomial presented as a product of
clause polynomials
, and each clause polynomial depends on at most three variables and is multilinear
Restricted factorizations of this type have also been studied in the context of
algebraization

[
16
]
.

There are many prior works on special cases of multivariate polynomial factoring.
Koiran and Ressyare
[
27
]
gave
three
different randomized algorithms for testing, if a given
polynomial
f
as a blackbox can be completely factored into linear polynomials and output the factorization, if it exists.
Medini and Shpilka
[
33
]
gave a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm in the blackbox setting for the case,
where the polynomial can be factored into linearly independent linear polynomials.

Factorization of sparse multivariate polynomials is well-studied in symbolic computation.
The classic work of Gathen and Kaltofen
[
45
]
gives a randomized polynomial-time algorithm in the case when the input and output factors are sparse, and the number of irreducible factors is bounded.
Volkovich
[
43
,
44
]
made interesting progress on derandomization of some special cases of sparse multivariate factoring.
Bhargava, Saraf, and Volkovich
[
3
]
gave a deterministic polynomial time algorithm for factoring sparse polynomials with bounded individual degree.
Special cases of depth-4 polynomial identity testing are related to questions about sparse polynomial factorization
[
13
,
44
,
6
]
.

The sequence of papers
[
30
,
29
,
5
]
made significant progress on derandomization of factoring constant depth circuits. The recent breakthrough result by
Bhattacharjee et al.
[
4
]
showed that all factors of constant depth circuits are computable by poly-sized constant depth circuits, and they gave a deterministic subexponential-time algorithm to compute those circuits.

1.1
Our results

We show general results
that exhibits properties of a class
\mathcal{C}
of polynomials,
such that we can compute the
constant-degree
factors
or the
sparse
factors of polynomials
f\in\mathcal{C}
.
Thereby we get a unified and simple way reprove some known results
and also some new results.

Constant-degree factors.

In Theorem
3.2

we show that the irreducible constant-degree factors of a polynomial
f\in\mathcal{C}

with their multiplicities
can be computed in polynomial time relative to

•

PIT for the
top-degree homogeneous component of polynomials in
\mathcal{C}
,

•

divisibility tests of the partial derivatives of
\mathcal{C}
by constant-degree polynomials.

In many cases,
class
\mathcal{C}
is
closed
under taking homogeneous components
and partial derivatives.
Then the above two oracles simplify to

•

PIT for
\mathcal{C}
,

•

divisibility tests of
\mathcal{C}
by constant-degree polynomials.

Considering the above general algorithmic framework,
the PIT comes from step 1 and divisibility from step 5.
The Hilbert Irreducibility Theorem (see Theorem
2.11
)
yields a randomized way to do step 2.
The main point here is that we derandomize this step for constant-degree factors.

For a specific class
\mathcal{C}
,
it suffices now to consider the complexity of the above two points
that we already described before.
For example for
\mathcal{C}
being polynomials that have only constant degree factors,
this yields a polynomial time algorithm for computing the constant degree factors,
because PIT is in polynomial time and we can skip the divisibility test (Theorem
3.1
).
For
\mathcal{C}
being
sparse polynomials
,
we get a quasi-polynomial-time algorithm (Corollary
3.3
),
and
for
\mathcal{C}
being polynomials computed by
constant-depth circuits
,
we get subexponential-time (Corollary
3.4
).

The last two results were already shown by
Kumar, Ramanathan and Saptharishi
[
30
]

with a different technique, using Hensel lifting.
However,
there is a subtle difference.
In our case,
we maintain the setting.
That is, when the input is given whitebox,
we use whitebox algorithms, and similarly for blackbox.
On the other hand,
the factoring algorithm by Kumar, Ramanathan and Saptharishi
[
30
]
requires blackbox PIT algorithms,
even when the input is given in whitebox.
This is due to PIT for the
resultant polynomial

where the unknown factor is involved.

In the above two results,
this difference does not matter because we have the same complexity bounds
for whitebox and blackbox algorithms there.
An example where this difference matters are commutative

read-once oblivious arithmetic branching programs
(
\rm ROABP
).
There is a polynomial time whitebox PIT algorithm for ROABPs
[
36
]
, while the best-known blackbox PIT algorithm for commutative ROABPs runs in
quasi-polynomial time
[
14
,
12
]
.
Moreover,
it follows from work of Forbes
[
8
]

that the divisibility test for
\rm ROABP
s by
linear
polynomials
reduces to a PIT for
\rm ROABP
s.
Hence,
we have again polynomial, resp. quasi-polynomial time
for the divisibility test in the whitebox, resp. blackbox setting.
It follows that the linear factors of polynomials computed by commutative
\rm ROABP
s
can be computed in polynomial time in the whitebox setting,
whereas it takes quasi-polynomial time in the blackbox setting
(Corollary
3.8
).

Sparse factors.

In Theorem
4.2

we show that the irreducible sparse factors of a polynomial
f\in\mathcal{C}

with their multiplicities
can be computed in polynomial time relative to

•

PIT for the
top-degree homogeneous component of polynomials in
\mathcal{C}
,

•

divisibility tests of the partial derivatives of
\mathcal{C}
by constant-degree polynomials,

•

irreducible projection to bivariate polynomials.

Again,
if class
\mathcal{C}
is
closed
under taking homogeneous components
and partial derivatives,
the first two items boil down to a
PIT for
\mathcal{C}
and
divisibility tests of
\mathcal{C}
by constant-degree polynomials.

For
\mathcal{C}
being
sparse polynomials
,
the difficulty lies in the third point:
only subexponential-time algorithms are known to derandomize
Hilbert Irreducibility Theorem for sparse polynomials
[
4
]
.
The hardness stems from the fact that a sparse polynomial may have both sparse and non-sparse irreducible factors. Hence, preserving irreducibility for sparse polynomials will not preserve the factorization pattern, and therefore, it may be hard to get back the actual factor.
Nevertheless,
Theorem
4.2
pinpoints the challenge to compute the sparse factors of sparse polynomials
(Corollary
4.4
and
4.5
).

For polynomials that can be written as a sum of univariate polynomials,
a subclass of sparse polynomials,
the irreducible projection problem can be solved in polynomial time.
Therefore we can compute the sum-of-univariate factors of sparse polynomials
in polynomial time (Corollary
4.9
).

Preliminaries

We take
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
as the underlying field throughout the paper,
although the results hold as well over fields with large characteristic.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}(n,d)
be the set of
n
-variate polynomials of degree at most
d
,
with variables
{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})
.
By
\deg(f)
we denote the total degree of
f
.
For an exponent vector
{\boldsymbol{e}}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n})
,
we denote the monomial

{\boldsymbol{z}}^{{\boldsymbol{e}}}=z_{1}^{e_{1}}\,z_{2}^{e_{2}}\,\cdots\,z_{n}^{e_{n}}\,.

(2)

Its degree is
||{\boldsymbol{e}}||_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}
.
The number of monomials of a polynomial
f
with nonzero coefficient is called
the
sparsity of

f
and is denoted by
\textup{sp}(f)
.
The class of polynomials with sparsity
s
is denoted by

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(s,n,d)=\left\{\,p\in{\mathcal{P}}(n,d)\mid\textup{sp}(p)\leq s\,\right\}.

(3)

For
\deg(f)=d
,
we can write
f=\sum_{k=0}^{d}f_{k}
,
where
f_{k}=\textup{Hom}_{k}[f]
denotes the homogeneous component of
f
of degree
k
.
For the highest degree component,
we also skip the index, i.e. we define
\textup{Hom}[f]=\textup{Hom}_{d}[f]=f_{d}
.
For a class
\mathcal{C}
of polynomials, we define

\textup{Hom}[\mathcal{C}]=\left\{\,\textup{Hom}[f]\mid f\in\mathcal{C}\,\right\}.

(4)

We define class
\partial\mathcal{C}
as all the partial
derivatives of polynomials in
\mathcal{C}
,

\partial\mathcal{C}=\left\{\,\frac{\partial^{e}f}{\partial z^{e}}\mid f\in\mathcal{C},~z\text{ a variable of }f,\text{ and }e\geq 0\,\right\}.

(5)

Note that
f\in\partial\mathcal{C}
, because
f=\frac{\partial^{0}f}{\partial z^{0}}
.

Polynomial
f({\boldsymbol{z}})

depends on
variable
z_{i}
,
if
\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_{i}}\not=0
.
The variables that
f
depends on are denoted by
\textup{var}(f)
,

\textup{var}(f({\boldsymbol{z}}))=\left\{\,z_{i}\mid f\text{ depends on }z_{i}\,\right\}.

(6)

A polynomial
f
is called
irreducible
,
if it cannot be factored into the product of two non-constant polynomials.

Let
x
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})
be variables and
f(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})

be a
(n+1)
-variate polynomial.
Then we can view
f
as a univariate polynomial
f=\sum_{i}a_{i}({\boldsymbol{z}})\,x^{i}

over
\mathbb{K}[x]
, where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]
.
The
x
-degree of

f
is denoted by
\deg_{x}(f)
.
It is the highest degree of
x
in
f
.
Polynomial
f
is called
monic in

x
,
if the coefficient
a_{d_{x}}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is the constant
1
polynomial,
i.e.
a_{d_{x}}({\boldsymbol{z}})=1
,
where
d_{x}=\deg_{x}(f)
.

An algorithm runs in
subexponential time
,
if its running time on inputs of length
n
is bounded by

2^{O(n^{\varepsilon})}
,
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

2.1
Computational problems and complexity measures

For classes
{\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{Q}}
of multivariate polynomials, we define the following
computational problems.

•

{\rm PIT}({\mathcal{P}})
: given
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
, decide whether
p\equiv 0
.

•

{\rm Factor}({\mathcal{P}}|_{{\mathcal{Q}}})
: given
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
, compute all its irreducible factors in
{\mathcal{Q}}
with their multiplicities.

•

{\rm Div}({\mathcal{P}}/{\mathcal{Q}})
: given
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
, decide whether
q|p
.

The time complexity to solve these problems
we denote by
\textup{T}_{{\rm PIT}({\mathcal{P}})}
,
\textup{T}_{{\rm Factor}({\mathcal{P}}|_{{\mathcal{Q}}})}
, and
\textup{T}_{{\rm Div}({\mathcal{P}}/{\mathcal{Q}})}
, respectively.

The problems are further distinguished according to the access to the given polynomial
p
.
In the
whitebox
case,
one gets the representation of
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
, for example a circuit or a formula,
whereas in the
blackbox
case, one can only evaluate
p
.
For PIT,
a blackbox solution therefore means to compute a
hitting set
for a class
{\mathcal{P}}
.
This is a set
H\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that for every nonzero
p\in{\mathcal{P}}

there exists
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in H
such that
p({\boldsymbol{a}})\neq 0
.

The blackbox PIT-algorithm then simply evaluates
p
at all points
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in H
.
Hence,
the size of
H
is crucial for the running time
\textup{T}_{{\rm PIT}({\mathcal{P}})}
.
The
trivial hitting set
for
{\mathcal{P}}(n,d)

used in the Schwartz-Zippel PIT-Lemma
is

H_{n,d}=[d+1]^{n}

(7)

of size
|H_{n,d}|=(d+1)^{n}
,
i.e. exponential in the number
n
of variables.
We will use it for constant-variate polynomials.
Then the size is polynomial.

For the decision problems
{\rm PIT}({\mathcal{P}})
and
{\rm Div}({\mathcal{P}}/{\mathcal{Q}})

there is an associated
construction problem
.
In case of
{\rm PIT}({\mathcal{P}})
,
a construction algorithm
also yields a point
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
such that
p({\boldsymbol{a}})\not=0
,
in case when
p\not\equiv 0
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
be a nonzero polynomial.
A point
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
such that
p({\boldsymbol{a}})\not=0

can be computed in time
nd\,\textup{T}_{{\rm PIT}({\mathcal{P}})}
.

Proof.

In the blackbox case,
let
H_{0}
be the queries of the decision algorithm
on input of the zero-polynomial.
Note that
H_{0}
is a hitting set for the whole class
{\mathcal{P}}
.
Hence,
we can find
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in H_{0}
in time
\textup{T}_{{\rm PIT}({\mathcal{P}})}
such that
p({\boldsymbol{a}})\not=0
.

Still, some coordinates of
{\boldsymbol{a}}
might be
0
.
In this case,
we shift
{\boldsymbol{a}}
:
Let
t
be a new variable and consider
{\boldsymbol{a}}+t=(a_{1}+t,a_{2}+t,\dots,a_{n}+t)
.
Then
p({\boldsymbol{a}}+t)
is a nonzero polynomial in one variable of degree
d
.
Choose a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}-\{-a_{1},-a_{2},\dots,-a_{n}\}
of size
|S|=d+1
.
Then
{\boldsymbol{a}}+t
has only non-zero coordinates, for all
t\in S
,
and there is a
t\in S

such that
p({\boldsymbol{a}}+t)\not=0
.
For the time complexity to find the right
t
,
we have to add
(d+1)
evaluations of
p
.

In the whitebox case,
one can search for
{\boldsymbol{a}}
by assigning values successively to the variables
and do kind of a
self-reduction
.
For each variable, one tries at most
d
values from
\{1,2,\dots,d\}

for a polynomial of degree
d
.
If they all give
0
, definitely
d+1
works because it cannot be zero at
(d+1)
many values.
With
n
variables, this amounts to
nd
calls to the
{\rm PIT}
-decision algorithm.
∎

For time complexity,
we assume that the polynomials are given in some model of computation,
such as circuits, branching programs, or formulas.
With each model, we associate a
complexity measure
\mu:\mathbb{F}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.
For example, let
f\in\mathbb{F}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]
, some of the commonly used measures in the literature are:

•

\mu(f)=\textup{sp}(f)
, the number of monomials with nonzero coefficients,

•

\mu(f)={\rm size}_{\Delta}(f)
, the size of the
smallest
depth-
\Delta
circuit that computes
f
,

•

\mu(f)={\rm size}_{\rm ROABP}(f)
,
the
width
of the smallest read-once oblivious branching program (ROABP) that computes
f
.

We define classes of polynomials of bounded measure,

\mathcal{C}_{\mu}(s,n,d)\;=\;\left\{\,f\in{\mathcal{P}}(n,d)\mid\mu(f)\leq s\,\right\}.

(8)

When we skip the index
\mu
, we just refer to circuit size,

\mathcal{C}(s,n,d)=\left\{\,p\in{\mathcal{P}}(n,d)\mid p\text{ has a circuit of size }s\,\right\}.

(9)

We also consider polynomials that can be computed by circuits of size
s
and depth
t
,

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}(s,n,d)=\left\{\,p\in\mathcal{C}(s,n,d)\mid p\text{ has a circuit of depth }t\,\right\}.

(10)

We generally assume that all polynomials in this paper
can be
efficiently evaluated
at any point
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}

within the respective measure,
where we consider the unit-cost model for operations over
\mathbb{F}
.
This holds for all the computational models usually considered in the literature.

2.2
Transformation to a monic polynomial

Algorithms for factoring polynomials often assume that the given polynomial is monic.
If this is not the case for the given polynomial
f
,
we apply a transformation
\tau
to
f
that yields
a monic polynomial
\tau(f)
that we can factor.
From the factors of
\tau(f)
we can then reveal the factors of
f
.
Although this is standard in the literature,
we state it in the terms we introduced above.

Lemma 2.2

(Transformation to monic)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}=\mathcal{C}_{\mu}(s,n,d)
be a class of polynomials,

f({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
, and

f_{d}=\textup{Hom}_{d}[f]
be the homogeneous degree
d
component of
f
.
For a new variable
x
, and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
,
define a linear transformation
\tau_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
on the variables
z_{i}
:

\tau_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}:~~z_{i}\mapsto\alpha_{i}x+z_{i},

(11)

for
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
Let
f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
be the resulting polynomial.

We can compute
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
such that

\frac{1}{f_{d}(\alpha)}\,f_{\alpha}(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is monic in
x

in time

nd\,\textup{T}_{{\rm PIT}(\textup{Hom}[\mathcal{C}])}+{\rm poly}(snd)\,.

(12)

Proof.

Let
f({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathcal{C}
be a polynomial of degree
d

with
n
variables
{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})
.
To see what the transformation does,
let

f=f_{0}+f_{1}+\cdots+f_{d},

(13)

where
f_{k}=\textup{Hom}_{k}[f]
, the homogeneous degree-
k
component of
f
.
Consider the degree-
d
component,

f_{d}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}|_{1}=d}c_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}.

(14)

Then, for
f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
,
we have
\deg_{x}(f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})=d
and
the coefficient of the leading
x
-term
x^{d}
in
f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
is

f_{d}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}|_{1}=d}c_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
.

Hence, the PIT algorithm for the homogeneous component
f_{d}
of
f

yields an
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
such that
f_{d}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\not=0
,
by Lemma
2.1
.
Then the polynomial
\frac{1}{f_{d}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})}\,f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is monic in
x
.
∎

For simplicity of notation,
assume in the following that
f_{d}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=1
,
so that
f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is monic in
x
.

Since we work with the shifted polynomial, we need to ensure that the shift of variables does not affect the irreducibility of the factors; this is guaranteed by the following lemma. It is quite standard in the literature; for a nice proof, see
[
29
, Lemma B7]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathbb{F}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]
be an n-variate irreducible polynomial. Then, for every
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
, the polynomial
f({\boldsymbol{a}}x+{\boldsymbol{z}})
is also irreducible.

2.3
Basics of factoring and interpolation and PIT

Berlekamp
[
10
]

and Lenstra, Lenstra and Lovász
[
31
]
gave efficient deterministic factorization algorithms for
univariate
polynomials over finite fields and
\mathbb{Q}
, respectively.
Kaltofen
[
21
]
showed how to reduce the factorization of
bivariate

polynomials to univariate polynomials.
In fact, the reduction works for
k
-variate polynomials,
for any constant
k
.
In our case,
we use it for the case
k=3
.

Via standard interpolation, one can assume that the input is given as a dense representation.

Lemma 2.4

(Trivariate Factorization)
.

Let
f(x,y,z)
be a trivariate polynomial of degree
d
.
Then there exists an algorithm that outputs all its irreducible factors and their multiplicities in time
{\rm poly}(d)
.

The following lemma shows how to find the multiplicity of an irreducible
factor
g
of a polynomial
f
. It holds when char
(\mathbb{F})=0
, or large. For a concise proof, see
[
30
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.5

(Factor multiplicity)
.

Let
f({\boldsymbol{z}}),g({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathbb{F}[{\boldsymbol{z}}]
be non-zero polynomials and let
z\in\{z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{n}\}
be such that
\partial_{z}(g)\neq 0
and
g
is irreducible.
Then the multiplicity of
g
in
f
is
the smallest non-negative integer
e
such that
g\nmid\frac{\partial^{e}f}{\partial z^{e}}
.

Klivans and Spielman
[
26
]
derandomized the isolation lemma for PIT of sparse polynomials.
Their algorithm works over fields of
0
or large characteristic.

Theorem 2.6

(Sparse PIT and interpolation
[
26
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(s,n,d)
.
Then
{\rm PIT}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}})
can be solved in time

\textup{T}_{{\rm PIT}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}})}={\rm poly}(snd).

(15)

Furthermore,
given
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}
,
in time
{\rm poly}(snd)
one can compute a set of
evaluation points

{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}

of size
{\rm poly}(snd)

such that given the evaluations of
f
at all points in
{\mathcal{E}}
,
one can solve for the coefficients of
f
in time
{\rm poly}(snd)
.

Limaye, Srinivasan, and Tavenas
[
32
]
designed a deterministic subexponential-time PIT for constant-depth circuits.

Theorem 2.7

(PIT for constant depth circuits
[
32
, Corollary 6]
)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be a real number.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}(s,n,d)
be such that

s\leq{\rm poly}(n)
and
t=o(\log\log\log n)
.
Then
{\rm PIT}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}})
can be decided in time

\textup{T}_{{\rm PIT}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}})}=\left(ns^{O(t)}\right)^{O((sd)^{\varepsilon})}.

(16)

2.4
Divisibility testing reduces to PIT

Given a polynomial
f
to factor,
our algorithms might compute a polynomial
g
that is a
candidate for a factor, but in fact, is not a factor.
Hence,
we have to verify whether
g
is a factor of
f
,
i.e., whether
g|f
.
Therefore,
we are interested in the complexity of division algorithms.

When
f,g
can be computed by circuits of size
s
,
Strassen
[
42
]
showed that if
g|f
,
then
h=f/g
can be computed by a circuit of size
{\rm poly}(sd)
,
where
d=\deg(h)
.
Forbes
[
8
]
observed that
even in the case when
g\not|\,f
,
one can follow Strassen’s argument and obtain
a small size circuit that computes a polynomial
\widetilde{h}

such that
g|f\iff f=g\widetilde{h}
.
Hence,
we have a reduction from divisibility testing to PIT.

Lemma 2.8

(Divisibility reduces to PIT
[
8
, Corollary 7.10]
)
.

Let
g({\boldsymbol{z}})
and
f({\boldsymbol{z}})
be two polynomials of degree at most
d
.
Let

S=[2d^{2}+1]
and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that
g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\neq 0
.
Then there are constants
\{c_{\beta,i}\}_{\beta\in S,0\leq i\leq d}
,
computable in time
{\rm poly}(d)
,
such that for

\widetilde{h}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\sum_{\beta\in S}f(\beta{\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\sum_{0\leq i\leq d}c_{\beta,i}\,g(\beta{\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^{i},

(17)

we have

g({\boldsymbol{z}})|f({\boldsymbol{z}})\iff f({\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=g({\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\,\widetilde{h}({\boldsymbol{z}}).

(18)

A consequence from Lemma
2.8
is that
divisibility testing of a polynomial computed by constant-depth circuit
by a sparse polynomial is in subexponential time.

Corollary 2.9

(Constant depth by sparse division)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}(s,n,d)
and

{\mathcal{D}}_{\textup{sp}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(s,n,d)
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, we have that

{\rm Div}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}/{\mathcal{D}}_{\textup{sp}})
can be decided in time

\textup{T}_{{\rm Div}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}/{\mathcal{D}}_{\textup{sp}})}=\left(n\,(sd)^{O(t)}\right)^{O((sd)^{\epsilon})}\,.

(19)

Proof.

We apply Lemma
2.8
with

g\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}
and
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}
.
Then
\widetilde{h}
in (
17
) can be computed by a circuit of size
O(sd^{2})
and depth
t+2
.
Therefore, the polynomial

\widetilde{f}=f({\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})-g({\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\cdot\widetilde{h}({\boldsymbol{z}})

(20)

can be computed by a circuit of size
O(sd^{2})
and depth
t+4
.
By Lemma
2.8
,
we have
\widetilde{f}=0
iff
g|f
,
and by Theorem
2.7
,
the identity can be checked in time

\left(n\,(sd)^{O(t)}\right)^{O((sd)^{\epsilon})}
.
∎

We also consider divisibility of sparse polynomials by
constant-degree
polynomials.
Building on Lemma
2.8
,
Forbes
[
8
]
reduced the problem to a PIT
that can be solved in quasi-polynomial time.

Corollary 2.10

(Sparse by constant degree division
[
8
, Corollary 7.17]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(s,n,d)
and

{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}={\mathcal{P}}(n,\delta)
.
Then
{\rm Div}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}/{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta})
can be decided in time

\textup{T}_{{\rm Div}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}/{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta})}=(snd)^{O(\delta\log s)}\,.

(21)

2.5
Effective Hilbert’s Irreducibility Theorem

Factorization algorithms often start with
an effective version of Hilbert’s Irreducibility Theorem
due to Kaltofen and von zur Gathen.
It shows how to project a multivariate irreducible polynomial down to two variables,
such that the projected bivariate polynomial stays irreducible.
The proof shows the existence of
an
irreducibility certifying polynomial

G({\boldsymbol{b}},{\boldsymbol{c}})
in
2n
variables corresponding to the irreducible polynomial
g(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
.
The nonzeroness
of
G
proves the irreducibility of
g(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
and also gives a way to find an irreducibility-preserving projection to bivariate
(see
[
20
,
23
,
28
]
).

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
g(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
be an irreducible polynomial of total degree
\delta
with
n+1
variables
that is monic in
x
.
There exists a nonzero polynomial
G({\boldsymbol{b}},{\boldsymbol{c}})
of degree
2\delta^{5}
in
2n
variables such that for
{\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
,

G({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\neq 0\implies\widehat{g}(x,t)=g(x,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}t+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\text{ is irreducible,}

(22)

where
g(x,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}t+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=g(x,\beta_{1}t+\gamma_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}t+\gamma_{n})
.

The certifying polynomial
G
immediately yields a randomized algorithm
to construct the irreducible projection
\widehat{g}
via PIT.
The derandomization of Hilbert’s Irreducibility Theorem is a challenging open problem
in general.
Essentially it means to find a hitting set for
G
.

We define a corresponding computational problem.
Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(n,d)
be a class of polynomials and
g({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathcal{C}
.
Assume we have already computed an
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})

as in Lemma
2.2
that the shifted polynomial
g_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is monic.
Now we want to find a hitting set for
g_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
according to (
22
).

•

{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C})
:

Given
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
,
compute a set
H_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{2n}
such that for all
g\in\mathcal{C}
,
where
g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}x+{\boldsymbol{z}})
is monic in
x
, we have

g\text{ irreducible }\implies\exists({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\in H_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}~~~g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}x+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}t+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\in\mathbb{F}[x,t]~\text{ is irreducible}.

(23)

By
\textup{T}_{{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C})}

we denote the time complexity to compute
{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C})
.

Remark
.

A subtlety in the definition of
{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C})
is that
it asks to compute a hitting set
H_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
for the
shifted
polynomial
g_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
.
If
\mathcal{C}
is closed under variable shifts,
then the hitting set is for polynomials in
\mathcal{C}
.
However,
for example
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}
is not closed under variable shifts.
Hence,

{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}})
asks to compute a hitting set for
a non-sparse polynomial that comes from a shift of a sparse polynomial.

2.6
Isolation

Let
M_{\delta}
be the set of monomials in
n
variables

{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})

of degree bounded by
\delta
,

M_{\delta}=\left\{\,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{{\boldsymbol{e}}}\mid||{\boldsymbol{e}}||_{1}\leq\delta\,\right\}.

(24)

Note that
M_{\delta}
is polynomially bounded, for constant
\delta
,

|M_{\delta}|\leq\binom{n+\delta}{\delta}\leq(n+\delta)^{\delta}\leq(\delta+1)\,\delta^{\delta}\,n^{\delta}=O(n^{\delta}).

(25)

There is a standard way to map the multivariate monomials in
M_{\delta}

in a injective way to univariate monomials of polynomial degree.
For completeness,
we describe the details.

Consider the standard
Kronecker substitution
on
M_{\delta}
.
Define

\varphi:\;z_{i}\;\mapsto\;y^{(\delta+1)^{i-1}}\,.

(26)

By extending
\varphi
linearly to monomials
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{{\boldsymbol{e}}}\in M_{\delta}
,
we get

\varphi:\;{\boldsymbol{z}}^{{\boldsymbol{e}}}\;\mapsto\;y^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}(\delta+1)^{i-1}}\,,

(27)

Clearly,

\varphi
is injective on
M_{\delta}
.
However,
the degree of
y
can be exponentially large, up to
(\delta+1)^{n}
.
A way around is to take the exponents modulo some small prime number
p
.
We have to determine
p
in a way to keep the mapping injective on
M_{\delta}
.
Hence,
for any two terms
y^{e},y^{e^{\prime}}
we get from
\varphi
,
we have to ensure that
e\not\equiv e^{\prime}\pmod{p}
.
Equivalently
p\not|\,(e-e^{\prime})
.

We have
|e-e^{\prime}|\leq(\delta+1)^{n}
and, by (
25
),
there are
O(n^{2\delta})
many pairs
e,e^{\prime}
we get from
M_{\delta}
via
\varphi
.
Prime
p
should not divide any of these differences,
and hence,
p
should not divide the product
P

of all these differences.
The product
P
is bounded by

P\leq\left((\delta+1)^{n}\right)^{O(n^{2\delta})}=(\delta+1)^{O(n^{2\delta+1})}.

(28)

Hence,

P
has at most

\log P\leq R=O(n^{2\delta+1})

many prime factors.
By the Prime Number Theorem,
there are more than
\log P
primes in
[R^{2}]
.
Hence,
we can find an appropriate prime
p\leq R^{2}=n^{O(\delta)}
.

Lemma 2.12
.

There is a prime
p=n^{O(\delta)}
such that the linear extension of

\varphi_{p}:\;z_{i}\;\mapsto\;y^{w_{i}}\,,~~\text{where}~w_{i}=(\delta+1)^{i-1}\bmod{p}\,,~\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,n,

(29)

to monomials is injective on
M_{\delta}
.
Moreover,
we can find such a
p
in time
n^{O(\delta)}

and compute and invert
\varphi_{p}
in time
n^{O(\delta)}
.

Proof.

We already argued about the existence of prime
p
.
For the running time,
recall that
|M_{\delta}|=O(n^{\delta})
.
Therefore we can search for
p
and check whether it works on
M_{\delta}

in time
n^{O(\delta)}
.
At the same time we can compute pairs of exponents
({\boldsymbol{e}},k)

such that
\varphi_{p}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{{\boldsymbol{e}}})=y^{k}
.
These pairs can be used to invert
\varphi_{p}
.
∎

The mapping
\varphi_{p}
in Lemma
2.12
maintains
factors of degree
\delta
of a polynomial in the following sense.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let polynomial
f({\boldsymbol{z}})
factor as
f=gh
,
where
g({\boldsymbol{z}})
has degree
\delta
.
Let
\varphi_{p}
be the map from Lemma
2.12
.
Then we have
\varphi_{p}(f)=\varphi_{p}(g)\varphi_{p}(h)
,
and
g
can be recovered from
\varphi_{p}(g)
in time
n^{O(\delta)}
.

Note that in Lemma
2.13
,
we do
not
claim that irreducibility is maintained:
when
g
is irreducible, still
\varphi_{p}(g)
might be reducible. Consider the example
n=\delta=2
.
The weights
\{1,3\}
make sure that each monomial in

M_{2}=\{\,z_{1},~z_{2},~z_{1}^{2},~z_{1}z_{2},~z_{2}^{2}\,\}

gets mapped to a distinct power in
y
.
Let

g({\boldsymbol{z}})=1-z_{1}z_{2}
.
Observe that
g
is irreducible,
however

g(y,y^{3})=(1-y^{2})(1+y^{2})

is
reducible
.

We combine Lemma
2.12
and Theorem
2.11

to obtain a projection of a multivariate polynomial
to a
3
-variate polynomial that maintains irreducibility
of polynomials up to degree
\delta
.

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
g(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
be an irreducible polynomial of constant degree
\delta
with
n+1
variables
that is monic in
x
.
There exists
{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
with
w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}=n^{{\rm poly}(\delta)}
such that

\Psi(g)=g(x,y^{w_{1}}t+y^{w_{1}^{\prime}},\dots,y^{w_{n}}t+y^{w_{n}^{\prime}})\in\mathbb{F}[x,y,t]

(30)

is irreducible.
Moreover,
we can compute and invert
\Psi(g)
in time
n^{{\rm poly}(\delta)}
.

Proof.

Let
G({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})
be the polynomial of degree
2\delta^{5}
in
2n
variables
provided by Theorem
2.11
for
g
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
with
w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}=n^{{\rm poly}(\delta)}
be the exponents we get from Lemma
2.12
for
G
.
That is,

\widehat{G}(y)=G(y^{w_{1}},\dots,y^{w_{n}},y^{w^{\prime}_{1}},\dots,y^{w^{\prime}_{n}})\not=0\;.

(31)

Now, suppose that
\Psi(g)
is reducible.
Then it would also be reducible at a point
y=\alpha
, where
\widehat{G}(\alpha)\not=0
.
But then
\widehat{g}(x,t)=\Psi(g)(x,\alpha,t)
would be reducible too,
and this would contradict Theorem
2.11
.
We conclude that
\Psi(g)
is irreducible.

For the complexity,
we first determine prime
p
from Lemma
2.12

and then get the weights
{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}
from above.
For a given
g(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})=\sum_{k,{\boldsymbol{e}}}c_{k,{\boldsymbol{e}}}x^{k}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
,
we can compute
\Psi(g)
in time
n^{{\rm poly}(\delta)}
.
For a monomial of
g
,
the mapping looks as follows:

c_{k,{\boldsymbol{e}}}\,x^{k}\,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{{\boldsymbol{e}}}~\mapsto~c_{k,{\boldsymbol{e}}}\,x^{k}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}(y^{w_{i}}t+y^{w_{i}^{\prime}})^{e_{i}}\,.

(32)

For inversion,
we have given
h\in\mathbb{F}[x,y,t]

which is monic in
x
with
x
-degree
\leq\delta
.
We either have to compute
g
such that
\Psi(g)=h
,
or detect that
h
is
not
in the codomain of
\Psi
.

We set
t=0
,
i.e. we consider
h(x,y,0)
.
From (
32
)
we see that monomials then must have the form

c_{k,{\boldsymbol{e}}}\,x^{k}\,y^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}w^{\prime}_{i}}\,.

(33)

From these we get the exponents
k
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}

for monomial
x^{k}\,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
,
similar as in the proof of Lemma
2.12
.
In case we thereby get a degree
>\delta
,
then
h
is not in the codomain of
\Psi
.
Otherwise,
we have got a candidate
g
of degree
\delta
.
But we still have to check whether
\Psi(g)=h
,
because the inversion procedure ignores the variable
t
.
The running time for inversion is
n^{{\rm poly}(\delta)}
.
∎

The polynomial
g
of degree
\delta
we considered so far
can be thought to be a constant-degree factor of a given polynomial
f
of degree
d
.
Our goal would be to compute
g
.
It is now easy to extend the above results to hold for all
degree-
\delta
factors of
f
simultaneously.

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
f(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
be a polynomial of degree
d
with
n+1
variables
that is monic in
x
, and let
\delta
be a constant.
There exists
{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
with
w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}\leq dn^{{\rm poly}(\delta)}
such that
for any irreducible factor
g
of degree
\delta
of
f
,
we have that
\Psi(g)
is an irreducible factor of
\Psi(f)
.

Proof.

The proof goes along the lines of Corollary
2.14
,
but we choose the weights slightly larger so that
the
\widehat{G}(y)
polynomials for
all
the degree-
\delta
factors
g

of
f
are non-zero simultaneously.
That is, we choose prime
p
in Lemma
2.12
as
p=dn^{{\rm poly}(\delta)}
.
∎

3
Computing the low-degree factors

We show how to compute the factors of constant degree of a given polynomial
f
.
In Section
3.2
for the case when

all
factors of
f
have constant degree,
and in Section
3.3
for general
f
.
In both cases,
our algorithm starts by projecting the given polynomial
to a
3
-variate polynomial.
We start with this common part.

3.1
Projected constant degree factors

The following algorithm is an initiating step in both,
Algorithm
2
and
3

in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
.
It takes an
n
-variate polynomial
f({\boldsymbol{z}})
of degree
d
.
The final goal is to compute the factors of
f
of degree
\delta
,
for some given constant
\delta
.
The initial steps are to project
f
to a trivariate monic polynomial
and then factorize the projection.

In more detail,
the first step is to make
f({\boldsymbol{z}})
monic in a new variable
x
via Lemma
2.2
.
That is,
we compute
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
such that
the transformed polynomial
f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})=f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}x+{\boldsymbol{z}})
is monic.

Then we apply Corollary
2.15
to
f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
.
That is,
we compute the weights
{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
bounded by
dn^{{\rm poly}(\delta)}

and explicitly compute
\Psi(f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})\in\mathbb{F}[x,y,t]

of degree at most
\widetilde{d}=d^{2}\,n^{{\rm poly}(\delta)}
.
Note that the
x
-degree of
f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
has not changed by mapping
\Psi
.

The next step is to factor
3
-variate
\Psi(f_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})

via Lemma
2.4
.
Algorithm 1,
Projected-Factoring
, below summarizes the steps.

Algorithm 1

3.3

In Section

\textup{Hom}[\mathcal{C}_{\mu}]
and divisibility tests
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}

Theorem 3.2

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}=\mathcal{C}_{\mu}(s,n,d)

{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}={\mathcal{P}}(n,\delta)

.

{\rm Factor}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu}|_{{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}})

Proof.

Let

.
To compute the factors of degree

for

Kumar, Ramanathan, and Saptharishi
[
30
]

generalized sparse polynomials to polynomials computed by constant-depth circuits

Recall that

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}(s,n,d)=\left\{\,p\in{\mathcal{P}}(n,d)\mid p\text{ has a circuit of size $s$ and depth }t\,\right\}.

We state some properties of

(i)

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(s,n,d)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{2}}(s+1,n,d)

For a polynomial

all homogeneous components of

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{(t+1)}}(sd,n,d)

(see
[
34
, Lemma 2.3]
).

(iii)

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}
is closed under derivatives:
For a polynomial
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}(s,n,d)
,
a variable
z
and

,
we have

(see
[
34
, Lemma 2.5]
).

We apply Theorem
3.2
to compute

(Constant-degree factors of constant-depth
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}(s,n,d)

.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}
.
We consider the running times from Theorem
3.2
to factor

.
We argue that

\textup{T}_{{\rm PIT}(\textup{Hom}[\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}])}=\textup{T}_{{\rm Div}(\partial\mathcal{C}_{\textup{Depth-}{t}}/{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta})}=\left(n\,(sd)^{O(t)}\right)^{O((sd)^{\epsilon})}\,.

For the homogeneous components of

,

.

For the divisibility test,

3.4
Linear factors of
\rm ROABP
s

We can apply Theorem
3.2
in the case of

read-once oblivious arithmetic branching programs
,
\rm ROABP
s,
and compute
linear
factors.

\rm ROABP
s are arithmetic branching programs (ABPs)
where there is an order on the variables
and on every path of the ABP,
every variable is evaluated in this order and only once.
For
n
variables
{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})
,
we describe the order by a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}

on the indices,

(z_{\sigma(1)},z_{\sigma(2)},\dots,z_{\sigma(n)})
.
As size measure for
\rm ROABP
s, usually the width
w
is taken.
Its size as a graph is then bounded by
nw
,
where
n
is the number of variables.
Width and size can greatly vary depending on the variable order.
Let

\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}(w,n,d)=\left\{\,p\in{\mathcal{P}}(n,d)\mid p\text{ has an $\rm ROABP$ with order $\sigma$ of width }w\,\right\}.

(40)

When we skip the variable order
\sigma
,
it means that the width can be achieved by
some
order,

\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP(w,n,d)}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}(w,n,d)\,.

(41)

\rm ROABP
s generalize sparse polynomials:

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(s,n,d)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}(s,n,d)
, for any
\sigma\in S_{n}
.

For our arguments,
we need some folklore properties of
\rm ROABP
s.

Lemma 3.5

(Properties of
\rm ROABP
)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}(w_{1},n,d)
and
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}(w_{2},n,d)
.
Then

(i)

f+g\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}(w_{1}+w_{2},n,d)
,

(ii)

fg\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}(w_{1}w_{2},n,2d)
,

(iii)

\textup{Hom}_{d}[f]\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}((d+1)\,w_{1},n,d)
,

(iv)

\partial_{z^{e}}(f)\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}(w_{1},n,d-e)
,
for a variable
z
and
e\leq d
,

(v)

(a_{0}+a_{1}z_{1}+a_{2}z_{2}+\cdots+a_{n}z_{n})^{d}\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP_{\sigma}}(nd+d+1,n,d)
,
for
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{F}

and any
\sigma\in S_{n}

[
38
]
.

There are efficient PITs for
\rm ROABP
s in the literature.

.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP}=\mathcal{C}_{\rm ROABP}(w,n,d)
.

For factorization of a polynomial

.
The time for PIT of homogeneous components of

follows from Lemma

(iii) and Lemma

For divisions,

This is the case
\delta=1

(

4
Computing the sparse factors

Recall the class of sparse polynomials,

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(s,n,d)=\left\{\,p\in{\mathcal{P}}(n,d)\mid\textup{sp}(p)\leq s\,\right\}.

(43)

We want to compute the sparse factors of a given polynomial.
For a polynomial
f
from a class
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
,
we show that the sparse factors of
f
can be computed efficiently
relative to
a PIT for
\textup{Hom}[\mathcal{C}_{\mu}]
,
a divisibility test of
f
by sparse candidate factors,
and an irreducibility preserving projection of sparse polynomials,

{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}})
(see Section
2.5
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Given
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}
,
we can test whether
f
is irreducible
in time
{\rm poly}(snd)+\textup{T}_{{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}})}
.

Proof.

We first transform
f({\boldsymbol{z}})
via Lemma
2.2
into a polynomial
f_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(x,{\boldsymbol{z}})
that is monic in the new variable
x
.
Note that
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
can be computed
in time
{\rm poly}(snd)
by Theorem
2.6
.

Then,
by solving
{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}})
, one can find a set
\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
such
that
f({\boldsymbol{z}})
is reducible if and only
if
f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}x+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}t+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
is reducible,
for every
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\in\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
.
Whether a bivariate polynomial is reducible can be checked in time
{\rm poly}(d)
by Lemma
2.4
.
∎

Theorem 4.2

(Sparse factors)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}=\mathcal{C}_{\mu}(s,n,d)

and
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(s,n,d)
.
Then

{\rm Factor}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu}|_{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}})
can be solved in time

\textup{T}_{{\rm Factor}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu}|_{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}})}={\rm poly}(snd)\,(\textup{T}_{{\rm PIT}(\textup{Hom}[\mathcal{C}_{\mu}])}\,+\,\textup{T}_{{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}})}\,+\,\textup{T}_{{\rm Div}(\partial\mathcal{C}_{\mu}/\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}})})\,.

(44)

Proof.

Let
f({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathcal{C}_{\mu}(s,n,d)
and let
g
be a
s
-sparse irreducible factor of
f
with multiplicity
e
,
that is
f=g^{e}h
,
where
\gcd(g,h)=1
.
Let
\deg(g)=d_{g}
and
\deg(h)=d_{h}
.

The first step is to transform
f
to a monic polynomial
by finding an
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
such that
\textup{Hom}_{d}[f]({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\neq 0
.
Observe that
\textup{Hom}_{d}[f]=(\textup{Hom}_{d_{g}}[g])^{e}\,\textup{Hom}_{d_{h}}[h]
.
Therefore, we also have
\textup{Hom}_{d_{g}}[g]({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\neq 0
.

The second step is to solve
{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}})
and compute
a set
\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
such that
g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}x+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}t+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
remains irreducible for some
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\in\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
.
We call such a
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
a
good point
for
g
.

Since we do not know
g
for now,
we also do not know which pair
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\in\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
is a good point for
g
.
For that reason,
we iteratively try all pairs
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\in\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
in the following.
Our algorithm will detect when a pair is not good at some point and
then ignore that pair.
Consider a good point
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\in\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}

and compute bivariate polynomial
\widehat{f}
,

\widehat{f}(x,t)=f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}x+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}t+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})

(45)

in dense representation by interpolation.
Then factor
g
is mapped to
\widehat{g}(x,t)=g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}x+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}t+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
,
a factor of
\widehat{f}
.
We factorize
\widehat{f}
over
\mathbb{F}
by Lemma
2.4
.
Let
F=\{\widehat{g}_{1},\widehat{g}_{2},\ldots,\widehat{g}_{m}\}
be the set of irreducible factors of
\widehat{f}
.
Note that for a good point
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
,
we have
\widehat{g}\in F
.

Our task now is to find out which polynomials in
F
are coming from sparse factors of
f

and to recover these factors.

4.1
Sparse factors of constant-depth circuits

Corollary 4.4

4.2
Sum-of-univariate factors of sparse polynomials

Let us consider the family of polynomials that can be written as a sum of univariate polynomials,

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}}(n,d)=\left\{\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,p_{i}(z_{i})\mid p_{i}\in{\mathcal{P}}(1,d),\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,n\,\right\}\,.

(54)

Note that
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}}(n,d)\subsetneq\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(nd+1,n,d)
.

Given a sparse polyomial,
we show that its factors that are sums of univariates can be computed in polynomial time.
The result was already shown by Volokovich
[
43
]

with a different technique.
We show that it also follows via Corollary
4.3
.

We already know that PIT for sparse polynomials is in polynomial time.
Moreover,
Saha, Saptharishi, and Saxena
[
37
]
showed that
divisibility of a sparse polynomial by a sum of univariates is in polynomial time.

Theorem 4.6

(
[
37
, Theorem 5.1]
)
.

Let

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}(s,n,d)
and

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}}(n,d)
.
Then

{\rm Div}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}/\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}})

can be computed in time
\textup{T}_{{\rm Div}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{sp}}/\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}})}={\rm poly}(snd)
.

Therefore,
it suffices to give bounds on the time for irreducible projection
{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}})

(see Section
2.5
).
We show that
\textup{T}_{{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}})}
is polynomially bounded.
We crucially use the following theorem.

Theorem 4.7

(
[
37
, Theorem 5.2]
)
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}}(n,d)
with
|\textup{var}(p)|\geq 3
.
Then
p
is irreducible.

Lemma 4.8
.

\textup{T}_{{\rm Irred\mbox{-}Proj}(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}})}\;\leq\;O(n^{3}d^{30})
.

Proof.

Let
p({\boldsymbol{z}})=\sum_{i\in[n]}p_{i}(z_{i})\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}}(n,d)
be irreducible
and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in(\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
such that
p({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}x+{\boldsymbol{z}})
is monic in
x
.
In order to apply Theorem
4.7
,
we consider
\textup{var}(p)
, the variables that
p
depends on.

If
|\textup{var}(p)|\leq 2
, we can directly derandomize Theorem
2.11
:
Suppose
\textup{var}(p)=\{z_{1},z_{2}\}

so that
p({\boldsymbol{z}})=p(z_{1},z_{2})
.
There is a polynomial
P
in
2\cdot 2=4
variables of degree
2d^{5}

such that any non-root of
P
gives an irreducible projection of
p
,
where we set the other variables of
p
to zero,

z_{i}=0
for
i=3,4,\dots,n
.
Hence,
the trivial hitting set (
7
) of size
(2d^{5}+1)^{4}=O(d^{20})

can be used for PIT for
P
.
Since we want to handle the blackbox case,
we do not assume that we have
p
in hand.
Hence,
we do not know whether actually
\textup{var}(p)=\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
.
So finally we will try all
\binom{n}{2}
possibilities to choose
2
variables.
For each possibility, say
\{z_{i},z_{j}\}
, we take the same hitting set,
but put the values at
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
and set the remaining
z
-variables to
0
.
That way we get
\binom{n}{2}
hitting sets,
and take their union to get the final hitting set of size
O(n^{2}d^{20})
.

Now let
|\textup{var}(p)|\geq 3
.
Similar as in the above case,
assume for now that
\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\}\subseteq\textup{var}(p)
,
and in the end,
we will try all
\binom{n}{3}
possibilities to choose
3
variables.

Consider the substitution

\psi:z_{i}\mapsto\alpha_{i}x
, for
i\geq 4
.
Then

\psi(p)\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{SU}}(4,d)
,
since
\sum_{i=4}^{n}p_{i}(\alpha_{i}x)
is a univariate polynomial in
x
.
Because
|\textup{var}(\psi(p)|\geq 3
,
polynomial
\psi(p)
is irreducible by Theorem
4.7
.

Now we substitute also the first
3
variables by

\phi:z_{i}\mapsto\alpha_{i}x+z_{i}
, for
i\in[3]
.
Then

\widehat{p}(x,z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=\phi(\psi(p))=p_{1}(\alpha_{1}x+z_{1})+p_{2}(\alpha_{2}x+z_{2})+p_{3}(\alpha_{3}x+z_{3})+\sum_{i=4}^{n}p_{i}(\alpha_{i}x)

(55)

is also irreducible and monic in
x
.
Therefore,
by Theorem
2.11
,
there exists a polynomial
P
of degree
2d^{5}
in
2\cdot 3=6
variables
such that any non-root of
P
gives an irreducible projection of
p
,
where we set the other variables of
p
to zero,

z_{i}=0
for
i\geq 4
.
Hence,
we may again take the trivial hitting set (
7
) of size
(2d^{5}+1)^{6}=O(d^{30})
.
Now we can argue similarly as above
and build the union of hitting sets over all
\binom{n}{3}
possibilities
to choose
3
variables.
Hence,
we end up with a hitting set of size
O(n^{3}d^{30})
.
∎

Conclusion

Finally, we collect some open questions.

1.

Can we decide whether a given sparse polynomial is irreducible in deterministic quasi-polynomial time? The proof may already give a good bivariate projection that preserves irreducibility. Then Theorem
4.2
would give us a deterministic quasi-polynomial time algorithm to find the irreducible sparse factors of a sparse polynomial.

2.

Can we find
bounded individual degree
sparse factors of a sparse polynomial (that may not have bounded individual degree) in deterministic quasi-polynomial time?
Volkovich asked if multilinear factors of a sparse polynomial can be found in deterministic polynomial
time
[
43
]
.
Unfortunately, we do not have any deterministic polynomial time algorithm even to test if a sparse polynomial is divisible by a multilinear polynomial.

3.

Given a blackbox computing the product of sparse irreducible polynomials
f_{i}
with bounded individual degree, find the factors
f_{i}
in deterministic polynomial/quasi-polynomial time.
Bhargava, Saraf, and Volkovich
[
3
]
gave a quasi-polynomial time algorithm, when the input polynomial is sparse with bounded individual degree.

4.

Finally, we highlight a question from
[
3
]
.
Given a blackbox computing the product of two (or more) unknown sparse irreducible polynomials, find those irreducible factors in deterministic quasi-poly or polynomial time.
