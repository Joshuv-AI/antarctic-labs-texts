Toward a sustainable model for novice ultrasound training: mixed-methods analysis of student portfolios and objective outcomes in Korean Medicine
Authors: Hyangyu Park, Yejin Han, Eunbyul Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltrasound is an essential educational tool for anatomy, physiology, diagnosis, and procedure in medical education. Various educational cases have been reported to date, yet novice experiences in non-traditional, resource-constrained contexts remain underexplored. This study explores Korean Medicine (KM) students’ perceptions of introductory ultrasound education, learning experiences, and the process of learning transfer.MethodsWe analysed students’ learning portfolios from an introductory musculoskeletal ultrasound training program for KM students. Quantitative data included self-assessment of goal achievement, pre- and post-training self-efficacy, self-directed learning indicators, and objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) scores. Qualitative data comprised reflective narratives that were analysed using framework analysis and subsequently interpreted alongside quantitative data.ResultsQualitative analysis identified four themes: “perceptions of the learning environment,” “challenges and insights during training,” “assessment and further learning,” and “application and transfer.” Correlation and multiple regression analyses showed that self-assessment, self-efficacy change, and self-video activity were not significantly associated with OSCE performance. Students valued instructor’s supportive guidance and portable ultrasound practicality in the learning environment. Ultrasound skill acquisition enhanced equipment operation, probe proficiency, anatomical recognition, and diagnostic competency, leading students to reappraise ultrasound as “a real-time anatomical visualization and safe acupuncture guidance tool.” Students’ self-efficacy increased significantly after training (p < .001), primarily due to perceived improvements in performance speed and accuracy and greater confidence in procedural performance. Students’ plans for further learning varied, from short-term practice to future certification acquisition. In terms of ‘application and transfer,’ they identified clinical barriers including insurance ineligibility, high equipment costs, and the limitations of portable devices.ConclusionsMusculoskeletal ultrasound training integrated into the KM curriculum may enhance students’ self-efficacy and support the integration of anatomical knowledge with clinical application. Although no significant quantitative predictors of OSCE performance were identified, the findings suggest important pedagogical implications regarding novice students’ cognitive load, self-assessment difficulties, and the potential role of structured feedback and longitudinal educational support in ultrasound learning. Future KM ultrasound education may benefit from more longitudinal and educationally integrated approaches that support the development and sustained application of ultrasound competency in resource-sensitive educational contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09583-z.Keywords: Ultrasound, Korean Medicine, Competency-based education, Portfolio, Mixed-methods study

Background

Ultrasound imaging has exerted a profound and sustained impact across modern medicine, with its clinical applications continually expanding into diverse specialties. As ultrasound provides real-time imaging without radiation exposure, it is not only inherently safe but has also become an indispensable tool for diagnosis and procedural guidance—particularly in point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) settings where it can address specific clinical questions instantaneously [1, 2]. Accordingly, proficiency in ultrasound is now regarded as essential clinical competency in various fields, including emergency medicine, internal, and family medicine, general surgery, and anesthesiology. Furthermore, the potential role of POCUS in improving the accessibility of diagnostic imaging in resource-limited environments has been increasingly emphasized [3].

From an educational perspective, ultrasound serves as more than just a diagnostic instrument; it is an effective tool that facilitates the understanding of anatomical and physiological structures, physical examination, and procedural skills acquisition. In recent decades, ultrasound has been increasingly integrated into medical school curricula worldwide [4, 5]. Specifically, it is valued for strengthening the link between basic anatomical knowledge and clinical application, as it allows students to apply their understanding of gross and cross-sectional anatomy in real time. However, ultrasound training requires hands-on practice with equipment before students can perform image acquisition, and its implementation often faces barriers such as the high cost of equipment and limited faculty resources [6]. Specifically, little is known about how students experience and evaluate the introductory phase of ultrasound training in non-traditional settings, such as Korean Medicine (KM) education.

In South Korea, following a Supreme Court ruling in December 2022 supporting the use of diagnostic ultrasound by Korean Medicine doctors, there has been a surge in demand for ultrasound education among both graduates and undergraduates [7]. In clinical practice, where musculoskeletal disorders constitute a substantial proportion of traditional medicine care, ultrasound imaging has emerged as a key modality for enhancing the anatomical accuracy and safety of acupuncture and interventional procedures [8]. As legal authorization and clinical necessity align, systematic and structured ultrasound education has become increasingly important [7].

We have previously developed and validated an introductory musculoskeletal ultrasound training module and objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) focused on volar wrist scanning and diagnostic criteria for carpal tunnel syndrome [9]. This module was designed for absolute beginners and emphasized basic image acquisition skills, including depth adjustment, length measurement, and cross-sectional area calculation. Although the student survey indicated high satisfaction and strong intentions to pursue further learning, the prior study did not examine how students experienced the learning process, which aspects of the learning environment influenced their engagement, or how they perceived the transferability of ultrasound skills to future clinical practice [10]. In other words, the module’s educational impact was measured quantitatively, but the underlying learning mechanisms remain unknown.

In the present study, we aimed to conduct an in-depth analysis of both the quantitative and qualitative data contained in student portfolios to address the following research questions:

How do students perceive the instructional design, learning resources, and interactions with teachers within a resource-constrained introductory ultrasound curriculum?

Our findings will contribute to understanding student perspectives and to designing and implementing effective ultrasound training, not only in traditional medicine but also across various medical education contexts that are newly introducing ultrasound curricula.

Methods

Resources and implementation

The ultrasound training examined in this study was conducted in 2022 as part of a clinical skills course at Wonkwang University, College of Korean Medicine. The curriculum focused on volar wrist (carpal tunnel) scanning and the diagnosis of carpal tunnel syndrome, with the primary learning objective being “to understand and utilize the fundamental functions of ultrasound.” Students completed seven weeks of ultrasound training, which consisted of: (1) online pre-learning, (2) a one-hour hands-on session with an instructor followed by self-directed practice, and (3) group practice using portable ultrasound devices one week later.

In the eighth week, students’ practical performance was evaluated using a previously developed OSCE whose content validity was established through review by eight domain experts [9]. The checklist consisted of 15 tasks (total score: 28 points), with individual items weighted from 1 to 3 points; partial credit was awarded for 2- and 3-point items. Assessed domains included probe handling, identification of anatomical structures, examination according to the ultrasound diagnostic criteria for carpal tunnel syndrome, and explanation of test results. All administrations were conducted by a single trained examiner. All hands-on sessions were conducted in a clinical skills center to provide a safe learning environment and were guided by an educational philosophy that allows mistakes and encourages repeated practice. To promote self-directed learning, students were permitted to independently access ultrasound equipment in the clinical skills center. Self-directed learning attitudes and reflective practice were emphasized throughout the training process.

The ultrasound devices used for training included one HS40 and one SonoAce R7 system (Samsung Medison, Seoul, Korea), and two Minisono devices (Alpinion Medical Systems, Seoul, Korea). The HS40 was used for instructor-led practice sessions and the OSCE, the SonoAce R7 system for self-directed learning, and the Minisono devices for group practice.

The learning portfolio consisted of the following components: “my goals,” “self-assessment of goal achievement,” “pre- and post-practice self-efficacy,” “procedural steps,” “my learning content,” “assessment and feedback,” and “reflection on my learning.” The “self-assessment of goal achievement” was evaluated using a 4-point scale (1 = insufficient, 2 = meeting minimum requirements, 3 = sufficient, 4 = excellent) [11]. “Pre- and post-practice self-efficacy” was assessed both before the start of training and after course completion using the statement, “I can perform this procedure in real practice,” which students rated on a 10-point scale (1 = cannot perform at all, 10 = can perform very well). In this study, “self-assessment” referred to students’ self-evaluation of their ultrasound learning and procedural performance (rated on the 4-point scale), whereas “self-efficacy” referred to their confidence in performing ultrasound procedures (rated on the 10-point scale). For “Procedural steps,” students listed each step of the procedure in sequential order based on the pre-learning videos. “My learning content” included hands-on practice activities, portable ultrasound scan images, and self-directed learning regarding the clinical application of the procedure. “Assessment and feedback” included instructor/peer feedback and OSCE scores provided to students. “Reflection on my learning” allowed students to freely describe their self-evaluation of learning and identify future learning needs.

Analysis

To ensure objectivity and maintain participant anonymity, all student names and identifiers were de-identified before data extraction was handled by one author (EC). Subsequently, separate authors (YH and HP) analyzed the quantitative components (scores for “self-assessment of goal achievement” and “pre- and post-practice self-efficacy”) and qualitative components (“reflection on my learning) of the portfolios. In addition, to explore the association between learning outcomes and students’ self-directed learning, the number of self-recorded videos demonstrating procedural performance submitted by each student throughout the clinical skills course was used as an indicator of self-directed learning [12].

We employed concurrent triangulation mixed-methods design, in which quantitative and qualitative data were derived simultaneously from students’ clinical skills portfolios and analysed in parallel. Quantitative data included self-assessment scores (1–4 scale), self-efficacy ratings before and after the ultrasound practice session (1–10 scale), and ultrasound OSCE scores. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables, and changes in self-efficacy were examined using paired t-tests. The association between post-practice self-efficacy and OSCE performance was analysed using Pearson’s correlation. Multiple linear regression was conducted with self-assessment, self-efficacy change, and self-video activity as independent variables and OSCE scores as the dependent variable. These three variables were selected to represent conceptually distinct dimensions of self-regulated learning relevant to procedural skill acquisition [13, 14]: self-assessment indexed the self-evaluative dimension (the accuracy of students’ judgments of their own competence), change in self-efficacy reflected the motivational dimension (students’ evolving confidence in performing the procedure), and self-recorded video activity served as a behavioral indicator of engagement in self-directed practice [12]. Prior to these analyses, statistical assumptions were examined: normality via the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots, linearity and homoscedasticity via residual plots, independence of residuals via the Durbin–Watson statistic (1.77), and multicollinearity via variance inflation factors (all VIF < 1.3). All quantitative analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Student reflections were written in Korean and ranged from approximately 6 to 350 words in length, with most students providing moderately detailed accounts of their learning experiences. Students’ reflective narratives on ultrasound practice were analysed using framework analysis [15, 16] with a deductive approach. A priori analytical categories were drawn from Kang and Jung’s framework [17], which categorized reflective journals into four domains including “learning environment,” “learners’ self-reflection,” “learning content,” and “learning evaluation/application.” In the present study, these categories were adapted to the context of novice ultrasound education and reorganized into four themes: “perceptions of the learning environment,” “challenges and insights during ultrasound training,” “assessment and further learning,” and “application and transfer” of learning. Students’ reflections were then coded according to these thematic categories. The subthemes were developed inductively as the researchers identified recurring meanings and distinctions within the data. Coding was performed using the qualitative data analysis software ATLAS.ti (Web; ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH) to ensure consistency.

One researcher (HP) coded 30% of the data using the coding scheme, and another researcher (EC) reviewed the coded data. Following the agreed-upon coding method, HP coded the remaining data, and EC reviewed it and resolved any discrepancies through consensus. Representative quotations were selected by HP to illustrate the range of perspectives within each subtheme, prioritizing clarity and typicality, and translated into English by HP and reviewed by EC to ensure accuracy and fidelity to the original meaning. EC, who was responsible for data extraction and de-identification, was also involved in the design and implementation of the ultrasound curriculum, while HP had no direct involvement in curriculum delivery. In integrating the analysis results, the two researchers presented the findings by research question, linking quantitative and qualitative results in a complementary manner.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Wonkwang University (WKIRB-202307-SB-050, date of approval: July 14, 2023). The need for written informed consent was waived because the study posed minimal risk to participants and there were no reasonable grounds to assume refusal of participation.

Results

Among the 105 students included in the portfolio analysis, 16 commented on prior exposure to or perceptions regarding ultrasound. Among them, 14 indicated that this training represented their first hands-on experience with an ultrasound device. One student (No. 36) reported having learned only the basic concepts of ultrasound in the meridian and acupuncture points course within the regular curriculum. Another student (No. 41) reported participation in an extracurricular musculoskeletal ultrasound course offered to a small group of volunteers during vacation. After completion of the training, self-assessed goal achievement was rated as “excellent” by 13 students, “sufficient” by 59 students, “meets minimum standard” by 31 students, and “insufficient” by 2 students. In the post-training OSCE, the total score was 28 points, with the highest score was 25 and the lowest score was 3 (16.31 ± 4.65, Q1 = 13, Q2 = 16, Q3 = 20) (Fig. 1). Perceptions of the learning environment and resources

Students’ perceptions of the learning environment were categorized into three themes: “interactions with faculty,” “educational resources and facilities,” and “instructional methods and assessment.” Regarding interactions with faculty, students generally valued supportive guidance. It was noted that “the instructor’s encouragement and kind explanations, despite my lack of skill, helped me gain confidence in ultrasound” (Student 37). Another student mentioned that “under the instructor’s guidance, I was able to achieve the learning goals, although the short session was not enough to gain full proficiency” (Student 97). In contrast, some students felt a lack of engagement, stating that “It was disappointing that I did not receive direct feedback from the preceptor” (Student 100).

In terms of educational resources and facilities, much of the feedback focused on the portable ultrasound device. Some students pointed out technical limitations, such as lower image resolution compared with console devices, operational inconvenience due to the touch-screen interface, and short continuous usage time. Conversely, other students perceived the equipment more positively, noting that “the resolution was not significantly different from the fixed equipment we used previously; it is quite functional for a portable device” (Student 26) and “the touch-screen interface actually made certain operations, such as measuring distance and area, much easier” (Student 91).

Finally, concerning instructional methods and assessment, most students expressed high satisfaction, often using terms such as “interesting” or “highly rewarding.” One student reflected that “it was my first time operating an imaging device, and as the session went smoothly, it was the most rewarding OSCE experience I have had to date” (Student 53).

Challenges and insights during ultrasound skill acquisition

Students’ perceptions of the challenges and insights during training were categorized into five subthemes: “training process,” “perceived technical difficulties,” “emphasis on key aspects,” “inquiries and curiosity,” and “perceived educational value and utility.” Students practiced ultrasound in groups after familiarizing themselves with the procedures through the instructor’s demonstration video. They learned to operate the equipment by repeatedly adjusting settings such as gain, depth, and focus position to observe how visibility of anterior wrist structures changed, with a focus on reducing procedural time. Students endeavored to maintain natural and precise probe handling, recognizing that anatomical locations vary according to each individual’s physique. One student reflected, “I focused so much on learning how to operate the device that I couldn’t practice as if I were treating a real patient” (Student 91).

Overall, students found the procedure challenging owing to their lack of familiarity with the device and its limited resolution. Many experienced trial and error in probe handling, with one noting, “It is difficult to adjust the angle so that the median nerve and flexor retinaculum are clearly visible” (Student 80).

In their portfolios, students documented key insights gained during practice, such as the necessity of pre-learning the target structures and the importance of improving probe handling skills. Specifically, students recognized the technical requirement of avoiding anisotropy by ensuring the probe is not held at an oblique angle, with one noting, “It is important to hold the probe so that it is vertical to the wrist” (Student 5). Another student identified that developing a tactile sense for manipulation is the core of the skill, stating, “I believe it is essential to develop a ‘feel’ for the specific angles at which the images appear most clearly” (Student 91). Additionally, students emphasized the need for proficiency in identifying orientation markers and accurately locating anatomical structures within a limited timeframe.

Beyond technical skills, some students considered the practicalities of clinical application, reflecting on patient comfort and efficient positioning for procedures. This led to broader clinical inquiries; for instance, one student questioned the impact of ultrasound gel on hygiene during procedures: “I once read that one can perform acupuncture while viewing ultrasound, but I wonder if the gel might interfere with a sterile treatment procedure” (Student 51).

Ultimately, the training session allowed students to re-evaluate the educational and clinical significance of ultrasound diagnostic devices. They expressed high expectations for ultrasound as a tool that enables the direct confirmation of cross-sectional anatomy and serves as a safe, effective diagnostic instrument. Student 1 said, “When learning related anatomical structures, ultrasound functions as an effective learning tool because it allows for a visceral and intuitive view of the arrangement of structures through real-time imaging.” Student 13 added, “As a non-invasive diagnostic tool, ultrasound is expected to be widely used in clinical practice for safe acupuncture and diagnosis.”

Assessment and further learning

“Assessment and further learning” were categorized into three themes: “factors influencing ultrasound skill,” “changes in self-efficacy following ultrasound training,” and “self-directed practice and future learning plans.”

Quantitative variables related to assessment included self-assessment of learning, pre- and post-practice self-efficacy, self-video activity, and ultrasound OSCE scores. Correlation and multiple regression analyses were conducted. The correlation analysis showed that none of the variables was significantly associated with OSCE performance: self-assessment (r = − .09, p = .383), post-practice self-efficacy (r = .01, p = .934), self-efficacy change (r = .02, p = .822), and self-video activity (r = .003, p = .972) (Additional file 1). Because several variables deviated from normality (Shapiro–Wilk p < .05), Spearman’s rank-order correlations were additionally computed and yielded a consistent non-significant pattern (all ρ ≤ |0.08|, p > .43). The multiple regression model was likewise not statistically significant, R² = 0.012, adjusted R² = −0.018, F(3, 101) = 0.40, p = .751; none of the predictors contributed significantly to OSCE performance (Additional file 2).

The qualitative data offer further context for these non-significant associations. There were seven students whose self-efficacy decreased, and among them, two students had OSCE scores of 20 points or higher (within the top 25% score). The directly stated reasons for the decrease in self-efficacy included the following: “As it was my first time performing ultrasound, I was not skillful at handling the probe” (Student 42, self-efficacy 8 → 6, OSCE score 9); “When I watched videos, I thought I would be able to do it, but when I actually tried it, it did not go as well as I had expected” (Student 47, self-efficacy 5 → 2, OSCE score 12); and “When practicing the skill, the structures were clear for some subjects, but I struggled for a long time with others. I realized this was not due to differences in body types, but because I did not position the probe in the correct direction” (Student 66, self-efficacy 8 → 3, OSCE score 11).

Meanwhile, students’ self-efficacy regarding ultrasound procedural performance increased significantly following the educational intervention, with the mean score rising from 2.98 (SD = 2.17) to 6.84 (SD = 1.86); mean difference = 3.86, 95% CI [3.34, 4.38], t(104) = 14.77, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.44, indicating a large effect (Table 2).

Students’ self-perceived reasons for underperformance in the ultrasound OSCE were categorized as follows: insufficient technical practice resulting in poor image quality, inability to complete all procedures within the time limit, becoming rushed owing to nervousness, lack of familiarity with operating the ultrasound device, and difficulty arising from differences in wrist size compared with the individuals they had practiced on. Particularly, through practice, students realized that a three-dimensional and accurate understanding of anatomical structures is essential for correctly interpreting ultrasound images. In addition, while writing their portfolios, students reflected on their learning experiences and critically examined their own shortcomings, such as difficulties in acquiring clear images and identifying anatomical structures. While some felt “flustered and nervous using ultrasound for the first time,” they also gained confidence, noting that “as I understood each structure one by one, I felt that I could interpret ultrasound findings more skillfully next time.”

These reflections led to plans for additional learning and hands-on practice. Students’ future learning plans ranged from short-term goals, such as “making time to practice once more next week” (Student 11) and “practicing more to achieve more delicate palpation and proficient probe manipulation” (Student 64), to broader goals, including “studying ultrasound for other body parts as well” (Student 33). Some students also articulated long-term plans extending beyond graduation, such as “attending conferences or lectures” (Student 16), “preparing for the “Registered in Musculoskeletal Sonography (RMSK) certification” (Student 36), and “studying further in order to use ultrasound in a Korean Medicine clinic” (Student 39).

“In the future, if I am to perform examinations not only on the wrist but also on various other body parts, I realized that I need to systematically organize and understand what anatomical structures appear, how diagnoses are made, and what normal versus pathological findings look like for each specific region and disease before proceeding with the scan” (Student 77).

“Additionally, I tried to check whether the acupuncture needle could be visualized. While inserting a needle at Shousanli (LI10), I placed the probe right next to it and carefully observed the screen. Although the needle itself was not visible, I could roughly estimate the insertion site and depth by observing the movement of surrounding structures such as the muscle during needle insertion. However, when the insertion was paused, the needle position could not be confirmed” (Student 26).

Perceived clinical applicability and transfer of learning

Students’ perceptions of the application and transfer of learning were categorized into three themes: “integration with prior ultrasound exposure,” “intention to use in clinical practice,” and “perceived barriers to clinical application.” This ultrasound course served as an opportunity to recall previous ultrasound practice experiences and to strengthen students’ motivation for future application. One student reflected:

“During the RMSK 1 course, when I first held a probe, the textbook-recommended grip felt rather uncomfortable. However, in this practice session, I did not experience significant discomfort when performing the three movements, and I came to realize that there is a reason behind every procedural guideline. Although it has been nearly a year since the MSK 2 course ended, this session allowed me to confirm that some of that experience still remains in my body, and it became a time to renew my commitment to using ultrasound after graduation” (Student 31).

Furthermore, there was a notable shift toward a positive attitude regarding participation in postgraduate programs:

“I suddenly recalled that a rehabilitation medicine resident at Jeonju Hospital once told me about the RMSK examination. At that time, when I asked my classmates, no one said they would attempt the exam. Probably because we did not have the opportunity to actually use ultrasound equipment, there was naturally no motivation to take the exam. In the current situation, where using ultrasound equipment feels quite appealing through hands-on practice, I think quite a few students may reconsider the RMSK exam” (Student 9).

Some students also expressed a strong intent to integrate ultrasound into their future practice to establish “accurate and reliable rapport with patients” (Student 1) and to have a “safer tool for performing Korean Medicine procedures.” Some also committed to “furthering [their] studies for practical clinical application” (Student 75).

Some students perceived barriers to the future use of ultrasound diagnostic devices. The reasons cited included the inability of Korean Medicine doctors to claim insurance reimbursement for ultrasound use, financial burden associated with purchasing ultrasound equipment, and limitations of portable ultrasound devices, such as low image resolution and limited usable operating time.

“Currently, it is impossible to claim insurance fees, and the equipment itself is so expensive that it feels quite difficult to justify placing it in a general clinic. I found the image quality of relatively affordable portable devices to be disappointing, resulting in low practical utility. The limited battery life was another concern. Ultimately, I feel somewhat skeptical about whether an individual practitioner can feasibly utilize ultrasound in the current environment” (Student 9).

Discussion

Principal findings

We analysed student portfolios from a musculoskeletal ultrasound training program implemented within KM education. We aimed to explore practical learning processes and barriers from students’ perspectives, thereby deriving a sustainable educational model for resource-limited settings and providing empirical evidence for effective ultrasound curriculum design. Our findings indicate that while the training significantly enhanced students’ self-efficacy, this perceived confidence did not directly correlate with their objective performance on the OSCE. Furthermore, qualitative analysis revealed that although novices grappled with technical challenges such as probe handling and image interpretation, they ultimately perceived ultrasound as a vital tool for bridging the gap between knowledge and clinical practice.

Sustainable implementation and scalability in resource-limited settings

The high cost of ultrasound equipment and the shortage of faculty instructors are major barriers to implementing ultrasound training [6]. In this study, despite being educated with limited resources (one faculty for 105 students, one console, and two portable handheld devices), students did not cite “lack of equipment” as a critical issue in their portfolios. This is likely owing to the long-term 24-week clinical rotation, which enabled a schedule that ensured one-on-one faculty instruction and sufficient self-practice time, with 4–5 students per device.

However, this model has limited scalability for typical 15- or 16-week academic semesters in South Korea. To maximize educational effectiveness within limited resources and timeframes, adopting mastery learning and peer-assisted learning models should be considered [18–22]. Specifically, by clearly defining achievement goals for each module and utilizing peer tutors to provide close feedback, the curriculum can address the lack of faculty feedback noted in this study, while allowing faculty to focus on resolving students’ complex difficulties.

Meanwhile, students expressed negative perceptions of portable ultrasound, citing low image resolution and operational inconvenience. This likely reflects novices’ tendency to rely heavily on high-resolution images, as lower image quality can impose additional challenges for inexperienced students in identifying anatomical structures compared with standard systems [23]. Nevertheless, given the evolving healthcare landscape, training on portable devices remains crucial. POCUS has established itself as a vital tool for immediate decision-making in clinical settings [1, 2]. This is particularly relevant given the surging demand for home-based primary care in South Korea and the expanding role of Korean Medicine doctors in integrated community care [24]. Although the learning curve may be slower for beginners owing to technical limitations, developing POCUS competency is essential for enhancing point-of-care diagnostic capabilities. Therefore, such training should be continuously integrated into undergraduate, postgraduate, and continuing education.

Cognitive and psychomotor dynamics in novice ultrasound training

The qualitative analysis revealed that novices experienced substantial cognitive load while simultaneously performing psychomotor skills—specifically, fine probe manipulation—and the visuospatial task of matching 2D ultrasound images to actual 3D anatomical structures. This suggests that during the initial learning phase, the instructor’s role must go beyond simple error correction; it requires close supervision that functions as essential scaffolding to manage the student’s cognitive overload. While facing these challenges, students deepened their learning by asking substantive questions about clinical practice, such as how the use of gel affects the hygiene of acupuncture procedures. Ultimately, this process of struggle and inquiry served as a decisive turning point, enabling students to reframe ultrasound not merely as a diagnostic device but as an essential educational tool to enhance anatomical understanding and support accurate patient diagnosis and treatment.

Implications for assessment strategies and feedback

The quantitative analyses offer important insights into how novice students experience and regulate ultrasound learning. First, the absence of a significant correlation between self-assessment and OSCE performance suggests that students had difficulty accurately judging their own level of competence. In other words, students’ perceived goal attainment did not reliably reflect their objectively assessed performance. This misalignment indicates that many students may not yet possess well-developed self-awareness or calibration skills in the context of early ultrasound training. Such findings underscore the need for structured formative support that helps students monitor their progress during training, identify specific areas of weakness, and translate feedback into actionable learning plans. Rather than assuming that repeated practice alone leads to accurate self-evaluation, ultrasound curricula may benefit from explicit instructional strategies—such as guided self-assessment checklists [25], benchmark examples of performance [26], or mid-course feedback sessions [27]—that scaffold students’ ability to evaluate their own competence and regulate subsequent learning.

Second, the multiple linear regression analysis showed that none of the examined variables—including self-assessment, change in self-efficacy, and self-video activity—significantly predicted OSCE performance. Although self-video submission frequency was conceptualized as an indicator of greater self-directed engagement [12], increased independent practice did not translate into higher assessed performance in this cohort. This finding suggests that the quantity of self-directed activity alone may be insufficient to explain performance outcomes in introductory ultrasound education. Instead, OSCE performance may be more strongly influenced by other, unmeasured factors, such as the quality of practice, students’ motivation for ultrasound learning, or adaptive feedback from instructors. From an educational design perspective, these results highlight the need to identify additional key predictors of ultrasound performance and to align learning support with those variables.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single university within a specific KM educational context, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other medical education systems, curricula, or ultrasound training pathways. Additionally, the data were collected in 2022, prior to the Supreme Court’s ruling clarifying the use of ultrasound by Korean Medicine doctors. Consequently, the perceptions of clinical utility and motivation among the students in this cohort may differ from those of current students, who are now training within a clearer legal landscape. Second, the study involved the retrospective analysis of student portfolio data collected as part of an educational program rather than data prospectively designed for research purposes. In addition, the qualitative findings relied heavily on students’ self-reported reflections, which may have been influenced by recall bias or social desirability bias. Third, because the analysis relied on routinely collected portfolio data, potentially relevant factors such as prior ultrasound exposure and the quality of self-directed practice could not be modeled, as the portfolio data did not capture these in a consistent, quantifiable form (only 16 of 105 students commented on prior exposure). Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence regarding the effectiveness of self-directed practice. Finally, the study did not include long-term follow-up or objective clinical performance outcomes beyond the immediate educational setting. Future studies are needed to examine whether the observed educational experiences translate into sustained ultrasound competency and clinical application over time.

Despite these limitations, this study possesses significant strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply learning analytics to student portfolios regarding ultrasound training specifically within the context of KM. Methodologically, it integrates quantitative data (self-efficacy, self-assessment) with qualitative reflections and objective OSCE scores, enabling a multifaceted understanding of students’ perceptions and educational outcomes. Furthermore, by analyzing the portfolios of the entire cohort of 105 students rather than relying on a self-selected sample of volunteers, this study minimizes selection bias and provides a comprehensive representation of the student’s experience.

Implications for future research and education

Future ultrasound education research should move toward more holistic and longitudinal approaches that capture how competence is developed, supported, and sustained over time, particularly in emerging and resource-sensitive educational contexts. Rather than relying primarily on short-term performance outcomes or easily quantifiable indicators, future studies should examine a wider range of determinants, including the quality and structure of practice, students’ engagement with feedback, and contextual factors influencing transfer to assessment and clinical settings. At the same time, sustained faculty development will be essential to equip educators with the pedagogical skills needed to coach students in deliberate practice and reflective learning, especially where faculty time and technological resources are limited. Aligning curriculum design, assessment strategies, and faculty roles with these principles may enable ultrasound education to function not merely as an isolated technical skill module but as a scalable and sustainable learning pathway that supports lifelong professional development across the health profession continuum.

Conclusions

This mixed-methods study suggests that musculoskeletal ultrasound practical training integrated into the KM curriculum may enhance students’ self-efficacy and strengthen the connection between anatomical knowledge and clinical application. Qualitative findings revealed high cognitive load and psychomotor challenges typical of novice learners, and notably, neither self-assessed competence nor self-efficacy change was significantly associated with OSCE performance, suggesting that perceived and actual competence may diverge in the early stages of ultrasound skill acquisition. Although practical barriers to clinical transition were identified, students perceived ultrasound as a useful tool for improving procedural accuracy and safety. These findings may provide educational implications for future KM ultrasound curriculum development, including the incorporation of mastery learning, peer-assisted learning, and longitudinal integrated approaches spanning undergraduate education to postgraduate continuing professional development.
