Exploring Mistreatment of Obstetrics and Gynecology Residents in the Clinical Learning Environment
Authors: Jennifer M. Keller, Karen George, AnnaMarie Connolly, Lisa Yanek, Erika Banks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obstetrics and gynecology residents frequently report experiencing mistreatment, especially on labor and delivery. Interventions to reduce mistreatment will be important to improve the clinical learning environment and patient care.

Mistreatment of trainees, defined as discrimination, harassment, or abuse in the clinical learning environment, remains an ongoing challenge in graduate medical education. Mistreatment is associated with burnout,1,2 worsened performance,3 and depression4 and raises concerns for patient safety and workforce retention. Recent surveys of resident physicians detail prevalence, types, and sources of mistreatment.1,5,6 Hu et al1 reported that nearly half of general surgery residents experienced mistreatment, with 19% reporting occurrence at least a few times a month. Similar frequencies were observed in internal medicine5 and emergency medicine6 programs, with 48.3% and 45.1% of residents reporting mistreatment, respectively. In the study published by Hu et al,1 female general surgery residents experienced mistreatment at higher rates than male residents, with 70.6% of female residents reporting that they had experienced mistreatment of any kind.

In obstetrics and gynecology, mistreatment experiences of resident physicians have not been reported on a national scale. In a prior survey of 60 obstetrics and gynecology programs, the prevalence of sexual harassment was found to be high (69.1%) for both men and women.7

We sought to describe self-reported mistreatment among U.S. obstetrics and gynecology residents. The objectives of this study were to determine 1) the prevalence of mistreatment and associated factors, 2) the source of the mistreatment, and 3) the location where the mistreatment occurred. Given the female predominance in obstetrics and gynecology and previously higher rates of mistreatment reported by female residents,1 we postulated that there would be a high prevalence of mistreatment among obstetrics and gynecology residents. We also hypothesized that rates of gender discrimination and sexual harassment would be higher than other types of mistreatment.

METHODS

The study was approved by the Health Media Lab IRB (IRB No. 2760). We conducted a national survey of U.S. obstetrics and gynecology residents in January 2025 using an electronic survey administered at the end of the annual in-service training examination conducted each January by the Council on Resident Education in Obstetrics and Gynecology. Examinees were informed of the purpose of the survey and provided informed consent to participate. Responses were confidential, anonymous, and not linked to individual programs. The Council on Resident Education in Obstetrics and Gynecology examination is taken by non–U.S. –based residents and nonresidents who audit the examination. Therefore, respondents were excluded if they were not residents, were not enrolled in a U.S.–based residency program, did not consent, or had left all the questions pertaining to mistreatment unanswered. Individuals were not required to answer any survey questions, including demographic items.

The study followed the American Association for Public Opinion Research reporting guidelines.8 The survey contained self-reported demographic questions about race, ethnicity, relationship status, pregnancy status, sexual orientation, and gender to facilitate description of potential differences in experiences of reported mistreatment across different demographic groups. In addition, respondents were asked about program characteristics (size, type [community, university, combined community and university, military], program location [census region]) and career plans after graduation. The remainder of the survey was adapted from a previously published and validated instrument given at the time of the in-training examination in general surgery1 and emergency medicine6 residencies. Questions focused on types of mistreatment experienced by respondents (verbal or emotional abuse; gender, racial, and pregnancy or childcare discrimination; and sexual harassment) and sources of mistreatment (patient or patient's family, attending, nurse, fellow resident). For the purposes of this study, the previously validated survey was adapted to include questions regarding the clinical setting where the mistreatment occurred (operating room, labor and delivery unit, inpatient unit, emergency department, and outpatient setting). The survey asked respondents to describe the frequency of mistreatment if experienced in the 6 months before the survey. Frequency options ranged from “never” to “now and then/few times per year” to “daily.” The full survey used in this study is included in Appendix 1 (available online at https://links.lww.com/AOG/E787) and includes a listing of types of mistreatment and the specific behaviors categorized under each mistreatment type.

Because of the low numbers of respondents in some demographic categories, some demographics were combined to create a single category. This ensured protection for those with marginalized identities from being identified and assisted with the analysis. Gender diverse was defined as transgender female, transgender male, genderqueer/nonconforming, and other identify. Lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer+ (LGBQ+) included people who responded lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer, or other orientation. Those who selected American Indian, Alaska Native, or Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander as their race were combined with those who answered “other” to create one unified variable.

The survey asked respondents to describe the frequency of mistreatment if experienced. Frequency options ranged from “never” to “now and then/few times per year” to “daily” and were combined as “ever” for analysis. Given that mistreatment should be considered a “never event,” the term “ever experienced” was used to most inclusively describe resident experiences with mistreatment. Respondents had to complete all questions in a mistreatment section to be categorized as ever/never for each type of mistreatment; for example, to determine whether gender discrimination occurred, each respondent had to complete all seven pertinent questions. To be classified as “ever mistreated” regarding any gender discrimination, respondents had to answer affirmatively to experiencing any behavior that was classified as gender discrimination; to be classified as “never” for gender discrimination, respondents had to respond that they had never experienced any of the behaviors categorized as gender discrimination.

All variables were examined for missingness. Outcomes of interest (verbal/emotional abuse, gender discrimination, racial discrimination, pregnancy/childcare discrimination, sexual harassment, and any mistreatment) were examined by characteristics of interest and tested with χ2 or Fisher exact tests. Because associations were tested for multiple outcomes and characteristics, values of P<.00083 were considered to be statistically significant by Bonferroni correction (0.05/6 outcomes/10 characteristics=0.00083). Multivariable logistic regression models were run for each of the outcomes of interest accounting for gender, race, and sexual orientation. Interactions between 1) gender and race, 2) race and sexual orientation, and 3) gender and sexual orientation were examined by the addition of interaction terms to the multivariable models. All analyses were performed with SAS 9.4.

RESULTS

Overall, 4,158 of the 6,287 individuals (66.1%) who took the in-service examination in January 2025 both consented to participate in and answered at least one of the mistreatment questions on the survey. Individuals who did not answer any of the mistreatment questions, were not residents, or for whom U.S. census region could not be determined were excluded, resulting in 3,943 U.S. residents being included in the analysis. Of those, 3,392 (86.3%) were female, 37 (0.9%) were gender diverse, and 407 (10.4%) were male. Of those who provided information on race or ethnicity, 561 (14.3%) identified as Asian, 364 (9.3%) as Black or African American, 259 (6.6%) as Hispanic or Latino, 113 (2.9%) as Middle Eastern or North African, and 2,339 (59.6%) as White. Residents from each year of residency (PGY-1–4) were represented as follows: 1,033 (26.2%) of the respondents were in their PGY-1, 958 (24.3%) were in PGY-2, 1,026 (26.1%) were in PGY-3 year, and 920 (23.4%) were in their PGY-4 year. Verbal or emotional abuse was the most common type of mistreatment and was reported as “ever experienced” by 57.5% of residents (2,250 of 3,915) responding to that item on the survey, with gender discrimination as the second most experienced type of mistreatment, similarly reported by 52.0% of residents (2,022 of 3,888). Racial discrimination was reported by 25.8% (1,003 of 3,882), discrimination based on pregnancy or childcare status by 14.8% (576 of 3,915), and sexual harassment by 9.3% (362 of 3,897). The most common behaviors reported in the verbal or emotional abuse category were “being repeatedly reminded of mistakes,” reported by 1,193 of residents (31.6%), and “being shouted at or the target of spontaneous anger,” reported by 1,175 of residents (30.0%). The most common behavior reported in the gender discrimination category was being mistaken for a nonphysician, with 828 (21.3%) of residents reporting that they experienced this a “few times per month or more.” The most common type of discrimination based on race or ethnicity reported was “being mistaken for another person of the same race,” and 308 (7.9%) of all residents reported experiencing this behavior a “few times per month or more.” The most common type of sexual harassment was “being subjected to crude or sexually demeaning or explicit remarks,” which 278 (7.1%) of residents reported experiencing in the prior 6 months.

Overall, 77.9% of obstetrics and gynecology residents (3,191 of 4,094) reported at least one experience of mistreatment. This “ever experienced” composite value was inclusive of all types and all frequencies of mistreatment from “now and then” or “once or twice per year” to “daily.”

Residents who experienced mistreatment reported that the most frequent sources were patients or patients' families (68.9%), attendings (67.7%), and nurses (64.2%) at similar rates overall. Other residents and fellows (53.9%) were also frequent sources of mistreatment. Differences were observed in the source of mistreatment based on the type of mistreatment described (Table 2). When gender discrimination was reported, the most common source was patients or patients' families, with 83.5% of residents who experienced gender discrimination reporting that the source was a patient or a patient’s family. The most common source of racial discrimination was nurses (80.3%). Attendings were the most common source for resident reported verbal or emotional abuse (72.4%), discrimination based on pregnancy/childcare (67.9%) and sexual harassment (54.4%).

The location where mistreatment was reported most frequently overall was labor and delivery. In addition, each type of mistreatment was reported to have occurred most frequently on labor and delivery, although mistreatment was commonly reported as occurring in the operating room and in outpatient settings as well (Table 3). Residents reported verbal or emotional abuse most commonly on labor and delivery (69.5%), followed by the operating room (50.4%) and then the outpatient setting (20.4%). For residents reporting gender discrimination, this was also reported most frequently on labor and delivery (71.1%), followed by the outpatient setting (52.4%) and then the operating room (47.4%). Racial discrimination was similarly reported most often on labor and delivery (81.7%), followed by the operating room (54.3%) and then the outpatient setting (43.3%). Lastly, of residents who reported experiencing sexual harassment, this was most often reported on labor and delivery (54.1%), followed by the operating room setting (34.5%) and then the outpatient setting (21%). These percentages sum to greater than 100 because residents could select more than one location.

Resident-reported rates of “ever experienced” any type of mistreatment differ according to demographic characteristics (Table 4). Female residents and those who identified as gender diverse reported higher rates of mistreatment as indicated by the higher composite “ever mistreatment” rate than men, with 91.9% of gender-diverse residents reporting mistreatment compared with 80.1% of female and 67.8% of male residents. There were also differences based on race and ethnicity. The number of Black residents who reported “ever experiencing” mistreatment was significantly higher than their White counterparts, with 86.4% of Black residents reporting mistreatment compared with 77.1% of White residents. There were no statistically significant differences in rates of any type mistreatment for those identifying as Asian, Hispanic/Latino, or Middle Eastern/North African. The LGBQ+ residents reported “ever experiencing” any type of mistreatment more frequently than their straight counterparts. Although residents with disabilities reported “ever experiencing” any mistreatment more frequently than those without disabilities, the difference was not statistically significant. There were no differences in overall rates of reported ever experiencing mistreatment frequency based on PGY, program type, relationship status, or parenting/pregnancy status. A multivariable regression analysis as outlined previously demonstrated gender, race and ethnicity, and sexual orientation each to be independently associated with “ever experiencing” any type of mistreatment (Appendix 3, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E787).

Although 25.8% of all respondents reported experiencing racial discrimination, these rates were significantly higher in Black residents (66.3%), Asian residents (52.1%), Middle Eastern/North African residents (28.8%), and Hispanic or Latino residents (26.8%) compared with White residents, 11.7% of whom reported experiencing racial discrimination (P<.0001).

Respondents identifying as gender diverse or female reported significantly higher frequency of gender discrimination at rates of 77.8% and 55.1%, respectively, compared with those identifying as male (28.2%, P<.0001).

The LGBQ+ residents reported frequencies of gender discrimination (59.3% vs 51.2%, P=.0004), sexual harassment (13.1% vs 8.4%, P=.0003), and verbal or emotional abuse (66.4% vs 55.8%, P<.0001) at statistically significantly higher rates than their straight counterparts.

Lastly, residents with a disability reported significantly higher frequencies of gender discrimination (68.5% vs 52.0%, P=.0007), sexual harassment (20.4% vs 8.8%, P<.0001), and verbal or emotional abuse (76.2% vs 56.7%, P=.0001) than those without a disability (Supplemental Table 2: Type of Mistreatment by ability status).

There were no differences in composite “ever mistreatment” reported by residents based on their relationship or parenting status (Appendix 3: Type of Mistreatment Based on Relationship Status), parenting status (Appendix 4: Type of Mistreatment Based on Parenting Status), and program type (Appendix 5: Type of Mistreatment Based on program type). Residents in PGY-3–4 were more likely to report all types of discrimination and mistreatment than residents in PGY-1–2 (Appendix 6).

No statistically significant interactions were observed between gender and race, gender and sexual orientation, or race and sexual orientation.

DISCUSSION

This comprehensive survey provides important insight into the clinical learning environment in obstetrics and gynecology and demonstrates that mistreatment is common among obstetrics and gynecology residents, with over half of residents reporting that they have “ever experienced” any type of mistreatment in the prior 6 months. Addressing such mistreatment would likely contribute to improved educational experiences and quality of care and reduce burnout in the future obstetrics and gynecology workforce.1–4,9,10

Although a direct comparison with the data on mistreatment of residents in other specialties is not feasible, the rate of overall mistreatment of residents in obstetrics and gynecology (78%) was comparable to the rates reported in general surgery (50% overall, 70% of female residents), internal medicine (48% overall, 65% of female residents), and emergency medicine (45% overall, 65% of female residents).1,5,6 In each of these studies, mistreatment was more prevalent in female residents. In line with the existing literature, we identified increased rates of mistreatment reported by historically marginalized groups, calling out the importance of specific interventions targeted at mitigating identity-based discrimination that residents experience during training. This is particularly important in obstetrics and gynecology given the increased racial and gender diversity of obstetrics and gynecology trainees relative to other specialties.11

Although specialty leaders have begun to address these concerns through cultural and institutional changes,12 knowledge of the types of mistreatment residents most commonly report, the most common sources, and the clinical spaces where these incidents occur may help residency programs, graduate medical education offices, obstetrics and gynecology departments, hospitals, and our national organizations institute actionable interventions to decrease mistreatment. Organizations must identify ways to decrease the mistreatment that residents experience originating from patients because patients/patients' families are most common sources of mistreatment overall for trainees in obstetrics and gynecology, emergency medicine, and internal medicine.5,6 This is critical because mistreatment from patients has been demonstrated to negatively influence trainees' well-being, learning, and professional identity.13 Interventions focused on faculty in both general surgery and obstetrics and gynecology should focus on reducing verbal and emotional abuse of trainees. In both specialties, faculty are responsible for a significant amount of verbal and emotional abuse experienced by trainees. The most frequently reported behaviors in this category were 1) being shouted at or the target of spontaneous anger, 2) repeated reminders of errors or mistakes, and 3) persistent criticism of work. These behaviors reflect faculty attempting to provide feedback to the residents. Faculty development focused on providing feedback may help to decrease the frequency of these behaviors.

In addition, interventions directed toward building better collegiality with nursing colleagues in obstetrics and gynecology should focus on reducing racial discrimination experienced by residents because racial discrimination was commonly attributed to nursing colleagues. In obstetrics and gynecology, other residents and fellows were frequently a source of verbal or emotional abuse. Much higher rates of residents reporting this type of mistreatment from a peer (56%) were reported compared with emergency medicine (6%) and general surgery (20%).1,6 Residency programs should consider supporting trainees with resources to improve how they provide feedback to each other, manage conflict, and work in teams, particularly in settings where the patient acuity is high.

This study was the first survey of resident-reported mistreatment that examined where mistreatment occurred. In this survey, all types of reported mistreatment were most common on labor and delivery. This is not surprising given the dynamic, team-based, interdisciplinary, acute care nature of labor and delivery; the need for urgent and emergent communication; and the 24/7 nature of the specialty where teams may be fatigued at night and less likely to communicate effectively.14

In addition to being the first study to examine the location of mistreatment, other strengths of this study include that the data were obtained from a large national survey offered to all obstetrics and gynecology residents; that the study used a validated tool; and that the response rate to the survey was robust.

Potential weaknesses include that the survey was administered with the in-service examination, and residents might have been hesitant to disclose mistreatment despite reassurances of anonymity. Recall bias may have influenced resident responses, with more recent experiences with mistreatment reported more frequently. Future research might initiate and measure the effectiveness of interventions, particularly on labor and delivery, to help faculty, nurses, and trainees improve the clinical learning environment.

This study of obstetrics and gynecology residents provides important and highly concerning data and highlights an urgent need for our specialty to focus on improving the clinical learning environment, particularly on labor and delivery. With the recognition of the well-documented advantages of workforce diversity15 and the deliberate efforts within obstetrics and gynecology to cultivate a clinical workforce reflecting the demographic and experiential characteristics of populations served,16,17 it is essential to address factors that may hinder optimization of inclusive, professional, supportive, and high-performing clinical environments.
