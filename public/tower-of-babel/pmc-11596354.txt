Global trends and current research in post-traumatic osteoarthritis: A bibliometric and visualization analysis from 2010 to 2024
Authors: Songnian Gao, Yanwu Zhang, Yongliang Ge, Hui Lu, Min Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:To investigate global trends and current research on post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) from 2010 to 2024 using bibliometric and visualization techniques.Methods:A bibliometric analysis was conducted using data from the Web of Science Core Collection. The study examined publication trends, author contributions, institutional collaborations, keyword co-occurrence, and citation patterns, employing CiteSpace software to analyze key metrics such as publication frequency, centrality, and clustering.Results:A total of 3100 articles were published between 2010 and 2024, with a steady increase over the years, peaking at 320 articles in 2023. Most publications were from the USA (1141 articles), China (502), and Germany (268), with key fields being Orthopedics, Surgery, and Rheumatology. Early research focused on different types of osteoarthritis, while recent studies highlight therapeutic advances such as cartilage repair and oxidative stress. Co-citation analysis identified influential authors like Lohmander LS, and key research clusters include total hip arthroplasty and regenerative medicine.Conclusion:Over the past decade, PTOA research has expanded substantially, driven by contributions from Orthopedics and Surgery, and supported by growing international collaboration, particularly between the United States, China, and European countries. Future research directions should prioritize elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying PTOA, advancing diagnostic methodologies, and developing innovative therapeutic approaches to improve patient outcomes. The interdisciplinary nature and international cooperation observed are essential to addressing the complex challenges posed by PTOA.

1. Introduction

Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) is an osteoarthritic condition precipitated by trauma, frequently observed following joint injuries and sports-related damage, with knee ligament tears, meniscal injuries, joint fractures, and dislocations being the most prevalent.[1] In contrast to primary osteoarthritis, PTOA predominantly affects young and middle-aged adults who are more physically active and have higher functional demands, necessitating earlier and more frequent surgical interventions.[2] International studies have shown that PTOA incurs annual costs exceeding $3 billion to healthcare systems, profoundly impacting patients’ quality of life and imposing significant societal burdens.[3]

Despite the growing burden of PTOA, understanding the broader research landscape surrounding it requires a systematic approach to identify key contributors and trends in this evolving field. Bibliometrics is a quantitative method for analyzing academic literature and is a pivotal tool for mapping scientific research’s intellectual landscape and evolutionary dynamics.[4] By examining publication patterns, citation networks, and collaborative relationships, bibliometric studies elucidate the development of specific research areas, identifying influential works, key contributors, and emerging topics. This transition to bibliometric analysis allows for a more structured and objective evaluation of how PTOA research has developed over time.

To further enhance the precision of this bibliometric exploration, specific tools are employed to visualize these complex relationships. CiteSpace, a software developed by Dr Chaomei Chen at Drexel University, is a powerful tool for visualizing and analyzing trends and patterns within scientific literature.[5] It combines the characteristics of both “maps” and “spectra,” allowing for the creation of comprehensive visual representations that demonstrate the evolution of research topics and identify emerging trends and frontiers.[6] The broad application of CiteSpace across various scientific fields underscores its robustness and effectiveness in conducting detailed bibliometric analyses, instilling confidence in its use for PTOA research. This study introduces new insights into the shifting focus of PTOA research, particularly the growing emphasis on regenerative medicine and molecular mechanisms, which were not as extensively explored in previous bibliometric studies.

Although PTOA represents a distinct subtype of osteoarthritis, it shares many pathophysiological characteristics with other forms of osteoarthritis, such as knee and hip osteoarthritis. Situating PTOA within the broader context of osteoarthritis research enhances the study’s interdisciplinary relevance, as insights from PTOA can inform broader osteoarthritis research, particularly in areas such as joint injury, cartilage repair, and inflammatory mechanisms. This study aims to conduct a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of PTOA literature from 2010 to 2024 using Web of Science Core Collection data. The analysis will encompass several critical aspects, including publication trends, author contributions, institutional collaborations, keyword co-occurrence, and citation patterns. Search terms used in the bibliometric analysis included “traumatic arthritis,” “post-traumatic arthritis,” “traumatic osteoarthritis,” and ‘PTOA’. Articles were included if they focused on PTOA in human subjects and were published in peer-reviewed journals in English. Exclusion criteria involved studies focusing on non-traumatic forms of osteoarthritis or those lacking sufficient relevance to PTOA research. By employing advanced bibliometric techniques and visualization tools such as CiteSpace, this study intends to construct a detailed knowledge map of PTOA research, revealing the field’s historical evolution, current hotspots, and prospective future directions. The findings from this bibliometric study will offer valuable insights for researchers, clinicians, and policymakers involved in PTOA research and management. These insights will not only facilitate the identification of leading researchers and institutions but also promote collaboration and guide evidence-based decision-making in the treatment and prevention of PTOA, underscoring the practical relevance and applicability of this research.

2. Methods

2.1. Search strategy

The references for this article were obtained from the Web of Science citation index databases, specifically the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI-EXPANDED) and the Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI). The search was conducted within the Web of Science Core Collection, covering the period from 2010 to 2024, with the final search date being May 6, 2024. The search terms used were “TS = (traumatic arthritis) OR TS = (post traumatic arthritis) OR TS = (traumatic osteoarthritis) OR TS = (PTOA),” with the language filter set to “English.” These 4 search terms were connected using “OR,” resulting in a total of 4439 relevant articles retrieved. Only peer-reviewed articles published in English were included, while conference proceedings and non-peer-reviewed articles were excluded. This search focused on Orthopedics, Rheumatology, and Sports Medicine within the Web of Science database. These specific terms were chosen based on their frequent use in previous studies and established terminology within the field of PTOA. Initial exploratory searches and consultations with experts in the field further validated that these terms would comprehensively capture the most relevant literature on PTOA. No additional filters, such as document or source type, were applied during the search.

2.2. Bibliometrics and statistical analysis

2.2.1. Data extraction and selection criteria

The retrieved literature was exported in “Plain text file” format, selecting “Full Record and Cited References” for content. For data extraction, all retrieved articles were initially screened by title and abstract to assess their relevance to PTOA. Studies were included if they explicitly focused on PTOA in human subjects and were published in peer-reviewed journals. Non-relevant studies, such as those focusing on non-traumatic osteoarthritis or animal models, were excluded. Full-text screening was conducted for potentially relevant articles to ensure they met the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 3100 articles were selected for inclusion in the bibliometric analysis.

2.2.2. Data processing and deduplication

This export included titles, authors, publication years, research institutions, keywords, and abstracts. The exported files were renamed to a format recognizable by CiteSpace V6.2.R4, such as “download xxx.txt.” To ensure data accuracy, a two-step deduplication process was employed. First, the plain text files exported from the Web of Science database, named in the “download xxx.txt” format, were imported into CiteSpace. The “Remove Duplicates” function was then applied, with options “Article,” “Review,” “Export Wos,” and “Export DOl” selected. The “Block size” was set to 500 to balance computational efficiency and data resolution, effectively removing duplicate records.[7] This process resulted in 3100 unique records.

2.2.3. Co-occurrence and citation analysis

The analysis’s time span was set from 2010 to 2024, with a one-year time slice. This period was selected to capture the most recent advancements and trends in PTOA research. The starting point of 2010 was chosen because this marks a significant increase in the volume of PTOA-related publications, indicating growing interest and progress in the field. Extending the analysis to 2024 allows for including the latest research, providing a comprehensive overview of developments in the past decade and beyond. All term sources were selected by default. The database threshold (topN per slice) was set to 50. The author and institution collaborated on pruning using Pathfinder, sliced networks, and the merged network methods. The keyword co-occurrence analysis utilized the Minimum Spanning Tree algorithm, which minimizes the total distance between nodes and provides a more precise representation of relationships. The timezone view was chosen for the co-occurrence map, while other settings remained the default.

Co-occurrence analysis was conducted on node types, including Keywords, Country, Institution, Author, and Category. Citation analysis was performed on cited authors and references, creating a visual knowledge map. These analyses provide insights into the evolving research landscape and highlight significant trends in PTOA research.

2.2.4. CiteSpace methodology

CiteSpace integrates mathematical, visual, and analytical methodologies.[8] These approaches help visualize complex relationships within scientific literature, allowing for the identification of research clusters, emerging trends, and key contributors in PTOA research. By using CiteSpace, we were able to map the evolutionary processes within the field, providing practical insights into how research has developed over time and where future research efforts may be focused.[9]

2.2.5. Key analysis metrics

The included literature analysis encompasses several key aspects: publication volume, disciplinary distribution, major research contributors, research hotspots, and significant research documents.[8,10]

CiteSpace analysis identified key research trends over time. Earlier studies (2010–2016) primarily focused on injury-related topics such as traumatic brain injury and necrosis factor-alpha. In contrast, recent research (2017–2024) has shifted toward therapeutic interventions like cartilage repair and cruciate ligament reconstruction, as well as biological mechanisms like oxidative stress.

This shift reflects the evolving priorities in PTOA research, moving from injury mechanisms to a greater focus on treatment and molecular processes (Fig. 9).

4. Discussions

4.1. Current research status of PTOA

Over the past decade, there has been a steady increase in research on PTOA, reflecting growing academic interest. Orthopedics remains the dominant discipline, followed by Surgery, Sport Sciences, and Rheumatology.[14] A notable surge in publications occurred in 2019, indicating heightened research activity.

Recent studies have increasingly adopted a multidisciplinary approach, moving beyond clinical fields. For example, the OPTIKNEE consensus aims to improve knee health following traumatic injury to prevent osteoarthritis.[15] Additionally, research on the disease’s progression using models like the instrumented sheep knee offers valuable insights.[16] The recognition of osteoarthritis as a metabolically active inflammatory disorder has prompted exploration into adjunctive therapies.[17]

Furthermore, there is a growing emphasis on patient education and holistic management strategies, including programs like Tai Chi therapy.[18] This shift highlights the move towards collaborative, multidisciplinary efforts to address osteoarthritis and the development of evidence-based therapeutic recommendations.[19] Bibliometric studies also suggest an increasing focus on understanding PTOA mechanisms, laying the groundwork for novel treatments.[20]

The trend towards multidisciplinary research underscores the need for collaborative approaches, which are crucial for advancing PTOA research and fostering innovation.

4.2. Mechanisms of onset and progression of PTOA

Recent research has delved deeply into understanding the underlying mechanisms of PTOA, with particular emphasis on the roles of articular cartilage and inflammatory cytokines.[13] Acute joint trauma can severely damage cartilage collagen, stimulating chondrocytes to release reactive oxygen species, which in turn accelerates chondrocyte damage and matrix degradation. This cascade of events often leads to chondrocyte death, which is considered a critical precursor to structural joint damage in PTOA.[17–20] In vivo studies further highlight that a single impact can induce apoptosis in chondrocytes, and the severity of this apoptosis increases with greater load intensity.[21]

A key driver of PTOA progression is the elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines that follow joint injuries.[22] Over the past decade, studies have consistently demonstrated that the inflammatory response, mediated by macrophages and the release of cytokines, is closely linked to the degree of cartilage damage observed in patients. This inflammation not only promotes matrix degradation but also exacerbates the structural decline of the joint.[23–25] For example, in patients with ACL injuries, the synthesis of lubricin, a critical synovial fluid component, is significantly reduced after injury, and it may take up to a year for these levels to return to normal.[26]

Another significant factor contributing to PTOA development is the occurrence of sports-related injuries. Research has identified common injuries, such as ACL and meniscal tears, glenohumeral dislocations, and ankle instabilities, as key risk factors for PTOA. These injuries often lead to osteoarthritis within 10 to 20 years, with imaging studies revealing early signs of degeneration in athletes post-ACL rupture or meniscal tear.[27–31] Notably, meniscal injuries are found in approximately 63% of knee osteoarthritis patients, underscoring their strong association with cartilage loss and joint degeneration.[32] Additionally, glenohumeral dislocations and patellar dislocations frequently result in long-term functional limitations, and up to 90% of ankle osteoarthritis cases are linked to traumatic injuries.[33–36]

Beyond sports injuries, intra-articular fractures play a critical role in accelerating PTOA onset. The mechanical impact from these fractures places immense stress on both the extracellular matrix and chondrocytes, triggering the release of damage-associated molecular patterns such as fibronectin. This, in turn, activates a cycle of continuous cell death and cartilage destruction, further perpetuating joint damage.[37,38] Synovitis, decreased bone density, and changes in joint mechanics have also been linked to worsening cartilage damage, highlighting the multifactorial nature of PTOA progression.[39–43]

4.3. Prevention and treatment of PTOA

The prevention and treatment of PTOA remain at the forefront of research, with bibliometric analysis indicating a growing interest in both pharmacological interventions and surgical strategies. As highlighted by our bibliometric findings, the frequency of research related to inflammation, cartilage repair, and surgical techniques has increased significantly, reflecting the evolving priorities in PTOA management. Studies have confirmed the efficacy of intra-articular injections of anti-cytokine drugs – such as interleukin-1 receptor antagonists and soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor II – which significantly reduce cartilage degeneration and synovial inflammation following joint fractures.[37] This aligns with the research hotspots identified in our analysis, emphasizing the critical role of anti-inflammatory therapies in the field.

Early intervention using low-intensity pulsed ultrasound, highlighted in the literature as a growing area of interest, has also shown promise. It reduces synovial inflammation and matrix degradation while promoting type II collagen expression, thus delaying cartilage damage.[38] This trend corresponds with the increase in studies focused on noninvasive treatment modalities, as observed in our bibliometric clustering.

Preventative strategies such as proprioceptive training and protective gear usage, which reduce injury recurrence by up to 60%, also reflect the increasing focus on sports injury prevention – a theme prevalent in recent PTOA research.[41] The bibliometric analysis further underscores the shift towards a holistic approach to prevention, with keywords such as “injury prevention” and “neuromuscular training” appearing more frequently in recent years. The timing of post-injury exercise has also been identified as critical, with animal studies showing that well-timed rehabilitation enhances therapeutic efficacy.[42] Interestingly, bibliometric data reveal an increase in publications questioning the effectiveness of ACL reconstruction, with recent research suggesting it may not reduce PTOA progression compared to non-surgical approaches.[43]

Our analysis also highlights the importance of subchondral bone remodeling in PTOA progression, with studies suggesting that remodeling byproducts from subchondral bone may exacerbate joint degradation. The potential for anti-resorptive agents to inhibit osteoclast activity and prevent PTOA has emerged as a key research area.[39] This aligns with the bibliometric clusters focused on “bone remodeling” and “cartilage degeneration,” further reinforcing the connection between bone health and PTOA progression.

While pharmacological and animal model studies dominate PTOA treatment research, our analysis also emphasizes the critical role of surgical interventions and neuromuscular function training. There is a therapeutic window post-injury, during which chondrocytes can be saved from apoptosis to preserve joint integrity.[44,45] Continuous passive motion therapy and the elimination of dysfunctional chondrocytes post-injury are increasingly recognized as effective interventions.[46]

Key therapeutic targets identified through bibliometric analysis include caspases, whose inhibition can protect chondrocytes from apoptosis, and anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1, IL-4, and IL-10.[47,48] These findings reflect the broader trend toward therapies that address both the inflammatory and degenerative aspects of PTOA. Growth factors such as bone morphogenetic protein 7 and fibroblast growth factor 2 are also gaining traction, as they show promise in reducing cartilage catabolism and promoting regeneration.[49–51] This trend is corroborated by the growing research focus on “regenerative medicine” and “molecular therapies,” as highlighted in our bibliometric analysis.

Antioxidants such as berberine, quercetin, and N-acetylcysteine have also emerged as significant in reducing chondrocyte death caused by mechanical stress.[52–54] N-acetylcysteine, in particular, has shown promise in increasing proteoglycan content at injury sites. While glucosamine remains widely used, its efficacy is debated, as reflected in the research clusters on “glucosamine efficacy” and “clinical trials” identified in our bibliometric review.[55,56]

Finally, the rise of regenerative medicine techniques, such as autologous chondrocyte implantation, represents a future direction for treating younger PTOA patients who may not be suitable candidates for joint replacement.[57] This area, identified as a major emerging research cluster, underscores the field’s movement toward biological treatments and personalized medicine – trends that our analysis suggests will continue to grow.

4.4. Implications of bibliometric findings for PTOA research and treatment

The bibliometric analysis identifies several key trends that are shaping the future of PTOA research and therapeutic strategies. The focus on anti-inflammatory therapies and regenerative medicine marks a notable shift away from traditional surgical methods, moving toward more targeted approaches. This transition highlights the growing interest in addressing the molecular and cellular mechanisms behind PTOA, as evidenced by prominent research clusters such as inflammation and cartilage repair. These developments suggest that future treatments will emphasize early intervention and joint preservation rather than symptom management alone.

5. Limitations

It is important to acknowledge several limitations of this study. Although the Web of Science is one of the most authoritative literature databases, it does not encompass all publications relevant to this research. This reliance on a single database may have excluded relevant literature from other sources. Furthermore, the search was restricted to articles written in English, which may have introduced a language bias by excluding pertinent studies published in other languages. This limitation could affect the comprehensiveness of the study, as valuable contributions from non-English-speaking regions might not have been captured. However, given that English is the dominant language of international scientific discourse, we believe the impact of this limitation on the overall findings is minimal.

6. Conclusion

This bibliometric analysis shows a significant rise in PTOA research from 2010 to 2024, led by contributions from the USA, China, Germany, and England. Orthopedics and Surgery are the main disciplines, with institutions like the University of California System and Harvard University playing key roles. The increasing focus on diagnosis and molecular mechanisms reflects advancements that could improve clinical outcomes. However, challenges remain in understanding PTOA pathways and developing diagnostic tools. Future research should prioritize addressing these gaps through continued international collaboration.
