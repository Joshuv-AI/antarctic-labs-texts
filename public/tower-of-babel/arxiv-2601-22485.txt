Abstract
. Large language models (LLMs) have been widely integrated into critical automated workflows, including contract review and job application processes. However, LLMs are susceptible to manipulation by fraudulent information, which can lead to harmful outcomes. Although advanced defense methods have been developed to address this issue, they often exhibit limitations in effectiveness, interpretability, and generalizability, particularly when applied to LLM-based applications. To address these challenges, we introduce FraudShield, a novel framework designed to protect LLMs from fraudulent content by leveraging a comprehensive analysis of fraud tactics. Specifically, FraudShield constructs and refines a fraud tactic–keyword knowledge graph to capture high-confidence associations between suspicious text and fraud techniques. The structured knowledge graph augments the original input by highlighting keywords and providing supporting evidence, guiding the LLM toward more secure responses. 1.
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs)
38
are used in decision-making areas, including investment analysis
45
, contract review
27
, and job-search assistance
47
due to their strong capabilities.
However, LLMs remain vulnerable to conventional security and privacy threats, such as illegal actions
26
, misinformation
7
, and adversarial attacks
17
;
18
.
Beyond these risks, online fraud presents a critical yet underexplored challenge in LLM security.
If deceived, LLM-based agents will make incorrect decisions, and users lacking proper alerts can fall victim to fraudulent communications, resulting in financial losses and broader societal harm.

Unfortunately, fraud activities in real-world scenarios often involve complex, immersive, and multi-turn interactions. Early works in this area focused on training fraud detectors for specific types of fraud, such as identifying fake job postings
3
and detecting credit card fraud
6
. These detectors (e.g., Naive Bayes classifier) rely on fixed patterns and lack an in-depth understanding of textual semantics, making them unsuitable for detecting fraud in LLM interactions, which are often intricate and continuously evolving
23
.
Inspired by in-context learning techniques, recent methods—such as Safety Prompt
38
, Self-Reminder
41
, and Goal Prioritization
46
—have been proposed to enhance LLM safety against threats like jailbreaking and prompt injection. While these approaches can be extended to defend against fraud attacks, we surprisingly find that none of them are effective in this context; in fact, some may even weaken the model’s ability to recognize and respond to fraudulent behaviors.
We explain that their designs (e.g., specialized prompts) are tailored to LLM safety and fail to take fraudulent tactics into account, which limits their ability to defend against fraud attacks across diverse scenarios.
Additionally, they often fail to uncover the underlying rationales behind fraudulent behaviors, leading to limited effectiveness and poor interpretability.

To address these limitations, we introduce FraudShield, a novel
knowledge graph empowered
framework designed to safeguard LLMs against a broad range of fraud and phishing inducements through the analysis of fraudulent tactics.
To account for the evolving nature of fraudulent behaviors, we analyze their root causes and systematically categorize fraudulent tactics into four types:
urgency pressure
,
suspicious information
,
sensitive request
, and
credibility claims
. Inspired by the chain-of-thought mechanism, FraudShield counters fraudulent behavior in a two-stage manner. In the first stage, it detects keywords associated with each fraudulent tactic and interprets them as evidence. In the second stage, it highlights the identified keywords as well as their corresponding tactic and rationale in context, guiding LLM to respond appropriately.

However, the naive adoption of the proposed pipeline faces several limitations:
(
i
) Prompting LLMs to detect fraud-related keywords can introduce bias, leading to the identification of numerous irrelevant keywords. (
ii
) The extracted keywords may overlap with each other and conflict across identified tactics, resulting in ambiguous interpretations. These issues can degrade performance on benign datasets and hinder clarity for both LLM and human-based analysis.
To address the first limitation, we prompt the LLM to produce a rationale and confidence score for each detected keyword–tactic pair, then filter out pairs with low confidence to reduce false positives.

To tackle the second limitation, we introduce a graph-based refinement process. We model the keyword-tactic pairs as a knowledge graph, which allows us to systematically cluster overlapping keywords and resolve conflicts, resulting in a more coherent and structured presentation.

To enable LLMs to respond appropriately, we propose the use of XML-style tags to highlight keywords and their associated tactics in the context.
With these tailored designs, FraudShield can effectively defend against fraud attacks while preserving utility for benign interactions.

Figure 1.
When encountering fraudulent job postings, both the vanilla LLM and the safety prompt–based defense fail to guard the model against fraudulent content, whereas FraudShield successfully identifies red flags and advises caution.

Our experiments with four LLMs across five fraud types in the Fraud-R1 dataset
44
demonstrate that FraudShield significantly outperforms baseline methods (e.g., Self-Reminder). It improves the Defense Success Rate (DSR) by 23.88% in Helpful Assistant settings and 47.17% in Role-play settings on average.
As shown in Figure
1
, FraudShield identifies warning signs in fraudulent job postings while Vanilla LMs do not.
It excels across all scenarios in both the helpful assistant and role-play settings, effectively handling both single- and multi-round conversations. FraudShield not only safeguards LLMs against fraud attacks, but also enables seamless integration into practical applications
such as messaging apps, social platforms, and online transaction websites, serving as an alert system.
Moreover, our user study reveals that the highlighted keywords generated by the
graph-based
strategy also help human users better understand and respond to fraudulent activities, which further reinforces its effectiveness as a safeguard.
Our main contributions can be summarized as follows.

•

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to defend LLMs against a range of potential frauds in both single-turn and multi-turn interactions.

•

We propose FraudShield, a novel framework that employs a graph-based refinement process to systematically analyze and highlight fraud-related evidence, thereby safeguarding LLMs with an effective, generalizable, and interpretable defense.

•

We empirically validate that FraudShield effectively defends LLMs against a wide range of online fraud attempts while preserving their functionality for genuine user interactions.

Related Work

Online fraud.

Unlike traditional types of economic crime, online fraud crosses geographic and legal boundaries, encompasses various cybercrimes that use the internet to deceive victims through malicious invitations, requests, notifications, or offers, often for financial gain or to compromise personal safety
10
.
Fraudulent activities include celebrity impersonation, phishing, and hacking
40
. Phishing, a prevalent online fraud, generally involves sending deceptive emails that imitate legitimate sources
13
. With technological advancements, fraud has extended beyond email to encompass text messages, social networking sites, and phone calls
1
;
19
;
39
.
In 2024, online fraud resulted in approximately 12.5 billion in economic losses in the United States
11
, while in the UK, a report by the Global Anti-Scam Alliance found that individuals lost £11.4 billion to fraud
14
.
Recognized as a rapidly growing crime
29
, online fraud presents a significant challenge to social governance, particularly harming the elderly
5
.
The World Economic Forum identifies cyber threats as one of the most significant global risks.
As LLMs and LLM-based systems become integral to automated decision-making processes, it is crucial to develop robust safeguards against fraud and phishing.

Defense against fraud in LLMs.

Fraud in real-world role-play scenarios poses unique challenges, as LLMs can be manipulated to adopt victim roles. Existing defense methods are often limited to specific tasks such as financial fraud
2
,
phone scams
34
,
and phishing emails
33
.
Their rigid structures and single-turn evaluation approaches fail to capture the complexities of real-world fraud, which often involves multi-turn role-play interactions and evolving strategies.
Recent advances in LLM safety, such as Safety Prompt, Self-Reminder
41
, and Goal Prioritization
46
, aim to prevent malicious behavior and enhance model responsibility when faced with harmful inputs. Recent work has also explored automated red teaming approaches for LLM-based agents
16
and platforms
37
.
However, these strategies often overlook fraudulent tactics and remain vulnerable to fraud attacks. As LLMs are increasingly integrated into real-world applications and fraud schemes continue to evolve, it is essential to enhance the LLM’s ability to recognize and refuse fraudulent interactions.

Knowledge extraction and knowledge graph-based LLM reasoning.

LLMs excel at Information Extraction (IE)
42
, parsing unstructured text to identify entities and their relationships
24
. This capability is often used to build Knowledge Graphs (KGs), which provide structured, explicit knowledge that can improve downstream reasoning tasks. Combining KGs with LLMs has been approached in several ways. Early study
25
embeds KG knowledge directly into neural networks during pre-training or fine-tuning. However, this close integration can reduce explainability and complicate knowledge updates
22
. More recent methods combine KGs with LLMs by converting relevant knowledge into textual prompts, but this often flattens the rich structural information of the graph into linear text
32
. FraudShield differs from these approaches by using a dynamic, task-specific graph refinement process. It first employs the LLM’s IE capabilities to identify potential fraud indicators, then uniquely constructs a bipartite tactic-keyword graph to model their relationships.

Methodology

As shown in Figure
2
, we introduce FraudShield, a novel framework to protect LLM from fraudulent content by analyzing deceptive tactics. It prioritizes tactics with higher confidence scores, strategically highlights key terms, and uses selected evidence to boost LLMs’ awareness of fraud.

Problem formulation.
Given an input text sequence
x
, our goal is to enhance the reasoning abilities of LLMs to effectively assess suspicious requests, ensuring a secure response
y
that discerns fraudulent inputs. This involves two main objectives:
The
effectiveness goal
indicates that the defended LLM must accurately identify and reject fraudulent inputs. In contrast, the
utility goal
indicates that the defended LLM must maintain its performance on legitimate inputs (w/o fraud), continuing to produce the desired outputs.
The overall objective is formalized as follows:

(1)

y=\arg\max_{y_{i}}\mathcal{M}(y_{i}|x),

where
\mathcal{M}
is an LLM,
x
is a prompt, and
y_{i}
indicates all possible results of
y
.
Our approach aims to optimize the process to get a secure
y
even if
x
contains fraud through the following steps.

Step 1: Fraud tactics extraction.

While existing defense methods
41
;
46
aim for safer and more responsible outputs, they aren’t specifically designed for fraud detection. This gap becomes evident as the diverse forms of fraud scenarios make identification challenging. However, these scenarios share common tactics. FraudShield identifies common fraud tactics by analyzing the shared traits of diverse fraudulent behaviors, aiming to enhance LLMs’ ability to detect more nuanced fraudulent information. Based on established descriptions of common fraud tactics
12
;
44
, we ask three experienced professionals to categorize these characteristics into four primary tactics and to distill prevalent fraud patterns within each. Table
1
provides a comprehensive overview of these common fraud tactics and their corresponding descriptions, with more details available in Appendix
A.2
.
Using the information extraction and classification capabilities of LLMs
42
;
35
, we provide the input
x
to the LLM to extract fraud tactics, expressed as:

(2)

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\left(t_{i},\{(k_{i}^{j},s_{i}^{j},r_{i}^{j})\}_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{n},

where each fraud tactic
t_{i}
is linked to
m_{i}
keywords as supporting evidence. Each keyword
k_{i}^{j}
linked to an confidence score
s_{i}^{j}
and a rationale
r_{i}^{j}
.
This structure encapsulates each fraud tactic’s supporting keywords, scores, and evidence.

Step 2: Bipartite graph-based tactic association.

To systematically model and refine the relationships between detected keywords and fraud tactics, we construct a bipartite graph to get the keyword-tactic associations for analysis.

First, we extract all keywords
k_{i}^{j}
from
\{(k_{i}^{j},s_{i}^{j},r_{i}^{j})\}
linked to tactic
t_{i}
in
\mathcal{F}
, forming the set
\mathcal{K}
:

(3)

\mathcal{K}=\cup_{i=1}^{n}\cup_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\{k_{i}^{j}\}.

Figure
2
shows keyword overlap, where shorter keywords can be subsets of longer ones, causing confusing representations and misclassification.

To address overlap, we consolidate overlapping keywords into a single cluster.
We organize keywords into clusters
\mathcal{K}^{*}
. Each cluster
\mathcal{K}_{u}
in
\mathcal{K}^{*}
contains a keyword and all longer keywords containing it:

(4)

\mathcal{K}^{*}=\left\{K_{u}|K_{u}=\{k\in\mathcal{K}|k_{u}\subseteq k\}\text{ for each }k_{u}\in\mathcal{K}\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{T}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}
denote the set of predefined fraud tactics. We then construct a
weighted bipartite graph

G=(\mathcal{K}^{*}\cup\mathcal{T},E)
, where the two disjoint vertices sets are the keyword clusters from
\mathcal{K}^{*}
and the fraud tactics from
\mathcal{T}
. An edge
(K_{u},t_{v})\in E
is created between a keyword cluster
K_{u}\in\mathcal{K}^{*}
and a tactic
t_{v}\in\mathcal{T}
if any keyword within
K_{u}
is identified as evidence for
t_{v}
. The weight of this edge,
w(K_{u},t_{v})
, is defined by the average confidence score
\overline{s_{u}^{v}}
associated with all keywords in the cluster for that specific tactic.

This initial graph shows all potential keyword-tactic relationships. However, it faces two main issues: (
i
)
low-confidence associations
caused by bias from prompting LLMs to detect keywords, which can lead to the identification of irrelevant keywords. As shown in Table
8
in the Appendix, selecting keywords with lower confidence scores can misclassify benign text, reducing the method’s usefulness, and (
ii
)
ambiguities
where one keyword cluster links multiple conflicting tactics, complicating LLM attribution.

To address these challenges, we perform a two-stage refinement on the graph. First, to eliminate weak and irrelevant links, we
prune the graph
by removing all edges with a weight below a predefined confidence threshold
\tau
. Second, to resolve ambiguity, for each keyword cluster vertex
K_{u}
in the pruned graph, we select only the edge with the maximum weight, effectively assigning the cluster to its most probable tactic. This process yields the final set of high-confidence, unambiguous associations, denoted as
\mathcal{R}
:

(5)

\mathcal{R}=\left\{(K_{u},t_{v})\mid\overline{s_{u}^{v}}=\max_{t_{v^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{T}}\{\overline{s_{u}^{v^{\prime}}}\mid\overline{s_{u}^{v^{\prime}}}\geq\tau\}\right\}.

The resulting set
\mathcal{R}
contains definitive pairs
(K_{u},t_{v})
, providing a clear and reliable mapping from each relevant keyword cluster to its most likely fraud tactic for the subsequent steps.

Step 3: Evidence selection.

In this step, we aim to associate each fraud tactic
t_{i}
with the most relevant supporting evidence that meets a specified threshold
\tau
. For each fraud tactic
t_{i}
, we analyze its associated scores
\{s_{i}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{m_{i}}
and select the maximum score
s_{i}^{j^{*}}
among those satisfying
s_{i}^{j^{*}}\geq\tau
. The rationale
r_{i}^{*}
linked to this maximum score is selected as the supporting evidence.
We define the set of supported evidence
\mathcal{E}
derived from
\mathcal{F}
, including pairs
(t_{i},r_{i}^{j^{*}})
for each tactic
t_{i}
and its rationale
r_{i}^{j^{*}}
.

(6)

\mathcal{E}\!=\!\left\{(t_{i},r_{i}^{j^{*}})\!\mid\!(t_{i},\{(k_{i}^{j},s_{i}^{j},r_{i}^{j})\}_{j=1}^{m_{i}})\!\in\!\mathcal{F},\,\!j^{*}\!=\!\arg\max_{j}s_{i}^{j},\,s_{i}^{j^{*}}\!\geq\!\tau\right\}_{i=1}^{n}.

This structure effectively links each fraud tactic to its most significant supporting rationale.
This set of supporting evidence,
\mathcal{E}
, is a critical component for the next step, as it provides the LLM with explicit justifications to guide its final in-depth analysis.

Step 4: In-depth analysis.

After identifying keywords linked to fraud tactics and rationales by constructing the keyword-tactic graph to obtain high-confidence associations (
\mathcal{R}
) and selecting the strongest rationales (
\mathcal{E}
), it is essential to effectively summarize and emphasize these elements to predict the likelihood of fraud within the text.
We use the instruction capability of XML tags
4
;
28
;
30
to help LLM associate keywords with fraud tactics, emphasizing these keywords to highlight potential threats.
Our approach involves prompting LLMs with two components: (
i
) an XML-tagged version of the input (
<tactics>keywords</tactics>
) from
\mathcal{R}
to emphasize fraud tactics of keywords, and (
ii
) supporting evidence
\mathcal{E}
as the rationale
to provide more substantial evidence for identified fraudulent content to enhance the model’s capacity for deeper reasoning.
This method enhances keyword identification contextually for LLMs and visually for human users.

We search the original text
x
for each longest keyword in each cluster
K_{u}
and add the associated fraud tactic to produce an enhanced text
x^{\prime}
.
This is achieved by wrapping detected keywords
k
in XML tags representing the fraud tactic
t
. For example, a fraudulent URL “jdfinance.cn” categorized under
Suspicious Information
tactic is encapsulated with
<Suspicious Information>
and
</Suspicious Information>
.
We formalize this process with a function
f
that maps each word or phrase
w_{i}
in the sequence
\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\}
to its an enhanced or unchanged form as follows:

(7)

\begin{aligned} f(w_{i})=\begin{cases}\texttt{<}t\texttt{>}w_{i}\texttt{</}t\texttt{>},&\exists(K_{u},t_{v})\in\mathcal{R}\text{ s.t., }w_{i}\!=\!\text{Longest}(K_{u})\\
w_{i},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\end{aligned}

The enhanced text
x^{\prime}
is obtained by applying
f
to each word or phrase in
x
:

(8)

x^{\prime}=\{f(w_{1}),f(w_{2}),\ldots,f(w_{n})\},

where
f
examines each word or phrase
w_{i}
in the text
x
. If
w_{i}
matches a keyword
k
in
\mathcal{R}
, it is replaced with
<
t
>
w_{i}
</
t
>
, where
t
is the corresponding tactic for
k
. Otherwise,
w_{i}
remains unchanged.

Based on the enhanced text
x^{\prime}
and evidence
\mathcal{E}
. The FraudShield could finally formulate as:

(9)

y=\arg\max_{y_{i}}\mathcal{M}\left(y_{i}|x^{\prime},\mathcal{E}\right).

4.
Experiments

4.1.
Dataset

We use Fraud-R1
44
, a benchmark dataset from social media, news reports, lectures, and previous single-task fraud datasets. It includes a collection of fraudulent cases, consisting of 1,071 single-round and 1,071 multi-round interactions, created to evaluate an LLM’s ability to defend against fraud in five real-world scenarios: Fraudulent Service, Impersonation, Phishing Scams, Fake Job Posting, and Online Relationship.
These scenarios are assessed in two common real-world settings: the Helpful Assistant, where LLMs provide advice before user decisions, and Role-play, used in LLM-based agent systems and persona-based LLM research.

4.2.
Models

We conducted an evaluation of four distinct LLMs, comprising two API-based models: GPT-4o-mini
21
and GPT-o3-mini
31
, in addition to two open-weights models: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
15
and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
36
.

4.3.
Baselines

To evaluate our method holistically, we compare FraudShield with the following baselines:
(i)
Vanilla LM
, which directly presents tasks and the corresponding questions, predicting the outcomes of the questions through in-context learning;
(ii)
Safety Prompt

38
, which is the official safety prompt of LLaMA-2 illustrated in Appendix
A
;
(iii)
Self-Reminder

41
, which adds instructions before and after the user’s query to remind LLMs to respond responsibly;
and (iv)
Goal Prioritization

46
, which designs a prompt that explicitly instructs the LLM to prioritize safety over helpfulness, reinforced with in-context examples to demonstrate appropriate responses to benign and harmful queries.

4.4.
Metrics

Defense success rate at round
k
(DSR@k).

To evaluate the effectiveness of LLMs in identifying fraudulent requests, we employ the metric DSR@k. This metric assesses the LLMs’ ability to
successfully detect and refuse
fraud inducements at different stages within a conversation.
Following
44
, we employ GPT-4o as a judge model (details in Appendix
A.3
).
For each input
x
, a “Defense Success” is recorded if the model successfully identifies and detects the fraud inducement by the
k
-th round. The values for
k
range from {1, 2, 3, 4}, with
k=1
indicating the initial stage of the conversation when the model first encounters it.

In practical applications, especially in LLM-based agents, we prefer LLMs to decisively reject fraudulent information upon first encounter, rather than identifying risk after several rounds, so
DSR@1 (abbreviated as DSR) measures the model’s effectiveness in single-round conversations, highlighting its ability to detect fraud during the initial interaction.
The formal definition of DSR@k is:

(10)

\text{DSR@k}=\frac{|\{s_{i}\,|\,\text{Defense Success }s_{i}\text{ by round }k\}|}{\text{Total number }n\text{ of test samples}}.

Accuracy (ACC).
To assess whether FraudShield’s sensitivity to keywords might lead to excessive rejection or result in errors due to interference from detected irrelevant fraud evidence, thereby affecting the normal execution of tasks and reducing accuracy, we evaluate utility using ACC on the MMLU dataset
20
. We prompt LLMs to generate options such as “A”, “B”, “C”, or “D”. We determine the accuracy by checking whether the final answer starts with the correct option. This metric ensures that the LLM maintains its performance on legitimate inputs (w/o fraud), continuing to produce the desired output.

5.
Discussion

User study.
To quantitatively evaluate the effectiveness of FraudShield’s keyword highlighting in improving human detection of fraudulent content, we conduct a user study with 200 participants. Participants are randomly assigned to one of two conditions: a treatment group that views fraudulent texts with keywords highlighted by FraudShield, and a control group that views the same texts without highlights. After reading the text, each participant completes a questionnaire to assess their accuracy in identifying the scam and the perceived impact of the highlights on their alertness. The results demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in fraud detection. We find that 97% of participants in the highlighted group correctly identify the text as a scam, compared to only 76% in the control group. Furthermore, the highlights serve as a highly effective warning signal. Of the participants in the highlighted group who correctly identify the scam, over 95% report that FraudShield increases their alertness. These findings confirm that FraudShield not only enhances users’ ability to detect threats but is also perceived as a clear and interpretable security tool. Detailed results and the questionnaire design are available in Appendix
B
.

In-Context defense vs. fine-tuning.

To explore the possibility of directly incorporating FraudShield’s defensive capabilities into a model’s parameters, we investigated whether its generated outputs could serve as a high-quality dataset for supervised fine-tuning (SFT))
9
. We fine-tuned a Llama-3.1-8B model on these outputs to enhance its intrinsic defensive capabilities against fraudulent requests. While this SFT model achieved a strong and comparable average DSR, this investigation revealed several inherent trade-offs, which highlight the unique advantages of our in-context, training-free approach: (
i
) Broader Applicability: FraudShield is a training-free framework, making it immediately applicable to both open-source and closed-source LLMs (e.g., GPT-4 series, Claude 3) where fine-tuning is not feasible
8
. (
ii
) Enhanced Interpretability: Our approach provides structured, actionable feedback by highlighting suspicious keywords and explicitly identifying fraud tactics. This allows FraudShield to function as a real-time alert system for end users, a capability that the black-box nature of a fine-tuned model lacks. (
iii
) Improved Robustness against Concealed Frauds: While SFT performs well on average, FraudShield demonstrates greater robustness against certain concealed fraud types, such as Online Relationship and Fake Job Posting scams, where its focus on specific keywords provides a clear advantage
43
. (
iv
) Full Utility Preservation: FraudShield preserves the model’s capabilities on benign tasks, showing no degradation on the MMLU benchmark (70.60% ACC), whereas the SFT model exhibits a slight drop in accuracy (70.55%), suggesting a minor tendency towards over-refusal
22
.

Potential risks.

While FraudShield showcases its capabilities in improving the robustness of LLMs against fraudulent content, it is crucial to recognize the potential risks.
Attackers could repurpose defensive mechanisms that use LLMs to counter fraud, thereby enhancing and producing more convincing fraudulent content.
By analyzing patterns and vulnerabilities exposed during defensive testing, malicious actors might exploit these insights to craft increasingly sophisticated and deceptive schemes. This interplay between defense and offense underscores the ethical challenges in AI security.
We strongly advocate for responsible AI practices, ensuring that the methodologies outlined in this work are confined to defensive research and system protection, rather than enabling adversarial exploitation.
Additionally, we also advocate for broader measures, including potential legislation, to address the systemic challenge of fraud emerging within LLM ecosystems.

Limitations and Failure Cases.

Despite FraudShield’s strong overall performance, it exhibits limitations in scenarios involving highly nuanced or subtle fraudulent texts.
Appendix
C.3
showcases subtle fraud attacks that result in defense failures by FraudShield.
Although FraudShield extracts keywords and provides evidence, and LLM issues warnings in these cases, it does not directly block the fraudulent request, indicating that the identified risks require further clarification before making a judgment.
These failure cases underscore a critical area for future work: enhancing FraudShield ’s contextual reasoning and its ability to interpret subtle fraudulent cues. Improving performance in these ambiguous situations is essential for developing a truly robust defense against the full spectrum of evolving fraud tactics.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce FraudShield, a novel framework designed to enhance the robustness of LLMs against fraud and phishing inducements.

By analyzing fraudulent tactics, constructing and refining a tactic-keyword knowledge graph to generate high-confidence evidence, and highlighting this evidence using XML-based tagging, FraudShield provides an effective, generalizable, and interpretable defense that is applicable across diverse models and attack scenarios.

Experimental results demonstrate that our approach enhances the robustness of LLMs against fraud attacks, while preserving their performance on benign user interactions. Additionally, a user study shows that our
graph-based
strategy also enhances human awareness of fraudulent behaviors, further validating its effectiveness and interpretable. We believe this approach can be broadly applied to strengthen fraud awareness in LLMs across diverse domains.
