Destroy and reconstruct: A microneedle platform for combined NIR-II photothermal therapy and asiaticoside-mediated tissue regeneration in cutaneous amyloidosis
Authors: Zhiqiang Zhang, Long Chen, Chenxi Fang, Guoqiang You, Meitao Duan, Jungang Ren, Ming Chen, Chen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutaneous amyloidosis (CA) is a chronic skin disorder characterized by dermal amyloid deposition, accompanied by fibroblast hyperproliferation and keratinocyte apoptosis, yet current treatments are limited by poor transdermal delivery and inadequate lesion clearance. Here, we present a “destroy-reconstruct” strategy using dissolving microneedles (MNs) co-loaded with asiaticoside (AS) and a NIR-II photothermal agent (IR1048), modified with octaarginine (R8) for enhanced cellular uptake. The optimized AI-MN@R8 platform exhibited excellent mechanical strength, rapid in vivo dissolution within 20 min, and a photothermal conversion efficiency of 33.58% under 1064 nm laser irradiation. In vitro studies demonstrated that R8 modification significantly enhanced cellular uptake, and the combination of photothermal therapy and AS synergistically suppressed human skin fibroblasts (HSF) proliferation and migration via inhibition of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway. In a rat model of localized CA, AI-MN@R8 combined with laser irradiation achieved targeted dermal delivery, real-time temperature elevation to approximately 47, and near-complete amyloid clearance, as confirmed by Congo red and Thioflavin 488 staining. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses further revealed restored dermal architecture, reduced collagen deposition, and normalized epidermal keratin expression (KRT5/KRT14). Mechanistically, Western blot analysis of skin tissues confirmed that AI-MN@R8 treatment regulated the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, inhibited Caspase-3 activation, and downregulated fibrosis-related proteins (COL1A1/COL3A1), reversing the fibrotic microenvironment. No systemic toxicity was observed in major organs. This synergistic photothermal-pharmacological platform offers a safe and effective strategy for CA treatment with high clinical translational potential.Keywords: Dissolving microneedles, Asiaticoside, NIR-II photothermal therapy, Cutaneous amyloidosis, EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway

Introduction

Cutaneous amyloidosis (CA) is a chronic skin disorder characterized by localized dermal amyloid deposition without internal organ involvement [1,2]. Clinically, CA manifests as persistent pruritus, hyperkeratosis, and visible lesions that significantly impair patients’ quality of life [3]. The most common subtypes include macular, lichen, and nodular amyloidosis [4,5]. Currently, the pathogenesis of CA remains incompletely understood, although the apoptosis theory and the secretion theory are among the most widely recognized hypotheses. According to the classical apoptosis theory, excessive apoptosis of epidermal keratinocytes leads to the release of keratin into the dermis, where it subsequently undergoes degradation and aggregation to form amyloid deposits [6,7], In contrast, the secretion theory proposes that basal keratinocytes, upon stimulation, can directly secrete amyloid precursor proteins, thereby contributing to amyloid deposition without undergoing apoptosis [8,9]. The pathological process of CA primarily involves two cell types: epidermal keratinocytes (such as HaCaT cells) and dermal fibroblasts (such as HSF cells). In CA lesions, dermal fibroblasts exhibit hyperproliferation and aberrant secretion of extracellular matrix components, which not only contributes to amyloid production but also results in excessive collagen deposition and tissue fibrosis [10]. The multifactorial nature of CA underscores the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies.

Current treatments for CA remain largely empirical with limited efficacy. Topical corticosteroids alleviate pruritus and inflammation but do not address the underlying pathology and may cause skin atrophy [11]. Oral retinoids and immunomodulators are constrained by substantial adverse effects [12,13]. Physical modalities such as laser therapy have shown inconsistent outcomes and risk of hyperpigmentation [14,15]. Importantly, the efficacy of both pharmacological and physical interventions is severely hampered by the thickened stratum corneum and dense dermal amyloid deposits, which impede drug penetration. Thus, there is a critical need for a delivery platform that can bypass these barriers and enable targeted, synergistic therapy.

Microneedle (MN) technology offers a transformative solution for transdermal drug delivery, enabling painless penetration of the stratum corneum and direct delivery of therapeutics into the dermis [[16], [17], [18]]. Dissolving MNs fabricated from hyaluronic acid (HA) and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) are particularly attractive due to their biocompatibility and controlled release capabilities [[19], [20], [21]]. Furthermore, MNs can co-deliver multiple agents, enabling combination therapies [[22], [23], [24]]. For CA management, dissolving MNs can deliver therapeutic cargos precisely to lesional dermis, promote skin tissue repair, and reduce systemic drug exposure risks.

Photothermal therapy (PTT) has emerged as a minimally invasive approach for localized skin diseases [25,26]. PTT utilizes agents that convert near-infrared (NIR) light into localized heat for targeted tissue ablation. NIR-II window (1000-1350 nm) laser offers deeper tissue penetration than conventional NIR-I [27,28]. IR1048, a NIR-II photothermal agent, exhibits excellent conversion efficiency, good biocompatibility, and low toxicity [29,30]. When delivered via microneedles, IR1048 can be precisely deposited into amyloid-rich dermis, and laser irradiation can generate sufficient heat to disrupt compact amyloid aggregates-a strategy suggested to be effective for amyloidosis [31,32]. This “destroy” strategy addresses the intractable amyloid deposits in CA.

Benefiting from deep tissue penetration, NIR-II phototheranostic materials have witnessed rapid progress recently. Tang et al. developed NIR-II-excited type-I polymer photosensitizers for efficient hypoxic tumor PDT [33]. Chen et al. reported ultrabright NIR-II probes to realize real-time single immune cell tracking in deep tissues [34]. Hu et al. designed LDH-confined photosensitizers with boosted singlet oxygen yield for combined PDT and tumor immunotherapy [35]. Zhang et al. prepared defect-engineered LDH nanosheets to treat hypertrophic cardiomyopathy via NIR-II PDT [36]. Hu et al. fabricated NIR-II responsive nanomotor microneedles for acne bacterial infection therapy [37]. Different from the above systemic therapeutic platforms, we propose IR1048-loaded dissolving microneedles for local minimally invasive skin photothermal treatment.

Following physical disruption of amyloid deposits, effective tissue regeneration is equally crucial. Asiaticoside (AS), a bioactive saponin from Centella asiatica, possesses potent wound-healing, anti-inflammatory, and anti-fibrotic properties [[38], [39], [40]]. Notably, PTT-induced thermal injury and AS-mediated tissue regulation exist two independent functional axes, which need to be clearly distinguished for mechanistic interpretation: AS exerts dual therapeutic effects rather than single post-PTT wound repair. On the one hand, AS relieves acute inflammatory damage triggered by photothermal ablation and accelerates the repair of damaged skin barriers. On the other hand, AS directly inhibits the pathological hyperproliferation and extracellular matrix over-secretion of CA-derived fibroblasts under the fibrotic microenvironment, independently blocking progressive dermal fibrosis. [41,42]. However, the precise molecular targets and underlying signaling pathways through which asiaticoside (AS) exerts its therapeutic effects against cutaneous amyloidosis (CA) progression remain incompletely defined and warrant rigorous experimental validation.

Monotherapy based on isolated NIR-II photothermal intervention is limited to removing existing amyloid deposits, with no capacity to curb the sustained hyperproliferation of dermal fibroblasts that continuously generate amyloid precursors. By comparison, single asiaticoside treatment is capable of alleviating fibrotic progression yet fails to degrade dense, insoluble amyloid aggregates trapped within thick lesional dermis. Cutaneous amyloidosis possesses two core pathological hallmarks: irreversible amyloid deposition and fibroblast-induced dermal fibrosis, which cannot be simultaneously alleviated via monotherapy. Against this dual pathological backdrop, we rationally designed a synergistic “destroy-reconstruct” therapeutic strategy that integrates PTT-mediated amyloid ablation with AS-driven anti-fibrotic tissue remodeling to achieve comprehensive lesion management.

To further enhance delivery through dense pathological barriers, we incorporated the cell-penetrating peptide octaarginine (R8) into our microneedle system. R8 has been shown to improve cellular uptake and tissue penetration by facilitating transport across cell membranes [43,44].

Building upon the mechanistic rationale outlined above, we developed a multifunctional microneedle system (AI-MN@R8) co-loaded with IR1048 and AS, modified with R8 to enhance cellular uptake. We systematically characterized the microneedles for morphology, mechanical strength, skin penetration, photothermal performance, and biocompatibility. In vitro studies elucidated cellular uptake mechanisms, photothermal cytotoxicity, and AS-mediated molecular pathways. A localized CA rat model was optimized and established to evaluate in vivo therapeutic efficacy under 1064 nm laser irradiation. Through this integrated “destroy-reconstruct” paradigm, we aim to provide a highly translatable strategy for the effective treatment of CA.

Material and methods

Reagents and materials

Polyvinylpyrrolidone K90 (PVP K90) and hyaluronic acid (HA, 10,000 Da) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Asiaticoside (AS, purity ≥ 98%) and IR1048 (purity ≥ 98%) were obtained from Bide Pharmatech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Octa-arginine (R8) was synthesized by Qiangyao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and rhodamine B (RhB) were purchased from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HP-β-CD) and acetonitrile (HPLC grade) were obtained from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) powder was purchased from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). Insulin (recombinant human) and thioflavin T (ThT) were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Congo red, thioflavin 488 (Th488), and Masson's trichrome staining kits were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). DAPI, 4% paraformaldehyde, and antifade mounting medium were obtained from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). All chemicals were of analytical or HPLC grade and used as received. Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm) was produced by a Milli-Q system (Millipore, USA).

HSF and immortalized human keratinocytes (HaCaT) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). HSF cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. HaCaT cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells at passages 3-8 were used for all experiments.

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (200 ± 20 g, 6-8 weeks) were purchased from Xiamen Fudexin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xiamen, China). Animals were housed under controlled conditions (23 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 5% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xiamen Medical College (Ethics Approval No. 20240919004).

Fabrication and characterization of microneedles

Preparation of blank microneedles and process optimization

Blank microneedles (BMN) were fabricated using a double-layer casting method. The tip matrix solution was prepared by dissolving HA (20%, w/v) and PVP K90 (10%, w/v) in ultrapure water, followed by swelling at 4 °C overnight. The base solution was prepared similarly with PVP K90 (10%, w/v). The tip solution was added to a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to fill the cavities. After removing excess solution, the mold was centrifuged again for 15 min and dried at 40 °C for 0.5 h. The base solution was then added, centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min, and dried at 40 °C for 6 h. The microneedles were carefully demolded and stored in a desiccator.

To optimize the fabrication process, an orthogonal design L9(33) was employed with three factors: HA concentration (A: 10%, 15%, 20%), PVP K90 concentration (B: 10%, 15%, 20%), and drying temperature (C: 40 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C). The quality of the fabricated microneedles was evaluated using a weighted scoring system based on four parameters: mechanical strength (40%), bubble formation (20%), needle formation rate (20%), and tip bending (20%). The scoring criteria for each parameter were as follows: For mechanical strength, the force per needle was measured using a texture analyzer. Scores of 90-100, 80-89, 70-79, 60-69, and < 60 points were assigned for forces of ≥ 0.42 N, 0.35-0.42 N, 0.28-0.35 N, 0.21-0.28 N, and < 0.21 N per needle, respectively. For bubble formation, scores of 100, 90, 80, 70, and 60 points were assigned for no visible bubbles, occasional tiny bubbles, a few small bubbles, numerous bubbles, and a large number of bubbles, respectively. For needle formation rate, scores of 100, 90, 80, 70, and 60 points were assigned for formation rates of ≥ 99%, 90-99%, 80-90%, 70-80%, and 60-70%, respectively. For tip bending, scores of 100, 90, 80, 70, and 60 points were assigned for no visible bending, slight blunting, obvious blunting, noticeable bending, and severe bending, respectively. The total score for each formulation was calculated as the weighted sum of these four parameters. The optimal formulation was determined by range analysis (R value), and the validation was performed using three independent batches.

Preparation of drug-loaded microneedles (AI-MN@R8)

For drug-loaded microneedles (AI-MN@R8), AS (2 mg/mL), IR1048 (1.2 mg/mL), and R8 (0.5 mg/mL) were added to the tip matrix solution and stirred until completely dissolved. AS (0.5 mg/mL) was also added to the base solution. The fabrication procedure followed the same double-layer casting and centrifugation protocol as described for blank microneedles. For fluorescence imaging studies, FITC (1 mg/mL) and RhB (0.5 mg/mL) were used as substitutes for AS and IR1048, respectively.

The working concentrations of AS (2 mg/mL), IR1048 (1.2 mg/mL), and R8 (0.5 mg/mL) were determined through preliminary gradient screening, with a core screening criterion that the drug concentration in the intradermal reservoir generated after microneedle dissolution matches the effective AS-H concentration adopted in cellular Western blot and functional assays. For AS, concentrations lower than 2 mg/mL failed to exert satisfactory anti-proliferative and anti-fibrotic effects on HSF cells, while concentrations exceeding 2 mg/mL triggered drug precipitation and compromised microneedle moldability; the 2 mg/mL dosage perfectly replicates the effective cellular treatment concentration within skin reservoirs. For IR1048, doses below 1.2 mg/mL could not produce sufficient hyperthermia (>45 °C) to disassemble amyloid fibrils upon laser irradiation, whereas higher concentrations caused pigment deposition, and 1.2 mg/mL was chosen to achieve the target therapeutic temperature of ∼47 °C without off-target injury. For R8, 0.5 mg/mL was selected as the moderate dosage to guarantee adequate cell-penetrating capacity without excessively elevating precursor solution viscosity, which would otherwise lead to incomplete mold filling and defective microneedle structures. Overall, these optimized concentrations balance intact microneedle fabrication, consistent in vitro and in vivo effective drug levels, robust therapeutic activity, and favorable biosafety.

Morphological characterization

The morphology of blank and drug-loaded microneedles was examined using an optical microscope (Leica ICC50 HD, Germany) and a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi FLEXSEM1000Ⅱ, Japan). For SEM imaging, microneedle arrays were cut into appropriate sizes, mounted on conductive tape, and sputter-coated with gold before observation.

Mechanical strength testing

Mechanical strength was evaluated using a texture analyzer (TA.XTplusC, Stable Micro Systems, UK). Individual microneedle arrays (5 × 5 needles) were fixed with the needles facing upward. A compression test was performed with a pre-trigger force of 0.049 N, a test speed of 0.01 mm/s, and a displacement of 640 μm. The force-displacement curves were recorded, and the force per needle was calculated by dividing the total force by the number of needles.

Zeta potential measurement

The Zeta potentials of BMN and BMN@R8 composite matrix were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments, UK). Briefly, BMN and BMN@R8 matrix samples were dissolved in ultrapure water at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter to remove large aggregates. The measurements were performed at 25 °C. Each sample was measured in triplicate, and the results were reported as mean ± SD.

Skin penetration depth assessment

SD rats were anesthetized and the dorsal hair was removed. Blank or drug-loaded microneedles were manually inserted into the skin and maintained for 10 min. The rats were then euthanized, and the skin tissue was excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (4-5 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The penetration depth was measured under an optical microscope.

Rheological characterization of MN matrix solutions

The rheological properties of the tip matrix solutions (with and without drugs) were characterized using a modular rheometer (MCR302, Anton Paar, Austria). Steady shear tests were performed at 25 ± 0.1 °C over a shear rate range of 1–1000 s−1 to evaluate viscosity changes. Dynamic oscillatory tests were conducted at an angular frequency range of 0.1-100 rad/s within the linear viscoelastic region to determine the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″). All rheological characterizations of tip matrix solutions were conducted at 25 ± 0.1 °C, consistent with the room-temperature conditions used for microneedle casting and molding described in Section 2.2.2, rather than physiological skin temperature (32 °C).

Drug distribution imaging by confocal microscopy

Fluorescently labeled microneedles (FITC for AS, RhB for IR1048) were prepared as described in Section 2.2.2. The microneedles were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica DMI8, Germany) with excitation/emission wavelengths of 488/520 nm for FITC and 561/580 nm for RhB. Z-stack images were acquired from the needle tip to the base.

Structural characterization

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded using a TENTZSCH spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) with the KBr pellet method over a wavenumber range of 4000-400 cm−1. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using an XRD-6100 diffractometer (Shimadzu, Japan) with Cu-Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA) at a scanning rate of 8°/min over a 2θ range of 5-60°. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed using a DSC 200 F3 instrument (Netzsch, Germany) under a nitrogen atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C/min from 37 °C to 300 °C.

UV-Vis-NIR Spectral analysis

The UV-Vis-NIR absorption spectra of AS, IR1048, BMN, and AI-MN@R8 solutions were recorded using a UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) over a wavelength range of 190-1100 nm. All samples were dissolved in ultrapure water or ethanol as appropriate.

Photothermal performance evaluation

Photothermal effect and concentration dependence

The photothermal performance of free IR1048 and AI-MN@R8 was evaluated using a 1064 nm continuous-wave laser (VCL1064nmM0-3.5W, Beijing Honglan Optoelectronic Technology Co., Ltd., China). Free IR1048 and dissolved AI-MN@R8 solutions were prepared at concentrations of 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 μg/mL (based on IR1048 content). Each sample (200 μL) was placed in a 96-well plate and irradiated with the laser at a power density of 1.0 W/cm2 for 10 min. Ultrapure water served as a negative control. The temperature changes were recorded in real-time using an infrared thermal imaging camera (SINGARD M45, Shenzhen Xingzhida Technology Co., Ltd., China). Thermal images were captured at 30 s intervals, and temperature-time curves were plotted for each concentration.

Photothermal cycling stability

To assess photothermal stability, AI-MN@R8 solution (30 μg/mL, based on IR1048) was subjected to three consecutive laser on/off cycles. Each cycle consisted of 10 min of laser irradiation (1064 nm, 1.0 W/cm2) followed by 10 min of natural cooling. The temperature was monitored continuously using the infrared thermal imaging camera. The maximum temperature achieved in each cycle was recorded and compared to evaluate the photostability and photothermal repeatability of the formulation.

In vitro drug release study

The in vitro release profiles of AS and IR1048 from AI-MN@R8 were investigated using Franz diffusion cells with an effective permeation area of approximately 1.8 cm2. The receptor compartment was filled with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, containing 0.5% Tween 80 to maintain sink conditions) and maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C under constant stirring at 600 rpm. AI-MN@R8 patches were placed on the donor compartment with the needle tips facing downward, ensuring full contact with the receptor medium. At predetermined time intervals (30 min, and 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48 h), 1 mL of the receptor medium was withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh pre-warmed PBS to maintain constant volume.

The collected samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter and analyzed for AS content using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and for IR1048 content using UV-Vis spectrophotometry. For AS quantification, the HPLC system (Essentia LC-16, Shimadzu, Japan) was equipped with a C18 column (WondaSil, 250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and 2 mmol/L HP-β-CD solution (24:76, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The detection wavelength was set at 205 nm, the column temperature was maintained at 35 °C, and the injection volume was 10 μL. For IR1048 quantification, the absorbance was measured at 1048 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan). The cumulative release percentage of each drug was calculated based on the standard curves established in Section 2.2.9, and the release profiles were plotted as cumulative release (%) versus time. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Biocompatibility and safety evaluation

Hemolysis assay

The hemocompatibility of blank microneedles (BMN) and drug-loaded microneedles (AI-MN@R8) was evaluated using an in vitro hemolysis assay. Fresh rabbit blood was collected from the ear marginal vein into heparinized tubes (10 IU/mL). The blood was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The red blood cell (RBC) pellet was washed three times with sterile saline (0.9% NaCl) and then resuspended in saline to obtain a 2% RBC suspension. BMN and AI-MN@R8 were dissolved in PBS to prepare serial dilutions at concentrations of 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 mg/mL. For the assay, 0.2 mL of each sample was mixed with 0.2 mL of the 2% RBC suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Saline and 1% Triton X-100 were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected. Skin barrier recovery after microneedle insertion

To evaluate the skin barrier recovery following microneedle insertion, SD rats were anesthetized and the dorsal hair was removed. Blank and drug-loaded microneedles were manually inserted into the skin and maintained for 10 min. After removal, the rats were euthanized at 0, 24, and 48 h post-insertion. Skin tissues from the insertion sites were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (4-5 μm), and stained with H&E. The skin morphology and the closure of microchannels were examined under an optical microscope to assess the recovery process.

In vivo photothermal safety assessment

To evaluate the potential thermal damage to surrounding healthy tissue during photothermal therapy, SD rats were anesthetized and the dorsal hair was removed. AI-MN@R8 patches were inserted into the skin and maintained for 5 min to allow complete dissolution. The treated areas were then irradiated with a 1064 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2) for 0, 2, 4, and 6 min. Immediately after irradiation, the rats were euthanized, and skin tissues were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with H&E. Histological analysis was performed to assess any signs of thermal injury, including epidermal necrosis, dermal edema, or inflammatory cell infiltration.

In vitro cellular studies

Cellular uptake assay

Human skin fibroblasts (HSF) were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For the uptake assay, cells were seeded in confocal culture dishes at a density of 3 × 104 cells per dish and cultured overnight to allow adhesion. To evaluate the cellular internalization efficiency, DiL (a lipophilic fluorescent dye) was used as a substitute for IR1048 due to its similar hydrophobic properties and compatibility with confocal microscopy. The cells were incubated with free DiL, DiL-loaded microneedles (DiL-MN), or DiL-loaded microneedles modified with R8 (DiL-MN@R8) at an equivalent DiL concentration of 20 μg/mL. After incubation for 1, 2, or 4 h, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with pre-cooled PBS. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed with PBS, and stained with DAPI (5 μg/mL) for 5 min to visualize nuclei. After washing, the cells were mounted with antifade mounting medium and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica DMI8, Germany). The mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell viability assay

HSF and immortalized human keratinocytes (HaCaT) were cultured in DMEM/F12 and DMEM, respectively, both supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing serial dilutions of the test formulations. For blank microneedles (BMN), concentrations ranged from 0.78 to 50 mg/mL. For AS and AS-MN, concentrations ranged from 9.0 to 600 μg/mL (based on AS). For IR1048-MN and AI-MN@R8, concentrations ranged from 3.0 to 39 μg/mL (based on IR1048). For groups requiring laser treatment, cells were irradiated with a 1064 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2) for 5 min at 4 h post-administration. After 24 h of incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 h. TUNEL apoptosis assay

HSF cells were seeded in confocal culture dishes at a density of 4 × 104 cells per dish and cultured overnight in DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS. The cells were then treated with AS, IR1048, AI-MN, or AI-MN@R8 at appropriate concentrations. For laser-treated groups, cells were irradiated with a 1064 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2) for 5 min at 4 h post-administration. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. The cells were then stained with DAPI for 5 min to visualize nuclei. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope, and the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells was calculated using ImageJ software.

EdU proliferation assay

HSF cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight in DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS. The cells were treated with Control, AS, AS-MN, IR1048, IR1048-MN, or AI-MN@R8. For laser-treated groups, cells were irradiated with a 1064 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2) for 5 min at 4 h post-administration. After 24 h of incubation, EdU working solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 3 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, and incubated with Click reaction mixture for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope, and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated using ImageJ software.

Western blot analysis

HSF cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured until reaching 80–90% confluence. Two series of cell treatments were performed separately. For drug intervention groups, cells were incubated with serially diluted AS (100, 300, 500 μg/mL) or positive pathway inhibitors (10 μM erlotinib for EGFR, 1 μM ZSTK474 for PI3K, 5 μM perifosine for AKT) for 24 h [[45], [46], [47]]. For photothermal experimental groups, cells were treated with free IR1048 or IR1048-MN at an equal IR1048 concentration of 20 μg/mL. After 12 h incubation, designated groups received 1064 nm laser irradiation for 5 min, followed by continuous incubation for another 12 h to complete the total 24 h treatment duration.

After all treatments, cells were rinsed with pre-cooled PBS and lysed with RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Cell lysates were kept on ice for 30 min, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The BCA protein quantification kit was adopted to detect total protein concentration. Equivalent protein samples (30 μg per lane) were separated via SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA-containing TBST buffer for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting EGFR, p-EGFR (Tyr1068), PI3K, p-PI3K (Tyr458), AKT, p-AKT (Ser473), GAPDH and β-tubulin (purchased from Cell Signaling Technology or Proteintech, dilution ratio: 1:1000–1:5000). After thorough washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Target protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence reagent and captured by a chemiluminescence imaging instrument (Touch Imager, Easy Bio, China). ImageJ software was utilized to quantify band gray values, and target protein expression was normalized to internal reference GAPDH or β-tubulin. All cellular experiments were conducted in biological triplicate.

Network pharmacology and molecular docking

To predict the potential molecular targets of asiaticoside (AS) in the treatment of cutaneous amyloidosis (CA), a network pharmacology approach was employed. The chemical structure of AS was obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and its SMILES format was used for target prediction via the TargetNet database (http://targetnet.scbdd.com/), with a probability threshold > 0. The obtained targets were standardized to UniProt IDs using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). CA-related disease targets were retrieved from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/), and OMIM (https://omim.org/) databases using the keyword “cutaneous amyloidosis”. The overlapping targets between AS and CA were identified using Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/). A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) with a confidence score ≥ 0.7, and visualized using Cytoscape software. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

For molecular docking, the three-dimensional structures of target proteins (EGFR, Caspase-3, Bcl2, and IGFR) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database. The protein structures were processed using AutoDock software (https://autodock.scripps.edu/) by removing water molecules and adding hydrogen atoms. The structure of AS was prepared by adding atomic charges and assigning rotatable bonds. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina, and the binding affinities were calculated. The docking results were visualized using PyMOL software (https://pymol.org/) to analyze the interaction between AS and the target proteins.

Animal studies

Establishment of the localized cutaneous amyloidosis rat model

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (200 ± 20 g) were used for model establishment. Insulin fibrils were prepared under optimized conditions: recombinant human insulin (1 mg/mL) dissolved in HCl solution (pH 2.0) was incubated at 63 °C for 6 h with continuous shaking. The formation of mature amyloid fibrils was confirmed by thioflavin T fluorescence and transmission electron microscopy. For in vivo modeling, rats were anesthetized and the dorsal hair was removed. The optimized insulin fibril suspension (1.0 mg/mL, 100 μL) was injected intradermally at multiple sites every other day for 45 days. The successful establishment of the CA model was confirmed by macroscopic observation of skin lesions (thickening, hyperkeratosis, and nodule formation) and histopathological examination, including Congo red staining (apple-green birefringence under polarized light) and thioflavin 488 fluorescence staining.

Animal grouping and treatment protocol

After successful model establishment, rats were randomly divided into eight groups (n = 5 per group): (1) Normal (healthy rats), (2) Model (untreated), (3) Clobetasol Propionate (positive control, topical application, twice daily), (4) BMN (blank microneedles), (5) AS-MN (AS-loaded microneedles), (6) IR1048-MN + Laser (IR1048-loaded microneedles with laser), (7) AI-MN@R8 (co-loaded microneedles without laser), and (8) AI-MN@R8 + Laser (co-loaded microneedles with laser). For microneedle groups, patches were applied to the lesional area with manual pressure for 15 s and maintained for 1 h to ensure complete dissolution. Treatments were administered every other day for 39 days. For laser-treated groups, 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 6 min) was performed 12 h after microneedle administration, based on the in vitro drug release profiles of IR1048-loaded microneedles.

In vivo fluorescence imaging of drug delivery

To evaluate the in-situ drug delivery efficiency, IR1048-loaded microneedles (IR1048-MN and AI-MN@R8) were applied to the lesional skin. At 15 min post-application, rats were anesthetized and imaged using a small animal in vivo imaging system (Lumina XRMS, PerkinElmer, USA) with excitation at 785 nm and emission at 815 nm. Blank microneedle-treated rats served as negative controls.

Real-time photothermal monitoring

During laser treatment, the skin surface temperature was monitored in real-time using an infrared thermal imaging camera (SINGARD M45, Shenzhen, China). Thermal images were recorded at 1 min intervals throughout the 6 min irradiation period. Temperature-time curves were plotted to evaluate the photothermal performance of IR1048-loaded microneedles.

In vitro photothermal disaggregation assay of insulin amyloid fibrils

In vitro-synthesized insulin amyloid fibrils were utilized as a substitute model for cutaneous amyloid aggregates to explore the photothermal clearance effect. Four distinct treatment groups were set up: untreated control group, 47 °C constant-temperature water bath group with 6 min incubation, free IR1048 group subjected to 6 min laser irradiation, and AI-MN@R8 group receiving identical 6 min laser exposure. After all thermal or photothermal interventions, Thioflavin T (ThT) staining was implemented, and equivalent volumes of ThT working solution were mixed with each sample. The fluorescence intensity of each mixture was subsequently measured by a microplate reader, where the detected fluorescence signal was positively correlated with the residual amount of intact amyloid fibrils, thereby quantitatively evaluating the fibril-disrupting capacity of different treatments.

Macroscopic evaluation of skin recovery

Macroscopic photographs of the lesional skin were captured at 6-day intervals throughout the 39-day treatment period using a digital camera. Skin recovery was assessed based on the resolution of erythema, nodule softening and flattening, reduction of hyperkeratosis, and restoration of normal skin texture.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

At the end of the treatment period, rats were euthanized, and skin tissues from the lesional area were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (4-5 μm). Serial sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for general morphology, Masson's trichrome staining for collagen deposition, Congo red staining for amyloid detection under brightfield and polarized light, and thioflavin 488 (Th488) staining for high-sensitivity amyloid visualization. For immunohistochemical analysis, sections were stained with Ki67 antibody to assess cell proliferation, and TUNEL staining was performed to detect apoptosis. All stained sections were imaged using a digital slide scanning system (Motic, China) or fluorescence microscope. Quantification of staining intensity and positive cell percentages was performed using ImageJ software.

Systemic safety assessment

To evaluate the potential systemic toxicity of the treatments, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected from each group at the end of the experiment. The organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with H&E. Histological examination was performed under a light microscope to assess any signs of tissue damage, inflammation, or necrosis.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 software. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of AI-MN@R8

Morphology, mechanical strength, and skin penetration

Table S1 summarizes the results of L9(33) three-factor three-level orthogonal experiments for dissolving microneedle formulation screening (n = 3). Composite score was adopted to synthetically assess needle forming quality and mechanical properties.Range analysis of the orthogonal experimental results revealed that the factors influencing the composite score of microneedles ranked in the following order: C (drying temperature) > A (HA concentration) > B (PVP K90 concentration). The optimal combination was determined to be A3B1C1, corresponding to 20 wt% HA, 10 wt% PVP K90, and a drying temperature of 40 °C. To validate the optimized formulation, seven independent batches of microneedles were fabricated under the optimal conditions (20% HA, 10% PVP K90, 40 °C). The composite scores for the seven batches were 97.2, 96.4, 98.0, 99.6, 96.8, 100.0, and 94.8, with a mean value of 97.5 ± 1.8. The consistently high scores and low batch-to-batch variability demonstrated excellent reproducibility and confirmed the reliability and stability of the optimized fabrication process.

The successful fabrication of blank and drug-loaded microneedles was confirmed by optical microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Fig. 1A–B, both blank microneedles (BMN) and AI-MN@R8 exhibited intact microneedle arrays with uniform morphology, sharp tips, and smooth surfaces. The drug-loaded microneedles displayed a darker coloration at the tips due to the presence of IR1048, indicating homogeneous distribution of the photothermal agent within the polymeric matrix. No visible bubbles or drug crystals were observed, confirming the quality of the optimized fabrication process.

The surface charge properties of the microneedle matrix were further evaluated by Zeta potential measurements. As shown in Fig. 1C, BMN exhibited a Zeta potential of −38.33 ± 1.01 mV. After the incorporation of R8 into the matrix BMN@R8, the Zeta potential significantly increased to −4.43 ± 0.17 mV. This substantial positive shift indicates that the introduction of R8 effectively neutralized the negative surface charge of the microneedle matrix, which can be attributed to the electrostatic interactions between the positively charged guanidinium groups of R8 and the negatively charged carboxyl groups of HA. The near-neutral Zeta potential of BMN@R8 confirms successful R8 incorporation and provides direct evidence for the electrostatic complexation between R8 and HA within the microneedle matrix.

Mechanical strength is a critical parameter determining the skin penetration capability of microneedles. As presented in Fig. 1D–E, the force-displacement curves demonstrated that both BMN and AI-MN@R8 exhibited sufficient mechanical resistance without fracturing under compression. The calculated force per needle was 0.45 ± 0.03 N for BMN and 0.46 ± 0.02 N for AI-MN@R8, both substantially exceeding the reported minimum force required for skin penetration (0.058 N per needle). No significant difference was observed between the two groups, indicating that drug loading did not compromise the mechanical integrity of the microneedles.

The skin penetration capability was further evaluated using H&E staining of rat skin after microneedle application. Fig. 1F and S1 showed that both BMN and AI-MN@R8 successfully penetrated through the stratum corneum and epidermis, creating clear microchannels extending into the dermis. The measured penetration depths were approximately 580 μm and 600 μm for BMN and AI-MN@R8, respectively, which is sufficient to reach the dermal layer where amyloid deposits are located. These results confirm that the HA/PVP K90-based microneedles possess adequate mechanical strength to overcome the skin barrier and enable direct delivery of therapeutic agents to the target site.

Drug distribution and structural integrity

The spatial distribution of AS and IR1048 within the microneedles was visualized using confocal laser scanning microscopy with FITC-labeled AS and RhB-labeled IR1048. As shown in Fig. 1G, green fluorescence (FITC) was uniformly distributed throughout the entire microneedle structure, from the tip to the base, indicating homogeneous dispersion of AS within the polymeric matrix. In contrast, red fluorescence (RhB) was predominantly localized at the needle tips, with minimal signal detected in the base layer. This spatial separation aligns with our design strategy, where the photothermal agent IR1048 is concentrated at the tips to maximize local photothermal effect upon laser irradiation, while AS is distributed throughout to provide sustained pharmacological activity following microneedle dissolution.

The physical state and molecular interactions within the microneedle matrix were characterized by FT-IR, XRD, and DSC. FT-IR analysis (Fig. 1H and S2) revealed characteristic absorption peaks of HA (3400 cm−1, O-H stretch; 1616 cm−1, C=O stretch) and PVP K90 (1655 cm−1, lactam C=O stretch) in both BMN and AI-MN@R8. In the drug-loaded formulation, additional peaks corresponding to AS (1678 cm−1, ester carbonyl) were observed, confirming successful drug incorporation. Notably, the absorption bands in the 1650-1530 cm−1 region exhibited broadening and red-shifting, likely due to electrostatic interactions between the positively charged R8 and the carboxyl groups of HA, as well as hydrogen bonding among the components.

XRD analysis (Fig. 1I and S3) showed that AS and IR1048 in their pure forms exhibited sharp characteristic diffraction peaks, indicating high crystallinity. However, these crystalline peaks were completely absent in the AI-MN@R8 pattern, which instead displayed a broad amorphous halo similar to that of BMN. This indicates that both drugs were molecularly dispersed within the HA/PVP K90 matrix in an amorphous state. DSC analysis (Fig. 1J) further supported this finding, as the endothermic melting peak of AS at approximately 245 °C disappeared in the AI-MN@R8 thermogram, confirming the transition from crystalline to amorphous state. The amorphous state is advantageous for transdermal delivery as it typically enhances drug solubility and dissolution rate, facilitating rapid release upon skin insertion.

UV-Vis-NIR absorption spectra of AS, IR1048, BMN, and AI-MN@R8 were recorded over a wavelength range of 190-1100 nm (Fig. S4). BMN exhibited no characteristic absorption peaks across the entire spectrum, excluding optical interference from the matrix materials. AS showed a sharp characteristic absorption peak at 205 nm, consistent with its molecular structure. Free IR1048 displayed a strong NIR-II absorption peak at 1043 nm, which matches the excitation window of the 1064 nm laser used in this study. After co-loading into AI-MN@R8, two distinct absorption peaks were simultaneously observed at 205 nm (AS) and 1040 nm (IR1048). The slight blue shift of approximately 3 nm for the IR1048 peak suggests minor dielectric constant modulation by the polymeric matrix but does not indicate any structural alteration. These results confirm that both AS and IR1048 were successfully and stably co-loaded into the microneedle system while retaining their respective optical properties.

Rheological characterization of the MN matrix solutions was performed to assess processability. As shown in Fig. S5, both blank and drug-loaded tip solutions exhibited typical shear-thinning behavior, with viscosity decreasing as shear rate increased, facilitating mold filling during centrifugation. Dynamic oscillatory tests showed that the loss modulus (G″) consistently exceeded the storage modulus (G′) across the angular frequency range, indicating predominantly viscous behavior that enables complete cavity filling while maintaining sufficient structural integrity for demolding. Drug loading did not significantly alter the rheological properties, confirming good compatibility between the therapeutic agents and the HA/PVP K90 matrix.

Collectively, the characterization results demonstrate that the AI-MN@R8 microneedles were successfully fabricated with optimal mechanical properties, sharp tips, and sufficient skin penetration capability. The homogeneous dispersion of AS throughout the microneedle matrix, combined with tip-localized IR1048, enables a dual-action therapeutic strategy: photothermal ablation mediated by the concentrated IR1048 at the tips, followed by sustained pharmacological modulation by AS upon microneedle dissolution. The conversion of both drugs to an amorphous state within the polymeric matrix, driven by hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, likely contributes to their rapid release upon contact with interstitial fluid. The incorporation of R8 further enhances cellular uptake through electrostatic interactions with cell membranes, potentially improving the intracellular delivery of both therapeutic agents. These favorable physicochemical and mechanical properties establish a solid foundation for the subsequent in vitro and in vivo evaluation of the AI-MN@R8 platform.

Photothermal performance, In vivo drug release and safety of AI-MN@R8

Photothermal effect and stability

The photothermal properties of free IR1048 and AI-MN@R8 were evaluated under 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2). As shown in Fig. 2A–B, both free IR1048 and AI-MN@R8 exhibited concentration-dependent temperature elevation. At a concentration of 60 μg/mL, free IR1048 reached a maximum temperature of approximately 64 °C after 10 min of irradiation, while AI-MN@R8 achieved a comparable or slightly higher temperature under the same conditions. In contrast, the control group (ultrapure water) showed only minimal temperature increase (approximately 6 °C), confirming that the observed photothermal effect was attributable to IR1048. Notably, the nearly identical heating profiles between free IR1048 and AI-MN@R8 indicate that encapsulation within the microneedle matrix did not compromise the photothermal conversion capability of IR1048, which is essential for maintaining therapeutic efficacy after formulation.

Photothermal stability is a critical parameter for practical therapeutic applications. Fig. 2C shows the temperature evolution of AI-MN@R8 (30 μg/mL) over three consecutive on/off cycles of laser irradiation. The maximum temperature achieved in each cycle remained highly consistent, with no significant attenuation observed across the three cycles. This excellent photothermal stability suggests that IR1048 is well-protected within the polymeric matrix, preventing photobleaching or thermal degradation during repeated laser exposure. Such stability ensures reliable and reproducible photothermal effects during multiple treatment sessions.

As shown in Fig. 2D, the infrared thermal images visually confirmed the concentration-dependent photothermal effect. At 60 μg/mL, the AI-MN@R8 group exhibited a bright yellow-red thermal signal with a maximum temperature of approximately 64 °C after 10 min of irradiation, comparable to the free IR1048 group. The control group showed a uniform dark blue thermal image with minimal temperature elevation. The intermediate concentration groups displayed progressively increasing thermal signals from blue-green to yellow-red, consistent with the quantitative temperature-time curves. These visual observations further substantiate that encapsulation within the microneedle matrix does not impair the photothermal conversion capability of IR1048.

The photothermal conversion efficiency (η) was calculated based on the cooling curves following laser irradiation. As presented in Fig. 2E–F, the cooling data for both free IR1048 and AI-MN@R8 showed good linear fitting (R2 = 0.99 and 0.96, respectively), validating the reliability of the calculation. The calculated η values were 29.53% for free IR1048 and 33.58% for AI-MN@R8. The slightly higher efficiency observed for AI-MN@R8 may be attributed to the HA/PVP K90 matrix, which acts as a dispersing agent to prevent IR1048 aggregation in aqueous solution, thereby minimizing fluorescence quenching and enhancing photothermal conversion. This enhanced efficiency is advantageous for achieving effective hyperthermia with lower laser power or shorter irradiation times.

In vivo dissolution and drug release behavior

The in vivo dissolution behavior of microneedles was evaluated by observing morphological changes at different time points after skin insertion. As shown in Fig. 2G–I, both blank microneedles and AI-MN@R8 exhibited rapid swelling and dissolution upon contact with subcutaneous interstitial fluid. At 1 min post-insertion, the originally sharp needle tips began to swell and blunt. At 3–5 min, the needle body significantly shortened, with more than half of the needle structure dissolved. By 10 min, the needle body had almost completely dissolved, leaving only small protrusions on the base. At 20 min, the needles were fully dissolved and merged with the base layer.

Quantitative analysis of the residual needle length (Fig. 2I) confirmed that both BMN and AI-MN@R8 achieved approximately 80% dissolution within 10 min and complete dissolution within 20 min. No significant difference was observed between BMN and AI-MN@R8, indicating that drug loading did not affect the dissolution rate. This rapid dissolution behavior ensures that the encapsulated therapeutics are efficiently released into the dermis within a short time frame after MN application.

The in vitro release profiles of AS and IR1048 from AI-MN@R8 dissolving microneedles were characterized using Franz diffusion cells (Fig. 2J and K). Free AS and free IR1048 exhibited rapid passive diffusion; free AS reached ∼90% cumulative release at 12 h, whereas free IR1048 was almost fully released within 2 h.

Without laser irradiation, AI-MN@R8(AS) and AI-MN@R8(IR1048) presented remarkable sustained release. At 12 h, their cumulative release values were only ∼55% and ∼52%, which slowly increased to ∼92% and ∼89% at 48 h, respectively. This sustained delivery behavior may be attributed to the dense polymeric matrix of HA/PVP K90 after hydration and electrostatic complexation between cationic R8 peptide and negatively charged cargos.

After 1064 nm laser irradiation at the 13th hour, both MN groups displayed markedly accelerated drug release. The 13 h laser timepoint was determined by preliminary release tests. After microneedles fully dissolve and form an intradermal drug reservoir, more than 50% of IR1048 is released at 12 h. This sufficient local IR1048 content guarantees effective ∼47 °C hyperthermia for amyloid ablation and pathological cell elimination upon laser irradiation. Notably, laser treatment was primarily designed to ablate amyloid aggregates and eliminate pathological HSF/HaCaT cells via ∼47 °C mild hyperthermia, rather than to boost drug release. At 24 h, the cumulative release of laser-treated formulations was significantly higher than non-irradiated groups. The photothermal effect of AI loosens the polymer matrix and dissociates R8-drug electrostatic interactions, thereby promoting rapid payload diffusion.

In summary, the AI-MN@R8 microneedle platform achieves two release modes: sustained drug delivery under physiological conditions and laser-triggered on-demand fast release, showing great potential for precisely regulated transdermal treatment.

Skin barrier recovery after microneedle insertion

The skin barrier recovery following microneedle insertion was evaluated by H&E staining of rat skin at 0, 24, and 48 h post-application. As shown in Fig. 2L, at 0 h, distinct microchannels were visible in the epidermis and dermis for both BMN and AI-MN@R8 groups. By 24 h post-insertion, these microchannels had largely closed, with no evidence of significant inflammation, edema, or tissue necrosis. At 48 h, the skin structure appeared fully restored, with intact epidermal layers and well-organized dermal collagen, comparable to untreated healthy skin. This rapid and complete recovery, consistent with previous reports on dissolving microneedles, indicates that the microneedle-induced microinjury is transient and reversible, supporting the feasibility of repeated applications in clinical settings.

Biocompatibility and safety

The hemocompatibility of the microneedle formulations was assessed using an in vitro hemolysis assay. As shown in Fig. 2M and S6, no visible hemolysis was observed in any of the BMN or AI-MN@R8 concentration groups (1.25-20 mg/mL), with the supernatant remaining clear and the RBC pellets intact, similar to the negative control (saline). In contrast, the positive control (Triton X-100) exhibited complete hemolysis with a bright red supernatant. Quantitative analysis revealed hemolysis rates below 1% for all tested concentrations, well below the internationally accepted safety threshold of 5%. These results confirm the excellent hemocompatibility of both BMN and AI-MN@R8, which is critical for transdermal delivery systems that may encounter blood capillaries upon skin penetration.

The in vivo photothermal safety was assessed by applying AI-MN@R8 to rat skin followed by 1064 nm laser irradiation for varying durations (0-6 min). H&E staining (Fig. S7) revealed no obvious thermal damage, such as epidermal necrosis, dermal edema, or inflammatory cell infiltration, even after 6 min of continuous irradiation. The skin structure remained intact throughout. The maximum temperature achieved under these conditions was approximately 47 °C, which is sufficient to induce mild hyperthermia for disrupting amyloid aggregates and inducing apoptosis in abnormal cells, while avoiding the irreversible necrosis associated with excessive heating (>60 °C). This temperature profile demonstrates that the laser parameters used in this study (1.0 W/cm2, 6 min) are safe for in vivo application and provide a suitable therapeutic window for effective photothermal therapy without collateral damage to surrounding healthy tissues.

Cellular uptake, photothermal cytotoxicity, and apoptosis induction

The DiL-MN group (without R8) showed the lowest fluorescence intensity across all time points, likely due to the physical barrier imposed by the polymeric matrix, which hindered dye release and diffusion. In contrast, the DiL-MN@R8 group displayed significantly enhanced cellular uptake, with fluorescence intensity increasing rapidly within the first 2 h and reaching the highest levels among all groups at each time point.

Quantitative analysis of mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3B) confirmed that DiL-MN@R8 achieved approximately 2.5-fold higher uptake compared to free DiL at 2 h post-incubation. This enhancement can be attributed to the cell-penetrating properties of octa-arginine (R8), a cationic peptide that facilitates membrane translocation through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged cell membranes. Furthermore, the ability of R8 to enhance drug delivery into target cells within the dense amyloid-laden dermis may overcome the spatial barriers that limit conventional topical therapies.

Photothermal cytotoxicity in HSF and HaCaT cells

Prior pathological studies have clarified the core pathogenic roles of HSF and immortalized HaCaT in CA. Overproliferative dermal HSFs continuously secrete extracellular matrix and accelerate amyloid aggregation within lesions, while damaged and overactive epidermal HaCaTs release keratin fragments that serve as the primary precursor of dermal amyloid fibrils. Therefore, controlled photothermal cytotoxicity targeting abnormal HSFs is a critical therapeutic mechanism for CA, yet non-selective thermal injury to normal HaCaTs will trigger excessive keratin release and aggravate pathological amyloid deposition. These dual risks necessitate systematic in vitro cytotoxicity evaluation of both cell lines to define a safe and effective laser treatment window.

The cytotoxic effects of various formulations on HSF and HaCaT cells were assessed using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 3C–D, BMN exhibited excellent biocompatibility across a wide concentration range (0.78-50 mg/mL), with cell viability remaining above 95% for both cell types, confirming the safety of the HA/PVP K90 matrix.

For HSF cells (Fig. 3E–F), under dark conditions, free AS, AS-MN, and AI-MN@R8 showed minimal cytotoxicity, with cell viability exceeding 80% even at the highest concentrations (600 μg/mL for AS, 39 μg/mL for IR1048), indicating that AS and the microneedle matrix are well-tolerated by fibroblasts. In contrast, laser irradiation dramatically altered the cytotoxicity profile. The IR1048-MN + Laser group exhibited concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, with cell viability decreasing to approximately 60% at 30 μg/mL (IR1048) and further declining to about 30% at the highest concentration. The AI-MN@R8 + Laser group displayed comparable cytotoxicity, with cell viability dropping to approximately 35% at the highest concentration. Notably, the free IR1048 + Laser group showed a similar photothermal effect, with cell viability reduced to approximately 25% at 39 μg/mL, confirming that encapsulation within the microneedle matrix did not compromise the photothermal efficacy of IR1048.

For HaCaT cells (Fig. 3G–H), under dark conditions, all formulations exhibited excellent biocompatibility, with cell viability remaining above 95% across all tested concentrations. Upon laser irradiation, HaCaT cells appeared slightly more sensitive to photothermal treatment than HSF cells. The IR1048-MN + Laser group reduced HaCaT cell viability to approximately 50% at 30 μg/mL, while the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group achieved approximately 45% viability at the same concentration. At the highest concentration (39 μg/mL), the free IR1048 + Laser group reduced viability to approximately 20%, the IR1048-MN + Laser group to approximately 25%, and the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group to approximately 20%, demonstrating potent photothermal cytotoxicity. This differential sensitivity between HSF and HaCaT cells highlights the importance of precise laser parameter regulation for in vivo CA treatment. Excessively long laser exposure or overheating will cause massive death of normal epidermal HaCaTs, generating abundant amyloidogenic keratin fragments and counteracting therapeutic outcomes. In our treatment protocol, we limited continuous laser irradiation to 6 min to restrict the peak local temperature to ∼47 °C, a mild hyperthermia condition that only eliminates abnormally proliferative HSFs within amyloid deposits while avoiding severe lethal damage to epidermal keratinocytes.

The selective cytotoxicity of photothermal treatment offers a therapeutic window for targeting abnormal cells within the amyloid-laden dermis while preserving surrounding healthy tissue. The consistent photothermal cytotoxicity between free IR1048 and microneedle-formulated IR1048 confirms that encapsulation within the HA/PVP K90 matrix does not impair the photothermal conversion capability of the agent, which is essential for maintaining therapeutic efficacy after formulation.

Consistent with cell viability data, HaCaT keratinocytes show stronger sensitivity to NIR-II PTT than HSF fibroblasts. This distinction mainly stems from two factors. On one hand, HaCaT cells maintain high basal glycolysis and high intracellular ROS levels, which are further amplified under heat stress to trigger apoptosis. HSFs rely on aerobic metabolism and rapidly upregulate HSP70/HSP90 to resist thermal damage. On the other hand, thinner cell membranes make keratinocytes more susceptible to heat injury. Notably, this differential sensitivity benefits cutaneous amyloidosis treatment. Pathogenic HaCaT cells produce amyloid precursors, while dermal HSFs sustain normal skin matrix. Moderate PTT selectively eliminates lesion-related keratinocytes with mild injury to fibroblasts, avoiding scarring and offering a safe therapeutic window for local CA therapy.

Apoptosis induction

To elucidate the mode of cell death induced by the photothermal treatment, TUNEL staining was performed to detect DNA fragmentation, a hallmark of apoptosis. As shown in Fig. 3I, minimal TUNEL-positive cells were observed in the control, AS, and AI-MN groups under dark conditions, indicating negligible spontaneous apoptosis. Upon laser irradiation, both the IR1048-MN + Laser and AI-MN@R8 + Laser groups exhibited abundant TUNEL-positive cells with bright red fluorescence, confirming the induction of apoptosis.

Quantitative analysis (Fig. 3J) revealed that the IR1048-MN + Laser group achieved an apoptosis rate of 47.3%, while the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group showed a significantly higher rate of 73.1%. The enhanced apoptosis in the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group can be attributed to the combined effect of photothermal heating and R8-mediated cellular uptake. The efficient internalization of IR1048 mediated by R8 ensures that more photothermal agent is delivered into the cytoplasm, generating localized heat that effectively triggers the apoptotic cascade upon laser irradiation.

The induction of apoptosis rather than necrosis is particularly important for therapeutic applications, as apoptosis is a programmed, non-inflammatory form of cell death that avoids triggering excessive immune reactions. This “clean” cell death mechanism is desirable for clearing pathological cells without causing collateral tissue damage or exacerbating inflammation. The high apoptosis rate achieved by AI-MN@R8 + Laser (73.1%) demonstrates the potent pro-apoptotic effect of this synergistic strategy, supporting its potential for effective lesion clearance in CA.

Asiaticoside suppresses HSF proliferation and migration via EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway

Inhibition of proliferation and migration

The effect of asiaticoside (AS) on HSF cell proliferation was evaluated using the EdU incorporation assay, which specifically labels cells in the S-phase of the cell cycle. As shown in Fig. 4A and S8, the control group exhibited abundant EdU-positive cells (green fluorescence), with a proliferation rate of approximately 30%, reflecting the inherent proliferative activity of HSF cells under normal culture conditions. Treatment with free AS (500 μg/mL) and AS-MN (500 μg/mL, based on AS) significantly reduced the number of EdU-positive cells, with proliferation rates decreasing to 21% and 24%, respectively. This inhibitory effect was further enhanced in the IR1048-MN + Laser group (15%) and the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group (12%), demonstrating the combined anti-proliferative effect of photothermal treatment and AS-mediated regulation. Notably, the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group exhibited the lowest proliferation rate, indicating a synergistic effect between photothermal ablation and AS-induced growth suppression.

The migratory capacity of HSF cells was assessed using a wound healing assay. Fig. 4B and S9 shows that the control group achieved nearly complete wound closure (approximately 100%) by 48 h, reflecting the high migratory activity of HSF cells. Treatment with a low concentration of AS (50 μg/mL) did not significantly affect migration, with a healing rate of approximately 95%. However, at a higher concentration (500 μg/mL), free AS significantly inhibited migration, reducing the healing rate to 54% at 48 h. Similarly, AS-MN (500 μg/mL) exhibited a comparable inhibitory effect (65% healing rate), confirming that the microneedle-delivered AS retained its anti-migratory activity.

The inhibitory effects of AS on both proliferation and migration are mechanistically significant for CA therapy, as dermal fibroblasts are implicated in the aberrant production of amyloid components. The excessive proliferation and migration of fibroblasts contribute to the progressive accumulation of pathological deposits and tissue fibrosis. By suppressing these processes, AS may help to reduce amyloid burden and prevent disease recurrence. The enhanced inhibitory effect observed in combination with photothermal treatment suggests that the “destroy” (photothermal) and “reconstruct” (AS) strategies may work synergistically to achieve comprehensive lesion clearance.

Network pharmacology and molecular docking

To predict the potential molecular targets and pathways underlying the therapeutic effects of AS in CA, a network pharmacology approach was employed. A total of 81 overlapping targets between AS and CA were identified (Fig. S10). PPI network analysis revealed that EGFR, STAT3, CASP3, and BCL2 were among the top hub genes (Fig. S11). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated significant enrichment of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and the apoptosis pathway (Fig. S8). Molecular docking further confirmed favorable binding affinities of AS with EGFR (−4.16 kcal/mol), Caspase-3 (−4.44 kcal/mol), and Bcl2 (−9.31 kcal/mol) (Fig. S12). These in silico findings suggest that AS may exert its anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects through modulation of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling cascade and apoptosis-related proteins.

Modulation of EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling

Based on the network pharmacology predictions, we next examined the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by Western blot analysis. As shown in Fig. 4C–H, treatment with AS did not alter the total protein expression levels of EGFR, PI3K, or AKT. However, AS significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of these proteins in a concentration-dependent manner. At the highest concentration (500 μg/mL), AS reduced p-EGFR expression by approximately 60%, p-PI3K by approximately 55%, and p-AKT by approximately 50% compared to the control group. The positive control drugs (erlotinib for EGFR, ZSTK474 for PI3K, and perifosine for AKT) exhibited even stronger inhibitory effects, confirming the specificity of the signaling pathway modulation.

Notably, neither free IR1048 with laser irradiation (IR + L) nor IR1048-MN combined with laser stimulation (IR-MN + L) exerted obvious regulatory effects on the EGFR/PI3K/AKT cascade. The total and phosphorylated protein abundances of EGFR, PI3K and AKT in these two photothermal groups were comparable to those of the blank control group, demonstrating that the photothermal component IR1048 and its microneedle formulation fail to interfere with the activation of this proliferative signaling pathway.

The selective inhibition of phosphorylation without affecting total protein levels indicates that AS acts upstream of the signaling cascade, likely by interfering with the activation of EGFR at the receptor level. The EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway is a well-established regulator of cell proliferation, migration, and survival in various cell types. In fibroblasts, activation of this pathway promotes cell cycle progression, resistance to apoptosis, and enhanced migratory activity. The observed downregulation of p-EGFR, p-PI3K, and p-AKT by AS provides a mechanistic explanation for the reduced proliferation and migration observed in the functional assays.

The molecular docking results, combined with the Western blot findings, suggest a dual mechanism of action for AS in CA therapy. First, by inhibiting EGFR phosphorylation, AS disrupts the downstream PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, thereby suppressing fibroblast proliferation and migration. Second, by binding to Bcl2, AS may promote apoptosis in aberrant cells, complementing the photothermal effect. Importantly, the photothermal moiety IR1048 merely serves as an auxiliary therapeutic module without interfering with the core EGFR/PI3K/AKT inhibitory function of AS. This dual regulatory activity positions AS as a multi-target therapeutic agent capable of addressing both the cellular and molecular hallmarks of CA.

The collective findings from the EdU, wound healing, Western blot, and molecular docking analyses establish that AS exerts its anti-proliferative and anti-migratory effects through inhibition of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This molecular mechanism provides a rational basis for combining AS with photothermal therapy, where photothermal ablation “destroys” existing amyloid deposits and abnormal cells, while AS “reconstructs” healthy tissue by preventing excessive fibroblast activity and promoting proper tissue regeneration.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of AI-MN@R8 combined with laser

In situ drug delivery and photothermal monitoring

The ability of AI-MN@R8 to deliver therapeutics directly to the dermis was evaluated using in vivo fluorescence imaging. As shown in Fig. 5A, the BMN group showed no fluorescence signal, confirming the absence of background interference. In contrast, both IR1048-MN and AI-MN@R8 groups exhibited strong near-infrared fluorescence signals precisely localized to the application site at 15 min post-application. The fluorescence was confined to the lesional area with no detectable diffusion to surrounding healthy tissue or systemic circulation, demonstrating that the microneedle platform enables targeted, in situ delivery of therapeutics to the amyloid-laden dermis.

(B) Temperature-time curves during laser irradiation. (C) Infrared thermal images of skin surface during 1064 nm laser irradiation. (D) ThT fluorescence quantification of insulin amyloid fibrils after different thermal/photothermal treatments. (E) Macroscopic photographs of skin lesions over 39-day treatment period. (F) Body weight changes of rats across groups. All data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 6); ns = no significant difference (p > 0.05), *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.

Real-time photothermal monitoring was performed during laser irradiation. Fig. 5B–C shows that the BMN + Laser group exhibited minimal temperature elevation, reaching only 39.0 °C after 6 min of irradiation, confirming that the laser alone does not induce significant heating. In contrast, both IR1048-MN + Laser and AI-MN@R8 + Laser groups displayed rapid temperature increases within the first minute, reaching a plateau of approximately 47 °C after 6 min. This temperature range is optimal for photothermal therapy, as it is sufficient to disrupt amyloid aggregates and induce apoptosis in abnormal cells while avoiding irreversible thermal damage to surrounding healthy tissues. The consistent heating profiles between IR1048-MN and AI-MN@R8 confirm that co-loading with AS and R8 does not compromise the photothermal efficiency of IR1048.

Notably, the laser irradiation duration was set to 5 min for in vitro cellular assays and 6 min for in vivo rat skin treatment due to divergent heat conduction conditions. Monolayer cultured cells lose heat quickly to surrounding culture medium, so 5 min irradiation is enough to reach effective hyperthermia. In contrast, multilayer rat skin impedes heat diffusion; pre-time gradient tests confirmed 6 min irradiation was required to stabilize dermal temperature at ∼47 °C. This 47 °C mild hyperthermia window was screened via pre-experimental temperature gradients and consistent with reported mild skin phototherapy standards: temperatures below 45 °C fail to disrupt amyloid deposits, while temperatures above 48 °C induce irreversible epidermal and dermal tissue damage. 47 °C balances efficient amyloid disaggregation and minimal off-target skin injury, matching the 45–50 °C safe mild hyperthermia range widely utilized for cutaneous NIR-II phototherapeutic platforms.

As shown in Fig. 5D, ThT fluorescence intensity positively corresponds to the abundance of intact insulin amyloid fibrils. The Con group exhibited the highest fluorescence value. Compared with the Con group, the 47 °C water bath group, IR1048 + Laser group and AI-MN@R8 + Laser group all displayed significantly lower fluorescence intensity (p < 0.01). This result confirms that insulin-derived amyloid protein aggregates can be disrupted at a temperature of approximately 47 °C. Among all treatment groups, the 47 °C water bath group maintained the highest fluorescence signal, while the two photothermal groups produced even weaker fluorescence, demonstrating that IR1048-based photothermal treatment achieves stronger clearance of amyloid fibrils than constant mild heating.

Macroscopic skin recovery

Body weight monitoring throughout the treatment period (Fig. 5E) revealed steady weight gain in all groups, with no significant differences between treatment groups and the healthy control group, except for the positive control (clobetasol propionate) group, which showed slightly reduced weight gain. This observation suggests that the AI-MN@R8 platform, even with repeated laser irradiation, does not induce systemic toxicity or adversely affect overall health.

The therapeutic outcomes were evaluated by monitoring macroscopic skin changes throughout the 39-day treatment period. As shown in Fig. 5F, the Model group exhibited some spontaneous improvement over time, likely due to the inherent regenerative capacity of rodent skin, but residual hyperkeratosis and pigmentation persisted at the end of the observation period. The BMN and AS-MN groups showed moderate improvement, while the IR1048-MN + Laser group demonstrated more pronounced lesion regression. Notably, the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group exhibited the most remarkable recovery, with nearly complete resolution of skin lesions. By day 36, the skin in this group appeared smooth, with restored hair growth and normal texture, closely resembling the healthy control group.

Amyloid depletion and tissue remodeling

Histopathological analysis was performed to assess amyloid clearance and tissue regeneration. Congo red staining (Fig. 6A) revealed that the Model group retained extensive amyloid deposits in the dermis, with strong apple-green birefringence under polarized light. Quantitative analysis of Congo red-positive area (Fig. 6B) confirmed that the Model group possessed the highest proportion of amyloid deposition, which was significantly elevated compared with the Normal group. The CP positive control group achieved obvious amyloid clearance. The BMN, AS-MN, and IR1048-MN + Laser groups showed partial reduction in amyloid staining, but residual deposits remained visible, accompanied by moderately decreased Congo red-positive area relative to the Model group. In contrast, the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group exhibited near-complete clearance of Congo red-positive material, with only occasional faint staining observed. Excluding the CP positive control group, the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group displayed the lowest percentage of positive staining area among all experimental groups, indicating the effective disruption and elimination of amyloid aggregates. No significant difference was found between the Normal group and the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group (ns).

H&E and Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 6C) provided further insights into tissue architecture. The Model group displayed disorganized dermal collagen fibers with extensive amorphous deposits. In the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group, the dermis showed organized collagen bundles with a wave-like pattern characteristic of healthy skin. Masson's trichrome staining revealed increased blue-stained collagen deposition in the treated group, indicating successful tissue remodeling. Although the regenerated collagen fibers appeared slightly thinner and less densely packed than those in healthy skin, the overall architecture was substantially restored, demonstrating the tissue-reconstructive potential of the combined therapy.

Thioflavin 488 staining was adopted for high-sensitivity tracing of residual amyloid fibrils within dermal lesions, as displayed in Fig. 6D, while the corresponding quantitative fluorescence intensity statistics are presented in Fig. 6E. We extracted and calculated the overall fluorescence intensity from all stained skin slices, and performed targeted statistical comparisons between the model group and core therapeutic groups to objectively quantify residual amyloid loads across different interventions. Quantitative statistical results illustrated that the AI-MN@R8 + Laser treatment group produced a prominent decline in Thioflavin 488 fluorescence intensity relative to the untreated Model group, and the residual amyloid level after treatment was very close to that of healthy normal skin tissue. This quantitative numerical evidence solidifies our histological observation, and clearly verifies that the synergistic photothermal and asiaticoside-based therapeutic strategy can effectively break the characteristic β-sheet spatial structure of amyloid fibrils, and ultimately realize efficient clearance of pathological amyloid aggregates accumulated in the dermis.

Suppression of abnormal proliferation and apoptosis

Ki67 immunohistochemistry (Fig. 7A) was performed to evaluate cell proliferation in the lesional skin. The Model group exhibited numerous Ki67-positive cells in both the epidermis and dermis, reflecting the hyperproliferative state characteristic of CA. Quantitative statistics of Ki67-positive cell ratio (Fig. 7C) further verified that the Model group possessed the highest proportion of proliferative cells, which was markedly higher than the Normal group. The BMN group maintained a high Ki67-positive cell ratio comparable to the Model group with no significant improvement (ns). Only the CP positive control, AS-MN, IR1048-MN + Laser and AI-MN@R8 + Laser groups significantly reduced the proportion of proliferative cells relative to Model. Notably, the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group showed a marked reduction in Ki67-positive cell ratio with the lowest proliferation level among all treatment groups, indicating effective suppression of abnormal fibroblast proliferation. This finding aligns with the in vitro results demonstrating AS-mediated inhibition of HSF proliferation via the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway.

TUNEL staining (Fig. 7A) was performed to assess tissue apoptosis, and the corresponding TUNEL-positive area percentage was quantified in Fig. 7D. The Model group exhibited extensive TUNEL-positive staining area, which corresponded to severe scattered apoptotic cells and reflected sustained pathological cell injury in lesions. Consistent with the staining images, quantitative data confirmed the Model group had the largest TUNEL-positive area (p < 0.01 vs. Normal). While each intervention group lowered the apoptotic area to varying degrees, the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group achieved the minimal TUNEL-positive area percentage, showing a significant decline compared with the Model group (p < 0.01). These results indicated that the combined therapy successfully resolved the underlying cellular abnormalities and relieved pathological apoptosis. The low background of apoptosis in the AI-MN@R8 + Laser treated group suggests that the photothermal effect selectively clears pathological amyloid-associated cells without triggering widespread unregulated cell death in normal tissue.

Systemic safety assessment

To evaluate potential systemic toxicity, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected and stained with H&E. As shown in Fig. 7B, no histopathological abnormalities, including necrosis, inflammation, or structural disruption, were observed in any of the treatment groups compared to the healthy control group. The normal architecture of these vital organs confirms that the AI-MN@R8 platform, including the microneedle components, IR1048, AS, and repeated laser irradiation, does not induce systemic toxicity or off-target effects.

Molecular mechanism of AI-MN@R8 in vivo

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of AI-MN@R8 in the localized CA rat model, Western blot analysis was performed on skin tissues harvested from the lesional area at the end of the 39-day treatment period. Key proteins involved in apoptosis regulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and epidermal structure maintenance were examined.

Regulation of the bax/bcl-2 ratio to intervene in apoptosis initiation

The dense amyloid deposits in CA can induce cellular cytotoxicity and trigger apoptosis. Bax and Bcl-2 are key regulatory proteins that determine cell fate, and their ratio is a central indicator of apoptosis initiation. As shown in Fig. 8A, compared with the Normal group, the Model group exhibited a significant increase in pro-apoptotic Bax expression and a marked decrease in anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 expression, resulting in a dramatically elevated Bax/Bcl-2 ratio (p < 0.01), confirming the presence of strong apoptotic signals in the lesional skin. After AS treatment, this ratio was significantly reduced compared with the Model group (p < 0.05). Notably, in the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group, the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio decreased most remarkably to near-Normal levels (p < 0.01 vs. Model group). These results demonstrate that AI-MN@R8 effectively modulates the local CA microenvironment, exerting a clear cytoprotective effect at the initiation stage of the apoptotic signaling pathway.

Regulation of Caspase-3 activation

To verify the intervention of AI-MN@R8 in the execution phase of apoptosis, we examined the expression levels of total Caspase-3 and its activated cleaved form (Cleaved Caspase-3). As shown in Fig. 8B–C, the Model group exhibited decreased total Caspase-3 expression and significantly elevated Cleaved Caspase-3 expression (p < 0.01), indicating that cells in the lesional area had entered the irreversible terminal execution phase of apoptosis. Although the AS monotherapy group partially reduced Caspase-3 activation, the regulation was incomplete. Compared with the Model group, the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group exhibited strongly inhibited Cleaved Caspase-3 expression (p < 0.01), and total Caspase-3 levels were restored toward baseline. Combined with the Bax/Bcl-2 results, these findings demonstrate that the AI-MN@R8 treatment effectively blocked abnormal apoptosis at both the initiation and execution stages.

Regulation of COL1A1 and COL3A1 expression to remodel dermal matrix

Long-term deposition of amyloid proteins in the dermis induces persistent foreign body reactions and chronic inflammation, prompting fibroblasts to over-secrete extracellular matrix components, leading to severe tissue fibrosis. We examined the expression levels of COL1A1 and COL3A1, the main collagen components in the dermis. As shown in Fig. 8D–E, the Model group exhibited significantly upregulated protein levels of both COL1A1 and COL3A1 compared with the Normal group (p < 0.01), confirming that the microenvironment of the lesional area had shifted toward collagen densification and matrix stiffening. The AS group showed partial inhibition of collagen production, but the effect was limited. After combined intervention with AI-MN@R8 + Laser, the expression levels of COL1A1 and COL3A1 were significantly downregulated compared with the Model group (p < 0.01). This confirms that AI-MN@R8 effectively blocks the continuous secretion of dense collagen, inhibits skin microenvironment fibrosis, and promotes the remodeling of the dermal microenvironment toward physiological homeostasis.

Regulation of KRT5 and KRT14 expression to restore epidermal structure

The classical "fibrillar protein origin theory" suggests that excessive apoptosis of epidermal keratinocytes is a core trigger of localized CA. Apoptosis leads to the denaturation and degradation of the cytoskeletal proteins KRT5 and KRT14, which then deposit in the dermis. Additionally, long-term pathological stimulation induces acanthosis and hyperkeratosis in the local epidermis, manifesting as an abnormal surge in total keratin content in the lesional tissue. As shown in Fig. 8F–G, the Model group exhibited significantly higher expression levels of KRT5 and KRT14 than the Normal group (p < 0.01), confirming that local stimulation induced excessive proliferation of epidermal cells. After drug intervention, the AS group showed some inhibition of keratin overexpression. In the AI-MN@R8 + Laser group, the protein expression levels of KRT5 and KRT14 were further significantly downregulated (p < 0.01), approaching the baseline levels of the Normal group. Combined with the anti-apoptotic mechanism analysis, this result demonstrates that the AI-MN@R8 system inhibits keratin production by suppressing upstream apoptotic signals, thus not only completely cutting off the production pathway of amyloid proteins at the source but also effectively eliminating epidermal hyperplasia.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a dual-action microneedle platform (AI-MN@R8) co-loaded with the NIR-II photothermal agent IR1048 and asiaticoside (AS) for the synergistic treatment of cutaneous amyloidosis (CA). The microneedles exhibited excellent mechanical strength, rapid dissolution, and superior photothermal conversion efficiency (33.58%) under 1064 nm laser irradiation. The incorporation of the cell-penetrating peptide R8 significantly enhanced cellular uptake, while AS suppressed HSF proliferation and migration through inhibition of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. In a localized CA rat model, AI-MN@R8 combined with laser irradiation achieved targeted dermal delivery, precise temperature control (approximately 47 °C), and near-complete amyloid clearance, as confirmed by Congo red and Thioflavin 488 staining. Western blot analysis of skin tissues further revealed that the treatment regulated the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, inhibited Caspase-3 activation, downregulated fibrosis-related proteins (COL1A1/COL3A1), and restored epidermal keratin expression (KRT5/KRT14), effectively reversing the fibrotic and apoptotic microenvironment. No systemic toxicity was observed in major organs. This “destroy-reconstruct” strategy offers a safe, efficient, and clinically translatable approach for CA treatment. Future studies should focus on long-term safety evaluation and exploration of additional molecular targets to facilitate clinical translation.
