Feasibility, acceptability, and initial outcomes of a psychological adjustment and reintegration program for transitioned military veterans
Authors: Madeline Romaniuk, Elise Saunders-Dow, Kelly Brown, Philip J Batterham
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReintegration and adjustment to civilian life after military service is crucial for veterans’ mental and physical health. However, there is a lack of evidence-based interventions in Australia that specifically address the psychological and cultural factors associated with improved adjustment and mental health in this group. The aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and initial outcomes of a novel group intervention program, developed to address this gap in supports and services for Australian veterans.MethodsA non-controlled, within-subjects longitudinal design was employed with a feasibility framework including assessment of demand, implementation, integration, experience, perceived effectiveness, and burden or discomfort. The program included eight weekly 2.5-hour group sessions using cognitive-behavioural and acceptance and commitment therapy techniques. Participants included 24 transitioned veterans who completed the Military-Civilian Adjustment and Reintegration Measure, Quality of Life Enjoyment and Satisfaction Questionnaire–Short Form, Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21, and PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 at pre-intervention, post-intervention, and three-month follow-up.ResultsHigh participant ratings of engagement, experience and usefulness of the program were found, as well as perceived improvement in adjustment to civilian life as a result of the program. Sound program retention (82.8%), completion (87.5%), and manual adherence rates (89.6%) were also found. Significant improvements were found in adjustment and reintegration scores from pre-intervention to post-intervention (p = < 0.001) and from pre-intervention to follow-up (p = < 0.05), with large effect size (ηp2 = 0.344). All participants who completed the program reported they would recommend the program to another veteran.ConclusionThis study demonstrated the feasibility and acceptability of a novel group intervention program designed for military veterans in Australia. The program showed promising initial evidence of effectiveness in improving adjustment and reintegration to civilian life and provides an essential first step towards addressing a substantial gap in services for veterans struggling to adjust to civilian life. Permanent separation from military service and subsequent adjustment and reintegration to civilian life is a challenge that all veterans must navigate at some point. While some veterans are able to traverse this process autonomously without concerns, a substantial proportion experience difficulties and require support [1]. Prior research has found high rates of reintegration difficulties including up to 44% among U.S veterans [2], up to 42% among Canadian veterans [3] and between 48% and 78% among Australian veterans [4, 5]. Difficulties with reintegration and adjustment also appear to persist long-term if the veteran is not supported, with research demonstrating a high proportion of veterans continue to experience problems up to eight years after separating from service [4, 6]. Military transition research has also shown that an overwhelming majority of veterans report interest in support services for adjustment post-separation [1, 7, 8].

Difficulties with adjustment and reintegration are associated with poorer outcomes in mental health [5], social and family relationships [1, 9–11], employment and financial security [1,2,12), physical health [12] and stable housing [13, 14]. In Australia, military veterans adjusting to civilian life are twice as likely to report high psychological distress and have significantly higher mental health symptoms compared to serving military members [16.17]. Distress levels among this group have also shown to be almost three times higher than the general Australian population [15]. Suicidality has also been shown to be elevated among veterans in Australia, U.S. and UK [16–18], with documented suicide rates shown to be significantly higher among veterans when compared to both serving members and the general population [19]. Evidently, supporting veterans through the adjustment and reintegration process is vital, but programs and interventions intended for this purpose should be informed by evidence regarding the key factors that contribute to this process.

For more than a decade there has been recognition that military-civilian reintegration and adjustment encompasses more than practical challenges and includes social, psychological, and cultural factors [20–27]. A systematic review synthesizing a portion of this literature, including eighteen qualitative and mixed-methods studies, found that among those with difficulties adapting to civilian life, reintegration was marked by a pervasive sense of loss of culture and community, identity, and purpose [28]. The review found that in transitioning to civilian life, veterans experienced a distinct lack of structure, support, and camaraderie – all important aspects of their military service. In addition, prior to separation, military service was perceived as engrained into parts of members’ identities, making it difficult to achieve a sense of self in a civilian context. Veterans’ military service was also regarded with a strong sense of contribution, cause, and accomplishment, which by comparison, diminished motivation or purpose engaging in employment or activities in civilian life post-service. The study emphasized that psychological and cultural aspects of reintegration play a significant role in overall adjustment, a finding that has been repeated in subsequent research [29–31].

Extending upon the findings of the systematic review, a mixed-methods study investigated the experience of transition in order to empirically determine factors that contribute to healthy adjustment and reintegration to civilian life [5]. This research included a large qualitative study of the lived experience of transition among Australian Defence Force (ADF) veterans (n = 100), as well as a separate quantitative survey of ADF veterans (n = 725). The study employed structural equation modelling to determine a model of psychological adjustment and cultural reintegration via confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Results indicated that of the initial themes developed in the qualitative component of the study, five factors contributed most to overall adjustment and reintegration; purpose and connection, help seeking, negative beliefs about civilians, resentment and regret, and problematic regimentation [5]. Results also revealed that lower scores on each of these factors (i.e. more difficulties in that area) were significantly associated with higher mental health symptoms, lower quality of life, reduced functioning and poorer self-reported adjustment overall [5].

Subsequent research has reiterated the importance of these psychosocial and cultural factors [30]. Flack and Kite [32] investigated the role of social connectedness and identity on wellbeing among transitioned veterans. They found social connectedness mediated the relationship between military identity and wellbeing, and that a particularly ingrained military identity was negatively related to social connectedness. A similar finding was described in a recent qualitative study of social group engagement and identity among veterans [29]. The authors found that while social support is an important aspect of adaptive transition, reconciling the military identity when re-engaging with civilian groups may be challenging, and a source of conflict [29]. Additionally, Tarbet and colleagues [30] conducted a qualitative interpretive meta-synthesis of reintegration of veterans from the USA and UK. They constructed nine overall themes which also included loss of identity, loss of purpose and resentment towards civilians [30]. Further, factors related to military culture, beliefs about civilians, and help seeking have also subsequently been established to play a crucial role in transition and healthy adjustment [33, 34].

Collectively this research suggests there is an opportunity to develop and provide psychosocial interventions targeting these factors to better support transitioned veterans. Despite this, there remain limited services in Australia offering evidence-based programs to address psychological and cultural factors. While some programs promoting psychosocial transition support do exist currently, including online coaching and brief education workshops, they do not comprehensively address relevant transition factors and/or are not evidence-based interventions [35, 36]. Further abroad, a variety of psychosocial approaches have been identified to enhance the military-civilian transition for U.S veterans [37]. These include peer support groups, parenting and family support programs, engagement with veteran service organizations, employment and education-focused initiatives [37]. Additionally, a systematic review of well-being interventions for adjustment to civilian life highlighted several U.S programs that focus on expressive writing, psychological therapies, resilience building, and fostering connections in relationships [38]. While a number of these programs demonstrated positive outcomes for veterans (such as reduced aggression, anger, and improved marital satisfaction), the effectiveness of these programs in improving adjustment and reintegration specifically does not appear to have been assessed. It is also unclear how the content of these programs might translate for veterans in other countries, and whether they specifically target the psychological and cultural factors that have been associated with improved adjustment and mental health in this group.

Aims and objectives

In order to address this gap in supports and services for veterans in Australia, a face-to-face evidence-informed and evidence-based adjustment and reintegration intervention program was developed [39]. The program was developed to address key psychological and cultural factors (including social connection, purpose, identity, help seeking, negative beliefs about civilians, unresolved resentment and regret, and problematic regimentation) and engage evidence-based psychological strategies to target difficulties in each area. This study evaluated the feasibility, acceptability, and initial outcomes of the developed program, piloted within an outpatient psychiatric hospital for military veterans.

Methods

Study design

A non-controlled, within-subjects longitudinal design was employed with a Feasibility and Acceptability evaluation framework informed by Bowen and colleagues [40] and Sekhon and colleagues [41]. Participants completed an intervention consisting of eight group sessions delivered once a week for 2.5 h each session. Psychosocial outcome measures were completed by participants at three time points: pre-intervention/baseline (week 1), post-intervention (week 8), and three-month follow-up (week 20). The intervention was delivered within the outpatient day centre of the Keith Payne Unit (KPU), a psychiatric unit for military veterans within Greenslopes Private Hospital (GPH). As described further below, feasibility of the program was assessed through indicators of demand, implementation, and integration. Acceptability was examined through indicators of experience, perceived effectiveness, and burden or discomfort.

Participants

Participants were 24 military veterans aged 27 to 65 years. A summary of participant demographic information is presented in Table 1. Inclusion criteria for enrolment were: (1) male or female separated military personnel; (2) aged 18–75 years; (3) self-reported difficulties adjusting to civilian life post-service assessed using the question: “Have you experienced difficulties adjusting to civilian life?” (4) an indication that the majority of the program would be relevant as evidenced by M-CARM scores demonstrating potential difficulties in at least three of the five M-CARM factors (i.e., help seeking, social connection and purpose, resentment and regret, beliefs about civilians, regimentation). Exclusion criteria included: hospitalised as an in-patient for a psychological condition, or presented to an emergency department with acute psychiatric symptoms within the previous four weeks. The majority of participants were male (91.7%) and had served in the Army (79.2%). Participants had served approximately 10 years on average in the ADF and had been separated from service between less than one and 42 years.

Procedure

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Department of Defence and Veterans’ Affairs Human Research Ethics Committee (DDVA HREC; number: 332 − 21). Following approval, potential participants were referred to the research team by clinical staff of the outpatient day centre of KPU at GPH. All referrals were received via the clinical staff at the KPU day centre and were generated from a specific referral by a treating psychiatrist as well as reviewing waitlists for other programs and offering the current program to those patients who had expressed interest in participating in a group program. A member of the research team contacted potential participants via telephone or email to provide further details about the study. Potential participants were provided with a copy of the Participant Information Sheet and Consent Form (PISCF) via post, email, or in-person. Potential participants were given the opportunity to ask questions and were notified in the PISCF that decisions about participation in the research would not impair any existing or foreseeable future relationship between participants and researchers or any other organizations (such as their health provider or hospital). Potential participants underwent eligibility screening to ensure they met the inclusion criteria, which was completed either over the phone, in-person, or via an online survey. The program was held weekly at GPH for eight weeks. Each program session was between 120 and 150 min in duration, totalling a maximum of 20 h. On the first and last day of the program, participants completed a series of questionnaires (listed in the measures section). Three months following the program, participants completed the measures again as well as a follow-up program questionnaire. There were five program cohorts/groups delivered, with between two and seven participants per group. As recruitment occurred in 2021 during the COVID-19 pandemic, group sizes were limited by room capacity restrictions to meet social distancing requirements at GPH.

Facilitators of the program also provided feedback via facilitator questionnaires as well as participation in a structured facilitator discussion at the conclusion of program delivery. The facilitator discussion included six participants and was guided by the principal investigator who asked specific questions in relation to acceptability and feasibility of the program (see supplementary material). The facilitator discussion ran for 60 min, and notes were taken by two members of the research team.

Intervention

Target population

The program was designed for separated military personnel who have experienced difficulty adjusting to civilian life following transition out of service. The program may be relevant for any veteran irrespective of length/type of service or time since separating from military service. While piloted within an outpatient psychiatric hospital setting, there was no requirement to have a current psychiatric diagnosis to attend this program.

Program aims

The overall aim of the program is to highlight psychological and cultural aspects that might be hindering participants’ adjustment to civilian life in order to build insight, consider impact on current adjustment, and provide an introduction and direction for future work in each area. The objective is to help participants identify and utilise new knowledge, skills and evidence-based strategies to facilitate improved adjustment. The program offers validation of past experiences while focusing on current needs and opportunities for healthy adjustment through individual reflections, group discussions and personal goal setting. The program manual [39] was developed by two doctoral-trained clinical psychologists with a combined 20 years’ experience working with the veteran community, and it was informed by research evidence identifying key factors relevant to psychological adjustment and cultural reintegration to civilian life post-service for ADF veterans [5, 28, 42].

Program content and structure

The program content focuses on core factors and domains that are associated with adjusting to civilian life after service [5]. The program covers eight sessions including: Session 1: Social Connections, Session 2: Communication Skills in Civilian Life, Session 3: Finding Purpose, Session 4: Identity, Session 5: Beliefs About Civilians, Session 6: Resentment and Regret, Session 7: Regimentation, Session 8: Help-Seeking. A session outline of the program is presented in Table 2. The program utilises a strengths-based framework, with program content and therapeutic techniques grounded in evidence-based psychological theory/therapeutic models including Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT). Both CBT and ACT have demonstrated transdiagnostic efficacy within veteran populations [43–47].

The framework aims to be person-centred, focuses on strengths, and promotes hopefulness, autonomy, and self-determination. For each session the program combines (a) psychoeducation/background of each topic, (b) group discussion, (c) personal reflection and/or written tasks, (d) intervention activities/exercises, and (e) where applicable, review of the prior week personal practice. Session content is typically structured to lead with psychoeducation and personal reflection, followed by practical exercises and implementation goals.

Facilitation & honouring lived experience

The development of this program was informed by a large qualitative study of transition out of the ADF using a phenomenological approach, the study of lived experience. While the program was developed by clinical psychologists, the use of lived experience rather than ‘clinical expertise’ heavily informed the psychoeducation and contextual background of each of the session topics. As such, facilitators do not take on the role of ‘expert’ or ‘teacher’ in these sessions and importantly view the participants as the experts in these experiences. Facilitators are encouraged to take the position of ‘reflecting back’ and checking if they hold true for the group, allowing room for correction or enrichment of the background content. A facilitation manual was also developed [48], and facilitator training was provided by the principal investigator/clinical psychologist.

Program refinement and co-design

Throughout the duration of the pilot, there was opportunity to integrate facilitator and participant feedback as program delivery occurred. Prior to commencing the delivery of the programs, a series of facilitator workshops were run by the principal investigator. These workshops offered training in the program manual content as well as aims, delivery framework and background research informing development. Facilitators were also invited to provide feedback on manual content during these workshops, and flag any aspects of the program that, in their experience, might not be suitable for the target population. Weekly supervision was also provided by program developers/clinical psychologists for the duration of program delivery. These sessions provided the opportunity to resolve any clinical issues/group dynamics arising, but also provided vital feedback regarding manual content and delivery as it occurred. In response to this feedback, the original manual underwent a series of revisions and refinements. The manual used with the first cohort was revised most heavily with specific exercises removed and new exercises added. Following this early revision, smaller refinements were made to structure or delivery method, but specific exercises remained consistent. Facilitator feedback was also integrated into the manual when possible, using suggestions collected during the structured facilitator discussion as well as feedback forms. The final program manual retained the original eight session structure and order [49].

Delivery

Two allied health clinicians facilitated each group. One facilitator was a registered psychologist from the research team, who was present at all groups. The second facilitator rotated between allied health clinicians/staff working at the day centre of the KPU and included a mental health social worker, occupational therapist, mental health nurse and two clinical psychologists. All program facilitators had experience working with the veteran community and delivering group interventions.

Measures

Military-civilian adjustment and reintegration measure (M-CARM)

The M-CARM is a 21-item self-report questionnaire assessing psychological adjustment and cultural reintegration to civilian life. The measure includes five factors demonstrated to be important in healthy adjustment to civilian life following military service, among Australian veterans. Higher scores indicate better adjustment and reintegration. The measure has strong psychometric properties [5]. Cronbach’s Alpha was 0.80 in this sample.

Quality of life enjoyment and satisfaction questionnaire – short form (Q-LES-Q-SF)

The Q-LES-Q-SF is a 14-item self-report measure designed to assess an individual’s subjective experience of life enjoyment and satisfaction. Respondents rate each item along a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (Very poor) to 5 (Very good) according to how satisfied they were in the item domain over the previous week, with higher scores indicating greater quality of life, satisfaction and enjoyment (range 14–70). Previous research has indicated the questionnaire has excellent psychometric properties [50]. Cronbach’s Alpha was 0.91 in this sample.

Depression anxiety stress scale-21 (DASS-21)

The DASS-21 [51] is a 21-item self-report questionnaire that assesses the degree of severity of depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms. The scale has demonstrated strong psychometric properties and is routinely used in research and clinical practice [51–53]. Cronbach’s Alpha was 0.91 in this sample.

PTSD checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5)

The PCL-5 [54] is a 20-item self-report questionnaire that assesses the presence and severity of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) symptoms. The PCL-5 has demonstrated strong psychometric properties [55, 56] and is used regularly for monitoring symptom change over time, as well as screening individuals for a provisional diagnosis of PTSD. Cronbach’s Alpha was 0.92 in this sample.

Post-program and 3-month feedback questionnaires

The post-program and 3-month follow up feedback questionnaires contain questions designed specifically for this study to examine participants’ opinions and experiences regarding the developed reintegration program, express their satisfaction or dissatisfaction about aspects of the program, as well as answer open-ended questions about the program and potential impact. Both questionnaires were administered once.

Facilitator feedback questionnaire

The facilitator feedback questionnaire contains questions designed specifically for this study to examine facilitator opinions of the program in relation to appropriateness of content, pace, engagement of participants, as well as express their satisfaction or dissatisfaction about aspects of the program and provide suggestions on how the program might be revised/improved. Facilitators completed the questionnaire within a week of each program conclusion.

Manual adherence checklist

The adherence checklist was developed specifically for this study to record adherence to the program manual by facilitators. For each session of every program, facilitators indicated from the listed manual content whether each topic/section was ‘not present’, ‘partially present’, ‘mostly present’, or ‘fully present’.

Analysis

The assessment of feasibility and acceptability used a combination of process variables as well as self-report measures including feedback questionnaires examining experiences, satisfaction with the program, and perceived effectiveness. After data screening, frequencies and descriptive statistics of demographic and military service variables were conducted. One-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc analysis was conducted to compare participants’ scores on outcome measures across data collection time points. ANOVA assumptions were checked prior to running analyses. Numerical ratings on the post-program feedback questionnaire averaged across all respondents.

Results

Feasibility

Demand

A total of 42 unique referrals to the program were received, and 19% declined to be screened (n = 8). At the screening stage, 14.7% (n = 5) were excluded for either not meeting eligibility criteria (n = 2) or being unable to attend the program at the scheduled time (n = 3), see participant enrolment flow chart presented in Fig. 1. Following request from the GPH clinical team, the eligibility age criteria was increased from 70 to 75 years of age due to potential participant interest from this age bracket. The proportion of eligible participants enrolling in the study was 100%, as all those screened as meeting eligibility criteria subsequently enrolled in the program. During the facilitator discussion it was generally agreed by GPH staff responsible for recruitment of all KPU day programs, that the pilot program had demonstrated sufficient demand to justify offering it routinely at least twice yearly.

Participant enrolment flowchart

Implementation & integration

The program retention rate was 82.8%. Of the 29 participants who commenced the program, 13.8% (n = 4) withdrew from the program and one dropped out (3.4%). A participant was considered withdrawn if they informed the team of their intention to stop attending the program and provided a reason, versus ‘dropped out’ which meant they stopped attending the program but did not contact the research team or provide a reason. Reported reasons for withdrawal included; not ready for a group setting, too much to consider at the current time, already knew information, and prioritising inpatient care for existing conditions. The proportion of completed sessions across all participants (intervention adherence rate) was 87.5%, with 45.8% completing the program in full. The manual adherence rate across all 5 groups was 89.6%, which included facilitators rating each topic of each session as ‘fully’ or ‘mostly’ present. An average of 3.6% of manual content was rated as ‘not present’ and 6.8% rated as ‘partially present’ by facilitators.

Rates of attendance by session are provided in supplementary material, with an average of 7 out of 8 sessions attended. Across all cohorts, program session 6 ‘Resentment and Regret’ and session 7 ‘Regimentation’, had the highest non-attendance frequency. As participants were provided with the program manual of all sessions at the start of the program, it may be possible that these topics were seen as potentially most difficult to complete, and therefore avoided most frequently. While participants who missed these sessions gave no specific feedback that non-attendance was a conscious decision based on session content, the ‘Resentment and Regret’ session was in fact reported as the most difficult part of the program by a number of participants in the qualitative feedback.

The referral pathway into this program aimed to replicate the existing process for programs delivered at the day centre of KPU. According to the structured facilitator discussion, clinical staff reported this referral process was efficient, and the program generally well-integrated within existing GPH resources and facilities. It was noted however, that depending on frequency, additional staffing may be required if the program was offered routinely.

Six GPH clinicians who were external to the research team completed feedback forms regarding their experience facilitating the program. The feedback forms involved providing ratings to a number of questions on a Likert scale from 1 to 10 (1 = not at all − 10 = extremely). On average, the facilitators rated the appropriateness of the program content for the audience 8 out of 10. On average, they rated the appropriateness of the pace of the intervention 7 out of 10, with qualitative feedback indicating that more time for each session would be useful. Additionally, facilitators were asked if they had any difficulty learning and delivering the program’s content which resulted in an average rating of 2 out of 10, indicating low difficulty. During the facilitator discussion, facilitators commented the program felt like it filled a gap in relation to current programs and services offered at GPH. Facilitators explained that topics in the piloted program arise repeatedly during existing programs (such as negative beliefs about civilians) but are only part of peripheral discussions and not addressed or focused upon directly. There was general agreement that it felt satisfying to be able to devote a specific program to these topics and facilitate a more robust exploration of these issues with participants.

Acceptability

Experience and perceived effectiveness

Table 4 displays the results of the post-program and follow-up feedback questionnaire. Participants provided ratings to a number of questions evaluating their experience on the program on a Likert scale from 1 to 10. On average, the participants rated their overall experience on the program 8.4 out of 10 (1 = poor – 10 = excellent) and rated usefulness of the program 7.5 out of 10 (1 = not at all useful – 10 = extremely useful). Participants were also asked if they received the type of help they wanted from the program, which was rated 7.6 out of 10 on average (1 = definitely not – 10 = definitely yes). Additionally, the participants were asked to rate their enjoyment of being in a group setting, and the average response was 8.2 out of 10 (1 = not at all enjoyable – 10 = extremely enjoyable). Furthermore, all participants reported that being in a group had a positive impact on the program.

Three months following the conclusion of the program, participants completed an additional feedback questionnaire, see Table 4. These results indicated that 58.3% of participants had used strategies or new knowledge from the program since its completion, and 66.7% reported that as a result of the program, they had sought, or planned to seek, additional professional support to improve their adjustment to civilian life. Further, 75% of participants reported the program prompted them to make changes in their life. Qualitative examples of reported changes are presented on Table 5, and fell within the topics of ‘reduced regret/blame’, ‘shifted beliefs about civilians’, ‘seeking help’, ‘changed perspective’, ‘self-reflection’ and ‘feeling better/more calm’.

Finally, on a scale of 1 to 10 (1 not engaged at all − 10 extremely engaged), program facilitators rated overall level of participant engagement as 8 out of 10 on average. During the facilitator discussion, facilitators commented on the decent levels of engagement from participants despite it being delivered in an afternoon timeslot, explaining that current programs are routinely offered in the morning due to engagement typically dropping in the afternoon. While engagement was well rated by facilitators, there was consensus that engagement would improve further if delivered in the morning and time extended to allow a slower pace and more frequent breaks for participants.

Burden or discomfort

Three enrolled participants (10.3%) withdrew from the program due to reasons related to discomfort including being in the group setting and the topics addressed. Non-attendance in the program across sessions more generally appeared to be unrelated to burden or discomfort, and instead explained by conflicting appointments/commitments, illness and other personal barriers. Feedback on the post-program questionnaire revealed that 54.2% of participants reported they experienced discomfort beyond what they would have expected on the program. Participants qualitative feedback regarding the most difficult part of the program included common ideas including ‘change’ (Learning how much I need to change), ‘being open’ (Showing feelings and expressing myself) and ‘length/amount of content’ (Large amount of content in a small amount of time), see Table 5. The feedback related to program length and amount of time was also raised by program facilitators in both feedback forms and the facilitator discussion.

Adjustment and reintegration to civilian life

A repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to examine the effect of time (pre-intervention, post-intervention, and 3-month follow-up) on participants’ scores on the M-CARM. Analysis revealed there was a significant main effect of time, F(2, 42) = 11.03, p < .001. ηp2 = 0.344. Planned comparisons of each timepoint using Bonferroni correction showed that there was a significant improvement in M-CARM scores from pre-intervention to post-intervention (p = < 0.001) and from pre-intervention to follow-up (p = < 0.05).

Exploratory psychosocial outcomes

A repeated measures ANOVA of participants’ scores on the Q-LES-Q-SF demonstrated no significant main effect, F(2, 42) = 2.89, p = .06, ηp2 = 0.121. Similarly, a series of repeated measures ANOVAs were conducted to examine changes on participants’ scores on the DASS-21 and PCL-5. No significant main effect was found on DASS subscales including Depression, F(2, 42) = 2.55, p = .09, ηp2 = 0.108, Anxiety, F(2, 42) = 2.133, p = .13, ηp2 = 0.092, and Stress F(2, 42) = 0.386, p = .68, ηp2 = 0.018 or on the PCL-5, F(2, 42) = 0.218, p = .81, ηp2 = 0.010.

Discussion

This study piloted an 8-week face-to-face group program for transition to civilian life to determine the feasibility, acceptability and impact on adjustment and reintegration among veterans who had permanently separated from military service. This study addresses a critical gap in services and supports for veterans in Australia, however the results of this study are only preliminary and form an essential first step in conducting future controlled trials of the intervention.

Feasibility of the program was assessed through indicators of demand, implementation, and integration. Demand for the program was reasonable with a steady number of referrals generated throughout the recruitment period, despite COVID-19 restrictions. Feedback provided by clinical staff at GPH confirmed that there was sufficient demand demonstrated during the pilot to suggest the program could be offered routinely as part of the existing suite of programs at GPH. The 100% recruitment rate also suggested that the eligibility criteria and screening procedures were suitable. Implementation was shown to be viable with sound program retention, completion, and manual adherence rates, comparable to general retention rates in CBT delivery for a range of mental health disorders [57]. Integration of the program into the existing GPH system was also achievable, demonstrated through use of existing referral pathways as well as utilisation of existing staff and GPH facilities to deliver the programs.

Acceptability of this program was examined through indicators of experience, perceived effectiveness, and burden or discomfort. High participant ratings related to engagement, overall experience and usefulness of the program were found, as well as perceived improvement in subjective adjustment and reintegration to civilian life as a result of the program. Burden and discomfort indicators included a withdrawal/dropout rate of 17.2%. This rate is in line with overall attrition rates found in adult psychotherapy across settings (19.7%) [58], is slightly lower than treatment dropout rates found among veterans and their families (24%) [59], and is substantially lower than dropout rates found among veterans in treatment for PTSD (36%) [60]. Interestingly, just over half of participants reported they experienced discomfort beyond what they would have expected during the program. This may be explained by the focus of the program, reintegration to civilian life, being perceived as having less potential to evoke distress compared to a trauma-focussed recovery program for instance (also offered at GPH). While already part of the current consent procedures, in future delivery or trials of the program, it may be important to provide additional details regarding session content and potential for discomfort prior to enrolment. Despite the discomfort, 100% of participants reported they would recommend the program to a fellow veteran. It is expected that psychological work carries a level of discomfort that may be required to elicit the building of insight which may contribute to action or change. The recommendation rate suggests that while discomfort may have been higher than expected, it did not inhibit perceived benefit or inflate dropout/withdrawal rates. In addition, feedback from participants indicated that three months on, the majority of participants had used strategies or new knowledge from the program, had sought or were planning to seek further support for adjustment/reintegration and had made positive changes in their life as a result of the program.

Assessing all feedback from program participants and facilitators, the most dominant area of improvement raised was in relation to the large amount of manual content to cover in a small amount of time. During supervision sessions and the facilitator discussion, it was recommended that the program structure be adjusted to match the current Department of Veterans’ Affairs (DVA) funded programs at KPU, extending each session to a ‘half day’ structure of at least 3.5 h. The facilitators agreed this allows for a slower pace, more frequently scheduled breaks for participants and more group discussion - creating a less didactic approach. Facilitators also saw value in some flexibility in the delivery approach, including participant-driven selection of exercises and activities most relevant to each cohort.

In relation to the primary target outcome of the program, results demonstrated that psychological adjustment and reintegration significantly improved from pre-intervention to post-intervention, and improvement was maintained three months following conclusion of the program. Despite the manual being revised most substantially following cohort 1 delivery, this did not appear to impact participant outcomes, as cohorts 1 and 4 experienced the largest mean improvement in adjustment and reintegration scores. Secondary or exploratory outcomes showed no significant changes to self-reported mental health symptoms. As the program did not contain any specific intervention targeting symptoms of depression, anxiety or PTSD, this finding is expected. That said, it may be worth continuing to explore mental health outcomes in future delivery or trial of the program as this study may not have been powered to observe notable changes in mental health outcomes.

Implications

This is the first evidence-informed group program in Australia designed specifically to target established psychosocial factors related to psychological adjustment and cultural reintegration to civilian life for service veterans, using evidence-based clinical and theoretical frameworks. An evidence-based adjustment and reintegration program has the potential to lessen the substantial burden of negative psychosocial outcomes in the veteran community that have been associated with transition out of military service [15, 17, 18, 61]. The results of this study are an essential first step in developing an effective, evidence-based intervention - addressing a crucial gap in services in Australia.

Limitations

There are a number of limitations of this study that may be addressed in future research. This was not a randomised controlled study, as such, quantitative outcomes are indicative of potential trends only with no comparison group included. The sample was small and comprised of predominantly male outpatients of GPH, who had served in the army, which may limit generalizability of results. It was also conducted at a single site, so feasibility findings may be only applicable to that specific site. The study also recruited from a convenience sample of selected participants with an interest in a group intervention. As such, participant bias may have occurred. That said, as the study followed existing recruitment procedures for group programs at GPH, findings are still pertinent for translation and application to real-world settings. Additionally, the group sessions were not audio-recorded so bias may have been introduced with facilitator completed adherence checklists. Outcomes were also limited to perceived psychological transition (i.e., self-report assessment) and did not include assessment of behavioural indices of transition-related psychosocial functioning. Finally, low power is usually associated with failing to detect a true effect, however, low power may also result in an over-estimation of effect size and can raise uncertainty over the variability of the estimate. As this study was underpowered, quantitative results should be interpreted with caution.

Future directions

Given that feasibility and acceptability was established, and a large effect size found for the primary outcome measure of adjustment and reintegration, there is evidence that a randomised controlled trial may be an appropriate next step. There may also be merit in piloting delivery of the program in the community, rather than hospital-based delivery, potentially creating a more accessible service to those veterans who are not existing patients of a hospital or receiving ongoing psychiatric care. Given the wide range of time since separation in the current pilot cohort, future investigations may also consider comparing cohorts of veterans based upon time since separation, to determine if this has an impact on outcomes and intervention effectiveness. Further, while there was some level of co-design with hospital staff, a specific structured co-design process with veterans would also be valuable. Additionally, there is a considerable amount of evidence indicating that the inclusion of peer support in mental health services and programs for veterans is associated with meaningful benefits [35, 62–64]. As such, exploring the inclusion of peer support on this program may be an important consideration. This could include the addition of adjunct peer support to the current program, or potentially a primarily peer-facilitated version of the program with outcomes compared in a superiority or non-inferiority trial. Finally, an economic evaluation including cost-effectiveness and cost-benefit analysis of the program, would be an important consideration in future delivery.

Conclusion

Despite substantial evidence of the debilitating impact of poor transition and adjustment on mental health and wellbeing for separated military veterans, there remains limited evidence-based services for veterans in Australia targeting established psychological and cultural factors crucial for adjustment to civilian life. This study demonstrated that a novel, evidence-informed and evidence-based skills-building group intervention for military veterans was feasible and acceptable with promising initial evidence of effectiveness in the sustained improvement of adjustment and reintegration to civilian life post-service. The study also generated data to enable the design of a randomised controlled trial in the future and provides an essential first step towards addressing a substantial gap in services for veterans struggling to adjust to civilian life.
