Changes in the gut microbiome during fasting‐induced molting and its effects on gut–liver function
Authors: Hao Zhang, Zhixuan Luo, Jun Zhang, Chenxu Wang, Yadong Tian, Donghua Li, Yujie Guo, Yanhua Zhang, Wenting Li, Yujie Gong, Xiangtao Kang, Ruirui Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFasting‐induced molting (FIM) rejuvenates the laying cycle in hens; however, the fasting process may result in intestinal dysbiosis, which compromises host immunity and disrupts the intestine‐liver function. Consequently, investigating the changes in gut microbiota during FIM and its impacts on gut‐liver metabolic homeostasis is crucial for enhancing the efficiency of FIM in laying hens. Here, a total of 90 laying hens, aged 60 weeks, were selected for the FIM. Samples were collected at four time points: the day before fasting, the 15th day of fasting, the 5th day of refeeding, and the 47th day of refeeding. Metagenomic sequencing and non‐targeted metabolomics were employed to investigate the roles of the gut microbiota and metabolites in the remodeling of gut–liver function, focusing on intestinal injury‐repair mechanisms and changes in liver function. During the fasting period, the abundance of harmful microbiota and metabolites increase, leading to intestinal injury. This process activates the TGF‐beta signaling pathway, promoting intestinal stem cell proliferation. Simultaneously, liver function dysfunction as evidenced by elevated bile acid levels in the liver and serum and activation of the non‐classical bile acid synthesis pathway. During the refeeding period, the previously observed effects were reversed, leading to the remodeling of the intestinal microbiota and gut‐liver function. We identified key microorganisms (Liquorilactobacillus mali and Tissierellia bacterium KA00581) and functional metabolites (d‐Panthenol and 3‐hydroxyanthranilic acid (3‐HAA)) involved in intestinal‐liver function during FIM. Notably, d‐Panthenol promotes chicken small intestinal organoid branching and growth, enhances barrier function, and reduces inflammation. Our study underscores the role of gut microbiota in gut‐liver injury during FIM in laying hens, suggesting potential probiotic‐ or metabolite‐based interventions to mitigate gut‐liver injury and facilitate recovery.Keywords: bile acid, fasting‐induced molting, gut microbiota, intestinal remodeling, liver function, metabolome, TGF‐beta signaling pathway

INTRODUCTION

Molting, a natural physiological phenomenon in poultry, involves feather replacement and a temporary cessation of reproduction, decreasing egg production [1]. Natural molting is typically prolonged and inconsistent, negatively affecting the maintenance of peak egg production and presenting challenges for intensive management [2]. Research has shown that penguins complete their feather molting by fasting for 2–5 weeks, maintaining optimal body conditions and feathers during breeding seasons to attract mates and protect offspring [3, 4]. Female poultry incubate their eggs without male assistance and are unable to leave the nest to forage, resulting in spontaneous anorexia [2]. During the incubation period, they enhance their resistance to hunger by reducing their activity and metabolic rates, indicating strong physiological and behavioral adaptations. Consequently, the induced molting process in chickens can be simulated by fasting to shorten the molting duration.

Fasting‐induced molting (FIM) is widely used in the egg industry because it offers a short cycle, an extended subsequent peak‐laying period, and reduced production costs. Additionally, FIM improves meat quality and slaughter traits of aged hens, thereby providing a basis for the sustainable utilization of aged laying hen resources [5]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that FIM can disrupt intestinal homeostasis and trigger gut microbiota dysbiosis [6, 7]. This dysbiosis involves increased pathogens, pro‐inflammatory metabolites, impaired barrier function, and mucosal inflammation, leading to microbial translocation and systemic inflammation [6, 8]. Importantly, gut microbiota regulates bile acid biotransformation and enterohepatic circulation; fasting‐induced dysbiosis may alter bile acid composition and signaling, thereby weakening hepatic immune homeostasis [9, 10]. This ultimately contributes to the dysfunction of the gut and liver, resulting in reduced immunity and higher mortality rates in chickens during FIM. Furthermore, the remodeling of gut‐liver function following refeeding is essential for laying hens to achieve a new peak‐laying period [11, 12, 13, 14]. Therefore, understanding how microbiota dysbiosis affects gut‐liver function is important for improving hens' health and performance during FIM.

To address these issues, we selected 90 aged laying hens for FIM. Metagenomic sequencing and non‐targeted metabolomics were used to analyze the changes of gut microbiota and metabolites. By integrating the mechanisms of intestinal injury‐repair and changes in liver function, we explored the roles of gut microbiota and metabolites in the gut–liver function remodeling process, identifying specific species and metabolites that influence FIM. Our findings elucidate a regulatory network governing gut microbiota's impact on gut‐liver health during FIM, providing a foundation for probiotic‐ or mrtabolite‐based interventions to mitigate damage and support remodeling during refeeding.

RESULTS

Gut microbial–metabolite dynamics during FIM

To investigate FIM‐induced gut microbiota alterations, we performed metagenomic sequencing of cecal contents. Fasting significantly altered cecal microbiota composition (Figure 1A–C), decreasing species diversity. At the phylum level, fasting reduced the abundance of Bacteroidetes but increased that of Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria (Figure 1D). At the genus level, the abundances of Bacteroides and Prevotella decreased, whereas those of Olsenella, and Lachnoclostridium increased during the fasting period (Figure 1E). Based on the top 50 microbial genera identified by the random forest model (Table S1), we observed stage‐specific dynamic correlations (Figure S1A). At F0, Faecalimonas was the dominant genus, showing a negative correlation with Candidatus Woesearchaeota noname. By F15, a shift occurred, with Candidatus Aminicenantes noname, Enterobacter, and Slackia rising to prominence; notably, Slackia displayed a negative correlation with Arcanobacterium. And, we identified the characteristic bacterial species by linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe). For instance, Escherichia coli, Lachnoclostridium sp. An76, and Cloacibacillus sp. An23 were clustered during F15; Lactobacillus crispatus, Ligilactobacillus salivarius, Ligilactobacillus aviarius, and Bacteroides uniformis were clustered during R5 (LDA > 3 and p < 0.05) (Figure 1F). Next, Principal Co‐ordinates Analysis (PCoA) results showed that there was a difference in microbial function during FIM (Figure S1B). During fasting, pathways such as immune system and endocrine and metabolic disease were significantly enriched, indicating an increased susceptibility of the intestine to pathogenic invasion. During refeeding (R5 and R47), pathways related to replication and repair and lipid metabolism were significantly enriched, indicating improved adaptability of microorganisms in terms of energy metabolism and host health regulation (Figure S1C). These results indicate that pathogenic‐bacteria increase during fasting, and that probiotic levels rise again after refeeding.

Microorganisms are inextricably linked to metabolites, and the gut metabolites were significantly changed during FIM (Figure 1G–J). The enrichment of secondary bile acid biosynthesis during FIM points to a dysregulation of bile acid metabolism (Figure S1D). Based on the top 50 metabolites identified by the random forest model (Table S2), we observed stage‐specific dynamic correlations (Figure S1E). For instance, at F15, salicylic acid (SA) and N‐acetyl‐Asp‐Glu became prominent. N‐acetyl‐Asp‐Glu demonstrated a negative correlation with 4‐(1‐adamantyl)−2‐methyl‐1,3‐thiazol; at R47, 4‐(1‐adamantyl)−2‐methyl‐1,3‐thiazol became prominent. These findings reveal significant metabolic shifts and interrelationships across stages, highlighting the dynamic nature of the metabolic landscape in response to varying conditions.

Intestinal mucosa remodeling during FIM

Our findings demonstrate that fasting significantly impaired intestinal mucosa structure and function. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed extensive atrophy and disruption in the jejunum and ileum during fasting (Figure 2A; Figure S2A), with Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) showing epithelial cell shedding (Figure 2B). Fasting led to reduced villus height and villus/crypt ratio, coupled with increased crypt depth (p < 0.05) (Figure 2C–E; Figure S2B–D). During refeeding, the jejunum and ileum gradually started remodeling from atrophy. We further investigated the inflammatory response and oxidative damage in the jejunum during FIM. During the fasting period, the mRNA expression levels of P62, NF‐κB1, IL‐1β, and IL‐8, as well as the protein level of IL‐1β, significantly increased (p < 0.05) (Figure 2F–K). Concurrently, the concentrations of catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH‐PX) decreased (p < 0.05) (Figure 2L,M), while the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) increased (p < 0.05) (Figure 2N). Notably, the concentration of superoxide dismutase (SOD) remained unchanged during FIM (Figure 2O). These findings indicate that fasting induces intestinal inflammation and oxidative damage.

To elucidate jejunal mucosal remodeling, we analyzed cell proliferation, differentiation, autophagy, and apoptosis during FIM. During fasting, we observed a significant upregulation of proliferation (CCNB1, CDK1, DLL1, OLFM4, BMI1, and LGR5) (Figure S3A–F) and autophagy (BECLIN1, ERN1, and P53) genes (p < 0.05) (Figure S3G–I). Concurrently, differentiation genes (SLC2A2, SLC5A1, and CALB1) (Figure S3J–L) and pro‐apoptotic genes (TNF‐α, FASL, and Caspase‐3) were downregulated (Figure S3M–O), while anti‐apoptotic genes (PDGFR and BCL2) increased (p < 0.05) (Figure S3P,Q). Refeeding consistently reversed these mRNA expression patterns for all tested genes. Importantly, protein expression of key markers (CDK1, LGR5, SLC2A2, SLC5A1, CALB1, BCL2, and BECLIN1) mirrored their mRNA changes (Figure S4A–I). Additionally, LC3II/LC3I protein levels, elevated during fasting, significantly decreased upon refeeding (p < 0.05). Additionally, LC3II/LC3I protein levels, elevated during fasting, significantly decreased upon refeeding (p < 0.05) (Figure S4G,J). IF staining indicated that fasting significantly increased Ki67 proliferating stem cells (Figure S5A) while reducing the epithelial expression of SLC2A2, SLC5A1, and CALB1, which subsequently recovered to baseline levels following refeeding (Figure S5B–D). FIM dynamically reshaped jejunal mucosa by concurrently inducing intestinal stem cell activation and autophagy‐mediated villus atrophy during fasting and followed by rapid regeneration and repair upon refeeding.

RNA‑seq analysis of the mechanism underlying intestinal mucosa remodeling

To elucidate intestinal mucosa injury‐repair mechanisms during FIM, jejunal samples underwent RNA‐seq analysis. PCA, Venn diagram, and multiple scatter plots revealed significant genetic differences across time points (Figure 3A–C). WGCNA of jejunum transcriptome data, integrating villus height, crypt depth, and villus/crypt ratio (Figure S6A,B), identified blue, green‐yellow, yellow, and red modules as vital for intestinal villi regeneration (Figure 3D; Figure S6C–G). The hub genes from the blue, green‐yellow, yellow, and red modules were collectively integrated for GO and KEGG analyses. GO analysis revealed that hub genes were notably enriched in tissue development, including epithelial development, circulatory system development, and cell proliferation (Figure 3E; Table S3). Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that hub genes were notably enriched in signaling pathways, including TGF‐beta signaling pathways (Figure 3F), which were closely stem cell proliferation and differentiation and inflammatory response. Activation of the TGF‐β signaling pathway during fasting was collectively indicated by heatmap analysis of key TGF‐β signaling gene counts, qRT‐PCR, and Western Blotting (Figure 3G–L; Figure S6H–S).

Changes in liver function during FIM

To understand the impact of FIM on liver function, we performed liver transcriptome sequencing, revealing dynamic gene expression patterns (Figure 4A–C). Series test of cluster analysis was performed on the differentially expressed genes identified from the liver transcriptome. Genes from modules 2 and 5, which showed similar expression patterns, were selected for further functional annotation (Figure S7). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses indicated that the liver underwent metabolic reprogramming during fasting, accompanied by enhanced detoxification processes and disturbances in bile acid metabolism (Figure 4D,E; Table S4). Liver function assessment showed fasting significantly increased serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase ratio (AST/ALT), total bile acids (TBA), and total bilirubin (TBIL‐Z), while decreasing albumin (ALB) (Figure 4F–J), indicating impaired liver function and bile acid accumulation [15]. Targeted metabolomics of liver and serum bile acids confirmed an overall increase during fasting (Figure S8A,B), with specific elevations of taurodeoxycholic acid (TDCA), taurolithocholic acid (TLCA), ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), and lithocholic acid (LCA), which then decreased upon refeeding (Figure 4K–T). qRT‐PCR revealed fasting downregulated CYP7A1 and upregulated CYP46A1, CYP7B1, and CYP27A1 (Figure S8C–F), suggesting activation of non‐classical bile acid synthesis pathways [16, 17].

Integration analysis of cecal microbiota impact on intestinal and liver health

To investigate the relationship between changes in gut–iver health and the metabolites, we conducted a combined analysis of cecal metabolites with the transcriptomes of the jejunum and liver. Shared pathways between cecal metabolites and jejunum transcriptomes included ABC transporters, arachidonic acid metabolism, primary bile acid biosynthesis, and pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (Figure 5A). This analysis identified a total of 53 key metabolites, including l‐serine, arachidonic acid (AA), LCA, D‐panthenol, and 3‐hydroxyanthranilic acid (3‐HAA) (Figure S9A). Association analysis results revealed that l‐serine, LCA, and AA showed positive relationships with TGF‐beta signaling and intestinal injury, while d‐panthenol and 3‐HAA exhibited opposing effects (Figure 5B). Similarly, common pathways enriched by cecal metabolites and liver transcriptomes included ABC transporters, primary bile acid biosynthesis, arachidonic acid metabolism, and pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (Figure 5C). This analysis identified a total of 56 key metabolites, including l‐serine, AA, D‐panthenol, LCA, and 3‐HAA (Figure S9B). The association analysis results revealed that l‐serine, LCA, and AA were closely associated with liver injury, whereas D‐Panthenol and 3‐HAA played important roles in liver function repair (Figure 5D).

To investigate associations between intestinal metabolites and the gut microbiota, we applied MaAsLin2 to perform multivariable linear modeling with confounder adjustment. Notable associations (|coef| > 2 and q < 0.05) include: Liquorilactobacillus mali is positively independent associated with D‐Panthenol; Tissierellia bacterium KA00581 is positively independent associated with 3‐HAA; Pelosinus fermentans is positively independent associated with l‐serine and with LCA; Fibrobacter sp. UWOV1 is positively independent associated with AA (Figure 5E; Table S5). Based on this, we hypothesized that Liquorilactobacillus mali and Tissierellia bacterium KA00581 facilitate the functional remodeling of both the gut and liver, while Pelosinus fermentans and Fibrobacter sp. UWOV1 was associated with impaired gut‐liver function during fasting.

d‐Panthenol promotes the growth of chicken small intestinal organoids

Based on our transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, we investigated the effects of D‐Panthenol on intestinal function using a chicken small intestinal organoid model. Organoids were cultured with 0, 10, 100, and 1000 µM D‐Panthenol. By Day 3, budding was observed in all groups, and by Day 5, the number of buds and the organoid area had increased (Figure 6A).

By Day 7, compared to controls, the budding rates in the 10, 100, and 1000 µM groups were significantly higher (p < 0.05) (Figure 6B), with the 100 µM group showing the most significant increase in area (p < 0.05) (Figure 6C). qRT‐PCR analysis on Day 7 revealed 100 µM D‐Panthenol significantly upregulated proliferation (OLFM4, CDK1, and LGR5), differentiation (SLC2A2 and SLC5A1), and barrier function (CLDN1) genes (p < 0.05) (Figure 6D–I). Concurrently, inflammation‐related genes (IL12B, INFγ, IFNβ, IL13, iNOS, and INFα) were significantly reduced (p < 0.05) (Figure 6J–O). These results indicate that 100 µM D‐Panthenol promotes the proliferation and differentiation of small intestinal organoids, enhances their barrier function, and mitigates inflammation.

DISCUSSION

FIM is an approach to reactivate the production performance of laying hens, but it involves a period of fasting that can lead to energy deficiency. This energy deficit disrupts the gut microbiota composition and compromises gut–liver functionality, ultimately contributing to higher mortality rates. Consistent with previous studies, our data demonstrate that fasting enhances pathogenic bacterial abundance, consequently increasing the gut′s susceptibility to invasion [6, 18]. During fasting, Candidatus Aminicenantes noname, Enterobacter, and Slackia become predominant genera. We hypothesize that these bacteria have the ability to ferment and utilize undigested carbohydrates. Notably, some members also exhibit pathogenic potential; for example, Enterobacter species can readily cause infections when host immunity is compromised or the physical barrier is disrupted [19]. After refeeding, probiotic levels increase, improving the adaptability of the gut microbiome and playing a crucial role in energy metabolism, host health regulation, and the maintenance of microbial ecological balance. During R5, Ligilactobacillus salivarius and Bacteroides uniformis were clustered, contributing to the maintenance of the gut microbiota balance and the regulation of anti‐inflammatory responses [20, 21, 22, 23]. During R47, Butyricicoccus porccorum were clustered, potentially influencing the growth and renewal of intestinal epithelial cells through the production of beneficial short‐chain fatty acids [24]. Although metagenomic sequencing reveals subtle shifts in the gut microbiota during FIM, knowledge of the specific functions of most involved microbes remains limited.

Metabolites act as a crucial bridge between gut microbiota and host health. The shifting correlation patterns (e.g., SA and N‐acetyl‐Asp‐Glu prominence at F15; 4‐(1‐adamantyl)‐2‐methyl‐1,3‐thiazole prominence at R47) point to a dynamic reprogramming of metabolic routes likely reflecting changes in mucosal immunity, barrier function, or energy metabolism as the condition evolves. The increase in compounds such as SA during F15 concurrently implies adaptive or protective responses by the body to manage fasting stress, regulate inflammation, or preserve homeostasis [25]. During fasting, the enrichment of metabolic pathways involved in secondary bile acid metabolism indicates substantial gut and liver functional impairment. Bile acid accumulation not only damages liver cells and triggers inflammation but also impairs intestinal barrier function by disrupting enterohepatic circulation, which can subsequently lead to injury of the intestinal mucosa [26, 27]. While KEGG identifies metabolite functions, direct physiological impact requires further investigation. Thus, we analyzed intestinal and liver function changes to clarify metabolite roles during FIM.

Consistent with previous studies, the intestine underwent dynamic changes during FIM, transitioning from inflammation and oxidative damage to repair [6]. Uniquely, our study found increased proliferative capacity of intestinal cells during fasting in hens, followed by rapid differentiation of intestinal stem cells upon refeeding. In a study involving mice, fasting for 24 h significantly reduced the proliferation of crypt cells, whereas refeeding enhanced intestinal cell proliferation [28]. We hypothesize that hens, with higher fat reserves from late laying, utilize fat autophagy during fasting to provide essential nutrients [29, 30], thus preserving intestinal stem cell regenerative and repair capacities. In addition, we found that TGF‐beta signaling pathways are activated during fasting. The TGF‐beta signaling pathway plays a central role in regulating inflammatory responses, controlling autophagy, and modulating cell proliferation, all of which are essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis and promoting tissue repair [31, 32]. This leads us to speculate that the TGF‐beta pathway is a critical regulator of intestinal mucosal remodeling.

FIM profoundly impacts liver function due to significant nutritional and energy fluctuations [9]. During fasting, the concentrations of bile significantly increased in both the liver and serum samples, along with the activation of non‐classical bile acid synthesis pathways in the liver. Under physiological conditions, the classical pathway converts cholesterol to 7α‐hydroxycholesterol via CYP7A1, resulting in the production of bile acids CA and CDCA. In contrast, the alternative pathway is initiated by CYP27A1 and generates CDCA through 7α‐hydroxylation catalyzed by CYP7B1, with CYP8B1 also playing a role in bile acid hydroxylation [33]. This study found that fasting suppresses the expression of CYP7A1 while upregulating CYP27A1 and CYP7B1, indicating that fasting activates the non‐classical pathway of bile acid synthesis. The activation of this pathway during fasting leads to the synthesis of bile acids, which help regulate energy balance in response to altered metabolic demands [33]. However, this activation can promote abnormal bile acid accumulation, impair bile excretion, and cause liver cell damage [34], disrupting cellular integrity and promoting inflammation and fibrosis, which may ultimately lead to cirrhosis. High concentrations of bile acids can be toxic to liver cells, causing damage to cell membranes, leading to cell death, and subsequently affecting liver function [35]. If the issue of bile acid accumulation is not resolved, this damage may have a negative impact on overall health, prolong the recovery time during FIM, reduce the increase in egg production rates, and increase mortality rates.

A comprehensive examination of the gut‐liver function is essential for understanding metabolism, immunity, and disease mechanisms [36, 37]. Focusing solely on either the gut or liver neglects their intricate interactions, hindering a full understanding of how fasting impacts overall health. MaAsLin2 analysis revealed Liquorilactobacillus mali to be independently and positively associated with D‐Panthenol, and Tissierellia bacterium KA00581 to be independently and positively associated with 3‐HAA. Both of these metabolites are implicated in gut and liver repair processes. d‐Panthenol and 3‐HHA may have positive effects on intestinal and liver functions through their antioxidant and cell‐protective effects [38, 39, 40]. Our study demonstrated 100 µM D‐Panthenol significantly promoted chicken small intestinal organoid proliferation and differentiation, and enhanced barrier function by increasing CLDN1 and reducing inflammation [41]. Thus, D‐Panthenol may serve as a valuable exogenous additive to improve gut health in laying hens during FIM. Furthermore, Pelosinus fermentans showed independent positive associations with l‐serine and LCA, while Fibrobacter sp. UWOV1 was independently and positively associated with AA. These findings suggest their involvement in gut and liver damage. Elevated levels of LCA and AA are positively correlated with metabolic disorders in the gut and with liver inflammation [42, 43, 44]. Conversely, increased l‐serine metabolism exerts beneficial effects on both the gut and liver [45, 46]. We hypothesized that this dysfunction enhances l‐serine metabolism, thereby reducing the damage to the gut and liver. Correlation analysis of gut microbiota, metabolites, and gut‐liver function offers insights into their roles, but does not establish causality. Further experimental validation is required to confirm the relationships mentioned above.

CONCLUSIONS

The study investigated the dynamic processes of the gut microbiota, metabolites, intestinal remodeling, and liver function during FIM. During fasting, harmful microbiota and metabolites increased, causing oxidative damage, intestinal inflammation, and villus atrophy. The process activated the TGF‐beta signaling pathway, promoting intestinal stem cell proliferation. Moreover, liver function dysfunction, and non‐classical bile acid synthesis was activated, causing bile acid accumulation in the liver. During refeeding, the previously observed effects were reversed. We identified key microorganisms (Liquorilactobacillus mali and Tissierellia bacterium KA00581) and functional metabolites (d‐Panthenol and 3‐HAA) associated with intestinal‐liver function during FIM. This study provides evidence at the organoid level that d‐Panthenol can serve as a candidate exogenous additive to improve intestinal health in laying hens undergoing FIM. This study elucidated the correlations among the gut microbiota, metabolites, and gut–liver function, offering guidance for enhancing FIM management and feeding strategies to prolong the lifespan of laying hens.

METHODS

Animals and experimental design

A total of 90 laying hens, aged 60 weeks with similar body weights, were divided into 9 replicates of 10 hens each. The study was conducted at the Poultry Germplasm Resource Factory of Henan Agricultural University in Yuan Yang County, Henan Province, from April to July 2022. The chickens were maintained in a room with a temperature of 18°C–25°C and humidity of 60–70%. All chickens were fed a corn‐soybean meal basal diet [47]. The FIM experiment was focused on four time points: F0, F15, R5, and R47 (Figure S10). In this study, the weight loss during F15 was maintained below 30%, and the second peak of egg production occurred during R47.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Metagenomic sequencing data were deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive under accession number CRA021343 [7]. This gene set was aligned and annotated against various databases, including KEGG, for functional analysis of the microbial community. Inter‐group comparisons of Shannon diversity were performed using the Kruskal‐Wallis test. PCoA based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities at various taxonomic levels was employed to visualize inter‐sample relationships. To identify species significantly contributing to community differentiation, LEfSe was performed [48]. LEfSe combines the non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis test with linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to estimate the effect size of differentially abundant features among groups (q < 0.05 and LDA score > 3). The sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were performed using the Omicsmart online platform (http://www.omicsmart.com).

Cecal contents metabolite analysis

The frozen cecal contents were thawed on ice and extracted using an 80% methanol buffer. The extracted samples were subsequently analyzed by liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry according to the manufacturer's instructions. Significantly differentiated metabolites were identified by combining PLS‐DA VIP scores with BH‐FDR corrected one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) results. The significance thresholds were set at VIP ≥ 1 and q < 0.05. Differentially expressed metabolites were visualized using a volcano plot and subjected to KEGG enrichment analysis. The sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were performed using the Omicsmart online platform (http://www.omicsmart.com).

Random forest feature selection and SparCC‐based correlation network analysis

Random forest analysis identified the top 50 significant gut microbiota and metabolites by Mean Decrease Gini importance, with statistical significance evaluated via permutation tests (n = 200) and BH‐FDR correction (q < 0.05) [49]. Raw abundance data were CLR‐transformed and regressed against covariates (year and fasting status) to obtain residuals. SparCC correlations were calculated from these residuals, with robust correlations (|R| > 0.7, q < 0.05) visualized via heatmaps [50]. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2) using the compositions (version 2.0‐9) and psych (version 2.6.1) packages.

H&E staining and immunofluorescence staining

Intestinal tissue samples were fixed in paraffin and cut into 4‐μm sections with a microtome (Leica Biosystems). Each section was then stained with H&E. Ten villi with intact linear structures were selected from the stained sections. Villus height and crypt depth were measured using CaseViewer software. The mean of these measurements was recorded for each sample, and the villus/crypt ratio was calculated. Jejunal tissue sections were prepared for immunofluorescence. The sections were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies (Ki67 (1:400), SLC2A2 (1:200), SLC5A1 (1:100), and CALB1 (1:200)) at 4°C overnight. The antibody information is shown in Table S6. Following incubation, the cells were rinsed with phosphate‐buffered saline with Tween (PBST) and treated with secondary antibodies (1:200) for 60 min at room temperature. Stained sections were visualized under a confocal microscope.

Scanning electron microscopy

Jejunum samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and incubated with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at 4°C. They were dehydrated in an increasing ethanol gradient for 20 min at each step and then immersed in a 1:1 mixture of ethanol and tert‐butanol for 30 min. The samples were then incubated in tert‐butanol for 1 h. Dehydration was completed using a Hitachi Model HCP‐2 critical point dryer (Hitachi Koki) with liquid CO2. Finally, the dried samples were coated with gold‐palladium and analyzed using digital SEM (Hitachi S‐4800).

qRT‐PCR analysis

High‐quality RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Prime Script RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa), followed by qRT‐PCR using a Light Cycler 96 system (Roche) and the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit (Takara). The primer sequences for these genes are shown in Table S7. qRT‐PCR was performed to analyze the mRNA levels of the reference gene using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Western blotting

Total protein extracts from the jejunum tissue samples were collected and quantified using the Bradford assay. The specific operational steps for the western blotting were based on the instructions provided by Pillai‐Kastoori et al. [51]. The antibody information is shown in Table S6. The expression of β‐actin was used as an internal loading control, and the optical density of protein bands was measured using ImageJ software.

Constructing cDNA library and sequence data analysis

Jejunum and liver tissue RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and quantified using a NanoDrop ND‐2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and integrity was assessed via agarose gel electrophoresis. High‐quality RNA samples were selected for library preparation and sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq. 6000 platform (illumina). Quality control of raw sequencing reads was conducted using FASTQ by removing reads with adapters, high N ratios, or low quality, resulting in high‐quality clean reads. These clean reads were aligned to the GRCg7b reference genome using HISAT2, and the transcripts were assembled using StringTie software. Gene expression was quantified using RNA‐Seq by Expectation‐Maximization to obtain raw counts. For downstream differential expression analysis and gene expression comparisons, raw counts were used. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq. 2 (version 1.30.0) with the following criteria: |log2Fold‐Change| > 1 and q < 0.05 [52]. The jejunal transcriptome sequencing analyses were performed using the Omicsmart online platform (http://www.omicsmart.com). The liver transcriptome sequencing analyses were performed using the Majorbio Cloud (www.majorbio.com).

Weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA employs co‐expression analysis to uncover gene interactions linked to traits (villus height, crypt depth, and villus/crypt ratio), thereby elucidating the complex interplay between gene expression and these traits. Using the “WGCNA” package in R (version 4.5.2) [53], the training set genes were networked with a soft threshold function ensuring a correlation coefficient |R| > 0.80 for a scale‐free network. Module correlations between traits were calculated and visualized using heatmaps. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of gene significance were conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package in R and the DAVID database.

Series test of cluster analysis and functional enrichment

To characterize dynamic transcriptional responses in liver tissues during FIM, time‑series clustering analysis was performed in R (version 4.5.2). Gene expression data were first log2‑transformed, and genes with low expression were removed to reduce noise. The remaining genes were standardized using Z‑score transformation to ensure comparable expression patterns across samples. Temporal expression patterns were identified using the K‑means clustering algorithm. The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the elbow method by evaluating the within‑cluster sum of squares across different cluster numbers. Genes were then assigned to clusters representing distinct temporal expression trends across the four time points (F0, F15, R5, and R47). For each cluster, the average standardized expression profile was calculated to represent the characteristic temporal trajectory of that module. To further evaluate clustering reliability, fuzzy c‑means clustering implemented in the e1071 package was applied to estimate membership scores for each gene. Genes with higher membership values were considered to have stronger associations with their corresponding temporal patterns. Genes within each cluster were subsequently used for downstream functional enrichment analysis.

Detection of serum liver function indicators

Liver function indicators, including ALT, AST, ALP, ALB, TBA, GGT, TBIL‐Z, and DBIL‐Z were analyzed using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (BK280).

Targeted bile acid metabolomics sequencing

First, liver samples were weighed and ground for metabolite extraction at low temperature, while serum samples were mixed with methanol for extraction [54]. After centrifugation to obtain supernatants, both were analyzed using LC‐ESI‐MS/MS (UHPLC‐Qtrap) for qualitative and quantitative detection, with concentrations calculated via standard curves to quantify target substances.

Integration of genes or microbiota and metabolites

We explored the shared KEGG metabolic pathways involving genes and metabolites, evaluated their interactions, and constructed an integrated functional model. Correlations between genes and metabolites were analyzed by OmicShare tools (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/) [55]. We performed Maaslin2 analysis to assess the correlations between microorganisms and metabolites using the Maaslin2 package in R [56], and network analysis was performed using Cytoscape (version 3.8.2) [57].

Isolation and culture of chicken small elongated organoids

Small intestines from 18‐day‐old AA broiler embryos were dissected, opened longitudinally, and washed with PBS. Tissues were minced into 1–2 mm³ fragments and digested in 2.5 mmol/L EDTA at 4°C for 30 min. After vortexing for 30 s, crypt cells were filtered through a 100 μm strainer, centrifuged at 50 × g for 5 min, washed with PBS, and pelleted at 200 × g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in DMEM, mixed with undiluted Matrigel at a 2:1 ratio, and seeded into 24‐well plates (50 μL per well, 100–150 crypts per well). Following solidification at 37°C for 30 min, 500 μL of pre‐warmed complete medium was added per well. Complete medium was prepared by mixing l‐WRN‐conditioned medium and high‐glucose DMEM (1:1) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1× B‐27, 1× N‐2, 1 mmol/L l‐glutamine, 10 mmol/L HEPES, 10 mmol/L nicotinamide, 500 ng/mL human R‐spondin 1, 100 ng/mL human Noggin, 50 ng/mL human EGF, 50 ng/mL FGF2, 1 μmol/L Jagged‐1, 3 μmol/L CHIR99021, 0.5 μmol/L galunisertib, 10 μmol/L SB202190, and 5 μmol/L Y‐27632. The medium was refreshed every 2 days. Primary organoids were passaged on Day 7 and subsequently every 5 days.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 25.0). Group comparisons were evaluated using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test for normally distributed data, or the Kruskal–Wallis test for non‐normally distributed data. Each experiment was performed independently with at least three replicates to ensure reliability. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The results are presented as the mean ± SEM. GraphPad Prism (version 8.0) was used to generate clear and comprehensive visual representations of the data.
