Role of ivabradine for anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity: a meta-analysis
Authors: Muhammad Umar, Aizaz Ali, Avirup Guha, Mirza Mohammad Ali Baig, Fazia Khattak, Daniel Addison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAnthracyclines are widely used anticancer agents but are limited by dose-dependent cardiotoxicity. Ivabradine selectively reduces heart rate without negative inotropy and may offer cardio protection in cancer patients, though its efficacy in anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity (AIC) remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing ivabradine versus placebo in adult patients receiving anthracycline therapy. PubMed, Cochrane Central, Embase, Web of Science, Google Scholar, Scopus and ClinicalTrials.gov, and reference lists were searched through August 2025. Outcomes included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), heart rate, blood pressure, NT-proBNP, and strain-based parameters. Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane RoB 2 tool.ResultsThree RCTs (n = 210) met inclusion criteria. Ivabradine showed no significant effect on LVEF (MD 0.32%, 95% CI − 0.90 to 1.54; p = 0.61) or NT-proBNP. Heart rate reduction was directionally favorable but not statistically significant (MD − 4.11 bpm, 95% CI − 8.69 to 0.46; p = 0.08). Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were unchanged. Strain-based outcomes were inconsistently reported, precluding pooled analysis.ConclusionsGiven the limited sample size, heterogeneity, and variability in endpoint definitions, current evidence is insufficient to establish a definitive cardioprotective role for ivabradine in AIC. Larger, rigorously designed trials with standardized imaging and biomarker endpoints are needed to determine its role.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-026-00491-1.Keywords: Anthracycline, Cardiotoxicity, Ivabradine, Meta-analysis, Heart Rate

Introduction

Anthracyclines remain essential anticancer agents but are limited by dose-dependent cardiotoxicity that can manifest as left ventricular dysfunction and heart failure [1, 2]. Proposed mechanisms include oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury, and topoisomerase IIβ–mediated cardiomyocyte damage [3–5]. Although conventional cardioprotective therapies (e.g., ACE inhibitors/ARBs and β-blockers) have been studied, results have been inconsistent, and tolerability may be limited in some patients [6–8]. Ivabradine selectively inhibits the sinoatrial “funny” current to reduce heart rate without negative inotropy [9], making it an appealing adjunct in cancer patients who frequently present with unexplained tachycardia [10]. However, whether ivabradine has a benefit in preventing or attenuating anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity (AIC) remains uncertain.

Methods

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and registered the protocol in PROSPERO (CRD420251110005). An expanded literature search was conducted across PubMed (MEDLINE), Cochrane Central, Embase, Web of Science, Google Scholar, Scopus and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception through August 2025. The full electronic search strategies for each database, including Boolean operators and MeSH terms, are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also screened. The study selection process is summarized in a PRISMA flow diagram (Supplementary Fig. 1).

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) enrolling adult cancer patients receiving anthracycline-based chemotherapy that compared ivabradine (alone or alongside standard therapy) versus placebo. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. A detailed summary of the risk of bias across included studies is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Given anticipated heterogeneity, we prespecified subgroup considerations based on cancer type, anthracycline dose, and duration of follow-up; however, these analyses were not feasible due to limited data. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and interpreted in the context of clinical and methodological variability. Given differences in study design, populations, and endpoint definitions (particularly for GLS), results were interpreted cautiously and complemented with narrative synthesis where appropriate.

Results & discussion

From 20 records, 3 RCTs (n = 210; ivabradine n = 95, placebo n = 115) met inclusion criteria [11–13]. Participants received anthracyclines (e.g., doxorubicin/epirubicin) for malignancies including breast cancer, lymphoma, and sarcoma. Ivabradine was administered orally (approximately 10–15 mg/day with titration) for 3–12 months. The overall risk of bias ranged from low to high across trials, with common concerns including incomplete outcome reporting and deviations from the intended interventions (Table 1). Notably, there was substantial variability in study populations, anthracycline dosing, follow-up duration, and definitions of cardiotoxicity endpoints across included trials.

Across all three RCTs, ivabradine showed no significant effect on LVEF compared with placebo (MD 0.32%, 95% CI − 0.90 to 1.54; p = 0.61; I² =0%) [11–13]. Two studies contributed to heart-rate analysis at 6 months and demonstrated a non-significant reduction with ivabradine (MD − 4.11 bpm, 95% CI − 8.69 to 0.46; p = 0.08; I² =0%) [11, 13]. Systolic blood pressure at 6 months was similarly unchanged (MD − 1.38 mmHg, 95% CI − 6.20 to 3.44; p = 0.57; I² =0%), and diastolic blood pressure showed a borderline reduction (MD − 3.27 mmHg, 95% CI − 6.62 to 0.07; p = 0.05; I² =15%) [11, 13]. NT-proBNP did not differ between groups (MD − 13.68 pg/mL, 95% CI − 45.51 to 18.15; p = 0.40; I² =0%) [11, 12]. Strain-based findings were mixed and could not be pooled: this was largely due to inconsistent definitions (e.g., ≥ 10% vs. > 15% GLS reduction) and differences in reporting formats, further limiting comparability [11–13]. The pooled effect of ivabradine on LVEF is shown in the forest plot (Fig. 1), demonstrating no significant difference between ivabradine and placebo (MD 0.32%, 95% CI − 0.90 to 1.54; I² = 0%).

Overall, the pooled evidence from three small RCTs suggests no clear signal of benefit of ivabradine on conventional markers such as LVEF; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size, heterogeneity in study design, and inconsistent endpoint definitions. These findings should be considered in the context of existing literature. A recent network meta-analysis of randomized trials evaluating cardioprotective strategies in patients receiving anthracyclines demonstrated modest benefits with established therapies such as beta-blockers and renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, while also highlighting the limited availability of high-quality evidence for alternative approaches [14]. In contrast to prior syntheses, our study specifically evaluates ivabradine, a heart rate–modulating agent with a distinct mechanism of action. The absence of a clear benefit in our analysis likely reflects both true uncertainty and the limited number of adequately powered randomized trials. While ivabradine produced a directionally favorable heart-rate signal, this did not translate into measurable improvements in LVEF or NT-proBNP, suggesting that heart-rate reduction alone may be insufficient to counter the underlying cytotoxic mechanisms of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity [5]. Additionally, strain-based parameters, which may detect earlier myocardial injury, were inconsistently defined and reported across studies, precluding robust synthesis and limiting definitive conclusions [15].

Limitations

Limitations include the small number of RCTs, limited sample size, variable follow-up duration (3–12 months), and heterogeneous reporting of GLS/diastolic parameters and biomarkers. These constraints limit subgroup analyses and may have reduced power to detect modest benefits, especially in higher-risk phenotypes (e.g., persistent tachycardia or intolerance to β-blockers). Prior evidence syntheses similarly highlight the paucity of randomized data for ivabradine in this setting [14]. Future trials should standardize cardiotoxicity definitions (LVEF/GLS thresholds), harmonize time points, include troponin and strain-based endpoints, and evaluate whether ivabradine provides additive benefit when layered on established cardioprotective strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, based on currently available randomized evidence, there is insufficient evidence to support a definitive cardioprotective role of ivabradine in anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity. Given the methodological limitations and heterogeneity of existing studies, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Larger, rigorously designed trials with standardized imaging and biomarker endpoints and longer surveillance are needed.
