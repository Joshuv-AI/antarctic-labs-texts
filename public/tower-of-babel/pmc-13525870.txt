Selective Packaging of miR‐22 and miR‐29 Orthologues into Extracellular Vesicles of the Porcine Whipworm Trichuris Suis
Authors: Bradley Whitehead, Maibritt Mardahl, Eline P. Hansen, Bastian Fromm, Anne Borup, Anders Toftegaard Boysen, Jasmine C. L. Atay, Rikke Nørregaard, Allan Stensballe, Sidsel D. Andersen, Robin B. Gasser, Marco Brucale, Francesco Valle, Andrea Ridolfi, Eske Nøhr Glud, Merete Fredholm, Andrew R. Williams, Stig M. Thamsborg, Johan Palmfedt, Peter Nejsum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHelminths modulate host immunity, and in this respect, the porcine whipworm Trichuris suis has been explored clinically as an immunotherapy for inflammatory diseases. T. suis secretes a complex mixture of excretory/secretory products (ESP), including extracellular vesicles (EVs), nanosized membranous particles with bioactive cargoes of lipids, proteins and nucleic acids that are implicated in intercellular communication within organisms. Here, we characterise the EV protein and miRNA cargo of T. suis to identify potential roles in host–parasite communication. In contrast to EVs from clade III/V helminth species characterised to date, we show that T. suis EVs did not suppress LPS‐induced TNF production by human macrophages under the conditions tested, in contrast to non‐vesicular ESP fractions which did. By generating the first extracellular miRNA complement for T. suis and quantifying EV‐associated versus free miRNAs across larval and adult stages, we identify selective enrichment of two miRNAs in adult EVs with homology to human miR‐22‐3p and miR‐29‐3p, canonical anti‐fibrotic and tumour‐suppressor miRNAs. Functional assays in human colon cancer epithelial cells reveal that these helminth‐derived orthologues downregulate conserved oncogenic targets, indicating evolutionary conservation of their regulatory capacity. Our findings uncover a previously unrecognised axis of whipworm biology in which selective EV‐mediated export of anti‐fibrotic miRNAs has the potential to modulate host tissue remodeling independently of classical immunomodulation, with implications for understanding helminth‐driven epithelial homeostasis and cancer risk.

Introduction

Helminths infect more than 1.5 billion people globally, with the human whipworm Trichuris trichiura affecting ∼500 million individuals (Vos et al. 2016). These infections are typically chronic (Akelew et al. 2022) and characterised by robust immunomodulation, including suppression of Th1 responses and induction of regulatory Th2 immune profile with expansion of Treg cells. Motivated by these host immune characteristics, controlled infections with the porcine whipworm T. suis have been evaluated as therapies for inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, including ulcerative colitis (Schölmerich et al. 2017; Bager et al. 2010; Voldsgaard et al. 2015; Summers et al. 2005; Yordanova et al. 2021). Despite favourable safety profiles, clinical efficacy has been limited, prompting a shift toward identifying specific immunomodulatory components within helminth secretomes. We and others have shown that T. suis excretory/secretory products (ESP) suppress inflammatory responses through small molecules such as PGE2 and immunomodulatory proteins (Laan et al. 2017; Leroux et al. 2018; Zakeri et al. 2021). In addition, helminth extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as candidates for interspecies communication and immune modulation, exemplified by our recent work showing that Ascaris suum EVs induce PD‐L1 expression on host monocytes that attenuates T cell activation (Borup et al. 2022).

However, chronic helminth infections shape host tissues in ways that extend beyond immune modulation. Excessive tissue remodelling and progressive fibrosis are key determinants of morbidity in several helminth infections, and in some settings may predispose to cancer development. Indeed, three helminths are classified as Group I carcinogens by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (Brindley and Loukas 2017), including the liver flukes Clonorchis sinensis and Opisthorchis viverrini, which cause cholangiocarcinoma (Brindley et al. 2015; Qian and Zhou 2017), and Schistosoma haematobium, which induces squamous cell carcinoma (Mostafa et al. 1999).

In contrast, soil‐transmitted helminths have not been associated with increased cancer risk in humans, despite causing epithelial hyperplasia, crypt remodelling, persistent tissue repair and immune dampening (Maizels et al. 2014). However, recent work in mice indicates that whipworm infections may influence epithelial biology more than previously appreciated. Trichuris muris infection can promote colonic epithelial dysplasia in mice in the absence of exogenous carcinogens (Hayes et al. 2017). A recent faecal‐miRNA atlas further implicates wound‐healing and fibrotic pathways in host responses to T. muris and identified downregulation of host anti‐fibrotic miRNAs, including miR‐29. These findings highlight a gap in our understanding of how whipworms modulate host tissue remodelling and how host and pathogen regulatory nucleic acids contribute to these processes (Layton et al. 2025).

Given this emerging landscape, the composition and function of Trichuris secreted products including EVs, warrant renewed investigation as the molecular basis of T. suis host modulation and in particular the contribution of EVs versus soluble secretions, remains poorly defined. Trichuris belongs to clade I of the Nematoda and is phylogenetically distant from the clade III and clade V species have dominated helminth EV research. Its miRNA repertoire and the host responses it elicits cannot therefore be assumed to mirror those systems. Here we ask three questions: (a) does the T. suis secretome contain bona fide EVs distinct in cargo from non‐vesicular secretions; (b) which fraction(s) of the secretome account for the documented anti‐inflammatory activity of T. suis products; and (c) does the EV‐associated miRNA cargo include sequences with conserved regulatory potential in mammalian cells? To address these questions we generated the first annotated extracellular miRNA complement for T. suis, profiled the EV proteome and immunomodulatory activity of fractionated secretions, and tested whether selected EV‐enriched parasite miRNAs retain regulatory function on conserved mammalian targets in vitro.

Results

Size‐exclusion Chromatography of Adult T. suis ESP Yields EV‐enriched Fractions With Vesicular Morphology Determined by AFM

Adult T. suis excretory/secretory products (ESP) were fractionated using size exclusion chromatography (SEC) and the highest particle numbers were within fractions 7–9, consistent with the expected elution of mammalian EVs (Figure 1A). Non‐EV fractions (11‐24) showed the highest protein concentrations (Figure 1B). EV enrichment across SEC fractions was assessed using the particle‐to‐protein ratio as a relative enrichment metric, rather than as an absolute measure of EV purity, given the complexity of helminth ESP (Figure S1). Single‐particle AFM morphometry of adult T. suis EVs samples identified abundant vesicular structures (Figure 1C) having a lognormal size distribution peaked at ∼50 nm and ranging between 30 and 200 nm (Figure 1D). As expected for intact vesicles (Ridolfi et al. 2020), their contact angle was determined to be normally distributed, with an average of 99 ± 14° (Figure 1E). Both the contact angle average value and its variance are consistent with values previously measured on EVs of mammalian and helminth origin (Borup et al. 2022; Zakeri et al. 2021; Boysen et al. 2020; Whitehead et al. 2015).

Adult T. suis EVs Associate With Human Macrophages in Vitro

Subsequently, we assessed whether adult T. suis EVs are internalised by human macrophages, as uptake is a prerequisite for delivery and function of EV miRNA cargoes (Figure 1F–I). Fluorescent EVs at low (Figure 1G) and high dose (Figure 1H) were incubated with differentiated THP‐1 cells overnight, showing internalisation of fluorescently labelled EVs in a dose‐dependent manner. Unlabelled control EVs (Figure 1F) and dye label controls (Figure 1I) showed no increased cellular fluorescence. We note that lipophilic dye labelling cannot formally distinguish vesicle internalisation from membrane association in the absence of a 4°C control. The uptake assay is therefore presented as evidence consistent with cellular association of EVs, not as proof of cytoplasmic delivery, this caveat is relevant to the interpretation of EV‐mediated miRNA function discussed below.

Proteomic Profiling Distinguishes EV‐enriched Fractions From EV‐depleted Secretome Fractions

In the absence of antibodies against T. suis EV proteins, a mass spectrometry‐based proteomics approach was used to validate EV enrichment through homology to known EV proteins and to comprehensively characterise the T. suis EV proteome, thereby providing insight into their biological functions. Proteins contained within the adult T. suis ESP, EV, and EV‐depleted fractions, isolated using ultracentrifugation, were identified by LC‐MS/MS. Protein level alterations are presented in volcano plots (Figure 2A–C) showing significant differences between the EV proteome and that of ESP and EV‐depleted supernatants (SN) (Figure 2A–C), consistent with selective packaging of proteins in EVs. In total, 452 proteins were identified across all fractions, of which 418 were detected in EVs, 192 in ESP and 76 in EV‐depleted supernatants. A total of 227 proteins were unique to EVs, with 117 and 31 proteins shared with ESP or EV‐depleted supernatants (SN), respectively, and 43 proteins were present in all fractions, while only 32 and 2 were unique to the ESP and the EV‐depleted supernatant, respectively (Figure 2D and Table S1). Differential expression analysis was performed comparing EV, ESP and SN which are presented as volcano plots with an enrichment of proteases in EVs compared to supernatants and ESP (Figure 2A–C and Table S1). GO annotations enriched in proteins identified within EVs included membrane components, hydrolase and protease activities, further increasing confidence in the enrichment of EVs (Figure 2E).

Volcano plots showing differentially regulated proteins in EV (A), ESP (B), and SN (C) (n = 3), significance determined as Log2 fold‐change ≥1, adjusted p value ≤ 0.05. Venn diagram of proteins identified in EV, SN, ESP (D). GO annotation enrichment analysis of EV, ESP and SN (E).

Non‐Vesicular ESP Fractions, but Not Adult EV‐Enriched Fractions, Suppress LPS‐Induced TNF Release in Vitro

To investigate a potential role of T. suis EVs in host immune modulation, we employed an in vitro model using lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐induced inflammation, which we have previously shown can be attenuated by A. suum EVs (Borup et al. 2022). We and others have previously shown that T. suis soluble products (SP) generated from worm homogenates and ESP are anti‐inflammatory, and this was confirmed using this model (Figure 3). Inhibition, by both SP and ESP, was dose‐dependent and significant at the lowest dose of 20 µg/ml (Figure 3A,B). Doses were normalised on a per‐macrophage particle basis for EVs and on a protein‐concentration basis for ESP and EV‐depleted fractions. Pre‐incubation of THP‐1 cells with EVs before LPS stimulation did not inhibit TNF production at the highest dose of 25,000 EVs/cell that corresponded to 5.2 µg/mL EV protein (Figure 3C). Likewise, fractions 11–18 from SEC separation failed to inhibit TNF production at all concentrations tested (Figure 3D). In contrast, fractions eluting later from SEC columns and therefore absent in EVs (fractions 19–24) inhibited LPS induced TNF production at the lowest concentration of 5 µg/mL that was lower than the comparable protein concentration of the highest EV dose confirming the role for non‐vesicular ES components in the attenuation of LPS mediated inflammation (Figure 3E).

In‐Depth miRNA Sequencing Provides a Fully Annotated Extracellular miRNA Complement for T. suis

As no annotated miRNA complement currently exists for T. suis, we produced and annotated the miRNA complement for T. suis using the prediction software miRMiner, employing publicly available small RNA‐seq data representing all developmental stages of T. suis (Jex et al. 2014) and performed small RNA‐seq analysis on T. suis EVs and free miRNA in EV‐depleted supernatants. We guided our annotation using the prediction software miRMachine and the T. suis reference genome, comparing to the known miRNA complement from the related nematode A. suum, which contains at least 96 miRNAs representing 53 families. Our final complement revealed 68 unique miRNA hairpin precursor genes (miRNA families) and 135 mature miRNAs, from which 68 were predicted as star sequences (the predicted non‐dominant miRNA within the hairpin). For comparison, distantly related, but well‐studied roundworms Caenorhabditis briggsae and Caenorhabditis elegans harbour at least 186 and 146 miRNAs representing 89 and 90 families, respectively. A direct comparison of families present within these worms highlights the close relationship between miRNAs of both parasitic and free‐living nematodes (Figure 4A).

MiRNAs are Life‐Stage‐Specific Packaged Into EVs

Subsequently, we quantified and normalised the frequency of mature miRNAs within the publicly available worm data and our primary RNA‐seq analysis of adult and larval T. suis EVs and EV‐depleted supernatants using miRdeep‐quantify. In all samples, 90 mature miRNAs were identified, 10 of which were novel sequences. Expression levels were compared to existing datasets showing expression of miRNAs in adult, larvae and the stichosome (Figure 4B,C). Two miRNAs were unique to EVs, and 11 were unique to EV‐depleted supernatants, while 16 miRNAs identified in L1 EVs were also present in both adult EVs and EV‐depleted supernatants. In total, 61 miRNAs were common to adult EVs and EV‐depleted supernatants that were not present in L1 EVs (Figure 4D), and 63 miRNAs were restricted to adult worms and not present in L1 EVs. When absolute miRNA levels were studied, significant differences in transcription were evident between adult EVs and supernatant (Figure 4E). Comparing adult and L1 EV miRNAs in a differential transcription analysis revealed 16 miRNAs to be upregulated in the L1 EV samples, but these RNAs were also present in the adult EVs at varying abundances. Seven miRNAs were upregulated in L1 EVs, including tsu‐miR‐9‐o1‐5p, tsu‐miR‐9‐o2‐5p and tsu‐miR‐7‐5p, and 17 miRNAs were upregulated in adult EV vs. L1 EV, including tsu‐miR‐29‐3p and tsu‐miR‐22‐3p (Figure 4F). Interestingly, tsu‐miR‐22‐3p was highly transcribed in adult worms, but not in the stichosome (Jex et al. 2014), and was present in adult EV samples at an enriched level compared to L1 EVs, suggesting the stichosome as an unlikely tissue source of adult EVs.

Lineage‐specific Distribution of tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and ttsu‐miR‐29‐3p Orthologues Across Helminth EV miRNA Datasets

To place these findings in the context of comparative helminth EV miRNA biology, we performed pairwise Smith–Waterman alignments of tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p against all mature miRNAs reported in published EV miRNA datasets for T. muris and the clade V nematode Heligmosomoides polygyrus bakeri (Table S1) (White et al. 2020). tsu‐miR‐22‐3p showed strong conservation with a T. muris miR‐22‐3p ortholog (≈87% identity, perfect 7/7 seed match), with sequence divergence confined to the 3′ compensatory region, a pattern consistent with functional orthology between congeners. No corresponding miR‐22 ortholog was detected in the H. polygyrus EV dataset. Conversely, tsu‐miR‐29‐3p showed no convincing match in the T. muris EV repertoire (best hit < 73% identity, no seed match ≥ 5/7), but a strong match was identified in H. polygyrus EVs to hpo‐miR‐83‐3p (≈89% identity, perfect 7/7 seed match). Importantly, miR‐83 is the historical C. elegans designation for the nematode miR‐29 family ortholog. T. suis EVs are therefore unusual among the three species in carrying both miR‐22 and miR‐29 family orthologs, while T. muris EVs carry only miR‐22 and H. polygyrus EVs carry only miR‐29/miR‐83. These observations support the interpretation, consistent with established nematode miRNA seed‐homology frameworks, that secreted helminth miRNAs draw from an ancient, conserved nematode miRNA pool but are deployed in a lineage‐specific manner and that the cross‐species functional reach of any individual parasite miRNA cannot be assumed without direct alignment.

T. suis miRNAs tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p Show Homology to Human Tumour Suppressor miRNAs

The adult EV‐enriched tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p were selected for further analysis, given the recognised transcription of human miRNAs in the colon, and T. suis mature miRNA sequences were compared to both Homo sapiens and Sus scrofa miRNA complements to establish homology. As expected, tsu‐miR‐22‐3p showed the highest homology to human and pig miRNAs, hsa‐miR‐22‐3p and ssu‐miR‐22‐3p, respectively (Figure 5A,B). In humans (and pigs) there are three members of the miR‐29 family, hsa‐miR‐29a/b/c and, despite highly similar sequences and many overlapping targets, there are known differences in transcription levels in tissue/cell types and in targets for these miRNAs. Therefore, we performed a sequence alignment that showed that tsu‐miR‐29‐3p had the highest homology to hsa‐miR‐29b‐3p (one base pair mismatch) and ssc‐miR‐29b‐3p (Figure 5C). Importantly, we identified a single region in the genome of T. trichiura corresponding to these T. suis miRNAs, suggesting that the human whipworm produces the same miRNAs as those tested here (Figure 5D).

tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p Assessment in TGF‐β‐induced Fibrosis Assay in human Kidney Cells (HKC)‐8 Cells

Having identified that orthologues of miR‐22 and miR‐29, mammalian miRNAs that are among the best‐characterised endogenous regulators of fibrosis and tumour suppression, were enriched in T. suis adult EV preparations, we next asked whether these parasite tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p miRNA sequences retain conserved regulatory capacity on mammalian targets. The HKC‐8 cell line is a well‐characterised in vitro model of TGF‐β–induced renal fibrosis in which both miR‐22 and miR‐29 family members have established anti‐fibrotic activity, and in which COL1A1 protein induction provides a quantitative readout of regulatory function. We used a TGF‐β‐stimulated epithelial (HKC‐8 cell) model that is a well‐characterised in vitro model of TGF‐β‐induced renal fibrosis in which both miR‐22 and miR‐29 family members have established anti‐fibrotic activity. Collagen (COL1A1) induction provides a functional readout of regulatory potential. We therefore assessed COL1A1 protein levels following transfection of synthetic parasite tsu‐miR‐22‐3p or tsu‐miR‐29‐3p mimics (Figure 6A). COL1A1 protein levels were quantified using Western blot analysis. tsu‐miR‐29‐3p reduced COL1A1 expression compared with both TGF‐β‐treated cells and TGF‐β‐treated cells transfected with a scrambled control miRNA. In contrast, tsu‐miR‐22‐3p induced only a modest, non‐significant reduction in COL1A1 expression (Figure 6A). The tsu‐miR‐29‐3p mimic significantly reduced collagen I protein in this assay, supporting conserved sequence‐level regulatory capacity; the tsu‐miR‐22‐3p mimic showed a smaller effect under the conditions tested.

Parasite miRNA Mimics Show Conserved Target Regulation in SW480 Cells by Quantitative Proteomics

To evaluate broader conserved regulatory capacity, we transfected SW480 cells, chosen for low endogenous miR‐22/miR‐29 expression with parasite miRNA mimics and performed quantitative proteomics. This approach captures miRNA effects on translation and protein abundance and allows comparison of downregulated proteins with experimentally validated mammalian targets.

To ensure the validity of our results, we selected a proteomics approach over transcriptomics, given the nature of miRNA inhibition of translation, which can be mediated by RNA decay or steric inhibition of translation. To control for the effects of the transfection carrier and non‐specific effects of nucleic acid delivery into cells, we transfected scrambled control RNA and proteins that were downregulated at 48 and 72 h post‐transfection by the scrambled RNA, were removed from our analysis as non‐specific (Figure 6B). This experiment showed that, at 48 h, 9 proteins were downregulated by tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and 10 proteins were downregulated by tsu‐miR‐29‐3p transfection. At 72 h, tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p induced the downregulation of 25 and 34 proteins, respectively (Figure 6C–E).

To determine whether T. suis miRNAs function in a similar manner to their tumour suppressor homologs, downregulated proteins were compared with experimentally validated targets of the human miRNAs, which showed 50% of proteins downregulated by tsu‐miR‐22‐3p were known targets of the human hsa‐miR‐22‐3p (Figure S3). When comparing with targets of hsa‐miR‐29a‐3p, hsa‐miR‐29b‐3p, and hsa‐miR‐29c‐3p, most targets were common to hsa‐miR‐29b‐3p, in accordance with the sequence analysis conducted above. However, there was a significant overlap between validated targets of the miR‐29 family; after aggregating all common targets, we showed that 78% of proteins downregulated by tsu‐miR‐29‐3p were validated targets of the human miR‐29‐3p family (Figure S3). Together, these data show that the two specific T. suis EV miRNAs investigated in depth here, tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p, share both sequence and target‐regulatory features with their homolog miRNAs. Whether this conservation extends to other T. suis EV‐associated miRNAs will require systematic functional testing of additional candidates. Subsequent analysis of publicly available transcriptomics datasets confirmed that MIR22 and MIR29B are downregulated in colon cancer compared with normal colonic tissues (Figure 7A,B).

T. suis miRNAs tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p Target Key Oncogenes Impacting Survival in Colon Cancer

To ascertain the biological relevance of the proteins downregulated by tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p in human colon cancer, we assessed each gene individually using the TCGA‐Colon cancer cohort. Gene expression of tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p target proteins was assessed in tumour versus normal control tissue (Figure 7C), and the results showed a number of genes that were significantly differentially regulated, with 37 upregulated and 26 downregulated in tumours.

To estimate the effects of gene expression on overall survival, Kaplan‐Meier graphs were generated using stages 3 and 4 colon cancer patients. In summary, of 82 proteins significantly downregulated by these miRNAs, the upregulation of 19 of these genes is associated with significant reductions in overall survival, whilst increased expression of 6 genes is associated with longer survival (Figure S4). As an exploratory in silico analysis, these findings suggest that several proteins downregulated by the parasite miRNA mimics are clinically relevant in colon cancer; however, the survival associations are hypothesis‐generating and do not establish a net biological effect of the miRNAs in vivo. Survival curves are presented for the tsu‐miR‐22‐3p targets CLIC4 and GTF3C4 (Figure 7D,E) and for the tsu‐miR‐29‐3p targets CRMP1 and ADSS.

Discussion

Our study provides the first demonstration that adult T. suis produces extracellular vesicles and selectively packages functional miRNAs into these EVs. Using orthogonal, physical, and proteomic validation approaches, we confirm that the secretome contains bona fide EVs enriched for canonical helminth and mammalian EV‐associated proteins (Kalra et al. 2012; Sotillo et al. 2020; Hansen et al. 2019; Eichenberger et al. 2018; Mardahl et al. 2025), establishing a foundation for understanding vesicle‐mediated host–parasite interactions in whipworms.

A major finding of this work is that the potent anti‐inflammatory activity of T. suis ES on LPS‐induced macrophage TNF release is not recapitulated by EVs and instead resides within the non‐vesicular ESP fraction. The principal anti‐inflammatory effector of T. suis ES has been previously characterised by our group as the lipid mediator prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) (Laan et al. 2017), which signals through host EP receptors to suppress macrophage TNF production. As a small lipid mediator, PGE2 would be expected to partition with the soluble rather than vesicular fraction during SEC, the localisation of TNF‐suppressive activity to fractions 19–24, and its absence from EV‐containing fractions at exposure levels matching the EV protein content of biologically active ESP, is consistent with prior characterisation of the T. suis ES anti‐inflammatory machinery. The functional immunomodulation experiments described here used EVs from adult T. suis worms; EV cargo and function in helminths are well established to be developmentally regulated, and our data therefore do not exclude the possibility that L1, L2 or L3 T. suis EVs may have distinct immunomodulatory properties. The contrast with EVs from A. suum and Nippostrongylus brasiliensis suggests that EV‐mediated immunomodulation is not a universal feature of nematode EVs but is instead species‐ and stage‐specific, an interpretation supported by concordant observations from Angiostrongylus cantonensis and T. muris where EVs likewise display limited immunomodulation (Eichenberger et al. 2018; Wan et al. 2021), observations should also be interpreted in the context of Trichuris’s phylogenetic position as a clade I nematode, distinct from the clade III (Ascaris) and clade V (Nippostrongylus, Heligmosomoides) species in which immunomodulatory EV functions have been characterized (Eichenberger et al. 2018; Borup et al. 2025).

Our miRNA profiling reveals that T. suis exhibits strong developmental and compartment‐specific control of miRNA release, including selective enrichment of miRNAs into adult EVs. Notably, tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p showed high homology to human miR‐22‐3p and miR‐29b‐3p, respectively, both well‐established regulators of fibrosis and proposed tumour suppressor miRNAs (Zhang et al. 2012; Xia et al. 2017; Leng et al. 2021; Inoue et al. 2015). These EV‐enriched parasite miRNAs were functional in human epithelial cells, suppressing conserved oncogenic targets with substantial overlap with experimentally validated human miRNA targets. However, only tsu‐miR‐29‐3p was shown to downregulate COL1A1 protein expression in an in vitro model of renal fibrosis. Taken altogether the cross‐species functional conservation observed here strengthens a biological plausibility that these parasite miRNAs may influence host cellular processes.

Why whipworms would export orthologues of antifibrotic, tumour‐suppressor miRNAs remain an open question. One possibility is that delivery of such miRNAs helps limit host tissue damage in the niche where T. suis resides. Helminths frequently exploit TGF‐β‐driven wound‐healing and anti‐inflammatory pathways (Worthington et al. 2013), yet excessive fibrosis would compromise the parasite's long‐term survival. Human miR‐22‐3p and miR‐29 family members negatively regulate collagen deposition and fibroblast activation, and the synergistic antifibrotic action of these miRNAs is well established. Thus, selective packaging of these miRNAs into EVs may reflect an evolved strategy to fine‐tune host tissue remodelling while avoiding pathological scarring, although this hypothesis requires direct experimental testing. Several caveats to the interpretation of these findings require acknowledgment. First, our SW480 proteomic dataset establishes that synthetic tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p mimics regulate conserved human targets, but we have not directly demonstrated that EV exposure (rather than mimic transfection) recapitulates this regulation. Demonstrating overlap between EV‐treated and mimic‐treated proteomic profiles is a key experiment required to formally establish EV‐mediated functional delivery and remains an active line of investigation. While the focus of our study was the effect of these miRNAs on colonic epithelial cells, the broad number of targets of individual miRNAs suggests that these miRNAs may exert distinct effects in other cell types, including immune cells that requires further investigation. For instance, recent work has shown that human miR‐29 targets T‐bet in T cells, thereby limiting Th1 responses and mitigating colitis (Czopik et al. 2024).

Surprisingly, this work suggests that T. suis and T. muris appear to diverge. T. muris infection downregulates host miR‐29, can induce dysplasia in the absence of chemical carcinogens, and triggers host miRNA responses linked to wound healing (Hayes et al. 2017; Layton et al. 2025). Of note, neoplasms induced by T. muris infection were reduced by blockade of T reg cells, suggesting helminth‐mediated immune suppression may promote conditions favourable to oncogenesis (Hayes et al. 2017). It is unknown whether T. suis also downregulates host miR‐29 and whether secretion of tsu‐miR‐29 is a compensatory measure. Direct alignment of tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p against the published T. muris EV miRNA dataset (White et al. 2020) confirms that T. muris EVs do contain a miR‐22 ortholog (perfect 7/7 seed identity with tsu‐miR‐22‐3p) but no detectable miR‐29 family ortholog. The same alignment against published H. polygyrus bakeri EV miRNAs identifies a miR‐29 family ortholog (hpo‐miR‐83‐3p, the miR‐83 designation reflecting historical C. elegans nomenclature for the nematode miR‐29 ortholog) but no miR‐22 ortholog. T. suis therefore appears unusual in packaging both miR‐22 and miR‐29 family orthologs into EVs (Table S1). A potential yet speculative co‐evolutionary explanation is that parasite strategies may align with host longevity; given that whipworms can survive for years in humans (at least 6 years have been documented), there is likely stronger evolutionary pressure to minimize pathology in longer‐lived hosts compared to short‐lived species.

Finally, it is important to note that the miR‐22 and miR‐29 families are ancient. Therefore, we believe that this is not an example of convergent evolution of miRNA sequence but a potential repurposing of endogenous miRNAs via excretion in EVs. These findings also raise broader comparative questions. The helminths classified as Group I carcinogens are characterised by profound, chronic fibrosis, yet orthologues of tsu‐miR‐29‐3p are absent in these species and orthologues of miR‐22 show mismatches in the seed region that may impair function in human tissues (Stroehlein et al. 2018; Ovchinnikov et al. 2017). This may be a result of the massive reorganisation of miRNA complements in trematodes compared to nematodes (Fromm et al. 2013).

In summary, our data redefine the functional landscape of the T. suis secretome. While the canonical anti‐inflammatory activity resides in non‐vesicular ESP components, EVs carry miRNAs with the potential to modulate host tissue remodelling pathways independently of immune suppression. This previously unrecognised axis expands current models of helminth–host interaction and suggests that whipworm EVs may influence epithelial integrity and fibrosis in ways relevant to parasite persistence and possibly cancer biology. Further work is now needed to determine whether these parasite‐derived miRNAs are delivered directly via EVs and act on host tissues in vivo and whether similar strategies are employed or absent across other helminth species and the implications in fibrosis.

Methods

T. suis Excretory/Secretory Products (ESP) and Soluble Products (SP) Generation

Embryonated T. suis eggs were washed three times, hatched by mechanical stimulation for release of stage one larvae (L1) and incubated for 24 h at 37°C (5% CO2) in RPMI containing Antibiotic‐Antimycotic (Gibco) Media was collected and stored at –80°C until further processing. T. suis adult worms were obtained from the caecum and colon of pigs, necropsied seven weeks after inoculation with a single dose of 10,000 infective eggs. After collection, worms of both sexes were washed four times for 15 min in HBSS plus four times one hour in RPMI‐AA + Gentamicin (Gibco) and incubated for three days, at a density of 30 worms/15 mL at 37°C (5% CO2) for ESP production. For T. suis adults, the media was exchanged and collected as worm‐conditioned media every 24 h and stored at −80°C until further processing. T. suis soluble product (SP) was generated as previously described, briefly, T. suis worms were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen prior to homogenisation in PBS and sterile filtration using Minisart (Sartorius) 0.2 µm filters (Laan et al. 2017). Endotoxin level of SP was below detection limits assessed using ToxinSensor Chromogenic LAL Endotoxin Assay Kit (Genscript). The Danish Animal Experiments Inspectorate (Permission 2015‐15‐0201‐00760) approved the experiment and used procedures.

EV Enrichment by Ultracentrifugation (UC) for miRNA and Proteomic Analysis and Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) for AFM and Functional Studies

ESP from worm‐conditioned media was pre‐cleared by centrifugation (2000 × g for 15 min) prior to concentration on Amicon centrifugal filters with a 3 kDa cut‐off (Millipore). EVs from ESP were purified for RNA sequencing (three replicates) and proteomic analysis (three replicates) by differential centrifugation as follows: 300 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 15 min and 10,000 × g for 30 min with ultracentrifugation (UC) at 110,000 × g (40.800 RPM Ti‐50 rotor). The supernatant from the first UC (EV‐depleted ESP) was collected and used for RNA sequencing and proteome analysis. The UC pellet was then washed once in PBS prior to re‐isolation by UC at 110,000 × g and the resulting EV pellet was processed for RNA sequencing and proteome analysis.

Adult T. suis EVs for atomic force microscopy and functional cell studies were isolated using size exclusion chromatography (SEC) using qEVoriginal/70 nm columns (iZON Science Ltd.) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol by loading of 500 µL of ESP that had been concentrated using Pierce centrifugal filters with a 100 kDa cutoff (Thermo Scientific). Fractions were collected as 0.5 mL SEC fraction volume with fractions 7–10 and 19–24 pooled but not concentrated We have previously determined that the use of either UC or SEC is sufficient for selective enrichment of small EVs and to deplete EVs from non‐vesicle secretory products such as free proteins or nucleic acids in the worm secretome (Borup et al. 2022; Hansen et al. 2019).

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

The concentration and size of particles in the EV sample from UC was measured on a Nanosight LM10 (Malvern, UK) as previously described (Hansen et al. 2019; Whitehead et al. 2023). Size and concentration of particles in each fraction from SEC were determined using the Nanosight 300. Filtered PBS (0.2 µm) was used as a diluent. The camera settings and detecti were maintained for analysis of all fractions. A constant flow of 20 µL/min was used to infuse the samples into the system and results for each sample were aggregated from five 30 s video captures. NTA software 3.2 (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) was used for the analysis.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

AFM was used to estimate the size and stiffness of individual T. suis EVs via a morphometric mechanical characterisation as described elsewhere (Ridolfi et al. 2020, 2023). Briefly, samples were deposited on borosilicate glass coverslips (Menzel Gläser) coated with poly‐l‐lysine (MW 100k, Sigma‐Aldrich). Glass slides were first cleaned in 3:1 H2SO4/H2O2 ‘Pirañha’ solution and rinsed with ultrapure water, then sonicated (Elmasonic Elma S30H) in acetone, isopropanol and ultrapure water (Millipore Simplicity UV) for 30’ each. Clean slides were exposed for 5’ to a 30 mA air plasma (Pelco Easiglow), incubated for 30’ in a 0.01 mg/mL PLL solution in 100 mM borate buffer (pH 8.33) at room temperature, rinsed with ultrapure water and dried with nitrogen. A 10 µL droplet of the EV sample was deposited on the PLL‐functionalized glass slide, left to adsorb for 30 min at 4°C then inserted in the AFM fluid cell without further rinsing. The concentration of each sample was adjusted in successive technical repetitions to maximise the surface density of non‐adjoining vesicles.

AFM images were collected on a Multimode8 microscope (Bruker, USA) equipped with a Nanoscope V controller and JV piezo scanner. Samples were measured in a sealed fluid cell filled with ultrapure water using ScanAsyst Fluid+ probes (Bruker, USA). Raw AFM images were processed with Gwyddion 2.61 (Nečas and Klapetek 2012) for background subtraction, then analysed with custom Python scripts to extract two parameters for each vesicle: its original diameter in solution, and the contact angle it assumed upon substrate adhesion. The latter was demonstrated to be correlated with the vesicle's mechanical stiffness.

LC–MS/MS and Proteomic Data Analysis of T. suis ESP, EV‐depleted Supernatants and EVs

Proteomic analysis of T. suis ESP, EV‐depleted supernatants (SN) and EVs was performed in bioloigical triplicates using UPLC‐nano ESI MS/MS as previously described using modified filter‐aided sample preparation (FASP) (Boysen et al. 2020; Hansen et al. 2019). 100 µg of protein were reduced using 12 mM tris‐(2‐carboxyethyl) phosphine (Thermo Fisher) and alkylated using 50 mM chloroacetamide (Sigma‐Aldrich). Digestion proceeded overnight at 37°C using a 1:50 trypsin/protein ratio.

Two technical replicates of each of the three biological replicates were analysed with a UPLC‐nanoESI MS/MS with a RSLC nanopump module (Dionex) coupled to an Exactive mass spectrometer HF (Thermo Fisher). Samples were loaded using reversed phase Acclaim PepMap RSLC C18 (2 cm) column (Dionex) and then separated using an Acclaim PepMap RSLC C18 (75 cm) column (Dionex). The mass spectrometer was ran in positive mode (m/z 375–1400), up to 12 precursor ions with a mass window of m/z 1.6 based on the highest intensity for HCD fragmenting. The normalised collision energy was 27. Selected precursors were excluded dynamically for fragmentation for 30 s. Protein identification was performed in MaxQuant by querying tryptic peptides against T. suis female and male proteomes, UP000030758 and UP000030764, respectively. Data analysis and filtering was performed in Perseus (v1.6.14.0) with two (or more) Razor and unique peptides and 2/3 valid values in at least one group for protein intensities were required for protein differential expression quantification (Tyanova et al. 2016). Protein differential abundance analysis was performed using MaxQuant LFQ intensities. Zero LFQ intensity values were treated as missing values prior to log2 transformation. Technical replicate values were collapsed at the biological replicate level by averaging log2‐transformed LFQ intensities; the EV1b technical replicate was excluded based on prior quality control. Proteins were retained for analysis if they had at least two razor plus unique peptides and were not annotated by MaxQuant as reverse hits, potential contaminants, or site‐only identifications. Pairwise differential abundance was assessed using limma linear modelling with empirical Bayes moderation, using proteins with sufficient non‐missing biological replicate values in both groups. P‐values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure, FDR ≤ 0.05. For visualisation of shared and unique proteins between samples, Venn diagrams were created using Venny v2.1. Protein blast, mapping and GO annotation was performed using OmicsBox (Götz et al. 2008). The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD029915.

Protein Quantification

Protein concentration of all worm products and qEV fractions were determined using the Pierce BCA Kit as described by the manufacturer (ThermoFisher). The purity of EVs used for subsequent cell stimulations was estimated using particle to protein ratio as described by Webber and Clayton (Webber and Clayton 2013). Example calculation for fraction 9: ∼3 × 1010 particles/mL/30 µg/mL = ∼109 to represent particles/protein (µg).

EV Labelling

Adult T. suis EVs, isolated using SEC, were fluorescently labelled using 3,3′‐dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorate (DiO). Briefly, EVs were incubated in 10 µM DiO in PBS for 1 h at room temperature prior to re‐isolation using UC at 110,000 × g (40.800 RPM Ti‐50 rotor) for 70 min. Pellets were washed with PBS and EVs re‐isolated at 110,000 × g for 70 min. Dye controls were prepared in the same way in the absence of EVs as per EV guidelines (Welsh et al. 2024; White et al. 2023).

Functional Analysis: Uptake and Cell Stimulations

Passages 12–25 of the monocytic leukemic cell line THP‐1 (TIB‐202) purchased from ATCC were used for EV uptake and cell stimulations. To stimulate macrophage polarisation, 100 nm Phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA, Sigma‐Aldrich) and 10% heat‐inactivated Fetal Bovine Serum (Biowest) was added to culture media (RPMI 1640 with stable Glutamine, Biowest). For EV uptake studies, THP‐1 cells were polarised and seeded at 100,000 cells per well in chamber slides. After 24 h of macrophage polarisation, the media was exchanged to DMEM (Biowest) +10%FBS and allowed to rest for 24 h. Cells were incubated overnight with unlabelled or DiO labelled EVs at low (4000 vesicles/cell) or high dose (8000 vesicles/cell). Cells were washed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde prior to mounting and counterstaining with ProLong Diamond antifade mountant with DAPI and visualised using a Leica DM 2000 microscope. Images were processed in ImageJ 1.52a (NIH).

For cell stimulations, THP‐1 cells were likewise polarised to macrophages using PMA and seeded at 50,000 cells per well in 100 µL media volume and 24 h later, worm products were added as follows: EVs were added to cells in a per macrophage manner, that is, 0, 6250 and 25,000 EVs were added per macrophage seeded in the culture well. ESP and EV‐depleted ESP were added in incremental doses using the protein concentration determined by BCA. 10 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS clone O26:B6, Sigma‐Aldrich) was added 30 min post‐worm product stimulation. 24 h after LPS addition, supernatants were harvested, and TNF release was measured using the Human TNF‐alpha DuoSet ELISA as described by the manufacturer (R&D Systems). Mean and standard deviation (sd) of three independent experiments shown, statistical significance was determined using ANOVA with correction by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test in GraphPad Prism 10.2.

Small RNA Sequencing and miRNA Annotation

Total RNA was isolated from EVs derived from adult worm EVs, larvae L1 EVs and the adult EV‐depleted supernatants (SN), in triplicate. The miRCURY RNA Isolation Kit—Cell & Plant was used according to the manufacturer's protocol (Exiqon A/S). The remaining procedures were carried out at Exiqon A/S, Vedbæk, Denmark. This involved QC, small RNA library preparation and size selection (18–70 nts). The samples were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq run (1 × 50 bp single‐end read) (Illumina Inc.), providing an average of 49.5 M raw reads per sample. Raw reads were adapter trimmed, 5′‐AGATCGGAAGAGC‐3′, Phred quality filtered (Q = 30), length filtered (18–27 nt) using the software package miRTrace in qc mode (Kang et al. 2018). Since there was no miRNA complement available for T. suis, we used miRMiner (Wheeler et al. 2009) to create one. We manually curated the miRMiner output following annotation criteria by Fromm et al. (2015) and using the curated miRNA gene database miRGeneDB 2.1 (Fromm et al. 2022). We used the perl script ‘quantify’ to count the frequency of reads in each sample that mapped against the T. suis miRNA complement using miRDeep2 (Friedländer et al. 2012). The resulting count matrix, and metadata.txt file was used as input for differential expression analysis using R package DEseq2 (Love et al. 2014).

TGF‐β‐Induced Fibrosis Assay in HKC‐8 Cells

HKC‐8 (Human Kidney Proximal Tubular Epithelial) cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific‐Waltham, MA, United States), supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States) and 50 U/mL Penicillin‐Streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and maintained at a maximum of 80% confluence.

HKC‐8 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 6‐well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with serum‐free DMEM for 24 h. Cells were then transfected with tsu‐MiR‐22‐3p, tsu‐MiR‐29‐3p miRIDIAN miRNA mimics (Horizon Discovery Biosciences), or a scrambled miRNA control (mirVana, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). The miRNAs were diluted in Opti‐MEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States) and mixed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). This was incubated at room temperature for 5 min to allow complex formation. Each well was transfected with 25 pmol of miRNA and 7.5 µL Lipofectamine RNAiMAX, and 30 min after transfection, 10 ng/mL Transforming Growth Factor Beta (TGF‐β; Merck, Rahway, NJ, United States) was added to the culture medium to induce fibrosis. Following 24 h of incubation, HKC‐8 cells were harvested for protein extraction.

Western Blotting

HKC‐8 cells were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and lysed using M‐PER Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). Total protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Protein samples were mixed with 2% sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) and dithiothreitol (DTT). Equal amounts of protein were then loaded onto a 12% Criterion TGX Stain‐Free gel (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Proteins from the gel were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane using the Trans‐Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Total protein bands were visualised using No‐Stain Protein Labelling Reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States) and captured with the Azure 600 Western Blot Imager (Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA, United States). The membrane was subsequently blocked with 5% skimmed milk in TBS containing 0.1% Tween‐20 (TBS‐T) for 1 h at room temperature. Following blocking, the membrane was washed three times with TBS‐T and incubated overnight at 4°C with a collagen one primary antibody (1:500, ab34710, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom). The blot was then washed three times in TBS‐T and incubated with the secondary antibody (P0448, Dako, Agilent Technologies) for one hour at room temperature. After final washes with TBS‐T, protein bands were detected using ECL‐Prime detection reagent (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, United States) and visualised using the Azure 600 Western Blot Imager. Western blot band intensity was quantified using ImageJ (1.53t) software, with signal intensities normalised to total protein levels.

Transfection of SW480 Colon Cancer Cells with Tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and Tsu‐miR‐29‐3p

Colorectal adenocarcinoma (KRAS:G12V and TP53:R273H; P309S mutant, microsatellite stable) SW480 cells (Sigma Aldrich) were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6‐well plates and cultured in DMEM+Pen/Strep prior to transfection with 10 nM (20 pmol/well) tsu‐miR‐22‐3p, tsu‐miR‐29‐3p miRIDIAN miRNA mimics (Horizon Discovery Biosciences) or mission control negative control (Sigma Aldrich) in the absence of antibiotics using Lipofectamine RNAimax (Thermofisher), as per manufactures instructions, in triplicate. Cells were harvested in RIPA buffer with Halt protease inhibitors (Pierce) at 48 and 72 h post‐transfection prior to proteomic analysis.

Proteomic Analysis of Transfected SW480 Cells

Proteomics analysis was performed as previously described (Schmid et al. 2023). Briefly, 20 µg of protein were separated by SDS‐PAGE followed by reduction using tris(2‐carboxyethyl)phosphine TCEP (Sigma Aldrich) blocking of cysteines by alkylation using bromoacetamide (Sigma Aldrich) and in‐gel trypsin (Sigma Aldrich) digestion. Peptides were subsequently purified Using PepClean C18 Spin Columns (Pierce) and then evaporated using a speed vac.

Samples were analysed by nano liquid chromatography (Easy‐nLC 1200, Thermo Scientific)‐tandem mass spectrometry (Q‐Exactive HF‐X Hybrid Quadrupole Orbitrap, Thermo Scientific). Peptides were trapped by a pre‐column (Acclaim PepMap 100 C18, pore size: 100 Å, particle diameter: 3 µm, inner diameter: 75 µm, length: 2 cm, Thermo Scientific) and separated further with a reverse phase analytical column (PepMap RSLC C18, pore size: 100 Å, particle diameter: 2 µm, inner diameter 75 µm, length 25 cm, Thermo Scientific) using a 80 min gradient from 5% to 90% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid at a 270 nL/min flowrate. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive mode and higher collision dissociation (HCD) and normalised collision energy of 29 was used for peptide fragmentation. The full scan/MS1 resolution was 60,000, automatic gain control (AGC) target was 3 × 106, maximum injection time was 80 ms, and scan range from 352 to 1700 m/z. The fragmentation scan/MS2 resolution was 15,000. The MS was operated in data dependent mode and up to 12 of the most intense peaks were fragmented. Dynamic exclusion was set to 15 s and unassigned and single charge ions were excluded from fragmentation.

Data Analysis

Proteins were identified and quantified using MaxQuant (Cox and Mann 2008) (version 1.5.3.30) with its Andromeda algorithm against the Homo sapiens isoform replete database retrieved from Uniprot. Settings included enzyme: trypsin, max missed cleavage sites: 2, Precursor mass tolerance: 10 ppm, fragment mass tolerance: 0.02 Da, dynamic modification: oxidation, static modification: carbamidomethyl, false discovery rate (FDR): 0.01 at protein and peptide level. The ‘match between runs’ was applied and iBAQ data were used for quantification using Perseus (Tyanova et al. 2016) with fold change ±2 and permutation‐based FDR < 0.05 were deemed significant. Proteins that were significantly downregulated or upregulated at the same time point in scrambled control treated cells were removed from further analysis as being non‐specific. Pathway analysis and GO enrichments were performed using Enrichr (Xie et al. 2021) and downregulated proteins were compared to experimentally validated hsa‐miR‐22‐3p and hsa‐miR‐29‐3p targets derived from TarBase v.9 (Skoufos et al. 2024).

Human miRNA Expression in Colon Cancer and TCGA Expression and Survival Analysis

Expression of hsa‐miR‐22 and hsa‐miR‐29 in cancer patients was determined via accessing publicly available datasets. For hsa‐miR‐22 expression was retrieved from GDS4515 for comparison of microsatellite‐unstable colorectal tumour and normal colonic mucosa (Alhopuro et al. 2012). For hsa‐miR‐29b, expression levels were compared for tumour (MSI and hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer aggregated) and normal colonic controls and retrieved from GSE30454 (Balaguer et al. 2011) and analysed using GEO2R (Clough et al. 2024).

The expression in TCGA‐COAD cohort of genes corresponding to proteins significantly downregulated by tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p was compared to normal controls as follows. The colorectal adenocarcinoma tumour RNA‐seq dataset from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) was downloaded via the TCGA data portal (Zhu et al. 2014). RNA‐seq and clinical data was available for 481 primary solid tumours and 41 healthy tissues. Expression counts were Means of M‐values (TMM) normalised, filtered and log2 transformed before differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the limma software package (v 3.58.1) (Ritchie et al. 2015) with normal tissue as reference and tumour tissue as the comparator. All genes corresponding to proteins significantly downregulated by tsu‐miR‐22‐3p and tsu‐miR‐29‐3p were analysed.

The association of significantly downregulated proteins with clinical outcomes in colon cancer was assessed using KMplot (Kovács et al. 2023) using JetSet best probe set. Patients were stratified by median expression with stages 3 and 4 cohorts selected and overall survival assessed.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Data presented are the mean and SD of three biological replicates or three independent experiments. Differentially expressed miRNAs or proteins were determined significant with fold change ±2 and adjusted p value < 0.05. For cell immune experiments, significance was determined using ANOVA with correction by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test in GraphPad Prism 10.2.
