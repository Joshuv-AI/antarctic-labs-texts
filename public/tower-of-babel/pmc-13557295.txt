Childhood Rosacea: Clinical, Diagnostic, and Therapeutic Insights into an Underestimated Pediatric Condition
Authors: Vincenzo Piccolo, Julia Nowowiejska-Purpurowicz, Andrea Bassi, Dirk Van Gysel, Alex Alexopoulos, Talia Kakourou, Caterina Mariarosaria Giorgio, Giuseppe Argenziano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRosacea is a very frequent skin disease; however, its prevalence in children is underestimated, and there are not enough data on this dermatosis in the pediatric population.ObjectiveThis study aimed to give comprehensive insight into childhood rosacea (CR), pointing out predisposing factors, clinical presentation, diagnostic clues, and therapy of this subgroup of the disease spectrum.MethodsThis is a narrative review of the existing data on rosacea in children.ResultsChildren affected by rosacea often present with recurrent erythema of the convex areas of the face as an early sign of the disease, mostly occurring after cold/heat/sun exposure or triggered by crying/playing/exercise. Phymatous lesions do not occur in children, and Morbihan disease has been very rarely reported. On the other hand, special rosacea subtypes, such as periorificial dermatitis, granulomatous rosacea, or idiopathic facial aseptic granuloma, are observed more often in children. The diagnosis is usually made based on the clinical picture; however, dermoscopy, and in some cases other imaging techniques, may aid the diagnosis. It is still a matter of debate whether a child affected by rosacea will become an adult with rosacea over time. The holistic approach to therapy of rosacea in children should include in particular the avoidance of triggering factors, proper skin care, and finally, adequate pharmacological treatment. Although procedural therapeutic options are frequently used in adults, their use in children should be tailored to the patient’s age and discussed with the parents or caregivers.ConclusionRosacea may also occur in children. Therapeutic options do not significantly differ from those in adults; however, they must be adjusted to the child’s age.Keywords: Rosacea, Childhood, Children, Pediatric, Therapy

Introduction

Rosacea is a very common skin disease, usually occurring in fair-skinned adult individuals. It commonly presents with variable, mostly facial clinical manifestations ranging from recurrent/persistent erythema to telangiectasia, burning sensation, papules, pustules, and, in the final stages, rhinophyma [1]. Although the etiology is not yet thoroughly understood, genetic predisposition, lighter skin phototype, humoral/hormonal factors, tobacco, diet, alcohol, weathering, Demodex folliculorum, and some medications may influence the appearance and recurrences of rosacea lesions [1].

Childhood rosacea (CR) is an underreported condition with just a few reviews published on the topic [2].

Objective

The aim of our study was to give comprehensive insight into CR, highlighting the predisposing factors, clinical presentation, diagnostic clues, and therapy of this special subgroup of the disease spectrum.

Predisposing Factors

In contrast to adults, limited data are available on the predisposing factors associated with CR. Moreover, most triggers often present in adults, including smoking, alcohol, spices, and hormonal factors [2], are missing in children.

Regarding genetic predisposition, it is highly likely to play a role in CR [1], as many affected children have one or both parents affected by rosacea (personal observation).

Fair phototype makes a child’s skin prone to the development of rosacea [1,2], perhaps because of its hypersensitivity to climate, environmental factors, and reduced tolerance to sun.

A very interesting discussion may be held about Demodex involvement in children. We know that the role of Demodex in adult rosacea is still controversial because its presence is often encountered even in healthy individuals [1,3]. Contrarily, finding Demodex on facial skin before puberty is highly unusual. Ayelsworth et al. found that Demodex folliculorum and Demodex brevis are exceptionally found in cutaneous biopsies of healthy individuals before the age of 20, with an impressive increase in older patients [4]. Based on this assertion, it could be argued that, whereas finding Demodex in adults on microscopy does not add any diagnostic support, revealing Demodex in children with rosacea may be a good diagnostic indicator, as it is normally absent in the healthy control pediatric population.

Clinical Presentation

CR does not essentially differ from the adult form. However, the existence of mild or atypical forms as well as special subsets of the disease, along with the high frequency of facial dermatitis in this age group, makes the diagnosis challenging and often delayed (Table 1).

As an early sign of the disease, children affected by rosacea often present with recurrent erythema of the convex areas of the face, mostly occurring after cold/heat/sun exposure or triggered by crying/playing/exercise. Over time, recurrent erythema tends to become persistent, thus producing on the face the appearance of telangiectasia, namely, clinically visible linear dilated vessels on the cheeks and nose (Figure 1A) [2].

Clinical pictures of rosacea variants. A) classic papulopustular rosacea: erythema, papules, pustules on the cheek; B) perioral dermatitis: numerous small papules disseminated around the lips; C) granulomatous rosacea: numerous small firm orange papules; D) IFAG: solitary pinkish-reddish nodule on the cheek; E) ocular rosacea: recurrent chalazia, with persistent erythema on the cheeks visible.

Another common but later sign of CR is the occurrence of papules/pustules on the face without comedones, different from acne. On palpation, papules often show an increased consistency, while pustules may be spiny (Figure 1A) [2].

The late, advanced stages with rhinophyma are not seen in the pediatric population [2].

Special Clinical Subsets

Periorificial dermatitis: CR may present as a unique manifestation with multiple tiny papules/pustules and erythema usually affecting perioral and perinasal skin, with a typical sparing of the skin closer to the orifices (Figure 1B). It is commonly associated with corticosteroid use (topical or inhaled), “physical” sunscreens, and exposure to rosin (an ingredient in flavored chewing gum) [5].

It is commonly associated with corticosteroid use (topical or inhaled), “physical” sunscreens, and exposure to rosin (an ingredient in flavored chewing gum) [5].

Granulomatous rosacea: It consists of infiltrated persistent red-to-orange papules and/or nodules typically affecting the face of children (Figure 1C) whose diagnosis is often missed, and mistreatment with topical steroids is started. Sometimes, different sites are simultaneously involved, for example, perioral and periocular area. Some forms of periorificial dermatitis may evolve into granulomatous rosacea, especially if misdiagnosed and improperly treated. Some scientists believe that lupus miliaris disseminatus faciei (LMDF) is a variant of granulomatous rosacea; however, the main difference between rosacea and LMDF is the lack of vascular abnormalities and exacerbation after sun exposure in LMDF [5].

Idiopathic facial aseptic granuloma (IFAG): It consists of single to multiple red-to-orange infiltrated granulomatous nodules on the face, typically affecting cheeks or eyelids (Figure 1D). Formerly ‘pyodermite froide du visage’, IFAG is typically sterile and tends to resolve even spontaneously over time, sometimes draining seropurulent material and leaving hyperpigmented scars [6]. Dermoscopy findings are reported below. Ultrasound examination may help in the diagnosis, typically showing a well-defined hypoechoic lesion with no calcium deposits inside [2,7].

Ocular rosacea: It is a special atypical presentation of CR whose diagnosis is often missed and delayed. Children with ocular rosacea often report photosensitivity, epiphora, conjunctivitis, blepharitis, recurrent chalazions (Figure 1E) and, exceptionally, corneal ulcers. The ocular signs of rosacea may be very relevant in the pediatric population, and we personally believe that it is always worth screening for them, even to support a diagnosis of previously suspected CR [2].

Another atypical rosacea presentation is Morbihan disease; however, there are hardly any reported cases of this entity in children [2]. The name Morbihan originates in a area in Bretagne, France, where the first case was observed. It is considered a late stage of rosacea and is characterized by persistent erythema and symmetrical edema of the central and upper part of the face [8].

Diagnosis and Differential Diagnosis

Fortunately, the diagnosis of CR is easy in most cases, as it often presents with signs that overlap with adult forms. However, diagnostic criteria have been proposed and could be very helpful in supporting the diagnosis, mostly in mild/atypical forms and special subsets.

According to the 2019 National Rosacea Society Expert Committee, rosacea may be diagnosed if at least one of the diagnostic features occurs or if two or more major features occur, typically in a centrofacial distribution [9] (Table 2). According to Chamaillard et al., rosacea in children should be diagnosed based on at least two of the following criteria: i) facial flushing with recurrent or permanent erythema; ii) facial telangiectasia with no other causative disease; iii) papules and pustules without comedones; iv) preferential distribution of lesions on convex areas of the face; v) ocular manifestations (one of the following: relapsing chalazia, ophthalmic hyperemia, keratitis) [10]. There are no specific criteria for the diagnosis of the special subtypes of rosacea (Table 2).

Dermoscopy, as a reliable, noninvasive, non-time-consuming tool, supports the diagnosis of CR and is a useful aid in the differential diagnosis. The most common clues detected at dermoscopy are perifollicular erythema and polygonal (angulated) linear vessels and follicular findings such as pustules and white follicular linear scaling (namely ‘Demodex tails’) (Figure 3A) [13].

Dermoscopic findings in rosacea. A) classic papulopustular rosacea; dermoscopy reveals perifollicular erythema and polygonal linear vessels (arrow), and follicular findings such as pustules (yellow circle) and white follicular linear scaling (white circle); B) dermoscopy of granulomatous rosacea shows predominantly orange globules; C) dermoscopy of IFAG shows structureless white areas and blurred vessels.

Granulomatous rosacea lacks the typical dermoscopic findings of CR and is characterized by predominantly structureless orange areas (Figure 3B) [14]. As shown in a recent paper, IFAG typically shows a combination of red/orange structureless areas and blurred vessels (Figure 3C) [15].

Reflectance confocal microscopy may be used in very complex cases; however, this diagnostic technique is expensive and not widely available in clinical settings [16].

Ultrasound examination may aid the diagnosis in case of IFAG (Figure 4A–B) [2].

Biopsy is rarely required in CR. It may give diagnostic support in granulomatous rosacea and IFAG [2], thus permitting the exclusion of the relevant differential diagnoses.

Differential diagnosis of CR may be challenging for different reasons. First of all, in very young children, the rate of facial dermatoses is high, thus leading clinicians to overlook the other more common conditions. Secondly, skin diseases could coexist concurrently, thus making the strict distinction among them somewhat complex. Thirdly, the easy access to topical treatments such as corticosteroids and their consequent abuse/misuse can masquerade or alter the typical presentation of CR. Most differential diagnoses are:

Systemic lupus erythematosus, which may manifest with acute cutaneous lupus in its most widely recognized form of the ‘malar rash’, which typically spares the nasolabial folds (Figure 5A) [17].

Atopic dermatitis typically presents with itchy eczema associated with dry skin. Lesions are often seen on other body sites beyond the face. The presence of other minor signs, including keratosis pilaris, pityriasis alba, and Dennie-Morgan infraorbital folds, may help in differential diagnosis (Figure 5B) [18].

Therapy

The holistic approach to therapy of rosacea in children should include primarily the avoidance of triggering factors, proper skin care, and adequate pharmacological treatment. In adults, procedural therapeutic options are frequently used; however, in children, they should be adjusted to the patient’s age and discussed with the parents. The main problem is the lack of guidelines specific to the pediatric population, the lack of approval of topical agents for children, differences in approval between the countries, and age limits in case of tetracyclines [2].

The first goal of treatment of rosacea in children is to avoid risk factors and limit triggers such as sun/heat exposure; lifestyle changes may also be advised, in particular dietary factors and smoking (even electronic cigarettes) among adolescents [1,2].

Skin care should include cleaning the face twice a day with mild topical agents and using soothing moisturizing creams (which may also include sunscreen, simplifying the routine). It is recommended avoiding irritating activities during cleansing, such as vigorous rubbing, exfoliants, and substances that stimulate blood circulation [22].

An important therapeutic consideration is that all the medications commonly prescribed in adult rosacea are not approved for use in children, so parents should be informed about the off-label use.

Although guidelines for the treatment of rosacea in children do not exist, a large body of evidence supports the safe use of all the therapies usually given to adult patients, as described below.

Topical Treatments

Among topical agents, the ones that may be recommended are presented in Table 4 [2,21]. For mild cases, topical medications are enough [23]; they include topical metronidazole, ivermectin, and azelaic acid, with the latter preferred in adolescents with overlap between acne and rosacea. Topical pimecrolimus and tacrolimus may be another possible alternative or second line for pediatric rosacea when the abovementioned agents are not suitable [2], given their safety profile and large use for other common conditions such as atopic dermatitis. Given the stinging or burning sensation sometimes reported by the patients, patient and family support may be the key to treatment compliance. All these treatments should be associated with sunscreens applied daily and every two hours in cases of prolonged sun exposure.

Topical treatments are the same as those used in mild forms, whereas oral medications include treatments used in adults as well, but readapted in children [2].

The drug of choice in adults, i.e., tetracyclines, may be prescribed in children as well, but they are contraindicated before the age of 8 [2]. Among tetracyclines, lymecycline, doxycycline, and minocycline may be prescribed [2,21]. Doxycycline is available in most countries in two doses: 100 mg and 40 mg in slow-release (SR) capsules. Nowadays, the first-line recommendation for adults is 40 mg SR once a day. Surprisingly, lymecycline is hardly mentioned in the literature, whereas it is approved for rosacea in children older than 12 years of age in many countries. Treatment should be prolonged until remission is achieved; again, 12 weeks should be a reasonable duration. However, it may be longer in some patients [23].

Apart from age limitations, tetracyclines are usually well tolerated, even if parents should be informed about photosensitivity and potential minor bowel side effects [24]. In patients younger than 8 years or when tetracyclines are contraindicated or non-tolerated, oral macrolides are the preferred option [21].

Although there is no agreement about the drug of choice and treatment duration, interesting and very effective therapeutic schemes are used, achieving adequate response. Historically, continuous daily administration of oral erythromycin was suggested [25]. More recently, pulsed oral treatments with macrolides, in particular azithromycin, have been proposed [26].

There is no agreement about the intervals between the pulses, but weekly or every-other-week pulses are the most common schemes. Treatment duration is again a matter of debate, and it seems that at least four to six courses of macrolides are necessary. Therefore, informing parents that the results may not be evident immediately after the first cycle of treatment is concluded.

The role of macrolides in the treatment of dermatological conditions is not new, perhaps due to their potent antiinflammatory effect on skin [26]. Thus their use in rosacea is justified, as in other conditions such as acne or pityriasis lichenoides.

Other oral treatments have been used in children with rosacea, including oral isotretinoin, oral metronidazole, and most recently, oral ivermectin [2,21].

Treatment must also be tailored to the specific clinical manifestation. In cases of erythema alone, brimonidine, metronidazole, azelaic acid, and topical calcineurin inhibitors (TCI) are mainly recommended [27]. In papulopustular disease, the following agents are commonly used: azelaic acid, topical and systemic antibiotics, topical and systemic ivermectin, pimecrolimus, and isotretinoin [27].

Procedural treatment, particularly intense pulsed light (IPL) and laser therapy, are widely used in adults and are especially indicated in cases of extensive erythema and telangiectasia. IPL, Nd:YAG, and pulsed dye laser (PDL) treatment are recommended for rosacea subtype I [27].

Ocular rosacea benefits from the same systemic intervention as cutaneous rosacea, in particular oral medications. Ocular hygiene and artificial tear eyedrops should also be introduced. However, ophthalmological consultation is recommended to ensure a multidisciplinary approach in these complex patients [2]. Topical azithromycin and calcineurin inhibitors are mentioned as the first-line treatment [17].

Although IFAG tends to spontaneously resolve over time, pharmacological treatment is often recommended due to esthetic concerns: either a long course of topical ivermectin (up to one year, personal observation), oral macrolides [21], or isotretinoin [2]. An alternative strategy is watchful waiting. Surgical excision is rarely necessary [21].

Prognosis

The prognosis of childhood rosacea is good [2], in particular when the diagnosis is made correctly and immediately and proper treatment is initiated, resulting in a shorter disease duration. On the other hand, in misdiagnosed cases or atypical presentations, the disease may have a prolonged course and necessitate long-term treatments.

However, it is still a matter of debate whether a child affected by rosacea will become an adult with rosacea over time [2,21]. Contrarily, it seems that even patients with a severe form of disease in childhood do not recur in adulthood, once recovered after adequate therapy [2]. This apparently anomalous evolution suggests that pediatric rosacea is a distinctive entity.

Final Remarks and Conclusions

Rosacea is undoubtedly an underestimated condition in children. Atypical presentations and peculiar scenarios often result in misdiagnosis and erroneous treatment, making the recognition of this condition even more complex.
