Optimization of maximal quantum f \displaystyle f -divergences between unitary orbits
Hoang Minh Nguyen, Hoang An Nguyen, Cong Trinh Le

Abstract
. Maximal quantum f \displaystyle f -divergences, defined via the commutant Radon–Nikodym derivative, form a fundamental class of distinguishability measures for quantum states associated with operator convex functions. In this paper, we study the optimization of maximal quantum f \displaystyle f -divergences along unitary orbits of two quantum states. For any operator convex function f : ( 0 , + ∞ ) → ℝ \displaystyle f:(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R} , we determine the exact minimum and maximum of U ⟼ S ^ f ( ρ ∥ U ∗ σ U ) U\longmapsto\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|U^{*}\sigma U) over the unitary group, and derive explicit spectral formulas for these extremal values together with complete characterizations of the unitaries that attain them. Our approach combines the integral representation of operator convex functions with majorization theory and a unitary-orbit variational method. A key step is to show that any extremizer must commute with the reference state, which reduces the noncommutative optimization problem to a spectral permutation problem. As a consequence, the minimum is achieved by pairing the decreasing eigenvalues of ρ \displaystyle\rho and σ \displaystyle\sigma , while the maximum corresponds to pairing the decreasing eigenvalues of ρ \displaystyle\rho with the increasing eigenvalues of σ \displaystyle\sigma . Hence, the range of the maximal quantum f \displaystyle f -divergence along the unitary orbit is exactly the closed interval determined by these two extremal configurations. Finally, we compare our results with recent unitary-orbit optimization results for quantum f \displaystyle f -divergences defined via the quantum hockey-stick divergence, highlighting fundamental structural differences between the two frameworks. Our findings extend earlier extremal results for Umegaki, Rényi, and related quantum divergences, and clarify the distinct operator-theoretic nature of maximal quantum f \displaystyle f -divergences.

Introduction

Quantifying the dissimilarity between quantum states is a cornerstone problem in quantum information theory, underpinning diverse topics such as state discrimination, statistical inference, resource theories, and thermodynamics. Among the most effective mathematical tools for describing this dissimilarity are
quantum divergences
, which extend classical information distances such as the Kullback–Leibler and Rényi divergences into the noncommutative operator setting. These quantities measure the distinguishability between quantum states and form the foundation of key operational concepts including entanglement, coherence, and mutual information
[
8
]
. There are some types of Rényi divergences which were attracted by many mathematicians, e.g. Umegaki’s relative entropy, the (conventional) Rényi divergences, the sandwiched
\displaystyle\alpha
-Rényi divergences, the
\displaystyle\alpha
-
\displaystyle z
-Rényi divergences,…
[
1
,

,
7
,
8
,
13
]
.

In the classical setting, Csiszár and Ali–Silvey
[
3
]
introduced the
\displaystyle f
-divergence between two probability distributions
\displaystyle p,q
on a finite set
\displaystyle X
:

S_{f}(p\|q)=\sum_{x\in X}q(x)f\!\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right),

(1.1)

where
\displaystyle f:(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is convex. The relative entropy corresponds to
\displaystyle f(t):=\eta(t):=t\log t
, while the Rényi divergences can be expressed as (
[
8
]
)

D_{\alpha}(p\|q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\bigl(\operatorname{sign}(\alpha-1)S_{f_{\alpha}}(p\|q)\bigr),\quad f_{\alpha}(t):=\operatorname{sign}(\alpha-1)t^{\alpha}.

In this paper we consider the
maximal quantum f-divergences
(which is also called
the quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergence defined through the commutant Radon–Nikodym derivative
), a framework introduced by D. Petz and M. B. Ruskai
[
14
]
(see also the work of Hiai and Mosonyi
[
8
]
for many other kinds of quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergences and their properties). Specifically, for an operator convex function
\displaystyle f:(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and for any
\displaystyle\rho,\sigma\in\mathbb{P}_{n}^{+}
, the
maximal quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergence
of
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma
is defined as

\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|\sigma):=\text{Tr }\left[\sigma^{1/2}f(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2})\sigma^{1/2}\right]=\text{Tr }\left[\sigma f\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\right)\right].

(1.2)

For general
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}
, their maximal quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergence is defined by taking the limitation as follows.

\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|\sigma)=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho+\epsilon I\|\sigma+\epsilon I),

(1.3)

where
\displaystyle I
is the identity matrix in
\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{n}
.
This definition reflects a genuine noncommutative interaction between
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle(0,+\infty)

f(x)=f(1)+f^{\prime}(1)(x-1)+c(x-1)^{2}+\!\int_{[0,+\infty)}\!\!\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{x+s}\,d\lambda(s),\quad x\in(0,+\infty),

(1.4)

\displaystyle c\geq 0
and a positive measure
\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle[0,+\infty)
satisfying
\displaystyle\!\int_{[0,+\infty)}\!\!\frac{1}{1+s}\,d\lambda(s)<+\infty
.

Since unitary transformations preserve the spectra of density matrices, two quantum states related by unitary conjugation are physically indistinguishable. The
unitary orbit
of a quantum state
\displaystyle\rho
is

\mathbb{U}_{\rho}=\{U^{*}\rho U\mid U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}\},

(1.5)

where
\displaystyle\mathbb{U}_{n}
denotes the set of unitary matrices.

Optimization of quantum divergences
\displaystyle S_{f}(\rho\|\sigma)
over the unitary orbits captures the extremal distinguishability achievable through spectral rearrangement:

\min_{V,W\in\mathbb{U}_{n}}S_{f}(V^{*}\rho V\|W^{*}\sigma W)

(1.6)

and

\max_{V,W\in\mathbb{U}_{n}}S_{f}(V^{*}\rho V\|W^{*}\sigma W).

(1.7)

The pioneering work on these topics was carried out by Zhang and Fei (
[

]

[

[

\displaystyle f

with respect to the quantum hockey-stick divergence for convex and twice differentiable functions
\displaystyle f:(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle f(1)=0
.

For any operator convex function
\displaystyle f:(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, our main aim in this paper is to consider the following extremal problems:

\min_{V,W\in\mathbb{U}_{n}}\widehat{S}_{f}(V^{*}\rho V\|W^{*}\sigma W)

(1.8)

and

\max_{V,W\in\mathbb{U}_{n}}\widehat{S}_{f}(V^{*}\rho V\|W^{*}\sigma W).

(1.9)

Note that, for any unitary matrices
\displaystyle V
and
\displaystyle W

Hence, instead of considering problems (

\min_{U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}}\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|U^{*}\sigma U)

(1.11)

Beyond the final spectral formulas, a key novelty of this work lies in the proof techniques developed for Claims

This approach reveals that, although maximal quantum

2.1.
Notation

Throughout this paper, we use
\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{n}
(and similarly
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{n}
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}^{+}
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{U}_{n}
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{n}
) to denote the sets of complex
\displaystyle n\times n
matrices, Hermitian matrices, positive semidefinite matrices, positive definite matrices, unitary matrices, and density matrices, respectively.

Recall that a
quantum state
is represented by a density matrix
\displaystyle\rho\in\mathbb{D}_{n}
, that is,
\displaystyle\rho\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1
.

Note that, every Hermitian matrix
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
admits the decomposition

A=A_{+}-A_{-},

where
\displaystyle A_{+},A_{-}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
are the positive and negative parts of
\displaystyle A
arising from its eigenvalue decomposition.

For matrices
\displaystyle A,B\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
, we write
\displaystyle A\leq B
if
\displaystyle B-A\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
, that is, if
\displaystyle B-A
is positive semidefinite.

For
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
,
\displaystyle B\in\mathbb{P}_{n}^{+}
, denote
\displaystyle\dfrac{A}{B}:=AB^{-1}
. If
\displaystyle B\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
, the notation
\displaystyle\dfrac{A}{B}
means
\displaystyle\dfrac{A}{\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}(B+\epsilon I)}
.

For a vector
\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by

x_{1}^{\downarrow}\geq\cdots\geq x_{n}^{\downarrow}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{1}^{\uparrow}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}^{\uparrow}

the components of
\displaystyle x
arranged in nonincreasing and nondecreasing order, respectively. The vectors
\displaystyle x^{\downarrow}
and
\displaystyle x^{\uparrow}
will denote
\displaystyle x
with its entries ordered in descending and ascending order.

For any
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
, we denote by
\displaystyle\lambda(A)=(\lambda_{1}(A),\dots,\lambda_{n}(A))
the vector of eigenvalues of
\displaystyle A
, with

\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}^{\downarrow}(A)\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{1}^{\uparrow}(A)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}^{\uparrow}(A)

representing the eigenvalues in decreasing and increasing order, respectively. We use
\displaystyle\lambda^{\downarrow}(A)
to refer both to the ordered eigenvalue vector
\displaystyle(\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow}(A),\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\downarrow}(A))
and to the corresponding diagonal matrix

\text{diag}(\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow}(A),\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\downarrow}(A)),

and similarly for
\displaystyle\lambda^{\uparrow}(A)
.

For vectors
\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
\displaystyle y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write

\left<x,y\right>:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i};\quad x\circ y:=(x_{1}y_{1},\dots,x_{n}y_{n}).

For
\displaystyle y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
whose components are positive, denote

\dfrac{x}{y}:=x\circ y^{-1}=\big(\dfrac{x_{1}}{y_{1}},\cdots,\dfrac{x_{n}}{y_{n}}\big).

If components of
\displaystyle y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
are non-negative,
\displaystyle\dfrac{x}{y}
is defined by taking the limit.

Majorization theory for vectors in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}

This section reviews several fundamental concepts and results concerning majorization relations between vectors in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For comprehensive expositions, see
[
2
,

]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
\displaystyle y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
be vectors in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We say that
\displaystyle x
is
weakly majorized by

\displaystyle y
, denoted
\displaystyle x\prec_{w}y
, if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{\downarrow}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}^{\downarrow},\qquad 1\leq k\leq n.

If, in addition, equality holds for
\displaystyle k=n
, we say that
\displaystyle x
is
majorized by

\displaystyle y
and write
\displaystyle x\prec y
.

The following result establishes a relationship between the trace of products of matrices and their eigenvalues.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
2
, Problem III.6.14]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle A,B\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
. Then

\left<\lambda^{\downarrow}(A),\lambda^{\uparrow}(B)\right>\leq\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(AB\right)\leq\left<\lambda^{\downarrow}(A),\lambda^{\downarrow}(B)\right>.

2.3.
Rearrangement principles for supermodular and submodular functions

Let
\displaystyle r=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
and
\displaystyle d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
be vectors in
\displaystyle(0,+\infty)^{n}
such that

r_{1}\geq\cdots\geq r_{n},\qquad d_{1}\geq\cdots\geq d_{n}.

A function
\displaystyle f:(0,+\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
supermodular
if

\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial r\,\partial d}(r,d)\geq 0,

and
submodular
if the above inequality is reversed. The function
\displaystyle f
is called
strictly supermodular
(resp.
strictly submodular
) if

\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial r\,\partial d}(r,d)>0\quad(\mbox{resp. }\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial r\,\partial d}(r,d)<0).

If
\displaystyle f
is strictly supermodular, then for all
\displaystyle d_{1}\geq d_{2}
and
\displaystyle r_{1}\geq r_{2}
, we have (see, e.g.
[
16
]
):

f(d_{1},r_{1})+f(d_{2},r_{2})\geq f(d_{1},r_{2})+f(d_{2},r_{1}),

(SM)

and the inequality (SM) holds strictly whenever
\displaystyle d_{1}>d_{2}
and
\displaystyle r_{1}>r_{2}
:

f(d_{1},r_{1})+f(d_{2},r_{2})>f(d_{1},r_{2})+f(d_{2},r_{1}).

A fundamental consequence of supermodularity and submodularity is the

rearrangement principle
(see
[
6
, Ch. 10]
and

[
16
, Ch. 2]
): for any permutation
\displaystyle\pi
of
\displaystyle\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(r_{i},d_{\pi(i)})

is maximized when
\displaystyle(r_{i})
and
\displaystyle(d_{\pi(i)})
are ordered in the same sense and
minimized when they are ordered in opposite senses if
\displaystyle f
is supermodular,
while the opposite conclusion holds if
\displaystyle f
is submodular.

These rearrangement principles are used repeatedly in the proofs of
Claims
4.2
,
4.3
,
4.4
to reduce unitary-orbit optimization problems to purely
spectral permutation problems.

2.4.
Unitary-orbit variational method and commutant structure

Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{P}_{n}^{+}
. Its unitary orbit is

\mathbb{U}_{A}:=\{U^{*}AU:U\in\mathcal{U}_{n}\},

which is a smooth homogeneous manifold under the action of the unitary group.
Its tangent space at
\displaystyle A
is given by

T_{A}\mathbb{U}_{A}=\{[A,K]:K^{*}=-K\}

(see, e.g.
[
2
, (VI.37)]
).

Accordingly, every smooth curve on
\displaystyle\mathbb{U}_{A}
through
\displaystyle A
can be written as

A(t)=e^{-tK}Ae^{tK},\qquad K^{*}=-K.

Let
\displaystyle F
be a Fréchet differentiable real-valued function defined on

\displaystyle\mathbb{U}_{A}
. Stationarity of
\displaystyle F
at
\displaystyle A
with respect to unitary variations
means that

\dfrac{d}{dt}F\!\left(e^{-tK}Ae^{tK}\right)\Big\lvert_{t=0}=0\quad\text{for all }K^{*}=-K.

Equivalently, the gradient
\displaystyle\nabla F(A)
is orthogonal to the tangent space

\displaystyle T_{A}\mathbb{U}_{A}
with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, which yields
the first-order optimality condition

[A,\nabla F(A)]=0.

Such commutation conditions for stationary points on unitary similarity orbits
are standard in optimization on matrix manifolds; see, e.g.

[
2
, Theorem VI.4.3]
.

In Claims
4.2
,
4.3
and
4.4
, this variational method is used to show that any optimizer must commute with the reference state
\displaystyle\rho
. Consequently, the optimization problem reduces to a spectral rearrangement problem involving the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma
.

3.
Main results

Let
\displaystyle f:(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an operator convex function. Then
\displaystyle f
has the integral representation given by (
1.4
). We consider the problems (
1.10
) and (
1.11
) for the quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergences
\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{f}
with respect to commutant Radon-Nikodym derivative.

Let
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma
be quantum states. It follows from the spectral theorem that there exist unitary matrices
\displaystyle V^{\downarrow}
and
\displaystyle V^{\uparrow}
such that

\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)=V^{\downarrow*}\rho V^{\downarrow}\mbox{ and }\lambda^{\uparrow}(\rho)=V^{\uparrow*}\rho V^{\uparrow},

(3.1)

and, there exist unitary matrices
\displaystyle W^{\downarrow}
and
\displaystyle W^{\uparrow}
such that

\lambda^{\downarrow}(\sigma)=W^{\downarrow*}\sigma W^{\downarrow}\mbox{ and }\lambda^{\uparrow}(\sigma)=W^{\uparrow*}\sigma W^{\uparrow}.

(3.2)

Denote

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{f}(\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)\|\lambda^{\downarrow}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=f(I)+c\left[\operatorname{Tr}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)^{2}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\sigma)^{-1}-1\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{[0;\infty)}\operatorname{Tr}\dfrac{\lambda^{\downarrow}(\sigma)^{-1}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)^{2}}{\lambda^{\downarrow}(\sigma)^{-1}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)+sI}d\lambda(s)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{[0;\infty)}\-2\operatorname{Tr}\dfrac{\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)}{\lambda^{\downarrow}\left(\sigma\right)^{-1}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)+sI}d\lambda(s)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{[0;\infty)}\operatorname{Tr}\dfrac{\lambda^{\downarrow}(\sigma)}{\lambda^{\downarrow}(\sigma)^{-1}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)+sI}d\lambda(s).

(3.3)

Similarly, denote

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{f}(\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)\|\lambda^{\uparrow}(\sigma))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=f(I)+c\left[\operatorname{Tr}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)^{2}\lambda^{\uparrow}(\sigma)^{-1}-1\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{[0;\infty)}\operatorname{Tr}\dfrac{\lambda^{\uparrow}(\sigma)^{-1}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)^{2}}{\lambda^{\uparrow}(\sigma)^{-1}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)+sI}d\lambda(s)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{[0;\infty)}\-2\operatorname{Tr}\dfrac{\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)}{\lambda^{\uparrow}\left(\sigma\right)^{-1}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)+sI}d\lambda(s)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{[0;\infty)}\operatorname{Tr}\dfrac{\lambda^{\uparrow}(\sigma)}{\lambda^{\uparrow}(\sigma)^{-1}\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)+sI}d\lambda(s).

(3.4)

The following results are our main results in this paper.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma
be quantum states. Then

\min_{U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}}\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|U^{*}\sigma U)=\widehat{S}_{f}(\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)\|\lambda^{\downarrow}(\sigma));

\arg\min_{U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}}\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|U^{*}\sigma U)=W^{\downarrow}V^{\downarrow*}.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma
be quantum states. Then

\max_{U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}}\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|U^{*}\sigma U)=\widehat{S}_{f}(\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)\|\lambda^{\uparrow}(\sigma));

\arg\max_{U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}}\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|U^{*}\sigma U)=W^{\uparrow}V^{\downarrow*}.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma
be quantum states. Then, the set
\displaystyle\Big\{\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|U^{*}\sigma U),U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}\Big\}
is exactly the interval
\displaystyle\Big[\widehat{S}_{f}(\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)\|\lambda^{\downarrow}(\sigma)),\widehat{S}_{f}(\lambda^{\downarrow}(\rho)\|\lambda^{\uparrow}(\sigma))\Big]
.

Proof.

Note that the map
\displaystyle U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}\longmapsto(U^{*}\sigma U)
is continuous. Moreover, it follows from the continuity of the function
\displaystyle f
and the linearity of the trace function that the map
\displaystyle U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}\longmapsto\operatorname{Tr}[(U^{*}\sigma U)f((U^{*}\sigma U)^{-1/2}\rho(U^{*}\sigma U)^{-1/2})]
is continuous. Then we get the continuity of the function
\displaystyle U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}\longmapsto\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|U^{*}\sigma U)
.

On the other hand, it is well-known that the unitary orbit
\displaystyle\mathbb{U}_{\sigma}
is connected and that the image of a connected set under a continuous map is also connected
[
15
, Theorem 4.22]
. It follows that the set
\displaystyle\Big\{\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|U^{*}\sigma U):U\in\mathbb{U}_{n}\Big\}
is connected and therefore fills out the interval between the minimum and maximum values obtained in Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
.
∎

4.
Proof of main results

By definition,

\widehat{S}_{f}(\rho\|\sigma):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sigma^{1/2}f(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2})\sigma^{1/2}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sigma f\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\right)\right].

(4.1)

Using the integral representation (
1.4
) of the operator convex funtion
\displaystyle f
, we have an explicit representation for
\displaystyle f(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2})
.

\displaystyle\displaystyle f\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=f(I)+f^{\prime}(I)\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)+c\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{[0;+\infty)}\dfrac{\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)^{2}}{\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}+sI}d\lambda(s).

It implies that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma f\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=f(I)\sigma+\sigma f^{\prime}(I)\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)+c\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{[0;+\infty)}\dfrac{\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)^{2}}{\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}+sI}d\lambda(s).

By linearity of the trace function, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\sigma f\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=f(I)\operatorname{Tr}\sigma+f^{\prime}(I)\operatorname{Tr}\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+c\operatorname{Tr}\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\int_{[0;+\infty)}\operatorname{Tr}\dfrac{\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)^{2}}{\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}+sI}d\lambda(s)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=f(I)\operatorname{Tr}\sigma+f^{\prime}(I)\operatorname{Tr}\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+c\operatorname{Tr}\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\int_{[0;+\infty)}\operatorname{Tr}\dfrac{\sigma^{1/2}\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)^{2}\sigma^{1/2}}{\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}+sI}d\lambda(s).

Note that

\operatorname{Tr}\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)=0;\mbox{and }\operatorname{Tr}\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sigma^{-1}\rho^{2}\right)-1.

(4.2)

In fact, we have

\operatorname{Tr}\sigma\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-I\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sigma^{1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}-\sigma\right)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)-\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma)=0.

Conclusion

Recently, Hirche and Tomamichel (
[
9
]
, 2024) investigated a new class of quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergences for convex and twice differentiable functions
\displaystyle f:(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle f(1)=0
. More explicitly, for a pair of quantum states
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma
, Hirche and Tomamichel defined the quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergence with respect to the quantum hockey-stick divergence as

D_{f}(\rho\|\sigma)=\int_{1}^{\infty}f^{\prime\prime}(s)E_{s}(\rho\|\sigma)+\frac{1}{s^{3}}f^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)E_{s}(\sigma\|\rho)\,ds,

(5.1)

where
\displaystyle E_{s}(\rho\|\sigma)=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho-s\sigma)_{+}]
represents the quantum hockey-stick divergence,
\displaystyle A_{+}
denotes the positive part of the eigen-decomposition of a matrix
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
.

Li and Yan (
[
11
]
, 2025) studied the unitary orbit optimization of the quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergences
\displaystyle D_{f}(\rho\|\sigma)
with respect to the quantum hockey-stick divergence for convex and twice differentiable functions
\displaystyle f:(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle f(1)=0
.

In this paper, we have determined the exact extremal values of the maximal quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergence, defined via the commutant Radon–Nikodym derivative, over the unitary orbits of two quantum states. We derived explicit spectral expressions for both the minimum and maximum and provided complete characterizations of the unitaries that achieve these extrema.

A central contribution of this work is methodological. The proofs of Claims
4.2
–
4.4
combine unitary-orbit variational calculus with rearrangement theory for supermodular and submodular functions

[
6
,
16
]
. This approach allows us to rigorously show that any optimizer must commute with the reference state
\displaystyle\rho
, thereby reducing a highly noncommutative optimization problem to a tractable spectral
rearrangement problem involving the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\sigma
.

This framework differs fundamentally from the recent work of Li and Yan
[
11
]
, which studies unitary-orbit optimization for quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergences defined via the hockey-stick divergence of Hirche and
Tomamichel
[
9
]
. While both approaches ultimately yield extremal formulas governed by spectral majorization, the underlying operator mechanisms are distinct: the present work relies on the operator perspective
\displaystyle f(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2})
and commutant structure, whereas the hockey-stick framework is driven by properties of the Hermitian difference
\displaystyle\rho-s\sigma
.

Overall, our results extend and complement previous optimization studies for Umegaki, Rényi, and Hellinger-type divergences, and establish a structurally distinct theory for maximal quantum
\displaystyle f
-divergences.
