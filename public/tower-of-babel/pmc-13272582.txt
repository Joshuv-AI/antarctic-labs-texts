Tryptophan supplementation in low-protein diets of sows during late gestation and lactation: Effects on sow performance and piglet growth
Authors: Yuan Gao, Mengliao Wan, Zhiwei Kong, Qiuping Guo, Fengna Li, Yehui Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimd to investigate the effects of tryptophan (Trp) supplementation in low-protein diets on sow performance and piglet growth, and its potential mechanisms. Sixty-six multiparous sows (Large White × Landrace) with similar parity (2.87 ± 0.34 parities), body weight (250.75 ± 11.24 kg), and backfat thickness (16.06 ± 1.94 mm) were randomly assigned to six groups. There were eleven sows in each group and one replicate per sow. They were fed the following diets: normal protein diets (18% crude protein [CP], control group [CON]) or low-protein diets (15% CP) containing different levels of Trp (0.15%, 0.21%, 0.27%, 0.33%, and 0.39%, respectively). The results showed that low-protein diets containing 0.21% Trp achieved performance comparable to the CON group. Specifically, no significant differences were observed between the 0.21% Trp group and the CON group in sow average daily feed intake, milk yield from 3 to 21 d, and piglet growth parameters (litter weight and individual weight at 3, 14, and 21 d, as well as average daily gain (ADG) from 3 to 21 d (P > 0.05). Furthermore, compared with the CON group, the 0.21% Trp group significantly increased the levels of fat-free dry matter and immunoglobulin A (IgA) in colostrum, reduced milk urea nitrogen (MUN), elevated serum total glutathione (T-GSH) in sows, and increased serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) in piglets (P < 0.05). Additionally, compared with the CON group, the 0.21% Trp group promoted intestinal health in piglets, as evidenced by increased ileal villus height, upregulation of intestinal physical barrier-related genes (occludin and claudin-1) in the jejunum and ileum, downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and IFN-γ), upregulation of antioxidant genes (GSH-PX and CAT), as well as an increase in the abundance of the beneficial gut microbiota Christensenellaceae_R-7_group (P < 0.05). The experiment lasted from 107 d of gestation to 21 d of lactation. Data of this study suggested that low-protein diets containing 0.21% Trp improved sow performance and piglet growth to levels comparable to normal protein diets, probably due to the higher feed intake and milk yield of sows, the improvements in intestinal function and gut microbiota of piglets, and the higher antioxidant capacity of sows and piglets.

Introduction

Given that the nutritional demands cannot be covered by the voluntary feed intake, lactating sows generally mobilize their body reserves (especially fat and protein) to support milk production (Tokach et al., 2019). In this context, increasing dietary protein levels is an effective way to save sow body protein mobilization and hence augment milk production during lactation (Strathe et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2009). However, the positive effects of high-protein diets are often achieved at the expense of environment due to nitrogen excretion (Oenema, 2004). Apart from nitrogen excretion, excessive protein intake can also affect sow energy utilization efficiency, resulting in a reduction in milk yield (Pedersen et al., 2019; Strathe et al., 2020). To address this issue, there is growing interest in the application of low-protein diets, which can enhance sow nitrogen utilization and thus reduce environmental pollution (Huber et al., 2015; Johannsen et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2023; Zhang and Trottier, 2019). Of note, low protein intake during lactation can cause a high mobilization of body protein in sows, which is correlated with increased body loss and reduced milk production (Costermans et al., 2020b,a). Interestingly, inclusion of crystalline amino acids (AAs) in diets for lactating sows provides the opportunity to meet the AA requirements while reducing dietary protein levels, thus improving milk production and reducing muscle protein mobilisation (Hojgaard et al., 2019; Tokach et al., 2019).

Tryptophan (Trp) is an essential AA (EAA) that cannot be synthesized in animals and must be obtained through the diet. Apart from serving as a substrate for protein synthesis, it has a variety of other well-documented physiological and pharmacological functions, including increasing feed intake and utilization (Wu, 2009; Zhang et al., 2007), ameliorating animal stress response (Koopmans et al., 2012), regulating immune function (Bai et al., 2017), and improving intestinal barrier function of weaned piglets (Liu et al., 2025). Of note, when added as a dietary supplement for animals, Trp can be converted into serotonin, kynurenine, and indole through metabolic reactions (Bai et al., 2017). Serotonin, as a neurotransmitter, plays a critical role in regulating appetite, mood, and stress response, thus influencing the central nervous system (Brummelte et al., 2017; Turner et al., 2006). Kynurenine and indole exert roles in regulating immune responses, enhancing body resistance, and maintaining gut homeostasis (Kaur et al., 2019; Plitman et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021). The broad physiological effects of Trp underscore its role in maintenance of sow performance.

In sow production, the role of Trp has gradually attracted widespread attention. Available evidence has shown that dietary Trp supplementation during late pregnancy and lactation can significantly increase feed intake and milk yield, improve reproductive performance, and reduce weight loss of sows, as well as improve the growth performance and survival rates of piglets (Libal et al., 1997). Dietary Trp supplementation only during late pregnancy increased sow estradiol levels, thereby increasing piglet birth weight and weaning survival rate (Fan et al., 2016; Miao et al., 2019; Munn et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2025). Dietary Trp supplementation only during lactation not only increased sow feed intake, elevated progesterone and estradiol levels, but also increased the abundance of beneficial fecal microbiota, and improved piglet litter weight (Greiner et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2025). However, some studies have indicated that Trp supplementation only with an appropriate level can significantly increase feed intake, reduce weight loss, and improve milk yield, and insufficient or excessive Trp supplementation do not achieve these effects (Varvel, 2019). Trp is limiting in corn-based pig diets and may negatively influence the feed intake and impair intestinal function if supplied in insufficient levels (Fan et al., 2016; Franco et al., 2014; Le Floc'h et al., 2012; Miao et al., 2019). In contrast, excessive Trp supplementation to protein-restricted diets triggered gastrointestinal disorders of piglets, leading to symptoms such as diarrhea, vomiting, and indigestion (Moehn et al., 2012). As such, there is a need re-evaluate the suitable quantity of Trp into low-protein diets for sows during late pregnancy and lactation, which may provide better guidance for the use of Trp in swine production.

Although low-protein diets hold great promise for increasing nitrogen utilization and decreasing environmental pollution, there is still a long way to go in the application of low-protein diets in sow production. In this context, to fill a gap in this field, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of low-protein diets supplemented with different levels of Trp during late pregnancy and lactation on sow performance and piglet growth, and its potential mechanisms. The current study hypothesized that supplementing an appropriate level of Trp could compensate for the detrimental effects of low-protein diets on sow production and lactation performance by increasing feed intake, immune function, and antioxidant capacity, thereby improving growth performance of piglets. These findings not only highlight the crucial role of Trp in sow production performance but also fill a gap in the understanding of the application of low-protein diets in swine production.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Institute of Subtropical Agriculture, Chinese Academy of Science (Beijing, China), and all procedures in this study were conducted according to the guidelines established by the committee (ISA-2023–0002).

Animals and experimental design

Sixty-six multiparous sows (Large White × Landrace) with similar parity (2.87 ± 0.34 parities), body weight (250.75 ± 11.24 kg), and backfat thickness (16.06 ± 1.94 mm) were randomly divided into six groups on 107 d of gestation. There were 11 sows in each group and one replicate per sow. The experiment lasted from 107 d of gestation to 21 d of lactation. The control (CON) group was fed a normal protein diet (18% crude protein [CP]), while the remaining five groups were fed low-protein diets (15% CP) containing different levels of standard ileal digestibility (SID) Trp (0.15%, 0.21%, 0.27%, 0.33%, and 0.39%, respectively). The control diet was formulated to contain 18% CP in accordance with NRC (2012) recommendations to establish a valid nutritional reference. The low-protein diet was set at 15% CP, with the reduction applied after ensuring adequate supply of all EAAs. This design aimed to balance sow health maintenance with the targeted physiological benefits of a low-protein diet, such as reduced nitrogen excretion, while also exploring the feasibility of implementing a CP level below the current Chinese feed industry standard (T/CFIAS 8001–2022) (China Feed Industry Association, 2022). The Trp supplementation gradient was established based on a systematic review of previous literature (Pampuch et al., 2006; Paulicks et al., 2006; Tokach et al., 2019) and NRC (2012). The lower end (0.15% SID Trp) was set as a deficient level to elicit observable negative effects, while the upper end (0.39% SID Trp) exceeded recent estimates of lactating sows’ requirements (approximately 0.27%–0.30% SID Trp) to ensure coverage of the response plateau. This gradient was designed to comprehensively span the suboptimal, optimal, and supra-optimal phases of the dose–response curve, enabling accurate modeling and determination of the optimal SID Trp level. The SID lysine (Lys) in experimental diets was set at 0.80% based on the previous study (Han, 2024), and other SID AAs and net energy levels were formulated according to NRC (2012) nutrient requirements. The experiment was conducted at the Longmen Township breeding base of Hunan Haoyi Agricultural Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Zhuzhou, Hunan, China). The sows were housed in farrowing pens at the farm, individually confined in stalls, with ventilation and cooling provided by a wet curtain and fan system, maintaining the room temperature between 22 and 28 °C. Each farrowing pen was equipped with a heat lamp and a heated box to support piglet growth. During the experiment, sow feeding and piglet health management were conducted according to the farm's standard procedures. Sows were not fed on the day of farrowing, and after farrowing, their daily feed intake increased from 1 to 5 kg before switching to ad libitum feeding. During the restricted feeding period, sows were fed twice a day (08:00 and 14:30), and during the ad libitum feeding period, they were fed three times a day (08:00, 14:30, and 20:00). Piglets were cross-fostered within the groups within 3 d. Daily management and immunization procedures followed the farm's protocols. Routine barn inspections were conducted, recording the barn's humidity and temperature, and monitoring sow feed intake and general condition, as well as the growth and health of the piglets.

Sample collection

Body weight and backfat thickness of the sows were measured on gestation 107 d and at weaning. Backfat thickness was measured using an ultrasound backfat scanner (Renco Lean-Meater, Renco Corporation, Golden Valley, MN, USA) at a location 65 mm to the right of the midline at the last rib, with measurements performed by the same technician as previously described (Tan et al., 2018). At farrowing, the number and weight of liveborn piglets, stillborn piglets, and mummified piglets were recorded. Additionally, cross-fostering of piglets within each group was conducted within 48 hpostpartum, and the litter size of each sow was adjusted to 12.22 ± 0.95 piglets. There were no differences in the number of piglets between groups. Milk yield was calculated according to the following equation:Milkyield=(0.88+3.7×Averagedailygain[ADG])×Numberofpiglets.

Piglets were weaned at 21 d of lactation. The weight of the piglets was measured after cross-fostering and at 14 and 21 d after farrowing, and the numbers of piglets were also recorded to calculate the litter weight gain and ADG during lactation.

Blood samples were collected from the ear marginal vein of sows under fasting conditions on the day of farrowing and at 20 d postpartum, while colostrum and milk samples were obtained from the mammary teats. Colostrum and milk were collected using a standardized manual milking procedure. Briefly, at the time of sampling (after farrowing for colostrum or 1 d before weaning for milk), the teats from the anterior, middle, and posterior mammary regions were cleaned. One teat from each region was then selected for manual milking. Each gland was milked continuously until milk flow ceased, without the use of oxytocin. Finally, the milk collected from the three glands was pooled in equal volumes to form a composite sample (approximately 25 mL per sow for colostrum and 10 mL per sow for milk) and stored at −20 °C until analysis. At the end of the experiment, eight piglets from each group (each from a different sow, with similar weight and an equal sex distribution) were randomly selected, anesthetized, and euthanized for collections of blood, jejunum, ileum, and colonic content samples. Blood samples from both sows and piglets were allowed to clot and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain serum. Furthermore, serum, colostrum, and milk samples were stored at −80 °C. The middle segments of jejunum (2 cm) and ileum (2 cm) were preserved in fixative solution for the intestinal morphology analyses, while jejunal and ileal mucosa and colonic content samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently maintained at −80 °C for further analyze.

Diet analysis and chemical composition

Crude protein was analyzed according to the Kjeldahl method (GB/T 6432–2018) (China National Standard, 2018b) using a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (Model Kjeltec 9, FOSS Analytical A/S, Hillerød, Denmark). Crude fiber was analyzed via the filtration method (GB/T 6434–2022) (China National Standard, 2022) with a fiber analyzer (Model FT12, C. Gerhardt GmbH & Co. KG, Königswinter, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). Calcium content was analyzed in accordance with GB/T 6436-2018 (China National Standard, 2018a). Total phosphorus (P) was analyzed by the spectrophotometric method (GB/T 6437–2018) (China National Standard, 2018c) using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Model UV-2600, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Amino acids were analyzed following GB/T 18246-2000 (China National Standard, 2019) with an automatic amino acid analyzer (Model L8800, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Chiyoda, Tokyo, Japan). Dry matter was determined by drying samples in a constant-temperature oven at 105 ± 2 °C to a constant weight according to GB/T 18246-2000 (China National Standard, 2014). Crude ash content was measured by combusting samples in a muffle furnace (Model 5E-MF6000, Changsha Kaiyuan Instrument Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) at 550 ± 20 °C to a constant weight in line with GB/T 6438-2007 (China National Standard, 2007). Organic matter content was calculated as the difference between dry matter and crude ash contents. Other nutritional components were estimated based on the China Feed Database (2021).

Analyses of the nutrient composition of colostrum and milk

Colostrum and milk samples were thawed at 4 °C. Subsequently, a 5 mL aliquot was subjected to a five-fold dilution by the addition of 20 mL of physiological saline (resulting in a total volume of 25 mL) and vortexed thoroughly. This specific dilution factor was empirically selected to align with the linear calibration range of the milk analyzer, thereby ensuring measurement accuracy. The routine nutritional components of the colostrum and milk (including milk protein, fat, lactose, fat-free-dry matter, urea nitrogen, and total solid) were measured using a milk component analyzer (MilkoScan FT + 200 Type 76150, FOSS Analytical A/S, Hillerød, Denmark).

Determination of serum biochemical parameters of sows and piglets

The concentrations of total protein (TP; 03183734190), albumin (ALB; 03183688122), blood urea nitrogen (BUN; 04460715190), glucose (GLU; 04404483190), calcium (CA; 05061482190), triglycerides (TG; 20767107322), total cholesterol (CHOL; 03039773190), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C; 03038866322) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C; 04399803190), as well as the activities of alanine aminotransferase (ALT; 20764957322), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; 20764949322) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; 20767123322), were analyzed in sows and piglets using a fully automated serum biochemistry analyzer (Cobas c311, F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd., Basel, Basel-Stadt, Switzerland). The kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Free AA profiles in the sow serum, piglet serum, and sow milk

Equal amounts of serum samples were mixed with 8% sulfosalicylic acid, left to stand at 4 °C for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 4000 × g at 4 °C for 5 min to collect the supernatant. Subsequently, the free AA profiles in the serum were analyzed using an automatic amino acid analyzer (L8800, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Chiyoda, Tokyo, Japan) with standards obtained from Sigma Chemicals (A6282, Sigma–Aldrich Co., LLC, St. Louis, MO, USA). The methods were previously described (Gao et al., 2021).

Milk AA concentrations were determined by an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatograph (ACQUITY UPLC, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a photodiode array (PDA) detector (ACQUITY UPLC PDA Detector, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Briefly, 0.5 mL of milk samples were mixed with 0.5 mL of n-hexane and centrifuged at 4 °C, 10,000 × g for 10 min to remove fat. The remaining steps were carried out in accordance with the serum free AA detection protocol.

Assessment of antioxidant enzyme activities in the serum of sows and piglets

The serum total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-2-1), the activities of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD; A001-3-2), catalase (CAT; A007-1-1), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; A005-1-2), as well as the malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1-2) content were measured using the corresponding commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), following the manufacturer's instructions.

Assessment of immunoglobulins in colostrum, milk, and serum samples

The immunoglobulin G (IgG; CSB-E06804p), immunoglobulin A (IgA; CSB-E13234p), and immunoglobulin M (IgM; CSB-E06805p) levels in colostrum, milk and serum were detected using the corresponding ELISA kits (Wuhan Huamei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China), with the procedure carried out according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Intestinal morphology

The jejunum and ileum morphology were determined using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, as previously described (Duan et al., 2018). Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm using a rotary microtome (RM2255, Leica Biosystems Nussloch GmbH, Nussloch, Baden-Württemberg, Germany). Sections were stained with H&E, and observed under a light microscope (BX53, Olympus Corporation, Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan). Villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were assessed by Image-Pro Plus 6.0 analysis software (version 6.0, Media Cybernetics, Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) on images at 200-fold magnification in five randomly selected fields, respectively.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA of the jejunum and ileum mucosa samples was extracted using the Trizol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1621, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Fermentas), Hanover, MD, USA), and the relative mRNA expression of target genes was subsequently quantified by RT-qPCR (Zheng et al., 2025). The full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in Table S1. All primer sequences (Table S2) were synthesized by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) analyses of piglet colon

Fecal samples were thawed and precisely weighed (1 g, accurate to 0.0001 g) into a 15 mL centrifuge tube. Following the addition of 4 to 5 mL of ultrapure water, the mixture was vortexed for 30 s and subsequently centrifuged at 8000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, and this extraction procedure was repeated twice. The combined supernatants were pooled and brought to a final volume of 10 mL. An aliquot of the diluted supernatant was then mixed with meta-phosphoric acid solution in a 9:1 ratio. After filtration through a 0.22-μm membrane, the solution was subjected to SCFA analysis using a gas chromatograph (Agilent 6890A, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) (Duan et al., 2019).

Gut microbiota analyses

Colonic content samples were sent to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing. The detailed procedures were as follows: total microbial DNA was extracted from colonic contents using the E. Z.N.A. Soil DNA Kit (D5625, Omega Bio-tek, Inc., Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted DNA was verified by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels, and its concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The hypervariable V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with the primer pair 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) on a thermal cycler. PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (28104, QIAGEN GmbH, Hilden, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). The purified amplicons were used to construct libraries following Illumina's standard library preparation protocols. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (MiSeq PE300, Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw sequences were processed using QIIME2, which included quality filtering, removal of chimeric sequences, operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering, and taxonomic annotation (Zheng et al., 2025, 2022).

Statistical analyses

The experimental data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 software (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and the results are presented as mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). Bar charts were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.4.2. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed across six groups, ANOVA uses the following model:Xij=μ+ai+£ij,where Xij represents the dependent variable; μ represents the overall mean; ai represents the effect of dietary treatment; and £ij represents the random error. Linear and quadratic relationships were evaluated among the five low-protein groups. Statistical significance was considered when P < 0.05.

Results

Sow and piglet performance

As shown in Table 2, the weight loss in the 0.15% Trp group was significantly higher than that in the other groups, and the average daily feed intake (ADFI) in the first week was significantly lower than in the other groups (P < 0.05). Reducing dietary protein levels and varying the Trp levels did not have a significant effect on backfat loss on sows (P > 0.05). Significant differences were observed in the average daily SID Trp intake among the groups (P < 0.001). Furthermore, as the dietary Trp level increased, the average daily SID Trp intake of sows in the low-protein groups also increased (Linear, P < 0.001). The newborn litter weight of live piglets in the 0.15% Trp group was significantly lower than that of the CON group and the other groups (P = 0.031). No significant differences were observed in other reproductive performance indicators (P > 0.05). Compared with the CON group, the 0.15% Trp and 0.39% Trp groups exhibited a significant reduction in the litter weight of piglets at 14 or 21 d, as well as individual weight of piglets at 14 d (P < 0.05). In addition, compared with the CON group, the 0.15% Trp group showed a significant decrease in the litter weight of piglets at 21 d and ADG of piglets from 3 to 21 d (P < 0.05). Furthermore, compare to the 0.21% Trp group, the 0.15% Trp groups had significantly lower individual weight and litter weight of piglets at 14 and 21 d, as well as ADG of piglets and milk yield from 3 to 21 d (P < 0.05). Notably, among the low-protein diet groups, the litter weight of piglets at 21 d, individual weight of piglets at 14 d, ADG of piglets and milk yield from 3 to 21 d reached their peak in the 0.21% Trp group (quadratic, P < 0.05), which were increased to the levels obtained in the CON group. Free AA levels in the milk of sows and serum of piglets

Discussion

Although the beneficial effects of Trp supplementation on sows have been well documented in the literature (Libal et al., 1997), the use of Trp in low-protein diets for sows has been poorly investigated. The current study showed that the Trp level within the range of 0.21% to 0.39% in sows’ low-protein diets (15% CP) augmented ADFI during the first week of lactation to levels comparable to the 18% CP diets, coupled with a marked decrease of weight loss, with the best effects observed at the level of 0.21%. This was similar to the result of a previous study, which showed that dietary Trp level at 0.32% (CP = 18%) increased feed intake of lactating sows (Miao et al., 2019). The elevation in ADFI observed in this study decreased the weight loss of sows, which normally occurs during lactation as a result of large body tissue catabolism (Clowes et al., 2003). These results therefore confirm the observation of previous studies (Fan et al., 2016; Miao et al., 2019). The reason for these beneficial effects of Trp is unclear but may be related to its metabolites serotonin and 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), since serotonin and 5-HT exert critical roles in appetite regulation (Curzon, 1990; Mosnier et al., 2009; Young and Leyton, 2002).

During lactation, maternal feed intake is strongly correlated with suckling piglet growth (Hawe et al., 2020), which prompted us to investigate whether Trp supplementation in low-protein diets improved the growth of piglets. Interestingly, in the current study found that maternal Trp supplementation at a level of 0.21% in low-protein diets during late gestation and lactation increased the live birth weight, litter weight and individual weight at 14 and 21 d, and ADG from 3 to 21 d of piglets to levels comparable to the normal protein diets, with insufficient (0.15%) or excessive (0.27%, 0.33%, and 0.39%) Trp supplementation being ineffective. The present observations are consistent with earlier reports which showed that total Trp levels within the range of 0.26% to 0.60% in normal protein diets (CP = 17%–18%) increased piglet growth and/or survival when supplemented 6 to 11 d (at farrowing house entry) prior to parturition and throughout lactation (Fan et al., 2016; Miao et al., 2019; Munn et al., 2021). The Trp requirement of lactating sows is 0.17% for multiparous sows according to NRC (2012). Therefore, maternal required Trp level in low-protein diets for piglets’ growth is slightly higher than the recommended value of NRC (2012). Based on the abovementioned findings, the current study draws a conclusion that maintaining an adequate level of Trp (0.21%) in low-protein diets of lactating sows could benefit both sow performance and piglet growth.

The growth of suckling piglets is mainly determined by sow milk yield and nutrients (Lalles et al., 2009), since at least 50% of dietary nitrogen and energy of sows can be transferred to the piglets via the milk (Theil et al., 2014). These findings prompted us to undertake further analyses of milk yield. In the current study. The current study found that a marked increase of milk yield was observed in response to 0.21% Trp supplementation, which was 3.45% higher than that in the normal protein diet. These results support the hypothesis that Trp (as a precursor of 5-HT) regulates mammary gland function through neuroendocrine pathways, thereby improving milk quality (Liu et al., 2024; Miao et al., 2019). Available evidence from lactating dams has shown that blood calcium concentrations could be reflected by circulating 5-HT concentrations (Weaver et al., 2016). The results of the current study showed that serum calcium concentrations of the 0.21% Trp group was significantly higher in comparison with that in the normal protein group, thus improving milk production and piglet growth (Munn et al., 2021). Apart from milk yield, milk nutrients were also improved by 0.21% Trp supplementation. In detail, supplementing 0.21% Trp in a low-protein diet restored the concentrations of fat, fat-free dry matter, and immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM, and IgA) in colostrum to levels comparable to or even higher than those in the CON group, coupled with increases in the concentrations of milk Lys, Gly, Cys, and Tyr as well as the ratio of EAA to TAA. The colostrum fat could supply about 50% of the total energy required by newborns, thus improving the growth of piglets (Chen et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022). The colostrum immunoglobulins are also of great importance to new-born piglets, since they can offer passive immunity to piglets to protect against microbial infections (Borad and Singh, 2018). Thus, the increased immunoglobulin in colostrum of sows might also contribute to the growth of piglets, since the colostrum IgA, IgM, and IgG were positively correlated with the ADG of piglets. The effects of Trp on immunoglobulins may be related to its metabolites regulating immune system function, promoting the synthesis and secretion of immunoglobulins in breast milk (Seo and Kwon, 2023). Apart from colostrum nutrients, improved milk AA compositions is another contributing mechanism for the growth of piglets. The free AA in sow milk are closely related to the free AA in piglet serum, providing an important AA source for piglets, which plays a crucial role in their growth and development (Yao et al., 2023). In parallel with the positive correlation between free AA and piglets’ growth, here we also showed that the concentrations of milk Lys, Tyr, and Gly and the ratio of EAA to TAA were positively correlated with the weaning weight of piglets. Additionally, the 0.21% Trp supplementation showed a significant reduction in MUN levels, indicating improved nitrogen utilization efficiency in the sows, which indirectly suggests AA balance at this Trp level (Baker et al., 1995). Therefore, elevated milk yield and/or improved milk nutrients might contribute significantly to the increased ADG of piglets from 0.21% Trp-fed sows, which were also correlated well with increased feed intake of sows.

Besides milk yield and nutrients, improved antioxidant system of sows and new-born piglets may also exert crucial roles in the growth of piglets (Yin et al., 2015). Accumulating and emerging lines of evidence from sows and rats have revealed a strong association between the growth of offspring and improved maternal and fetal antioxidant capacities (Azad et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2012). These results are well-matched with the current research since serum CAT, T-GSH, and GSH-Px in sows fed the low-protein +0.21% Trp diets were increased to levels comparable to or even better than those in sows fed the normal protein diets, coupled with increased activities of serum CAT and T-AOC in piglets. In contrast, the serum MDA content in both sows and piglets in the 0.21% Trp group were decreased to levels comparable to those in the CON group. Furthermore, correlation analyses indicated a significant association between serum MDA, CAT, and T-GSH in piglets and their growth performance. The antioxidant enzymes such as CAT, T-GSH, and GSH-Px could help mitigate oxidative stress (Galasso et al., 2021), whereas MDA is positively associated with oxidative stress (He et al., 2023). Therefore, these results data of the current study show that Trp supplementation of the sows’ low-protein diets from late gestation to lactation markedly augmented the antioxidant capacity of the sows and piglets, which may help to explain the improvement in piglet growth.

Another important reason responsible for the improved growth of piglets from sows in the 0.21% group may be due to the improved intestinal morphology and function. First, the results of this study showed that 0.21% Trp supplementation in the low-protein diets elevated the ileal VH of piglets, which is an indicator of enterocytes’ maturation rate and an increase in VH indicates better mucosal digestion and absorption capacity (Duan et al., 2018, 2025). Next, the current study found that 0.21% Trp supplementation significantly upregulated the relative mRNA expression of intestinal barrier-related genes (ZO-1, occludin, claudin-1, MUC1, MUC2, and MUC4) in the jejunum and ileum of piglets, and downregulated the relative mRNA expression of genes related to inflammation (IL-6, INF-γ, and NF-κB). Physical barrier-related parameters of intestines are usually reflected by tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-1. Upregulated mRNA expression of these genes indicates an improvement in intestinal integrity and barrier function (Huang et al., 2023). Mucins such as MUC1, 2, and 4 make up the mucus layer of the intestine, further forming the intestinal chemical barrier, with an ability to prevent the penetration of harmful substance into the epithelium (Birchenough et al., 2023; Wan et al., 2018). In addition, the relative mRNA expression of genes related to antioxidant capacity (GSH-Px and CAT) in the jejunum and ileum of piglets were significantly upregulated in the 0.21% group. Therefore, these results suggest that adding 0.21% Trp to the low-protein diets of sows during the late gestation and lactation improved the intestinal morphology, barrier function, and intestinal antioxidant capacity of piglets, which aligns with previous studies (Chen et al., 2024; Tossou et al., 2016). Notably, the 0.39% Trp supplementation induced a pro-inflammatory state, reducing the expression of intestinal barrier-related genes. The results were consistent with previous findings, which show that excessive Trp supplementation can negatively impact the piglet intestine, possibly due to microbial imbalance or metabolic overload (Jiang et al., 2015; Tossou et al., 2016).

Given the important role of the gut microbiome in host health and Trp metabolism (Gao et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2021), the current study further evaluated the effect of Trp on the gut microflora of piglets. In this study, the gut microbiota of piglets in the 0.21% Trp group showed beneficial changes, including a significant increase in the relative abundance of the beneficial bacterium Christensenellaceae_R-7_group. Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, a butyrate-producing bacterium, plays a crucial role in maintaining gut health and regulating immune function (Chen and Vitetta, 2018; Perego et al., 2018). Additionally, compared to other low-protein groups, the 0.21% Trp group showed the highest relative abundance of Lactobacillus and the lowest relative abundance of the potentially harmful bacterium Escherichia-Shigella. Lactobacillus plays a significant role in pathogen defense and enhancing immunity in piglets(Lamas et al., 2016; Li et al., 2016; Wegmann et al., 2015), while Escherichia-Shigella is a potential pathogenic bacterium associated with intestinal diseases, capable of inducing gut inflammation and diarrhea (Fukuda et al., 2011). These findings suggest that the improved gut microbiota in the 0.21% Trp group contributes to improving host health. Moreover, the relative abundance of norank_f_Muribaculaceae was significantly decreased in the 0.15% and 0.39% Trp groups. Previous studies have shown that norank_f_Muribaculaceae is positively correlated with antioxidant activity and immune function (Hu et al., 2021), and its decreased abundance may negatively impact the immune response and antioxidant capacity in piglets. Trp metabolism and the gut microbiota interact closely to maintain intestinal homeostasis. On one hand, Trp-derived metabolites (e.g., indole and kynurenine) directly inhibit the growth of pathogens and promote the proliferation of beneficial bacteria, while also improving the gut microenvironment through activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor AhR pathway, thereby remodeling the microbial community structure (Gao et al., 2022; Lamas et al., 2016). On the other hand, gut microbiota participates in the indole pathway of Trp metabolism, and their resulting metabolites enhance intestinal barrier function, modulate immune responses, and suppress inflammation (Dodd et al., 2017; Gandhi et al., 2010). Furthermore, host-derived Trp metabolites—including kynurenine, which exhibits antimicrobial activity, and 5-HT, which regulates intestinal motility and secretion—act synergistically to promote functional balance in the gut (Hou et al., 2023; Latorre et al., 2016). Additionally, correlation analyses revealed a significant relationship between microbial diversity and piglet growth performance. In conclusion, appropriate levels of Try modulate the composition of the gut microbiota, thereby influencing host immune and metabolic functions and promoting piglet growth, a conclusion that is further supported by previous research (Chen et al., 2024).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Trp supplementation at an appropriate level (0.21%) in a reduced-protein diet (15% CP) during late gestation and lactation could effectively improve sow performance and piglet growth to levels in the normal protein diet (18% CP), probably due to the higher feed intake and milk yield of sows, the improvements in intestinal function and gut microbiota of piglets, and the higher antioxidative capacity of sows and piglets (Fig. 7).
