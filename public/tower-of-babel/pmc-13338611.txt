Response-Adapted Surgery for Residual Calcifications in HER2-Positive Breast Cancer After Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy
Authors: Jeong Eon Lee, Cho Eun Lee, Seok Jin Nam, Seok Won Kim, Jonghan Yu, Byung Joo Chae, Se Kyung Lee, Jai Min Ryu, Eun Young Ko, Ji Soo Choi, Eun Sook Ko, Jeongmin Lee, Boo-Kyung Han, Woong Ki Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The clinical significance of residual mammographic microcalcifications after neoadjuvant systemic therapy in human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive (HER2+) breast cancer remains unclear. Traditionally, persistent calcifications have prompted wide excisions or mastectomies under the assumption that they indicate residual disease. However, accumulating evidence suggests that calcifications may persist as treatment-related or biologically-attenuated changes, rather than as viable carcinomas, particularly in the era of dual HER2-directed therapy. Recent Korean studies demonstrate that patients with favorable radiologic response who achieve pathologic complete response maintain excellent local control after breast-conserving surgery, even when residual calcifications are present. A large multicenter cohort study reported a 5-year local recurrence-free survival rate of 97.4% and a validated prediction model showed strong discriminatory performance. This Brief Communication synthesizes emerging evidence on the biological basis and clinical implications of residual calcifications after neoadjuvant therapy, with emphasis on recent Korean data. Current evidence supports a response-adapted surgical approach in selected patients, emphasizing clip-guided excision of the invasive index lesion and consistent delivery of whole-breast irradiation rather than routine removal of the entire pretreatment calcification field in patients with HER2+ breast cancer. Prospective validation with a long-term follow-up is warranted to further refine patient selection and confirm the safety of this strategy in clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

Mammographic calcifications have long influenced surgical planning in breast cancer. Before the widespread adoption of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC), the geographic extent was regarded as a surrogate for disease distribution, and complete removal of the calcification field was recommended to minimize the risk of ipsilateral breast tumor recurrence [123]. Consequently, patients with extensive calcification frequently undergo mastectomy even when breast-conserving surgery (BCS) is technically feasible.

Although calcifications are a common radiological finding in patients with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive (HER2+) breast cancer [4], the treatment landscape has fundamentally changed. Dual HER2-directed therapy combined with chemotherapy now achieves substantially higher rates of pathological complete response (pCR) [567]. Multiple radiologic-pathologic correlation studies demonstrate that persistent calcifications after therapy do not reliably indicate residual invasive carcinoma and may instead reflect treatment-related stromal remodeling, dystrophic calcification, or limited residual in situ foci with reduced biological relevance [389].

Recent multicenter cohorts demonstrate excellent local control among patients achieving pCR, including those with residual ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) (ypTis), when whole-breast irradiation is delivered [1011]. These findings challenge the traditional requirement to excise the entire pretreatment calcification field and suggest that oncologic risk is more closely related to treatment-response biology than to the anatomic extent of pretreatment calcifications.

Against this background, an important clinical question emerges: in HER2+ patients with high likelihood of complete response on post-NAC imaging, is it oncologically safe to limit initial surgery to clip-guided BCS irrespective of the calcification extent? This Brief Communication synthesizes emerging evidence, with particular emphasis on recent Korean data, and outlines a response-adapted surgical approach that aligns operative decision-making with treatment biology rather than pretreatment calcification anatomy [12].

METHODS

We conducted a focused literature review of peer-reviewed studies evaluating the clinical significance of persistent calcifications after NAC in HER2+ breast cancer, emphasizing outcomes relevant to local control and surgical decision-making. We prioritized recent Korean multicenter and institutional cohorts that specifically addressed post-NAC calcification patterns and prognosis [1011121314] and incorporated complementary international evidence from radiologic-pathologic correlation [89] and imaging-response studies [1516] to contextualize Korean findings within broader clinical evidence.

Studies were included if they involved patients treated with contemporary NAC regimens, including HER2-directed therapy; reported outcomes stratified by pathologic response or post-NAC calcification status; and provided follow-up data for local recurrence, disease-free survival, or overall survival. For each included study, we extracted the study design, NAC regimen details, pCR definitions, imaging assessment methods, surgical margin status, oncological outcomes, and follow-up duration. For this review, pCR was defined as absence of residual invasive carcinoma in the breast (ypT0/Tis ypN0), consistent with contemporary Korean practice [10] that considers ypT0 and ypTis as equivalent categories for oncologic interpretation. Clinical complete response (cCR) of the index lesion was defined as complete resolution of the invasive mass on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), regardless of persistent residual calcifications on mammography.

RESULTS

Several Korean institutional and multicenter cohorts have consistently reported favorable oncologic outcomes among patients achieving pCR after NAC, irrespective of residual calcifications. In a Samsung Medical Center (SMC) cohort of 150 hormone receptor-negative (HR−)/HER2+ patients, residual calcifications were not associated with local recurrence-free, disease-free, distant metastasis-free, or overall survival (all p > 0.5, median follow-up of 52.5 months) [12]. Similarly, a dual-center analysis of 323 patients from the Kangbuk Samsung Hospital and SMC reported no locoregional recurrence among patients with pCR and residual calcifications during a median follow-up period of 71 months [13]. A large multicenter study involving 2,803 Korean patients who underwent BCS demonstrated superior 5-year local recurrence-free survival in patients achieving pCR compared to non-pCR groups (97.4% vs. 91.5%–94.0%, p < 0.05), independent of margin status. Furthermore, among 286 patients with pCR and ypTis, margin involvement was not statistically associated with local recurrence risk (hazard ratio, 2.49; 95% confidence interval, 0.32–19.37; p = 0.366) (Table 1) [10121314]. This result should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of events and wide confidence interval in this subgroup.

Risk prediction and imaging studies further supported selective surgical de-escalation. A validated prediction model identified HR−/HER2+ tumors with a high Ki-67 index as strong predictors of the absence of residual carcinoma within calcification fields, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.844 [14]. Imaging-response studies showed that calcifications did not compromise the MRI-based prediction of pCR (AUC 0.89 vs. 0.91; AUC 0.85 vs. 0.83) and that MRI outperformed mammography in detecting residual invasive disease [111516]. International radiologic-pathologic correlation studies similarly reported poor concordance between residual calcifications and viable carcinoma [89].

Across Korean and international cohorts, persistent calcifications after NAC showed limited association with residual invasive disease, especially in HR−/HER2+ tumors, and were not associated with worse oncologic outcomes when pCR was achieved and standard adjuvant therapy was administered.

DISCUSSION

Persistent mammographic calcifications after NAC have traditionally been interpreted as markers of residual disease extent and have therefore been used to justify wider excision or mastectomy [123]. However, in the contemporary era, converging evidence supports a clinically actionable reinterpretation: persistent calcifications should not automatically mandate wider resection or mastectomy in HER2+ disease when pCR is ultimately confirmed and standard adjuvant therapy is delivered.

The key conceptual shift is that oncologic risk appears to be driven primarily by biologic treatment response rather than by the anatomic footprint of calcifications. Results from Korean multicenter and institutional cohorts consistently demonstrate excellent local control and survival among patients achieving pCR that underwent BCS followed by whole-breast irradiation, even when calcifications persist [101213]. These findings support a response-adapted model in which persistent calcifications are regarded as an imperfect surrogate rather than a direct mandate for total excision of their anatomic extent.

Importantly, this interpretation is not confined to Korean experience. Radiologic-pathologic correlation studies from other healthcare settings have shown that post-treatment calcifications frequently reflect stromal remodeling or treatment-related changes rather than viable invasive carcinoma, and that calcification extent correlates poorly with residual tumor burden [89]. In these international cohorts, approximately 38%–55% of residual calcifications contained no invasive disease, and the extent of calcification showed a poor correlation with tumor burden (correlation coefficient 0.23; p = 0.08). Such external coherence strengthens the argument that routine decision-making regarding the surgical extent based solely on the calcification field risks overtreatment in a subset of good responders [171819]. However, it is equally important to acknowledge studies that urge caution. Lee et al. [1] prospectively evaluated whole mastectomy specimens from patients whose invasive tumors responded to NAC but whose calcification extent was unchanged and found viable invasive or in situ disease around the residual calcifications in four of five cases; only one patient had a true pCR, leading the authors to conclude that complete removal of residual microcalcifications is necessary because residual cancer location cannot be predicted from preoperative imaging alone. Azam et al. [9] reported that, across mixed-subtype cohorts, mammographic calcifications correlated only weakly with the maximum dimension of invasive disease (R2 = 10.8%) and overestimated invasive disease extent by 57% overall; notably, in HR−/HER2+ patients specifically, calcifications overestimated invasive disease by 86%, and benign-only calcifications were most prevalent in this subgroup (47.8%), underscoring that imaging-pathology discordance is particularly pronounced in HER2+ disease. Feliciano et al. [3] demonstrated that in 34 of 90 patients (37.8%), residual calcifications were associated with DCIS or invasive carcinoma on final pathology, and concluded that loss of MRI enhancement alone does not predict absence of residual tumor with sufficient accuracy to leave calcifications in place. Collectively, these findings reinforce that the response-adapted approach proposed herein applies specifically to patients with a confirmed radiological complete response (rCR) of the invasive index lesion, favorable tumor biology, and guideline-concordant adjuvant radiation, and should not be extended to broader or unselected patient populations.

Within this context, a response-adaptive surgical approach can be articulated as a pragmatic framework (Figure 1), whereby (i) surgical clip placement is used to mark the invasive index lesion before NAC [20]; (ii) multimodal reassessment after NAC is integrated with MRI, targeted ultrasound, and mammography [1121]; and in cases of cCR (iii) surgery focused on clip-guided excision of the original invasive tumor bed, irrespective of residual calcifications, followed by standard whole-breast irradiation when BCS is selected. In this framework, extension of resection to encompass the entire pretreatment calcification field is not a routine practice but may be considered selectively based on a multidisciplinary review and the pathology of the surgical specimen [1214].

Response-adapted surgical algorithm for HER2-positive breast cancer with residual calcifications following neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

This algorithm shifts the surgical focus from the anatomic extent of pretreatment calcifications to the biological response of the invasive component. Steps I and II: Surgical clip placement in the index lesion and multimodal post-treatment imaging assessment (MRI, ultrasound, and mammography) guide response evaluation. Step III: Patients achieving an rCR of the invasive mass undergo clip-guided lumpectomy targeting the original tumor bed, regardless of residual calcifications. Step IV: Further management is stratified according to pathological response and biological risk factors. Patients with invasive pCR (ypT0/Tis ypN0) and favorable biology (HR−/HER2+, high Ki-67) proceed with standard whole-breast irradiation without wider excision, whereas those presenting residual invasive disease or unfavorable biology (HR+/HER2+, low Ki-67) are considered for selective re-excision or mastectomy.

Critically, the proposed message is conditional, and should be explicitly stated to avoid inappropriate extrapolation. The favorable outcomes summarized in these cohorts occurred in settings where standard local therapy (including whole-breast irradiation after BCS) and indicated systemic therapy were consistently delivered. The implication is not that calcifications are irrelevant, but that calcifications alone should not automatically dictate wider resection or mastectomy in patients who demonstrate rCR or cCR following NAC, particularly when postoperative pathology confirms invasive pCR and guideline-concordant adjuvant therapy is administered [101222].

Several limitations remain. Follow-up in many datasets was mid-term, and longer observation periods are needed to fully characterize late events. Although encouraging, the 5-year local recurrence-free survival of 97.4% reported in the largest Korean cohort may not be sufficient to exclude late locoregional events; longer term, 10-year follow-up will be required to confirm the durability of this approach in HER2+ disease. Calcification biology is heterogeneous, and determinants of calcification persistence beyond subtype and proliferation remain poorly understood. Generalizability of prediction models requires external validation in non-Korean populations. Differences in breast density, mammographic screening protocols, NAC regimen composition, and post-NAC imaging practices between Korean and Western or other non-Asian healthcare settings may influence both the prevalence of residual calcifications and the reliability of imaging-based response assessment. Therefore, direct application of the proposed algorithm to these populations should be undertaken with caution pending prospective validation in geographically diverse cohorts. Most importantly, the overarching challenge is the accurate preoperative identification of true pCR. Prospective experience (including tumor-bed biopsy strategies) indicates that currently available approaches may not achieve the reliability required to avoid surgery, underscoring the need for meticulous pathology and multidisciplinary imaging interpretation within any de-escalation pathway [23].

In summary, available Korean and international evidence supports a biologically informed shift away from surgical decision-making based solely on residual calcifications after NAC in HER2+ breast cancer. A response-adapted, lesion-focused strategy––centered on clip-guided excision of the index lesion with standardized multimodality reassessment and consistent adjuvant radiation therapy––appears oncologically reasonable in appropriately selected patients, although prospective validation with standardized imaging-pathology correlation and redefined selection criteria remains necessary.
