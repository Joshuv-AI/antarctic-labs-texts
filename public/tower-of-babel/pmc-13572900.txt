Assessment of Pain Characteristics, Attribution, and Nociplasticity in Patients With Inflammatory Myopathies: An International Survey Study
Authors: Mohammad Radaideh, Juan Schmukler, Alexander Best, Joshlean Fair, Manuel Lubinus, Yvonne C Lee, Didem Saygin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIdiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs) have historically been viewed to cause painless weakness and are therefore poorly understood. We aimed to report pain characteristics in patients with IIMs and estimate the contributions of fibromyalgia and common painful comorbidities to pain intensity.MethodsAn international electronic survey was circulated to individuals who self‐reported IIMs and pain attributable to myositis. Questions included pain characteristics, perceptions of provider understanding of their pain, comorbidities, and the Fibromyalgia Survey Questionnaire. Hierarchical multiple linear regression was used to examine the contribution of fibromyalgia symptom burden and other painful comorbidities to pain intensity (0–10 visual analog scale), adjusting for age, sex, country, IIM subtype, and duration.ResultsA total of 552 participants (mean age 58.0 years; 81.3% female) with pain attributable to myositis were included. Mean pain intensity was 4.6 out of 10. Most participants reported pain starting near diagnosis. The most painful areas were back, neck, and upper legs. The majority reported that their pain was not well understood by their care team. Approximately 60% met the fibromyalgia criteria and reported higher, more persistent, and widespread pain. Pain intensity was the highest in overlap myositis and lowest in inclusion body myositis. Fibromyalgia symptom burden explained 20% of variance in pain intensity, whereas osteoarthritis, degenerative disc disease, and diabetic neuropathy were not significant contributors.ConclusionMost patients with self‐reported IIMs experience moderate, persistent pain that is not well understood by their care team. Fibromyalgia symptom burden accounted for a substantial proportion of pain variability, suggesting an important nociplastic component, though additional mechanisms likely contribute.

INTRODUCTION

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs) are a heterogeneous group of autoimmune diseases including dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), immune‐mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), inclusion body myositis (IBM), overlap myositis (OM) and antisynthetase syndrome (ASyS). 1 Clinical manifestations of IIMs include symmetrical proximal weakness (~70%), rash (~54%), interstitial lung disease (~30%), dysphagia (~26%), and arthralgia/arthritis (~32%). 2 Symptoms of IIMs can vary by the specific IIM subtype. For example, DM is characterized by proximal muscle weakness and skin findings such as heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, and shawl sign, whereas ASyS more commonly presents with interstitial lung disease and arthritis. 3 Extramuscular manifestations are uncommon in patients with IMNM and IBM, whereas they are notably prominent in patients with OM. 3

Besides these symptoms, recent studies highlight pain as a prevalent symptom experienced by patients with IIMs. 4 Contrary to a common belief that myositis causes painless weakness, pain prevalence ranges from 64% to 86%, with 45% of patients reporting pain levels >3 out of 10 on a numeric rating scale. 5 , 6 Additionally, 71% of patients with IIMs report pain interference (ie, hindrance of their daily activities by pain) with worse fatigue and physical function and higher perceived disease activity among patients with pain. 7 Mechanisms underlying myositis‐related pain are not fully understood and may involve nociceptive components (due to muscle and/or fascia inflammation) and nociplastic process (central sensitization). Despite the high prevalence and significant burden of pain, characteristics of pain experienced by patients with myositis have not been previously reported beyond pain intensity. Understanding pain characteristics can help provide critical insights into underlying mechanisms of pain in these patients, which can then improve the management strategies.

In this large cross‐sectional international survey study, our aims were three‐fold. First, we provided an in‐depth characterization of pain including intensity, diurnal change, pattern, aggravating and alleviating factors, perceived response to analgesics, distribution of pain, attribution of pain, and patients’ perceptions of provider understanding of their pain across IIM subtypes. Second, we examined the differences in pain characteristics based on meeting the fibromyalgia diagnostic criteria as the prototypical nociplastic condition. Lastly, we estimated the independent relative contributions of fibromyalgia symptom burden and common painful comorbidities such as osteoarthritis to pain intensity among patients with IIMs.

METHODS

Survey

An anonymous electronic survey was created in English and deployed using REDCap, a secure, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)‐compliant electronic data capture platform. This study was reviewed by the Rush University Medical Center Institutional Review Board and was deemed exempt (25071801). The survey was circulated and remained open between September 2025 and January 2026 through digital outreach of the study team and patient organizations including The Myositis Association and Myositis Support and Understanding with regular reminders throughout this period.

The survey consisted of three sections: (i) demographics (age, sex, country of residence) and disease information (IIM subtype, disease duration, and comorbidities), (ii) pain characteristics, and (iii) the Fibromyalgia Survey Questionnaire (FSQ). The survey targeted patients with IIMs who report pain attributable to myositis; therefore, those with no pain attributable to myositis were not able to proceed to the sections after demographics. Questions related to pain characteristics included pain intensity (0–10 visual analog scale) at the time of survey and during a myositis flare, diurnal variation, pattern, aggravating and alleviating factors of pain, and perceived response to acetaminophen and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory medications (NSAIDs). Additionally, two questions that allowed free‐text responses were attributions of pain to myositis and perceived effective modalities for management of myositis‐related pain.

The FSQ consists of the Symptom Severity Scale (SSS) and the Widespread Pain Index (WPI). 8 The SSS evaluates the severity of three core symptoms experienced over the past week on a three‐point scale: fatigue, trouble thinking or remembering, and waking unrefreshed as well as the presence of the following symptoms in the past six months: lower abdominal pain or cramps, depression, and headache. The SSS score ranges from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating greater symptom burden. The WPI is the number of body regions in which the participant reports pain or tenderness over the past week. WPI scores range from 0 to 19, with higher scores indicating a more diffuse pain distribution. The Fibromyalgia Severity Scale (FSS) is calculated as the sum of the SSS and the WPI and ranges from 0 to 31, with higher scores reflecting greater overall fibromyalgia symptom burden and pain severity. We used the 2016 Fibromyalgia Diagnostic Criteria for fibromyalgia classification. 8 Based on this criteria, a participant met fibromyalgia criteria if all three of the following were fulfilled: (i) generalized pain (defined as pain in at least four of five regions [left upper region, right upper region, left lower region, right lower region, and axial region]), (ii) presence of symptoms for at least three months, and (iii) WPI score of ≥7 with an SSS score of ≥5 or a WPI score between 4 and 6 with an SSS score of 9 or more.

Study population

Participants were included if they self‐reported a diagnosis of IIM, reported pain attributable to myositis, and completed the required demographic and pain‐characteristic questions needed for the primary analyses. Participants with incomplete required demographic or pain‐characteristic data were excluded from the cohort. Analyses involving fibromyalgia classification were only for participants who completed the FSQ.

Statistical analysis

This was a cross‐sectional survey study. Variables were summarized using descriptive statistics with means and SDs for continuous variables and total numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed using t‐test and chi‐square test, as appropriate. The free‐text answers were analyzed using ChatGPT (GPT‐5.3) to identify recurring themes, summarize common response patterns, and select representative patient quotes. 9 , 10 For each free‐text question, prompts were designed to ask ChatGPT to group similar responses into clinically meaningful themes, provide brief theme definitions and frequency of each theme, and identify representative quotes. The generated themes and selected quotes were then reviewed by investigators, refined as needed, and finalized. Hierarchical multiple linear regression analyses were conducted, in which blocks of variables were entered according to the following sequence: (i) demographic and disease‐related factors (age, sex, country [grouped based on geographic region], IIM subtype, and IIM duration), (ii) comorbid painful conditions (osteoarthritis, degenerative disc disease, and diabetic neuropathy), and (iii) the FSS. The goal was to isolate the contribution of each block of factors to pain intensity (outcome variable) after controlling for all variables entered on preceding blocks. Model fit was assessed using R2 and adjusted R2; changes in R2 between models were used to evaluate the incremental explanatory power of other comorbidities and fibromyalgia symptom burden. Data analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0 (IBM Corp).

RESULTS

Study population

A total of 797 participants accessed the survey. After exclusion of patients who reported no myositis‐related pain (n = 59), those with incomplete demographic or pain‐characteristic data (n = 184), and those with diagnoses other than IIM (n = 2), 552 participants who reported self‐attributed myositis‐related pain were included in the analysis. Of these, 438 participants completed the FSQ and were included in analyses related to fibromyalgia classification.

Mean (±SD) age of the total cohort (n = 552) was 58.0 (±13.8) years, with the majority of participants being female (81.3%; Supplementary Table 1). Most participants resided in the United States (n = 350, 63.4%), followed by Australia (n = 67, 12.1%), Canada (n = 51, 9.2%), Germany (n = 24, 4.3%), the United Kingdom (n = 19, 3.4%), and other countries (n = 41; 7.4%). DM was the most common reported subtype (n = 225, 40.7%), followed by IBM (n = 155, 28.0%), PM (n = 61, 11.0%), IMNM (n = 40, 7.2%), ASyS (n = 33, 5.9%), and OM (n = 22, 3.9%), whereas 2.8% were unsure of their subtype. Approximately 14% of participants reported IIM duration of less than one year. Among those who reported disease duration of ≥1 year, the mean disease duration was 7.3 (±7.6) years. Self‐reported prevalence of painful comorbidities was 24.6% for osteoarthritis, 17.7% for degenerative disc disease, 14.8% for fibromyalgia, and 3.8% for diabetic neuropathy.

Among 438 participants who completed the FSQ, 266 (60.7%) met the fibromyalgia criteria (Table 1). The prevalence of criteria‐defined fibromyalgia differed across IIM subtypes, with the highest prevalence in patients with OM (n = 17, 85.0%), followed by ASyS (n = 22, 75.9%), DM (n = 118, 63.4%), PM (n = 31, 63.2%), IBM (n = 56, 51.3%), IMNM (n = 15, 48.4%), and unspecified (n = 7, 2.6%). The majority of the patients (71.4%) reported that their pain was not well understood by their care team, which was significantly more common in patients who did not meet fibromyalgia criteria.

Attribution of pain to myositis

The majority of the patients expressed confidence in their responses to the question, “How do you determine or know that the pain you are describing is specifically related to your myositis and not due to other conditions or factors?” with only 6.4% indicating uncertainty. Nearly half of the participants (53.4%) attributed their pain to myositis based on the distribution of their pain to myositis‐affected body areas, whereas others based it on worsening with activity or use (14.3%); pain occurring in association with weakness (14.3%); pain tracking with flares, rash, muscle enzyme elevation, or disease activity (14.1%); and timing of pain starting with myositis diagnosis or not having a similar pain before myositis diagnosis (13.3%; Supplementary Table 2).

Pain characteristics of patients with IIMs

Pain intensity

Mean pain intensity at the time of survey completion was 4.6 (±2.3) out of 10, with higher intensity at 7.4 (±2.2) during flares (Table 1). Pain intensity was the highest among patients with OM (5.5 ± 2.3) and the lowest among patients with IBM (4.2 ± 2.4; Supplementary Table 3). Most participants (68.8%) indicated worsening pain during flares, whereas 21.5% were unsure and 5.2% reported no worsening. Patients with DM were most likely to report worsening pain with flares (85.3%), whereas patients with IBM were least likely to report worsening pain during flares (41.9%). Patients with all IIM subtypes reported at least a two‐point increase in their pain during flares including patients with IBM. Patients who met fibromyalgia criteria reported significantly higher levels of pain (5.5 ± 2.0 vs 3.7 ± 2.3) and were more likely to report worsening pain during flares (81.2% vs 62.2%) compared to those who did not meet fibromyalgia criteria.

Diurnal variation of pain intensity

Diurnal change of pain was variable in patients with IIMs and within each IIM subtype, with no clear predilection toward one specific pattern (Tables 1 and 2). Patients with IBM were the only exception, with 40.0% reporting worse pain in the evening compared to morning. For patients who met fibromyalgia criteria, the most common pattern was worsening in the evening (32.3%), whereas it was variable with different patterns of changes among patients who did not meet fibromyalgia criteria (20.9%–24.4% for each pattern; Table 1).

Pain patterns

The majority of patients with IIMs (77.2%) reported persistent pain, whereas 17.2% reported pain attacks with painless intervals (Table 1). Approximately 60% of patients with IBM and over 75% of patients with other IIM subtypes (77.5%–97.0%) reported persistent pain (Supplementary Table 3). Patients who met fibromyalgia criteria were significantly more likely to report persistent pain than those who did not meet fibromyalgia criteria (90.2% vs 67.4%).

Pain location

The most painful body areas were low back (78.0%), neck (71.9%), and upper legs (71.2%–71.9%) in all patients with IIMs (n = 438) and across all IIM subtypes (Figure 1). Pain distribution was more widespread among patients who met fibromyalgia criteria, with most body regions affected in over 70% of the patients. The most painful body areas for patients who did not meet the fibromyalgia criteria were similar, with low back (62.2%), upper legs (50.0%–51.2%), and neck (51.2%).

Exacerbating factors

Physical activity or exercise was the most frequently selected pain trigger (76.9%), followed by overexertion (70.8%), poor sleep or fatigue (65.2%), stress or emotional distress (55.9%), and cold weather or temperature changes (55.9%; Table 1). Prolonged inactivity and infections were reported as pain triggers by approximately 44% to 46% of patients. These findings were similar across IIM subtypes. Patients who met fibromyalgia criteria were significantly more likely to report worsening with physical activity, overexertion, poor sleep, stress, temperature changes, and infections than those who did not meet fibromyalgia criteria (Table 1).

Alleviating factors

Perceived benefit from analgesics were variable, with 31.3% and 38.9% of patients reporting benefit from acetaminophen and NSAIDs, respectively. Approximately 44.5% and 31.8% reported no benefit from acetaminophen and NSAIDs, respectively. These findings were similar across IIM subtypes except IBM (Supplementary Table 3). Patients with IBM (41.3%–47.7%) reported higher rates of benefit from acetaminophen and NSAIDs than patients with other IIM subtypes. Benefit from acetaminophen and NSAIDs were similar between patients who met and did not meet fibromyalgia criteria.

A total of 542 patients responded to the question, “What modalities, medications, or methods have you found helpful in reducing or managing your myositis‐related pain?” with 10.1% reporting that they have not found an effective method. The most commonly reported effective modality was exercise (movement, stretching, or physical therapy; 28.8%), followed by warmth (heat, hot baths, heating pads, sauna/infrared; 25.6%), NSAIDs (22.3%), disease‐directed nonsteroid therapies (such as intravenous Ig, methotrexate; 22.0%), rest (sleep, activity modification; 19.2%), and glucocorticoids (18.3%; Supplementary Table 4).

Contributions of painful comorbidities and fibromyalgianess to pain levels

In the first model including age, sex, geographic region, IIM subtype, and duration, only age was significantly associated with pain levels (Table 2). This model accounted for a small but statistically significant proportion of variance in pain intensity (adjusted R2 = 0.03, P < 0.01). In model 2, comorbid painful conditions (osteoarthritis, degenerative disc disease, and diabetic neuropathy) were added and did not significantly improve model fit (adjusted R2 = 0.04, ΔR2 = 0.01, P = 0.3), with none of the added predictors reaching significance. In model 3, the FSS was added and produced a large, significant increment in explained variance (ΔR2 = 0.20, P < 0.001), bringing the total variance explained to 27% (adjusted R2 = 0.24). The FSS was the only significant predictor in the final model, whereas age, sex, geographic region, IIM subtype, and duration and comorbid painful conditions were nonsignificant.

DISCUSSION

In this large international cross‐sectional survey study, most patients with IIMs and myositis‐related pain reported at least moderate, persistent pain, which began around the time of their myositis diagnosis. Pain was attributed to myositis by patients primarily based on the distribution of their pain to myositis‐affected body areas. Conventional analgesics (acetaminophen and NSAIDs) were perceived as beneficial in less than 40% of participants. Together, these findings highlight the urgent need to better understand myositis‐related pain.

Similar to other systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases, nociplastic mechanisms may play an important role in pathogenesis of myositis‐related pain. Central sensitization is the key feature of nociplastic pain and refers to heightened excitability within the central nervous system and amplification of sensory input, which results in increased pain perception. 12 , 13 Over time, ongoing neuroimmune signaling including cytokine‐mediated inflammation and activation of glial cells can further amplify pain transmission in the spinal cord and brain. 14 As a result, pain may become more widespread and out of proportion to the degree of active inflammation, and it can persist even when disease activity is low or in remission. 15 In our study, prevalence of the criteria‐defined prototypical nociplastic pain condition, fibromyalgia, was 60.7%. This is remarkably high compared to pooled prevalence of fibromyalgia in IIMs in general (~34%) and other systemic autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (21%). 16 , 17 The high rates of criteria‐defined fibromyalgia in our study are likely due to only including participants who experience pain rather than all‐comers. As expected, patients who met fibromyalgia criteria reported more severe, more widespread, and more persistent pain, greater worsening during flares, and more frequent exacerbation with activity, overexertion, poor sleep, stress, and temperature changes than those who did not meet fibromyalgia criteria. Moreover, fibromyalgia symptom burden was a significant contributor of pain intensity on hierarchical models, supporting that nociplastic features may contribute to the pain experience in a subset of patients.

Although nociplasticity appears to be an important contributor to pain, it did not account for the entire pain experience. In the hierarchical models, FSS explained 20% of the variance in pain intensity, meaning that the majority of the variance remained unexplained. Demographics, disease duration, and subtype and other common painful comorbidities, including osteoarthritis, degenerative disc disease, and diabetic neuropathy, did not significantly explain the variance in pain intensity. These findings suggest the presence of other factors that may play a role in myositis‐related pain. Even though we could not test this question in our study, several findings suggest a role of disease‐related nociceptive mechanisms. 5 This interpretation may be supported by the observations that pain intensity increased substantially during flares, from 4.6 to 7.4, and that nearly 70% of participants reported worsening pain during flares. In addition, pain in the upper legs was reported by 72% of participants because thigh musculature is the most commonly affected muscle group in IIMs. Improvement of pain levels in many patients with immunomodulatory therapies and glucocorticoids may also support this hypothesis. Future studies are needed to better define the role of disease‐related mechanisms in driving pain intensity in this population.

Pain characteristics were broadly similar across IIM subtypes, but some differences were noted. Similar to prior studies, 18 , 19 patients with IBM reported the lowest pain intensity, whereas patients with OM reported the highest pain intensity among IIM subtypes. 7 , 20 The lower pain intensity reported by patients with IBM may reflect its distinct disease phenotype, which is often characterized by slowly progressive weakness rather than acute inflammatory flares. These findings may not be surprising because patients with OM are more likely to experience potentially painful extramuscular manifestations such as arthritis, digital ulcers, and Raynaud phenomenon in addition to myositis compared to other IIM subtypes. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size for some of the IIM subtypes in this study.

The majority of the participants, particularly those who did not meet the fibromyalgia criteria, reported that their pain was not well understood by their care team. This finding may result from pain not being recognized as a common symptom of myositis among health care professionals. Consistent with this finding, a recent study showed that nearly one‐third of all myositis‐related posts on Reddit include pain, suggesting that patients may be seeking information, validation, and support on pain beyond the clinical setting. 21 Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of greater awareness among health care providers to facilitate more patient‐centric care and ultimately improve the outcomes of patients with myositis.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a cross‐sectional survey study; therefore, we cannot infer causality or determine relationships among fibromyalgia symptom burden, disease activity, comorbidities, and pain intensity. In addition, inclusion was restricted to participants who attributed their pain to myositis, resulting in a pain‐enriched cohort; therefore, pain severity and the proportion meeting the fibromyalgia criteria may be higher than in the broader population with IIMs. This survey was electronic, only in English, and primarily distributed through online outreach via patient organizations; therefore, the results may not be generalizable to the whole population with IIMs due to possible selection bias with participants possibly representing individuals with greater symptom burden and engagement with their disease. Data were self‐reported, including IIM subtype and comorbidities; therefore, recall and misclassification bias are possible. Using the FSQ to understand the contribution of nociplasticity to pain levels may not be the optimal approach; future studies that incorporate quantitative sensory testing could be helpful to further examine this question. Additionally, we used large language model assistance for free‐text responses to the pain attribution question, which is a new and explorative approach. The limitations of this approach include variability in results based on model versions and prompts and missing the minority perspectives compared to a human analyst. 22 Therefore, findings should be interpreted with these constraints. Finally, we did not collect measures of disease activity, such as physician global assessment or creatine kinase levels; therefore, we could not directly assess the contribution of active myositis to pain intensity in this study.

This study also has important strengths. It is the largest international study to date specifically focused on pain in patients with IIM and the first to provide an in‐depth description of pain characteristics in this population. Beyond pain intensity, our study captured multiple aspects of pain (eg, distribution, triggers, alleviating factors) that have not been previously studied.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive characterization of pain among participants with self‐reported IIM and myositis‐related pain. It offers further insights into how patients attribute their pain to myositis and explores differences across IIM subtypes and fibromyalgia as a potential contributor to myositis‐related pain. Future studies should use other tools including quantitative sensory testing to assess the mechanistic role of nociplasticity in myositis‐related pain.
