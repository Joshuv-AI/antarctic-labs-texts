SDPR–STK38 axis controls the proliferation–differentiation balance in alveolar type II cells
Authors: Jie Wang, Xuepei Lei, Yiying Huang, Jiaming Tang, Guiying Shi, Hang Li, Lin Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlveolar type II (AT2) cells act as progenitors that sustain gas exchange and drive postinjury repair. Disruption of their proliferation–differentiation balance promotes pulmonary fibrosis and acute respiratory distress syndrome, but the core regulatory mechanisms are unclear. Serum deprivation response protein (SDPR, cavin‐2), a caveolae‐associated protein involved in proliferation and lipid metabolism, may modulate AT2 fate. This study investigated how the SDPR–STK38 axis regulates AT2 proliferation and differentiation and its impact on lung homeostasis and regeneration.MethodsSDPR knockout (SDPR− / −) mice and wild‐type littermates were used to evaluate alveolar structure, AT2/AT1 composition, and lung function at baseline and after LPS‐induced acute lung injury. Histology, immunostaining, and lung function tests were combined with mass spectrometry and co‐immunoprecipitation to identify SDPR‐interacting proteins. Gain‐ and loss‐of‐function assays in lung tissues and cells were used to assess how SDPR and STK38 regulate GSK‐3β/cyclin D1 signaling and Notch–Hes1–dependent AT2 differentiation. Data were analyzed using standard statistical tests appropriate for the experimental design.ResultsSDPR deficiency disrupted alveolar architecture and impaired lung function, accompanied by excessive AT2 expansion and reduced differentiation into AT1 cells. Proteomic and biochemical analyses identified STK38 as a novel SDPR‐binding protein. SDPR loss increased STK38 expression, enhanced GSK‐3β/cyclin D1 signaling, and promoted AT2 proliferation, while simultaneously reducing Hes1 expression, impairing vacuole formation, and attenuating AT2 differentiation. In the LPS model, SDPR− / − mice developed more severe pathological injury and declined lung function compared to wild‐type controls.ConclusionsThe SDPR–STK38 axis coordinately controls the proliferation–differentiation balance of AT2 cells via GSK‐3β/cyclin D1 and Notch–Hes1 signaling. SDPR deficiency drives aberrant AT2 expansion, blocks differentiation toward AT1 cells, and aggravates acute lung injury, highlighting this pathway as a potential therapeutic target for promoting alveolar regeneration.

BACKGROUND

Maintaining pulmonary homeostasis and, therefore, normal respiratory function requires tightly regulated proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis within the alveolar epithelium. This functional gas exchange unit comprises two epithelial cell types: type I (AT1) and type II (AT2) alveolar cells. 1 AT1 cells are extremely thin and cover approximately 95% of the alveolar surface, forming an ultrathin barrier that mediates gas transfer between the airspace and capillary blood. 1 In contrast, AT2 cells are cuboidal and synthesize surfactant proteins and lipids, which reduce surface tension and prevent alveolar collapse, 2 regulate transepithelial ion and water transport, 3 and secrete immunomodulatory factors. 4 AT2 cells function as facultative stem cells capable of self‐renewal and differentiation into AT1 cells during homeostasis and after injury, 5 , 6 When AT1 cells are damaged by mechanical stress or infection, neighboring AT2 cells proliferate and transdifferentiate through an intermediate state before AT1 characteristics are acquired to restore the epithelium. 7 , 8 Recent studies have highlighted that their regenerative capacity is tightly linked to cellular maintenance mechanisms, such as autophagy, which reprograms metabolic pathways to meet the high energy demands of alveolar repair. 9 However, the molecular signals that regulate AT2 self‐renewal, lineage commitment, and plasticity remain incompletely understood. Multiple pathways, including Notch, 10 Hedgehog, 11 Wnt, 12 and thyroid hormone receptor‐β, 13 govern AT2‐to‐AT1 differentiation, and their dysregulation results in hyperproliferation or fibrosis. Recent single‐cell transcriptomic analyses have revealed heterogeneity within AT2 and transitional populations and suggested that aging impairs AT2 differentiation, contributing to lung disease. 14 During acute lung injury 15 and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, AT2 cells proliferate, and their senescence activates fibroblasts and promotes fibrosis. 16

Another unresolved aspect of alveolar biology concerns lamellar bodies, lysosome‐related organelles in AT2 cells that store surfactant. Proteomic analyses have identified more than 500 lamellar body proteins, including previously unrecognized phospholipid‐binding proteins, such as SDPR. 17 The alveolar microenvironment, consisting of macrophages, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells, provides essential cues that regulate AT2 behavior 18 , 19 ; for example, fibroblasts and lipofibroblasts supply Wnt 12 and PDGFRα 20 signals that maintain AT2 stemness. How these exogenous cues integrate with endogenous cell cycle regulators to coordinate AT2 self‐renewal, differentiation, and survival remains a key unresolved question.

Our research focuses on SDPR (also known as cavin‐2), a member of the cavin family that regulates caveolar morphology. 21 Caveolae are membrane invaginations enriched in caveolin and cavin proteins. 22 SDPR stabilizes the caveolar coat through its interaction with cavin‐1, and its deficiency selectively eliminates endothelial caveolae in the lungs and adipose tissues. 21 SDPR overexpression produces deeper caveolar invaginations, whereas its loss leads to shallower or flattened structures. 23 These structural changes influence mechanotransduction and lipid signaling. In addition to its structural functions, SDPR exerts inhibitory effects on cancer metastasis by regulating fatty acid oxidation 24 and promoting apoptosis. 25 In lung adenocarcinoma, the expression levels of CAV1 and CAVIN1/2/3 are markedly reduced, whereas high SDPR levels are associated with a more favorable prognosis. 26 SDPR overexpression induces apoptosis and S‐phase arrest, thereby suppressing cancer cell proliferation and migration. 26

Our preliminary analyses indicated high SDPR expression in alveolar epithelial cells. Single‐cell transcriptomic data showed that SDPR was highly expressed in AT1, transitional, and AT2 cells. However, whether SDPR regulates AT2 stemness remains unknown. Multiomics analyses (GWAS, eQTL and mQTL) predict that SDPR is associated with lung injury pathways involving ERK/AKT/STAT3 signaling. 23 In a rat model of ventilator‐induced lung injury, SDPR expression was downregulated in injured lungs and AT2 cells; SDPR overexpression downregulated inflammation and autophagy by binding to ERK1/2 and inhibiting MAPK/ERK signaling, thereby alleviating pathological damage. 27 These findings suggest that SDPR confers protection against mechanical stress and inflammation. Nevertheless, the physiological functions of SDPR in normal AT2 cells remain poorly understood. Whether SDPR influences AT2 stemness and how it integrates with cell cycle regulators to modulate lung regeneration remain unresolved questions.

In summary, we generated an SDPR knockout (SDPR−/−) mouse model and an SDPR knockout A549 cell line. SDPR deletion leads to alveolar septal thickening and disrupts the balance between AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation. Mechanistically, SDPR interacts with STK38 to regulate Hes1 and cyclin D1 signaling, thereby influencing AT2 cell fate. These findings identify SDPR and STK38 as key regulatory nodes that control AT2 cell fate and provide novel molecular targets for therapeutic interventions.

METHODS

Mice

The SDPR−/− mice and littermate controls were generated in our laboratory. Male and female cohorts of approximately 10‐week‐old mice were randomly selected for the experiments. All mice were housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions in a barrier facility at the Institute of Laboratory Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the institute (no.: BL24001) and conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines.

Murine model of LPS‐induced ALI

SDPR−/− mice and their wild‐type littermates were subjected to an acute lung injury (ALI) model induced by intratracheal lipopolysaccharide (LPS) instillation. The mice were anesthetized, and tracheal exposure and intubation were performed under sterile conditions. A total of 40 μL LPS (5 mg/mL in phosphate‐buffered saline [PBS]) was delivered into the trachea, whereas control animals received an equal volume of PBS. After recovery, mice were monitored and maintained for 48 h. Pulmonary function was subsequently assessed using a standardized lung function testing system. The mice were then euthanized, and lung tissues were collected for histological evaluation. The left lung was fixed, paraffin‐embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess lung injury, including alveolar wall thickening, inflammatory infiltration, edema, and hemorrhage.

Smith scoring method

Lung injury was evaluated using a semiquantitative scoring system. Pulmonary edema, alveolar and septal inflammation, alveolar and interstitial hemorrhage, atelectasis, and hyaline membrane formation were individually scored on a scale of 0–4. A score of 0 indicated no detectable lesion; 1 represented involvement of less than 25% of the field; 2 represented 25%–50%; 3 represented 50%–75%; and 4 represented more than 75% of the high‐power field. The total injury score for each sample was calculated as the sum of all five parameters. For each animal, 10 randomly selected high‐power fields were examined, and the mean score was used as the final lung injury index.

Cell lines

A549 and HEK293T cells (Procell, Wuhan, China) were cultured under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). A549 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium (Life Technologies, California, USA), whereas HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Life Technologies). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% dual antibody.

Plasmid constructs, lentiviral production, and cell transfection

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting human SDPR mRNA (designed based on NCBI sequences) were cloned into the pSicoR vector (Addgene, USA), and the target sequences are listed in Table S1. Lentiviral knockdown constructs for SDPR (pLV‐hU6‐SDPR‐shRNA01; sh‐SDPR) and the corresponding nontargeting control (sh‐NC) were obtained from SyngenTech (Beijing, China). The SDPR overexpression plasmid (PCDH_CMV‐SDPR‐3 × Flag; PCDH‐SDPR) and its empty‐vector control (PCDH‐NC) were obtained from the same supplier. The STK38 overexpression vector (pCMV‐mCherry‐STK38‐3 × HA; PCDH‐STK38) and its control were purchased from Miaoling Bioscience (Wuhan, China). Knockdown plasmids were packaged into lentiviral particles (Addgene, USA). A549 cells were transduced with lentiviruses at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100–150 in the presence of 10 μg/mL Polybrene (Life Technologies). For transient overexpression assays, HEK293T cells were transfected with the corresponding plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000.

Noninvasive measurement of lung function

Pulmonary function was assessed in mice using whole‐body plethysmography (WBP, DSI, Minnesota, USA). The WBP system was integrated using the DSI Buxco FinePointe software for data acquisition. After calibration of all channels, mice were placed in the chamber and allowed to acclimatize for approximately 30 min. Once respiratory patterns stabilized, continuous recordings were acquired for 30 min. Raw data were exported to Microsoft Excel for calculation of mean values. Each data point in the statistical plots represents an individual animal. The evaluated parameters included respiratory frequency (f), minute ventilation (MVb), tidal volume (TVb), inspiratory time (Ti), expiratory time (Te), and expiratory flow at 50% of tidal volume (EF50).

Dry/wet weight ratio and lung index measurement

After euthanasia by cervical dislocation, lung tissues were immediately harvested, and wet weights were recorded. Tissues were then placed in a 60°C oven for 24 h until a constant dry weight was obtained. The dry‐to‐wet weight ratio was calculated as the dry weight divided by the wet weight. The lung index was determined by dividing the wet lung weight by the total body weight of the mouse.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as described previously. 28 Briefly, after fixation, the samples were permeabilized with Triton X‐100 and blocked for 30 min. Primary antibodies were applied overnight, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope. All antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Isolation and culture of alveolar type II cells

Primary AT2 cells were isolated from 6‐ to 8‐week‐old mice following a standard protocol, as described previously. 29 Freshly harvested lung tissues were minced and enzymatically digested to obtain single‐cell suspensions. Suspensions were filtered through a 70‐μm strainer, and AT2 cells were purified using magnetic‐activated cell sorting (MACS) with anti‐EPCAM microbeads and MS columns (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Purified AT2 cells were seeded into culture dishes and used for subsequent experiments.

Co‐immunoprecipitation

HEK293T cells were cotransfected with the indicated combinations of tagged plasmids. At 48 h posttransfection, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer. Clarified lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with tag‐specific antibodies. The antibodies and their dilution ratios are listed in Table S3. Protein–antibody complexes were captured using protein A/G magnetic beads, washed extensively, and eluted. Coprecipitated proteins were resolved by SDS‐PAGE and detected by Western blot using appropriate antibodies.

Western blot

Freshly isolated AT2 cells, A549 cells, and lung tissues were lysed on ice for 30 min in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a 100× protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‐PAGE on 4%–20% gradient gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h and sequentially incubated with primary and species‐appropriate secondary antibodies. The membranes were then washed and imaged using the Odyssey CLx near‐infrared system (LI‐COR Biosciences, Nebraska, USA) at 700 and 800 nm. All antibodies used for immunoblotting are listed in Table S3. Image Studio Lite 5.2.5 software was used for densitometric analysis. The signal intensity of each band was normalized to the corresponding internal loading control to obtain relative protein abundance.

Reverse transcription and quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of RNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan) on a StepOnePlus Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt) and normalized to that of a housekeeping gene. All qPCR primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Flow cytometry analysis

Peripheral blood samples from mice were collected using a FACSAria II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo software. Antibody information is provided in Table S4.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from freshly collected mouse lung tissues for transcriptomic analysis. Prior to library construction, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was removed or messenger RNA (mRNA) was enriched using poly‐T oligonucleotide‐coated magnetic beads, thereby improving sequencing sensitivity. RNA was then fragmented, and first‐strand cDNA was synthesized with random hexamer or oligo‐dT primers, followed by second‐strand synthesis. Double‐stranded cDNA was end‐repaired, A‐tailed, ligated to Illumina adapters, and amplified by PCR. Library concentration was measured using Qubit fluorometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the fragment size distribution was evaluated using capillary electrophoresis. Library preparation, sequencing, and initial bioinformatic analyses were conducted by Majorbio (Shanghai, China). Sequencing was performed using paired‐end reads (2 × 150 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were trimmed to remove adaptor sequences and low‐quality bases, and short reads were discarded. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using STAR or HISAT2. Gene‐level counts were generated using featureCounts. Normalization and differential expression analyses were conducted in R/Bioconductor using DESeq2 or edgeR, which model read counts with negative binomial distributions. Gene expression changes were considered significant after following false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing.

Quantification and statistical analysis

To ensure reproducibility, experimental procedures were repeated independently using biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using the F‐test. For data that met both criteria, unpaired t‐tests were used for two‐group comparisons. RNA‐seq data were analyzed using the R/Bioconductor environment. Differentially expressed genes were identified using negative binomial regression with the Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed when the corrected p‐value was <0.05 and the absolute log2 fold change was ≥1. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using hypergeometric testing, with significance defined as an FDR‐corrected p‐value <0.05. Specific analytical methods and parameters are provided in the figure legends.

RESULTS

SDPR deficiency induces alveolar septal thickening and reduces alveolar number

To characterize the expression profile of SDPR in murine lung tissue across different ages, Western blot analysis was performed on samples from 2‐, 6‐, 12‐, and 15‐month‐old mice. SDPR expression was detectable in all age groups (Figure S1A); therefore, adult mice were used for subsequent experiments. SDPR−/− mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9‐based gene editing. Quantitative PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated marked reductions in SDPR mRNA (Figure S1B) and protein levels (Figure 1A,B) in SDPR−/− lungs compared to SDPR+/+ controls. Consistent with the Western blot findings, immunohistochemical staining revealed strong SDPR expression in the pulmonary epithelium of SDPR+/+ mice, whereas no signal was detectable in the lung epithelial cells of SDPR−/− mice (Figure 1C). Gross examination revealed that SDPR−/− lungs appeared darker than SDPR+/+ lungs, suggesting possible inflammatory infiltration or edema (Figure 1D). The lung coefficient, calculated as the percentage ratio of lung weight to body weight, was significantly increased in SDPR−/− mice relative to wild‐type controls (Figure 1E). A reduced dry‐to‐wet weight ratio in SDPR−/− lungs indicated mild edema (Figure 1F). Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed a significant decrease in the mean number of alveoli per field in SDPR−/− mice, indicative of alveolar collapse (Figure 1G). SDPR−/− lungs also exhibited marked alveolar septal thickening and increased cellularity per field (Figure 1H), and quantitative analysis confirmed a significant elevation in alveolar septal cell counts (Figure 1I). Smith scoring further demonstrated increased lung injury scores in SDPR−/− animals (Figure 1J). Collectively, these findings indicate that SDPR deficiency induces pathological alterations in the lungs, including alveolar septal thickening and structural deterioration.

SDPR deletion evokes rapid and shallow breathing in mice

To evaluate the impact of SDPR deletion on respiratory function, noninvasive whole‐body plethysmography (WBP) was performed in conscious mice, comparing SDPR−/− mice with SDPR+/+ controls. SDPR−/− mice exhibited a significantly increased respiratory frequency accompanied by greater fluctuation in amplitude (Figure 2A,B). In addition, tidal volume was significantly reduced (Figure 2C), whereas minute ventilation was compensatorily elevated (Figure 2D). Both inspiratory and expiratory times were markedly shortened (Figure 2E,F). Moreover, expiratory flow at 50% tidal volume significantly increased (Figure 2G). Collectively, these findings indicate that SDPR deletion induces an unstable respiratory pattern characterized by rapid and shallow breathing.

SDPR knockout causes enhanced proliferation and impaired differentiation in lung alveolar type II cells

To investigate the cause of the reduced lung function associated with SDPR deficiency, we analyzed the cellular composition of mouse lung tissues. The results demonstrated a marked increase in SP‐C and a decrease in AGER signals in SDPR−/− mice compared to SDPR+/+ mice (Figure 3A), indicating a reduction in AT1 cells (Figure 3B) and an increase in AT2 cells (Figure 3C). Overall, the AT2‐to‐AT1 cell ratio was reduced in SDPR−/− mice (Figure 3D). Evaluation of proliferation and differentiation using PCNA and Cldn4 staining showed increased PCNA expression (Figure 3E) and reduced Cldn4 expression (Figure 3F) in knockout tissues, consistent with enhanced proliferation and impaired differentiation. Quantitative analysis using ImageJ confirmed a significant increase in the proportion of PCNA‐positive cells (Figure 3G) and a reduction in Cldn4‐positive cells in SDPR−/− mice (Figure 3H). To further assess the impact on AT2 cells, primary AT2 cells were isolated and cultured in vitro. At the 1‐week time point, both SDPR+/+ and SDPR−/− groups exhibited robust expression of the proliferation marker PCNA (Figure S2A). Similarly, the expression of another key cell cycle regulator, CyclinD1, was clearly detectable in both groups at this stage (Figure S2B), indicating an active proliferative state during the initial phase of culture. By 2 weeks, wild‐type cells initiated AGER expression and no longer expressed PCNA, whereas knockout cells continued to express PCNA and lacked AGER expression, indicating that SDPR deletion promotes proliferation while impairing differentiation (Figure 3I,J). In vitro analyses of proliferation dynamics and AT2 cell proportions further demonstrated faster proliferation rates (Figure 3K) and a higher percentage of AT2 cells in the knockout group (Figure 3L), confirming that SDPR deficiency enhances AT2 cell proliferation and suppresses differentiation.

Western blot analyses further substantiated these observations, showing increased PCNA expression in lung tissues (Figure 3M), with quantitative densitometry confirming significantly elevated PCNA levels in SDPR−/− mice compared to SDPR+/+ controls (Figure 3N). Similar findings were obtained in isolated AT2 cells, which also exhibited a marked increase in PCNA protein levels (Figure 3O) and corresponding statistical quantification (Figure 3P). Collectively, these results indicate a pronounced proliferation‐associated response in the absence of SDPR.

SDPR deficiency promotes proliferation and impairs differentiation via NF‐κB activation and Notch signaling suppression

RNA sequencing was performed to elucidate the pathways and functional mechanisms through which SDPR regulates AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation. Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed substantial alterations in SDPR− / − mice compared to controls, with 94 genes upregulated and 78 genes downregulated (Figure 4A,B). These findings indicate that SDPR deficiency disrupts gene networks involved in AT2 cell homeostasis. KEGG functional annotation showed that, among the primary categories of metabolism, environmental information processing, cellular processes, organismal systems, and human diseases, upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in SDPR− / − lungs were predominantly enriched in the secondary categories “immune system” and “signal molecules and interaction” compared to controls (Figure 4C), whereas downregulated DEGs were mainly clustered in “signal transduction” and “signal molecules and interaction” (Figure 4D). Based on these secondary categories, further KEGG enrichment analysis of the upregulated genes demonstrated significant enrichment in the “NF‐κB signaling pathway,” “Th17 cell differentiation,” and “TNF signaling pathway” (Figure 4E,F), indicating that SDPR deficiency induces inflammatory responses in the lung and promotes immune cell differentiation. In addition, the upregulation of NF‐κB, a transcriptional regulator of cyclin D1, suggests enhanced proliferative activity in lung tissue. Detailed analysis of the downregulated DEGs within the “signal transduction” and “signal molecules and interaction” categories revealed marked enrichment in the “focal adhesion” and “PI3K–Akt signaling pathway” (Figure 4G, H). These pathways are critically involved in the morphological remodeling and flattening of AT2 cells during their differentiation into AT1 cells, and their suppression implies impaired AT2‐to‐AT1 differentiation. Consistently, the differentiation marker claudin‐4 (Cldn4) was also significantly enriched among the downregulated genes (Figure 4A). Finally, GSEA of the DEGs demonstrated increased activity of the G2/M checkpoint pathway in SDPR− / − lungs (Figure 4I), consistent with augmented cell proliferation, whereas Notch signaling was significantly attenuated (Figure 4J), which may further contribute to abnormal alveolar epithelial cell differentiation.

qPCR validation showed a significant increase in CyclinD1 mRNA expression, a key marker of proliferation (Figure 4K), whereas Cldn4 mRNA levels were markedly reduced (Figure 4L). Notch pathway components were also altered: Notch1 and its downstream target Hes1 were downregulated (Figure 4M,N), whereas the ligand Jag1 was upregulated (Figure 4O). Additionally, elevated c‐jun expression indicated the activation of the MAPK pathway (Figure 4P). These patterns suggest that diminished Notch1 signaling reduces Hes1 expression, Jag1 upregulation may reflect the loss of inhibitory feedback, and Notch pathway inhibition contributes to the dysregulation of AT2 cell differentiation in SDPR‐deficient lungs.

SDPR deficiency remodels AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation through STK38–GSK‐3β/cyclin D1 and Notch1–Hes1 signaling pathways

To further investigate the molecular interactions of SDPR, we performed mass spectrometry (MS) to identify potential SDPR‐binding proteins, followed by Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. A Venn diagram of identified proteins showed that 73 proteins (24.3%) were unique to the experimental group (G2_Spec), 178 proteins (59.1%) were unique to the control group (Ctrl_Spec), and 50 proteins (16.6%) were shared between the two groups (Figure 5A). Notably, STK38 was identified among the top 10 candidate proteins (Table S5), suggesting a possible direct interaction with SDPR. GO enrichment analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of genes involved in translation and ribosomal structure (Figure 5B). Similarly, KEGG analysis indicated strong enrichment of the ribosome pathway (Figure 5C), implying that SDPR may influence downstream signaling by regulating protein translation and ribosomal function. To validate the potential SDPR–STK38 interaction suggested by the MS data, co‐immunoprecipitation (co‐IP) assays were performed. In cells coexpressing Flag‐SDPR and HA‐STK38, a specific SDPR–STK38 complex was detected in the experimental IP, whereas no corresponding band appeared in the control IP, confirming that SDPR and STK38 physically interact (Figure 5D). To further delineate the key signaling mechanisms through which SDPR regulates AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation, cyclin D1, a proliferation‐associated effector identified in the RNA‐seq enrichment of the NF‐κB pathway, was examined. Western blot analysis revealed a marked increase in cyclin D1 protein levels in both lung tissues (Figure 5E) and primary AT2 cells (Figure 5F) from SDPR− / − mice compared to controls, indicating enhanced proliferative activity. Consistent with our proteomic and bioinformatic findings, this increase in cyclin D1 was accompanied by elevated STK38 expression and a significant reduction in both total and phosphorylated GSK‐3β, suggesting that SDPR loss attenuates GSK‐3β‐dependent cyclin D1 degradation and consequently facilitates AT2 cell hyperproliferation. In parallel, the Notch downstream effector Hes1 was assessed, and its expression was markedly reduced in SDPR‐deficient samples, implicating impaired Notch/Hes1 signaling in the observed defects in AT2 differentiation. Additional pathways enriched by RNA‐seq were further evaluated to determine whether classical signaling cascades contributed to these phenotypes. Western blot demonstrated no significant changes in core MAPK/SMAD pathway components, including ERK1/2, JNK, p38, and SMAD2/3, in either lung tissue lysates or primary AT2 cells, indicating that these canonical axes are not major mediators of the SDPR‐dependent effects. In summary, consistent with our in vivo observations, SDPR knockdown in A549 cells significantly altered the expression profiles of STK38, p‐GSK‐3β, and Hes1 (Figure S3A). The robustness of these findings was further validated by quantitative statistical analyses, which demonstrated significant alterations in these key signaling markers across whole lung tissues (Figure S3B), isolated primary AT2 cells (Figure S3C), and A549 cell lines (Figure S3D). Collectively, these results establish that SDPR deficiency induces a systemic dysregulation of the STK38/GSK‐3β/Hes1 signaling axis, both in vivo and in vitro. Collectively, these results demonstrate that SDPR regulation of AT2 cell behavior does not primarily depend on the classical MAPK/SMAD pathways but instead acts through an SDPR–STK38 axis that reshapes the GSK‐3β/cyclin D1 signaling cascade to enhance proliferative output, while concurrently perturbing the Notch1/Hes1 pathway and thereby impairing AT2‐to‐AT1 differentiation.

We considered that under physiological conditions, SDPR maintains stable cell cycle progression by restraining STK38 expression, preserving GSK‐3β stability, and limiting cyclin D1 accumulation. In contrast, SDPR depletion induced STK38 upregulation, reduced GSK‐3β activity, and diminished cyclin D1 degradation, resulting in enhanced proliferation of AT2 cells. In parallel, SDPR‐dependent caveolar integrity supported proper engagement and proteolytic activation of Notch and its ligands, enabling Hes1 signaling and promoting AT2‐to‐AT1 differentiation. Together, our MS screening, enrichment analyses, and biochemical validation demonstrated that SDPR modulated AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation through multiple mechanisms, including direct interaction with STK38 and regulation of downstream pathways such as the GSK‐3β/cyclin D1 axis and Notch/Hes1 signaling.

SDPR deficiency exacerbates pathological injury and impairs pulmonary function in acute lung injury

To determine whether SDPR contributes to the resolution of acute lung injury, we established a murine model of LPS‐induced acute lung injury via intratracheal LPS instillation and assessed lung pathology, quantitative injury indices, and pulmonary function 48 h after exposure. Histopathological analysis revealed significantly exacerbated lung injury in SDPR−/− mice compared to SDPR+/+ controls. SDPR−/− lungs exhibited pronounced vascular congestion, interstitial and alveolar edema, and dense inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 6A,B). Quantitative assessments further supported these findings: LPS exposure significantly increased the lung weight‐to‐body weight ratio (lung coefficient) and decreased the lung dry/wet weight ratio in both SDPR+/+ and SDPR−/− mice (p < 0.05; Figure 6C,D). These effects were more severe in SDPR−/− mice, indicating greater pulmonary edema and tissue injury in the absence of SDPR.

Consistent with the aggravated structural injury, pulmonary function was more impaired in SDPR‐deficient mice after LPS exposure. SDPR−/− mice exhibited a significantly higher respiratory rate than SDPR+/+ mice (Figure 6E), whereas tidal volume remained similar between the groups (Figure 6F). Consequently, minute ventilation was markedly elevated in SDPR−/− mice (Figure 6G). In addition, SDPR−/− mice displayed shortened inspiratory and expiratory times (Figure 6H,I) and increased expiratory flow at 50% of tidal volume (Figure 6J), reflecting a rapid and shallow breathing pattern and more severe functional decline. After LPS exposure, body weight decreased markedly in all mice. Although SDPR− / − mice exhibited a more pronounced reduction in body weight than control mice, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure S4A). Flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood at 48 h after LPS revealed a robust systemic inflammatory response. LPS‐treated mice exhibited a significant increase in circulating T cells compared to PBS‐treated controls (Figure S4B), which was largely attributable to an expansion of CD8+ T cells (Figure S4C). Myeloid cells showed a decreasing trend in LPS‐exposed mice, although the change was not statistically significant (Figure S4E). Other leukocyte subsets, including B cells (Figure S4F), dendritic cells (Figure S4G), NK cells (Figure S4H), and monocytes/macrophages (Figure S4I), remained largely unchanged. Notably, the inflammatory response was more pronounced in SDPR−/− mice, consistent with their more severe lung injury. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that SDPR deficiency significantly exacerbates tissue damage and functional impairment after LPS‐induced acute lung injury. SDPR deficiency compromises AT2 cell‐mediated immune defense, marked by altered antimicrobial protein production, and triggers a significant surge in pro‐inflammatory biomarkers such as IL‐1β. This inflammatory cascade drives extensive immune cell infiltration by upregulating chemokines and adhesion molecules, ultimately exacerbating pulmonary inflammation and increasing the susceptibility of SDPR−/− mice to lung fibrosis. 30

DISCUSSION

Our investigations using SDPR‐deficient mice demonstrated that the loss of SDPR profoundly disrupted the proliferative–differentiative balance of AT2 cells. As a membrane‐associated protein involved in signal transduction, SDPR appears to function as a critical mediator of intracellular regulatory pathways 23 ; its absence compromises essential signals required to maintain AT2 homeostasis. Through analyses of SDPR−/− mice and sh‐SDPR A549 cell models, we observed that SDPR deletion altered lung architecture, induced rapid and shallow breathing, expanded the population of proliferative AT2 cells, and concurrently suppressed their differentiation toward the AT1 lineage. Mechanistically, our findings indicated that SDPR deficiency enhanced AT2 proliferation by upregulating cyclin D1, while simultaneously reducing Notch activity and suppressing the Hes1 pathway, thereby impairing differentiation. Collectively, these alterations shifted the AT2 proliferative–differentiative equilibrium toward a hyperproliferative but differentiation‐deficient state, ultimately contributing to impaired lung function.

Proteomic analysis identified STK38 as a protein strongly associated with SDPR, prompting further investigation of its functional relationship. Our co‐immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated that SDPR physically interacts with STK38 and may anchor this kinase to membrane microdomains (Figure 5D), thereby regulating its spatial accessibility to downstream substrates. This finding adds an additional layer of regulation to the emerging understanding of STK38 as a multifunctional kinase involved in cell‐cycle progression, apoptosis, and stress responses. Based on our data, SDPR functions as an upstream modulator of STK38; its loss disrupts STK38 localization and activity, destabilizes the quiescent state of AT2 cells, and shifts them toward persistent proliferation rather than differentiation into AT1 cells, consistent with the sustained PCNA expression and reduced Cldn4 levels observed in SDPR‐deficient lungs (Figure 4I,J). Previous studies have shown that STK38 promotes the G1/S transition through cyclin D1, 31 regulates MYC transcription 32 and NF‐κB signaling, 33 and participates in p38/MAPK cross talk. 34 Loss of SDPR‐mediated scaffolding likely releases STK38 from membrane‐associated compartments, thereby enhancing its pro‐proliferative activity while attenuating GSK‐3β and Notch signaling outputs. Considering that alveolar regeneration requires precise coordination among the Wnt/β‐catenin, 35 Notch, 35 and Hippo pathways, 36 the SDPR–STK38 axis may represent a central integrative node that maintains AT2 cell fate balance. Future proteomic and structural studies are required to define the interaction domains and identify additional effectors within this regulatory complex. Notably, our study is the first to characterize SDPR as an upstream regulator of AT2 cell fate, acting through STK38 to modulate the proliferation–differentiation program, a conclusion supported by integrated evidence from cell lines, primary AT2 cultures, lung organoids, and in vivo mouse models.

Despite extensive efforts to characterize the interaction between SDPR and STK38, the precise mechanisms by which the SDPR–STK38 complex regulates downstream signaling in AT2 cells remain unresolved. In particular, it is still unclear whether and how this complex coordinates the GSK‐3β/cyclin D1 pathway and the Notch/Hes1 axis to regulate the balance between AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation. But the aberrant activation of the Notch signaling pathway is a recognized driver of pathological remodeling in pulmonary fibrosis, supporting our conclusion that SDPR loss–induced Notch suppression impairs the delicate balance of AT2 cell differentiation. 37 Moreover, although our data suggest a potential protective role for SDPR during acute lung injury, the manner in which SDPR functions within LPS‐induced inflammatory environments to mitigate tissue damage has not yet been determined. The temporal and context‐dependent contributions of SDPR during the injury–repair process therefore remain an important unanswered question. AT2 cell‐mediated immune defense against LPS‐induced injury is evidenced by the production of antimicrobial proteins, specifically defensin 2, lipocalin 2, and lysozyme. 38 And pro‐inflammatory mediators such as IL‐1 and TNF released from dysfunctional AT2 cells can activate RhoA/ROCK signaling in the pulmonary endothelium, promoting the formation of actin stress fibers and intercellular gaps that compromise the alveolar–capillary barrier. 39 The significant surge in pro‐inflammatory biomarkers, particularly IL‐1β, in SDPR−/− mice underscores a hyperinflammatory state that drives lung injury. Interestingly, recent evidence suggests that dysregulated cellular processes, such as excessive ferritinophagy, can also contribute to this inflammatory milieu by activating NF‐kB signaling and releasing free iron. The parallel between SDPR deficiency‐induced inflammation and the ferritinophagy‐driven cytokine production suggests that SDPR might be essential for maintaining a broader homeostatic balance that prevents such maladaptive responses in AT2 cells. 40

Future studies integrating high‐resolution structural analysis, phospho‐proteomics, and AT2 cell‐specific genetic perturbation are essential to elucidate how SDPR–STK38 signaling is dynamically modulated and to determine whether this regulatory module can be leveraged to improve alveolar repair after acute lung injury.

In conclusion, our findings identify the SDPR–STK38 axis as a critical checkpoint for maintaining AT2 cell homeostasis and preserving the proliferative–differentiative balance required for alveolar integrity. By establishing SDPR as an upstream regulator that orchestrates STK38‐dependent signaling, this study provides new mechanistic insights into the molecular foundations of alveolar epithelial stability. These results also highlight the SDPR–STK38 interaction as a previously unrecognized regulatory node that may be particularly vulnerable during injury. Given the central role of AT2 cells in epithelial regeneration and lung repair, targeting components of this axis, or restoring its functional architecture, may offer a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance tissue regeneration after acute or chronic lung injury. Further investigation is necessary to determine whether modulation of SDPR–STK38 signaling can be utilized to promote controlled AT2 differentiation, limit aberrant proliferation, and ultimately improve outcomes in pulmonary diseases.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, this study identified STK38 as a novel SDPR‐binding partner and revealed that the SDPR–STK38 axis is a critical regulator of the proliferation–differentiation balance of alveolar type II cells. By enhancing GSK‐3β/cyclin D1 signaling and suppressing Notch1–Hes1‐mediated differentiation, SDPR deficiency drives aberrant AT2 expansion, impairs AT1 lineage commitment, and exacerbates lung injury and functional decline after LPS exposure. These findings highlight SDPR as an essential determinant of alveolar homeostasis and repair and suggest that targeting the SDPR–STK38 pathway may provide a promising therapeutic strategy to promote lung regeneration and improve outcomes in acute lung injury and related pulmonary diseases.
