Military Service and Survival Among Persons With ALS in the U.S. National ALS Registry, 2011–2023
Authors: D Kevin Horton, Jaime Raymond, Theodore Larson, Marc G Weisskopf, Ileana Howard, Paul Mehta
Published: 2026

Introduction

Military service has been associated with an increased risk of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) in multiple epidemiologic studies. Veterans have consistently been reported to experience higher ALS incidence and mortality than the general population, leading the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) to recognize ALS as a presumptively service‐connected condition [1, 2, 3]. Proposed explanations have included environmental toxicants, physical trauma, intense physical activity, combustion products, and other occupational exposures encountered during military service, although the mechanisms underlying these associations remain uncertain [4, 5, 6].

Less is known about whether military service is associated with survival after diagnosis. Previous studies have examined military‐related exposures and prognostic factors within veteran ALS cohorts [7, 8], and recent Registry work has compared clinical characteristics and diagnostic trajectories between veterans and nonveterans [9]. However, direct comparisons of survival after ALS diagnosis between veterans and nonveterans remain limited. Determining whether veterans experience different survival trajectories could inform prognostic assessment, clinical care, resource allocation, and future research.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the association between military service and survival after ALS diagnosis among participants enrolled in the U.S. National ALS Registry.

Methods

Data Source

The National ALS Registry (Registry) was created by the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in 2010 to improve understanding of the epidemiology of ALS in the United States [10]. Because ALS is not nationally reportable, Registry participants are identified through large administrative databases (e.g., Medicare, Veterans Health Administration, Veterans Benefits Administration) and a secure online self‐enrollment portal [11, 12, 13]. Portal‐enrolled participants may complete up to 18 optional surveys addressing demographic characteristics, clinical features, and environmental and occupational exposures.

Study Cohort and Analysis Period

Five National ALS Registry surveys provided data for this study: the Demographics, Military History, Smoking and Alcohol History, Disease Progression, and Clinical and Care Utilization surveys. The Demographics survey provided demographic variables; the Military History survey provided military service status; the Smoking and Alcohol History survey provided smoking and alcohol variables; the Disease Progression survey provided Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Functional Rating Scale–Revised (ALSFRS‐R) data [14]; and the Clinical and Care Utilization survey provided site of onset information.

Participants were included if they completed the Military History survey, which was required to ascertain military service status, and had valid ALS diagnosis and mortality follow‐up information required to calculate survival time. ALS diagnosis date was obtained from participant self‐report recorded during Registry enrollment. Analyses were restricted to participants completing the Military History survey between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2023.

Exposure Definition

Military service was defined based on self‐reported history of service in the United States Armed Forces. Participants were classified as veterans (ever served) or nonveterans (never served).

Outcome Definition

The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality following ALS diagnosis, ascertained using death data obtained through linkage with CDC's National Death Index (NDI) [15] and Registry administrative records. The NDI is a well‐established source for mortality ascertainment in U.S. epidemiologic studies, with validation studies demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity for identifying deaths. However, ascertainment depends on the completeness and accuracy of identifying information used for record linkage, and imperfect linkage may result in some deaths being missed [16]. Participants without an observed death were administratively censored on December 31, 2023.

Covariates

The primary adjustment set included age at diagnosis, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking history, and alcohol use. These variables were selected a priori based on established associations with ALS prognosis and observed differences between veterans and nonveterans. Age at diagnosis was calculated based on self‐reported birth month and year and ALS diagnosis date. Smoking variables included ever smoking status and self‐reported cumulative pack‐years; alcohol variables included ever alcohol use and self‐reported total years of alcohol consumption.

Baseline disease severity was approximated using self‐reported ALSFRS‐R assessments. For each participant, the assessment completed closest to ALS diagnosis within ±6 months, including retrospectively completed assessments, was selected. The ±6‐month window balanced temporal proximity to diagnosis with adequate sample size. Because not all participants completed the Disease Progression survey or had an assessment within ±6 months of diagnosis, ALSFRS‐R data were available for only a subset of participants. Therefore, analyses incorporating ALSFRS‐R were conducted as a sensitivity analysis rather than included in the primary models.

Site of ALS symptom onset was categorized as limb, bulbar, trunk/global, or respiratory based on the initial body region in which weakness was reported before ALS diagnosis. Because onset site information was available only for participants who completed the Clinical and Care Utilization survey, adjustment for onset site was evaluated in a sensitivity analysis rather than incorporated into the primary models.

Additional Registry variables, including education, self‐reported height and weight at age 40, from which body mass index (BMI) at age 40 was calculated, trauma history, healthcare utilization, and selected nonmilitary environmental and occupational exposures, were evaluated in exploratory analyses but were not included in the primary analytic models.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared by military service status using chi‐squared tests for categorical variables and t‐tests or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Continuous variables were summarized using mean (standard deviation [SD]) when the distribution was reasonably symmetric and median (interquartile range [IQR]) when the distribution was skewed; categorical variables are presented as n (%).

For each participant, observed time was calculated from ALS diagnosis to death or, for participants without an observed death, to the administrative censoring date. Median observed times were then summarized by military service status. Survival distributions were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier methods and compared using the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between military service and mortality after ALS diagnosis. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using residual‐based diagnostic methods. Sequential models were adjusted for age at diagnosis, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking history, and alcohol use. Smoking pack‐years, years of alcohol use, and ALSFRS‐R score (when included in sensitivity analyses) were modeled as continuous variables.

Sensitivity analyses evaluated the robustness of findings after accounting for baseline ALSFRS‐R score, ALS symptom onset site, and left truncation (delayed entry into the Registry). The left‐truncated analyses accounted for potential survivorship bias arising from delayed Registry entry because participants with rapidly progressive disease may die before having the opportunity to enroll.

Kaplan–Meier curves were presented descriptively, whereas hazard ratios from Cox regression models were considered the primary measure of association. Median observed times were included as a simple descriptive summary of time from diagnosis to death or censoring, whereas Kaplan–Meier methods estimated survival over time while accounting for incomplete follow‐up. Cox models were used for adjusted inference regarding the association between military service and mortality, and left‐truncated Cox models evaluated whether this association was robust to delayed Registry entry after ALS diagnosis.

Separate exploratory analyses among veterans evaluated survival differences by military branch of service using Kaplan–Meier methods and the log‐rank test to explore heterogeneity within the veteran cohort. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Ethics Approval

The Registry operates under CDC Institutional Review Board approval, and all participants provide informed consent at registration.

Results

Study Population

From 2011 to 2023, 8643 participants met the study cohort criteria, including 1735 veterans and 6908 nonveterans (Figure 1). Veterans were older at diagnosis and substantially more likely to be male. Survival Analysis

Veterans experienced significantly poorer survival after ALS diagnosis than nonveterans (Figure 2). The median observed time from ALS diagnosis to death or censoring was 3.77 years among veterans and 4.79 years among nonveterans, an approximate 1‐year difference. Kaplan–Meier estimated 5‐year survival was lower among veterans (47.1% vs. 57.4%). In Cox proportional hazards models, military service was associated with increased mortality across all model specifications (Table 2). In the primary adjusted model, veterans had a 25% higher mortality hazard than nonveterans (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.15–1.35).

Supplementary Analyses

Among participants completing the Clinical and Care Utilization survey with nonmissing site of onset information, onset patterns differed by military service status, with limb onset more common among veterans and bulbar onset less common among veterans (Table S1).

Additional analyses accounting for delayed entry into the Registry using left‐truncated Cox proportional hazards models yielded estimates similar to the primary analyses. The association also persisted after additional adjustment for site of onset (Table S2).

Exploratory analyses among veterans found no significant differences in survival across military branches (log‐rank p = 0.23; data not shown).

Discussion

In this large cohort of National ALS Registry participants, military service was associated with significantly poorer survival after ALS diagnosis. This survival disadvantage persisted after adjustment for demographic, behavioral, and clinical factors. Given the limited life expectancy associated with ALS, the approximate 1‐year difference in median observed time from diagnosis to death or censoring was clinically meaningful. Collectively, these findings support further evaluation of military history as a potential prognostic factor in ALS, although the biological and environmental mechanisms underlying this survival disparity remain uncertain.

Relatively little research has examined survival after diagnosis, and existing veteran cohort studies lacked nonveteran comparators [7, 8]. The present findings extend this literature by directly comparing survival after ALS diagnosis between veterans and nonveterans within the National ALS Registry. Prior work has described diagnostic differences, older age at diagnosis, and distinct clinical characteristics among veterans with ALS [9, 17]. Analyses of the former U.S. National Registry of Veterans with ALS identified several factors associated with survival, including age, smoking history, body mass index, and disease progression [7]. The GENEVA study reported associations between selected military‐related exposures and ALS survival, although most factors were not significant and analyses were restricted to veteran populations [8]. Established prognostic studies identify age, onset site, functional status, and sex as key determinants of ALS survival [18, 19, 20], providing important context for interpreting the present findings.

Our findings suggest military service may serve as a marker of factors influencing survival that are not fully captured in existing prognostic frameworks. Adjustment for baseline ALSFRS‐R score did not attenuate the association, suggesting that measured differences in disease severity were unlikely to explain the observed survival disparity. The somewhat stronger association observed in the ALSFRS‐R‐adjusted analysis may reflect differences between participants with and without available functional assessments rather than confounding by baseline disease severity.

The mechanisms underlying poorer survival among veterans remain uncertain. Although the Registry includes information on pesticide use, physical activity, civilian work history, and head and neck injuries, detailed military‐specific information such as combat‐related toxicant exposures and military occupational specialty is unavailable for exposure‐specific analyses. These unmeasured exposures may partially contribute to the observed association through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, or neuroinflammation [4, 5, 6, 21].

Alternative explanations should also be considered. A recent National ALS Registry analysis found that male veterans reported a longer interval from symptom onset to ALS diagnosis than male nonveterans (20.8 vs. 16.7 months) [9]. Differences in diagnostic timing could therefore contribute to observed differences in survival measured from the date of diagnosis. Clinical phenotype was also considered. Veterans exhibited more limb onset and less bulbar onset than nonveterans. Because bulbar onset is generally associated with shorter survival [20, 22], these differences would not be expected to explain the poorer survival observed among veterans. Consistent with this interpretation, adjustment for onset site only modestly attenuated the association between military service and mortality. Unmeasured differences in comorbidity burden, healthcare utilization, and socioeconomic factors could also contribute to the observed survival disadvantage.

This study has several limitations. Military service history was self‐reported and limited to a dichotomous veteran/nonveteran measure. ALS diagnosis date was also self‐reported and may be subject to error, potentially resulting in misclassification of survival time. Detailed information on military occupational specialty and service‐related exposures was unavailable. We also could not determine whether veterans received ALS care primarily through VA or civilian healthcare settings or whether healthcare setting influenced Registry participation, which could contribute to the observed survival difference. Because Registry participation occurs after ALS diagnosis, individuals who died before enrollment could not be included, creating potential prevalent‐survivor bias. Although left‐truncated analyses yielded similar results, they cannot account for individuals who died before enrollment. Baseline ALSFRS‐R and onset‐site information were unavailable for many participants, reducing sample sizes in sensitivity analyses. The proportional hazards assumption was not fully met for military service; therefore, the hazard ratio should be interpreted as an average association over follow‐up. The Registry collected information on usual alcohol consumption; however, the present analysis characterized alcohol exposure using ever use and total years of consumption and did not account for drinking quantity or frequency, leaving potential for residual confounding by alcohol consumption intensity. Residual confounding by unmeasured factors, including comorbidity burden, healthcare utilization, socioeconomic characteristics, and military‐related exposures, may remain.

Conclusions

Military service was associated with significantly poorer survival after ALS diagnosis in this large national cohort, and the association remained evident after adjustment for demographic, behavioral, and clinical factors. These findings suggest military history warrants consideration as a potential prognostic factor in ALS, and that future work should incorporate detailed military exposure histories, military occupational specialty information, comorbidity burden, and healthcare utilization data to clarify the mechanisms underlying this survival disadvantage.
