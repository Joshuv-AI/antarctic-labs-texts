Self-efficacy, personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities/preferences in psychological distress: a predictive, variance-based PLS-SEM structural model
Authors: Ahmed Mohamed Ahmed Zayed, Saud Ayed Ayad Alshammari, Suleiman Muhammad Suleiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContemporary Arab societies face mounting social and economic pressures that shape mental health outcomes through psychological and organizational factors. This study examines self-efficacy, personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities as key influences on psychological distress, mapping their interactions across diverse adult age groups to identify pathways for improving well-being. We employed Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) in a quantitative correlational design with 328 Arabic-speaking adults aged 18–65 years (M = 32.5, SD = 12.1). Validated Arabic versions of established scales assessed the constructs. We first confirmed measurement quality through reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity tests before estimating direct and indirect structural paths. The measurement model showed in our Arab sample, as depicted good reliability, (Cronbach’s α = 0.74–0.92) and validity (AVE = 0.56–0.72), with empirically distinct constructs. Self-efficacy positively associated personal autonomy (β = 0.51, p < .001) and life arrangements (β = 0.46, p < .001) but not distress directly. Autonomy mediated self-efficacy’s protective effect against distress (indirect β = –0.17, p < .001), while health priorities unexpectedly increased distress (β = 0.35, p < .001). The model explained 30% of distress variance. In Arab sociocultural contexts, the relationship between self-efficacy and psychological distress operates exclusively through experienced personal autonomy and related organizational resources. These findings suggest that interventions may be more effective when they strengthen efficacy beliefs alongside autonomy-supportive daily structures.Keywords: Self-efficacy, Personal autonomy, Life arrangements, Health priorities, Psychological distress, PLS-SEMSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Among the cognitive resources that equip individuals to navigate socially and organizationally demanding environments, self-efficacy - the belief in one’s capacity to organize and execute goal-directed behavior under challenging conditions - occupies a theoretically central position1,2. Unlike stable personality traits, self-efficacy is context-sensitive in practice, local evidence: practically it strengthens or erodes in response to mastery experiences, social modeling, physiological feedback, and verbal persuasion, and it gradually shapes how individuals deploy motivational, affective, and behavioral resources in the face of adversity3–6.

Converging evidence from occupational, educational, and clinical contexts documents inverse associations between self-efficacy and psychological distress, with the magnitude of these associations moderated by the degree of role autonomy afforded by the immediate environment. For example, palliative care workers with strong self-efficacy experience less emotional exhaustion despite regular exposure to patient suffering7. Similarly, university students facing heavy academic demands benefit from self-efficacy in reducing distress levels8, and academic anxiety9. A recent study showed how an autonomy-supportive learning climate boosts economics students’ self-efficacy through student choice opportunities, verbal encouragement, small success experiences, and a positive atmosphere encouraging active participation10. In work settings, the protective effects of self-efficacy depend heavily on the degree of role autonomy: moderate freedom enhances these benefits, while excessive demands coupled with constraints can turn resources into stressors11. Employment shapes not just financial stability but also personal identity, psychological balance, and overall life satisfaction12. Job disruptions are associated with eroding self-efficacy beliefs, triggering cycles of distress, diminished motivation, and prolonged unemployment13,14.

However, a critical examination of this literature reveals notable inconsistency. While the majority of studies report inverse associations between self-efficacy and psychological distress, a non-trivial subset of investigations has documented weak, non-significant, or even positive relationships under specific conditions. For instance, in high-demand environments where individuals possess strong self-efficacy but face structural barriers―such as role ambiguity, resource scarcity, or discriminatory institutional climates―elevated self-efficacy may paradoxically intensify distress by amplifying the perceived gap between aspired and attainable outcomes2,15,16. This inconsistency across findings suggests that the direct path from self-efficacy to psychological distress is moderated or mediated by contextual and dispositional factors that the extant literature has yet to map comprehensively. The present study is positioned to address precisely this gap: specific to Arab adults rather than replicating the bivariate examination of self-efficacy and distress, it investigates the indirect mechanisms using our model―personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities―through which self-efficacy exerts its influence on psychological distress. This approach aligns with calls in the methodological literature for mediation-focused structural models capable of unpacking the conditional nature of well-established psychological relationships17,18.

These patterns extend to educational contexts as well. Among teachers, autonomy―channeled through self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation―boosts job satisfaction and commitment19. Digital learning environments show similar dynamics, where targeted feedback and self-directed learning strengthen both self-efficacy and autonomy, improving motivation and academic performance20. Across these varied professional and educational settings, the consistent thread is a dynamic interplay between efficacy beliefs and autonomous motivation - a relationship whose deeper structural architecture, particularly in Arab cultural contexts, remains inadequately mapped.

At its core, self-efficacy influences how people appraise challenges, persist in goals, allocate resources, build resilience, and recover from setbacks21. A recent study in the Arab context concluded that self-efficacy was one of the key personal resources for university academics in overcoming occupational stress and enhancing work engagement22. In organizational and entrepreneurial contexts, these mechanisms predict adaptation, performance, and life engagement. English language learners, for instance, draw on self-efficacy for resilience and autonomous learning through emotional and strategic self-regulation6.

Nonspecific psychological distress - a transdiagnostic construct encompassing anxiety, depressive affect, and psychological tension - has been consistently linked to impaired coping capacity and diminished quality of life across diverse populations23. Its salience is particularly acute in Arab societies, where structural and sociocultural pressures compound one another in ways that intensify vulnerability. Elevated youth unemployment, restricted occupational mobility, and inadequate mental health infrastructure collectively sustain chronic rather than episodic distress profiles across much of the region24.

Socioculturally, strong collectivist orientations shape how individuals in Arab societies appraise autonomy and self-efficacy: rather than arising from individualistic self-assertion, these constructs are embedded within familial obligation networks and community interdependence, a dynamic that alters their relationship with psychological distress relative to Western individualistic contexts25,26. Consequently, findings derived from WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) samples cannot be uncritically generalized to Arab adults, as the psychological pathways linking personal resources to distress may operate through culturally contingent mechanisms27. Empirical verification within this sociocultural context is therefore not merely an exercise in cross-cultural replication but a theoretically necessary investigation of culturally embedded psychological processes.

While prior work has examined individual constructs in isolation, no study has modeled their joint structural influence using PLS-SEM in a diverse Arab adult sample - the gap this investigation is designed to address.

Theoretical model and research gap

This study addresses the central research question: To what extent do self-efficacy, personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities account for variance in psychological distress among Arab adults, and through which mediating pathways do these effects operate within an integrated PLS-SEM framework?

This study advances understanding of self-efficacy, personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities as key psychological and organizational resources that collectively account for variations in psychological distress among Arab adults. Strong self-efficacy equips individuals with effective coping strategies, helping to reduce distress across diverse challenges―from academic stress and unemployment to chronic health issues and crises like COVID-1928,29. In contrast, low self-efficacy often leads to feelings of helplessness, loneliness, diminished control, avoidance behaviors, and heightened psychological strain30.

Self-determination theory views personal autonomy as the experience of acting in alignment with one’s own values and choices―a fundamental driver of psychological well-being31,32. When satisfied, autonomy is positively associated with life satisfaction and vitality; when frustrated, it contributes to distress and depressive symptoms33. In daily life, autonomy appears through how people organize work, family, leisure, and self-care. Rigid or imbalanced arrangements increase stress, while flexible patterns build resilience34,35.

Health priorities reflect the weight individuals place on physical and mental well-being amid competing daily demands. Balanced attention to health, paired with practical self-care, can protect against distress by enhancing personal agency34. Yet excessive focus―especially when blocked by real-world constraints or conflicting priorities―may instead generate anxiety36.

The hierarchical placement of constructs in the proposed model requires explicit theoretical justification. Self-efficacy is conceptualized as the primary exogenous driver because, Aligned with social cognitive theory1, it represents a generative belief system that shapes individuals’ capacity to mobilize motivational, cognitive, and behavioral resources necessary for autonomous agency. When individuals perceive themselves as capable of managing situational demands, they become more able to act in accordance with personally endorsed values-capturing the essence of experienced autonomy31. Autonomy subsequently facilitates more intentional structuring of daily life and more deliberate prioritization of health‐related behaviors. Each mediating construct incrementally reduces exposure to psychological distress. This sequential logic aligns with Conservation of Resources theory37, which posits that psychological resources operate in gain cycles: possessing one resource (self‐efficacy) enables the accumulation and activation of additional resources (autonomy, life organization, health prioritization), collectively mitigating the likelihood of resource depletion-the proximal antecedent of distress.

An alternative conceptual argument that might be put forward here is that autonomy logically precedes self-efficacy; one must first believe that outcomes are self-determined before believing one can effectively manage them. This theoretically plausible alternative model is explicitly discussed as a limitation and direction for future research in the “Discussion” section. Although research confirms the importance of these factors, most studies examine them separately or through simple correlations (e.g., self-efficacy with distress; autonomy with well-being21,29. These investigations typically draw from Western samples or narrow groups like students and specific professions, leaving a clear gap: no prior work has modeled them together using PLS-SEM in diverse Arab adult populations.

To fill this gap, we developed and tested an integrated structural model based on self-determination theory and stress-resource frameworks. The model positions self-efficacy, personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities as predictors converging on psychological distress. We used variance-based PLS-SEM, deal for complex latent structures and our sample size of N = 32838.

Theoretically, this research makes two distinct contributions: (a) it tests an integrated structural model that incorporates mediation mechanisms within a non-Western cultural context, thereby extending self-determination theory and Conservation of Resources theory to Arab populations where their applicability has not yet been structurally examined; and (b) it addresses the inconsistency in self-efficacy―distress findings by demonstrating that the relationship is not direct and unitary but rather contingent on intervening personal and organizational resources. Practically, the findings provide empirically grounded guidance for designing psychological interventions, institutional support programs, and public health policies targeting self-efficacy enhancement, autonomy support, and life arrangement flexibility as protective factors against psychological distress among Arab adults.

Theoretical framework

Self-efficacy and psychological distress

Bandura’s (1997) social cognitive theory positions self-efficacy not as a reflection of objective skill but as a metacognitive judgment about whether available capabilities are sufficient to meet situational demands. This distinction is theoretically consequential: two individuals with equivalent competencies may respond to identical stressors in fundamentally different ways depending on their efficacy appraisals, with higher appraisals associated with sustained goal pursuit, adaptive coping selection, and greater psychological resilience39. The empirical literature - spanning longitudinal and cross-sectional designs across student, occupational, and clinical populations - consistently documents inverse associations between self-efficacy and indicators of psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and tension, even under conditions of sustained adversity such as the COVID-19 pandemic21,28,29. These protective associations appear to operate through multiple behavioral and cognitive channels: efficacy beliefs orient individuals toward problem engagement rather than avoidance, facilitate social support mobilization, and sustain positive reappraisal under threat30,34.

As elaborated in the hypotheses below, the question of whether this influence operates through direct appraisal or through the sequential activation of downstream resources is itself a theoretically open question that the present data are designed to address.

Personal autonomy within self-determination theory

Self-determination theory identifies three basic psychological needs - autonomy, competence, and relatedness - as universal foundations of human well-being whose satisfaction or frustration determines the quality of motivation and psychological functioning across life domains31,40. Of these, autonomy - experienced when individuals perceive their actions as self-endorsed expressions of personal values rather than responses to external pressure or internal compulsion - has demonstrated the most consistent associations with distress-related outcomes: its satisfaction predicts vitality and life satisfaction, while its frustration is linked to depressive symptomatology and diminished adaptive functioning33,41. Institutional and relational environments that support volitional choice - in educational, occupational, and family domains - amplify intrinsic motivation and strengthen psychological resistance to stress32.

Within the structural model tested here, personal autonomy is theorized to occupy a pivotal mediating position between self-efficacy and psychological distress. Individuals who hold strong efficacy beliefs are better positioned to make decisions congruent with their values, resist externally imposed constraints, and organize daily commitments in ways that preserve a sense of volitional authorship over their lives - conditions under which the autonomy need is satisfied and distress is attenuated. Autonomy thus functions not merely as an outcome of efficacy but as the psychological mechanism through which efficacy translates into emotional protection, connecting upstream cognitive resources to downstream affective outcomes including the organization of life arrangements and the management of health-related priorities.

Life arrangements as organizational resources

Life arrangements describe how people divide their time, energy, and resources across work, family, self-care, leisure, and social connections. A long-term care study identified five key client preference areas: health, daily life, family/friends, living conditions, and finances. Interviews highlighted the importance of rapport with caregivers, feelings of safety, contact with loved ones, and help organizing daily routines42. According to conservation of resources theory, maintaining balance and flexibility across these areas helps protect psychological and physical energy from being drained by stress34.

Within the Conservation of Resources framework34, role overload and boundary permeability between work and family domains constitute primary mechanisms of resource depletion, documented as antecedents of both burnout and generalized psychological distress in longitudinal investigations35.

In the present model, life arrangements are conceptualized as the behavioral crystallization of self-efficacy and autonomy: individuals who hold strong efficacy beliefs and experience their choices as self-determined are expected to construct more adaptive daily configurations, which in turn reduce exposure to the cumulative strain that characterizes resource-poor environments. The structural path from self-efficacy through life arrangements to distress thus represents a behavioral mediation pathway that complements the more proximal psychological pathway through autonomy.

Health priorities/preferences in the model

Health priorities capture how much weight people give to physical and mental well-being when making daily decisions, especially alongside other demands like work or family. Health psychology suggests that weaving health concerns into routine choices serves as a preventive measure against both psychological strain and physical problems. Balanced attention to health builds a sense of control and effectiveness34,36.

However, this area involves real tensions. Excessive worry about health can create anxiety itself, particularly when life circumstances block healthy actions or when personal habits conflict with what’s recommended. Health priorities may function either as a protective self-regulatory resource or as a source of distress, depending on whether they are grounded in available capacity or in perceived resource scarcity. This view aligns with Hobfoll’s resource investment framework (1989).

Psychological distress as the ultimate outcome

Within the present framework, nonspecific psychological distress serves as the distal outcome criterion - the variable toward which all theorized pathways ultimately converge. Captured through self-report measures sensitive to symptoms of anxiety, depression, and psychological tension in non-clinical populations23, distress in this model is understood as the product of an imbalance between environmental demands and the personal resources available to meet them34. The four constructs examined here - self-efficacy, personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities - are positioned as a resource constellation whose coordinated influence is expected to account for meaningful variance in distress levels among Arab adults, as formalized in the hypotheses that follow.

Method

Participants

The study included 328 participants who provided complete data across all key demographic and psychological measures. This sample size supports robust PLS-SEM analysis as it satisfies the minimum threshold recommended for variance-based structural equation modeling with the present number of latent constructs and indicator paths38. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the sample. Females made up 69.5% of participants (n = 228) compared to 30.5% males (n = 100). Age distribution covered two main groups: 65.9% aged 18–39 years (n = 216) and 34.1% aged 40–65 years (n = 112). Nationality breakdown showed Egyptians (48.5%, n = 159) and Saudis (49.1%, n = 161) forming the core, with other nationalities comprising a minimal proportion. Most participants rated their economic status as average (92.4%, n = 303), were single (65.9%, n = 216), and lived in urban areas (69.2%, n = 227). Health data indicated that 85.4% (n = 280) reported no medical complaints and 92.1% (n = 302) were not receiving any treatment at the time of data collection, a profile that reduces the potential confounding influence of chronic illness or pharmacological intervention on self-reported psychological distress.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected over a period of nine months, from February to October 2025, using a self-administered electronic questionnaire distributed via Google Forms. The survey was administered in Arabic to ensure linguistic accessibility for the target population. Participants were recruited from Egypt and Saudi Arabia through a snowball sampling strategy, whereby initial contacts within the researchers’ professional and personal networks were invited to participate and subsequently encouraged to share the survey link with individuals in their social circles via social media platforms, including WhatsApp. Snowball sampling was deemed appropriate given the cross-national scope of the study and the absence of a centralized sampling frame for the target population of Arabic-speaking adults; this approach is recognized as methodologically defensible in quantitative survey research when participants are accessible primarily through social networks and when population boundaries cannot be defined a priori43,44. The target population comprised Arabic-speaking adults aged 18 years and above residing in Egypt and Saudi Arabia.

To ensure data quality and minimize common method bias, several procedural controls were implemented. First, the Google Forms platform was configured to restrict submissions to one response per Google account, thereby preventing duplicate entries. Second, the estimated completion time was specified in the survey preamble, and responses completed in under three minutes-indicating insufficient engagement with item content-were flagged and excluded from analysis45. Third, two attention-check items were embedded within the questionnaire; responses failing both checks were removed prior to analysis46. Fourth, participants were explicitly informed of the voluntary and anonymous nature of their participation, which has been shown to reduce socially desirable responding in self-report surveys77.

Measures

The study employed three standardized instruments to operationalize the latent constructs in the proposed structural model. Self-efficacy served as the primary exogenous (independent) construct; personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities/preferences as endogenous mediators and psychological distress as criterion endogenous (dependent) construct. The following subsections describe each instrument, its theoretical basis, and its role in the model.

General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE) exogenous construct

General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE) exogenous construct

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE)39 measures optimistic self-beliefs regarding individuals’ capacity to manage novel and demanding situations across diverse life domains. The original scale comprises 10 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree). For the present study, a 4-item version was retained following item-level evaluation; items that did not meet the minimum factor loading threshold were removed to optimize model fit within the Arab community sample. Full details of the item reduction procedure and the retained items are reported in the “Results” section and Appendix A, respectively.

2.Maastricht Personal Autonomy Questionnaire (MPAQ) mediating constructs

Maastricht Personal Autonomy Questionnaire (MPAQ) mediating constructs

The Maastricht Personal Autonomy Questionnaire (MPAQ)47 is a multidimensional instrument originally developed to assess autonomy-related constructs in older adult populations. The full instrument comprises 16 items distributed across three subscales: personal autonomy (5 items), life arrangements (5 items), and health priorities/preferences (6 items). In the present study, item-level screening based on standardized factor loadings retained 12 of the original 16 items: all five personal autonomy items were retained, four of the five life arrangements items were retained following the removal of one item with a loading below 0.70, and three of the six health priorities/preferences items were retained following the removal of three items with insufficient loadings. The resulting 12-item version spans the three theorized subscales and preserves the core conceptual domain of each dimension. Although the instrument was originally developed for older adults, its psychometric applicability across the broader 18–65 age range of the current sample was supported by prior cross-cultural adaptation evidence. Item-level details for all retained and removed items are provided in Appendix A.

3.Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) Endogenous (Dependent) construct

Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) Endogenous (Dependent) construct

The Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10)48 is a widely validated screening instrument that assesses nonspecific psychological distress through 10 items capturing the frequency of anxiety and depressive symptoms over the preceding four weeks, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = none of the time to 5 = all of the time). A 9-item version was used in the present study following the removal of one item that exhibited cross-loading patterns inconsistent with unidimensional model fit. Details of this decision are reported in the “Results” section; item-level descriptive statistics are provided in Appendix A.

Item reduction across the GSE and K10 followed the criterion-referenced approach recommended for PLS-SEM applications, wherein items failing to meet the minimum reflective indicator loading threshold of 0.70 are candidates for removal, provided that the retained items continue to represent the full conceptual domain of the construct38,49. This procedure is consonant with non-Western scale adaptation practices that prioritize local model fit while preserving content validity.

Control variables

To strengthen the internal validity of the structural model and reduce the risk of spurious associations among latent constructs, four demographic variables were incorporated as covariates in the analysis: sex (male/female), age (continuous, in years), marital status (single/married/other), and self-rated economic status. These variables were selected on the basis of their documented associations with psychological distress in the broader literature48,50 and their representation in the present sample. By statistically controlling for these demographic characteristics, the estimated structural paths more accurately reflect the theorized relationships among the psychological constructs of interest51.

Ethical compliance

The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee at the University of Hail, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (Approval No. H-2026-025, dated 27 January 2026). All procedures involving human participants were carried out in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees, the Declaration of Helsinki, and other relevant guidelines and regulations. Data were collected using an anonymous online questionnaire administered via Google Forms. Before accessing the survey, potential participants were presented with an information page that explained the aims of the study, the procedures, estimated time required, confidentiality safeguards, and the voluntary nature of participation. Only individuals who indicated their agreement on this page were able to proceed to the questionnaire, so informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants.

All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Analytic strategy

The present study employed Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), implemented in SmartPLS 452, as the primary analytic framework. The analytic procedure was organized into three sequential phases-measurement model assessment, structural model estimation, and multi-group analysis-the results of which are reported in full in the “Results” section. The present section provides the theoretical and methodological rationale for the analytic choices made.

Rationale for PLS-SEM over covariance-based SEM

The decision to adopt PLS-SEM rather than covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM) was informed by several theoretically and empirically grounded considerations specific to the present study.

First, PLS-SEM is particularly well-suited to research designs that are explicitly predictive in orientation―that is, studies concerned with explaining variance in endogenous constructs and evaluating the relative contribution of theorized predictors―rather than designs aimed at strict confirmatory testing of a theoretically specified covariance structure38. The present study sought to assess the extent to which self-efficacy, personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities collectively predict psychological distress, a goal more naturally aligned with the variance-based logic of PLS-SEM than with the distributional assumptions of CB-SEM53.

Second, CB-SEM requires large samples and multivariate normality to produce stable maximum likelihood estimates, particularly in models with multiple latent constructs and mediation pathways54. Although the present sample (N = 328) is adequate for PLS-SEM―which produces consistent estimates through ordinary least squares regression without imposing distributional assumptions―it may be suboptimal for the more demanding estimation requirements of CB-SEM in models of comparable complexity38. Furthermore, data collected via snowball sampling from diverse community populations cannot be assumed to satisfy multivariate normality, an assumption that is particularly consequential for CB-SEM model fit indices38.

Third, the structural model in the present study incorporates multiple reflective latent constructs with theorized mediation pathways, a configuration for which PLS-SEM offers robust bootstrapping-based inference for indirect effects without requiring distributional assumptions18,38. This is particularly relevant given the study’s emphasis on testing the mediating roles of autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities in the self-efficacy–distress relationship.

Fourth, PLS-SEM provides a comprehensive suite of predictive accuracy indicators―including the coefficient of determination (R2), Stone-Geisser’s Q² statistic for predictive relevance, and Cohen’s f² for effect size estimation―that collectively enable a nuanced evaluation of model performance beyond simple path significance. These indicators are directly relevant to the study’s objective of building an empirically grounded predictive model of psychological distress in Arab adults.

Taken together, these considerations establish PLS-SEM as the methodologically appropriate choice for the present study. We acknowledge, however, that future research employing larger probability-based samples may benefit from replicating the proposed model using CB-SEM to evaluate its confirmatory fit under more stringent distributional assumptions55.

Analytic phases

The analysis proceeded in three phases. In Phase 1, the measurement model was evaluated to establish the reliability and validity of all reflective constructs prior to structural estimation, following the criteria recommended by Hair et al.,38 for PLS-SEM applications in psychological research. In Phase 2, the structural model was estimated to test the hypothesized direct and indirect paths among the latent constructs, with bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) used to generate bias-corrected confidence intervals for all path coefficients and indirect effects18. In Phase 3, multi-group analysis (MGA) was conducted to examine whether the structural paths varied systematically across theoretically relevant subgroups, specifically gender, age cohort (18–39 vs. 40–65 years), and health complaint status, following measurement invariance verification procedures appropriate for PLS-SEM56. Full results for all three phases are presented in the “Results” section.

Measurement model assessment

Evaluation criteria

The measurement model was evaluated against the following criteria, in accordance with PLS-SEM guidelines for reflective constructs38: (a) indicator reliability: standardized factor loadings ≥ 0.70; (b) internal consistency reliability: Cronbach’s alpha (α) ≥ 0.70, composite reliability (rho_C) ≥ 0.70, and rho_A ≥ 0.70; (c) convergent validity: average variance extracted (AVE) ≥ 0.50; and (d) discriminant validity: assessed via the Fornell-Larcker criterion, whereby the square root of each construct’s AVE exceeds its correlations with all other constructs, and via the Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT), with values below 0.85 indicating adequate discriminant validity57.

Item reduction

Initial examination of standardized factor loadings from the PLS-SEM measurement model identified items failing to meet the minimum retention threshold of 0.7038. Item reduction affected three of the five scales as follows.

For the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE), six of the original 10 items (items 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 10 in the original Schwarzer & Jerusalem,39 numbering) were removed because their standardized loadings fell below 0.70, indicating below threshold contribution to the latent construct’s explained variance. The four retained items continued to represent the core conceptual domain of optimistic self-belief in coping capacity.

For the Maastricht Personal Autonomy Questionnaire (MPAQ), four of the original 16 items were removed across two subscales. One item from the life arrangements subscale was removed due to a loading below 0.70, and three items from the health priorities/preferences subscale were similarly removed for the same reason. The personal autonomy subscale retained all five original items. The resulting 12-item version retained adequate content coverage across all three dimensions of the autonomy construct.

For the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10), one item from the original 10 items (item 1: " During the last 30 days, about how often did you feel tired out for no good reason?“) was removed due to cross-loading on a secondary factor, which affected unidimensional fit of the psychological distress construct. The nine retained items preserved comprehensive coverage of anxiety and depressive symptomatology.

Across all three scales, the retained items continued to cover the core conceptual domains of their respective constructs, culturally calibrate supporting the content validity of the reduced versions despite the item removal49. Full item-level details, including original item numbers, abbreviated content in English and Arabic, standardized loadings, means, and standard deviations for all retained items are listed in Appendix A alongside their item-level means, standard deviations, and final standardized loadings.

Multi-group analysis (MGA)

Theoretical rationale and hypotheses

The decision to conduct multi-group analysis was theoretically motivated by documented gender, age, and health-status differences in the psychological mechanisms linking self-efficacy to distress. With respect to gender, research consistently indicates that women report higher levels of psychological distress and exhibit stronger autonomy-based coping orientations than men, suggesting that the autonomy → distress pathway may be more pronounced among female participants50,58. With respect to age, younger adults navigating identity consolidation and occupational transitions may be more susceptible to distress when autonomy is constrained, whereas older adults may draw on accumulated coping resources that attenuate this relationship34,59. With respect to health status, the presence of medical complaints introduces an additional source of resource depletion that may moderate the protective function of self-efficacy and autonomy37. These theoretical considerations motivated the following directional hypotheses: (H_MGA1) the autonomy → distress path will be stronger among females than males; (H_MGA2) structural paths will be stronger among younger adults; and (H_MGA3) the protective effects of autonomy will be attenuated among participants with health complaints.

Discussion

The present study examined the extent to which self-efficacy, personal autonomy, life arrangements, and health priorities predict psychological distress among Arab adults within an integrated PLS-SEM structural model. The absence of a significant direct path from self-efficacy to psychological distress (β = − 0.04, p = .45), juxtaposed with the large effect size of self-efficacy on personal autonomy (β = 0.51, f2 = 0.36), constitutes the most theoretically consequential finding of this study and demands interpretation within - and partially against - the prevailing assumptions of social cognitive theory. This discussion interprets each major finding in relation to the theoretical frameworks that motivated the study and evaluates its implications for existing knowledge.

Before proceeding, an important interpretive caveat applies to all findings discussed in this section: the cross-sectional design of the study permits the estimation of structural associations among latent constructs but does not establish temporal precedence or rule out reverse causation. Throughout this discussion, terms such as “predicted,” “associated with,” and “accounted for” are used in their statistical sense, denoting structural associations within the model rather than causal relationships. Causal language - where it appears - should be understood as reflecting the theoretical framework guiding the study rather than an empirical claim established by the present data.

Measurement model validity

The satisfactory psychometric performance of all five constructs in an Arab adult sample carries theoretical significance beyond mere methodological adequacy. The successful adaptation of the GSE39 and K1048 in this context, following item reduction based on loading criteria, suggests that the core psychological constructs these instruments operationalize - self-belief in coping capacity and nonspecific psychological distress - possess cross-cultural validity when appropriately calibrated. This result indicates that aligns with growing evidence that self-efficacy and distress are not exclusively Western psychological phenomena but represent universal regulatory mechanisms that nonetheless require culturally sensitive measurement60. The adequate performance of the MPAQ across the full 18–65 age range, despite its original development for older adults47, further suggests that autonomy-related constructs are meaningfully operative across the adult lifespan in Arab populations - a finding that itself warrants further investigation in future research.

The primacy of self-efficacy as a resource generator: extending social cognitive theory

The finding that self-efficacy exerted large effects on both personal autonomy (β = 0.51, f2 = 0.36) and life arrangements (β = 0.46, f2 = 0.27), while showing no significant direct effect on psychological distress (β = − 0.04, p = .45), represents a pattern clearly visible in the structural model presented in Fig. 1.

Within Bandura’s (1997) social cognitive theory, self-efficacy is conceptualized as a generative belief system that enables individuals to mobilize cognitive, motivational, and behavioral resources. The prevailing assumption in the literature has been that this mobilization translates directly into reduced distress through enhanced appraisal and coping21,29. The present findings reveal an Arab-specific pattern: self-efficacy showed no significant direct association with distress but was instead associated with higher volitional autonomy and structural life organization, which in turn attenuated distress. This suggests that in role-complex social environments characterized by strong collectivist obligations and constrained individual agency - conditions prevalent in Arab societies26 - the association between self-efficacy and distress appears to operate through downstream psychological resources rather than direct appraisal pathways.

This interpretation supports the Conservation of Resources theory37, which posits that resources operate in caravans: the possession of one resource (self-efficacy) facilitates the acquisition of others (autonomy, structured life arrangements), and it is the accumulation of this resource contribution caravan that ultimately buffers against distress. The present structural model provides the initial structural evidence of this resource caravan logic in a diverse Arab adult sample, thereby extending Hobfoll’s framework beyond its original Western clinical contexts.

This resource-generative function of self-efficacy finds direct empirical support in the Arab academic context. Zayed22, in a comprehensive structural model conducted among university academics across Arab institutions, demonstrated that self-efficacy constituted one of the most potent personal resources for overcoming occupational stress and sustaining work engagement, operating through psychological resilience as a mediating mechanism. The convergence between their findings and those of the present study is theoretically significant: across two methodologically distinct investigations and two different Arab population segments - university academics and community adults - self-efficacy consistently emerges not as a direct distress-suppressor but as a resource mobilizer that activates downstream protective mechanisms, whether resilience in occupational settings22 or personal autonomy in community contexts (present study). This convergence strengthens the generalizability of the resource-generative model of self-efficacy within Arab cultural settings and suggests that this pattern may reflect a culturally embedded psychological dynamic rather than a sample-specific artifact.

Notably, the non-significant direct path from self-efficacy to distress diverges from findings in acute stress contexts - such as academic examination periods61 and psychiatric patient populations62 - where direct protective effects of self-efficacy have been observed. We interpret this divergence as reflecting a contextual boundary condition: direct efficacy-distress relationships may be more detectable when distress is episodic and situationally bounded, whereas in chronic, role-embedded distress environments - such as those faced by Arab adults navigating persistent economic instability and shifting work-family demands - the protective influence of self-efficacy is necessarily mediated by dispositional and structural resources that buffer cumulative strain.

Full mediation through personal autonomy: integrating social cognitive and self-determination theories

The sole statistically significant mediation pathway - Self-Efficacy → Personal Autonomy → Psychological Distress (β = − 0.17, 95% CI [-0.23, − 0.11]) ―provides a theoretically integrative finding that advances understanding beyond either social cognitive theory or self-determination theory in isolation.

Self-determination theory posits that autonomy - the experience of acting from personal volition rather than external compulsion - is a fundamental psychological need whose satisfaction is essential for well-being and whose frustration generates distress31,33. The present findings demonstrate that self-efficacy is a significant upstream determinant of experienced autonomy in Arab adults: individuals who hold strong beliefs in their coping capacities are more likely to perceive their actions as self-initiated and value-congruent, thereby satisfying the autonomy need and attenuating distress. This finding establishes a theoretically coherent bridge between social cognitive theory’s emphasis on competence beliefs and self-determination theory’s emphasis on volitional experience, suggesting that these two frameworks are not parallel but sequential in their operation: competence beliefs were positively associated with the psychological conditions under which autonomy tends to be experienced, which then protects against distress.

This sequential logic is consistent with findings among medical students, where self-efficacy reduced distress through sequential coping mediation63, and among adolescents, where full mediation in mental health outcomes was observed64. However, the present study extends these findings to a culturally distinct adult population in which autonomy is not simply a dispositional variable but is negotiated within dense networks of familial and social obligation - a context where the role of self-efficacy in enabling autonomous functioning may be particularly consequential.

The non-significance of the mediation paths through life arrangements (β = − 0.04, p = .10) and health priorities/preferences (β = − 0.02, p = .30) is itself theoretically informative. It suggests that the structural organization of daily life and the prioritization of health behaviors, while predicted by self-efficacy, do not independently attenuate distress once personal autonomy is accounted for. This pattern implies that it is the subjective experience of volitional control - not its behavioral manifestations in organized routines or health practices - that constitutes the active psychological ingredient in the self-efficacy-distress relationship.

The Counterintuitive role of health priorities: extending Hobfoll’s resource-stress paradox

The positive association between health priorities/preferences and psychological distress (β = 0.35, p < .001) constitutes the most counterintuitive finding of this study and requires careful theoretical interpretation rather than dismissal as a statistical anomaly.

A straightforward reading of this finding - that attending to one’s health increases distress - would be theoretically untenable. A more nuanced interpretation, grounded in Hobfoll’s (1989, 2002) resource conservation framework, suggests that heightened health prioritization in the present sample reflects not proactive wellness investment but reactive hypervigilance: individuals who are already experiencing psychological or somatic distress become preoccupied with health concerns precisely because they perceive their health resources to be depleted or threatened. Under this interpretation, the direction of influence may be bidirectional or even reverse-causal, with distress elevating health preoccupation rather than - or in addition to - health preoccupation elevating distress. This interpretation converges with the health anxiety literature, which documents that heightened health monitoring under conditions of perceived vulnerability amplifies rather than attenuates distress65.

This outcome also extends Hobfoll’s resource-stress paradox - the observation that resource investment under conditions of scarcity can paradoxically intensify stress - to the domain of health-related cognition in Arab adult populations, where access to mental health resources is structurally constrained24. Future research should examine this relationship longitudinally to disentangle its directional structure and test whether health prioritization moderates the efficacy-distress relationship under varying levels of actual health status.

Cross-demographic patterns: theoretical implications of multi-group findings

Although formal MGA permutation tests did not yield statistically significant between-group differences for most paths - indicating that the core structural associations are broadly stable across the sample - descriptive variation in path magnitudes across subgroups carries theoretically interpretable signal, as elaborated below. The substantially degraded model fit among participants with health complaints (NFI = 0.358 vs. 0.839 overall) is noteworthy and is addressed as a theoretically meaningful boundary condition rather than a purely methodological artifact.

The multi-group analysis revealed a pattern of structural consistency across gender, age, and health status subgroups, with the autonomy → distress and health priorities → distress paths remaining significant across all groups. This consistency is itself theoretically meaningful: it suggests that the identified psychological mechanisms - the role of volitional autonomy in buffering distress and the paradoxical distress-amplifying effect of health preoccupation - are not idiosyncratic to any particular demographic subgroup but represent relatively stable features of psychological functioning in this Arab adult sample.

The model fit statistics for participants reporting health complaints (NFI = 0.358; SRMR = 0.12) preclude meaningful structural interpretation for this subgroup. An NFI value below 0.40 indicates that the hypothesized structural configuration explains substantially less covariance among the observed indicators than a null intercept-only baseline model for this subsample - a level of misspecification that renders path coefficient comparisons with other subgroups uninterpretable rather than merely imprecise. The structural paths reported for this group in Table 10 are therefore retained for descriptive transparency only and should not be read as evidence either for or against the theoretical model within this population segment. The poor fit most likely reflects a combination of the small subgroup sample size (n = 48), which yields unstable PLS-SEM estimates38, and the possibility that psychological distress operates through illness-specific appraisal pathways - catastrophizing, illness identity consolidation, and ruminative health monitoring - that the present model, constructed without illness-specific mediating constructs, structurally cannot represent65,66. The investigation of this subgroup requires a dedicated study designed from the outset with health-status-stratified sampling and illness-specific mediators before any cross-group inference is warranted.

Theoretical contributions

Collectively, the findings of this study make three distinct contributions to the theoretical literature.

First, they demonstrate that the relationship between self-efficacy and psychological distress is not direct in chronic, role-complex social environments but is fully mediated by experienced personal autonomy. This finding refines social cognitive theory1 by specifying a boundary condition - contextual role complexity and collectivist cultural orientation - under which direct efficacy-distress pathways are attenuated and mediated pathways become dominant. This conclusion is corroborated by converging evidence from the Arab academic context: Zayed et al.,22 similarly found that self-efficacy’s influence on work-related outcomes among Arab university academics was channeled through psychological resilience rather than operating as a direct protective factor, suggesting that indirect mediated pathways represent a structurally consistent feature of how self-efficacy functions across diverse Arab populations and outcome domains.

Second, the study provides empirical support for a sequential theoretical integration of social cognitive theory and self-determination theory, demonstrating that competence beliefs were positively associated with volitional experience (autonomy) which then buffers distress. This integration advances both theories by specifying their sequential rather than parallel operation in Arab adult populations.

Third, the counterintuitive positive effect of health priorities on distress extends Hobfoll’s (1989) Conservation of Resources theory by demonstrating that heightened health prioritization was positively associated with distress, a pattern compatible with paradoxical resource investment effects under conditions of perceived resource scarcity and structural constraint - a dynamic that has not previously been documented in Arab community samples and that has implications for health promotion interventions that emphasize health awareness without simultaneously addressing structural resource access.

Conclusions

This investigation was designed to examine the structural relationships among self-efficacy, personal autonomy, life arrangements, health priorities/preferences, and psychological distress in a diverse Arab adult sample, using variance-based PLS-SEM to test an integrated theoretical model grounded in social cognitive theory1, self-determination theory31, and Conservation of Resources theory37. The findings yield three theoretically precise conclusions that either refine, extend, or challenge prior assumptions in each of these frameworks.

What the study confirms, challenges, and extends

First, the study challenges the prevailing assumption within social cognitive theory that self-efficacy is associated with lower psychological distress through direct appraisal and coping mechanisms1,21. The absence of a significant direct path from self-efficacy to distress (β = − 0.04, p = .45), combined with the significant indirect path through personal autonomy (β = − 0.17, 95% CI [-0.23, − 0.11]), establishes that in chronic, role-embedded Arab social environments, the protective function of self-efficacy is not direct but is contingent upon the activation of volitional autonomy. This constitutes a culturally specified boundary condition for social cognitive theory that has not previously been empirically documented in Arab community samples.

Second, the study extends self-determination theory31 by identifying self-efficacy as a significant upstream determinant of experienced autonomy. Prior research within the SDT framework has extensively documented the consequences of autonomy satisfaction and frustration for well-being33, but has paid comparatively less attention to the antecedents of autonomy experience. The present finding that individuals with stronger efficacy beliefs are more likely to experience their actions as volitionally self-initiated - and thereby achieve distress protection - advances SDT by specifying a cognitive-motivational pathway through which autonomy need satisfaction is enabled. In the Arab sociocultural context, where collectivist obligations and structural constraints on individual choice are prevalent25,26, the role of self-efficacy as an enabler of experienced autonomy is particularly consequential: it suggests that internal competence beliefs can partially compensate for external autonomy constraints, a dynamic with direct implications for how autonomy support should be conceptualized in non-Western settings.

Third, the study extends Conservation of Resources theory37 by documenting a counterintuitive resource-stress paradox in the health domain: heightened health priorities/preferences - rather than protecting against distress - were positively associated with psychological distress (β = 0.35, p < .001). This finding is interpretable within Hobfoll’s framework as evidence of reactive hypervigilance: individuals experiencing resource depletion or threat become preoccupied with health preservation precisely because they perceive their health resources to be imperiled, thereby amplifying distress rather than mitigating it. This paradox has previously been theorized in health anxiety research65 but has not been structurally modeled in Arab community populations, where access to healthcare resources is structurally uneven24. The present findings therefore extend Hobfoll’s framework to a previously undocumented cultural and structural context.

Why the Arab context matters theoretically

The Arab sociocultural context is not merely a geographic backdrop for these findings - it constitutes a theoretically relevant moderating environment that shapes the psychological pathways under investigation in ways that are not captured by research conducted in Western individualistic societies. Specifically, three contextual features distinguish the Arab setting: (a) strong collectivist cultural norms that subordinate individual autonomy to familial and communal obligations, rendering the experience of personal volitional control both more constrained and more psychologically significant when achieved26; (b) structural conditions including elevated youth unemployment, limited occupational mobility, and restricted mental health service access, which sustain chronic rather than episodic distress profiles24; and (c) the embeddedness of health prioritization within cultural attitudes toward illness disclosure and help-seeking that differ substantially from Western norms48. Together, these features create a psychological environment in which the indirect, resource-mediated model of self-efficacy identified in this study is theoretically expected and empirically confirmed - and in which direct efficacy-distress pathways, common in Western acute-stress contexts, are attenuated. Future research should test whether this contextual pattern replicates across other non-Western collectivist settings, such as East Asian or Sub-Saharan African populations, to establish the broader boundary conditions of the proposed model.

The finding that the structural model remained consistent across gender, age, and health subgroups - with autonomy and health priorities maintaining their directional relationships across all groups - further suggests that these associations were not demographic artifacts but reflect relatively stable features of psychological functioning in this population. The descriptively stronger autonomy → distress protective effect among females (β = − 0.34 vs. − 0.29) and younger adults (β = − 0.32 vs. − 0.28), while not reaching formal statistical significance in MGA testing, is theoretically interpretable in terms of greater role conflict exposure among women and heightened autonomy sensitivity during identity-formative life stages58,59. The negligible direct contribution of life arrangements to distress - despite being significantly predicted by self-efficacy - underscores that it is the subjective experience of volitional control, rather than the objective organization of daily activities, that constitutes the psychologically active protective mechanism.

The theoretical findings outlined above carry direct practical implications for psychological intervention design and public health policy in Arab contexts, elaborated systematically in the Conclusions section.

Practical implications: intervention, institutional, and policy recommendations

The structural findings of this study translate into concrete guidance for practitioners, institutional leaders, and policymakers working within Arab contexts, addressed here across three distinct levels.

At the individual intervention level, the mediation findings indicate that efficacy-enhancement programs will yield the greatest distress-reduction benefit when they are explicitly designed to translate competence gains into expanded volitional choice. Cognitive-behavioral and acceptance-based interventions that build self-efficacy while simultaneously supporting value-congruent decision-making - rather than focusing on efficacy beliefs in isolation - are more likely to activate the autonomy pathway identified as the sole significant mediator. Programs such as autonomy-supportive coaching32 and self-regulation training67 offer evidence-based frameworks that can be culturally adapted for Arab adult populations.

At the institutional level, workplaces and educational institutions in Arab countries should be encouraged to adopt autonomy-supportive management and pedagogical practices - specifically, offering meaningful choice within role obligations, providing competence-affirming feedback, and minimizing controlling supervisory behaviors. Converging evidence confirms that environments structured around genuine choice and competence affirmation strengthen the well-being dividends of self-efficacy19,33 - a pattern the present data suggest extends to Arab adult institutional contexts. Institutional policies that systematically constrain individual choice - even when well-intentioned - may inadvertently sever the efficacy-autonomy pathway that the present model identifies as the primary distress-protective mechanism.

At the public health policy level, the paradoxical finding that heightened health prioritization amplifies distress - rather than attenuating it - has direct implications for health communication campaigns in Arab societies. Awareness programs that intensify health vigilance without simultaneously expanding access to affordable and culturally acceptable healthcare services risk exacerbating distress among already vulnerable populations. Health promotion initiatives should therefore be structurally coupled with measurable expansions in healthcare access and culturally sensitive mental health services - particularly in communities where resource scarcity transforms health awareness into a burden rather than a protective asset34.

Limitations and future directions

Several methodological boundaries qualify the conclusions drawn from this study, and each points toward a specific corrective direction for future inquiry. The cross-sectional design is the most consequential constraint. Structural associations estimated from a single time point cannot establish whether self-efficacy precedes autonomy, whether distress amplifies health preoccupation rather than the reverse, or whether the mediation pathway identified here reflects a stable causal mechanism or a cross-sectional artifact. Resolving these directional ambiguities requires cross-lagged panel designs with repeated measurement waves, which would permit temporal ordering tests among the key constructs68. Experimental manipulation of autonomy support in naturalistic Arab institutional settings would provide an additional and more decisive causal test of the identified mediation pathway. Additionally, although the cross-sectional design was implemented within a bounded nine-month window, data collected via rolling snowball sampling across two countries may reflect temporal variation in contextual stressors - such as economic fluctuations or sociopolitical events - that could introduce unsystematic variance in distress scores across the collection period. Future studies should consider fixed-window data collection or time-stamped sensitivity analyses to assess the stability of structural estimates across collection phases.

The demographic profile of the present sample diverges from national population parameters in Egypt and Saudi Arabia in ways that bear directly on the precision - and not merely the generalizability - of the reported structural estimates. Female participants constituted 69.5% of the analytic sample, against an approximate parity distribution in both national populations69; single adults represented 65.9% of respondents, whereas marriage constitutes the statistically predominant adult status in both countries70; General Authority for Statistics Saudi Arabia,71; and urban residents comprised 69.2% of the sample relative to population urbanization rates of approximately 43% in Egypt and 84% in Saudi Arabia. As Winship and Radbill72 and Groves73 document, systematic compositional imbalances of this magnitude can both inflate and attenuate structural parameter estimates in ways that standard model diagnostics do not detect or correct.

This limitation was not addressed analytically in the present investigation. Inverse probability weighting or propensity-score reweighting procedures - which adjust sample composition toward population proportions prior to structural estimation74,75 - were not applied, and no sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine the stability of path coefficients under alternative compositional assumptions. Readers should therefore treat the reported path estimates as approximations that most accurately characterize the psychological dynamics of urban, predominantly single, and female adults within the Egyptian and Saudi populations, rather than as unbiased representations of population-level parameters. Future investigations should prioritize stratified probability-based sampling - explicitly balanced across gender, marital status, and residential type - before structural replication of the proposed model is undertaken in either national context.

To address this limitation transparently, the present path coefficients should be understood as approximations of the underlying population parameters rather than unbiased estimates thereof. Ideally, this concern would have been addressed analytically through inverse probability weighting or propensity-score-based reweighting procedures that adjust the sample composition toward population proportions prior to structural estimation74,75. The absence of such reweighting constitutes a recognized constraint on the precision - though not necessarily the directionality - of the reported path coefficients. Future investigations should prioritize probability-based sampling frames stratified across gender, marital status, and residential type to produce structurally unbiased population-level parameter estimates. Pending such replication, the present findings are best interpreted as reflecting the psychological dynamics of urban, single, and female adults within the Egyptian and Saudi populations - a theoretically coherent subgroup, but not a representative cross-section of either national adult population.

Geographically, restricting the sample to Egypt and Saudi Arabia - while enabling a meaningful cross-national Arab comparison - limits generalizability to the Levantine, North African, and Gulf subregions, which differ substantially in political conditions, economic structures, and gender role norms. Whether the indirect efficacy-autonomy-distress pathway identified here operates similarly across these subregions, or whether it represents a pattern specific to the Egyptian-Saudi sociocultural context, remains an open empirical question.

Four directions follow from these limitations. First, longitudinal cross-lagged designs should test the temporal ordering of self-efficacy and autonomy, given the theoretically plausible alternative that autonomy precedes and enables efficacy beliefs rather than the reverse31,40,68. Second, probability-based sampling studies should replicate the proposed model with demographically representative Arab adult samples to verify the stability of the structural estimates under conditions of compositional balance. Third, cross-cultural replication in other non-Western collectivist societies - East Asian, Sub-Saharan African, and South Asian populations - would test whether indirect resource-mediated efficacy pathways represent a broadly collectivist psychological pattern or a specifically Arab phenomenon. Fourth, randomized controlled trials or quasi-experimental designs that systematically introduce autonomy-supportive conditions in Arab workplaces and educational institutions - measuring pre-post changes in efficacy beliefs, experienced autonomy, and distress trajectories - would provide the most rigorous test of the mediation mechanism identified here and generate the evidence base needed for culturally grounded intervention programs33.

A further constraint pertains to the developmental scope of the Maastricht Personal Autonomy Questionnaire. The MPAQ was originally constructed and validated in samples of older adults managing chronic physical illness47, a context in which autonomy-related constructs carry a particularly salient meaning because they are constrained by health-imposed functional boundaries76. The application of this instrument to a predominantly younger and largely healthy adult sample - in which 85.4% of participants reported no medical complaints - introduces an interpretive asymmetry: items anchored to health-constrained daily functioning (e.g., “I try to find things I like doing and can cope with in my present state of health”) may carry different psychological referents for individuals who do not experience such constraints, potentially attenuating item-construct correspondence and contributing to the weak R2 observed for health priorities (R2 = 0.005) and the non-significant life arrangements pathway. No published evidence of MPAQ validity in samples spanning the 18–65 age range was available at the time of this investigation; the instrument’s applicability across this broader developmental window should therefore be treated as an empirical question requiring dedicated cross-validation rather than an established psychometric property. Future research deploying this instrument in non-clinical adult populations should first establish age-stratified measurement equivalence before incorporating MPAQ subscales into structural models.
