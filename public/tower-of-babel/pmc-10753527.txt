Laser-Pulse-Length Effects in Ultrafast Laser Desorption
Authors: Marco Schmidt, Robert Irsig, Dumitru Duca, Christian Peltz, Johannes Passig, Ralf Zimmermann
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractShortening the laser pulse length opens up new opportunities for laser desorption (LD) of molecules, with benefits for mass spectrometry (MS) sampling and ionization. The capability to ablate any material without the need for an absorbing matrix and the decrease of thermal damage and molecular fragmentation has promoted various applications with very different parameters and postionization techniques. However, the key issues of the optimum laser pulse length and intensity to achieve efficient and gentle desorption of molecules for postionization in MS are not resolved, although these parameters determine the costs and complexity of the required laser system. Here, we address this research gap with a systematic study on the effect of the pulse length on the LD of molecules. Keeping all other optical and ionization parameters constant, we directly compared the pulses in the femtosecond, picosecond, and nanosecond range with respect to LD-induced fragmentation and desorption efficiency. To represent real-world applications, we investigated the LD of over-the-counter medicaments naproxen and ibuprofen directly from tablets as well as the LD of retene and ship emission aerosols from a quartz filter. With our study design, we excluded interfering effects on fragmentation and LD efficiency from, for example, collisional cooling or postionization by performing the experiments in vacuum with resonance-enhanced multiphoton ionization as the postionization technique. Regarding LD-induced fragmentation, we already found benefits for the picosecond pulses. However, the efficiency of LD was found to continuously increase with decreasing pulse length, pointing to the application potential of ultrashort pulses in trace analytics. Because many interfering effects beyond the LD pulse length could be excluded in the experiment, our results may be directly transferable to the LD applied in other techniques.

Introduction

Laser desorption is the key process for the laser sampling of molecular analytes in mass spectrometry (MS). At higher laser irradiances, a part of the ejected material is charged (laser desorption/ionization, LDI) and can be readily detected in the MS setup.1 To reduce molecular fragmentation and enhance selectivity, the desorption and ionization steps are often separated, allowing the optimization of these processes individually.2,3 Such two-step approaches can also improve the sensitivity and quantification,4−6 also for matrix-assisted LDI (MALDI),7 the dominant laser sampling and imaging technology in MS.8

LD with postionization is often used for small and complex samples that are vulnerable to molecular fragmentation, e.g., in aerosol analytics,9,10 single-cell metabolomics,11 or high-resolution imaging.8 In addition to the LD “softness”, its “efficiency”, i.e., the number of released molecules at a given laser pulse energy, and the ratio between these monomers and clusters/droplets are crucial as it determines the sensitivity and the sample consumption. Note that MALDI is often not suitable in the aforementioned applications if the analyte molecules are relatively small and interfere with the matrix substance.12

For the matrix-free LD with postionization, a number of studies indicate benefits of ultrashort fs- and ps-lasers, e.g., with respect to the aforementioned parameters, softness13−17 and efficiency.18,19 Also, thermal damage to the substrate, e.g., tissue, is reduced for fs-desorption20−22 as well as matrix effects.23 Beyond LD, laser-pulse-length effects have been investigated for LDI23 and MALDI.24−26

The physics underlying LD is complex, and there is an ongoing debate about the role of the numerous mechanisms that determine pulse-length effects in LD.1,2,27,28 However, some aspects are consensual: fs-pulses directly interact with the electronic system before nuclear motion sets in, and they also do not irradiate the released material. Thus, extended heating and thermal damage are reduced, and direct ejection (e.g., via Coulomb repulsion) can significantly contribute to plume formation.23,25 Picosecond laser pulses correspond to the time scale of electron–phonon coupling. For example, ps-excitation of OH vibrations effectively couple with the translational motion required for ablation, minimizing energy transfer into the sample.15,29 Nanosecond pulses irradiate the target much longer until the early stages of the emerging plume and there is time for heat propagation, surface melting, and thermal vaporization.23,30

Direct comparison experiments for different pulse lengths were reported for laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy or inductively coupled plasma-MS (ICP-MS), where stoichiometric aspects are of particular interest.1,31,32 For LDI of particles, only minor differences for ultrashort pulses were found.33,34 In LD of molecules, the laser-pulse-length effects on substrate damage are well documented.2,35,36 However, for the desorbed molecules, only a few comparisons have been published, and results for pulse-length effects were partially inconsistent: for example, Steward et al.25 found reduced molecular fragmentation for fs-pulses, while Schätti et al.18 reported only limited fragmentation differences but improved efficiency. Some of the comparison experiments between ns-pulses and ultrashort pulses were performed with different laser systems and optical parameters, e.g., wavelengths.18,23 Other publications evaluated performance results by comparing different setups with many varying parameters beyond the pulse lengths.19 Of note, such performance results are a product of many processes involved in the respective technique, for example, matrix effects, pressure or gas flows, and postionization. This emphasizes the urgent need for direct comparisons and fundamental studies to provide a solid decision basis for the most suitable technology in LD-based applications, also considering the costs and complexity of ultrafast lasers.

Here, we target this research gap by presenting the first systematic study on LD of molecules, covering the full time scale of pulsed lasers from femtoseconds to picoseconds and nanoseconds. We isolated the LD pulse-length effect from the indirect downstream effects of collisions and atmospheric pressure postionization conditions by performing our experiments in vacuum and by using selective and soft gas-phase ionization via resonance-enhanced multiphoton ionization (REMPI).37−52 The different systems allowed us to isolate the two major characteristics of LD: medications to study the fragmentation of the active substance and filter samples for desorption efficiency experiments.

Experimental Section

Samples and Mass Spectrometry

We used two samples: direct desorption from tablets to investigate the pulse-length effect on fragmentation and retene sprayed on a quartz filter as a homogeneous representation of a real aerosol sample. In addition, a real-world sample containing particles emitted from a ship engine was analyzed, see Streibel et al. for details.39 The tablets were purchased from a drugstore (1A Pharma, naproxen—250 mg/tablet; RedCare, ibuprofen—400 mg/tablet). The tablet surface was sanded to remove possible coatings. For the filter experiments, 1 mg retene (Sigma-Aldrich, technical grade) was dissolved in 1 mL EtOH and sprayed on small pieces of a quartz fiber filter (Quartz-Microfibre Discs, Munktell & FILTRAK GmbH, Germany). The samples were then transferred through an airlock into the vacuum chamber (<10–5 mbar) of a reflectron time-of-flight (TOF) mass analyzer (RFT10, Stefan Kaesdorf GmbH, Germany). There, the samples were cooled with liquid nitrogen to minimize self-evaporation.

The timing cycle was initiated by the desorption laser that triggers a pulse delay generator with a 20 Hz repetition rate using a fast photodiode (11HSP-FS1, Standa Ltd., Lithuania). The delay generator controls the delay between desorption and ionization; here, the delay time was fixed to 30 μs, a value found to be optimal for all the laser pulses studied here. As determined in preparatory experiments, this value is sufficient to avoid interfering signals from ions formed by the desorption pulse. It also ensures low fragmentation at sufficient signal strength, see Supporting Information Figure S1 and compare with Milasinovic et al.5 for a deeper analysis of the pulse delay effects. Ninety nanoseconds after the postionization pulse, the ions were extracted into the TOF mass spectrometer where they were separated by their mass to charge ratio. The signal at the detector was then recorded by a Digitizer Card (Acqiris DP 110, Acqiris SA, Switzerland) and analyzed using custom software.

Optical Setup

Two different lasers were used to cover the LD pulse length from 50 fs to 6 ns. The optical parameters were kept the same during all the experiments with the exception of the changes discussed below. The fs-laser (Astrella-USP-1K, Coherent Inc., U.S.) produces pulses from 50 fs to 6 ps duration. For stretching the pulses to 500 ps, a reflecting chirped Bragg grating was used (BG Pulse, OptiGrate Inc., U.S.).40 Pulses of 6 ns duration were generated by an optical parametric oscillator system (Opolette HE 355, Opotek Inc., U.S.). All the parameters, except the pulse length, were kept constant for each performed measurement; see Table 1 for details. The pulse length was measured using an autocorrelator system (PulseCheck NX 50, APE GmbH, Germany), and the stretched pulses with a custom interferometer with second harmonic generation and a fiber spectrometer (HR 2000+, Ocean Optics Inc., U.S.). The desorbing laser pulse was focused by a concave mirror on the sample surface with an angle of incidence of 45°. Beam profiles of the desorption pulses were recorded with a camera (Ophir SP904, OphirSpiricon GmbH, Germany) and provide a relatively flat profile without substantial high-intensity spots, as shown in Figure S2. At a distance of 4 mm from the sample, a 266 nm Nd:YAG laser (ULTRA 20, Quantel, France) irradiated the desorbed molecules perpendicular to their direction of propagation and ionized them via REMPI, see Figure 1.

Results and Discussion

Pulse-Length Effect on Molecular Fragmentation

We evaluated the influence of the laser pulse length on fragmentation in the LD of active substances from tablets. The pulse energy as well as the delay and distance between the desorption and ionization pulse were kept constant. Figure 2a shows the resulting naproxen mass spectra for different pulse lengths between 50 fs and 6 ns, normalized to the molecular ion signal of naproxen at m/z = 230. For ibuprofen, shown in Figure 2b, normalization of the mass spectra is also performed to the highest peak; depending on the pulse length, it is the molecular ion signal or a fragment ion signal. REMPI ionization with 266 nm is selective for the aromatic ring structure of the active substance. Therefore, no signals from other ingredients of the tablets are visible in the mass spectra. Up to a few ps-pulse lengths, only minor differences in the fragment pattern can be noticed, while in particular the smaller fragments increase at 500 ps and remain at this level for the ns pulse. This observation is in line with a recent study, where the authors conclude that the desorption processes at about 40 ps pulse length resemble the ns desorption more than the mechanisms induced by fs-pulses.25 Further studies suggest that a transition between the mechanisms underlying fragmentation occurs in the low-ps range: for resonant excitation of the OH vibrations in the mid-IR, it was shown that pulses of less than 10 ps duration couple increasingly with the translational motion for laser ablation, and less energy is transferred into the sample compared to ns pulses, eventually reducing the fragmentation.15 Also, for solid metals, a solid–liquid phase transition was observed a few ps after laser excitation,41 indicating the onset of electron–phonon coupling and potentially associated changes in the desorption process on this time scale. While fundamental processes have similar time scales, the fragmentation depends also on the substance. In our experiment, we observed only a limited overall difference in the fragmentation of naproxen. However, the structurally very similar molecule ibuprofen reveals stronger overall fragmentation, as shown in Figure 2b. In Figure 2c, the survival yield (SY) of the molecules is depicted—defined as the molecule peak area divided by the sum of the molecule and the fragment peak area.5 For naproxen, this value decreases by about 30% when using long pulses and for ibuprofen by about 50%. However, in a real-world application with complex mixtures, the prominence of the molecular peak can make a big difference for the identification of a substance. The very similar trends for ibuprofen and naproxen in Figure 2c emphasize the reproducibility of the experiment and suggest that the underlying mechanisms on their individual time scales are very similar, while the analyte molecule’s fragility largely determines the amount of fragmentation.

Schätti et al.18 reported no substantial fragmentation changes between fs- and ns-pulses in the LD of polypeptides. In contrast, Steward et al.25 found substantial differences in the fragmentation pattern of 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid when comparing the resonant excitation and LD with fs- and ps-pulses. Furthermore, low fragmentation in LD with fs-pulses was discussed as a key requirement for the soft laser sampling of proteins under atmospheric pressure.16,19 Obviously, the amount of fragmentation is hardly predictable and depends on the substance and the experimental conditions. This is probably why the results currently found in the literature do not show a clear trend. For applications, it should however be noted that ns- and fs-LD with postionization is typically much softer than electron impact ionization (see, e.g., a comparison for naproxen in Figure S4). It is also softer than LDI, where higher-laser intensities are used to produce a sufficient number of ions directly at the sample surface.53 Specifically, for fs-LD and fs-LDI, the internal energies of precursor ions can be nearly as low as those for traditional MALDI, as shown in experiments using thermometer ions.2,5,42

Pulse-Length Effect on Desorption Efficiency

A quartz fiber filter with homogeneous coverage of retene was used to study the effect of the laser pulse length on the desorption efficiency. Retene is often found in environmental samples as it is a marker of forest fires.43,44 Furthermore, retene is a REMPI-active substance, and its fragmentation is nearly constant for all pulse lengths used here; see Figure S5. The spectra were recorded with the same setup and the same parameters as the experiments with the naproxen before; see the inset of Figure 3a or S5 for an example of a mass spectrum. For every laser pulse length, a new spot on the sample was exposed to the laser until the parent molecule peak dropped to 4 mV ns. The retene ion signal (m/z = 234) as a function of time for different pulse lengths is depicted in Figure 3a (1 s corresponds to 20 laser shots). From the signal decay, it can be clearly seen that the sample consumption increases with the pulse length, i.e., the shortest pulses produce the highest number of free molecules available for postionization until the sample material in the spot is removed from the filter. Thus, the shortest pulses have the highest LD efficiency. As shown in Figure 3b, the differences in the total ion yield from the retene molecule are considerable, with a factor of 17 between the shortest and the longest pulses. This result roughly corresponds to the findings of Schätti et al.,18 who compared fs- and ns-pulses in the UV range. Judge et al. found a 12–100 times higher “neutral capture efficiency” of analyte molecules released from the substrate and captured by an electrospray system when comparing fs-LD/electrospray MS with ns-MALDI/electrospray MS.45,46 These experiments involve more parameters beyond LD that we could exclude in our study, e.g., atmospheric pressure, complex postionization, and ion transfer, and they partly compare different instruments. However, the results for LD efficiency show the same trend with regard to pulse lengths.

In contrast to the fragmentation of naproxen and ibuprofen from tablets, considerable pulse-length effects on the desorption efficiency can be noticed already in the fs range; see Figure 3b. This points to changes in the dominant desorption mechanisms on the ultrashort time scale. It is well accepted that fs-pulses induce nonlinear photoionization and avalanche ionization, forming localized plasma in the volume underneath the surface.2 In water and organic samples, short-lived bubbles are formed at moderate temperatures, and a part of the material is finally ejected via stress release when the bubbles collapse.3,35 In a recent study, Steward et al. found that ions gain low kinetic energy if released by fs-desorption.25 This behavior was attributed to the increasing role of weak Coulomb repulsion for fs-pulses. In contrast to Steward et al., we investigated neutral molecules for postionization instead of LDI-formed ions. However, Coulomb repulsion and stress release following nonlinear photoionization by strong laser fields also induce the ejection of neutral species.2,35 The role of the pulse length in the plume dynamics will be subject to a follow-up study.

In contrast to such nonthermal mechanisms in fs-LD, it was early recognized that thermal desorption mechanisms become dominant above the few-ps time scale.1,47 These processes deposit more pulse energy in sample heating, melting, and thermal vaporization, and thus probably more of the material is ejected as clusters and droplets.18 The observed changes in desorption efficiency with pulse length in the few-ps range indicate a gradual transition between nonthermal effects (such as Coulomb explosion) and thermal effects (Figure 3b).48 For practical considerations, our results show that additional benefit for LD efficiency can be achieved by further shortening the pulse duration in the sub-ps range, as obvious from the results shown in Figure 3b.

Considering the limitations of our setup, it has to be noted that the underlying matrix can have a substantial effect on the desorption mechanism.45 The quartz fibers in our experiment can be expected to produce field enhancements and thermal energy confinement at their tips and edges. Such effects are harnessed in LD from nanostructured materials.49,50 Indeed, we observed small changes in fragmentation patterns between the individual spots on the filter, particularly at the beginning of sampling. However, these effects typically leveled out after a few seconds, without affecting the general time course of the parent ion signal.

In order to prove whether the results we observed for retene are directly transferable to the analysis of real-world samples, we performed the same experiment with filter samples from ship emissions (Figure 4). The filter was loaded with particles from a research ship engine, running with heavy fuel oil.39 The inset in Figure 4a shows the characteristic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, like alkylated phenanthrenes, chrysenes/benzo[a]anthracenes, benzopyrenes/benzofluoranthenes, and benzo[ghi]perylenes.39,51 For each pulse length, the total ion signal decay is plotted as a function of time. The signal decay curves for the ship emission aerosol are well comparable to the experiment with retene; the desorption efficiency increases here by a factor of 13 for the shortest pulse duration compared to the longest, as shown in Figure 3b. We also observed a similar temporal trend of the individual signals from different substances, supporting the assumption that the pulse-length effects on desorption efficiency are not restricted to individual species but can be exploited for the analysis of complex mixtures. Because we used REMPI postionization to minimize interfering effects from charge transfer and fragmentation, we were limited to aromatic molecules. Future studies should investigate the effect strength for different structures and larger molecules with different postionization techniques.

Conclusions

Our investigations of the pulse-length effects in LD support a trend toward reduced fragmentation and improved efficiency for ultrashort pulses. However, the observed fragmentation effect is less pronounced and depends on the analyte. In the long-pulse limit, the desorption process was ascribed to be thermally driven effects,1 while for shorter pulses (and increasing peak intensities) nonthermal effects contribute.2 We observed the main improvements of the fragmentation behavior in the low-ps range, in agreement with the literature.15,25 This suggests that already ps-laser systems may utilize much of these advantages, while the more complex fs-lasers might not provide additional benefit in many applications. However, since already minor structural changes of the analyte molecule can have a large effect and due to the inconsistent results from other studies,18,25 sophisticated analyses of the underlying mechanisms are needed to develop soft and efficient LD for intriguing applications.

For the desorption efficiency, we find more pronounced pulse-length effects, consistent with previous experiments.18 The efficiency improves by more than one order of magnitude for the shortest pulses. We could also show that this increase is continued in the sub-ps range, giving evidence for the transition to nonthermal desorption mechanisms. Regarding the substantial improvements in sample material economy, it can be concluded that fs-laser pulses are highly advantageous for samples with low analyte concentrations and especially for the LD from very small structures, e.g., thin cuts of tissues, small cell systems, or even individual cells or particles.
