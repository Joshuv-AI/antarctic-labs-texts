Association of Environmental Factors and Aging Levels Under Different Urbanization Levels in China
Authors: Zehua Zheng, Yiting Liu, Jiawei Zang, Huaiyue Xu, Kailai Lu, Jie Ban, Qing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn China, the processes of population aging and urbanization are persistently accelerating. To gain insights into the association of environmental factors and aging levels under different urbanization levels, this study examined how these factors correlate with aging levels across varying degrees of urbanization in China. County‐level data encompassing aging levels and 22 pertinent factors were collated for the years 2010 and 2020. Aging levels were quantified by the proportions of elderly population aged 65+ and 85+ within the total population. The counties were categorized into three urbanization levels: high, medium, and low. Fixed effect models and random forest models were established to scrutinize the impacts and significance of independent variables on aging levels under different urbanization levels. Notably, the aging levels across all urbanization levels rose by a factor of 1.5–2.1 from 2010 to 2020. The results of the two models indicate that there are urbanization gradient differences in the impact factors: in low‐urbanization areas, resident savings and PM2.5 are the important variables; in medium‐urbanization counties, resident savings and air pollution such as CO, SO2, PM2.5, and O3 are the important variables; in high‐urbanization counties, climate factors such as average temperature in January and July are the important variables. Moreover, air pollutants show age‐specific associations, and medical resources only have a significant supporting impact on LA85+. The study reveals the transformation rule of the aging driving mechanism in the urbanization process from “economic foundation support” to “life quality adaptation,” providing a scientific basis for formulating differentiated health aging policies.Keywords: aging level, urbanization level, environmental and socioeconomic factors

Introduction

Population aging has emerged as a global phenomenon, presenting substantial challenges worldwide (Q. Wang et al., 2022). In China, population aging is progressing at an accelerating pace. Projections suggest that by 2050, the number of Chinese residents aged 65 and older will reach 365 million, accounting for 26.1% of the total population (Fang et al., 2020). Meanwhile, to accommodate substantial internal migration and population growth, China is undergoing rapid urbanization, which induces profound changes in ecological and living environments (Gong et al., 2012; Li et al., 2016, 2022). The dynamic interactions among urbanization, environmental change, and population aging jointly shape the health and well‐being of elderly population.

China's process of aging and urbanization exhibits both global and unique characteristics. On the one hand, China shares common developmental patterns with other countries. Similar to Japan, it faces severe rural‐urban migration‐driven aging disparities (Inoue & Inoue, 2024; Kim, 2021). Like India and Brazil, it grapples with rapid urbanization‐related environmental pollution (Bravo et al., 2016; Halder & Kasemi, 2025; Halder et al., 2025). Consistent with European countries, China has increasingly prioritized elderly health protection and environmental quality improvement (Bunker et al., 2016; Ebi et al., 2021; Gong et al., 2012). Studies in these developed countries suggest a strong emphasis on environmental justice and elderly‐friendly urban design, while studies in these developing countries highlight that rapid, unregulated urbanization exacerbates environmental inequalities for the elderly population.

On the other hand, China's uniqueness is striking. Firstly, some studies suggest that urbanization exacerbates inequalities in household income and consumption, influences age structure, education levels, GDP, and medical services, and contributes to disparities in health and pension provisions for the elderly population across different urbanization levels (Chen et al., 2016; Gong et al., 2012; Hu et al., 2019). Secondly, traditional pollutants have declined significantly due to strict industrial emission controls, but PM2.5 and O3 pollution remain persistent, especially in medium‐ and low‐urbanization areas undergoing industrialization (Kocak et al., 2023; Lin & Zhu, 2018). And, during extreme weather events, low‐urbanization areas have weaker healthcare infrastructure and cooling/heating facilities (Luo et al., 2020). These environmental factors associated with urbanization have been closely linked to chronic diseases, cardiovascular ailments, and cancer within the elderly population demographic (Deering, 2023; Ebi et al., 2021; Hong et al., 2019). The heightened mortality and morbidity rates from these diseases among the elderly constitute a significant factor influencing the level of aging and pose a notable barrier to fostering healthy aging. Finally, China's large population base means that even small changes in environmental factors can have substantial impacts on the elderly. It is a scale unmatched by any other country.

Meanwhile, existing studies leave some critical research gaps and are difficult to fully reveal the influencing factors of aging in China. First, for post‐urbanization aging population in Japan, some studies have mainly characterized urban‐rural aging differentials at different regional levels, with a primary focus on demographic drivers such as population migration (Inoue & Inoue, 2024; Kim, 2021). Few studies have systematically integrated socioeconomic status, air pollution and climatic factors into a unified analytical framework. Second, for developing countries represented by India and Brazil, relevant studies have been largely confined to single megacities, focusing on the health effects of air pollution on elderly populations (Bravo et al., 2016; Halder & Kasemi, 2025; Halder et al., 2025). There is a scarcity of nationwide fine‐scale studies that link multi‐dimensional environmental factors to population aging structure. Third, European research has advanced understanding of climate vulnerability and age‐friendly urban design for elderly population, but these findings are mostly derived from contexts with mature and stabilized urbanization processes (Bunker et al., 2016; Ebi et al., 2021). These studies lack analyses on gradient disparities in the determinants of population aging using a quantitative hierarchical urbanization framework, and thus fails to fully uncover the dynamic shifting trends of aging driving forces amid rapid urbanization transitions.

Therefore, based on the uniqueness of China's national conditions, there is a pressing need for a comprehensive study that addresses the following critical issues. First and foremost, an examination of the spatial distribution characteristics of aging levels and their evolving trends throughout China is imperative. Secondly, identifying the pivotal environmental factors that have the greatest correlations on aging levels under varying degrees of urbanization is crucial. Lastly, elucidating the role of urbanization in the dynamic fluctuations of aging levels is of utmost importance. Given the concurrent shifts in population aging, urbanization, and environmental factors currently observed in China, it is imperative to delve into the intricate relationships that exist among these three pivotal factors.

This study integrated data on urbanization, population, and the environment, establishing both fixed effect models and random forest models to explored the relationship between environmental factors and aging levels across various urbanization levels in China. The insights derived from this research offer valuable scientific evidence to inform the development of policies and measures aimed at promoting healthy aging.

Data and Methods

Data Collection and Preprocessing

Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is aging level. Aging level for each county was measured by the ratio of the number of elderly population aged 65+ or 85+ in the whole population (for convenience, hereafter referred to as “LA65+” or “LA85+”), and data were obtained from the Sixth and Seventh National Population Censuses of China.

Assessment of the Urbanization Level

All counties (2,854 counties in 2010 and 2,835 counties in 2020) were divided into three levels of urbanization in China in 2010 and 2020 (as shown in Table S1 in Supporting Information S1). Based on a previous study (Q. Wang et al., 2021), the level of urbanization was assessed through the weights set of GDP, night light index, population density and non‐agricultural population and divided into three grades: low (0–3), medium (4–7), and high (8–10). The detailed calculation method for the urbanization level is shown in Supporting Information S1—Calculation method of urbanization level.

Independent Variable

After considering the factors that may affect aging levels, the 22 independent variables were selected and grouped as socioeconomic and environmental, including Gross Domestic Product (GDP), household savings deposit balance (Resident savings), average population crowding level (Population density), number of beds in hospitals and health centers (Number of beds), number of health technicians in hospitals and health centers (Medical personnel), average night light index (ANLI), normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), PM2.5, PM10, O3, NO2, SO2, CO, average temperature in January (Temp‐Jan.), average temperature in July (Temp‐July), land use and land cover change in farm land/wood land and meadow land/waters/construction land/unused land (LUCC‐Farm/LUCC‐Wood‐Meadow/LUCC‐Waters/LUCC‐Construction/LUCC‐Unused). Meanwhile, to enable comparison across counties of different sizes, socioeconomic variables were normalized to a per‐thousand‐people basis. Data spatiotemporal resolution and source is shown in Table S2 in Supporting Information S1. Data applicability is shown in Availability Statement.

Spatial Scales Preprocessing

ArcGIS was used to unify data of various spatial scales to the county scale, and the coordinate system uniformly adopts WGS‐84. Zonal Statistics As Table was used to calculate the raster means of variables within counties. Spatial Analyst was used to calculate the area proportion of the cross‐county raster within county, and then allocate the raster values proportionally. For county‐level adjustments caused by policies, the counties in 2010 are adjusted according to the scope of counties in 2020. The drawing review of map is No.(2019)1822.

Identification of Significant Influencing Factors

Variable Screening

In order to identify the linear influence of different factors on the aging levels under different urbanization levels, the fixed effects (FE) model was adopted to eliminate individual fixed effects with county‐level administrative divisions when the research data were panel data. Because the FE model is sensitive to multicollinearity, several preliminary tests were conducted (Tavares, 2022). Firstly, for multicollinearity diagnosis, this study calculated the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) of independent variables under different urbanization levels (as shown in formula 1), and the results showed that the VIF values of PM2.5 and PM10 were greater than 10 (as shown in Table S3 in Supporting Information S1). Secondly, pairwise correlation analysis showed that a significant correlation between PM2.5 and PM10 as well as other air pollutants (as shown in Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1). Based on the above results and the theoretical significance of PM2.5, PM10 was excluded from the model. Meanwhile, variables with a weak correlation with the dependent variable (|r| < 0.1), such as LUCC‐Farm, LUCC‐Waters, LUCC‐Construction and LUCC‐Unused, were also excluded. Finally, stepwise multiple linear regression was implemented to further screen candidate variables, with the screening criteria specified as the F‐test and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The validation metrics included VIF, P value and R 2. Candidate variables were introduced into the model when their P value were less than 0.05 and removed when their P value were greater than 0.10 (as shown in Table S4 in Supporting Information S1).

In the formula: j—Single independent variable; R 2—Coefficient of determination.

Model Building and Optimization

The study time only included two time points (2010 and 2020), and the time fixed effects had merely one degree of freedom, which was likely to induce multicollinearity in the model. Therefore, based on the variables screened above, four specifications of the model were separately estimated, namely the model controlling for individual effects, the model controlling for time effects, the model controlling for two‐way effects, and the model controlling for individual effects and time trends. The R 2‐within was used as the core criterion for model comparison (as shown in Table S5 in Supporting Information S1). Ultimately, the fixed effects model controlling for individual effects and time trends was selected for the analysis (as shown in formula 2). Notably, in that model, with T = 2 the time trend collapses essentially to a first‐difference effect (Baltagi, 2021). We further estimated first‐difference regressions to confirm robustness (as shown in Table S6 in Supporting Information S1). Comparing the fixed effects model results with the first‐difference regressions results, the signs of all core explanatory variables under different urbanization levels remain completely consistent, and their statistical significance levels are largely matched. All analyses were implemented using Python 3.12.

In the formula: Y—aging level; i—county; t—year; α—entity fixed‐effect; β—coefficient; γ—time trend coefficient; X—independent variable; ε it —error term.

Importance Analysis of the Variables

Variable Screening

In order to analyze the importance of different factors on aging levels under different urbanization levels, random forest models were adopted. The model variable screening procedure followed three sequential steps: Firstly, although the random forest model is not affected by collinearity, the highly redundant characteristics between PM2.5 and PM10 can reduce the importance of PM2.5, and PM10 was excluded. Then, a random forest model is constructed using all the independent variables, aiming to exclude variables with an importance score less than 1% and eliminate low‐information features. Finally, the models variables were optimized one by one based on different urbanization levels and different aging levels by adjusting or removing variables with lower rankings and lower importance scores. The importance score of variables is based on the random forest model to measure the contribution of variables to the prediction accuracy of “LA65+/LA85+,” and is mainly used to identify the key factors affecting aging levels under different urbanization levels.

Model Building and Optimization

Random forest models were constructed based on a stratified modeling strategy, taking urbanization levels (low/medium/high) and aging levels (LA65+/LA85+) as the stratification criteria. Given substantial differences in sample size and variable distribution across strata, hyperparameters were tuned independently for each stratum via grid search, with out‐of‐bag R‐squared (OOB‐R 2) as the primary optimization target, a strict overfitting constraint that the gap between training set R 2 and OOB‐R 2 must be less than 0.2, and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Squared Error (MSE) as auxiliary accuracy indicators; the final optimized hyperparameters for each stratum (as shown model parameter in Tables S7 and S9 in Supporting Information S1). To comprehensively evaluate the generalization performance of the optimized models, OOB‐R 2, MAE and MSE were reported as core generalization metrics. Then, we supplemented 5‐fold shuffled cross‐validation (CV) for each stratum independently. The average R 2 and MAE across five folds were calculated as supplementary indicators of out‐of‐sample generalization. Variable importance was calculated using the mean decrease in impurity (MDI) metric. For this regression task with continuous aging levels, node impurity is defined as MSE, so the importance of each feature corresponds to the average reduction in MSE contributed by the feature across all splits of all decision trees in the ensemble, reflecting its overall contribution to prediction accuracy. However, MDI‐based importance has an inherent bias of overestimating the importance of high‐cardinality continuous features relative to low‐variance features, which may cause the relatively high importance scores of variables. We adopted three methods to reduce the risk of bias. Firstly, overfitting control during hyperparameter tuning was implemented to suppress the amplification of MDI cardinality bias caused by overfitting. Secondly, multi‐seed consistency verification was performed with three independent random seeds, and the mean Spearman correlation coefficient of variable importance rankings was calculated to reduce random fluctuation of MDI estimates. Thirdly, the fixed effects model results can also be used as verification indicators. All analyses were implemented using Python 3.12.

The out‐of‐bag R 2 (OOB‐R 2), which reflects out‐of‐sample generalization performance, ranged from 0.42 to 0.82 (R 2: 0.60 to 0.94), with the gap between training and OOB R 2 controlled within 0.2 for all models. To further verify generalization robustness, we supplemented 5‐fold cross‐validation for all strata; the cross‐validation R 2 was highly consistent with the OOB‐R 2, confirming reliable out‐of‐sample performance (as shown in Figure 3, Tables S10 and S11 in Supporting Information S1).

Overall, from low to high urbanization levels, the impact factors on aging levels have undergone a transformation from “economic support” to “economic and traditional pollution” to “climate and environmental quality.” Among them, the high importance score of PM2.5 in the low‐urbanization group in 2020 indicates that it should emphasize the balance between economic development and environmental protection in its future urbanization process, so as to avoid repeating the mistake of worsening pollution in the medium‐urbanization group.

Comparison of Two Model Results

The random forest model results showed both consistencies and divergences with the fixed‐effect model results, which can be attributed to fundamental methodological differences between the two approaches. The fixed‐effect model estimates the linear partial effect of each independent variable on aging levels, with individual and time effects controlled for. In contrast, the random forest model quantifies the relative contribution of each variable to overall prediction accuracy, which incorporates nonlinear relationships. Combining the two perspectives allows for a more comprehensive characterization of the driving mechanisms of population aging across different urbanization levels.

For core driving factors including resident savings, PM2.5, and Temp‐Jan., the two methods yielded highly consistent findings. In low‐urbanization level, resident savings consistently exhibited the strongest explanatory power among socioeconomic factors. Similarly, PM2.5 and Temp‐Jan. were identified as key environmental determinants across multiple urbanization levels. Resident savings and January temperature showed significantly positive coefficients in the fixed‐effect model, while PM2.5 showed a significantly negative coefficient; all three variables had high importance scores in the random forest model.

Notably, some divergences were also observed. Firstly, variables with linear effects but limited incremental predictive power showed significant coefficients in the fixed‐effect model but relatively low importance in the random forest model. For example, medical personnel for LA85+ in low‐urbanization counties had a significant positive linear association (β = 0.016, p < 0.01) in the fixed‐effect model, but accounted for only 2.95% of importance score in the random forest model. This discrepancy is likely because medical resource indicators are highly correlated with other socioeconomic variables. Their independent linear effect can be detected in the fixed‐effect model, but their contribution to nonlinear prediction accuracy is partially shared with correlated factors and thus diluted in the random forest model. Secondly, variables with strong nonlinear effects may exhibit higher predictive importance than their linear effect would suggest. For example, NDVI for LA85+ in low‐urbanization counties showed both a significant positive linear effect (β = 0.404, p < 0.01) and high variable importance (9.72%) in the random forest model, indicating that NDVI affects the size of the elderly population through both linear demographic pathways and nonlinear environmental‐health pathways. For LUCC‐Farm, the random forest model captured notable predictive contributions that were not detected as significant linear effects in the fixed‐effect model, as their impacts on aging levels may operate through complex interactive pathways rather than a simple linear relationship.

Discussion

This study investigated the present status of aging and urbanization levels' distribution across China, delving into the critical factors related to aging levels that necessitate attention in counties with varying degrees of urbanization. The findings uncover that the aging levels throughout China are potentially related to regional land use and cover, meteorological conditions, and air pollution, among other factors. Notably, these correlations exhibit variability across different urbanization levels. Furthermore, disparities in the exposure of the elderly population to environmental factors—such as air pollution, and non‐optimal temperatures—warrant attention.

This study revealed significant spatio‐temporal disparities in the distribution of the elderly population throughout China. Specifically, when comparing data from 2020 to 2010, we observed a general upward trend in LA65+. Notably, LA65+ increased substantially in the southwest, east, central, and northern regions, whereas it declined markedly in the south. Similarly, LA85+ exhibited comparable changes to LA65+, with the exception of a notable increase in the northeast. Furthermore, our analysis indicated that LA65+ and LA85+ were 1.5–2.1 times their 2010 baselines. Among various urbanization levels, LA65+ in low‐urbanization counties rose by a factor of 1.6, and LA85+ in high‐urbanization counties rose by a factor of 2.1 —these represented the maximum multiples for each indicator, respectively. These discrepancies may stem from urban‐rural migration and differing paces of socioeconomic development (Han et al., 2022; Li et al., 2016; The Lancet, 2022; Wu et al., 2021). The swift economic growth in China has spurred migration toward highly urbanized areas, consequently leading to a decline in aging levels within densely populated regions and an exacerbation of aging in less populated areas—a trend paralleling observations in Japan and other nations (Marois et al., 2020; Watanabe et al., 2022). Firstly, owing to geographical advantages, market dynamics, and advantageous policies, the economic prosperity of high‐urbanization areas in the east and southern coastal regions surpasses that of other regions. The abundance of job opportunities and superior incomes in these economically advanced areas has prompted a considerable migration of working‐age individuals from rural to urban areas, from less developed to more developed regions, and from inland to coastal areas (Chen et al., 2018). Jiangsu Province serves as an illustrative example of this phenomenon. Despite the relatively higher fertility rate in northern Jiangsu compared to the south, the aging rate in the north significantly exceeds that of the south. This disparity arises because the southern regions are more economically developed, causing a substantial influx of adult labor forces to the northern regions, leading to a depletion of the younger population and an accelerated aging process in the north (Gu, 2014). Secondly, higher educational attainment among elderly population and superior healthcare quality have enhanced the quality of life and life expectancy of the elderly in the high‐urbanization eastern and southern regions (Chen et al., 2022).

Then, the important impact factors affecting the aging level are different under different urbanization levels and the gradient transformation of important impact factors under different urbanization levels is a shift from survival needs to development needs to quality needs. Firstly, in low‐urbanization counties, the weak foundation of public services and social security leads to the aging levels being highly dependent on the economic capacity of families and individuals, and resident savings, as a core indicator of economic security, directly affects the elderly's ability to maintain their livelihoods. Elevated resident savings facilitate access to enhanced healthcare services and a superior life quality, thereby promoting longevity among elderly population (Guo & Zhong, 2025). This is one plausible reading of the gradient and that alternative explanations (selective migration, baseline rural‐urban differences in mortality before 2010) cannot be ruled out in the present design. With the advancement of urbanization, in medium‐urbanization counties, the acceleration of industrialization leads to the coexistence of economic growth and environmental pollution. The traditional pollutants such as SO2 and PM2.5, which are closely related to industrial production, become key factors affecting the aging level, as the health risks brought by long‐term exposure to pollution significantly affect the settlement willingness and health condition of the elderly (Kocak et al., 2023). In high urbanization areas, the improvement of economic development and public service equalization weakens the constraint effect of economic factors; conversely, the elderly's demand for life quality is highlighted. The climate factors such as temperature and environmental quality such as PM2.5 become core factors driving changes in aging levels. Firstly, PM2.5 was found to significantly elevate cerebrovascular, cardiovascular, diabetes, genitourinary, and especially respiratory mortality or morbidity risks in the elderly (Bunker et al., 2016; Chen & Hoek, 2020; Wang et al., 2024). Secondly, non‐optimum temperature significantly increase the risk of cardiovascular and respiratory mortality among the elderly; optimum temperature regions have lower temperature‐related mortality rates, which improves elderly population survival rates and raises the local aging level (Bunker et al., 2016; Yao et al., 2025).

Furthermore, an aging society and urbanization change the number of people exposed to harmful air pollution. From the perspective of urbanization process, on the one hand, China has witnessed large‐scale rural‐to‐urban migration over the past decades, with more than half of the total population now residing in urban areas (Ji, 2018). On the other hand, rising urbanization levels are typically accompanied by industrial structural upgrading and enhanced pollution governance capacity, leading to a significant negative association between urbanization rate and ambient pollutant concentrations—that is, less urbanized regions generally bear a heavier air pollution burden (Lin & Zhu, 2018). This study found that air pollutants—as CO, O3, SO2, and PM2.5—exert important impact on both LA65+ and LA85+, aligning with conclusions drawn from relevant studies (Ge et al., 2017; Vanasse et al., 2017; Wensu et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2021). Notably, in the annual‐average random forest model analysis, SO2 exhibited high importance score among air pollutants. In the 2020 random forest model analysis, PM2.5 had a high important score and became the dominant pollutant. This transition is highly consistent with the trajectory of China's air pollution governance over the past decade. Driven by the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan and large‐scale ultra‐low emission retrofits in industrial sectors, national ambient SO2 concentrations have declined dramatically (Wei et al., 2023). As traditional coal‐combustion and industrial pollutants represented by SO2 are effectively controlled, PM2.5 has gradually replaced SO2 as the primary air pollutant associated with different aging levels, reflecting an epidemiological transition in pollution‐aging associations amid China's industrial upgrading and air quality improvement (Kocak et al., 2023; Lin & Zhu, 2018). This is consistent with the finding in the random forest model that PM2.5 has a important score on aging levels under low urbanization level. Population aging will be the leading contributor to increased deaths attributable to PM2.5 air pollution, which will counter the positive gains achieved by improvements in air pollution and healthcare (Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2023). But PM2.5 has negative linear coefficient in LA65+ in the fixed‐effect model. As mentioned earlier, LA65+ is more concentrated in medium‐ and high‐urbanization counties, and these counties have lower PM2.5 concentrations., which results in a negative coefficient. To sum up, This finding carries clear policy implications: low‐ and medium‐urbanization counties undergoing urbanization should shift their elderly population environmental health protection focus from traditional industrial gaseous pollutants to fine particulate matter management. Next, the relationship between CO and aging levels has not received much attention. However, this study found that CO has important impact on LA85+. A nationwide analysis showed significant associations between short‐term CO exposure and cause‐specific YLL (Y. Wang et al., 2021). Perhaps this impact is not significant in the young‐elderly population, but it is worthy of attention in the old‐elderly population.

For temperature, this study found that a important impact of Temp‐Jan. on both LA65+ and LA85+, especially in high‐urbanization group, and the importance score of the climatic factor in LA65+ is generally higher than that in LA85+ with the same urbanization levels. Extreme winter cold significantly reduces elderly population’ settlement willingness and health levels. elderly population are willing to move to places with warm winters, and at the same time, these warm winter regions also protect the elderly health from the impact of low temperatures (Chen & Goutte, 2025; H. Wang et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). It is worth noting that for LA85+, the importance score of Temp‐July is higher in low urbanization group. The higher importance score of Temp‐July for LA85+ in low urbanization areas is because these areas lack cooling facilities and heat‐resilient infrastructure, while LA85+ with degraded physiological functions are highly sensitive to extreme summer heat, leading to a sharp rise in heat‐related health risks (Bunker et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2019).

The aging level has been changing as the aging transition takes place in China. However, there is a difference in aging levels for different urbanization levels. Understanding the main factors associated with this evolution and urbanization level differences is important for designing appropriate policy measures that better contribute to healthy aging health. Therefore, this study innovatively combines the aging levels, urbanization levels and associated factors, and evaluates the importance of associated factors under different circumstances. However, several limitations should be noted. Firstly, due to the limitations of data acquisition, the data of NO2, SO2, and CO in 2018 are used to replace that in 2020, and the data of SO2 and CO in 2013 are used to replace that in 2010. This substitution introduces some measurement error against the background of China's air pollution control policies. For SO2 and NO2, China's nationwide emission transformation and Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan launched after 2013 drove a dramatic decline in national SO2 and NO2 emissions from 2013 to 2020. As a result, the 2018 SO2 and NO2 levels overestimate true 2020 SO2 and NO2 levels. Meanwhile, SO2 emissions had already declined slightly between 2010 and 2013 due to earlier desulphurization policies, so the 2013 SO2 levels slightly underestimate true 2010 levels. CO presents the smallest measurement error, as its emissions declined slowly and steadily over the decade, leading to only mild deviation from cross‐year substitution. In terms of estimation impact, all above errors compress the magnitude of 10‐year changes in pollutant concentrations, which slightly underestimates the absolute value of regression coefficients linking air pollutants to aging indicators. However, this bias does not reverse the sign of estimated coefficients or alter the statistical significance of core variables, nor does it change the relative pattern of effect values under different urbanization levels. Secondly, the panel data set only covers two discrete census years. This short‐panel structure restricts the ability to identify flexible, non‐linear time‐varying relationships between environmental/socioeconomic factors and aging levels, and the single linear time trend term cannot capture heterogeneous annual fluctuations between the two cross‐sections. Thirdly, this study lacks a direct internal migration indicator at the county level. Population density is used as a proxy to reflect the cumulative effect of population agglomeration and outflow, but it cannot distinguish between natural population growth and mechanical population growth, nor can it identify migration flows of different age groups. To some extent, the migration‐related mechanism explanations in this study are based on theoretical deduction. Future research can further verify the causal mechanism of migration on population aging when county‐level age‐specific migration data become available. Fourth, the current three urbanization level classification was determined based on previous study and the levels are more concerned with social and economic factors. Alternative threshold settings may moderately alter sample sizes within each stratum, the magnitude of effect estimates, and variable importance rankings. Future study could incorporate environmental factors when setting the level of urbanization and employ continuous urbanization scores to further test the robustness of the stratified findings and identify more precise inflection points. Fifth, this study did not perform a stratified analysis for gender and geographical partition. If gender and geographical partition are performed, some factors may have different effects on aging level, such as temperature in different regions. Finally, due to the limitations of short panel structure studies, we are unable to conduct a trend study about the influence of urbanization level on aging level and make causal inferences about the environmental factors affecting aging level. Therefore, we use an approach based on contemporaneous observations of independent variables, and we refer to these effects as associated factors or effects rather than determinants to prevent any interpretation of causality between factors and aging levels. In future studies, we will expand the panel data set to include multi‐year continuous monitoring data to capture dynamic annual changes in aging drivers and will look for evidence for these associations at the individual level.
