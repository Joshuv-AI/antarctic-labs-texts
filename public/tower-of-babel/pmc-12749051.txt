The occurrence of thromboembolism among patients with coronavirus disease 2019: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ziyan Wu, Yan Li, Jialin Chen, Qitao Guo, Yujie Pan, Song Liu, Jiarong Liu, Cheng Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe results of reported thrombosis occurrences in patients with COVID-19 are inconsistent. Objectives To elucidate the occurrence of thromboembolism in COVID-19 patients with different types. The search was conducted up to May 10, 2024. The observational studies reporting the occurrence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and/or arterial thromboembolism (ATE) in COVID-19 patients were included, which were independently evaluated by two researchers. The outcomes were VTE and ATE, including deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, and stroke. The effect sizes were combined using a random-effects model with inverse variance weighting, and a 95% confidence interval was calculated through arcsine transformation. A total of 224 studies was included. The occurrence of VTE was 5.8% (95% CI, 5.0%-6.7%, I2 = 99.912%; 91 studies; 4,545,285 patients). The occurrence of VTE was higher in the intensive care unit compared to the ward (13.2%, 95% CI, 11.7%-14.7%; I2 = 96.840%; 47 studies; 172,571 patients, vs. 3.2%, 95% CI, 2.9%-3.5%; I2 = 95.714%; 40 studies; 1,046,738 patients; P < 0.001), and was even lower among outpatient and discharged cohorts (0.0%, 95% CI, 0.0%-0.0%; I2 = 99.410%; 10 studies; 2,566,194 patients, vs. 0.7%, 95% CI, 0.4%-1.1%; I2 = 98.924%; 16 studies; 828,884 patients; P < 0.001). In contrast, the occurrence of ATE was lower, which was 2.6% (95% CI, 1.8%-3.5%, I2 = 99.924%; 44 studies; 2,884,839 patients). This study found that COVID-19 patients had a relatively high risk of VTE and ATE, but with significant variations among different types. Consequently, the selection of anticoagulant measures for them should be careful. Introduction

Since its initial identification in 2019, the virus, now known to be Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has propagated rapidly on a global scale, affecting over 200 countries and territories worldwide. The World Health Organization has formally declared the infection caused by SARS-CoV-2, also known as the Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), to be a public health emergency of international concern1,2. In excess of 90% of the subjects who did not survive the illness developed acute respiratory distress syndrome and respiratory failure. Furthermore, coagulation abnormalities were identified in 50% of the non-survivors3.

COVID-19 patients are considered to be at high risk for thrombosis. Thrombosis is a major complication of cardiovascular disease, resulting in venous thrombosis, myocardial infarction (MI), or acute ischemic stroke4. However, the prevalence of thrombosis in patients with COVID19 exhibits considerable variance across existing literature, ranging from 0% to 80%5,6. Meta-analyses have been employed to assess the prevalence of thrombosis in inpatients with COVID-197–9. A thorough analysis delves into the disparities between hospitalized and nonhospitalized patients with regard to demographic, clinical, and social characteristics10. However, the extent of these discrepancies in thrombosis rates remains to be elucidated. The objective of this study is to provide further insight into the incidence of thrombosis among patients with SARS-CoV-2 who have been discharged and outpatients diagnosed with the disease. Furthermore, the study will compare thrombosis occurrence across various categories of patients with COVID-19, including inpatients (intensive care unit [ICU] patients, ward patients), discharged patients, and outpatients, and evaluate the necessity of administering anticoagulant medications to prevent thrombosis in clinical practice.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on the PROSPERO platform (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/, CRD42024541026) in accordance with the Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE)11 guideline and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA2020) for reporting.

Search Strategy, study Selection, data Extraction, and quality assessment

A systematic search for literature reporting thromboembolic events in COVID-19 patients was conducted across five major databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Ovid, and Embase. The search was conducted up to May 10, 2024. The primary outcomes included VTE and ATE, and the secondary outcomes encompassed deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), myocardial infarction (MI), and stroke. The diagnosis of thrombotic disease was confirmed through a range of modalities, including ultrasound screening, computed tomography (CT) angiography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and electrocardiography, among others. Studies that focus on thrombus in a single specific location, such as the lower limbs, the upper limbs, or the liver, were excluded from the present study. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS)12 was utilized to assess the quality of the included studies. Detailed methods concerning search strategy, study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment are provided in eMethods in Supplement 1.

Statistical analysis

The occurrence of thromboembolism was described by a forest plot, which was created based on a random-effects model. Initially, the occurrence of thromboembolism in individual studies was calculated from the extracted data. The 95% confidence interval (CI) was subsequently determined through arcsine transformation. The pooled thrombosis rates were then aggregated by effectively weighting the sample size of each study using inverse variance weighting. The Cochrane Q test was employed to evaluate the heterogeneity among the included studies, with a 2-sided p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Additionally, I² statistics were utilized to assess the heterogeneity among the studies.

To investigate the sources of heterogeneity among the studies, a meta-regression analysis was performed on the continuous variables. The continuous variables included the mean age, sex ratio, receiving prophylactic anticoagulation ratio, the number of total patients, and the proportion of inpatients (including ICU and ward), outpatients, discharged patients. Subsequently, subgroup analyses were conducted on categorical variables, including multicenter versus single-center studies, prospective versus retrospective studies, the country of the study, and whether more than 50% of patients received thromboprophylaxis. Moreover, an additional subgroup analysis was conducted, with a focus on ICU patients, ward patients, outpatients, and discharged patients.

To assess the presence of publication bias, we employed funnel plots in conjunction with the Egger test. For studies identified as having publication bias, the trim-and-fill method was applied for correction. A symmetrical inverted funnel shape, as depicted in a funnel plot, suggests the absence of significant publication bias, given that the effect size of each study and its precision estimate (i.e., standard error) resemble this shape. A p-value of less than 0.05 from the Egger test indicates the presence of publication bias. All analyses were conducted using Stata software version 15 (Stata, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Study characteristics

A total of 61,151 records were identified, resulting in the final inclusion of 224 articles (Fig. 1, eReferences 1–224 in Supplement 1), which encompass 8,762,901 COVID-19 patients. Detailed results regarding study characteristics (Table S1) and quality evaluation (Table S2) are presented in eResults in Supplement 1.

Flowchart of the Systematic Search and Selection Process. A total of 61,151 articles were retrieved, and the titles and abstracts of 29,564 articles were reviewed, as well as the full text of 293 articles. This process resulted in the inclusion of 224 studies.

VTE

In this meta-analysis, a random-effects model was constructed to ascertain the weighted pooled occurrence of VTE, which was found to be 5.8% (95% CI, 5.0%−6.7%, I2 = 99.912%; 91 studies; 4,545,285 patients; Figure S1). This finding indicates that COVID-19 patients are at an elevated risk for VTE. Both the funnel plot and the Egger test (p = 0.004) suggested that the study results were subject to publication bias (Figure S2). However, we confirmed the stability of the study results through the trim-and-fill method, indicating that publication bias did not significantly affect the overall estimates. VTE encompassed DVT (4.8%, 95% CI, 4.2%−5.3%, I2 = 99.750%; 118 studies; 3,591,730 patients; Figure S3) and PE (7.2%, 95% CI, 6.4%−7.9%, I2 = 99.808%; 161 studies; 3,442,823 patients; Figure S4). Other results of the funnel plot and Egger tests are illustrated in Figure S5-S9. In instances where research findings indicated publication bias, the trim-and-fill method was employed to rectify the results, thereby facilitating an assessment of the research data’s reliability (Table S3).

Subgroup analysis of inpatients

The majority of the population were inpatients, and the findings related to the occurrence of thromboembolism were similar to those observed in the overall COVID-19 cohort. The occurrence of VTE was 6.1% (95% CI, 5.7%−6.6%, I2 = 98.324%; 79 studies; 1,313,226 patients; Figure S10), including DVT (5.3%, 95% CI, 4.8%−5.8%, I2 = 98.646%; 102 studies; 1,001,457 patients; Figure S11) and PE (7.1%, 95% CI, 6.5%−7.7%, I2 = 98.622%; 131 studies; 1,018,974 patients; Figure S12).

Subgroup analysis of ICU vs. Ward patients

The occurrence of VTE varies significantly based on the disease status and type of patient hospitalized. Inpatients with COVID-19 can be categorized into two distinct groups based on their disease status: ICU patients and ward patients. A significantly higher occurrence of VTE was observed in the ICU compared to the ward (13.2%, 95% CI, 11.7%−14.7%; I² = 96.840%; 47 studies; 172,571 patients, vs. 3.2%, 95% CI, 2.9%−3.5%; I² = 95.714%; 40 studies; 1,046,738 patients; P < 0.001; Fig. 2). This finding indicates that ICU patients may be at an increased risk of thrombosis. A consistent conclusion can be drawn regarding DVT (9.8%, 95% CI, 8.5%−11.3%; I2 = 97.694%; 46 studies; 170,445 patients, vs. 3.6%, 95% CI, 3.0%−4.3%; I2 = 98.652%; 36 studies; 729,556 patients; P < 0.001; Figure S13) and PE (11.6%, 95% CI, 10.3%−13.0%; I2 = 97.595%; 63 studies; 185,633 patients, vs. 5.1%, 95% CI, 4.4%−5.8%; I2 = 98.664%; 50 studies; 738,299 patients; P < 0.001; Figure S14).

The “Total” column indicates the total number of COVID-19 patients. Squares are used to indicate the occurrence of VTE, with horizontal lines denoting 95% CI. The size of the squares corresponds to the weight of the data point. Diamonds are used to represent pooled estimates, with the outer points of the diamonds indicating the 95% CI.

Subgroup analysis of outpatients vs. discharged patients

The occurrence of VTE was observed to be significantly lower among non-hospitalized patients when compared to the inpatient population. Patients exhibiting mild symptoms of COVID-19 do not require hospitalization or have been discharged. In this non-hospitalized subgroup, the occurrence of VTE was found to be low; this was particularly evident among outpatients, who demonstrated a nearly indiscernible risk of VTE (0.0%, 95% CI, 0.0%−0.0%; I² = 99.410%; 10 studies; 2,566,194 patients, vs. 0.7%, 95% CI, 0.4%−1.1%; I² = 98.924%; 16 studies; 828,884 patients; P < 0.001; Figure S15). A similar low occurrence of DVT was also observed in outpatient and discharged patients. At the same time, no substantial differences were present (0.0%, 95% CI, 0.0%−0.0%; I² = 99.209%; 10 studies; 1,900,632 patients, vs. 0.3%, 95% CI, 0.1%−0.7%; I² = 98.345%; 11 studies; 601,817 patients; P = 0.159; Figure S16). However, the occurrence of PE was higher in outpatients than in discharged patients (1.0%, 95% CI, 0.6%−1.6%; I² = 99.378%; 18 studies; 1,901,326 patients, vs. 0.6%, 95% CI, 0.3%−0.9%; I² = 82.849%; 15 studies; 53,085 patients; P < 0.001; Figure S17).

ATE

A subsequent analysis of ATE in COVID-19 patients was also conducted. The findings of the study indicated that the pooled occurrence of ATE was 2.6% (95% CI, 1.8%−3.5%, I²=99.924%; 44 studies; 2,884,839 patients; Figure S18), including MI (1.7%, 95% CI, 1.2%−2.4%, I2 = 99.949%; 57 studies; 5,989,089 patients; Figure S19) and stroke (1.2%, 95% CI, 1.0%−1.4%, I2 = 99.589%; 83 studies; 3,806,912 patients; Figure S20).

Subgroup analysis of inpatients

Similarly, the occurrence of ATE in inpatients was found to be analogous to that observed in the overall population. The occurrence of ATE was 3.2% (95% CI, 2.1%−4.5%; I² = 99.545%; 38 studies; 618,234 patients; Figure S21), which included MI (2.0%, 95% CI, 1.7%−2.3%; I² = 99.134%; 43 studies; 3,255,056 patients; Figure S22) and stroke (1.3%, 95% CI, 1.0%−1.6%; I² = 98.298%; 62 studies; 786,368 patients; Figure S23).

Subgroup analysis of ICU vs. ward patients

A comparative analysis of the occurrence of ATE revealed a significantly higher occurrence in the ICU than in the ward (7.3%, 95% CI, 5.1%−9.9%; I2 = 93.156%; 21 studies; 89,934 patients, vs. 2.0%, 95% CI, 1.0%−3.3%; I2 = 96.903%; 15 studies; 439,099 patients; P < 0.001; Figure S24). Moreover, a comparison of MI (3.0%, 95% CI, 2.0%−4.2%; I2 = 98.620%; 22 studies; 144,853 patients, vs. 1.0%, 95% CI, 0.4%−1.8%; I2 = 99.485%; 15 studies; 532,014 patients; P = 0.001; Figure S25) and stroke (2.4%, 95% CI, 1.8%−3.1%; I2 = 97.286%; 31 studies; 157,713 patients, vs. 0.8%, 95% CI, 0.5%−1.3%; I2 = 99.128%; 22 studies; 558,317 patients; P < 0.001; Figure S26) between the two groups also demonstrated a high prevalence of thrombosis in the ICU cohort.

Subgroup analysis of outpatients vs. discharged patients

There is no significant difference found in the occurrence of ATE between outpatients and discharged patients (0.3%, 95% CI, 0.1%−0.4%; I² = 98.918%; 4 studies; 2,126,500 patients, vs. 0.6%, 95% CI, 0.1%−1.4%; I² = 91.856%; 6 studies; 10,566 patients; P = 0.142; Figure S27). However, a lower prevalence of MI was observed among outpatients. (0.2%, 95% CI, 0.1%−0.4%; I² = 98.983%; 3 studies; 1,390,366 patients, vs. 0.7%, 95% CI, 0.5%−0.9%; I² = 97.185%; 12 studies; 1,123,585 patients; P < 0.001; Fig. 3). Additionally, no substantial discrepancy was observed between the two groups with respect to the occurrence of stroke (0.3%, 95% CI, 0.1%−0.7%; I² = 99.767%; 8 studies; 1,658,089 patients, vs. 0.6%, 95% CI, 0.3%−1.0%; I² = 99.561%; 13 studies; 1,123,748 patients; P = 0.263; Figure S28).

Meta-regression analysis and other subgroup analysis

A meta-regression analysis was conducted to investigate the potential contribution of various continuous variables to the observed heterogeneity, as well as their association with the outcomes (Table 1, S4-S8). The occurrence of VTE was found to be significantly higher in the patients with a greater mean age (p = 0.011, Figure S29). Moreover, the incidence of both VTE and PE was found to be notably reduced in the female population (p < 0.05, Figures S30-S31). In relation to the variable proportion of patients receiving prophylactic anticoagulation, a significant and positive correlation was identified with all indicators, with the exception of MI (p < 0.05, Figs. 4, S32-S35). Additional detailed results from the meta-regression analysis (Figures S36-S44) and other subgroup analysis (Tables S9-S18) can be found in eResults in Supplement 1.

Discussion

A substantial number of observational studies have documented the occurrence of thromboembolism in patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection during the epidemic. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the outcomes of thrombosis are classified into two categories: VTE and ATE. The patient population is stratified into categories of inpatients (those admitted to ICU and those managed in wards), as well as outpatients and discharged patients.

A 2021 study on COVID-19 inpatients by Tan et al. reported an occurrence of VTE of 14.7% (56 studies; 16,507 patients), while the occurrence of ATE was 4.0% (19 studies; 8,249 patients), which is significantly higher than the findings reported in this study8. The observed discrepancy can be largely attributed to the observation derived data from studies conducted during the early stages of the pandemic, a period that was accompanied by greater disease severity. Conversely, the present study encompasses the complete duration of the ongoing outbreak of COVID-19, as well as a more extensive patient population. This provides an updated, more realistic and objective representation of thromboembolism occurrence in the specific cohort of COVID-19 patients.

The present study revealed significant differences in the risk of thrombosis among various groups of COVID-19 patients. ICU patients exhibited the highest risk of thrombosis, followed by ward patients, and finally, outpatients and discharged patients. A meta-analysis of outpatient and discharged patients reported the occurrence of VTE and ATE events at rates of 0.28% (6 studies; 85,051 patients) and 0.23% (3 studies; 12,371 patients), respectively, among outpatients. In the case of discharged patients, the occurrence of VTE and ATE events was 1.16% (13 studies; 17,728 patients) and 1.46% (6 studies; 7,816 patients), respectively13. These results are consistent with the findings of our study. However, the study did not include an examination of secondary outcomes of VTE and ATE (e.g., DVT, PE, MI, and stroke), which constitutes a notable strength of the present research.

An important observation from this research is that the occurrence data reported for certain secondary outcomes was higher than that reported for primary outcomes. This discrepancy can be attributed to the conceptualization of secondary outcomes as autonomous indicators, which resulted in their data not being incorporated into the primary outcomes. Furthermore, a significant number of studies reported data for secondary outcomes separately, particularly the PE indicator. This phenomenon is likely attributable to its high occurrence and the increased fatality rate associated with the combination of COVID-19 and PE, which may lead to a heightened focus on the PE indicator14. In the present study, a total of 161 articles were identified that reported on the PE indicators in patients with overall cases of COVID-19. Of these, 86 studies (53.4%) reported PE but did not report VTE. This discrepancy may be a contributing factor to the observed higher prevalence of PE compared to VTE in our final research results.

The findings of the meta-regression analysis suggested that the heterogeneity in the combined results was not exclusively attributable to the clinical environment of COVID-19 patients. Additionally, the analysis indicated that factors such as age, gender, and the proportion of anticoagulant medications used significantly contributed to this heterogeneity. The proportion of thromboprophylaxis within the population merits consideration, as it contrasts with the intended purpose of clinical thromboprophylaxis.

A re-examination and comparison of the data yielded the finding that the aforementioned conclusion should be attributed to the fact that the majority of individuals with a high proportion of drug anticoagulation were COVID-19 inpatients which inherently possesses a higher risk of thrombosis. For instance, in our subgroup analysis comparing VTE occurrence in patients with prophylactic use of anticoagulants exceeding 50% or not, we found that 99.2% (555,835/560,167) of patients in the high anticoagulation group were inpatients (excluding mixed cohorts). Conversely, among patients with anticoagulation rates not exceeding 50%, 1.1% (202,155/1,862,772) were inpatients, 21.4% (398,530/1,862,772) were treated as outpatients, 28.4% (529,584/1,862,772) were discharged, and the remainder were in the mixed cohort. Moreover, the administration of medications for the control of thrombosis in patients with more severe conditions does not appear to yield satisfactory outcomes.

A substantial body of clinical randomized controlled trials (RCTs) has been conducted to evaluate the efficacy of anticoagulants in preventing thrombosis in COVID-19 patients. For instance, an RCT by Piazza et al. in 2023 examined outpatients with COVID-19, utilizing rivaroxaban as an intervention and a placebo as a control. The study’s findings indicated that rivaroxaban did not demonstrate a substantial reduction in VTE and ATE events15. Piazza et al. have proposed explanations for this conclusion, including insufficient sample size and a lower-than-expected incidence of thrombosis. In light of our findings, one possible reason anticoagulant medications are ineffective in outpatient settings is the low incidence of thrombosis in this population. Consequently, these drugs failed to demonstrate a clear advantage over a placebo. Furthermore, the efficacy of the conventional anticoagulant heparin in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 remains controversial in numerous RCTs16–24. Partial RCTs indicated that higher-dose heparin reduces the incidence of thrombosis in comparison with low-dose heparin18,21,22,24. However, other studies suggested that there is no significant difference between the two in terms of reducing thrombotic outcomes16,17,19,20,23. Moreover, when examining single subgroups, including ICU and ward, no consistent conclusion can be drawn. Therefore, the efficacy of anticoagulant interventions for COVID-19 patients requires further investigation in subsequent studies.

Finally, a comparative analysis was conducted between COVID-19 patients and non-COVID-19 patients. A 2022 study by Gao et al.. reported a 10% prevalence of VTE among non-COVID-19 ICU patients (42 studies; 27,344 patients)25. A comparison with our findings indicates that ICU patients with COVID-19 face an elevated risk of VTE. However, there is a paucity of systematic studies that have examined thrombosis incidence in other non-COVID-19 subgroups (ward patients, outpatients, discharged patients). This limitation impedes the capacity to accurately evaluate the disparities in risk among these groups. A 2021 meta-analysis by Mai et al. compared the occurrence of VTE in patients with and without COVID-1926. The analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of VTE between the two groups. However, within the ICU subgroup, COVID-19 patients exhibited a higher risk of VTE.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, 73 studies of inpatient cohorts failed to provide adequate data on each thrombosis indicator in the ICU and ward subgroups. Secondly, the analysis identified 17 articles that featured mixed cohorts of inpatients and outpatients, 6 articles with mixed cohorts of inpatients and discharges, and 4 articles with mixed cohorts of inpatients, outpatients, and discharges. The inability of these studies to distinguish the individual thrombus data for each subgroup limits our assessment of thrombosis rates in subgroup analyses. Thirdly, in the context of data collection on the age and gender of COVID-19 patients, 29 studies reported the mean age, while 37 studies reported the median age. This discrepancy is incongruent with the age data utilized for the analysis (the mean age of COVID-19 patients). Fourthly, the data collection regarding thromboprophylaxis in the COVID-19 population was also incomplete. A total of 90 studies did not indicate whether corresponding thromboprophylaxis measures were implemented or did not clearly report the proportion of thrombosis prevention in the population. These unknown aspects of the research data may introduce bias into the study results. Fifthly, in comparison to the number of studies on VTE, there were significantly fewer studies focusing on ATE. Sixthly, the heterogeneity among the studies was notably significant. Despite conducting subgroup analyses that reduced the heterogeneity of the combined results, a high level of heterogeneity remains. Seventhly, the study did not explicitly address the impact of other baseline thrombosis risk factors, such as smoking status, obesity, prior thrombotic events, and underlying cardiovascular disease, on the findings.

Eighthly, to ensure a more accurate and rigorous integrated analysis while minimizing the introduction of unnecessary bias, studies that specifically focused on certain special populations27–29 or specific types of thrombosis30–32 were excluded. Finally, the present study incorporated exclusively articles published in English, which limits the representativeness of studies from other countries.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the occurrence of VTE and ATE in COVID-19 patients. The inpatient population under investigation exhibited a relatively high risk of thrombosis, particularly within ICU settings. The occurrence of ATE was found to be lower than that of VTE. However, the occurrence of thromboembolism is lower among outpatients and discharged patients. Therefore, it is recommended that appropriate thromboprophylaxis measures be implemented for inpatients, while anticoagulation strategies should be tailored for ICU and ward patients. Conversely, there is an absence of compelling evidence to support the routine administration of prophylactic anticoagulation to outpatients and discharged patients. Additionally, the selection of anticoagulant medications for clinical patients should be approached with caution. The optimal strategy for thromboprophylaxis in COVID-19 patients remains to be determined through further research.
