Vascular implications of non-pulsatile flow extracorporeal life support
Authors: Alex Bartlett, Antonio Gachineiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extracorporeal life support has become a widely integrated tool in treating refractor cardiopulmonary failure. Under normal physiologic conditions, arterial blood flow is inherently pulsatile, and cyclic shear stress plays a pivotal role in maintaining endothelial homeostasis and vascular integrity. In contrast, contemporary ECMO systems generate continuous, non-pulsatile flow, representing a fundamental deviation from native hemodynamics. While non-pulsatile ECMO pumps remain the clinical standard for their reliability and ease of use, their non-physiologic approach to augmenting perfusion is increasingly recognized as a detriment to vascular health. Continuous-flow disrupts numerous signaling and regenerative pathways integral to endothelial proliferation. Through culminative dysregulation of nitric oxide expression, pathological angiogenesis, and accelerated denudation of von Willibrand Factor, patients experience heightened risk of hemorrhagic complications, while thrombotic and embolic events arise through other distinct mechanisms. This review aims to centralize current evidence regarding vascular health to identify causative agents of endothelial insult associated with non-pulsatile VA-ECMO. We further summarize experimental and clinical studies discussing the impact of altered shear forces and their contribution to endothelial dysregulation.

Introduction

Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) is an established rescue therapy for refractory cardiogenic shock and acute cardiopulmonary failure, with expanding utilization driven by circuit reliability and institutional experience [1–5]. Despite these advances, VA-ECMO remains associated with high rates of hemorrhagic and thrombotic complications that substantially contribute to increased mortality and prolonged stays intensive care unit stays [6–11].

While these complications are often attributed to the severity of underlying illness, accumulating evidence suggests that the hemodynamic environment imposed by contemporary VA-ECMO systems may independently contribute to vascular injury. Nearly all modern ECMO platforms rely on centrifugal pumps that generate continuous, non-pulsatile flow, representing a marked departure from physiological arterial pulsatility [12–15]. Pulsatile shear stress is a fundamental regulator of endothelial homeostasis. Its loss disrupts nitric oxide signaling and destabilizes shear-sensitive hemostatic and angiogenic pathways [16–25]. Disruption of these pathways has been strongly associated with endothelial dysfunction impeding vascular repair and promoting pathological angiogenesis.

Mechanistic consequences of sustained non-pulsatile flow have been extensively described in durable left ventricular assist device (LVAD) populations, where loss of pulsatility is linked to acquired von Willebrand syndrome, gastrointestinal angiodysplasia, and other bleeding complications [6, 9, 21, 26]. However, extrapolation of LVAD findings to VA-ECMO remains limited by fundamental differences in patient acuity, oxygenator exposure, cannulation strategy, and duration of support. Beyond loss of pulsatility, VA-ECMO exposes the vasculature to additional mechanical stressors, particularly oxygenator-related shear and abnormal arterial flow patterns that elevate diastolic pressure and create regional shear gradients [12, 27, 28].

These combined hemodynamic perturbations create regional shear stress gradients that may disproportionately affect cerebral and splanchnic vessels, where endothelial turnover is high and autoregulatory mechanisms are particularly sensitive to mechanical disturbance. Despite the clinical relevance of these effects, vascular complications of VA-ECMO are frequently examined in isolation—fragmented across studies focused on bleeding, neurologic injury, vasoplegia, or device mechanics—without an integrated, mechanism-based framework. As a result, device-imposed endothelial injury is often conflated with disease severity, limiting recognition of potentially modifiable contributors to ECMO-associated morbidity.

In this review, we synthesize experimental and clinical evidence examining the vascular consequences of non-pulsatile VA-ECMO. We focus on mechanistic links between altered shear stress and endothelial dysfunction, including: glycocalyx degradation, impaired endothelial repair, vWF cleavage, nitric oxide dysregulation, baroreceptor desensitization, and pathological angiogenesis. By distinguishing device-imposed vascular effects from underlying disease processes, this review highlights non-pulsatile flow exposure as a potentially modifiable contributor to vascular complications during VA-ECMO support.

Literature search and selection

This narrative review was conducted through a targeted literature search to identify experimental, translational, and clinical studies relevant to vascular and endothelial responses to non-pulsatile flow during extracorporeal life support. Electronic databases including Pubmed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar were searched for articles published from inception through January 2026. Search terms included combinations of: “ECMO,” “VA-ECMO,” “non-pulsatile flow,” “pulsatile flow,” “shear stress,” “endothelial dysfunction,” “von Willebrand factor,” “glycocalyx,” “nitric oxide,” “angiogenesis,” and “extracorporeal circulation.” To minimize the risk of omitting relevant publications, all search terms were systematically applied using both abbreviated forms and their full-length equivalents.

Studies were selected based on relevance to the mechanistic and clinical themes of this review, including in-vitro experiments, animal models, and human observational or interventional studies. Additional sources were identified through manual review of reference lists from relevant articles. Given the narrative nature of this review, formal inclusion and exclusion criteria were not predefined; however, priority was given to studies with direct relevance to endothelial physiology and hemodynamic shear associated with non-pulsatile extracorporeal life support.

Overview of VA-ECMO and vailable therapeutics

Clinical context for VA-ECMO

VA-ECMO provides temporary cardiopulmonary support in patients with refractory circulatory collapse unresponsive to conventional medical and temporary mechanical therapies. Patient selection incorporates shock etiology, physiologic reserve, vascular anatomy, anticipated reversibility, and institutional expertise [1–5]. Although clinical indications vary—including cardiogenic shock, extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation, post-cardiotomy shock, massive pulmonary embolism, and acute cardiomyopathies—the hemodynamic exposure imposed by VA-ECMO is largely consistent across these contexts [5, 29].

Irrespective of indication, VA-ECMO subjects the arterial vasculature to sustained non-physiologic flow characterized by elevated diastolic pressure and altered shear stress profiles [12–15]. Consequently, while patient-specific factors influence outcomes, the vascular effects discussed in this review primarily reflect device-imposed flow dynamics rather than indication-specific pathology.

While this review focuses primarily on VA-ECMO due to its direct hemodynamic alterations, related extracorporeal modalities such as veno-venous ECMO (VV-ECMO) and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) share overlapping physiologic effects. Shear-mediated endothelial injury and inflammation are broadly transferable across extracorporeal platforms, though the vascular consequences described in this review are most pronounced and clinically relevant in the context of VA-ECMO.

Arterial return cannulation and regional shear stress

Arterial return during VA-ECMO may be established via central or peripheral cannulation, commonly involving the femoral artery, axillary artery, or ascending aorta. From a vascular perspective, the relevance of cannulation strategy lies not in access logistics, but in regional exposure to abnormal shear forces and pressure gradients [27, 30, 31].

Peripheral cannulation introduces retrograde arterial flow, while central cannulation may generate high-velocity jetting directed toward the aortic arch and supra-aortic vessels. Computational fluid dynamics and Doppler-based assessments demonstrate that these configurations produce localized shear stress heterogeneity exceeding physiologic norms, particularly within the aortic arch and carotid circulation with moderate appreciation in splanchnic vasculature [12, 27, 28]. These effects are amplified by the absence of pulsatile shear relief, resulting in sustained endothelial loading.

Although cannulation strategy may influence regional vulnerability, this review does not attempt to compare cannulation approaches. Rather, we focus on their shared consequence: persistent exposure to non-physiologic shear stress at and distal to the arterial return interface, which may predispose susceptible vascular beds to endothelial injury and dysregulation.

Pump technology and flow characteristics

Contemporary VA-ECMO platforms rely almost exclusively on centrifugal pumps on account of their reliability and relative ease of deployment [12–15]. These systems generate forward flow via continuous impeller rotation, producing relatively stable volumetric output with minimal intrinsic pulsatility.

While advantageous for maintaining systemic perfusion, centrifugal pump technology inherently eliminates cyclical systolic–diastolic shear variation. Adjustments in pump speed alter flow magnitude but do not restore physiologic arterial pulsatility [32]. As a result, increasing flow alone does not reproduce the cyclical shear stress patterns required for endothelial mechanisms. The absence of pulsatile variation leads to sustained, unidirectional shear exposure associated with impaired disruption of shear-sensitive hemostatic pathways. These limitations underscore that flow magnitude and flow quality represent distinct hemodynamic variables, both of which are relevant to vascular function during VA-ECMO support.

Experimental strategies incorporating variable-speed or pulsatile ECMO flow have demonstrated proof of concept in preclinical and in-vitro models; however, clinical translation remains limited by technical complexity, hemodynamic instability, and lack of integrated closed-loop feedback systems [33–37]. Several experimental strategies have been developed to reintroduce pulsatility into extracorporeal circulation, most commonly through modulation of centrifugal pump speed or the addition of external pulsatile flow generators. Variable speed pump algorithms can create pseudo-pulsatile waveforms by rapidly adjusting rotational speed, while newer systems incorporate ECG synchronous control to align flow augmentation with the native cardiac cycle. In parallel, adjunctive devices capable of generating pulsatile flow independent of intrinsic cardiac activity have demonstrated feasibility in preclinical models, including systems producing diastolic augmentation and physiologic waveform profiles.

Early investigations suggest that these approaches may enhance hemodynamic delivery, improve microvascular perfusion and reduce circuit thrombogenicity compared with non-pulsatile support. However, these technologies remained largely investigational, with ongoing challenges related to hemocompatibility, device complexity, and consistent physiologic waveform generation limiting widespread clinical adoption. Consequently, non-pulsatile flow delivery remains the dominant clinical paradigm, forming the mechanical foundation for the endothelial and vascular consequences explored in subsequent sections.

Vascular physiology

The vascular endothelium is a dynamic, metabolically active organ responsible for regulating vascular tone and permeability, effectively mediating inflammatory signaling. Endothelial function is tightly coupled to mechanical forces generated by blood flow along the vessel wall, particularly shear stress magnitude and temporal variation [16, 17, 38]. Under physiologic conditions, cyclic fluctuations in shear stress generated by pulsatile flow activate protective signaling pathways that maintain endothelial integrity and vascular homeostasis.

A principal mediator of flow-dependent endothelial signaling is nitric oxide (NO). NO is synthesized by endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) in response to pulsatile shear stress and contributes to vasodilation, inhibition of platelet aggregation, and suppression of vascular smooth muscle proliferation [17–19]. Loss of cyclic shear stimulation reduces eNOS activity and NO bioavailability, promoting vasoconstriction and leukocyte adhesion, collectively culminating into systemic microvascular dysregulation— a hallmarks of endothelial dysfunction observed during temporary mechanical circulatory support [23, 39].

Von Willebrand factor (vWF) represents another critical shear-sensitive component of endothelial regulation. Under physiologic flow conditions, vWF circulates as high-molecular-weight multimers essential for platelet adhesion at sites of vascular injury [40, 41]. Sustained non-physiologic shear stress promotes mechanical unfolding of vWF multimers, exposing cleavage sites targeted by ADAMTS13 and resulting in functional depletion of high-molecular-weight vWF [19–21]. This shear-mediated process represents a key mechanistic pathway contributing to bleeding complications during non-pulsatile circulatory support and is discussed in further details in subsequent Sects. [6, 9, 21].

Anticoagulation strategies—most commonly unfractionated heparin (UFH) introduce additional biologic influences on endothelial function during VA-ECMO. While necessary to mitigate circuit and systemic thrombosis, UFH has been associated with pro-inflammatory effects though modulation of endothelial activation and leukocyte-endothelial interactions. Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that subsequent systemic inflammation amidst heparin exposure may contribute to degradation of the endothelial glycocalyx, further compromising vascular barrier function and amplifying capillary permeability. When occurring in parallel with shear-mediated endothelial injury, a multifactorial environment exists in which mechanical and pharmacologic factors converge to interrupt vaso-dynamic processes. Accordingly, anticoagulation should be considered a necessary therapeutic, but a potential modifier of endothelial physiology during extracorporeal life support.

Baroreceptor-mediated autonomic regulation is similarly dependent on pulsatile arterial mechanics. Baroreceptors located within the carotid sinus and aortic arch respond to cyclic arterial wall stretch, enabling dynamic modulation of heart rate and vascular tone [16, 42]. Exposure to non-pulsatile flow blunts this cyclic stimulation, leading to sustained baroreceptor loading with subsequent receptor desensitization and impaired autonomic responsiveness. Clinically, this manifests as vasoplegia and diminished capacity to adapt to acute hemodynamic perturbations [23, 43].

Beyond acute signaling pathways, shear stress also regulates endothelial repair and angiogenic balance. Cyclic mechanical strain promotes activation of pro-survival and regenerative pathways, including: PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-mediated signaling, facilitating endothelial turnover and vascular remodeling [44]. In contrast, sustained unidirectional shear suppresses these pathways, shifting endothelial gene expression toward pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic states and impairing vascular repair [39].

The angiopoietin–Tie2 signaling axis plays a central role in maintaining vascular stability under varying hemodynamic conditions. Angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1) promotes endothelial quiescence and barrier integrity through Tie2 activation, whereas angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) destabilizes this interaction during stress states [22, 24]. Sustained non-physiologic shear stress favors Ang-2 expression, particularly in the presence of inflammatory mediators, promoting pathological angiogenesis and vascular fragility [20, 22, 25]. These effects are most evident in vascular beds with high endothelial turnover, such as the gastrointestinal and cerebral microcirculation.

Collectively, these mechanotransductive pathways illustrate how loss of physiologic pulsatility disrupts endothelial signaling at multiple levels, affecting autonomic regulation and structural integrity. In the setting of VA-ECMO, where non-pulsatile flow exposure is combined with critical illness and systemic inflammation, these disruptions provide a mechanistic foundation for the vascular complications explored in subsequent sections.

Vascular implications of non-pulsatile flow

Reliance on non-pulsatile centrifugal pumps during VA-ECMO introduces a fundamental deviation from physiologic hemodynamics. Although sufficient to restore global perfusion, the absence of pulsatility produces sustained non-physiologic shear forces that disrupt endothelial signaling and accelerate vascular injury [16–21]. These effects are magnified by oxygenator-related shear and regional flow disturbances at arterial return sites, contributing to both systemic and regional vascular dysfunction.

Erosion of the glycocalyx and endothelial matrix

The endothelial glycocalyx forms a critical interface between circulating blood and the vascular wall, regulating permeability, mechanotransduction, and antithrombotic function [38, 45]. Sustained non-physiologic shear stress during VA-ECMO promotes degradation of glycocalyx components, including proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans, resulting in increased endothelial permeability and inflammatory activation [38, 45].

Shear-mediated glycocalyx disruption is not uniformly distributed throughout the vasculature. Computational modeling and flow visualization studies demonstrate that arterial return cannulation generates regions of elevated shear stress and flow turbulence, particularly in proximity to the aortic arch and branch vessels [12, 27, 28]. In these regions, loss of pulsatile shear relief further exacerbates endothelial loading, accelerating glycocalyx erosion and endothelial denudation.

Once the glycocalyx is disrupted, endothelial barrier function deteriorates, amplifying inflammation and microvascular instability and predisposing patients to hemorrhage and organ injury [7, 45]. Although critical illness itself contributes to glycocalyx vulnerability, non-pulsatile flow exposure represents an additive mechanical insult that may amplify vascular injury during VA-ECMO support.

Impaired endothelial proliferation and repair

Beyond structural erosion, non-pulsatile flow exposure interferes with endothelial regenerative capacity. Under physiologic conditions, cyclic shear stress stimulates endothelial turnover and repair through activation of PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK, and VEGF-mediated signaling pathways [44]. These pathways promote cell survival and migration, mediating angiogenic remodeling in response to vascular injury.

Sustained unidirectional shear suppresses these regenerative signals, shifting endothelial gene expression toward pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic profiles [39]. As a result, endothelial repair is delayed, and vascular fragility is prolonged. In the setting of VA-ECMO—where ischemia-reperfusion injury and systemic inflammation are common—impaired endothelial regeneration may exacerbate microvascular dysfunction and prolong tissue hypoxia despite restoration of macrohemodynamic parameters [4].

Vascular beds with high baseline turnover appear particularly susceptible, with the cerebral and splanchnic circulations demonstrating early and sustained vulnerability. Blunting of shear-responsive repair mechanisms in these regions may contribute to persistent microvascular injury during prolonged ECMO support.

von Willebrand Factor degradation

Shear-mediated degradation of von Willebrand factor (vWF) represents one of the most clinically significant consequences of non-pulsatile support. vWF is synthesized and released as high-molecular-weight multimers that facilitate platelet adhesion under conditions of pulsatile flow [40, 41]. Sustained non-physiologic shear stress induces mechanical unfolding of vWF multimers, exposing cleavage sites targeted by ADAMTS13 and resulting in depletion of functional vWF [19–21].

This process underlies acquired von Willebrand syndrome (AvWS), which is well documented in patients receiving non-pulsatile, temporary mechanical circulatory support and strongly associated with bleeding complications [6, 9, 21]. In VA-ECMO, vWF degradation is compounded by oxygenator-related shear, anticoagulation requirements, and endothelial injury, creating a multifactorial bleeding diathesis [7, 46].

Notably, hemorrhagic complications persist across diverse anticoagulation strategies, suggesting that mechanical shear—rather than pharmacologic anticoagulation alone—is a primary driver of AvWS during ECMO support [7, 47]. These observations reinforce the importance of flow characteristics as a modifiable determinant of hemostatic balance.

Impaired Nitric Oxide release and vasoplegia

Nitric oxide (NO) is a key regulator of vasodynamics, including: vascular tone, platelet inhibition, and leukocyte adhesion. Under physiologic conditions, pulsatile shear stress stimulates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity, promoting NO release and maintaining vascular homeostasis [17–19]. Non-pulsatile flow exposure blunts this mechanotransductive stimulus, resulting in reduced NO bioavailability [23, 47].

Diminished NO signaling disrupts normal regulation of vascular tone and impairs microvascular perfusion. Rather than causing a uniform change in systemic vascular resistance (SVR), this leads to inconsistent vascular behavior across different vascular beds with reduced ability of vessels to appropriately respond to physiologic stimuli. Clinically, this presents as vasoplegia, based on the impaired responsiveness to vasoconstrictors and associated low/unstable SVR [39, 43, 48].

Patients with pre-existing endothelial dysfunction—including those with cardiogenic shock, sepsis, or post-cardiotomy inflammation—may be particularly vulnerable to NO dysregulation. In these populations, loss of pulsatility may accelerate endothelial injury and exacerbate vascular instability.

Beyond its role in regulating systemic vascular tone, NO is a critical mediator of microvascular perfusion and the endothelial-leukocyte interface. Reduced NO bioavailability during non-pulsatile support contributes to impaired capillary recruitment, increased leukocyte adhesion, and enhances oxidative stress within microcirculation. Inflammatory activation associated with VA-ECMO further amplifies this effect through increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which scavenge NO and exacerbate endothelial dysfunction. This combination of reduced NO synthesis and increased NO degradation promotes a pro-inflammatory, and pro-thrombotic environment, that further impairs microcirculation and oxygen delivery despite restoring global perfusion.

Clinically, these alterations may contribute to the development of vasoplegia and hemodynamic instability in gastric microvasculature. Impaired NO signaling disrupts the balance between vasodilation and vasoconstriction, resulting in heterogenous vascular responses across different organ systems. This may manifest as simultaneous systemic vasodilation and regional hypoperfusion, further complicating hemodynamic management and increasing reliance on vasoactive support.

Baroreceptor dysfunction

Arterial baroreceptors located in the carotid sinus and aortic arch rely on cyclic arterial stretch to regulate autonomic tone and blood pressure homeostasis [16, 42]. Non-pulsatile VA-ECMO reduces pulsatile arterial deformation, resulting in sustained baroreceptor loading and diminished phasic signaling.

Prolonged exposure to elevated diastolic pressure and altered flow dynamics promotes baroreceptor desensitization, impairing reflex sympathetic responses and contributing to autonomic dysregulation [23, 43]. These effects are particularly relevant when arterial return flow is directed toward the aortic arch or supra-aortic vessels, where baroreceptor density is highest.

Baroreceptor dysfunction may further compromise cerebral autoregulation and systemic vascular tone, amplifying risks of neurologic injury and vasoplegia during prolonged ECMO support.

Pathological angiogenesis

Angiogenic balance is tightly regulated by shear-sensitive signaling pathways, including the angiopoietin–Tie2 axis. Angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1) stabilizes endothelial junctions and promotes vascular quiescence, whereas angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) antagonizes Tie2 signaling during stress states [22, 24].

Sustained non-physiologic shear stress favors Ang-2 expression, particularly in inflammatory environments, leading to endothelial destabilization and pathological angiogenesis [20, 22, 25]. The resulting neovessels are often fragile and prone to leakage, contributing to bleeding complications, especially within the gastrointestinal and cerebral microvasculature.

These angiogenic changes, combined with vWF depletion and impaired endothelial repair, create a permissive environment for recurrent hemorrhage during prolonged VA-ECMO support.

Systemic implications of non-pulsatile flow

Vascular consequences of non-pulsatile VA-ECMO extend beyond localized endothelial injury and manifest as systemic complications that significantly influence patient outcomes. Although survival remains the primary endpoint of extracorporeal life support, bleeding and vascular instability represent the most frequent and clinically consequential complications, with thrombotic events emerging in more severe or prolonged courses [6–8]. These events are multifactorial in origin; however, mounting evidence suggests that device-imposed alterations in flow dynamics contribute meaningfully to their development.

Neurological complications

Neurologic injury represents one of the most devastating complications associated with VA-ECMO. Cerebral autoregulation relies on dynamic adjustments in vascular tone in response to fluctuations in arterial pressure and metabolic demand [49–51]. Non-pulsatile support attenuates pulse pressure variability, potentially impairing the cerebral vasculature’s ability to maintain stable perfusion across changing systemic conditions [52].

Loss of pulsatility, combined with elevated diastolic pressures and altered shear profiles, may disrupt endothelial signaling within the cerebral microcirculation, increasing susceptibility to both ischemic and hemorrhagic injury. These effects are compounded by shear-mediated degradation of vWF, anticoagulation requirements, and regional flow disturbances associated with arterial return cannulation [9, 46, 53].

Clinical data from the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) registry and retrospective cohort studies report neurologic complications in approximately 7–16% of adult VA-ECMO patients, with ischemic stroke being the most frequently observed event [10, 54]. The true incidence is likely underestimated, as neurologic assessment is often limited by sedation, neuromuscular blockade, and logistical challenges associated with neuroimaging during extracorporeal life support [1, 53]. Additionally, non-pulsatile alterations to cerebral perfusion may impair cerebral autoregulation by reducing cyclic arterial stretch and disrupting pressure dependent vascular responses. This results in a more passive means of cerebral circulation, where regional blood flow becomes increasingly dependent on systemic pressure rather than intrinsic autoregulatory mechanisms. In this context, fluctuations in perfusion pressure combined with endothelial dysfunction and microvascular fragility may predispose patients to both ischemic and hemorrhagic injury.

Intracranial hemorrhage represents one of the most common and severe neurologic complications during VA-ECMO and is associated with substantial mortality. Non-pulsatile flow dynamics, in combination with anticoagulation and platelet dysfunction, create a permissive environment for cerebral bleeding; particularly in regions with impaired autoregulation and high microvascular vulnerability.

Collectively, these observations suggest that non-pulsatile flow hemodynamics may contribute to cerebral vulnerability during VA-ECMO by impairing autoregulation and promoting endothelial dysfunction, although delineating device-related effects from disease severity remains challenging.

Gastrointestinal hemorrhage

Gastrointestinal bleeding is among the most frequently reported complications of VA-ECMO and represents a significant source of morbidity. Although early mechanistic insights were derived largely from non-pulsatile LVAD populations, similar patterns of bleeding have been increasingly recognized in ECMO cohorts [9, 11].

Gastrointestinal vasculature is uniquely susceptible to hemodynamic dysfunction due to its dense microvascular network and reliance on tightly regulated mucosal perfusion. Despite preservation of global mesenteric blood flow during VA-ECMO, alterations in pulsatility and shear stress may impair microcirculatory distribution, resulting in regional mucosal hypoxia [55, 56]. Such mismatch between macrovascular flow and microvascular perfusion creates a vulnerable environment for which endothelial injury can occur.

Building on previously described shear mediated alterations in von Willebrand factor and angiogenic signaling pathways, chronic exposure to non-pulsatile flow has been associated with development of gastrointestinal angiodysplasia [9, 21, 57]. These lesions are characterized by dilated, fragile submucosal vessels prone to recurrent bleeding and are thought to be derived from dysregulated angiogenesis and impaired vascular remodeling. Further, the gastrointestinal mucosa appears particularly sensitive to these changes in regions with high baseline endothelial turnover [20, 26].

In this context, bleeding risk is not solely a consequence of systemic anticoagulation or coagulopathies but also reflects local microvascular fragility and impaired endothelial repair mechanisms in the gastrointestinal tract. Histopathologic studies in mechanically supported patients have demonstrated submucosal hemorrhage and capillary rarefaction manifesting in ischemic injury, supporting this mechanistic framework [5].

Reported rates of gastrointestinal bleeding during VA-ECMO range from 10 to 25%, with higher incidence observed during prolonged support and in patients receiving dual antithrombotic therapy [56, 58]. Although prophylactic strategies such as anticoagulation modification and endoscopic surveillance have been explored, these approaches do not directly address the underlying hemodynamic drivers of bleeding risk.

Pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular strain

Pulmonary vascular dysfunction represents an additional systemic consequence of non-pulsatile VA-ECMO, with important implications for right ventricular (RV) performance. Pulsatile shear stress within the pulmonary circulation contributes to endothelial NO production and maintenance of low pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) [59]. Loss of pulsatility may therefore favor sustained pulmonary vasoconstriction through impaired NO signaling.

VA-ECMO also alters left atrial and pulmonary venous pressures, particularly in patients with pre-existing left ventricular dysfunction. Inadequate ventricular unloading or elevated systemic vascular resistance may increase pulmonary artery wedge pressure, promoting pulmonary venous congestion and secondary pulmonary hypertension [6]. Over time, sustained non-physiologic flow may contribute to structural remodeling of pulmonary arterioles, including medial hypertrophy and adventitial fibrosis, as demonstrated in experimental models [60, 61].

Clinically, elevated PVR during VA-ECMO increases RV afterload and may precipitate RV dilation causing septal shift and impaired left ventricular filling. These effects are particularly pronounced in patients with pre-existing pulmonary disease, where modest increases in PVR can result in disproportionate hemodynamic compromise [60, 62].

Although VA-ECMO provides effective gas exchange support, preservation of pulmonary perfusion remains important for lung recovery. Microvascular dysregulation and capillary leakage associated with non-pulsatile flow exposure may impair ventilation–perfusion matching and exacerbate pulmonary edema during prolonged support [13].

Anticoagulation, inflammation, and hemorrhagic interplay

Hemorrhagic complications during VA-ECMO are traditionally attributed to systemic anticoagulation regiments—most commonly with UFH. While anticoagulation is essential to prevent circuit thrombosis, emerging evidence suggests that bleeding risk in ECMO is not solely a consequence of pharmacologic anticoagulation, but rather reflects a complex interplay between anticoagulation, inflammation, and subsequent endothelial dysfunction [63]. Exposure of blood to artificial surfaces within the ECMO circuit initiates a systemic inflammatory response characterized by activation of leukocytes, cytokines, and activation of compliment pathways. This inflammatory paradigm promotes endothelial activation, injury, and predisposes vascular barriers to impaired hemostatic regulation.

In parallel, anticoagulation modifies this environment through both anticoagulant and non-anticoagulant effects. UFH has been shown to influence endothelial permeability and leukocyte adhesion, destabilizing the glycocalyx and potentially amplifying endothelial vulnerability amidst ongoing inflammatory stress. Importantly, these pharmacologic effects occur in conjunction with shear-mediated alterations in vWF and platelet function, creating a multifactorial coagulopathy that cannot be fully explained by anticoagulation methods alone. Recent analyses have emphasized that ECMO associated bleeding represents a form of endotheliopathy by which inflammation, mechanical shear, and anticoagulation converge to disrupt vascular integrity [63].

This integrated framework helps to explain why bleeding complications persist despite variability in anticoagulation strategies across institutions. It also underscores the limitations of approaches that focus solely on anticoagulation adjustments without addressing the underlying mechanical and inflammatory drivers of endothelial dysfunction. Accordingly, bleeding during VA-ECMO should be understood as a systems-level phenomenon rather than viewed in isolation as pharmaceutical misuse.

Discussion

The widespread adoption of VA-ECMO has transformed the management of refractory cardiopulmonary failure; however, its clinical success continues to be tempered by a high burden of vascular complications. Hemorrhage, thrombosis, vasoplegia, and neurologic injury remain among the most frequent adverse events and contribute substantially to morbidity and mortality during extracorporeal life support [6–8, 10]. These complications are traditionally attributed to systemic inflammation and anticoagulation requirements. The synthesis presented in this review supports a complementary and underappreciated perspective: the non-pulsatile hemodynamic environment imposed by contemporary VA-ECMO systems represents an independent and biologically relevant contributor to vascular injury.

A unifying theme across experimental, translational, and clinical studies is the loss of pulsatile shear stress—a fundamental regulator of endothelial homeostasis. Physiologic pulsatility provides cyclical mechanical cues necessary for maintaining NO bioavailability, preserving vWF multimer integrity, regulating angiopoietin–Tie2 signaling, and sustaining baroreceptor sensitivity [16–25]. Non-pulsatile support replaces this dynamic environment with sustained, unidirectional shear, favoring maladaptive endothelial responses. These responses include: glycocalyx degradation, impaired endothelial repair, vWF cleavage, autonomic dysregulation, and pathological angiogenesis, forming a convergent mechanistic framework linking non-physiologic flow to the vascular complications commonly observed during VA-ECMO.

Importantly, the vascular stress profile of VA-ECMO is not equivalent to that of durable non-pulsatile left ventricular assist devices. Although LVAD literature provides valuable mechanistic insight into shear-mediated endothelial injury, VA-ECMO introduces additional factors such as: oxygenator-related shear, retrograde arterial flow, localized high-velocity jetting at return cannulation sites, and sustained elevation of diastolic arterial pressure [12, 27, 28]. These factors generate regional shear gradients that may disproportionately affect cerebral and splanchnic vascular beds, where endothelial turnover and autoregulatory sensitivity are high. Recognizing these distinctions is critical to avoid inappropriate generalization while still leveraging transferable physiologic principles from broader temporary mechanical circulatory support experience.

The clinical implications of device-imposed vascular injury are substantial. Acquired von Willebrand syndrome, vasoplegia, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and neurologic complications are consistently reported across VA-ECMO cohorts despite heterogeneity in patient selection and anticoagulation strategies [6–11, 21]. The persistence of these complications across institutions and protocols suggests that pharmacologic optimization alone is insufficient. Rather, the mechanical characteristics of flow delivery itself warrant closer scrutiny as a modifiable contributor to morbidity.

From a translational perspective, these observations support renewed interest in restoring physiologic flow dynamics during extracorporeal life support. Experimental and preclinical investigations of pulsatile or variable-speed ECMO configurations demonstrate proof of concept, but clinical adoption remains limited by technical complexity, hemodynamic instability, and lack of integrated closed-loop feedback systems [33–37]. Moreover, the optimal magnitude, timing, and regional distribution of pulsatility remain undefined, underscoring the need for targeted mechanistic and translational studies.

This review has several limitations. As a narrative synthesis, it integrates heterogeneous data derived from invitro models, animal studies, computational simulations, and clinical observations, many of which are indirect or extrapolated from other temporary mechanical circulatory support platforms. In addition, disentangling device-related vascular effects from disease severity in critically ill populations remains inherently challenging. Nevertheless, the consistency of mechanistic signals across platforms and study designs strengthens the conclusion that non-pulsatile flow exposure contributes meaningfully to endothelial dysfunction during VA-ECMO support.

In summary, while VA-ECMO provides life-saving circulatory support, its reliance on non-pulsatile flow hemodynamics introduces non-physiologic shear forces that compromise vascular integrity. The evidence synthesized in this review supports the concept that many vascular complications traditionally attributed to illness severity are, at least in part, device-imposed and potentially modifiable. Recognizing and addressing this physiologic mismatch represents an important step toward improving the safety and effectiveness of extracorporeal life support.

Conclusion

VA-ECMO provides life-saving circulatory support in patients with refractory cardiopulmonary failure; however, its reliance on non-pulsatile hemodynamics introduces non-physiologic shear forces that compromise vascular integrity. Loss of pulsatile shear disrupts endothelial signaling pathways critical to vascular homeostasis, including nitric oxide bioavailability, von Willebrand factor stability, angiogenic balance, and autonomic regulation. These mechanisms collectively contribute to the hemorrhagic and thrombotic vascular complications commonly observed during VA-ECMO support.

The evidence synthesized in this review supports the concept that many vascular complications traditionally attributed solely to disease severity and anticoagulation strategies are, at least in part, device-imposed and potentially modifiable. While pharmacologic optimization and patient selection remain essential, they do not address the underlying mechanical mismatch between contemporary ECMO flow delivery and physiologic vascular requirements.

Future advances in extracorporeal life support should prioritize strategies that restore physiologic flow dynamics without compromising hemodynamic stability or clinical practicality. Continued mechanistic, translational, and engineering efforts aimed at reintroducing pulsatility represent a rational pathway toward improving vascular outcomes and reducing morbidity associated with VA-ECMO therapy.
