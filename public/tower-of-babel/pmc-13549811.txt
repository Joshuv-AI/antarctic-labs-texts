Developing an interprofessional self-reflection logbook in reproductive health in low-middle income countries: educational design research
Authors: Fatikhu Yatuni Asmara, Marco Versluis, Dian Puspita Dewi, Diana Nur Afifah, Jan-Jaap Reinders, Tri Nur Kristina, Nynke Scherpbier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRemoteness from institutions challenges supervision in interprofessional education (IPE) in a community setting in reproductive health in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC). In this setting, IPE would benefit from an instrument that supports self-reflection. The use of logbooks could provide such an instrument. This study aims to develop and validate a logbook as a self-reflection instrument.MethodsThe authors used an Educational Design Research (EDR) approach to develop a logbook as a self-reflection instrument for an IPE on reproductive health in a community setting in Indonesia. We evaluated the IPE program and conducted a literature review (Phase 1: analysis and exploration). Based on Phase 1, we developed a logbook referred to the Gibbs Reflective Cycle and asked experts, instructors, and students to provide feedback (Phase 2: design and construction). We conducted a pilot test and evaluated the instructors’ and students’ perceptions of using the logbook (Phase 3: evaluation and reflection). The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare the perceptions across disciplines.ResultsThe logbook integrated shared decision-making into IPE competencies and enabled assessment of students’ self-reflection. Both students and instructors reported positive perceptions, with mean scores of 4.35 (standard deviation [SD]=0.41) and 4.30 (SD=0.44), respectively. No significant differences in the perceptions were found across disciplines (p=0.719 and 0.096).ConclusionThe involvement of students, instructors, and experts in an iterative process in EDR developed and validated a logbook as a self-reflection instrument in reproductive health in LMIC.Keywords: Community setting, Educational design research, Interprofessional education, Logbook, Self-reflection

Introduction

Interprofessional collaboration (IPC) is a cooperative approach involving two or more healthcare professionals working together to deliver high-quality, patient-centered care [1]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), IPC is especially important for strengthening communication and relationships between healthcare providers and communities [2]. Implementing IPC in community settings contributes to family and reproductive health by minimizing burnout among health workers and addressing workforce shortages through improved collaboration and leadership [3,4].

IPC requires a range of competencies, including ethics, role clarification, communication, teamwork, patient-centered care, conflict resolution, and collaborative leadership, as outlined in frameworks such as the Interprofessional Education Collaborative (IPEC, 2016) and the Canadian Interprofessional Health Collaborative (CIHC, 2010). Interprofessional education (IPE) supports the development of these competencies and can be implemented in diverse environments, including classrooms, hospitals, and communities [5]. Studies in Indonesia and the Philippines highlight that community-based IPE enhances communication skills and provides meaningful hands-on learning experiences. Another research shows that integrating IPE with community-based education can improve community health outcomes [6].

Developing interprofessional competencies requires self-reflection, enabling students to recognize strengths and weaknesses, prevent problems, plan improvements, and enhance future performance [7]. Evidence shows that self-reflection within IPE helps strengthen collaborative skills, communication, teamwork, and the integration of theory with practice [8,9].

In rural community settings where supervision is limited, instruments that support independent reflection are particularly useful. Unlike IPE in a clinical setting, where instructors guide the students directly, a community setting is often far from the faculty, and the instructors are often not on-site [6]. Logbooks encourage active learning by prompting students to document and reflect on their experiences [7]. Although logbooks have been widely studied in hospital environments [8,10] and within single professions—such as medicine, nursing, dentistry, and pharmacy [10,11], their potential to support interprofessional learning in community settings with limited supervision remains underexplored.

Developing and implementing a logbook in certain contexts, such as in IPE in reproductive health in a community setting, can be challenging because research on logbooks as instruments for self-reflection in these settings is lacking. The logbook must be designed to encourage independent learning and self-reflection by involving all stakeholders and addressing contextual factors gradually during the logbook development and design process [12]. This study used an Educational Design Research (EDR) approach—comprising analysis and exploration, design and construction, and evaluation and reflection—to develop and validate a logbook for self-reflection and to explore the experiences of students and instructors using it [13].

This study aimed to develop and validate a logbook for self reflection in interprofessional learning within a community setting in an LMIC, addressing the gap in previous research that focused only on single profession hospital logbooks. The logbook was guided by the reflective cycle by Gibbs [14], which includes description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, and action plan. Through this cycle, students describe experiences, express thoughts, assess positives and negatives, analyze lessons, propose alternatives, and plan future actions. As an active and continuous model, Gibbs’ cycle helps students identify areas for improvement and supports ongoing reflection [15].

Methods

1. Settings

The logbook was developed within a community based IPE program using a family attachment approach in a rural area far from the faculty. Groups of 4–5 third year Medicine, Nursing, and Dietetics students cared for a pregnant woman and her family through three stages: assessment and planning, intervention, and evaluation. Instructors provided supervision through campus meetings or online when needed, focusing on care and teamwork but not on promoting student self reflection.

2. Design

The study was conducted from October 2023 to February 2024 at the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Diponegoro, Semarang, Indonesia, using an Educational Design Research (EDR) approach to develop a logbook for community based IPE in reproductive health. EDR involves stakeholders in an iterative, step by step process across three phases: analysis and exploration, design and construction, and evaluation and reflection [13]. Experts, students, instructors, and faculty contributed feedback and participated in a pilot test to refine the logbook (as indicated in Fig. 1).

3. Design, participants, data collection, and data analysis

1) Phase 1: analysis and exploration

The analysis phase evaluated the 2023 Interprofessional Education Program at the University. Students, instructors, and faculty were interviewed to explore experiences with self reflection in community settings, including understanding, practice, and tools. The research team prepared interview guidance, and 1st, 3rd, 4th, and 6th authors conducted online interviews and analysis. A deductive thematic approach was applied, with coding, theme identification, review, definition, and summarization. Quotes were coded by participant type, discipline, and number (e.g., S01MMd, I02FNs, T03F). In the exploration phase, two authors (F.Y.A. and T.N.K.) reviewed literature on self reflection, IPE competencies, and feedback.

2) Phase 2: design and construction

The research team developed the first draft of the logbook based on Phase 1 findings. To evaluate clarity and usability, an open ended questionnaire was designed and tested with students and instructors, focusing on IPE activities, competency clarity, reflection question simplicity, and learning relevance. A separate group of experts, instructors, and students reviewed the draft, completing the questionnaire online after receiving materials 2 weeks earlier. Written suggestions were collected. Data were analyzed through thematic analysis, using coding, categorization, and theme refinement in six steps [16]. Guided by Phase 1 coding, the resulting themes shaped the second draft, refined with reflection theories.

3) Phase 3: evaluation and reflection

This phase aimed to pilot test the second draft of the logbook, examining its support for students’ self-reflection and instructors’ feedback. New groups of students and instructors, distinct from earlier phases, were trained to use the logbook. Students reflected on their IPE learning experiences for 2 weeks, recording entries that instructors later reviewed and discussed in person. Both groups then completed a questionnaire designed by the research team. The questionnaire assessed clarity and feasibility, usefulness in identifying challenges, solutions, and plans, its role in facilitating feedback, and time required. Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from total disagreement to total agreement, providing structured insights into the logbook’s effectiveness and usability. When the mean score for each statement exceeds 3, it suggests that participants view the logbook positively. Conversely, if the mean score falls below 3, revisions to the logbook are necessary based on that statement. Additionally, when there are no differences in perception across different disciplines indicate that the logbook is feasible for all instructors and students.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) examined the questionnaire’s structure, with factor loadings above 0.40 deemed adequate and explained variance acceptable at 50% or high at ≥60% [17]. EFA is applied during the early stages of instrument development. It explores the pattern or dimension of the data set and identifies items that do not empirically belong to the intended construct and should be removed from the questionnaire [17]. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with α>0.70 indicating sufficient consistency. Perceptions of logbook development were analyzed by describing participant characteristics and calculating mean scores [18]. Moreover, this study compared participants’ perceptions based on their disciplines, as the various points of view may warrant different actions in the design process or in future use. We wondered whether different experiences with reflection among various disciplines would impact their views on using the logbook for self-reflection [19]. A Shapiro-Wilk test assessed normality; as data were not normally distributed, the Kruskal-Wallis test compared instructors’ and students’ perceptions across disciplines, with significance set at p<0.05. Phase 3 findings provided quantitative evidence to refine the third logbook draft.

4. Research team and reflexivity

The authors formed an interprofessional team consisting of various healthcare professionals from HIC and LMIC. The first author (F.Y.A.) is an educational nurse, the second author (M.V.) is a gynecologist and senior researcher, the third author (D.P.D.) is a medical educationalist, the fourth author (D.N.A.) is a professor in nutrition, the fifth author (J.J.R.) is a work and organizational psychologist and senior researcher, the sixth (T.N.K.) is a professor in medical education, and the seventh author (N.S.) is a general practitioner and a professor of general practice.

5. Ethics statement

The Ethics Board of the Faculty of Medicine at Universitas Diponegoro approved the study (No. 339/EC/KEPK/FK-UNDIP/IX/2023). The participants received comprehensive information regarding the study’s objectives and procedures. Informed consent was obtained from the students, ensuring that their participation in the study would not influence their grades.

Results

The EDR approach supported the development and validation of a logbook as a self-reflection instrument for a reproductive health IPE activity. The three phases were conducted iteratively rather than occurring separately.

1. Phase 1: exploration and analysis

In the evaluation of the IPE program in 2023, we interviewed 21 students, six instructors, and three members of the IPE faculty to identify self-reflection practices in IPE. Our analysis revealed four themes: understanding of self-reflection, implementation of self-reflection, development of instruments for self-reflection, and the instructors’ roles in self-reflection (Supplement 1).

Our literature review identified key elements for developing the logbook, including the role of self-reflection, guiding reflection questions, IPE competency frameworks, and effective feedback practices. Self-reflection and feedback are essential in community-based IPE, helping students evaluate competency development, identify challenges, and plan improvements. Although logbooks are effective tools for reflection, they have mostly been used in clinical and single-profession contexts.

Reviewing IPE competencies, such as IPEC (2011) and CIHC (2010), showed the need for adaptation to reproductive health contexts in LMICs. For example, while collaborative leadership is emphasized in high-income settings, its application differs in LMICs, where medical students may display condescending attitudes toward other professions, and nursing or midwifery students often feel excluded due to longstanding hierarchies.

Based on the 2023 IPE program evaluation and the literature review, we concluded that a logbook is appropriate for supporting meaningful self-reflection in reproductive health IPE in LMICs and should be clear and easy to use.

2. Phase 2: design and construction

The logbook developed based on the findings of Phase 1 consisted of five sections: introduction, IPE competencies, instructions on how to complete the logbook, and self-reflection, with an appendix on feedback and debriefing.

The introduction explained the purpose of the logbook, its learning objectives, and the importance of reflective practice in IPE. The second section presented an overview of IPE competencies, combining elements from the IPEC (2011) and CIHC (2010) frameworks. These included six competencies, namely ethics and values, professional roles and responsibilities, communication, teamwork, role clarification, interprofessional communication, conflict resolution, and collaborative leadership.

The third section summarized instructions for both students and instructors. Students were asked to complete self-reflection for each IPE learning stage—assessment and planning, intervention, and evaluation—while instructors were expected to provide feedback at each stage, either in person or online, to support student improvement.

In the fourth section, students reflected on their learning progress using guiding questions based on the reflective cycle by Gibbs [14] (description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, and action plan). These questions were applied across the IPE competencies.

The last (fifth) part was an appendix on feedback and debriefing. Instructors are informed about a debriefing session script adapted from Promoting Excellent and Reflecting Learning in Simulation (PEARLS). Instructors will use this script as guidance in conducting a debriefing session and giving feedback to students.

The first draft was sent to experts, instructors, and students for feedback. Thematic analysis of responses from two experts, 14 instructors, and 26 students generated four themes: competency descriptions, clarity of instructions, self-reflection guidance, and assessment of student reflection (Table 1).

In Phase 2, stakeholders recommended shared decision-making as an additional competency. The stakeholders believed it was crucial to engage all group members and patients concerning patients' health issues and interventions. Additionally, the phase 2 findings incorporated a self-reflection assessment as part of the logbook. Instructors were advised to score students’ self-reflections rather than only providing feedback. This led us to revisit Phase 1 and to review the literature on shared decision-making and on approaches to assessing self-reflection in logbooks.

Instructors provided narrative feedback and assessed students’ reflections at the end of each learning stage by giving scores from 1 (“cannot be assessed”) to 6 (“very good”). While the scoring scale simplifies the instructor’s task of assessing students, it may lack objectivity. To address this, a rubric was developed to accompany the scores, enhancing their objectivity [20]. The revised version produced in Phase 2 became the second draft of the logbook.

In this phase, the logbook was pilot-tested with 33 students using the second draft of the logbook for self-reflection, and 33 instructors provided feedback on the students’ self-reflection in the logbook. The study analyzed 12 questionnaire statements using EFA. A single factor or dimension emerged, experiences of using a logbook, with factor loadings ranging from 0.547 to 0.832 (>0.40) and an explained variance of 50%, meeting the minimum acceptable threshold. Instructors expressed favorable opinions on the logbook’s efficacy in aiding students’ comprehension of their learning process while acquiring IPE competencies (4.79±0.49). Conversely, students rated the logbook most highly for its role in facilitating the exchange of pertinent feedback (4.64±0.49). Discussion

This study demonstrates the development and validation of the logbook as an instrument for self-reflection in reproductive health IPE in LMICs. Stakeholder input added shared decision-making and assessment on self-reflection. Students and instructors found the logbook useful, with no differences in their perceptions across disciplines. This discussion reflects on the process of the logbook development and the perception of using the logbook as a self-reflection instrument by the instructors and students.

1. Development of the logbook

In this study, the EDR approach provided opportunities to involve a range of stakeholders, including students and instructors, in the development of the logbook. Empowerment of stakeholders resulted in constructive input on which competencies should be included in the logbook and how students could be assessed, and had an overall positive impact on the project [13].

During Phase 2 of the EDR, instructors recommended adding shared decision-making as an IPE competency. This competency was considered important because it helps students understand how to involve pregnant women and their families in healthcare decisions. While shared decision-making in many high-income countries focuses mainly on individuals, in Indonesia, it often requires family involvement. Families play a major role in determining how health problems are managed, and delays in decision-making can contribute to delayed care and maternal deaths [21]. Shared decision-making also improves communication, reduces errors, and strengthens collaboration among health professionals [22]. Therefore, enhancing both patient family communication and interprofessional teamwork is essential to supporting balanced and effective healthcare decisions [23].

2. The validation of the logbook

During the pilot test, as a validation of the logbook, both students and instructors found the logbook to be a well received instrument for self reflection. Notably, there were no differences in perceptions between students and instructors across disciplines. This consistency may be explained by the use of the EDR approach, which involved stakeholders—students and instructors—throughout all phases and iterations of the logbook’s development [13]. This aligns with the principle that a logbook should be acceptable to all users [24]. Their participation enabled them to contribute meaningfully to shaping the logbook, particularly within its specific context. Stakeholders provided valuable input regarding essential content and implementation, including the integration of shared decision making as an IPE competency and its inclusion in the self reflection assessment [25].

The instructors and students had differing perceptions on the role of the logbook in facilitating the learning process to achieve IPE competencies, as well as its function in the giving and receiving of feedback, as identified in statements no. 2 and 10 of the questionnaires. The possible explanation is that logbooks encourage students to reflect on their learning experiences, enabling students to analyze their interprofessional interactions, understand teamwork dynamics, and internalize collaborative skills [26]. Logbook also supports integration of the concept and clinical practice by providing interactive learning with real-world application. This integration facilitates students to acquire interprofessional competencies, particularly interprofessional skills and attitude, as IPE learning strategies are more effective when the students apply the learning strategies actively [27].

From the study findings, the students perceived that the logbook helped them to give and receive feedback. Logbook facilitates students’ self-reflection by capturing their learning process, including feedback given from instructors and peers. Therefore, the feedback will be more personal rather than in a general direction [28]. Moreover, using a logbook in students’ reflection enhances mutual relationship and responsible feedback between instructors and students, then students use feedback to improve their learning performance [19].

Conversely, both instructors and students expressed less favorable perceptions regarding the time spent utilizing a logbook in the students’ learning process. This may be attributed to the fact that many students are not fully aware of the logbook’s role in enhancing their learning performance to achieve competencies. As a result, they feel obligated to complete it primarily due to faculty requirements and the necessity of obtaining a completion score from instructors [29]. Furthermore, the complexity of the guidance questions within the logbook may impede students’ ability to complete it [30]. Consequently, students may perceive that engaging in reflection through a logbook requires additional time. Additionally, the implementation of an electronic logbook is recommended to save time and facilitate easier access to feedback for students [31].

3. Strengths and limitations

This study aimed to develop an IPE logbook for community settings in LMICs, addressing a gap since most previous logbook studies focus on single professions in clinical contexts. Because the study was conducted in a specific Indonesian setting, the findings may not be generalizable; wider implementation is needed to assess its impact on student self reflection. A limitation of the EDR approach is potential bias when researchers also serve as teachers. To minimize this, students gave informed consent and were assured their grades would not be affected. Additionally, study processes and findings were discussed within an independent research team.

Although the questionnaire used in the study was valid and reliable, variability among the statements indicated an explained variance of 50%. Some items focused on the logbook’s function—such as helping students identify learning processes related to IPE competencies—while others emphasized usability, such as ease of completion and user friendliness. This mix suggests the construct may be multifaceted and warrants further exploration to better distinguish functional and practical dimensions, even though the finding of EFA showed only one dimension was identified. Despite these limitations, the statistical analysis demonstrated overall positive perceptions, moderate explained variance, and strong internal consistency, confirming the logbook’s psychometric robustness for assessing interprofessional self reflection. This robustness was strengthened by stakeholder involvement and their readiness to implement the logbook in practice. However, we would recommend redeveloping the questionnaire to achieve a higher level of explained variance, especially when using it to assess students’ and instructors’ perceptions of employing a logbook as a self-reflection instrument more broadly.

4. Suggestions

To improve logbook quality, regular training for all users and ongoing assessment of logbook use are recommended to strengthen students’ self assessment, reflection skills, and IPE competencies. A mixed methods approach is suggested for future research to better understand logbook implementation, particularly in students’ competencies and patients’ outcomes. The study could compare the development of IPE competencies between two groups: one using a logbook as a self-reflection tool and the other not using it. These competencies could be based on self-reported results by students or assessments by teachers or peers. This could be complemented by qualitatively identifying students’ experiences with logbooks in relation to their competency development. A challenge in educational research in general is to actually measure a change in collaborative behavior in the workplace (on the ‘does’ level), let alone finding a direct relation between education and patient outcomes.

This study reports only the development of the logbook; therefore, implementation results should be presented separately, as reporting full EDR outcomes in one article is challenging [15].

5. Conclusion

This study showcases the development and validation of a logbook for self-reflection using an EDR approach in the context of reproductive health in LMIC. By involving stakeholders in an iterative process, constructive contributions were made, including shared decision-making as a competency and assessment of self-reflection. Further research is recommended to understand the implementation of the logbook and its evaluation.
