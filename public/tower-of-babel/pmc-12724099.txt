The psychometric properties of the International Trauma Questionnaire – Child and Adolescent version in Turkish culture
Authors: Fatmanur Çimen, İsmail Seçer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: The International Trauma Questionnaire – Child and Adolescent Version (ITQ-CA) is an assessment tool designed to evaluate ICD-11 defined post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and Complex PTSD (CPTSD) symptoms in children and adolescents and is widely utilized on an international scale.Objective: In this study, the psychometric properties of the ITQ-CA in the Turkish cultural context were evaluated.Method: The research sample consisted of 204 children and adolescents aged 7–17 who experienced the earthquakes centred in Kahramanmaraş, Turkey, in February 6, 2023. To determine the construct validity of the scale, four different models were established based on ICD-11, and their structures were analysed through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).Results: The CFA results indicated that among the models tested, only the first-order six-factor model demonstrated an acceptable fit. Although it was determined to exhibit better model fit compared to other models, high correlations between subscales and inappropriate factor loadings in items (Heywood cases) were observed. This indicates a potential issue of model misspecification, suggesting that the obtained results should be interpreted with caution.Conclusions: This study provides evidence of the validity and reliability of the ITQ-CA, a widely used international assessment tool, in the Turkish cultural context. However, consistent with the literature, Heywood cases were observed in the models tested. These results indicate that models used to examine trauma symptoms with the ITQ should be carefully evaluated.KEYWORDS: Trauma, complex post-traumatic stress disorder, Heywood cases, ITQ-CA

Introduction

Trauma is defined as an individual’s encounter with a stressful experience that disrupts their life, and one of the most common and severe consequences of trauma exposure is post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (The National Child Traumatic Stress Network, 2024). Although PTSD does not develop in every individual exposed to trauma, approximately 37% of those exposed are reported to meet the criteria for PTSD (Santiago et al., 2013). PTSD in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11; World Health Organization, 2018) is defined by three core symptom clusters – re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal – while Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (CPTSD) extends beyond these symptoms to include disturbances in self-organization (DSO), encompassing affective dysregulation, negative self-concept, and interpersonal difficulties (Cloitre et al., 2018). ICD-11, provides a streamlined framework for the definition and categorization of stress-related disorders, distinguishing between PTSD and CPTSD.

Research in the literature indicates that the relationship between the nature of a traumatic event (e.g. single or repeated, interpersonal or non-interpersonal, intentional or unintentional), the traumatic responses it triggers, and the resulting PTSD is not linear and can follow a complex trajectory at the individual patient level (Seedat, 2025). Exposure to natural disasters, as one form of traumatic experience, can have profound effects on children and adolescents, often resulting in the development of trauma-related symptoms (Hiller et al., 2016; Lai et al., 2013; Rubens et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2017). In studies conducted with children and adolescents exposed to earthquakes, both PTSD and CPTSD symptoms have been examined; however, the literature on CPTSD remains relatively limited.

According to a study by Rossi et al. (2022), which used ICD-11 criteria, the probable prevalence of PTSD and CPTSD among adolescents exposed to natural disasters was reported as 9.11% and 4.06%, respectively. In the earthquakes centred in Kahramanmaraş, Türkiye, on February 6, 2023, Yakşi and Eroğlu (2024) found that the probable prevalence of PTSD was 80% five months after the disaster. Similarly, in a study conducted by Koçak et al. (2024), it was reported that more than half of the children experienced probable severe trauma four months after the disaster.

PTSD frequently coexists with other mental health disorders, exhibiting high comorbidity rates. Studies have reported that PTSD is commonly associated with depression and anxiety (Murphy et al., 2021), suicidal tendencies (Karatzias et al., 2019), and sleep disorders (Herring et al., 2024). In the literature, it has been reported that CPTSD symptoms, particularly those within DSO dimension – such as negative self-concept and relational disturbances – overlap significantly with depression (Fung et al., 2022; Vang et al., 2021). Accordingly, recent studies have increasingly examined the co-occurrence rates of CPTSD and depression (Hyland et al., 2018; Karatzias et al., 2019). In a study screening depressive patients, Fung et al. (2022) found that 57% of individuals diagnosed with depression might also meet the criteria for CPTSD. Additionally, other research has reported that 67% to 89% of individuals with CPTSD symptoms exhibit depressive symptoms (Hyland et al., 2021; Karatzias et al., 2019). A study conducted by Hyland et al. (2018) demonstrated that the DSO dimension of CPTSD is closely associated with depressive symptoms, whereas the PTSD structure is more strongly related to generalized anxiety disorder (GAD).

Given the profound impact of PTSD on individuals’ psychological, social, and physiological functioning, accurately assessing PTSD symptoms is essential for both diagnosis and intervention. Therefore, there is a critical need for valid and reliable measurement tools that can comprehensively evaluate PTSD symptoms in diverse populations and cultural contexts. While numerous instruments have been developed for PTSD diagnosis, it is crucial to prioritize those that have been updated or designed in accordance with recent revisions in diagnostic classifications (Cohen, 2010; Dowdy-Hazlett et al., 2021).

Measurement of trauma symptoms

A review of the literature reveals that the most recently developed assessment tool for measuring CPTSD is the ITQ, created by Cloitre et al. (2018). Initially developed as a 28-item scale (Cloitre, 2016), the ITQ was later revised to its final 12-item version (Cloitre et al., 2018). Designed as an internationally applicable instrument, the ITQ has been translated into and utilized in 30 different languages. Studies have supported the scale’s latent structure, confirming both its six-factor model and its higher-order two-factor model, which encapsulates PTSD and DSO alongside the broader CPTSD symptom cluster (Redican et al., 2021). ITQ-CA was developed as a parallel measure to the adult ITQ for assessing PTSD symptoms in children and adolescents aged 7–17 (Cloitre et al., 2018). The 12-item scale has been adapted and validated across multiple cultures (Ho et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021; Parhoon et al., 2024).

To facilitate the assessment of ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD in children and adolescents, the International Trauma Questionnaire – Child and Adolescent version (ITQ-CA) was developed. The ITQ-CA is a self-report tool designed to capture both PTSD and DSO symptoms in youth, providing a culturally adaptable and psychometrically sound measure (Murphy et al., 2021; Vang et al., 2021). Despite its increasing use internationally, research examining the validity and reliability of the ITQ-CA in Turkish children and adolescents remains limited. However, in the Turkish cultural context, the assessment instruments used for trauma screening among children and adolescents are based on the DSM framework, and there are no valid and reliable instruments that assess trauma symptoms within the ICD framework. Although the ITQ-CA has been translated into Turkish, its validity and reliability have not yet been examined in the Turkish context. This situation makes it impossible to evaluate CPTSD symptoms in research or clinically oriented studies involving children and adolescents. Considering the prevalence of trauma and CPTSD symptoms among children and adolescents (Chiu et al., 2023; Elliott et al., 2021; Stahlschmidt et al., 2020; Tamir et al., 2025), it appears that there is a need for ICD-based assessment instruments within the Turkish cultural context.

Objectives of the study

The primary objective of the present study is to evaluate the psychometric properties of ITQ-CA in a Turkish sample. Specifically, the study examined its factorial validity, internal consistency, and convergent and discriminant validity using measures of depression and anxiety (RCADS).

In this study, it was first hypothesized that both the first-order six-factor correlated model and the second-order two-factor model of the ITQ-CA, reflecting the ICD-11 conceptualization of PTSD and CPTSD, would demonstrate an acceptable model fit. In addition, as suggested by previous research, it was hypothesized that PTSD symptoms would show stronger associations with anxiety, whereas DSO symptoms would be more closely related to depression (Fung et al., 2022; Hyland et al., 2018; Karatzias et al., 2019; Murphy et al., 2021; Vang et al., 2021). These hypotheses guided the confirmatory factor analysis conducted in the study.

Method

ITQ turkish translation

A review of the literature indicates that while ITQ-CA has been translated into Turkish, no study has been identified that comprehensively details the translation and adaptation process or conducts validity and reliability analyses for the Turkish form (International Trauma Consortium, http://www.traumameasuresglobal.com/). Consequently, within the scope of this research, the existing Turkish translation of the ITQ-CA was re-evaluated, and its suitability for the Turkish cultural context was assessed. The Turkish form of the scale was reviewed by multiple language experts proficient in both Turkish and English and was re-translated into English. The re-translated English form was compared with the original form to ensure its appropriateness. It was determined that the translated form exhibited parallelism with the original form.

Participants & prosedure

The sample for this study consists of children aged 7–17 who experiencing the February 6, 2023 earthquakes centred in Kahramanmaraş, which have been described as the disaster of the century in Turkey. This study involved a specific sample consisting of children and adolescents who had been exposed to a traumatic event. Therefore, a power analysis was not conducted prior to the analyses. Although the required sample size for Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) may vary depending on factors such as study design, level of missing data, scaling, estimation method, and model complexity, a sample size greater than 200 is generally considered adequate (Boateng et al., 2018; Mol et al., 2020).

Data collection was conducted in Hatay, one of the areas that suffered the most severe destruction. In order to collect data, firstly, after obtaining the necessary permissions, school administrators in the Hatay region were contacted and after briefly introducing the purpose of the research. In line with the purpose of the research, data collection was carried out in the classes determined with the guidance of school administrators. Parents of these pupils were contacted, and data were not collected from the students if the parents did not give permission. The data were collected online via Google Forms. In the introduction of the form, the participants were informed that participation in the research is voluntary and that the information collected within the scope of the research will ensure confidentiality and reliability. In addition to the measurement tools, a demographic information form (including gender, age, etc.) was included. Since each question was marked as mandatory, no missing data were recorded. Of the 204 children and adolescents in the study, 110 (53.9%) were female and 94 (46.1%) were male, with a mean age of 15.20 years (SD = 2.36).

Measures

International Trauma Questionnaire – Child and Adolescent Version (ITQ-CA): The scale was developed by Cloitre et al. (2018) to assess post-traumatic symptoms in children and adolescents, based on the ICD-11 framework for PTSD and CPTSD. The scale consists of 12 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale [0: Never, 4: Almost always]. It evaluates PTSD through six items under the subdimensions of re-experiencing, avoidance, and sense of threat. In addition, it assesses DSO through six items related to affective dysregulation, negative self-concept, and relational disturbances. By summing the PTSD and DSO dimensions, a 12-item measure of CPTSD is obtained. The Turkish version of the scale was accessed from https://www.traumameasuresglobal.com/itqca.

Revised Children’s Anxiety and Depression Scale (RCADS-25): The scale developed by Ebesutani et al. (2012) to assess depression and anxiety in children and adolescents consists of 25 items and two subdimensions, using a 4-point Likert scale [0: Never, 3: Always]. The Turkish adaptation was conducted by Seçer and Şimşek (2015), who reported high reliability (α = .92), confirming its suitability for the Turkish cultural context. In the present study, the scale also demonstrated high reliability (α = .93).

Statistical analysis

CFA was performed to examine the construct validity of the ITQ-CA. During the CFA stage, the fit indices of four models were evaluated. Model 1 is a unidimensional model in which all items load onto a single latent factor. Model 2 is a correlated model encompassing six correlated subdimensions. Model 3 is a higher-order CPTSD model, in which the six subdimensions from Model 2 load onto a second-order CPTSD factor. Model 4 is a correlated two-factor model, explaining the covariance between the second-order PTSD and DSO factors. Model 4 aligns most closely with the ICD-11 classification of PTSD and CPTSD (Kazlauskas et al., 2020).

For model fit evaluation in CFA, χ²/df ≤ 5 was considered acceptable, while ≤ 3 indicated good model fit. For CFI and TLI, values ≥ .90 were considered acceptable, and > .95 indicated good fit. For RMSEA and SRMR, values ≤ .08 were acceptable, and ≤ .06 indicated good fit (Collier, 2020). CFA analyses were performed using AMOS 21. To determine the reliability of the scale, McDonald’s omega (ω) was calculated, as it provides a robust estimate of internal consistency. Values above .70 were considered indicative of high reliability (Nunnally, 1978). In addition to examining factorial validity and internal consistency, the study evaluated the convergent and discriminant validity of the ITQ-CA by analysing correlations with depression and anxiety symptoms measured by the RCADS. Values between .70 and .90 were considered strong, values between .40 and .60 moderate, and values between .10 and .30 weak (Dancey & Reidy, 2011).

Results

Before conducting data analysis, the suitability of the data for analysis was first evaluated. In this regard, the dataset was examined for skewness, kurtosis, and the presence of outliers. Regarding the study measures, RCADS-25 scores had a mean of 25.73 (SD = 15.33), and ITQ-CA scores had a mean of 17.78 (SD = 10.50). An examination of the skewness and kurtosis values indicates that they fall within the normal range (RCADS-25: skewness = 0.67, kurtosis = 0.39; ITQ-CA: skewness = 0.25, kurtosis = −0.44), suggesting that the data exhibit a normal distribution. To further investigate the source of the Heywood case, modification indices were examined. The analysis revealed that Item 6 (Being jumpy) had a high modification index, suggesting that its misfit might be contributing to the estimation problem. To resolve this issue, and in line with previous literature, the residual variance of Item 6 (Being jumpy) was constrained to a small positive value (.01). When the analyses were repeated, it was determined that the revised model yielded acceptable fit indices (χ²/df (101.658/40): 2.54, CFI: .94, IFI: .94, SRMR: .04, RMSEA: .08, AIC: 7129.66).

In Models 3 and 4, all second-order factor loadings were estimated as 1.00, indicating potential identification problems due to the high correlations among first-order factors. This result suggests that the second-order models may not adequately represent the underlying data structure and should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

In today’s world, where trauma is a common experience, it is essential to provide assessment tools aligned with updated diagnostic revisions to accurately evaluate and diagnose PTSD symptoms, especially in children and adolescents, who are among the most vulnerable to trauma’s effects. In this regard, ITQ serves as a valid and reliable internationally recognized tool that assesses PTSD, DSO, and CPTSD symptoms according to the latest ICD-11 criteria. While the Turkish adaptation of the adult version of this scale has been completed, there remains a lack of instruments designed to evaluate PTSD, DSO, and CPTSD specifically in children and adolescents. Thus, this study examined the psychometric properties of the Turkish adaptation of ITQ-CA within the Turkish cultural context.

Among the models developed based on ICD-11, only the first-order six-factor Model 2 demonstrated an acceptable fit. Consistently with this finding, studies examining the psychometric properties of the ITQ-CA in children and adolescents across different cultures have also reported that the first-order six-factor model exhibits the best fit indices (Ben-Ezra et al., 2018; Li et al., 2021). However, in a study conducted by Parhoon et al. (2024) on children and adolescents, the fit indices of both a single-factor model and a two-factor model were examined, with findings indicating that the two-factor model provided a better fit for the data structure. Studies examining the psychometric properties of the ITQ-CA in children and adolescents across different cultures have also reported findings consistent with this study, indicating that the first-order six-factor model has the best fit indices (Ben-Ezra et al., 2018; Li et al., 2021).

As reported in numerous studies, the ITQ measurement tool has two established structural models, both of which generally demonstrate good fit with the data. However, only the second-order two-factor model of the ITQ, grounded in ICD-11, includes distinct PTSD and DSO factors. In contrast, the correlated first-order six-factor model does not specify a second-order structure and therefore does not differentiate between PTSD and DSO factors. For this reason, the two-factor second-order model is considered more appropriate for clinical population studies, while the six-factor first-order correlated model is typically preferred in general population research (Peraud et al., 2022). Nevertheless, some studies have also reported that the two-factor second-order model offers the best fit even in general population samples (Cyr et al., 2022; Redican et al., 2021).

Furthermore, studies examining the psychometric properties of the ITQ-CA in children and adolescents traumatized by different traumatic experiences have reported that the two-factor higher-order model of the ITQ-CA demonstrates better fit indices (Haselgruber et al., 2020; Ho et al., 2022; Løkkegaard et al., 2023). When considering these findings collectively, it appears that a greater number of studies support the first-order six-factor model and the two-factor higher-order model of the ITQ-CA as having good fit indices. The variations observed in the literature may be explained by differences in the traumatic events experienced by the study samples. PTSD is known to have a heterogeneous structure, with symptom presentation and manifestation differing across individuals. Therefore, this variability may influence the findings (Li et al., 2021). This study, in contrast, was conducted exclusively with children and adolescents were exposed to a potentially traumatic event by earthquakes and does not include a clinical population.

An important finding to note in this study is that, despite the acceptable fit of the first-order six-factor Model 2, the model revealed some issues, including improper factor loadings and a latent variable correlation exceeding 1. A review of the literature shows that similar Heywood cases have been reported in studies using ITQ, such as that by Karstoft et al. (2025). This calls for cautious interpretation of findings, as Heywood cases may indicate model misspecification. However, it is important to understand that a Heywood case does not necessarily imply a misspecified model; factors such as small sample size, very low factor loadings, or multicollinearity among factors can also lead to such anomalies (Farooq, 2022; Hinde et al., 2025).

In this study, the relatively small sample size, due to the focus on a specific group of children and adolescents exposed to trauma, may have contributed to this issue. Additionally, irregularities in parameter estimates can stem from various causes, including non-normality, outliers, or poorly defined factors. In this sample, no evidence of non-normality or outliers was found. Nevertheless, each latent factor in Model 2 comprised only two items, which increases the likelihood of unstable factor solutions that may not replicate well (Chen et al., 2001). Moreover, if the covariances between certain items across different factors are not properly modelled, this can inflate item loadings. In other words, the model may show inflated factor loadings. While factor loadings above 1 are not inherently problematic, this study addressed the issue by constraining the error variance of the problematic item to a small positive value, which resolved the Heywood case. The resulting model showed no factor loadings exceeding 1, indicating the robustness of the factor structure defined.

Notably, item 6 (Being jumpy) shared substantial variance specifically with item 5 (Being overly careful (checking to see who is around me)), item 9 (Feeling like a failure) with item 11 (Not feeling close to other people), and item 12 (Having a hard time staying close to other people) with its corresponding paired item, all of which belong to the same subscales. This pattern, indicated by the modification indices, suggests that the observed inflated factor loadings may be driven by these high item-level correlations rather than widespread multicollinearity across the model. These findings highlight the importance of carefully examining item-level relationships in addition to the overall factor structure to ensure a robust model estimation.

Another important finding to consider in the study is that, in the alternative second-order models (Model 3 and Model 4), all second-order factor loadings were estimated to be exactly 1.00. This outcome suggests a potential model identification or specification issue rather than a true representation of the data structure. In a study conducted by Sele et al. (2020) and examining the psychometric structure of the ITQ, it was reported that having only two indicators representing each of the six first-order factors and two correlated second-order factors posed a risk of overparameterization. In another research sampling soldiers and veterans and carried out by Karstoft et al. (2025), Model 4 of the ITQ was employed, and the second-order factor loadings were found to range from .72 to 1.19. Similarly, in their examination of the ITQ-CA's psychometric structure, Hinde et al. (2025) indicated that the model may be susceptible to multicollinearity or overfitting.

Another finding obtained from this study is that the PTSD and DSO dimensions, which constitute PTSD and CPTSD, showed strong and significant relationships with depression and anxiety. The aim of examining these correlations was to evaluate the convergent validity of the ITQ-CA in children and adolescents exposed to trauma. Specifically, DSO showed a higher correlation with depression compared to PTSD, whereas both PTSD and DSO were strongly correlated with anxiety. This pattern partially deviates from the initial hypothesis but still supports the convergent validity of the ITQ-CA. These findings highlight the interconnected nature of post-traumatic stress symptoms and general emotional distress in youth, emphasizing the importance of considering comorbidity when interpreting ITQ-CA scores. Previous studies using the adult version of the ITQ have similarly suggested that intense emotional distress may serve as an appropriate indicator of re-experiencing (He et al., 2024; Karatzias et al., 2016).

In the literature, depression has been suggested to be both a cause (An et al., 2019) and a consequence (Arbisi et al., 2012) of PTSD, indicating a reciprocal relationship between the two disorders (Geng et al., 2019). Depression contributes to relational disturbances through negative self-concept, which in turn activates the sense of threat. Accordingly, depressive symptoms such as feelings of worthlessness are known to closely overlap with negative self-cognition, a key component of DSO. This indicates a significant overlap between DSO and depressive symptoms (Lin et al., 2025). Haselgruber et al. (2020) found a strong association between CPTSD’s DSO symptoms and depression, while Gilbar (2020) demonstrated that affective dysregulation within DSO symptoms is closely related to depressive symptoms. These findings suggest that CPTSD and depression may have a reciprocal influence on each other (Geng et al., 2019). Consistently, Yousefi and Abdoli (2024) reported that individuals with high PTSD and DSO scores exhibited increased levels of anxiety, depression, and difficulties in emotion regulation. In line with this, the literature suggests that the shared dimension of PTSD and anxiety is the fear response, which manifests as an alarm reaction to real or perceived threats. Accordingly, negative and intrusive memories activate intense emotions, leading to a heightened sense of threat. This, in turn, results in maladaptive coping strategies such as hyperarousal and fear-specific avoidance responses (Holmes et al., 2016; Zoellner et al., 2014).

Strengths and limitations

The findings of this study indicate that the ITQ-CA is a valid and reliable assessment tool for use within the Turkish cultural context. To the best of the researchers’ knowledge, this study provides the first instrument for diagnosing and evaluating current PTSD and CPTSD symptoms in children and adolescents in Turkish culture. Moreover, the practical implications of these findings are considerable. The availability of a culturally adapted, valid, and reliable tool like the ITQ-CA can greatly enhance early identification of PTSD and CPTSD symptoms among earthquake-affected youth, facilitating timely referrals to mental health services and supporting tailored intervention plans that address both core PTSD symptoms and disturbances in self-organization characteristic of CPTSD.

While this study has notable strengths, it also has certain limitations. The primary limitation is its cross-sectional design, which restricts understanding of how the variables interact over time. Therefore, conducting longitudinal studies is important to better clarify the relationships between anxiety, depression, and trauma symptoms. Another significant limitation is that the sample consisted solely of children and adolescents exposed to a natural disaster, specifically an earthquake. Future research should thus examine the validity and reliability of this measurement tool in children and adolescents who have experienced other types of traumatic events. Additionally, since the study was conducted with a specific group exposed to potentially traumatic events, the sample size was relatively small. Further studies with larger samples are needed to more robustly validate the scale.

Although only one of the four models tested in this study demonstrated acceptable fit indices, the identification of this model provides support for the ITQ-CA's factorial validity. The presence of Heywood cases in some models highlights the importance of careful model evaluation and suggests that certain items, such as Item 6 (Being jumpy), may require refinement or potential revision in future adaptations. This also raises the possibility that additional items could enhance the scale’s psychometric properties, which should be explored in future research involving both clinical and general population samples. Finally, although significant relationships between depression and anxiety were identified, more comprehensive investigations are warranted.
