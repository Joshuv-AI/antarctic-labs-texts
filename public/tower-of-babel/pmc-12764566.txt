Probiotics intervention reduces oxidative stress–driven myocardial injury
Authors: Olia Hamzeh, Sahar Rostami-Mansoor, Farideh Feizi, Nasrin Nosratiyan, Mehrdad Halaji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aging is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, characterized by progressive structural and functional decline in the heart. In addition to oxidative stress, which is a key player in aging, recent research suggests that the gut-heart axis may contribute to age-related cardiac dysfunction, with probiotics emerging as a potential therapeutic strategy. While probiotics have been extensively studied for their benefits in metabolic, neurological, and immune disorders, their impact on cardiac aging remains largely unexplored. This study investigates the cardioprotective effects of Lacticaseibacillus casei and Bifidobacterium breve in a D-galactose-induced aging model. Male BALB/c mice were subcutaneously injected with D-galactose (100 mg/kg) to induce aging and subsequently received oral supplementation with L. casei (2 × 109 CFU/mL), B. breve (2 × 109 CFU/mL), or their combination (2 × 109 CFU/mL) daily for eight weeks. The results revealed that probiotics mitigated myocardial damage, including inflammation, congestion, cardiomyocyte degeneration, edema, and fibrosis. Furthermore, probiotics enhanced Sirt1 gene expression, increased glutathione (GSH) levels, and reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, highlighting their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties in ameliorating cardiac aging. We found that L. casei demonstrated superior performance compared to B. breve, and the combination therapy outperformed the single-strain treatments. Overall, these findings suggest that probiotic interventions may serve as a promising strategy for combating oxidative stress and age-related cardiac deterioration.

Introduction

On the basis of the latest report of the World Health Organization (WHO), the world’s aging population is increasing, which is causing more global age-related diseases1. Aging is the dominant independent risk factor for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), which threaten the lives of the majority of older individuals2. Therefore, focusing on CVDs and finding new approaches for preventing and impeding heart failure (HF) are vital for increasing the lifespan of elderly individuals and reducing age-related issues.

Although the mechanisms of aging are not fully understood, chronic oxidative stress and an imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants are thought to be major mechanisms of cardiac aging3. Compared with other tissues, the heart produces more reactive oxygen species (ROS) because it consumes more basal oxygen to increase energy and maintain cardiomyocyte functions4,5. Impaired heart functions, including cardiac systolic and diastolic dysfunctions, heart structure remodeling, and cellular and molecular abnormalities (e.g., myocardial cell apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and calcium mishandling), occur during the aging process6.

Treatment with D-galactose (D-gal), a reduced sugar, causes oxidative stress in heart tissues by unbalancing the oxidant/antioxidant system and causing cardiomyocyte damage, resulting in a heart aging model in animals7. Exposure to D-gal also decreases Sirtuin1 (Sirt1) expression, a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-dependent histone deacetylase closely related to lifespan extension. Sirt1 acts as a cardioprotective molecule that protects the heart from aging and ischemia/reperfusion injury, resists hypertrophic and oxidative stresses, inhibits cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and regulates cardiac energy metabolism8,9. Studies have demonstrated that enhancing Sirt1 improves apoptotic signaling and cardiac function in senescent hearts10.

Gut dysbiosis, a hallmark of aging, can accelerate aging process by contributing to increasing oxidative stress and systemic inflammation11. The gut microbiome generates bioactive metabolites such as those of endocrine organs that can directly or indirectly affect host physiology12. Previous studies have shown that the levels of gut microbiome-derived metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), primary and secondary bile acids (BAs), trimethylamine N-oxide, and phenylacetylglutamine, play critical roles in cardiac health and disease13,14. Hence, targeting the gut microbiome has been a novel strategy for managing CVD15. Lowering gut dysbiosis after a high-fiber diet and acetate supplementation decreased blood pressure, normalized cardiac hypertrophy, and improved cardiac function in an experimental model of hypertension-induced HF16.

Previous studies showed that probiotic/s supplementation may help alleviate cardiac dysfunction by modulating gut hemostasis. For example, oral probiotic Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus consumption ameliorates left ventricular infarct size and improves cardiac function in rat models of HF17. In patients with HF, the administration of Saccharomyces boulardii for 3 months also reduced the levels of biochemical and inflammatory biomarkers and improved cardiac systolic function compared with those in the control group18. Bifidobacteria and Lactobacillus are among the most abundant and beneficial genera in the healthy human gut microbiome, playing key roles in maintaining gut homeostasis, supporting immune function, and contributing to overall physiological health19,20. These genera are also widely used in the fermentation industry due to their probiotic properties and ability to produce health-promoting metabolites21,22. Lacticaseibacillus casei have demonstrated robust antioxidant activity thus may show cardioprotective effects23,24.

Considering this background, the present study was designed to test the possible therapeutic effects of a combination therapy with probiotics L. casei and Bifidobacterium breve on myocardial remodeling, anti-aging gene expression, and oxidant/antioxidant content in a D-gal-induced aging model.

Methods

Probiotics preparation

L. casei IBRC-M 10,711 (provided by Iranian Biological Resource Center, Tehran, Iran) and B. breve PTCC: 1367 (provided by Iranian Research Organization for Science and Technology, Tehran, Iran) were cultivated in de Mann Rogosa Sharpe (MRS) broth at 37 °C in an oxygen-free condition for 48 h. To purify the bacteria, we centrifuged the tubes containing the cultured bacteria three times (3,000 rpm) and used to completely remove the culture medium. Following centrifugation, the bacterial pellet was resuspended in sterile normal saline to a final concentration of 2 × 109 CFU/mL, as determined by the 7 McFarland turbidity standard. To prepare active and viable probiotics, this step was done daily. The administered volume was 0.1 mL per mouse.

Animals and experimental groups

Animal procedures were conducted as we described previously25. We conducted the animal experiments according to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Babol University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.MUBABOL.AEC.1401.042). This study was accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Briefly, male BALB/c mice (20–25 g) were kept under standard environmental conditions in the animal house of Babol University of Medical Sciences. Food and water were available ad libitum. After one week of adaptation, the animals were randomly divided into 5 groups as follows: (1) The Saline + Prob Mix group was subcutaneously treated with normal saline as a vehicle for D-gal and orally administered with a mixture of L. casei and B. breve (2 × 109 CFU/mL); (2) The D-gal + Saline group was subcutaneously treated with 100 mg/kg D-gal (Merk, Germany) and orally administered normal saline as a vehicle for probiotics; (3) The D-gal + L. casei group was subcutaneously injected with 100 mg/kg D-gal and orally administered L. casei at a concentration of 2 × 109 CFU/mL; (4) The D-gal + B. breve group was subcutaneously injected with 100 mg/kg D-gal and 2 × 109 CFU/mL of B. breve orally; (5) The D-gal + Prob Mix group was subcutaneously injected with 100 mg/kg D-gal and 2 × 109 CFU/mL of the combination of L. casei and B. breve orally. Each group contains seven mice. Both interventions including model induction and probiotic suplementation were daily administered for 8 weeks.

Sample collection

One day following the final exposure, mice were euthanized via isoflurane overdose using the open-drop method. Isoflurane was applied to absorbent material (e.g., gauze) at a concentration of 10%, calculated as container volume (mL) × 0.005. The absorbent material was placed behind a physical barrier within a transparent, non-porous container to prevent direct contact with the anesthetic. Mice were introduced into the chamber, and depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal withdrawal reflex (toe pinch). Isoflurane exposure was continued until complete cessation of respiration was observed for at least 60 s. Subsequently, cervical dislocation was performed to ensure death. Then, the heart apexes of four mice in each group were carefully dissected and stored in a cryogenic tube at −80 °C for quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT‒PCR), and the remaining parts were stored in a separate cryogenic tube at −80 °C for biochemical tests. In addition, three hearts of each group were placed in 10% formalin for histological assessments.

Histological assessments

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and trichrome staining were applied to check myocardial histopathology. The serial Sect. (5 μm thickness) were taken from the heart tissue by a microtome (Leica RM 2135, Germany). The tissue sections were cleared with xylene, hydrated in an ethanol series, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin or the Mallory trichrome. Following washing, the sections were dehydrated in ethanol, immersed in xylene, and mounted with Entellan. Pathological changes, including congestion, inflammation, edema, and cellular damage in H&E, and fibrosis in Mallory trichrome, were observed under a light microscope (Olympus, USA).

A semiquantitative scoring system was used to assess the total damage in H&E staining by categorizing sections into four grades: rank 1 (0%–25%), rank 2 (25%–50%), rank 3 (50%–75%), and rank 4 (75%–100%). Four random microscopic fields per heart section for all observed damages, including congestion, inflammation, edema, and cellular damage.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA of apex tissue was extracted using an RNA isolation kit (Parstous, Mashad, Iran). Then, the reverse transcription was conducted using a cDNA synthesis Kit (Parstous, Mashad, Iran). DNA amplification was done by 2X Master Mix Green (Ampliqon, Denmark). The mRNA expression level of Sirt1 was quantified using gene-specific primers (Table 1) and the 2⁻ΔΔCt Livak method. qPCR was performed under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. Hypoxanthine guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT) served as an internal control.

Biochemical assessments

The malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and reduced glutathione (GSH) were evaluated in the heart tissues of each group using an enzyme labeling instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Kiazist, Iran, and Navand Salamat, Iran, respectively). MDA level was normalized to the weight of the brain tissue, and GSH was normalized to the total protein milligrams using the Bradford method.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.6.1). Normality of the data was assessed using the D’Agostino-Pearson omnibus (K2), and visual inspection via QQ plots. The results parametric data were analyzed using one-way and two-way ANOVA and Least Significant Difference (LSD) or Tukey’s as the post-hoc tests. Nonparametric data was analyzed by Kruskal‒Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of mean (SEM), and P ≤ 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Probiotics L. casei and B. breve and their combination attenuated congestion, inflammation, edema, and cardiomyocyte damage in a D-gal-induced heart aging model

Histopathological changes in myocardial tissue were investigated in the experimental groups via H&E staining. Normal heart tissue with a slight amount of congestion was observed in the Saline + Prob Mix (Fig. 1A). Treatment with D-gal resulted in several pathologies in the myocardial tissue, including congestion in the vessels and capillaries (Fig. 1B and C), inflammation (Fig. 1D), and edema and cardiomyocyte hypochromia (Fig. 1E).

Early probiotic administration attenuated most of the damage in the heart tissue. Statistical analysis using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post-test showed that the Saline + Prob Mix and D-gal + L. casei groups had a significant reduction in congestion given score compared with that of D-gal + Saline group (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, respectively). There was also a significant decrease in the Saline + Prob Mix and D-gal + Prob Mix in the inflammation score compared with that of D-gal + Saline group (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the Saline + Prob Mix, D-gal + B. breve, and D-gal + Prob Mix groups experience a marked attenuation in edema and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy compared to D-gal + Saline group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1F).

Co-administration of probiotics L. casei and B. breve and monotherapy with L. casei alleviated cardiac fibrosis in a D-gal-induced heart aging model

Mallory trichrome stain was employed for observing collagen deposition in the heart tissue of the aging mouse models. As shown in Fig. 2A, the Saline + Prob Mix resulted in a normal amount of collagen in the myocardial tissue. However, the level of cardiac fibrosis increased in the perivascular space and vascular wall as well as in the cardiac interstitium in the D-gal + Saline group (Fig. 2B). There was still a small amount of fibrosis in the D-gal + B. breve group (Fig. 2C) and a nuance fibrosis in D-gal + L. casei group (Fig. 2D). The D-gal + Prob Mix presented almost normal conditions for collagen production in the myocardial tissue (Fig. 2E).

Co-administration of probiotics L. casei and B. breve improved anti-aging gene expression in a D-gal-induced heart aging model

The gene expression level of Sirt1, an antiaging gene, was evaluated in the apex tissue of the heart in D-gal-induced aging mice. The Kruskal‒Wallis test followed by uncorrected Dunn’s multiple comparisons test as the post hoc revealed that Sirt1 gene expression was greater in the Saline + Prob Mix and D-gal + Prob Mix groups than in the D-gal + Saline group (p < 0.01). Furthermore, gene expression level of Sirt1 in D-gal + L. casei was significantly lower compared with those of the Saline + Prob Mix (p < 0.05) and D-gal + Prob Mix (p < 0.01) groups (Fig. 3).

Co-administration of probiotics L. casei and B. breve regulated oxidant/antioxidant markers in a D-gal-induced heart aging model

The antioxidant effect of L. casei and B. breve on the cardiac tissue of D-gal-induced aging mice was investigated by assessing GSH and MDA levels. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA, followed by the LSD test as a post hoc showed that the GSH level had a significant increase in the Saline + Prob Mix and D-gal + Prob Mix groups compared to D-gal + Saline group (p < 0.05). The level of GSH was also significantly higher in D-gal + Prob Mix group than the D-gal + L. casei group (p < 0.05).

In addition, the statistical analysis of cardiac MDA levels showed a significant decrease in the Saline + Prob Mix (p < 0.05), D-gal + L. casei (p < 0.01), and D-gal + Prob Mix (p < 0.05) groups compared to the D-gal + Saline group. In addition, the MDA levels were significantly lower in D-gal + Prob Mix group compared to the D-gal + L. casei and D-gal + B. breve treated mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

Recently, research has demonstrated that age-related gut dysfunctions cause a decline in cardiovascular health in elderly individuals and in an animal aging model26. In this study, we showed that supplementation with the combination of probiotics L. casei and B. breve reduces myocardial damage, including extensive vascular congestion, inflammation, edema, cardiomyocyte damage, and fibrosis, in a D-gal-induced aging model. Their mixture also increases antiaging gene expression, ameliorate the oxidative stress biomarker MDA, and increase the levels of the antioxidant marker GSH.

Myocardial damage is one of the consequences of aging. Chronic low-grade inflammation due to age-related immune dysregulation, increased oxidative stress, and a decline in lymphatic vessel function causes the infiltration of mononuclear cells and the production of inflammatory mediators in myocardial tissue27. Furthermore, vascular congestion occurs as a consequence of endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness due to increased collagen deposition28. In addition, edema is caused by lymphatic insufficiency, which impairs fluid clearance; increased vascular permeability allows plasma leakage into tissues; and venous congestion leads to fluid retention29. Disrupted mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and inflammation during the aging process promote the apoptosis and necroptosis of cardiomyocyte cells30. With age, collagen progressively accumulates in the vascular walls and cardiac tissue, reducing the flexibility of arteries and the heart muscle. This structural stiffening leads to increased myocardial rigidity, diminishing both arterial and cardiac compliance. Excessive fibrosis becomes a key factor in impaired myocardial relaxation, ultimately contributing to diastolic dysfunction. Over time, this process can strain the ability of the heart to fill efficiently during diastole, increasing susceptibility to heart failure and other cardiovascular complications31. In this study, D-gal administration caused myocardial damage, including congestion, inflammation, edema, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, and fibrosis. However, early probiotic supplementation relieves these damages. Notably, the combination therapy demonstrated superior efficacy across most of the evaluated cardiac damage parameters, with the exception of vascular congestion, which was more effectively alleviated in the L. casei group. The fact that probiotics have anti-inflammatory effects is well established. They can control systemic inflammation through the modulation of the local immune system in the gut by decreasing the levels of the proinflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) and interleukin (IL)−6 and increasing the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, which further affects the whole body32. L. casei administration caused a reduction in tissue inflammation, as observed by H&E staining, in an endotoxin- and d-galactosamine-induced liver injury model33. The probiotic Bifidobacterium animalis can also reduce pancreatic cell necrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration in mice with pancreatic damage34. Another study reported that treadmill exercise ameliorated myocardial fibrosis via trichrome staining in an aging model35.

Excessive ROS attacks crucial biological macromolecules and promotes aging36. Oxidative stress causes and accelerates cardiac remodeling and inflammation during the aging process37. Pharmacological interventions involving antioxidant agents are considered promising solutions for CVD treatment38. Several previous studies have confirmed the antioxidant effects of some probiotic strains39. In the present study, supplementation with the combination of L. casei and B. breve resulted in attenuated MDA and increased GSH levels in cardiac tissue. L. casei single therapy only induced a marked reduction in MDA levels, whereas B. breve did not produce significant effects on these oxidative stress parameters. As B. breve caused an attenuation in myocardial damage, it may exert its effects by regulating other signaling pathways. MDA is one of the byproducts of lipid peroxidation due to oxidative stress exposure, and its increased level is correlated with cardiovascular dysfunction40. In addition, GSH is an important antioxidant protein that promotes cardiac protection by controlling cellular oxidoreductase reactions41. The results of our previous studies on assessing the effects of these probiotics on brain aging also showed that B. breve consumption could not significantly enhance the antioxidant system25. A previous study showed that Lactobacillus plantarum could improve myocardial dysfunction by activating the nuclear factor erythroid 2–2-related factor 2/antioxidant response element (Nrf2/ARE) signaling pathway42. This probiotic also enhances longevity and healthy aging through exerting its antioxidant effects43. Generally, past studies revealed that probiotics could exert antioxidant activities through different mechanisms, including producing antioxidant enzymes, metabolites with antioxidant activity (e.g., butyrate, exopolysaccharides, and glutathione), regulating plasma lipid profile, and chelating metals44. Specifically, it has been shown that probiotics regulate oxidative stress in heart tissue through the colonization of health-related bacteria such as Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Akkermansia, as well as the production of SCFAs such as acetate, butyrate, and caproate, which help scavenge free radicals45. A similar mechanism may underlie the observed effects of L. casei and its combination with B. breve.

Disrupted Sirt1 activity with age is implicated in the pathogenesis of CVD. Sirt1, which has both antiaging and antioxidant properties, protects against age-related damage in the heart46. Our data indicate that only the combination of the probiotics L. casei and B. breve increased the Sirt1 gene expression level. In line with this result, L. plantarum with galacto-oligosaccharide (GOS) alleviated oxidative stress and aging by activating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/Sirt11 and restoring hepatic antioxidant capacity in D-gal-induced aging model mice47. In addition, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum exerts antiaging effects through the upregulation of Sirt1 levels in an animal model48. Resveratrol, a plant-derived antioxidant, impedes age-related cardiomyocyte damage through the activation of Sirt1 in mice49. However, it is unclear how probiotics can regulate Sirt1 expression levels, but since Sirt1 activity is regulated by NAD + levels and energy metabolism pathways50, it seems that probiotics can affect energy metabolism pathways by regulating gut microbiota diversity, enriching anti-inflammatory SCFAs, and modulating hormone levels51,52. Taken together, these findings suggest that Sirt1 upregulation in aging via probiotic supplementation can effectively reduce age-associated myocardial damage. However, more studies are needed to confirm these results and elucidate the mechanism of action.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the investigation focused exclusively on the effects of L. casei and B. breve on myocardial tissue in a D-gal-induced aging model, without evaluating broader cardiac functional parameters. Future studies incorporating post-probiotic electrocardiographic analysis and comprehensive blood biomarker profiling are needed to provide a more complete understanding of probiotic-mediated cardiac effects. Secondly, resource constraints restricted the scope of molecular investigations, including an in-depth analysis of signaling pathways. Thirdly, this study did not assess microbiome profile and produced metabolite by microbiome post-supplementation. Future study should focus on this to uncover the mechanism of action of L. casei and B. breve. Additionally, low sample size may limit the detection of smaller effect sizes and the generalizability of the findings, and analyzing with a more valid post-hoc test. Future studies with larger cohorts and formal power analyses will be necessary to validate and expand upon these results. Furthermore, adding a naïve group would enhance the robustness of the data. Addressing these aspects in future research will help validate the beneficial role of probiotics L. casei and B. breve in cardiac aging and reinforce their potential therapeutic applications.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that D-gal administration induces significant myocardial damage, including congestion, inflammation, edema, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, and fibrosis. Treatment with the probiotics L. casei and B. breve mitigated these pathological changes, with combination therapy showing superior efficacy across most parameters. Notably, vascular congestion was more effectively alleviated by L. casei alone. The cardioprotective effects appear to be mediated through modulation of oxidative stress and upregulation of an antiaging protein in cardiac tissue. These findings suggest that probiotic supplementation may represent a promising strategy for preventing age-related cardiac dysfunction, warranting further mechanistic and translational studies.
