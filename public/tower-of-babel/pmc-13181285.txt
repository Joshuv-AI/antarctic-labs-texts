Community perceptions of socio-economic, environmental, and governance dimensions of manganese mining in Serenje District, Zambia
Authors: Maggie Mwape, Memory Mfungo, Christopher Mulwanda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Manganese has become increasingly important in the global energy transition, yet its extraction raises significant socio-economic, environmental, and governance concerns in mining-affected communities. This study examined community perceptions of the socio-economic benefits, environmental impacts, and governance dimensions of formal manganese mining in Serenje District, Zambia. Using a mixed-methods design, the study combined a household survey of 120 respondents across three communities with semi-structured interviews involving 14 purposively selected key informants, including company managers, industrial miners, traditional leaders, trade union representatives, and civil society stakeholders. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics and a Chi-Square test, while qualitative data were analysed thematically and interpreted alongside survey findings.The findings show that manganese mining is widely perceived as contributing positively to employment and local livelihoods, although key informants identified weak investment, poor infrastructure, ineffective policies, and market volatility as major constraints to long-term sector sustainability. Respondents also perceived notable environmental and health risks, particularly deterioration in water quality, biodiversity loss, respiratory problems, waterborne diseases, and skin conditions. Perceptions of Zambia's mining legal framework were mixed, with weak enforcement, outdated regulations, and limited institutional capacity identified as major governance concerns. Education level significantly influenced perceptions of mining's contribution to economic growth, while age and gender did not.The study contributes to resource governance and sustainable development debates by providing context-specific evidence on how mining is experienced and interpreted at the community level in an emerging manganese-producing district. It highlights the tension between perceived short-term socio-economic gains and longer-term environmental, health, and governance risks. The study recommends strengthening regulatory enforcement, improving institutional capacity, expanding environmental and public health monitoring, and adopting more inclusive mining policies that better reflect community experiences and priorities.

Introduction

Globally, mineral resources have become central to debates on sustainable development, energy transition, and environmental governance. The accelerating demand for critical minerals such as lithium, cobalt, and manganese reflects their indispensable role in renewable energy technologies, electric vehicle batteries, and low-carbon industrial processes [1], [2], [3]. While these resources offer opportunities for economic growth and technological advancement, their extraction often raises complex socio-economic and ecological challenges, particularly in resource-dependent regions [2].

Across Africa, mining has long been a driver of economic activity [4], yet its environmental and social consequences remain profound, including land degradation, water contamination, and community displacement ([5], [6]; [7]). Studies highlight persistent issues such as land degradation, biodiversity loss, water contamination, and community displacement, which undermine the sustainability of mining-led development [8], [9]. Moreover, weak institutional frameworks and limited value addition have constrained the continent's ability to fully benefit from its mineral wealth, leaving many communities vulnerable to poverty and inequality despite abundant resources [10], [11]).

Zambia, strategically located in central Africa, exemplifies these dynamics. With a formal mining history spanning over a century, the country has established itself as a significant player in the global industry, particularly through copper, which remains the cornerstone of its economy [12], [13]). Yet, despite this mineral abundance, Zambia has struggled to translate extraction into inclusive and sustainable development, with poverty and inequality persisting. In recent years, manganese has emerged as a critical mineral within Zambia's mining landscape, attracting attention for its applications in steel production, battery manufacturing, and renewable energy technologies [14]. Production has steadily increased, with reserves estimated at 60 million metric tonnes, positioning Zambia among Africa's leading producers [15]. However, the sector faces challenges including limited value addition, market volatility, labour violations, community displacement, and environmental risks such as air and water pollution, biodiversity loss, and land degradation. These challenges are compounded by weak institutional and legal frameworks that lack effective mechanisms for regulation, accountability, and the promotion of responsible practices.

Despite extensive research on mining and development in Africa, much of the literature has focused on macro-level economic outcomes, environmental impacts, and governance challenges, with comparatively less attention given to how local communities perceive these dynamics, particularly in emerging mineral sectors such as manganese ([5], [6]). While mining remains central to economic development in Zambia, there is limited empirical evidence capturing community-level perspectives on the socio-economic benefits, environmental impacts, and effectiveness of governance frameworks in newly expanding mining regions, such as Serenje [32; 4].

Despite growing attention to mining and development in Africa and Zambia in particular, limited research has examined how local communities perceive the socio-economic, environmental, and governance dimensions of emerging mineral sectors such as manganese. This study addresses this gap by analysing community-level perceptions of formal manganese mining in Serenje District, Zambia. By focusing on lived experiences, the study contributes to resource governance and sustainable development debates by providing context-specific evidence that can inform more inclusive and responsive mining policies.

Therefore, this study seeks to contribute to literature by situating community perceptions within broader debates on resource governance and sustainable development. Specifically, it seeks to; a) assess perceptions of socio-economic benefits and sustainability of manganese mining in Serenje, with particular attention to value addition, b) examine community views on environmental impacts, including air and water quality, land use, and biodiversity, and c) evaluate perceptions of Zambia's mining legal framework, focusing on its effectiveness in regulating manganese mining. By addressing these objectives, the study aims to bridge the gap between national resource strategies and local realities, offering evidence to inform more inclusive, accountable, and sustainable mining policies.

Methods and data collection protocols

Study area

The study was conducted in three communities of Serenje District in Zambia's Central Province. Serenje (Fig. 1), one of the 11 districts in the province, lies within agroecological zone IIa and receives annual rainfall ranging from 800 mm to 1000 mm, supporting predominantly rainfed agricultural systems. Like other parts of Zambia, the district experiences a humid subtropical climate with three distinct seasons: a cool-dry season (May–August), a hot-dry season (September–October), and a rainy season (November–April) [16]. Serenje was purposively selected for this study due to its prominence in manganese production, with sampled communities located near mining sites to capture the direct socio-economic impacts. Manganese mining in Serenje presents a double-edged sword: while it diversifies livelihoods and injects cash into local economies, offering alternative income streams beyond subsistence farming, it simultaneously threatens long-term sustainability. The expansion of mining activities risks degrading the environment through deforestation, soil erosion, and water contamination, thereby destabilizing traditional farming-based livelihoods that remain central to household food security. This dynamic underscore the tension between short-term economic gains and the preservation of ecological systems that underpin rural livelihoods.Fig. 1Map of Serenje District in Zambia's Central Province, showing the three study communities located near manganese mining sites.Fig. 1

Sampling procedure and data collection

This study employed a mixed-methods approach, combining qualitative and quantitative techniques to investigate the perceived socio-economic contributions of manganese mining, associated environmental challenges, the viability of the mining sector, and the mining legal framework in Zambia. The use of mixed methods enhanced validity through triangulation, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the sector [17]. The study focused on communities located near formal manganese mining operations and on stakeholders linked to these operations, including company managers and industrial miners. Artisanal and small-scale miners were not the primary focus of the study.

Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was pretested among 15 individuals two months earlier to ensure clarity, internal consistency, and contextual relevance. To achieve representative coverage across the three study communities (Kabamba, Kanona and Mailo), a stratified random sampling design was adopted. Each community was treated as a stratum, with an equal quota of 40 respondents per stratum. Equal allocation was adopted to ensure balanced representation across the three study communities and to allow comparison of perceptions across sites experiencing manganese mining under different local conditions. This allocation was not intended to mirror underlying population proportions in each community, but rather to provide comparable descriptive insights across the selected sites. Within each stratum, households were systematically selected by randomly identifying the first household and then choosing every third household thereafter, resulting in a total sample of 120 respondents.

Household heads were specifically targeted because they (i) typically hold primary responsibility for household welfare and resource allocation, making them well-positioned to articulate socio-economic impacts of mining, and (ii) often serve as key decision-makers in community affairs, thereby providing insights into both family-level and collective responses to mining activities [18]. This sampling strategy ensured that perspectives most relevant to the manganese mining value chain were captured [19].

Qualitative data collection involved semi-structured interviews administered to 14 key informants being managers (from different mining companies operating within Serenje), stakeholders (i.e., Traditional leaders, Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs), and Trade union representatives), and miners (i.e., industrial miners employed by companies), document analysis of reports and publications, and structured surveys to quantify challenges and perceptions. The key informants were selected purposively based on their direct involvement in, knowledge of, or influence on manganese mining activities in Serenje District. Selection criteria included institutional role, experience with mining-related issues, and the ability to provide informed perspectives on socio-economic, environmental, and governance dimensions of the sector. The aim was not statistical representation, but to capture diverse stakeholder viewpoints relevant to the study objectives. Secondary data was drawn from academic journals, government reports, NGO publications, industry documents, and media sources to supplement primary findings and provide contextual depth.

Informed consent was secured verbally, with explanations provided in culturally appropriate language to ensure participants clearly understood the purpose of the research and their rights. Each participant was informed that participation was voluntary, that they could decline or withdraw at any stage without consequence, and that their identities would remain anonymous since no names or personal identifiers were recorded. Despite being given the option to withdraw, all participants agreed to take part. For secondary data, responsible use was maintained through accurate citation and acknowledgement of original sources.

Data analysis

Following validation, the dataset was imported into SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) for advanced statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise household demographic characteristics, participants perception of manganese mining contribution to economic growth in the community, long term economic viability of the manganese mining, contribution to employment, perceptions on mining activities on water quality, as well as mining activities and associated health issues in the community and around the district. And to examine whether demographic factors, such as gender, age, and level of education influenced community perceptions of mining's contribution to economic growth in Serenje town, a Chi-Square test of independence was conducted. This test assessed the association between these demographic variables and respondents' perception categories. The quantitative component of the study was primarily exploratory and aimed at describing patterns in community perceptions rather than developing predictive models. Given the relatively small sample size and the context-specific design of the study, descriptive statistics were considered appropriate for summarising household responses, while the Chi-Square test was used selectively to examine whether key demographic characteristics were associated with perceptions of mining's contribution to economic growth.

Qualitative data from interviews with key informants was analysed thematically. Themes were developed around the research questions, including economic challenges in manganese mining, environmental and biodiversity impacts, and Zambia's mining legal framework. Data coding involved identifying meaningful units, grouping similar codes into categories, and refining these into themes that captured key patterns in participants' responses. The strategy combined deductive elements with openness to inductive insights, allowing new themes to emerge. This flexible approach ensured findings were both conceptually informed and grounded in lived experiences. Thematic insights were then triangulated with quantitative results, strengthening validity and providing a holistic understanding of the manganese mining value chain.

Demographic characteristics of respondents

The gender distribution among respondents shows that males constitute 55% (n = 66) while females account for 45% (n = 54) out of the total sample size of 120. The age distribution of respondents in Serenje district reflects a youthful demographic profile, with the majority falling within the younger age brackets. Specifically, 35% (n = 42) of participants were aged between 18 and 30 years, underscoring the strong representation of youth in the study. This was followed by the 31–40 age group, which accounted for 25% (n = 30), further contributing to the dynamic mix of younger respondents actively engaged in mining-related socio-economic activities.

Age groups with comparatively lower representation included those aged 41–50 years, comprising 16.7% (n = 20), and 51–60 years, representing 15% (n = 18). Discussion

The mixed-methods approach strengthens the study by combining household survey responses on perceived benefits and risks with key informant insights on the structural and governance factors shaping those views. The findings show that community perceptions of formal manganese mining in Serenje reflect both socio-economic benefits and concerns about environmental, health, and governance challenges. Evidence from household surveys, key informants, and secondary literature is interpreted as complementary and interconnected dimensions of mining sustainability at the community level.

Demographic characteristics and gender perspectives

The survey revealed slightly higher male participation, reflecting men's greater involvement in mining activities and decision-making. This demographic balance is important for interpreting socio-economic and environmental perceptions in the manganese mining value chain. Male dominance highlights the gendered nature of mining communities, while female representation ensures household and community-level perspectives are included. Studies on Zambia's mining sector confirm that gender disparities remain significant, with men dominating formal employment [20]. These gendered patterns reflect differences in access to economic opportunities and exposure to mining-related risks. Men are more likely to benefit from employment, while women, through their household roles, may experience environmental and health impacts more directly. This suggests an unequal distribution of benefits and risks, indicating the need for more inclusive and gender-responsive mining governance.

This means mining policy should go beyond employment and make sure women are actively involved in environmental decisions, compensation, and community planning. Women may feel the effects of mining differently at home and in their communities. Adopting an inclusive approach ensures that strategies cater to the diverse needs of both male and female participants, fostering comprehensive and equitable policies [21]. Such inclusivity is essential for creating sustainable and effective practices within the manganese value chain in Serenje, echoing broader calls for gender-sensitive mining policies in Zambia.

Age distribution and intergenerational insights

In terms of respondents' age groups, the results highlight the predominance of younger age groups in the manganese mining value chain, while also capturing perspectives from older participants whose experiences provide valuable insights into long-term socio-economic and environmental impacts. From a policy perspective, this age distribution suggests that interventions in mining-affected communities should balance youth employment needs with long-term environmental and livelihood concerns. These patterns suggest that perceptions of mining are shaped by generational priorities. Younger respondents tend to emphasise employment opportunities, while older participants are more likely to focus on long-term environmental and livelihood impacts. This points to the need for policies that balance short-term economic gains with long-term sustainability.

Economic challenges and opportunities

Lack of investment was perceived as the most critical economic challenge, underscoring the sector's dependence on sustained financial support for growth and modernisation. Poor infrastructure and ineffective policies highlight structural and governance barriers that constrain mining operations and long-term viability, while market volatility reflects vulnerability to global demand and pricing. These perceptions reported by key informants mirror broader assessments of Zambia's mining sector documented in the literature [12].

The findings suggest that manganese mining is perceived as an important source of local employment and income opportunities in Serenje. However, these perceived benefits appear to coexist with structural concerns, including limited investment, weak infrastructure, and policy uncertainty. This indicates that local support for mining may be tied less to confidence in the sector's long-term stability than to the immediate livelihood opportunities it creates. Similar tensions between economic dependence and structural vulnerability have been noted in studies of African mining communities. In tandem, the survey and interview findings suggest that positive community perceptions of employment and livelihood benefits coexist with stakeholder concerns about the sector's weak structural foundations. This mixed-methods insight is important because it shows that local support for mining may persist even where institutional and economic conditions raise questions about long-term sustainability. Education and perceptions of economic growth

The results show that education level significantly influenced respondents' perceptions of mining's contribution to economic growth in Serenje town, while age and gender did not. This finding suggests that individuals with higher levels of education are more likely to recognise the broader economic benefits of mining, consistent with studies highlighting the role of education in shaping awareness of development processes [24]. This may be explained by the role of education in shaping awareness and interpretation of mining impacts. More educated individuals are better able to engage with economic and environmental information, while less-educated respondents may rely more on direct experience. This underscores the importance of accessible communication and inclusive community engagement in mining governance.

Literature indicates that education enhances the ability to critically evaluate both the opportunities and challenges associated with extractive industries, thereby influencing community attitudes. Beyond education, other factors such as employment opportunities, environmental impacts, and governance practices have also been shown to shape perceptions of mining in African contexts, with communities that benefit directly from jobs or infrastructure tending to view mining more positively [25]. These findings reinforce the importance of education and socio-economic benefits in determining how communities perceive mining's role in local development.

Health impacts

Community perceptions of respiratory problems, waterborne diseases, and skin conditions suggest that health concerns are a central part of how mining is experienced in everyday life. Although these concerns are perception-based, they are important because they shape risk awareness and trust in institutions. In contexts with limited health monitoring, communities rely on lived experiences to interpret risks. This points to the need for improved health surveillance, clearer communication, and stronger institutional accountability in mining governance.

Environmental impacts

Perceptions of declining water quality and biodiversity loss indicate that community members understand mining not only as an economic activity, but also as a source of ecological risk. These perceptions are important because they reflect how environmental change is experienced locally and how the environmental legitimacy of mining is judged by affected communities. In this regard, environmental concerns are not separate from questions of development but are central to whether mining is viewed as sustainable or socially acceptable. These perceptions reported by key informants, mirror broader assessments of Zambia's mining sector documented in the literature. Differences between household and key informant perspectives reflect variations in experience and knowledge. Households emphasise water quality due to daily use, while key informants focus on biodiversity and land use based on broader expertise. This suggests that effective environmental governance should integrate both local experiences and technical knowledge. For instance, in Zambia's Copperbelt confirm that mining operations significantly affect water resources, necessitating robust monitoring and community engagement [8], [26]. Respondents expressed highlighted concerns over regarding ecological consequences, particularly biodiversity loss and land use changes. Most key informants viewed impacts as considerable, consistent with regional studies showing mining-induced habitat loss and reduced species diversity in Zambia's ecosystems [8], [9].

Governance and legal framework

This study found that key informants expressed varied views on the effectiveness of Zambia's mining legal framework and the institutional challenges affecting its implementation. These challenges reflect underlying institutional weaknesses, including limited enforcement capacity and regulatory gaps. Such constraints can reduce community trust and weaken the effectiveness of mining policies. Strengthening accountability and enforcement is therefore essential for improving governance outcomes. The key informants identified weak enforcement, unclear legal provisions, outdated regulations, and limited institutional capacity as major governance concerns. These stakeholder perceptions align with the existing literature, which points to persistent regulatory and enforcement challenges in Zambia's mining sector [10], [27]. While recent legal reforms, such as the Minerals Regulation Commission Act of 2024, indicate efforts to strengthen governance, the findings suggest that implementation remains a key concern. From a policy perspective, the findings show that legal reform alone may be insufficient unless accompanied by stronger enforcement systems, clearer implementation guidelines, improved institutional capacity, and greater accountability to affected communities. This emphasis on lived experience and community interpretation is consistent with other studies showing that mining-affected communities often evaluate the sector not only in terms of formal regulation, but also in relation to everyday wellbeing, inclusion, and environmental security [21], [28].

Study contribution and policy implications

The novelty of this study lies in its systematic integration of community perceptions across demographic, socio-economic, environmental, health, and governance dimensions of manganese mining in Serenje District, offering a holistic view that goes beyond the narrowly economic or environmental focus of prior research. By capturing household heads' perspectives, the study highlights how gender, age, and education shape perceptions of mining's contributions and challenges, revealing the double-edged nature of mining as both a livelihood opportunity and a source of environmental and health risks. These findings imply that sustainable mining governance in Zambia must prioritize inclusive, perception-driven policies that address gender disparities, intergenerational concerns, and community-identified risks, while strengthening institutional frameworks to balance short-term economic gains with long-term livelihood security and ecological integrity.

Directions for future research

Future research should extend these findings by integrating community perceptions with empirical, longitudinal, and interdisciplinary investigations to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the manganese mining sector. While respondents offered valuable insights into socio-economic, health, and environmental impacts, these remain perception-based and require validation through medical and epidemiological studies on respiratory problems, waterborne diseases, and manganese toxicity, as well as environmental monitoring of water quality, biodiversity, and land use changes. Further research should also examine gendered and generational dynamics to capture differentiated participation and impacts, alongside critical assessments of legal and institutional frameworks to address persistent governance gaps. Finally, studies on economic resilience and market volatility are needed to link local experiences with global demand patterns. By combining community voices with scientific evidence, future research can generate robust, locally grounded knowledge to inform inclusive and sustainable mining policies.

Limitations of the Study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the study sites were purposively selected based on their proximity to manganese mining activities in Serenje District. As a result, the findings are context-specific and cannot be statistically generalised to all mining communities in Zambia. Second, although the household survey yielded descriptive insights from 120 respondents, the qualitative component was based on 14 key informants. While these informants provided depth and contextual understanding, their perspectives should not be viewed as representative of all stakeholders in the sector. Third, many findings related to health, water quality, and biodiversity are based on community and stakeholder perceptions rather than direct scientific, medical, or ecological measurements. Therefore, the primary contribution of this study is the documentation of locally grounded perceptions and governance concerns, which should be supplemented by future environmental monitoring and epidemiological research. In addition, the quantitative analysis was primarily descriptive and exploratory, which limits the extent to which more complex statistical relationships could be examined.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study highlights that while manganese mining in Serenje is widely perceived as economically viable and beneficial to employment, it also raises significant concerns related to health, environmental degradation, and weak institutional capacity. The findings underscore the gendered and generational dynamics shaping community perceptions, as well as the divided views on the effectiveness of Zambia's mining legal framework. To address these challenges, policies should prioritize strengthening enforcement mechanisms, modernise outdated regulations, and invest in infrastructure to enhance resilience. Equally important is the need for inclusive strategies that integrate women's and youth perspectives, ensure equitable distribution of benefits, and safeguard biodiversity and water resources through rigorous monitoring. Finally, collaboration between government, communities, and independent researchers is recommended to validate health and environmental impacts, thereby grounding interventions in both scientific evidence and local realities.
