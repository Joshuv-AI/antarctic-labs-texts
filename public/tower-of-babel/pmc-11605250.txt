Mucocutaneous Ulcerations and Pancytopenia Secondary to Methotrexate Toxicity in a Patient With Rheumatoid Arthritis: A Case Report
Authors: Thinley Dorji, Tshering Penjor, Sangay Tenzin, Tshering Pedon, Sangay Wangchuck, Sonam Yangchen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTToxicity from methotrexate overdosing is life threatening condition that requires prompt recognition and early treatment. A 71‐year‐old man from rural Bhutan with diabetes mellitus had symmetrical small joint pain for 3 years associated with early morning stiffness. He was evaluated and diagnosed with seropositive rheumatoid arthritis in a hospital in neighbouring India. He was initiated on Methotrexate and Hydroxychloroquine. The patient had inadvertently taken Methotrexate 10 mg OD for 2 weeks. Following this, he developed painful ulcers on the lips preventing him from swallowing and ulcers around the corona on the penis. He also had intermittent fever with chills. On examination, he had fluid responsive hypotension and pancytopenia without bleeding manifestations. He was managed with intravenous folinic acid, subcutaneous granulocyte colony stimulating factor and intravenous cloxacillin. He had an uneventful recovery and methotrexate has been restarted following adequate patient education. Education of patient and family members is key in preventing medication errors. Early identification and timely management of methotrexate toxicity is key in preventing mortality.Keywords: adverse drug event, antidote, health literacy, leucovorin, medication errors

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory condition that causes persistent pain symptoms and disability resulting from joint destruction [1]. In 2020, an estimated 17.6 million people worldwide had rheumatoid arthritis with an age‐standardized global prevalence are of 208.8 cases per 100,000 population [2]. The age‐standardized female‐to‐male ratio of prevalence was 2.45 and prevalence peaked in the age group 75–79 years with 828.2 cases per 100,000 population [2]. While deaths due to rheumatoid arthritis has declined in developed countries [2], there still remains a major challenge in the diagnosis, management, and prevention of disability and deaths in developing countries.

Those with symptoms of rheumatoid arthritis require early evaluation, appropriate means for diagnosis, and early initiation of management based on disease activity [3]. For those with newly diagnosed rheumatoid arthritis, it is recommended to initiate treatment with conventional synthetic disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) and re‐evaluate treatment response, efficacy, and tolerability within 3 months [4]. Methotrexate is generally the first‐line drug for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and other forms of inflammatory arthritis [5].

Of the various postulated mechanisms of action, given that methotrexate is effective even with concurrent folic acid or folinic acid supplementation, it is unlikely that the inhibition of cellular proliferation is required for anti‐inflammatory effects [5]. Methotrexate‐mediated inhibition of an enzyme, 5‐aminoimidazole‐4‐carboxamide ribo‐nucleotide transformylase, involved in the de novo purine biosynthesis pathway, leads to extracellular accumulation of adenosine. Adenosine is a potent stimulus for a family of receptors that have inhibitory effects on nearly all inflammatory cells [5]. Low‐dose methotrexate has been proven safe and well tolerated in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. However, adverse drug reactions remain one of the common causes of its discontinuation.

Bhutan is a small country in the eastern Himalayas with a population of 0.7 million. Arthritis remains one of the common conditions presenting to hospitals with 8630 cases in 2020, 7963 cases in 2021, and 8021 cases reported in 2022 with bimodal predominance in the age groups 25–49 years and among those > 60 years [6]. Healthcare services in Bhutan are delivered free of cost by the government through its three‐tiered system. The primary healthcare centers constitute the basic structures at the primary level, the district and general hospitals constitute the secondary level, and the referral hospitals constitute the tertiary care level. For the diagnosis and care of patients with rheumatoid arthritis, physicians are available at five districts out of 20 in the country. The country has 26 physicians and only one rheumatologist as of 2024. For diagnosis in Bhutan, testing of anti‐cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti‐CCP) is available in three tertiary care hospitals while additional rheumatologic tests are carried out through transporting patient specimens to India. Often, patients with rheumatologic conditions need to travel to referral hospitals or to the capital city for consultations with physicians. In district and primary healthcare centers, there are no diagnostic tests for rheumatologic conditions. In addition, the availability of the drugs for rheumatoid arthritis is also linked to the levels of the hospitals in the three‐tiered healthcare delivery system.

In this case report, we describe a Bhutanese male patient who was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis and initiated on methotrexate at a hospital in a bordering town in India. The patient suffered from a methotrexate overdose because he had consumed the wrong dosage of the medication. In this article, we discuss the challenges in the diagnosis and management of methotrexate toxicity in a resource‐limited setting.

Case Presentation

Case History

A 71‐year‐old male, a farmer residing in one of the villages, with diagnosed diabetes mellitus on metformin 1 g BD, had been having pain in the small joints of his bilateral hands for 3 years. In the initial stages, the pain was intermittent and lasted for a few days. Gradually, the pain lasted longer duration and was associated with early morning stiffness lasting for more than 30 min. It progressed to include pain in his bilateral elbows and shoulders. For this, he had visited a tertiary care hospital in India and was diagnosed with seropositive rheumatoid arthritis. The patient was prescribed csDMARDs with methotrexate 10 mg/week, hydroxychloroquine 200 mg HS, folic acid 5 mg OD.

Two weeks after returning home, he developed multiple ulcers on the lips and oral mucosa, and around the corona region on the penis. The lesions appeared around the same time and were painful. A few days later, he developed intermittent fever with chills. By this time, it had become difficult for the patient to swallow his meals. There was no cough, abdominal pain, or pain on micturition.

On history taking, his family members suggested that these lesions could have been due to a new set of medications that he was taking. Upon inquiry, the patient had been taking methotrexate 10 mg once daily for the past 2 weeks with a cumulative dose of 70 mg/week.

Patient Examination

The patient characteristics on examination were: height = 152 cm, weight = 42 kg, temperature 99°F, PR 72/min, BP 99/60 mmHg, RR 20/min, SpO2 99% on room air, RBG 122 mg/dL. Ulcers were observed on the lips and oral mucosa (Figure 1a) and around the corona of the penis (Figure 1b). No pallor, icterus, cyanosis, clubbing, cervical or inguinal lymphadenopathy, edema, ecchymosis, or purpura were observed. No obvious joint deformities were observed. Cardiovascular system: S1 and S2 normal, no murmur; respiratory system: bilateral vesicular breaths; abdomen: soft, non‐tender, no organomegaly; nervous system: oriented to time, place, and person, no cranial nerve, or focal deficits. Detailed joint examinations were not documented during this admission.

Differential Diagnosis

A differential diagnosis of malignancy or nutritional deficiency was considered. Peripheral blood smears did not show any atypical cells. Based on the clinical history, the patient was managed for methotrexate toxicity.

Patient Management

He was admitted to the medicine ward and managed with intravenous folinic acid (leukovorin) 15 mg/m2 (20 mg) three times a day for 5 days and three doses of sub‐cutaneous granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor 300 μg OD for 3 days. As the patient had neutropenia with fever, he received empirical cloxacillin for 5 days. He had hypotension that resolved with intravenous fluids and hypokalemia that resolved with intravenous supplementation. For his skin care, he received daily dressings with normal saline and paraffin. The patient had an unremarkable recovery with healing of his wound, normalization of his cell counts and return of appetite.

Two weeks after follow‐up at the outpatient visit, he had normal cell counts following which methotrexate was re‐initiated at 7.5 mg/week with adequate patient education on correct drug dosing.

Discussion

Patients with rheumatoid arthritis in developing countries often have poorer outcomes due to delays in establishing diagnosis and initiation of appropriate treatment. The diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis in Bhutan is particularly challenging primarily due to lack of adequate access to testing facilities. As seen in this case, this patient had visited a hospital in India where he had got his diagnosis confirmed and treatment initiated. In Bhutan, the availability of DMARDs is determined by the National Essential Medicines List 2023 (Table 2) [7]. For this patient residing in a rural village, drug choices are often selected from those that are available at the local primary healthcare center.

While methotrexate is one of the key drugs recommended in rheumatoid arthritis [3], it is known to cause hepatotoxicity, gastrointestinal side effects, pulmonary toxicity, hematologic toxicity, nephrotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and infections [8]. Additional toxic effects of long‐term low‐dose methotrexate therapy include diminished peripheral blood white cell counts, stomatitis, and hair loss, likely due to inhibition of cellular proliferation due to antifolate properties [5]. To prevent these toxic effects, concomitant administration of folic acid or folinic acid (except on the day of methotrexate administration) is recommended. Side effects, such as tiredness, rheumatoid nodulosis, and hepatic fibrosis, are attributed to adenosine. As methotrexate is excreted both as methotrexate and as 7‐hydroxymethotrexate (the major metabolite of methotrexate) in the urine, dose reduction is recommended in those with impaired renal function to prevent adverse events [9]. Therefore, while prescribing methotrexate, patient education on the symptoms of side effects and timely follow‐up are key in ensuring patient safety.

Based on the availability for monitoring and follow‐up facilities, the dose of methotrexate may be initiated at 7.5 mg/week and titrated up to 25–50 mg/week with an overall aim to treat‐to‐target and achieve optimal patient outcomes [3, 8]. There are noticeable pharmacogenetic factors that determine serum levels and disease response through modulation of cellular transporters and intracellular metabolism [8, 10]. In addition, lifestyle factors such as current smokers were associated with lesser likelihood of response to methotrexate and TNF inhibitors [11].

This patient presented with mucositis and neutropenia with a declining absolute neutrophil count. Patients with methotrexate toxicity should be hospitalized for prompt initiation of treatment and supportive care. Wherever possible, serum levels of methotrexate may be measured for therapeutic dosing of folinic acid against standard normograms. The antidote for methotrexate poisoning is folinic acid aimed at replenishing intracellular folate levels; as methotrexate and folinic acid compete for a common uptake path into the cell, a higher proportion of folinic acid is required to achieve rescue in the presence of methotrexate [12]. In an open‐label randomized controlled trial among 38 patients with low‐dose methotrexate toxicity, comparison of intravenous leukovorin 16 mg q6h versus 25 mg q6h continued until hematologic recovery or for 10 days, whichever was earlier, did not show differences in 30‐day mortality or median time to recovery of white blood cell or platelet counts [13].

As methotrexate is eliminated through the kidneys [5], there should be adequate urine output to allow for diuresis. The urine should be maintained at an alkaline pH greater than 7 to prevent crystallization of methotrexate in the renal tubules. Urinary alkalinization may be achieved through the administration of sodium bicarbonate in intravenous fluid or through carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide). Additional supportive therapy, such as wound dressing, intravenous antibiotics, or granulocyte colony‐stimulating agents, may be used when the absolute neutrophil count is on a decreasing trend.

Other forms of therapy for methotrexate toxicity include extracorporeal techniques such as hemofiltration, hemodialysis, and glucarpidase [12]. Glucarpidase is a carboxypeptidase enzyme that hydrolyzes the carboxyl terminal glutamate residue from folic acid and its analogues, converting them into inactive metabolites [14]. Glucarpidase cleaves methotrexate into two inactive metabolites. However, availability and cost limit the use of glucarpidase in developing countries [12].

There are cases of mortality reported due to methotrexate poisoning. A 65‐year‐old female with psoriasis who had accidentally consumed methotrexate 7.5 mg OD for 15 days had pancytopenia with an absolute neutrophil count of 100/mm3 with deranged coagulation parameters [15]. A 60‐year‐old female with rheumatoid arthritis who had consumed methotrexate 7.5 mg OD for 15 days had pancytopenia that was managed with leukovorin rescue, granulocyte colony stimulating factor, component support, and intravenous antibiotics; she died of septic shock [16]. In a case series of 15 patients with methotrexate toxicity managed at a rheumatology center in India, two patients died due to neutropenic sepsis [17]. In a set of 38 patients followed up in an open‐label trial, there were 17 mortalities over a 30‐day follow‐up period [13].

One of the most common reasons for methotrexate toxicity is wrong dosing [18]. This brings to focus on the importance of providing adequate education to the patient and caregivers on dosing and reporting of potential adverse effects [19]. In Bhutan, a survey among nurses at the National Referral Hospital reported 62.7% medication errors such as omission, wrong time and wrong dose [20]. With a literacy rate of 71.4% among adults [21], it is recognized that additional efforts must be taken on providing adequate information on medication use, especially those with potential of serious adverse events. While reporting of adverse drug reactions is a mandatory requirement as per national health standards, the level of reporting received by the national pharmacovigilance center is low [22]. This case study therefore calls for team efforts involving clinicians, pharmacists and nurses in providing adequate medication related education to patients and their caregivers.

Conclusion

Methotrexate overdosing is a potential life‐threatening adverse event. The identification and management of such adverse events is challenging in resource‐limited settings. Prevention of methotrexate toxicity requires providing adequate education to patients and their caregivers and timely follow‐up.
