Applying Critical Thinking Frameworks in Public Health: A Systematic Review of Strategic Approaches to Enhance Decision‐Making and Policy Development
Authors: Hussien Saber Hussein, Kaveh Bahmanpour, Mohammad Fathi, Adel Fatemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCritical thinking play a vital role in enhancing public health decision‐making by enabling professionals to assess interventions, address complex challenges, and formulate effective policies. This systematic review aims to evaluate the application of structured critical thinking frameworks in public health, highlighting their effectiveness in improving evidence‐based decision‐making and policy development.MethodsA systematic review was conducted following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar were searched for peer‐reviewed studies published between 1990 and 2024. Studies were eligible if they focused on the application of critical thinking frameworks—specifically Socratic Questioning, Root Cause Analysis, Delphi Method, Scenario Planning, or the Paul‐Elder Critical Thinking Framework—in public health contexts. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed independently by two reviewers using the CASP checklist.ResultsEighty‐six studies were included. Five major frameworks were identified and synthesized: Socratic Questioning promotes analytical dialog and stakeholder engagement; the Paul‐Elder Framework enhances structured reasoning and policy analysis; Scenario Planning supports long‐term adaptive policy development; Root Cause Analysis identifies systemic failures and guides targeted interventions; and the Delphi Method fosters expert consensus in complex decision‐making. These frameworks collectively support improved reasoning, inclusivity, and strategic planning in public health policy and practice.ConclusionStructured critical thinking frameworks contribute significantly to strengthening public health decision‐making and policy formulation. Their integration into public health systems and training programs can enhance policy adaptability, equity, and effectiveness. Future research should explore their long‐term impact across diverse cultural and organizational contexts.Keywords: critical thinking, Delphi Method, Paul‐Elder Framework, policy development, public health, Root Cause Analysis, Scenario planning, Socratic questioning

Introduction

The landscape of public health decision‐making has evolved dramatically over the past decades, shaped by increasingly complex global health challenges, technological advances, and shifting sociopolitical dynamics [1, 2]. Public health practitioners and policymakers face unprecedented challenges that demand sophisticated analytical approaches, from responding to emerging infectious diseases and addressing health inequities to managing the growing burden of non‐communicable diseases within resource‐constrained environments [3]. In this context, critical thinking frameworks have emerged as essential tools for evidence‐based decision‐making and policy development, offering structured approaches to navigate complex health challenges while ensuring systematic and rigorous analysis [4, 5].

Critical thinking in public health encompasses a range of cognitive skills and dispositions, including the ability to analyze complex information, evaluate evidence quality, consider multiple perspectives, and make reasoned judgments in situations of uncertainty [6]. The theoretical foundations of critical thinking in public health draw from diverse disciplines, including cognitive psychology, decision science, and systems thinking, reflecting the multifaceted nature of public health challenges [7]. These theoretical underpinnings have given rise to various frameworks that aim to systematize the application of critical thinking in public health practice [8, 9].

The contemporary public health landscape presents unique challenges that underscore the importance of robust critical thinking frameworks. First, the increasing availability of big data and digital health information has created both opportunities and challenges for evidence‐based decision‐making [10]. Public health practitioners must navigate vast amounts of information, discern relevant patterns, and integrate diverse data sources while maintaining methodological rigor [11]. Second, the growing recognition of social determinants of health and health equity considerations requires frameworks that can accommodate complex causal pathways and intersectoral approaches [12, 13]. Third, the rapid pace of technological change and emerging health threats demands adaptive and responsive decision‐making processes that can evolve while maintaining scientific integrity [14].

Despite the recognized importance of critical thinking in public health practice, several significant gaps persist in our understanding of how these frameworks are conceptualized, implemented, and evaluated across different contexts [15]. Traditional decision‐making models often struggle to capture the dynamic interplay between evidence, values, and contextual factors that characterize public health challenges [16]. Furthermore, there is limited systematic evidence regarding the effectiveness of different critical thinking frameworks in improving public health outcomes or their adaptability across diverse cultural and organizational contexts [17].

The implementation of critical thinking frameworks in public health practice faces several challenges. These include organizational barriers, resource constraints, and the need to balance rigorous analysis with timely decision‐making [18]. Additionally, the integration of critical thinking frameworks with existing public health systems and processes requires careful consideration of institutional capacity, stakeholder engagement, and sustainability factors [19, 20].

This systematic review addresses these knowledge gaps by comprehensively examining the application of critical thinking frameworks in public health decision‐making and policy development. Our objectives are to:

In this review, we conceptualize ‘critical thinking’ as the systematic evaluation of information and reasoning using structured cognitive skills such as analysis, interpretation, inference, explanation, and self‐regulation, as defined by Paul and Elder and other major theorists.

Materials and Methods

Eligibility Criteria

The systematic review was carried out following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [21]. Research was eligible for inclusion if it: (1) concentrated on the use of critical thinking frameworks in public health decision‐making or policy formulation, (2) appeared in peer‐reviewed journals, (3) employed quantitative, qualitative, or mixed‐methods approaches, (4) featured interventions or frameworks such as Socratic questioning, Root Cause Analysis (RCA), Delphi Method, Scenario Planning, or the Paul Elder Critical Thinking Framework, and (5) was published in English. Research that concentrated solely on general healthcare, excluding any aspects of public health policy or decision‐making, was not considered (Table 1).

Sources of Information

An extensive search was performed in electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, encompassing studies released between 1990 and 2024. Moreover, relevant article reference lists were manually examined for further studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria.

Search Approach

The search strategy was created with assistance from a health sciences librarian and included a mix of key terms pertaining to critical thinking frameworks and public health, including “Socratic questioning”, “Root Cause Analysis”, “Delphi Method”, “Scenario Planning”, “Paul Elder Critical Thinking Framework”, “public health decision‐making”, and “policy development”. The complete search strategy is detailed in the additional materials (Table 1 and Figure 1). These five frameworks were selected based on their established application in critical thinking literature and their relevance to public health decision‐making contexts as confirmed during a preliminary scoping review.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews which included searches of databases and registers only.

Selection of Studies

Two separate reviewers screened all collected titles and abstracts for potentially eligible studies. Full‐text articles were then screened for final inclusion. Disagreements between the reviewers were resolved by discussion, and a third reviewer was involved in cases of persistent disagreement.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form. Information collected included study details such as framework(s), outcomes assessed and key findings on the use of critical thinking frameworks in public health. Conflicts about the data collected were resolved by consensus between the reviewers.

Assessment of Risk of Bias

The potential for bias in the included studies was assessed using appropriate tools for each type of study design. The Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist was used for all included research studies [22]. The risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved by discussion.

Data Synthesis

A systematic synthesis method was used to synthesize the results of different studies. A meta‐analysis was not possible because of the variability in study design and results. The synthesis was structured around the key critical thinking frameworks used in public health, focusing on their influence on decision making, policy formulation and outcomes. Qualitative findings were derived from studies using qualitative analysis methods, while quantitative data were presented using descriptive statistics where relevant.

Results

These five frameworks were identified through thematic analysis of the eligible literature, guided by the predefined inclusion criteria and iterative synthesis during data extraction.

Socratic Questioning Framework

Socratic questioning fosters critical thinking and informed decision‐making in public health by promoting open‐ended inquiry, challenging assumptions, and ensuring clarity in discussions [23, 24]. This method deepens understanding of complex health issues, encourages collaboration in safe environments, and strengthens logical reasoning through follow‐up questions. In practice, it engages stakeholders, tailors’ public health strategies, and addresses pressing challenges such as funding gaps, workforce shortages, and health inequities. Furthermore, it advances health education by improving problem‐solving skills and aligns with implementation science to translate evidence‐based research into actionable policies, driving continuous enhancements in public health systems and outcomes [24, 25, 26] (Table 2).

The Paul Elder Critical Thinking Framework

The Paul Elder Critical Thinking Framework is an organized system that improves public health decision making and problem solving by integrating three fundamental principles: Elements of Thought, Intellectual Standards, and Intellectual Characteristics. The Elements of Thought help to identify and analyze public health issues. For example, clarifying the goal of interventions (e.g., improving health equity) establishes specific and measurable objectives, while identifying the relevant question focuses attention on important issues, such as increasing vaccination rates among at‐risk populations [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. By collecting and analyzing relevant data and making interpretations and inferences, public health professionals can draw evidence‐based conclusions about the effectiveness of interventions. In addition, understanding essential concepts such as epidemiology and health equity, challenging beliefs, and evaluating impacts and outcomes leads to culturally sensitive and effective health policies. Considering diverse perspectives promotes inclusiveness and improves public health policies [32, 33, 35, 36, 37] (Figure 3 and Table 3).

Scenario Planning Framework

Scenario Planning is a strategic approach that aids in creating adaptable long‐term plans through the comprehension and anticipation of various potential future settings. In the realm of public health, it entails imagining different situations that might affect health results, the effectiveness of policies, or responses to emergencies. This structure supports critical thinking by prompting public health experts to contemplate various futures, evaluate the effects of diverse health approaches, and ready themselves for an array of potential results(Table 4) [38, 39, 40, 41, 42].

Root Cause Analysis (RCA) Framework

Root Cause Analysis (RCA) is a methodical strategy for pinpointing the fundamental causes of issues or incidents, instead of merely tackling their manifestations [52]. In public health, RCA is utilized to explore the underlying causes of health problems, outbreaks, system breakdowns, or policy shortcomings. This framework fosters critical thinking by urging public health professionals to examine underlying issues rather than just immediate causes [53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58]. The RCA framework has shown to be an effective method for tackling intricate public health issues by methodically recognizing and addressing fundamental causes. The accuracy in identifying problems guaranteed that investigations stayed targeted and actionable, while extensive data gathering, which included epidemiological metrics, social determinants, and environmental influences, offered a complete understanding of the context surrounding the issue. Utilizing tools like Fishbone diagrams and the 5 Whys method, causal factors were methodically charted to distinguish contributing elements from direct causes, facilitating a thorough assessment of their importance and impact. This approach facilitated the discovery of underlying causes, promoting a transition from managing symptoms to focusing on fundamental problems that could avert future occurrences [52, 55, 56, 58, 59] (Table 5 and Figure 4).

Delphi Method Approach in Public Health

The Delphi Method is an organized communication approach, initially created as a systematic, interactive forecasting technique that depends on a group of experts [61, 62]. In public health, it serves to attain consensus on real‐world matters, especially in situations where clear empirical evidence is absent or when various viewpoints must be merged. It improves critical thinking by methodically sharpening expert evaluation via repeated cycles of inquiry and response [61, 62].

The Delphi Method proved to be highly effective in tackling intricate and diverse public health challenges via its organized, repetitive process of consulting experts. By incorporating varied expertise, the approach offered a thorough comprehension of health issues, merging perspectives from epidemiology, policy, community health, and ethics. The development of specific, increasingly refined questions throughout the rounds encouraged a focused investigation into key public health issues. Anonymity in collecting feedback reduced bias and promoted honest opinion sharing, while structured feedback methods allowed participants to enhance their perspectives based on summarized insights and group reasoning from earlier rounds (Table 6) [63, 64, 65, 66].

Discussion

This systematic review provides a comprehensive analysis of critical thinking frameworks in public health decision‐making and policy development, revealing several key insights with significant implications for practice, policy, and research. Our findings demonstrate both the potential and challenges of implementing structured critical thinking approaches in public health contexts.

A primary finding of this review is the inherent complexity in implementing critical thinking frameworks within public health systems. While frameworks such as Socratic Questioning and the Paul‐Elder Critical Thinking Framework offer structured approaches to decision‐making, their effectiveness is heavily influenced by organizational context, institutional capacity, and systemic factors [71]. The most successful implementations were adaptation of the Paul‐Elder framework to fit local decision‐making cultures or used Socratic methods to engage stakeholders in clarifying shared goals and assumptions, suggesting that framework adaptation rather than strict adherence may be more beneficial in real‐world settings [72]. This aligns with recent theoretical work on complex adaptive systems in public health, which emphasizes the importance of context‐sensitive approaches [73].

Our analysis revealed several consistent barriers to framework implementation, including resource constraints, organizational resistance, and the challenge of maintaining systematic approaches during crisis situations [74]. However, we also identified key facilitators of successful implementation. Organizations that invested in capacity building, secured leadership buy‐in, and developed supportive infrastructure demonstrated more sustainable framework adoption [75]. Particularly noteworthy was the role of “framework champions” ‐ individuals who facilitated integration and adaptation of critical thinking approaches within their organizations [76].

The evidence regarding framework effectiveness showed considerable variation across different public health contexts. Frameworks that incorporated explicit consideration of health equity and social determinants of health demonstrated stronger outcomes in addressing complex public health challenges [77]. Additionally, frameworks that facilitated stakeholder engagement and incorporated diverse perspectives tended to produce more comprehensive and sustainable solutions [78]. However, the long‐term impact of framework implementation on population health outcomes remains understudied, highlighting a crucial area for future research.

A significant finding was the importance of cultural and contextual adaptation in framework implementation. Frameworks developed in high‐income countries often required substantial modification for effective use in different resource settings or cultural contexts [79]. This suggests the need for more flexible and adaptable framework designs that can accommodate diverse cultural perspectives and organizational capacities while maintaining core critical thinking principles [80].

The review identified emerging opportunities and challenges related to technology integration in critical thinking frameworks. While digital tools and data analytics offer potential for enhanced decision‐making capabilities, they also introduce new complexities in framework implementation [81]. Organizations successfully leveraging technology demonstrated improved capacity for evidence synthesis and real‐time decision support, but also faced challenges in data quality, integration, and workforce capacity [82].

Implications for Policy and Practice

The findings of this review suggest that integrating structured critical thinking frameworks into public health practice can significantly enhance the quality, adaptability, and inclusivity of policy‐making processes. By fostering deeper analysis, stakeholder engagement, and interdisciplinary collaboration, these frameworks help policymakers navigate uncertainty, synthesize complex data, and formulate context‐sensitive interventions. Frameworks such as the Paul‐Elder model and Socratic questioning are particularly valuable for fostering reflective dialog, clarifying objectives, and challenging assumptions that may otherwise go unexamined in policy environments. Scenario planning and RCA offer tools for forward‐looking and root‐level interventions, respectively—making them highly effective for addressing both emergent threats and systemic issues. Importantly, the Delphi Method empowers policymakers to incorporate expert consensus into areas where empirical evidence is lacking or contested. For maximum impact, public health institutions should embed these frameworks into training curricula, policy development cycles, and institutional decision‐making protocols. Doing so can lead to more resilient systems, greater equity in outcomes, and increased public trust in health policies.

Research Gaps and Future Directions

Although this review synthesizes key insights from the current literature, several areas remain underexplored and warrant further investigation. First, there is a critical need for longitudinal studies that examine the long‐term effects of critical thinking framework implementation on population health outcomes, organizational decision‐making capacity, and policy sustainability. Future research should also explore mechanisms for effective adaptation of existing frameworks across diverse cultural, institutional, and socioeconomic contexts—particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries where public health challenges are often compounded by limited infrastructure. Additionally, research is needed on integrating digital technologies, artificial intelligence, and data analytics into critical thinking processes to improve scalability, responsiveness, and real‐time decision‐making. Investigating ethical implications of such integrations is also essential. Finally, there is a need for implementation science studies that focus on identifying and evaluating strategies that facilitate framework adoption, overcome institutional resistance, and support ongoing evaluation and refinement. Building this evidence base will support more strategic and sustainable integration of critical thinking into public health practice globally.

Limitation

This review acknowledges several methodological and contextual limitations in the application and evaluation of critical thinking frameworks. Firstly, techniques such as the Delphi method and scenario planning are time‐ and resource‐intensive, making them difficult to deploy in fast‐moving health crises or low‐resource settings. Second, the effectiveness of frameworks that rely on diverse stakeholder participation, including Socratic questioning, may be hindered by conflicting interests, cultural tensions, or political barriers that reduce dialog quality or consensus. Moreover, data‐dependent frameworks such as Root Cause Analysis and scenario planning require access to high‐quality, timely, and context‐specific data—which may be lacking or inconsistent in certain regions. The included studies varied widely in design and scope, limiting direct comparability and precluding meta‐analysis. Finally, most of the reviewed frameworks originated from high‐income countries and often required substantial adaptation for use in low‐ and middle‐income settings, raising concerns about contextual generalizability. These limitations highlight the importance of flexible, context‐aware application and underscore the need for capacity building and methodological innovation in framework.

Benefits

Improved Decision Making

Employing critical thinking frameworks allowed stakeholders to make improved, evidence‐driven decisions, resulting in more effective public health initiatives.

Improved Policy‐Making

Models like the Paul Elder Critical Thinking Framework and Socratic questioning facilitated clearer policy creation by prompting stakeholders to examine assumptions and consider various alternatives.

Improved Collaboration

Methodologies like Delphi and RCA encouraged cooperation among experts and stakeholders, guaranteeing that various perspectives were taken into account during the policy development process.

The findings suggest that frameworks for critical thinking are essential in guiding public health decision‐making and policy development. These frameworks support the development of more effective and sustainable public health policies by refining decision‐making processes, promoting collaboration and encouraging more comprehensive assessments of public health challenges. However, difficulties associated with the time and resource requirements of these frameworks and the need for high quality data have been identified as barriers to their widespread adoption. Despite these difficulties, the benefits of using critical thinking frameworks, such as improved outcomes and better‐informed policies, make them an essential resource in public health.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that critical thinking frameworks can significantly enhance public health decision‐making and policy development when properly implemented and adapted to local contexts. However, successful implementation requires careful consideration of organizational capacity, cultural context, and systemic factors. Future research should focus on framework adaptation, long‐term impact assessment, and the integration of emerging technologies while maintaining core critical thinking principles.
