Bridging the Gap Between Depression and Epilepsy: A Call for Integrated Neuropsychiatric Care
Authors: Paolo Brambilla, Reetta Kälviäinen, Bettina Schmitz, Marcin Siwek, Allan H Young
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract“Brain health” encompasses key functions such as cognition, emotion, and behaviour, and is increasingly relevant given its role across neurological and psychiatric conditions. One of these, depression is the most common psychiatric comorbidity in people with epilepsy (PWE). When unrecognized and untreated, depression is associated with increased seizure severity and poorer treatment response, significantly impacting patients’ prognosis and quality of life; conversely, the rate of epilepsy is 2-fold higher in individuals with incident depression compared to those without depression. Several studies have confirmed a strong bidirectional relationship between epilepsy and depression; an advisory panel of psychiatrists and neurologists with expertise in epilepsy and mood disorders convened for a virtual meeting held in 2024 to assess the impact of the interplay between these two conditions. A comprehensive, interdisciplinary approach to discussion was adopted to address challenges in diagnosing and managing depression in PWE, focusing on early intervention, patient education, and tailored treatments strategies; additionally, the meeting emphasized the importance of integrated care between neurologists and psychiatrists to address this unmet medical need. The authors identified depression in PWE as being underdiagnosed due to overlapping symptoms, stigma, and limited psychiatric care integration. Management is challenging as some antiseizure medication worsen depression and certain antidepressants may lower the seizure threshold, requiring careful selection and monitoring to balance efficacy and safety. Potential interactions between these medicines underscore the importance of carefully selecting therapeutic combinations to minimize adverse effects. Effective management involves an interdisciplinary approach, integrating neurologists and psychiatrists. Key strategies include early screening, psychoeducation, a personalized approach to pharmacological and nonpharmacological treatment, and increased awareness among healthcare providers, patients, and caregivers regarding the overlap of neurological and psychiatric disorders. Furthermore, educational resources, as well as digital tools, can help inform and educate patients and caregivers on holistic brain health management.Keywords: epilepsy, depression, treatment resistant epilepsy, multidisciplinary care

Introduction

“Brain health” is a comprehensive concept encompassing optimal brain functioning across cognitive, sensory, social–emotional, behavioural, and motor domains, enabling individuals to reach their full potential throughout life, regardless of the presence or absence of disorders.1 Promoting and understanding brain health is becoming increasingly important due to the growing burden of neurological disorders and their associated comorbidities.2 A key aspect of this broader focus is the recognition of the interplay between neurological and psychiatric conditions. Within this framework, depression and epilepsy are closely interconnected, significantly impacting physical, cognitive, emotional, and social functioning. This highlights the need for integrated approaches to better understand and manage the complex relationship between the two conditions.

Several population-based studies, supported by experimental research, have suggested a bidirectional relationship between major depressive disorders (MDD) and epilepsy. People with epilepsy (PWE) have a 2–3 times greater risk of developing a psychiatric comorbidity than those without,3 with MDD being the most prevalent comorbidity. Conversely, the cumulative incidence of epilepsy after an MDD diagnosis is higher compared to the population without MDD. At 5 years, the incidence is 1.10% (vs 0.32% in the group without depression) increasing to 4.19% at 35 years of follow-up (vs 2.06%).4 Moreover, depression is the most frequently observed psychiatric comorbidity in individuals with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). The pooled prevalence of depression in PWE has been reported as 27% and 34% in population-based and clinical settings, respectively.5 In some studies, the prevalence of depression in patients with uncontrolled epilepsy, has been reported to be as high as 54%.6 Similarly, the hazard ratios for patients with depressive symptoms developing DRE were 3.3 times greater than those for patients without these symptoms.7 Additionally, a strong link has been found between the severity of a newly diagnosed depression and the subsequent development of epilepsy, along with poorer seizure outcomes,8 and evidence has shown that the risk of developing epilepsy is more than two times higher in individuals with incident depression compared with people without depression.5 Moreover, individuals with epilepsy have a higher risk of suicide, even if coexisting psychiatric disease, demographic differences, and socioeconomic factors are taken into account.9

The well-established bidirectional relationship between depression and epilepsy presents significant challenges for both patients and healthcare providers (HCPs). A major gap remains in the awareness, diagnosis, and management of depression in PWE among both HCPs (neurologists, psychiatrists) and patients. Key barriers include under recognition, undertreatment, and overlapping symptoms, which can complicate diagnosis and intervention. Treatment-related challenges further exacerbate these issues.10 Interactions between antiseizure medications (ASMs) and antidepressants (ADs) can influence the efficacy of both treatments and increasing the risk of side-effects.11 Some ADs may lower the seizure threshold, potentially exacerbating epilepsy symptoms in certain patients; similarly, some ASMs have mood-modulating properties, having a significant therapeutic effect on depression symptoms, depressive cognitive symptoms, and related conditions and certain ASMs may also trigger or worsen depressive symptoms.10

This expert opinion paper, developed as an outcome of a multidisciplinary expert meeting, explores the intricate relationship between epilepsy and depression. It highlights the specific challenges identified during the discussions, including the under-recognition of depression in PWE, overlapping clinical manifestations, and treatment interactions. Drawing on expert insights and evidence-based approaches, the paper outlines strategies to address these issues. Key priorities include fostering a structured interdisciplinary collaboration, promoting early diagnosis and intervention, and enhancing education for patients and HCPs to bridge gaps in awareness and management. Finally, it highlights the importance of personalized treatment strategies to optimize outcomes and improve quality of life (QoL) in this population.

Materials and Methods

A multidisciplinary advisory panel of three psychiatrists and two epileptologists from Europe, all with extensive expertise in epilepsy and mood disorders, convened to discuss the interplay between epilepsy and depression. The panel aimed to review emerging evidence, identify current challenges in clinical practice, and propose potential solutions.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and PubMed prior to the meeting. Keywords included “drug-resistant epilepsy”, “antidepressants”, “antiseizure medications”, “uncontrolled seizures”, and “depression in epilepsy”. Additional relevant references were identified through citations in papers focused on depression in epilepsy. In total, 77 key references relevant to depression and epilepsy (including the bidirectional relationship, the impact on severity, treatment challenges, and management strategies) were selected to inform the plenary presentation and discussions.

A virtual expert meeting was held in February 2024. The Chair delivered brief presentations summarizing key data, followed by facilitated discussions to align expert opinions. Epileptologists focused on the direct impact of uncontrolled seizures on mood as well as the possible negative impact of ASMs on mood, behaviour, and cognitive functioning. Psychiatrists highlighted diagnostic challenges and treatment strategies for depression in PWE. Nonpharmacological approaches, interdisciplinary collaboration, and integrated care pathways were also explored. Through these discussions, the panel identified gaps, challenges, and solutions, leading to recommendations summarized in this commentary to guide HCPs, patients, and caregivers.

Results

Shared Molecular Mechanism Between Depression and Epilepsy

Extensive research confirms a bidirectional relationship between epilepsy and depression, though the precise mechanisms remain under investigation.8,12–14 Epilepsy and depression share several molecular mechanisms, including neuroinflammation, neurotransmitter imbalances, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysfunction, and alterations in neuroplasticity. These overlapping pathways contribute to the high comorbidity between the two conditions and have significant clinical implications for diagnosis and treatment.12

Neuroinflammation and Oxidative Stress

Chronic neuroinflammation plays a key role in both epilepsy and depression. Increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-1β, tumor necrosis factor alpha, and IL-6, have been observed in both conditions, contributing to neuronal hyperexcitability and excitotoxicity.15 In epilepsy, IL-1β is involved in seizure propagation and neuronal damage, while in depression, elevated pro-inflammatory markers have been linked to disrupted neurotransmitter function and mood disturbances. Oxidative stress further exacerbates neuroinflammation, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, which is implicated in both epilepsy and depression pathogenesis.15

Neurotransmitter Imbalance

Dysregulation of serotonin (5-HT), dopamine, and glutamate neurotransmission are key features of both epilepsy and depression.16 In depression, abnormal changes in neurotransmission contribute to mood dysregulation, while in epilepsy, alterations in these neurotransmitters can affect seizure susceptibility. Reduced 5-HT1A receptor binding has been observed in patients with temporal lobe epilepsy and comorbid depression, suggesting a shared serotonergic dysfunction.16 Additionally, glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity, which plays a role in seizure generation, is also implicated in depression via synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss.

HPA Axis Dysregulation

Hyperactivity of the HPA axis is a common finding in both conditions.16 Elevated cortisol levels and dysregulated glucocorticoid receptor function are associated with increased seizure susceptibility and depressive symptoms.17 Experimental models of epilepsy-induced depression have demonstrated excessive HPA activation, which further contributes to neuronal excitability and reduced stress resilience.17 Moreover, IL-1β signaling in chronic epilepsy has been shown to activate the HPA axis, leading to increased serotonin auto receptor expression, disrupting serotonergic transmission and worsening depressive symptoms.16

Neuroplasticity and Brain Structural Changes

Neuroplasticity alterations, including hippocampal atrophy, dendritic remodeling, and synaptic dysfunction, are well-documented in both epilepsy and depression.18 Chronic stress and prolonged seizures can lead to hippocampal volume reduction, a key feature in both conditions. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor, a crucial mediator of neuroplasticity, is often reduced in both epilepsy and depression, contributing to cognitive impairment and mood disturbances. The shared structural changes, particularly in the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex, reinforce the bidirectional nature of the relationship between epilepsy and depression.18

Early Screening and Diagnosis

The bidirectional relationship between depression and epilepsy makes diagnosis challenging in both conditions.19 Depression often goes underrecognized in PWE, while epilepsy can remain undiagnosed in people with depression due to overlapping symptoms, seizure-related mood changes, and the impact of ASMs on affective symptoms.19 Early and accurate diagnosis is crucial to improving patient outcomes, requiring a multidisciplinary approach that integrates neurologists, psychiatrists, and other HCPs.

Diagnosis of Depression in People with Epilepsy

Diagnosing depression in PWE is complicated by the diverse ways in which depressive symptoms manifest in the context of epilepsy.19 Mood disturbances may occur years before epilepsy diagnosis, potentially representing a prodromal phase or be a symptom of the etiology of the epilepsy; “in-between seizures” (interictal depression) is the most common form of depression in epilepsy and resembles MDD;19 and “following seizures” (postictal depression) may occur within hours to days after a seizure and as a consequence of epilepsy treatment or disease, due to factors such as medication side-effects, the psychosocial burden, and underlying neurobiological changes.10

Several depression screening tools are available, but their effectiveness varies depending on the extent to which they can distinguish depression from seizure-related mood changes. The Neurological Disorders Depression Inventory for Epilepsy (NDDI-E) is the most widely recommended screening tool due to its specificity for epilepsy-related depression.19 It is validated across multiple languages and patient populations; it is short (six items), making it practical for clinical settings and designed to minimize confounding factors, such as ASM side-effects or cognitive impairments, which often distort results in other depression scales.19

Other commonly used tools include:20 the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), which is comprehensive but may be affected by epilepsy-related cognitive issues; the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-D), which is used in research but is less practical for clinical settings; the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale – Depression Subscale (HADS-D), which is a general tool that does not account for epilepsy-specific factors; the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), which is occasionally used but lacks validation in PWE; the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), which is useful but may overestimate depression due to symptom overlap with epilepsy. Tools such as PHQ-9 or HADS are easy-to-use, time-saving self-assessment scales that do not require any special training, making them suitable for use by clinicians who are not mental health professionals.

Underdiagnosed or untreated depression can negatively affect treatment adherence, complicate seizure control, and worsen overall prognosis.5 Given the overlapping pathophysiology, routine screening for depression in PWE should be standard practice. Early identification can improve both seizure control and mental health outcomes.

Diagnosis of Epilepsy in People with Depression

Epilepsy is also often underdiagnosed in people with depression, particularly in cases of nonconvulsive epilepsy or focal seizures that manifest with subtle symptoms.24 An electroencephalogram (EEG) should be considered in patients with treatment-resistant depression, unexplained cognitive dysfunction, or episodic mood changes.24

A critical consideration when assessing epilepsy in people with depression is the presence of functional seizures (also known as psychogenic non-epileptic seizures).25 These episodes resemble epileptic seizures but are not associated with abnormal electrical activity in the brain; instead, functional seizures are thought to arise from psychological distress, trauma, or underlying psychiatric conditions, particularly depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder.25

Integration of care. Advancing Treatment Strategies

Atypical Features of Depression in Epilepsy

Unlike primary mood disorders, depression in epilepsy frequently presents with nonclassical symptoms, influenced by the interplay between seizure activity, ASM effects, and neurobiological mechanisms.10 This overlap makes it difficult to differentiate primary depressive disorders from epilepsy-related affective disturbances. Furthermore, additional comorbidities may mask or worsen depression. Anxiety symptoms frequently co-occur with depression in PWE, often masking the underlying mood disorder. Dysthymic symptoms and anergia (low energy, fatigue) are more common in epilepsy-related depression than in primary mood disorders10 and disruptions in circadian rhythms are common in PWE28 and contribute to depressive symptoms. These atypical manifestations make early screening, tailored diagnostic criteria, and multidisciplinary assessment crucial to ensure timely intervention and effective management.

The Impact of Seizure Control on Depressive Symptoms

The degree of epilepsy seizure control plays a pivotal role in the development, persistence, and severity of depressive symptoms.3 DRE is strongly associated with higher rates of depression, with prevalence estimates ranging from 30% to 50%. Uncontrolled seizures lead to greater functional impairment, loss of independence, and social isolation, contributing to learned helplessness and chronic stress, which are core factors in depression. Furthermore, the uncertainty and unpredictability of seizures exacerbate anxiety and mood instability, increasing the burden of comorbid depression.3

Successful seizure control, whether through medication, vagus nerve stimulation, or epilepsy surgery, has been linked to improvements in depressive symptoms; however, in some cases, better seizure control does not lead to improvements in depression, likely because both conditions share underlying neurobiological mechanisms rather than one directly causing the other.6

The Impact of Depression on Seizure Control

Depression is not just a comorbidity in epilepsy, it also plays an active role in worsening seizure control. Research suggests that unrecognized or undertreated depression in PWE can negatively influence seizure frequency, treatment adherence, and overall neurological outcomes.26 Studies indicate that PWE with comorbid depression are more likely to experience frequent seizures, even when accounting for epilepsy severity. Longitudinal research suggests that individuals with higher levels of depressive symptoms have a greater likelihood of transitioning from well-controlled epilepsy to DRE.7 Furthermore, patients with depression have a higher risk of experiencing breakthrough seizures, even if they previously had stable control.26 Depression is also associated with dysregulation of neurotransmitters, including serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, all of which influence neuronal excitability and seizure susceptibility. An overactive HPA axis, common in depression, can increase seizure vulnerability by altering cortisol and inflammatory cytokine levels.16

Depressive symptoms such as apathy, cognitive impairment, and hopelessness contribute to poor adherence to ASMs.29 Nonadherence is one of the strongest predictors of breakthrough seizures, increased hospitalization, and epilepsy-related complications. In addition, negative self-perception associated with depression may lead some patients to feel that treatment is futile, resulting in intentional or unintentional medication noncompliance.6

Depression-related insomnia, hypersomnia, and sleep fragmentation increase the risk of seizures. Sleep deprivation is a well-established seizure trigger. In addition, chronic stress, rumination, and anxiety, all common in depression, can lower the seizure threshold. Studies show that stressful life events significantly correlate with an increase in seizure frequency, and depressive patients often experience heightened sensitivity to stress.16

Targeted Pharmacological Interventions

The authors emphasize the importance of understanding both the psychotropic profiles of ASMs and the psychiatric profile of the patients when selecting appropriate treatment strategies. Due to the complex interaction between epilepsy and comorbid depression, clinicians must consider how specific ASMs may influence mood and behaviour. Understanding the shared pathways between epilepsy and depression underscores the need for targeted treatment strategies addressing both seizure control and mood stabilization while minimizing adverse effects and drug interactions; careful drug selection and monitoring are, therefore, crucial to balancing seizure control and mood stabilization. The choice of medication should consider seizure type, psychiatric history, potential drug interactions, and patient-specific risk factors.

In regard to which ASM should be used in PWE and comorbid depression, the authors recommend the use of ASMs that have either mood-stabilizing properties or a low risk for depression. Recent consensus statement supports the use of lamotrigine, valproate, eslicarbazepine acetate, carbamazepine, or oxcarbazepine in patients with depression.30 Evidence suggests that lamotrigine has a positive impact on mood, and few drug interactions with other psychotropic drugs.31 It modulates glutamate release and enhances serotonergic function, making it a suitable option for PWE experiencing depressive symptoms.31 Valproate is commonly used in epilepsy and may help stabilize mood in PWE.30 Carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine have mood-stabilizing effects but may induce sedation and cognitive side-effects.30 In addition, cenobamate has recently been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) as an add-on drug for focal seizures; cenobamate is an ASM with proven effectiveness in patients with highly refractory focal‐onset seizures,32 which might indirectly decrease the associated mood disorders. Conversely, some ASMs may exacerbate depressive symptoms and should be used with caution.31 Levetiracetam, topiramate, and phenobarbital have been associated with mood disturbances, increased irritability, and depressive symptoms.21 Zonisamide has been linked to cognitive impairment and mood changes in some individuals.23

Most of the authors agree that depression manifests differently across neurological conditions, complicating treatment. They emphasize that ADs should be maintained even when depressive symptoms improve, given their integral role alongside ASMs.

Among the authors, epileptologists consistently identified serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) and SSRIs as a common first-line treatments due to their efficacy in moderate to severe depression. Despite the longstanding concern that ADs may lower the seizure threshold,23 the panel agreed that most SSRIs and SNRIs are safe for PWE at therapeutic doses. SSRIs (eg sertraline, escitalopram) are generally well-tolerated and effective for treating depression in PWE. SNRIs (eg venlafaxine, duloxetine) may be considered in cases where SSRIs are insufficient.30 In contrast, bupropion, a norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitor, has a higher seizure risk and is typically avoided in epilepsy.33

In line with epileptologists, psychiatrists reported broader use of drugs such as duloxetine, vortioxetine, sertraline, and citalopram. Vortioxetine showed promise in patients with DRE with high levels of comorbidities, with a minimal impact on epilepsy.34 Sertraline was also discussed, highlighting that more than 50% of PWE experience remission from major depression within 4 months of initiating treatment.35 Additionally, the use of trazodone in treating depression among PWE was a subject of debate due to the lack of evidence supporting its use in this patient population. Trazodone is an antidepressant approved for MDD in adults and has demonstrated efficacy in alleviating depressive symptoms, including anxiety, agitation, and insomnia.36 Drawing on their clinical experience, the authors considered trazodone a potential treatment option for depression in PWE, both as monotherapy and as part of combined approach. Reported advantages included improvements in mood and the management of anxiety and sleep disturbances associated with depression. Nevertheless, further research is warranted to better characterize its efficacy, safety, and place in therapy in this population.

The co-administration of ASM and ADs may result in drug–drug interactions.26 For instance, carbamazepine induces liver metabolites, which accelerates the degradation of ADs and lowers their efficacy.26 Furthermore, other drugs such as barbiturates, phenytoin, and cenobamate are also important inducers of isoenzymes that may cause significant interactions. The potential drug–drug interactions should also be taken into consideration when selecting therapies to treat depression in PWE. Additionally, the possibility of cumulative adverse events associated with co-administration of ASMs and ADs in PWE and depression should be considered and closely monitored. Miziak et al,37 have provided a comprehensive review highlighting that the potential additive effects of ASMs and ADs may have an impact on various traits like body weight or bone health.

Despite these challenges, there are clear benefits to finding and starting appropriate medications that treat depression in PWE; in fact, antidepressive therapy should be continued 6 months after the depressive symptoms have resolved,33,37 as discontinuation may worsen both the depression and epilepsy symptoms (Table 2). With regards to the selection of ADs there is little comparative evidence to support the choice of one antidepressant therapy over another,38 so the therapy should be chosen based on a comprehensive assessment of the characteristics of the disease and the patient’s profile.30 The International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) guidelines recommend psychological treatment, particularly cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), as the first line therapy for mild depressive episodes. For moderate to severe depression, combining psychological therapy with SSRIs is advised.33 Bupropion, clomipramine and maprotiline are less recommended as they appear to have the most pronounced proconvulsant effects.33 A large amount of evidence has demonstrated that most ADs can be safely administered to individuals with epilepsy when prescribed appropriately. Notably, the elevated risk of developing epilepsy following a diagnosis of depression persists even when controlling for antidepressant usage, suggesting a more intricate relationship between these two conditions beyond medication effects.5

Looking ahead, the development of new treatments for epilepsy and comorbid depression should include comprehensive investigations into potential psychiatric risks. Early and effective communication of psychiatric risk data to HCPs will enable more informed prescribing decisions and improved patient outcomes. Additionally, the authors advocate the development of clear guidelines to enable neurologists to treat depression earlier and more effectively. Depression frequently co-occurs with other neurological disorders, making psychiatric expertise essential in managing complex cases. The panel unanimously agreed that ADs should be considered part of the broader treatment plan for PWE. The discussion reinforced the necessity of personalized treatment approaches and collaboration between neurologists and psychiatrists to optimize patient outcomes.

Several nonpharmacological options should also be considered, including neurostimulation techniques (severe depression, treatment-resistant depression), psychotherapy (mild, moderate, or severe depression), and lifestyle interventions (mild to moderate depression).33 Neurostimulation techniques such as vagus nerve stimulation, deep brain stimulation, focal cortical stimulation, and transcranial magnetic simulation have been used in both depression39 and epilepsy,40 but their use may be limited to specific patients and depends on availability with the treating centers/countries. In addition, psychotherapy approaches, such as CBT, have shown significant promise in addressing depression in PWE. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 54 randomized controlled trials including 372 participants with various neurological conditions, including epilepsy, demonstrated that CBT interventions resulted in reduced depression scores.41 Finally, changes to lifestyle, including sleep, diet, and exercise routines can help PWE.42 Exercise has shown benefits in patients with depression43 and in PWE.44 It is important to consider that factors beyond psychopharmacotherapy can also contribute to an increased risk of seizures, and these should be actively managed or eliminated where possible. Antipsychotics (eg clozapine), non-psychiatric medications such as stimulants (eg amphetamine, dextroamphetamine, and methylphenidate),45,46 as well as certain herbal preparations,47 have the potential to induce seizures and should be carefully monitored.

Future Directions

The optimal care of patients with depression and epilepsy should encompass both psychopharmacology and nonpharmacological management, such as psychotherapy and lifestyle interventions to form an integrated care package that addresses both neurological and psychiatric aspects.

To foster and deliver this integrated care approach, it is crucial for both neurologists and psychiatrists to develop an integrative perspective throughout the diagnostic journey and treatment-planning process. Additionally, there is a need to raise awareness among HCPs regarding the overlap of neurological and psychiatric disorders. By adopting a holistic approach in the assessment of depression in PWE, HCPs can potentially mitigate the negative impacts of both conditions, leading to more tailored and effective interventions, resulting in not only improved seizure control and mood management but also enhanced QoL. Similarly, the adoption of a multidisciplinary approach to neuropsychiatric care can help mitigate cognitive impairments associated with epilepsy and depression, improving the neuropsychological functioning and overall cognitive performance.

Materials, such as educational slide decks, infographics, and webinars, could be developed and disseminated via digital platforms to inform and educate on holistic brain health management. For patients, digital care packages may have already been implemented to assist with epilepsy care48 and could be expanded to include also patients presenting with depression in epilepsy. In the medium-to-long term, expert-led consensus guidelines could be developed to increase diagnosis and guide effective earlier treatment selection in these patients. Furthermore, additional research is necessary to better understand the underlying mechanisms linked to brain diseases and to optimize future combination therapies. This includes a comprehensive exploration of potential psychiatric risks when developing treatment options for patients with both epilepsy and comorbid depression. In fact, there are several ongoing trials investigating therapies for depression in PWE.

Conclusions

The intricate and bidirectional relationship between epilepsy and depression presents significant challenges for diagnosis, treatment, and overall patient care. Extensive evidence highlights that both conditions share common neurobiological mechanisms, including disruptions in monoaminergic transmission, HPA axis dysregulation, neuroinflammation, and structural brain changes. These overlapping pathophysiological features underscore the need for routine screening for depression in PWE and for careful assessment of seizure risk in individuals with depression.

Depression in epilepsy frequently manifests with atypical features (such as peri-ictal mood changes, irritability, anxiety, and intermittent symptom-free periods) that may not align with conventional diagnostic criteria such as those in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5 TR). This, coupled with high comorbidity with anxiety disorders and seizure-related phenomena, and the impact of ASMs, complicates diagnosis and contributes to persistent underreporting and undertreatment. Early identification through validated tools such as the NDDI-E, and the implementation of multidisciplinary, integrated care approaches involving neurologists, psychiatrists, and mental health professionals, are essential for improving clinical outcomes.

Depression adversely affects seizure control, treatment adherence, QoL, and long-term prognosis in PWE. Conversely, uncontrolled seizures and the burden of living with epilepsy contribute to the onset and worsening of depressive symptoms. These reciprocal influences highlight the critical need for personalized treatment strategies that account for individual comorbidities, ASM-related mood effects, and patient preferences. Pharmacological management must be carefully tailored to avoid negative interactions between ADs and ASMs. Therefore, while SSRIs and SNRIs are commonly used, it is important to consider alternative ADs with different mechanisms of action, such as trazodone or vortioxetine. These alternatives may provide effective treatment options, including in those cases where first-line treatments prove ineffective or poorly tolerated. Furthermore, considering ASMs with a higher probability of seizure freedom will have a positive impact not only on epilepsy but also on the control of depressive symptoms and anxiety.

Nonpharmacological interventions, including psychoeducation, cognitive behavioral therapy, and digital health tools, can also support holistic care.

An integrated care package, including both pharmacological and lifestyle interventions, is needed to improve outcomes and enhance QoL of patients with depression and epilepsy. Early education for patients about psychiatric comorbidities and available treatment options is essential in preventing double stigmatization and reducing the reluctance to seek treatment. This approach can also significantly improve adherence to current treatment regimens.

Ultimately, a comprehensive, patient-centered strategy that integrates early screening, accurate diagnosis, and individualized treatment can significantly enhance the QoL and outcomes for individuals living with both epilepsy and depression.
