Intraventricular giant cavernous malformation mimicking central neurocytoma characterized by digital subtraction angiography: illustrative case
Authors: Mehek Sharma, Cargill H. Alleyne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Diagnosis is established using MRI, which characteristically demonstrates a heterogeneous popcorn-like lesion with a hemosiderin rim. Cavernous malformations (CMs) are low-flow vascular lesions affecting 0.4%–0.9% of the general population. They commonly manifest as seizures and focal neurological deficits.1 Familial forms are linked to mutations in the CCM1, CCM2, or CCM3 genes and often manifest with multiple lesions.2 CMs occur across all age groups but peak clinically in young to middle-aged adults (20–40 years), with male predominance for hemorrhage risk.3 Pathophysiologically, CMs arise from abnormal endothelial cell junctions lacking pericyte coverage, elastic tissue, and an intact basal lamina, which leads to the formation of dilated thin-walled vascular channels prone to microhemorrhages, hemosiderin deposition, and perilesional gliosis.4,5

Although classically described as angiographically occult due to slow blood transit through dysplastic channels, rare early reports have documented subtle angiographic findings such as dense venous pooling or localized venous stain in intracerebral or intraventricular cases.6 On MRI, CMs typically exhibit a popcorn appearance with mixed signal intensity and a hemosiderin rim.7 In contrast, atypical or giant CMs (> 4 cm) may exhibit heterogeneous enhancement, mass effect, edema, and acute hemorrhage, frequently mimicking high-grade glioma or other intraventricular neoplasms.7 Such imaging overlap can lead to clinically consequential preoperative misdiagnosis, underscoring the importance of recognizing atypical presentations. Here, we present a rare case of an intraventricular giant CM that radiologically mimicked a central neurocytoma.

Illustrative Case

A 30-year-old male presented with a recent-onset seizure and otherwise unremarkable neurological examination. The patient had no significant prior medical history, family history of vascular malformations, or known genetic disorders. No prior neurosurgical or neurological interventions had been performed. MRI of the brain without and with contrast (Stealth protocol; 19 mL of ProHance) demonstrated a large intracranial mass centered in the frontal horn of the left lateral ventricle, measuring 5.55 × 5.6 × 4.3 cm (craniocaudal × anteroposterior × mediolateral). The lesion was heterogeneous and predominantly nonfluid T2 hyperintense, with scattered internal areas of high T1 and low T2 signal intensity. On T1-weighted imaging, the lesion was predominantly hypointense. Following gadolinium administration, there was diffuse heterogeneous enhancement. The lesion demonstrated low signal intensity on gradient echo imaging. Significant mass effect was present, including compression of the right lateral ventricle and approximately 7 mm of left-to-right midline shift, with mild compression of the frontal horn and body of the right lateral ventricle (Fig. 1). No additional intracranial lesions were identified. Based on the patient’s age, intraventricular location, and heterogeneous enhancement, a central neurocytoma was initially favored in the radiological differential diagnosis. However, the lesion demonstrated atypical internal signal characteristics with apparent vascular-type spaces that raised suspicion for an underlying vascular lesion rather than a typical central neurocytoma. Because of these imaging findings, the unusually large lesion size, and concern for a potentially hypervascular intraventricular neoplasm, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed to better characterize its vascular anatomy and determine whether preoperative embolization might be beneficial. Cerebral angiography demonstrated normal opacification of the right and left internal and external carotid arteries without evidence of aneurysm, focal stenosis, or early draining veins. The anterior cerebral arteries were deviated contralaterally due to mass effect from the periventricular lesion. During the arterial phase, minute vessels arising from the lenticulostriate and internal cerebral arteries supplied the lesion, forming a fine meshwork of vascularity. Surgical Removal

The patient underwent a left frontal craniotomy for tumor resection using operative microscopy and Stealth neuronavigation (Medtronic). Resection was aided by use of a Vycor retractor and the Sonopet (Stryker). On microsurgical exposure, it became readily apparent that the lesion was not a central neurocytoma. Rather than the expected gray, solid, lobulated intraventricular mass, the lesion demonstrated the characteristic mulberry-like appearance of a CM composed of thin-walled vascular sinusoids. These characteristic intraoperative findings strongly favored the diagnosis of a CM before histopathological confirmation. An external ventricular drain was placed after resection. Final histopathological evaluation, which returned within 1 week of surgery, confirmed the diagnosis of a CM. Postoperative MRI confirmed gross-total resection with resolution of midline shift. The patient remained neurologically intact and seizure free postoperatively. The external ventricular drain was removed prior to discharge 1 week after surgery. No perioperative complications or adverse events were encountered. Three years postoperatively, he remained neurologically stable without recurrence of the lesion on MRI (clinical timeline summarized in Table 1). Overall, the patient tolerated the intervention well, reporting satisfaction with the surgical outcome and an uncomplicated recovery. Discussion

Observations

CMs are typically considered angiographically occult, low-flow vascular lesions with characteristic MRI features that allow confident noninvasive diagnosis in most cases. Consequently, DSA typically fails to demonstrate direct opacification of the lesion, and diagnosis is generally established using MRI, revealing a heterogeneous popcorn-like lesion with a well-defined lobulated core and surrounding hemosiderin rim on susceptibility-sensitive sequences.8,9 However, rare reports have documented subtle angiographic abnormalities associated with CMs, indicating that these lesions may not always be entirely angiographically occult.

Literature Review

A review of previously reported cases demonstrating angiographic abnormalities in CMs is summarized in Table 2. Ito et al. reported a cavernous hemangioma of the middle cranial fossa demonstrating a feeding artery and tumor stain, which suggests that some lesions may exhibit limited arterial supply detectable on angiography.10 Numaguchi et al. subsequently reported an intraventricular CM with dense venous pooling and localized venous staining on angiography.6 Additional evidence of these indirect angiographic patterns has been reported in larger surgical series. Savoiardo et al. identified capillary blush or early venous drainage in a subset of CMs within a cohort of surgically verified lesions.11 Similarly, Rigamonti et al. reported venous pooling or capillary blush in several lesions examined with conventional carotid angiography.12 Importantly, these findings occurred in the absence of angiographic features characteristic of high-flow vascular malformations, reinforcing that CMs may occasionally produce subtle angiographic abnormalities without demonstrating true arteriovenous shunting. Collectively, these reports suggest that angiographically visible CMs may represent a distinct subset of otherwise angiographically occult lesions. Although the available literature is limited and lesion size was inconsistently reported, several common themes emerge. First, the angiographic abnormalities were predominantly venous in nature, including venous pooling, localized venous staining, capillary blush, and early venous drainage, rather than findings indicative of true arteriovenous shunting. Second, angiographic visibility appears to occur more frequently in atypical lesions, particularly those located within the ventricular system or other uncommon intracranial locations such as the skull base. Notably, both the intraventricular CM reported by Numaguchi et al. and the present case demonstrated venous phase abnormalities despite the traditional expectation that CMs are entirely angiographically occult.6 Third, while lesion size was inconsistently reported, our case raises the possibility that giant CMs may be more likely to demonstrate detectable angiographic findings because increased lesion volume, mass effect, and altered venous drainage may produce subtle angiographic signatures. While a definitive relationship cannot be established from the small number of available cases, these observations suggest that atypical location and altered venous hemodynamics may increase the likelihood of demonstrating angiographic abnormalities.

While our case shares the venous phase predominance described in earlier reports such as that by Numaguchi et al., it differs in that angiography also demonstrated discrete lenticulostriate arterial feeders forming a fine vascular meshwork within the lesion. 6 To our knowledge, this pattern has not previously been described in intraventricular CMs. The unusually large size of the lesion (5.6 cm), associated mass effect with 7 mm of midline shift, and MRI appearance mimicking a central neurocytoma distinguish it further from the smaller lesions reported in earlier series. These features illustrate how DSA can provide valuable preoperative vascular characterization in atypically located intraventricular CMs. Importantly, such findings may be more apparent in giant lesions, in which increased lesion volume and venous outflow obstruction may produce detectable angiographic signatures despite the low-flow nature of CMs.

Role of Angiography

Although MRI remains the primary diagnostic modality for CMs, angiography may still be useful in selected cases, particularly when lesions are large, atypically enhancing, or located in surgically complex regions where vascular supply must be delineated prior to resection. In the present case, angiography provided valuable information regarding arterial supply and venous drainage patterns, assisting in operative planning and exclusion of alternative vascular pathologies such as arteriovenous malformation or aneurysm. While advanced MRI techniques including diffusion-based imaging and perfusion analysis have been explored to improve differentiation between vascular malformations and neoplasms, these approaches remain investigational and are not yet routinely applied to CMs.13,14 In contrast, selective DSA can reveal subtle vascular signatures such as arterial feeders, venous pooling, capillary blush, or venous stasis, which may provide important diagnostic and surgical planning information. In large or unusually located CMs, such as those arising within the ventricular system, angiography may therefore complement MRI by clarifying vascular anatomy and identifying patterns that support the diagnosis of a low-flow vascular lesion.15,16

Surgical Management and Considerations

Resection remains the preferred approach for symptomatic CMs or lesions that are diagnostically indeterminate.17 The intraventricular CM posed unique technical challenges due to its deep location and proximity to critical venous structures. In the present case, preoperative angiography helped delineate the lesion’s arterial supply and venous drainage patterns, and neuronavigated microsurgical dissection and piecemeal removal enabled safe gross‑total resection with preservation of surrounding ventricular structures.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this report include detailed multimodal imaging correlation with MRI and DSA, intraoperative visualization and histopathological confirmation of the lesion, and long-term clinical and radiographic follow-up demonstrating durable seizure freedom and the absence of recurrence. The case also contributes a rare description of angiographic findings in a giant intraventricular CM with comparison to the existing literature. A key limitation of this report is its single-patient design, which limits generalizability. The observed angiographic findings, including lenticulostriate arterial supply and venous stasis, may be related to the lesion’s large size and intraventricular location and may not be representative of smaller, typical CMs that are usually angiographically occult. Further cases are needed to better define the utility of DSA in atypical presentations.

Lessons

This case highlights an uncommon but important phenomenon: CMs may occasionally demonstrate detectable angiographic abnormalities despite their traditional classification as angiographically occult lesions. Recognition of these subtle patterns, including tumor stain, venous pooling, capillary blush, or venous stasis, can aid clinicians in interpreting atypical angiographic findings and avoiding misclassification as higher-flow vascular malformations.
