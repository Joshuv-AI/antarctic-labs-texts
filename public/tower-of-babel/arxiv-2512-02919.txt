Congruences for the ratios of Rankin–Selberg L L -functions
P. Narayanan & A. Raghuram

Abstract
. A well-known principle states that a congruence between objects should give rise to a corresponding congruence between the special values of L L -functions attached to these objects. In this article we computationally investigate this principle for Rankin–Selberg L L -functions attached to pairs of holomorphic cuspforms.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
11F33; 11F30, 11F67

1.
Introduction

A well-known principle with origins in Iwasawa theory states that a congruence between objects should translate to a congruence between the special values of

L
-functions attached to these
objects. The reader is referred to Vatsal
[
19
]
for an instance of this principle, and for a brief discussion of its historical origins. In this article we computationally
investigate this principle for Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions attached to pairs of holomorphic cuspforms.

Suppose we have two primitive
holomorphic cusp forms
f,f^{\prime}\in S_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
; by a form being primitive, one means it is an eigenform, a newform, and is normalised by taking the first
Fourier coefficient to be
1
.
Suppose

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a(n,f)e^{2\pi inz}
and
f^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a(n,f^{\prime})e^{2\pi inz}
are their Fourier expansions.
Let
\mathbb{Q}(f)
(resp.,
\mathbb{Q}(f^{\prime})
) denote the number field
generated by the Fourier coefficients of
f
(resp.,
f^{\prime}
), and take
F
to be any number field containing
\mathbb{Q}(f)
and
\mathbb{Q}(f^{\prime})
.
Let
\mathfrak{P}
be a prime ideal of the ring of integers of

F
. We say
f
is congruent to
f^{\prime}
modulo
\mathfrak{P}
if
a(n,f)\equiv a(n,f^{\prime})\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}
for all
n\geq 1
; denote this congruence as
f\equiv f^{\prime}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}.

Suppose
g\in S_{l}(\Gamma_{1}(M))
.
For the Rankin–Selberg
L
-function
L(s,f\times g)
to have critical points one needs
k\neq l
.
Assume for the moment that
k>l
. Then the set of critical points of
L(s,f\times g)
is
\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:l\leq m\leq k-1\}
. Since
f
and
f^{\prime}
have the same weight,
this set is also the set of critical points for
L(s,f^{\prime}\times g).

For any of these critical points, one expects a congruence between the algebraic parts of
L(m,f\times g)
and
L(m,f^{\prime}\times g).

Of course, one has to be careful in clarifying what one means by the algebraic part of an
L
-value. Keeping in mind the shape of the rationality
results due to Shimura for these
L
-values (see Thm.
2.7
), one observes that if one had at least two critical points, then the ratio

L(m,f\times g)/L(m+1,f\times g)
of successive critical values for the completed
L
-function is algebraic and
lies in the compositum of the number fields
\mathbb{Q}(f)
and
\mathbb{Q}(g)
.
Let us enlarge the number field
F
above also to contain
\mathbb{Q}(g)
. The purpose of this paper is to investigate the principle:

f\equiv f^{\prime}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}\ \ \implies\ \ \frac{L(m,f\times g)}{L(m+1,f\times g)}\equiv\frac{L(m,f^{\prime}\times g)}{L(m+1,f^{\prime}\times g)}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}.

It is possible that the ratios of
L
-vaues are not integral in
F
; the congruence is to interpreted as the difference of the
ratios has a positive
\mathfrak{P}
-adic valuation.

An obvious variation to consider is to take
k<l,
i.e., freeze the larger weight cuspform
f
and take two congruent cuspforms

g,g^{\prime}
of smaller weight. One may also consider a
congruence between a cuspform and an Eisenstein series such as the celebrated Ramanujan congruence.
If
g
is an Eisenstein series then
L(s,f\times g)
is a product of the form

L(s,f\otimes\chi_{1})\cdot L(s,f\otimes\chi_{1})
for algebraic Hecke characters
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
of
\mathbb{Q}
.

In this article, we verify that the expected congruences hold in the following cases:

(1)

f,f^{\prime}\in S_{24}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
,
\mathfrak{P}=144169
,
g\in S_{12}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
.

(2)

f,f^{\prime}\in S_{30}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
,
\mathfrak{P}=51349
,
g\in S_{12}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
.

(3)

f,f^{\prime}\in S_{13}(\Gamma_{0}(3),\chi)
,
\mathfrak{P}=13
,
g\in S_{6}(\Gamma_{0}(3))
.

(4)

f\in S_{26}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
, and
g,g^{\prime}\in S_{13}(\Gamma_{0}(3),\chi)
,
\mathfrak{P}=13
.

(5)

f\in S_{24}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
,
g\in S_{12}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
,
g^{\prime}
a weight
12
Eisenstein series,
\mathfrak{P}=691.

(This is the Ramanujan congruence.)

In (1) and (2),
f^{\prime}
is a Galois conjugate of
f
, but in (3)
f^{\prime}
is not a Galois conjugate of
f
.

The computations rely on two algorithms.
The first algorithm, reviewed in
2.2
, computes the special values of Rankin-Selberg
L
-functions, and is essentially due to Shimura and Hida.
Roughly speaking, it relies on the interpretation of the
L
-value as a Petersson inner product of a cusp form with a holomorphic projection which is
due to Shimura
[
15
]
, and the computation of the holomorphic projection recursively from the given data and an appropriate Eisenstein series
due to Hida
[
5
]
.
The second well-known algorithm, reviewed in
2.3
,
computes the
L
-values of modular forms from the Hecke-equivariant pairing between a space of cusp forms
and a suitable space of modular symbols. It should be mentioned that there is also an algorithm by Tim Dokchitser
[
2
]
to compute the
L
-values of modular
forms numerically.
All the computations in this paper are done on SAGE
[
14
]
.

Based on these examples, we formulate a conjecture in Conj.
4.1
. In the companion article
[
11
]
, we prove a
variation of this conjecture using the machinery of Eisenstein cohomology as developed by
Harder and the second author in
[
3
]
. As the proofs in
[
3
]
involves a cohomological interpretation of
Langlands’s theorem on the constant term of an Eisenstein series, the results and proofs in
[
11
]
are completely independent of
the computational techniques this paper.

2.
Preliminaries and the main algorithms

In this section, we begin by reviewing some preliminaries from Shimura
[
15
]
on modular forms
and the special values of Rankin-Selberg
L
-functions and some results of Hida
[
5
]
on the holomorphic projection.
We present two algorithms which will be used to verify the congruences mentioned in the introduction.

2.1.
Review of some results of Shimura and Hida

Let
\Gamma_{1}(N)
be the standard Hecke congruence subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
; the volume of
its fundamental domain
\Phi_{1}(N)
with respect to the measure
y^{-2}dxdy
will be denoted
\mu(\Phi_{1}(N)).
Let
M_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
be the space of holomorphic modular forms of weight
k
for
\Gamma_{1}(N)
.
For
f,\,h\in M_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
such that
fh
is a cusp form, define the Petersson inner product
\langle f,h\rangle
by:

\langle f,h\rangle\ :=\ \mu(\Phi_{1}(N))^{-1}\int_{\Phi_{1}(N)}\overline{f(z)}h(z)y^{k-2}\,dxdy.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character modulo
N
.
Let
M_{k}(N,\chi)
(resp.,
S_{k}(N,\chi)
) denote the space of holomorphic modular (resp., cusp) forms of weight
k
for
\Gamma_{0}(N)
with nebentypus character
\chi
.
Assume
l<k
.
Suppose
f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a(n,f)q^{n}\in S_{k}(N,\chi)
and
g=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a(n,g)q^{n}\in M_{l}(N,\psi),
where
q=e^{2\pi iz}
,
then the Rankin–Selberg convolution is defined as the Dirichlet series:

D(s,f\times g)\ :=\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a(n,f)a(n,g)n^{-s}.

For an integer
\lambda\geq 0
and a Dirichlet character
\omega
modulo
N
such that
\omega(-1)=(-1)^{\lambda}
, define an Eisenstein series:

\displaystyle E^{*}_{\lambda,N}(z,s,\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{0}(N)}\omega(d)(cz+d)^{-\lambda}|cz+d|^{-2s},\quad(\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}),

where
\Gamma_{\infty}=\bigl\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&m\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}|m\in\mathbb{Z}\bigr\}
.
For the same
\lambda
and
\omega,
define:

E_{\lambda,N}(z,s,\omega)=\sum_{(0,0)\neq(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\omega(n)(mNz+n)^{-\lambda}|mNz+n|^{-2s}.

The following relation holds between
E_{\lambda,N}^{*}
and
E_{\lambda,N}
:

E_{\lambda,N}(z,s,\omega)=2L_{N}(2s+\lambda,\omega)E^{*}_{\lambda,N}(z,s,\omega),

where
L_{N}(s,\omega)
denotes the Dirichlet
L
-series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega(n)/n^{s}
and the subscript
N
means that
with
\omega(n)=0
if
{\rm gcd}(n,N)\neq 1.
If
\omega
is primitive modulo
N
then this condition is automatic and the subscript may be dropped.
One has the following integral representation:

2(4\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L_{N}(2s+2-k-l,\chi\psi)D(s,f\times g)\ =\\
\int_{\Phi_{0}(N)}\bar{f_{\rho}}g\cdot E_{k-l,N}(z,s+1-k,\chi\psi)\,y^{s-1}\,dxdy.

Here
\Phi_{0}(N)
is the fundamental domain for
\Gamma_{0}(N)\backslash\mathbb{H}
and
f_{\rho}(z)=\overline{f(-\overline{z})}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\overline{a_{n}}e(nz)
. Define
E_{\lambda,N}^{*}(z,\omega)=E_{\lambda,N}^{*}(z,0,\omega)
.

The Maaß–Shimura differential operators on the space of smooth functions on the Poincare upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
by

\delta_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2iy}+\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right),\ \ 0<\lambda\in\mathbb{Z},\quad{\rm and}\quad\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}=\delta_{\lambda+2r-2}\cdots\delta_{\lambda+2}\delta_{\lambda},\ \ 0\leq r\in\mathbb{Z}.

It is understood that
\delta_{\lambda}^{(0)}
is the identity operator. Define
d=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}=q\frac{d}{dq}.
Then

(1)

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}\ =\ \sum_{0\leq t\leq r}\binom{r}{t}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+r)}{\Gamma(\lambda+t)}(-4\pi y)^{t-r}d^{t},

where the symbol
d^{0}
means the identity operator; see
[
15
]
. The action of these operators on
1/4\pi y
is given by the following

Lemma 2.1
.

d^{n}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi y}\right)=\frac{n!}{(4\pi y)^{n+1}},\quad n=0,1,2,\dots,

\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi y}\right)=(-4\pi y)^{-(r+1)}\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{r}\binom{r}{t}(-1)^{t+1}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+r)\Gamma(t+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda+t)}.

Proof.

The first is proved by induction on
n
; the second from the first and (
1
).
∎

Let
A_{r}
denote the set of all functions of the form
h(z)=\sum_{\nu=0}^{r}y^{-nu}g_{\nu}(z)
with holomorphic functions
g_{\nu}
on
\mathbb{H}
which have
Fourier expansions
g_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{\nu n}q^{n}
. Elements of
A_{r}
may be called
nearly holomorphic modular forms.

The following lemma is
[
15
, Lem. 7]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For a positive integer
k>2r
and a Dirichlet
\chi
modulo
N
, suppose that an element
h
of
A_{r}
satisfies

(2)

\begin{split}h(\gamma(z))(cz+d)^{-k}\in A_{r}\>\>\mbox{for all}\>\>\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\\
h(\gamma(z))(cz+d)^{-k}=\chi(d)h(z)\>\>\mbox{for all}\>\>\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(N).\end{split}

Then
h(z)=\sum_{\nu=0}^{r}\delta_{k-2\nu}^{(\nu)}h_{\nu},
with
h_{\nu}
of
M_{k-2\nu}(N,\chi)
being uniquley determined by
h
.

The following orthogonality relation is
[
15
, Lem. 6]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
f\in S_{k}(N,\chi)
,
g\in M_{l}(N,\bar{\chi}),
and
k=l+2r
with a positive integer
r
. Then
\langle f_{\rho},\delta_{l}^{(r)}g\rangle=0
.

As a consequence of the above two lemmas one has:

Lemma 2.4
.

\langle f_{\rho},\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}E_{\lambda,N}\rangle\ =\ \langle f_{\rho},h_{0}\rangle.

The following theorem is one of the main results from
[
15
]
:

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose
f\in S_{k}(N,\chi),

g\in M_{l}(N,\psi)
, and
l+2r<k
with a non-negative integer
r
. Then

D(k-1-r,f\times g)\ =\ c_{r}\pi^{k}\langle f_{\rho},\ g.\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}E_{\lambda,N}^{*}(z,\chi\psi)\rangle,

where
\lambda=k-l-2r
, and
c_{r}
is the rational number explicitly given by:

c_{r}\ =\ \frac{\Gamma(k-l-2r)}{\Gamma(k-1-r)\Gamma(k-l-r)}.\frac{(-1)^{r}4^{k-1}N}{3}\prod_{p|N}(1+p^{-1}),

where the product is taken over all prime factors
p
of
N
.

When
\lambda=2
and
\omega=1\!\!1
is the trivial character modulo
N
, then
E_{\lambda,N}^{*}(z,\omega)
is not holomorphic at
\infty
;
its Fourier expansion at
\infty
is given by

E_{2,N}^{*}(z,1\!\!1)\ =\ \frac{c}{4\pi y}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}q^{n},

where
c
and all the
c_{n}
are in
\mathbb{Q}
. This is due to Hecke; see, for example, Miyake
[
9
, p. 288]
.

Let
f\in S_{k}(N,\chi)
and
g\in M_{k}(N,\psi)
. Then as noted above

E_{\lambda,N}^{*}(z,\chi\psi)\ =\ \frac{c}{4\pi y}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}q^{n}\ =:\ \frac{c}{4\pi y}+E,

with
c\neq 0
only when
\lambda=2
and
\chi\psi=1\!\!1
. Then, by Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2

(3)

g.\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}E_{\lambda,N}^{*}=g\cdot\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}\left(\frac{c}{4\pi y}+E\right)\\
=g\cdot\frac{c\cdot c^{\prime}}{(-4\pi y)^{r+1}}+g\cdot\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}(E)=\begin{cases}\sum_{\nu=0}^{r+1}\delta_{k-2\nu}^{(\nu)}h_{\nu}&\mbox{if $\chi\psi=1\!\!1,\lambda=2$,}\\
\sum_{\nu=0}^{r}\delta_{k-2\nu}^{(\nu)}h_{\nu}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
h_{\nu}\in M_{k-2\nu}(N,\chi\cdot\psi)
and
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}
is obtained from Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 2.6
.

The
h_{\nu}
, as in equation (
3
), satisfy the following recursive relations:

(i)

When
\lambda=2
and
\chi\psi=\mbox{trivial}
then

\frac{\Gamma(k-(r+1))}{\Gamma(k-2(r+1))}h_{r+1}=cg\sum_{t=0}^{r}\binom{r}{t}(-1)^{t+1}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+r)\Gamma(t+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda+t)}

and

\frac{\Gamma(k-\nu)}{\Gamma(k-2\nu)}h_{\nu}=\binom{r}{r-\nu}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+r)}{\Gamma(\lambda+r-\nu)}gd^{r-\nu}E_{\lambda,N}^{*}(z,\chi\psi)\\
-\sum_{n=\nu+1}^{r+1}\binom{n}{n-\nu}\frac{\Gamma(k-n)}{\Gamma(k-n-\nu)}d^{n-j}h_{n}\qquad\text{for $\nu=0,1,\dots,r$}.

(ii)

When
\lambda\neq 2
or
\chi\psi\neq\mbox{trivial}
then

\frac{\Gamma(k-\nu)}{\Gamma(k-2\nu)}h_{\nu}=\binom{r}{r-\nu}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+r)}{\Gamma(\lambda+r-\nu)}gd^{r-\nu}E_{\lambda,N}^{*}(z,\chi\psi)\\
-\sum_{n=\nu+1}^{r}\binom{n}{n-\nu}\frac{\Gamma(k-n)}{\Gamma(k-n-\nu)}d^{n-j}h_{n}\qquad\text{for $\nu=0,1,\dots,r$}.

Proof.

Statement
(ii)
is proved in Hida
[
5
, p.177]
. For
(i)
, when
\lambda=2
and
\chi\psi=\mbox{trivial}
,

g.\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}E_{\lambda,N}^{*}=g\cdot\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}\left(\frac{c}{4\pi y}+E\right)=g\cdot\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}\left(\frac{c}{4\pi y}\right)+g\cdot\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}\left(E\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{r+1}\delta_{k-2n}^{(n)}h_{n}.

Use (
1
) and expand both sides of the above equation to get:

g\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{r}\binom{r}{t}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+r)}{\Gamma(\lambda+t)}(-4\pi y)^{t-r}d^{t}\left(\frac{c}{4\pi y}+E\right)\\
=\sum_{n=0}^{r+1}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\binom{n}{t}\frac{\Gamma(k-n)}{\Gamma(k-2n+t)}(-4\pi y)^{t-n}(d^{t}h_{n}).

The left hand side of the equation becomes

cg\cdot(-4\pi y)^{-(r+1)}\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{r}\binom{r}{t}(-1)^{t+1}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+r)\Gamma(t+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda+t)}+g\cdot\delta_{\lambda}^{(r)}E.

By comparing the coefficients of
(-4\pi y)^{t}
for
t=0,1,\dots,r+1,
one gets the desired equality.
∎

For a nomalized eigenform
f\in S_{k}(N,\chi)
, and a modular form
g\in M_{l}(N,\psi)
, define the completed
Rankin–Selberg
L
-function (with a ‘classical’ normalization) as:

(4)

L(s,f\times g)=L_{\infty}(s,f\times g)L_{N}(2s+2-k-l,\chi\psi)D(s,f\times g),

where
L_{\infty}(s,f\times g)=(2\pi)^{-2s}\Gamma(s)\Gamma(s+1-l)
is the archimdean factor and for a
Dirichlet character
\omega
modulo
N
put
L_{N}(s,\omega)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega(n)n^{-s}
with
\omega(n)=0
if
(n,N)=1.

If
g
is also a normalized eigenform in
S_{l}(N,\psi),
then
L(s,f\times g)
satisfies a functional equation

(5)

L(s,f\times g)\approx L(k+l-1-s,f^{\rho},g^{\rho}).

See Shimura
[
15
, Section 3]
and Hida
[
5
, Section 9]
. The line of symmetry for this functional equation is
\text{Re}(s)=(k+l-1)/2.

An integer
m
is
critical
for
L(s,f\times g),
if the Gamma factors on both sides of the functional equation are finite at
s=m,
i.e.,
if
\Gamma(m)\Gamma(m+1-l)
and
\Gamma(k+l-1-m)\Gamma(k-m)
are finite.
Therefore the critical set is:

\{m\in\mathbb{Z}\,|\,l\leq m\leq k-1\}.

The following result of Shimura gives an algebraicity theorem for the values
of the Rankin–Selberg convolution at critical points to the right of the line of symmetry:

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
f
be a normalized eigenform of
S_{k}(N)
,
g
an element of
G_{l}(N)
, and
m
a positive integer. Suppose
l<k
and
\frac{1}{2}(k+l-2)<m<k
. Then
\pi^{-k}\langle f,f\rangle^{-1}D(m,f\times g)

belongs to
\mathbb{Q}(f)\mathbb{Q}(g)
, the compositum of
\mathbb{Q}(f)
and
\mathbb{Q}(g)
. Moreover, for every automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{C}
, we have

\sigma\left(\pi^{-k}\langle f,f\rangle^{-1}D(m,f\times g)\right)\ =\ \pi^{-k}\langle f^{\sigma},f^{\sigma}\rangle^{-1}D(m,f^{\sigma}\times g^{\sigma}).

One deduces that for an integer
m
with
\frac{1}{2}(k+l-2)<m<m+1<k
then

\frac{D(m,f\times g)}{D(m+1,f\times g)}\in\mathbb{Q}(f)\mathbb{Q}(g).

The parameters determining the abelian part and the infinite part of
L(s,f\times g)
in (
4
)
depend only on the weights and the nebentype characters involved. So for two
f,f^{\prime}\in S_{k}(N,\chi)
and
g\in M_{l}(N,\psi)
one has

(6)

L_{\infty}(s,f\times g)L_{N}(2s+2-k-l,\chi\psi)=L_{\infty}(s,f^{\prime}\times g)L_{N}(2s+2-k-l,\chi\psi).

For brevity, let
\omega=\chi\psi.

Suppose
\omega
is a Dirichlet character modulo
N
which is primitive.
Then
L_{N}(s,\omega)=L(s,\omega)
. Let
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
such that
\omega(-1)=(-1)^{\epsilon}
. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\equiv\epsilon\pmod{2}
it is well known that

L(m,\omega)=(-1)^{1+(m-\epsilon)/2}\frac{\mathfrak{g}(\omega)}{2\sqrt{-1}^{\epsilon}}\Bigl(\frac{2\pi}{N}\Bigr)^{m}\frac{B_{m,\bar{\omega}}}{m!},

where
\mathfrak{g}(\omega)
is the Gauss sum associated to
\omega
and
B_{m,\bar{\omega}}
is the generalized Bernoulli number for the character
\bar{\omega}=\omega^{-1}
. See Neukirch
[
12
]
. From this it follows that

(7)

\frac{L(m,\omega)}{L(m+2,\omega)}=(m+2)(m+1)\Bigl(\frac{N}{2\pi}\Bigr)^{2}\frac{B_{m,\bar{\omega}}}{B_{m+2,\bar{\omega}}}.

If
\omega
modulo
N
is not primitive take
\omega^{\text{prim}}
to be the primitive Dirichlet character defined by
\omega
.
Then
L_{N}(s,\omega)=\prod_{p|N}\left(1-\omega^{\text{prim}}(p)p^{-s}\right)L(s,\omega^{\text{prim}}).

Hence, from (
7
) and Theorem
2.7
, for any integer
m
with
\frac{k+l-2}{2}<m<m+1<k
, it follows that:

(8)

\frac{L(m,f\times g)}{L(m+1,f\times g)}\ \in\ \mathbb{Q}(f)\mathbb{Q}(g).

This is proved in greater generality in the case of
L
-functions attached to cohomological cuspidal automorphic representations of

({\rm GL}_{n}\times{\rm GL}_{n^{\prime}})/F,
when
nn^{\prime}
is even and
F
a totally real number field, by Harder and the second author
[
3
]
.

2.2.
An algorithm for the algebraic part of
D(m,f\times g)

If
f\in S_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
is a newform then

f_{\rho}=\overline{f(-\overline{z})}=f^{\rho}
is again a newform of
S_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N)),

where, recall that for any automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{C}
, one defines

f^{\sigma}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a(n,h)^{\sigma}q^{n}.

Input:

f\in S_{k}(N,\chi)
a newform of level
N,
and
g\in S_{l}(N)
an arbitrary cusp form with algebraic Fourier coefficients.

Output:
The algebraic number
\frac{D(m,f\times g)}{\pi^{k}\langle f,f\rangle}

for
\frac{k+l-2}{2}<m<k.

Step 1.

Extend
f_{\rho}
to a basis consisting of eigenforms
S_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
which we denote by

\mathcal{B}=\{f_{0}:=f_{\rho},f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\}
. (The dimension is
n
.)

Step 2.

2.3.1.
Special values of
L
-functions via modular symbols

Let
\Gamma
be a congruence subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}.

Define
\mathbb{M}_{2}
to be the free abelian group on the symbols

modulo the relations

\{\alpha,\beta\}+\{\beta,\gamma\}+\{\gamma,\alpha\}=0
for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
and modulo all torsion.
Let
\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]_{n}
be the abelian group of homogeneous polynomials of degree
n
in two variables
X
and
Y
.
Make
\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]_{n}
into a
\Gamma
-module: if
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma
and

P\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]_{n}
then define
(\gamma P)(X,Y)=P(dX-bY,-cX+aY).
The abelian group
\mathbb{M}_{2}
can also be made into a
\Gamma
-module by the action
g\{\alpha,\beta\}:=\{g\alpha,g\beta\}
for all
g\in\Gamma
and
\{\alpha,\beta\}\in\mathbb{M}_{2}
. Define the
\Gamma
-module
\mathbb{M}_{k}=\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]_{k-2}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{M}_{2}

with the
\Gamma
acting diagonally.

Definition 2.1
.

(Modular Symbols)
For an integer
k\geq 2
and a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
, the space
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\Gamma)
of weight
k
modular symbols for

\Gamma
is the quotient of
\mathbb{M}_{k}
by all the relations
\gamma x-x
for
x\in\mathbb{M}_{k}
, and
\gamma\in G
and by any torsion.

For
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]_{k-2}
and
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
the associated
Manin symbol
is

[P,\gamma]:=\gamma(P\otimes\{0,\infty\})\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\Gamma)

When
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}(N)
and if
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right),\ \gamma^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a^{\prime}&b^{\prime}\\
c^{\prime}&d^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{1}(N)

are such that
(c,d)\equiv(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})\pmod{N}
, then
[P,\gamma]=[P,\gamma^{\prime}]
. Hence, the Manin symbol
[P,\gamma]
is determined by

P
and the lower two entries
c,d
of the matrix
\gamma
. So we take
[P,(c,d)]
itself to be a Manin symbol. For the following theorem, see,
for example, Stein
[
17
, Prop. 8.3]
.

Theorem 2.8
.

The Manin Symbols generate
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\Gamma).

One can define Hecke operators on the space of modular symbols; see Stein
[
17
, Section 8.3]
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
S_{k}(\Gamma),\bar{S}_{k}(\Gamma)
denote the space of holomorphic and anti-holomorphic cusp forms respectively. Then the pairing

(\cdot,\cdot):(S_{k}(\Gamma)\oplus\bar{S}_{k}(\Gamma))\times\mathbb{M}_{k}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

((f_{1},f_{2}),P\otimes\{\alpha,\beta\})\ \mapsto\ \int_{\alpha}^{\beta}f_{1}(z)P(z,1)dz+\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}f_{2}(z)P(\bar{z},1)d\bar{z}

is Hecke equivariant, i.e., for
x\in\mathbb{M}_{k}(\Gamma)
we have

(T_{n}(f_{1},f_{2}),x)=(f_{1},f_{2},T_{n}(x)).

For a cusp form
f\in S_{k}(N)
, the associated
L
-function is

(9)

D(s,f)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{s}},\quad{\rm and}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}f(it)t^{s}\frac{dt}{t}=(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)D(s,f),\quad\Re(s)\gg 0.

Consider the modular symbols
X^{j}Y^{k-2-j}\otimes\{0,\infty\}
for
j=0,1,2,\dots,k-2
.
Then the non-degenerate and Hecke equivariant bilinear pairing
(\cdot,\cdot)
of these modular symbols against the cusp form
f
is equal to

(f,X^{j}Y^{k-2-j}\otimes\{0,\infty\})=\int_{0}^{i\infty}f(z)z^{j}dz=i^{j+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(it)t^{j}dt.

Hence, by (
9
), we get the following relation between the bilinear pairing and the special value of
L
-function. For
m=1,2\dots,k-1
,

(10)

\displaystyle D(m,f)=\frac{(-2\pi\sqrt{-1})^{m}}{(m-1)!}\cdot(f,X^{m-1}Y^{k-2-(m-1)}\otimes\{0,\infty\}).

For
m=1,2\dots,k-1
, define algebraic part of
L(m,f)
by:

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{D(m,f)}{(-2\pi\sqrt{-1})^{m-1}D(1,f)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\cdot\frac{(f,X^{m-1}Y^{k-2-(m-1)}\otimes\{0,\infty\})}{(f,Y^{k-2}\otimes\{0,\infty\})}\;\;\text{for }m=1,3,\dots,

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{D(m,f)}{(-2\pi\sqrt{-1})^{m-2}D(2,f)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\cdot\frac{(f,X^{m-1}Y^{k-2-(m-1)}\otimes\{0,\infty\})}{(f,X^{1}Y^{k-3}\otimes\{0,\infty\})}\;\;\text{for }m=2,4,\dots.

2.3.2.
The algorithm

Input:

f\in S_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
a newform. A prime
p
for which
a(p,f)
is also given.

Output:

\frac{D(m,f)}{(-2\pi\sqrt{-1})^{m-1}D(1,f)}
for
m=1,3,\dots,
and

\frac{D(m,f)}{(-2\pi\sqrt{-1})^{m-2}D(2,f)}
for
m=2,4,\dots
.

Step 1.

For a given eigenform
f\in S_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
construct the space of modular symbols
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N)).

Step 2.

Find a Manin basis for
\mathbb{M}_{k}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
. Call them
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}
.
Express the modular symbols
b_{i}:=X^{i-1}Y^{k-2-(i-1)}\otimes\{0,\infty\}
for
i=1,2,\dots,k-1
in terms of the basis
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}
.
Say
b_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ji}e_{j}
for
i=1,2,\dots,k-1
. Let
A
be the matrix
[a_{ij}]
.

Step 3.

For a prime
p
find the matrix of the
p
-th Hecke operator
T_{p}
with respect to the basis
e_{1},e_{2},\cdots e_{n}
. Call that matrix to be
B=[b_{ij}].

Step 4.

Form a matrix
\mathcal{L}
as follows:

\mathcal{L}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}I_{k-1}&A^{t}\\
\hline\cr\\
\mathbf{0}_{n,k-1}&B-a(p,f)\cdot I_{n}\end{array}\right).

Step 5.

Perform row reduction on
\mathcal{L}
. Call the resulting matrix
\mathcal{L}_{\text{red}}
. For the values
i=1,3,5\cdots
, find a column in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{red}}
such that the first entry of the column is non-zero. Let such a column be
C_{\text{odd}}=(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n+k-1})^{t}
. So
c_{1}\neq 0.
Similarly, find a column
C_{\text{even}}=(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{n+k-1})^{t}
of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{red}}
such that
d_{2}\neq 0
.

Step 6.

Return:

\dfrac{1}{(i-1)!}\cdot\dfrac{c_{i}}{c_{1}}\text{ for $i=1,3,5,\cdots$ and }\dfrac{1}{(i-1)!}\cdot\dfrac{d_{i}}{d_{2}}\text{ for $i=2,4,\cdots$}.

This is the desired value due to

\displaystyle\frac{(f,X^{i-1}Y^{k-2-(i-1)}\otimes\{0,\infty\})}{(f,Y^{k-2}\otimes\{0,\infty\})}=\frac{c_{i}}{c_{1}}\>\>\>\mbox{for}\>\>i=1,3,5\cdots,

\displaystyle\frac{(f,X^{i-1}Y^{k-2-(i-1)}\otimes\{0,\infty\})}{(f,XY^{k-3}\otimes\{0,\infty\})}=\frac{d_{i}}{d_{2}}\>\>\>\mbox{for}\>\>i=2,4\cdots

and the equations (
11
) and (
12
).

2.4.
Sturm’s criterion for congruence between cusp forms

To check congruence between cusp forms we will use the following criterion of Sturm
[
18
]
; see also Stein
[
17
, p. 173]
.

Theorem 2.10

(Sturm)
.

Let
\mathfrak{P}
be a prime ideal of a number field
K
. Let
f,g\in M_{k}(\Gamma,\mathcal{O}_{K})
be modular forms of weight
k
for
a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
of level
N
with Fourier coefficients in the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of
K
. Let
m=[SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma].

Suppose

a(n,f)\equiv a(n,g)\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}

for all

n\leq\begin{cases}\frac{km}{12}-\frac{m-1}{N}&\mbox{if $f-g\in S_{k}(\Gamma,\mathcal{O}$}),\\
\frac{km}{12}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
f\equiv g\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}
.

The bound for
n
in the above theorem is called the Sturm bound for
\Gamma
and weight
k
.

4.
A conjecture on the ratios of the special values of the Rankin-Selberg L-functions

The main purpose of this article is to investigate the principle:

f\equiv f^{\prime}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}\ \ \implies\ \ \frac{L(m,f\times g)}{L(m+1,f\times g)}\equiv\frac{L(m,f^{\prime}\times g)}{L(m+1,f^{\prime}\times g)}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}.

Keeping the above examples in mind which lend credence to this principle, while
accounting for the exceptional situation as in Thm.
3.4
, we formulate the following conjecture:

Conjecture 4.1
.

Let
f,f^{\prime}\in S_{k}(N,\chi)
and
g\in S_{l}(M,\psi)
be normalized eigenforms with
k-l>2
. Let
\mathfrak{P}\subset\mathbb{Q}(f)\mathbb{Q}(f^{\prime})\mathbb{Q}(g)
be a prime ideal such that

a(n,f)\equiv a(n,f^{\prime})\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}\;\;\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

For an integer
m
with
\frac{k+l}{2}-1<m\leq k-2,
assume:

(15)

v_{\mathfrak{P}}\left(\frac{L_{\infty}(m,f\times g)L_{N}(2m+2-k-l,\chi\psi)}{L_{\infty}(m+1,f\times g)L_{N}(m+4-k-l,\chi\psi)}\right)\geq 0.

Then for all such
m
one has the congruence:

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{L(m,f\times g)}{L(m+1,f\times g)}\ \equiv\ \frac{L(m,f^{\prime}\times g)}{L(m+1,f^{\prime}\times g)}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}.

Now, let
f\in S_{k}(N,\chi)
and
g,g^{\prime}\in S_{l}(M,\psi)
be normalized eigenforms and
\mathfrak{P}\subset\mathbb{Q}(f)\mathbb{Q}(g^{\prime})\mathbb{Q}(g)
be a prime ideal such that

a(n,g)\equiv a(n,g^{\prime})\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Assume (
15
) is satisfied. Then

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{L(m,f\times g)}{L(m+1,f\times g)}\ \equiv\ \frac{L(m,f\times g^{\prime})}{L(m+1,f\times g^{\prime})}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}.

In the preceding section, we computationally verified this Conjecture
4.1
in many examples.
Especially, note that the hypothesis (
15
) has been introduced to take care of the exceptional situation in
Thm.
3.4
. As mentioned in the introduction, in the companion article
[
11
]
, we prove a
variation of this conjecture using the machinery of Eisenstein cohomology as developed by
Harder and the second author in
[
3
]
.
