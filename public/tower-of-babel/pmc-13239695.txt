IFN-γ PET imaging stratifies and predicts response to CD137 agonism in a syngeneic colorectal tumor model
Authors: Alexander John Deck, Allen-Dexter Saliganan, Savannah Lane, Hyejeong Jang, Kang Chen, Seongho Kim, Nerissa T. Viola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe secretion of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) by activated immune cells is one of the hallmarks of response to cancer immunotherapy. Hence, detection of this cytokine via imaging can help determine an activated antitumor immune response. We have previously shown that positron emission tomography (PET) imaging of IFN-γ using [89Zr]Zr-Deferoximane (DFO)-anti-IFN-γ measured changes in its intratumoral expression after immune modulation. However, assessment of its potential to predict and identify treated responders (TR) versus treated non-responders (TNR) at the early stages of treatment, before any morphological effects occur, are key clinical needs. Thus, we evaluated whether IFN-γ PET can predict response to immunotherapy in a preclinical mouse model.MethodsBiolayer interferometry and neutralization assays were performed. [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ was administered to syngeneic CT26 murine colorectal tumors grown on immunocompetent mice that were either untreated (control) or treated with a CD137 agonist. Tumor uptake of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ was compared between groups. The uptake of a 89Zr-labeled irrelevant immunoglobulin was measured in separately treated CT26-bearing mice. Expression of IFN-γ was analyzed via ELISA and in situ hybridization assays after PET imaging. Imaging-derived IFN-γ uptake was evaluated for its ability to predict treatment response and disease outcomes.ResultsThe affinity of the DFO-anti-IFN-γ for IFN-γ remained similar to the unconjugated anti-IFN-γ. Binding did not perturb the signaling function of the cytokine. The uptake of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ strongly correlated with detected tumor levels of IFN-γ. The tracer stratified TR from TNR with high accumulation associated with tumor regression. A strong inverse correlation was further demonstrated between the tracer uptake and tumor growth slopes, where the higher the uptake, the slower the tumor growth. Accumulation of the 89Zr-labeled IgG in treated tumors was lower compared with the uptake of the IFN-γ tracer across all groups. Cox regression analysis revealed that increased uptake (>13.9 %ID/mL (percent injected dose per milliliter)) was significantly associated with improved overall survival.ConclusionDelineating IFN-γ through PET imaging stratified tumor response at the early stages of CD137 agonism prior to tumor morphological changes. Thus, IFN-γ PET holds promise as a predictive biomarker of response to immunotherapy.Statement of significanceIFN-γ PET imaging is predictive of response to immunotherapy, potentially bridging an unmet clinical need for early detection of antitumor immunity.

Introduction

Interferon-γ (IFN-γ) is a pleiotropic cytokine that has been robustly studied for its role in antitumor immunity. Secreted by many immune cells, such as CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTLs), CD4+ T helper 1 cells and natural killer (NK) cells, IFN-γ promotes tumor death and CTL-mediated tumor killing as well as the recruitment of other immune cells into the tumor microenvironment.1 The presence of IFN-γ has been known to correlate with an antitumor immune response in vivo in animal models and patient studies.24 Thus, the detection of IFN-γ within the tumor microenvironment may provide important insights into patient treatment outcomes. This idea, in combination with the evolving landscape of immunotherapy, can help to improve personalized medicine by guiding go-or-no-go decisions on treatment continuity during the early stages of immunotherapy.

Immunotherapy has become a first line of treatment in many types of cancer, but the response rates have been moderate, ranging from 10% to 69%.5 Continuing patients on inefficacious immunotherapies is not beneficial; however, a reliable and repeatable method that can non-invasively predict early stages of response is lacking with current diagnostic assessments relying only on changes in tumor morphology. Several groups have targeted different components of the immune microenvironment to monitor responses to immunotherapy via positron emission tomography (PET) imaging of immune cell markers. LaSalle et al developed a tracer against granzyme B, a cytolytic effector molecule of CTLs and NK cells, and used it to monitor tumor response to immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI).6 Other groups have targeted cell-surface markers associated with tumor-infiltrating CTLs, such as CD8,7 CD69,8 OX409 and ICOS.10 Thus far, at the time of this writing, only the imaging agents specific to CD811 12 and granzyme B13 have been translated to patients.

Previously, we developed and evaluated a radiotracer, [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ, which targets murine IFN-γ, to monitor response to immunotherapy. The tracer was proven effective in assessing antitumor immunity in both an HER2 cancer vaccine model and an ICI-sensitive model.14 15 However, both studies were unable to stratify immune response and establish the predictive potential of the tracer at early stages of treatment prior to any observable changes in tumor growth. Thus, herein, we seek to evaluate whether [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ can predict tumor responders from non-responders. We deployed the tracer for PET imaging of syngeneic CT26 colorectal tumors treated with an immune checkpoint agonist to CD137 (4–1BB). CD137 is an immunomodulatory target that mediates CD8+ T-cell priming, activation and expansion16 with several agonists in clinical trials. We validated the radiotracer’s tumor uptake against IFN-γ protein and genetic expression. A Cox regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the association of tracer uptake with tumor survival.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

CT26 murine colorectal cancer cells, purchased from American Type Culture Collection (CRL-2638, RRID: CVCL_7256), were grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 media supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and L-glutamine in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells were passed when confluency reached 80% and routinely checked for Mycoplasma (Lonza MycoAlert LT07-318).

Mice

All animal-based experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Wayne State University (Protocol #IACUC-23-10-6206). Both male and female BALB/c mice (Inotiv) were subcutaneously inoculated with 105 CT26 cells (day 0) on the right shoulder. Once tumors were palpable, mice were randomly assigned to either control untreated (n=5–6) or treated cohorts (n=7–8). Treated groups were administered anti-CD137 (12.5 mg/kg, Bio X Cell Cat# BE0239, RRID: AB_2687721) intraperitoneally on days 11, 12, 13 and 14. Tumor dimensions were monitored every 2–3 days with the volumes calculated using the formula for an ellipsoid: (length × width × height × π)6. Mice were denoted as responders (n=4) versus non-responders (n=4) based on complete tumor elimination.

Biolayer interferometry and neutralization assay

To determine the binding affinity of the antibody, biolayer interferometry (BLI) was performed (Sartorius Octet Red96) using streptavidin-coated biosensors. Samples of anti-IFN-γ and anti-IFN-γ-deferoxamine (DFO) were run with or without the presence of IFN-γ (GenScript, Z02916-1). Analysis was performed using the BLI Data Analysis HT 10 software. Neutralization of IFN-γ activity by the anti-IFN-γ (Invitrogen Cat# 14–7313-81, RRID: AB_468471) was determined using B16-Blue IFN-γ cells (InvivoGen Cat. No. bb-ifng) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, anti-IFN-γ was incubated with IFN-γ (BioLegend 575306) at an antibody:protein ratio of 10:1 overnight at 37°C in 96 well plates containing 105 B16-Blue cells (InvivoGen) (n=7). As controls, IFN-γ and IFN-β (GenScript Z03109-25) were added in separate wells. Cell supernatant was added to separate wells containing QUANTI-Blue solution (InvivoGen). The plates were incubated at 37°C for 3 hours followed by measurement with a plate reader at 650 nm.

Antibody conjugation and radiochemistry

Conjugation and radiolabeling were performed according to previous literature.14 15 17 Antibodies were first buffer exchanged into saline using spin desalting columns (Zeba MWCO:30 kDa). The pH was adjusted to ~9 for conjugation with p-SCN-Bn-DFO (macrocyclics) at a 1:5 mole ratio (antibody:DFO) and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. The reaction was purified using spin desalting columns (Zeba, MWCO: 7 kDa) using saline.

[89Zr]Zr-oxalate (3D Imaging, LLC) was radiolabeled to DFO-anti-IFN-γ or DFO-IgG at room temperature and at pH ~7.14 15 The reaction was allowed to incubate for 45 min to 1 hour at room temperature with gentle agitation. Radio-instant thin layer chromatography (radio-iTLC) was performed to monitor the reaction using a silica coated strip (Agilent SGI0001) as the stationary phase and 50 mM EDTA as the mobile phase. Purification was performed using spin column centrifugation (Zeba, MWCO: 7 kDa) to remove unbound 89Zr. Radio-iTLC confirmed purity after purification. Stability studies were performed in sterile saline and determined via radio-iTLC at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours post-purification.

PET/CT imaging

All mice (n≥5 per group) intravenously received 200±10 µCi (7.4±0.4 MBq) of either [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ or [89Zr]Zr-DFO-IgG in sterile saline through the lateral tail veins on day 14. PET/CT scans (Bruker Albira Si PET/CT) were acquired at ~72-hour post-injection (p.i.) while the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane during imaging. PET scans were reconstructed using maximum likelihood expectation maximization in 12 iterations and 0.75 mm voxel resolution. Images were then analyzed for tracer uptake, expressed as the percent injected dose per milliliter of tissue (%ID/mL) in accordance with published guidelines for reporting uptake values for preclinical PET images.18 %ID/mL measures the accumulation of the radiotracer relative to the dose injected and normalized to the volume of tissue drawn within the region-of-interest. Analysis was either by manually drawing on regions of interest on several different planar sections to generate volumes of interest or by semi-automated three-dimensional (3D) threshold segmentation of the tumor using PMOD (V.4.3, RRID: SCR_016547) V.4.3. CT images were used for reference to confirm PET uptake within the tumor. Representative PET images were generated using Imalytics Preclinical (RRID: SCR_026980). To investigate spatial distribution, 3D surface plots were generated using RStudio (V.2025.09.2+418). Detected events were downloaded as .csv files from Imalytics Preclinical and plotted within RStudio. The detected coincident gamma emissions or events were split according to four categorical intensities: low (0–500,000 Bq/mL); moderately low (500,000–1,000,000 Bq/mL); moderately high (1,000,000–1,500,000 Bq/mL); and high (1,500,000–2,000,000 Bq/mL).

Ex vivo analysis via ELISA and in situ hybridization

Tumors were excised immediately following imaging and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen or fixed in formalin and allowed to decay. Snap-frozen tumors were analyzed for IFN-γ using ELISA (RayBiotech cat. ELM-IFNg-1, RRID:SCR_005517) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Formalin fixed tissues were sectioned into 5 µm slices and stained with H&E. Messenger RNA (mRNA) was detected using in situ hybridization (ISH) kits for Ifng and Cd8 (RNAscope, Indica Labs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantification of mRNA was performed via HALO (Indica Labs, RRID:SCR_018350).

Statistical and data analysis

All data are expressed as mean±SD unless otherwise stated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.4.1 and the statistical software R V.4.4.0. The association between the uptake derived from [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ PET imaging and treatment response was evaluated using Firth’s logistic regression for rare events, to reduce small-sample bias. The model estimated the OR and corresponding 95% CIs for being classified as a responder, with uptake as the independent variable. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to derive an uptake threshold for distinguishing responders from non-responders, which was subsequently applied downstream. The predictive relationship between uptake and overall survival (OS) was analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression, with OS defined as the time from tumor inoculation to death or humane euthanasia. Uptake was evaluated both as a continuous and a binary variable based on the ROC-derived cut-off. Firth’s penalized Cox regression was employed to address potential bias due to the limited number of observed deaths in the high uptake group. HRs and 95% CIs were reported, with higher uptake associated with improved OS, as visualized by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. To further assess the predictive value of uptake, tumor growth curves were analyzed by calculating tumor growth slopes based on linear regression models fitted to serial tumor volume measurements for each mouse. The relationship between uptake and tumor growth slopes was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis, with correlation coefficients (r) and p-values reported to quantify the strength and significance of the association. Tumor growth slopes were also compared against the ROC-derived uptake threshold to assess whether this threshold stratified mice into slow-growing versus fast-growing tumor groups. For comparisons, two-sample t-tests were used. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

DFO-anti-IFN-γ has high affinity for IFN-γ without interfering with its activity

Given the key role of IFN-γ in antitumor immunity, it is critical that its downstream function is not perturbed by the radiotracer. Thus, we determined whether anti-IFN-γ affects IFN-γ activity using a neutralization assay that detects secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase (SEAP) produced after IFN-γ binds to its receptors on B16-Blue cells. Comparable levels of SEAP were produced by IFN-γ alone and IFN-γ pre-incubated with anti-IFN-γ. IFN-β, which served as a negative control, did not induce SEAP expression (online supplemental figure 1A). In comparison to anti-IFN-γ (KD~0.125 nM), the DFO-anti-IFN-γ conjugate retained high affinity for murine IFN-γ with a KD of ~0.15 nM. These results indicate that the anti-IFN-γ does not detrimentally alter the activity of IFN-γ, and that the affinity of its DFO conjugate to IFN-γ is retained.

[89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ detects a robust immune response in vivo

Using the DFO-anti-IFN-γ conjugate, we synthesized the radiotracer [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ in excellent yields (>80%) and purities (>95%) with a specific activity of 148±0.37 MBq/mg (4±0.1 mCi/mg). The radiotracer remained stable for up to 72 hours at 37°C in saline based on radio-iTLC (online supplemental figure 2). We then sought to evaluate whether the radiotracer can stratify antitumor response induced by CD137 agonism, which provides co-stimulation to T cells and can induce the regression of various established tumors in mice.1922 Mice bearing CT26 colorectal cancer tumors were treated with an agonistic anti-CD137 antibody23 followed by the administration of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ and PET imaging at 72 hours p.i. Compared with untreated control (CTRL) mice, CD137 agonism resulted in tumor regression in five of eight treated mice. Out of the five mice, four mice displayed complete tumor regression ~26 days after treatment while one mouse showed partial response and was designated as a non-responder (figure 1A). PET/CT images displayed marked differences in tracer accumulation in the tumors of CTRL mice, treated responders (TR), and treated non-responders (TNR) (figure 1B). When quantified via 3D segmentation, tracer uptake within the tumor was significantly higher in TR mice than in TNR mice (14.9±1.0 vs 11.0±1.0%ID/mL, p=0.0016) (figure 1C). In addition, TR mice exhibited higher tumorous accumulation of the radiotracer versus CTRL (11.3±1.6 %ID/mL, p=0.0060). There was no statistical difference in tracer uptake between TNR and CTRL tumors (p=0.7064). Of note, no significant differences in tumor morphology were observed between treated and control tumors on the day of radiotracer administration (figure 1D). Quantification of tumorous uptake via manual segmentation displayed similar results, wherein the TR group displayed higher tracer uptake versus CTRL and TNR cohorts (online supplemental figure 3A).

To control for tracer specificity, a separate cohort of treated mice was imaged with non-specific [89Zr]Zr-DFO-IgG. Moderate tumor accumulation of the irrelevant tracer was observed, stemming from the inherent enhanced permeation retention of biologics within the blood vessels of CT26 tumors.24 However, uptake of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-IgG was significantly lower than the uptake of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ in the TR cohort (p=0.0002). In addition, the IFN-γ tracer displayed elevated uptake in CTRL (p=0.0104) and TNR (p=0.0192) mice compared with those tumors that received the non-specific probe. We propose this observed difference to stem from IFN-γ produced by endogenous tumor infiltrating lymphocytes within CT26 tumors.25 Collectively, these results support the premise that [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ probe is specific to the cytokine and can stratify tumor response by delineating IFN-γ.

IFN-γ PET imaging correlates with intratumoral IFN-γ

In a separate cohort of mice, we determined the correlations between radiotracer uptake and intratumoral IFN-γ protein and transcript levels. Tumors were collected from mice immediately after imaging for analysis of IFN-γ expression. From the ELISA of tumor lysates, the IFN-γ protein expression was significantly higher in treated tumors than CTRL tumors (p<0.0001) (figure 2A). Importantly, a strong positive correlation is shown between the PET tracer uptake versus IFN-γ, where the radiotracer uptake correlated with IFN-γ protein in the tumor (figure 2B). This same strong positive correlation also holds for manually drawn uptake values (online supplemental figure 3B). Thus, confirming that IFN-γ PET can delineate and measure the cytokine in vivo.

IFN-γ PET spatially associates with antitumor immunity

We investigated the spatial distribution patterns of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ within the tumors of imaged mice. In CTRL tumors, <8% of the detected emissions were in the moderately high range with the majority of emissions classified under the low or moderately low categories (figure 4A). In contrast, TR tumors had different spatial distribution patterns with <27% of emissions within the moderately high to high range (figure 4B). Furthermore, treated groups that demonstrated tumor clearance displayed high-intensity emissions (1,500,000–2,000,000 Bq/mL) spanning throughout the tumor core (figure 4B). TNR tumor distribution patterns appear similar to CTRL tumors with only 0.08–4% of tumors falling within the moderately high range with no high emission events (figure 4B). Collectively, this demonstrates that the spatial distribution of IFN-γ is indicative of tumor response.

IFN-γ PET predicts immune response to CD137 agonism

We next investigated the potential of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ as a predictive imaging biomarker. In a separate cohort of mice treated with CD137 agonism, the uptake of the tracer was significantly higher in TRs compared with TNRs and CTRLs combined (14.90±0.98 vs 11.20±1.34; p=0.0005) (figure 5A), indicating a strong association between tracer uptake levels and treatment response. Firth logistic regression analysis further supported this relationship, showing that higher tracer uptake was associated with increased odds of being a responder (OR=3.93, 95% CI 1.40 to 31 708.63; p=0.0023) (table 1). ROC curve analysis demonstrated that uptake was a highly accurate discriminator of responder status, with an area under the curve of 100% (95% CI 100% to 100%) (figure 5B). The optimal uptake cut-off value identified from the ROC curve was 13.90, achieving both 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity. To further visualize this relationship, we generated a predicted probability plot using logistic regression. The analysis revealed a strong monotonic relationship between uptake and the probability of being a responder, with the transition occurring around the same optimal threshold (13.90), reinforcing the discriminatory power of uptake (figure 5C). Cox regression analysis revealed that higher uptake was significantly associated with improved OS (table 2).

When modeled as a continuous variable, uptake showed a protective effect (HR=0.57, 95% CI 0.36 to 0.91; p=0.0175). This association was further visualized in the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, which showed that all responders in the high uptake group survived through the follow-up period, while the median OS for the low uptake group was 21 days (figure 5D). To evaluate the predictive performance of the uptake cut-off value, we examined the relationship between uptake and tumor growth slopes in the C2 mouse cohort. A clear inverse correlation was observed, with higher uptake values corresponding to slower tumor growth (figure 5E and online supplemental figure 3C). Using the optimal uptake threshold (13.90) identified from the ROC analysis (figure 5B), three mice were predictively identified as potential responders.

Discussion

While immunotherapy has become a first line of treatment in patients with cancer, there is still no standardized effective method to predict and assess successful antitumor immunity. To bridge this need, new PET radiotracers targeting active immune cell molecules68 1012 and soluble proteins14 15 17 are developed and investigated for their feasibility to monitor immune response. In our previous work, we showed that a [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ radiotracer determined response to both immune checkpoint inhibitor (anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) + anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1))17 and vaccine-based therapies;15 although, it did not stratify response to immunotherapy nor establish a satisfactory predictive value. In this study, we challenged the radiotracer’s potential to distinguish responsive from non-responsive tumors to immunotherapy. We employed a CD137 agonist, which stimulates IFN-γ production by activating CTLs and NK cells26 and has been shown to induce heterogeneous responses to CT26 tumors.27 28 In doing so, this treatment modality permits the testing of the radiotracer’s ability to stratify immune responses. Our results show that CD137 agonism elicited IFN-γ-producing CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes leading to arrested tumor growth with a concomitant increase in intratumoral IFN-γ expression in TRs. These dynamic events were detected by [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ at the early stages of treatment while devoid of tumor volume differences between CTRL and treated groups as evident from increased tracer uptake in CT26 tumors that responded to the immunotherapy. To identify responders in this scheme, a threshold uptake of >13.90 %ID/mL of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ was established. Mice bearing tumors that met this criterion had enhanced OS. Applying this to our previous work using the same CT26 tumor-bearing mice, treated with anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1, the tracer uptake of nine treated mice was investigated. Eight tumors had uptake values above the 13.9 threshold cut-off and demonstrated tumor rejection. The uptake of one tumor failed to meet the limit and evidently displayed tumor progression. This suggests that this predictive value may be applicable to other immunotherapies. Moreover, the strong correlation between the PET tracer uptake in tumors versus IFN-γ protein expression (figure 2B) confirms that the PET tracer can be a direct measure of the cytokine in vivo and in real time. These findings highlight the potential of IFN-γ PET as a non-invasive tool for early treatment assessment, stratifying response prior to visible tumor changes.

The value of imaging IFN-γ PET not only lies in determining patient response in Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved immunotherapies but also in guiding new and emerging immunotherapies that are in clinical trials, including those that target CD137. The first generation of CD137 agonists, such as BMS-663513 and PF-05082566, have largely failed in clinical trials due to variable responses resulting in limited efficacy or adverse events.29 Despite this disappointing outcome, second-generation agonists are now in development,29 of which there are >20 active trials at the time of writing. Thus, non-invasive monitoring and identification of responses to these new treatments are critical to gage their success. Along these lines, IFN-γ PET has the potential to support these trials as it can be used to predict antitumor immunity at the early stages of treatment, long before morphological changes of the tumors can be observed.

Prior preclinical studies employed [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) PET, a marker of metabolism to visualize immune response in CT26 tumors after treatment with a CD137 agonist.30 Increased PET signal in treated versus control mice was observed due to increased glycolytically active CD45+ leukocytes.30 However, a major limitation of [18F]FDG in imaging intratumoral immune response is that it does not distinguish immune versus non-immune cells within the tumor microenvironment. Because [18F]FDG is an analog of glucose, it is indiscriminately scavenged by any metabolically active cell; thus, it cannot relay key information on immune-specific events leading to antitumor immunity.

Our results are especially meaningful because this demonstrates the predictive potential of IFN-γ PET and it further establishes this approach as a robust marker of adaptive immunotherapies. Admittedly, our study has several limitations. First, a full-length IgG (~150 kDa) has limited tumor penetrance and poor extravasation and thus may not be able to delineate IFN-γ within the entire tumor. To address this, we are now developing a smaller antibody fragment, which will enable better penetration and lead to shorter wait times between administration and image acquisition. Second, although IFN-γ can induce tumor apoptosis,31 it also induces immunosuppressive effector molecules, such as programmed death-ligand 1 on tumor cells, to promote tumor immune evasion, mainly at low doses.32 33 Thus, it is paramount to stratify low-dose tumor evasion levels of IFN-γ from levels that can trigger tumor apoptosis. Moreover, the established predictive value of IFN-γ PET is thus far only applicable to the treatment and specific tumor model that we have tested here and to some extent, in our prior work.17 Further validation using other immunotherapy modalities and tumor models is necessary to validate similar results.

Conclusion

Our work establishes the utility of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-anti-IFN-γ in distinguishing responders from non-responders in cancer immunotherapy and supports the use of this PET tracer as a predictive imaging marker of active immune response in vivo. Translation of IFN-γ PET is underway to assist in guiding immunotherapy decisions to assist in improving treatment outcomes of FDA-approved and developing immunotherapies.
