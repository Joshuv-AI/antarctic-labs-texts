Utilization of an Anteromedial Cortical Window for Tibial Component Removal During Revision Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Bailey J. Ross, Jacob Glassman, Grayson Nour, Jacob M. Wilson, Jose A. Rodriguez, Ajay Premkumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Revision total knee arthroplasty often entails removal of well-fixed components. Tibial component removal is particularly challenging due to (I) mechanical barriers that limit circumferential disruption when pegs, keels, or stems are present; (II) restricted access to implant-cement and cement-bone interfaces within the proximal canal; and (III) the proximity of critical structures, including the collateral ligaments, patellar tendon, popliteal artery, and distal femur. We present a novel anteromedial cortical window technique that facilitates removal of well-fixed tibial components by improving access to the implant-cement, burr-cement, and cement-bone interfaces within the proximal tibial metaphysis. This technique is suited for cases not requiring posterolateral exposure, offering a less morbid alternative to tibial tubercle osteotomy with the option for conversion if greater exposure is needed.

Introduction

Annual volume of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has increased considerably in recent years, and utilization of revision TKA (rTKA) is growing at an even fast rate [1,2]. A core principle of rTKA is implant removal while preserving the extensor mechanism, collateral ligaments, and neurovascular structures, and minimizing bone loss which are paramount to minimize risk of complications such as periprosthetic fracture [[3], [4], [5]]. Removal of components consequently must be approached with careful consideration of implant design and fixation, especially in the setting of well-fixed implants. Key technical challenges include limitations of circumferential disruption of the component-bone interface when pegs, keels, or stemmed components are involved, particularly from an anterior midline approach [6].

Many supplemental techniques have been described to help facilitate adequate exposure for implant removal in rTKA, including wide medial peel, quadriceps snip, V-Y turndown, extensor banana peel, and tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO), among others [[7], [8], [9]]. Following exposure, the general progression of tibial component removal includes (I) atraumatic removal of the polyethylene liner, then (II) circumferential disruption of the interface between the tibial tray and underlying bone, and finally (III) removal of the tibial component with an axially directed force [8]. Occasionally, despite adequate disruption of the cement-bone interface underneath the tibial component, the implant will remain well-fixed due to an inability to remove cement and access the cement-bone interface in the proximal metaphysis. Large axial forces with this intact cement-bone interface may increase the risk of metaphyseal bone loss and periprosthetic fracture [5]. Utilization of a TTO can help facilitate access to this interface for easier tibial component removal; however, this technique carries risk of complications such as nonunion and extensor lag [10,11].

To address the shortcomings and limitations of existing methods described in the literature for the removal of well-fixed tibial stems, we describe a novel technique of creating an anteromedial cortical window to facilitate the removal of well-fixed tibial components by improving access to the implant-cement, burr-cement, and cement-bone interface within the proximal metaphysis. This technique avoids violation of the extensor mechanism, allows for thorough debridement and removal of impediments to extraction along the implant-bone and cement-bone interfaces, and minimizes risk of excessive iatrogenic bone loss and periprosthetic fracture during implant extraction.

Surgical technique

Exposure

A standard medial parapatellar approach is used for exposure during rTKA as this technique allows for maximum lateral subluxation of the extensor mechanism. The incision may be extended proximally and/or distally to achieve adequate exposure based upon factors such as patient body habitus, implant size and type, arthrofibrosis, and the presence of a flexion contracture. Once adequate full-thickness skin flaps have been developed, the vastus medialis oblique is identified and a medial parapatellar arthrotomy is performed. As previously described [12], a quadriceps snip may be utilized as needed. Following arthrotomy, an extensive synovectomy and debridement of scar tissue and liberation of the medial and lateral gutters is performed with electrocautery. A proximal medial tibial peel is performed and if necessary, a partial release of the lateral patellar retinaculum may be performed to increase excursion of the extensor mechanism and allow for adequate lateralization of the extensor mechanism.

The polyethylene liner is removed and the posterior aspect of the proximal tibia is further exposed. In many cases, the femoral component will be removed next to decrease soft tissue tension, decrease risk of traction injuries to the extensor mechanism and collateral ligaments, and create additional exposure for tibial component extraction. Bent Hohmann retractors are placed along the medial and lateral proximal tibia and the knee is subsequently hyperflexed and externally rotated to deliver the proximal tibia anteriorly.

Removal of tibial component

As previously described, bony overgrowth is removed to allow for adequate circumferential visualization of the tray and host bone interface. The patellar tendon is retracted laterally and protected, and a combination of oscillating and reciprocating saws and/or osteotomes can be utilized to disrupt the tibial component-bone interface. If the tibial component remains well-fixed despite utilization of the appropriate axially directed forces, an anteromedial cortical window can be created to facilitate disruption of implant fixation within the proximal tibial metaphysis.

Anteromedial cortical window

A corticotomy is made 1 cm medial to the tibial tubercle and is 1.5 cm in width and 3.5 cm in length. Specific dimensions of the corticotomy can be modified as needed depending on the specific clinical scenario (eg, location of an offset coupler for an offset tibial stem extension, which may prevent superior tibial component removal). Using a ruler, the corticotomy site is outlined with a marking pen or electrocautery starting at the top of the tibial plateau. If possible, a small superior ledge may be preserved. The corticotomy is then started with a microsagittal saw along the longitudinal borders, followed by the inferior border, and is subsequently completed with a 1/4;-inch straight osteotome (Fig. 1). This step is performed meticulously to avoid comminution of the cortical fragment being removed or propagation of fracture lines into adjacent regions of the tibial plateau or metaphysis. The cortical piece is then removed en bloc and saved on the back table. With an anteromedial cortical window now created (Fig. 2), the implant-cement and cement-bone interfaces within the proximal metaphysis may be accessed and disrupted through use of curved osteotomes, curettes, burrs, and/or rongeurs (Supplemental Videos 1 and 2). Once this interface has been adequately cleared, tibial component removal may be reattempted. It must be noted that reinsertion of the fragment is not mandatory; it may be replaced when excellent bone apposition and stability are present, but fixation does not rely on the fragment. In cases where reinsertion is not feasible and/or there is concern for avascularity of the fragment, the revision implant should bypass the corticotomy site and achieve adequate zone 2 and 3 fixation thus yielding a stable construct.

Discussion

rTKA burden is projected to increase by 149% by 2040 [1]. Estimates of revision burden are often based primarily on increased primary TKA volume and projections on revision rates. Consequently, rates of repeat rTKA are also expected to increase. Infection continues to be the leading cause of failure, followed by aseptic loosening and instability [13]. With concurrent usage of well-fixed tibial stems, there is a need for additional techniques to aid in tibial stem removal that minimizes patient morbidity. In this context, access to the implant-bone interface in the proximal intramedullary canal may be necessary for tibial component removal. Attempts to direct an axial force to remove components with intact cement account for the elevated risk of periprosthetic fracture and potential for exacerbating the loss of host bone [5,9].

Current strategies for removal of well-fixed tibial components that are unable to be removed from the top include primarily the use of a TTO, which can provide adequate exposure to access and remove cement and implants [7,10]. However, this technique violates the extensor mechanism, which may introduce morbidity and increase the risk of complications, such as nonunion, fixation failure, and extensor lag [10,11]. Furthermore, use of this technique may be associated with postoperative weightbearing and range of motion restrictions which can negatively impact patient recovery. While certain cases may require TTO, for specific cases which require access to a specific region in the proximal tibial metaphysis such as removal of tibial components with longer intramedullary stems and offset couples, an anteromedial cortical window may allow for sufficient exposure while avoiding violation of the extensor mechanism and minimizing patient morbidity.

It must be noted that this technique does not improve access to the posterolateral proximal tibia, and if this is required, a TTO remains the preferred technique. However, the anteromedial cortical window provides a reproducible, less morbid option for more limited exposure needs. Furthermore, because the corticotomy does not extend as distal as a TTO, the lateral limb of the window can be extended distally and converted to a TTO if intraoperative circumstances require more extensile exposure without compromising fixation.

Limitations

Several limitations must be mentioned. While the authors have performed this technique for approximately 10 cases, most rTKA does not require the use of a window for implant extraction, and there is a lack of patient outcome data to warrant statistical analysis of complication rates, morbidity, and reoperation. In this small cohort, we have not encountered complications such as nonunion, fixation failure, or extensor lag. A key risk of using this technique is cortical propagation or crack of fracture lines through adjacent segments of the tibial plateau and/or proximal metaphysis. The authors have not encountered this possible complication and mitigate this risk through careful, meticulous bony work. It is also possible that, due to imprecise bony cuts, bone loss, or comminution, difficulty achieving a press-fit with reinsertion of the bony block may occur. Furthermore, there may be a lower potential for bony healing of the bone block given that this fragment is devitalized. The authors have encountered this infrequently, and in such cases, reinsertion of the bone block is not mandatory as the revision construct implanted will bypass this region and secure fixation in zones 2 and 3 within the proximal tibia. Although this technique improves access to the implant-cement and cement-bone interfaces within a portion of the proximal metaphysis, it does not provide circumferential access to this region. It also does not provide access to the posterolateral corner, nor to points of fixation more distally in the intramedullary canal. Thus, it must be noted that this technique does not obviate the need for more extensive exposure (eg, TTO) in certain clinical scenarios, though as outlined previously, the lateral limb of the corticotomy site may be converted to a TTO if needed.

Summary

Utilization of an anteromedial cortical window offers a promising approach for the removal of specific well-fixed tibial components during rTKA. By enhancing access to the implant-cement, burr-cement, and cement-bone interfaces within the proximal metaphysis, this technique helps facilitate seamless tibial component removal while minimizing risk of periprosthetic fractures, loss of proximal tibial bone stock, and violation of the extensor mechanism. This technique is simple, reproducible, and may serve as a valuable tool for tibial component removal in select revision cases to avoid the morbidity of more extensive exposures when limited access is sufficient while allowing for easy conversion to TTO if needed.
