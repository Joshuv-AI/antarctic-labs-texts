PROD: Palpative Reconstruction of Deformable Objects through Elastostatic Signed Distance Functions
Hamza El-Kebir

Abstract
We introduce PROD (Palpative Reconstruction of Deformables), a novel method for reconstructing the shape and mechanical properties of deformable objects using elastostatic signed distance functions (SDFs). Unlike traditional approaches that rely on purely geometric or visual data, PROD integrates palpative interaction—measured through force-controlled surface probing—to estimate both the static and dynamic response of soft materials. We model the deformation of an object as an elastostatic process and derive a governing Poisson equation for estimating its SDF from a sparse set of pose and force measurements. By incorporating steady-state elastodynamic assumptions, we show that the undeformed SDF can be recovered from deformed observations with provable convergence. Our approach also enables the estimation of material stiffness by analyzing displacement responses to varying force inputs. We demonstrate the robustness of PROD in handling pose errors, non-normal force application, and curvature errors in simulated soft body interactions. These capabilities make PROD a powerful tool for reconstructing deformable objects in applications ranging from robotic manipulation to medical imaging and haptic feedback systems.



I
Introduction

The ability to accurately reconstruct the geometry and mechanical properties of deformable objects is crucial for applications in robotics, medical imaging, and haptic interaction. Traditional approaches to reconstruction predominantly rely on visual scanning or contact-based surface tracing, which focus on external shape recovery but fail to capture the intrinsic material properties that govern an object’s response to force. In contrast, biological systems leverage
palpation
—a process that integrates touch, pressure, and force-induced deformations—to infer both geometric and mechanical characteristics of an object in real time.

Palpation has long been utilized in medical diagnostics to assess tissue stiffness and detect abnormalities. Techniques such as dynamic mechanical palpation enable the measurement of viscoelastic properties in soft tissues, providing quantitative insights into pathological conditions
[
1
]
. Force feedback as a result of varying tissue properties prove to be key in determining the efficacy of surgical incision techniques such as electrocautery
[
2
]
, which bears significant interest in effectuating minimally invasive (micro)surgery
[
3
]
. Additionally, studies on force modulation strategies have demonstrated the efficacy of active probing mechanisms in identifying local variations in stiffness within soft tissues
[
4
,
5
]
. While these methods successfully extract material properties, they often lack integration with comprehensive shape reconstruction frameworks, leaving an opportunity for approaches that simultaneously recover geometry and elasticity.

Recent advances in 3D reconstruction have demonstrated the effectiveness of signed distance functions (SDFs) in capturing intricate geometries. SDF-based methods have been widely adopted for reconstructing rigid bodies due to their ability to encode surface boundaries implicitly while maintaining a continuous representation of object topology. For instance, Chen
et al.

[
6
]
introduced gSDF, a geometry-driven approach that reconstructs 3D hand-object interactions from monocular RGB images, while SDF-SRN
[
7
]
proposed a method for learning SDF representations from single-view images. Although these methods excel in modeling complex geometries, they do not account for the deformability of soft bodies, where shape varies in response to force.

Soft body reconstruction presents unique challenges due to the nonlinear and time-dependent nature of material deformations. While SDFs have primarily been applied to rigid objects, recent work has attempted to extend them to soft bodies. Xu
et al.

[
8
]
introduced GSurf, a framework that learns SDFs directly from Gaussian primitives to address issues such as incomplete reconstructions and fragmented surfaces, and Liu
et al.

[
9
]
proposed ReDSDF, a regularized deep SDF approach that computes smooth distance fields at arbitrary scales to improve the representation of high-dimensional manifolds. Despite these advances, existing methods focus predominantly on shape recovery without explicitly modeling the underlying mechanical properties or force-driven deformations of the material.

An alternative to model-driven approaches is the use of vision-based tactile sensors to infer shape and mechanical properties. GelSight
[
10
,
11
]
employs an elastomeric surface and high-resolution imaging to measure contact forces and surface deformations, enabling detailed reconstruction of object topography. Similarly, Insight
[
12
]
provides real-time 3D haptic sensing, capturing force directionality and surface compliance. While such external sensing systems offer rich tactile feedback, they rely on high-resolution imaging hardware and calibration procedures, limiting their adaptability in scenarios where direct interaction with soft objects is required. These systems also do not inherently model the dynamic response of a deformable body under continuous palpation, and do not strive to reconstruct the underformed shape of the body.

Intrinsic reconstruction methods that rely directly on palpative interactions offer an alternative to externally instrumented techniques. By actively probing an object and measuring its force response, it is possible to infer both its undeformed shape and material properties. However, prior work in this area has primarily focused on estimating local stiffness variations
[
13
]
, rather than reconstructing a full 3D model that captures both geometry and elastodynamic behavior. A key limitation of existing palpation-based methods is their reliance on empirical stiffness estimation, without integrating a formal mathematical model that accounts for the time-dependent deformation response.

To address these challenges, we introduce
PROD
(Palpative Reconstruction of Deformables), a novel framework for reconstructing both the geometry and mechanical properties of deformable objects using elastodynamic signed distance functions. Unlike conventional SDF-based methods that assume rigid geometry, PROD models soft materials as elastodynamic systems governed by Newtonian mechanics and non-Hertzian contact mechanics. By applying controlled probing forces and measuring surface deformations, as illustrated in Figure
1
, our method reconstructs the signed distance field of the object while simultaneously estimating its material properties. In particular, the key contributions of our work are:

1.

Elastostatic SDF Reconstruction
: We derive a governing Poisson equation that estimates the signed distance function from sparse pose data obtained through palpative interactions.

2.

Recovery of Undeformed Geometry
: By leveraging steady-state elastodynamic assumptions as well as a combination of Hertzian and non-Hertzian contact theory, we develop an inversion method that recovers the undeformed SDF from deformed observations.

3.

Estimation of Material Properties
: We propose a force-displacement analysis that enables the extraction of an object’s Young’s modulus and curvature, offering insights into its stiffness and compliance through a process of multiple probes.

4.

Applications to Soft Robotics, Non-Destructive Testing, and Biomechanics
: We discuss potential uses of PROD in robotic grasping, medical robotics, non-destructive testing, and haptic feedback systems.

By integrating force-based palpative exploration with computational elastostatics and contact mechanics, PROD provides a physics-informed approach to reconstructing soft objects that extends beyond purely geometric methods. In the following Sections, we formalize our approach, present theoretical guarantees for convergence, and validate our method on simulated deformable objects.

II
Preliminaries

We first consider the application of elastodynamic equations on signed distance functions, as defined in this Section.

II-A

Signed Distance Functions

Fig. 1
:
An illustration of the deformation of a spherical object under flat punch action. The undeformed surface is given in blue, whereas the red and green surface show deformation with increased pressure.

Signed distance functions (SDFs) are defined as functions
\phi:\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfy a particular form of the
eikonal equation

[
14
, p. 93]
. The general eikonal equation is defined as
[
14
, p. 416]

\|\nabla\phi(x)\|=\frac{1}{\psi(x)},\quad x\in\Omega,

(1)

where
\Omega
is an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and

\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a positively valued function. In physical terms, the solution
\phi(x)
to this nonlinear partial differential equation can be interpreted as the shortest time needed to travel from the boundary
\partial\Omega
to
x\in\Omega
, with
\psi(x)
giving the
speed at
x
.

For a signed distance function, the speed will be unity throughout
the domain, and
x
will not be constrained to lie in a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying (
1
) with
\psi

\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\Omega:=\{\phi(x)\leq 0:x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
. Then, given that
\psi(x)\equiv 1
, the time to travel from the boundary
\partial\Omega
to any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
will simply be equal to the shortest distance from
x
to
\partial\Omega
. This distance is signed, meaning that for
x
with
\phi(x)<0
will be in the interior of
\Omega
,
\phi(x)=0
implies that
x\in\partial\Omega
, and
\phi(x)>0
implies that
x\not\in\Omega
. We formally defined the signed distance function as follows:

Definition 1

(Signed Distance Function (SDF))
.

A function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
signed distance function
of a set
\Omega
if it satisfies:

1.

Eikonal equation:

\|\nabla\phi(x)\|=1,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(EE)

2.

Zero distance at the boundary:

\phi(x)=0,\quad\forall\ x\in\partial\Omega,

(BZ)

3.

Inward-facing normal at the boundary:

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\partial\Omega,\exists\epsilon>0,\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \phi\left(x+\epsilon\nabla\phi(x)\right)<\phi(x).

(IN)

II-B

Elastodynamic Deformation

Assuming a homogeneous and isotropic material with linear elasticity and a known Young’s modulus
E
and a density
\rho
, the elastodynamic equation under a force field
f:\Omega_{0}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
reads

\rho\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial t^{2}}=\nabla\cdot\sigma+f(x,t),\quad\sigma=E\varepsilon,

(2)

where
v(x,t)
is the time varying
deformation field
and
\varepsilon
is the strain tensor. We proceed by developing a similar elastodynamic equation that applies directly to a signed distance field.

III
Reconstruction of Elastodynamic SDFs

In light of the elastodynamic deformation equation (
2
), as well as the Definition
1
, we directly obtain the following elastodynamic equation that governs the signed distance function:

\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial t^{2}}+\gamma\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}-E\Delta\phi=u(t)w_{r}(x-p(t))\langle q(t),\nabla\phi\rangle,

(3)

where
\phi=\phi_{0}+\delta\phi
, with
\phi_{0}
denoting the undeformed state. Let
w_{r}
be a weighting function parameterized by a radius
r
, such that
\int_{\Omega_{0}}w_{r}(x)dx=\pi r^{2}
. This is commonly known as a
flat punch
model, wherein a cylinder is pressed with its flat side into a surface in the normal direction. This is illustrated in Figure
1
.

In a steady-state case where
(u,p,q)
are constant, we obtain the following elastostatic equation after expanding
\phi
:

\begin{split}E\Delta(\delta\phi)&=-uw_{r}(x-p)\langle q,\nabla\phi_{0}+\nabla\delta\phi\rangle-E\Delta\phi_{0},\\
\frac{\partial\delta\phi}{\partial n}&=-1\ \mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{0}.\\
\end{split}

(4)

We now consider whether it is prudent to assume steady-state conditions in a biomedically relevant scenario.

III-A

Correctness of Steady-State Assumptions

We proceed by approximating the settling time of a linearized elastodynamic system under small perturbations. We obtain the following PDE:

\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\delta\phi}{\partial t^{2}}+\gamma\frac{\partial\delta\phi}{\partial t}-E\Delta\delta\phi=0.

Performing a modal decomposition in the Laplace domain, with solution
\delta\phi(x,t)=\hat{\phi}(x)\exp(st)
, we obtain:

\rho s^{2}\hat{\phi}+\gamma s\hat{\phi}-E\Delta\hat{\phi}=0.

Dividing by
\hat{\phi}
, and taking
\lambda
to be the eigenvalues of the Laplacian
-\Delta
, we have

\rho s^{2}+\gamma s+E\lambda=0.

Given this dispersion relation, we find the (elastic) disturbance propagation speed to be
c\approx\sqrt{E/\rho}
assuming that
\gamma
is small relative to
E
and
\rho
. Let
\ell
be a characteristic length scale for the deformable object under consideration, for instance its thickness. We define
T_{e}:=\ell/c
to be the time it takes for an elastic wave to propagate a distance the length of the characteristic length. If the contact time, or forcing time,
T_{c}
is such that
T_{c}\gg T_{e}
, then we may safely ignore dynamic terms and assume attainment of a steady-state condition within a period
T_{c}
.

We verify this time scale separation assumptions on human adipose tissue, which has a density of
\rho=960
kg/m
3
and a Young’s modulus
E
between 4.48–11.50 kPa
[
15
]
. Assuming that the contact time is at least
T_{c}=100
ms and we have a characteristic length scale of
\ell=5
mm, we find
T_{e}\geq 2.31
ms is such that
T_{c}\gg T_{e}
is satisfied.

IV
Unique SDF Estimation from Pose Data

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an unknown
continuum
, or compact, closed, and path connected set. We assume that we are given
N

poses

Z_{N}:=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
z_{i}=(p_{i},q_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
such that each
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a position on
\partial\Omega
and
q_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{3}
is the inward surface normal of
\Omega
at
p_{i}
. We seek to reconstruct the unknown signed distance function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of
\Omega
on the basis of these
N
poses.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an unknown
continuum
, or compact, closed, and path connected set. Assume that
N

poses

Z_{N}:=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are given, where
z_{i}=(p_{i},q_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
such that each
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a position on
\partial\Omega
and
q_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{3}
is the inward surface normal of
\Omega
at
p_{i}
.

Let
\hat{q}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{B}_{1}^{n}
be the interpolated normal field such that
\hat{q}(p_{i})=q_{i}
. Then, the solution to the Poisson equation

\Delta\phi=\nabla\cdot\hat{q}.

(5)

yields the a
pseudo-signed distance function
of
\partial\Omega
satisfying (
BZ
) and (
IN
).

Proof.

Let
K:=\mathrm{convex}(\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})
be the convex hull of the given positions, and let
\phi_{0}(x):=\|x-\mathrm{center}(K)\|-\mathrm{diam}(K)/2
be a circumscribing sphere that encloses
K
. This will be our initial guess for the SDF
\phi
.

Let
\hat{q}:K\to\mathcal{B}_{1}^{n}
be the interpolated normal field on
K
such that
\hat{q}(p_{i})=q_{i}
. Our problem reduces to an energy minimization problem of the following objective functional:

F_{0}(\phi):=\int_{K}\|\nabla\phi(x)-\hat{q}(x)\|^{2}\|dx.

(6)

Expanding the functional
F_{0}
, we obtain:

F_{0}(\phi)=\int_{K}\left(\|\nabla\phi(x)\|^{2}-2\nabla\phi(x)\cdot\hat{q}(x)+\|\hat{q}(x)\|^{2}\right)dx.

(7)

The term
\|\hat{q}(x)\|^{2}
can be dropped since it does not depend on
\phi
. Furthermore, we can shrink
K
to the unknown
\Omega
without loss of generality.
Hence, let the new energy functional be
F(\phi):=\int_{\Omega}\left(\|\nabla\phi(x)\|^{2}-2\nabla\phi(x)\cdot\hat{q}(x)\right)dx
.

Computing the first variation of
F
, we obtain

\begin{split}\delta F&=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}F(\phi+\epsilon\delta\phi)\right|_{\epsilon=0}\\
&=\int_{K}\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\left(\|\nabla(\phi+\epsilon\delta\phi)\|^{2}-2\nabla(\phi+\epsilon\delta\phi)\cdot\hat{q}\right)dx.\\
&=\int_{K}\left(2\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla(\delta\phi)-2\hat{q}\cdot\nabla(\delta\phi)\right)dx.\end{split}

Next, we apply integration by parts to obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla(\delta\phi)dV&=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\phi\ \delta\phi\hat{n}dS-\int_{\Omega}\Delta\phi\ \delta\phi dV\\
&=-\int_{\Omega}\Delta\phi\ \delta\phi dV,\end{split}

since
\phi
is zero along
\partial\Omega
. Similarly, we have

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\hat{q}\cdot\nabla(\delta\phi)dV&=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\hat{q}\cdot\delta\phi\cdot\hat{n}dS-\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\hat{q})\delta\phi dV\\
&=-\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\hat{q})\delta\phi dV,\end{split}

since
\delta\phi
is zero along the surface
\partial\Omega
. We can now construct the following Euler-Lagrange equation by setting
\delta F=0
for all variations
\delta\phi
gives
-2\Delta\phi+2\nabla\cdot\hat{q}=0
, yielding Poisson equation (
5
).
∎

Remark 1
.

The Poisson equation (
5
) arises directly when enforcing that
\nabla\phi
aligns with normal field
\hat{q}
. The Poisson equation ensures that the Laplacian of the SDF matches the divergence of the normal field. If
\hat{q}
is a valid normal field of some surface, the solution of this (
5
) will recover an SDF up to some additive constant.

To resolve the additive constant, we enforce Dirichlet boundary conditions
\phi(p_{i})=0
for
i=1,\ldots,N
, yielding the following PDE to uniquely reconstruct
\phi
from
Z_{N}
, which we denote by
\phi_{N}
:

\Delta\phi_{N}=\nabla\cdot\hat{q},\quad\phi_{N}(p_{i})=0,\ i=1,\ldots,N.

(8)

Let us now verify if the eikonal equation (
1
) holds for
\psi\equiv 0
. Since the solution to Poisson equation (
8
) minimizes the energy functional
E(\phi)=\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\phi-\hat{q}\|^{2}dx
, we ensure that
\nabla\phi

aligns
with
\hat{q}
. Since
\hat{q}
is normalized, we have
\|\hat{q}(x)\|=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence
\nabla\phi\approx\hat{q}
implies that
\|\nabla\phi\|\approx 1
.

Since
gradient alignment
, and not
gradient magnitude
, is enforced in the Poisson equation, we note that the correct gradient magnitude is obtained at the zero-level set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\phi(x)=0\}
given the Dirichlet boundary conditions
\phi(p_{i})=0
. Additionally, this boundary condition is essential for uniqueness, since in its absence
\phi+c
is also a correct solution for any constant
c
. We proceed by showing how we can turn the pseudo-SDF obtained in Theorem
1
can be transformed into an equivalent true SDF that satisfies (
EE
).

IV-A

Global Satisfaction of the Eikonal Condition

We introduce the following iterative evolution step to enforce the eikonal equation (
EE
) across the entire domain:

\partial_{t}\phi+\operatorname{sign}(\phi_{0})(\|\nabla\phi\|-1)=0.

(9)

When initialized using a pseudo-SDF
\phi_{0}
, this PDE will produce a signed distance function that satisfies (
EE
). At each iteration, the equation is a Hamilton–Jacobi PDE, which can be efficiently solved using upwind schemes.

Since (
9
) is a time-evolving PDE, we propose an alternative iterative update scheme that preserves the zero-level set:

Theorem 2
.

Let
\hat{\phi}
be a pseudo-signed distance function such that
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\phi(x)=0\}=\partial\Omega
of some volume
\Omega
. Suppose that (
EE
) is not satisfied for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, Algorithm
1
produces a true signed distance function that satisfies Definition
1
within an error
\epsilon>0
and preserves the zero-level set of
\hat{\phi}
:

Data:

\epsilon>0
,
\Delta t>0
,
\hat{\phi}

Result:

\bar{\phi}

k\leftarrow 0
;

\phi^{(0)}\leftarrow\hat{\phi}
;

while

\max_{x}|\|\nabla\phi^{(k)}(x)\|-1|\geq\epsilon

do

Solve
\phi^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\phi^{(k)}-\Delta t\cdot\operatorname{sign}(\hat{\phi})(\|\nabla\phi^{(k)}\|-1)
;

k\leftarrow k+1
;

end while

\bar{\phi}\leftarrow\phi^{(k)}
;

Algorithm 1

Reinitialization of a pseudo-SDF into a zero-level set preserving true SDF.

Proof.

We show that the evolution equation

\partial_{t}\phi+\operatorname{sign}(\phi_{0})(\|\nabla\phi\|-1)=0

ensures that:

\phi^{(0)}(x)=\hat{\phi}(x)=0\ \forall x\in\partial\Omega\ \Rightarrow\ \phi^{(k)}(x)=0\ \forall x\in\partial\Omega,\ \forall k.

The proof follows by stationarity of the zero-level set under the evolution PDE. Since (
9
) only modifies
\phi
based on
\|\nabla\phi\|-1
, it does not change the values of
\phi
at
\phi=0
, because at the zero-level set,
\operatorname{sign}(\phi(x))=0
and remains unchanged throughout the process.

More formally, for any fixed level set
\phi=c
, the reinitialization PDE can be rewritten in level-set advection form:

\partial_{t}\phi+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\phi=0,\quad\text{where }\mathbf{v}=-\operatorname{sign}(\phi)(\|\nabla\phi\|-1)\frac{\nabla\phi}{\|\nabla\phi\|}.

Since the velocity
\mathbf{v}
is always directed
along the normal to the level set
, and is zero whenever
\|\nabla\phi\|=1
, the zero-level set itself remains stationary during evolution. This ensures that:

\{x:\phi^{(0)}(x)=0\}=\{x:\phi^{(k)}(x)=0\},\quad\forall k.

Thus, Algorithm
1
correctly reinitializes
\hat{\phi}
into a true signed distance function while preserving its zero-level set. This completes the proof.
∎

V
Convergence of SDF Estimate

We would now like to show that the zero level set of
\phi_{N}
, constructed from
Z_{N}
using the approach from Section
IV
, converges to that of
\phi
for
N\to\infty
. We do so by considering the Hausdorff distances between these two level sets:

\begin{split}&d_{N}:=d_{\mathrm{H}}(\phi_{N}^{-1}(0),\phi^{-1}(0))=d_{\mathrm{H}}(\partial\Omega_{N},\partial\Omega)\\
&=d_{\mathrm{H}}(\{x:\phi_{N}(x)=0\},\{x:\phi(x)=0\})\\
&=\max\left\{\sup_{x\in\phi_{N}^{-1}(0)}\inf_{y\in\phi^{-1}(0)}\|x-y\|,\sup_{x\in\phi^{-1}(0)}\inf_{y\in\phi_{N}^{-1}(0)}\|x-y\|\right\}.\end{split}

We would like to show that
\lim_{N\to\infty}d_{N}=0
.

Before we continue, we make the following lax assumption about
Z_{N}
:

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
Z_{N}
is self-consistent for all
N
, i.e., all samples
z_{i}=(p_{i},q_{i})\in Z_{N}
lie on a continuous manifold
\partial\Omega
with Lipschitz constant
L
. Furthermore, we assume that for
N\to\infty
, the set
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
becomes dense on
\partial\Omega
and that the interpolated normal field
\hat{q}_{N}
converges uniformly to the true normal field
q
. Finally, we assume that
q_{N},q\in(L^{2}(\Omega_{0}))^{n}
and
\partial\Omega\in C^{1,1}(\Omega_{0})
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
Z_{N}
be a set of
N
poses on a Lipschitz continuous manifold
\partial\Omega
that satisfies Assumption
1
, and let
\phi_{N}
be the signed distance function constructed from
Z_{N}
based by solving (
8
) and regularizing it through Algorithm
1
. Let
\phi
be the true SDF that defines
\partial\Omega
.

Then, for
N\to\infty
, the following statements hold

1.

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|\phi_{N}-\phi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{0})}=0
,

2.

\lim_{N\to\infty}d_{N}=\lim_{N\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{H}}(\phi_{N}^{-1}(0),\phi^{-1}(0))=0
,

3.

d_{\mathrm{H}}(\Omega_{N},\Omega)=d_{\mathrm{H}}(\{x:\phi_{N}(x)\leq 0\},\{x:\phi(x)\leq 0\})=0
.

Proof.

Since (
8
) is a second-order elliptic PDE, we find that
\phi_{N},\phi\in H^{2}(\Omega_{0})\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{0})
, where
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{0})
is a subpsace of the first-order Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega_{0})=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega_{0}):\nabla u\in(L^{2}(\Omega_{0}))^{n}\}
. By elliptic regularity theory, in particular the Babuška–Lax–Milgram theorem
[
14
, §6.2.1, p. 315]
, we find existence and uniqueness of (
8
) are satisfied, as well as the fact that the solution depends continuously on
q
and
q_{N}
. Furthermore, by Assumption
1
, given that
\phi_{N},\phi\in H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
, it follows that this continuous dependence extends to the gradient of
q
and
q_{N}
, yielding:

\|\phi_{N}-\phi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{0})}\leq C\|q_{N}-q\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{0})},

for some constant
C
. Hence, given uniform convergence of
\lim_{N\to\infty}\|q_{N}-q\|_{\infty}=0
, we find that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\|\phi_{N}-\phi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{0})}=0
. Finally, given the Lipschitz continuity of
\phi
, we can show that the zero level sets
\phi_{N}^{-1}(0)
converges to
\phi^{-1}(0)
in the Hausdorff sense using an
\epsilon
–
\delta
argument.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

|\phi(x)|<\delta/2\ \Rightarrow\ d(x,\partial\Omega)<\epsilon/2.

Furthermore, there exists
N
such that
|\phi(x)-\phi_{N}(x)|<\delta/2
by uniform convergence, yielding

|\phi_{N}(x)|\leq|\phi(x)-\phi_{N}(x)|+|\phi(x)|<\delta\ \Rightarrow\ d(x,\partial\Omega_{N})<\epsilon/2.

Finally, we have that

|\phi(x)|,|\phi_{N}(x)|,|\phi(x)-\phi_{N}(x)|<\delta/2

implies

d_{\mathrm{H}}(\partial\Omega,\partial\Omega_{N})\leq d(x,\partial\Omega)+d(x,\partial\Omega_{N})<\epsilon,

which follows by the triangle inequality. Hence, we have shown that
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\phi_{N}^{-1}(0),\phi^{-1}(0))
converges to zero for
N\to\infty
. If
\phi_{N}
and
\phi
both satisfy the eikonal equation (
EE
), then it follows that
\lim_{N\to\infty}d_{\mathrm{H}}(\Omega,\Omega_{N})=0
. This completes the proof.
∎

We now consider the problem of uniquely determining undeformed SDFs from deformed pose data.

VI
Unique Undeformed SDF Estimation from Deformed Pose Data

We consider the following assumptions:

Assumption 2

(Material Properties)
.

We assume the object is composed of a homogeneous and isotropic linear elastic material with Young’s modulus
E
and Poisson’s ratio
\nu
. We define the plane strain modulus as
{E^{*}}=E/(1-\nu^{2})
. All deformations are assumed to be purely elastic. In its unloaded state, the object’s shape is described by a signed distance function (SDF)
\phi_{0}
.

Assumption 3

(Force Application and Contact)
.

A total normal force
P_{i}
is applied to the object at a point
p_{i}
on its surface, directed along the surface normal
q_{i}
. The force is distributed over a circular area of radius
r
, modelled as contact with a rigid, flat-ended cylindrical punch of radius
r
. We assume no tangential forces (frictionless contact). After deformation, the object’s shape is described by the deformed SDF
\phi_{i}
. We assume the contact results in the application point being on the deformed surface, i.e.,
\phi_{i}(p_{i})=0
. Furthermore, we assume that the material point corresponding to
p_{i}
was initially inside the undeformed object, i.e.,
\phi_{0}(p_{i})<0
.

Let us now treat the case of zero curvature
\kappa
.

Proposition 2

(Undeformed SDF Estimate: Flat Case)
.

Consider the loading conditions of Assumption
3
applied to an initially flat elastic half-space (
\kappa=0
). Let
p_{i}
be the center of the contact area after force
P_{i}
is applied. The value of the undeformed SDF
\phi_{0}
evaluated at
p_{i}
is approximately related to the flat-case indentation depth
\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}
:

\phi_{0}(p_{i})\approx-\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}=-\frac{P_{i}(1-\nu^{2})}{2Er}=-\frac{P_{i}}{2{E^{*}}r}.

Proof.

Let
p_{i}
be the location on the deformed surface where the load is centered (
\phi_{i}(p_{i})=0
). Let
\mathbf{x}_{0,i}
be the material point in the undeformed configuration that moves to
p_{i}
after deformation, such that
p_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{0,i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i})
, where
v_{i}
is the displacement field.

Under the assumption of a rigid flat punch of radius
r
pressing into an elastic half-space, the material surface within the contact area undergoes a uniform normal displacement
\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}
. The relationship between the total force
P_{i}
and this displacement is given by standard contact mechanics results
[
16
]
as:

\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}=\frac{P_{i}(1-\nu^{2})}{2Er}=\frac{P_{i}}{2{E^{*}}r}.

The displacement vector at the center point
\mathbf{x}_{0,i}
is predominantly normal to the surface. If
q_{i}
is the outward normal at
p_{i}
, the displacement vector is approximately
v_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i})\approx-\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}q_{i}
.

We relate the deformed and undeformed SDFs using a first-order Taylor expansion of
\phi_{0}
around
\mathbf{x}_{0,i}
:

\phi_{0}(p_{i})=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i}))\approx\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i})+\nabla\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i})\cdot v_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i}).

Assuming the undeformed point
\mathbf{x}_{0,i}
was on or very close to the original surface,
\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i})\approx 0
. The gradient
\nabla\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i})
is the normal vector
q_{0,i}
to the undeformed surface at
\mathbf{x}_{0,i}
. For small deformations, we approximate
q_{0,i}\approx q_{i}
. Substituting the displacement vector:

\phi_{0}(p_{i})\approx 0+q_{i}\cdot(-\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}q_{i})=-\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}(q_{i}\cdot q_{i}).

Since
q_{i}
is a unit vector,
q_{i}\cdot q_{i}=1
. Therefore,

\phi_{0}(p_{i})\approx-\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}=-\frac{P_{i}}{2{E^{*}}r}.

This shows that the value of the undeformed SDF at the final contact point is approximately the negative of the indentation depth required in the flat case.
∎

We now incorporate the effect of initial surface curvature
\kappa
using the additive approximation developed previously. This result is related to the superposition of a Boussinesq’s solution to a point load
[
17
]
.

Proposition 3

(Undeformed SDF Estimate: Curved Case)
.

Consider the loading conditions of Assumption
3
applied to an initially curved elastic body. Let
p_{i}
be the center of the contact area after force
P_{i}
is applied. Let the initial undeformed shape near
p_{i}
have a local curvature represented by
\kappa(\mathbf{x})
. The value of the undeformed SDF
\phi_{0}
evaluated at
p_{i}
is approximately related to the total indentation depth
\delta_{\mathrm{total},i}
, which includes both elastic deformation and geometric flattening:

\phi_{0}(p_{i})\approx-\delta_{\mathrm{total},i}\approx-\left(\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}+\delta_{\mathrm{geom},i}\right),

where
\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}=P_{i}/(2{E^{*}}r)
is the flat-case elastic indentation, and
\delta_{\mathrm{geom},i}
represents the average initial geometric gap over the contact area due to curvature.

Specifically:

•

If the curvature is approximately constant
\kappa_{i}
over the contact area:

\delta_{\mathrm{geom},i}\approx\frac{\kappa_{i}r^{2}}{4},\quad\text{so}\quad\phi_{0}(p_{i})\approx-\left(\frac{P_{i}}{2{E^{*}}r}+\frac{\kappa_{i}r^{2}}{4}\right).

•

If the curvature
\kappa(\rho,\theta)
varies significantly over the contact area (in polar coordinates
(\rho,\theta)
centered at
p_{i}
on the tangent plane), and approximating the initial gap as
h(\rho,\theta)\approx\kappa(\rho,\theta)\rho^{2}/2
:

\delta_{\mathrm{geom},i}=\langle h\rangle_{i}=\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\kappa(\rho,\theta)\rho^{2}}{2}\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta.

Then:

\phi_{0}(p_{i})\approx-\left(\frac{P_{i}}{2{E^{*}}r}+\langle h\rangle_{i}\right).

Proof Sketch.

The total indentation
\delta_{\mathrm{total},i}
required at the center
p_{i}
must achieve two goals: (1) deform the material elastically as if it were flat, corresponding to
\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}
, and (2) close the initial geometric gap
h(\mathbf{x})
that exists between the tangent plane at
p_{i}
and the curved surface over the contact radius
r
.

The additive approximation assumes these contributions can be superimposed:

\delta_{\mathrm{total},i}\approx\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}+\delta_{\mathrm{geom},i}.

The term
\delta_{\mathrm{geom},i}
represents the effective depth needed to flatten the initial curvature over the contact area. A reasonable estimate for this is the average value of the initial gap
h(\mathbf{x})
over the circular contact area
A=\pi r^{2}
:

\delta_{\mathrm{geom},i}=\langle h\rangle_{i}=\frac{1}{A}\iint_{A}h(\mathbf{x})\,dA.

For small distances
\rho
from the center
p_{i}
on the tangent plane, the gap due to curvature
\kappa
is approximately
h(\rho)\approx\kappa\rho^{2}/2
.

Case 1: Constant Curvature
\kappa_{i}
.

The average gap is:

\begin{split}\langle h\rangle_{i}&=\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{r}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}\rho^{2}}{2}\right)\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta=\frac{2\pi}{\pi r^{2}}\frac{\kappa_{i}}{2}\int_{0}^{r}\rho^{3}\,d\rho\\
&=\frac{\kappa_{i}}{r^{2}}\left[\frac{\rho^{4}}{4}\right]_{0}^{r}=\frac{\kappa_{i}r^{2}}{4}.\end{split}

Case 2: Varying Curvature
\kappa(\rho,\theta)
.

Assuming
h(\rho,\theta)\approx\kappa(\rho,\theta)\rho^{2}/2
, the average gap becomes the integral expression stated in the Proposition.

Using the same Taylor expansion argument as in the proof of Proposition
2
, but recognizing that the total required normal displacement is now
\delta_{\mathrm{total},i}
, we arrive at:

\phi_{0}(p_{i})\approx-\delta_{\mathrm{total},i}\approx-(\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}+\delta_{\mathrm{geom},i}).

Substituting the expressions for
\delta_{\mathrm{flat},i}
and
\delta_{\mathrm{geom},i}
gives the results stated.

VII
Young’s Modulus Determination

We can estimate the material’s Young’s modulus by applying incremental forces at approximately the same location and measuring the resulting change in indentation depth. This method relies on the insight that the geometric contribution to indentation, due to initial curvature, cancels out when considering the difference between two indentation states.

Proposition 4

(Young’s Modulus Estimation)
.

Let the conditions of Assumption
3
hold for two consecutive probes,
i
and
i+1
, at approximately the same location. Let the applied forces be
P_{i}
and
P_{i+1}
, with
P_{i+1}>P_{i}
. Let the corresponding contact points be
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
, and assume the surface normal
q
remains approximately constant (
q_{i}\approx q_{i+1}\approx q
). Assume the Poisson’s ratio
\nu
is known.

If the displacement of the probe between the two steps,
\Delta p=p_{i+1}-p_{i}
, occurs purely along the normal direction
q
, then the magnitude of this displacement corresponds to the change in indentation depth,
\Delta\delta=\|\Delta p\|
. The plane strain modulus
{E^{*}}
can be estimated as:

{E^{*}}\approx\frac{P_{i+1}-P_{i}}{2r\Delta\delta}=\frac{P_{i+1}-P_{i}}{2r\|p_{i+1}-p_{i}\|}.

Consequently, the Young’s modulus
E
is estimated as:

E={E^{*}}(1-\nu^{2})\approx\frac{(P_{i+1}-P_{i})(1-\nu^{2})}{2r\|p_{i+1}-p_{i}\|}.

Proof.

We use the additive approximation for the total indentation depth from Proposition
3
. Let
\delta_{j}
be the total indentation depth under force
P_{j}
at point
p_{j}
. Assuming the curvature
\kappa
and punch radius
r
are constant for both small incremental steps at approximately the same location:

\delta_{i}\approx\frac{P_{i}}{2{E^{*}}r}+\delta_{\mathrm{geom}},\quad\delta_{i+1}\approx\frac{P_{i+1}}{2{E^{*}}r}+\delta_{\mathrm{geom}},

where
\delta_{\mathrm{geom}}
is the geometric indentation term (e.g.,
\kappa r^{2}/4
for constant curvature).

The change in indentation depth,
\Delta\delta
, between state
i
and
i+1
is:

\Delta\delta=\delta_{i+1}-\delta_{i}\approx=\frac{P_{i+1}-P_{i}}{2{E^{*}}r}.

Note that the geometric term
\delta_{\mathrm{geom}}
cancels out.

Under the crucial assumption that the probe displacement
\Delta p=p_{i+1}-p_{i}
occurs purely along the normal direction
q
(i.e.,
\Delta p
is parallel to
q
), the magnitude of this displacement is precisely the change in indentation depth
\Delta\delta=\|p_{i+1}-p_{i}\|
.

More generally, if the motion is not purely normal,
\Delta\delta=|\langle p_{i+1}-p_{i},q\rangle|
. Assuming pure normal motion simplifies to
\Delta\delta=\|p_{i+1}-p_{i}\|
.

Substituting this into the expression for
\Delta\delta
:

\|p_{i+1}-p_{i}\|\approx\frac{P_{i+1}-P_{i}}{2{E^{*}}r}.

Rearranging to solve for
{E^{*}}
:

{E^{*}}\approx\frac{P_{i+1}-P_{i}}{2r\|p_{i+1}-p_{i}\|}.

Finally, using the definition
{E^{*}}=E/(1-\nu^{2})
, we find the estimate for Young’s modulus:

E\approx\frac{(P_{i+1}-P_{i})(1-\nu^{2})}{2r\|p_{i+1}-p_{i}\|}.

∎

Remark 2
.

The accuracy of this method hinges on the assumption that the probe displacement
\|p_{i+1}-p_{i}\|
accurately reflects the change in normal indentation
\Delta\delta
. Any tangential motion or significant change in the surface normal between probes introduces error.

VIII
Estimation of Young’s Modulus and Curvature from Compliance Variation

While the two-point method (Proposition
4
) estimates Young’s modulus by assuming a locally linear force-displacement relationship (constant incremental compliance), it cannot determine curvature as the geometric indentation term cancels out. However, if the incremental compliance itself changes with the load level, this variation may contain information about the underlying curvature, particularly if the contact behavior transitions between regimes.

We propose a method based on the hypothesis that contact behaves like Hertzian contact at a contact radius
a<r
at low forces, where the model assumes a sphere of radius
R=1/\kappa
on a flat surface. From this low-force regime, we transition towards the flat-punch model (contact radius fixed at
a=r
) at higher forces
[
16
, Ch. 5]
:

•

Hertzian Regime (
a<r
):
Indentation
\delta\approx CP^{2/3}
, where
C=(9\kappa/(16{E^{*}}^{2}))^{1/3}
. The incremental compliance is
m(P)=\mathrm{d}\delta/\mathrm{d}P\approx C(2/3)P^{-1/3}
.

•

Flat Punch Regime (
a=r
):
Additive model
\delta\approx P/(2{E^{*}}r)+\delta_{\mathrm{geom}}
. The incremental compliance is
m=\mathrm{d}\delta/\mathrm{d}P\approx 1/(2{E^{*}}r)
, which is constant.

The transition force is approximately
P_{\mathrm{trans}}\approx(4/3){E^{*}}r^{3}\kappa
. If probing occurs across this transition, the measured incremental compliance
m_{j}=\Delta\delta_{j}/\Delta P_{j}
should decrease as the force level increases.

Proposition 5

(Estimation of E and
\kappa
from Varying Compliance)
.

Consider a series of
N\geq 3
probes (
j=1,\dots,N
) applied at the same nominal location with increasing forces
P_{j}
. Assume the conditions of Proposition
4
regarding normal displacement hold for each increment. Let
\nu
and
r
be known. Assume the lowest forces (
P_{1},P_{2}
) correspond to a predominantly Hertzian regime, and the highest forces (
P_{N-1},P_{N}
) correspond to the flat-punch regime.

Procedure:

1.

Calculate incremental forces
\Delta P_{j}=P_{j+1}-P_{j}
and displacements
\Delta\delta_{j}=\|p_{j+1}-p_{j}\|
for
j=1,\dots,N-1
.

2.

Calculate the incremental compliance for each interval:
m_{j}=\Delta\delta_{j}/\Delta P_{j}
.

3.

Estimate the compliance in the high-force (flat-punch) regime,
m_{\mathrm{high}}
. This could be
m_{N-1}
or an average over several high-force intervals if
N
is large.

4.

Estimate the plane strain modulus
{E^{*}}
using the high-force compliance:

\hat{{E^{*}}}=\frac{1}{2rm_{\mathrm{high}}}.

Estimate Young’s modulus as
\hat{E}=\hat{{E^{*}}}(1-\nu^{2})
.

5.

Estimate the compliance in the low-force (Hertzian) regime,
m_{\mathrm{low}}
(e.g.,
m_{1}
). Let
P_{\mathrm{low}}
be the average force in this interval,
P_{\mathrm{low}}=(P_{1}+P_{2})/2
.

6.

Estimate the Hertz constant
C
by assuming
m_{\mathrm{low}}\approx\mathrm{d}\delta/\mathrm{d}P|_{P_{\mathrm{low}}}
from the Hertzian model:

\hat{C}\approx m_{\mathrm{low}}\frac{3}{2}P_{\mathrm{low}}^{1/3}.

7.

Estimate the curvature
\kappa
using the Hertz relation
\hat{C}^{3}=9\hat{\kappa}/(16\hat{{E^{*}}}^{2})
and the previously estimated
\hat{{E^{*}}}
:

\hat{\kappa}=\frac{16\hat{C}^{3}\hat{{E^{*}}}^{2}}{9}.

Substituting the expressions for
\hat{C}
and
\hat{{E^{*}}}
:

\begin{split}\hat{\kappa}&\approx\frac{16}{9}\left(m_{\mathrm{low}}\frac{3}{2}P_{\mathrm{low}}^{1/3}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{1}{2rm_{\mathrm{high}}}\right)^{2}\\
&=\frac{16}{9}\cdot m_{\mathrm{low}}^{3}\cdot\frac{27}{8}P_{\mathrm{low}}\cdot\frac{1}{4r^{2}m_{\mathrm{high}}^{2}},\end{split}

\hat{\kappa}\approx\frac{3}{2}\frac{P_{\mathrm{low}}m_{\mathrm{low}}^{3}}{r^{2}m_{\mathrm{high}}^{2}}.

Proof Sketch.

The estimation of
\hat{{E^{*}}}
follows directly from equating the high-force incremental compliance
m_{\mathrm{high}}
with the theoretical constant compliance
1/(2r{E^{*}})
from the flat-punch model.
The estimation of
\hat{C}
relies on approximating the incremental compliance
m_{\mathrm{low}}=\Delta\delta_{1}/\Delta P_{1}
at low forces with the derivative
\mathrm{d}\delta/\mathrm{d}P
from the Hertzian model
\delta=CP^{2/3}
, evaluated at the average force
P_{\mathrm{low}}
. Specifically,
\mathrm{d}\delta/\mathrm{d}P=C(2/3)P^{-1/3}
, which is rearranged to solve for
C
.
The final estimation of
\hat{\kappa}
uses the definition of the Hertz constant
C=(9\kappa/(16{E^{*}}^{2}))^{1/3}
, substituting the previously estimated values
\hat{C}
and
\hat{{E^{*}}}
and solving for
\kappa
. The algebraic simplification yields the final expression.

X
Conclusion

This work introduced PROD, a novel framework for reconstructing both the geometry and mechanical properties of deformable objects using elastodynamic signed distance functions. The proposed method demonstrated robustness in handling pose errors, non-normal force applications, and curvature inaccuracies in simulated scenarios. Outstanding future work includes validating PROD on a robotic platform with ex vivo tissue to assess real-time performance, as well as conducting sensitivity and robustness analyses to evaluate the method’s applicability across diverse materials and probing conditions. These extensions will further solidify the practical viability of PROD in robotic manipulation, medical imaging, and haptic feedback systems.
