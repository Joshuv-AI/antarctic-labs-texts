Comparative transcriptome analysis reveals the responses of sweetpotato to continuous cropping stress
Authors: Zhiyuan Gao, Meikun Han, Meng Kou, Yaya Hu, Chen Li, Lanfu Liu, Mengjiao Lan, Xinliang Liu, Yaling Lei, Weijing Jiao, Zhimin Ma, Qiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContinuous cropping obstacles (CCO) severely limit the sustainable development of sweetpotato (Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam.) industry. However, the transcriptional regulatory network of sweetpotato in response to continuous cropping stress (CCS) is unreported. Accordingly, transcriptome sequencing was employed to elucidate this network in two cultivars (Shangshu19 and Yizi138).ResultsCCS markedly inhibited the growth of Yizi138, while its inhibition on Shangshu19 was relatively minor. Additionally, significantly more differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected in Shangshu19 compared with Yizi138. Metabolic pathways, plant hormone signal transduction, and the MAPK signaling pathway exert crucial functions in defending Shangshu19 against CCS. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) demonstrated that multiple genes in the MEdarkred module, such as IbPR1, IbWRKY70, and IbCRK10, exhibited a positive correlation with continuous cropping tolerance, suggesting that these genes may be critically involved in boosting sweetpotato tolerance to continuous cropping. In contrast, continuous cropping impaired the photosynthetic system and energy metabolism in Yizi138, inhibiting its growth.ConclusionsShangshu19 enhances its tolerance to CCS via three strategies: regulation of key pathways, maintenance of physiological functions, and construction of a coordinated regulatory network mediated by IbPR1, IbWRKY70, and IbCRK10. These findings provide a theoretical foundation and genetic resources for breeding continuous cropping tolerance sweetpotato cultivars.

Background

Sweetpotato, which belongs to the genus Ipomoea (family Convolvulaceae), is a vegetatively propagated crop characterized by strong drought tolerance, resistance to barrenness, wide adaptability, and high yields [1–2]. It also serves as a vital source of food, feed, and industrial raw material [3]. At present, China is the world’s largest sweetpotato producer [4], and in 2024, its national sweetpotato production reached 51.57 million tons [5]. The sustainable stability and increase of sweetpotato yield in China are vital to the sustainable development of agriculture and related industries [6].

With the advancement of modern agriculture in China, large-scale, specialized, and intensive sweetpotato production has developed rapidly. However, owing to limited cultivated land resources, continuous cropping is prevalent in major sweetpotato producing areas, limiting yields. Moreover, in recent years, both the area and duration of continuous cropping have increased considerably, rendering sweetpotato continuous cropping obstacles (CCO) increasingly severe. CCO are described as the phenomenon in which the consecutive planting of the same crop in the same field for several years under normal cultivation and management practices leads to reduced crop yield, deteriorated quality, poor growth performance, and aggravated occurrence of diseases and insect pests [7]. Studies have shown that continuous cropping significantly reduces sweetpotato yield, increases disease incidence, and causes widespread plant mortality [8–9]. Xu et al. [10] reported that CCS significantly reduced sweetpotato yield and affected the nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium nutrient status of storage roots, resulting in increased nitrogen and phosphorus contents but decreased potassium content in tuberous roots. Huang et al. [11] revealed that, compared with crop rotation, CCS decreased the contents of starch, soluble sugar and the dry matter rate of sweetpotato. Our field investigation found that CCS decreased the maximum vine length, branch number and yield of sweetpotato. Similarly, Li et al. [8] confirmed that CCS significantly increased the disease incidence and reduced the yield of sweetpotato. This has severely restricted the healthy and sustainable development of China’s sweetpotato industry. Thus, elucidating the mechanisms of sweetpotato CCO and breeding tolerant cultivars are critical for the industry’s sustainable development.

At present, research on CCO has been extensively documented in a range of crops, including maize, soybean, protected vegetables, and tuberous root medicinal plants such as Rehmannia glutinosa [12–16]. Studies have demonstrated that these obstacles arise from a complex interplay of biotic and abiotic factors in the plant-soil-microbe system, including root exudates, soil nutrients, and soil microbial community composition [9, 15]. Nevertheless, relevant research on sweetpotato CCO remains relatively scarce. Our previous studies revealed that continuous cropping impacts rhizosphere soil microbial community structure of sweetpotato, increasing harmful microbial abundance, and ultimately reducing yield [9, 17–18]. Xu et al. explored the soil nutrient factors in driving CCO [19]. Research on breeding for continuous cropping tolerance in sweetpotato is still in its infancy. Xu et al. screened sweetpotato accessions for continuous cropping tolerance by calculating yield ratios between CCO-affected fields and normal fields combined with cluster analysis. They identified one highly tolerant cultivar (Su 33) and four moderately tolerant accessions (Xu A1-144, Xu D1-95, Ningzi 1, and Xu B21-38), providing valuable germplasm resources for sweetpotato production and breeding of new continuous cropping-tolerant cultivars [10]. However, continuous cropping-tolerant sweetpotato breeding encounters numerous difficulties, among which two are particularly prominent. First, sweetpotato is an allohexaploid species (2n = 6x = 90) with a large and highly heterozygous genome, which poses great challenges to molecular marker-assisted breeding. Second, CCS is a complex composite stress, whose molecular response network is far more intricate than that of single stresses, and the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain unclear.

Driven by the ongoing revolution in high-throughput sequencing technologies, researchers have gradually focused on the molecular mechanisms underlying CCO and explored their impacts on plant gene expression. Similar to other types of adverse environmental stresses, such as salinity and drought, which affect gene expression profiles of plants, continuous cropping inevitably induces the expression of specific genes in vivo [20]. For instance, Hang et al. observed 412 DEGs in the roots and 5,020 DEGs in the leaves of Pinellia ternata after 2 years of continuous cropping, relative to first-year plants. Furthermore, hub genes in the roots were mainly enriched in pathways including sugar synthesis and metabolism, phosphate ion transport, jasmonic acid metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, plant defense response, and plant-pathogen interaction [21]. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses by Huang et al. [22] also revealed that continuous cropping perturbed metabolic pathways and secondary metabolite biosynthesis in sugar beet. Additionally, Shen et al. [23] demonstrated marked differences in the expression levels of genes associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, and plant-pathogen interaction in Panax ginseng under continuous cropping stress (CCS). Similarly, continuous cropping has also been shown to downregulate nutrition-related genes in strawberries while upregulating defense-related genes [24].

However, research on the molecular mechanisms underlying CCO remains scarce, and the gene expression responses of sweetpotato to CCS have not yet been reported. Therefore, we employed transcriptome sequencing to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the responses of sweetpotato to CCS, providing a theoretical foundation for breeding continuous cropping tolerant sweetpotato cultivars, thereby promoting sustainable sweetpotato production.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sample collection

Shangshu19 (S19) and Yizi 138 (Y138) were used in this study. According to field observations and preliminary production practices, S19 exhibited better growth and yield stability under continuous cropping conditions than Y138. Therefore, S19 and Y138 were used as tolerant and sensitive genotypes, respectively. Healthy and uniform seedlings of S19 and Y138 were provided by the Institute of Cereal and Oil Crops, Hebei Academy of Agricultural and Forestry Sciences (Hebei, China). The field experiments were conducted in Dongming Village, Xinle City, Hebei Province, China (38.37° N, 114.66° E). In brief, the experimental field was divided into four sections as follows: newly cultivated land, and land previously cultivated with S19 and Y138 for 1, 2, and 3 years. Three replicate plots were established for each treatment and for each cultivar. Further, each plot comprised six 10-m rows with the row spacing at 0.8 m. In May, S19 and Y138 seedlings were planted in the four different plots described above, corresponding to continuous cropping durations of 0, 1, 2, and 3 years, representing the first, second, third, and fourth planting years, respectively, and harvested in October. During the root bulking stage, samples from the stem base of sweetpotato plants were collected. Three biological replicates were established per treatment. Stem bases from three plants per cultivar per treatment were pooled, cut into pieces, and thoroughly mixed to form one biological replicate. After processing, samples were promptly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Determination of field agronomic traits and calculation of continuous cropping tolerance indices

The maximum vine length and branch numbers of sweetpotato plants were measured, and yields were determined at harvest. Furthermore, the continuous cropping tolerance was evaluated by calculating the tolerance index to continuous cropping (TCC) as previously described [25], with appropriate modifications (Eq. (1)). RNA extraction and cDNA library preparation and sequencing

TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to extract total RNA from the plant tissues following the manufacturer’s protocol, and to remove genomic DNA, DNase I (Takara Bio Inc., Kyoto, Japan) was employed. Thereafter, the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to determine RNA quality, and the ND-2000 (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) was used to quantify the RNA. In brief, oligo (dT) beads were used for polyA selection to isolate mRNA, and fragmentation buffer was employed to fragment the mRNA. cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-base addition, and ligation of Illumina-indexed adaptors were performed in accordance with Illumina’s protocol. Libraries were then subjected to size selection for 200–300 bp cDNA target fragments using 2% low-range ultra-agarose gel electrophoresis followed by PCR amplification. Quantification was conducted using the TBS380 prior to sequencing of paired-end libraries on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform (150 bp*2, Shanghai BIOZERON Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Read quality control and mapping

Raw paired-end reads were trimmed and quality controlled using Trimmomatic (v0.39). Thereafter, clean reads were individually aligned to the sweetpotato genome (https://sweetpotato.uga.edu/) using HISAT2 software (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml) in the orientation mode [26–27].

Differential gene expression and enrichment analyses

To identify DEGs between the two sweetpotato cultivars under different continuous cropping durations, the fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM) method was used to determine gene expression levels. Further, DEGs were identified using the R package edgeR with thresholds at: |log2FC| > 1 and false discovery rates (FDR) < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted with Goatools (https://github.com/tanghaibao/Goatools) and KOBAS (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/home.do), respectively.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

Genes were included in the WGCNA based on the requirement that their mean FPKM values were > 1. Thereafter, the gene expression matrix was derived from transcriptome data of 24 samples (continuous cropping-tolerant S19 and sensitive Y138, under 0, 1, 2, 3 years of continuous cropping, with three biological replicates) under CCS. The soft-thresholding power was set at 9, and for module–trait correlation, two key traits were selected: continuous cropping year (0, 1, 2, 3 years) and cultivar type (tolerant S19 vs. sensitive Y138), which reflect the time-dependent effect of CCS and differential cultivar responses, respectively. Correlation coefficients between gene modules and these continuous cropping treatments were determined using the eigengene values of different modules. Additionally, module membership (kME) was used to identify hub genes with high intramodular connectivity. Genes with kME > 0.7 were selected as module members, as they are more representative of the expression pattern of the corresponding module.

RT-qPCR analyses

For RT-qPCR validation, nine DEGs were used, and ARF (JX177359) served as the internal reference gene [28]. RT-qPCR was performed as previously described [26]. The 2−ΔΔCT method was employed to calculate the relative expression levels, based on three independent experiments [29]. The primers (Table S1) used were designed using Primer3Plus software.

Statistical analyses

Data were presented as means ± SD (n = 3). Significant differences among treatments were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Effects of continuous cropping on sweetpotato growth

The growth of cultivar Y138 was significantly inhibited by CCS. Notably, the maximum vine length, branch number, and yield of Y138 decreased progressively with increasing continuous cropping duration, and root rot and plant death were frequently observed under 3 years of continuous cropping. In contrast, the continuous cropping tolerant cultivar S19 exhibited relatively minor changes under the different continuous cropping durations (Fig. 1a). Further analysis showed that S19 had significantly higher TCC values than Y138 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1b, c, d). Specifically, the TCC values based on yield, maximum vine length, and branch number in S19 were 3.29–5.55, 1.99–5.74, and 2.31–9.74 times those in Y138, respectively. Over the 3-year continuous cropping period, the TCC values of S19 decreased slightly by an average of 32.0%, 14.5%, and 6.0% for yield, maximum vine length, and branch number, respectively. In contrast, the TCC values of Y138 declined drastically by an average of 83.0%, 73.9%, and 77.8% for the same three traits, with the reduction being 2.6-, 5.1-, and 13-fold higher in Y138 than in S19. These results confirmed that S19 exhibits greater tolerance to continuous cropping than Y138.

Transcriptome analysis

A total of 24 cDNA libraries (corresponding to two cultivars × four continuous cropping durations × three biological replicates, designated 0-S19, 0-Y138, 1-S19, 1-Y138, 2-S19, 2-Y138, 3-S19, and 3-Y138) were constructed. High-throughput sequencing yielded 1,308,443,314 clean reads, and quality evaluation showed Q20 and Q30 values above 94% across all libraries, with average guanine and cytosine (GC) contents at ~ 50%, implying balanced base composition and high sequencing quality (Table S2). On average, 84% of the clean reads were mapped to the reference genome, confirming reliable transcriptome coverage. Additionally, Pearson correlation analysis of gene expression levels, visualized as a heatmap (Fig. 2), further confirmed good biological reproducibility among the replicates.

DEGs analysis

For the purpose of elucidating how CCS affects gene expression patterns in S19 and Y138, DEGs were identified with the gene expression profiles of 0-year continuous cropping plants serving as the control. Results demonstrated that, relative to the control, 7409, 6658, and 7075 DEGs were identified in S19 under 1, 2, and 3 years continuous cropping, respectively, with 2911 common DEGs across the three years (Fig. 3a, b). Similarly, following 1, 2, 3 years of continuous cropping, 5970, 4547 and 5040 DEGs were detected in Y138, respectively, with 1446 common DEGs across the three years (Fig. 3c, d). Notably, comparative analysis indicated that more DEGs were observed in S19 than in Y138 in each continuous cropping year. Furthermore, 5946, 6508, 6903, and 5251 DEGs were identified in S19 vs. Y138 at the 0, 1, 2, and 3 years of continuous cropping stages, respectively. DEG numbers showed a single-peak pattern, peaking at 2 years of continuous cropping, with 1353 DEGs shared across all four treatments (Fig. 3e, f).

GO and KEGG analyses results

GO analysis was carried out on the 2,911 and 1,446 DEGs common to S19 and Y138, respectively, after continuous cropping for 1, 2, 3 years (Fig. S1a, b). The results showed that DEGs were enriched in molecular function (MF) terms, such as catalytic and kinase activities, oxidoreductase activity, and biological process (BP) terms, such as phosphorylation, in both cultivars. Additionally, S19’s DEGs were further enriched in heme binding, tetrapyrrole binding, carbohydrate metabolic process, and transmembrane transport, indicating that S19 possibly maintained their growth vigor under CCS via enhanced photosynthetic (heme/tetrapyrrole) and energy metabolism (carbohydrates) systems [30]. In contrast, Y138’s DEGs were enriched in transferase activity, ion binding, and protein modification process, macromolecule modification, and phosphorus metabolic process. The 1353 common DEGs identified in the S19 vs. Y138 comparison were enriched in catalytic activity, ion binding, defense response, response to stress, and response to stimulus (Fig. 4a). These findings indicated key differences in stress-coping mechanisms between S19 and Y138, with more significant DEGs expression in S19 facilitating its adaptation to continuous cropping.

KEGG analysis was performed on the same sets of 2,911 and 1,446 DEGs common to S19 and Y138, respectively, after continuous cropping for 1, 2, 3 years. DEGs in both cultivars were enriched in biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, starch and sucrose metabolism, and Tropane, piperidine and pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis (Fig. S2a, b). In S19, DEGs were further enriched in predominantly upregulated metabolic pathways, whereas genes involved in plant hormone signal transduction and the MAPK signaling pathway – plant were predominantly downregulated. However, DEGs in Y138 were enriched in downregulated photosynthesis and oxidative phosphorylation, together with downregulated thermogenesis, while zeatin biosynthesis was upregulated. The 1353 common DEGs identified in the S19 vs. Y138 comparison were significantly enriched in biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, glucosinolate biosynthesis, cutin, suberin, and wax biosynthesis, and plant-pathogen interaction. (Fig. 4b).

WGCNA results

WGCNA based on FPKM values identified 18 co-expression modules (Fig. 5a), among which MEsteelblue module (607 genes) was the largest and MEdarkorange2 module (31 genes) the smallest. Correlation analysis further indicated that the MEdarkred, MEdarkmagenta, and MEdarkorange2 modules (comprising 427, 145, and 31 genes, respectively) exhibited a significant positive correlation with continuous cropping tolerance (R > 0.5, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5b, c). These three modules exhibited strong positive correlations with S19 under 3, 1, and 2 years continuous cropping conditions, respectively (consistent with module order). Notably, the gene expression pattern of the MEdarkred module shifted from negative to positive with the extension of S19 continuous cropping duration, showing negative correlations with the 0- and 1- year continuous cropping, but positive correlations with 2- and 3- year continuous cropping. Further analysis of genes in the MEdarkred module identified multiple candidate genes, including PR (IbPR1), transcription factors (TFs) (IbWRKY70, IbbHLH93, IbERF13), cytochrome P450 family genes (IbCYP82D47, IbCYP736A12), oxidoreductases, kinases, chitinases, zinc finger proteins, transferases, and metabolism-related genes (Fig. S3a, b, c; Table S3). These candidate genes displayed higher expression levels in S19 than in Y138 under 2-year and 3-year CCS, implying their potential critical roles in mediating continuous cropping tolerance in sweetpotatoes.

Verification of DEGs expression by RT-qPCR

For validation of the RNA-seq data, nine DEGs were chosen to undergo RT-qPCR analysis. Detailed information including gene IDs and putative functions of these genes is listed in Table S4. The relative expression levels of these nine genes quantified via RT-qPCR were compared with their FPKM values derived via RNA-seq. The results thus obtained demonstrated a highly consistent trend between the two datasets (Fig. 6). Additionally, linear regression analysis (Fig. 7) revealed a significant linear correlation between the two datasets (y = 1.2386x + 0.1831, R² = 0.85), indicating a high degree of concordance and confirming the authenticity and reliability of the transcriptome data. Among these DEGs, the FPKM values and RT-qPCR relative expression levels of six stress-tolerance related genes (IbPR1、IbACO3、IbCYP82D47、IbCRK10、IbCHI、IbWRKY70) were gradually upregulated with increasing continuous cropping years, and their expression levels were consistently higher in the tolerant cultivar S19 than in the sensitive cultivar Y138, implying that these genes may serve as positive regulators contributing to continuous cropping tolerance in sweetpotato. By contrast, the other three genes (IbERF109、IbMYB44、IbbHLH162) showed high expression under control conditions (0 years of continuous cropping), and their transcript levels fluctuated but remained lower than the control after continuous cropping. Moreover, these three genes were expressed significantly higher in Y138 than in S19 under continuous cropping for 1, 2, and 3 years, suggesting that they may act as negative regulators of continuous cropping tolerance in sweetpotato.

Discussion

CCO are prevalent in diverse crops and impair plant growth. In Codonopsis pilosula, vine length, root length/diameter/dry weight/fresh weight, and yield declined progressively with prolonged continuous cropping [31]. However, the responses to continuous cropping vary significantly across crop species and genotypes [32–33]. Ma et al. observed that continuous cropping inhibits pea growth, exerting stronger suppressive effects on the Dingwan10 than on the Yunwan8 [34]. Likewise, our results indicated that continuous cropping compromised sweetpotato growth, exerting more severe inhibition on the sensitive genotype Y138. In contrast, the tolerant genotype S19 was less affected, indicating a distinct genotypic difference in continuous cropping tolerance between the two cultivars.

Transcriptome sequencing, a high-throughput, high-resolution, and sensitive approach that has been extensively used in recent years, provides a powerful tool to assess regulatory mechanisms and functional genes in crops [35]. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying sweetpotato responses to CCS, transcriptome sequencing was conducted on genotypes S19 and Y138 subjected to 0, 1, 2, and 3 years of CCS. Results showed that S19 had more DEGs than Y138 across all cropping durations, indicating a more active and complex transcriptome regulation. This extensive gene expression reprogramming may underpin the strong continuous cropping tolerance of S19. In contrast to our results, Ma et al. found that continuous cropping resulted in more DEGs in the continuous cropping sensitive Dingwan10 than in the relatively tolerant Yunwan8 [34], possibly due to interspecific genetic divergence. Alternatively, this discrepancy may be associated with the dominant CCS types and divergent reproductive modes. In sweetpotato, CCS is primarily driven by soil-borne pathogens [17], and the tolerant S19 employs an active adaptation-reprogramming strategy by activating numerous defense-related DEGs, whereas the sensitive Y138 exhibits a passive response. In pea, by contrast, stress mainly arises from autotoxic allelopathy such as erucamide that cause membrane injury and oxidative stress [36, 37]. The tolerant Yunwan8 maintains homeostasis with fewer DEGs, whereas the sensitive Dingwan10 has abundant DEGs due to stress-induced transcriptional dysregulation. Reproductive modes also contribute to this divergence. Sweetpotato is a vegetatively propagated crop with a narrow genetic background. S19 relies on inducible defense mechanism to induce massive DEGs under CCS, while Y138 shows a weak stress response with fewer DEGs. In contrast, pea undergoes seed propagation and can accumulate stress resistance genes via genetic recombination. The tolerance of Yunwan8 is attributed to the homeostatic expression of core stress-resistant genes, which avoids large-scale transcriptome adjustment. However, Dingwan10 has dramatic transcriptome fluctuations and more DEGs due to insufficient regulatory stability. These distinct responses reflect species-specific survival strategies, highlighting the complexity of molecular mechanisms in plant responses to CCS.

In this study, the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs in S19 were significantly enriched in metabolic pathways, plant hormone signal transduction, and the MAPK signaling pathway – plant, suggesting the establishment of an active defense system against CCS in S19, primarily via global metabolic network regulation, hormone signal transduction, and stress-signal cascade. In contrast, the DEGs in Y138 showed significant enrichment in pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation and photosynthesis, accompanied by a predominance of downregulated genes (17 of the 18 genes in the oxidative phosphorylation pathway and 7 of the 8 genes in the photosynthesis pathway were downregulated). Photosynthesis synthesizes carbohydrates essential for plants. The inhibition of photosynthesis directly constrains plant growth and development. Previous study has shown that continuous cropping reduces the net photosynthetic rate of Pinellia ternata. Consistently, after two years of continuous cropping, DEGs were significantly enriched in photosynthesis pathway, with 13 genes downregulated under CCS, including PtPsaA, PtPsaD, and PtPsbW [38]. Similarly, in our study, key photosynthesis-related genes, including IbpsbB (K02704), IbpsbC (K02705), IbpsbD (K02706), IbFNR (K02641), IbpsaI (K02696), and Ibbeta (K02112) were downregulated (Fig. 8). This finding was in line with the result of Wang et al., who reported that continuous cropping downregulates PopsbB, PopsbC, PopsbD, and Pobeta in Polygonatum odoratum [39]. Collectively, these findings suggested that continuous cropping possibly impairs the photosynthetic system as well as energy metabolism in Y138, thereby inhibiting its growth.

Additionally, DEGs were also enriched in the plant-pathogen interaction pathway. Previous studies have reported an increase in the population of harmful microorganisms in sweetpotato rhizosphere soil under continuous cropping [8, 9, 17, 18], suggesting potential differences in the abilities of S19 and Y138 to respond to continuous cropping-related pathogenic pressure. The annotated DEGs in this pathway included IbCML8 (K13448), IbRPM1 (K13457), IbPR1 (K13449), and IbFLS2 (K13420) (Fig. 9). Notably, among them, the expression level of IbPR1 was the highest, with its FPKM value in S19 significantly higher than that in Y138. Furthermore, IbPR1 expression in the S19 also increased significantly as the continuous cropping duration increased, aligning with the research of He et al., who found that CtPR1 is significantly upregulated in Codonopsis tangshen in response to CCS [41]. Therefore, IbPR1 may be a key gene underlying the continuous cropping tolerance of S19.

As a robust tool for mining transcriptome data, WGCNA has been extensively employed to screen key modules and core genes associated with stress tolerance in crops [42–43]. In this study, the transcriptome data of sweetpotato grown under continuous cropping was subjected to WGCNA. The MEdarkred module showed a significant positive correlation with continuous cropping tolerance in sweetpotato and enriched in multiple defense-related genes, such as PR genes, transcription factors, kinases, and chitinases. These genes may form a molecular regulatory network that drives tolerance to continuous cropping in sweetpotato via synergistic interactions. This result aligned with the research conducted by Chen et al. [24], who observed the upregulated expression of defense-related WRKY transcription factors, PR1, and peroxidase genes in strawberry root systems under CCS. Similarly, it has been shown that defense-associated genes, including PoPR1, chitinase, and peroxidase, play critical roles in the response of Polygonatum odoratum to CCS [44].

The PR1 gene is an indispensable component of plant innate immune responses against biotic and abiotic stresses. Studies have demonstrated that inducing PR1 expression not only confers pathogens resistance, but also elicits broad-spectrum stress tolerance, thereby making it a promising target for resistance breeding [45–46]. In the present study, our findings further validated that IbPR1 significantly increased after continuous cropping of S19, highlighting its critical role in tolerance to CCS. WRKYs play pivotal roles in plant responses to environmental stress [47]. They are well-known SA-induced regulators of defense genes, particularly PR1 [48]. After continuous cropping of strawberries, the expression of FaWRKY25/32/33/45 was significantly upregulated. Among them, FaWRKY32 and SA can also jointly activate pathogenesis related proteins, further inducing defense related genes and synergistically resisting CCS [24]. Previous studies have demonstrated that overexpression of ItfWRKY70 significantly increased drought tolerance in transgenic sweetpotato [49]. After inoculation with Uromyces vignae, VaWRKY70 was significantly upregulated, playing an important role as a positive regulatory factor in Vigna angularis disease resistance [50]. In this study, IbWRKY70 was significantly upregulated in the tolerant cultivar S19 under CCS, indicating that IbWRKY70 may also plays a positive role in continuous cropping tolerance. Additionally, genes such as cysteine-rich receptor-like protein kinase 10 were identified to be positively correlated with continuous cropping tolerance in S19. Altered expression of protein kinase genes constitutes an active response of plants to environmental stress. Notably, 59.15% of protein kinase genes were upregulated in the leaves of the continuously cropped Polygonatum odoratum [44]. Collectively, these results indicated that the aforementioned genes exert critical functions in the continuous cropping tolerance of S19, thereby providing valuable genetic resources for the breeding of continuous cropping tolerant sweetpotato cultivars.

In this study, transcriptome sequencing coupled with WGCNA and RT-qPCR was employed to preliminarily elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying sweetpotato responses to CCS, providing a theoretical basis and genetic resources for breeding continuous cropping tolerant sweetpotato cultivars. Regardless, the study had some limitations. It focused solely on the transcriptional level and excluding post-transcriptional, protein, and metabolic analyses, this study is unable to fully decipher the multi-dimensional regulatory network. Meanwhile, functional validation of these candidate genes has not been conducted. Given that CCO are associated with numerous factors in plant-soil-microbe system, further in-depth and systematic studies are warranted in the future, which should involve multi-omics integrative analyses, including microbiome and metabolome analyses, as well as functional validation of the candidate genes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, transcriptome sequencing was performed on S19 and Y138 under 0, 1, 2, and 3 years of continuous cropping. Under CCS, the tolerant genotype S19 initiated an active defense mechanism through the following three dimensions. First, it regulated key pathways, including metabolic pathways, plant hormone signal transduction, the MAPK signaling pathway, and plant-pathogen interaction. Second, it maintained normal physiological functions by enhancing photosynthesis and carbohydrate metabolism. Third, it constructed a coordinated regulatory network by coordinating IbPR1, IbWRKY70, and other defense genes. These changes effectively alleviated growth inhibition and yield loss. In contrast, the sensitive genotype Yizi138 mainly exhibited inhibited key pathways and impaired core functions. Its oxidative phosphorylation and photosynthesis were significantly repressed under continuous cropping stress, causing energy deficiency, metabolic disorders, and weakened stress tolerance, which ultimately led to severe growth inhibition. A schematic model of sweetpotato response to CCS was constructed (Fig. 10). These findings provide valuable genetic resources and a theoretical basis for breeding continuous cropping tolerant sweetpotato cultivars.
