The ‘Sessiz İstila’ dataset: emotion-annotated turkish tweets on refugee discourse and emoji lexicon
Authors: Hasri Akbar Awal Rozaq, Darmawan Wicaksono, Nevfel Boz, Mahmud Isnan, Bens Pardamean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article describes an emotion-annotated corpus of Turkish-language posts published on X (formerly Twitter) that discuss ‘sessiz istila’ (‘silent invasion’), a contested phrase used in Turkish public debate to frame the presence of refugees, particularly Syrian refugees, as a threat. We retrieved posts using the academictwitteR interface to the Twitter Academic Research API with the keyword ‘sessiz istila’, restricted to publicly available Turkish-language posts published between 1 June 2021 and 31 December 2022, yielding 47,024 posts. We normalized every post with a rule set implemented in R that replaces retweet markers, URLs, user mentions, and hashtags with Turkish placeholder tokens, converts text to lowercase, and substitutes each emoji with a Turkish description drawn from a purpose-built emoji lexicon of 3664 entries released alongside the corpus. Emotion labels were assigned automatically by an Emotion Recognition Model obtained by fine-tuning BERTurk (bert-base-turkish-cased) on a class-balanced subset of the TREMO dataset containing 18,018 validated entries, 3003 for each of the six Ekman categories (happiness, fear, sadness, disgust, surprise and anger). We retained predictions with softmax confidence below 0.6 but flagged them as ‘ambiguous’ rather than discarding them so that users can apply their own confidence policy. The released corpus therefore contains 40,880 confidently labeled posts and 6144 ambiguous posts, each with its creation timestamp, normalized text, date, year, and emotion label. The dataset supports secondary research on the circulation of anti-immigrant framing and on the reactions it provokes, on event-driven emotional dynamics in social media, and on benchmarking and error analysis of emotion classifiers for Turkish, an agglutinative and comparatively low-resource language. The balanced TREMO training file and the Turkish emoji lexicon are reusable across corpora.

Background

The phrase ‘sessiz istila’ (‘silent invasion’) gained prominence in Turkish social media as a way of framing the presence of refugees, and especially Syrian refugees, as a cultural, economic, and demographic threat, and it circulates within a broader current of populist nationalism and anti-refugee sentiment in Türkiye [3]. Work on this discourse has so far been mainly qualitative, documenting its hostility through discourse and content analysis [14] but does not measure how the emotional composition of the discourse changes over time or at scale.

Measuring that composition computationally is difficult for Turkish. Turkish is agglutinative, so a single root can carry positive or negative meaning depending on its suffixes, and emotion resources developed for English transfer poorly, which is why dedicated Turkish resources have had to be built as word norms [9], emotion lexicons [8], and elicited labeled corpora such as TREMO [4]. Transformer models pre-trained on Turkish corpora, particularly BERTurk [15], an adaptation of the BERT architecture [16] to Turkish corpora, improve on these approaches [7,10]. The same pattern holds for other comparatively low-resource languages, where fine-tuned BERT variants outperform earlier approaches on affect-related classification [17]. Emoji resources are a concrete instance of the same gap: no Turkish emoji description set with coverage comparable to the English-language Unicode emoji list was available for pre-processing. However, the treatment of such tokens measurably affects transformer-based sentiment models [12].

This dataset was therefore compiled with two objectives: to make available a large, timestamped, and consistently normalized corpus of the ‘sessiz istila’ discourse, and to release the auxiliary resources needed to process it, namely a class-balanced Turkish emotion training file derived from TREMO [4], labeled with the six basic emotions [9] and a Turkish emoji lexicon [11]. In relation to the companion research article [2], which reports the architecture and evaluation of the Emotion Recognition Model, this data article adds the complete file-level description, column dictionary, distributional summaries, and processing provenance needed to reuse the data independently of that analysis.

Two of the seven files are English-language counterparts of the Turkish data files, added so that the corpus can be inspected and filtered by researchers who do not read Turkish. They reproduce their source file row for row and column for column and add a single column, text_EN and Entry_EN respectively, containing an English translation of the Turkish text column. The translations were produced automatically with a large language model instructed to preserve register, sarcasm and profanity without softening them, to leave emoji, hashtags and handles unchanged, and to render the normalization placeholders consistently as (retweet), (link) and (user). All 25,062 distinct Turkish strings across the two files were translated, so no cell of either English column is empty. These columns are machine-generated and were not verified by a human translator: the Turkish columns remain the authoritative text, the emotion labels apply to the Turkish text and not to its translation, and the English columns should be treated as a reading aid rather than as data for measurement.

Of the 47,024 posts in the main corpus, 40,880 (86.9%) received a confident emotion label, and 6144 (13.1%) were flagged as ambiguous. Table 5 reports the distribution of confident labels for the full observation window and separately for each year; percentages are computed over confidently labeled posts only, so they are unaffected by the choice of confidence threshold. Anger (43.6%) and surprise (33.4%) dominate the corpus overall, but the two years differ markedly: surprise leads in 2021 (56.2%), whereas anger leads in 2022 (44.5%).

Because retweets and repeated posts were retained (Section 4.2), the row counts in Table 5 are not counts of distinct messages. The 47,024 rows correspond to 8398 distinct normalized texts, so 38,626 rows (82.1%) repeat a text that appears elsewhere in the file; 38,885 rows (82.7%) begin with the retweet placeholder, and the single most frequent text occurs 6215 times. Among the 8139 rows that are not retweets, only 14.6% are repeats, so the duplication is almost entirely retweet-driven rather than a product of repeated original posting. This matters for interpretation: Table 6 recomputes the distribution of Table 5 over distinct texts, and the balance shifts markedly. Anger falls from 43.6% of rows to 30.2% of distinct texts. In comparison, fear rises from 11.2% to 23.9%, showing that the row-level dominance of anger is driven by a small number of heavily retweeted messages rather than by a correspondingly large number of distinct angry posts. Users studying the composition of the discourse should therefore work from the distinct-text figures, whereas users studying circulation and diffusion should work from the row-level figures; both are available in the released file because no de-duplication was applied.

Retention of retweets has a second consequence for the text itself. The API returns the body of a retweet truncated to its first 140 characters and terminated by an ellipsis. Since retweets make up 82.7% of the rows, 30,929 rows (65.8%) carry text that is cut off in this way; among distinct texts, the figure is 1132 of 8398 (13.5%). Truncated records are identifiable in the file because they end with the ellipsis character. This matters in two ways for reuse. The classifier saw only the visible portion of these posts, which is one plausible source of low-confidence predictions, and any measurement of post length, keyword presence, or lexical content computed over the file will be computed on partial text for most rows. Users who require complete message text should restrict their analysis to records that do not end in an ellipsis, and users of the English columns should note that a translation inherits the truncation of its source.

Matching the keyword does not by itself make a post hostile to refugees, and the corpus should not be read as a collection of xenophobic speech. To put a figure on this, we drew a random sample of 400 distinct normalized texts (seed 42). We coded each one for its stance toward the ‘sessiz istila’ framing under a four-category rubric: endorsing or amplifying the framing, mentioning it without evaluation, arguing against it or defending refugees, and indeterminate. Table 7 reports the results. A little over half the sample endorses or amplifies the framing, a quarter mentions it without evaluation, and roughly one post in seven argues against it. The coding was performed automatically by a large language model following the written rubric and has not yet been validated against human annotation, so these proportions are indicative rather than exact. They are nevertheless enough to establish the point that governs reuse: the corpus documents the discourse surrounding the phrase, including opposition to it, so a study of anti-refugee expression would need to filter the corpus rather than take it whole.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Fig. 4 illustrates the workflow for constructing and evaluating the dataset. The following sections describe each process.Fig. 4Workflow followed to collect, normalize, annotate, and release the data.Fig 4 dummy alt text

Workflow followed to collect, normalize, annotate, and release the data.

Data retrieval

Posts were retrieved from X (formerly Twitter) through the Twitter Academic Research product track using the academictwitteR package for R [5], executed on 26 January 2023 while free academic API access was still available. The retrieval call was, verbatim: get_all_tweets(query = "sessiz istila", exact_phrase = T, start_tweets = "2021–06–01T00:00:00Z", end_tweets = "2022–12–31T00:00:00Z", lang = "tr", n = Inf). Setting exact_phrase = T makes academictwitteR enclose the keyword in double quotes before submitting it to the v2 endpoint, so the two words were matched only as an adjacent phrase and never as a conjunction of the separate terms; matching is case-insensitive, and no further operators, filters or negations were used. The argument lang = "tr" restricted results to posts flagged by the platform as Turkish, and only posts publicly visible at retrieval time were returned; the window therefore runs from 1 June 2021 00:00 UTC to 00:00 UTC on 31 December 2022, which is why the latest record in the released file precedes that instant. Protected accounts, deleted posts, and non-Turkish results were therefore excluded by construction. The query returned 47,024 posts, and the earliest and latest records in the released file are 2021–06–08 10:00 and 2022–12–30 23:36, respectively.

Text normalization

Normalization was implemented in R with regular-expression substitutions applied in the following order, each replacing a platform-specific element with a Turkish placeholder so that the syntactic structure of the post is preserved while the identifying content is removed. The patterns are written as R string literals, in which the backslash is doubled, so \\w denotes the regular-expression token \w:"RT @\\w+:" → "(retweetlemek)" (retweet markers)"\\w+://\\S+" → "(bağlantı adresi)" (URLs)"@[a-z,_,A-Z,0–9]*" → "(kullanıcı)" (user mentions)"#(\\w+)" → "(\\1)" (hashtags, hash symbol removed and the token kept)

"RT @\\w+:" → "(retweetlemek)" (retweet markers)"\\w+://\\S+" → "(bağlantı adresi)" (URLs)"@[a-z,_,A-Z,0–9]*" → "(kullanıcı)" (user mentions)"#(\\w+)" → "(\\1)" (hashtags, hash symbol removed and the token kept)

Punctuation was deliberately kept because it carries information about sentence boundaries and emphasis that the transformer tokenizer can exploit. All characters were then converted to lowercase. Stop words were not removed and no stemming or lemmatization was applied, since pre-trained transformer models are known to lose accuracy when the input is stripped in this way. Duplicate posts were retained rather than de-duplicated, because repeated and retweeted content is itself the object of study in diffusion research on emotionally charged messages [13], and because pre-processing choices of this kind measurably affect transformer-based sentiment models [12].

Turkish emoji lexicon

No Turkish emoji resource with coverage comparable to the English-language Unicode emoji list was available, so we constructed one for this study by translating that list. Each entry in the released file emojis_emoticons_Turkish.csv [11] holds the Unicode code point, the emoji glyph, the official English description, and a Turkish translation of that description; the file contains 3664 entries. A Python routine scans every post for emoji glyphs and replaces them with the corresponding Turkish description in parentheses; for example, becomes (elleri çırpmak). Emojis are therefore represented as ordinary Turkish tokens that the BERTurk tokenizer can process, rather than being discarded.

Training data preparation

Emotion labels were produced with a model fine-tuned on TREMO [4], a Turkish emotion dataset built from participant-written statements validated by human annotators. Only entries carrying a validated emotion were used. The validated pool is unbalanced, with surprise the smallest category at 3003 entries, so every category was down-sampled to that size, giving a balanced training file of 18,018 entries across the six Ekman categories [18]: happiness, fear, sadness, disgust, surprise, and anger, released as TREMODATAcalibrationApr2024.csv. We lowercased entries to match the corpus and encoded labels as happy = 0, fear = 1, sadness = 2, disgust = 3, surprise = 4, and anger = 5. We split the balanced file with scikit-learn train_test_split using test_size = 0.2 and random_state = 42, yielding 14,414 entries for training and 3604 entries for held-out testing. Fig. 5, Fig. 6 show the effect of this step: the validated TREMO pool is uneven across the six categories, whereas the released training file is uniform at 3003 entries per category.Fig. 5Distribution of the validated TREMO entries before balancing.Fig 5 dummy alt textFig. 6Distribution of the released training file TREMODATacalibrationApr2024.csv after balancing (3003 Entries Per Emotion).Fig 6 dummy alt text

The balanced file was then divided into a training and a test partition by a random 80/20 split (train_test_split, test_size = 0.2, random_state = 42), giving 14,414 training and 3604 test entries. The split was not stratified. Stratification was unnecessary here because the file is balanced by construction at exactly 3003 entries per emotion, so a simple random draw is expected to reproduce the uniform class proportions to within roughly one percentage point per class at this sample size; imposing stratification would have constrained the partition without materially changing its composition. The fixed seed makes the partition reproducible, and the released file contains all 18,018 entries, so users who prefer a stratified or cross-validated design can construct one themselves.

Model fine-tuning

We tokenized text with the BERTurk bert-base-turkish-cased tokenizer, a Turkish-language model pre-trained on Turkish corpora following the BERT architecture [16], with truncation enabled and dynamic padding to the longest sequence in each batch, and converted it to tensor batches. Truncation is unproblematic here because posts are short, unlike long-document settings where input length is itself a design constraint [19]. We fine-tuned it with the Hugging Face Transformers Trainer API on a GPU runtime in Google Colab. The run was configured for three epochs over the 14,414 training entries with a training batch size of 16, the configuration reported in the companion research article [2]. The notebook also retains a second, manually written training loop that runs for six epochs; it is an exploratory variant kept for reference and was not used to produce the released labels, which come from the Trainer configuration of Table 10. The loss values quoted below were logged during a re-run of the same configuration on the same split, bounded at 1200 optimization steps so that evaluation could be recorded at every logging interval; the accuracy and the per-category scores are computed from the confusion matrix of Fig. 7 under Eqs. (1)-(3). Table 10 lists the full configuration; arguments left at Hugging Face defaults, including the learning rate, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, and random seed, are listed with the default value in effect. Performance on the held-out 20% split reached an overall accuracy of 92.62% (3338 correct predictions out of 3604), with per-category precision between 0.9118 and 0.9567, recall between 0.8992 and 0.9447, and F1 between 0.9070 and 0.9480. The highest F1 scores were obtained for happy (0.9480) and disgust (0.9401), and the lowest for sadness (0.9070) and anger (0.9106). Inference and labeling of the corpus

We applied the fine-tuned model to all 47,024 normalized posts. For each post, we tokenized the text with a fixed maximum length of 128 tokens and padded it to that length; we converted the raw logits to a probability distribution with softmax, and compared the highest probability against a confidence threshold of 0.6. Posts whose maximum probability met or exceeded the threshold received the corresponding emotion label; posts below the threshold were assigned the internal value −1, which is written to the released file as the string ‘ambiguous’. This procedure yielded 40,880 confidently labeled posts and 6144 ambiguous posts. We then exported the labeled records with the creation timestamp, a minute-resolution date field, and the calendar year, resulting in the five Turkish-language columns documented in Table 2. We retained both a full-precision timestamp and a minute-resolution field because created_at preserves the exact ordering of posts that diffusion analyses require. In contrast, the truncated date field is the key on which the daily and monthly aggregates reported in Section 3 were computed. We dropped all user-level metadata at this stage, so the published files contain no personal identifiers. The notebook writes this output under a working file name; the file released in the Zenodo record is silentinvasionv4_2021_2022_with_emotions_v14.csv, which is that output with the derived date and year columns added.

The threshold of 0.6 was not tuned against ground truth, because no human-annotated labels exist for these posts. Inference was run at 0.5, 0.6 and 0.7, and 0.6 was retained as the middle and more conservative of the three. It sits well above the 1/6 ≈ 0.167 that chance alone would give a six-way classifier and above the 0.5 point below which the leading class need not command a majority of the probability mass, while remaining low enough that most of the corpus still receives a label. At 0.6, 86.9% of posts were labeled. Because the value is a convention rather than an optimum, posts falling below it were flagged rather than deleted, so that users are free to include the ambiguous rows in their analyses or to exclude them, and Section 3 reports how much of the corpus each choice involves (40,880 labeled posts against 6144 ambiguous ones). Re-deriving labels under a different threshold requires the per-post softmax scores, which the record now contains.

The probability distribution behind every label is released as well. The file softmax_scores_corpus.csv holds one row per post, aligned with the main corpus through created_at, and reports the six class probabilities, their maximum, and the label that the 0.6 threshold yields; Table 11 documents its columns. The first version of the record did not contain this file, because the inference routine exported only the label that survived the threshold and discarded the distribution at the end of the run. A user who prefers a threshold other than 0.6 therefore needs no model at all: applying the chosen cut-off to max_prob in softmax_scores_corpus.csv relabels the corpus in a single pass, and the six probabilities also support analyses that dispense with a hard label. Regenerating the scores instead takes the notebook cited in Section 3: load model_tremo_v13 into a BertForSequenceClassification built on dbmdz/bert-base-turkish-cased with num_labels = 6, tokenize the text column of the corpus at max_length = 128 with padding to that length, and apply softmax to the logits, with the label ids of Section 4.4. Run in this way, the released scores reproduce the released emotion column on all 47,024 rows, yielding the same 40,880 labeled and 6144 ambiguous posts.

The emotion labels released in silentinvasionv4_2021_2022_with_emotions_v14.csv are model-generated and were not manually verified. Human validation enters this dataset only indirectly: the fine-tuning corpus is restricted to TREMO records with the ValidatedEmotion field, which the TREMO authors validated. The 92.62% accuracy reported above was measured on a held-out split of TREMO, a corpus of elicited emotion statements, and should therefore be read as an upper bound rather than as the accuracy attained on the X posts, which differ in register, length, and the presence of irony, slang, and emoji. The confidence threshold of 0.6 filters low-certainty predictions but does not verify correctness. Users who need verified labels for a subset of the corpus should manually re-annotate that subset.

Software

Collection and normalization were performed in R (version 4.6.1) with academictwitteR (version 0.3.1); emoji substitution, fine-tuning, and inference were performed in Python (version 3.13) with the Hugging Face Transformers (version 5.15.0) and PyTorch (version 2.13.0) libraries on Google Colab GPU runtimes; the balanced TREMO file, the corpus, and the lexicon were written as UTF-8 CSV. The R Markdown scripts and the Python notebook are deposited at https://github.com/dwicak/TwitterScrapping-for-Sezzis-Istila.

Limitations

The emotion labels are model-generated rather than human-annotated, with a held-out accuracy of 92.62%, making them more appropriate as silver-standard labels. The six-category Ekman scheme also cannot capture emotions such as anxiety, hope, irony, and sarcasm. At the same time, using TREMO introduces a domain shift from elicited text to social media language.

The corpus is limited to posts containing “sessiz istila” and is therefore not representative of Turkish X users or refugee discourse. Nor is every post in it hostile to refugees: in a sample of 400 distinct texts, 56.2% endorse the framing while 13.5% argue against it (Table 7), so the corpus documents a contested debate rather than xenophobic speech alone. Most rows carry truncated text because the API returns retweet bodies cut to 140 characters, and 65.8% of rows end in an ellipsis. The data are temporally imbalanced, with 73.8% of labeled posts from May–June 2022. Retained duplicates make frequency reflect posting and retweeting activity rather than the number of distinct messages or authors: 82.1% of rows repeat a text that appears elsewhere in the file, almost entirely through retweets. The emotion distribution shifts appreciably when it is computed over distinct texts instead of rows (Table 6), so analyses of discourse composition should use the distinct-text figures. For privacy, we release only normalized text, with post IDs and user-level metadata removed.

Ethics Statement

The authors have read and follow the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief. This work is based on data collected from a social media platform. We did not seek informed consent from individual account holders because the posts were publicly available at the time of retrieval; instead, we fully anonymized the participant data. During curation, we removed post identifiers, usernames, user identifiers, and all other user-level metadata; we then redistributed only the normalized post text, with user mentions replaced by a placeholder token, so the released files contain no personal identifiers, and no individual account can be traced. We collected the data through the Twitter Academic Research API and complied with the platform’s data redistribution policy [20]. The study involved no human participants and no animal experiments. The emotion labels released here were generated by the fine-tuned model rather than by human annotators; the human validation on which the model rests was carried out by the creators of the TREMO dataset [4], who validated the participant-written statements used for fine-tuning, and only entries carrying a validated emotion were used in this study.
