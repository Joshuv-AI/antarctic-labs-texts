Evaluating Delivery of Trauma-Informed Care in the Trauma Bay
Authors: Erin E. Isenberg, Reynaldo Olivo, Bahaa Succar, Louis Perkins, Allison E. Berndtson, Marianna Frazee, Rachel D. Appelbaum, Amelia Maiga, Lindsey Loss, Echo Meyers, Mackenzie Cook, Jaclyn A. VanDerWal, Daniel N. Holena, Gregory Lombana, Stepheny Berry, Linda Dultz, Ryan P. Dumas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow consistently are trauma-informed care (TIC) principles applied during initial trauma resuscitation, and does their use differ between English- and Spanish-speaking patients?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 291 trauma resuscitations, basic and empathetic communication and behaviors were infrequently observed. Spanish-speaking patients received significantly fewer TIC behaviors and less comprehensive evaluations than English-speaking patients.MeaningThese findings suggest that TIC is inconsistently and inequitably delivered during trauma resuscitation, supporting greater integration of TIC principles into trauma education, quality improvement, and clinical practice to better align bedside care with Advanced Trauma Life Support standards.

Introduction

Trauma-informed care (TIC) is an approach to communication that acknowledges the impact of prior traumatic experiences on illness and recovery and promotes interactions designed to reduce the risk of revictimization and to foster trust in the health care system. During resuscitation after traumatic injury, patients often experience stress compounded by chaotic conditions, unfamiliar personnel, loud noises, and rapid decision-making. A history of physical and psychological trauma is disproportionately prevalent among patients with violent injuries and may further influence their experience. These circumstances make the consistent application of TIC principles essential, and the American College of Surgeons recommends routine implementation of TIC at all trauma centers to improve patient engagement, adherence to treatment, and outcomes.

Implementation of TIC in outpatient and behavioral health care settings has been associated with reductions in depression and anxiety, increased trauma disclosures, greater patient engagement, and improved wellness for both patients and clinicians. The Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) curriculum recently incorporated recommendations for the use of TIC in the setting of acute traumatic injury, including for patients whose preferred language is not English. However, in the absence of prior ATLS guidance or emphasis on TIC, it remains unclear how consistently these principles are currently implemented in patient care settings. A recent scoping review of trauma-informed approaches for injured patients identified only 3 relevant studies—1 expert opinion, 1 qualitative review, and 1 case-control study—none of which quantitatively assessed the delivery of TIC behaviors during resuscitation for traumatic injury.

To address this gap, we performed a prospective, multicenter observational study to quantify the application of TIC principles during the evaluation of injured patients. Leveraging a novel use of trauma video review (TVR) that expands on prior single-center findings, we also examined whether the delivery of TIC differed between English- and Spanish-speaking patients. We hypothesized that adherence to recommended TIC principles would be limited and that observed application would differ across language groups.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board at all participating centers. At 5 of the 6 participating centers, informed consent was obtained as part of the standard consent for care. At the remaining center, separate consent for review of videographic data was obtained from the patient within 7 days of the recording. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Data Source and Study Population

Audiovisual data of trauma patient encounters were collected at 6 American College of Surgeons–verified level I trauma centers. TVR provides an opportunity to systematically examine real-time patient-clinician interactions. The videos were securely stored for a temporary period after recording as per institutional protocol at the participating centers. Each institution’s review period was different depending on time to obtain regulatory approval and study team availability. All enrollment occurred between March 2024 and April 2025. Videos were screened for eligibility using predefined criteria and reviewed by the study team at each center using a standardized data collection tool. At the time of video collection, none of the participating centers had a comprehensive formal TIC curriculum embedded into trauma resuscitation practice and all centers were continuing to teach the 10th edition of ATLS.

Patients were included if they were alert (Glasgow Coma Scale score >13), medically stable (systolic blood pressure >90; heart rate <120), and aged 16 years or older. Patients who required emergency intervention for airway, breathing, or circulation, with video or audio disruptions or poor quality, had injuries that impaired verbal communication, were pregnant or incarcerated, or needed chemical sedation during the evaluation were excluded. The encounter was reviewed from the time of arrival through completion of the secondary survey and adjunct imaging. TIC behaviors were assessed at the team level rather than attributed to individual clinicians.

Checklist Development

The data collection tool (eAppendix in Supplement 1) was developed based on ATLS recommendations, literature on TIC, and a pilot study from Kundu et al. It included observed behaviors and communication from clinicians in the trauma bay. The tool was finalized by consensus from all participating centers and included clear definitions for all metrics. Before study initiation, a training session was held with all participating sites to standardize the application of the checklist. Reviewers could only review data at their respective institution. Each site had 1 to 3 reviewers.

Exposures and Outcomes

Patient characteristics were abstracted from the trauma registry and included age, sex, race, ethnicity, mechanism of injury, Injury Severity Score (ISS), and hospital length of stay (LOS). Race and ethnicity were self-reported as Asian, Black or African American, Native American or Pacific Islander, White, or other and not Hispanic or Latino or Hispanic or Latino. Preferred language was documented during the TVR and compared to the preferred language in the electronic medical record (EMR). All patients who preferred a language other than English preferred Spanish, dichotomizing our exposure to English-speaking patients and Spanish-speaking patients. Seven patients spoke Spanish during their trauma encounter but had English documented as their preferred language in the EMR. Two patients spoke English during their trauma encounter but had Spanish as their preferred language in the EMR. For analysis, we used the preferred language the patient spoke during the trauma encounter.

We grouped TIC into 3 domains: basic communication, empathetic communication, and comprehensive evaluation. Basic communication included observation of simple components of trauma-informed communication, such as nurses and physicians introducing themselves, and the use of an interpreter for Spanish-speaking patients. Empathetic communication documented components such as verbal reassurances (eg, “we are going to take good care of you”), physical reassurances (eg, holding the patient’s hand), privacy, and communication with patients before invasive or painful interventions were performed (eg, exposure, intravenous line insertion, painful examinations). We coded these as none, simple, or comprehensive. We defined simple reassurances as 1- to 2-word directives toward the patient, such as “relax” or “little stick.” Comprehensive reassurances were defined as more detailed phrases, such as “we’re going to take good care of you” or “we need to roll you over to look for injuries, this may hurt.”

The comprehensive evaluation portion of the checklist documented ATLS-directed elements of the primary and secondary survey that required engagement with the patient, such as taking a history and performing a motor and sensory examination. We evaluated the components of the history according to ATLS, including allergies, medications, past medical history, last meal, and event history. We also included additional elements of the history relevant to TIC (eg, substance use, prior trauma, suicidal ideation, preferred pronouns) for a total of 12 components.

Statistical Analysis

First, characteristics for English-speaking patients and Spanish-speaking patients were compared. Percentages were calculated for the total cohort for each variable and then compared for English-speaking patients and Spanish-speaking patients. Variables that were missing or unable to assess from the video were excluded. We used χ2 for categorical variables, t tests for continuous variables, and Wilcoxon rank sum tests for count variables. We predetermined statistical significance to be 2-sided P < .05. Analyses were performed using Stata version MP 18.1 (StataCorp). Data were analyzed from August to November 2025.

A sensitivity analysis was performed on at least 1 outcome for each domain to account for possible clustering at the center level. Binary outcomes were analyzed using mixed-effects logistic regression and proportions were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, with trauma center specified as a random intercept and language group as a fixed effect.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 291 patient encounters (median [IQR] age, 41 (28-63) years; 162 male [55.7%]) were included in the study (Table 1). By race, there were 56 Black or African American patients (20.1%), 186 White patients (66.8%), and 33 patients who identified as other races (11.8%); 84 patients (28.9%) were Hispanic or Latino and 194 patients (66.8%) were not Hispanic or Latino. English was the preferred language for most patients (236 patients [81.1%]), followed by Spanish (52 patients [17.9%]). Most Spanish-speaking patients identified as White (32 patients [61.5%]) or other race (18 patients [35.6%]) race. Most injuries were due to blunt mechanisms (216 injuries [74.2%]). The median (IQR) ISS was 9 (1-14), and the median (IQR) hospital LOS was 3 (1-8) days. Comparisons between English-speaking patients and Spanish-speaking patients showed no significant differences in age (median [IQR], 46 [43-49] vs 41 [36-47] years; P = .92), sex (68 females [35.1%] vs 12 females [25.0%]; P = .19), injury mechanism (blunt: 176 patients [74.6%] vs 38 patients [73.1%]; P = .92), ISS (median [IQR], 9 [1-14] vs 8 [2-13]; P = .84), or hospital LOS (median [IQR], 3 [1-8] vs 3 [1-9] days; P = .70).

Basic Communication

The patients’ preferred language was most often determined by the clinician initiating communication and the patient responding in their preferred language (224 encounters [77.0%]), followed by emergency medical services providing a preferred language in the handoff (28 encounters [9.6%]) (eTable in Supplement 1). In comparison, explicit inquiry about language preference occurred in only 11 encounters (3.8%). Among Spanish-speaking patients, a professional interpreter was present for the complete evaluation in 7 encounters (13.5%) and for part of the evaluation in 10 encounters (19.2%). In 4 encounters (7.7%), a team member attempted to contact an interpreter, but no interpreter was available during the encounter. Clinicians attempted ad hoc interpretation with Spanish-speaking patients in 44 encounters (88.0%), and a friend or family member was used for interpretation in 3 encounters (6.0%).

Basic communication behaviors during the trauma bay encounter were inconsistently performed (Table 2). A physician introduced themselves in 74 of 257 encounters (28.8%), while any nurse introduction occurred in only 26 of 260 encounters (10.0%). English-speaking patients were significantly more likely than Spanish-speaking patients to receive a physician introduction (71 of 208 encounters [34.1%] vs 3 of 49 encounters [6.1%]; P < .001). There was no significant difference in nursing introductions between English- and Spanish-speaking patients (24 of 211 encounters [11.4%] vs 2 of 49 encounters [4.1%]; P = .13). Orienting the patient to their location (eg, hospital, emergency department) occurred in 52 of 264 encounters (19.7%), with no significant differences between English- and Spanish-speaking patients (47 of 216 encounters [21.8%] vs 5 of 48 encounters [10.4%]; P = .07). The primary contact information was requested in 32 of 279 encounters (11.5%), without a significant difference by language (24 of 230 encounters [10.4%] vs 8 of 49 encounters [16.3%]; P = .24).

Empathetic Communication

Empathetic communication behaviors were less frequent among Spanish-speaking patients compared with English-speaking patients (Table 3). The median (IQR) number of verbal reassurances was 2 (1-3) overall but lower for Spanish-speaking patients than English-speaking patients (median [IQR], 1 [0-1] vs 2 [1-4] assurances; P < .001). Physical reassurance was provided in 125 of 280 encounters (44.6%) but was less common among Spanish-speaking patients than English-speaking patients (7 of 51 encounters [13.7%] vs 118 of 229 encounters [51.5%]; P < .001). Full blanket coverage was provided to most patients (236 of 288 encounters [81.9%]) with no difference between English- and Spanish-speaking patients (193 of 236 encounters [81.8%] vs 43 of 52 encounters [82.7%]; P = .88). Privacy through door or curtain closure was observed in one-third of encounters overall and was less frequent for Spanish-speaking patients compared with English-speaking patients (7 of 40 encounters [17.5%] vs 68 of 186 encounters [36.7%]; P = .02). Confrontational communication was rare (10 of 288 encounters [3.5%]) and did not differ between English- and Spanish-speaking patients (8 of 236 encounters [3.4%] vs 2 of 52 encounters [3.9%]; P = .87). A summary of findings and next steps was provided to 108 of 261 patients (41.4%), with similar rates among English- and Spanish-speaking patients (90 of 212 encounters [42.5%] vs 18 of 49 encounters [36.7%]; P = .46).

Across all encounters, patients underwent a median (IQR) of 6 (5-7) potentially invasive or painful interventions, with no differences between English- and Spanish-speaking patients (median [IQR], 6 [5-7] vs 7 [6-8] interventions; P = .06). Communication occurred before a mean (SD) of 55.4% (29.5 percentage points) of interventions, but this proportion was significantly lower among Spanish-speaking patients compared with English-speaking patients (mean [SD], 46.5% [28.8 percentage points] vs 57.3% [29.4 percentage points]; P = .02). Comprehensive communication occurred before only a mean (SD) of 23.4% (27.0 percentage points) of interventions overall and was markedly less frequent among Spanish-speaking than English-speaking patients (mean [SD], 13.2% [21.9 percentage points] vs 25.6% [27.5 percentage points]; P = .001).

Comprehensive Examination

Nearly all patients had all components of the Glasgow Coma Scale assessed (275 patients [97.9%]), with no difference by language. Only one-third of patients (97 patients [34.2%]) had a complete physical examination (pain, motor, and sensory examination in all extremities) done as part of their secondary survey. However, Spanish-speaking patients were less likely to be engaged for the physical examination compared with English-speaking patients (7 patients [13.7%] vs 90 patients [38.6%]; P < .001). The median (IQR) number of history components assessed was 4 (1-6) overall and was higher among Spanish-speaking patients than English-speaking patients (median [IQR], 5 [4-7] vs 3 [1-5] components; P < .001). However, among Spanish-speaking patients, only 16 (31.4%) had their histories collected with a certified medical interpreter (CMI). Most patients (20 patients [58.8%]) had their histories collected using ad hoc interpretation, and 5 Spanish-speaking patients (9.8%) had their histories collected in English. Completion of the core ATLS components of the history occurred in 80 encounters (27.8%) overall and was more frequent among Spanish-speaking patients compared with English-speaking patients (25 patients [48.1%] vs 55 patients [23.3%]; P < .001).

Sensitivity Analysis

Our sensitivity analysis results were consistent with our main analysis. In mixed-effects logistic regression accounting for clustering by trauma center, English-speaking patients had significantly higher odds of receiving a physician introduction compared with Spanish-speaking patients (odds ratio [OR], 5.4 [95% CI, 1.5-18.9]; P = .009) and were significantly more likely to receive physical reassurance (OR, 5.8 [95% CI, 2.3-14.1]; P < .001). In a mixed-effects linear regression model accounting for trauma center–level clustering, English-speaking patients had a significantly higher proportion of interventions preceded by communication compared with Spanish-speaking patients (mean difference, 15.7 [95% CI, 6.6-24.8] percentage points; P = .001).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we report the first multicenter analysis using TVR to measure adherence to TIC principles in the resuscitation bay, to our knowledge. We found that trauma teams significantly underused basic communication and interpreters as appropriate, potentially missing an opportunity to build trust. Additionally, teams infrequently applied empathetic, trauma-informed strategies, such as providing reassurances or communicating about painful interventions, at a time when patients were at risk for further traumatization. Finally, non–English-speaking patients received fewer components of TIC and less comprehensive examinations.

Our study found that fundamental communication behaviors, such as introductions and orientation, were rare. ATLS identifies rapport-building during the initial trauma resuscitation as foundational and recommends introductions, explaining why the patient is in the hospital, and setting expectations. In our study, physician introductions occurred in fewer than one-third of encounters, and any nurse introduction in only 1 in 10 encounters, despite often having 2 to 4 nurses involved in the resuscitation. These findings are consistent with prior qualitative work showing that patients receiving trauma care frequently report feelings of anonymity and detachment during their initial evaluation. Prioritizing technical tasks without engaging the patient as a person may undermine trust and autonomy and contradicts guidelines promoting patient-centered communication even in time-sensitive environments.

Patients are legally and ethically entitled to CMIs. Preferred language was established upfront only in 13% of patients, and fewer than 15% of Spanish-speaking patients had a CMI present throughout the evaluation, in addition to receiving fewer observed TIC communication behaviors. This appeared to be driven largely by underuse of CMIs and reliance on ad hoc interpretation when perceived to be sufficient or expedient. Prior studies demonstrate that ad hoc communication is associated with lower trust and satisfaction compared with professional interpretation. ATLS recommends identifying preferred language, using a CMI during evaluation, avoiding using family members for interpretation, and standardizing protocols so all patients receive guideline-concordant care in their preferred language. These findings highlight an opportunity to improve equitable communication and reduce fear, confusion, or mistrust related to trauma resuscitation. The limited use of empathetic communication, including verbal and physical reassurances, privacy measures, and preintervention explanations, highlights a broader gap in patient-centered trauma care. Verbal reassurances were uncommon (median of 2 reassurances during a 14-minute encounter), especially for Spanish-speaking patients, and usually occurred without interpreter assistance. While approximately half of English-speaking patients received at least 1 physical reassurance, only one-eighth of Spanish-speaking patients did. Spanish-speaking patients were also half as likely to have privacy provided via a door or curtain. While reduced verbal communication may partly reflect interpreter access or communication barriers, lower rates of physical reassurance and privacy suggest a broader pattern of depersonalization, disengagement, or implicit bias. Encouragingly, most teams maintained appropriate blanket coverage to prevent unnecessary physical exposure, and confrontational communication was rare.

Most interventions were performed without explanation or warning. Prior studies show that communication and respect are frequent sources of dissatisfaction in acute care, whereas empathetic communication improves satisfaction, reduces anxiety and pain, and enhances recall and understanding. TIC and ATLS emphasize restoring patients’ sense of control through anticipatory communication, yet our findings indicate that trauma teams often prioritized efficiency over emotional safety. These straightforward behaviors represent feasible opportunities to improve TIC delivery.

Although Glasgow Coma Scale assessment was near universal, only a minority of patients (40% of English-speaking patients and 14% of Spanish-speaking patients) were engaged for a complete physical examination. This suggests that language barriers hinder both communication and clinical completeness. Conversely, Spanish-speaking patients were more likely to have the core ATLS elements of history documented, although fewer than half had a complete history and most were collected without a CMI. The secondary survey is a critical opportunity to engage patients in their care, elicit social and medical history, and identify injuries. Ensuring completion of this assessment, supported by CMI use when indicated, is a necessary goal for trauma programs to provide safe, effective care.

Implications

Our study has several important implications for education, trauma systems, and research. These findings underscore the importance of the 11th edition ATLS curriculum’s expanded emphasis on TIC. However, given the low adherence to TIC behaviors observed across all domains in this study, we expect it will take more effort to address the gap between recommended standards and clinical reality. There is a need for TIC education to be incorporated more broadly and consistently into the curriculum, assessment framework, and accountability structures of nursing and medical education to promote its application in all patient encounters. Organizations and governing bodies can play a key role in this as follows: (1) the Association of American Medical Colleges can integrate TIC as a required, assessed competency in undergraduate medical education; (2) the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education and American Board of Surgery can incorporate TIC-specific milestones into surgical residency training standards; (3) the American College of Surgeons can embed TIC performance metrics into trauma center verification requirements and continuing medical education pathways; (4) the Emergency Nurses Association and National League for Nursing can include TIC into trauma nursing certification and Bachelor of Science in Nursing curricula; and (5) ATLS can include testing of TIC as a required skill, not merely a recommended practice, in future curriculum iterations.

Additionally, trauma centers should adopt multifaceted strategies that integrate TIC principles into training, quality improvement, and operational workflows. These efforts may include structured video review and debriefing that evaluate communication behaviors alongside technical performance, simulation-based education that emphasizes empathetic communication and shared decision-making, and institutional policies that promote the consistent use of CMIs during trauma resuscitations. Continued research is essential to identify high-performing teams, understand the factors contributing to suboptimal or inequitable communication, evaluate interventions that improve TIC practices and patient outcomes, and develop effective strategies for broad implementation. Together, these initiatives represent actionable, patient-centered strategies to strengthen trauma-informed communication, improve patient trust and engagement, and promote more equitable, compassionate care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, some clinicians may have been certified bilingual. However, participating sites reported few certified bilingual staff and ad hoc and English communication still counted as valid communication with patients in our data analysis, meaning disparities would persist even if the communication had been delivered using certified bilingual staff. Second, although reviewers underwent standardized training and used predefined variable definitions, interrater reliability was not formally assessed. Third, participating trauma centers routinely perform video review for performance improvement, introducing the possibility of a Hawthorne effect that increased adherence to TIC behaviors. Similarly, because we studied stable, alert, communicating patients, our study likely overestimates the true prevalence of TIC behaviors in trauma bay resuscitations.

Conclusions

This multicenter cross-sectional study using TVR found substantial underutilization of key TIC practices during acute injury resuscitation, including fundamental, empathetic, and patient-centered communication and evaluation. These gaps were particularly pronounced among non–English-speaking patients, even for language-independent behaviors, demonstrating inequities in the delivery of TIC that require interventions beyond improving CMI utilization. This provides critical baseline TIC performance data against which future efforts to evaluate implementation of ATLS 11th edition standards can be measured. Improving trauma care quality and equity will require moving TIC from an aspirational recommendation to a measurable standard of trauma team performance through integration of trauma-informed principles into education, routine training, team performance review, and institutional policy.
