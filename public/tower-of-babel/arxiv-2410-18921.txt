From Blind Solvers to Logical Thinkers: Benchmarking LLMs’ Logical Integrity on Faulty Mathematical Problems
A M Muntasir Rahman ♯, Junyi Ye ♯, Wei Yao ♯, Sierra S. Liu †, Jesse Yu †, Jonathan Yu †

Abstract
Consider the math problem: “Lily received 3 cookies from her best friend yesterday and ate 5 for breakfast. Today, her friend gave her 3 more cookies. How many cookies does Lily have now?” Many large language models (LLMs) in previous research approach this problem by calculating the answer “1” using the equation “3 - 5 + 3.” However, from a human perspective, we recognize the inherent flaw in this problem: Lily cannot eat 5 cookies if she initially only had 3. This discrepancy prompts a key question: Are current LLMs merely Blind Solver that apply mathematical operations without deeper reasoning, or can they function as Logical Thinker capable of identifying logical inconsistencies? To explore this question, we propose a benchmark dataset, FaultyMath , which includes faulty math problems of rich diversity: i) multiple mathematical categories, e.g., algebra, geometry, number theory, etc., ii) varying levels of difficulty, and iii) different origins of faultiness—ranging from violations of common sense and ambiguous statements to mathematical contradictions and more. We evaluate a broad spectrum of LLMs, including open-source, closed-source, and math-specialized models, using FaultyMath across three dimensions: (i) How accurately can the models detect faulty math problems without being explicitly prompted to do so? (ii) When provided with hints—either correct or misleading—about the validity of the problems, to what extent do LLMs adapt to become reliable Logical Thinker ? (iii) How trustworthy are the explanations generated by LLMs when they recognize a math problem as flawed? Through extensive experimentation and detailed analysis, our results demonstrate that existing LLMs largely function as Blind Solver and fall short of the reasoning capabilities required to perform as Logical Thinker 1 1 1 Data&Code: https://github.com/JunyiYe/FaultyMathProblem .

1
Introduction

Our exploration begins with the example presented in Figure
1
, where a logically flawed math problem still leads prominent large language models (LLMs) to produce various numerical answers. This outcome is surprising, as we would expect an intelligent AI system not only to provide correct solutions to valid math problems but also to identify when a problem is logically inconsistent and therefore should not yield any answer, even without explicit hints about its flawed nature. This observation prompts us to re-examine the current development trajectory of LLMs for mathematical problem-solving
(
Ahn et al., 2024
;
Hendrycks et al., 2021a
;
Imani et al., 2023
)
, which generally assume that the provided math problems are valid and require only computational solutions, without considering the possibility of logical flaws. This raises a crucial question: Are contemporary LLMs merely
Blind Solver
that execute mathematical operations without deeper reasoning, or can they evolve into
Logical Thinker
capable of detecting logical inconsistencies?

To address this question, we introduce a new dataset,
FaultyMath
, which incorporates three dimensions of diversity: (i) a range of mathematical categories, including algebra, geometry, number theory, etc., (ii) varying levels of difficulty, and (iii) a rich variety of falsehoods, such as violations of common sense, insufficient information, mathematical contradictions, etc.

The creation of
FaultyMath
was a multi-step process. Initially, we recruited participants from the American Mathematics Competitions (AMC) to manually generate faulty math problems. However, this led to a dataset that was disproportionately skewed toward specific mathematical categories and types of logical flaws. To create a more balanced and comprehensive dataset, we developed a three-stage pipeline: (i) We used GPT-4
(
Achiam et al., 2023
)
to modify valid problems from the MATH dataset
(
Hendrycks et al., 2021a
)
into flawed ones, generating 2,000 potential faulty math problems; (ii) we then prompted GPT-4 to perform self-evaluation, identifying 635 of these as likely faulty; (iii) finally, human annotators reviewed these problems, confirming 363 as genuinely faulty.

We conducted an extensive evaluation on
FaultyMath
using ten popular LLMs, including both open-source, closed-source, and math-specialized models, to assess their capacity to function as
Logical Thinker
. Our evaluation focused on three key aspects: (i) Without any hints, how effectively can LLMs recognize that a given math problem is faulty? Unfortunately, even the best-performing model, Gemini 1.5 Pro, identified only 33.33% of the faulty problems successfully. (ii) When provided with hints—either factually correct or misleading ones, how reliably do LLMs behave as
Logical Thinker
? Our results indicate that factually correct hints about the problem’s faultiness significantly improve performance, but models still fall short of perfection. Moreover, LLMs demonstrated vulnerability to misleading hints, with models like Gemini and GPT-4 exhibiting confirmational bias when influenced by incorrect hints. (iii) How accurate are the explanations generated by LLMs when they correctly identify a faulty problem? Our findings reveal that models such as Gemini and Qwen-1.5 consistently produced correct explanations when they recognized falsehoods, highlighting their ability to articulate reasoning effectively in such cases.

In summary, this study advocates for the development of LLMs that go beyond mere
Blind Solver
and evolve into true
Logical Thinker
. The key contributions of this paper are threefold:

•

We identify the critical gap in LLMs’ mathematical reasoning abilities, highlighting the need to shift from
Blind Solver
to
Logical Thinker
.

•

We introduce
FaultyMath
, a balanced dataset comprising 363 faulty math problems, specifically designed to evaluate this aspect of LLM performance.

•

Through extensive analysis, we provide comprehensive insights into the strengths and limitations of current LLMs in mathematical reasoning and their capacity to detect logical inconsistencies.

Logical Thinker
from
Blind Solver
, and introduces a collection of faulty math problems, both with and without hints, to evaluate their true capacity for mathematical reasoning.

FaultyMath
Curation and Statistics

Generating faulty math problems is not only inherently a creative process, but it is also unconventional, as it is not a typical human task.
Our initial approach involved enlisting American high school math competition (AMC) participants to create faulty math problems from scratch. However, this effort resulted in a problem set that is biased toward particular mathematical categories and specific types of falsehoods. We then explored data augmentation via LLMs, using this seed dataset created by high-school students, but this approach also failed. Inspired by recent studies on generating synthetic data using LLMs
(
Lou et al., 2024
;
Yuan et al., 2023
)
, we extensively experimented with modifying valid problems from the MATH dataset using GPT-4 by giving it well-crafted prompts. This approach significantly improved the likelihood of generating genuinely false math problems.
We began by generating a pool of raw false math problems using GPT-4. After a carefully designed filtering process conducted by GPT-4, human evaluators assessed the falsehood of these problems. The entire process is summarized in Figure
2
, with details provided in the following section.

3.1
FaultyMath
Curation

Valid to Raw Faulty Math Problem.

To compile a diverse collection of faulty math problems across different categories (e.g., Algebra, Geometry), we used the MATH dataset
(
Hendrycks et al., 2021a
)
as our source for valid problems. We selected 400 problems from each of five categories—Algebra, Geometry, Counting & Probability, Number Theory, and Precalculus—totaling 2,000 valid problems. Given its creativity and strong performance on the MATH dataset, we chose GPT-4 as our LLM for this stage.

We provided GPT-4 with each valid math problem and its correct solution, instructing it to

•

convert the problem into a false and unsolvable version, and

•

generate a brief explanation of why the problem is faulty—a crucial step that significantly improved the generation of genuinely faulty problems.

LLM Self-Filtering.

We found that many of the 2,000 generated false math problems were actually solvable, so we implemented a self-verification process to filter out those that did not meet the criteria for being truly unsolvable.

In this self-evaluation process, GPT-4 was initially presented with a “raw false math problem” to solve. After providing a solution, it was prompted with a “simple explanation” of why the problem was unsolvable, encouraging GPT-4 to reflect on its initial solution. Finally, we posed a multiple-choice question to GPT-4, asking for its final decision on the entire conversation. The choices (A, B, or C) are outlined in Figure
2
.

If GPT-4 outputs “A”, it implies that GPT-4 believes the raw faulty math problem to be a genuine faulty math problem but not a challenging one to identify. If GPT-4 outputs “B”, it implies that GPT-4 considered the raw false math problem to be a genuine false math problem and a challenging one that it could not identify to be false when initially presented. An output of “C” implies that the false math problem is a valid math problem with a possible solution.

We filtered out the problems marked “C” and “A,” as they had a lower likelihood of being genuine and challenging false math problems. This left us with 635 problems.

Human Verification.

The remaining 635 math problems were then reviewed by human annotators for verification. Each annotator independently assessed all 635 problems to determine whether they were genuinely false, meaning no solution existed as presented. Through this manual verification process, 363 problems were confirmed as genuine false math problems by both annotators. The other 272 problems were found to be solvable and were discarded.

Evaluation Method

The primary task for LLMs in this research is to determine whether they can identify issues in given false math problems. However, directly asking this question would signal to the LLMs that the problem is false, thereby influencing their response. The same constraint applies to human annotators; we cannot explicitly ask whether the questions are false. Instead, we ask them to solve the problems without providing any hints. By analyzing their solutions, we can determine whether the falsehood was detected. For instance, if a solution mentions that the problem is solvable only with additional constraints, it indicates awareness of the issue. Consequently, evaluating the LLMs’ responses to false math problems requires careful and nuanced assessment.

To tackle this challenge, we sought to
develop an accurate automated evaluator using an LLM, designed to closely align with human assessments
. We first established a gold standard by having two PhD-level reviewers evaluate responses from GPT-4-Turbo (2024-04-09 Update) on the dataset. The reviewers found that GPT-4-Turbo failed to identify issues in 300 of the 363 genuine false math problems, achieving a gold-standard accuracy of 17.36% (63 out of 363 identified). We then tested different auto-evaluation prompts with two other LLMs, GPT-4 and GPT-4o, comparing their performance against the gold standard. We use
LLM_{s}
to denote the LLM to be evaluated (i.e., the problem solver) and
LLM_{e}
to denote the LLM as an evaluator.

\mathcal{Q}_{3}
: How does LLM performance compare to human performance on
FaultyMath
without any hints?

To answer this question, we recruited 15 graduate-level students to participate in our study.
For this human benchmarking stage, we needed to ensure that the human participants did not get any hints that the problems were false math problems to keep their responses unbiased. The participants were provided with the false math questions and asked to write down their responses. Each participant was given 10 problems from our dataset to solve within 72 hours. They were strictly prohibited from using any AI tools. The participants were asked to solve 10 problems, not all 363 problems, for two main reasons. Firstly, to reduce their effort and prevent fatigue-induced errors. Secondly, to prevent students from identifying that there is a prominent pattern (all mathematical questions are false and unsolvable) in the questions they were facing. The students’ responses were manually evaluated by the same evaluators that evaluated GPT-4’s responses in our auto evaluation-method step to keep the evaluation consistent.

We observed an average accuracy of 21.33% in identifying false math problems by the human participants. This performance is 1.6% higher than the average of our 10 LLMs, which is 19.72% (table
2
). Even though the performance gap is small, it suggests that this task is comparatively easier for human beings than LLMs. However, we also need to address that this is not an apple-to-apple comparison. LLMs were solving one problem at a time whereas the human participants had 10 questions to sense a pattern of falsehood. Due to input-window limitation, we cannot expect LLMs to reason through even two questions in many cases, let alone 10. On the other hand, a single LLM, arguably, has in its possession more mathematical knowledge than a graduate-level student; what we have observed is the failure to reason from them better than humans.

\mathcal{Q}_{4}
: How do the LLMs react when they are asked to simply output “Yes” or “No”?

Traditional NLP tasks evaluate systems based on explicit task instructions, where the system knows the evaluation criteria. For example, in machine-translation tasks, if the instruction is to translate “Gracias” from Spanish to German, a correct response would be “Danke.” In contrast, if the instruction changes to: “Translate the following
English
to German: ‘Gracias’”, a correct response would clarify that “Gracias” is Spanish, not English, and then provide the German equivalent, “Danke.” Essentially, evaluating the system based on the acknowledgement of the underlying issue in the task instruction is something novel we have done in our primary experiment,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
. We can consider
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
as an “Evaluation-unaware” experiment where the system is not aware of the falsehood in the task instruction and not aware that it will be evaluated based on the identification of such falsehood. For
\mathcal{Q}_{4}
, we want to test what happens when the system
is
aware of the possibility of the falsehood.

The prompt for this “Evaluation-aware” experiment was “
Is the above math problem a false and unsolvable math problem? Output ‘Yes’ if it is, ‘No’ otherwise.
”. Since LLMs tend to have a bias towards always outputting “Yes” or “No,” we flipped the statement to force the LLMs to choose logically in another round of experiments with the following prompt. “
Is the above math problem a valid and solvable math problem? Output ‘Yes’ if it is, ‘No’ otherwise.
” Notice how, for the same sample in the dataset, we expect the LLM to respond in “Yes” in one and “No” in another.

The results are detailed in Table
3
. We can observe that, the contrast between switching “Yes” and “No” is very high in some LLMs. This adds to the notion that some of the LLMs have a high bias towards selecting “Yes” or “No” and their mathematical reasoning capacity without step-by-step generative approach is very weak.

\mathcal{Q}_{5}
: How accurate are the explanations provided by LLMs when identifying false math problems?

To deepen our understanding beyond binary performance evaluation, we investigate the correctness of LLMs’ explanations when
they identify a math problem as false. Specifically, we selected the top three LLMs and examined 21 samples, which all three LLMs correctly identified as false.
