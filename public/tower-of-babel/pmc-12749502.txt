Effect of tele-support on attention levels of emergency medical technicians during simulated out-of-hospital cardiac arrest
Authors: Vincenz Scharner, Christina Hafner, Philipp Metelka, Lena Reischmann, Josef Michael Lintschinger, Stefan Ulbing, Nikolaus Frimmel, Peter Gröpel, Michael Wagner, Harald Willschke, Alexander Hermann, Martina Hermann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTelemedicine in the form of a provider-to-provider telemedical application, in which an expert supports the medical team at the patient’s location with medical and non-technical skills is slowly being introduced in preclinical emergency medicine. While the benefit of a tele-team leader could be demonstrated for simulated neonatal resuscitation scenarios, the effect of tele-support for emergency medical technicians (EMTs) on their attention, cognitive load, workload, and performance in case of an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in adult patients is still unknown. As the impact of tele-support in OHCA is rarely investigated, this study aims to evaluate the effect of tele-team leadership during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) on EMTs’ attention, workload, and performance. Forty-three teams performed two simulated OHCA scenarios (one with and one without tele-support) in a randomized order, while wearing eye-tracking glasses (Tobii Pro 3.0). Each team consisted of three persons, of which two were EMTs (team leader and team member) and one confederate from the study team. Tele-support was provided by an experienced emergency physician using Corpuls Mission. The primary outcome was visual attention spend to four predefined areas of interest (AOI), including the patient, medical equipment, monitor, and other staff. Performance of advanced life support and cognitive load were evaluated using time-to-event data and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration Task Load Index, respectively. Tele-support significantly improved visual attention among team leaders, as indicated by a significant condition × AOI interaction (F = 4.88, P = 0.006). Team leaders with tele-support attended more often to the equipment (P = 0.008) and less often to other staff (P = 0.017). Furthermore, they spent less time focusing outside of the task-relevant AOIs (P = 0.002). For team members, the tele-support condition did not significantly change their visual attention. There was no significant difference between conditions for the times to start chest compressions, administration of adrenaline, defibrillation, ventilation, intubation, and the consideration of reversible causes. However, there was a significant difference of whether reversible causes were considered. Without tele-support, as many as 10 teams (26.3%) did not consider reversible causes in the scenario whereas only 2 teams (5.3%) did so when tele-support was provided (P = 0.008). Tele-support significantly improved team leaders’ visual attention during simulated OHCA, while having a positive impact on the resuscitation performance by increasing guideline adherence. Introduction

Due to the technical progress, telemedicine increasingly adds new tools and ways of treatment to medical care1–3. Via remote real-time audio- and video-connection, a medical expert can be linked directly with patients and medical personnel1,3,4. In case of a provider-to-provider telemedical application, the expert can support the medical team at the patient’s location with medical skills (e.g. second opinion) and with non-technical skills (e.g., team leadership, coordination, or situational awareness).

Especially in emergency and stressful situations such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), these non-technical skills are important and correlate with patients’ survival5. Although optimal performance of advanced life support (ALS) is well described in current guidelines, the coordination of all necessary steps by a focused team leader is recommended6,7. While the benefit of a tele-team leader could be demonstrated for simulated neonatal resuscitation scenarios, the effect of tele-support for emergency medical technicians (EMTs) on their attention, cognitive load, workload, and performance in case of an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in adult patients is still unknown8. Eye tracking is an established tool to evaluate visual attention, which allows insights into the healthcare provider’s focus and gaze patterns, especially suited for stressful emergency situations9,10. It has been successfully used in both real and simulated scenarios, particularly for team leaders, providing point-of-view information that can enable investigators to comprehend the providers’ perspective and to improve clinical practice or training11.

As the impact of tele-support in OHCA is rarely investigated, this study aims to evaluate the effect of tele-team-leadership during CPR on the EMTs’ attention, workload, and performance in a simulated setting.

Methods

Ethics approval

This study is a cooperation between the Medical University of Vienna, Johanniter-Unfall-Hilfe Wien/Niederösterreich, Austrian Red Cross, State Association of Burgenland, and Ludwig Boltzmann Institute Digital Health and Patient Safety. The study was conducted between June 2023 and August 2023 in Vienna and in Mattersburg (Burgenland), Austria. Ethical approval (Number 1253/2022) was obtained by the local Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna. We adhered according to the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines regarding research on human subjects and followed the tenets of Good Scientific Practice.

Participants

Within this study, 86 EMTs with and without advanced education from either Johanniter Unfall Hilfe Wien/Niederösterreich or Austrian Red Cross, State Association of Burgenland participated and performed two simulated OHCA scenarios (with and without tele-support). All EMTs participated voluntarily after recruitment through these partner organizations. Each team consisted of three persons, of which two were EMTs (team leader and team member) and one confederate from the study team. The confederate acted as an EMT with advanced education to standardize team composition and ensure that team leadership was consistently executed by a person with a similar skill level across all teams. Their actions were scripted and limited to specific interventions defined by protocol, reducing their impact on scenario variability. Tele-support was provided by an experienced emergency physician using Corpuls Mission.

Study design

This study was conducted in a crossover design. As depicted in Fig. 1, the teams were randomized to start with one of two conditions, either with or without tele-support, and complete the other condition afterwards.

Randomization consort flow-diagram. ALS advanced life support.

All team members were equipped with medical work material resembling the equipment that they are familiar with as close as the simulated set-up allowed. Standardized emergency simulation scenarios with standardized set-up of the emergency patient dummy, equipment, and lighting were used for all participants to ensure reproducibility in combination with optimal conditions for the calibration of and recording with the eye tracking glasses. Both participants from each team (i.e. the team leader and the team member) were equipped with eye-tracking glasses (Tobii Pro 3.0, Stockholm, Sweden). During the emergency scenario, the participants were tasked with providing optimal emergency care to the simulated patient. In scenarios with tele-support, an experienced emergency physician (MH, CH) proactively supported the team in providing optimal care. For this purpose, the tele-physician used a structured checklist (Supplemental File 1). Real-time video and audio communication between the tele-physician and the emergency team was provided using a telephone affixed in a harness to the chest of the team leader on site, and hemodynamic and CPR parameters (e.g., heart rhythm of the manikin, CPR pressure depth, time) of the monitor were hosted via secure data transfer to the tele-physician via Corpuls Mission (Corpuls, Kaufering, Germany), a remote communication software. The tele-support system using Corpuls Mission performed without technical interruptions during the simulations, likely due to the stable Wi-Fi environment in the controlled indoor setting.

Cardiac arrest simulation scenario

Prior to the start of the scenario, the team was informed that they are responding to the scene of a 50-year-old man who was found unresponsive by bystanders. Participants were informed to assume that it was a nontraumatic event and the environment is safe. The bystanders had left the scene prior to arrival to avoid uncontrolled variables (e.g., noise, unpredictable behavior, emotional responses) that could impact eye-tracking data quality and team dynamics. No other history on this patient was available. The scenario time started when the participants entered the simulation room. After check of vital signs, diagnosis of OHCA, and start of CPR including application of defibrillator pads, the initial rhythm was ventricular fibrillation, which remained unchanged for the first five rhythm analyses. The protocol demanded the use of the device’s automated external defibrillator mode in compliance with Austrian EMTs’ scope of practice. The defibrillator used was the Corpuls3 (Corpuls, Kaufering, Germany). At the sixth rhythm analysis, the rhythm converted to sinus rhythm with ST elevation in II, III and aVF and a palpable pulse indicating return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). The scenario was stopped after 14 min. The simulated cardiac rhythm did not change based on any other interventions to provide consistency between scenarios and study groups.

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint of this study was visual attention of the team leaders. It was tested whether tele-support would distract the team during the OHCA simulation scenario. Areas of interest (AOI) were predefined by the study team and included the patient, medical equipment (including emergency backpack, oxygen bag), monitor of the defibrillator, and other providers (staff). In addition, attention paid to areas outside of the predefined AOIs (labeled “elsewhere”), indicating distraction was also recorded. The differences in the time spent in each AOI (i.e. dwell time) between the 2 conditions (no support, tele-support) was analyzed. A detection rate of > 80% of the participant’s eye movements was considered a sufficient quality detection for analysis.

Secondary outcomes

Differences in cognitive workload

Workload was measured with the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Task Load Index (TXL)12. Participants scored their perceived workload on six dimensions (mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance demand, effort, and frustration) using a scale from 0 to 20. Higher score represented higher workload. The NASA questionnaire was answered at the end of each scenario (Supplemental File 2).

Performance of CPR

The CPR performance was evaluated using the length of time until the occurrence of the following points of interest: the first chest compressions, the first administration of adrenaline, the first defibrillation, the first ventilation and application of an airway device, and the first consideration of reversible causes.

Subjective experience with eye-tracking and tele-support

The usability of eye-tracking glasses was evaluated by all participants at the end of the study using a questionnaire with 11 items about discomfort and distraction associated with wearing the glasses. In addition, participants were also asked to evaluate their experience with tele-support using four questions about its usefulness and applicability to pre-clinical care. Responses were made on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) (Supplemental File 3).

Sample size calculation

An a priori sample size calculation with the G*Power software for a repeated measure analyses of variance (ANOVA) with two repeated measures (with tele-support vs. without tele-support) revealed that a sample size of at least 34 teams would provide sufficient power (0.80) to detect an effect at the alpha level of 0.05 with a medium effect size (f = 0.25).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sample. Repeated measures ANOVA were conducted to examine whether visual attention, workload, and CPR performance changed across the tele-support conditions, with controlling for the order effect (tele-support condition first vs. second). All significant results from these analyses were post-hoc analyzed with pairwise t tests, using Bonferroni adjusted alpha levels. The difference in whether teams considered any reversible causes during performance was examined with Chi-square test. Finally, descriptive statistics were used to evaluate the subjective experience with eye-tracking and tele-support, while Mann-Whitney U test was conducted to test whether team leaders and team members differed in their perceptions. All analyses were performed with SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The level of significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Data were collected from 86 participants. Of them, 43 persons were team leaders, the other 43 persons were team members. Team leaders had an average of 10.8 (± 6.5) years of EMS (emergency medical service) experience and the majority of them had prior experience in simulation-based training. Team members were active in EMS for about five years in average. Participants’ characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Primary outcome (visual attention)

Eye-tracking recordings of 39 team leaders and 36 team members had sufficient quality and were used for analysis. Tele-support significantly improved visual attention among team leaders (Fig. 2), as indicated by a significant Condition × AOI interaction (F = 4.88, P = 0.006). Team leaders with tele-support attended more often to the equipment (P = 0.008) and less often to other staff during the CPR scenario (P = 0.017). Furthermore, they spent less time focusing “elsewhere,” i.e. outside of the task-relevant AOIs (P = 0.002). For team members, the tele-support condition did not significantly change their visual attention (Fig. 2).

Secondary outcomes

Differences in cognitive workload

Two participants did not complete all NASA items and were thus removed from the analysis. The overall workload decreased only marginally among team leaders after they were provided with tele-support (F = 3.97, P = 0.056). Of the single subscales, reduction in mental demands (P = 0.006; Table 2) was the only significant finding. For team members, the provision of tele-support did not change their overall workload. Only the perception of physical demands decreased significantly in this group (P = 0.046; Table 2).

CPR performance

Complete performance data were available from 38 teams. Overall, performance did not differ between conditions; the length of time to start chest compressions, administration of adrenaline, defibrillation, ventilation, intubation, and the consideration of reversible causes were similar regardless of whether tele-support was provided. However, there was a significant difference of whether reversible causes were considered. Without tele-support, as many as 10 teams (26.3%) did not consider reversible causes in the scenario whereas only 2 teams (5.3%) behaved this way when tele-support was provided (P = 0.008).

Subjective experience with eye-tracking and tele-support

None of the participants removed the eye-tracking glasses during the simulation scenario. The use of the glasses was perceived easy and not distracting by more than 70% of participants (Fig. 3). The majority of participants (> 80%) also denied any discomfort related to the glasses. The use of eye-tracking was thus perceived feasible in simulation settings; 67% of team leaders and 52% of team members would even be willing to wear the glasses during a real emergency scenario. Team leaders and team members did not significantly differ in the subjective experience with eye-tracking except for one discomfort item; team members reported more often that the glasses sled down on their nose (P = 0.006). Regarding tele-support, the majority of participants (> 80%) found the tele-support easy to handle, the quality of communication very good, and agreed that they would like to use tele-support also in a real scenario (Fig. 3). However, 31% of team leaders and 15% of team members also reported that tele-support distracted them from the task at hand to some degree. There were no significant differences between team leaders and team members in the subjective experience with tele-support.

Discussion

In this prospective trial, we compared attention and performance of 43 teams in two simulated OHCA scenarios, either with or without tele-support, while participants were wearing eye-tracking glasses. The primary outcome, which was dwell time in task-relevant AOIs, demonstrated that tele-support significantly improved visual attention among team leaders, spending less time focusing outside of the task-relevant AOIs as well as attending more closely to the equipment and less often to other staff.

In contrast, tele-support did not significantly change team members’ visual attention. A possible explanation for the lack of change in team members’ gaze behavior is that team members were for a large proportion of their time occupied with chest compressions during which their visual attention was mostly tied up with the patient’s chest.

In the NASA TXL for team leaders, there was a trend towards an overall reduction in cognitive workload for the tele-support condition. However, only the single subscale of mental demand was significantly reduced. For team members the single subscale of physical demand was reduced in the tele-support condition. A possible explanation why physical demands were significantly reduced by team members is that they mostly performed chest compressions and thus physical demands were in general higher (and appropriate) for them.

A major concern for tele-support during ALS, next to distraction of the EMTs, was a potential negative impact on the quality and efficiency of the resuscitation performance. As there were no significant differences in the times to reach the ‘milestones of ALS’, it could be shown that tele-support did not delay the EMTs’ actions. While it did not shorten the length of time until the occurrence of relevant milestones, it has to be considered that those times were very short for both conditions. In contrast, an evaluation of telemedicine in the emergency medical system has shown that telemedicine can significantly improve patients’ vital signs during life-threatening conditions13.

Despite the clear-cut and structured guidelines for ALS, reversible causes were significantly more likely to be considered and discussed as a team in the tele-support condition, thereby increasing guideline adherence for ALS6. In a retrospective analysis of cardiac arrests conducted in Australia, a significant survival benefit for recognizing and treating reversible causes could be established14. These data are corroborated by an analysis of recorded in and out of hospital resuscitations in Belgium15 as well as the results of a propensity score matched study of in hospital cardiac arrests conducted in Norway16 all pointing towards a survival benefit for considering and treating reversible causes during cardiopulmonary resuscitation. It can consequently be concluded that tele-support significantly improved quality of ALS by increasing the guideline adherence and aiding the teams in considering reversible causes.

Both, the eye-tracking glasses and the tele-support were perceived positively by the majority of participating EMTs and the EMTs would also be ready to use tele-support during a real mission.

Limitations

All simulation studies are limited by their design and relating their results to the real-life scenario always includes a certain degree of extrapolation. It has to be considered that this form of tele-support was new for all participants. If tele-support was integrated into EMTs education, training and missions, it can be assumed that its positive effects would increase further. Additionally, the controlled environment (e.g. bystanders removed from the scene) may not fully represent the medical and technical challenges faced in the field, such as connectivity issues or dynamic scene elements. Participants’ awareness of being studied might have influenced their behavior (Hawthorne effect). Also, tele-support was a novel intervention to all participants, which may have impacted their perceived usefulness or interaction with the technology.

Conclusion

Tele-support significantly improved team leaders’ visual attention during simulated OHCA, while having a positive impact on the resuscitation performance by increasing guideline adherence. Further, tele-support was viewed positively by a majority of study participants. Further research is needed to determine feasibility, technical requirements, and outcomes in real prehospital settings before widespread implementation can be recommended.
