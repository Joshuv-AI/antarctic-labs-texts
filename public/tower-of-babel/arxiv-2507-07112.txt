Abstract
We present a geometric method for finding travelling wave solutions to the generalized Korteweg–de Vries (gKdV) equation u t + u x ​ x ​ x + a ⁡ ( u ) ​ u x = 0 , u_{t}+u_{xxx}+a(u)u_{x}=0, where a ⁡ ( u ) a(u) is a smooth function characterizing the nonlinearity. Using the travelling wave ansatz, the gKdV equation reduces to an ordinary differential equation (ODE), which we analyze via the 𝒞 ∞ \mathcal{C}^{\infty} -structure-based method, a geometric framework involving sequences of involutive distributions and Pfaffian equations. Starting with the symmetry ∂ z \partial_{z} , we construct a 𝒞 ∞ \mathcal{C}^{\infty} -structure for the ODE and apply the stepwise integration algorithm to obtain an implicit general solution. We illustrate the method by deriving explicit solutions for specific forms of a ⁡ ( u ) a(u) , including the modified KdV and Schamel–KdV equations, as well as power-law nonlinearities.

Introduction

The Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation is a significant contribution to mathematics and science. It is a nonlinear partial differential equation that describes the evolution of waves on shallow water surfaces. Its origins are traced back to 1834, when the naval engineer J. S. Russell observed a wave in the Union Canal in Scotland that maintained its shape and velocity while traveling through the channel. He named it a “wave of translation”
[
1
]
. This discovery makes him spent several years researching empirically into these waves, building water wave tanks and even challenging the mathematical community to prove their existence.

Russell’s challenge to explain the existence of solitary waves was taken up by numerous researchers. Among them, Boussinesq stood out as the first to mathematically explain Scott Russell’s solitary wave phenomenon. He presented several works trying to explain the issue of persistent solitary waves of permanent form on a fluid interface to the Paris Academy of Sciences in 1871 and 1872
[
2
,
3
]
.

Dutch mathematicians D. J. Korteweg and his student G. de Vries built upon the work of Boussinesq to further investigate the solitary wave phenomenon observed by Russell. In 1895, they derived a simpler nonlinear partial differential equation that could be used to model the behavior of waves in a shallow channel
[
4
]
. After a change of variables their equation can be rewritten as

u_{t}+u_{xxx}+6uu_{x}=0.

This equation, nowadays known as the Korteweg–de Vries equation (KdV), describes the evolution of a one-dimensional wave in a dispersive medium. By solving the KdV equation, Korteweg and de Vries were able to demonstrate that the solitary wave observed by Russell could be described mathematically. Their work provided a more comprehensive understanding of the behavior of water waves and has had significant implications for fields such as oceanography, meteorology, and engineering
[
4
,
5
]
. Furthermore, the KdV equation has led to the discovery of new mathematical structures and techniques, such as integrable systems and inverse scattering theory
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. In fact, the KdV equation is one of the first nonlinear partial differential equations found to be integrable, establishing a foundational moment in the study of integrable systems.

Over the years, the KdV equation has been generalized in numerous ways to incorporate more complex phenomena and to enable the study of higher-dimensional and more intricate wave structures. The generalized KdV (gKdV) equation is a family of equations

u_{t}+u_{xxx}+a(u)u_{x}=0,

(1)

where
a\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, containing the original KdV equation as a particular case
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. These equations have attracted significant attention due to their rich mathematical structure and diverse applications in different areas, for example:

•

For
a(u)=u^{2}
, equation (
1
) is known as the modified Korteweg–de Vries equation, and it is used to describe the behavior of warn plasma with negative ions
[
12
]
and dusty plasmas
[
7
]
.

•

For
a(u)=\alpha\sqrt{u}
, equation (
1
) becomes the Schamel equation, which describes the propagation of ion-acoustic waves in a cold-ion plasma, in which specific electrons are trapped and do not exhibit isothermal behavior during the wave’s passage
[
13
]
.

•

For
a(u)=\alpha\sqrt{u}+\beta u
, with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, equation (
1
) corresponds to the Schamel–Korteweg–de Vries equation
[
14
]
, and for
{a(u)=1+\alpha\sqrt{u}+\beta\ln(|u|)}
the logarithmic Schamel equation. They both model different trapping scenarios for the electrons.

•

For
a(u)=2\alpha u-\beta u^{2}
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, equation (
1
) is called Gardner equation, and it has applications in hydrodynamics, plasma physics and quantum field theory
[
15
,
16
]
.

•

Finally, for
a(u)=u\ln(|u|)
equation (
1
) receives the name of Logarithmic KdV equation, which describes solitary waves in harmonic chains with Hertzian interaction force
[
17
]
.

In this work, we address the problem of finding exact solutions to the gKdV, and more specifically traveling wave solutions. These are solutions whose shape remains unchanged while they propagate at a constant speed. To this end, we reduce equation (
1
) to an ordinary differential equation (ODE), and then apply the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure-based method of integration, which is a geometric method that allows us to find the integral manifolds of a distribution generated by a set of vector fields
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

In Section
2
we briefly review the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure-based method of integration. In Section
3

4
we analyze some particular cases of the function
a(u)

2
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure-based method of integration

Given an open subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define a distribution on
U
of rank
r
as a submodule of the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U)
-module of vector fields
\mathfrak{X}(U)
generated by a set of
r
pointwise linearly independent vector fields
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{r}\}
. We will denote this distribution by
\mathcal{S}(\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{r}\})
, and it will be said to be involutive if

[Z_{i},Z_{j}]\in\mathcal{S}(\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{r}\})

for all
i,j=1,\ldots,r
, where
[-,-]
denotes the Lie bracket
[
21
,
22
]
.

For
m\geq 2
, consider the jet bundle
J^{m-1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
, with coordinates
(z,y,y_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1})
, where
y_{i}=\frac{d^{i}y}{dz^{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,m-1
(see
[
23
]
for details). Define the standard volume form on
J^{m-1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
as

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=dz\wedge dy\wedge dy_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{m-1}.

An
m
th-order ODE is, in this geometric setting, an expression of the form

y_{m}=\phi(z,y,y_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1}),

(2)

where
\phi
is a smooth function defined on an open subset
U\subseteq J^{m-1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
. We can associate to equation (
2
) the (trivially) involutive distribution
\mathcal{S}(\{Z\})
, generated by the vector field

Z=\partial_{z}+y_{1}\partial_{y}+\cdots+\phi\partial_{y_{m-1}},

defined on
U
.

The integral manifolds of this distribution corresponds to the prolongation of solutions to the ODE (
2
) in the jet space
J^{m-1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})

[
23
]
. To look for these integral manifolds, we will use the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure-based method of integration.
This geometric method was developed in
[
18
,
19
]
, and it is based on the notion of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure, which is a generalization of the concept of solvable structure
[
24
,
25
]
.

Definition 1
.

An ordered collection of vector fields
\langle X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\rangle
is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure for equation (
2
) if the distribution

\mathcal{S}(\{Z,X_{1},\ldots,X_{i}\})

has constant rank
i+1
, and it is involutive, for
1\leq i\leq m
.

The main result concerning
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structures is that they can be used to integrate the ODE (
2
) by solving
m
completely integrable Pfaffian equations (see
[
18
, Theorem 4.1]
). For the reader’s convenience, we present an outline of the integration procedure below:

1.

We define the 1-forms

\omega_{i}={X_{m}\,\lrcorner\,\ldots\,\lrcorner\,\widehat{X_{i}}\,\lrcorner\,\ldots\,\lrcorner\,X_{1}\,\lrcorner\,Z\,\lrcorner\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\quad 1\leq i\leq m,

(3)

where
\widehat{X_{i}}
indicates omission of
X_{i}
and
\lrcorner
denotes interior product.

2.

The Pfaffian equation
\omega_{m}\equiv 0
is completely integrable (see
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
). A solution
I_{m}=I_{m}(z,y,y_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1})
can be found by solving the system of linear homogeneous first-order PDEs arising from the condition
dI_{m}\wedge\omega_{m}=0
.

3.

Once the function
I_{m}
is found, we consider the level sets
\Sigma_{(C_{m})}
implicitly defined by
{I_{m}(z,y,y_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1})=C_{m}}
, where
C_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
. And we look for a local parametrization
\iota_{m}
of
\Sigma_{(C_{m})}
, with domain an open subset
U_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

4.

We compute the pullbacks

\omega_{i}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{m})}}:=\iota_{m}^{*}(\omega_{i}),\quad 1\leq i\leq m.

Observe that
\omega_{m}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{m})}}=0
.

5.

We repeat the process for the Pfaffian equation
\omega_{m-1}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{m})}}\equiv 0
, which is now completely integrable, and it is defined on a space of one dimension less, that is, on
U_{m}
instead of
J^{m-1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
. The procedure gives rise to new 1-forms

\omega_{i}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{m-1},C_{m})}}:=\iota_{m-1}^{*}(\omega_{i}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{m})}}),\quad 1\leq i\leq m,

defined on certain open set
U_{m-1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
.

Now, both
\omega_{m}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{m-1},C_{m})}}
and
\omega_{m-1}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{m-1},C_{m})}}
are null 1-forms, so we proceed with the Pfaffian equation
\omega_{m-2}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{m-1},C_{m})}}\equiv 0
, which is now completely integrable.

6.

In each iteration, for
m-1\geq k\geq 1
, it is solved the completely integrable Pfaffian equation

\omega_{k}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{k+1},\ldots,C_{m})}}\equiv 0,

and a solution

I_{k}=I_{k}(z,y,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1};C_{k+1},\ldots,C_{m})

is obtained. Then, a local parametrization
\iota_{k}
, defined on an open subset
U_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, of the level set
\Sigma_{(C_{k},\ldots,C_{m})}
,
C_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, is obtained. The process terminates when a local parametrization
\iota_{1}
of the level set
\Sigma_{(C_{1},\ldots,C_{m})}
is obtained.

7.

Finally, the composition of local parametrizations,
\iota_{m}\circ\cdots\circ\iota_{1}
, yields a parametric expression for the integral curves of
Z
, which are the prolongations, in the jet space
J^{m-1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
, of the explicit solutions to the ODE (
2
). Alternatively, the expression

I_{1}(z,y;C_{2},\ldots,C_{m})=C_{1},\quad C_{1}\in\mathbb{R},

provides an implicit general solution to the ODE (
2
).

Readers interested in a more detailed explanation of the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure integration process, and in reviewing illustrative examples, are referred to
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

3
Travelling wave solutions to the gKdV equation

We now consider the problem of finding traveling wave solutions to equation (
1
). To this end, we employ the standard ansatz consisting of the transformation

\begin{array}[]{l}z=x-ct,\ y(z)=u(x,t),\end{array}

(4)

which reduces the PDE (
1
) to the third-order ODE

-cy_{1}+y_{3}+a(y)y_{1}=0.

(5)

The corresponding vector field in the jet space
J^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
is

Z=\partial_{z}+y_{1}\partial_{y}+y_{2}\partial_{y_{1}}+(cy_{1}-a(y)y_{1})\partial_{y_{2}}.

In order to find exact solutions to (
5
) using the method proposed in Section
2
, we first need to identify a suitable
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure. We begin by observing that the vector field
\partial_{z}
is a Lie symmetry of (
5
); indeed,
[\partial_{z},Z]=0
, as can be easily verified. Therefore,
\mathcal{S}(\{Z,\partial_{z}\})
is involutive, and we can use the symmetry
X_{1}=\partial_{z}
as the first vector field in our
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure.

To find the second vector field
X_{2}
, we consider the following ansatz:

X_{2}=\partial_{y_{1}}+\eta\partial_{y_{2}},

where
\eta=\eta(z,y,y_{1})
is a smooth function to be determined. According to Definition
1
, the distribution
\mathcal{S}(\{Z,X_{1},X_{2}\})
must be involutive, which requires that the Lie brackets
[Z,X_{2}]
and
[X_{1},X_{2}]
lie in
\mathcal{S}(\{Z,X_{1},X_{2}\})
. We compute these Lie brackets and impose that they are linear combinations of
Z
,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, obtaining the following determining equations for
\eta
:

\displaystyle\eta^{2}y_{1}+\eta_{y}y_{1}^{2}+\eta_{y_{1}}y_{1}y_{2}-\eta y_{2}+\eta_{z}y_{1}=0,

(6)

\displaystyle\eta_{z}=0.

A particular solution
\eta=\dfrac{y_{1}}{y}
for the PDE system (
6
) is obtained, and then the second vector field will be

X_{2}=\partial_{y_{1}}+\dfrac{y_{1}}{y}\partial_{y_{2}}.

For the last vector field in the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure, we may choose any vector field
X_{3}
that is linearly independent of
Z,X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, since the distribution
\mathcal{S}(\{Z,X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\})
is then trivially involutive. A natural choice is
X_{3}=\partial_{y_{2}}
. Thus, we have the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure
\langle X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\rangle
for equation (
5
) given by

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{z},

(7)

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=\partial_{y_{1}}+\dfrac{y_{1}}{y}\partial_{y_{2}},

\displaystyle X_{3}

\displaystyle=\partial_{y_{2}}.

Now, we proceed by using the method described in Section
2
to integrate the ODE (
5
). First, we calculate the 1-forms (
3
)

\displaystyle\omega_{1}

\displaystyle=X_{3}\,\lrcorner\,X_{2}\,\lrcorner\,A\,\lrcorner\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}=-y_{1}dz+dy,

(8)

\displaystyle\omega_{2}

\displaystyle=X_{3}\,\lrcorner\,X_{1}\,\lrcorner\,A\,\lrcorner\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}=-y_{2}dy+y_{1}dy_{1},

\displaystyle\omega_{3}

\displaystyle=X_{2}\,\lrcorner\,X_{1}\,\lrcorner\,A\,\lrcorner\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}=-y_{1}\left(\dfrac{y_{2}}{y}+a(y)-c\right)dy+\dfrac{y_{1}^{2}}{y}dy_{1}-y_{1}dy_{2}.

A solution to the completely integrable Pfaffian equation
\omega_{3}\equiv 0
is the smooth function defined by

I_{3}=yy_{2}-\frac{1}{2}y_{1}^{2}-H_{1}(y),

where
H_{1}(y)
is any smooth function satisfying

H_{1}^{\prime}(y)=y(c-a(y)).

(9)

We define the level sets

\Sigma_{(C_{3})}=\left\{(z,y,y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\;\middle|\;I_{3}(z,y,y_{1},y_{2})=C_{3}\right\},

with
C_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
. The restriction of the 1-forms (
8
) to these level sets are

\displaystyle\omega_{1}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{3})}}=\iota_{3}^{*}(\omega_{1})=-y_{1}dz+dy,

(10)

\displaystyle\omega_{2}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{3})}}=\iota_{3}^{*}(\omega_{2})=-\frac{y_{1}^{2}+2H_{1}(y)+2C_{3}}{2y}dy+y_{1}dy_{1},

\displaystyle\omega_{3}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{3})}}=\iota_{3}^{*}(\omega_{3})=0,

where
\iota_{3}
is the local parametrization of
\Sigma_{(C_{3})}
given by

\iota_{3}(z,y,y_{1})=\left(z,y,y_{1},\dfrac{y_{1}^{2}+2H_{1}(y)+2C_{3}}{2y}\right),\quad y\neq 0.

We follow by looking for a solution to the Pfaffian equation
\omega_{2}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{3})}}\equiv 0
, and we obtain the expression

I_{2}=\dfrac{y_{1}^{2}-2yH_{2}(y)+2C_{3}}{y},\quad y\neq 0,

where
H_{2}
is any smooth function satisfying

H_{2}^{\prime}(y)=\dfrac{H_{1}(y)}{y^{2}}.

(11)

We restrict again to level sets, in this case:

\Sigma_{(C_{2},C_{3})}=\left\{(x,y,y_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\;\middle|\;I_{2}(z,y,y_{1};C_{3})=C_{2}\text{ and }y\neq 0\right\},\quad C_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

and pull back the 1-forms (
10
) with the local parametrization

\iota_{2}(z,y;C_{3})=\left(z,y,\sqrt{y(C_{2}+2H_{2}(y))-2C_{3}}\right).

(12)

In this way, we obtain

\displaystyle\omega_{1}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{2},C_{3})}}=-\sqrt{y(C_{2}+2H_{2}(y))-2C_{3}}dz+dy.

(13)

\displaystyle\omega_{2}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{2},C_{3})}}=0,

\displaystyle\omega_{3}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{2},C_{3})}}=0,

Finally, a solution for the Pfaffian equation
\omega_{1}|_{\Sigma_{(C_{2},C_{3})}}\equiv 0
is

I_{1}=z-H_{3}(y;C_{2},C_{3}),

where
H_{3}
satisfies

H_{3}^{\prime}(y;C_{2},C_{3})=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{y(C_{2}+2H_{2}(y))-2C_{3}}}.

(14)

Observe that the expression

z-H_{3}(y;C_{2},C_{3})=C_{1}

(15)

is an implicit general solution to the ODE (
5
). Then the traveling wave solutions to the gKdV equation (
1

u(x,t)=F(x-ct-C_{1};C_{2},C_{3}),

(16)

where

This is a very general expression, which depends not only on the parameters

in the definition of equation (

4

In this case, the PDE (

Observe that this Pfaffian equation can be solved by quadrature, by evaluating

\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{-2y^{3}+cy^{2}+C_{2}y-2C_{3}}}\,dy,

but this calculation is not straightforward. So we assume

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have presented a systematic, geometric approach for obtaining travelling wave solutions to the gKdV equation, characterized by an arbitrary nonlinearity function
a(u)
.

The core of our methodology rests on the theory of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structures. We successfully constructed a suitable
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-structure for the ODE resulting from applying the travelling wave ansatz to the gKdV equation, which enabled the application of a stepwise integration algorithm. This process, which involves solving a sequence of integrable Pfaffian equations, allowed us to derive a general implicit solution for the travelling waves.

To showcase the practical utility of our general solution, we have examined several physically significant examples of the gKdV equation. By particularizing the function
a(u)
, we were able to derive explicit solutions, including those for the modified KdV and Schamel-KdV equations, as well as for cases with general power-law nonlinearities.
