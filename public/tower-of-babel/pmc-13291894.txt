Development and Implementation of an AI System for Generating Clinical Urine Drug Test Sign-Outs
Authors: Nathan Laha, Michael Keebaugh, Hsuan-Chieh Liao, Bright Amankwaa, Olumuyiwa Adesoye, Abed Pablo, William S. Phipps, Andrew N. Hoofnagle, Geoffrey S. Baird, Patrick C. Mathias, Brody H. Foy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionCan an artificial intelligence (AI)–based system be used to enable more-rapid interpretation and clinical sign-out of urine toxicology tests?FindingsIn this prognostic study involving 83 553 urine drug tests at a single medical center, AI prediction of substance use patterns was highly accurate across 26 substances (mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, >0.99). Following integration into the clinical workflow, AI-based preliminary test interpretations reduced clinical sign-out times by 28.5 seconds on average (23% efficiency gain), while retaining high accuracy.MeaningThese findings suggest that AI-based preliminary interpretation of urine drug testing results is fast and accurate and may provide substantial efficiency gains to the clinical service.

Introduction

Urine drug tests (UDTs) are used in diverse clinical care settings, including emergency medicine, chronic pain management, and addiction medicine.1,2,3 For patients with substance use disorders in particular, UDTs provide objective data to monitor medication adherence and support treatment adjustments.3,4

Many UDT results and interpretations are straightforward; however, some cases involve complex pharmacokinetics, overlapping metabolic pathways, or assay cross-reactivity.2,5 In these cases, ordering practitioners may benefit from consultation with a clinical chemist with toxicology expertise, to assess the most likely substance use pattern.6 Although such consultations are valuable, they can be time intensive, leading to increased turnaround times and operational costs.

Recent advances in natural language processing tools, particularly large-language models (LLMs), offer potential to reduce these bottlenecks.7,8 Modern LLMs have the ability to extract and process multimodal clinical data to produce textual outputs.7,9 As such, there is wide interest in the use of LLMs to enhance clinical sign-outs across medicine.9 Yet, clinical deployment of LLMs remains rare, in large part because of the risks that generative models pose, such as hallucinations or output instability.10,11,12,13 Although the LLM regulatory landscape is still rapidly evolving, these additional risks may increase regulatory barriers and/or decrease the ability to safely deploy such systems into clinical workflows. As an alternative approach, we hypothesize that many clinical test interpretations can instead be generated through nongenerative machine-learning (ML) approaches, which avoid hallucination issues.

Here, we investigated whether nongenerative ML tools could enhance toxicology services through generation of preliminary textual interpretations of clinical UDTs. We used LLMs to develop a large, structured dataset from historical toxicology sign-out notes, which was then used to train a classic ML model for substance use prediction. Predictions were used to generate artificial intelligence (AI)–based clinical sign-out notes, which were incorporated into a human-in-the-loop sign-out workflow. This approach achieved expert-level accuracy while greatly reducing sign-out time, demonstrating a practical framework for integrating AI and natural language processing tools into complex clinical workflows.

Methods

This study follows the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD)+AI reporting guideline. All data collection and analysis were performed under a waiver of informed consent due to minimal risk, with an approval protocol from the University of Washington institutional review board.

Study Objectives and Design

We aimed to develop and validate an AI tool for aiding interpretation of UDTs. The intended workflow was for the AI system to predict the patient’s substance use from their UDT results and create a preliminary textual interpretation for review by the clinical chemistry fellows and attending physicians. To achieve this, we used a multistaged study design. First, we performed a large-scale analysis of retrospective data, to allow for development of the underlying predictive and text-generation models. Following this, we performed a prospective user study and validation of the AI interpretations, based on mock sign-outs of historical UDT cases. Finally, we performed a prospective analysis of accuracy and usage rates of the AI interpretations, when integrated and deployed into the live clinical UDT workflow. There was no patient or public involvement in the study design, given the primary goal was to support operational (nonpatient public facing) workflows. The AI interpretation tool was designed solely to be used by trained clinical chemistry clinicians (residents, fellows, and attending physicians) in an expert human-in-the-loop framework.

UDT Workflow

The UW Medicine (UWM) UDT workflow is summarized in detail in the eMethods in Supplement 1. In short, UWM uses a 2-stage process,6 with qualitative immunoassay screening for broad drug categories, followed by (if necessary) confirmatory mass spectrometry testing for opiates, amphetamines, or benzodiazepines. All results were first reviewed and interpreted by a clinical chemistry reader (a pathology resident [not an author of this study], fellow [B.A., H.L., M.K., O.A., or other clinical chemistry fellow], or clinical toxicologist [H.L.]) and were then signed out by a board-certified clinical pathologist (W.S.P., A.N.H., or G.S.B.). From February 2021 onward, case sign-outs were performed using an advanced web application interface developed by UWM to support this service.14,15 Over time, many components were added to UWM’s immunoassay and confirmatory tests. These are briefly summarized in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

Clinical Experts and Labelers

Clinical data labeling and evaluation of accuracy of AI interpretations was performed by members of the UWM clinical chemistry team. This included 3 readers: 2 clinical chemistry fellows (both male PhDs, M.K. and B.A.) and 1 clinical toxicologist (female PhD, H.L.), and 3 clinical chemistry attending physicians (all male; 1 MD [W.S.P.] and 2 MD plus PhD [A.N.H. and G.S.B.]). All 6 experts regularly perform preliminary (reader) or final (attending physician) sign-outs of UDT results as part of their clinical duties.

Clinical Data

We collated all UWM UDT results with interpretative notes from January 1, 2014 (as far back as these data are regularly available in our health record system), to February 29, 2024. Data were pulled via Structured Query Language queries to UWM’s health record databases. Data included UDTs from UWM patients, as well as from UWM reference laboratory and client populations (2784 of 10 676 tests [26.1%] as of 2024). UDTs were excluded if they did not have immunoassay test results or an interpretative note. The set of detectable substances varied over the study period because of changes in UWM’s patient population and laboratory testing protocols. For this study, we restricted analysis to all substances that were detectable under 2024 protocols and had a detected prevalence above 0.1% (23 substances total) according to 2024 data. These substances were alcohol, alprazolam, amphetamine, barbiturate, buprenorphine, cannabinoids, clonazepam, cocaine, codeine, diazepam, fentanyl, heroin, hydrocodone, hydromorphone, lorazepam, methamphetamine, methadone, methylphenidate, morphine, oxycodone, oxymorphone, temazepam, and tramadol. When confirmatory testing was not performed, interpretations could mention drug categories, including opioids, benzodiazepines, or amphetamines (distinct from amphetamine), instead of a specific chemical structure. For each substance or category, clinical interpretations could mention use (positive), no use (negative), or distance use (intermittent or low-dose use). UDT interpretations that contained mention of substances not in the above list were still included, but predictive models for those substances were not developed.

LLM Feature Extraction

To allow for predictive model training, clinical sign-out notes were first converted to substance use labels through use of a Bidirection and Auto-Regression Transformer language model,16 fine-tuned to provide sentence content classifications.17 Full details of the label extraction process are given in the eMethods in Supplement 1. An example of the labeling process is given in eFigure 1 in Supplement 1. Labeling accuracy was calculated by comparing outputs with human labels, across 520 random UWM interpretations from between 2022 and 2024. Each interpretation was manually labeled by a clinical chemistry fellow as positive, negative, or distant use for each of the 26 substances and/or categories (13 520 labels total), with the labeler blinded to the LLM labels. All disagreements between the LLM and human expert were manually re-examined and classified as either resulting from an LLM error, human error, or being indeterminate. The size of 520 cases provided power to detect case-level accuracy differences of 1.9%, assuming overall accuracy of 95%.

Substance Use Prediction

LLM-derived labels were used to train ML classification models for substance use, with full details described in eMethods in Supplement 1. In brief, immunoassay and mass spectrometry data were used alongside practitioner-input expected substance lists to train 23 individual substance models and 3 substance category models, using gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost18). Models were trained using an 80% to 20% train-test split at the patient level, with hyperparameter tuning and synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE)19 to handle class imbalances. Substances were classified on the basis of the overall highest predicted probability across the 3 classes (positive, negative, or distant use). Temporal stability was assessed by training models using data from 2014 to 2020, 2014 to 2021, and 2014 to 2022, and testing the model using all data from new patients in 2021, 2022, and 2023, respectively. To increase stability and decrease risk of bias, model predictions were only made using UDT results and practitioner-input expected substances; models did not have access to demographic data or other health record variables.

Interpretation Generation

A flowchart process was used to convert substance use predictions into a textual interpretation. Each prediction was first tagged with metadata about the case and substance (eg, whether the substance was a therapy) using the YAML markup language.20 Substances with the same labels and tags were then grouped together (eg, all positive therapies or all expected negative substances) and mapped to interpretative text snippets (eg, “These findings are consistent with clonazepam and oxycodone therapy”). The snippets were then joined together to create an overall test interpretation. The text snippets were designed iteratively in consultation with the clinical chemistry team to match the writing style they typically used for sign-outs.

Web Application Integration and Evaluative Studies

To allow for predeployment testing, we designed a development version of the preexisting UWM toxicology sign-out web application14 that included an option to generate the AI interpretation via a single button. Clicking this button generated an interpretation and pasted it into the sign-out box, while allowing for additional editing by the user. To allow for rapid functionality, AI interpretations were pregenerated and cached for each case any time new test results became available in the application.

Alongside the AI interpretations, the development application also integrated a new non-AI feature: a button that automatically imported a list of each patient’s recent medication dispenses into a medication list box in the app. This feature was created to improve operational efficiency and acted as a control comparator for the integration and uptake of AI and non-AI features. To evaluate the use of both tools, 400 UDT cases from 2023 were imported into the development application. Each case was uniformly randomized to provide access to either tool (or both, or neither). Three readers (B.A., H.L., and M.K.) then performed a mock sign-out of approximately 100 of these cases each (394 total), with size chosen as the largest number our clinical team had capacity to perform. For each case, the sign-out time was measured, and accuracy of the AI interpretation was manually evaluated compared with the reader sign-out. Expert adjustments to the AI interpretation were classified as grammatical (eg, minor text changes), rewording (substantial text changes that do not alter meaning), adding context (additional text that provides important context but does not alter substance use patterns), or overriding an incorrect substance use statement. Because of a coding bug, sign-out times were not measured for 139 of 394 cases. As such, the timing evaluation was limited to 255 cases (evenly spread across the 4 randomizations), whereas accuracy evaluation was performed in all 394 cases.

Deployment and Postdeployment Evaluation

The AI interpreter was deployed in August 2025, under Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) and College of American Pathology (CAP) regulatory guidelines for custom software and laboratory information system tools, and after creation of a deployment and ongoing verification plan. All 1323 cases signed out between August 5, 2025, and September 19, 2025, were manually reviewed to assess whether the AI interpreter was used, after exclusion of external or reference laboratory samples (leaving 939 cases), as these cases could not have medication lists automatically imported into the application, biasing use estimates of the non-AI tool. Two hundred of the cases (100 cases where the AI interpreter was used and 100 where it was not used) were manually reviewed to evaluate whether the AI interpretation (even if not used) was accurate or underwent rewording.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical differences between event rates were calculated using χ2 tests, and between means using t tests, with a standard significance threshold of 2-sided P < .05. Differences between human and LLM labelers were calculated using the McNemar test. To assess model fairness, we analyzed model performance separately across major demographic subgroups. All analysis was performed using Python software version 3.12 (Python Foundation). All ML models were trained using standard scikit-learn packages in Python.

Results

Cohort Summary

The cohort consisted of 83 553 cases from 26 459 patients (12 413 male [46.9%]; mean [SD] age at first test, 47.5 [16.7] years). Patients had a median (IQR) of 1 (1-3) test each, with 14.2% (3757 patients) having 5 or more tests, and 4.3% (1138 patients) having 10 or more tests. All patients in the cohort had an immunoassay test, while 15.9% (13 280 of 83 553 tests) had confirmatory testing for benzodiazepines, 50.7% (42 397 of 83 553 tests) for opioids, and 8.9% (7424 of 83 553 tests) for amphetamines. Compared with the training set, cases in the deployment environment (year 2025) were less likely to be male (2486 of 9653 patients [44.4%] vs 12 410 of 26 459 patients [46.9%]; P < .001), were White at a similar rate (7375 of 9653 patients [76.4%] vs 20 426 of 26 459 patients [77.2%]; P = .07), and were slightly older (mean [SD] age, 52.8 [17.1] vs 52.1 [17.5] years; P = .008).

LLM Labels of Substance Use vs Human Data Labeling

Compared with expert labels, LLM-based labeling of substance use patterns was 99.9% accurate (13 509 of 13 520 tests). Across 520 interpretations, the LLM achieved significantly lower error rates than the human labeler per substance (11 of 13 520 substances [0.08%] vs 23 of 13 520 substances [0.17%]; P = .04). The LLM’s error rate per case was lower than that of the human labeler but the difference was not statistically significant (11 of 520 cases [2.1%] vs 22 of 520 cases [4.2%]; P = .05) (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). LLM errors were mostly associated with high-complexity cases or with adjudications between positive and distant use of a substance. Conversely, human errors were most often in simple cases that were obvious under rereview.

Accuracy of AI Predictions for All Substances

AI prediction of substance use patterns was highly accurate across all categories, with mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values greater than 0.99 (Figures 2A and 2B), with only 3 substances falling below this threshold. These 3 models were for immunoassay-only benzodiazepines and immunoassay-only barbiturates (which are expected to be less accurate given the lack of confirmatory testing), and oxymorphone (which has low prevalence [<0.2%] and is a metabolite of oxycodone, making identification harder). Accuracy was greater than 94% for all substances and greater than 98% for all but 2 substances (eFigure 5 in Supplement 1). Model performance was well calibrated (probability distribution and calibration plots for each individual model are given in eFigures 6 and 7 in Supplement 1), and broadly equivalent across major demographic subgroups, with no group-specific AUC values less than 0.97 (model performance across major demographic subgroups is given in eFigure 8 in Supplement 1). Performance was also stable across substance categories (mean, minimum, and maximum AUCs for each class of substances are given in eFigure 9 in Supplement 1) and when training each model using only historic data from prior years (AUCs, sensitivity, and specificity when training only using cases prior to a given year are given in eFigure 10 in Supplement 1).

Accuracy and Suitability of AI Clinical Interpretations

AI textual interpretations were also highly accurate. Across 161 cases, AI interpretations had error rates similar to those of the expert readers (eFigure 5 in Supplement 1) (13 of 161 cases [8.1%; 95% CI, 4.4%-13.5%] vs 12 of 161 cases [7.5%; 95% CI, 4.0%-12.6%]; P = .84), although this analysis is partially limited by the small sample size. However, the primary reason for errors differed substantially between the 2 groups. Reader mistakes were most often due to not mentioning a positive substance or failing to mention use of a recreational substance such as alcohol or cannabinoids (in cases where this was then mentioned by the attending physician). Conversely, AI mistakes were most often due to calling a substance negative instead of distant use as a result of not having access to data that were below the mass spectrometry reporting limit but above the limit of detection (to which readers and attending physicians had manual access).

AI Interpretations and Speed of Clinical Sign-Outs

Under randomization of access in a replica of the clinical workflow for 255 cases (eFigure 11 in Supplement 1), use of the AI interpreter led to substantial efficiency gains. Clinicians using neither the AI tool nor a control non-AI assistive tool averaged 126 seconds per case sign-out. Use of the AI tool alone decreased this time by 28.5 seconds (23% efficiency gain), whereas use of both the AI and non-AI tools together decreased this time by 65 seconds (51% efficiency gain) (Figure 3; eFigure 12 in Supplement 1). These improvements scaled with experience, with improvements of 35%, 45%, and 65% from the most to least experienced reader.

These efficiency improvements were seen without compromising accuracy. In analysis of 394 retrospective cases (198 of which had the AI interpreter active), AI interpretations were unchanged by the reader in 132 of 198 sign-outs (70.2%) and only underwent light grammar modifications in a further 31 of 198 cases (15.7%) (Figures 4A and 4B). AI interpretations were only overridden because of incorrect substance use classification in 7 of 198 cases (3.5%), 3 of 7 of which were due to complex examples of sample adulteration. Accuracy of the predicted substance use pattern was highest for cases with simple substance use patterns but decreased as the total number of detected substances grew (eFigure 13 in Supplement 1).

Clinical Use of AI Interpretations After Deployment

After deployment, AI interpretations retained high use, being used for more than two-thirds of cases by 3 of the 4 readers on service (rates, 94.0% [140 of 149 cases], 70.7% [29 of 41 cases], and 68.7% [202 of 294 cases]) (Figure 5A). Usage was low (31 of 277 cases [11.2%]) for one reader, but low usage was also seen for the non-AI tool (71 of 277 cases [25.6%], suggesting they may be resistant to workflow changes, whether AI enabled or not. Readers were seen to modulate their use of both the AI and non-AI tools depending on which attending physician’s case they were evaluating, with one reader modulating their use of AI from 20% (3 of 15 cases) to 100% (26 of 26 cases) across attending physicians (Figure 5B). Despite this, the interpretation accuracy was similar regardless of whether the reader used AI and compared with what AI alone would have produced (Figure 5C). Discussion

In this prognostic study, we outline the development and deployment of an AI tool for generating clinical interpretations of UDTs. This tool provides accurate test interpretations and greatly improves sign-out efficiency.

Recent improvements in LLMs have generated substantial excitement about their potential value in medicine.7 However, few generative LLM tools have been clinically deployed, owing to risks of model instability and hallucination.10,11,12 Our study illustrates an alternate way for LLMs to support clinical operations, while minimizing these risks: through conversion of textual data into training targets for classic ML models. We believe this can enable deployment of more stable classic ML models, which face substantially lower regulatory barriers than generative models.21

UDTs reflect a common clinical challenge for this framework: an ordered test generates complex data that may require specialist interpretation or consultation. This need introduces substantial costs, increases test turnaround times, and limits the test volume a medical center can feasibly provide. In this context, our results suggest that AI test interpretation can provide substantial efficiency gains, allowing centers to both save costs and offer testing to more patients. The results in eFigure 13 in Supplement 1 also suggest that these tools may provide triage value, thereby enabling rapid interpretation in simple settings and allowing clinicians to focus more effort on the highest need cases. Although our study focused on UDTs, we envision this approach being beneficial for services with similar interpretation challenges, such as hemoglobin electrophoresis, tissue pathology analysis, and electrocardiograms. However, we also note that compared with other pathology tests, UDTs are particularly sensitive and regularly performed for vulnerable populations, with incorrect test results having the potential to drive significant social or emotional harm.1 Importantly, we found our approach was equally performant across major demographic subgroups. We still recognize that ongoing, demographically aware monitoring of the tool is important to support its continued use.

Following deployment, the AI tool’s use varied substantially across attending physicians, with some embracing AI usage and others being more resistant. This is consistent with the literature, which shows varied clinician perspectives on AI tools in medicine.12,22,23,24 Interestingly, Figure 5 showed that readers modulated their use of AI tools to align with the case’s attending physician. This highlights a key tension: that AI-based interpretation may allow for more consistent and standardized reporting but that attending physicians may be more resistant to writing tools that do not match their own writing style. This suggests the need to engage carefully with clinical stakeholders25 and to balance tailoring to individual users against the potential benefits of standardization. Interestingly, we also found that the AI and non-AI tools provided similar-sized, additive efficiency gains. This highlights that although AI tools can provide substantial benefit, there are also many simple, non-AI workflow improvements that can similarly provide benefit.

We deployed this framework as a human-in-the-loop system, under CAP-CLIA guidelines for custom software development. Currently, these guidelines do not have AI or ML requirements, beyond broader software guidance. However, we believe that future CAP-CLIA and US Food and Drug Administration guidelines will likely incorporate AI or ML requirements and potentially have specific regulatory pathways for generative models.26 Given this, we believe our framework has a key advantage: we believe that the use of nongenerative models will allow for ongoing lower regulatory burden than generative text models because of the lack of hallucination-related safety risks.

Limitations

Our study provides a strong proof of concept but has key limitations. Our study is retrospective and single center, and the study protocol was not preregistered. We also note that the postdeployment validations are based on small sample sizes. As such, there is need for external validation of our findings, including in centers that use different testing protocols and/or assays that cover different substances. Beyond this, we see many avenues for future work in which AI or ML tools may assist the UDT workflow. This includes LLM-based medical record review for risk score calculation (eg, the Opioid Risk Tool27), agentic-based confirmatory testing, and expansion of the tool to provide practitioner-tailored interpretive reporting.

Conclusions

In this prognostic study, we investigated whether an AI-based tool for generating preliminary interpretations of UDTs could improve clinical workflow efficiency. We found that AI-based interpretations were highly accurate, and when integrated into an existing workflow, led to substantial reductions in average sign-out time. Our results reflect a highly novel deployment of AI for assistive sign-out of toxicology testing. We believe this provides a strong example of how AI tools can enhance the modern clinical laboratory.
