Mean Field Games with Reflected Dynamics


Abstract
This paper establishes the existence of an equilibrium for a class of mean field games governed by reflected stochastic differential equations. The analysis is developed within the framework of relaxed controls and the associated martingale problem, providing a flexible approach that ensures the compactness and continuity properties required for the existence proof. Under a suitable convexity assumption, we recover an equilibrium for the original (non-relaxed) mean field game. Furthermore, by imposing a uniform ellipticity condition, we establish the existence of a Markovian equilibrium.

Introduction

The study of Mean Field Games (MFGs), introduced in
[
17
,
24
,
23
,
25
]
, offers a powerful framework for analyzing approximate Nash equilibria in large, symmetric population games where players interact only through the empirical distribution of all players’ states or strategies. This approach, as described in
[
10
]
, involves approximating the empirical distribution with an externally specified distribution and then examining the optimization problem of a representative player. The next step is to identify a fixed point where the distribution of the representative player’s optimal state or strategy matches the specified distribution.

In this paper, we study a class of MFGs with reflection, based on the standard fixed-point formulation.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a deterministic flow of probability measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. For a given
\mu
, we consider the stochastic control problem consisting in minimizing the following cost function over all admissible controls
(u_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}h(t,X_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}K_{t}+g(X_{T},\mu_{T})\Bigg],

(1.1)

where the state process satisfies the reflected dynamics

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}K_{t},\\[6.0pt]
X_{0}\sim\lambda,\\[4.0pt]
X_{t}\geq 0\quad\text{a.s. for all }t\in[0,T],\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}K_{t}=0\quad\text{a.s.}\end{cases}

Here
K
is an adapted, non-decreasing process with
K_{0}=0
, enforcing the state constraint through the Skorokhod condition.

A mean field equilibrium is a pair
(\mu,u)
such that
u
is optimal for the control problem associated with
\mu
, and the consistency condition

\mu_{t}=Law(X_{t}),\qquad t\in[0,T],

(1.2)

is satisfied, where
X
denotes the corresponding optimal state process.

The existence of mean field game equilibria in the setting without reflection has been established using three main approaches. The first is the analytical approach introduced by Lasry and Lions in
[
25
]
, which is based on the study of a coupled system of forward–backward partial differential equations. The backward equation is the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation associated with the representative agent’s optimization problem, while the forward equation is a Kolmogorov–Fokker–Planck equation describing the evolution of the state distribution arising from the consistency condition.

A second line of work adopts a probabilistic approach based on the Pontryagin maximum principle. In this framework, the optimal control problem is characterized through adjoint processes, and the mean field equilibrium is obtained by solving a system of McKean–Vlasov forward–backward stochastic differential equations. We refer, for instance, to
[
5
,
8
,
9
]
for developments along these lines.

A third approach, proposed by Lacker in
[
22
]
, relies on a weak formulation of stochastic control problems. This method allows for relaxed controls and is formulated in terms of martingale problems, providing a game-theoretic interpretation of MFGs. Using this relaxed solution concept, Lacker proves existence by first establishing the upper hemicontinuity of the representative agent’s best-response correspondence with respect to a given flow of measures
\mu
, via Berge’s maximum theorem. He then applies the Kakutani–Fan–Glicksberg fixed-point theorem to show the existence of a measure-valued process
\mu
such that the law of the state process, under an optimal response to
\mu
, coincides with
\mu
itself.

In the reflection setting, Bayraktar et al.
[
2
]
developed a mean field game formulation for a large symmetric queueing system operating under heavy-traffic conditions, where numerous strategic servers interact weakly through the empirical distribution of their queue lengths. The authors analyzed this model using the first MFG approach, assuming that the volatility is nondegenerate and independent of both the mean field and the control variables. In the absence of mean field interactions—namely, in the framework of a controlled reflected stochastic differential equation (RSDE)—related problems have been thoroughly examined in several works (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
20
,
19
,
21
,
27
]
).

Extending the results of
[
22
]
, we investigate mean field games (MFGs) with reflection as described in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). Motivated by Lacker’s methodology, our analysis employs the relaxed control framework for stochastic optimal control problems to study the reflected MFG setting. The proposed approach builds upon the seminal contributions of El Karoui et al.
[
12
]
and the general martingale problem framework introduced by Haussmann and Lepeltier
[
15
]
, integrating these techniques to address the reflection component within the MFG structure.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
recalls the notion of relaxed controls for stochastic control problems governed by systems of reflected stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and introduces the framework of MFGs with reflection.
This section also presents the standing assumptions on the coefficients and states the main existence results, namely Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
, together with the proof of the latter.
Finally, Section
3
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Assumptions and the main result

. When

For a finite time horizon

, and by

\|x\|_{t}:=\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|x_{s}|,\quad t\in[0,T],\quad x\in\mathcal{C}[0,T].

For

, denote by

the image of

under the map
\mathcal{C}[0,T]\ni x\mapsto x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
.

For

, let

denote the set of

for some (and thus for any)

. For

and

, let

denote the

W_{E,p}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(E\times E)}\left(\int_{E\times E}d^{p}(x,y)\gamma(dx,dy)\right)^{1/p},

(2.1)

where
\gamma
has marginals
\mu
and

.

Unless otherwise stated, the space
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
is always equipped with the metric

Given

|\mu|^{p}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{p}\mu(dx).

Similarly, for

\|\mu\|^{p}_{t}:=\int_{\mathcal{C}[0,T]}\|x\|_{t}^{p}\mu(dx).

2.1
The Stochastic control problem

We consider the following stochastic optimal control problem:

\inf_{u}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t},u_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}h(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}K_{t}+g(X_{T}),\right),

(2.2)

subject to

\begin{cases}X_{t}=X_{0}+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X_{s},u_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s},u_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s}+K_{t},&t\in[0,T];\\
X_{0}\sim\lambda;\\
X_{t}\geq 0\quad\text{a.s};\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\mathrm{d}K_{t}=0\quad\text{a.s.},\end{cases}

(2.3)

where
(K_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is non-decreasing process, the real-valued process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
represents the state process and, and
u
belongs to the set of controls, which consists of adapted processes taking values in a compact subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
.

We will use the compactification method to study the control problem
(
2.2
)
, where the state is given by the reflected SDE (
2.3
). By introducing a relaxed version of the classical control, we reformulate the problem as a martingale problem on a suitable canonical space.

Now, we introduce the concept of control for the system described by (
2.3
).

Definition 2.1
.

We call an admissible system control a set
\alpha=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P},B,u,X,K)
such that

1)

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space equipped with a filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
;

2)

u
is a
U
-valued process,
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable;

3)

K
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-valued process,
\mathbb{F}
-adapted; the sample paths of
K
are in
\mathcal{A}[0,T]
,i.e., for each
\omega\in\Omega
,
K_{.}(\omega)\in\mathcal{A}[0,T]
;

4)

B
is a standard Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
, and
X
, the state process, is
\mathbb{F}
-adapted with sample paths in
\mathcal{C}[0,T]
;

5)

\mathbb{P}\circ(X_{0})^{-1}=\lambda
;

6)

The processes
X
and
K
satisfy the system described by (
2.3
).

The collection of controls is denoted by
\Lambda
.

It is well known from the theory of reflected SDEs
[
26
,
28
]
that, under the Lipschitz condition, the set
\Lambda
is nonempty.

The cost functional corresponding to a control
\alpha\in\Lambda
is defined as:

J(\alpha):=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t},u_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}h(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}K_{t}+g(X_{T})\right).

(2.4)

2.1.1
Relaxed control

In order to apply the compactification method, we now reformulate the problem. Since the Brownian motion in the definition of controls is not known in advance, we can reformulate the above control problem as an equivalent martingale problem. This simplifies taking limits. In fact, let

\mathcal{L}\phi(s,x,u):=\dfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(s,x,u)\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)+b(s,x,u)\phi^{\prime}(x).

(2.5)

Then we can show that
\alpha
is a control if and only if
\alpha
satisfies
(1)-(3)
, and
(5)
in Definition
2.1
, and condition
(6)
is replaced by

6
{}^{\prime})

The process
X
is continuous,
\mathbb{F}
-adapted process, such that

i)

For each
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathcal{M}^{\phi}
is
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
-continuous martingale, where

\mathcal{M}^{\phi}_{\cdot}:=\phi(X_{\cdot})-\int_{0}^{\cdot}\mathcal{L}\phi(s,X_{s},u_{s})\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{\cdot}\phi^{\prime}(X_{s})\mathrm{d}K_{s},

(2.6)

and
\mathbb{P}
-a.s for every
t\in[0,T]
;

ii)

X_{t}\geq 0
;

iii)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\mathrm{d}K_{t}=0
.

Remark 2.1
.

Using the same arguments as in the proof of Proposition IV-2.1 in
[
18
]
, we can show that the existence of weak solutions to the system (

) is equivalent to the existence of solutions to the associated martingale problem
\mathbf{(6^{\prime})}
.

Now, we introduce a relaxed control framework, where the controls are adapted processes taking values in the space of probability measures on
U
. Specifically, the

-valued process

is replaced by an

-valued process

Definition 2.2
.

A tuple
r=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P},Q,X,K)
is called a relaxed control if

1)

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space equipped with a filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
;

2)

Q:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(U)
is an
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable process, and
K
is an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted process such that
K\in\mathcal{A}[0,T]
;

3)

X
is an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted process,
X\in\mathcal{C}[0,T]
and for each
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathcal{M}^{\phi}
is
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
-martingale, where

\mathrm{}\mathcal{M}^{\phi}_{\cdot}:=\phi(X_{\cdot})-\int_{0}^{\cdot}\int_{U}\mathcal{L}\phi(s,X_{s},u)Q_{s}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{\cdot}\phi^{\prime}(X_{s})\mathrm{d}K_{s},

(2.7)

and
\mathbb{P}
-a.s for every

, the following holds true

4)

X_{t}\geq 0
;

5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\mathrm{d}K_{t}=0
;

6)

\mathbb{P}\circ(X_{0})^{-1}=\lambda
;

The collection of relaxed controls is denoted by
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
.

The cost functional corresponding to a relaxed control

is defined by

\hat{J}(r):=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}f(t,X_{t},u)Q_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}h(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}K_{t}+g(X_{T})\right).

(2.8)

Remark 2.2
.

i)

The relaxed control
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P},X,Q,K)
is said to be strict if
Q_{t}=\mathrm{}\delta_{u_{t}}
for some progressively measurable
U
-valued process
u
.

ii)

Any strict control corresponds to a relaxed control. Indeed, If
\alpha=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P},X,u,K)\in\Lambda
, then by considering
r=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P},X,Q,K))
where
(Q_{t}(\mathrm{d}u):=\delta_{u_{t}}(\mathrm{d}u))
, it is straightforward to verify that
r
is a relaxed control. Moreover, we have

\hat{J}(r)=J(\alpha),

therefore,

\inf_{r\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}\hat{J}(r)\leq\inf_{\alpha\in\Lambda}J(\alpha).

To obtain the reverse inequality, define, for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
, the set

S(t,x):=\{(\sigma^{2}(t,x,u),b(t,x,u),e):e\geq f(t,x,u),u\in U\}.

If
S(t,x)
is convex for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
, it can be shown that for any relaxed control there exists a strict control with an equal or smaller cost. Indeed, since the cost associated to
K
is independent of the control, by the reasoning outlined in the proof of Theorem 3.6 in
[
15
]
, for any relaxed control
r=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},P,X,Q,K)
, there exists a progressively measurable

-valued process
\tilde{u}
and an
\mathbb{R}^{+}
-valued process
\tilde{v}
such that, for almost all
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{U}\sigma^{2}(t,X_{t}(\omega),u)Q_{t}(\omega,\mathrm{d}u),\int_{U}b(t,X_{t}(\omega),u)Q_{t}(\omega,\mathrm{d}u),\int_{U}f(t,X_{t}(\omega),u)Q_{t}(\omega,\mathrm{d}u)\right)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sigma^{2}(t,X_{t}(\omega),\tilde{u}_{t}(\omega)),b(t,X_{t}(\omega),\tilde{u}_{t}(\omega)),f(t,X_{t}(\omega),\tilde{u}_{t}(\omega))+\tilde{v}_{t}(\omega)\Big).

Then,
\rho=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},P,X,\tilde{u},K)
is a strict control with an equal or smaller cost.

2.1.2
Control rules

In the following, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{+}
the space of all continuous functions from
[0,T]
to
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and by
\mathcal{A}^{+}
the subset of
\mathcal{C}^{+}
consisting of all non-decreasing functions. Both spaces are equipped with the topology of uniform convergence. We denote by
\mathcal{U}
the set of all measures on
[0,T]\times U
such that the first marginal is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,T]
and the second marginal is a probability measure on
U
.
The space
\mathcal{U}
is equipped with the 2-Wasserstein metric, naturally adapted from (
2.1
), as follows:

d_{\mathcal{U}}(q_{1},q_{2}):=W_{[0,T]\times U,2}\left(\frac{q_{1}}{T},\frac{q_{2}}{T}\right).

Since
U
is compact, this definition makes
\mathcal{U}
a compact, complete, and separable metric space.

We consider the canonical space

\Omega=\mathcal{C}^{+}\times\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{A}^{+},

and the filtration
\mathbb{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is generated by the coordinate projections
X
,
Q
,
K
. Specifically, for each
\omega=(x,q,k)\in\Omega
,

X(\omega)=x,\,\,Q(\omega)=q,\,\,K(\omega)=k,

and for
t\in[0,T]
,
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{K}_{t}
, where

\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}=\sigma(X_{s},s\leq t),\,\,\mathcal{F}^{Q}_{t}=\sigma(Q(F),F\in\mathcal{B}([0,t]\times U)),\,\,\mathcal{F}^{K}_{t}=\sigma(K_{s},s\leq t).

The following arguments demonstrate that relaxed controls can be defined using projections mapping. Indeed, since
[0,T]
and
U
are compact, each
q\in\mathcal{U}
can be disintegrated as the following

q(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)=q_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t,

for some measurable
\mathcal{P}(U)
-valued function
(q_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

From the definition of the space
\mathcal{U}
and the Lemma 3.2 in
[
22
]
, there exists an
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{Q})_{t\in[0,T]}
-predictable
\mathcal{P}(U)
-valued process
\Gamma
such that for each
q\in\mathcal{U}
,

\Gamma_{t}(q)=q_{t},\,\text{a.e}.\,\,t\in[0,T].

Thus, the process
Q^{0}_{t}:=\Gamma_{t}\circ Q
is
\mathbb{F}
-predictable. As a result, for each
\omega=(x,q,k)
,

Q(\omega)(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)=q(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)=q_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t=\Gamma_{t}(q)(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t=\Gamma_{t}\circ Q(\omega)(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t=Q^{0}(\omega)(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t.

This implies an adapted disintegration of Q in terms of the
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable process

Q^{0}:[0,T]\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}(U)

and therefore enables us to define control rules. For now on, we let
(Q_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}:=(Q^{0}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
for simplicity.

Definition 2.3
.

For the canonical path space
\Omega
, the canonical filtration
\mathbb{F}
, and the coordinate projections
(X,Q,K)
introduced above, if
r=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P},X,Q,K)
is a relaxed control, then
\mathbb{P}
is called a control rule. We denote by
\mathcal{R}
the space of control rules. In this case, the associated cost functional is defined as

\tilde{J}(\mathbb{P}):=\hat{J}(r).

Remark 2.3
.

It is evident that

\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{R}}\tilde{J}(\mathbb{P})\geq\inf_{r\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}\hat{J}(r).

Conversely, for any relaxed control
r
, one can construct a control rule
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\tilde{J}(\mathbb{P})=\hat{J}(r)
. The proof, similar to that of
[
16
, Proposition 2.6]
, applies here in the context of reflected SDEs. In simpler terms, the optimization problems involving relaxed controls and control rules are equivalent. Therefore, it suffices to consider control rules.

The next proposition represents
\mathcal{R}
in terms of reflected SDEs driven by martingale measure.
For the notation of martingale measure and related stochastic integrals used in the result below, we refer to the article
[
13
]
.

The following proposition is frequently used in the main text.

Proposition 2.1
.

The existence of a solution
\mathbb{P}
to the martingale problem
(
2.7
)
is equivalent to the existence of a weak solution to the following reflected SDEs:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}=\displaystyle\int_{U}b(t,\tilde{X}_{t},u)\tilde{Q}_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t+\displaystyle\int_{U}\sigma(t,\tilde{X}_{t},u)\tilde{M}(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)+\mathrm{d}\tilde{K}_{t},\,\,&\tilde{X}_{0}\sim\lambda;\\
\tilde{X}_{t}\geq 0\,\,\text{a.s};\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{X}_{t}\mathrm{d}\tilde{K}_{t}=0\,\,\text{a.s},\end{cases}

(2.9)

where
\tilde{X}
,
\tilde{M}
and
\tilde{K}
are defined on some extension
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{Q}})
and
\tilde{M}
is a martingale measure with intensity
\tilde{Q}
. Moreover, the two solutions are related by

\mathbb{P}\circ(X,Q,K)^{-1}=\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}\circ(\tilde{X},\tilde{Q},\tilde{K})^{-1}.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{P}
solves the martingale problem (
2.7
), it follows from Proposition A.2 in
[
14
]
that there exists an extension of
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
,
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},(\tilde{\mathcal{F}})_{t\in[0,T]},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
, on which a tuple of adapted stochastic processes
(\tilde{X},\tilde{Q},\tilde{K},\tilde{M})
is defined such that for all
t\in[0,T]

\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}=\int_{U}b(t,\tilde{X}_{t},u)\tilde{Q}_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t+\int_{U}\sigma(t,\tilde{X}_{t},u)\tilde{M}(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}t)+\mathrm{d}\tilde{K}_{t},

and two tuples are related by
\mathbb{P}\circ(X,Q,K)^{-1}=\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}\circ(\tilde{X},\tilde{Q},\tilde{K})^{-1}
.

The processes
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{K}
are non-negative due the fact that

(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},(\tilde{\mathcal{F}_{t}})_{t\in[0,T]},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
is an extension of
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
. For the Skorokhod condition, we have

\tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{X}_{t}\mathrm{d}\tilde{K}_{t}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\mathrm{d}K_{t}\right)=0,

since
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{X}_{t}\mathrm{d}\tilde{K}_{t}
is non-negative, we obtain
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{X}_{t}\mathrm{d}\tilde{K}_{t}=0

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s.

The inverse can be verified by applying Itô’s formula.

2.2
Relaxed MFGs with controlled RSDE dynamics

We consider a mean field game associated with controlled RSDEs of the form (
1.1
) and (
1.2
).

As a first step, we fix
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{C}^{+})
and study the optimal control problem of a representative agent, which consist in minimizing

\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},u_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}h(t,X_{t},\mu_{t})\mathrm{d}K_{t}+g(X_{T},\mu_{T})\right),

over controls
u
, subject to:

\begin{cases}X_{t}=X_{0}+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X_{s},\mu_{s},u_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s},\mu_{s},u_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s}+K_{t},&t\in[0,T];\\
X_{0}\sim\lambda;\\
X_{t}\geq 0\quad\text{a.s};\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\mathrm{d}K_{t}=0\quad\text{a.s.},\end{cases}

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mu
be a given probability measure in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{C}^{+})
. We say that
P
is a control rule w.r.t
\mu
if it satisfies the following conditions:

1)

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F})_{t\in[0,T]},P)
is the canonical probability space and
(X,Q,K)
are the coordinate projections.

2)

For each
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathcal{M}^{\mu,\phi}
is well defined
P
-continuous martingale, where

\mathcal{M}^{\mu,\phi}_{t}:=\phi(X_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}\mathcal{L}\phi(s,X_{s},\mu_{s},u)Q_{s}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\phi^{\prime}(X_{s})\mathrm{d}K_{s}.

(2.10)

3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\mathrm{d}K_{t}=0

P
.a.s.

4)

P\circ(X_{0})^{-1}=\lambda.

For a fixed measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{C}^{+})
, the corresponding set of control rules is denoted by
\mathcal{R}(\mu)
, the cost functional corresponding to a control rule
P\in\mathcal{R}(\mu)
is

J(\mu,P):=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},u)Q_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}h(t,X_{t},\mu_{t})\mathrm{d}K_{t}+g(X_{T},\mu_{T})\right),

and the (possibly empty) set of optimal control rules is denoted by

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mu):=\arg\min_{P\in\mathcal{R}(\mu)}J(\mu,P).

Definition 2.5

(Relaxed and Markovian MFG solutions)
.

A probability measure
P
is called a
relaxed MFG solution
if

P\in\mathcal{R}^{*}(P\circ X^{-1}),

that is,
P
constitutes a fixed point of the set-valued mapping

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{C}^{+})\ni\mu\longmapsto\{\,P\circ X^{-1}:P\in\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mu)\,\}.

Moreover:

1.

If there exists a progressively measurable control process
u
such that

P\big[\,Q(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}u)=\delta_{u_{t}}(\mathrm{d}u)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\big]=1,

then
P
is referred to as a
strict MFG solution
.

2.

If there exists a measurable function

\hat{q}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{P}(U)
such that

P\big[\,Q(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}u)=\hat{q}(t,X_{t})(\mathrm{d}u)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\big]=1,

then
P
is called a
relaxed Markovian MFG solution
.

3.

If there exists a measurable function

\hat{\alpha}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to U
such that

P\big[\,Q(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}u)=\delta_{\hat{\alpha}(t,X_{t})}(\mathrm{d}u)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\big]=1,

then
P
is called a
Markovian (strict) MFG solution
.

Assumption
(A)
.

We impose the following conditions on the coefficients
b,\sigma,f,h,
and
g
.

(
Regularity
)

The functions
b,\sigma,f,h,
and
g
are measurable in
t
and continuous in
(x,\mu,u)
on

[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\times U
; in addition,
h
is continuous in
t
.

(
Lipschitz and Growth Conditions
)

There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for all

(t,u)\in[0,T]\times U
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
,

|b(t,x,\mu,u)-b(t,y,\nu,u)|+|\sigma(t,x,\mu,u)-\sigma(t,y,\nu,u)|\leq C_{1}\big(|x-y|+W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu,\nu)\big),

and

|\sigma(t,x,\mu,u)|+|b(t,x,\mu,u)|\leq C_{2}\left(1+|x|+\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}|z|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big)^{1/2}\right).

(
Dependence on the Measure
)

The functions
\sigma^{2},f,g,h
are locally Lipschitz in the measure argument, uniformly in
(t,x,u)
.
For each
\varphi\in\{\sigma^{2},f,g,h\}
, there exists
C_{3}>0
such that for all

(t,x,u)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times U
and

\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
,

|\varphi(t,x,\mu_{1},u)-\varphi(t,x,\mu_{2},u)|\leq C_{3}\big(1+F(W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{1},\delta_{0}),W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{2},\delta_{0}))\big)W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),

where
F
is locally bounded in its arguments.

(
Polynomial Growth
)

There exists constant
C_{4}>0
such that for all

(t,x,\mu,u)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\times U
,

|g(x,\mu)|\leq C_{4}\left(1+|x|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}|z|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}z)\right),

|f(t,x,\mu,u)|\leq C_{4}\left(1+|x|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}|z|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}z)\right),

|h(t,x,\mu)|\leq C_{4}\left(1+|x|+\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}|z|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}z)\Big)^{1/2}\right).

(
Control Set
)

The control space
U
is compact subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
.

(
Initial Distribution
)

The initial law
\lambda
admits a finite moment of order
q^{\prime}>2
, i.e.

\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
.

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big|\tilde{X}^{n}_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t}\big|^{2}\right)&\leq 2\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}\big|(\tilde{X}^{n}_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t})(b(t,\tilde{X}^{n}_{t},\mu^{n}_{t},u)-b(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\mu_{t},u))\big|\tilde{Q}_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}t\right)\\

(3.2)

By applying the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality, we obtain a constant

, such that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\Big|\int_{0}^{s}\int_{U}(\tilde{X}^{n}_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t})(\sigma(t,\tilde{X}^{n}_{t},\mu^{n}_{t},u)-\sigma(t,\tilde{X}_{t},\mu_{t},u))\tilde{M}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}u)\Big|\right)\\

(3.3)

By plugging (

) into (

), selecting a suitable

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big|\tilde{X}^{n}_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t}\big|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}\Big|(\tilde{X}^{n}_{s}-\tilde{X}_{s})(b(s,\tilde{X}^{n}_{s},\mu^{n}_{s},u)-b(s,\tilde{X}_{s},\mu_{s},u))\Big|\tilde{Q}_{s}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle+C\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}\bigl|\sigma(s,\tilde{X}^{n}_{s},\mu^{n}_{s},u)-\sigma(s,\tilde{X}_{s},\mu_{s},u)\big|^{2}\tilde{Q}_{s}(\mathrm{d}u)\mathrm{d}s\right)

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\tilde{X}^{n}_{t}-\tilde{X}_{t}|^{2}\right)\to 0.

(3.4)

Similarly, by using the fact that

we can show that

(3.5)

Define
P^{n}:=\mathbb{Q}\circ(\tilde{Q},\tilde{X}^{n},\tilde{K}^{n})^{-1}

) and (

) implies that

in

and by using Itô’s formula, we can verify that

.

Lemma 3.2

from

Let

, and thus

Lemma 3.3
.

, the mapping

We can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{n}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}^{P^{n}}\!\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}\!\big(f(t,X_{t},\mu^{n}_{t},u)-f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},u)\big)Q_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 45.52458pt+\,\mathbb{E}^{P^{n}}\!\Bigg[g(X_{T},\mu^{n}_{T})-g(X_{T},\mu_{T})+\int_{0}^{T}\big(h(t,X_{t},\mu^{n}_{t})-h(t,X_{t},\mu_{t})\big)\,\mathrm{d}K_{t}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}^{P}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},u)Q_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(X_{T},\mu_{T})+\int_{0}^{T}h(t,X_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}K_{t}\Bigg].

3.6

]

\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}f(t,x_{t},\mu_{t},u)\,q_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(x_{T},\mu_{T})+\int_{0}^{T}h(t,x_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}k_{t}\Big|\leq C\big(1+\|x\|_{T}^{2}+\|k\|_{T}^{2}\big).

By the Lipschitz-type dependence of

g
on the measure argument, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{P^{n}}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{U}|f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t}^{n},u)-f(t,X_{t},\mu_{t},u)|\,Q_{t}(\mathrm{d}u)\,\mathrm{d}t+|g(X_{T},\mu_{T}^{n})-g(X_{T},\mu_{T})|\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\big(1+F(W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t}^{n},\delta_{0}),W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t},\delta_{0}))\big)W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t}^{n},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t.

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\big(1+F(W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t}^{n},\delta_{0}),W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t},\delta_{0}))\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}t\Bigg)^{1/2}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{T}W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t}^{n},\mu_{t})^{2}\mathrm{d}t\Bigg)^{1/2}.

Since
\sup_{n}W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t}^{n},\delta_{0})<\infty
and

is locally bounded,
while Lemma
3.5
ensures that

,
the entire right-hand side converges to

.

Next, by the Lipschitz-type dependence of
h
on the measure argument, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{P^{n}}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}|h(t,X_{t},\mu_{t}^{n})-h(t,X_{t},\mu_{t})|\,\mathrm{d}K_{t}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\,\mathbb{E}^{P^{n}}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\big(1+F(W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t}^{n},\delta_{0}),W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t},\delta_{0}))\big)W_{\mathbb{R}^{+},2}(\mu_{t}^{n},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}K_{t}\Bigg].

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+F(W_{\mathcal{C}^{+},2}(\mu^{n},\delta_{0}),W_{\mathcal{C}^{+},2}(\mu,\delta_{0}))\big)W_{\mathcal{C}^{+},2}(\mu^{n},\mu)\,\mathbb{E}^{P^{n}}(K_{T}).

Since
\sup_{n}W_{\mathcal{C}^{+},2}(\mu^{n},\delta_{0})<\infty

and, by Step 2.1 of Lemma
3.2
,

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}^{P^{n}}(K_{T}^{q^{\prime}})^{1/q^{\prime}}<\infty
,
the right-hand side converges to
0
as

. Then
\mathcal{J}^{n}_{1}\to 0.

Finally, combining both limits yields

|J(\mu^{n},P^{n})-J(\mu,P)|\longrightarrow 0,

which establishes the continuity of
J
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

If
W_{\mathcal{C},p}(\mu^{n},\mu)\to 0
, then
\mu^{n}_{t}\to\mu_{t}
for every
t\in[0,T]
.

Indeed, since any continuous function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
|\varphi(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{p})
also satisfies

|\varphi(x_{t})|\leq C(1+|x_{t}|^{p})\leq C(1+\|x\|_{T}^{p}),\quad x\in\mathcal{C},

and the mapping
\mathcal{C}\ni x\mapsto\varphi(x_{t})\in\mathbb{R}

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
h:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}

be continuous and satisfy the linear growth condition

|h(t,x,\mu)|\leq C\big(1+|x|+\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)\big)^{1/2}\big),\qquad(t,x,\mu)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Then the functional

\Phi(x,k,\mu):=\int_{0}^{T}h(t,x_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}k_{t}

is continuous on

\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{A}([0,T];\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}))
,
where the first two spaces are equipped with the uniform topology
and the last with the Wasserstein topology.

Proof.

Let
(x^{n},k^{n},\mu^{n})\to(x,k,\mu)
uniformly on
[0,T]
.
Since
h
is continuous, we have for each
t\in[0,T]
,

h(t,x^{n}_{t},\mu^{n}_{t})\to h(t,x_{t},\mu_{t}).

By the Heine–Cantor theorem,
h
is uniformly continuous on compact subsets, hence

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|h(t,x^{n}_{t},\mu^{n}_{t})-h(t,x_{t},\mu_{t})|\to 0.

We can therefore write

\Phi(x^{n},k^{n},\mu^{n})-\Phi(x,k,\mu)=\int_{0}^{T}\big(h(t,x^{n}_{t},\mu^{n}_{t})-h(t,x_{t},\mu_{t})\big)\,\mathrm{d}k^{n}_{t}+\int_{0}^{T}h(t,x_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}(k^{n}_{t}-k_{t})=:I_{1}^{n}+I_{2}^{n}.

For the first term, we have

|I^{n}_{1}|\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|h(t,x^{n}_{t},\mu^{n}_{t})-h(t,x_{t},\mu_{t})|\,|k^{n}_{T}|.

Since
(k^{n})
converges uniformly to
k
, the sequence
(|k^{n}_{T}|)
is bounded, and hence
I^{n}_{1}\to 0
.

For the second term, observe that
h(\cdot,x_{\cdot},\mu_{\cdot})
is continuous on
[0,T]
, and therefore bounded.
Since
k^{n}\to k
uniformly, it follows from the convergence of Riemann–Stieltjes integrals that

\int_{0}^{T}h(t,x_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}k^{n}_{t}\longrightarrow\int_{0}^{T}h(t,x_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}k_{t},

that is,
I_{2}^{n}\to 0
.

Combining both terms, we obtain

\Phi(x^{n},k^{n},\mu^{n})\longrightarrow\Phi(x,k,\mu),

which establishes the desired continuity.
∎
