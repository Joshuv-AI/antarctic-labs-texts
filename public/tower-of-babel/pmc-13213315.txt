Cluster analysis identifies high-risk phenotypic groups for revision following 1–3 level lumbar fusion in a disadvantaged inner-city population
Authors: Regina Golding, Sachin Mehta, Priya Singh, Taikhoom Dahodwala, Arjun Gupta, Jessica Goldring, Ta’ir Rocker, Ahsan Ahmed, Anthony Terraciano, Hema Brijlall, Xiuyi Yang, Omair Qureshi, Nathan Hawley, Evan Olsen, Pamela Zuckerman, Andrew Gitkind, Daniel Berman, Ananth Eleswarapu, Jonathan Krystal, Mitchell S Fourman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe number of patients undergoing single- and multilevel lumbar fusion continues to rise. Although prior studies suggest that radiographic factors and preoperative patient-reported outcomes may predict surgical results, evidence regarding the predictive value of patient demographics on perioperative and postoperative outcomes remains limited. Using cluster analysis to define distinct demographic-based patient phenotypes, this study examines how patient demographics influence surgical outcomes in a disadvantaged, inner-city population.MethodsThis is a retrospective, single-center cohort study including 459 patients who underwent a 1–3 level lumbar fusion between 2022 and 2024. The primary outcome was revision surgery at maximum follow-up. A 2-step cluster analysis was performed using SPSS 30 (IBM). Comparisons were performed between clusters using Chi-squared test for categorical variables and a one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s posttest for continuous variables. Intra-cluster analysis was run using multivariate regression. p < .05 was considered significant.ResultsA total of 459 patients underwent 1–3 level lumbar fusion (mean age 62.1 ± 10.9 years; 69.0% female; mean body mass index (BMI) 31.3 ± 5.6), with 92.4% identifying as non-white and 29.3% as non-English speaking. Mean operative time was 286 minutes, blood loss 206 mL, and hospital stay 4.7 days. Within 90 days, 19.2% visited the ER, 10.2% were readmitted, and 4.1% returned to the operating room (OR); 5.5% of patients ultimately required revision fusions, and 12.1% remained on chronic opioids at final follow-up. Cluster analysis identified 3 patient subgroups differing significantly by age, language, race, insurance, substance use, psychiatric medication, and prior laminectomy (all p < .001). Cluster 1 (younger, psychiatric/substance use) had higher revision rates (9.1% vs. 2.0–3.1%, p = .01).ConclusionsDistinct patient groups show differing lumbar fusion risks; younger patients with psychiatric and substance-use factors face higher revision rates and potentially worse recovery.Keywords: Cluster analysis, Lumbar fusion, Patient demographics, Revision surgery, Social determinants of health, Spine surgery

Introduction

The number of lumbar fusions performed yearly in the treatment of stenosis has increased several-fold over the past decade [1,2]. There are myriad open and minimally invasive lumbar fusion approaches described. The approach of choice varies based on underlying pathology, symptoms, location, bone density, surgeon preference, and geography [1,3]. Despite notable advances in surgical technology, indication, and approach, lumbar fusion surgery has some of the highest rates of patient dissatisfaction, and 1-in-5 patients who undergo a lumbar fusion will require revision surgery [4,5].

Evidence on the prognostic indicators for a successful lumbar fusion surgery outcome is limited, with notable disagreement within the literature. Several reports have associated greater preoperative pain with lower postoperative satisfaction while others have shown the opposite [6,7]. Psychosocial factors, such as depression and opioid use, have been associated with worse postoperative outcomes [8,9]. When comparing minimally invasive and open lumbar fusions, prior studies have shown little difference in long term outcomes such as reported patient satisfaction and reported pain. However, minimally invasive techniques have been associated with less blood loss, a shorter hospital length of stay, and less patient-reported pain [10,11]. While tobacco use, age over 60 years, and elevated BMI have previously been associated with an increased risk of revision surgery, findings remain inconsistent [[12], [13], [14]]. For example, tobacco use has been shown to increase the rate of pseudoarthrosis in multilevel fusion procedures, but not in single-level fusions [15]. Similarly, when age was stratified into 3 groups (55–64 years, 65–74 years, and ≥ 75 years), no significant differences were observed in all-cause reoperation or revision surgery rates among the groups [16]. A systematic review of surgical risk in spine surgery found mixed results for BMI, with twelve studies showing no association with revision surgery and 4 demonstrating a correlation [17].

Surgical outcomes are likely influenced in a multi-factorial manner. Groups of patient factors that are often found in concert––a patient phenotype––may identify an “at-risk” surgical population. Cluster analysis is an increasingly popular way to group individuals by age, sex, demographics and comorbidities, permitting study of these factors as they naturally occur within a patient population [18]. While cluster analysis has been successfully used to evaluate spine surgery outcomes in patients with metastatic cancer, works that used cluster analysis in degenerative spine surgery have largely focused on radiographic factors [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. This study uses cluster analysis to evaluate how demographic and preoperative patient characteristics influence perioperative and postoperative outcomes following an elective 1–3 level lumbar fusion for stenosis.

Methods

This retrospective, single-center study included patients older than 18 years of age who underwent a 1–3 level lumbar fusion between 2022–2024 with a minimum 6-month follow-up. No industry or external funding supported this study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board with the need for written informed consent waived.

All data were extracted from the electronic health record using 2 reviewers to mitigate potential bias and inaccuracies. All patients over 18 years of age underwent an elective 1–3 level lumbar fusion from January 1, 2022 to December 12, 2024 were included. Exclusion criteria included patients who underwent emergent surgery, had lumbar fusions for trauma, tumors or infections, or had missing data. A total of 3 patients out of 462 were excluded due to missing data in any collected categories. Data collection from the electronic health record included demographic data, social determinants of health such as race, insurance status, smoking history, cannabis use history, psychiatric history, comorbidities as measured with the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), prior surgical history, and surgical characteristics such as approach, number of levels fused, intraoperative complications, intraoperative transfusion, estimated blood loss (EBL), and operative duration [22]. Race was self-reported and defined using the United States Census race categories. Insurance status was defined as commercial, Medicaid, Medicare Advantage, and Medicare plans A and B. Tobacco, cannabis, and opioid use were self-reported or obtained from prescription records. Due to Institutional Review Board (IRB) restrictions, inclusion of objective urine/serum drug screen data was prohibited. The primary outcome was revision surgery. Secondary outcomes were hospitalization length of stay, non-home discharge, 90-day return to operating room (OR), 90-day emergency room (ER) visits, 90-day readmission, persistent radiculopathy or active pain management at max follow-up, and chronic opioid use at max follow-up.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 30.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). A 2-step unsupervised cluster analysis was performed using age at OR, body mass index (BMI), sex, insurance status, tobacco use, cannabis use, psychiatric history, CCI, and prior laminectomy.

Intra-cluster analysis was performed with logistic regression that included age at OR, BMI, sex, insurance status, cannabis use, psychiatric medication use, Charlson comorbidity index, and prior laminectomy.

Differences between clusters in perioperative and postoperative outcomes, including the primary and all secondary outcomes, were then compared using the chi-squared test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-test, as appropriate. p values < .05 were considered significant.

Results

A total of 459 patients underwent a 1–3 level lumbar fusion during the study period. Cohort characteristics are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 62.1 ± 10.9 years (Median: 62.7 years, 95% CI 61.1–63.1), 316 of 459 (69.0%) patients were female, and the mean body mass index (BMI) at the time of surgery was 31.3 ± 5.6 (median 31, 95% CI 30.8–31.9). The population was overwhelmingly non-white (423/459, 92.4%), with 149 (32.4%) of patients identifying as Black or African American, 3 (0.7%) identifying as American Indian or Alaska Native, 7 (1.5%) identifying as Asian, and 244 (53.2%) identifying as Other. In order to provide additional granularity to the “Other” category, we attempted to interrogate ethnicity data and found that 92.6% identified as Spanish/Hispanic/Latino, meaning that the “Other” race category effectively represents patients of Spanish/Hispanic/Latino heritage. One-hundred-thirty-four (29.3%) patients were non-English speaking, 153 (33.4%) received Medicaid, and 256 (55.9%) received Medicare. Seventy-three (15.9%) and 44 (9.6%) patients were active tobacco or cannabis users, respectively. The mean age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity index was 3.8 ± 2.0 (median 3, 95% CI: 3.6–4.0), and 78 (17.0%) patients had a prior laminectomy.

Mean length of surgery was 286 minutes (median: 266, 95% CI: 276–297) and mean EBL was 206 mL (median: 200, 95% CI: 187–225). Thirty-one (6.8%) patients had intraoperative complications, including 5 vascular injuries (related to an anterior approach), 12 dural tears, 2 navigation failures, 8 altered surgical plans, and 3 neurologic deficits. The mean hospital stay was 4.7 days (median: 4, 95% CI: 4.4–5.0). Ninety-nine (21.6%) patients were discharged to a skilled nursing facility (SNF) or acute rehab. Within 90 days of surgery, 88 patients (19.2%) visited the ER, 47 (10.2%) were readmitted, and 19 (4.1%) returned to the operating room. Twenty-five (5.5%) patients required revision fusion surgery > 90 days post-surgery, 119 (26.0%) returned to pain management following their surgery, and 55 (12.1%) remained on chronic opioids at maximum follow-up. Mean follow-up was 8.9 ± 8.8 months (0.03–3.23 years).

Cluster analysis yielded 3 cohorts (n = 99–198). Groups were termed based upon their primary classification variables: 1 – younger, psychiatric medications and substance use; 2 – older, non-English speaking; and 3 – non-white patients on commercial insurance (Fig. 1).

Cluster analysis of baseline characteristics. Heatmap of standardized demographic, social, and perioperative variables across three clusters. Red indicates higher values and blue indicates lower values. Variables are grouped using hierarchical clustering.

Clusters differed in age (60.1 vs. 65.6 vs. 62.4, p < .001), non-English primary language (18% vs. 100% vs. 0%, p < .001), race (p < .001), insurance status (p < .001), preoperative opioid use (p < .001), cannabis use (p < .001), tobacco use (p < .001), psychiatric medication use (p < .001), and history of a prior laminectomy (p < .001). Clusters did not differ with regard to BMI, New York state ADI, national ADI, sex, and CCI.

Cluster 1 was significantly more likely to require revision surgery (9.1% vs. 2.0% vs. 3.1%, p = .01). Cluster 1 also trended towards increased 90 d return to OR (12.1% vs. 9.1% vs. 3.1%, p = .053) and persistent opioid use (16.2% vs. 9.1% vs. 8.6%, p = .056). Clusters did not differ with regard to EBL, operative duration, intraoperative complications, intraoperative transfusions, non-home discharge, hospital LOS, 90 days readmission, 90 days ER visit, persistent pain, and time to follow-up (Table 2).

Within cluster 1, intraoperative transfusion was independently associated with age at OR (β-coefficient: 0.9, 95% CI 0.84–0.99) and antipsychotic use (OR: 10.3, 95% CI 1.24–85.66). Non-home discharge was associated with age (β-coefficient: 1.05, 95% CI 1.01–1.1) and preoperative opioid use (OR: 2.8, 95% CI 1.28–6.15). Cannabis use was protective of nonhome discharge (OR 0.2, 95% OR: 0.05–0.995). Ninety-day readmission and 90-day ER visit were associated with preoperative opioid use (OR: 3.05, 95% CI 1.28–7.28 and OR: 4.1, 95% CI 2.00–8.40 respectively). Antidepressant use was protective from persistent pain (OR 0.48, 95% CI 0.239–0.967). Persistent opioid use was associated with BMI (β-coefficient 1.15, 95% CI 1.06–1.23).

Within cluster 2, Medicaid (OR 0.03, 95% CI 0.002–0.32) and Medicare advantage (OR 0.04, 95% CI 0.005–0.37) were protective of intraoperative complication. Age was associated with non-home discharge (β-coefficient 1.08, 95% CI 1.02–1.14). Persistent opioid use was associated with antipsychotic use (OR 8.44, 95% CI 1.17–60.72).

Within cluster 3, nonhome discharge was associated with age (β-coefficient 1.05, 95% CI 1.005–1.10) and CCI (β-coefficient 1.46, 95% CI 1.14–1.868). Female sex was protective of 90-day reoperation (OR 0.08, 95% CI 0.01–0.75) and persistent radicular pain (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.20–0.90). Persistent opioid use was associated with BMI (β-coefficient 1.13, 95% CI 1.04–1.23) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Optimizing lumbar fusion outcomes has traditionally relied on population-based studies, which often aggregate patients with diverse characteristics and may obscure clinically meaningful subgroups. Cluster analysis permits the identification of common patient phenotypes, allowing us to identify “at-risk” groups that are found within our patient population. A cluster analysis of patients who underwent a 1–3 level lumbar fusion for degenerative stenosis at a large, inner-city academic tertiary referral center identified phenotypic clusters of younger patients who smoke, non–English-speaking patients, and patients with commercial insurance. Younger patients with a more extensive history of pain who smoke tobacco and cannabis have an increased risk of revision surgery, with trends toward 90-day return to the OR and persistent opioid use.

Cluster 1 presented for surgery at a younger age and in worse pain, as indicated by higher rates of preoperative opioid use, and had higher rates of depression, as indicated by greater antidepressant use. Although they are younger, the majority are on government insurance. They are also more likely to smoke tobacco, use cannabis, and be prescribed preoperative opioids and psychiatric medications. This cluster was associated with higher rates of revision surgery.

On intra-cluster analysis, some of the key features of Cluster 1—tobacco use, prescribed preoperative opioid use, and psychiatric medication use—were independently associated with revision surgery. Smoking is well known to alter gene expression at the fusion, leading to worse bone healing and fusion, resulting in increased risk for pseudoarthrosis and revision surgery [12,23,24]. Cannabis use has also been associated with increased risk of reoperation within 3 years, although the exact biology underlying this phenomenon is less well-understood. D’Antonio et al. [25] speculated it may be due pain or adjacent level disease or surgeons wanting to reduce cannabis pain management. Furthermore, psychiatric disorders have been associated with increased odds of revision surgery after lumbar fusion; however, the reasons for this are unclear [26]. Preoperative opioid use is likely due to uncontrolled preoperative pain, increased opioid tolerance/dependence, or increased pain sensitivity via opioid-induced hyperalgesia and has previously been shown to be associated with higher perioperative complication rates and revision surgery [[27], [28], [29]]. Opioids have also been associated with immunomodulation leading to altered bone healing, osteoblast inhibition, and potential downregulation of VEGF leading to poorer healing and increased rates of revision surgery [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. Cluster 1 was also younger. While our intra-cluster analysis did not show age as an independent risk factor for revision surgery, other studies have associated younger age at the time of surgery is associated with an increased risk of revision surgery [32]. Not only was this cluster younger, but it also had a much higher rate of prior laminectomy surgery. Revision lumbar fusions have been associated with worse outcomes and increased rates of future revision surgery [33].

No patient in cluster 2 spoke English as their primary language; however, cluster 2 was not at increased risk for any adverse outcomes. This differs from prior findings. A 2024 systematic review associated low English proficiency with a longer hospital length of stay and 30-day venous thrombosis after joint replacement, while a 2023 systematic review showed those with lower levels of English proficiency were more likely to experience a nonhome discharge [34,35]. Professional interpreter services and language-specific education have been shown to improve outcomes in non-English speaking patients [36]. In a cohort where over a quarter of patients did not speak English, surgeon experience with professional interpreters may explain why limited English proficiency had little impact on outcomes. However, this merits further investigation.

Black patients composed the majority of cluster 3, which also had the most patients with commercial insurance. Numerous studies emphasize race as a poor predictor of outcomes in healthcare [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. In other studies, black patients, compared to white patients, were more likely to experience post-operative complications, increased hospital LOS, and 30- and 90-day readmission [37]. In this study, cluster 3 was not at increased risk for adverse surgical outcomes compared to the other clusters, and race was not independently associated with any adverse events in the intra-cluster analysis.

The present work created clusters based on demographic data, which is unique in the literature. Prior studies use radiologic data such as severity of decompression, central canal area, and preoperative alignment [18,20,21]. While this data is easily collected and objective, differences were rarely clinically significant based on patient reported outcomes and pain scores [20,41]. Cluster analysis based on preoperative pain showed an association between high preoperative pain and low surgical satisfaction [6]. Within orthopedics, there is a paucity of clustering on demographic data, with literature preferring univariant analysis. However, univariant analysis risks missing the influence of nonlinear and interconnected variables, including social determinants of health [42,43]. This project is unique as it utilizes cluster analysis based on demographic and social data in a disadvantaged population. Additionally, its large sample size and relatively recent surgeries reduce the risk of bias as a result of newer surgical techniques.

This project has several limitations beyond those intrinsic to retrospective reviews. First, our data is collected from a single center that largely serve a non-white, immigrant, socioeconomically disadvantaged population with limited generalizability to more affluent, rural populations. This study did not discern surgical approach or the use of MIS techniques which may mitigate the increased risk profile of our older obese patients. We also did not include formal patient-reported outcome metrics, revision surgery, opioid consumption, and re-engagement with pain management as surrogates for an unsatisfactory outcome. Further research will include validated metrics for patient satisfaction, pain, and frailty in addition to radiographic measurements of sagittal alignment and sarcopenia. Despite these limitations, we believe that our unique look at an inner-city, multimorbid population still allows us to derive value from these findings.

Conclusion

Cluster analysis revealed 3 distinct patient phenotypes with markedly different risk profiles. The younger, psychiatric medication/substance-use cluster demonstrated significantly higher revision rates and trends toward worse postoperative outcomes, underscoring the importance of psychosocial and behavioral factors in lumbar fusion recovery. In light of these findings, we recommend early, preoperative, multidisciplinary collaboration with psychiatrists and substance abuse specialists—through psychotropic medication reconciliation, substance use counseling, and substance replacement therapy—to optimize patients for spine surgery and maximize surgical outcomes.
