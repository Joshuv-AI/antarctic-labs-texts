History of Presentation

A 34-year-old primigravida woman (G1P0) with a history of hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was referred at 16 weeks' gestation from an outside facility to the cardio-obstetrics clinic after routine prenatal laboratory testing revealed severe HTG. She was asymptomatic and denied alcohol use. Physical examination was notable only for obesity and a gravid abdomen; vital signs and cardiopulmonary examination were unremarkable.

Past Medical History

Her medical history was significant for T2DM treated with metformin and dyslipidemia treated with fenofibrate before pregnancy. She had no personal history of pancreatitis. She had no prior pregnancies and no known family history of dyslipidemia or premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Investigations

Initial prenatal laboratory work-up was remarkable for a total cholesterol of 176 mg/dL, triglycerides (TG) of 1,562 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) of 23 mg/dL, and an unreportable low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) level given marked triglyceride elevation. Hemoglobin A1c was 6.5%. Liver function tests and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio were within normal limits. Thyroid function testing was within normal limits for gestational age.

Obstetric ultrasound performed at 8 weeks 2 days and again at 12 weeks 6 days confirmed a viable singleton pregnancy with normal amniotic fluid volume and no structural abnormalities. Maternal complete transthoracic echocardiogram showed normal biventricular function with no regional wall motion abnormalities.

Management

The patient was managed collaboratively by a multidisciplinary team including maternal-fetal medicine, cardio-obstetrics, a registered dietitian, and endocrinology. Given TG levels >1,500 mg/dL and the associated risk of acute pancreatitis during pregnancy, a strategy focused on rapid TG reduction while minimizing fetal risk was implemented. To optimize glycemic control in the setting of concurrent T2DM and reduce TG levels, metformin was discontinued and replaced with a basal bolus insulin regimen using insulin glargine and insulin lispro, titrated to pregnancy-specific fasting and postprandial glucose targets (Figure 1C). Fenofibrate was discontinued because of limited safety data in early pregnancy. TG-lowering therapy was initiated with omega-3 acid ethyl esters and supplemented with over-the-counter fish oil. In parallel, the patient was placed on a very-low-fat diet, restricting fat intake to approximately 10% to 15% of total daily calories and total carbohydrate intake to <50 g/d. She received structured nutritional counseling by a dietitian, including individualized meal planning and education through each trimester.

Serial monitoring of lipid levels was performed every 6 weeks, with glycemic indices (hemoglobin A1c) assessed each trimester. Given the patient's history of T2DM, a detailed fetal anatomy ultrasound and fetal echocardiogram were scheduled at 19 weeks' gestation to evaluate fetal growth and cardiac development. Genetic testing for inherited lipid disorders was discussed but declined.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient progressed through the second and third trimesters without maternal complications. She was evaluated with biweekly high-risk obstetrics visits and regular endocrinology and dietitian follow-up, with trimester-based evaluations by cardiology and nutrition. TG levels improved markedly within weeks of initiating insulin therapy, omega-3 acid ethyl esters, and dietary modification, with sustained improvement over 4 months (Figure 1A). Follow-up lipid testing demonstrated total cholesterol of 169 mg/dL, HDL-C of 40 mg/dL, TG of 287 mg/dL, and LDL-C of 72 mg/dL. Glycemic control improved, with an A1c of 4.9% with no episodes of hypoglycemia. The patient maintained a healthy level of weight gain of 30 pounds (13.6 kg) over gestation with structured nutritional guidance.

A fetal anatomy ultrasound and fetal echocardiogram performed at 19 weeks' gestation identified tetralogy of Fallot with appropriate growth and amniotic fluid. The patient was planned for vaginal delivery. However, at 33 weeks, she developed superimposed preeclampsia with severe features, and caesarean delivery was recommended given nonreassuring fetal status. Her delivery was complicated by postpartum hemorrhage, placental abruption, and disseminated intravascular coagulation requiring blood transfusions. Omega-3 ethyl esters were held during admission because of an associated increased risk of postpartum hemorrhage when taken close to delivery.2 The patient was discharged on postpartum day 3 without further complications.

Discussion

Severe HTG during pregnancy is clinically significant, as triglyceride levels exceeding 500 to 1,000 mg/dL are associated with increased risks of acute pancreatitis, preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and adverse maternal and fetal outcomes.3 Pregnancy-related insulin resistance, hormonal changes, and reduced lipoprotein lipase activity can exacerbate HTG, particularly in women with preexisting diabetes or metabolic disease.3, 4, 5 Additionally, modifiable lifestyle factors such as high-fat diets, alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity contribute to the development and severity of HTG during pregnancy.6

Physiologic changes in lipid metabolism occur throughout pregnancy. Early gestation is characterized by an anabolic state that facilitates energy storage in preparation for later gestational demands.4 Rising levels of estrogen and progesterone stimulate hepatic TG and cholesterol synthesis. As pregnancy progresses, a metabolic shift toward catabolism occurs because of maternal insulin resistance and elevations in human placental lactogen, resulting in increased lipolysis in adipocytes. HTG during pregnancy arises because of both increased production and decreased clearance of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins due to increased insulin resistance, increases in estrogen, and decreases in lipoprotein lipase activity, with secondary increases in circulating LDL due to very-low-density lipoprotein metabolism.1 Although TG levels may rise 2-to 3-fold during pregnancy, they typically remain below 300 mg/dL by the third trimester in uncomplicated pregnancies.7 Levels significantly exceeding 300 mg/dL suggest superimposed pathology and warrant prompt evaluation.

Given these physiological changes, preconception or early pregnancy screening for dyslipidemia is recommended by the National Lipid Association to identify women at risk.8 Early identification allows for timely lifestyle modification and close monitoring, which may reduce the risk of adverse maternal and fetal complications.9 Management of HTG in pregnancy is challenging and requires careful balancing of maternal-fetal nutritional needs with the risk of metabolic complications. Lifestyle modification, particularly dietary changes, remains the cornerstone of therapy. The National Lipid Association recommends a very-low-fat diet when TG levels exceed 1,000 mg/dL, as they did in this case. This dietary approach, typically limiting fat intake to 10% to 15% of total daily calories, can result in substantial reductions in circulating TG levels within weeks through reduced intestinal chylomicron formation, but requires close dietary supervision to ensure adequate intake of essential fatty acids and micronutrients during pregnancy. Dietitian-guided nutritional therapy is essential during pregnancy and postpartum to ensure adherence to a very-low-fat diet while maintaining adequate nutrition. Beyond dietary changes, current guidelines recommend 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity for women during pregnancy to improve insulin sensitivity.

Pharmacologic options for TG lowering during pregnancy remain limited given concerns regarding fetal safety. Statins are not routinely recommended during pregnancy, although emerging observational data suggest a low risk of teratogenicity.10 Omega-3 fatty acids are generally considered safe and may be used to lower TG levels in severe cases, although these agents have limited testing in human pregnancy.2 Fibrates may be considered selectively during the second trimester when maternal benefit outweighs potential fetal risk, although supporting data are limited and practice patterns vary.6 Commonly used TG-lowering agents during pregnancy are summarized in Figure 1B.

This case highlights the importance of early recognition and multidisciplinary management of severe HTG in pregnancy as summarized in Figure 2. Despite TG levels exceeding 1,500 mg/dL, outpatient management with close surveillance was achieved, and an early referral to a coordinated cardio-obstetric care team enabled individualized decision-making. Although fetal and maternal complications developed in this case, coordinated multidisciplinary care allowed for rapid reduction of TG and careful monitoring throughout the pregnancy. Case reports indicate that such coordinated care can lead to rapid reduction in TG levels and improved outcomes in patients with HTG-related complications, including pancreatitis.6,11

There are currently no standardized guidelines regarding timing of delivery or postpartum lipid management in patients with severe HTG. Decisions regarding delivery timing should be individualized, considering maternal metabolic stability, fetal maturity, and obstetric considerations.6 Postpartum follow-up is essential, as women with pregnancy-related metabolic complications are at increased risk for persistent dyslipidemia, diabetes, and future cardiovascular disease,5 and thus a long-term cardiology follow-up plan should be in place after delivery to ensure ongoing monitoring.

Conclusions

This case highlights the challenges associated with managing severe HTG during pregnancy in a patient with concomitant type 2 diabetes mellitus, and it underscores the importance of multidisciplinary management and early identification, aggressive dietary intervention, and individualized management strategies including insulin therapy and selective use of pharmacologic triglyceride-lowering agents. Given the potential for serious maternal and fetal complications, formal dietary consultation, consideration of pharmacologic therapy in select cases, and close monitoring throughout the perinatal period are essential. Preconception counseling and structured postpartum follow-up are also critical to mitigate long-term cardiovascular risk and support maternal health beyond pregnancy.
