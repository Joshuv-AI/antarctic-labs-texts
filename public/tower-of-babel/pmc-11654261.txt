Access and coverage of malaria services in Haiti in the context of elimination: a scoping review of the literature
Authors: Kenny Moise, Aude Melody Achille, Roubens Duvilaire, Jimmy Fedna, Roody Thermidor, Bertiny Bourdeau, Jessica Maureen Nestor, Gérald Lerebours, Jean Hugues Henrys, Christian Raccurt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHaiti is on the verge of possibly eliminating malaria, an endemic parasitic infection primarily caused by Plasmodium falciparum on the island of Hispaniola. Owing to its associated morbidity and mortality, malaria is a leading public health priority in Haiti. This scoping review aims to identify the scope of research on access and coverage of malaria surveillance, diagnosis, and treatment in Haiti in the elimination setting.MethodsWe compiled, analyzed, and synthesized the literature on access and coverage of antimalarial services in Haiti in the elimination setting based on Arksey and O’Malley’s framework for scoping reviews and held a formal meeting with the PNCM leadership and stakeholders of malaria programs in Haiti. Results were reported following PRISMA-ScR guidelines.ResultsWe found 376 citations and five technical reports related to our keywords from 2016 to 2023 and retained 225 after eliminating duplicates. Based on our selection criteria, we retrieved 81 citations and excluded 56 after in-depth readings. Therefore, the review includes 26 articles and five technical reports. The Ministry of Health established a strategic plan covering the 2021–2030 period, providing a framework for operational planning to increase efficiency, strengthen the health system, and ensure the sustainability of interventions. However, the current weaknesses in surveillance underline a lack of well-trained personnel, difficulty in establishing the epidemiological profile of malaria, unreliability, lack of completeness of data, and delay in their transmission. In addition, access to malaria diagnosis and treatment in Haiti is limited due to a lack of available state-of-the-art testing modalities, variations in the geographic availability of rapid diagnostic tests and microscopy, and the widespread use of substandard antimicrobial drugs.ConclusionResearch and reports on malaria surveillance, diagnosis, and treatment access and coverage in Haiti are limited; however, the available sources reveal significant shortcomings and challenges in implementing WHO-recommended policies and strategies for malaria elimination. Future studies should aim to fill this gap and investigate the determinants of the persistence of malaria cases in certain municipalities and departments of the country.Keywords: Malaria, Elimination, Haiti, Surveillance, Diagnostic, Antimicrobial drugs

Introduction

Malaria, a parasitic infection caused by various types of Plasmodium, a genus of unicellular eukaryotes including falciparum, vivax, ovale, malariae, and knowlesi, remains a significant global health challenge [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates, approximately 249 million people worldwide were affected by malaria from all types of Plasmodium in 2022, resulting in 608,000 deaths [1]. In that year, Haiti was on the verge of eliminating malaria, primarily caused by Plasmodium falciparum, on the island of Hispaniola (encompassing Haiti and the Dominican Republic) [2]. The prevalence of P. falciparum parasitemia is the highest in coastal Southeastern localities and shows significant variations, ranging from 0–34.5% [3]. In general, and Haiti in particular, women and children under five years old are the most vulnerable to complications of the disease, including cerebral malaria and multiorgan failure [4]. As a result, malaria is one of Haiti’s top ten causes of morbidity and mortality, making it a leading national public health priority [5].

Haiti’s eradication efforts date back to the global malaria eradication campaign of the 1950s and 1960s when nationwide case positivity rates reached 0.2% [2]. However, in the context of major political shifts in Haiti and internationally, funding shortfalls caused by the decline in US aid through the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) in 1970 undermined the achievement of expected results [6, 7].

In 2005, the Ministère de la Santé Publique et de la Population/ MSPP (Haitian Ministry of Health/ MoH) launched the Programme National de Contrôle de la Malaria/PNCM (National Malaria Control Program), replacing the Service National d’Eradication de la Malaria/SNEM (National Malaria Eradication Service), which operated from 1961 to 1988 when it was dismantled [2].

Haiti aims to eliminate malaria by meeting WHO’s criteria of an absence of outbreaks of at least three linked indigenous malaria cases due to local transmission by mosquitoes nationally for three consecutive years [8]. To reach this goal, the Plan Stratégique National d’Elimination de la Malaria (National Strategic Plan for Eliminating Malaria in Haiti) was introduced by the PNCM in 2016 [7, 9]. Besides vector control, the plan revolves around community and institutional management of malaria and strengthening the health system through epidemiological surveillance. Beforehand, a study in 2013 established the feasibility of eliminating malaria on the island of Hispaniola by 2020 based on studies of technical aspects considering ecological, operational, and financial parameters [7]. Assessing current coverage and access to services would make measuring progress or delays concerning the strategic plan’s objectives possible. This scoping review aims to identify and synthesize the existing research on access to services and coverage of antimalarial services in Haiti in the elimination setting while identifying key areas where further research is needed. Our specific objectives were to identify the scope of research on the access and coverage of malaria surveillance, diagnosis, and treatment. These objectives laid the foundations for understanding the functioning of the national healthcare system and the fight against malaria in Haiti.

Methods

We conducted a scoping review based on a framework established by Arksey and O’Malley [10]. Our review followed the six specific stages proposed in the framework: (a) identification of the research question, (b) identification of relevant studies, (c) selection of studies, (d) data charting, (e) collection, summary, and report of results, and (f) consultation. We accomplished the latest stage in coordination with the leadership of the PNCM.

Furthermore, we used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines to identify critical items to report [11].

We defined access as promptly obtaining necessary, affordable, convenient, acceptable, and effective personal health services [12]. However, coverage is intended as resource allocation, as suggested by Tanahashi [13].

Specific objectives

Our specific objectives were to identify the scope of research on the access and coverage of malaria surveillance, diagnosis, and treatment. These objectives laid the foundations for understanding the functioning of the national healthcare system and the fight against malaria in Haiti.

Search strategies

We searched MEDLINE-PubMed, Google Scholar, and the grey literature, namely the MoH, WHO, and USAID reports, for articles and technical reports published in English and French. We last searched these databases in December 2023 for studies and reports conducted in Haiti from 2016 to 2023. We considered the following combinations of keywords based on their relevance to our review objectives: “Haiti[AND]Malaria,” “Haiti[AND]malaria[AND]surveillance,” “Haiti[AND]malaria[AND]coverage[AND]access.”

Selection criteria

An initial screening was made based on the articles’ titles related to our objectives. After reading the abstracts, reports, and studies conducted between 2016 and 2023 in Haiti were selected to reflect the studies’ timeliness and relevance to local researchers and the PNCM. We considered ecological, retrospective, and prospective observational studies and randomized control trials. Furthermore, we only included technical reports published on official governments’ and international non-governmental organizations’ websites.

We excluded the “vector control” topic because a previous systematic review published in 2016, which was included in our selection, covered it [2]. In addition, we excluded studies conducted in high-income countries.

The selection and thorough reading of articles were performed independently by four researchers.

Data abstraction

We developed a data abstraction tool and included eight items: publication year, first authors, title, aims, methods, and critical findings relevant to our review. Study data were then entered into an electronic spreadsheet using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, 2018). We excluded no article during data abstraction.

Data analysis

Following our review objectives, we synthesized and reported the collected data in the context of the three themes of the WHO T3 strategy for malaria: diagnosis, treatment, and surveillance. We followed the recommendations of Levac and colleagues for organizing and reporting study findings for scoping reviews [14].

Consultation

After collecting, summarizing, and reporting data, the authors held a formal meeting with the PNCM leadership and shareholders of malaria programs in Haiti. The objective of the consultation was to share the preliminary findings of our review and provide guidance on how the results will be integrated into the assessment of access to and coverage of malaria services in Haiti.

Results

We found 376 citations from PubMed and Google Scholar and five technical reports from the gray literature related to our keywords and retained 225 after eliminating duplicates. Based on our selection criteria, we retrieved 81 citations and excluded 56 after in-depth readings. Therefore, the review includes 26 articles and five technical reports, as detailed in Fig. 1.

General information on the healthcare system

Since the Alma-Ata conference in 1978, Haiti has adopted “Primary Health Care Strategies” with a three-tiered organizational model: primary level (community health centers, health centers, and reference-level community hospitals), departmental hospitals at the second level, and university hospitals at the tertiary level [9]. The organization and delivery of services are guided by an essential service package at each institutional level. The system relies heavily on external cooperation for financial support. Challenges include resource disparities, low service utilization, frequent stock-outs, absence of a referral system, and insufficient community involvement in health service management [9]. Despite various awareness campaigns by the MoH regarding the dangers of counterfeit drugs, self-medication remains common due to limited access to high-quality medications [15]. Therefore, the MoH established a strategic plan for community health covering the 2021–2030 period, providing a framework for operational planning to increase efficiency, strengthen the health system, and ensure the sustainability of interventions.

Malaria in Haiti

Malaria is endemic in Haiti and is primarily caused by P. falciparum, similar to West African and South American countries. There is a negligible presence of Plasmodium malariae and Plasmodium vivax; the latter was detected by rapid screening using multiplex antigen and antibody detection in the Southwestern region in a context of low transmission and near-elimination [16, 17]. The lack of P. vivax is thought to be due to a high prevalence of the FYES allele, limiting the expression of the Duffy antigen and susceptibility to P. vivax [18].

It follows a seasonal pattern of two rainy-season transmission peaks, influenced by natural hazards and migration patterns near the Dominican Republic border [2, 19]. Its incidence increased in 2010 following the January earthquake, declining since 2011, accelerated by elimination efforts [20]. Between 2014 and 2019, most cases were reported in the Southern peninsula and the Center department in urban and rural areas [20]. Furthermore, coastal areas of the southeastern department had a variable prevalence of P. falciparum parasitemia; however, no cases were detected in localities situated at 600 m [3].

Malaria reaches suburban regions of Haiti’s megalopolis, Port-au-Prince. Environmental factors such as a topographic wetness index, aridity index, elevation, slope, distance to water, evapotranspiration, and accessibility to cities are positively or negatively correlated to the annual malaria incidence rate, depending on the region of the country [21].

Surveillance of malaria in Haiti

The 1990s represented a significant turning point in the global fight against malaria. In 1998, WHO launched the Roll Back Malaria initiative, which led to the Abuja Declaration on Roll Back Malaria in Africa in 2000 [22]. This declaration set ambitious objectives, including halving the malaria mortality in Africa by 2010, ensuring that at least 60% of people suffering from malaria have access to affordable and appropriate treatment within 24 h of the onset of symptoms, providing personal and community protective measures such as insecticide-treated mosquito nets to at least 60% of those at risk, particularly pregnant women and children under five years old, and providing at least 60% of all pregnant women at risk of malaria with access to chemoprophylaxis or presumptive intermittent treatment. According to WHO’s World Malaria Report, malaria incidence declined by 37% while mortality decreased by 60% in 2015 [23]. Furthermore, death rates have decreased by 65% among children under five years old. Between 2000 and 2015, global malaria incidence stabilized, achieving Target 6 C of the Millennium Development Goals [23]. Encouraged by such results, an international technical strategy was launched by WHO in May 2015, providing new and more ambitious targets by 2030, such as achieving at least a 90% reduction in malaria incidence and mortality rates globally compared to 2015, the elimination of malaria from at least 35 countries in which malaria was transmitted in 2015, including Haiti, and the prevention of malaria re-establishment in malaria-free countries [4, 8].

WHO initiated the T3 strategy (Test. Treat. Track) in 2012 to improve malaria diagnosis and treatment access and intensify epidemiological surveillance [24]. The latter implies evaluating the incidence (immediate notification of symptomatic and asymptomatic cases) and malaria-related mortality. Furthermore, it should assess the temporal evolution of malaria and, more precisely, the resurgences, geographic areas, groups of most affected individuals, and the historical and ecological context [24].

Epidemiological surveillance in Haiti follows different stages: case notification, epidemiologic investigation, individual case management and local response, and information processing and dissemination [25].

Diseases and unusual conditions are reported at both community and institutional levels. At the community level, citizens, leaders, organizations, and health workers relay information informally. At the level of health institutions, specific forms are used by epidemiologic surveillance officers, healthcare providers, and laboratory personnel to notify based on established definitions for probable, confirmed, and excluded cases. Notification records are sent weekly from local health institutions to health departments and from health departments to the central level, with local transport or the Internet facilitating communication. However, notifications are immediate for relevant epidemiological alerts, including confirmed malaria cases and deaths. Surveillance data is entered into an electronic platform, allowing direct reporting at the departmental and central levels, mainly by epidemiologists, surveillance officers, program managers, and the centrally based malaria surveillance coordinator. Data quality is first ensured at the departmental and then at the national level by measuring pre-defined indicators such as the regularity of notifications, compliance with policies and procedures, analysis of the coherence and internal consistency of the data with those from other sources, adequate shipment of specimens to laboratories, etc [25].

According to the Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030, countries aiming to eliminate malaria should investigate individual cases or clusters to understand risk factors and eliminate transmission areas while incorporating data collected from the private sector [8]. In Haiti, malaria case investigation begins in the residence of the first case detected and involves active case detection in a 200 m radius around the index case [25]. An investigation form is completed to identify cases actively and classify them – the malaria surveillance manual defines suspected, confirmed, excluded, imported, autochthon, and Indigenous cases; – determine the period and source of exposure if needed; establish spatial distribution and epidemiologic profiles; and analyze environmental samples. Since 2019, Haiti has adopted the micro-stratification of malaria risk with strata (1) zero cases, (2) less than five cases, (3) less or equal to 50 cases, and (4) at least 50 cases, all during the past three years and provided a robust surveillance is implemented. However, the private sector remains unengaged in malaria surveillance in Haiti [26].

Once data is collected and processed, it is transmitted through the system from local health institutions to the departmental level and finally to the central level. The central level then organizes a weekly situation room, an information center providing rapid access to various information formats, such as maps, tables, and reports. Monthly epidemiological bulletins, monthly and annual statistical reports, and scientific articles are also published to ensure comprehensive communication of findings and guide decision-making.

Furthermore, epidemiological surveillance in Haiti follows three modalities, including passive surveillance via routine data collection for notifiable diseases. As of August 2020, it encompassed 676 health centers involved in routine care delivery and public health program implementation, such as the malaria elimination plan, i.e., 60% of national health institutions managed by the MoH or non-governmental organizations [27]. Furthermore, active surveillance of certain priority diseases involves community investigations and visits to medical institutions by epidemiologic surveillance officers. Lastly, based on pre-established case definitions, a syndromic surveillance approach is adopted to identify conditions or diseases under surveillance. The presence of a fever or report of a history of fever defines a suspected case of malaria. The syndromic modality of surveillance in Haiti involves a National Sentinel Surveillance System (SNSS) – a subset of the broader surveillance system – composed of 17 reference-level community hospitals and departmental and university hospitals managed by the MoH. It was initiated by the DELR and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Pan American Health Organization (PAHO)/ WHO, and other partners in 2010 to detect and respond to epidemics after the earthquake [28]. Since its creation, the system has gradually evolved toward laboratory-based surveillance with sample collection and microbiological confirmation. A confirmed malaria case is defined by the presence of the parasite in the thick and thin blood smear or by a positive result by the RDT in the presence of a fever.

DELR/MoH statistical reports typically include a weekly number of confirmed malaria cases nationwide and by geographic department, stratified by diagnostic modalities (RDT or microscopy), as well as the number of investigated cases and collected samples. It supports the elimination efforts by improving the early detection of malaria cases in targeted areas for rapid response and case management and further identification and characterization of transmission foci.

Additionally, the PNCM piloted a national antimalarial drug resistance molecular surveillance program using the SNSS based on the system’s strength, including, among other factors, the number of malaria cases identified, the observed skills of laboratory technicians, and the surveillance system’s accuracy. Despite limited resources, the pilot program proved the feasibility of such a molecular surveillance program in the elimination setting. Positive test specimens are prepared on filter paper from ten sentinel sites across several departments and sent to the National Public Health Laboratory for quality control, which includes microscopy and PCR testing. A portion of these specimens is then recovered for genetic sequencing, and the data is subsequently entered and analyzed. However, this surveillance has been temporarily halted since 2021 due to a lack of resources.

A pilot observation was initiated in four stratum-4 communes in the South using a simplified WHO protocol in response to the ongoing local outbreaks and concerns about changes in first-line treatment efficacy. Parasitemia is assessed on Day 0, Day 3, and Day 7 of treatment. According to the WHO guidelines, for an effective treatment response, parasitemia must decrease by at least 75% on Day 3 and by 100% on Day 7.

The area and duration of the pilot project were extended after observing unsatisfactory responses to chloroquine in specific outbreaks before proceeding with genomic investigations to understand these findings better. However, data and specimens are sporadically sent from the department. On the other hand, there is no active surveillance of partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives.

Coverage and access to malaria diagnosis in Haiti

One of the goals of the T3 strategy in countries where malaria is endemic is to ensure the availability of rapid diagnostic tests and microscopy [24]. Accordingly, it involves assessing needs, procuring tests, managing quality and the supply chain, and monitoring and evaluating screening programs.

In Haiti, the diagnosis of malaria is based on microscopic testing. Furthermore, the MoH authorized using three brands of rapid diagnostic tests (RDT): First Response Malaria Ag HRP2, CareStart Malaria NRP2 (Pf), and SD Bioline Malaria Ag Pf [29]. In early 2010, the MoH and CDC conducted a field assessment of RDTs performance compared to microscopy in four health facilities. Only three sites returned samples for comparison, with two demonstrating acceptable sensitivity and specificity of RDTs. However, the three approved brands have been selected based on WHO laboratory evaluations. At the same time, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has limited diagnostic utility in the community due to the high cost of the test [30]. After the 2010 earthquake, the PNCM approved a national policy based on rapid testing to detect asymptomatic cases in the elimination settings [31]. Thus, by March 2014, more than 200 institutions (including 900 trained healthcare personnel), in partnership with the malaria elimination program, conducted 300,000 rapid tests. The impact of this initiative was to move Haiti closer to the pre-elimination phase, according to a multi-program report of routine surveillance data [31]. A study conducted in 2015 determined the number of malaria tests conducted and positive tests per geographic department, highlighting the variations in the use of microscopic and rapid diagnostic tests.

Detection method of Plasmodium falciparum

No issues have been reported with the use of conventional RDT authorized in Haiti. However, a high-sensitivity RDT (SD Bioline Malaria Ag Pf High Sensitive) was developed and compared to the conventional RDT developed by the same manufacturer. The aim was to enhance the monitoring of changes in infection prevalence due to a high number of asymptomatic infections and a decline in care-seeking behavior [32]. While both tests are based on P. falciparum-specific Histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2), the high-sensitivity RDT has been shown to have limited utility in household and group surveys in the elimination context in Haiti due to the absence of parasite DNA despite the detection of HRP2 (false positives) [32].

Submicroscopic malaria is common in pregnant women, with 1.2% positive microscopic tests compared to 16.4% PCR-positive tests in the same sample from six geographic departments [33]. In the elimination context, high-sensitivity RDT (Alere Malaria Ag P.f RDT Ultra-Sensitive) has shown a higher performance than conventional RDT in pregnant women in the Colombian malaria-endemic region with the ability to detect peripheral and placental malaria [34]. The Alere Malaria Ag P.f RDT Ultra-Sensitive and the SD Bioline Malaria Ag Pf High Sensitive tests are both pre-qualified by WHO [35]. These studies demonstrate the potential value of the former in diagnosing malaria during pregnancy in low-transmission settings and highlight the latter’s limited effectiveness in the malaria context within Haiti. Overall, it has been hypothesized that access to malaria diagnosis is limited in most health centers in Haiti [30]. Therefore, a cost-saving strategy is using multiplex Plasmodium antigen and IgG detection to select samples of interest for PCR testing [16].

Coverage and access to malaria treatment in Haiti

The acquisition and regular monitoring of the efficacy of therapeutic combinations based on artemisinin for uncomplicated cases due to P. falciparum is imperative to the T3 strategy proposed by WHO [24].

The MoH recommends (updated in 2012) chloroquine (CQ) and single-dose primaquine in dual therapy as the first-line therapy for uncomplicated cases. Following the WHO’s recommendations for treating P. falciparum malaria, the latter is used as a single-dose gametocide. As a second line, artemisinin derivatives are recommended: (1) Artemether-Lumefantrine, (2) Artesunate-Clindamycin, and (3) Quinine-Clindamycin. At present, there is no chloroquine resistance in Haiti [36]. However, only one survey conducted between 2006 and 2007 revealed the presence of CQ-resistant haplotype P. falciparum parasites in Haiti, possibly due to importation or the rise of a single point mutation at position 76 in the P. falciparum CQ resistance transporter (pfcrt) gene on chromosome 7 leading to the development of CQ resistance, according to the authors [37]. However, no parasites carrying drug resistance mutations nor treatment failure in patients were found in a recent study [36, 38].

Access to malaria treatment in Haiti is limited. The availability of primaquine remains low, with only 31% of public and mixed institutions having the drug, according to a MoH survey [26]. Based on annual inventories, two primaquine stock acquisitions are made yearly with assistance from PAHO/WHO for emergency access. As a result, most patients rely on street markets for antimicrobials, and self-medication is rampant [15]. A study revealed the substandard quality of drugs acquired from the streets in Haiti, with chloroquine showing an 89% spectral match with the authentic medication. However, there was heterogeneity in the spectral quality within several packs of chloroquine, suggesting the possibility of counterfeited medications [39].

WHO recommends intermittent preventive treatment of pregnant women in areas of moderate-to-high transmission of malaria in sub-Saharan Africa [8]. It is recommended in Haiti’s elimination setting. However, many access barriers prevent its implementation, including lack of antenatal care, long walking distance from health facilities, and poor relationships with healthcare providers, as described above [7].

The current system reports a shortage of multi-skilled health workers (10.47 health professionals / 10,000 inhabitants or 1,047 providers per 1,000 inhabitants against 2.5 / 1,000 inhabitants, the ratio recommended by WHO) in addition to their poor distribution (88% of providers are concentrated in urban areas against 12% in rural areas, while 49% of the population lives in rural areas) [40]. To face the challenges posed by the lack of primary healthcare providers, Haiti relies on a network of community health workers (CWH) [40]. The CHWs are salaried community members, trained and equipped by the MoH and partner NGOs, and work as part of a family health team composed of 60 CHWs and four auxiliary nurses serving 60.000 inhabitants, mainly in rural areas. They target vulnerable populations such as pregnant women and reduce barriers to the uptake of malaria prevention, diagnosis, and treatment [7]. However, despite the reach of CHWs, community members face access barriers to malaria services and mistrust rooted in political history. A study emphasized the need to couple community health programs with Vodou priests and traditional healers to overcome those barriers, especially in communities experiencing mistrust of foreigners and conventional health care [41].

Discussion

Our scoping review described the structure and functioning of Haiti’s health care system and the fight against malaria. We further identified various studies that assessed the access and coverage of malaria services in Haiti in the context of its potential elimination.

Integrating quantitative and qualitative analysis, mixed methods are the least common for assessing malaria services. However, their relevance has been demonstrated in understanding the social, economic, and cultural contexts of health systems performance in LMIC [42]. In the Haitian context, an explanatory sequential design where qualitative data are collected and used to explain the quantitative data is critically needed, rather than the usual distinct presentation of quantitative and qualitative results [43]. To overcome the challenges of limited time, funding, and expertise, we need to emphasize multidisciplinary collaboration.

Furthermore, key indicators need to be outlined, taking our findings into account, to evaluate malaria testing, treatment, and surveillance promptly. They include the proportion of suspected malaria cases tested in health institutions and the community and the proportion of confirmed cases treated in health institutions and the community. However, establishing the number of fever cases screened for malaria may be challenging.

The MoH defines a malaria case as anyone with a fever or a history of fever ≥ 38 degrees Celsius in the last 48 h or in whom a healthcare provider suspects malaria. However, fever cases are not systematically reported nor tested for malaria, as confirmed by the PNCM leadership. Therefore, based on the case definition, an indicator such as the proportion of febrile individuals screened for malaria would amount to 100% despite a probable lack of testing. A medical record-based survey of patient symptoms would generate more accurate data about that indicator, as suggested by papers in this scoping review. However, such a strategy would be operationally challenging due to a socially and politically volatile situation affecting areas of higher malaria transmission in Haiti [44]. In a similar context, routine data were used to make inferences about testing practices by determining the proportion of patients tested for malaria after excluding the confirmed malaria cases [45]. In Guinea and Senegal, routine data analysis and data from a sample of health facilities showed discrepancies between the proportions of febrile cases tested for malaria, providing evidence of a lack of testing. Analyzing surveillance data and data from private institutions could help infer statistics on testing fever cases for malaria in Haiti.

This review has some limitations. Firstly, some selection bias is inherent to our search strategy. We limited our search to Haiti and for a specific period because we aimed to gather the most up-to-date data to inform the progress on access and coverage of malaria services. However, it may have fostered a lack of comprehensiveness despite answering our research questions. In addition, the variety of research designs that we found may indicate restricted bias.

Furthermore, part of the grey literature may have been excluded because of a limited strategy to gather unpublished technical reports. When conducting our review, we relied on official websites and privileged communication with the PNCM to compile technical reports related to the malaria program. However, the PNCM leadership shared reports via different communication channels irregularly and unsystematically. Therefore, some of the reports may have been unavailable from the PNCM or only available in other MoH directorates or non-governmental organizations due to the fragmented health system, limiting our access.

Conclusion

While the scope of research on the access and coverage of malaria surveillance, diagnosis, and treatment in Haiti is limited, shortcomings and challenges in implementing WHO-recommended policies and strategies for malaria elimination are highlighted. Despite establishing a strategic plan for eliminating malaria in Haiti covering the 2021–2030, the Haitian MoH and the PNCM still need to strengthen epidemiologic surveillance, testing capacity, and access to malaria treatment.

Therefore, policymakers and political decision-makers should prioritize laboratory infrastructure and testing capacity. Furthermore, they should enhance epidemiologic surveillance, data integrity, and reliability, implement strong regional collaboration for antimalarial drug procurement while enforcing drug market regulations, build on the reach of community health workers to involve communities and in-country stakeholders, and secure sustainable funding to support their outreach efforts.
