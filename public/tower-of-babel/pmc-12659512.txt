Examining the role of school food gardens, food waste systems and cooking facilities in experiential education: a cross-sectional survey of primary school teachers’ practices
Authors: Jessica V Kempler, Claire Margerison, Janandani Nanayakkara, Alison Booth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExperiential learning about food, nutrition and sustainability (FNS) at school promotes population health and environmental sustainability. Little is known about actual practices in school-based experiential FNS education, outside of time-limited interventions. This study aimed to address this gap by analysing current approaches to experiential FNS education at school, including the presence and use of school food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities.MethodsA cross-sectional survey of Australian primary school teachers (August 2022-October 2023) collected quantitative and qualitative data about (i) teacher perceptions about experiential FNS education; (ii) presence of food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities; and (iii) how school-based infrastructure is used for experiential FNS education. Statistical analyses including simple and adjusted logistic regression models were conducted using STATA. Content analysis was conducted using NVivo 15.ResultsParticipants were 413 Australian primary school teachers. Teachers agreed schools should have infrastructure such as food waste management systems (87.2%), cooking facilities (84.5%) and food gardens (82.3%) available for experiential FNS education. Most teachers reported their school had a food garden (76.0%), cooking facilities (58.6%) and a food waste management system (56.2%), though there was variability in the actual use of each type of infrastructure for experiential education. Food waste management systems were used most commonly for educational purposes. Each type of infrastructure was used to teach about a variety of topics related to nutrition, sustainability and cross-curricular learning. Teachers described a range of factors influencing their use of infrastructure for experiential FNS education , including access to adequate, fit-for-purpose and safe physical space; funding, time, staffing and workload; and leadership and prioritisation.ConclusionsConsolidating the use of food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities for experiential education in primary schools is an important next step for research, policy and practice. This includes addressing challenges regarding physical space, time and resourcing, and leadership support, to prioritise experiential FNS education in the school curriculum. Leveraging primary school-based infrastructure for educational purposes can support children to establish healthy and sustainable food practices, and promote the health of people and the planet now and into the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01761-7.Keywords: Primary school, Food education, Food literacy, Food sustainability, Nutrition, Experiential learning, Teacher, Children, Healthy eating, Environmental sustainability

Background

Healthy dietary patterns supported by sustainable food systems are critical to population health and environmental sustainability. While health promoting food behaviours are key for the prevention and management of non-communicable diseases, sustainable food systems contribute to the preservation of environmental, social and economic resources for current and future generations [1].The primary school years (approximately 5–12 years of age) represent a critical developmental period during which children’s dietary habits are being established [2] and ecological awareness begins to emerge [3]. Supporting the development of healthy and sustainable dietary habits during this time is crucial.

However, evidence consistently suggests that in Australia, children’s dietary intake is suboptimal. For example, only 6% of Australian children aged 5–12 years meet Australian Dietary Guideline recommendations for vegetable consumption [4], and up to 40% of dietary energy intake is derived from ‘discretionary’ (or unhealthy) choices [5]. Moreover, a recent analysis comparing the diets of Australian children with the EAT-Lancet Commission Planetary Health Diet [6] found that children’s diets do not meet targets for wholegrains, vegetables, and plant-based protein sources, but exceed targets for added fats and sugars [7]. Re-adjusting children’s dietary patterns to be healthier and more sustainable is essential to ensure that children consume appropriate foods to support their health, growth and development, whilst reducing environmental impacts now and into the future.

One mechanism for achieving this is through the development of children’s food literacy. Whilst there is no universal definition for food literacy, several food literacy models exist [8–10]. These broadly describe the inter-related knowledge, skills and behaviours that support personal interactions with food in a way that promotes personal health and environmental sustainability.

Systematic reviews consistently indicate that primary schools are well positioned to promote children’s food literacy and healthy and sustainable eating behaviours through food, nutrition and sustainability (FNS) education [11–17]. Whilst no formal definition of FNS education exists, it broadly includes (i) Food skills education: teaching students to plan, purchase, store, prepare and cook nutritious food; (ii) Nutrition education: teaching students about nutritious foods and eating patterns that promote good health and reduce the risk of disease; and (iii) Food sustainability education: teaching students to use nutritious food in a way that does not waste natural resources and can be continued into the future without harming our environment or health [18].

Studies suggest that school-based food and nutrition education programs can positively impact children’s nutrition knowledge [11, 12], dietary behaviours [11–14] and anthropometric measures [15–17]. Whilst research on the impact of food sustainability education initiatives at school is limited, studies suggest that food sustainability initiatives in primary school provide opportunities for children to engage in whole of food cycle education including growing, preparing and eating food at school as well as sustainably managing food waste [19]. Such initiatives have shown improvements in children’s attitudes towards sustainable food [20] as well as their fruit and vegetable intake [20, 21] and reductions in food waste behaviours [22].

Primary schools offer multiple avenues for children to learn about food, nutrition and sustainability, including through experiential learning activities. As a student-focused pedagogical approach, experiential learning empowers students to build knowledge by actively participating in real-world activities. This provides opportunities for students to experience, critically reflect, think and act as part of a learning by doing framework, which can enhance student interest, engagement and learning achievement [23].

Experiential learning approaches, for example, gardening, composting and cooking at school, actively engage students in hands-on experiences, compared to traditional didactic approaches where students are more passive recipients of information. Studies have shown that experiential cooking [24–31] and gardening [30, 32–36] programs at school can improve children’s food and nutrition knowledge [24, 28, 33], culinary [24, 26–31] and gardening [30, 34] self-efficacy and behaviours, food attitudes [25], preferences [28, 33] and behaviours [25–27, 31–33, 35, 36] and health and wellbeing outcomes [34] and can help children overcome food phobias [24, 27]. Indeed, a meta-analysis of primary school teaching interventions focusing on improving children’s eating habits found experiential learning approaches had a greater positive effect on children’s overall food consumption, energy intake and nutrition knowledge when compared with didactic approaches [13].

Further evidence suggests experiential learning about food can foster cross-curriculum learning and capabilities. For example, a recent analysis of a school garden program in semi-rural Australia found links to all general capabilities in the national curriculum, including critical and creative thinking, personal and social capability and intercultural and ethical understanding [37]. Further research of two Australian primary schools found integration of curriculum literacies into garden lessons demonstrated improvements in students’ writing skills and attitude towards learning, particularly for students with learning difficulties and behavioural issues [38].

While numerous intervention studies highlight the importance of experiential learning about food, nutrition and sustainability, little is known about how this approach is implemented in real-world school contexts, outside of time-limited or siloed interventions. Particularly, the degree to which schools have appropriate infrastructure to conduct experiential learning about food, nutrition and sustainability issues is unknown. This study seeks to address this gap by exploring presence and practices in the use of food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities in primary school, and how these infrastructures are being leveraged (or underutilised) for experiential learning about food, nutrition and sustainability.

Aim and objectives

The aim of this study was to analyse the presence and use of school-based infrastructure, including food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities, for experiential food, nutrition and sustainability (FNS) education in Australian primary schools. The objectives of the study were to analyse (i) teacher perceptions about the importance of having school-based infrastructure for experiential FNS education; (ii) the presence of food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities for experiential FNS education in primary schools; (iii) how school-based infrastructure is used for experiential FNS education; and (iv) associations between these factors and school-level characteristics, including school type, rurality, socioeconomic position and size.

Methods

Study design

Methods and results for this study are reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement [39] (see Supplement 1).

This study is part of a larger transnational analysis of approaches to FNS education in Australia, Sweden and the United Kingdom. Here were report on a subset of data collected from Australian respondents about the use of school-based infrastructure for FNS education. The study design used to collect data from Australian respondents has been previously described in detail [18] and is briefly summarised below.

A cross-sectional mixed-methods survey of Australian primary school teachers was undertaken between August 2022-October 2023. Eligible participants were current classroom teachers of primary school students in Foundation (Prep) – Grade Six at a registered Australian school. Using the STATA [40] sample size calculator, a sample of 388 respondents per country was calculated to be able to detect a 10% difference in country responses with 80% power. This would also allow estimation of prevalences (for example, presence of school-based infrastructure) with a maximum 95% confidence interval (CI) width of ± 5%. Convenience sampling was used to recruit respondents using an advertisement with an online survey link, circulated via social media and mailing lists from relevant health and education organisations. Teachers who clicked on the link and nominated ‘Australia’ as their country were directed to a voluntary survey on the REDCap platform [41], with embedded consent. Teachers were able to complete the survey if they provided consent and responded ‘yes’ to the screening question ‘Are you currently a classroom teacher of primary level students (Foundation - Grade 6) at a school in Australia?’

Survey instrument

Development of the transnational survey used for this study is described in detail elsewhere [18]. This study reports on Australian participant responses to a subset of questions from the transnational survey which captured data about (i) teacher perceptions about the importance of school-based infrastructure for experiential FNS education; (ii) presence of food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities for experiential FNS education; (iii) how school-based infrastructure is used for experiential FNS education; and (iv) school and teacher demographics.

Quantitative questions were presented using matrix format with 5-point Likert scales and single-select and multi-select responses (question topics, examples and response types are summarised in Table 1, see Supplement 2 for exact wording). For some questions, prompts (‘please provide some examples of…’ or ‘please specify…’) were used to glean specific information. The open-ended question ‘Is there anything else you think is important for us to know about your school’s facilities (e.g., vegetable garden, food waste management system or kitchen/cooking facilities) for teaching students about food and nutrition?’ was used to capture additional information deemed important by respondents.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA [40], including descriptive statistics for all variables. Sample characteristics were presented as frequencies and percentages. Teacher perceptions and presence of school-based infrastructure for FNS education were reported across categories of school characteristics (school type, rurality, socioeconomic position (SEP) and size), presented as frequencies and percentages. Person who uses school-based infrastructure and frequency of use for teaching were presented as percentages with 95%CI. For multi-select questions about the use of school-based infrastructure to teach different topics, frequencies and percentages were reported.

Frequency data were reported for the complete cohort of teachers. Teacher perceptions and presence of school-based infrastructure for FNS education was also reported for the complete cohort of teachers and for subgroups based on school type, rurality, SEP and school size.

Five-point Likert scale questions about teacher perceptions regarding school-based infrastructure for FNS education were grouped into three categories (agree/neither agree nor disagree/disagree). Four category questions about presence of school infrastructure that can be used for teaching were grouped into three categories (yes/unsure/no). Six option questions about frequency of using school infrastructure for teaching were dichotomized as ‘frequently’ (> once a week/once a week/once a fortnight) vs. ‘infrequently’ (once or twice a term/once or twice a year/< once a year).

School type was dichotomised as ‘Government’ vs. ‘Non-government’ (including Catholic, independent and other schools). To determine school rurality, school postcode was matched to the Australian Statistical Geography Standard [42], which classifies geographic areas as a major city, inner/outer regional Australia or remote/very remote Australia. Rurality was then dichotomised as ‘Major city’ or ‘Regional city’. Area-level SEP was determined using the Index of Relative Socioeconomic Advantage and Disadvantage [43]. Decile scores (1 = greatest disadvantage; 10 = greatest advantage) were allocated based on postcode and grouped as low-SEP (IRSAD 1–3), medium-SEP (IRSAD 4–6) or high-SEP (IRSAD 7–10). School size was grouped into three categories based on number of student enrolments (0–299, 300–599, ≥ 600).

Simple logistic regression models were fitted to analyse associations between teacher perceptions and presence of school-based infrastructure and each school characteristic (school type, rurality, SEP and size). Additionally, multiple independent variable regression models were fitted to analyse associations between outcomes and school characteristics independent of each other. There was no evidence of multicollinearity between school characteristics. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Content analysis

Content analysis of open-ended questions was conducted using NVivo 15 [44]. Quantitative content analysis was used to analyse responses to prompt questions (such as ‘please provide some examples of…’ or ‘please specify…’). A preliminary coding framework was developed based on the prompt question topics within the survey. Deductive coding was then used to assign words and phrases to specific codes within the preliminary framework.

Inductive content analysis was used to analyse responses to the open-ended question which asked for additional information deemed important by teachers. Responses were read for familiarisation and a first round of coding was undertaken by the lead author (JVK) to group similar words and phrases within text responses. To minimise the risk of bias, sections of text within each code were then re-read and shared with the research team, who discussed, compared and iteratively refined each code.

Results

Participants

A total of 720 teachers consented to participate in the study and met the screening criteria. Three hundred and seven participants did not complete the survey, leaving a final sample of 413 complete responses that were included in the analysis. Teachers were mostly female (n = 356, 86.2%) and had between 1 and 10 years of experience (n = 184, 44.6%). Most teachers worked in Government schools (n = 262, 68.3%) in the state of Victoria (n = 237, 57.4%), and all primary year levels were represented. Schools were mostly located in major cities (n = 262, 63.4%) and high-SEP areas (n = 219, 53.0%). School and teacher characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Presence and use of school-based infrastructure for FNS education

Food gardens

More than three quarters of teachers (n = 314, 76.0%; 95%CI = 71.7%, 79.9%) reported their school had a food garden that could be used for teaching purposes (Table 3). No relationships were observed between presence of a food garden and school characteristics after adjusting for covariates. Of teachers who reported their school had a food garden, around half stated the garden was used to teach students about food, nutrition and sustainability (n = 168, 53.5%) (Table 3). Of these, 13.1% of teachers (n = 22; 95%CI = 8.8%, 19.1%) reported only they used the garden to teach their students, whilst 58.9% (n = 99; 95%CI = 51.3%, 66.2%) stated they as well as others used the garden for teaching (Table 4). When promted, 145 teachers specified other people who use the garden for teaching, including other general (n = 54) or specialist (n = 28) teachers, specific kitchen, garden or environment leaders (n = 23), teaching aides and education support officers (n = 19), other school staff (for example, gardener, maintenance or canteen staff) (n = 15), community members such as parents and volunteers (n = 14), and the school garden club (n = 10). Five teachers specified the garden could be used by anyone at the school.

Food waste management systems

More than half of teachers (n = 232, 56.2%; 95%CI = 51.3%, 60.9%) reported their school had a food waste management system that could be used for teaching purposes (Table 3). Teachers from low-SEP areas were more likely to report their school had a food waste management system than teachers in medium-SEP areas (62.2% vs. 50.0%; p = 0.035, see Supplement 3), though this relationship was not observed when comparing presence of a food waste management system between low- and high-SES schools. Other school characteristics were not associated with the presence of a food waste management system. Of teachers who reported their school had a food waste management system, 66.8% (n = 155) reported this was used to teach students about food, nutrition and sustainability (Table 3). When prompted, 191 respondents described the food waste management systems used at their school, including compost (n = 156), worm farms (n = 59), recycling systems (n = 19), giving food scraps to animals (for example, chickens, goats, pigs or ducks) (n = 15) having a ‘green waste’ bin (n = 7), using a subpod (combined worm farm/composting system) (n = 2) or taking rubbish home (n = 1).

Of 155 teachers who reported the food waste management system was used for teaching, 14.8% (n = 23; 95%CI = 10.0%, 21.4%) reported only they used the system to teach their students, whilst 75.5% (n = 117; 68.0%, 81.7%) stated they as well as others used the food waste management system for teaching their students (Table 4). When prompted, 127 teachers specified other people who used the system for teaching, including general (n = 38) or specialist (n = 26) teachers, other staff (for example, canteen managers, gardeners and sustainability leaders) (n = 28), teaching aides and education support officers (n = 7) and community members (n = 5). Eighteen teachers specified that all classes in the school used the food waste management system, whilst seven described specific year levels having a particular role in food waste management (for example, being a ‘waste warrior’). Twenty teachers described a whole-school approach with everyone in the school community having access to the food waste management system.

Of 155 teachers who reported the food waste management system was used for teaching, 31.0% (n = 48; 95%CI = 24.1%, 38.7%) stated it was used more than once a week, with fewer reporting it was used once or twice a term (n = 34, 21.9%; 95%CI = 16.1%, 29.2%), once or twice a year (n = 27, 17.4%; 95%CI = 12.2%, 24.3%) or once a week (n = 23, 14.8%; 95%CI = 10.0%, 21.4%) (Table 4). Teachers who used the food waste management system for teaching most commonly used this to teach about food waste (n = 120), food sustainability (n = 105), the environment (n = 101) and gardening skills (n = 62) (Table 5).

Of 92 teachers who did not use their school’s food waste management system for teaching, 65.2% (n = 60; 95%CI = 54.8%, 74.3%) reported they would be interested in using this for teaching in the future. When prompted, thirty six teachers described factors that would enable them to use the food waste management system, including: appropriate infrastructure and equipment (n = 16), for example, compost bins in each classroom; time (n = 7); support (n = 4); professional development to support staff and student knowledge (n = 4); alignment to curriculum subjects (n = 3); funding (n = 2); and personnel (n = 2). Two teachers explained:

[We need a…] whole school approach and whole school student knowledge. My team tried a couple of years ago, but students in other year levels didn’t understand or care for what we are doing and kept putting other items in the compost which ruined it.[ID−350]

I need someone to champion it. We are a small school with just three classes. We are already overwhelmed with responsibilities. I have one community member who looks after 2 of our pods. We have 4 more with nothing happening.[ID−407]

Kitchens/cooking facilities

More than half of teachers (n = 242, 58.6%; 95%CI = 53.8%, 63.3%) reported their school had a kitchen or cooking facilities that could be used for teaching purposes, and this was not associated with school characteristics. Of these teachers, 59.5% (n = 144) reported the facilities were used to teach students about food, nutrition and sustainability (Table 3). Of 144 teachers who reported the school cooking facilities were used to teach their students, 13.9% of teachers (n = 20; 95%CI = 9.1%, 20.6%) reported only they used the facilities to teach their students, whilst 60.4% (n = 87; 95%CI = 52.1%, 68.1%) stated they as well as others used the facilities for teaching their students (Table 4). When prompted, 119 teachers specified others who used the kitchen/cooking facilities for teaching, including general (n = 41) and specialist (n = 30) teachers or specific food/kitchen teachers (n = 18), other school staff (for example, canteen manager or breakfast club facilitator) (n = 19), teacher aides and education support officers (n = 19), other class levels (n = 14) and community members (n = 10). Three teachers specified the kitchen/cooking facilities could be used by everyone at the school.

Of 144 teachers who reported that cooking facilities were used to teach students, more than a quarter stated this occurred once or twice a term (n = 41, 28.5%; 95%CI = 21.7%, 36.4%), with fewer reporting this occurred once a week (n = 28, 19.4%; 95%CI = 13.7%, 26.8%), more than once a week (n = 25, 17.4%; 95%CI = 11.9%, 24.5%) or once a fortnight (n = 21, 14.6%; 95%CI = 9.7%, 21.4%).

School cooking facilities were most commonly used to teach about food preparation/cooking (n = 118), healthy eating (n = 97) and for food tasting (n = 82) (Table 5). When prompted, 11 teachers specified the school’s cooking facilities were used for other teaching purposes, including culture (n = 2), food exposure (n = 1), general sustainability (n = 1), wellbeing (n = 1), social skills (n = 1), integrated learning (n = 1) and procedural writing (n = 1).

Of 135 teachers who did not use their school’s cooking facilities for teaching, 74.1% (n = 100; 95%CI = 66.0%, 80.8%) reported they would be interested in using the facilities for teaching in the future. When prompted, 61 teachers described factors that would enable them to use the school’s cooking facilities for teaching purposes, including adequate physical space and access to facilities (n = 16), personnel (n = 10), time (n = 9), having appropriate supplies, cooking equipment and recipes (n = 8), links to the curriculum (n = 6), leadership support (n = 4); funding (n = 4), addressing safety issues (n = 4) and integrating a whole of school approach (n = 1). Teachers described needing:

Actual kitchen facilities that work. Cooking equipment for all students rather than one bowl or some plates. [ID−300]

Time, money and curriculum outcomes that can be taught, assessed and reported on.[ID−342]

Factors influencing use of school-based infrastructure for experiential FNS education

Eighty-five teachers responded to the open-ended survey question, providing more information about their use of school-based infrastructure for experiential FNS education. This included describing a range of factors that impacted their use of infrastructure to teach about food, nutrition and sustainability, including physical infrastructure, time and resources, and leadership and prioritisation.

Physical infrastructure

A common concept was that infrastructure may be present, but not fit-for-purpose or appropriate for teaching. For example, one teacher stated:

We have a kitchen that is 13 years old that is poorly laid out and unsafe for younger and middle primary classes to use due to the type of cook tops. We have 4 raised garden beds but nowhere to safely create user friendly compost.[ID−331]

Teachers described having limited access to facilities, and for some this was due to the age of students, or needing to share facilities with other school programs or activities:

Currently the garden program is only in place for middle and senior students. Juniors are too little (have picked all the fruit off too soon and been put on a ban).[ID−353]

We share our very small kitchen with the school canteen. It’s not practical to use the kitchen. I bring appliances to my classroom.[ID−186]

Our cooking ‘facilities’ are using the staff room which is not suitable for younger children because the tables are too high, or portable equipment we can use in [the] classroom such as electric pans and camping stoves. It is challenging to manage clean up though because not all classrooms have sinks.[ID−328]

Others described infrastructure being present but unusable or in a state of disrepair. For example, two teachers explained the veggie garden is never used and overgrown[ID−85], or that the compost [is] unused since pre covid[ID−101]. Some teachers raised issues around safety and risk, as well as wildlife concerns:

Our school has excellent facilities and a wonderful program. The reason I don’t use the facilities is because my students will not remain inside the kitchen for the lesson. When I teach cooking we make simple food in our classroom. In the interests of safety I have no cooking facilities so I developed a program based around these limitations.[ID−204]

The ‘no sharing food’ and allergy issues have reduced people’s permission and confidence to deal with food at school.[ID−274]

We are sadly not allowed to compost food waste due to risk management concerns (including vermin).[ID−104]

Time and resources

In addition to inadequate infrastructure, several teachers discussed challenges regarding funding, time, staffing and workload:

Our school has 22 classes. I can’t imagine having a garden that would cater for 22 classes to use or kitchen for that matter. Also this topic could be so rich, but honestly where would the time and preparation come from? Would there be [a set] curriculum, if not, i can’t see it being taught. It would just be there as another cross curricular expectation that isn’t taught. Or added to science where the content is already too large to know how to fit it, so aspects are cut to make the units doable.[ID−101]

While these are important, all these things usually fall into… additional duties undertaken by a teacher but aren’t supported with additional release/non-contact time, a budget, or relief from other duties e.g. playground duty. They add to workload.[ID−21]

Money and time. Each school should be given funding for a kitchen, annual funding for [a] specific sustainable garden and be allocated compost bins and water tanks.[ID−22]

Leadership and prioritisation

Several teachers discussed the importance of leadership support and prioritisation of FNS education within the school community:

We use[d] to have veggie garden, a part-time teacher who cooked with the students once a month, and worms, compost bins in classrooms and hens that laid eggs. New school principal changed this all. New priorities.[ID−342]

We have all these facilities or have had (worm garden) but due to lack of support or lack of expertise in leadership and staff the only resource being used properly now is the kitchen with high school students (I’m in a P-12 school)… teachers who were passionate about this subject have either moved on or don’t have the time to mange [these] programs.[ID−26]

Large numbers of students and already full curriculum VS limited infrastructure = make it hard to do better without the investment of the whole educational community.[ID−413]

Two teachers described FNS education as being outside the scope of their role, and that funding and priorities would be better directed elsewhere within the school:

My school is an underfunded public school in Western Sydney. I’d prefer funding for better classroom facilities such as permanent classrooms before we worry about [a] kitchen or cooking facilities. Additionally, I don’t want to do this. Not my job![ID−149]

For me this is a parent responsibility. Yes it’s good to teach children about food, health etc. but not at the expense of reading, writing, mathematics, respectful relationships, science, behaviour and social skills.[ID−203]

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first analysis of the presence of food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities in Australian schools, and how such infrastructure is currently used in experiential FNS education. As such, our findings provide novel insights about the use of school-based infrastructure for FNS education, and subsequently, as a mechanism to support population health and environmental sustainability at school. Notably there were few associations between presence and use of infrastructure and school-level characteristics. However, from our analysis we identify three key findings that warrant further discussion: (i) Teachers support the use of school-based infrastructure for experiential FNS education; (ii) Infrastructure is often present, though there is variation in its use for educational purposes; and (iii) Support is needed to overcome barriers to using school-based infrastructure for experiential FNS education.

Teachers support experiential FNS education

Over 80% of teachers in our study agreed that schools should have food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities available for teaching students. This indicates our teachers acknowledged the importance of having school-based infrastructure which supports experiential learning about food, nutrition and sustainability. Whilst previous research has analysed the impact of interventions that integrate experiential food education at school, little is known about teacher perceptions of experiential food education outside of intervention studies. Although there is sparsity of research in this space, evidence suggests that the integration of experiential education in primary school is complex. For example, teachers may be reluctant to introduce experiential learning activities due to practicality, lack of commitment, time and space limitations and lack of learning resources and materials in ‘real-life’ learning environments [45]. Teachers may further lack training in delivering experiential learning, or be reluctant to sway from traditional teaching pedagogies [45]. Previous studies suggest teachers support FNS education at school for several reasons, including perceived benefits for children’s learning, health and wellbeing [46, 47]. However, the perceived value by teachers in our study of having supportive infrastructure for FNS education within the school environment is a novel insight, which suggests that teachers may indeed support experiential activities as a pedagogical approach for FNS education.

Presence and use of school-based infrastructure for FNS education is varied

In our study, over half of teachers reported their school had a food waste management system or cooking facilities that could be used for teaching. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report on the presence of these facilities in Australian primary schools. Over three quarters of teachers reported their school had a food garden that could be used for teaching. Whilst pedagogical school garden programs have increased in popularity in Australia in recent decades, there is no recent data about the prevalence of food gardens and their use for teaching in Australian primary schools. The most common school garden program in Australia, the Stephanie Alexander Kitchen Garden Program, has been established in 1146 primary/combined schools across the country [48]. This equates to approximately 15% of primary/combined schools across Australia [49]. Small-scale government funded programs have also been established to support the development of pedagogical food gardens in different Australian states. For example, the Queensland government’s Pick of the Crop reaches 207 schools and includes an optional food garden component supported by grants and local implementation support for participating schools [50]. The high proportion of teachers reporting their school had a food garden in our study may indicate that school food gardens are more prevalent than what is reported from government and not-for-profit initiatives.

The relatively high prevalence of food gardens, food waste management systems and cooking facilities reported by teachers in our study indicates such infrastructure is well positioned as a potential pedagogical tool in primary schools. Whilst well established evidence supports the use of food gardens and kitchens for teaching and learning in primary school, our findings suggest similar opportunities may exist for teaching using infrastructure such as compost systems, which is an area for future investigation.

Our study identified several novel findings about the actual use of infrastructure for FNS education. More than half of teachers who stated their school had a garden, food waste management system or cooking facilities at school reported these were used for teaching students about food, nutrition and sustainability. There was however, variation in frequency of use, with food gardens and waste management systems used at least once per week and cooking facilities used for teaching once or twice a term. However, to have a public health impact, it is likely that such facilities need to be used more frequently for educational purposes. For example, previous research has shown that at least six hours of cooking and food skills education is needed to improve children’s cooking efficacy and vegetable intake. However, these improvements were small, and it is likely that many more hours are needed for more substantial impacts [31].

Our teachers reported that food gardens, food waste management systems and cooking facilities were used by a variety of people in the school for teaching purposes, for example other general and specialist teachers, education support officers or the broader school community. Infrastructure was also used to teach about a variety of topics, including those related to nutrition, sustainability and the environment and food preparation, as well as cross-curricular learning. This reflects previous studies which show experiential learning at school, for example, through gardening and cooking [51] can improve healthy eating outcomes [52, 53] and eco-literacy and connection to nature [54], as well as cross-curricular learning and capabilities [37]. Furthermore, our teachers discussed using the school garden or cooking facilities for purposes beyond food-related learning, including supporting wellbeing and development of social skills, aligning with previous evidence about the benefits of gardening and cooking programs at school. For example, a recent scoping review found school garden programs can enhance experiences that promote social and emotional learning, including the development of relationship skills, responsible decision marking and self-awareness and self-management [55].

In our study, up to half of teachers reported that even when infrastructure was present, it was not used for teaching purposes. Notably, most teachers who did not use their school’s infrastructure for teaching stated that they would be interested in doing this in the future. This indicates an important opportunity for strengthening the utilisation of school-based food gardens, food waste management systems and cooking facilities as both a pedagogical approach for cross-curricular learning, and as a strategy for supporting students’ wellbeing.

Support is needed to overcome barriers to experiential FNS education

Our teachers described a range of barriers and enablers that influenced their ability to use food gardens, food waste management systems and cooking facilities for experiential FNS education. Several discussed resourcing including lack of time, resources and funding or the perception that experiential learning would increase staff workload and add to pressure within an already overcrowded curriculum. This reflects findings from recent systematic reviews where overcoming the barriers of time, funding and an overcrowded curriculum [56] and provision of adequate curriculum resources [57] have been identified as critical to implementation of food education in primary school.

Of particular importance is the lack of prioritisation of FNS education in Australia’s primary school curriculum. The Australian Curriculum, implemented in most states and territories, includes food and nutrition content through the learning areas Health and Physical Education, Sciences and Technologies [58], but it is not mandatory. Sustainability is embedded as a cross-curriculum theme [59], though the degree to which food sustainability is a focus is unknown. There is a small focus on food in some state-based curricula. For example, in New South Wales, nutrition is one of 14 topics teachers can choose from in the Personal Development, Health and Physical Education curriculum [60], and only 2.6% of the Victorian primary school curriculum is related to food and nutrition [61]. However, primary school performance in Australia is largely measured via the National Assessment Program Literacy and Numeracy (NAPLAN), which assesses student performance via annual literacy and numeracy tests [62]. This has resulted in strong policy pressure for improved student results in national literacy and numeracy testing across Australian schools, and an increasing domination of literacy and numeracy over teaching time [63]. Despite the (albeit limited) inclusion of FNS topics in the national and State/Territory curricula, teacher reports of limited time, resources and funding to support the implementation of FNS education are therefore unsurprising.

In our study, some teachers discussed the importance of leadership support for implementing FNS education, and in particular, programs being dismantled with staff changeover or a new principal that did not prioritise FNS education. This aligns with previous studies where school culture [64] and principal support [46, 65] have been identified as key factors which support the integration of food and nutrition into curricula. Beyond this, teachers in our study described barriers related to the physical infrastructure required for experiential FNS education, including limited access to safe, fit-for-purpose and accessible facilities. Challenges such as maintenance [66] and space [67] have previously been identified as barriers to school garden programs. However, barriers and enablers for using food waste management systems and cooking facilities for FNS education at school are largely unexplored, presenting an important area for future research. Collectively, our findings suggest a range of environmental mechanisms are needed to promote experiential FNS education in primary schools, including adequate physical infrastructure, time and resources and leadership and prioritisation within the school.

Strengths and limitations

Although the survey instrument used in this study was informed by existing literature and assessed for face validity, it was not explicitly tested for construct validity and reliability. Most respondents were based in high socioeconomic status areas within Victoria, which may limit the generalisability of the results to the wider Australian teaching population. However, our study had a large sample size and participants were mostly females employed in government schools. This aligns with national workforce data indicating that in Australia’s primary school sector, 82% of teachers are female [68] and 70% of schools are government schools [69]. This suggests our findings are likely to reflect the broader perspectives of Australian primary school teachers. Our study used a cross-sectional design, and as such, causal inferences between study outcomes and school characteristics could not be made. Additionally, the voluntary nature of the survey and moderate completion rate introduce the potential for self-selection bias. However, the sample size was large enough to allow for estimates of proportions with high precision, and participating teachers had a breadth of teaching experience across all primary school year levels and at schools of varying sizes, indicating the perceptions of a diverse group of teachers were reflected in the survey findings.

Implications for policy, practice and research

Findings from this study have implications for policy, practice and research. Our findings offer affirmation from teachers that they value experiential FNS education and agree that schools should have appropriate infrastructure in place to facilitate experiential learning about food. These findings can therefore be used to advocate for strategies that support experiential FNS education at school. This includes advocacy to government departments and policy makers to prioritise the establishment and maintenance of appropriate infrastructure at school, supported with adequate funding and resourcing. Education departments can further recognise the importance of experiential FNS education by embedding this within national and state curricula as a cross-curriculum priority. From a practical perspective, this research can be used to foster a school culture that supports experiential FNS education. For example, school leaders can be encouraged to audit and activate underutilised infrastructure and provide time and resources for teachers to engage in experiential FNS education. This includes encouragement for utilising these spaces for cross-curricular learning and whole-of-school community engagement. Future research should consider areas of experiential FNS education that are underexplored to date, for example, how food waste management systems can be effectively used for FNS education. This is particularly important given the large number of teachers in our study who reported their school had a food waste system that could be used for teaching. Research should also consider factors that enable the long-term sustainability of experiential FNS education initiatives, particularly how infrastructure can be maintained in the long term.

Conclusion

This study provides insights from primary school teachers about the presence and use of food gardens, food waste management systems and kitchens/cooking facilities for experiential FNS education at school. We found teachers support the use of such infrastructure for experiential FNS education, and that infrastructure is present in many Australian primary schools. However, the use of such infrastructure for FNS education is variable. Strategies are needed to overcome barriers regarding physical space, time and resourcing and leadership support, without adding to teacher workload and burden. Policy makers, health and education professionals and researchers should work to overcome these barriers. Ultimately, this will strengthen the position of experiential FNS education at school as a mechanism for supporting both population and planetary health currently and into the future.
