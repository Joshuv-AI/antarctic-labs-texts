Diagnostic value of MRI in medial patellar plica: limited classification but link to cartilage injury
Authors: Guihao Zheng, Yulong Ouyang, Shuilin Chen, Shuai Xu, Bei Hu, Guicai Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the diagnostic value of MRI in classifying medial patellar plica and to examine the relationship between plica classification and the extent of medial femoral condyle cartilage injury as seen on MRI.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on MRI data from 156 knees, all of which underwent arthroscopy. The sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of MRI in diagnosing medial patellar plica were calculated, and the consistency between MRI and arthroscopic findings was assessed. The correlation between MRI-based plica classification and the degree of cartilage injury observed arthroscopically in the medial femoral condyle was also analyzed.ResultsAmong the 156 knees from 134 patients, the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of MRI for diagnosing plica were 87.%, 61%, and 82%, respectively. There was poor agreement between MRI and arthroscopy for plica classification (p < 0.05, Kappa = 0.269). A weak correlation was observed between MRI findings and the degree of cartilage injury in the medial femoral condyle (p < 0.05, r = 0.179). The rates of cartilage injury among MRI-based type A, B, C, and D plica were 42.3%, 18.2%, 52.4%, and 69.2%, respectively.ConclusionMRI reliably detects medial patellar plica but has limited accuracy for subclassification. Type C and D plicas showed a weak trend toward association with medial femoral condyle cartilage injury, suggesting a potential relationship for future prospective studies.

Introduction

Plica around the knee are remnants of embryonic synovial tissue that have not fully resorbed. According to widely accepted classification standards, they can be divided into medial patellar, suprapatellar, infrapatellar, and lateral plica [1, 2]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the medial patellar plica is the most symptomatic type in the knee and typically affects both knees [3, 4]. When the medial patellar plica thickens and becomes inflamed, leading to fibrosis, it can cause clinical symptoms such as medial knee pain, swelling, clicking, leg weakness, and pseudoatresia, a condition known as medial plica syndrome (MPS) [5], However, due to the non-specific nature of these symptoms, diagnosing MPS can be challenging [6].

MRI is frequently used to diagnose medial patellar plica because of its detailed imaging capabilities, though its diagnostic value remains somewhat controversial [7, 8].

On MRI, a plica is defined as a synovial fold composed of fibrous tissue, typically visualized as a low signal band on both T1- and T2-weighted images, and should be distinguished from surrounding fat, synovial recesses, or other non-fibrous structures [9]. Small or asymptomatic plicae may also be detected on MRI, which could lead to overdiagnosis; therefore, imaging findings should be interpreted in combination with clinical assessment and arthroscopic confirmation.

Numerous studies have suggested that medial patellar plica can damage patellar cartilage and medial femoral condyle cartilage and that this damage may be closely related to the type of medial patellar plica present, However, the association between plica classification and the severity of cartilage injury remains controversial, as some studies have reported a correlation while others have found no significant relationship [6, 10]. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of MRI for classifying medial patellar plica and to analyze whether this classification is associated with medial femoral condyle cartilage injury.

Materials and methods

General information

Patients undergoing arthroscopic knee surgery from May 2020 to March 2021 were retrospectively included.

Inclusion criteria

Patients who presented with persistent knee discomfort (including pain, limited range of motion, leg weakness, difficulty with extension or flexion, locking, or clicking) and were evaluated by orthopedic specialists who determined arthroscopic examination was clinically indicated.

Complete MRI images of the affected knee were available.

Ipsilateral knee arthroscopy was performed within one month of the MRI examination.

Exclusion criteria

Previous knee surgery, including arthroscopic and open surgery.

Patients with major knee injuries who underwent conservative treatment.

Examination method and diagnostic analysis

MRI examination

A 1.5 T or 3.0 T superconducting magnetic resonance scanner was used with patients in the supine position and the knee slightly flexed at 15°, with the scanning range extending from the upper edge of the patella to the lower edge of the tibial plateau. Scanning ranged from the upper patella to the tibial plateau. Sequences included:Axial and sagittal T1-weighted FSE (TR/TE = 500/15 ms, slice thickness 3 mm, gap 0.3 mm).Axial and sagittal T2-weighted FSE with fat suppression (TR/TE = 3000/80 ms, slice thickness 3 mm, gap 0.3 mm).Coronal proton density-weighted FSE without fat suppression (TR/TE = 2000/30 ms, slice thickness 3 mm, gap 0.3 mm). Diagnosis was made using the criteria by Kobayashi et al. [11]: Type A, plica width < 5 mm. Type B, plica width > 5 mm but not covering the medial femoral condyle. Type C, plica with a large shelf-like appearance covering the anterior surface of the medial femoral condyle. Type D, plica width covering the anterior surface of the medial femoral condyle with a shelf-like appearance and a hole in the middle. Diagnosis was performed independently by two members: one musculoskeletal radiologist with over 10 years of experience in orthopedic MRI interpretation, and one orthopedic sports medicine surgeon and arthroscopy specialist, a chief physician and professor with more than 15 years of experience. Disagreements were resolved through discussion to reach consensus. (see Fig. 1).

Arthroscopy

Routine 30° arthroscopy was performed under epidural anesthesia. A knee arthroscope was placed through an anterolateral approach and examined in the following order: lateral sulcus, lateral patella, suprapatellar, medial patellar sulcus, medial space, intercondylar fossa, and lateral space. Medial patellar plica were classified according to the Sakakibara diagnostic classification [12]: Type A, the protrusion on the medial wall of the joint capsule was cord-like. Type B, the protrusion on the medial wall showed a shelf-like change, wide but not covering the anterior surface of the medial femoral condyle. Type C, the plica with a shelf-like change covered the anterior surface of the medial femoral condyle. Type D, the plica with a shelf-like change had two insertion points on the medial wall of the joint capsule, or there were holes in the middle of the plica (see Fig. 1).

Arthroscopic surgery was performed by the same senior physician, and the degree of arthroscopic medial femoral condyle cartilage injury was graded using the Outerbridge classification [13]: Grade 0, no abnormalities in articular cartilage. Grade I, softening, neovascularization, or pannus and swollen cartilage. Grade II, mild fibrosis of the cartilage surface, with cartilage injury thickness less than 50%. Grade III, severe fibrosis of the cartilage surface, with cartilage injury thickness greater than 50%, without clear bone exposure. Grade IV, complete cartilage defect with exposed subchondral bone.

Statistical analysis

The classification results of plicas diagnosed by MRI were compared with those diagnosed by arthroscopy. The sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of MRI in diagnosing plicas, as well as the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy for each classification, were calculated. SPSS statistical software was used for data analysis.

A Kappa concordance test was used to compare MRI findings with arthroscopic findings. A p-value (P) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Kappa statistic was interpreted as follows: Kappa < 0.4, poor agreement between the two methods. 0.4 ≤ Kappa < 0.75, fair agreement between the two methods. Kappa ≥ 0.75, good agreement between the two methods.

Given the potential influence of prevalence and bias on conventional Kappa values, the prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK) was additionally calculated for binary plica detection (presence vs. absence) and plica subclassification to provide a complementary measure of agreement.

The Spearman correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between MRI-based plica classification and the degree of arthroscopic medial femoral condyle cartilage injury. The same correlation analysis was performed for microscopic plica classification and the degree of arthroscopic medial femoral condyle cartilage injury. The results were interpreted as follows: r < 0.2, very weak positive correlation. 0.2 ≤ r < 0.4: weak positive correlation. 0.4 ≤ r < 0.6, moderate positive correlation. 0.6 ≤ r < 0.8, strong positive correlation. 0.8 ≤ r ≤ 1.0, very strong positive correlation. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General clinical data and magnetic resonance imaging and arthroscopic diagnosis of medial synovial plicas

A total of 156 knees from 134 patients were eligible for the study. The demographic and plica type distributions of the patients are shown in Table 1. Most of the included patients had meniscus injuries, and the majority had Sakakibara type B plica. The incidence of arthroscopic knee plica in the 156 cases was 80.1%. All knee pathologies included were confirmed via arthroscopy.

Among 139 knees, the incidence of medial femoral condyle cartilage injury was 40.87% in knees with medial patellar plica and 16.67% in knees without medial patellar plica. The difference was assessed using the Chi-square test and was statistically significant (p = 0.03).

MRI compared with arthroscopy in the diagnosis of medial patellar plica

The Kappa concordance test demonstrated that these two methods had fair agreement in the diagnosis of plica (P < 0.05, Kappa = 0.462). The accuracy of MRI in the diagnosis of medial patellar synovial plica was 82.05%. After adjusting for prevalence and bias, the prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK) was 0.64, indicating moderate agreement for plica detection.

The accuracy of MRI in the diagnosis of medial patellar synovial plica classification was 42.31%. The sensitivity and specificity of each classification are shown in Table 2. When comparing MRI diagnosis of medial patellar synovial plica classification with arthroscopy, the Kappa concordance test demonstrated poor agreement between these two methods for the diagnosis of plica classification (P < 0.05, Kappa = 0.269). When further adjusted using PABAK, the agreement for plica subclassification was − 0.154, indicating no meaningful agreement beyond chance.

For type A plicas, the Kappa consistency test (P > 0.05) was not statistically significant, leading to the combination of A and B plicas into a single type (A-B). Discussion

This study shows MRI is reliable for detection but unfit for classification of medial plica, with no prognostic utility.

The key finding of this study is that both MRI-based and arthroscopic classifications of medial patellar plica showed very weak positive correlations with the degree of medial femoral condyle cartilage injury assessed under arthroscopy. Subtype C and D plicas demonstrated a trend toward association with cartilage damage; however, the effect size was small and may lack clinical significance.

The incidence of medial patellar plica in the literature varies widely (18.5%–95%) [14–19]. In our study, the incidence was 80.1%, consistent with Nakayama et al., who reported 79.9% in 3,889 knees [18]. The distribution of plica types in our cohort was 21.8% for type A, 32.7% for type B, 16.0% for type C, and 9.6% for type D. These results are broadly comparable to previous reports, although some regional differences exist; for example, European studies reported higher type C and lower type D prevalence [8–20]. Overall, combining types A and B, our findings align closely with Nakayama et al., suggesting that medial patellar plica patterns may vary with geography, ethnicity, and sample characteristics. Clinically, these data highlight the importance of recognizing type distribution when assessing patients for potential medial plica syndrome.

Sakakibara classified medial patellar plica arthroscopically into four types, and Kobayashi et al. established corresponding MRI diagnostic criteria. While MRI is commonly used to detect medial patellar plica, its sensitivity and specificity have varied across studies. In our study, MRI showed 87.20% sensitivity, 61.29% specificity, and 82.05% accuracy for plica detection, which is generally consistent with previous reports [7, 21, 22]. Variations in diagnostic performance across studies may be influenced by MRI resolution, joint effusion, and methodological differences [23–26].

However, the accuracy of MRI for plica classification was only 42.31%, with a kappa value of 0.269 when compared to arthroscopy, indicating poor agreement. These results highlight that while MRI effectively identifies the presence of medial patellar plica, it is less reliable for precise subtype classification. Clinically, this underscores the importance of using arthroscopy as the reference standard for plica classification, while MRI remains valuable for non-invasive diagnosis and preoperative planning.

The kappa agreement for type A plicas alone was not statistically significant. Therefore, types A and B were combined into a single category (type A-B) for analysis. The diagnostic accuracy of MRI for this combined classification improved to 52.56% compared to arthroscopy, with a kappa value of 0.324, indicating poor but statistically significant agreement. These findings suggest that while MRI has limitations in precisely classifying plica subtypes, it can still provide useful information for distinguishing broader categories, such as type A-B versus C-D.

MRI is reliable for detecting medial patellar plica but demonstrates limited accuracy for subclassification. Sensitivity, specificity, and kappa agreement analyses for each subtype showed generally poor agreement between MRI (using the Kobayashi method) and arthroscopy, with slightly better correspondence for type D plicas. Although the sensitivity of MRI for type D plicas is only 46.67%, it remains relatively consistent with arthroscopic findings, suggesting that MRI is a useful imaging modality for diagnosing type D plicas. This finding aligns with the conclusions of Nakanishi et al. [7]. However, the classification of medial patellar synovial plicas under MRI according to Kobayashi’s criteria did not fully match arthroscopic diagnoses, potentially due to the accuracy limitations of the 5 mm demarcation. Future research may benefit from exploring improved MRI diagnostic criteria for medial patellar synovial plicas.

Large and thick medial patellar synovial plicas have long been known to potentially impinge upon and damage the cartilage of the medial femoral condyle and patellar cartilage, as observed through various modalities such as ultrasound, arthroscopy, and arthrography [9, 16]. Studies by Lyu et al. [27] and Liu et al. [28] have indicated that plica size, whether pathologically damaged or not, can still impact the medial femoral condyle during knee movement, leading to cartilage damage when pressure reaches a certain threshold [27, 28]. This raises questions about whether the size of the plica correlates with the degree of cartilage damage in the medial femoral condyle.

In our study, we observed that even type A plicas could impinge on the medial femoral condyle when sufficiently thick. Unlike previous reports suggesting no relationship between plica classification and cartilage injury [29], we found a positive correlation between medial patellar plica type and medial femoral condyle cartilage damage. No correlation with patient age was observed. Microscopically, we noted severe fibrotic thickening of the plica, forming a meniscus-like structure, rather than reduced size due to wear (Fig. 2). Given the negligible effect size, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and do not imply clinical relevance.

In our study, the incidence of medial femoral condyle cartilage injury was 40.87% in knees with medial plica, compared with 16.67% without, showing a significant difference. This rate was lower than the 80.2% reported by Christoforakis et al. [12] and the 96% reported by Guney et al. [30], but higher than the 24.7% reported in a later study by Christoforakis [31]. These discrepancies may relate to differences in study populations, inclusion criteria, and definitions of cartilage injury. Taken together, these findings suggest a statistically significant association between medial plica and cartilage injury; however, the magnitude of this association is limited and does not support a causal or clinically predictive relationship.

Christoforakis et al. reported that while synovial plica classification correlated with medial femoral condyle cartilage injury, the severity of cartilage injury was instead linked to plica pathology, which differs from our findings. In our study, we observed a very weak but statistically significant correlation between plica classification and both the presence and severity of cartilage injury, with type C and D plicas more likely to be associated with such damage. Although this suggests a potential relationship, the small effect size indicates limited clinical significance.

Additionally, we explored the association between MRI-based classification of medial synovial plicas and arthroscopically assessed cartilage damage of the medial femoral condyle. A statistically significant but very weak positive association was observed, a relationship that has not been extensively examined in previous studies. Given the negligible effect size, this finding does not support the use of MRI subclassification for clinical decision-making or preventive intervention at present and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating for future prospective investigations.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, the data collection may be subject to selection bias, which may not fully represent the general population. Second, the study relied solely on MRI and arthroscopy as diagnostic tools, but MRI has certain subjectivity and limitations in the classification of medial patellar plica and in assessing the degree of cartilage injury, which could affect the accuracy of the results. Additionally, the sample size was relatively small, particularly in the correlation analysis between specific types of plica (such as types C and D) and cartilage injury, which limited the statistical power of the analysis. Furthermore, this study did not account for patient symptoms, medical history, or other potential influencing factors such as age, gender, and body mass index (BMI), all of which could affect the classification of the plica and the occurrence of cartilage injury.

Future studies with larger, prospective cohorts are needed to validate the observed associations, incorporate relevant clinical variables, and clarify the role of specific plica subtypes, particularly C and D, in cartilage injury. These studies may also help determine whether selective preventive arthroscopic resection could be beneficial.

Conclusion

MRI is reliable for detecting medial patellar plica but shows limited accuracy in subclassifying plica types. Type C and D plicas on MRI exhibit a weak positive association with medial femoral condyle cartilage injury, though the effect size is small. These findings suggest a potential trend rather than a clinically definitive relationship and should be considered hypothesis-generating for future prospective studies investigating the clinical significance of plica morphology.
