The transcription factor AP-1 regulates the catalase family and oxidative stress adaptation in the dermatophyte Trichophyton rubrum
Authors: Monise F. Petrucelli, Mayara I. G. Azevedo, Vanderci M. Oliveira, Pablo R. Sanches, Antonio Rossi, Nilce M. Martinez-Rossi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxidative stress results from an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species and cellular antioxidant activity, thereby causing cellular damage. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the Basic Leucine Zipper (bZIP) transcription factor AP-1 plays a key regulatory role in antioxidant responses. In Trichophyton rubrum, oxidative stress is countered by antioxidant enzymes and three main regulators: the HOG pathway, AP-1, and SKN7. Here, we investigated the role of AP-1 using a Δap1 mutant of T. rubrum exposed to hydrogen peroxide or menadione. RT-qPCR revealed higher expression of HOG-pathway genes and lower expression of skn7 and catalase genes in the Δap1 mutant under oxidative stress conditions, together with reduced catalase activity. Lipid peroxidation assays indicated greater oxidative damage in the mutants, particularly after menadione exposure. In silico analysis identified an AP-1 consensus binding site in the catalase gene TERG_02005. Post-transcriptional analysis revealed alternative splicing events, specifically intron retention, both in TERG_02005 and in genes related to the spliceosome machinery, which also contained predicted AP-1 binding sites. These findings indicate that AP-1 plays a role in regulating oxidative stress-responsive genes in T. rubrum, particularly those that code for catalase. Additionally, AP-1 may influence spliceosome function, linking transcriptional and post-transcriptional responses to oxidative stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46633-4.

Introduction

Dermatophytes are the primary cause of fungal infections in humans. These are filamentous fungi that infect keratinized tissues, resulting in dermatophytosis. Among them, the anthropophilic species, Trichophyton rubrum is the primary etiological agent. Successful infection requires fungal adaptation to the host environment, which enables the acquisition of nutrients and immune evasion1,2. Like other microbes, T. rubrum is exposed to various environmental stimuli, including xenobiotics and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are generally recognized as stress factors3. Among ROS, hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is a relatively stable oxidant that can diffuse across membranes and generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals via Fenton chemistry, leading to oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA4,5. In contrast, menadione (vitamin K₃) acts as a redox-cycling agent that promotes intracellular superoxide generation through one-electron reduction reactions, thereby amplifying oxidative stress6. These distinct mechanisms result in different oxidative signatures within the fungal cell and may activate partially overlapping stress-response pathways.

When ROS production exceeds fungal antioxidant capacity, oxidative stress develops, potentially damaging membrane lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. To counteract this potential damage to vital cellular structures and biomolecules, fungi possess antioxidant systems, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione, and thioredoxins7. These systems are regulated by signaling networks that include the high-osmolarity glycerol (HOG) pathway, the AP-1 (activator protein 1) basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor (TF), and response regulator SKN7. Among these, Hog1 is the most conserved MAPK involved in osmotic and oxidative stress responses, whereas AP-1 and SKN7 are less conserved across species8.

Transcription factors such as AP-1 orchestrate stress responses by activating or repressing specific gene sets. In model yeasts, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe, AP-1 is activated by redox-sensitive pathways that promote the expression of genes involved in the defense response to oxidative stress. Additionally, it may act synergistically with SKN7 to enhance transcriptional responses under oxidative stress7–9.

Despite its extensive characterization in model organisms, AP-1 function in dermatophytes, particularly in oxidative stress adaptation, remains to be elucidated. Previous studies on T. rubrum have suggested that AP-1 negatively regulates virulence-associated traits and may contribute to infection chronicity10.

Beyond transcriptional regulation, alternative splicing (AS) expands proteomic diversity and enables rapid cellular responses to stress. Among AS types, intron retention (IR) is especially prevalent in fungi, and has been linked to processes such as stress adaptation, pathogenicity, and cell differentiation11–18. Unpublished RNA-seq data from the Δap1 mutant grown on different carbon sources revealed intron retention events in antioxidant-related genes, including those encoding catalases. These findings suggest a role for AP-1, not only in the transcriptional regulation of oxidative stress-response genes, but in post-transcriptional processes as well.

Therefore, we hypothesized that AP-1 plays a central role in modulating the oxidative stress response in T. rubrum through both transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation. Thus, here, we aimed to evaluate the expression of oxidative stress-related genes and investigate alternative splicing events in wild-type (WT) and Δap1 strains under hydrogen peroxide- and menadione-induced oxidative stress. The experiments reported herein, helped us elucidate the multifaceted role of AP-1 in T. rubrum, thereby expanding our understanding of fungal stress adaptation. Furthermore, our research uncovered potential targets for antifungal strategies.

Results

Higher lipid peroxidation levels occurred upon menadione exposure

To verify the occurrence of oxidative stress induction by menadione and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in WT and Δap1 strains, we measured lipid peroxidation by quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. Menadione stress-induction resulted in significantly higher MDA levels than hydrogen peroxide in both strains, indicating stronger lipid peroxidation (Fig. 1). After 1 h of exposure, MDA levels were significantly higher in the Δap1 strain compared to WT under both stress conditions. After 3 h, no significant difference was observed between strains under H₂O₂ treatment. In contrast, under menadione exposure, Δap1 displayed significantly higher MDA levels than WT at 1 h, but significantly lower levels than WT at 3 h. These results indicate a faster and more pronounced lipid peroxidation response in the absence of AP-1, particularly under 1 h of menadione-induced oxidative stress. Additionally, we evaluated the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for both oxidative stressors in the WT and Δap1 strains to determine whether the molecular and biochemical differences observed translated into altered growth sensitivity. MIC values for menadione and H₂O₂ were 0.02 and 0.3 mM for both strains, respectively. MIC values for menadione and H2O2 were 0.02 and 0.3 mM, respectively, for both strains.

AP-1 absence led to increased expression of HOG-pathway genes upon exposure to menadione or H2O2

To investigate how the absence of AP-1 affects the modulation of genes in the HOG and AP1-SKN7 pathways, we analyzed the expression levels of Ypd1 (phosphorelay protein), ssk1 (response regulator), hog1 (stress-activated MAP kinase), and the TF skn7 under oxidative stress (Fig. 2). In general, all three genes involved in the HOG pathway showed higher transcript levels in the Δap1 strain compared to the WT after exposure to H₂O₂ or menadione, suggesting activation of the HOG pathway in response to increased oxidative stress in the mutant strain. However, skn7, a TF that mediates a specific oxidative stress response pathway independent of AP-1, was downregulated in the mutant strain after 1 h of exposure to either oxidant, with no significant difference observed after 3 h of treatment, indicating a distinct transcriptional modulation of SKN7 compared to HOG pathway components.

Deletion of ap-1 affected the expression of antioxidant genes

We analyzed the expression of genes encoding antioxidant enzymes, including members of the thioredoxin, glutathione, and catalase gene families. The absence of AP-1 was associated with higher expression of the thioredoxin-encoding gene (TERG_06757) in the mutant strain after 1 and 3 h of exposure to H₂O₂ (Fig. 3) or menadione (Fig. 4). In contrast, several glutathione-related genes, such as Glutathione S-transferase (TERG_01685 and TERG_01405) and Glutathione reductase (TERG_08069) showed lower expression under both oxidative stress conditions. Interestingly, all catalase isoforms identified in the T. rubrum genome (TERG_02005, TERG_01252, and TERG_06353) were downregulated in the mutant strain under both oxidative stressors, except for TERG_06053, which was upregulated after 3 h of exposure to menadione (Fig. 4).

Catalase activity was impaired in the absence of AP-1

To assess the influence of AP-1 deletion on catalase activity, we measured this activity in the mutant and WT strains under oxidative stress. A reduction in catalase activity was observed in the Δap1 strain after 1 h of exposure to H₂O₂, particularly during the initial 5 min of measurement (Fig. 5a). Similarly, a marked decrease in the enzymatic activity was detected 3 h after exposure to H₂O₂ (Fig. 5b). Further, catalase activity was reduced to a larger extent in the mutant strain than in the WT strain after 1 h of exposure, based on readings obtained during the 15-min enzymatic reaction (Fig. 5c). However, after 3 h of menadione treatment, catalase activity levels in the mutant were statistically similar to those observed in the WT strain (Fig. 5d).

AP-1 deletion affects intron retention events in the catalase pre-mRNA and spliceosome-related genes

Given that AP-1 deletion affected the catalase gene expression and the enzymatic activity, we hypothesized that post-transcriptional mechanisms, such as intron retention, might also contribute to the observed regulatory alterations. Using unpublished RNA-seq data from the Δap1 mutant grown in minimal medium with glucose or keratin, we performed an in silico analysis of IR events in genes encoding catalases and spliceosome components. Intron retention events were detected only in TERG_02005, which encodes a catalase, and in TERG_02669, TERG_01198, and TERG_06409, which encode pre-mRNA splice genes (Table 1). Based on these findings, we assayed these IR events by RT-qPCR in WT and Δap1 strains under H2O2- or menadione-induced oxidative stress (Figs. 6 and 7).

Under H₂O₂ exposure, IR was detected only in TERG_02005, encoding a catalase. The total transcript (Fig. 6a) and IR-1 isoform levels (Fig. 6b) were reduced in the Δap1 strain compared to those observed in the WT strain. However, when analyzing isoform proportions, the Δap1 strain showed a higher proportion of IR-1 (~ 10%) than that of the WT after 1 h of exposure, suggesting that the mutant requires this intron-retained isoform earlier during growth in H₂O₂ compared with the WT strain (Fig. 6c). In turn, the IR events identified in the RNA-seq data from the Δap1 mutant grown in MM with glucose or keratin for 24 h and 48 h (Table 1) were detected in this study only under menadione exposure.

Analysis of total transcript expression (Fig. 7a) revealed that TERG_02005 and TERG_01198 displayed lower transcript levels in the mutant, whereas TERG_02669 and TERG_06409 displayed higher transcript levels, particularly after 1 h of exposure. Focusing on IR isoforms (Fig. 7b), TERG_02005 IR-1, TERG_02669 IR-1, and TERG_06409 IR-2 were upregulated in Δap1, whereas TERG_01198 IR-1 was downregulated. Further, isoform proportion analysis (Fig. 7c) showed that WT produced ~ 49% IR-1 isoforms for TERG_02005 after 3 h of menadione exposure, whereas Δap1 displayed only ~ 10%. Meanwhile, TERG_01198 IR proportion increased in both strains after 3 h, whereas TERG_02669 showed a trend toward increased IR from 1 h to 3 h in the WT, with no significant difference with respect to the mutant. For TERG_06409, both the strains showed a higher proportion of IR events at 3 h.

Intron retention events introduce premature stop codons

Intron retention events often introduce premature stop codons within transcripts, potentially resulting in truncated proteins or triggering nonsense-mediated decay (NMD). Our findings revealed that all IR events identified in TERG_02005, TERG_01198, TERG_02669, and TERG_06409 resulted in premature stop codons, which significantly affected protein translation (Fig. 8). Particularly, in the case of TERG_02005, intron retention resulted in the loss of the core catalase domain, producing a truncated protein of only 19 amino acids with no annotated domains or predicted functions. In pre-mRNA splicing–related genes, intron retention in TERG_01198 led to the loss of the WD40 repeat–containing domain, resulting in a truncated protein of 22 amino acids with no annotated domains. Similarly, intron 1 retention in TERG_02669 generated a 34-amino-acid protein lacking annotated domains. In contrast, intron 2 retention in TERG_06409 did not disrupt the functional domains, resulting in a protein of 326 amino acids, suggesting that this isoform may retain its functionality despite the occurrence of IR events Fig. 9.

AP-1 may directly regulate the catalase gene and pre-mRNA splicing-related genes

Given that both the catalase gene expression and the enzymatic activity were impaired in the Δap1 strain, we investigated whether these genes might be directly regulated by TF AP-1. In silico analysis revealed the presence of an AP-1 binding consensus site in the upstream region of TERG_02005, identified by scanning the 1,000 bp upstream DNA sequence for matches to the AP-1 DNA-binding motif, suggesting potential direct transcriptional regulation. Multiple predicted AP-1 consensus sites were also detected in other genes involved in pre-mRNA splicing, indicating that AP-1 may contribute to the transcriptional control of selected components of the splicing machinery. However, no consensus-binding sites were identified in TERG_01198, TERG_02669, or TERG_06409, genes in which intron retention events were observed, arguing against direct regulation of these specific IR events by AP-1.

Functional enrichment of the genes containing predicted AP-1 binding motifs in the T. rubrum genome revealed some categories particularly related to translation, tRNA processing, ATP binding, kinase and ligase activities, and structural constituents of ribosomes (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study highlights the multifaceted role of AP-1 in the dermatophyte T. rubrum, revealing its involvement in the regulation of antioxidant defense mechanisms and post-transcriptional processes using a Δap1 mutant strain. In the original characterization of the Δap1 mutant, the strain exhibited altered colony morphology, reduced radial growth on solid media, changes in cell wall properties, and modulation of keratinolytic activity, further supporting the role of AP-1 in regulating stress adaptation and biologically relevant phenotypic traits in T. rubrum10 Although AP-1 is widely recognized as a master regulator of oxidative stress responses in fungi such as S. cerevisiae7, its role in dermatophytes remained unclear. To address this gap, we tested two oxidative stress-inducing agents, H2O2 and menadione, on the WT and the Δap1 mutant strain of T. rubrum. Our results showed that menadione triggered larger MDA release, and consequently greater lipid peroxidation, in both the WT and Δap1 strains, suggesting that it induced a stronger oxidative stress response. Notably, In the absence of AP-1, MDA release was further increased after 1 h of exposure to menadione or H2O2, indicating that the lack of this TF increased fungal sensitivity to these toxic compounds. Interestingly, although MDA levels were markedly elevated in the Δap1 strain after 1 h of menadione exposure, a reduction was observed at 3 h (Fig. 1). This temporal decrease likely reflects partial cellular adaptation following the initial oxidative burst, due to activation of compensatory stress-response pathways. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that ap1 is required for oxidative stress adaptation in response to menadione, but not to paraquat or H2O210, suggesting that AP-1 activity in T. rubrum is stressor-specific rather than broadly induced. Mechanistically, this difference is consistent with the distinct modes of action of these stressors. Menadione acts as a redox-cycling quinone, generating superoxide anions through intracellular electron transfer reactions, which can propagate lipid peroxidation cascades. In contrast, H₂O₂ primarily functions as a diffusible oxidant that can be efficiently detoxified by catalases and peroxidases and is also involved in redox signaling processes. Thus, menadione is expected to trigger a more pronounced and sustained lipid-peroxidation response, which aligns with our observations6,19. Despite the increased oxidative damage observed in the Δap1 mutant at early time points, this did not translate into differences in MIC values, indicating that AP-1 primarily affects early stress adaptation rather than the minimal concentration required to inhibit fungal growth.

The HOG pathway, together with AP-1- and Skn7-related pathways, coordinates robust oxidative stress responses in fungi. In particular, in dermatophytes, the HOG1 MAPK pathway has been implicated in the adaptation to oxidative stress and virulence. In our study, the absence of AP-1 in T. rubrum was associated with increased expression of HOG pathway genes (ypd1, ssk1, and hog1), particularly after 3 h of exposure to either oxidative stressor tested. In addition, the transcript levels of skn7 were downregulated after 1 h of exposure to either stressor (Fig. 2). This contrasting expression pattern likely reflects signaling crosstalk within oxidative stress pathways, with enhanced HOG activation accompanying transient modulation of SKN7 expression in the Δap1 mutant. The upregulation of HOG pathway genes in the Δap1 strain likely reflects a compensatory response to increased intracellular oxidative stress, as previously evidenced by elevated lipid peroxidation levels. In the absence of AP-1, enhanced ROS accumulation may activate upstream phosphorelay components, leading to increased transcription of HOG pathway genes as an adaptive mechanism20. Conversely, the transient downregulation of skn7 after 1 h of exposure suggests a functional interplay between AP-1 and SKN7-mediated oxidative stress pathways. Although direct evidence of AP-1 and Hog1 interactions in dermatophytes is still lacking, studies on other filamentous fungi have shown that HogA, the Hog1 homolog, integrates chemical and oxidative stress signals and cooperates with AP-1 homologs, such as NapA/fAP1, to coordinate stress adaptation and developmental pathways21. Therefore, the increased expression of HOG pathway genes in the Δap1 mutant may reflect a reconfiguration of stress signaling to help fungal survival under oxidative conditions.

Considering the effects of AP-1 deletion on oxidative stress-related genes, we observed that ap1 contributes to the regulation of the thioredoxin-encoding gene (TERG_06757) under either stress condition tested. Additionally, the expression of genes encoding members of the glutathione superfamily was negatively affected in the Δap1 strain during exposure to menadione or H2O2 (Figs. 3 and 4). Previous studies in Saccharomyces cerevisiae have shown that the AP-1 homolog Yap1 directly regulates genes involved in glutathione biosynthesis (GSH1 and GSH2), glutathione-dependent enzymes (GPX2 and GLR1), and thioredoxin (TRX2) in response to oxidative stress22,23. However, our results revealed higher transcript levels of the thioredoxin-encoding gene in the Δap1 strain of T. rubrum. This divergence suggests that AP-1-dependent regulation of thioredoxin-related genes may not be fully conserved and could involve distinct regulatory mechanisms in filamentous fungi. Although AP-1- like transcription factors are commonly activated on exposure to oxidative stress, their targets and recognition of promoter sequences show variety. Each species may fit its AP-1 transcription factor, playing an appropriate role through evolution24.

Our data are consistent with the role for ap1 in the regulation of catalase-encoding genes under oxidative stress conditions, since all annotated catalase genes in T. rubrum genome (TERG_02005, TERG_01252, and TERG_06053) were downregulated in the Δap1 mutant after 1 h and 3 h of exposure to either stressor (except for TERG_06053 after 3 h of menadione exposure) (Figs. 3 and 4). This transcriptional repression was accompanied by a significant reduction in catalase enzymatic activity after 3 h of exposure to H2O2 or 1 h exposure to menadione (Fig. 5). Therefore, under H₂O₂ exposure, this reduction was consistently observed after prolonged stress, unlike menadione exposure, whose effect occurred only at the initial time. This result suggests that AP-1-dependent regulation of catalase activity may be stressor-dependent and influenced by the distinct ROS generated by each compound. In addition, we identified a putative AP-1 binding motif in the promoter region of TERG_02005, suggesting a direct regulatory interaction. These findings are consistent with the established roles of AP-1 homologs in other fungi. For example, in Aspergillus fumigatus, deletion of the AP-1 homolog (Afyap1) reportedly results in the complete loss of Cat2 activity and a reduction in Cat1 activity under oxidative stress. Moreover, AfYap1 binding motifs have been identified in the cat2 promoter region, reinforcing the hypothesis of direct transcriptional regulation25. Similarly, in Candida glabrata, the oxidative stress response depends on TFs Yap1p and Skn7p, which together orchestrate the adaptive transcriptional program required for resistance to high levels of H₂O₂. C. glabrata has a single catalase gene (CTA1) that is essential for in vitro oxidative stress resistance26. Altogether, these findings reinforce the conserved role of AP-1 homologs in the regulation of activity and provide the first evidence of AP-1-mediated regulation of catalases in dermatophytes.

In addition to the above, our data revealed the post-transcriptional regulation of TERG_02005 through intron 1 retention (Figs. 6 and 7). This alternative splicing event was detected in both WT and Δap1 strains but was more pronounced under menadione stress, reaching 49% of retained isoforms relative to total isoforms in WT after 3 h, whereas retention levels under exposure to H2O2 remained comparatively lower (~ 10%). In the Δap1 mutant, IR levels were similar (~ 8–10% of total isoforms); however, differential expression analysis revealed upregulation of intron 1-retained transcripts in the mutant compared to the WT at certain time points, such as 3 h under menadione exposure. Importantly, IR introduces a premature stop codon, leading to the loss of the catalase core domain and likely reducing the production of functional catalase proteins. This alternative isoform may participate in an adaptive mechanism to fine-tune oxidative stress responses.

Furthermore, our data indicate that AP-1 modulates the transcriptional expression of genes encoding pre-mRNA splicing factors under oxidative stress, particularly in response to menadione (Fig. 7). Although the Δap1 mutant displayed intron retention (IR) events reaching biologically relevant levels, IR was also observed in the WT strain under the same stress conditions. These observations suggest that the splicing alterations detected are primarily associated with stress-responsive transcriptional remodeling rather than direct regulation of splice-site selection by AP-1.

Notably, IR events introduced premature stop codons in several transcripts, potentially affecting the abundance or functionality of splicing factors, except for TERG_06409, in which intron 2 retention preserves predicted functional domains (Fig. 8). While several genes encoding pre-mRNA splicing factors contain predicted AP-1 binding motifs, supporting possible direct transcriptional regulation, our data do not demonstrate a direct mechanistic role of AP-1 in splice-site choice.

Functional enrichment analysis further indicated that AP-1-associated transcriptional networks extend beyond oxidative stress defense and include RNA-related processes such as tRNA processing, RNA modification, and nuclear export. Together, these findings suggest that AP-1 contributes to oxidative stress adaptation primarily through transcriptional regulation. At the same time, RNA-processing pathways emerge as an additional stress-responsive layer that may shape transcriptome plasticity under oxidative conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Alternative splicing is a recognized mechanism in fungi that regulates the adaptive responses to environmental challenges. Although less studied than in plants or animals, AS in fungi has been shown to diversify the transcriptome, particularly through intron retention, and to respond dynamically to stressful conditions13. In pathogenic fungi, changes in the proportion of alternative isoforms are relevant, as demonstrated for C6-type TFs and stress-related regulators in Candida and Trichophyton rubrum27,28. Consistent with these reports, our own data revealed that IR events, including catalases and spliceosome components, occur under oxidative stress. Previous studies have suggested that IR levels above 5% are unlikely to be stochastic and may represent functionally relevant regulatory events28,29. Altogether, these findings support the hypothesis that fungal stress adaptation involves transcriptional regulation and strategic modulation of AS to fine-tune protein function and cellular resilience16,18,30.

Therefore, this study expands our understanding of oxidative stress responses in T. rubrum by demonstrating that AP-1 plays a central role in coordinating transcriptional programs involved in antioxidant defense and stress signaling. We showed that AP-1 modulates the expression of catalases, components of the glutathione and thioredoxin systems, and genes associated with the HOG pathway, supporting its role as a key regulator of oxidative stress adaptation. In addition, oxidative stress exposure was associated with intron retention events in transcripts encoding catalases and spliceosome components, some reaching biologically relevant levels (> 5%). Although alterations in intron-retaining isoforms were observed in the Δap1 mutant, the overall splicing ratios between WT and mutant strains were largely similar, suggesting that these changes are more likely a consequence of stress-induced transcriptional remodeling rather than evidence of direct splicing control by AP-1. Collectively, our findings position AP-1 as a major transcriptional regulator of oxidative stress adaptation in T. rubrum and indicate that RNA-related processes represent an additional layer of stress-responsive regulation in dermatophytes, warranting further mechanistic investigation.

Materials and methods

Fungal strains and culture conditions

Trichophyton rubrum strain CBS118892 (Westerdijk Biodiversity Institute, Utrecht, The Netherlands) was used as the reference (wildtype, WT) strain, along with the previously constructed Δap1 null mutant strain10. Both strains were cultured on solid malt-extract agar medium (2% glucose, 2% malt extract, 0.1% peptone, and 2% agar, pH 5.7) at 28 °C for 20 d. Conidial suspensions were prepared by flooding the plates with a sterile 0.9% NaCl solution and filtering through fiberglass to remove hyphal fragments. Conidial concentrations were determined using a Neubauer chamber. Approximately 1 × 10⁶ conidia of each strain were inoculated into 100 mL of Sabouraud dextrose broth and incubated at 28 °C for 96 h in an orbital shaker at 120 rpm. The resulting mycelia were subjected to transcription and protein extraction.

Oxidative stress induction with H2O2 and menadione

First, we performed a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay following the M38-A reference method recommended by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI), with the following modifications: 100 µL of a conidial suspension containing 6 × 10⁴ conidia/mL of WT and mutant strains was added to each well of a 96-well plate. The final conidial concentration was adjusted to approximately 3 × 10 conidia per well in Sabouraud medium. Although RPMI 1640 is the standard medium for MIC determination according to the CLSI guidelines, instead, Sabouraud medium was used in this study to ensure optimal fungal growth and biomass production necessary for downstream transcriptional and protein analyses. This substitution allowed for sufficient mycelial yield without compromising the reliability of MIC assessment under the experimental conditions. MIC determination was performed using serial dilutions of H2O2 (ranging from 1.2 to 0.018 mM) and menadione (ranging from 0.08 to 0.00125 mM; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Next, as described in the Fungal Strains and Culture Conditions section, the obtained mycelia were transferred to 100 mL of Sabouraud medium supplemented with 0.21 mM H2O2 or 0.016 mM menadione (corresponding to 70% of the MIC value for both strains). Cultures were incubated for 1 and 3 h at 28 °C with constant agitation (120 rpm). Sabouraud medium without H2O2 or menadione served as the control treatment. Subsequently, biological material from three independent replicates of each treatment was collected by vacuum filtration using filter paper, and the resulting mycelia were dried, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction.

Lipid peroxidation assay

The lipid peroxidation assay was performed as described previously31, after minor modifications. Mycelial samples obtained from Sabouraud cultures with H2O2 and menadione were filtered under vacuum, weighed, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle. The resulting material was collected, and lipid peroxidation was measured by assessing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using a thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS) assay. Briefly, 1.1% (v/v) chilled phosphoric acid was added to the powdered mycelial samples, followed by incubation on ice for 15 min. After vigorous mixing, 0.4 mL aliquots of the homogenate were transferred to 15 mL tubes containing 0.4 mL of 1% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid (TBA) prepared in 50 mM NaOH with 4 mM butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT). For a blank, 0.4 mL of 3 mM HCl was added to 0.4 mL of the homogenate. The remaining 0.6 mL of the homogenate was lyophilized and the dry weight was determined for normalization. Subsequently, 0.2 mL of 7% (v/v) phosphoric acid was added to each reaction mixture, thoroughly mixed, and incubated at 98 °C for 1 h. After cooling on ice, the lipid fraction was extracted by adding n-butanol (1.5 mL), followed by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 4 min. The absorbance of the upper layer was measured at 532 nm, with the non-specific absorbance recorded at 600 nm and subtracted from the specific absorbance values. The TBARS levels were expressed as the net absorbance normalized to the dry weight of the fungal material.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was isolated from the fungal mycelia using the Illustra RNAspin Mini Isolation Kit (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) according to manufacturer guidelines. RNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To eliminate genomic DNA contamination, RNA samples were treated with DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the Platus Transcriber RNase H-cDNA First Strand Kit (Sinapse Inc., Miami, FL, USA) following manufacturer recommendations. The quality of the synthesized cDNA was verified by PCR amplification of a fragment of the constitutive β-tubulin gene, and PCR products were analyzed via agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA samples were then diluted to 70 ng/µL for use in reverse transcription–quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR).

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

Transcript quantification was performed using a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) with the primers listed in Supplementary Table 1. For the intron retention events, one of the primer pairs was designed to flank the intronic region. The primer concentrations were optimized to obtain amplification efficiencies between 90% and 110%. Reactions were prepared with Power SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA), including ROX dye as a passive reference32.

Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method33, with the gapdh and rpb234 genes included as internal reference controls. All assays were performed in three independent biological replicates, and data shown are means ± SD. The WT strain grown under H2O2-or menadione-induced oxidative stress served as a reference for normalization.

Alternative splicing analysis

Sequencing reads from a previous RNA-Seq experiment of the Δap1 mutant strain (unpublished data) were aligned to the T. rubrum reference genome using the STAR aligner [48]. Alternative splicing events were identified using the ASpli package in R version 4.3.135. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 Bioconductor package36, applying a Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p-value cutoff of 0.05 and a log₂ fold change threshold of ± 1.0 to define significant transcriptional modulation.

For detailed characterization of the isoforms, we analyzed the reading frames, conserved regions, and protein domains in both the intron-retained and conventionally spliced isoforms of TERG_02005, TERG_01198, TERG_02669, and TERG_06409. Protein translations were generated using the ExPASy Translate Tool37(accessed September 2025), and domain predictions were performed using Ensembl Fungi38, InterPro39(accessed September 2025), and PANTHER40,41(accessed September 2025). Graphical representations of the predicted isoforms were constructed using Illustrator for Biological Sequences (IBS 1.0)42.

Catalase activity assay

Catalase activity assays were conducted using crude extracts from WT and Δap1 strains grown in Sabouraud medium and exposed to H2O2 or menadione. Approximately 0.75 g of ground mycelium was suspended in 500 µL of Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 2 mM MgCl₂, 2 mM dithiothreitol, pH 8.0), vortexed, and centrifuged at 1,268 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant (protein extract) was collected and stored at − 80 °C until catalase activity was measured43. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) with a Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) standard curve (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

For the catalase activity assay, 2 mL of H2O2 (final concentration, 10 mM) prepared in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) was used as the substrate. As described previously, the reaction was initiated by adding 100 µg of protein extract diluted in 50 µL of the corresponding buffer30. The decomposition of hydrogen peroxide was monitored over time by measuring the absorbance at 240 nm for 10 and 15 min at room temperature. The amount of H2O2 consumed per min per mg of protein was calculated to determine the enzymatic activity of CAT in each protein extract. Phosphate buffer containing only the crude extract was used as a negative control. Data shown are means ± SD of three independent biological replicates.

Identification of consensus-binding sites for AP-1

The 5′ upstream regions (1 kb) of all genes analyzed in this study were evaluated for the presence of AP-1 binding motifs. These sites were defined by the consensus sequences 5′-TGACTCA-3’, 5′-TTACTAA-3’, and 5′-TTACGTAA-3’, along with their reverse complements (5′-TGAGTCA-3’, 5′-TTAGTAA-3’, and 5′-TTACGTAA-3′), identified using a custom Perl script based on sequence similarity. AP-1 belongs to the bZIP (basic leucine zipper) family of TFs that enable binding to specific stress response elements (STREs). The Yap Response Elements (YREs) used in this study were based on the experimentally characterized sequences44,45. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed to identify biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components associated with genes containing AP-1 consensus binding sites46.

Statistical analysis

Transcript quantification and enzymatic assay results were analyzed using an unpaired t-test. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Holm–Sidak method, and significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical significance is indicated by *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v.647 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
