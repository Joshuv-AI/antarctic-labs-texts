On the Assessment of Preserved Ratio Impaired Spirometry
Authors: Vito Brusasco, Riccardo Pellegrino
Published: 2023

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the paper by Magner and coworkers (1) on the evaluation of preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) in a large cohort of subjects with respiratory symptoms but no previous diagnosis of lung diseases. The results show that such a pattern was present in approximately 9% of the population examined by simple spirometry, a fact that reinforces the importance of the problem as well as the need for its correct interpretation. The study was designed on the premise that PRISm may represent a functional pattern in the transition from normal lung function to early airflow obstruction and that bronchodilator (BD) testing may help unravel its clinical significance. By examining pre-BD and post-BD spirometries, the authors found that subjects with post-BD PRISm had worse respiratory symptoms and disease burden than those with PRISm before BDs but normal spirometry afterward.

Apart from the clinical associations, the advantage of BD testing to unveil the nature of the physiological abnormality underlying PRISm remains elusive. If normal spirometry after BDs in subjects with pre-BD PRISm, which occurred in approximately one fourth of subjects, may reasonably reflect a BD effect compatible with mild asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, other changes after BDs have no clear physiological explanation and might have resulted from technical artifacts. For example, the post-BD obstructive pattern in subjects with pre-BD PRISm could have resulted from isolated volume response, a phenomenon more frequently observed in severe emphysema (2), but also could have been caused by a longer exhalation time or greater expiratory effort and intrathoracic gas compression after BDs. Conversely, the post-BD PRISm in subjects with no pre-BD PRISm could be explained by a shorter exhalation time or submaximal expiratory effort and less intrathoracic gas compression after BDs. Still, the majority of subjects with pre-BD PRISm also had post-BD PRISm. This might be simply due to biological variability (using the lower limit of normal, it is expected by definition that 5% of healthy subjects may meet PRISm criteria) or to a variety of disorders, including nonreversible obstructive diseases, restrictive diseases, and incomplete emptying due to expiratory muscle weakness or chest stiffness. Not surprisingly, obesity was more prevalent in cases of post-BD PRISm than in the post-BD normal spirometry group.

In 1999, i.e., long before the acronym PRISm was coined, Aaron and colleagues (3) stated that “<60% of patients with a classical spirometric restrictive pattern had pulmonary restriction confirmed on lung volume measurements.” Said in other words, a spirometric pattern with a reduction of FVC and a normal FEV1/FVC ratio could be consistent with decreased total lung capacity, i.e., restriction or increased residual volume, i.e., obstruction or expiratory muscle weakness, conditions that can be differentiated only by measuring absolute lung volumes. This was strongly recommended by the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society documents on lung function testing of 2005 (4) and 2022 (5). One would argue that matching BD response with clinical questionnaires could help in interpreting the PRISm pattern, though others may not support this approach because of the variety of conditions that are possibly associated with PRISm, the nonspecific nature of respiratory symptoms, and the variability of acute BD responses.

In conclusion, the results of Magner and coworkers’ study (1) reinforce our conviction that PRISm is a mere description of what the spirometry numbers already say, void of any physiological interpretation. We agree with the authors that subjects with PRISm require additional investigation, which is certainly preferable to a wait-and-see approach, but the first one should be the measurement of absolute lung volumes.
