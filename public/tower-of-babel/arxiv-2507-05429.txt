Reducing Disorder-Induced Backscattering in Photonic Crystal Waveguides through Inverse Design
Dominic Thompson, Antonia Neill, Nir Rotenberg, Stephen Hughes

Abstract
Photonic crystal waveguides (PCWs) allow for the engineering of photonic modes and band structures to control the flow of light and light-matter interactions within the waveguide. They have shown potential for enhancing optical nonlinearities, quantum dot single photon emissions, as well as optical buffers due to their ability to confine fields on-chip and produce slow-light modes. While these features are promising for applications in nanophotonics, PCWs are prone to high scattering losses due to disorder-induced backscattering, which has remained a significant problem for decades, across various waveguide designs. By combining a fast mode solving approach with physics-based scattering formulas and inverse design, we show how backscattering losses can be significantly reduced by over an order-of-magnitude, even when working at the same group index. We demonstrate substantial improvements for both W1-like waveguide modes as well topological waveguide modes. Our general methodology is fully three dimensional and can be used to introduce new PCWs for a variety of design metrics.

II
Theory and Methods

In this section, we first briefly discuss the GME method, and explain how it can be used to preform efficient inverse design of disorder-induced backscattering of PCWs. We start by summarizing the GME method, its required assumptions, and the resulting computational speed-up compared to conventional methods that use a full numerical solution of Maxwell’s equations. We then introduce the key analytical formula for describing backscattering that we will use throughout our optimizations, which we stress is in excellent agreement with various experiments. Finally, we describe the optimization method we employ for the inverse design, with a view to reduce backscattering, while retaining a well defined single mode within the photonic bandgap and below the light line. This ensures that the nominal mode
of interest remains lossless in the absence of disorder.

II.1
Guided Mode Expansion

The GME method solves for the photonic bands and mode structure as an eigenvalue problem for a given wave vector inside of a photonic crystal dielectric slab, returning also the field Bloch modes.

Assuming that the dielectric material is lossless and frequency-independent in the frequency range of interest, as us typical for semiconductors of interest at optical frequencies, the magnetic field modes are obtained from the following eigenvalue equation,

\bm{\nabla}\times\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon(\bm{r})}\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\right)=\left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right)^{2}\bm{H}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r}),

(1)

subject to Bloch’s boundary conditions, where
\bm{k}
indicates the wave vector
(within the Brillouin zone) of the mode, and
\epsilon(\bm{r})
is the position-dependent dielectric constant
The structure for the slabs can be seen in
Fig.
1
(a).

To solve this eigenvalue problem in the GME method, one can expand the magnetic field in a basis of the guided modes of the equivalent homogeneous slab, i.e., a slab with a specially averaged dielectric constant.
This is a huge advantage, as
the field distributions of these modes can be solved analytically. We work in the frequency domain, with the modes indexed by their wave vector
\bm{k}
, so we
can define the analytical form for the modes of the
inhomogeneous slab
to be
\bm{H}_{\bm{G}+\bm{k},\beta}^{\text{guided}}(\bm{r})
, where
\beta
is the mode index at the specified
\bm{G}+\bm{k}
point, and the
\bm{G}
s are the reciprocal lattice vectors corresponding to the homogeneous slab unit cell defined during computation.

For simplicity, we index these modes as
(\bm{G},\beta)\rightarrow\mu
. With this definition, the true photonic crystal slab modes can be written as
Andreani and Gerace 2006

\bm{H}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}\bm{H}_{\bm{k},\mu}^{\text{guided}}(\bm{r}).

(2)

For this expansion to be exact, the sum needs to be infinite, and the key approximation of the GME method is truncating this sum since it results in an incomplete basis. For high index semiconductors, however, only a few guided mode are needed in the expansion, typically two to three at most. In addition, for PCW problems, one has to ensure that the unit cell is large enough to avoid supercell effects in the transverse direction, but this
is easily checked and straightforward to satisfy for modes that are inside the photonic bands.

Substituting Eq. (
2
) into Eq. (
1
), yields

\sum_{v}\mathcal{H}_{\mu v}c_{v}=\left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right)^{2}c_{\mu},

(3)

with

\mathcal{H}_{\mu v}=\int\frac{1}{\epsilon(\bm{r})}\left[\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}_{\mu}^{*}(r)\right]\left[\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}_{v}(r)\right]d\bm{r},

(4)

where the
\bm{k}
and “guided” labels have been dropped for the sake of readability. Using this formulation, the modes and associated frequencies can be found by numerically computing the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathcal{H}
. Subsequently, the electric field modes be found using

\bm{E}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=\frac{i}{\omega\epsilon_{0}\epsilon(\bm{r})}\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r}).

(5)

Since the PCW is periodic,
we define the field modes in terms of their Bloch modes,

\displaystyle\bm{E}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=\bm{e}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}},

(6)

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=\bm{u}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}},

(7)

and we choose to normalize these modes such that
\int_{\text{cell}}\epsilon(\bm{r})|\bm{e}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})|^{2}d\bm{r}=1
for all
\bm{k}
values.

For all work done here, good numerical convergence was found when including all
|\bm{G}|<4\ [a/2\pi]
reciprocal lattice vectors (
N_{\bm{G}}=855
in total), three guided modes
and a computational unit cell with ten crystal unit cells to each side of the waveguide defect region. Using the Python package
Legume
as the implementation of GME
Minkov et al. 2020
, each
{\bm{k}}
point takes approximately 10 seconds on a standard desktop with an eight core processor, orders of magnitude faster than solvers such as COMSOL finite-element or FDTD.
Thus the entire bandstructure can be computed in minutes, using this fully 3D approach.

II.2
Basic Theory and Model of Disorder-Induced Backscattering

In a realistic PCW, the edges of the holes will be imperfect and deviate from the perfect
periodic lattice because of structural disorder. Since the disorder roughness is certainly very small compared to the size of the structural features of the PCW (e.g., a few percent is typical), it has been shown that the effects of this roughness can be studied using a perturbation theory approach. This can then be combined with Green functional theory to find the
analytical form
for the disorder-induced backscattering, as shown below
Hughes et al. 2005
;
Mann et al. 2015a
;
Patterson and Hughes 2010a
:

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{\text{back}}\rangle_{k}=

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{a\omega n_{g}}{2}\bigg)^{2}\int\int_{\text{cell}}d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}\langle\Delta\epsilon(\bm{r})\Delta\epsilon(\bm{r}^{\prime})\rangle

(8)

\displaystyle\times[\bm{e}_{k}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{e}_{k}(\bm{r})][\bm{e}^{*}_{k}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{e}^{*}_{k}(\bm{r}^{\prime})]e^{i2k(x-x^{\prime})},

where
a
is the lattice constant [see Fig.
1
(b)],
\omega
is the mode’s frequency,
k
is the magnitude of
\bm{k}
in the direction of the waveguide, and
n_{g}=c\frac{dk}{d\omega}
is the group index. This formula is the
disorder-induced loss associated with a nominally lossless waveguide mode below the light line.

The spatial integral in the loss formula is performed over the unit cell shown in Fig.
1
(b), giving the loss values per unit cell, which can easily be scaled to a particular length
(such as per mm or cm).
Specifically,
this is the intensity (or power) loss following the standard Beer-Lambert law for disorder-induced backscattering under a thin-sample approximation
Mann et al. 2013
;
Patterson and Hughes 2010b
. For longer samples, there will be multiple scattering events, resulting in Anderson localization
Crane et al. 2017b
;
Sapienza et al. 2010
;
Vasco and Hughes 2017
;
Patterson et al. 2009b
, though coupled mode equations can easily be solved to also obtain an expectation values for longer samples
Patterson et al. 2009a
, which also align very well with experimental data.

The key term describing the structural disorder is
\langle\Delta\epsilon(\bm{r})\Delta\epsilon(\bm{r}^{\prime})\rangle
, which defines the ensemble averaged disorder of the dielectric. We assume that this is well described by a statistical function with intra-hole correlation and fluctuations of the hole side walls, a model that has been used with success to explain various experiments
Crane et al. 2017a
;
Patterson et al. 2009c
;
Mann et al. 2013
;
Patterson et al. 2009a
. This function is dependent on
\Delta R
, the statistical disorder in the radius of each air hole, so that

\displaystyle\Delta\epsilon(\bm{r})=

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1})\Theta\left(\frac{h}{2}-z\right)\sum_{j}\Delta R(\tilde{\phi})

\displaystyle\times\delta\left(R_{j}-|\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho}_{j}|\right),

(9)

where
j

is the hole index,
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
are the permittivity values of the slab and surrounding material,
\Theta(h/2-z)
is the heavy-step function that brings all values outside the slab to zero,
\tilde{\phi}
is the polar angle around the hole,
R_{j}
is the radius of the hole,
\bm{\rho}
is position vector projected into the
x
-
y
plane, and
\bm{\rho}_{j}
is the
x
-
y
location of the hole.

Therefore, the change in the permittivity is localized to the edge of each hole, and is proportional to the change in radius caused by fabrication imperfections. We choose the correlation function of the change in radius to be

\displaystyle\langle\Delta R(\tilde{\phi})\Delta R(\tilde{\phi}^{\prime})\rangle=\sigma^{2}\exp\left(\frac{-R_{j}|\tilde{\phi}-\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}|}{l_{p}}\right)\delta_{j,j^{\prime}},

(10)

where
\sigma
and
l_{p}
are the standard deviation and the correlation length of the fluctuation noise on the hole edge, respectively. The effects of these parameters are visualized in
Fig.
1
(c). This correlation function has been shown to agree with fabricated structures with the statistical parameters depending on the quality of the fabrication
Hauff et al. 2022
;
Patterson et al. 2009b
;
Skorobogatiy et al. 2005
.

Finally, the full
backscattering formula,
for use in fast numerical calculations, can then be written as

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{\text{back}}\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}\bigg(\frac{a\omega_{k}n_{g}\sigma}{2}\bigg)^{2}(\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1})^{2}\int\int_{\text{cell}}d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}\Theta\bigg(\frac{h}{2}-|z|\bigg)\Theta\bigg(\frac{h}{2}-|z^{\prime}|\bigg)

\displaystyle\times\delta(R_{j}-|\bm{\rho}-\bm{\rho}_{j}|)[\bm{e}^{*}_{k}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{p}^{*}_{k}(\bm{r})][\bm{e}_{k}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{p}_{k}(\bm{r}^{\prime})]\exp\bigg(\frac{-R_{j}|\tilde{\phi}-\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}|}{l_{p}}+i2k(x-x^{\prime})\bigg),

(11)

where
\bm{p}_{k}(\bm{r})
is the polarization density is defined as

\bm{p}_{k}(\bm{r})=\left[e_{k,||}(\bm{r})+\epsilon(\bm{r})\frac{d_{k,\perp}(\bm{r})}{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}}\right],

(12)

which accounts for local field corrections; note here,
that
e_{k,||}(\bm{r})
is the component of the electric field parallel to the hole edge, and
d_{k,\perp}(\bm{r})
is the parallel component of the displacement field.
It is important to highlight that
due to local-field corrections, the effect os disorder is not symmetrical, and, e.g., can also cause disorder-induced frequency shifts of the mode
Mann et al. 2015b
.

Thus, from
Eq. (
II.2
), we see that the backscattering is dependent largely on two terms: the group index, and the electric field mode around the hole edges. The group index, as previously mentioned, is
n_{g}=c/{v_{g}}
, where
v_{g}
is the group velocity. The group index is proportional to the inverse of the band curvature, and characterizes the speed the light travels through the waveguide.
Since this factor simply provides a scaling to the loss, and
what matters most is the loss per slow down factor.
Thus for the main design problem, as is made clear from the analytical formula, Eq. (
II.2
), we are primarilyinterested reducing the field around the hole edge since when a large field interacts with perturbations in the hole edge, it results in backscattering.

We highlight that the above loss derivation is relevant for regions of the band structure where the frequency is single-valued within the band structure (i.e., single moded). If the frequency is double (or multi) moded, then the two, or modes, will couple to one another, increasing losses
Kuramochi et al. 2005
;
Parini et al. 2008
. While these additional losses could also be computed, it is not of interest for this current work, and we will also require that the designs of interest remain single mode in a frequency region of interest.

II.3
Inverse design process

The premise of inverse design is to treat certain design parameters,
\bm{x}
, as inputs to a function that gives the figure of merit for the device, and then to minimize the function under designated constraints.
Fundamentally, this is an optimization problem written as:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\tilde{k}}^{\text{W1}/\text{ZIW}}(\bm{x})

,

subject to

\displaystyle g_{i}(\bm{x})\leq 0,\ \ h

{}_{i}(\bm{x})=0,

(13)

where the functions
g_{i}
and
h_{i}
represent constraints placed on the final design,
\tilde{L}_{\tilde{k}}^{\text{W1}/\text{ZIW}}
is the function giving the figure of merit, and
\bm{x}
is the design parameters. There are a variety of ways to find good solutions within the design space, but we have chosen to use gradient based optimization as it is known to be effective
Deng et al. 2024
;
Nussbaum et al. 2021
;
Nussbaum et al. 2022
. Gradient based inverse design is only possible since the derivatives can be efficiently calculated for both GME and the formula for backscattering, Eq. (
II.2
)
Minkov et al. 2020
.

A typical gradient based optimization routine can be used to minimize the figure of merit of interest. This allows for a much more efficient search of the design space than conventional brute force methods. We define the figure of merit as,

\tilde{L}_{\tilde{k}}^{\text{W1}/\text{ZIW}}(\bm{x})=\langle\alpha_{\text{back}}\rangle_{\tilde{k}}/n_{g}^{2}(\tilde{k}),

(14)

with
\langle\alpha_{\text{back}}\rangle
defined by Eq. (
II.2
). The gradient information can be found through the use of the Python automatic differentiation package
Autograd
.

While optimizing the geometry of the PCW structure, we would like to preserve certain properties of the design to ensure that we are left with a usable result. For instance, the mode of interest should stay within the photonic band gap, be single-mode, and the structure needs to remain feasible to manufacture. This imposes constraints on the optimization problem, some of which are non-convex and highly nonlinear. This is handled through the
trust-constr
method from
Scipy
, which has been shown to be effective for complex constraints and large numbers of design parameters
Zhang et al. 2021
. During optimization, solutions may be found where the constraints are violated, but the method finds a final result that satisfies the constraints as seen in Fig.
2
, panels (a) and (c).

Effective Mode Volume and Enhanced Emission from Quantum Emitters

For application of PCWs in nonlinear quantum optics,
two key design metrics are effective mode volume
(per unit cell) and Purcell enhancement of a single quantum emitter (or dipole). The effective mode volume is a measure of the distribution of the mode withing the PCW (or a measure of the local field strength more generally), and governs the strength of the light-matter interaction, with a smaller mode volume leading to stronger light-matter interactions
Manga Rao and Hughes 2007
. As the field maximum, assuming the dipole is aligned in polarization with the mode, then

V_{\text{eff}}=\frac{\int_{\text{cell}}d\bm{r}\epsilon(\bm{r})|{\bm{e}}_{k}(\bm{r})|^{2}}{{\rm max}[\epsilon(\bm{r})|{\bm{e}}_{k}(\bm{r})|^{2}]},

(15)

though, more generally, it depends on
both the polarization of the emitter and the position of the emitter
Nussbaum et al. 2022
, which we use below.

The Purcell factor is a measure
of the dipole enhanced spontaneous emission rate in the medium,
relative to a homogeneous medium
Manga Rao and Hughes 2007
. For embedded
quantum dipole emitters (such as quantum dots), treated as point dipoles, it is defined as

\text{PF}({\bm{r}}_{0})=\frac{3\pi c^{2}an_{g}|\bm{e}_{k}({\bm{r}}_{0})\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}|^{2}}{\omega_{d}^{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}}

(16)

where the quantum emitter
(at position
{\bm{r}}_{0}
)
is assumed to be in the slab medium (with dielectric constant
\epsilon_{b}
),
has a dipole in the
\hat{\bm{n}}
direction, and an emission wavelength of
\omega_{d}
. If the quantum emitter dipole aligns with the electric field, the Purcell factor can be written as

\text{PF}({\bm{r}}_{0})=\frac{3\pi c^{2}an_{g}}{V_{\text{eff}}\omega_{d}^{2}\epsilon^{3/2}},

(17)

and we see explicitly that the enhancement depends on the group index as well as the effective mode volume.

Although we find that the mode volume and Purcell enhancement for the W1 waveguide design is slightly worsened through the optimization process, we find a relatively equivariant improvement in the mode volume and Purcell enhancement for the ZIW waveguide. Since we find alternate effects from the two waveguide designs, it appears that decreasing backscattering loss does not have an inherent impact on the mode volume or Purcell enhancement. Finally, the mode structure of each of the final designs is no longer symmetric, so to couple into the waveguide, a specialized coupler may be needed.

IV
Conclusions

We have shown how disorder-induced backscattering losses can be significantly reduced using inverse design by leveraging the speed of:
(i) a physics-based analytical mode formula,
(ii) the GME method to obtain
3D modes and dispersion,
(iii)
automatic differentiation, and
(iv) effective use of existing constrained optimization algorithms.

The reduction in losses could have
a significant impact on the performance of practical PCWs across various applications in photonics. The W1 and ZIW waveguides showed a reduction in loss of 6.8 and 6.1 times respectively, compared to previous methods which at most showed a decrease of a factor of two
Mann et al. 2013
. Special attention should be drawn to the improved W1-like designs since it is the lowest backscattering loss PCW, for its group index of
n_{g}=6.73
, to our knowledge, with
L_{\tilde{k}}^{\text{W1}}=0.027
cm
-1
(
-0.117
dB/cm). Note that this is using the statistical parameters
\sigma=3
nm and
l_{p}=40
nm for the noise around the hole edge, which is what has previously been used in the literature (and compared with select experiments).

The nature of fabrication noise of course depends on the exact fabrication set up used to produce the waveguide. If the noise parameters are not known for a specific fabrication then they can either be measured using imaging or matched to the loss values measured. As can be seen from Fig.
5
, the roughness has a large impact on the backscattering value, so reducing, or at very least knowing, the roughness is an important component of decreasing loss.
In addition, this method can easily be extended to improve or constrain many other important figures of merit in the PCW. For example, the group index, mode volume, Purcell enhancements, and bandwidth can all be controlled or improved using this method, while
also keeping the losses low
Nussbaum et al. 2021
;
Nussbaum et al. 2022
.

The inverse design problem here is challenging due to its extreme nonlinearity and nonconvexity, and while the method used here, ’trust-constr’ from
scipy
, has shown promising results, it is by no means the only method that could be used. Future work to investigate alternative gradient optimization methods and reinforcement learning methods in order to fully explore the space of possible designs is important. Our work is thus the first step in designing a system that can consistently and robustly design PCWs that can be targeted to specific applications.
