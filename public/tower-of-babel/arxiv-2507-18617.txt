Diffusion as a Signature of Chaos Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Nachiket Karve, Nathan Rose, David Campbell

Abstract
While classical chaos is defined via a system’s sensitive dependence on its initial conditions (SDIC), this notion does not directly extend to quantum systems. Instead, recent works have established defining both quantum and classical chaos via the sensitivity to adiabatic deformations and measuring this sensitivity using the adiabatic gauge potential (AGP). Building on this formalism, we introduce the “observable drift" as a probe of chaos in generic, non-Hamiltonian, classical systems. We show that this probe correctly characterizes classical systems that exhibit SDIC as chaotic. Moreover, this characterization is consistent with the measure-theoretic definition of chaos via weak mixing. Thus, we show that these two notions of sensitivity (to changes in initial conditions and to adiabatic deformations) can be probed using the same quantity, and therefore, are equivalent definitions of chaos. Numerical examples are provided via the tent map, the logistic map, and the Chirikov standard map.



In 1972, the mathematician Ed Lorenz coined the popular term “butterfly effect” to describe how tiny perturbations in a system’s initial conditions, such as the fluttering of a butterfly’s wings, could ultimately lead to large-scale outcomes like a tornado
Lorenz 1972
. This property of a system being sensitive to tiny perturbations has traditionally been used to define classical chaos. However, this notion of chaos does not manifest directly in quantum systems, since they do not exhibit such sensitivity. Instead, a quantum system is considered chaotic when its classical counterpart is chaotic
Berry 1989
. In an attempt to unify our understanding of both quantum and classical chaos, recent works have proposed an alternative definition of chaos via the sensitivity to adiabatic deformations
Pandey et al. 2020
;
Lim et al. 2024
. According to this view, a system (either quantum or classical) is chaotic when a tiny change to its Hamiltonian brings about large changes in its dynamics. In this work, we show that this new definition of chaos is essentially equivalent to our traditional understanding of chaos. To demonstrate this, we introduce the “observable drift” as a probe of chaos in generic dynamical systems, which is a generalization of the probe used to measure sensitivity to adiabatic deformations in Hamiltonian systems. We then show that this probe correctly identifies classical systems that are traditionally considered to be chaotic. The underlying reason for this equivalence is that both sensitivities – to initial conditions and to adiabatic deformations – manifest as decaying correlations between observables, thereby linking two seemingly distinct characterizations of chaos under a common framework.

I
Introduction

Sensitive dependence on the initial conditions (SDIC)
Guckenheimer 1979
;
Glasner and Weiss 1993
is a defining feature of chaos in classical dynamical systems. Generally, this sensitivity is captured by the Lyapunov exponent
Mawhin 2005
;
Oseledets 1968
, with a positive value indicating exponentially fast separation of nearby trajectories. Furthermore, chaotic systems become unpredictable beyond a time-scale that is inversely proportional to the Lyapunov exponent
Vallejo and Sanjuan 2017
. Systems with multi-dimensional phase spaces are characterized by a spectrum of Lyapunov exponents, where the largest exponent is usually used as an indicator of chaos time-scales
Benettin et al. 1980
.

However, the Lyapunov time is not always a good representative of the dynamics of the system and can fail to capture transient behavior. A striking example of this is the inner solar system, where previous studies have calculated the Lyapunov time to be about
5
Myrs
Mogavero et al. 2023
. This time-scale is much shorter than the estimated age of
\sim 5
Gyrs
Bouvier and Wadhwa 2010
of the solar system, as well as the dynamical half-life of Mercury, which is around
30-40
Gyrs
Mogavero and Laskar 2021
. It is thought that while the exact positions of planets along their orbit cannot be predicted beyond the Lyapunov time, the shape of the orbits and their relative positions are stable over the lifetime of the solar system
Ito and Tanikawa 2002
;
Hayes 2007
. Another key system that exhibits this kind of transient behavior is the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou (FPUT) model
Fermi et al. 1955
. In this non-integrable chain of weakly anharmonically coupled oscillators, evolution from a long-wavelength initial condition results in the formation of a metastable state characterized by nearly quasiperiodic dynamics of solitary waves
Zabusky and Kruskal 1965
;
Flach et al. 2005
;
Zabusky 2005
;
Flach et al. 2006
;
Pace et al. 2019
;
Karve et al. 2024
;
Deng et al. 2025
. Studies of this model have led to the notion of a stochasticity threshold in the non-linearity
Izrailev and Chirikov 1966
;
De Luca et al. 1999
;
Benettin and Ponno 2011
below which the system is non-thermalizing for a duration which far exceeds the Lyapunov time
Benettin et al. 2008
;
Benettin et al. 2018
;
Reiss and Campbell 2023
;
Lando and Flach 2025
;
Karve et al. 2025
. Furthermore, the KAM theorem
Kolmogorov 1979
;
Arnol’d 1963
asserts that this behavior is generic: that is, for systems with sufficiently weak integrability breaking a large fraction of initial conditions are non-thermalizing despite the absence of smooth integrals of motion.

Furthermore, Lyapunov exponents are obtained from the evolution of tangent vectors, which characterize the evolution of trajectories that are infinitesimally close to each other. This ignores the effects of non-linear terms, and therefore, Lyapunov exponents can fail to accurately capture the dynamics of finite separations between trajectories. As shown by Perron’s counterexample
Perron 1930
;
Leonov 2003
, it is possible to construct systems with positive Lyapunov exponents, but where nearby trajectories do not diverge from each other, and systems with negative Lyapunov exponents where nearby trajectories do diverge. Another example is the symmetric tent map
Crampin and Heal 1994
(defined and discussed in more detail in later sections), where persistent periodic correlations are observed in the chaotic regime
Shigematsu et al. 1983
;
Yoshida et al. 1983
;
Dörfle 1985
. See Fig.
1
, where two trajectories stay close to each other for times orders of magnitude longer than the Lyapunov time. Initially, when the separation between the two trajectories is relatively small, they do diverge from each other exponentially rapidly. However, the separation does not grow indefinitely and instead alternates between regions of exponential growth and decay. This is a consequence of the invariant density in this regime being limited to a narrow region of the phase space, which bounds the growth of the separation between the two trajectories.

A positive Lyapunov exponent implies that the divergence of nearby trajectories is exponentially fast. However, the concept of SDIC is much broader, as even systems where nearby trajectories diverge sub-exponentially are considered to exhibit sensitive dependence. An example is the Pomeau-Manneville map
Pomeau and Manneville 1980
;
Venegeroles 2012
, where the phase space has a sticky region near the fixed point at
x=0
. Generic trajectories spend a large amount of time near this fixed point, but occasionally show sudden, unpredictable bursts. However, the Lyapunov exponent of the fixed point is zero due to this sub-exponential separation, even though errors in the initial condition grow and span the entire phase space at large times. In fact, other definitions of chaos such as those given by Devaney
Devaney 2003
;
Banks et al. 1992
and Li-Yorke
Li and Yorke 1975
do not require exponential separation. In this work, we adopt a measure-theoretic view that chaos can be defined as weak mixing, since it implies both ergodicity and SDIC
He et al. 2004
;
Werndl 2009
.

In quantum systems, on the other hand, there is no direct analogue of SDIC, since time evolution is a quasi-periodic process. Therefore, a quantum system is considered to be chaotic when its semiclassical limit is chaotic and shows SDIC
Berry 1989
;
Jensen 1992
;
Maldacena et al. 2016
;
Andreev et al. 1996
. Traditional probes of quantum chaos have focused on the spectral properties of the system, such as the level spacing statistics
Block et al. 1957
;
Bohigas et al. 1984
;
Berry et al. 1977
;
Srednicki 1999
;
D’Alessio et al. 2016
. More recent studies have established defining quantum chaos via the sensitivity to adiabatic deformations,
Pandey et al. 2020
;
LeBlond et al. 2021
;
Pozsgay et al. 2024
;
Kolodrubetz et al. 2017
;
Świȩtek et al. 2025
;
Orlov et al. 2023
;
Sharipov et al. 2024
;
Peres 1984
and probing this sensitivity using the adiabatic gauge potential (AGP) operator. This formalism is well-defined in classical Hamiltonian systems
Lim et al. 2024
;
Bermúdez Manjarres 2024
and in
Karve et al. 2025
it was shown that this probe is related to the diffusion of time-integrated perturbations. Therefore, it has been suggested that both quantum and classical chaos should be defined via the sensitivity to adiabatic deformations instead of SDIC.

In this work, we attempt to reconcile these two notions of sensitivity. We generalize the formalism developed in
Karve et al. 2025
to probing chaos via the diffusion of generic time-integrated observables and show that this probe correctly classifies systems that exhibit SDIC as chaotic. We also show that this probe allows us to classify both transient and asymptotic dynamics into four distinct classes, namely, dissipative, regular, strongly chaotic, and weakly chaotic. Regular and dissipative dynamics are identified with no diffusion of the time-integrated observable, whereas strong and weak chaos correspond to normal and anomalous diffusion, respectively. We show that absolutely decaying correlations – a feature of weak mixing – cause this diffusion and that the rate of decay determines the strength of chaos. Simple examples of discrete-time maps are provided to demonstrate the efficacy of this method.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we provide a brief introduction to relevant concepts from ergodic theory. We show that weakly systems exhibit global SDIC: that is, any error in the initial conditions of a weakly mixing system is eventually amplified to span the entire accessible phase-space. This allows us to justify characterizing weak mixing as chaos. In Sec.
III
, we introduce the observable drift, which serves as our probe of chaos. This is a direct generalization of the AGP norm
Karve et al. 2025
and in Secs.
IV
and
V
we show that it correctly identifies weakly mixing systems as chaotic and purely non-mixing systems as regular or dissipative. In Sec.
VI
, we provide numerical examples that support our claims. We finally summarize our results and conclude in Sec.
VII
.

II
Chaos as Weak Mixing

There is no universally accepted definition of chaos, and different authors associate chaos with different levels of the ergodic hierarchy
Berkovitz et al. 2006
. Qualitatively, chaos characterizes the unpredictable nature of a system; any errors in describing the initial state of a chaotic system will be further amplified in the future. In this section, we show that errors in the initial state of a weakly mixing system are amplified to span the entire accessible phase-space. We use this to justify our characterization of weakly mixing systems as chaotic. Although topological definitions, such as Devaney
Devaney 2003
;
Banks et al. 1992
and Li-Yorke
Li and Yorke 1975
, are more common, this measure-theoretic view is still accepted
Werndl 2009
;
Wang et al. 2017
;
Kukuljan et al. 2017
.
Before diving into the details and justifying this notion of chaos, we first provide a brief review of relevant concepts from ergodic theory; more details can be found in standard textbooks
Walters 1982
;
Cornfeld et al. 1982
;
Viana and Oliveira 2016
. Although we focus on discrete-time systems in this paper, all arguments can be easily extended to continuous time.

A discrete-time dynamical system is defined on a space
M
, with the map
f:M\to M
defining time evolution
x_{n+1}=f(x_{n})
of states in
M
. The trajectory of any state
x\in M
is given by the set
\{f^{n}(x)\mid n\geq 0\}
, where
f^{n}
is the
n
th iterate of the map. We will also assume that
M
is a metric space, and that the function
d(x,y)\geq 0
defines a distance between any two states
x
and
y
.

In the study of dynamical systems, it is common to consider the dynamics on an ensemble of states. The ensemble is described by a probability density
\rho_{0}(x)
, which induces a probability measure
\rho_{0}
. More specifically, we will assume that the measure
\rho_{0}
is defined on the measurable space
(M,\mathcal{B})
, where
\mathcal{B}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
M
. As the ensemble evolves with time, the probability distribution also changes. The probability of finding the state
x
in the ensemble after
n
iterations is equal to the probability of finding
f^{-n}(x)
in the initial ensemble. Therefore, the
n
th iterate of the probability measure is given by

\rho_{n}=\rho_{0}\circ f^{-n}.

(1)

Over a long time, the time-average of the probability distribution can settle down into an invariant distribution. We will refer to this as the “natural” measure, and will denote it by
\mu
. Assuming that the limit exists, this measure is given by

\mu=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\rho_{0}\circ f^{-n}.

(2)

Clearly, the natural measure is invariant under the action of the map
f
, since
\mu=\mu\circ f^{-1}
. In general, this measure is not unique, since different initial ensembles can converge to different natural measures. For example, in non-integrable Hamiltonian systems, different natural measures are confined to different energy shells.

The property of ergodicity is defined with respect to an invariant measure; here, we focus specifically on the natural measure. A system is ergodic if a generic trajectory visits almost every point in the phase space with respect to the natural measure. In other words, the state space of an ergodic system cannot have independent subsets that do not visit each other
Oxtoby 1952
. Thus, only trivial subsets of
M
can be invariant under the action of
f
, that is

\forall A\in\mathcal{B}\text{ s.t. }f^{-1}(A)=A,\text{ either }\mu(A)=0\text{ or }1.

(3)

A crucial property of ergodic systems is that time averages can be replaced by phase space averages. For any
\mu
-integrable observable
O:M\to\mathbb{R}
, Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem
Birkhoff 1931
states that for
\mu
-almost any
x\in M
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}O(f^{n}(x))=\int_{M}d\mu\ O.

(4)

Although ergodicity requires a trajectory to visit almost the entire phase space, it does not imply SDIC. Trajectories initialized close to each other can stay close indefinitely. As an example, consider irrational rotations on the circle, given by the map

\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}+2\pi\alpha\ (\text{mod }2\pi),

(5)

where
\alpha
is an irrational number. Each iteration of the above map simply rotates the state by an angle
2\pi\alpha
. This map is ergodic, since
\alpha
being irrational guarantees that any trajectory will come indefinitely close to all points on the circle. However, a localized ensemble of initial conditions will always remain localized and will never spread out over the circle. Thus, any two trajectories in such an ensemble will remain at a constant separation.

Mixing systems, on the other hand, allow nearby trajectories to spread out. A system is called strongly mixing if for any two sets
A_{1},\ A_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
, any trajectory that starts in
A_{1}
forgets its initial condition after many iterations, and the probability of finding this trajectory in
A_{2}
only depends on the size of
A_{2}
: that is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu\left(A_{1}\cap f^{-n}(A_{2})\right)=\mu(A_{1})\mu(A_{2}).

(6)

This condition is slightly relaxed in weakly mixing systems by allowing this limit to hold on average. A system is called “weakly mixing" if for any two sets
A_{1},\ A_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left|\mu\left(A_{1}\cap f^{-n}(A_{2})\right)-\mu(A_{1})\mu(A_{2})\right|=0.

(7)

Thus, one expects nearby trajectories in mixing systems to diverge away from each other, since any initial ensemble must spread over the entire phase space.

Let us now define SDIC, which is a crucial feature of chaotic systems. As prescribed by Devaney’s definition of chaos, SDIC corresponds to the fact that any blob of initial conditions, no matter how small, will eventually grow larger than a certain threshold value. We refer to this definition as “Devaney SDIC."

Definition 1

(Devaney SDIC)
.

A system described by a bounded measure space
(M,\mathcal{B},\mu,f)
with a metric
d
is considered to depend sensitively on its initial conditions if
\exists\epsilon>0
, such that for any open set
B\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mu(B)>0
,
\exists n\geq 0
for which

\sup\left\{d\left(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y)\right)\mid x,y\in B\right\}\geq\epsilon.

(8)

Note that Devaney’s definition of chaos does not prescribe the value this threshold
\epsilon
must take. If
\epsilon
is much smaller than the dimensions of the space
M
, then the degree of unpredictability is minimal. Therefore, we will define a slightly stronger version of SDIC, which requires not only that nearby trajectories diverge, but that they separate as much as possible within the space. We will refer to this condition as “global SDIC." Given a blob of initial conditions
B
, we will quantify the separation between trajectories after
n
iterations by the diameter of
f^{n}(B)
, where the diameter of any open set is defined as

\text{dia}(B)=\sup\{d(x,y)\mid x,y\in B\}.

(9)

We will primarily be concerned with initial conditions in the support of
\mu
, where

\text{supp}(\mu)=\{x\in M\mid\forall N_{x}\in\mathcal{B},\ \mu(N_{x})>0\}.

(10)

Then, our SDIC condition will require any set of initial conditions to eventually span the entire support.

Definition 2

(Global SDIC)
.

Consider a system described by a bounded measure space
(M,\mathcal{B},\mu,f)
with a metric
d
. This system is considered to depend sensitively on its initial conditions if any open set
B\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
eventually spans the entire supported space: that is,

\sup\{\mathrm{dia}(f^{n}(B))\mid n\geq 0\}=\mathrm{dia}(\mathrm{supp}(\mu)).

(11)

Clearly, SDIC is not a sufficient condition for chaos. As noted in
Werndl 2009
, one can consider an expanding map, such as
x_{n+1}=cx_{n}
, with
c>1
and
x\in(0,\infty)
, where nearby trajectories diverge exponentially fast, and yet the system remains predictable. Notably, such a map is not ergodic, since a generic trajectory is not dense in the phase-space. Thus, for a system to be chaotic, we will also require it to be ergodic.

Mixing systems are by definition ergodic, and are also known to exhibit Devaney SDIC, as shown in
Werndl 2009
;
He et al. 2004
. We will now show that weak mixing also implies global SDIC.

will eventually spread over the support of

. Thus, no matter how small the uncertainty in the initial condition, it always becomes large in the future.

Proposition 1
.

Consider a system described by a bounded measure space
(M,\mathcal{B},\mu,f)
, with a metric
d
. This system exhibits global SDIC if it is weakly mixing.

Proof.

Consider an open set
B\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
and any two points
x,y\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
. We will show that for any two open neighborhoods
N_{x}
and
N_{y}
, there exists an
n\geq 0
for which
f^{n}(B)
simultaneously intersects both neighborhoods (see Fig.
2
for an illustration). A standard result in ergodic theory states that the product space of a weakly mixing system is also weakly mixing, and therefore, ergodic
Einsiedler and Ward 2012
. Thus, there exists an
n\geq 0
such that
f^{n}\times f^{n}(B\times B)
visits
B_{1}\times B_{2}
. This implies our claim that
f^{n}(B)
simultaneously intersects
N_{x}
and
N_{y}
. Since this is true for all neighborhoods of
x
and
y
,
\sup\{\mathrm{dia}(f^{n}(B))\mid n\geq 0\}
must be at least as big as
d(x,y)
. And since this is true for all
x
and
y
in the support, the global SDIC condition must also be true.
∎

Note that this SDIC condition does not require nearby trajectories to separate exponentially fast, and therefore, is a weaker condition than requiring a positive Lyapunov exponent. Nevertheless, even in systems where nearby trajectories diverge sub-exponentially, information about the initial condition is lost at large times. Therefore, we will consider global SDIC along with ergodicity, and consequently weak mixing, to be a characteristic feature of chaotic systems.

III
Observable Drift

A feature of weakly mixing systems is that, on average, correlations decay with time. The correlation between two measurable observables
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
is defined as

\displaystyle C_{n}(O_{1},O_{2})=\int_{M}d\mu\

\displaystyle\left(O_{1}\circ f^{n}\right)\cdot O_{2}

\displaystyle-\left(\int_{M}d\mu\ O_{1}\right)\left(\int_{M}d\mu\ O_{2}\right).

(12)

Note that in ergodic systems, the correlation function is time translational invariant, that is, the amount of correlation between
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
at iterations
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
on the trajectory depends only on
n_{1}-n_{2}
. Correlations in strongly mixing systems must decay, so that

\lim_{n\to\infty}C_{n}(O_{1},O_{2})=0,

(13)

while weakly mixing systems obey a more relaxed condition

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}|C_{n}(O_{1},O_{2})|=0.

(14)

Here, we demonstrate how this property of decaying correlations can be exploited to construct a probe of chaos.

Consider a measurable observable
O
in a generic bounded system (either ergodic or non-ergodic), so that
O
has a finite long-time average on any trajectory in the phase space:

\bar{O}(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}O(f^{n}(x)).

(15)

This time average is trajectory dependent in a generic system, but is equal to the phase space average for almost every trajectory in an ergodic system. Taking inspiration from the AGP formalism in
Karve et al. 2025
, we construct a random walk for this observable, by considering the cumulative displacement of
O
from its mean along a trajectory,

\Delta_{N}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\ \left[O(f^{n}(x))-\bar{O}(x)\right].

(16)

We refer to this quantity as the “observable drift”, or
O
-drift for short.

It is easy to see that the evolution of the drift depends on the amount of correlation between different points on the trajectory. In a non-chaotic system, where the motion is predictable, the
O
-drift is typically a regular, smooth function. However, in a highly chaotic system, the values that the observable takes at each point on the trajectory can be completely random and independent of each other. See Fig.
3
for a comparison between chaotic and non-chaotic trajectories. This stark contrast allows us to identify chaotic and regular trajectories.

Figure 3

A central object in the study of random walks is the mean squared displacement, which measures the spread of trajectories in a given ensemble over time
Ibe 2013
. In a similar fashion, we study the ensemble-averaged drift variance,
\langle\sigma_{N}^{2}\rangle
, where

\sigma_{N}^{2}(x)=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\Delta_{n}^{2}(x)-\left(\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\Delta_{n}(x)\right)^{2},

(17)

and

\langle\sigma_{N}^{2}\rangle=\int d\rho_{0}\ \sigma_{N}^{2}

(18)

denotes an average over a distribution of initial conditions given by the probability measure
\rho_{0}
. Note that this is a generalization of the AGP norm, or fidelity in Hamiltonian systems
Lim et al. 2024
;
Karve et al. 2025
;
del Campo et al. 2012
;
Funo et al. 2017
. We then define the diffusion exponent
\gamma(N)
as

\gamma(N)=\frac{\ln\langle\sigma_{N}^{2}\rangle}{\ln N}.

(19)

This diffusion exponent serves a two-fold purpose, by capturing both the transient dynamics and the long-time behavior on the natural measure. The value of the exponent
\gamma(N)
is representative of how the time-averaged measure
\bar{\rho}_{N}
converges to the natural measure
\mu
, where

\bar{\rho}_{N}=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\rho_{0}\circ f^{n}.

(20)

Karve et al. 2025
, the transient dynamics can be categorized into four distinct regimes: dissipative, regular, strongly chaotic, and weakly chaotic. We verify this claim in Sec.
VI
through numerical examples. These regimes are summarized in Table

. Furthermore, as
N\to\infty
,
\gamma(N)
converges to an asymptotic value
\gamma(\infty)
, which characterizes the behavior on the natural measure
\mu

While a rigorous analysis is presented in Secs.
IV
and
V
, we briefly outline the intuition here. Many chaotic and non-chaotic systems are characterized by attractors, and therefore any sufficiently wide distribution
\rho
will go through an initial period of convergence to the natural measure where the mean drift variance decays with time. We refer to this as the dissipative regime.
In Sec.
IV
, we demonstrate that this initial decay is
\langle\sigma_{N}^{2}\rangle\sim\mathcal{O}(1/N)
in the presence of a fixed point or limit-cycle attractor. Once the distribution becomes sufficiently localized, the evolution of the drift variance is dominated by the dynamics on the natural measure. In the case of a limit-cycle, the drift variance saturates to a finite value, indicative of regular dynamics. The drift variance is also expected to saturate if the natural measure is ergodic with a pure point spectrum
Walters 1982
;
Cornfeld et al. 1982
;
Viana and Oliveira 2016
. We refer to such systems as “purely non-mixing".

On the other hand, decaying correlations in chaotic systems lead to diffusion of the drift.
If the decay of correlations on the natural measure is sufficiently fast, i.e.
|C_{n}|<\mathcal{O}(1/n)
, then each step in the random walk becomes almost independent, and the mean drift variance shows linear growth
Gyu and Hae 2017
. We characterize this normal diffusion of the drift as strong chaos. On the other hand, if the decay of correlations is slow, i.e.
|C_{n}|>\mathcal{O}(1/n)
, then super-linear growth (anomalous diffusion) of the variance is observed, which is characterized as weak chaos.

It is important to stress here that weak and strong chaos are not analogous to weak and strong mixing. In fact, any weakly (and consequently, any strongly) mixing system can exhibit either weak or strong chaos. We also note that there are a number of different names used for these regimes in the literature. For example, the weakly chaotic regime is also known as the “nearly-integrable”, “weak integrability breaking”, or “maximally sensitive” regime
Pandey et al. 2020
;
Pozsgay et al. 2024
;
Lim et al. 2024
;
Vanovac et al. 2024
;
Świȩtek et al. 2025
;
Surace and Motrunich 2023
.

In systems with long transient dynamics, the transition from decay/saturation to growth of the drift variance can be used to define a crossover time. This time is a diagnostic of when the dynamics of the natural measure starts to dominate the dynamics of the transient distribution. As we will show later, this crossover time is highly dependent on the initial distribution. Importantly, the Lyapunov exponent is agnostic to the initial distribution and fails to capture such transient behavior.

This framework readily extends to continuous-time systems, where the drift and its variance are defined by replacing the sum over
n
with an integral over time:

\Delta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\ \left[O(\vec{x}(\tau))-\bar{O}\right],

(21a)

\sigma^{2}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\ \Delta(\tau)^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\ \Delta(\tau)\right)^{2}.

(21b)

This is dubbed as the “observable diffusion hypothesis" in
Karve et al. 2025
, where it was shown that this probe accurately captures a Hamiltonian system’s sensitivity to adiabatic deformations. As noted therein, selecting an appropriate observable is essential for obtaining meaningful results. Clearly, an observable that is a constant of motion is a bad choice, since its drift is trivially zero. Additionally, observables confined to a specific subsystem may fail to reflect the global behavior. While we cannot provide a general prescription for choosing the observable, the robustness of results can be assessed by comparing the drifts of a variety of observables.

It should also be noted that this framework is conceptually very similar to the 0-1 test of chaos, introduced by Gottwald,
et al

Gottwald and Melbourne 2004
;
Gottwald and Melbourne 2005
;
Gottwald and Melbourne 2009
. This binary test distinguishes between regular and chaotic dynamics in a time-series
\phi(n)
by coupling it to a two-dimensional system via

p(n+1)=p(n)+\phi(n)\cos cn,

(22a)

q(n+1)=q(n)+\phi(n)\sin cn,

(22b)

and analyzing the nature of diffusion in this driven system. Here
c
is some positive constant in
(0,2\pi)
. In this construction, the mean squared displacement of the vector
(q(n),p(n))
is calculated, and the authors demonstrate that the corresponding diffusion exponent is
0
for regular systems and
1
for chaotic systems. The conceptual similarities between the 0-1 test and our method are obvious, and therefore, we do not expect these two methods to have major disagreements. However, the 0-1 test requires a more complicated construction, since it involves driving an external system. Further, this is a binary test and does not distinguish between strong and weak chaos. It is also not clear whether information about the transient dynamics of the system can be extracted via the 0-1 test.

IV
Non-Chaotic Attractors

We will first study the behavior of the observable drift in systems with non-chaotic attractors – specifically, fixed point and limit cycle attractors. These are simple examples of non-ergodic systems, and it is easy to see how a fixed point attractor corresponds to dissipative dynamics, while a limit-cycle attractor asymptotically approaches regular dynamics. The analysis will be focused on one-dimensional maps, but should be generalizable to higher dimensional systems.

IV.1
Fixed Point Attractor

We prove the following proposition for trajectories in one-dimensional maps that converge to a fixed point attractor.

Proposition 2
.

Consider a one-dimensional map
f
with a stable fixed point
x^{*}
, such that
|f^{\prime}(x^{*})|<1
. If a trajectory
\{f^{n}(x)\}
converges to this fixed point, then the
O
-drift variance for some observable O, given by Eq.
17
, converges to zero as
N\to\infty
. Moreover, the variance scales as
\sigma_{N}^{2}(x)\sim\mathcal{O}(1/N)
for large
N
.

Before we prove this proposition, we state some useful lemmas.

Lemma 2.1
.

If a trajectory
\{f^{n}(x)\}
converges to a fixed point
x^{*}
, then it converges exponentially fast. That is,
|O(f^{n}(x))-O(x^{*})|<C\lambda^{n}
, for some constants
C>0
and
0<\lambda<1
.

Proof.

Since
x^{*}
is a stable fixed point,
|f^{\prime}(x^{*})|<1
. Therefore, assuming that
f^{\prime}
is continuous, we can find a compact neighborhood
U
around
x^{*}
such that
|f^{\prime}(y)|<\lambda<1
for all
y\in U
. Since the trajectory
f^{n}(x)
converges to
x^{*}
, we can find a
M
such that
\forall n\geq M
,
f^{n}(x)\in U
. For all such
n
, the mean value theorem allows us to write:

\displaystyle|f^{n}(x)-x^{*}|

\displaystyle=|f(f^{n-1}(x))-f(x^{*})|

\displaystyle=|f^{\prime}(y_{n})||f^{n-1}(x)-x^{*}|,

(23)

where
y_{n}\in U
. Consequently,
|f^{\prime}(y_{n})|<\lambda
, and

|f^{n}(x)-x^{*}|<\lambda|f^{n-1}(x)-x^{*}|<\lambda^{n-M}|f^{M}(x)-x^{*}|.

(24)

Choosing
D=\sup\bigl\{\frac{|f^{n}(x)-x^{*}|}{\lambda^{n}}\bigm|0\leq n\leq M\bigr\}
, we get

|f^{n}(x)-x^{*}|<D\lambda^{n}.

(25)

Since
O
is a smooth function of
x
, its derivative must be bounded on
U
. That is,
\forall y\in U
,
|O^{\prime}(y)|\leq\frac{C}{D}
, for some positive constant
C
. Then, the mean value theorem gives us:

|O(f^{n}(x))-O(x^{*})|=|O^{\prime}(z_{n})||f^{n}(x)-x^{*}|,

(26)

for some
z_{n}\in U
. Therefore,

|O(f^{n}(x))-O(x^{*})|\leq\frac{C}{D}|f^{n}(x)-x^{*}|\leq C\lambda^{n}.

(27)

∎

Lemma 2.2
.

The drift on such a trajectory converges exponentially fast to a finite value
\Delta_{\infty}(x)
in the limit
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

We note that
\Delta_{N}(x)
converges absolutely via the comparison test, since

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}|O(f^{n}(x))-O(x^{*})|\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N}C\lambda^{n}=C\left(\frac{1-\lambda^{N+1}}{1-\lambda}\right).

(28)

And therefore,

\lim_{N\to\infty}|\Delta_{N}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{1-\lambda}.

(29)

Further,

\displaystyle|\Delta_{N}(x)-\Delta_{\infty}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}|O(f^{n}(x))-O(x^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\lambda^{N+1}}{1-\lambda},

(30)

which proves exponential convergence.
∎

Finally, we use the above two lemmas to prove our original assertion.

Proof.

Let us define
\tilde{\Delta}_{n}(x)=\Delta_{n}(x)-\Delta_{\infty}(x)
. Then, the drift variance can be written as:

\sigma_{N}^{2}(x)=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{\Delta}_{n}^{2}(x)-\left(\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{\Delta}_{n}(x)\right)^{2}.

(31)

Both terms in the above equation converge to zero as
N\to\infty
, since:

\left|\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{\Delta}_{n}(x)\right|\leq\frac{C\lambda}{(1-\lambda)^{2}(N+1)},

(32a)

\left|\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{\Delta}_{n}^{2}(x)\right|\leq\frac{C\lambda^{2}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}(1-\lambda^{2})(N+1)}.

(32b)

Therefore, we conclude that
\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sigma^{2}_{N}(x)=0
. Further note that while
\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{\Delta}_{n}(x)
can decay faster than
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
, the term
\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{\Delta}_{n}^{2}(x)
has to decay as
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
, since it is bounded from below by a term that scales as
1/N
:

\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{0}^{2}(x)}{N+1}\leq\left|\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\tilde{\Delta}_{n}^{2}(x)\right|,

(33)

for every
N\geq 0
. Therefore,
\sigma_{N}^{2}(x)
shows
1/N
scaling at large
N
.
∎

IV.2
Limit-cycle Attractor

Any trajectory that converges to a limit-cycle attractor will eventually cycle through a finite number of points. We demonstrate that in this case, the drift variance converges to a finite value, and does so at a rate
\sim\mathcal{O}(1/N)
. To demonstrate this, we will consider the
O
-drift variance on a period-2 limit-cycle, but the claim can be easily generalized to any periodic orbit.

Proposition 3
.

Consider a one-dimensional map
f
with a stable period-2 limit-cycle
\{x_{0}^{*},x_{1}^{*}\}
. The
O
-drift variance on any trajectory
\{f^{n}(x)\}
that converges to this limit-cycle converges to a positive value
\delta^{2}/4
, where
\delta=\left|\frac{O(x_{1}^{*})-O(x_{0}^{*})}{2}\right|
. Further, the rate of convergence is
|\sigma_{N}^{2}(x)-\delta^{2}/4|\sim\mathcal{O}(1/N)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let us assume that
O(x_{1}^{*})>O(x_{0}^{*})
, and that even iterates of the trajectory converge to
x_{0}^{*}
, while odd iterates converge to
x_{1}^{*}
. For simplicity, we will denote
O(f^{n}(x))
by
O_{n}
henceforth. Since
\{x_{0}^{*},x_{1}^{*}\}
form a stable period-2 cycle, both
x_{0}^{*}
and
x_{1}^{*}
are stable fixed points of the second iterate of the map
g(x)=f(f(x))
, with
|g^{\prime}(x_{0}^{*})|<1
and
|g^{\prime}(x_{1}^{*})|<1
. Following Lemma
2.1
, we have:

|O_{2m}-O_{0}^{*}|<C\lambda^{2m},\quad|O_{2m+1}-O_{1}^{*}|<C\lambda^{2m+1},

(34)

for some
C>0
and
0<\lambda<1
. That is, the even and odd iterates of
f
converge exponentially fast to
O_{0}^{*}
and
O_{1}^{*}
respectively. Further, it is easy to see that the long-time average of
O
is simply:

\bar{O}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}O_{n}=\frac{O_{0}^{*}+O_{1}^{*}}{2}.

(35)

Then,

O_{2m}-\bar{O}=O_{2m}-O_{0}^{*}-\delta,

(36a)

O_{2m+1}-\bar{O}=O_{2m+1}-O_{1}^{*}+\delta,

(36b)

where
\delta=\frac{O_{1}^{*}-O_{0}^{*}}{2}
. Consequently, the drift can be expressed as:

\Delta_{N}(x)=\zeta_{N}(x)-\frac{1+(-1)^{N}}{2}\delta,

(37)

where,

\zeta_{N}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor}(O_{2m}-O_{0}^{*})+\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{N-1}{2}\rfloor}(O_{2m+1}-O_{1}^{*}).

(38)

Thus, the drift variance can be expressed as

\sigma_{N}^{2}(x)=\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}+\xi_{N}^{2}(x)-\frac{\delta}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\zeta_{n}(x)(-1)^{n},

(39)

where
\xi_{N}(x)
is the variance of
\zeta_{N}(x)
,

\xi_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\zeta_{n}^{2}(x)-\left(\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\zeta_{n}(x)\right)^{2}.

(40)

Following Lemma
2.2
,
\zeta_{N}(x)
must converge to a finite value at an exponentially fast rate,

|\zeta_{N}(x)-\zeta_{\infty}(x)|\leq\frac{C\lambda^{N+1}}{1-\lambda},

(41)

and
\xi_{N}(x)
must converge to zero as
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
. Finally, the last term in Eq.
39
can also be shown to converge to zero, since it is equivalent to
\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\delta}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^{n}(\zeta_{n}(x)-\zeta_{\infty}(x))
, and

\left|\frac{\delta}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^{n}(\zeta_{n}(x)-\zeta_{\infty}(x))\right|\leq\frac{\delta C\lambda}{(1-\lambda)^{2}(N+1)}.

(42)

Thus, this term converges to zero at a rate faster than
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
. Therefore, the drift variance converges to
\delta^{2}/4
, and does so as
\sim\mathcal{O}(1/N)
.
∎

V
Ergodic Systems

Ergodic systems are characterized by invariant densities where phase space averages coincide with time averages on typical trajectories. As such, we will focus on drift variances on these invariant densities. We claim that the following relation between the mean drift variance and the auto-correlation function of the corresponding observable holds:

Proposition 4

(Fluctuation-Dissipation Relation)
.

Given a discrete map
f
with an ergodic natural measure and a measurable observable
O
, the
O
-drift variance averaged over the natural distribution is related to the auto-correlation function,
C_{n}(O,O)
, as:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma^{2}_{N}\rangle=\sum_{r=-N}^{N}\frac{(N-|r|)(N+1-|r|)(N+2-|r|)}{6(N+1)^{2}}C_{r}(O,O).

(43)

Proof.

We first note that:

\langle\Delta_{N_{1}}(x)\Delta_{N_{2}}(x)\rangle=\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{N_{1}}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{N_{2}}C_{n_{1}-n_{2}}(O,O).

(44)

If we denote
n_{1}-n_{2}=r
, then the number of
C_{r}(O,O)
terms in the above equation is
\text{min}(N_{1}+1-|r|,N_{2}+1)
if
r\geq 0
, and
\text{min}(N_{1}+1,N_{2}-|r|)
if
r<0
. Using the fact that

\displaystyle\sum_{N_{1}=r}^{N}\sum_{N_{2}=0}^{N}\text{min}(N_{1}+1-r,N_{2}+1)=\frac{(N+1-|r|)(N+2-|r|)(2N+3+|r|)}{6},

(45)

we can write:

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\Delta_{n}(x)\right)^{2}\right\rangle=\sum_{r=-N}^{N}\frac{(N+1-|r|)(N+2-|r|)(2N+3+|r|)}{6(N+1)^{2}}C_{r}(O,O).

(46)

A similar argument gives us:

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\Delta_{n}^{2}(x)\right\rangle=\sum_{r=-N}^{N}\frac{(N+1-|r|)(N+2-|r|)}{2(N+1)}C_{r}(O,O).

(47)

The above two equations can be combined to give us the fluctuation-dissipation relation. A derivation of the continuous time version can be found in
Karve et al. 2025
.
∎

Purely Non-Mixing Systems

With this fluctuation-dissipation relation, we can deduce the behavior of the mean drift variance given the form of the auto-correlation function. We first focus on non-weakly-mixing systems with pure point spectra. Correlations in such systems are quasi-periodic, and can take the form

C_{r}(O,O)=\sum_{\omega}A_{\omega}\cos(\omega r),

(48)

with a discrete set of frequencies
\omega
. Inserting this into Eq.
43
, we find that

\langle\sigma^{2}_{N}\rangle=\sum_{\omega}A_{\omega}\left[\frac{1}{4\sin^{2}\frac{\omega}{2}}-\frac{1-\cos(N\omega)}{8N^{2}\sin^{4}\frac{\omega}{2}}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{3}}\right).

(49)

Thus, the drift variance approaches a finite value of
\sum_{\omega}\frac{A_{\omega}}{4\sin^{2}\frac{\omega}{2}}
as
N\to\infty
. One can easily check that this is equivalent to the expression found for limit-cycles if
\omega
corresponds to a single frequency. However, the convergence is faster here, since the second term in the above expression decays as
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{2})
.

V.2
Weakly Mixing Systems

Weakly mixing systems are characterized by decaying averaged absolute correlations; that is,

\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\sum_{s=1}^{r}|C_{s}(O,O)|=0.

(50)

Thus, to study the behavior of the drift in such systems, we define the averaged correlation,
\bar{C}_{r}(O,O)
, as

\bar{C}_{r}(O,O)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{s=1}^{r}C_{s}(O,O),

(51)

with
|r|\geq 1
. Clearly, this quantity must also decay to zero as
r\to\infty
in weakly mixing systems, since

|\bar{C}_{r}(O,O)|\leq\frac{1}{r}\sum_{s=1}^{r}|C_{s}(O,O)|.

(52)

Assuming that the correlation is an even function,
C_{-r}(O,O)=C_{r}(O,O)
, it is easy to show that the fluctuation-dissipation relation can be expressed in terms of the averaged correlation as

\displaystyle\langle\sigma^{2}_{N}\rangle=

\displaystyle\ \frac{N(N+2)}{6(N+1)}C_{0}(O,O)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r=1}^{N}\frac{r(N-r)(N+1-r)}{(N+1)^{2}}\bar{C}_{r}(O,O).

(53)

The first term in the above expression always grows as
\mathcal{O}(N)
, but the behavior of the second term depends on the exact rate of decay of the correlation. Assuming that the averaged correlation is bounded by a polynomial decay rate,
|\bar{C}_{r}(O,O)|\leq\frac{K}{r^{\alpha}}
for
r\geq 1
, we find that

\displaystyle\left|\xi_{N}\right|\leq K\left\{\frac{N}{N+1}H_{N}^{(\alpha-1)}-\frac{2N+1}{(N+1)^{2}}H_{N}^{(\alpha-2)}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{1}{(N+1)^{2}}H_{N}^{(\alpha-3)}\right\},

(54)

where
\displaystyle\xi_{N}=\sum_{r=1}^{N}\frac{r(N-r)(N+1-r)}{(N+1)^{2}}\bar{C}_{r}(O,O)
is the second term in Eq.
53
and
\displaystyle H_{N}^{(\alpha)}=\sum_{r=1}^{N}\frac{1}{r^{\alpha}}
is the generalized harmonic number. Using the fact that as
N\to\infty
,

H_{N}^{(\alpha)}\sim\begin{cases}\text{constant},&\alpha>1\\
\ln N,&\alpha=1\\
\displaystyle\frac{N^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha},&\alpha<1,\end{cases}

(55)

we deduce the following. When
\alpha>1
,
\xi_{N}
grows sub-linearly with
N
, implying that the mean drift variance asymptotically approaches the linearly growing term
\frac{N(N+2)}{6(N+1)}C_{0}(O,O)
– a characteristic of strongly chaotic systems. On the other hand, if
\alpha<1
, then
\xi_{N}
can grow super-linearly, but not faster than
\mathcal{O}(N^{2-\alpha})
. In general, this implies the growth of the drift variance is super-linear and is bounded between
\mathcal{O}(N)
and
\mathcal{O}(N^{2-\alpha})
, unless specific cancellations lead to sub-linear growth in very special cases. For example, specific cancellations leading to no growth do happen in purely non-mixing systems, where
\bar{C}_{r}(O,O)
can show polynomial decay even if
\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\sum_{s=1}^{r}|C_{s}(O,O)|
does not decay. We classify systems that show anomalous growth of the mean drift variance as weakly chaotic.

A similar analysis can be performed for strongly mixing systems. Assuming that the correlation function itself is bounded by a polynomial decay,
|C_{r}(O,O)|\leq\frac{K}{r^{\alpha}}
, we again observe weak chaos if
\alpha\leq 1
, and strong chaos otherwise. Furthermore, strong chaos is also observed when the correlation function decays exponentially,
C_{r}(O,O)=Ke^{-\lambda|r|}
, for example, in Anosov systems. The asymptotic behavior of the mean drift variance is

\langle\sigma^{2}_{N}\rangle=\frac{K}{6}\coth\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)N.

(56)

The symmetric tent map

x
-drift variance. In particular, we consider two different initial distributions, one wide and one narrow. Our first distribution is the uniform distribution over

VII
Conclusions

In this paper, we have shown how the AGP probe for chaos can be extended and generalized to all classical dynamical systems. We first argue that weakly mixing systems are chaotic, since any errors in the initial conditions must eventually span the entire accessible phase-space of such systems. This allows us to argue that chaotic systems can be identified by absolutely decaying correlations: that is

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N}|C_{n}(O_{1},O_{2})|=0.

(63)

By constructing the drift of an observable as a random walk, with each step given by the deviation of the observable from its mean, we show that decaying correlations result in indefinite growth of the drift variance, whereas quasi-periodic correlations result in its saturation. The strength of chaos is identified with the rate of decay of correlations: strong chaos corresponds to a fast decay, and therefore linear growth of the variance, while weak chaos corresponds to a slow decay and anomalous behavior. Dissipative dynamics is associated with a contraction of the phase-space density and is shown to correspond to a decreasing drift variance. We have also demonstrated the efficacy of this framework by providing numerical analysis of three discrete-time systems: namely, the tent map, logistic map, and the Chirikov standard map.

We list some open problems and unanswered questions that could be the subject of future work. While we have numerically studied some well-known one- and two-dimensional discrete-time systems, it remains to be seen how well this formalism works in more complex systems. Since the 0-1 test of chaos has been applied to much more complicated systems, a direct comparison with this test in a wide variety of systems would prove useful. Moreover, this method can be directly applied to quantum systems, making it a promising tool for studying both open and driven quantum dynamics. Additionally, while we have studied the second moment of the drift in this work, we expect higher moments to reveal finer details of the system. Specifically, it would be interesting to see if higher moments allow us to differentiate between different levels of the ergodic hierarchy.

Lastly, we hope that this work contributes to bridging the gap between quantum and classical chaos. As discussed previously, while classical chaos is defined as a sensitive dependence on the initial conditions, this notion cannot be directly translated to quantum systems. Instead, quantum chaos has been associated with the sensitivity to adiabatic deformations. In this paper, we show that these two definitions of chaos are equivalent. While the drift variance (or AGP norm) is known to probe sensitivity to adiabatic changes in both quantum
Pandey et al. 2020
and classical systems
Lim et al. 2024
;
Karve et al. 2025
, we show here that it also captures SDIC in classical dynamics. Since both forms of sensitivity ultimately stem from the decay of correlations, a unified definition of chaos in both quantum and classical systems may simply rest on the decay of two-point correlation functions.
