Eccentric Hexapod external fixator assisted closed reduction and intramedullary nail fixation for the treatment of femoral shaft fractures
Authors: Zhiming Zhao, Xu Chen, Yuxi Sun, Weiguo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveClosed reduction and intramedullary nail (IMN) fixation remains the gold-standard treatment for femoral shaft fractures (FSFs). However, technical challenges persist in achieving and maintaining anatomical alignment during the closed reduction process. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical efficacy of Hexapod external fixator (HEF)-assisted closed reduction and IMN fixation in the management of FSFs.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 25 patients undergoing closed reduction and IMN fixation with eccentric HEF assistance between June 2021 and January 2023. Operative parameters, including operative duration, blood loss, fluoroscopy frequency, reduction time, radiographic union time, and complication rates, were systematically recorded. Functional outcomes were evaluated at the final follow-up using the Liu et al. criteria, a validated scoring system for FSFs rehabilitation.ResultsAll 25 patients underwent postoperative follow-up for 18–24 months (mean: 23.5 months). Closed reduction was achieved in all cases without requiring conversion to open reduction. The final radiographic evaluation confirmed radiographic union at the fracture site in all patients. A single case (4%) of delayed union (defined as incomplete bridging callus at 6 months) was managed by transitioning from static to dynamic interlocking screw fixation, achieving radiographic union at 9 months postoperatively. No instances of neurovascular injury, implant failure, deep infection, or nonunion were observed. The mean operative duration was 76.5 min (range: 46–102), with an average blood loss of 110.0 mL (range: 60–180). Fluoroscopy frequency averaged 8.4 exposures (range: 4–17), while reduction time and radiographic union averaged 9.5 min (range: 7–13) and 5.9 months (range: 3–9), respectively. According to the Liu et al. criteria, 21 cases (84%) achieved excellent outcomes and 4 (16%) were graded as good, resulting in a 100% excellent-to-good rate.ConclusionThe use of eccentric Hexapod external fixator assisted closed reduction and intramedullary nail fixation for the treatment of FSFs offers a reliable solution. Its ability to minimize soft tissue damage, reduce radiation exposure, and achieve high union rates positions it as a promising alternative to existing technologies.

Introduction

The femoral shaft fracture represents one of the most prevalent types of fractures encountered in clinical practice, constituting roughly 6% of all fractures in the human body [1]. The escalating incidence of FSFs correlates with societal advancements in urbanization and industrialization, where high-energy trauma mechanisms—including motor vehicle collisions and falls from heights—have become increasingly prevalent [2]. The displacement of femoral shaft fractures is primarily attributed to strong muscle spasm and overriding of bone fragments, exacerbated by the degree of surrounding soft tissue injury. These factors collectively contribute to the difficulty in achieving anatomical reduction through closed techniques. The majority of adult FSF cases necessitate surgical intervention [3]. Contemporary surgical management of FSFs encompasses multiple modalities: closed reduction with IMN, external fixation systems, and open reduction/internal fixation using plate osteosynthesis [4–7]. Among them, closed reduction and IMN fixation are considered the preferred methods for the treatment of FSFs [8, 9], as they can produce high union rates and low complications. In clinical practice, the closed reduction of FSFs is mainly achieved by manual reduction or with the help of simple reduction tools. However, there are still difficulties in reduction, and sometimes it has to be switched to open reduction. This not only leads to aggravated local soft tissue damage, blood supply destruction, and the risk of bone nonunion, but also increases operative duration and infection probability [10]. How to achieve minimally invasive reduction is still a challenge for orthopaedic doctors. Currently, there are rare reports on the use of eccentric HEF to assist in the closed reduction of FSFs. Therefore, in order to solve this problem and achieve closed reduction, we applied eccentric HEF to assist in closed reduction and used IMN to fix and treat FSFs, achieving good clinical results.

Methods

This research is a retrospective cohort study that included 25 patients with FSFs admitted between June 2021 and January 2023, with clinical data sourced from the hospital’s clinical database. Demographic data of patient and mechanism of injury were recorded. Collected data included age, sex, cause of injury, injured side, AO Foundation/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) classification, and surgical interval. The surgical interval was defined as the duration between radiographic confirmation of fracture and initiation of operative treatment. Additionally, surgical details such as the operative duration, blood loss, fluoroscopy frequency, reduction time, radiographic union time, and complication rates were meticulously documented. We used HEF (Shanghai Carefix Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to assist in closed reduction and IMN fixation. The cohort comprised 14 male (56%) and 11 female (44%) patients, with a mean age of 39.6 years (range: 18–65). There were 12 cases of left femoral fracture and 13 cases of right femoral fracture. According to the AO/OTA classification, there were 7 cases of 32A1, 5 cases of 32A2, 4 cases of 32A3, 5 cases of 32B2, and 4 cases of 32B3. All patients were treated with closed reduction and IMN fixation. The causes of injury were divided into 16 cases of traffic accident injuries and 9 cases of falls from heights injuries. The interval from injury to surgery was 1–5 days with an average interval of 2.7 days (Table 1).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 20–60 years; (2) closed femoral shaft fracture (AO/OTA classification 32 A or 32B only); and (3) unilateral involvement without ipsilateral lower limb injuries. Exclusion criteria included: (1) open fractures or pathological fractures; (2) non-acute fractures (> 3 weeks post-injury); (3) pre-existing systemic comorbidities (e.g., uncontrolled cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis [T-score ≤ − 2.5]) contraindicating surgical intervention.

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (approval no. 2026–107) and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. All patients or their families have signed a written informed consent form.

Preoperative preparation

Preoperative management included immediate application of skeletal traction via a tibial tubercle pin with 5–8 kg weight adjustment based on body mass index (BMI), maintained until definitive surgical fixation.

Surgical technique

Patients were positioned supine under epidural or general anesthesia, with the unaffected limb placed in a semi-lithotomy posture (hip flexion 45–90°, abduction 30–45°) to facilitate intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging of the affected femur. The contralateral lower extremity was placed on the lower leg frame, and the affected lower limb was placed flat on the surgical traction bed. All procedures and postoperative evaluations were performed by a single orthopedic trauma team to minimize interoperator variability.

After disinfection, the surgical area was covered with a sterile surgical drape. A longitudinal skin marking was created along the femoral shaft axis, intersecting a perpendicular reference line drawn from the anterior superior iliac spine to optimize entry point localization. A 3 cm incision was made with the intersection point as the center. The subcutaneous tissue was dissected layer by layer until reaching the fascia lata, which was then incised to locate the apex of the greater trochanter. This apex served as the wire entry point, directed toward the femoral medullary cavity. Sequential reaming of the intramedullary canal was performed with flexible reamers in 0.5 mm incremental sizes, and a guide wire was then inserted into the proximal medullary cavity of the fracture for subsequent use. Under the guidance of a hole in a threaded square column, a threaded half pin with a diameter of 5 mm and as parallel as possible was implanted at the proximal and distal ends of the fracture. The depth of the half pin should pass through the cortex on one side of the femur. If the fixation is unstable, bicortical half pins can be inserted into the femur beyond the fracture line to provide additional stability. The insertion points were carefully selected to be approximately 7 cm away from the fracture site. Two half pins were inserted into the internal threaded square column hole connected to the HEF, fitted into the threaded inner sleeve, and fastened with bolts. Fracture reduction was achieved through controlled manipulation of the hexapod frame’s adjustment rings under real-time C-arm fluoroscopy, supplemented by traction bed-assisted axial alignment when required. After the fracture site was aligned with the line satisfactorily, six nuts on the quick universal adjustment rod were locked, and the guide wire was inserted directly into the distal medullary cavity of the fracture site. The HEF and half pin were were completely removed, and a drill suitable for the medullary cavity was used to expand the medullary cavity. An IMN of appropriate diameter and length was inserted. Then, the distal and proximal locking nails were locked under the guidance of a positioning device, and the tail cap was installed. The incision was sutured.

The surgical techniques described in this section were based on the patient’s past surgical records.

Postoperative management

In the initial phase of recovery (0–2 Weeks), the focus is on quadriceps contraction, Ankle Pumps, and Passive Knee Motion to maintain joint flexibility and muscle tone. Weight-bearing is permitted as tolerated immediately following IMN femur surgery, initially limited to approximately 10% of the patient’s body weight. As the patient progresses to the intermediate phase (2–6 Weeks), there is a gradual transition to partial weight bearing, ranging from approximately 10–20% of the body weight. This transition is carefully monitored using a weight scale, with the assistance of dual crutches or a walker to ensure precise control of the load on the affected limb. From 6 to 12 Weeks, the weight bearing is incrementally increased to 50% of the body weight, at which point the patient may transition to using a single crutch. The progression to full weight bearing should be based on the patient’s tolerance and healing progress.

All details regarding postoperative management protocols were extracted from the patients’ medical records.

Evaluation indicators

The operative duration, blood loss, fluoroscopy frequency, reduction time, radiographic union time, and complication rates were evaluated. Functional outcomes were analyzed at the final follow-up using the Liu classification system [11], a validated scoring protocol for femoral shaft fracture rehabilitation. Excellent: Radiographic union was achieved with cortical continuity, the medullary canal remained patent, limb length discrepancy was less than 2 cm, coronal and sagittal plane angulation was less than 10°, there was no rotational deformity, and the range of knee joint flexion exceeded 90°. Good: There is a dense and continuous callus passing through the fracture site, the fracture line is unclear, the limb shortening is 2–4 cm, the angle deformity is 10–15°, the rotation deformity is less than 5°, and the range of knee joint extension and flexion is 30–90°. Fair: A unilateral callus forms at the fracture site, a fracture line is still visible, the limb shortening is longer than 4 cm, the angular deformity is more than 15°, and the range of knee joint flexion and extension is less than 30°. Poor: A nonunion of bone occurs or pseudarthrosis forms (Table 2).

Results

All 25 patients underwent postoperative follow-up for 18–24 months (mean: 23.5 months). 25 cases of FSFs were treated with closed reduction and IMN fixation assisted by eccentric HEF. Closed reduction was achieved in all cases without requiring conversion to open reduction. At the last follow-up, the X-ray showed a complete union of the fracture site. One patient with a femoral shaft fracture was found to have delayed union after 6 months of observation. The distal locking nail was removed and replaced with dynamic fixation. After further observation for 3 months, the fracture healed without any complications such as neurovascular injury, bone nonunion, infection, or nail breakage. The average operative duration was 76.5 min (range: 46–102 min), the average blood loss was 110.0 ml (range: 60-180 ml), the average fluoroscopy frequency was 8.4 times (range: 4–17 times), the average reduction time was 9.5 min (range: 7–13 min), and the average radiographic union time was 5.9 months (range: 3–9 months). Per the validated Liu classification system, functional outcomes were graded as excellent in 21 patients (84%) and good in 4 (16%), yielding an overall success rate (excellent/good) of 100%. Among the four cases rated as good, one patient had a 4° external rotation deformity of the femur, another patient had a normal knee joint extension with a maximum flexion of 80°, and two patients showed posterior bowing deformities of the femur, measuring 12° and 10° respectively. Discussion

In the surgical treatment of FSFs, the accuracy of closed reduction and the smooth insertion of IMN guide wire into the distal medullary cavity of the fracture are key factors for the success of the surgery [12, 13]. Muscles cover the entire length of the femur, and the bony landmarks are not obvious. Conventional manual traction techniques frequently fail to achieve multiplanar fracture alignment due to inadequate control of rotational and angular displacements. In addition, the obstruction of muscles near the fracture site brings certain difficulties to closed reduction. Traditional traction beds can only achieve longitudinal traction, while angular traction in the front, back, and sides requires the assistance of assistants, which is not only time-consuming but often ineffective. Sometimes, it even has to be converted to open reduction, causing additional damage to the patient and disrupting the blood supply at the fracture site, which may lead to bone nonunion [14].

To address these issues, researchers have developed many closed reset devices. Previous innovations, including the unilateral joystick system (Jia et al. [15]) and quadrilateral external fixator (Gao et al. [16]), demonstrated partial success in clinical trials but exhibited limitations in maintaining rotational stability during IMN insertion. However, although these methods avoid causing secondary damage to the blood supply of the fracture site directly, they have the problems of poor stability and insufficient reduction accuracy when reducing through soft tissue. Our study demonstrates that the eccentric HEF-assisted closed reduction technique offers distinct advantages over existing methods. Compared to the “H” joystick described by Jia et al. [15], which reported an average operative duration of 85 min and a radiographic union time of 6 months, our approach achieved a shorter operative duration (76.5 min) and comparable union time (5.9 months) with a 100% excellent/good rate. Notably, Jia et al. [15] highlighted challenges in controlling rotational displacement with their device, whereas the HEF’s universal joint mechanism allowed simultaneous three-dimensional correction, eliminating the need for additional rotational adjustments. This may explain the reduced fluoroscopy frequency in our cohort (8.4 times vs. 12–15 times in traditional traction methods [14, 16]). Furthermore, compared to computer navigation systems [17], the TSF requires no preoperative parameter calibration, simplifying the workflow and reducing setup time. Although domestically developed reduction aids [18–20] improve fracture alignment accuracy, their clinical utility is constrained by procedural complexity, prolonged setup times, and elevated soft tissue invasiveness indices (> 15% reported pin-site infections). The Steinmann pin-based joystick technique [21, 22], while minimally invasive, demonstrates suboptimal rotational control due to smooth pin surfaces, with studies reporting 18–25% intraoperative redisplacement rates requiring revision maneuvers.

Although existing closed reduction devices have made some progress in solving the problem of fracture reduction, there is still room for improvement, especially in simplifying the structure, improving stability and accuracy, and reducing soft tissue damage. The hexapod external fixator (HEF) incorporates a six-axis universal joint system, enabling multiplanar correction of fracture deformities through controlled spatial reorientation of interconnected struts [23, 24]. Using this feature, two threaded half pins were connected to the HEF, which can be used for three-dimensional deformity reduction of FSFs. The two metal rings of the HEF can be well controlled. Once the fracture reduction reaches a satisfactory state, the nuts on the six quick universal adjustment rods can be locked to ensure the stability of the fracture site and reduce the risk of displacement. At this time, the guide wire is inserted into the distal medullary cavity of the fracture to facilitate the subsequent implantation of the IMN.

Our study demonstrated a 100% excellent/good rate according to Liu’s evaluation system, which aligns with previous studies emphasizing the importance of precise reduction. However, when compared to other studies utilizing the same evaluation criteria, our results highlight distinct advantages of HEF-assisted techniques. For instance, Yang et al. [25] evaluated femoral shaft fractures treated with IMN fixation and reported a union rate of 89.2% for grade I displaced fragments, but only 13.3% for grade IV displacements. In contrast, our cohort achieved complete radiographic union in all cases, despite including patients with complex AO/OTA classifications (e.g., 32B3). This discrepancy may stem from the HEF’s ability to achieve three-dimensional reduction, thereby minimizing third fragment displacement—a critical factor identified by Yang et al. as a predictor of nonunion. Notably, the HEF’s minimally invasive nature also reduced soft tissue damage compared to plate fixation [7], which is crucial for preserving periosteal blood supply—a factor emphasized in Liu’s evaluation system for assessing limb shortening and angular deformity. While Yang et al. highlighted the adverse effects of fragment displacement > 10 mm on healing, our technique’s precision in fragment alignment (evidenced by a mean reduction time of 9.5 min) may explain the absence of delayed union except in one case.

We applied the eccentric HEF to assist in the closed reduction of FSFs, which has the following advantages. (1) The HEF offers a precise three-dimensional reduction of fractures, minimizing the need for repeated adjustments and reducing both operation time and fluoroscopy exposure [26, 27]. (2) Its single-structure design, without excessive hinges, makes the HEF user-friendly and easy to master [28]. (3) The HEF enables closed insertion of the guide wire with high stability post-adjustment, ensuring minimal displacement during surgery and preserving the integrity of the fracture site’s blood supply. (4) The minimally invasive nature of TSF (only two half-pins inserted) also resulted in lower blood loss (110.0 ml vs. 150–200 ml in plate fixation [7]) and avoided soft tissue complications such as swelling or infection. These outcomes align with studies using double reverse traction devices [14], which reported similar union rates but required longer reduction times (15–20 min vs. 9.5 min in our study).

Despite the numerous advantages of the HEF in fracture reduction, there are certain disadvantages to consider. One such drawback is its application in patients with a body mass index (BMI) of ≥ 30, who are classified as obese. In these patients, the increased muscle strength can pose challenges during reduction [15]. The powerful muscle forces may necessitate limited open reduction to facilitate the proper alignment of fractures, which in turn, could increase the invasiveness of the procedure. Consequently, the use of HEF in obese patients with a high BMI should be approached with caution, and clinicians should be prepared to consider alternative reduction methods or combine HEF with limited open techniques to achieve optimal outcomes. Another limitation pertains to the instability associated with monocortical half-pin fixation on the femur. To address this, an additional monocortical pin is added at the proximal end of the fracture line. At the distal end of the fracture line, the monocortical half-pin can be inserted into the contralateral cortex of the femur and subsequently converted to double cortical fixation.

However, this study has certain limitations, including a small sample size and short follow-up time. In order to draw more accurate conclusions, large-scale, long-term follow-up and randomized controlled studies are needed in the future. Additionally, this study exclusively included AO/OTA type 32 A and 32B femoral shaft fractures. Type 32 C fractures were excluded because they typically present with severe comminution or segmental defects, which often require more complex reduction techniques. Future studies could evaluate the efficacy of HEF-assisted reduction in these challenging fracture patterns. However, the results of this study show that the use of eccentric HEF-assisted closed reduction and IMN fixation for the treatment of FSFs can effectively solve the problem of difficult reduction, achieve a perfect combination of precision and minimally invasive treatment, and achieve significant therapeutic effects, which is worthy of further promotion.

Conclusion

The use of eccentric Hexapod external fixator assisted closed reduction and intramedullary nail fixation for the treatment of FSFs offers a reliable solution. Its ability to minimize soft tissue damage, reduce radiation exposure, and achieve high union rates positions it as a promising alternative to existing technologies.
