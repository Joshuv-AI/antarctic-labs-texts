The efficacy of EMDR therapy in adults with a severe intellectual disability and posttraumatic stress disorder
Authors: Annemieke Hoogstad, Samantha Bouwmeester, Liesbeth Mevissen, Robert Didden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Individuals with severe intellectual disabilities (SID; IQ 20–35) are believed to be at heightened risk of developing posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). However, research targeting trauma treatment in individuals with SID remains limited, and controlled intervention studies are scarce.Objective: To investigate the effectiveness of Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) storytelling method in seven adults with SID and PTSD.Method: A multiple baseline design was used in seven adults with SID and PTSD to evaluate the efficacy of the EMDR storytelling method in reducing dysfunctional behaviours, PTSD symptoms, and challenging behaviours immediately after treatment and at three-month follow-up assessment.Results: Immediately after EMDR therapy and at three-month follow-up, significant reductions in dysfunctional behaviours and PTSD symptoms were observed at group level. At individual level, reductions in PTSD symptoms were observed in most participants, whereas results for dysfunctional behaviours were mixed. No group-level changes were found for challenging behaviours while individual outcomes were stable for the total score but varied across the BPI subscales (self-injury, stereotypical, and aggressive behaviours). No sessions were discontinued prematurely, and no adverse events were reported.Conclusions: This study provides preliminary support for the safety, suitability, and potential efficacy of the EMDR storytelling method in reducing PTSD symptoms.KEYWORDS: Severe intellectual disability, EMDR therapy, posttraumatic stress disorder, trauma treatment, EMDR storytelling method

Introduction

Individuals with severe intellectual disabilities (SID; IQ 20–35) are believed to be at heightened risk of developing posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), because they are more frequently exposed to traumatic events (Fogden et al., 2016; Santoro et al., 2018; Wigham & Emerson, 2015). Moreover, the cognitive and emotional impairments characteristic of SID may further compromise the processing of traumatic experiences (McCarthy et al., 2017). However, PTSD often goes unrecognized and untreated in this group (Hoogstad et al., 2026; Truesdale et al., 2019). PTSD is defined by exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence, followed by persistent intrusive recollections, avoidance of trauma-related stimuli, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and heightened arousal, causing significant distress or impairment (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Although awareness of trauma and its treatment in individuals with intellectual disabilities (ID) has grown in recent years (Byrne, 2022), research targeting individuals with SID remains limited, and controlled intervention studies are scarce. Nevertheless, individuals with SID may experience profound distress in response to trauma (Hoogstad et al., 2026; Kildahl & Jørstad, 2022; Mevissen et al., 2012), yet trauma-focused treatments are rarely offered in practice (Truesdale et al., 2019), leaving many without access to potentially effective interventions.

Several factors likely contribute to the above treatment gap. Assessment of PTSD in individuals with SID is particularly challenging due to severe verbal and cognitive limitations and lack of validated diagnostic instruments (Hoogstad et al., 2025). Consequently, PTSD is thought to present primarily through behavioural changes in individuals with SID (McCarthy et al., 2017; Rittmannsberger et al., 2019), and trauma exposure has been linked to increases in irritability, agitation, hyperactivity, noncompliance, and other challenging behaviours in this population (Kildahl et al., 2020b; Kildahl & Jørstad, 2022; Mevissen et al., 2012). Even when trauma-related symptoms are suspected (Rowsell et al., 2013), trauma-focused interventions are seldom implemented, possibly due to concerns about destabilizing the individual. Moreover, organizational barriers, such as limited training, time constraints, scarce resources, and high staff turnover, further hinder the recognition and treatment of PTSD in these individuals (Truesdale et al., 2019).

For individuals with SID, effective trauma interventions should place minimal demands on verbal communication and actively involve caregivers. The Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) storytelling method, originally developed for young children with limited language abilities, presents traumatic memories as a structured narrative read aloud by a caregiver rather than through verbal recollection (Lovett, 1999). This approach reduces reliance on verbal abilities and facilitates caregiver involvement, for example in constructing the narrative and interpreting the client’s reactions, making it particularly suitable for individuals with SID. Preliminary evidence suggests that the storytelling method reduces PTSD symptoms and emotional and behavioural problems in young children without ID (De Roos et al., 2025; Olivier et al., 2022; Potharst et al., 2025). A review identified two studies applying the storytelling method in individuals with SID (Byrne, 2022), both monitoring changes narratively. Barol and Seubert (2010) implemented EMDR therapy in six adults with ID, including the EMDR storytelling method in one individual with SID, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and bipolar disorder. Although traumatic memories were not directly targeted, the intervention focused on narratives related to current stress responses, supported by caregiver-administered distraction techniques, which were also applied at home. Caregivers reported that the participant became calmer and more focused following the intervention. Mevissen et al. (2012) applied the EMDR storytelling method in four individuals with SID, targeting traumatic memories (e.g. medical experiences, sexual abuse, death of loved ones), with a relative as ‘co-therapist’ during sessions, sometimes alongside a professional caregiver. Distracting tasks included buzzing devices, tapping, or auditory stimulation. Across 3–17 sessions, caregivers reported improvements in PTSD symptoms and overall functioning, with effects persisting up to 2.5 years in most cases. More recently, Hoogstad et al. (2024b) conducted an experimental single-case study using a multiple baseline design examining the EMDR storytelling method in three adults with SID and PTSD. Trauma-related memories (e.g. medical procedures, accidents, and violent incidents) were treated across four to eight sessions using distracting tasks such as buzzers, auditory stimulation, or tapping. Outcomes were assessed using standardized measures and showed reductions in PTSD symptoms, challenging behaviours, and participant-specific problematic behaviours that had emerged or worsened following trauma (hereafter ‘dysfunctional behaviours’). Although findings suggest that EMDR storytelling may facilitate trauma processing in individuals with SID, the evidence base remains limited by the small number of studies and the scarcity of experimental designs, highlighting the need for replication.

The current study therefore aimed to extend previous research by investigating the effectiveness of the EMDR storytelling method in seven adults with SID and PTSD using a nonconcurrent multiple baseline design. Consistent with the findings of Hoogstad et al. (2024b), it was hypothesized that EMDR therapy would lead to (1) a decrease in dysfunctional behaviours, (2) a reduction in PTSD symptoms, (3) a decrease in challenging behaviours, and (4) the absence of PTSD diagnosis following treatment.

Materials and methods

Design

A nonconcurrent multiple baseline design across participants was used, with weekly measurements of dysfunctional behaviour and PTSD symptom frequency across four phases: baseline (BL), intervention (I), post-intervention (PI), and three-month follow-up (FU). This design is suitable for small samples (≥3 participants; Kratochwill et al., 2013) and enhances internal validity by staggering intervention onset to control for confounding variables such as passage of time. Each phase lasted at least three weeks, with participants randomly assigned to baseline lengths of three, four, or five weeks.

Participants

Seven adults diagnosed with SID (four females, three males), aged 28–67 years (M = 41.43; SD = 13.48) participated in the study. Most participants had comorbid ASD (see Table 1). All received 24/7 care in a Dutch residential facility, fulfilled all DSM-5(TR) ≤ 6 years criteria for PTSD diagnosis, and were referred for EMDR treatment by their psychologist.

Procedure

Ethics approval was obtained from the Social Sciences Ethics Committee of Radboud University (ECSW-2020-100). Participants were recruited between October 2022 and February 2024 via EMDR therapists with treatment experience with individuals with SID from four Dutch residential facilities for individuals with ID. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥18, DSM-5(TR) diagnosis of SID and DSM-5(TR) PTSD diagnosis as assessed with Diagnostic Interview Trauma and Stressors – Severe Intellectual Disabilities (DITS-SID; Mevissen et al., 2021). A Dutch-speaking caregiver (e.g. professional caregiver, parent) was available for participation in EMDR sessions and assessments. Exclusion criteria included significant auditory impairments, participation in another trauma treatment, or anticipated changes in the context (e.g. relocation). For eligible clients, therapists contacted the participant’s legal representative, psychologist, and primary caregiver to seek participation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ legal representatives.

Following an intake with the therapist, professional caregiver, and, if applicable, a relative, the baseline phase began. Measurements were conducted via proxy reporting by a professional caregiver, who attended all therapy sessions; substitutes were used when this caregiver was unavailable. All EMDR sessions were video recorded with prior consent. The five therapists had completed at least Level I EMDR Europe-accredited training for children and adolescents. Treatment fidelity was monitored through supervision by an EMDR Europe consultant (LM; third author) before and during the study.

Intervention

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) therapy is a structured, evidence-based treatment for PTSD in children and adults without ID (De Jongh et al., 2024). The standard protocol comprises eight phases: history taking and treatment planning, preparation, assessment, desensitization, installation, body scan, closure, and re-evaluation (Shapiro, 2018), based on the Adaptive Information Processing model, which posits that psychopathology arises from maladaptively stored memories. In this study, the EMDR storytelling method (Lovett, 1999), an adaptation suitable for individuals with SID, was used without further modifications. Traumatic memories are activated via a narrative read aloud by a caregiver, accompanied by a concurrent distracting task (e.g. tactile or auditory) and optionally supported with objects or pictures. The method integrates the standard phases into broader steps: history taking, narrative preparation, an integrated reprocessing phase (memory activation, desensitization, installation of positive cognitions, body scan, closure), and re-evaluation. Emotional and physical responses are assessed jointly by the therapist and caregiver(s). In the current study, the EMDR therapist and caregiver(s) grouped traumatic and stressful events identified on the DITS-SID timeline (see ‘measures’) into themes (e.g. sexual abuse, distressing medical experiences) and selected the theme most closely related to the participant’s current symptoms, based on symptom onset or exacerbation and/or a thematic link with the complaints. Events within this theme were integrated into a narrative co-constructed by the therapist and a caregiver familiar with the participant’s abilities, tailored to the participant’s level of understanding. Historical information and narrative details were provided, whenever possible, by caregivers who had directly witnessed the events; otherwise, by caregivers closely familiar with the client’s life history. Treatment started with the narrative considered most relevant to the participant’s symptoms. During the EMDR sessions, caregivers rated story-related distress using the 0–10 Subjective Units of Disturbance scale. Participants received weekly EMDR sessions of 30–60 min, attended by a professional caregiver and, for one participant, also a relative. No sessions were discontinued prematurely, and no adverse events were reported. Some sessions occurred less frequently due to illness, vacations, or other events (participants 3, 6, and 7: ∼1 session per 1.5 weeks; participant 2: every 2 weeks). When sessions could not take place, they were rescheduled. Given limited evidence on the EMDR storytelling method in adults with SID (Byrne, 2022; McNally et al., 2021), the intervention duration was not predetermined. Treatment continued until trauma-related memories no longer evoked visible tension during EMDR sessions.

Measures

Visual analog scale

Dysfunctional behaviours were defined as individualized behaviours that emerged or worsened after traumatic events or were thematically linked to these, including PTSD symptoms and other problematic behaviours, and were therefore assumed to be trauma-related. For each participant, four to five relevant dysfunctional behaviours were collaboratively selected by the researcher and therapist. These behaviours were monitored by professional caregivers who worked closely with the participant, four caregivers for most participants, and for participant 4 the entire caregiver team, using a web-based application (E-SCED; http://www.e-sced.nl). The professional caregivers rated the occurrence on a 0 (‘not at all’) to 100 (‘very often’) Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at least three times per week. The VAS provides a practical, sensitive, and reproducible measure suitable for clinical settings without specialized training (Klimek et al., 2017), and weekly reminders and feedback supported consistent data entry.

Diagnostic Interview Trauma and Stressors – Severe Intellectual Disability

PTSD symptoms were assessed using the Diagnostic Interview Trauma and Stressors – Severe Intellectual Disability (DITS-SID; Mevissen et al., 2021), designed for individuals with moderate or severe ID based on DSM-5(TR) criteria for children aged six and under. Due to limited verbal communication skills, the interview is conducted with their caregiver or relative. It begins by identifying traumatic and stressful life events (visualized on a timeline), followed by 39 items assessing PTSD symptoms and four items evaluating ‘atypical’ symptoms (e.g. changes in eating, self-care, adaptability, and stereotypical behaviours). An interference score (0–8) quantifies the impact of symptoms on daily functioning. Each item is rated as ‘yes,’ ‘no,’ or ‘other.’ The scoring form determines whether DSM-5(TR) PTSD criteria for young children are met. For longitudinal assessment, a follow-up version of the interview is available. This version begins by identifying any new traumatic or stressful events since the previous administration, followed by an evaluation of PTSD and ‘atypical’ symptoms. In the current study, the DITS-SID follow-up version was administered weekly throughout all study phases to monitor distressing events and changes in PTSD symptomatology over time. The instrument demonstrates good psychometric properties: interrater reliability is good to excellent (k = .60–1.00) and satisfactory convergent validity with established behavioural and trauma measures (e.g. Aberrant Behaviour Checklist, Child and Adolescent Trauma Screener 3-6Y; Hoogstad et al., 2024a, 2024c, 2026).

Behaviour Problems Inventory

Challenging behaviours were assessed using the 51-item informant-based Behaviour Problems Inventory (BPI; Rojahn et al., 2001), which evaluates the frequency (0 = ‘never' to 4 = ‘once an hour') and severity (1 = ‘mild' to 3 = ‘severe') of self-injurious, stereotypic, and aggressive/destructive behaviours. The BPI demonstrates acceptable to excellent psychometric properties, including internal consistency (α = .40–.90), moderate to excellent inter-rater reliability (r = .41–.80; ICC = .58–.89), generally fair to excellent test–retest reliability (r = .28–.70; ICC = .65–.93), and convergent validity with related behavioural measures (e.g. ABC, ICAP) (Dumont et al., 2014; Gonzalez et al., 2009; Van Ingen et al., 2010). In this study, professional caregivers completed the BPI at BL, PI, and FU.

List with Changes

To monitor contextual influences on study outcomes, a custom-made List with Changes (LwC) was administered weekly to professional caregivers and was used descriptively. The checklist captured relevant changes in domains such as medication, daily activities, or living environment (e.g. routine changes, relocation, or roommate changes), staff stability (e.g. turnover or change of primary caregivers), other interventions (e.g. physiotherapy or speech therapy), and any additional contextual factors deemed relevant by the professional caregiver.

EMDR session form

A session form developed for this study was used to document the course of EMDR sessions and any (negative) side effects. For each session, the therapist recorded the treated memories, how often the story was read, type of support used to activate the traumatic memory (objects, pictures, touch, or sounds), caregiver-rated SUD (0 = ‘no tension'; 10 = ‘high tension'), any after-effects observed by the caregiver, and other notable observations for purposes including monitoring adherence to the EMDR protocol.

Statistical analyses

Primary outcomes (dysfunctional behaviours and PTSD symptoms) were analysed using visual and statistical methods. Visual inspection examined median shifts and variability of data within and across phases. Statistical testing employed the non-parametric Permutation Distance Test (PDT; Vroegindeweij et al., 2024), which corrects for lag-1 autocorrelation, to assess median differences between phases at both individual and group level. Comparisons included BL versus PI and BL versus FU for dysfunctional behaviours and PTSD symptoms, using a liberal type 1 error rate of .1 at individual level as the power at individual level is limited. Overall significance was assessed by summing the individual p-values and using the property that p-values are uniformly distributed (Onghena, 2005). For the secondary outcome (BPI frequency), Reliable Change Indices (RCI; Jacobson & Truax, 1991) were calculated using normative data (Rojahn et al., 2001, 2012), analysing only frequency scores due to high correlation with severity scores. Group-level differences were assessed with a non-parametric randomization test: observed t-values were compared to a null distribution generated by 1,000 permutations of phase labels, with two-tailed p-values representing the proportion of permuted t-values equal to or exceeding the observed statistic. Cohen’s d and 95% confidence intervals were computed as effect size measures.

Statistical tests of dysfunctional behaviours and PTSD symptoms

Dysfunctional behaviours

Medians for VAS per participant are shown in appendix A. At individual level (see Appendix B), in participant 2 and 7 several VAS scores decreased during PI and FU. In participant 6 two dysfunctional behaviours decreased during PI, while others exhibited no substantial change. During FU, two VAS scores decreased in participant 1 and 5. At group-level, all two phase comparisons yielded significant overall p-values (BL versus PI: sum of p-values = 12.10; overall p-value = .033; BL versus FU: sum of p-values = 11.87; overall p-value = .024). These results suggest that, although individual treatment effects varied, the intervention produced statistically significant group-level reductions across all phases, which persisted through the follow-up phase.

PTSD symptoms

At individual level, in most participants PTSD symptoms significantly decreased (see Appendix C), except for the PI and FU in participant 3 and the PI in participant 7. As for group-level effects, all phase comparisons yielded significant overall p-values (BL versus PI: sum of p-values = .762; overall p-value < .001; BL versus FU: sum of p-values = .789; overall p-value < .001), indicating that, after the intervention, there were significant group-level reductions across all phases, which persisted during the follow-up phase.

Challenging behaviours

At individual level, RCI analyses showed no significant changes for the total BPI score, with mixed results across the three subscales (self-injury, stereotypical, and aggressive behaviours; see Appendix D). In participant 1 stereotypical behaviours increased during PI but both stereotypical and self-injurious behaviours decreased at FU. In participant 2 self-injury increased during PI and FU but stereotypical behaviours decreased across both phases and aggressive behaviours decreased at FU. In participants 3 and 4 stereotypical behaviours increased during both phases; in participant 4 self-injury decreased at FU, while self-injury increased in participant 3 at FU. Participant 5 showed less stereotypical behaviours at PI, and participant 6 at both PI and FU. In participant 7 stereotypical behaviours decreased at FU but aggressive behaviours increased during both phases. At group level, no significant phase differences were observed in behaviour frequency (see Table 2). Effect sizes were small to negligible (Cohen’s d = −0.12–0.18).

Discussion

This study evaluated the efficacy of the EMDR storytelling method in seven adults with SID and PTSD. Significant reductions in dysfunctional behaviours and PTSD symptoms were observed at group level immediately post-therapy and at three-month follow-up. At individual level, most participants showed reductions in PTSD symptoms, whereas results for dysfunctional behaviours were mixed. Three participants no longer met DSM-5(TR) ≤ 6 years criteria for PTSD post-intervention and at FU. These results are consistent with studies reporting reductions in PTSD symptoms and certain dysfunctional behaviours following EMDR storytelling in adults with SID (Barol & Seubert, 2010; Hoogstad et al., 2024b; Mevissen et al., 2012) and young children without ID (De Roos et al., 2025; Olivier et al., 2022; Potharst et al., 2025). EMDR appears safe and suitable for individuals with SID, as no EMDR were discontinued, no adverse events occurred, and no further protocol adaptations were needed. Session numbers and cancellations varied across participants, but were primarily related to contextual factors common in this population, such as illness or logistical constraints, rather than adverse treatment reactions. The sample varied in PTSD severity and trauma type, including single-incident (e.g. accidents) and chronic trauma (e.g. repeated sexual abuse). EMDR therapy appeared effective across these types, consistent with evidence in individuals without ID showing benefits of trauma-focused therapy for both single and multiple traumas (Hoppen et al., 2023, 2024). Given that both ID and ASD are associated with increased PTSD risk (Kildahl et al., 2019; Quinton et al., 2026), the presence of ASD in over half the participants further underscores the relevance of EMDR storytelling for this vulnerable group. Individuals with co-occurring ID and ASD may present additional characteristics relevant to trauma treatment, such as altered sensory reactivity, heightened stress responses to changes in routine, and difficulties in communication and emotional expression (Kildahl et al., 2019; Dell’Osso et al., 2024). Clinicians should therefore consider individual sensory sensitivities, communication styles, and the need for predictability when applying trauma-focused interventions. The structured, caregiver-supported nature of the EMDR storytelling method may be particularly suitable for individuals with SID and ASD, as it provides predictability and involves caregivers in interpreting behavioural and emotional responses.

At individual level, changes in dysfunctional behaviours varied considerably between participants and did not follow a consistent pattern across different behaviour types. For instance, repetitive or compulsive behaviours decreased in some participants (pp2, pp6) but increased in others (pp1, pp4). Aggressive or other externalizing behaviours improved at follow-up in participant 3 yet increased in participant 7. Similarly, behaviours that could be interpreted as manifestations of re-experiencing (e.g. yelling ‘X, don’t do it!’, or screaming ‘Daddy!’) showed different trajectories across participants. Social withdrawal behaviours were an exception, decreasing post-intervention in three participants and remaining lower at follow-up in two. In individuals with ID and ASD, challenging behaviours, which overlap with dysfunctional behaviours, may reflect underlying mental health difficulties; however, behaviour type cannot be used to identify or differentiate specific mental health disorders (Kildahl et al., 2023). Accordingly, limited specificity in behavioural expression may explain the lack of uniform change across behaviour types following EMDR therapy.

Although dysfunctional behaviours, overlapping with both PTSD symptoms and CB, decreased at group level following EMDR therapy, no reductions in CB were observed at group level as measured by the BPI. While the total BPI score remained stable at individual level, outcomes varied across its subscales (self-injury, stereotypical, and aggressive behaviours). The use of a single assessment at BL, PI and FU to assess CB may have missed variability, so findings should be interpreted cautiously. In individuals with mild ID, Versluis et al. (2025) also reported reductions in PTSD symptoms but not in CB following EMDR therapy. In the current study, a reduction in CB was anticipated following EMDR therapy. This expectation is grounded in the conceptual overlap between certain CB and core PTSD symptoms, such as hurting others, and in the clinical view that behaviours including self-injury and aggression may function as trauma-related re-experiencing phenomena in individuals with ID and ASD (Kildahl et al., 2020a). Moreover, empirical evidence suggests that PTSD symptoms may mediate the link between traumatic experiences and CB (Rittmannsberger et al., 2020), implying that effective trauma treatment should indirectly alleviate these behaviours. However, CB are multidetermined and not necessarily trauma-related, and the absence of improvement may indicate the presence of additional causal or maintaining factors, such as environmental stressors, physical discomfort, or communication difficulties (see Kildahl et al., 2023), that are not directly targeted by EMDR therapy. Moreover, in individuals with more severe ID, a limited behavioural repertoire to express internal states may further complicate interpretation, as similar behaviours (e.g. stereotypical behaviours) can serve different functions across contexts or time: sometimes reflecting trauma-related distress, while at other times driven by factors such as pain or sensory regulation. Such functional heterogeneity may partly explain why reductions in PTSD symptoms do not necessarily translate into observable changes in CB. Furthermore, for many individuals with ID and ASD, CB can become stable behavioural repertoires (Totsika & Hastings, 2009), and therefore possibly resistant to short-term change, even when PTSD symptoms improve.

Notably, one participant showed no improvement in PTSD symptoms following treatment, and several participants continued to exhibit relatively high PTSD symptom levels despite significant reductions. However, PTSD symptoms were assessed only as present or absent, which may have limited sensitivity to change and overlooked variations in symptom severity. Although untested, psychotropic medication, commonly used in individuals with SID (Song et al., 2023), or other factors such as sex or trauma type (Semmlinger et al., 2024), may also influence treatment outcomes. Further research should investigate how these factors affect EMDR efficacy in individuals with SID.

Limitations

Although the small sample size typical for SCED reduces statistical power and increases uncertainty around the group level estimates, the sample is representative of the target population, supporting cautious generalization of the findings. Potential biases in this study include lack of blinding, reliance on informant-based observations, and confounding factors such as comorbidities or medication. Additionally, variability in treatment duration and session attendance across participants may complicate the attribution of observed changes specifically to the intervention. Future studies could incorporate multiple data sources (Scott & Havercamp, 2018), including physiological measures (Vos et al., 2012), standardize the number of EMDR sessions, extend follow-up periods, and explore intensive treatment formats. Addressing practical challenges, such as therapist availability and session continuity, may enhance outcomes, while using multiple therapists or increasing session frequency could also be considered. Finally, without a comparison condition, EMDR-specific effects cannot be determined; future studies should therefore include comparison groups (e.g. storytelling without a distracting task).

In conclusion, the findings provide preliminary support for the safety, feasibility, and potential efficacy of the EMDR storytelling method in individuals with SID and PTSD. PTSD symptoms and dysfunctional behaviours improved, whereas no group-level changes were found in challenging behaviours. Findings should be interpreted with caution and replicated in larger, controlled studies.
