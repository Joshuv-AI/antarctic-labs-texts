Ethical Use of Artificial Intelligence for Processing Medical Images
Authors: Yuliya Fedorchenko, Olena Zimba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) tools employ prompts and algorithms to perform tasks that typically require human expertise, hypothesis formulation, and critical evaluation. AI enables rapid analysis of complex imaging data, automates segmentation and lesion detection, and supports real-time image-guided interventions. Deep learning architectures (CNNs, RNNs, U-Net, and transformer-based models) facilitate advanced image classification, reconstruction, and interpretation, achieving clinical accuracies above 90% in multiple domains, including coronavirus disease 2019, oncology, and rheumatology. Generative AI platforms (MedGAN, StyleGAN, CycleGAN, SinGAN-Seg) further support synthetic image creation and dataset augmentation, mitigating data scarcity while preserving patient privacy. However, the integration of AI in healthcare presents significant ethical challenges. Key concerns include algorithmic bias, patient privacy, transparency, accountability, and equitable access. Biases—such as annotation, automation, confirmation, demographic, and feedback-loop bias—can compromise diagnostic reliability and patient outcomes. Ethical deployment requires rigorous data governance, informed consent, anonymization, standardized validation frameworks, human oversight, and regulatory compliance. Maintaining interpretability and transparency of AI outputs is essential for clinical decision-making, while professional training and AI literacy are critical to mitigate overreliance and ensure patient safety.

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools employ prompts and algorithms to perform tasks that typically require human efforts, hypothesis formulation, and critical evaluation. Generative Artificial Intelligence, defined by the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) of the National Library of Medicine of the United States, is “technique that generates synthetic artifacts by analyzing training examples; learning their patterns and distribution; and then creating realistic facsimiles.” (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/mesh/2108164).

As AI tools are continuously advancing—from generating complex textual contents to producing high-resolution, color-accurate images—clinical and diagnostic imaging is adjusting to these advances.

In medical imaging, AI significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy and prognosis, enabling earlier initiation of targeted therapies.1 This capability is critical for improving clinical outcomes, streamlining decision-making, and improving research reporting.

One of the key advantages of AI lies in its ability to rapidly process complex medical images, facilitating accurate disease classification and interpretation of positive or negative test results.2

However, current use of AI tools requires upgrading ethical standards to ensure that these technologies are deployed responsibly and safely. This opinion piece is aimed to discuss the role of AI tools in medical image processing and editing and it highlights ethical considerations in their clinical and research applications.

Ethical integration of AI in medical imaging

Ethical issues in AI-driven medical imaging mirror those observed in large language models, encompassing concerns such as algorithmic bias, patient privacy, transparency, accountability, and protection of intellectual property.3 Similar to how ChatGPT can reflect biases in its training data, AI tools for medical imaging may inherit dataset biases impacting diagnostic accuracy across diverse patient populations.3 AI tools for medical image processing should incorporate safeguards akin to those proposed for large language models, including bias monitoring, privacy protection, and reproducibility verification.3

The use of AI in diagnostics, for example in deep learning models for single-lead ECG analysis, raises several ethical concerns.4 Model reliability is intrinsically tied to data quality.5 Even trained on extensive datasets, AI tools remain susceptible to the effects of data imbalance, necessitating meticulously curated, representative datasets to mitigate biased outcomes.4

Medical images employed in AI-assisted diagnostics should be managed with the same ethical rigor as illustrative or educational visuals, ensuring that content neither causes distress nor compromises patient confidentiality. The broad distribution of such images for clinical, research, or training purposes necessitates explicit patient consent and anonymization to safeguard individuals’ privacy and avoid unethically publicizing sensitive images.6

Primary datasets from patients and secondary metadata should only be used with proper authorization. Ensuring data accuracy is crucial since flawed information may lead to incorrect conclusions. Processing real-time data is preferable while historical data may not represent future populations.7

The ethical deployment of AI tools necessitates a multifaceted approach that encompasses patient privacy, user accountability, clinical safety, and equitable access. Regulatory frameworks, including the European Commission guidelines, the United States FDA oversight, and the National AI Initiative Act of 2020, underscore the need for developing AI systems that are not only technically robust but also ethically grounded and legally compliant.8

The development of artificially intelligent medical devices (AIMDs) presents key ethical challenges, including safeguarding patient privacy, preventing harm, and ensuring equity for underrepresented populations. Beyond traditional principles of autonomy, beneficence, and justice, AIMD development requires robust oversight of all stakeholders to promote transparency, fairness, and accountability. Datasets must be representative, protect participant rights, and provide mechanisms for informed consent or refusal while maintaining confidentiality. Clear and interpretable AI outputs are essential to support informed clinical decision-making and uphold patient autonomy.9

Bias in medical imaging AI may affect diagnostic accuracy and patient care. Annotation bias arises when malignant findings are overemphasized, causing AI to underdetect benign lesions, while automation bias occurs when clinicians over-rely on AI outputs, ignoring other relevant data. Confirmation bias leads radiologists to favor initial hypotheses over AI alerts, and demographic bias limits AI performance when trained on unrepresentative populations. Feedback loop bias reinforces detection of prominent cases while neglecting subtle abnormalities. These biases highlight the critical need for careful dataset design, human validation, and continuous monitoring to ensure reliable and clinically meaningful AI deployment.10

Failures in dermatology and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) studies illustrate how confounding artifacts or limited datasets can produce unreliable results, emphasizing the need for rigorous human validation.8 Foundational AI models (FMs) offer generalizability but exacerbate issues of data scarcity, computational cost, and adversarial vulnerability, necessitating careful oversight.11

To mitigate these risks, AI systems should complement rather than replace human expertise. Maintaining human oversight preserves critical skills, safeguards patient safety, and ensures that AI serves as an augmentative tool for informed decision-making. Moreover, standardized validation frameworks, such as the Checklist for AI in Medical Physics, and prospective multi-tiered testing are essential to confirm algorithmic reliability and generalizability in real-world clinical contexts.8

The ethical integration of AI in healthcare demands careful consideration of data protection. Protecting patient information requires informed consent, secure storage, encryption, anonymization, and strict adherence to regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act in the United States and the General Data Protection Regulation in the European Union, thereby safeguarding autonomy, fostering trust, and ensuring publicity of legally compliant contents. Well-defined frameworks are essential to delineate the responsibilities of health professionals, AI designers, and healthcare institutions, encompassing validation of AI outputs, mitigation of algorithmic bias, risk management, and continuous monitoring to uphold patient safety and care quality. Enhancing the transparency and interpretability of AI decisions is crucial for informed clinical judgment while acknowledging their limitations and potential human biases.12

Patient-centered research and publishing enterprise may aid in resolving issues with public awareness of AI advantages and limitations. Importantly, most patients still prefer clinicians’ oversight in AI-assisted image interpretation.13 Parallel investigations among radiographers and radiologists reveal some critical issues in AI literacy, ethical preparedness, and regulatory comprehension.1415 For instance, a European Federation of Radiographer Societies–supported study identified inadequate formal training, ambiguous accountability frameworks, and excessive reliance on technology,14 while a survey in Saudi Arabia reported that 45% of radiographers lacked familiarity with AI.15

Cross-sectional evaluations of professional readiness further underscore these challenges. One study documented limited recognition of AI’s clinical utility and identified insufficient training as the primary impediment, despite nascent organizational strategies to facilitate adoption.16 Likewise, an online survey of 562 radiographers revealed strong enthusiasm for AI—91% regarded it as the future of radiology and 89% anticipated improvements in diagnostic accuracy—yet adoption was constrained by high costs, inadequate knowledge, job security concerns, and potential risks such as errors or cybersecurity threats.17

The application of AI in medical image analysis

The application of AI in medical image analysis spans a broad spectrum, including X-ray images, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), sonography, gross photos and histopathologies.71819 AI experts often lack a thorough understanding of the physical principles underlying medical image analysis, which may compromise the accuracy and clinical utility of AI-generated graphical materials.20 AI can tailor healthcare policies to individual patients’ needs, integrating imaging and clinical data to inform customized treatment approaches, enhance therapeutic effectiveness, and reduce adverse events. Additionally, AI may support image-guided surgical and other interventions by providing real-time visual augmentation, navigation guidance, and decision-making assistance, thereby improving precision and patient outcomes.21

AI research methods in medical imaging can be broadly classified into traditional machine learning, relying on predefined features of domain knowledge, and deep learning, processing neural networks’ data to autonomously extract relevant patterns.22 While conventional machine learning has improved the speed and accuracy of image analysis, its performance remains limited. Deep learning can autonomously extract intricate hierarchical features from large-scale datasets, yielding more precise, reliable, and clinically meaningful interpretations.23

Medical images typically comprise millions of fine-grained pixels, increasing data complexity and rendering segmentation of a challenging task. Segmentation partitions images into discrete components or regions of interest (ROIs), based on texture, color, contours, histograms of gradients, and global descriptors. Accurate object detection is often size-dependent, with larger structures detected more reliably. Dimensionality reduction techniques can efficiently distill key image features while preserving critical information for downstream machine learning tasks.24

Although CNNs, RNNs, and U-Net architectures excel at local feature extraction, they are limited in modeling long-range and global dependencies. Transformer-based architectures overcome these limitations through self-attention mechanisms, offering scalable frameworks for advanced medical image analysis.25 Models such as ViT, DeiT, TransUNet, and RadFormer provide task-specific capabilities, ranging from scalable classification to semantic segmentation and natural language–assisted clinical interpretation.25

Machine learning and deep learning approaches have greatly advanced in image classification, lesion detection, and segmentation, reaching above 90% accuracy in clinical evaluations.26 Advanced neural architectures support high-resolution reconstruction, cross-modality mapping, workflow optimization, automated reporting, and quality assurance, demonstrating AI’s increasing integration into clinical decision-making.26

Medical image analysis for diagnosis

The clinical utility of AI in diagnostics has been demonstrated across diverse pathologies. For COVID-19, integration of CT-derived radiomics and 3D lesion reconstruction using Mask R-CNN enabled precise quantification of lesion volume and distribution, achieving 86% sensitivity and 85% specificity.27 Qure.AI’s FDA-cleared qXR chest X-ray platform achieves over 90% accuracy and 95% sensitivity for pulmonary diseases, including lung tuberculosis, pneumonia, and lung nodules, illustrating AI’s capacity to enhance diagnostic workflows.28 In hepatocellular carcinoma, a GoogLeNet-based classifier demonstrated approximately 91% accuracy, 92% sensitivity, and 91% specificity, further highlighting AI’s potential for precise clinical image interpretation.29 Similarly, in the field of rheumatology, AI-driven analysis of nailfold capillaroscopy images has demonstrated approximately 90% sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing normal from pathological microvascular patterns characteristic of systemic sclerosis and related connective tissue diseases.30

Benchmark datasets have played a pivotal role in algorithmic validation and comparative research. Examples include BRATS (brain tumor MRI), KiTS (kidney CT), LiTS (liver CT), MURA (musculoskeletal radiographs), MedPix (diverse curated medical images), and the NIH Chest X-ray dataset.21 These platforms provide standardized references for evaluating segmentation, classification, and pathology detection models.

AI platforms for medical image generation and analysis

Generative AI has emerged as a transformative tool for synthetic medical image creation and dataset augmentation. GAN-based frameworks, including MedGAN, StyleGAN, StyleGAN2, SinGAN-Seg, and CycleGAN, synthesize high-fidelity images across modalities (MRI, CT) and enable feature manipulation for research, training, and education.31 – 37 These models mitigate data scarcity, preserve patient privacy, and support few-shot learning and lesion-specific generation.

AI model frameworks/architectures

MedGAN is an adaptive generative adversarial network designed to produce realistic medical images while addressing common GAN limitations, such as mode collapse and unstable convergence. Through Wasserstein loss and adaptive training, it achieves improved stability, supporting few-shot learning for disease detection and lesion localization, with validation on dermatology datasets showing enhanced convergence and image quality.33

StyleGAN is a robust framework for generating MR, CT, and 3D medical images. Its progressively growing generator ensures stable training for high-dimensional outputs, while latent space transformation enables precise control of image features. Resolution-specific noise adds variability, enhancing realism and diversity.35

StyleGAN2-based generative frameworks provide an efficient solution for medical image synthesis, overcoming data scarcity and class imbalance. They produce high-fidelity, lesion-specific images that faithfully replicate anatomical structures, validated through quantitative metrics and expert clinical assessment.34

SinGAN-Seg is an AI framework that generates medical images with segmentation masks from a single annotated image, overcoming limited or restricted datasets. The synthetic data enhance training of models like UNet++, improving performance in low-data scenarios and outperforming conventional GAN-based methods.36

CycleGAN, initially created for style transfer, has been broadly applied to medical image synthesis. Image fidelity is enhanced using gradient constancy, perceptual, or Wasserstein losses, with edge maps improving structural accuracy. Generator refinements, including residual networks and attention modules, help preserve critical anatomical features.37

Standalone platforms such as MONAI Label, Bing Image Creator, and TensorFlow facilitate image annotation, segmentation, and model development. MONAI Label integrates with PACS and visualization tools for precise 2D/3D delineation.38 Bing Image Creator transforms textual prompts into photorealistic anatomical images for medical education while protecting patient confidentiality.39 TensorFlow, although requiring adaptation for DICOM processing, has demonstrated performance comparable to established clinical models.32

Additional AI-driven platforms, including 3D Slicer, DEEP:PHI, Studierfenster, and the uAI Research Portal (uRP), provide modular, extensible frameworks for multimodal medical image analysis, incorporating deep learning, GAN-based inpainting, and visualization capabilities.40414243

AI platforms for medical image processing/analysis

3D Slicer 5.6.1 is an open-source, extensible platform for medical image analysis and visualization, accommodating multimodal datasets including CT, MRI, and PET. Its modular, plugin-driven architecture enables seamless integration of advanced algorithms tailored to diverse research objectives. The platform offers high-level visualization, quantitative analysis, and statistical modeling tools, supporting in-depth interpretation of complex imaging data.40

DEEP:PHI is a no-code AI platform that enables healthcare professionals to develop advanced medical imaging models without programming expertise, increasing accessibility to AI diagnostics. Its cloud-based design reduces reliance on local hardware, supporting resource-limited institutions. The intuitive interface facilitates rapid prototyping, standardization, and reproducibility, accelerating innovation.41

Studierfenster is a free, open-access platform for 2D and 3D (bio-)medical image analysis in web browsers. It supports manual segmentation, landmark annotation, metric computation, and VR/AR visualization. AI capabilities include CNNs for cranial implant and aortic landmark detection, and GANs for aortic inpainting. User testing demonstrated high usability and reliability. Its adaptable design also enables non-human and non-medical imaging applications.42

The uRP is an advanced modular framework for comprehensive analysis of multimodal imaging, including CT, MRI, and PET. It integrates automated segmentation, registration, classification, quantitative assessment, and visualization, with adaptable modules tailored to disease-specific applications such as neuroimaging, pulmonary, and musculoskeletal studies.43

Commercial and open-access creative tools such as Canva Magic Media, Meta AI, ChatGPT, and Google Gemini enable rapid text-to-image creation with limited customization and free access. Advanced platforms like NightCafe, Stable Diffusion, Adobe Firefly, Recraft, and Ideogram offer broader stylistic flexibility within usage limits, while Edraw.AI produces structured graphics and Midjourney delivers premium artistic imagery without a free option.44

As an example, we employed Recraft AI to process an image of a healthy hand and merge it with a publicly available, open-access image of a patient with hand osteoarthritis (OA) (Fig. 1). This process produced a synthetic image. The objective in this case was to illustrate that such a method could generate medical imaging resources, enabling clinicians and researchers to predict pathological features. Importantly, this approach may also facilitate the ethical use of medical images in research articles, particularly in cases when access to patient data is limited—such as with rare diseases—or when specific pathologies are difficult to obtain for study.
