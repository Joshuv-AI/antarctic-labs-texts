Prevalence of Elevated Lipoprotein(a) and Its Association with Cardiovascular Disease in Patients with Overweight and Obesity in Argentina
Authors: Ezequiel Hernán Forte, Matias Fernando Arrupe, Leonardo Bufi, Sergio Gimenez, Augusto Lavalle Cobo, Maria Gabriela Matta, Juan Patricio Nogueira, Nicolas Federico Renna, Emiliano Salmeri, Laura Schreier, Pablo Corral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is an established independent risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). Although both obesity and elevated Lp(a) are highly prevalent, their interrelationship and combined impact on ischemic heart disease (IHD) remain incompletely understood, particularly in Latin American populations.ObjectiveTo evaluate the distribution of elevated Lp(a) across body mass index (BMI) categories, assess its association with ischemic heart disease (IHD), and determine whether this association differs across BMI categories.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter observational study including 2975 adults from specialized centers in Argentina. Patients were stratified into BMI categories: Group 1: <25, Group 2 BMI: 25–29.9, Group 3: 30–34.9 kg/m2, with exploratory subgroups ≥35 and ≥40 kg/m2. Elevated Lp(a) was defined as >50 mg/dL or >125 nmol/L.ResultsMedian Lp(a) levels decreased across higher BMI categories. The prevalence of elevated Lp(a) declined across increasing BMI categories (34.6% in BMI <25 kg/m2, 29.8% in BMI 25-29.9, 30.1% in BMI ≥30, 26.4% in BMI ≥35 and 24.6% in BMI ≥40). However, after adjustment for demographic and cardiometabolic factors, overweight and obesity were associated with higher odds of elevated Lp(a) compared with normal weight (BMI 25–29.9: OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.38–2.82; BMI ≥30: OR 1.85, 95% CI 1.22–2.81) suggesting that crude prevalence estimates and adjusted associations were influenced by differences in baseline risk profiles across BMI categories. Elevated Lp(a) was independently associated with IHD across all BMI categories, without significant interaction between BMI and Lp(a).ConclusionsIn this cross-sectional study, elevated Lp(a) was associated with prevalent ischemic heart disease across BMI categories despite lower crude prevalence among individuals with higher BMI. Because of the observational cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be made. Nevertheless, these findings support consideration of Lp(a) assessment across the BMI spectrum.

Introduction

Lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)) is a cholesterol-rich lipoprotein particle composed of an apolipoprotein B-100 molecule covalently bound to apolipoprotein(a). Elevated Lp(a) is independently associated with a higher risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), including ischemic heart disease (IHD), stroke, and calcific aortic valve stenosis.1

Lp(a) levels are largely genetically determined and exhibit minimal intraindividual variability. However, it has been suggested that chronic inflammatory states, such as those present in obesity, could modulate plasma Lp(a) levels through inflammatory and hormonal mechanisms.2 Obesity is increasingly recognized as a chronic low-grade inflammatory condition characterized by adipose tissue dysfunction, insulin resistance, and altered hepatic metabolism. Several biological pathways have been proposed to explain potential interactions between obesity and Lp(a), including inflammatory cytokine signaling, insulin-mediated regulation of apolipoprotein(a) synthesis, and changes in hepatic lipoprotein metabolism. Several observational studies have reported an inverse association between body mass index (BMI) and circulating Lp(a) concentrations, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as the “Lp(a) paradox” in obesity and metabolic syndrome.3 However, available evidence remains inconsistent, and the biological basis of these observations has not been fully established with studies reporting inverse, positive, or neutral associations between BMI and circulating Lp(a) concentrations.

Lp(a) concentrations exhibit substantial interindividual and ethnic variability, largely determined by genetic factors, particularly variation within the LPA gene. Individuals of African ancestry generally have higher circulating Lp(a) concentrations than European or Asian populations, whereas data from Latin America remain comparatively limited.4 Because Latin American populations are characterized by substantial genetic admixture and a high prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors, extrapolation of findings from other regions may not be entirely appropriate.

Both obesity and elevated Lp(a) increase the risk of cardiovascular events, and despite being frequent conditions among our patients, the relationship between excess body weight and Lp(a) has not yet been clarified; evidence is limited, and findings are inconsistent across population studies.

The presence of elevated Lp(a) in the overweight population could further increase cardiovascular risk, making potential Lp(a) reduction strategies particularly relevant in patients with increased BMI.

A previous study from our research group evaluated the prevalence of elevated Lp(a) and its association with cardiovascular outcomes in a broad population cohort.5 However, that analysis did not specifically address the potential influence of BMI categories on the association between elevated Lp(a) and ischemic heart disease. In Argentina, 61.6% of the population has excess body weight.6 The current study extends those findings by examining the association between elevated Lp(a), BMI categories, and ischemic heart disease in a large multicenter Argentine cohort.

This approach enables a more refined assessment of cardiovascular risk in patients with excess body weight.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate whether elevated Lp(a) is associated with IHD across different BMI categories and to explore whether this association differs according to BMI category.

Methods

We conducted a multicenter cross-sectional observational study in specialized centers in Argentina between January 2022 and March 2025. Adult patients with a plasma Lp(a) measurement obtained in a convenience sample were included from local clinical laboratories at each participating center as part of routine patient care. Detailed information regarding assay methodology, calibration procedures, and laboratory harmonization was not systematically collected. Lp(a) concentrations were reported by participating laboratories either in mg/dL or nmol/L. Each participant contributed a single Lp(a) measurement. Elevated Lp(a) was defined according to guideline-recommended thresholds based on the reporting unit available (>50 mg/dL or >125 nmol/L). No unit conversion was performed.

Once 3000 patients had been reached, the first cut-off for sample analysis was made. Data on weight, height, and cardiovascular history were collected. Individuals with acute inflammatory diseases or severe liver diseases were excluded. Exclusion criteria were determined based on information available in medical records and physician documentation at participating centers. No centralized adjudication process was performed. Acute inflammatory diseases and severe liver diseases were identified according to the clinical diagnoses recorded in routine care.

Variables and Statistical Analysis

We analyzed demographic characteristics and the history of diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, smoking and documented IHD.

Missing data were handled using a complete-case approach. Patients with missing values for BMI or other key variables required for the primary analyses were excluded from the analytic dataset. No imputation procedures were performed because the proportion of missing data was low (<1% of the study population).

Patients were stratified by BMI: Group 1: BMI <25 kg/m2; Group 2: BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2; Group 3: BMI ≥30 kg/m2. As exploratory subgroups, we evaluated BMI ≥35 and BMI ≥40 kg/m2.

Elevated Lp(a) was defined as >50 mg/dL or >125 nmol/L, according to European Atherosclerosis Society recommendations.7 Continuous variables were compared using non-parametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis); associations were explored using multivariable logistic regression (model 1: Lp(a) prevalence adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, smoking, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia).

IHD was defined as history of acute myocardial infarction (diagnosed by clinical criteria, ECG, cardiac biomarkers, or prior hospitalization for MI) as well as history of angioplasty or myocardial surgical revascularization. The study focused on the presence of established ischemic heart disease as a binary variable, and information regarding the timing, extent, or severity of coronary disease was not systematically available.

The primary objective of the study was to evaluate the association between elevated Lp(a) and IHD across body mass index categories. For this purpose, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed with prevalent IHD as the dependent variable and elevated Lp(a) as the main independent variable (model 2: adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, LDL-C, smoking, diabetes, statin status and family history of IHD). An interaction term between elevated Lp(a) and continuous BMI was tested to assess potential effect modification.

To explore whether the association between elevated Lp(a) and IHD differed across BMI categories, analyses were repeated within predefined BMI strata (<25, 25–29.9, and ≥30 kg/m2), and an interaction term between BMI category and elevated Lp(a) was tested.

In secondary exploratory analyses, logistic regression models were also used to examine the distribution of elevated Lp(a) across BMI categories, using BMI <25 kg/m2 as the reference group.

Age-stratified analyses were performed using separate multivariable logistic regression models within each age quartile. Complete-case analysis was used, and participants with missing values in any covariate included in the multivariable models were excluded.

Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value <0.05. All analyses were performed using Jamovi (version 2.6.24.0) and Epi Info.

The prevalence of elevated Lp(a) was similar among patients with and without diabetes mellitus (32.2% vs 31.2%; p = 0.6). Likewise, median Lp(a) levels expressed in mg/dL and nmol/L were similar between patients with and without DM: 25.4 vs 23 mg/dL (p = 0.865); 30 vs 46 nmol/L (p = 0.682), respectively.

We assessed whether BMI modified the association between elevated Lp(a) and IHD by including an interaction term between Lp(a) and BMI in a multivariable model adjusted for age, sex, smoking, diabetes, LDL-C, statin use, and family history of premature cardiovascular disease. The interaction was not significant (OR 1.0017; 95% CI 0.958–1.046; p = 0.94).

Discussion

The principal findings of this study are threefold. First, elevated Lp(a) was independently associated with prevalent ischemic heart disease (IHD) across all BMI categories. Second, median Lp(a) concentrations and the crude prevalence of elevated Lp(a) decreased with increasing BMI. Third, despite these lower crude prevalence estimates, elevated Lp(a) remained associated with IHD irrespective of BMI, and no significant interaction between BMI and Lp(a) was observed. These findings suggest that the cardiovascular risk associated with elevated Lp(a) is largely independent of body weight.

Previous studies have reported inverse, positive, and neutral associations between adiposity and circulating Lp(a) concentrations. In our cohort, median Lp(a) levels and the prevalence of elevated Lp(a) progressively decreased across increasing BMI categories, consistent with prior reports suggesting an inverse relationship between BMI and Lp(a). Similar findings have been described in large population studies, including the meta-analysis by Willeit et al,8 in which higher BMI was associated with slightly lower baseline Lp(a) concentrations.

While the magnitude of the association appeared consistent across BMI strata, formal interaction testing showed no significant effect modification, indicating that BMI category did not meaningfully influence this relationship. The non-significant interaction term suggests that the association between elevated Lp(a) and IHD was broadly similar across BMI categories. However, interaction analyses generally require substantially larger sample sizes than analyses of main effects, and the present study may have had limited power to detect modest effect modification, particularly within the highest BMI categories.

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed, including hyperinsulinemia, insulin resistance, altered hepatic lipoprotein metabolism, and inflammatory regulation of apolipoprotein(a) synthesis.9 However, evidence remains inconsistent, and the present study was not designed to evaluate these mechanisms directly.

Clinical studies suggest that diet-induced weight loss may be associated with a transient increase in Lp(a) levels among individuals with obesity, whereas bariatric surgery appears to result in modest reductions.10 In pediatric populations and in individuals with metabolic syndrome, some studies have reported both positive correlations or inverse associations, indicating that the relationship between adiposity and Lp(a) may vary according to age, sex, and metabolic status.11

Potential mechanisms remain speculative and could involve alterations in hepatic synthesis or clearance pathways,12 although these hypotheses require further investigation. However, because direct measures of adiposity were not available, the present study cannot provide mechanistic insights into the relationship between body fat distribution and Lp(a).

Previous studies have shown an inverse relationship between Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) and Lp(a) levels.13 However, Kronenberg et al did not observe a significant correlation in European cohorts, suggesting that ethnic and genetic heterogeneity may act as important modulators of this relationship.7

An important finding of the present study is the apparent discrepancy between crude prevalence estimates and adjusted associations. Although elevated Lp(a) was less frequent among individuals with higher BMI in unadjusted analyses, multivariable adjustment identified higher odds of elevated Lp(a) in the overweight and obesity categories.

Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that excess body weight increases Lp(a) concentrations. Rather, crude prevalence estimates and adjusted odds ratios address different questions. The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the direction of the association was influenced by model specification, suggesting that residual confounding, referral patterns, indication bias, overadjustment, or collider bias may have contributed to the observed findings. Because participants were recruited from specialized referral centers rather than from a population-based sample, these adjusted associations should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

We did not observe significant differences in Lp(a) concentrations between individuals with and without diabetes mellitus. This finding contrasts with several previous studies reporting an inverse association between Lp(a) levels and diabetes risk.14 However, the relatively small number of patients within the lowest Lp(a) strata and the cross-sectional design of the present study limit the interpretation of this observation.

Elevated Lp(a) levels increase the risk of IHD. Our findings are consistent with the overall body of evidence and further show that elevated Lp(a) maintains an independent association with IHD in individuals with overweight and obesity. The association between elevated Lp(a) and IHD persisted after adjustment for cardiometabolic risk factors, suggesting that Lp(a)-related cardiovascular risk is not fully explained by differences in metabolic status. The prevalence estimates observed in the present study should be interpreted within the context of a referral-based centers cohort. Because participants were recruited from specialized centers, both elevated Lp(a) and ischemic heart disease may have been overrepresented relative to the general population.

Among individuals with BMI 25–30 and those with BMI ≥30, the odds of IHD were approximately 60% higher when elevated Lp(a) is present, even after adjustment for age, sex, DM, LDL-C, statin use, and family history of premature cardiovascular disease. In a cohort of more than 4600 participants from the MESA study, neither elevated Lp(a) nor a high waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) alone increased cardiovascular disease risk; however, when both factors were present simultaneously, risk increased markedly, more than doubled compared with the group without these abnormalities. In contrast, BMI did not modify this relationship.15 Our findings differ from those reported in MESA, where the association between Lp(a) and cardiovascular events appeared to vary according to waist-to-hip ratio. One possible explanation is that BMI and waist-to-hip ratio capture different aspects of body composition. Whereas BMI reflects overall body size, waist-to-hip ratio is more closely related to visceral adiposity and fat distribution. Because measures of central adiposity were not available in the present study, we cannot exclude the possibility that effect modification may be present when adiposity is assessed using more specific anthropometric markers. Therefore, the absence of interaction across BMI categories should not be interpreted as evidence that body fat distribution is unrelated to Lp(a)-associated cardiovascular risk. Differences in study design, population characteristics, and statistical power for interaction analyses may also have contributed to the discrepant findings. Other studies have found an additive association between Lp(a) levels, BMI, and the incidence of myocardial infarction.3

These findings support the biological understanding that Lp(a) concentrations are predominantly genetically determined and minimally influenced by other factors. Consequently, the risk associated with elevated Lp(a) appears largely independent of BMI, suggest that the observed association between elevated Lp(a) and IHD was similar across BMI categories, however, causal inference is limited by the observational design. This reinforces the importance of assessing Lp(a) across all BMI categories, including in individuals with overweight or obesity, and emphasizes that elevated Lp(a) should be considered a risk factor regardless of metabolic phenotype.

Limitations

This study has several limitations.

First, the cross-sectional observational design precludes assessment of temporal relationships and does not allow causal inference regarding the associations among BMI, Lp(a), and ischemic heart disease.

Second, participants were recruited from specialized referral centers using a convenience sampling strategy. Consequently, selection and indication biases may have influenced both the likelihood of Lp(a) testing and the prevalence of cardiovascular disease within the cohort, limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader Argentine population.

Third, Lp(a) measurements were obtained from multiple local laboratories without centralized testing or formal harmonization procedures. Detailed information regarding assay type, calibration, and isoform sensitivity was not available, and inter-laboratory variability may therefore have influenced absolute Lp(a) concentrations.

Fourth, BMI was the only anthropometric measure available. More specific markers of adiposity and fat distribution, including waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, waist-to-height ratio, visceral adiposity, and metabolic syndrome components, were not systematically collected. Therefore, we were unable to evaluate whether central adiposity modifies the association between Lp(a) and ischemic heart disease.

Finally, although multivariable adjustment included major cardiovascular risk factors, residual confounding cannot be excluded. The observed discrepancy between crude prevalence estimates and adjusted associations, together with the sensitivity of the results to model specification, suggests that some findings—particularly those regarding the relationship between BMI categories and elevated Lp(a)—should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Conclusions

Patients with overweight and obesity exhibited lower average Lp(a) levels than normal-weight individuals. However, elevated Lp(a) was independently associated with prevalent ischemic heart disease in individuals with excess body weight, with a magnitude of association like that observed in normal-weight individuals. These findings suggest that elevated Lp(a) confers cardiovascular risk across all categories of BMI and reinforce the importance of assessing Lp(a) regardless of weight.
