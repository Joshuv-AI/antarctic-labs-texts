Dynamical reweighting for estimation of fluctuation formulas
Raphaël Gastaldello, Gabriel Stoltz, Urbain Vaes

Abstract
We propose a variance reduction method for calculating transport coefficients in molecular dynamics using an importance sampling method via Girsanov’s theorem applied to Green–Kubo’s formula. We optimize the magnitude of the perturbation applied to the reference dynamics by means of a scalar parameter α \alpha and propose an asymptotic analysis to fully characterize the long-time behavior in order to evaluate the possible variance reduction. Theoretical results corroborated by numerical results show that this method allows for some reduction in variance, although rather modest in most situations.

Introduction

Molecular dynamics has been developing rapidly for over 70 years
[
4
]
. It relies on statistical physics, which offers a path to understand the macroscopic behavior of matter from its microscopic description. Despite severe constraints in terms of time and space scales, it has proved to be an extremely powerful tool for carrying out
in silico
experiments, testing physical theories on a computer and also helping to make up for the absence of analytical theories. Nowadays, numerical simulations are essential for understanding the macroscopic behaviour of matter, particularly when experiments are impossible or costly, such as in high-pressure or high-temperature regimes, or when studying as yet unknown or unsynthesised materials especially for electronic and biological systems on the nanometric scale. Another major objective of molecular simulation is to calculate macroscopic quantities and thermodynamic properties, providing quantitative information about molecular systems. We refer for instance to
[
1
,
18
,
43
]
for a large panel of numerical methods in statistical physics and molecular simulation and
[
25
,
27
]
for a mathematical perspective.

One of the aims of molecular dynamics is to understand the dynamical properties of matter. Transport coefficients such as those governing particle diffusion (self-diffusion), heat conduction (thermal conductivity), or momentum transport (shear viscosity), are examples of such properties. They determine the response of a system to an external perturbation; see
[
15
,
10
]
for an general overview and
[
26
, Section 5]
for a mathematical perspective on this subject. Once these coefficients have been estimated through molecular simulation, they can be fed to macroscopic evolution equations like the Navier–Stokes equation.

A standard method for computing transport coefficients, based on molecular dynamics, utilizes the well-known Green–Kubo formula
[
20
,
24
]
. This formula expresses transport coefficients as integrated time-correlations of relevant observables for the system at equilibrium. Another approach, relying on linear response theory, involves directly simulating non-equilibrium perturbations and measuring the resulting average response, a method known as non-equilibrium molecular dynamics (NEMD).

However, estimators derived from these methods often suffer from significant statistical errors, as discussed in
[
42
]
. Achieving convergence often demands simulating long trajectories, incurring substantial computational costs. Various variance reduction techniques can be used for systems at equilibrium, in particular importance sampling
[
8
]
and control variates methods
[
31
,
32
]
. Most variance reduction techniques cannot be used as such for nonequilibrium systems, and therefore require a dedicated treatment, see for instance
[
40
,
41
,
6
,
35
]
.

Concerning importance sampling, one has to resort to dynamical versions where a bias is introduced into the dynamics, and corrected for by some exponential weight based on Girsanov’s formula. This is known as “dynamical reweighting” or “Girsanov reweightings” in the theoretical chemistry literature. For example, in
[
9
]
this method is used to compute time-correlation functions, and the references
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
present how to implement the reweighting and use it to simulate proteins, or accelerate the exploration of the phase space. In all cases, the aim is to improve the computation of path ensemble averages.

Objectives of this work

The present work aims to study and assess the efficiency of the particular method described in
[
11
,
12
]

when used as a variance reduction technique for the calculation of transport coefficients.
Mathematically, these coefficients are expressed as integrated (auto)correlations in the Green–Kubo formalism
and can therefore be reformulated as ensemble averages with respect to the path measure associated with the stochastic dynamics.
In the context of non-degenerate diffusive dynamics,
the key idea of the method we study is to add a drift term in order to bias the dynamics:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q_{t}^{\alpha}=b(q_{t}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}t+\alpha\sigma(q_{t}^{\alpha})u(q_{t}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(q_{t}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(1.1)

This modified dynamics induces a path measure that is different from that associated with the reference dynamics (
\alpha=0
).
Therefore, for consistency,
statistical estimators based on the modified dynamics (
1.1
) must include
Girsanov weights,
which represent the likelihood ratio between the reference and modified path probability measures.
In this work, we wish to understand whether
employing a perturbed dynamics of the type (
1.1
) instead of the reference dynamics
can be useful for the calculation of transport coefficients through Green–Kubo’s formula.

Our contributions

The paper makes the following contributions.

•

We first consider the following stochastic differential equation (SDE),

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q_{t}=b(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(1.2)

In order to motivate this study,
we prove for this reference dynamics that the variance of the Green–Kubo estimator is proportional to the integration time
T
,
in the limit as
T
tends to infinity (see
Proposition

2.2
).

•

We then consider the dynamics (
1.1
)
incorporating an additional drift term
\alpha\sigma u
compared to (
1.2
),
with
\alpha
a scalar and
u
a vector field.
After defining a weighted Green–Kubo-type estimator in terms of the solution to this equation,
we show that, for any fixed time
T
,
there exists a unique
\alpha
that minimizes the variance of the weighted Green–Kubo’s estimator (see
Proposition

3.3
).

•

Finally, we characterize the asymptotic behavior,
as the integration time
T
tends to
+\infty
,
of the optimal value of
\alpha
and of the amount of variance reduction obtained with this asymptotically optimal value.

We emphasize that the main contribution of this work is the asymptotic analysis of the new estimator for the Green–Kubo formula. We however also explore the non-asymptotic regime through numerical simulations. The so-obtained results are quantitatively similar to those predicted by the asymptotic analysis, therefore achieving only a modest variance reduction. The development of numerical methods based on Girsanov’s theorem seems to be more effective for the Markov state models considered in
[
11
]
.

2
Mathematical framework

We first present in
Subsection

2.1
the general framework of our study, including a reference stochastic differential equation (SDE)
to describe the evolution of the system. We then present in
Subsection

2.2
the celebrated Green–Kubo formula for the computation of transport coefficients,
and provide a novel asymptotic quantification of the variance of the associated estimator.

2.1
General setting

We first present the general setting of our study and then specify the analysis to the overdamped Langevin dynamics.
We consider a time-homogeneous SDE defined on the state-space
\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{X}
is typically either the entire space
d
or a bounded domain with periodic boundary conditions
\mathbb{T}^{d}
(with
\mathbb{T}=\real/\mathbb{Z}
representing the one-dimensional torus):

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q_{t}=b(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(2.1)

Here

W_{t}\in\real^{m}
is a standard Brownian motion,
and the functions
b\colon\mathcal{X}\mapsto\real^{d}
and
\sigma\colon\mathcal{X}\mapsto\real^{d\times m}

are the drift and diffusion coefficients.
We introduce the following assumption for simplicity.

Assumption 1
.

The state space
\mathcal{X}
is the d-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the functions
b\colon\real^{d}\to\real^{d}
and
\sigma\colon\real^{d}\to\real^{d\times m}
are
C^{\infty}
, the diffusion matrix
\sigma\sigma^{\top}
is positive definite and uniformly bounded from below over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Consequently, the dynamics (
2.1
) admits a unique invariant probability measure
\mu
.

Remark 2.1
.

The existence of an invariant probability measure mentioned in
Assumption

1
is a consequence of the non-degeneracy of the matrix
\sigma\sigma^{\top}
,
which is reflected in the ellipticity of the infinitesimal generator associated to (
2.1
).
A variety of techniques can be employed to show this,
such as as those based on the classical Doeblin minorization condition.
See
[
37
, Theorem 6.16]
for an ergodicity result that applies directly to our setting,
or
[
2
]
for a more general result which also applies to degenerate diffusions.

Note that
Assumption

1
guarantees the existence and uniqueness of a strong solution of (
2.1
).
In the sequel,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
\|\cdot\|
refer respectively to the inner product and its associated norm in
L^{2}(\mu)
for the probability measure
\mu
from
Assumption

1
,
namely

\forall f,g\in L^{2}(\mu),\qquad\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}fg\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

We also introduce the projection operator

\displaystyle\Pi\varphi=\varphi-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\varphi],

(2.2)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\varphi]
is the expectation of a function with respect to
\mu
. We denote the Hilbert space

L^{2}_{0}(\mu)=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mu)\ \middle|\ \int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0\right\}=\Pi L^{2}(\mu),

which is the subspace of functions of
L^{2}(\mu)
with zero expectation with respect to
\mu
.
The infinitesimal generator associated to the SDE (
2.1
) is given by the following differential operator:

\mathcal{L}=b^{\top}\nabla+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\top}:\nabla^{2},

where
:
denotes the Frobenius inner product, and
\nabla^{2}
is the Hessian operator. More explicitly, for a given
C^{\infty}
test function
\phi:\real^{d}\rightarrow\real
, the operator
\mathcal{L}
acts as

\mathcal{L}\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sigma_{i,k}\sigma_{j,k}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}.

The Markov semigroup of operators
t\mapsto\operatorname{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}
associated with (
2.1
),
with generator
\mathcal{L}
,
gives the time evolution of expectation of functions
\varphi\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\real
,
henceforth called observables, along the dynamics:

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}\varphi\right)(q_{0})=\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\varphi(q_{t})\right].

(2.3)

Here,
the expectation is over all realizations of
W_{t}
in (
2.1
) for a dynamics starting at a deterministic initial condition
q_{0}
.
We now state an assumption on the semigroup that will be essential in the sequel.
It implies in particular that the operator
\mathcal{L}
is invertible on
L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
.

Assumption 2
.

Suppose that there exist
L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\lambda>0
such that

\forall t\geqslant 0,\qquad\|\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}_{0}(\mu))}\leqslant L\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}.

As a consequence the operator
\mathcal{L}
is invertible on
L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
, with

\mathcal{L}^{-1}=-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}dt,

and

\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}_{0}(\mu))}\leqslant\frac{L}{\lambda}.

A paradigmatic example of a dynamics for which
Assumptions

1
and
2
are satisfied is the overdamped Langevin dynamics

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q_{t}=-\nabla V(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(2.4)

where
\beta>0
is proportional to the inverse temperature, and
V
is a smooth potential.
The dynamics (
2.4
) admits the Gibbs probability measure with density

\mu(q)=\frac{1}{Z}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V(q)},\qquad Z=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta V}<+\infty,

as its unique invariant probability measure; see
[
3
]
for various results on this dynamics.

2.2
Computation of transport coefficients and Green–Kubo formula

Transport coefficients provide insights into how a system responds to perturbations from equilibrium.
One common method for computing them using molecular dynamics involves the famous Green–Kubo formula.
This formula expresses transport coefficients as integrated time-correlations between relevant fluxes within the equilibrium system.
The general form of the Green–Kubo formula is the following:

\displaystyle\rho=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\right]\mathrm{d}t,

(2.5)

where
R
,
S\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
and
\mu
is the invariant probability measure of (
2.1
).
A natural estimator of (
2.5
) is obtained by first truncating the time integral to some finite time
T
and then replacing the expectation by the empirical average over
J
independent realizations of the equilibrium dynamics (
2.4
)
with stationary initial conditions
q^{(j)}_{0}\sim\mu
.
This leads to the estimator

\widehat{\rho}_{T,J}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{0}^{T}R\Bigl(q_{t}^{(j)}\Bigr)S\Bigl(q_{0}^{(j)}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(2.6)

The truncation bias
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{T,J}\right]-\rho\right\rvert
is exponentially small in the integration time
T
,
in view of the exponential decay of correlations from
Assumption

2
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{T,J}\right]-\rho\bigr\rvert=\left\lvert\int_{T}^{+\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\right]\mathrm{d}t\right\rvert\leqslant\int_{T}^{+\infty}\left|\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}R,S\right\rangle\right|\mathrm{d}t,

so that, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{T,J}\right]-\rho\right\rvert\leqslant\|R\|\|S\|\int_{T}^{+\infty}L\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\|R\|\|S\|}{\lambda}L\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}.

(2.7)

The above inequality initially suggests that
T
should be sufficiently large to meet a small error threshold.
However, the estimator
\widehat{\rho}_{T,J}
of the Green–Kubo formula has a variance that increases linearly with time
T
,
as made precise in the following proposition.
Note that the next result was proven in a similar way in
[
35
]
at the same this work was completed.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
R,S\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
be two smooth observables. Under
Assumption

2
, the variance of the estimator
\widehat{\rho}_{T,J}
given in (
2.6
) scales asymptotically as
T
:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\to\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{V}\left(\widehat{\rho}_{T,J}\right)=\frac{2}{J}\|S\|^{2}\left\langle R,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}R\right\rangle.

(2.8)

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{V}(\widehat{\rho}_{T,J})=J^{-1}\mathbb{V}(\widehat{\rho}_{T,1})
,
it suffices to prove the result for
J=1
.
Denote by
\mathcal{R}\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
the solution to the Poisson equation
-\mathcal{L}\mathcal{R}=R
. The latter equation is well posed in view of
Assumption

2
.
By Itô’s formula,
it holds

\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{S(q_{0})}{\sqrt{T}}\bigl(\mathcal{R}(q_{0})-\mathcal{R}(q_{T})\bigr)+\frac{S(q_{0})}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

The function
S
is bounded uniformly over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
and so is
\mathcal{R}
by boundedness of
R
and elliptic regularity.
Therefore, the first term on the left-hand side of the above equality tends to 0 in squared expectation in the limit
T\to+\infty
.
Thus,
using Itô’s isometry for the second term,
we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}\right]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigl[\bigl\lvert S(q_{0})\bigr\rvert^{2}|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})|^{2}\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\left\lVert S\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigl[\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}\Bigr]+\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigl[\bigl\lvert S(q_{0})\bigr\rvert^{2}\Pi\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}(q_{t})\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t,

where we used stationarity and introduced
\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}:=|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})|^{2}=\nabla\mathcal{R}^{\top}\sigma\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}
.

By conditioning on
q_{0}
,
then using the tower property of conditional expectation
and the definition of the semigroup (
2.3
),
we obtain by
Assumption

2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigl[\bigl\lvert S(q_{0})\bigr\rvert^{2}\Pi\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}(q_{t})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl\lvert S(q_{0})\bigr\rvert^{2}\Pi\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}(q_{t})\,\middle|\,q_{0}\right]\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigl[\bigl\lvert S(q_{0})\bigr\rvert^{2}\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[\Pi\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}(q_{t})\right]\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigl[\bigl\lvert S(q_{0})\bigr\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}\Pi\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}(q_{0})\Bigr].

From the last expression,
by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and smoothness of the observable
{S\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\real^{d}}
we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Bigl[\bigl\lvert S(q_{0})\bigr\rvert^{2}\Pi\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}(q_{t})\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\left\lVert S\right\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}\Pi\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}\right\rVert\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant\frac{L}{T}\left\lVert S\right\rVert_{L^{4}(\mu)}^{2}\left\lVert\Pi\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}\right\rVert\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,\mathrm{d}t\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}0.

The result then follows from the equality
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\bigl[\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}\bigr]=2\langle R,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}R\rangle
,
see for example
[
37
, Theorem 6.12]
.
The latter expression can be justified by rewriting the carré du champ as as
\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{L}\mathcal{R}^{2}-2\mathcal{R}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{R}
and integrating against
\mu
.

3
Variance reduction using importance sampling

In the previous section, we showed that the variance of the time-truncated estimator of the Green–Kubo formula grows linearly in time,
with a constant prefactor including the term
\langle R,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}R\rangle
.
Thus, a natural idea to achieve variance reduction is to perturb the reference dynamics in such a manner that
\langle R,-\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}^{-1}R\rangle
is smaller than
\langle R,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}R\rangle
,
where
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
is the generator of the modified dynamics.
In order to define an estimator based on the modified dynamics that is asymptotically consistent as the integration time
T
tends to
+\infty
,
weights must be included in accordance with Girsanov’s theorem.
We first present in
Subsection

3.1
an estimator of
\rho
in (
3.3
) based on the modified dynamics.
Then, in
Subsection

3.2
, we study the dependence of the variance of the estimator in terms of the biasing parameter
\alpha
,
and provide an approximation of the value of
\alpha
which minimizes the variance.
Finally, we investigate the dependence of the optimal biasing parameter with respect to the integration time
T
.

3.1
Approximation based on Girsanov formula

In this section we study an approximation for the time-truncated Green–Kubo formula obtained using Girsanov’s theorem.
Let us place ourselves in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
,
and let
(q_{t}^{\alpha})
denote the solution to the overdamped Langevin dynamics
with biasing force
\alpha\sigma u\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\real^{d}
for
\alpha\in\real
:

\mathrm{d}q_{t}^{\alpha}=b(q_{t}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}t+\alpha\sigma(q_{t}^{\alpha})u(q_{t}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(q_{t}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}}_{t},\qquad q_{0}^{\alpha}=q_{0},

(3.1)

where
(W^{\mathbb{P}}_{t\geqslant 0})
is a standard Brownian motion with respect to
\mathbb{P}

and
u\colon\real^{d}\to\real^{m}
is a vector field.
We emphasize that this equation is considered with the same initial condition as (
2.1
). For notational convenience,
the upperscrit is omitted for the reference (unbiased) dynamics,
i.e.

q_{t}=q_{t}^{0}
.
Let also
\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{t}
denote the Doléans-Dade exponential of the process
X^{\alpha}
,
which is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{t}=\exp\left(X^{\alpha}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\langle X^{\alpha}\rangle_{t}\right),\qquad X^{\alpha}_{t}=-\alpha\int_{0}^{t}u(q_{s}^{\alpha})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{\mathbb{P}}.

(3.2)

We show in
Proposition

3.1
that,
as a consequence of Girsanov’s theorem,
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{T}\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t}^{\alpha})S(q_{0}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

(3.3)

Thus we define the following estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{T,J}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha,j})_{T}\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t}^{\alpha,j})S(q_{0}^{\alpha,j})\,\mathrm{d}t,

(3.4)

where
\bigl(q_{t}^{\alpha,j}\bigr)
,
for
j\in\{1,\dotsc,J\}
,
are
J
independent realization of the dynamics (
3.1
) and

X^{\alpha,j}_{t}=-\alpha\int_{0}^{t}u(q_{s}^{\alpha,j})\cdot\mathrm{d}W^{j,\mathbb{P}}_{s}.

It is clear that

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{A}_{T,J}^{\alpha}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}\right],

and

\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{A}_{T,J}^{\alpha}\right]=\frac{1}{J}\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}\right],

where

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}=\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{T}\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t}^{\alpha})S(q_{0}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}t.

(3.5)

In other words, the many-replica estimator (
3.4
) has the same bias as the one-replica estimator (
3.5
),
and a smaller variance by a factor
\frac{1}{J}
.
Therefore, it is sufficient to study the estimator (
3.5
).
The main goal is now to understand whether this estimator can be employed for variance reduction purposes.
To this end,
we first show the following result on the raw first and second moments of
\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For all
T\geqslant 0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{T}\right].

(3.6)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}\right\rvert^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}E_{T}(\alpha)\right],

(3.7)

with

E_{T}(\alpha)=\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}}_{t}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\lvert u(q_{t})\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

Proof.

Equality (
3.6
) is a direct application of Girsanov’s theorem.
We nonetheless make precise how to obtain this result as this will be useful to establish (
3.7
).

Since
b
is bounded,
the so-called Novikov condition
is fulfilled (see
[
33
, Exercise 4.4]
):

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|X^{\alpha}_{t}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)\right]<\infty.

Consequently, the stochastic process
t\mapsto\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{t}
is a martingale.
Thus, it follows from Girsanov’s theorem,
see e.g.
[
19
, Theorem 8.1]
and
[
33
, Theorems 8.6.4 and 8.6.8]
,
that the probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}
defined by its Radon–Nykodym derivative

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(\omega)=\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{T}

(3.8)

is equivalent to
\mathbb{P}
.
Girsanov’s theorem also gives that the process

W^{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}_{t}:=\alpha\int_{0}^{t}u(q^{\alpha}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+W^{\mathbb{P}}_{t},

(3.9)

is a standard Brownian motion with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}
,
and that, furthermore,
the process
(q_{t}^{\alpha})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a strong solution to

\mathrm{d}q_{t}^{\alpha}=b(q_{t}^{\alpha})+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}_{t},\qquad q_{0}^{\alpha}=q_{0}.

(3.10)

It immediately follows that (
3.6
) holds,
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{T}\int_{0}^{T}R(q^{\alpha}_{t})S(q^{\alpha}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:girsanov0}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}R(q^{\alpha}_{t})S(q^{\alpha}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:girsanov2}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

In the last equation,
we used that the
\mathbb{P}
-law of the process
(q_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}

is equal to the
\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}
-law of the process
(q^{\alpha}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.
This is clear in view of (
3.10
) and the fact that the
\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}
-law of
q^{\alpha}_{0}
is
equal to the
\mathbb{P}
-law of
q_{0}
,
since
q^{\alpha}_{0}=q_{0}
and
q_{0}
is independent of
\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{t}
.

To prove (
3.7
),
we use (
3.9
) to rewrite

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{T}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}u(q^{\alpha}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}}_{t}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\lvert u(q_{t}^{\alpha})\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}u(q^{\alpha}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}_{t}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\lvert u(q_{t}^{\alpha})\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

(3.11)

Therefore, by (
3.8
) and (
3.11
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left(\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{T}\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t}^{\alpha})S(q_{0}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{T}\left(\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t}^{\alpha})S(q_{0}^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}\left[\exp\left(-\alpha\int_{0}^{T}u(q^{\alpha}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W^{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}_{t}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\lvert u(q^{\alpha}_{t})\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\left(\int_{0}^{T}R(q^{\alpha}_{t})S(q^{\alpha}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\right)^{2}\right].

Since the
\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}
law of
(W^{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}_{t},q^{\alpha}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
coincides with
the
\mathbb{P}
law of
(W^{\mathbb{P}}_{t},q_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
,
we finally obtain (
3.7
).

In view of (
3.6
),
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}\right|^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}\right]^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}\right|^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{T}\right]^{2}.

(3.12)

The second term of this expression does not depend on
\alpha
and converges to
\rho^{2}
in the limit
T\to\infty
,
which motivates studying the scaling with respect to
\alpha
of the first term.

Proposition

3.1
, specifically equation (
3.7
),
is very useful for this purpose,
since it gives an expression in terms of the solution to the unbiased dynamics (
2.1
).
Let us introduce
F_{T}\colon\real\to\real
given by

\displaystyle F_{T}(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\widehat{A}_{T}^{\alpha}\right\rvert^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\,\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right],

(3.13)

where

\displaystyle X_{T}=-\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad\langle X\rangle_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\lvert u(q_{t})\bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(3.14)

and

\displaystyle\Phi_{T}(q)=\left(\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}.

(3.15)

For a fixed time
T
, the map
F_{T}
is a real function of a single variable.
The next natural step is to show the existence and uniqueness of an optimal
\alpha\in\real
that minimizes the variance. This is the purpose of the next proposition.
Let us first introduce the following additional assumptions.

Assumption 3
.

The observables
R
and
S
are continuous and non-zero, and so is the perturbation
u
.

Remark 3.2
.

It is in fact sufficient to assume that the observables are only
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
in
Assumption

3
.
For simplicity’s sake we retain the smoothness assumption.

Assumption 4
.

The initial condition
q_{0}=q^{\alpha}_{0}
of every dynamics considered in the sequel is sampled from the invariant probability measure
\mu
.
In particular, this implies that for every time
t\in\real_{+}
,
the distribution of
q_{t}
is
\mu
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Under
Assumptions

3
and
4
, for all
T>0
, the function
\alpha\mapsto F_{T}(\alpha)
is smooth,
with first, second and third derivatives given by

\displaystyle F_{T}^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\bigl(X_{T}+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\bigr)\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right],

(3.16a)

\displaystyle F_{T}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\langle X\rangle_{T}\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\left(\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}+X_{T}\right)^{2}\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right],

(3.16b)

\displaystyle F^{(3)}_{T}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\,\bigl(X_{T}+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\bigr)^{3}\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+3\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\,\langle X\rangle_{T}\left(X_{T}+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right].

(3.16c)

Furthermore, the function
F_{T}
is strictly convex and coercive.
Therefore, there exists a unique global minimizer
\alpha_{T}^{*}
characterized by

F_{T}^{\prime}(\alpha_{T}^{*})=0.

Proof.

We decompose the proof into 3 steps corresponding to the three main properties of
F_{T}
.

Differentiability

We show that for all
T>0
, the function
F_{T}
is differentiable.
To this end, we use a dominated convergence argument to differentiate under the expectation,
see
[
17
, Theorem 2.27]
.
Introduce

g(\alpha)=\Phi_{T}(q)\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right).

For a fixed realization
\omega\in\Omega
,
it is clear that the function
\alpha\mapsto g(\alpha)
is differentiable with derivative

\frac{\partial g}{\partial\alpha}(\alpha)=\Phi_{T}(q)\bigl(X_{T}+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\bigr)\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right).

(3.17)

Fix
m\in(0,\infty)
.
In order to show (
3.16a
),
it suffices to show that there exist a random variable
Y
and a constant
C\geqslant 0

independent of
\alpha
such that,
for all
\alpha\in[-m,m]
,
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl\lvert g(\alpha)\bigr\rvert\leqslant C<\infty,\qquad\left\lvert\frac{\partial g}{\partial\alpha}(\alpha)\right\rvert\leqslant Y,\qquad\mathbb{E}[Y]<\infty.

(3.18)

For the first inequality,
we note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl\lvert g(\alpha)\bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigl(\|S\|_{L^{\infty}}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}T\bigr)^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigl(\|S\|_{L^{\infty}}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}T\bigr)^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\exp\left(\alpha^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigl(\|S\|_{L^{\infty}}\|R\|_{L^{\infty}}T\bigr)^{2}\exp\left(m^{2}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}T\right)\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right]=:C.

(3.19)

The term on the right-hand side is the expectation
of the Doléans-Dade exponential at
T
of
X
,
which is equal to 1 so that
C
in (
3.18
) is given by (
3.19
).
In view of (
3.17
),
we have, for all
\alpha\in[-m,m]
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{\partial g}{\partial\alpha}(\alpha)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{T}\geqslant 0\}}\Phi_{T}(q)\Bigl(X_{T}+m\langle X\rangle_{T}\Bigr)\exp\left(mX_{T}+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{T}<0\}}\Phi_{T}(q)\Bigl(\lvert X_{T}\rvert+m\langle X\rangle_{T}\Bigr)\exp\left(\frac{m^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)=:Y_{1}+Y_{2}.

It remains to show that
\mathbb{E}[Y]<\infty
with
Y=Y_{1}+Y_{2}
.
We only prove that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]<\infty
,
since the result for
Y_{2}
is obtained by a similar reasoning.
By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi_{T}(q)^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl(X_{T}+m\langle X\rangle_{T}\bigr)^{4}\Bigr]^{\frac{1}{4}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\exp\left(2m\lvert X_{T}\rvert+m^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\Bigr]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The first two factors are finite,
because
R
,
S
and
u
are bounded,
and moments of the Itô integral
X_{T}
are finite by the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality
[
28
]
since
u
is bounded.
Indeed,
we have,
for any
p\in[1,+\infty)
and
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\int_{0}^{T}g(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right|^{p}\right]\leqslant C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(p)}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left|g(q_{t})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{p/2}\right].

(3.20)

The last factor is also bounded since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\exp\left(2mX_{T}+m^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\exp\left(2m^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\exp\left(2mX_{T}-2m^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\exp\left(2Cm^{2}\right)\exp\left(2mX_{T}-2m^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\Bigr],

with
C:=T\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{2}
.
The expectation of the second exponential on the right-hand side is equal to 1,
as this term is an exponential martingale by
[
34
, Section 3.7]
.
A similar reasoning,
which we do not make precise,
applies for higher order derivatives,
giving the smoothness of
F_{T}
and the formulas (
3.16b
) and (
3.16c
).

Strict convexity

Both terms in the definition (
3.16b
) of
F_{T}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)
are nonnegative which already shows that
F_{T}
is convex.
To show that the convexity is strict,
suppose there is
\alpha
such that
F_{T}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)=0
.
Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\langle X\rangle_{T}\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right]=0,

and so the argument of the expectation, being non-negative,
must be zero almost surely.
The exponential is almost surely non-zero,
so

\langle X\rangle_{T}\Phi_{T}(q)=\langle X\rangle_{T}\left(\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm a.s.}}{{=}}0,

and so

\langle X\rangle_{T}S(q_{0})\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm a.s.}}{{=}}0.

Given
Assumption

3
,
it is possible to consider
(q_{S},q_{R},q_{U})\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall q\in B(q_{S},\varepsilon),\qquad\bigl\lvert S(q)\bigr\rvert>0,

\displaystyle\forall q\in B(q_{R},\varepsilon),\qquad\bigl\lvert R(q)\bigr\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mu)},

\displaystyle\forall q\in B(q_{U},\varepsilon),\qquad\bigl\lvert u(q)\bigr\rvert>0.

In particular, since
R
is continuous,
it does not change sign in
B(q_{R},\varepsilon)
.
Given
Assumption

4
and using a controllability argument of the same type as in
[
29
, Lemma 3.4]
,
which hinges on the fact that the probability that a Brownian motion stays confined in a narrow tube around any continuous function starting at
0
is strictly positive,
it is possible to show that
\mathbb{P}\left[A\right]>0
,
with

A:=\left\{q_{0}\in B(q_{S},\varepsilon)\,\,\text{and}\,\,q_{T/8}\in B(q_{U},\varepsilon)\,\,\text{and}\,\,q_{t}\in B(q_{R},\varepsilon)\,\,\text{for all}\,\,\frac{T}{4}\leqslant t\leqslant T\right\}

This leads to a contradiction,
because for any
\omega
in this event of positive probability,
it holds by construction that
\langle X\rangle_{T}>0
and
S(q_{0})\neq 0
and

\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right\rvert\geqslant\left\lvert\int_{\frac{T}{4}}^{T}R(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right\rvert-\left\lvert\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{4}}R(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right\rvert\geqslant\frac{3T}{8}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}-\frac{T}{4}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}>0.

Coercivity

Fix
T>0
.
We showed previously that the event

A\subset B:=\Bigl\{\omega\in\Omega:\Phi_{T}(q)>0\,\,\text{and}\,\,\langle X\rangle_{T}>0\Bigr\}.

has positive probability.
Let

S_{T}(\alpha)=\mathbf{1}_{B}\Phi_{T}(q)\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X_{t}\rangle_{T}\right),

and note that

\lim_{|\alpha|\to\infty}S_{T}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}\infty\quad&\text{if $\omega\in B$},\\
0\quad&\text{if $\omega\notin B$}.\\
\end{cases}

(3.21)

Consider a sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
with
\alpha_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.
Then,
by definition of
F_{T}(\alpha)
,
Fatou’s lemma and (
3.21
),
it holds that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}F_{T}(\alpha_{n})\geqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[S_{T}(\alpha_{n})\bigr]\geqslant\mathbb{E}\bigl[\liminf_{n\to\infty}S_{T}(\alpha_{n})\bigr]=\mathbb{P}[A]\times\infty=\infty,

which gives the coercivity.

Conclusion of the proof

The function
F_{T}
is strictly convex and coercive,
which implies the existence and uniqueness of
\alpha^{*}_{T}
which minimizes
F_{T}
,
and concludes the proof.

3.2
Estimator of the optimal value

Proposition

3.3
establishes the existence and uniqueness of a value
\alpha^{*}_{T}

for which that the variance of the Girsanov estimator (
3.4
) is minimized.
In this section,
which is the main contribution of this work,
we study the asymptotic behavior with respect to the time
T
of this optimal value.
Because the function
F_{T}
is smooth
and
\alpha_{T}^{*}
is expected to vanish in the limit
T\rightarrow+\infty
,
we anticipate that a Taylor expansion of the form

\displaystyle F_{T}^{\prime}(\alpha_{T}^{*})\simeq F_{T}^{\prime}(0)+\alpha_{T}^{*}F_{T}^{\prime\prime}(0)

(3.22)

should be accurate when
T\gg 1
.
Such an expansion is convenient as it allows us to define an approximation
\widehat{\alpha}^{*}_{T}
of
\alpha^{*}_{T}

as the root of the right-hand side of (
3.22
),
namely

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{T}^{*}=-\frac{F_{T}^{\prime}(0)}{F_{T}^{\prime\prime}(0)}\cdot

(3.23)

This approximation is well-defined given that
F^{\prime\prime}_{T}(0)\neq 0
by
Proposition

3.1
.
The value (
3.23
) can be calculated based on the unbiased dynamics only.
By using the explicit expressions of
F^{\prime}_{T}(0)
and
F^{\prime\prime}_{T}(0)
given in (
3.16a
) and (
3.16b
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{T}^{*}=\frac{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]}{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\left(\int_{0}^{T}|u(q_{t})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\left(\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right)^{2}\right)\right]},

(3.24)

with
\Phi_{T}
defined in (
3.15
).
The remainder of this section is organized as follows.
In
Subsection

3.2.1
,
we study the asymptotic behavior with respect to
T
of
F_{T}^{\prime}(0)
and
F_{T}^{\prime\prime}(0)
(see
Lemmas

3.4
and
3.5
),
as well as that of the third derivative
F_{T}^{(3)}(\xi)
for small
\xi
(see
Lemma

3.6
).
These estimates enable to prove, in
Subsection

3.2.2
,
quantitative estimates on
\widehat{\alpha}^{*}_{T}
and then
\alpha^{*}_{T}
.

3.2.1
Asymptotic estimations

By a Taylor expansion, for all
a\in\real
there exists
\xi_{a,T}\in\left[-\frac{|a|}{T},\frac{|a|}{T}\right]
such that

\displaystyle F_{T}\left(\frac{a}{T}\right)-F_{T}(0)=\frac{a}{T}F^{\prime}_{T}(0)+\frac{a^{2}}{2T^{2}}F^{\prime\prime}_{T}(0)+\frac{a^{3}}{6T^{3}}F^{(3)}_{T}(\xi_{a,T}).

(3.25)

The three following lemmas (
Lemmas

3.4
,
3.5
and
3.6
) give the scaling with respect to
T
of each of the three derivatives on the right-hand side of (
3.25
).

Lemma 3.4
.

Under
Assumptions

2
,
3
and
4
the following limit holds:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{F^{\prime}_{T}(0)}{T}=D_{1},

(3.26)

where

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=-2\langle S^{2},\mathcal{R}\rangle\langle u,\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\|S\|^{2}\Bigl(2\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}^{-1}\Pi\left(u^{\top}\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\right),\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\right\rangle+\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}^{-1}\Pi\left(|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}|^{2}\right),u\right\rangle\Bigr),

with
\mathcal{R}
the unique solution in
L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
of the Poisson equation
-\mathcal{L}\mathcal{R}=R
.

Proof.

Note that the Poisson equation is well-posed because
R\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
.
Moreover by elliptic regularity
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
so that
\left\lVert\mathcal{R}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<+\infty
in particular.
Recall from
Proposition

3.3
that

-F^{\prime}_{T}(0)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\left(\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}\right].

(3.27)

We use Itô’s formula to write

\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{R}(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t=-\Bigl(\mathcal{R}(q_{T})-\mathcal{R}(q_{0})\Bigr)+\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

By substituting in (
3.27
), we obtain

\displaystyle-F^{\prime}_{T}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\Bigl(\mathcal{R}(q_{T})-\mathcal{R}(q_{0})\Bigr)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-2\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,\Bigl(\mathcal{R}(q_{T})-\mathcal{R}(q_{0})\Bigr)\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,\left(\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=:A_{1}(T)+A_{2}(T)+A_{3}(T).

Bounding the first term

Since
u
,
S
and
\mathcal{R}
are bounded uniformly on the torus,
we obtain by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Itô’s isometry that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\left\lvert A_{1}(T)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{4}\Bigl(\mathcal{R}(q_{T})-\mathcal{R}(q_{0})\Bigr)^{4}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}|u(q_{t})|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{4}{T}\|S\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\sqrt{T}\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}0.

Bounding the second term

The second term can itself be divided into two contributions:

\displaystyle A_{2}(T)

\displaystyle=-2\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\mathcal{R}(q_{T})\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\mathcal{R}(q_{0})\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=:-2B_{1}(T)+2B_{2}(T).

For
B_{1}(T)
, let us introduce
\displaystyle I_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}

and
\displaystyle J_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
.
We have that

I_{T}J_{T}=I_{T-\sqrt{T}}J_{T-\sqrt{T}}+\left(I_{T}J_{T}-I_{T-\sqrt{T}}J_{T-\sqrt{T}}\right).

Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{q})_{t\geqslant 0}
denote the natural filtration generated by
(q_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.
By conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{T-\sqrt{T}}
,
the sigma algebra which contains all information up to time
T-\sqrt{T}
,
then using the tower property of conditional expectation
and the definition of the semigroup (
2.3
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\mathcal{R}(q_{T})I_{T-\sqrt{T}}J_{T-\sqrt{T}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[S(q_{0})^{2}I_{T-\sqrt{T}}J_{T-\sqrt{T}}\mathcal{R}(q_{T})\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{T-\sqrt{T}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[S(q_{0})^{2}I_{T-\sqrt{T}}J_{T-\sqrt{T}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{R}(q_{T})\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{T-\sqrt{T}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[S(q_{0})^{2}I_{T-\sqrt{T}}J_{T-\sqrt{T}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{T}\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{R}\right)(q_{T-\sqrt{T}})\right].

Noting that the random variable
q_{T-\sqrt{T}}
is distributed according to
\mu
by stationarity (
Assumption

4
),
and using (
3.20
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\mathcal{R}(q_{T})I_{T-\sqrt{T}}J_{T-\sqrt{T}}\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{\|S\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}}{T}\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[|I_{T-\sqrt{T}}|^{4}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[|J_{T-\sqrt{T}}|^{4}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left\|\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{T}\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{R}\right\|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant L\sqrt{C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(4)}}\|S\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathcal{R}\|\frac{T-\sqrt{T}}{T}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\sqrt{T}},

and the right-hand side tends to 0 as
T\to\infty
.
Here
C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(4)}
is the constant from the BDG inequality (
3.20
),
and we used
Assumption

2
on the decay of the semigroup.
Next, writing

I_{T}J_{T}-I_{T-\sqrt{T}}J_{T-\sqrt{T}}=I_{T}(J_{T}-J_{T-\sqrt{T}})+J_{T-\sqrt{T}}(I_{T}-I_{T-\sqrt{T}}),

we have,
by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Itô’s isometry,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[S(q_{0})^{2}\mathcal{R}(q_{T})I_{T}(J_{T}-J_{T-\sqrt{T}})\Bigr]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[S(q_{0})^{2}\lvert\mathcal{R}(q_{T})\rvert\,\lvert I_{T}\rvert\,\lvert J_{T}-J_{T-\sqrt{T}}\rvert\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\|S\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{T}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left[|I_{T}|^{2}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left[|J_{T}-J_{T-\sqrt{T}}|^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\|S\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathcal{R}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\sqrt{T}\|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}T^{1/4}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}0.

The same computations hold for the term with
J_{T-\sqrt{T}}(I_{T}-I_{T-\sqrt{T}})
,
and so

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{B_{1}(T)}{T}=0.

For
B_{2}(T)
, we use Itô’s isometry and
Assumption

2
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{B_{2}(T)}{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\mathcal{R}(q_{0})\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})u(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\langle S^{2},\mathcal{R}\rangle\langle u,\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\rangle+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\mathcal{R}(q_{0})\Pi\left(\nabla\mathcal{R}^{\top}\sigma u\right)(q_{t})\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\langle S^{2},\mathcal{R}\rangle\langle u,\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\rangle+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle S^{2}\mathcal{R},\e^{t\mathcal{L}}\Pi\left(\nabla\mathcal{R}^{\top}\sigma u\right)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\langle S^{2},\mathcal{R}\rangle\langle u,\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\rangle.

Therefore we have shown that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{A_{2}(T)}{T}=2\langle S^{2},\mathcal{R}\rangle\langle u,\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\rangle.

Bounding the third term

As before, let
\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}
denote the expectation with respect to all realizations of the Brownian motion
W_{t}
in (
2.1
),
for a dynamics starting at a deterministic initial condition
q_{0}
.
By conditioning on
q_{0}
we first note that

\frac{A_{3}(T)}{T}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\frac{\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})}{T}\right],

with

\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0}):=\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,\left(\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right)^{2}\right].

Introduce the solutions of the following two Poisson equations:

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}\mathcal{F}=\mathfrak{f},\qquad-\mathcal{L}\mathcal{G}=\mathfrak{g},

with
\mathfrak{f}=\Pi\left(\nabla\mathcal{R}^{\top}\sigma u\right)
and
\mathfrak{g}=\Pi\left|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\right|^{2}
.
Hereafter we first show that

\forall q_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{d},\qquad\frac{\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})}{T}\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}2\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{F},\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\right\rangle+\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{G},u\right\rangle.

(3.28)

To this end, using
Lemma

A.1
with
f=u
and
g=h=\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}
,
we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})u(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\lvert\sigma(q_{t})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})\Bigr\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right].

(3.29)

Because
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]=0
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathfrak{f}(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathfrak{g}(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right].

We can again apply Itô’s formula to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathfrak{f}(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]=

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\Bigl(\mathcal{F}(q_{0})-\mathcal{F}(q_{T})\Bigr)\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{F}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right].

The first term on right-hand side is of order
\sqrt{T}
at most since the functions
\mathcal{F}
and
\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}
are bounded on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Indeed,
by a Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Itô’s isometry,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\Bigl(\mathcal{F}(q_{0})-\mathcal{F}(q_{T})\Bigr)\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant 2\|\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\left\lvert\int_{0}^{T}\sigma(q_{t})\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right\rvert^{2}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=2\|\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant 2\|\mathcal{F}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}\sqrt{T}.

For the second term, by Itô’s isometry and ergodicity,

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{F}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\,\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]=\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{F},\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\right\rangle.

With similar computations, we obtain that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|\sigma(q_{t})\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]=\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{G},u\right\rangle.

We have thus proved (
3.28
).
Using dominated convergence (the function
\mathcal{H}_{T}/T
being uniformly bounded,
which can be shown by computations similar to the ones used above),
we finally have

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{A_{3}(T)}{T}=\|S\|^{2}\bigl(2\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{F},\sigma^{\top}\nabla R\right\rangle+\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{G},u\right\rangle\bigr).

Conclusion of the proof

By combining all the previous results, we can finally conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{F^{\prime}_{T}(0)}{T}

\displaystyle=-2\langle S^{2},\mathcal{R}\rangle\langle u,\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\rangle-\|S\|^{2}\bigl(2\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{F},\sigma\nabla\mathcal{R}\right\rangle+\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{G},u\right\rangle\bigr),

which gives the claimed expression for
D_{1}
.

The next lemma provides an asymptotic equivalent for the second derivative of
F_{T}
at
0
.
The proof is slightly different from the one for the first derivative,
as it requires more sophisticated limit theorems.

Lemma 3.5
.

Under
Assumptions

2
,
3
and
4
the following limit holds:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{F^{\prime\prime}_{T}(0)}{T^{2}}=D_{2},

(3.30)

where
D_{2}=2\left\lVert S\right\rVert^{2}\left(\left\lVert\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R},u\rangle^{2}\right)
,
with
\mathcal{R}
the unique solution in
L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
of the Poisson equation
-\mathcal{L}\mathcal{R}=R
.

Proof.

Recall that (
3.16b
) implies that

F^{\prime\prime}_{T}(0)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}|u(q_{t})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\left(\int_{0}^{T}u(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{T}S(q_{0})R(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}\right].

Introduce
I_{T}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t
,
so that
F_{T}^{\prime\prime}(0)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}_{T}(0)=\,

\displaystyle T\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[S(q_{0})^{2}\lvert I_{T}\rvert^{2}\Bigr]+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[S(q_{0})^{2}\lvert I_{T}\rvert^{2}X_{T}^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[S(q_{0})^{2}\lvert I_{T}\rvert^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\Pi|u(q_{t})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr]=:\,A_{1}(T)+A_{2}(T)+A_{3}(T),

where
X_{T}
is defined in
3.14
.

Bounding the first term

By Itô’s formula,
it holds that

I_{T}=\mathcal{R}(q_{0})-\mathcal{R}(q_{T})+M_{T},\qquad M_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(3.31)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{A_{1}(T)}{T^{2}}=

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\left\lvert\frac{\mathcal{R}(q_{0})-\mathcal{R}(q_{T})}{\sqrt{T}}\right\rvert^{2}\right]+\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\left(\frac{M_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+2\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\left(\mathcal{R}(q_{0})-\mathcal{R}(q_{T})\right)\frac{M_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}\right].

(3.32)

The first term on the right-hand side of (
3.32
) converges to 0 as
T\to\infty
because
S
and
\mathcal{R}
are bounded.
For the second term,
we use Itô’s isometry and the limit
\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}f\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f\mathrm{d}\mu
in
L^{2}(\mu)
for all
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
to obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\left(\frac{M_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\sigma(q_{t})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert S\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\right\rVert^{2}.

Finally, by a Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
and the two previous convergence results, the third term in (
3.32
) tends to 0.
Therefore,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{A_{1}(T)}{T^{2}}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert S\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\right\rVert^{2}.

Bounding the second term

For the second term, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{A_{2}(T)}{T^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\left\lvert\frac{I_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}\right\rvert^{2}\,\left\lvert\frac{Y_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}\right\rvert^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[S(q_{0})^{2}\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})\Bigr],

with
\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})=\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[\left\lvert\frac{I_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}\right\rvert^{2}\,\left\lvert\frac{Y_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}\right\rvert^{2}\right]
.
By the central limit theorem for additive functionals of Markov processes
(see
theorem

A.2
)
it holds for all
q_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
that

\begin{pmatrix}Y_{1}^{T}\\
Y_{2}^{T}\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{T}/\sqrt{T}\\
Y_{T}/\sqrt{T}\end{pmatrix}\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{\rm Law}Y^{\infty}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),\qquad\Sigma:=\begin{pmatrix}\|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\|^{2}&\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R},u\right\rangle\\
\left\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R},u\right\rangle&\|u\|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Using the same reasoning as in
[
37
, Appendix C]
,
together with Isserlis’ theorem,
we have that
\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})
converges for every fixed
q_{0}
to a constant in the limit
T\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\lvert Y^{\infty}_{1}\rvert^{2}\lvert Y^{\infty}_{2}\rvert^{2}\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\lvert Y^{\infty}_{1}\rvert^{2}\Bigr]\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\lvert Y^{\infty}_{2}\rvert^{2}\Bigr]+2\Bigl\lvert\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y^{\infty}_{1}Y^{\infty}_{2}\bigr]\Bigr\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sigma\nabla\mathcal{R}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}+2\langle\sigma\nabla\mathcal{R},u\rangle^{2}.

Applying the dominated convergence theorem,
we deduce that

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{A_{2}(T)}{T^{2}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})\right]=\left\lVert S\right\rVert^{2}\left(\left\lVert\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}+2\langle\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R},u\rangle^{2}\right).

The dominated convergence theorem can indeed be applied since
\mathcal{H}_{T}(q_{0})
is uniformly bounded.
Indeed,
using (
3.31
), then the elementary inequality
(a+b)^{4}\leqslant 8a^{4}+8b^{4}

and finally the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[\left\lvert\frac{I_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}\right\rvert^{2}\,\left\lvert\frac{X_{T}}{\sqrt{T}}\right\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2T^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[\left\lvert I_{T}\right\rvert^{4}+\left\lvert X_{T}\right\rvert^{4}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2T^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[8\left\lvert M_{T}\right\rvert^{4}+\lvert X_{T}\rvert^{4}\right]+128\|\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2T^{2}}\left(C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(4)}\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[8\langle M\rangle_{T}^{2}+\langle X\rangle_{T}^{2}\right]+128\|\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2T^{2}}\left(8C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(4)}\|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\|^{4}_{L^{\infty}}T^{2}+C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(4)}\|u\|^{4}_{L^{\infty}}T^{2}+128\|\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\left(8C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(4)}\|\sigma^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{R}\|^{4}_{L^{\infty}}+C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(4)}\|u\|^{4}_{L^{\infty}}+\frac{128\|\mathcal{R}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{4}}{T^{2}}\right).

Bounding the third term

Let us introduce the solution
\psi\in L^{1}_{0}(\mu)
to the Poisson equation
-\mathcal{L}\psi=\Pi\lvert u\rvert^{2}
,
where
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
by elliptic regularity.
By Itô’s formula, it holds that

\int_{0}^{T}\Pi\lvert u(q_{t})\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\psi(q_{0})-\psi(q_{T})+\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\psi(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

and so

\displaystyle A_{3}(T)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[S(q_{0})^{2}\lvert I_{T}\rvert^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\psi(q_{t})^{\top}\sigma(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[S(q_{0})^{2}\lvert I_{T}\rvert^{2}\bigl(\psi(q_{0})-\psi(q_{T})\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=C_{1}(T)+C_{2}(T).

In view of
Proposition

2.2
and the boundedness of
\psi
,
it holds for the second term that
C_{2}(T)/T^{2}\to 0
in the limit
T\to\infty
.
For the first term, using again (
3.31
), Young’s inequality,
and the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality,
we deduce that
C_{1}(T)
is of order
T^{3/2}
as
T\rightarrow+\infty
,
and therefore

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{A_{3}(T)}{T^{2}}=0,

which concludes the proof.

The final lemma gives a bound on the third derivative on the right-hand side of (
3.25
).
The goal is to show that this term is negligible when divided by
T^{3}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Under
Assumption

3
,
for any
a>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T^{3}}\left(\underset{\xi\in[-\frac{a}{T},\frac{a}{T}]}{\sup}\left\lvert F^{(3)}_{T}\left(\xi\right)\right\rvert\right)=0.

Proof.

Recall the expression (
3.16c
) for
F^{(3)}_{T}(\alpha)
:

\displaystyle F^{(3)}_{T}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\,\bigl(X_{T}+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\bigr)^{3}E_{T}(\alpha)\right]+3\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\,\langle X\rangle_{T}\left(X_{T}+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)E_{T}(\alpha)\right],

with the notation

\displaystyle E_{T}(\alpha)=\exp\left(\alpha X_{T}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right).

Note first that

\displaystyle\left\lvert F^{(3)}_{T}(\alpha)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\Psi_{T}(\alpha)+3\Theta_{T}(\alpha),

where

\displaystyle\Psi_{T}(\alpha):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)\,\bigl(|X_{T}|+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\bigr)^{3}E_{T}(\alpha)\right],

\displaystyle\Theta_{T}(\alpha):=\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\Phi_{T}(q)\,\langle X\rangle_{T}\Bigl(|X_{T}|+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\Bigr)E_{T}(\alpha)\Bigr].

By Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Psi_{T}(\alpha)\leqslant\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)^{3}\,\right]\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(|X_{T}|+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)^{9}\right]\mathbb{E}\!\left[E_{T}(\alpha)^{3}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}.

We next bound each of the three terms in the product on the right-hand side.

Bounding the first term of the product

We recall the definition of
\Phi_{T}(q)
in (
3.15
) and introduce the solution
\mathcal{R}\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
of the Poisson equation
-\mathcal{L}\mathcal{R}=R
.
By using Itô’s formula we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)^{3}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{6}\right]\leqslant\|S\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{R}(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{6}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\|S\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\mathcal{R}(q_{T})-\mathcal{R}(q_{0})-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right)^{6}\right].

We now apply the elementary inequality
|x+y|^{p}\leqslant 2^{p-1}\Bigl(|x|^{p}+|y|^{p}\Bigr)
for
p\geqslant 1
to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)^{3}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{5}\|S\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Bigl(\mathcal{R}(q_{T})-\mathcal{R}(q_{0})\Bigr)^{6}+\left(\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\right)^{6}\right].

Since
\mathcal{R}
is bounded on the torus,
and using the Burkholder–David–Gundy inequality (
3.20
),
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi_{T}(q)^{3}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{5}\|S\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\left(2^{6}\|\mathcal{R}\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}+C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(6)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}|\nabla\mathcal{R}(q_{t})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{3}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{5}\|S\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\left(2^{6}\|\mathcal{R}\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}+C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(6)}\|\nabla\mathcal{R}\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}T^{3}\right).

Bounding the second term of the product

With manipulations similar to the ones used for the first term, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(|X_{T}|+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)^{9}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{8}\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[|X_{T}|^{9}\right]+\alpha^{9}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\langle X\rangle_{T}^{9}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{8}\left(C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(9)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\langle X\rangle_{T}^{9/2}\right]+\alpha^{9}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\langle X\rangle_{T}^{9}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{8}\left(C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(9)}+\alpha^{9}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{9}T^{9/2}\right)\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{9}T^{9/2}.

Finally,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(|X_{T}|+\alpha\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)^{9}\right]^{1/3}\leqslant 2^{8/3}\left(C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(9)}+\alpha^{9}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{9}T^{9/2}\right)^{1/3}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{3}T^{3/2}.

Bounding the third term of the product

We can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[E_{T}(\alpha)^{3}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\left(3\alpha X_{T}+\frac{3}{2}\alpha^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\left(3\alpha X_{T}-\frac{9}{2}\alpha^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\exp\left(6\alpha^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp(6\alpha^{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}T)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\left(3\alpha X_{T}-\frac{9}{2}\alpha^{2}\langle X\rangle_{T}\right)\right]

The Novikov condition is satisfied,
so the Doléans-Dade exponential has an expectation of
1
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[E_{T}(3\alpha)\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\Bigl(2\alpha^{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}T\Bigr).

Finally, by combining the three inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{T}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{13/3}\|S\|^{2/3}_{L^{\infty}}\left(\frac{2^{6}\|\mathcal{R}\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}}{T^{3}}+C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(6)}\|\nabla\mathcal{R}\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(9)}+\alpha^{9}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{9}T^{9/2}\right)^{1/3}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{3}T^{5/2}\exp(2\alpha^{2}\|X\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}T).

(3.33)

Then by taking
\alpha=\frac{a}{T}
and applying this inequality for
\xi\in[0,\frac{a}{T}]
, by dividing by
T^{3}
and bounding
\xi
by
\frac{a}{T}
in (
3.33
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\underset{\xi\in[-\frac{a}{T},\frac{a}{T}]}{\sup}\,\frac{1}{T^{3}}\Psi_{T}(\xi)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2^{13/3}}{\sqrt{T}}\|S\|^{2/3}_{L^{\infty}}\left(\frac{2^{6}\|\mathcal{R}\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}}{T^{3}}+C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(6)}\|\nabla\mathcal{R}\|^{6}_{L^{\infty}}\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(C_{\rm{BDG}}^{(9)}+a^{9}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{9}T^{-9/2}\right)^{1/3}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{3}\exp(2a^{2}\|X\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}T^{-1})

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}0.

Similar estimates can be used to prove the convergence of
\Theta_{T}
and conclude to the claimed convergence result.

3.2.2
Asymptotic behavior of the optimal value

We now come back to (
3.25
).
By using
Lemmas

3.4
,
3.5
and
3.6
,
we obtain that for all fixed
a\in\real

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}F_{T}\left(\frac{a}{T}\right)-F_{T}(0)=D_{1}a+\frac{D_{2}}{2}a^{2},

(3.34)

where
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are the constants introduced in from
Lemmas

3.4
and
3.5
.
The next step is to understand how the optimal value
\alpha^{*}_{T}
behaves.
Equation (
3.34
) suggests that
\alpha^{*}_{T}
scales as
\frac{1}{T}
,
which we justify rigorously based on the following result.

Proposition 3.7
.

Under
Assumptions

2
,
3
and
4
,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\argmin_{a\in\real}\left\{F_{T}\left(\frac{a}{T}\right)-F_{T}(0)\right\}=\argmin_{a\in\real}\left\{D_{1}a+\frac{D_{2}}{2}a^{2}\right\}.

(3.35)

Furthermore,
as a consequence of (
3.35
)

\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\min_{\alpha\in\real}F_{T}\left(\alpha\right)-F_{T}(0)\right)=-\frac{D_{1}^{2}}{2D_{2}}.

(3.36)

Proof.

Fix a sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in{\rm Nature}}
such that
t_{n}\to\infty
,
and consider the sequence of functions
\left(f_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined as

\displaystyle f_{n}(a)=F_{t_{n}}\left(\frac{a}{t_{n}}\right)-F_{t_{n}}(0).

(3.37)

This is a sequence of strictly convex coercive functions converging pointwise to
f_{\infty}(a)=D_{1}a+D_{2}a^{2}/2
on by (
3.34
).
Therefore, by
[
39
, Theorem 10.8]
,
this sequence converges uniformly to
f_{\infty}
on every compact subset of .
It is then straightforward to show
that
\left(\argmin_{a\in\real}f_{n}(a)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is contained in a compact set, which allows to prove (
3.35
).
We give a proof of this result in
Lemma

A.3
.
As a consequence, (
3.36
) directly follows from (
3.35
).

As a direct corollary of
Proposition

3.7
,
we have that

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}_{T}:=\argmin_{\alpha\in\real}\,F_{T}\left(\alpha\right)\underset{T\to\infty}{\sim}-\frac{D_{1}}{D_{2}T}.

(3.38)

This is the main result of this paper.
The latter equation gives us the scaling with respect to
T
of the parameter
\alpha
that minimizes the variance of the Girsanov estimator (
3.4
).
Equation (
3.36
) in
Proposition

3.7
,
together with
Proposition

2.2
,
also imply that

\displaystyle\frac{F_{T}(0)-F_{T}(\alpha^{*}_{T})}{F_{T}(0)}\underset{T\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{D_{1}^{2}}{4D_{2}\|S\|^{2}\left\langle R,-\mathcal{L}^{-1}R\right\rangle}\frac{1}{T}.

(3.39)

This last result shows that the variance can only be reduced by a factor that scales asymptotically as
T^{-1}
.

4.1
Numerical method

At the continuous-time level,
Girsanov’s theorem ensures that
the following equality holds for any functional
\mathcal{F}\colon C\bigl([0,T],\real^{d}\bigr)\to\real
on path space:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}\bigl(q_{[0,T]}\bigr)\right]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathcal{E}(X^{\alpha})_{T}\mathcal{F}\Bigl(q^{\alpha}_{[0,T]}\Bigr)\Bigr].

(4.1)

In the context of this paper,
the functional of interest is the truncated Green–Kubo integral:

\mathcal{F}\bigl(q_{[0,T]}\bigr)=\int_{0}^{T}R(q_{t})S(q_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t.

We consider Euler–Maruyama discretizations of
the reference dynamics
(q_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
given in (
2.1
) and the biased dynamics
(q^{\alpha}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
given in (
3.1
).
We denote by
\hat{q}_{n}
and
\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha}
the iterates of the numerical schemes
i.e.

\hat{q}_{n}
and
\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha}
are approximations of
q_{n\Delta t}
and
q_{n\Delta t}^{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\hat{q}_{n}+b(\hat{q}_{n})\Delta t+\sigma\Delta W_{n},

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{n+1}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha}+\Bigl(b(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})+\alpha\sigma u(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\Bigr)\Delta t+\sigma\Delta W_{n}=:\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha}+b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\Delta t+\sigma\Delta W_{n},

(4.2b)

where
(\Delta W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d Gaussian random variables with mean
0
and covariance
\Delta t\,\mathrm{I}_{d}
.
In order to implement the weighted Green–Kubo estimator (
3.4
),
it is also essential to discretize the Green–Kubo functional
\mathcal{F}
and the Girsanov weight.
A discretization of the functional, denoted by
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}\colon\real^{d(N+1)}\to\real
,
is obtained for instance by an approximation of the time integral using the left point rule:

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}\bigl(\hat{q}_{0},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}\bigr)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}R(\hat{q}_{n})S(\hat{q}_{0})\Delta t.

Ideally, the discretization of the Girsanov weight should be consistent with the discretization of the dynamics in (
4.2a
) and (
4.2b
),
in the sense that given finite discretized trajectories
(\hat{q}_{0},\dots,\hat{q}_{N})
and
(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha})
,
it should hold

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathcal{F}}\bigl(\hat{q}_{0},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}\bigr)\right]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[M^{\alpha}_{N}\Bigl(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha}\Bigr)\widehat{\mathcal{F}}\Bigl(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha}\Bigr)\Bigr],

(4.3)

where
M^{\alpha}_{N}
is the discretized Girsanov weight.
In the next paragraph, we motivate the discretization of this weight,
relying on the calculations provided in the appendix of
[
11
]
.
For the Euler–Maruyama discretization considered in (
4.2a
) and (
4.2b
),
the calculation of the likelihood ratio
M^{\alpha}_{N}
is relatively simple.
Let
\mu_{\Delta t,N}
and
\mu^{\alpha}_{\Delta t,N}
denote the Lebesgue densities of the random vectors
(\hat{q}_{0},\dots,\hat{q}_{N})
and
(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha})
,
respectively.
In order for (
4.3
) to hold,
the weight
M_{N}^{\alpha}
must coincide with the quotient
\mu_{\Delta t,N}/\mu^{\alpha}_{\Delta t,N}
.
Now the transition kernel describing
4.2a
has a density

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}(\hat{q}_{n+1}|\hat{q}_{n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta t\sigma^{2}}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\Delta t\sigma^{2}}\left\lvert\hat{q}_{n+1}-\hat{q}_{n}-b(\hat{q}_{n})\Delta t\right\rvert^{2}\right).

(4.4)

From this equation,
we deduce that

\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta t,N}(\hat{q}_{0},\dots,\hat{q}_{N})

\displaystyle=\mathscr{P}(\hat{q}_{0})\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}(\hat{q}_{n+1}|\hat{q}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{P}(\hat{q}_{0})}{(2\pi\Delta t\sigma^{2})^{n/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\Delta t\sigma^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left\lvert\hat{q}_{n+1}-\hat{q}_{n}-b(\hat{q}_{n})\Delta t\right\rvert^{2}\right).

A similar expression holds for
\mu^{\alpha}_{\Delta t,N}
,
with
b^{\alpha}
instead of
b
.
Given
Assumption

4
,
it follows that

\displaystyle M_{N}^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{\Delta t,N}(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha})}{\mu^{\alpha}_{\Delta t,N}(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha})}=\frac{\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\Delta t\sigma^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left\lvert\hat{q}_{n+1}^{\alpha}-\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha}-b(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\Delta t\right\rvert^{2}\right)}{\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\Delta t\sigma^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left\lvert\hat{q}_{n+1}^{\alpha}-\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha}-b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\Delta t\right\rvert^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{(\hat{q}_{n+1}^{\alpha}-\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\cdot\left(b(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})-b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\right)}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\!\exp\left(-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\left(|b(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})|^{2}-|b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})|^{2}\right)\Delta t}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

Writing
\hat{q}_{n+1}^{\alpha}-\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha}=b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\Delta t+\sigma\Delta W_{n}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{(\hat{q}_{n+1}^{\alpha}-\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\cdot\bigl(b(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})-b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\bigr)}{\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\frac{b(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\cdot b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\Delta t-|b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})|^{2}\Delta t+\sigma\bigl(b(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})-b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\bigr)\cdot\Delta W_{n}}{\sigma^{2}},

and therefore

M_{N}^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha})=\exp\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\bigl(b(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})-b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\bigr)\cdot\Delta W_{n}}{\sigma}-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\left\lvert b(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})-b^{\alpha}(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})\right\rvert^{2}\Delta t}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

Finally, since
b^{\alpha}=b+\alpha\sigma u
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle M_{N}(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha})=\exp\left(-\alpha\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})^{\top}\Delta W_{n}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|u(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})|^{2}\Delta t\right).

(4.5)

The above expression gives us the discrete Girsanov weight needed to reweight trajectories.
Note that (
4.5
) is the discrete counterpart of (
3.2
).
Thus, we have shown that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N}R(\hat{q}_{n})S(\hat{q}_{0})\Delta t\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[M_{N}(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha},\dots,\hat{q}_{N}^{\alpha})\sum_{n=0}^{N}R(\hat{q}_{n}^{\alpha})S(\hat{q}_{0}^{\alpha})\Delta t\right],

(4.6)

which is the discrete counterpart of (
3.3
).

5
Extensions and perspectives

In this final section,
we list possible extensions of the variance reduction method for the calculation of transport coefficients presented in this work.

•

The first natural extension is to optimize the functional form of the biasing drift
u
,
which amounts to optimizing the value of
\frac{D_{1}^{2}}{D_{2}}
in view of (
3.36
) and (
3.39
).
In dimension one,
it is natural to choose a perturbation of the form
u=-V^{\prime}
.
However,
this approach is impractical in high-dimensional setting,
such as for the simulation of proteins, as
V^{\prime}
will be large,
and will depend on irrelevant degrees of freedom such as the solvent.
A biasing by a function depending only on a selected number of degrees of freedom (encoded by a collective variable),
such as the free energy considered is
Subsection

4.2.2
,
is more relevant.

•

Throughout the theoretical part of this paper,
we have restricted our attention to the continuous-time setting,
but in practice the dynamics and estimators must be discretized,
as done in
[
11
,
12
]
.
Therefore, a natural direction for future work would be carry out the numerical analysis of the weighted Green–Kubo estimator,

i.e.
after time discretization,
and rigorously quantify the time-step bias.

•

Since, for a given
\alpha
, the stochastic process
\mathcal{E}_{t}(\alpha)
in (
3.3
) is a martingale,
we could also consider instead of (
3.5
) the estimator

\widehat{B}_{T}^{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{T}R\left(q_{t}^{\alpha}\right)S\left(q_{0}^{\alpha}\right)\mathcal{E}_{t}(\alpha)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Preliminary simulations suggest a variance reduction similar to that obtained earlier in this work.
However,
it is more difficult to perform the theoretical analysis developed here and thus to obtain precise analytical results.

•

As another research direction,
one could extend our results to the setting of degenerate diffusions,
such as underdamped Langevin dynamics,
which are often more relevant than overdamped Langevin dynamics in applications.
Let us mention that path reweighting has already been employed in this context, see
[
23
]
.

However,
given the relatively modest variance reduction observed in the numerical experiments,
it is unclear whether such a research effort is warranted and so it seems more relevant to focus on other more effective methods such as fluctuation methods
[
38
]
or methods based on Einstein’s formula
[
35
,
36
]
.

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

This section contains two useful auxiliary results.
The first one that follows concerns the expectation of the product of three Brownian integrals.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
T>0
,
and suppose that
f
,
g
and
h
are smooth functions on the torus.
Introduce the Brownian martingales

I_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}f(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad J_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}g(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad K_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}h(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

with
q_{t}
a continuous stochastic process adapted to
(W_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
.
Then, for any initial condition
q_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
,

\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[I_{T}\,J_{T}\,K_{T}\right]=\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\langle J,K\rangle_{T}I_{T}\right]+\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\langle I,K\rangle_{T}J_{T}\right]+\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\left[\langle I,J\rangle_{T}K_{T}\right],

where
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{T}
denotes the covariation process at time
T
.

Proof.

Letting
\Psi(I_{t},J_{t},K_{t})=I_{t}J_{t}K_{t}
and applying Itô’s formula,
we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Psi(I_{t},J_{t},K_{t})=

\displaystyle\,J_{t}K_{t}f(q_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t}+I_{t}K_{t}g(q_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t}+I_{t}J_{t}h(q_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+I_{t}\langle J,K\rangle_{t}+J_{t}\langle I,K\rangle_{t}+K_{t}\langle I,J\rangle_{t}.

By integrating in time,

\displaystyle\Psi_{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(I_{t}g(q_{t})h(q_{t})+J_{t}f(q_{t})h(q_{t})+K_{t}f(q_{t})g(q_{t})\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(J_{t}K_{t}f(q_{t})+I_{t}K_{t}g(q_{t})+I_{t}J_{t}h(q_{t})\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Then,
taking the expectation and using the martingale property of stochastic integrals,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[\Psi_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[I_{t}g(q_{t})h(q_{t})\right]+\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[J_{t}f(q_{t})h(q_{t})\right]+\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\!\left[K_{t}f(q_{t})g(q_{t})\right]\Bigr)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\biggl[I_{T}\int_{0}^{T}g(q_{t})h(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\biggr]+\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\biggl[J_{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(q_{t})h(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\biggr]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}^{q_{0}}\biggl[K_{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(q_{t})g(q_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\biggr],

which concludes the proof.

The following result combines a central limit theorem for martingales with non-stationary increments,
with a central limit theorem for additive functionals of Markov processes started at a point.
For results of the former type, see
[
21
,
44
]
;
and for results of the latter type,
see
[
7
, Section 8]
.

Theorem A.2
.

Suppose that
Assumption

1
is satisfied (recall in particular that
\sigma
takes values in
d×m
),
and let
(q^{x}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
,
for
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
denote the solution to

\mathrm{d}q^{x}_{t}=b(q^{x}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(q^{x}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad q^{x}_{0}=x.

Consider smooth functions
g\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\real^{n}

and
h\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\real^{m}
and denote by
\psi\in H^{1}_{0}(\mu)^{n}
denote the
n
-valued solution to the Poisson equation
-\mathcal{L}\psi=g
.
Then

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}g(q^{x}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\\[8.5359pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}h(q^{x}_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{\rm Law}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Sigma\right),

with

\Sigma=\left[\begin{matrix}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\nabla\psi\sigma\sigma^{\top}\nabla\psi^{\top}\right]&\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[\nabla\psi\sigma h\right]\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[h^{\top}\sigma^{\top}\nabla\psi^{\top}\right]&\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[h^{\top}h\right]\end{matrix}\right]\in\real^{(n+1)\times(n+1)},

where
\nabla\psi\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\real^{n\times d}
is the Jacobian matrix of
\psi
.

Proof.

Note first that, by Itô’s formula,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}g(q^{x}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\\[8.5359pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}h(q^{x}_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}-\mathcal{L}\psi(q^{x}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}h(q^{x}_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{\top}(q^{x}_{t})\nabla\psi(q^{x}_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}h(q^{x}_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}\end{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\begin{bmatrix}\psi(q_{T})-\psi(q_{0})\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\psi\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\real^{n}
is bound,
the second term tends to 0 almost surely.
Thus, by Slutsky’s theorem, it is sufficient to prove that

\mathcal{M}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{0}^{t}v(q^{x}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\rm Law}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Sigma\right),\qquad v(q):=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma^{\top}(q)\nabla\psi(q)\\
h^{\top}(q)\end{bmatrix}\in\real^{(n+1)\times m}.

To this end,
denote by
(M_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
the following sequence of martingales:

M_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\int_{0}^{kt}v(q_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
the matrix-valued quadratic variation (see
[
37
, Definition 3.18]
) of
M_{k}(t)
is given by

\langle M_{k}\rangle_{t}=\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{kt}v(q_{s})v(q_{s})^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Let
v^{i,j}(q)=v_{i}(q)\cdot v_{j}(q)
,
where for
i\in\{1,\dotsc,n+1\}

the notation
v_{i}
refers to the
i
-th row of
v
.
Denote by
\langle M_{k}\rangle_{t}^{i,j}
the entry on row
i
and column
j
of
\langle M_{k}\rangle_{t}
.
Then, writing
v^{i,j}=\Pi v^{i,j}+\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\!\left[v^{i,j}\right]
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle\langle M_{k}\rangle_{t}^{i,j}=\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[v^{i,j}\right]+\frac{1}{kt}\int_{0}^{kt}\Pi v^{i,j}(q_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s\right)t.

(A.1)

The second term in the bracket on the right-hand side is an ergodic average of a mean-zero observable.
By the usual reasoning involving a Poisson equation,
see e.g.
[
30
, Section 4.2]
or the proof of
Proposition

2.2
here,
it holds that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,\left|\frac{1}{kt}\int_{0}^{kt}\Pi v^{i,j}(q_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\right]\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0.

By continuity of the sum,
and since convergence in squared expectation implies convergence in distribution,
we conclude,
that for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\langle M_{k}\rangle_{t}^{i,j}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{\rm Law}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[v^{i,j}\right]t,\qquad\langle M_{k}\rangle_{t}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{\rm Law}\Sigma t.

(A.2)

Since realizations of
(M_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are almost surely continuous,
they almost surely exhibit no jumps.
Together with (
A.2
),
this implies that the assumptions of
[
44
, Theorem 2.1]
are satisfied,
and so this theorem gives

M_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{\rm Law}\sqrt{\Sigma}B\qquad\text{with respect to $D^{n+1}$},

where
B
is a standard
n+1
Brownian motion and
D\equiv D([0,\infty))
is the space of right-continuous real-valued functions on
[0,\infty)
endowed with the Skorohod
J_{1}
topology.
To conclude, we use the continuous mapping theorem
[
5
, Theorem 2.7]
.
For
t\geqslant 0
, let
\mathcal{P}_{t}
denote the pointwise evaluation functional

\mathcal{P}_{t}\colon D^{n+1}\ni f\mapsto f(t)\in\real^{n+1}.

It can be shown that this operator is continuous at any continuous
f
,
see
[
22
]
.
Since Brownian motion is almost surely continuous,
we deduce from the continuous mapping theorem that

\mathcal{P}_{t}M_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{\rm Law}\mathcal{P}_{t}\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}B\right)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t\Sigma),

(A.3)

This also follows from
[
14
, Theorem 3.10.2]
.
To conclude, we use that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{0}^{\lfloor t\rfloor}v(q^{x}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{t}v(q^{x}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\lfloor t\rfloor}{t}}M_{\lfloor t\rfloor}(1)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{t}v(q^{x}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

(A.4)

The first term in (
A.4
) converges to
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
by (
A.3
) with
t=1
.
For the second term in (
A.4
), we have by Itô’s lemma that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{t}v(q^{x}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{t}|v(q^{x}_{s})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right]\leqslant\frac{t-\lfloor t\rfloor}{t}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}},

so this term converges to 0 as
t\to\infty
in squared expectation,
and thus also in probability.
The statement then follows from another application of Slutsky’s theorem.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of functions defined by (
3.37
).
Then there exists a compact set
K\subset\real
independent of
n
such that

\left(\argmin_{\alpha\in\real}f_{n}(a)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset K.

Proof.

Defining
a_{\infty}=-\frac{D_{1}}{D_{2}}
,
let us rewrite
f_{\infty}
as

f_{\infty}(a)=\frac{D_{2}}{2}(a-a_{\infty})^{2}+f^{\min}_{\infty},\qquad f^{\rm min}_{\infty}=-\frac{D_{1}^{2}}{2D_{2}}.

Fix
\delta>0
, let
K_{\delta}=[a_{\infty}-\delta,a_{\infty}+\delta]
,
and let
\varepsilon=\frac{D_{2}\delta^{2}}{6}
.
By uniform convergence on compact subsets,
there exists
n_{\varepsilon}
such that

\displaystyle\forall n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon},\qquad\left\{\begin{aligned} &f_{n}(a_{\infty})&\leqslant f_{\infty}(a_{\infty})+\varepsilon=f^{\rm min}_{\infty}+\varepsilon=f^{\rm min}_{\infty}+\frac{D_{2}\delta^{2}}{6},\\
&f_{n}(a_{\infty}+\delta)&\geqslant f_{\infty}(a_{\infty}+\delta)-\varepsilon=\frac{D_{2}\delta^{2}}{2}+f^{\rm min}_{\infty}-\varepsilon=f^{\rm min}_{\infty}+\frac{D_{2}\delta^{2}}{3},\\
&f_{n}(a_{\infty}-\delta)&\geqslant f_{\infty}(a_{\infty}-\delta)-\varepsilon=\frac{D_{2}\delta^{2}}{2}+f^{\rm min}_{\infty}-\varepsilon=f^{\rm min}_{\infty}+\frac{D_{2}\delta^{2}}{3}.\end{aligned}\right.

Consequently, by convexity of
f_{n}
,
it holds that
\argmin_{\alpha\in\real}f_{n}(a)\in K_{\delta}
for all
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}
.
Since
\delta
was arbitrary,
this concludes the proof.
