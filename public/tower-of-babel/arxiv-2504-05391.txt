Multi-scale analysis of flow over heterogeneous urban environments
Maarten van Reeuwijk, Jingzi Huang

Abstract
A computationally efficient multi-scale planar-averaging framework for urban areas is developed, which enables efficient computation of coarse-grained velocity and scalar fields. We apply the multi-scale framework to a large-eddy simulation of an idealised heterogeneous urban environment of 512 buildings based on a typical London height distribution. We observe that for this geometry, the characteristic urban lengthscale ℓ ≈ 50 \ell\approx 50 m, which is the averaging lengthscale L L at which as much variance in the mean flow is resolved as is unresolved. For L > 400 L>400 m, the statistics become approximately homogeneous, suggesting that non-building-resolving numerical weather prediction (NWP) models can be applied without modification at resolutions of 400 m and above for the case under consideration. We derive the multi-scale planar- and Reynolds-averaged momentum equation and show that for neutral cases, NWP models require parameterisation of the distributed drag and the unresolved turbulence and dispersive stress. An a priori analysis reveals that the drag parameterisation from Sutzl et al. , 2020, Bound-Layer Meteorol. , 178 : 225–248 holds reasonably well for resolutions L L above 200 m. Below this value, the problem becomes inhomogeneous and the parameterisation works less well. The unresolved stresses are well represented by a k − ω k-\omega closure with a value of ω = 0.4 ​ s − 1 \omega=0.4\,\textnormal{s}^{-1} . However, an even more accurate closure can be derived from the Sutzl drag parameterisation that does not require further turbulence information.

1
Introduction

The resolution of Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) models has advanced significantly over recent decades, transitioning from synoptic-scale
O
(10 km) grids
(
Bryan et al. 2003
)
to convection-permitting
O
(1 km) systems
(
Baldauf et al. 2011
;
Tang et al. 2013
)
. This progress, driven by computational advancements, enables explicit resolution of critical atmospheric processes such as deep convection and orographic flows, reducing reliance on parameterisations
(
Lean et al. 2024
)
. Kilometre-scale models are now operational for weather forecasting, regional climate downscaling
(
Prein et al. 2015
;
Belušić et al. 2020
)
, and air quality assessments. However, a persistent limitation across NWP frameworks is the unresolved representation of urban morphology-buildings, vegetation, and surface heterogeneities that remain subgrid-scale features, necessitating parameterised approximations.

The urban canopy region is at the lowest level of the atmospheric boundary layer and is characterised by strong heterogeneity and complex interactions between the urban surface and the atmosphere. Traditional urban canopy models (UCMs) aggregate aerodynamic drag, radiative trapping, and energy exchange into bulk parameters such as roughness length, displacement height, and plan area density that connect to the lowest atmospheric level directly
(
Lipson et al. 2024
)
. Whilst this approach works reasonably well for areas with low-rise buildings, it becomes problematic for areas with tall buildings that extend substantially into the atmospheric boundary layer. Vertically distributed UCMs
(
Schoetter et al. 2020
;
Sützl et al. 2021
;
Lu et al. 2024
)
improve momentum and scalar flux partitioning by distributing drag, heat over the vertical. However, both approaches assume horizontal homogeneity within grid cells, which breaks down below
O
(1 km) resolutions, as urban landscapes exhibit heterogeneity at neighbourhood scales (about 100-500 m) due to variations in building height, street geometry, and land cover
(
Stewart and Oke 2012
)
. Such fine-scale variability drives localised microclimates, including urban heat islands, airflow channelling, and pollutant dispersion
(
Masson et al. 2020
)
, which are poorly captured by current parameterisations.

High-resolution simulation solves some of these problems. Recent studies demonstrate that hectometric models (HMs) improve predictions of near-surface winds and turbulence kinetic energy in urban areas by up to 30% compared to coarser models
(
Lean et al. 2024
)
, highlighting their potential to reduce dependency on empirical parameterisations. Moreover, resolving neighbourhood-scale features partially enters the ‘turbulence grey zone’, where subfilter turbulent fluxes coexist with resolved eddies
(
Honnert et al. 2020
)
. At these scales, models can better represent terrain-driven phenomena (e.g., cold pools in valleys, slope winds) and urban surface-atmosphere interactions
(
Brun et al. 2017
;
Smith et al. 2021
)
.

However, at high horizontal NWP resolutions it will become necessary to relax the UCM assumption of horizontal homogeneity, and to understand how the vertical distribution depends on the horizontal resolution. In order to be able to provide the answer to this question, a multi-resolution framework is required, whereby high-resolution simulations that resolve individual buildings can be coarse-grained to arbitrary NWP resolution, such that it is possible to diagnose
a priori
what parameterisation is needed to represent the buildings within the NWP model.
Coarse-graining is a well-established technique to study, e.g. grey-zone turbulence
(
Honnert et al. 2020
)
, but there is no established multi-scale framework that can be used for the urban canopy.

Coarse-graining methods apply a spatial averaging, or filtering, operation on a fully resolved field in order to obtain a ‘smoothed’ coarse-resolution field
(
Wyngaard 2004
;
Honnert et al. 2011
)
.
Spatial averaging has been routinely used for urban canopies, inspired by the formalism presented for vegetation canopies
(
Raupach and Shaw 1982
;
Finnigan 2000
;
Nepf 2012
)
.
Within the context of urban canopies,
Schmid et al. (2019)
provide a spatial-averaging framework based on the volume-averaging framework of
Whitaker (1999)
, which was developed for porous media flows. A unique aspect of NWP models is that near the surface (i.e. in the canopy layer), the horizontal resolution of NWP models is much larger than the vertical resolution, as variations in the vertical tend to be much stronger than in the horizontal. Indeed, typical NWP simulations have a resolution of
O
(1 m) in the vertical but
O
(1 km) in the horizontal; even in the context of a hectometric model, the cell aspect ratio will be at least 100. This suggests that an appropriate averaging region on the canopy layer can be defined as a thin slab, i.e. reducing a three-dimensional volume averaging to a two-dimensional planar averaging approach, which is the approach used in
Schmid et al. (2019)
and also for vegetation canopies
(
Raupach and Shaw 1982
)
.
However, the
Schmid et al. (2019)
framework is not straightforward to apply directly to simulation data, since analyses are typically performed using averages over the entire domain and not for averages of arbitrary length scale
L
.
In addition, the approach is computationally inefficient and does not explicitly use a planar averaging region.

The aim of this paper is to present a computationally efficient multi-resolution framework based on the
Whitaker (1999)
;
Schmid et al. (2019)
spatial averaging approach and to apply this framework to results from building-resolving large-eddy simulation.
This coarse-graining approach allows us to systematically study heterogeneity in the flow statistics.
In the context of NWP models, it facilitates analysis of the required drag and turbulence parameterisation for non-building resolving models at different horizontal resolutions.
The paper is structured as follows. §2 mainly introduces a multi-resolution spatial averaging framework to grain the coarse field from the original high-resolution one; §3 introduces the simulation details of LES; and §4 presents and discusses the multi-resolution results on drag force and turbulent momentum stress, respectively. The highlights are remarked in the §5.

2
Multi-resolution planar-averaging framework

In this section, we derive a multi-resolution framework in terms of convolution filters.
The main advantages of using convolution filters are: 1) that it standardises the methodology, bringing it close to the large-eddy simulation formalism
(
Pope 2000
)
; and 2) that convolutions can be computed efficiently using Fast-Fourier transforms (FFTs).
In addition, we formulate the spatial averaging purely in terms of a planar filter. The work in
Schmid et al. (2019)
is cast in planar form, but the integral representing the effect of the solid boundaries is still presented in the volume-averaging form. Here, we capitalise on recent work by
Van Reeuwijk et al. (2021)
who derive identities that permit a fully planar formulation. A description of how the volume averaging can be transformed to planar averaging and a derivation of the spatial averaging theorem using convolutions is provided in Appendix
A
. The main results needed for the convolution formalism are presented here.

2.1
Planar averaging using convolution filters

\mathcal{A}
. (a) plan view, (b) elevation view. The domain of
\Omega

\Omega_{f}

\Omega_{b}

\partial\Omega_{f}
(solid black lines). The building-surface 3-D normal vectors
\boldsymbol{N}

\mathcal{A}
with averaging length
L
is shown in red.

The domain of interest
\Omega
is comprised of a subdomain containing the fluid
\Omega_{f}
and a solid subdomain
\Omega_{b}
such that
\Omega=\Omega_{f}\cup\Omega_{b}
(Fig.
1
).
The boundary between the fluid and solid phase is denoted
\partial\Omega_{f}
, which has a (3-D) normal vector
\boldsymbol{N}
pointing into the fluid domain. The horizontal, spanwise and vertical coordinates are denoted
x
,
y
and
z
, respectively.
Since averaging will take place over the streamwise directions, the coordinate vector
\boldsymbol{x}
is denoted as
\boldsymbol{x}=[\boldsymbol{x}_{h};z]
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{h}=[x,y]^{T}
.
Denoting the filter associated with the area-averaging
\mathcal{A}
, the superficial average
(
Xie and Fuka 2018
, also called comprehensive average, the average normalised by the total area including the solid phase; cf.)
of an arbitrary scalar
\varphi
is given by

\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\int_{\Omega_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\varphi(\boldsymbol{y}_{h},z)\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}\,.

(1)

The presence of
\boldsymbol{x}
in the integration domain
\Omega_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
means that the integration region comprises all the fluid-occupied areas within the support of the filter.
This can be made explicit by defining a mask function
I_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
which is 1 when
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{f}
and 0 otherwise, which allows the convolution integral above to be expressed as

\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\Omega(z)}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})I_{f}(\boldsymbol{y}_{h},z)\varphi(\boldsymbol{y}_{h},z)\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}\,.

(2)

This is a standard planar convolution integral over the entire domain
\Omega
at given
z
which can be evaluated efficiently using the convolution theorem and Fast-Fourier transforms
(
Bracewell 2000
)
.

Formally, there are few restrictions on the filter, except that it satisfies the normalisation condition
\int\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{h}=1
. Here, a symmetric square filter is used, defined as

\mathcal{A}(x,y)\equiv\begin{cases}L^{-2}&\textnormal{if }\left(-\frac{L}{2}<x<\frac{L}{2}\right)\cup\left(-\frac{L}{2}<y<\frac{L}{2}\right)\,,\\
0&\textnormal{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(3)

where
L
is the averaging length scale.
Note that
\left\langle{\smash{\left\langle{\smash{X}}\right\rangle}}\right\rangle\neq\left\langle{\smash{X}}\right\rangle
in general. However, if the underlying characteristic length scale of the urban surface
\ell
is much smaller than the averaging length scale
L
, that is
L\gg\ell
, we expect
\left\langle{\smash{\left\langle{\smash{X}}\right\rangle}}\right\rangle\approx\left\langle{\smash{X}}\right\rangle
. In other words,
\left\langle{\smash{\left\langle{\smash{X}}\right\rangle}}\right\rangle\approx\left\langle{\smash{X}}\right\rangle
if there is a separation of scales between the characteristic urban lengthscale
\ell
and the averaging lengthscale
L

(
Schmid et al. 2019
)
.

In the filter formalism, the area (planar) fraction
\varepsilon=A_{f}/L^{2}
, where
A_{f}
is the fluid surface within the averaging region, is defined as

\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\int_{\Omega_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}.

(4)

This equation shows the interaction between the filter
\mathcal{A}
and the integration over the fluid domain
\Omega_{f}
, which may only cover part of the area over which
\mathcal{A}
filters.
The porosity
\varepsilon
can be used to calculate the intrinsic average
\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle{{}^{f}}
(the average normalised by only the area of the variable fluid phase), indicative of the average value of
\varphi
inside the fluid phase:

\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle{{}^{f}}=\varepsilon^{-1}\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle.

(5)

2.2
Differentiation rules

Since the solid phase is stationary, the spatial averages commute with time differentiation.
The same is not true for spatial differentiation.
For the planar filter
\mathcal{A}
, the scalar spatial averaging theorem
(
Whitaker 1999
, eq. 1.2-15)
is given by (see appendix
A
):

\left\langle{\smash{\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}}}\right\rangle=\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}}-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\varphi\frac{N_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\,\textnormal{d}s\,.

(6)

Here, the last term is a Leibniz-like horizontal line integral over the fluid-solid boundary
\partial\Omega_{f}
at given
z
within the support of
\mathcal{A}
.
The negative sign of the line integral is due to the (3D) surface normals
\boldsymbol{N}
pointing into the domain (the direction of the 3D vector
\boldsymbol{N}
is shown by the arrow in Fig.
1
c).
The quantity
\boldsymbol{N}_{h}=[N_{x},N_{y}]^{T}
is the component of
\boldsymbol{N}
in the horizontal
x-y
plane.
The term
|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|
accounts for the local surface orientation that need not be aligned with the horizontal direction.
For vertical surfaces,
|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|=1
(e.g., the horizontal vectors on the last building in Fig.
1
b), for angled surfaces
0<|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|<1
(e.g., the vectors on the middle building in Fig.
1
b), and for horizontal surfaces, for example, flat roofs,
|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|=0
(the vertical vector on the last building in Fig.
1
b).
Superficially, it seems the integral is undefined for horizontal surfaces, but it can be shown
(
Van Reeuwijk et al. 2021
)
that the integral becomes finite upon integrating over
z
. Physically, one can interpret this as a flat surface providing a finite contribution over an infinitesimal distance in
z
.
Note that the presence of these apparent singularities is an unavoidable consequence of using a planar average rather than a volumetric average, and its evaluation is discussed in §
3.2
.
To demonstrate the physical meaning of the line integral, it is useful to apply (
6
) to the mean pressure
\overline{p}
. The horizontal pressure gradient then becomes

\left\langle{\smash{\frac{\partial\overline{p}}{\partial x}}}\right\rangle=\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{p}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x}-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\overline{p}\frac{N_{x}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\,\textnormal{d}s\,.

This demonstrates that the filtering operation produces the form drag in the
x
-direction within the filter support. Note that under the assumption of flow in the positive
x
-direction and cuboids that face the wind head-on, we would have
N_{x}/|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|=-1
on windward vertical surfaces, +1 on leeward vertical surfaces and 0 on sidewalls. The top surface of buildings will not contribute to the form drag since
N_{x}=0
, although care must be taken to show that the apparent singularity caused by
|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|
= 0 does not cause trouble (see §
3.2
).

For the planar filter
\mathcal{A}
, the vector form of the spatial averaging theorem
(
Whitaker 1999
, eq. 1.2-23)
is given by (see appendix
A
)

\left\langle{\smash{\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}}}\right\rangle=\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{F_{i}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}}-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\frac{F_{i}N_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\textnormal{d}s\,,

(7)

where repeated indices imply implicit summation.
As an example of the application of this theorem, consider filtering the mean continuity equation
\nabla\cdot\overline{\boldsymbol{u}}=0
. Using (
7
) with
F_{i}=\overline{u}_{i}
results in

\left\langle{\smash{\frac{\partial\overline{u}_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}}}\right\rangle=\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}}-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\frac{\overline{u}_{i}N_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\textnormal{d}s=\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}}\,,

since
\overline{u}_{i}N_{i}=0
on
\partial\Omega_{f}
due to the impermeability of the buildings. Therefore,
\nabla\cdot\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\right\rangle=0
, implying that the filtered velocity field is also divergence-free.

If the averaging length
L
is much larger than the characteristic length scale
\ell
of the urban surface, the average concentration
\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle
and horizontal fluxes
\left\langle{\smash{F_{x}}}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{\smash{F_{y}}}\right\rangle
will be independent of
x,y
, implying that the spatial averaging theorems reduce to
(
Schmid et al. 2019
)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\smash{\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle}{\partial z}-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\varphi\frac{N_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\,\textnormal{d}s\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\smash{\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{F_{z}}}\right\rangle}{\partial z}-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\frac{F_{i}N_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\,\textnormal{d}s.

(9)

2.3
Triple decomposition

We define the Reynolds average of
\varphi
as an ensemble average
(
Schmid et al. 2019
)
:

\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,.

(10)

Deviations from the average value
\overline{\varphi}
are denoted
\varphi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
.
For statistically steady problems, time-averaging can be used in lieu of an ensemble average.
In the current paper, we will assume that Reynolds-averaging has been performed before spatial averaging, which avoids some of the complexities of simultaneous Reynolds and spatial averaging
(
Schmid et al. 2019
)
.
We thus decompose the average velocity into the local spatial average and its deviation from the average as
\overline{\varphi}=\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle{{}^{f}}+\overline{\varphi}^{\prime\prime}
.
This decomposition is only defined in the fluid domain, and the intrinsic average is used since this represents the average value inside the fluid domain.
Substitution into Eq. (
10
) results in the triple decomposition

\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\underbrace{\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle{{}^{f}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)+\overline{\varphi}^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t)}_{\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{x},t)}+\varphi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,.

(11)

For products, e.g. of a scalar flux
u_{i}\varphi
, this implies

\begin{split}\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u_{i}\varphi}}}\right\rangle&=\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}\,\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u_{i}^{\prime}\varphi^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle{\smash{\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u_{i}}}}\right\rangle{{}^{f}}\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle{{}^{f}}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\smash{\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u_{i}}}}\right\rangle{{}^{f}}\overline{\varphi}^{\prime\prime}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}^{\prime\prime}\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle{{}^{f}}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}^{\prime\prime}\overline{\varphi}^{\prime\prime}}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u_{i}^{\prime}\varphi^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle\,.\\
\end{split}

(12)

2.4
Reynolds- and area-averaged streamwise momentum equations

Ignoring buoyancy effects and assuming incompressible airflow, the (unsteady) Reynolds-averaged streamwise horizontal momentum equation is given by

\frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\overline{u}_{j}\overline{u}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial\overline{u_{j}^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial\overline{p}}{\partial x}=f+\nu\frac{\partial^{2}\overline{u}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}\,,

(13)

where
\overline{u}
represents the mean velocity in the streamwise
x
-direction,
\overline{p}
is the kinematic deviatoric pressure, and
f
is the volumetric forcing term, typically due to a pressure gradient.
This equation can be written in flux form as

\frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}=f,\quad\textnormal{where }\quad F_{i}=\overline{u}_{i}\overline{u}+\overline{u^{\prime}_{i}u^{\prime}}-\nu\frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial x_{i}}+\overline{p}\delta_{i1}

(14)

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.
Superficial averaging and application of the spatial averaging theorem Eq. (
7
) results in

\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{F_{i}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}}=\left\langle{\smash{f}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{f_{D}}}\right\rangle,

(15)

where
\left\langle{\smash{f}}\right\rangle=\varepsilon f
and the drag force induced by the buildings
\left\langle{\smash{f_{D}}}\right\rangle
is given by

\left\langle{\smash{f_{D}}}\right\rangle=-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\frac{F_{i}N_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\textnormal{d}s=-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\,\left(\overline{p}\frac{N_{x}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}-\nu\frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{N_{j}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\right)\textnormal{d}s\,.

(16)

This equation explicitly provides the drag force induced by the building surfaces that is comprised of a form drag associated with the pressure and a skin drag associated with the viscosity
(
Raupach and Shaw 1982
;
Finnigan 2000
;
Nepf 2012
;
Sützl et al. 2020
)
.

Considering the fact that NWP models take into account the net effect of buildings without actually resolving them, the governing equations are posed in terms of
superficial
quantities.
Recasting Eq. (
15
) in the standard form for the streamwise momentum equation, we thus obtain:

\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{p}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x}=\left\langle{\smash{f}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{f_{D}}}\right\rangle+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{i}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}},

(17)

where

\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{i}}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}\overline{u}}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u^{\prime}_{i}u^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle\,,

(18)

is the unresolved kinematic stress term. Note that we have not decomposed the term
\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}\overline{u}}}\right\rangle
into its components and that the viscous flux has been omitted since it is negligible in magnitude compared to the other fluxes.
Equation (
17
) highlights the two terms that need to be closed in order to represent the effect of buildings in a non-building resolving NWP model: 1) the distributed drag contribution
f_{D}
; and 2) the unresolved kinematic stress
\tau_{i}
.

2.5
Statistically steady flow with periodic boundary conditions

The case considered in this paper is a turbulent urban boundary layer flow with periodic boundary conditions and a free-slip top boundary at
z=h
in a statistically steady state. In this situation, Eq. (
17
) becomes

\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{p}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x}=\left\langle{\smash{f}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{f_{D}}}\right\rangle+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{i}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}},

(19)

which is not much of a simplification. No term except the time derivative can be assumed zero for an arbitrary averaging length
L
.
However, if the averaging length scale is very large, the problem will become homogeneous in
x
and
y
and all the horizontal derivatives will vanish. With this in mind, it is useful to rewrite the equation above as

\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{v}}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{p}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{x}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{y}}}\right\rangle}{\partial y}=\left\langle{\smash{f}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{f_{D}}}\right\rangle-\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w}}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{z}}}\right\rangle}{\partial z}\,,

(20)

Here, the horizontal terms on the left-hand side will vanish if the problem is homogeneous, leaving the terms on the right-hand side of the equation to balance.
The equation above indicates that an imbalance in the terms on the right-hand side implies inhomogeneity in
x
and
y
via horizontal terms on the left-hand side and vice versa.

Homogeneity is expected when
L\gg\ell
, i.e.
L
is much larger than the characteristic urban lengthscale
\ell
. In this case, the horizontal momentum equation simplifies to

\frac{\textnormal{d}\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{z}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}}{\textnormal{d}z}=\left\langle{\smash{f_{D}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}-\left\langle{\smash{f}}\right\rangle_{\infty}\,,

(21)

where

\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{z}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}=-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w}\,\overline{u}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}=-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w}^{\prime\prime}\overline{u}^{\prime\prime}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}\,,

(22)

note that
\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}=0
due to volume conservation. The subscript on the superficial average indicates the averaging length
L
, in this case
L/\ell\gg 1
which we denote as
L=\infty
. In the limit of
L\gg\ell
, the quantity
\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle_{\infty}

is identical to the conventional plane-average
\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle_{xy}
, which is typically used in computational studies
(
Coceal et al. 2006
;
Xie et al. 2008
;
Giometto et al. 2016
;
Nazarian et al. 2020
;
Sützl et al. 2020
)
. The superficial plane average of
\varphi
is defined as

\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle_{xy}(z,t)\equiv\frac{1}{A_{T}}\int_{\Omega_{f}(z)}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)\,\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{h}\,,

(23)

where
A_{T}=L_{x}L_{y}
is the total area of the horizontal plane.
Note that the plane average is distributive and that contrary to
\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle
,
\left\langle{\smash{\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle_{xy}}}\right\rangle_{xy}=\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle_{xy}
, i.e., this operator behaves like a regular Reynolds average.

Integrating Eq. (
21
) from height
z
to the domain top at height
h
results in the following cumulative stress relation
(
Sützl et al. 2020
)
:

\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{z}}}\right\rangle_{\infty}(z)=\tau_{f;\infty}(z)-\tau_{D;\infty}(z)\,,

(24)

where

\displaystyle\tau_{f;L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h},z)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{z}^{h}\left\langle{\smash{f}}\right\rangle_{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h},z^{\prime})\textnormal{d}z^{\prime}\,,

\displaystyle\tau_{D;L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h},z)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{z}^{h}\left\langle{\smash{f_{D}}}\right\rangle_{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h},z^{\prime})\textnormal{d}z^{\prime}\,,

are the cumulative shear stresses due to forcing and drag, respectively. They have been formulated here for an arbitrary length scale
L
. For
L\gg\ell
, the stresses are a function of
z
only.
At the ground surface
z=0
, the relation reduces to
\tau_{f;\infty}(0)=\tau_{D;\infty}(0)\equiv\tau_{0}
, where
{\tau_{0}}
is the kinematic surface shear stress
(
Pope 2000
)
.

In order to keep consistency in notation with earlier work, we will use that in the absence of a specific filter size in the subscript of a quantity, it is implied that
L=\infty
, e.g.
\tau_{f}\equiv\tau_{f;\infty}
; and we will use the conventional plane average notation
\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle_{xy}
instead of its equivalent
\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle_{\infty}
. With this in mind, the cumulative stress relation Eq. (
24
) is simply

\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{z}}}\right\rangle_{xy}=\tau_{f}(z)-\tau_{D}(z).

(25)

3.1
Case setup

In order to investigate multi-scale aspects of urban flows, we consider an idealised quasi-realistic urban morphology that has a staggered arrangement of cuboidal buildings of breadth and width
W=24\,\textnormal{m}
, whose height
H
is random (Fig.
2
).
This case is inspired by
Xie et al. (2008)
, but with a much larger simulation domain of
L_{x}\times L_{y}\times L_{z}=1\,536\,\textnormal{m}\times 768\,\textnormal{m}\times 512\,\textnormal{m}
, and using a height distribution representative for London
(
Sützl et al. 2021
)
rather than a Gaussian height distribution.

With a spacing of size
W
between the buildings, the domain will contain
N_{b}=32\times 16=512
buildings. This implies that the plan-area index
\lambda_{p}\equiv A_{p}/A_{T}=0.25
where the plan area and total area are defined as, respectively,
A_{p}=N_{b}W^{2}
and
A_{T}=L_{x}L_{y}
.
For the distribution of building heights
H
, we use an empirical relation which was obtained using metre-scale GIS data for the Greater London area
(
Sützl et al. 2021
)
.
It was found that the total building width
L_{b}
(i.e. the sum of the frontal widths of all buildings) at elevation
z
can be approximated as:

L_{b}(z;\alpha)=\frac{A_{F}}{h_{\max}}\frac{\alpha e^{-\alpha z/h_{\max}}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}\,,\quad\quad z\leq h_{\max}\,,

(26)

where the coefficient
\alpha(r)
was observed to depend on the maximum-to-mean-building height
r=h_{\max}/h_{m}
as

\alpha(r)=1.355r-0.7807\,.

(27)

Here,
h_{\max}
is the maximum building height,
h_{m}
is the mean building height, and
A_{F}
is the total frontal area of the buildings. Note that
A_{F}/h_{\max}
is the total building width
L_{b}
if all buildings would have height
h_{\max}
.
Note that above the maximum building height
L_{b}=0
.

The mean building height is set to
h_{m}=30\,\textnormal{m}
and the ratio is set to
r=2.5
, both of which are close to the average value of London reported by
Sützl et al. (2021)
.
Thus the maximum building height is
h_{\max}=75\,\textnormal{m}
and
\alpha=2.61
.
At
z=0
, the total building width
L_{b}(z;\alpha)=N_{b}W
, which implies that the frontal area can be determined from Eq. (
26
) as

A_{F}=\frac{1-e^{-\alpha}}{\alpha}N_{b}Wh_{\max},

(28)

resulting in
A_{F}=327\,138\,\textnormal{m}^{2}
and thus a frontal area index

\lambda_{f}\equiv A_{F}/A_{T}=0.28
.

In order to assign heights to each cuboidal building, note that Eq. (
26
) divided by
W
can be inverted to show the height
z
as a function of the number of buildings
L_{b}(z)/W
, which represents the distribution of heights as a function of the number of buildings (Fig.
2
(a), solid line).
The building heights can be determined by uniformly sampling this function for each
i=1,2,\ldots,N_{b}
. The first X buildings, where the function is not defined, are set to
h_{\max}
, and the minimum building height is set to
10
m. As a final step, the heights are randomly distributed using a uniform distribution to all
512
buildings as shown in the morphology in Fig.
2
(b). The total building width
L_{b}
is shown in Fig.
6
(a) against the height, above
z=10
m which is the minimum building height, the building width
L_{b}
, also indicating that the distributive frontal area smoothly decreases to zero at the top of the canopy region. Due to the minimum building height setting, the actual frontal area index is
\lambda_{f}=0.29
, slightly greater than previously designed.

The urban flows over this morphology are performed by the large-eddy simulation using our open-source code uDALES
(
Owens et al. 2024
)
, presenting the solid boundary using the immersed boundary method (IBM). The dynamics near the boundary are parametrised by the logarithmic wall functions
(
Uno et al. 1995
;
Suter et al. 2022
)
. The turbulent eddy viscosity is calculated following the
Vreman (2004)
subgrid model. The code employs a second-order central difference scheme on a staggered Arakawa C-grid for spatial discretisation and an explicit third-order Runge-Kutta scheme for time integration.

The simulation is neutral and a constant pressure gradient forcing
\textnormal{d}P/\textnormal{d}x=0.0042
kg m
-2
s
-2
is imposed on the streamwise (
x
) direction to drive the wind. The simulation has a grid number of
N_{x}\times N_{y}\times N_{z}=1\,024\times 512\times 512
(i.e., the grid size is
\Delta x\times\Delta y\times\Delta z=1.5\,\textnormal{m}\times 1.5\,\textnormal{m}\times 1\,\textnormal{m}
), with periodic boundary conditions applied in the lateral sides while the domain top is free-slip. The simulation runs for 10 000 s and the last 8 000 s is used to obtain converged time-averaged statistics.

3.2
Implementation details

Efficient computation of the convolution integral Eq. (
2
) is crucial to carry out a multi-scale analysis. In the spatial domain, the convolution integral needs to be evaluated
N_{x}\times N_{y}\times N_{z}
times for a single averaging length
L
which is computationally very expensive, particularly for high-resolution simulations: assuming a filter contains
n^{2}
grid cells, the convolution over the entire domain requires the number of operations of
O(n^{2}N_{x}^{2}N_{z})
where we have assumed
N_{x}=N_{y}
. This is acceptable for small filter scales but becomes computationally inhibiting for large filter sizes.
Therefore, we perform the convolution in the Fourier space by making use of the convolution theorem, which states that a convolution becomes a multiplication in Fourier space
(
Bracewell 2000
)
, thus reducing the computational complexity of a 1-D convolution to
O(N_{x}\log N_{x})
.
We thus compute the horizontal 2-D FFT of
I_{f}\varphi
and the filter
\mathcal{A}
, multiply them, and then transform the product back to physical space using an inverse FFT, obtaining the filtered results for all points in the plane simultaneously.

Repeating this for all
N_{z}
planes requires
O((\log N_{x})^{2}N_{x}^{2}N_{z})
operations, thus greatly reducing the computational cost, particularly for large simulation domains and large filters.
The algorithm can be downloaded from
Van Reeuwijk and Huang (2025)
.

The filtering is performed for nine different averaging lengths:
L=3,6,12,24,48,96,192,384,
and
768
m.

Calculating the drag force Eq. (
16
) also requires care as the line integral to evaluate since the building surfaces contain many horizontal surfaces for which
|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|=0
. In order to ensure an appropriate discretisation of this term, note that by definition, for any boundary quantity
\phi
,

\int\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}(z)}\frac{\phi}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}z=\oiint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\phi\textnormal{d}S,

(29)

i.e. integrating the line integral over
z
results in the total flux exchanged across the solid-fluid surface. In a discrete form, this equation is given by

\sum_{k}\smash{\left(\sum_{m:K_{m}=k}\frac{\phi_{m}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|_{m}}\Delta s_{m}\right)}\Delta z_{k}=\sum_{m}\phi_{m}A_{m},

(30)

where each cell-facet
m
has area
A_{m}
, surface normal
\boldsymbol{N}_{m}
, and its contribution
\phi_{m}
is linked to a grid cell at index
I_{m}
,
J_{m}
and
K_{m}
. Since both sides of this equation use all cell facets exactly once, it follows that

\frac{\Delta s_{m}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|_{m}}=\frac{A_{m}}{\Delta z_{K_{m}}},

(31)

which clarifies how
{\Delta s_{m}}/{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|_{m}}
needs to be evaluated; it also shows that this term is always nonzero and finite.
In order to be able to straightforwardly incorporate filtering, the surface quantity
\phi
is converted to a surface density field
\rho_{\phi}
that contains the boundary term and which is only non-zero in the cells next to the boundary:

\rho_{\phi;ijk}=\sum_{m\in M_{ijk}}\frac{\phi_{m}A_{m}}{\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z_{K_{m}}}

(32)

where
M_{ijk}\in\{m:I_{m}=i,J_{m}=j,K_{m}=k\}
is the set of all cell-facets associated with cell
i,j,k
.
Using Eq. (
32
), any surface quantity can be filtered straightforwardly using
\left\langle{\smash{\rho_{\phi}}}\right\rangle(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\rho_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}
. Here we introduce a volumetric field for two surface quantities:

•

the distributed drag term, by setting
\phi_{m}=-(\overline{p}_{m}\boldsymbol{e}_{x}-\nu(\nabla\overline{u})_{m})\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{m}
. Denoting the volumetric drag density by
\rho_{D}
, we have that
\left\langle{\smash{f_{D}}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\smash{\rho_{D}}}\right\rangle
.
Here it should be noted that
\nu(\nabla\overline{u})_{m}
follows from the wall shear stress as calculated by the wall functions and not the resolved velocity field
(
Owens et al. 2024
)
.

•

the frontal area term, by setting
\phi_{m}=-\min(\boldsymbol{e}_{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{m},0)
, where
\boldsymbol{e}_{u}
is a unit vector for the wind direction (where
e_{u;z}=0
).
This term can be understood by realising that the projected area of facet area
m
is given as
A_{m}|\boldsymbol{e}_{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{m}|
and that a contribution is only counted when it involves the windward side, i.e.
\boldsymbol{e}_{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{m}<0
.
Denoting the resulting frontal-area density
\rho_{L}
, we have that
\int\rho_{L}\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}y=L_{b}
and
\int\rho_{L}\textnormal{d}V=A_{F}
.

The variance of any quantity

(38)

(39)

\sigma^{2}_{R}
quantifies the deviation of the spatial average from the plane average,
and (twice) the interaction covariance
{\sigma^{2}_{I}}
are defined as, respectively,

\displaystyle{\sigma^{2}_{U}}

\displaystyle\equiv{\left\langle{\smash{(\overline{\varphi}-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle)^{2}}}\right\rangle_{xy}},

\displaystyle{\sigma^{2}_{R}}

\displaystyle\equiv{\left\langle{\smash{(\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle_{xy})^{2}}}\right\rangle_{xy}},

\displaystyle{\sigma^{2}_{I}}

\displaystyle\equiv{2\left\langle{\smash{(\overline{\varphi}-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle)(\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{\varphi}}}\right\rangle_{xy})}}\right\rangle_{xy}}.

These variances are dependent on the height
z
and the averaging length
L

The velocity and TKE variance data have very similar trends.
By definition, the total variance

Generally, for both quantities, the variances are small near the ground, are larger at the mean height and the maximum height levels due to the building height distribution, and should gradually vanish above the canopy region as the flow becomes uniform.

4.4
Canopy turbulence closures

As discussed in §
2.4
, the other key process that requires parameterisation for non-building resolving NWP simulation is the unresolved stress
\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{i}}}\right\rangle
.
Consistent with (
17
,
18
), we consider the vertical flux
\left\langle{\smash{\tau_{z}}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w}}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w}\,\overline{u}}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle
, which comprises a turbulent momentum flux
\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle
and a residual term
\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w}}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}}}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w}\,\overline{u}}}\right\rangle
. As demonstrated in Eq. (
12
), this residual term can be further decomposed associated, but for simplicity, we regard the residual as a whole and name it a dispersive-like term.

A common way to represent the unresolved fluxes in the canopy region is based on
K
theory, which parameterises the flux by linking the flux to the strain rate tensor
S_{ij}
employing an eddy diffusivity
K_{m}

(
Pope 2000
;
Wyngaard 2010
)
. For the vertical turbulence flux of horizontal momentum, this results in

\left\langle{\smash{\overline{w^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}}\right\rangle=-2K_{m}S_{31}\,,\quad\textnormal{where}\quad S_{ij}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{i}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{\overline{u}_{j}}}\right\rangle}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\,.

(44)

The eddy-diffusivity coefficient
K_{m}
can be modelled in many different ways
(
Hanjalić and Launder 2022
)
. We investigate two models here, the
k-l
model
(
Deardorff 1972
;
Bougeault and Lacarrère 1989
)
and the
k-\omega
model
(
Wilcox 1998
)
:

K_{m}=C_{k}l_{k}\left\langle{\smash{{k}}}\right\rangle^{1/2}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad K_{m}=\frac{\left\langle{\smash{k}}\right\rangle}{\omega}\,,

(45)

respectively, where
C_{k}
is a model constant,
l_{k}
is a turbulent mixing length,
\left\langle{\smash{{k}}}\right\rangle
is the superficially averaged turbulence kinetic energy and
\omega
is an inverse turbulence time scale.

5
Conclusions

A multi-scale framework was developed for investigating the flows over heterogeneous urban areas.
At the core of the framework is a coarse-graining method that employs convolution filters to obtain coarse-grained fields from a building-resolved high-resolution simulation.
By employing Fast-Fourier transforms, a computationally efficient procedure was developed to carry out coarse-graining, which is particularly important for the analysis of high-resolution simulations as considered here.

One of the central questions the paper aimed to address was: ”What are the requirements for non-building-resolving NWP models as their resolution is increased?”
To answer this question, a quasi-realistic case was considered, inspired by the building height distribution of London.
The multi-scale framework revealed that the flow has a characteristic length scale
\ell\approx 50
m, which is the resolution at which as much variance is resolved as is unresolved. This corresponds to roughly twice the building width. Heterogeneity effects, which challenge the NWP parameterisations for the land surface, become significant at resolutions of about 200 m and below for the case under consideration.

Given that future NWP models will likely run at resolutions at which heterogeneity effects are important, an immediate follow-up question is how to incorporate heterogeneity.
This requires a land-surface model that resolves the vertical distribution of the flow and turbulence
(
Lean et al. 2024
)
and requires: i) a model for the distribution of the drag over the canopy; and ii) a model for the unresolved turbulence and dispersive stresses in the canopy.
Since NWP models do not resolve the urban morphology and it is undesirable to have to modify the governing equations inside the canopy (e.g. the mass conservation equation), superficial (comprehensive) averages are the appropriate formulation to consider, see Eq. (
17
).
It is therefore highly desirable that the drag distribution and unresolved stress parameterisation are formulated in terms of superficial quantities also.

It was shown that the distributed drag parameterisation of
Sützl et al. (2020)
, which was obtained from plane-averaged analysis of an ensemble of eight different urban landscapes, can be successfully extended to a local formulation, cf Eq. (
40
).
The parameterisation generalises excellently, with an
R^{2}
value of 0.929 at 96 m resolution.
The attractive feature of this parameterisation is that it only requires the local distributed frontal area
\zeta_{L}
which can be calculated
a priori
and can be straightforwardly incorporated in NWP models
(
Sützl et al. 2021
)
.
Although this parameterisation is excellent for predicting the local drag distribution, the actual drag requires knowledge of the local surface stress which remains challenging and requires further work; a local version of the
Coceal and Belcher (2004)
;
Belcher (2005)
parameterisation Eq. (
43
) has a normalised root-mean-square error of 71% at 100 m resolution.

Several closures were considered for the unresolved and turbulent stresses in the urban canopy layer.
The two best-performing models were the
k-\omega
model, which requires information about the turbulence kinetic energy
k
and the inverse turbulence timescale
\omega
, and a new stress parameterisation based on the distributed drag, cf Eq. (
46
), that does not require further parameters.
At a resolution of 96 m, the latter performs better than the former, although both parameterisations have a substantial scatter associated with local heterogeneity effects.

The results reported here indicate that it is possible to incorporate substantial heterogeneity effects in non-building-resolving NWP models with relative ease.
However, further work is required.
First, it is desirable to ensure the validity of the parameterisations Eq. (
36
) and Eq. (
46
) on a much larger flow database, e.g. that of
Lu et al. (2024)
.
Furthermore, the atmosphere is non-neutral most of the time, and distributed versions of the sensible and latent heat fluxes, including the interaction with the surface stress, need to be explored.

.

Appendix A
Volume-averaging with convolution filters

The
Whitaker (1999)
volume averaging approach can be cast into a convolution filter approach by introducing a kernel
\mathcal{G}
to specify the averaging volume, which is a function that is
1/V
within the averaging domain and 0 outside of it, where
V
is the total averaging volume.
Using this kernel, the superficial volume average of an arbitrary scalar
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})
is given by

\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\int_{\Omega_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})}\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\varphi(\boldsymbol{y})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}\,,

(47)

where
\boldsymbol{x}
is the 3-D location and
\int\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{x})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{x}=1
, i.e. the kernel is normalised.
Note that in Eq. (
47
), integration only takes place over the fluid phase.
The fluid volume fraction
\varepsilon=V_{f}/V
, where
V_{f}
is the volume occupied by fluid inside the averaging volume, is given by

\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\Omega_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})}\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}.

(48)

A.1
Volume to area integration

An area-averaging filter can be obtained by defining
\mathcal{G}
as

\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h})\delta(z)\,,

(49)

where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta and
\boldsymbol{x}=[\boldsymbol{x}_{h},z]^{T}
.
With this definition of
\mathcal{G}
, the volume integral will become an area integral. The planar filter is also normalised:

\int\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{x})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int\int\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h})\delta(z)\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{h}\textnormal{d}z=\int\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{h}=1\,.

Using Eq. (
49
), the superficial average Eq. (
47
) becomes

\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\Omega_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\varphi(\boldsymbol{y}_{h},z)\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}\,.

(50)

The surface fluid fraction
\varepsilon=A_{f}/A
, where
A_{f}
is the fluid area and
A
is the filter area, is given by

\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\Omega_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}\,,

(51)

A.2
Differentiation rules

We start with deriving the differentiation rule for the divergence. Note that

\left\langle{\smash{\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}}}\right\rangle=\int_{\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}=\int_{\Omega_{f}}\nabla\cdot(\mathcal{A}\boldsymbol{F})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}-\int_{\Omega_{f}}\boldsymbol{F}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{A}\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}

(52)

Commuting the integration and differentiation operators in the first term needs to be done with care, since
\Omega_{f}
is dependent on space. It requires the
identity
(see
Van Reeuwijk et al. 2021
, for a derivation)
:

\int_{\Omega_{f}}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{G}\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\int_{\Omega_{f}}G_{z}\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\frac{\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\textnormal{d}s.

(53)

Using Eq. (
53
), Eq. (
52
) becomes

\left\langle{\smash{\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}}}\right\rangle=\frac{\partial\left\langle{\smash{F_{z}}}\right\rangle}{\partial z}-\int_{\Omega_{f}}\boldsymbol{F}_{h}\cdot\nabla_{h}\mathcal{A}\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\frac{\boldsymbol{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\textnormal{d}s,

(54)

where use was made of the fact that
\partial\mathcal{A}/\partial z=0
.
The first term in this expression is the vertical gradient of the superficial average of
F_{z}
.
The second term represents the filtered horizontal gradient (see below).
The last term represents the fluxes that are exchanged between the fluid and the solid domain inside the averaging area.
Note that
(
Leonard 1974
;
Pope 2000
)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{f}}\boldsymbol{F}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\cdot\nabla_{h}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}&=\int_{\Omega_{f}}\boldsymbol{F}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{y}_{h}}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}\\
&=-\int_{\Omega_{f}}\boldsymbol{F}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}_{h}}\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}\\
&=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}_{h}}\cdot\int_{\Omega_{f}}\boldsymbol{F}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h}-\boldsymbol{y}_{h})\textnormal{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{h}\\
&=-\nabla_{h}\cdot\left\langle{\smash{\boldsymbol{F}_{h}}}\right\rangle,\end{split}

which implies that the vector form of the spatial averaging theorem takes the form

\left\langle{\smash{\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}}}\right\rangle=\nabla\cdot\left\langle{\smash{\boldsymbol{F}}}\right\rangle-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\frac{\boldsymbol{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\textnormal{d}s.

(55)

Substitution of
\boldsymbol{F}=\varphi\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
where
i\in\{x,y,z\}
into Eq. (
55
) results in the the spatial averaging theorem for scalars:

\left\langle{\smash{\nabla\varphi}}\right\rangle=\nabla\left\langle{\smash{\varphi}}\right\rangle-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{f}}\mathcal{A}\varphi\frac{\boldsymbol{N}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{h}|}\textnormal{d}s\,.

(56)
