Introduction

Persistent symptoms of chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) following initial endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) often necessitate revision surgery, which is associated with poorer clinical outcomes [1, 2]. Residual tissue is identified in 64%–96% of revision cases, supporting continued development of tools to improve dissection completeness [3, 4]. Image‐guided surgery (IGS) is associated with increased surgical completeness and lower ESS revision rates [5]. However, IGS remains limited by its dependence on static preoperative imaging and costly hardware. Consequently, there is a divergence between the displayed CT images and the surgical field with progressive dissection [5]. In one study, intraoperative CT (iCT) images acquired during ESS led to alterations in the surgical plan in 30% of patients, illustrating the value of up‐to‐date imaging [6]. However, iCT is rarely used due to increased operative time, radiation exposure, and cost.

To overcome these shortcomings, we propose virtual intraoperative CT (viCT), a software‐based approach that leverages neural radiance fields (NeRF) to reconstruct the surgical scene from endoscopic video. By co‐registering reconstructions with preoperative CT images, surgically removed tissue can be algorithmically deleted from the CT [7, 8]. Building upon prior work conducted in 3D‐printed models, we evaluated viCT in cadavers [9].

Methods

Four cadaveric specimens underwent bilateral ESS in three sequential stages: (1) maxillary antrostomy, (2) anterior ethmoidectomy, and (3) completion of dissection (total ethmoidectomy, sphenoidotomy, and Draf 2A frontal sinusotomy). After each stage, a 2‐min endoscopic survey was recorded, consisting of two sequential, standardized 1‐min sweeps of the sinonasal cavity using a 0° and 30°, 4.0‐mm rigid endoscope (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany). The high‐resolution 4K endoscopic tower (Stryker 1688 platform; Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI) and light source intensity (70%) were kept consistent across surveys. No image optimization modes were used. Preoperative and stage‐matched post‐dissection CT scans were obtained with consecutive 0.625‐mm‐thick axial sections (Somatom Edge; Siemens, Forchheim, Germany).

Endoscopic frames were extracted as 2–3 s clips (60–90 frames at 30 frames/s) and processed using a previously described, custom NeRF‐based pipeline to reconstruct intraoperative anatomy in 3D, rendering at 0.5 frames/second [8, 9, 10]. Each reconstruction was registered to the preoperative CT in 3D Slicer (v8.5.1). Manual surface‐based alignment was performed by two authors. Each reconstruction was translated and rotated to the corresponding sinonasal region and refined by iterative manual adjustments, aligning its global surface curvature and anatomy to the corresponding soft‐tissue contours on the CT. Adjustments continued until the reconstruction showed visually concordant overlap with the CT across triplanar views. Registered 3D reconstruction voxels were mapped into the physical CT coordinates and tested for inclusion within the reconstructed resection mask. Voxels classified as removed anatomy were deleted to generate a viCT.

The viCTs were compared with post‐dissection ground‐truth CT images acquired after each surgical stage. Volumetric overlap metrics (Dice similarity coefficient [DSC] and Jaccard index) assessed the degree of overlap on a scale between 0 and 1. Surface distance accuracy was quantified using the Chamfer distance and the 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95).

Results

Twelve viCT updates were generated (bilateral dissection in four cadavers across three stages) and compared with corresponding ground‐truth iCT. Triplanar viCT volumes demonstrated the expected stepwise progression of dissection (Figure 1). Descriptive metrics of interrater reliability in registration of the reconstruction with the associated ground‐truth CT are shown in Table S1. Overall cadaveric surface and volumetric agreement was high across HD95 (0.652 ± 0.161 mm), Chamfer distance (0.089 ± 0.026 mm), DSC (0.888 ± 0.023), and Jaccard index (0.800 ± 0.037). Table 1 shows surgery‐stage‐specific measures. Bland–Altman analysis indicated a consistent but low overestimation of tissue volume by viCT of 2.6% (95% CI, −0.16%–5.36%).

Discussion

This study demonstrates the potential of a computer vision‐based approach to dynamically update intraoperative imaging to reflect tissue removal during ESS. Building upon prior phantom‐based experiments, this study introduces biologic tissue properties such as surface reflectivity, moist fluid‐surface interfaces, anatomic variability, and structural heterogeneity [9]. There was strong volumetric overlap and surface accuracy between the viCT and stage‐matched ground‐truth CT.

While these findings demonstrate proof‐of‐concept feasibility, several limitations exist. Although mean HD95 was submillimeter at 0.652 ± 0.161 mm, the standard deviation indicates non‐uniform performance across specimens and surgical stages, which may reflect the smaller sample size. Differences in manual registration between the two authors may have exacerbated deviations in HD95. Nevertheless, these values represent submillimeter accuracy. Error quantification is inherently limited because manual registration through alignment of surface curvature, using a method akin to iterative closest point (ICP), precludes independent calculation of target registration error (TRE) [10]. Residual error therefore reflects combined reconstruction and registration error, and it is representative of the error that would be seen clinically. In addition, manual registration remains a barrier to clinical translation, accounting for 80%–90% of the approximately 50‐min total processing time required to generate a viCT. Future work will focus on fully automated, computationally optimized deformable registration to improve processing speed. Finally, factors unique to live ESS that could degrade reconstruction fidelity, such as mucosal edema, bleeding, lens contamination, and tissue deformation, are incompletely represented in a cadaveric model.

Overall, these findings support continued development of viCT as a potential approach for updating preoperative imaging during ESS. With further validation, this software‐based adjunct may improve IGS accuracy by updating imaging to accurately reflect the changing surgical field and inform surgical decision‐making during ESS. In the future, viCT could be integrated into a vision‐based surgical navigation platform to potentially improve the extent of surgical dissection by enhancing the surgeon's visuospatial awareness.
