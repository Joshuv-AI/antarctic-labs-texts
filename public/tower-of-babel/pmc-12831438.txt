A century of emergence: trends and patterns in human viral pathogens, 1900–2024, a systematic review
Authors: Ashley Quigley, Rosalie Chen, Adriana Notaras, Atalay Goshu Muluneh, Samsung Lim, Abrar A. Chughtai, C. Raina MacIntyre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmerging infectious diseases (EIDs) pose ongoing global health threats, making it essential to understand their temporal, geographic, and biological patterns to inform prevention and control strategies.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of 212 human viruses reported between 1900 and 2024, drawing data from Scopus, PubMed, and grey literature, and applied an adapted PRISMA framework. Temporal trends, geographic origins, modes of transmission, and clinical syndromes were analyzed, complemented by a novel visualization tool for exploring patterns interactively.ResultsViral emergence peaked during 1950–1979 (n = 87) and again since 2000 (n = 52), with the United States (n = 42), China (n = 15), and Australia (n = 10) most frequently reported as initial detection sites, reflecting differences in surveillance and research capacity. RNA viruses, particularly influenza viruses and coronaviruses, dominated emergence events. Vector-borne and zoonotic pathogens accounted for 62% of EIDs. Temporal peaks corresponded with advancements in molecular diagnostics, laboratory infrastructure, and global surveillance networks. We identified 212 viruses, with most emerging between 1950 and 1979 (n = 87) and 52 since 2000. Febrile disease was predominant (27.4%), with respiratory and hemorrhagic diseases also prevalent.ConclusionOur findings highlight how technological, ecological, and socio-demographic factors shape viral emergence. The interactive visualization tool provides a resource for understanding historical trends, informing risk assessment, and guiding future surveillance strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12362-8.

Introduction

Infectious diseases remain one of the most persistent and consequential threats to global health and biosecurity. They are responsible for widespread human and animal morbidity and mortality and have triggered some of the most severe global health crises in modern history [1, 2]. Over the past century, the emergence and re-emergence of infectious diseases - ranging from HIV/AIDS [3] and SARS [4] to Zika virus [5], COVID-19 [6], and Mpox [7], have repeatedly challenged health systems, exposed gaps in surveillance infrastructure, and disrupted economies and societies. These events highlight the reality that pathogen emergence is not a rare anomaly but an ongoing process influenced by an increasingly interconnected and ecologically fragile world.

Pathogen emergence is driven by a confluence of biological, ecological, and anthropogenic factors. Zoonotic spillover has been implicated in many emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) in humans [8, 9]. Increased human-animal interaction, driven by urbanization, deforestation, agricultural intensification, and the wildlife trade, has created new opportunities for pathogens to jump species barriers [2, 10]. At the same time, climate change is altering the distribution and behaviour of vectors and hosts, expanding the geographic range of many diseases and enabling the re-emergence of others in previously unaffected areas [11, 12]. Human mobility, through international travel and trade, further accelerates the spread of pathogens, often outpacing the ability of public health systems to respond [13]. Many emerging infectious diseases are zoonotic in origin, reflecting complex interactions at the human-animal-environment interface. Adopting a One Health perspective is therefore critical to understanding the drivers and pathways of disease emergence. This approach recognises that anthropogenic activities such as land-use change, agricultural intensification, wildlife trade, and climate change can facilitate spillover events and accelerate pathogen spread [14].

Technological and scientific advancements have transformed the way we detect and understand emerging diseases. Genomic surveillance, environmental sampling, artificial intelligence (AI), and open-source intelligence (OSINT) tools are increasingly integrated into global surveillance systems [15, 16]. These tools enhance our ability to detect early signals of emerging threats and facilitate rapid data sharing. However, despite these innovations, early detection is often delayed by fragmented data sources, underreporting, lack of standardization, and the unpredictability of novel pathogen characteristics [1, 13]. Consequently, there remains a pressing need for tools that can consolidate historical emergence data, visualize trends, and support predictive modelling.

While extensive research exists on individual outbreaks and high-impact pathogens, there is limited literature that systematically explores the timeline of pathogen emergence at a global level. Most surveillance efforts and academic analyses focus on specific diseases, outbreak response, or predictive risk assessments within limited temporal or geographic frames. A broader temporal analysis of emerging pathogens can reveal important trends and insights that are often overlooked in single-event studies. Understanding these trends is essential for anticipating future threats, prioritizing surveillance in high-risk regions, and informing the allocation of resources in preparedness planning [13].

In this study, we present a retrospective analysis of the timeline of emerging and re-emerging human pathogens from the early 20th century to the present. Drawing on data curated over 18 months and supported by expert validation, we developed a visual timeline of pathogen emergence integrated into EPIWATCH® - an AI-driven epidemic early warning system [17]. The timeline captures the year of first detection or significant emergence event, the type of viral pathogen, and geographical context. It offers an interactive and accessible tool for researchers, public health professionals, and policymakers to explore the evolution of global biosecurity threats.

To our knowledge, no study has provided a comprehensive, interactive timeline of viral emergence events with this scope (1900–2024) linked to a publicly available tool. This work aims to fill an important gap in the literature by mapping and contextualizing pathogen emergence events over time. It also seeks to support the development of risk forecasting and biosurveillance capabilities by identifying temporal clusters, high-concern pathogen types, and inflection points in surveillance evolution. Ultimately, our study contributes to the growing body of knowledge needed to improve pandemic preparedness and global health security by helping to understand the patterns that underlie emerging infectious disease events.

Methods

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive research investigation of the emergence of zoonotic and human viral pathogens since 1900. We applied an adapted PRISMA framework to systematically identify, select, and synthesize emerging infectious disease events, ensuring methodological transparency in data sources, eligibility criteria, and selection processes (refer to supplementary material). A detailed literature search was conducted through the publication databases (PubMed and Scopus), and grey literature such as the World Health Organisations (WHO), U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Flutrackers [18] and ProMed [19] websites, and the AI databases Healthmap [20] and EPIWATCH® [21], for new infectious pathogens, using the following key terms; ‘Emerging infection/s’, ‘Emerging infectious disease/s’, ‘Emerging pathogen/s’, ‘Emerging virus/s’, ‘New infection/s’, ‘New infectious disease/s’, ‘New pathogen/s’, ‘New virus/s’, ‘New influenza/strains’, ‘New avian influenza’, ‘New avian influenza/strains’, ‘New coronavirus/strains’, ‘Novel infection/s’, ‘Novel infectious disease/s’, ‘Novel pathogen/s’, ‘Novel virus/es’, ‘‘Novel influenza/strains’, ‘Novel avian influenza’, ‘Novel avian influenza/strains’ and ‘Novel coronavirus/strains’. In all searches, materials were limited to those which were published in the English language. Publications from Scopus were searched from September 2022 to July 2023 (and again in January from July 2023 to July 2024) for relevant studies. For grey literature, the first 150 results per search term were screened (50 results per page × 3 pages) to balance thoroughness with feasibility. We retrieved all articles and identified relevant articles through title screening, followed by abstract, and then a full paper review. Publications were not limited by study design or year of publication; serological studies alone were initially excluded due to their potential lack of diagnostic certainty. However, events supported by subsequent confirmatory testing or definitive diagnostic results were included when available [22]. For the purposes of this study, we defined an ‘outbreak’ or ‘emergence event’ as the first documented occurrence of human infection with a novel or previously unrecognized viral pathogen, supported by clinical or laboratory evidence. We acknowledge that for many viral diseases, infection may precede clinical manifestation by variable periods ranging from days to years or may result in asymptomatic or latent carriage. Our temporal attribution reflects the earliest date at which human infection was identified and reported, which may not coincide with the true time of zoonotic spillover or infection onset. This operational definition was chosen to ensure reproducibility and alignment with available surveillance and publication records.

We extracted information on virus name (listed according to the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) [23], noting that the initial pathogen name may have been updated since the virus was discovered) i.e. Genus/species/subtype. For the purposes of showing emergence of viruses in humans, we analysed species level only, year, and country of emergence. Whether the pathogen was zoonotic/human, and the specific vector (if zoonotic) was recorded where available i.e. zoonotic viruses include those originating in non-human animals, including those transmitted via vectors. Vector-borne refers specifically to transmission through arthropods. “Human viruses” refer to viruses sustained by human-to-human transmission. The primary mode of transmission was categorised as environmental, human to human, vector-borne, zoonotic and not identified. A total of 212 articles were recruited and reviewed based on predefined inclusion criteria. We acknowledge that some viruses could conceptually fall into multiple transmission categories (e.g., zoonotic and vector-borne). To ensure consistency, each virus was assigned to a single primary transmission category based on the dominant mechanism at the time of emergence. For zoonotic pathogens, we prioritized the origin of spillover from animal reservoirs, while for other viruses, classification was based on the main route of human-to-human transmission. For example, Lassa virus was classified as zoonotic due to rodent spillover, whereas Dengue virus was classified as vector-borne because mosquito transmission represents the primary route of human infection. This approach allowed for standardized comparisons across time and transmission categories. Overlapping modes were not captured, which we acknowledge as a limitation that may affect interpretation. In line with a One Health framework, we systematically included both zoonotic and human-to-human transmissible viruses in our analysis. Zoonotic viruses were identified through documented spillover from animal reservoirs or vectors, acknowledging their ecological and environmental context. Where available, data on animal hosts and transmission modes were extracted to reflect the interconnected drivers of emergence i.e. the animal host was included if described in the literature. Additional searching to obtain host information, if not included, was outside the scope of this paper. This approach aligns with recent recommendations emphasising the importance of integrated surveillance across human, animal, and environmental health domains [14]. Where viruses were not listed in the ICTV database (such as those pending classification approval), they were designated as “Unclassified”. We then conducted additional literature searches, including primary research articles and reports, to attempt classification where possible. The Case Fatality Rate (CFR) was collected where possible, however was not included in the results for this study, as findings were inconsistent across multiple studies and may not be directly linked to a particular clade and or may not be representative of the subtype. While the study primarily emphasizes trends in virus emergence, areas of agreement and disagreement across the literature were noted to inform interpretation and contextualization of findings.

Due to a large volume of search results for the above-mentioned pathogens, data collection was restricted to only newly emerged viruses. Titles and abstracts of retrieved articles were then screened by co-authors (Quigley, Chen, Notaras, Muluneh and Chughtai) to assess their relevance to the research topic. Relevant articles were selected for full-text review, while irrelevant ones are excluded. Inter-author agreement was ensured through independent review by two co-authors (Quigley and Chen), with discrepancies resolved through consensus. This independent double-review process ensured the reliability of data extraction and minimized errors. Viruses were included if they were reported to have caused human disease. The year of emergence was defined as the year in which the virus was first reported in humans, based on available evidence. A virus was considered reported in humans if there were clinical diagnoses suggestive of infection, laboratory isolation of the virus from a human, or detection in human specimens through retrospective sampling. We acknowledge that certain diseases, such as poliomyelitis, were clinically recognized long before the viral cause was identified; however, for consistency and clarity, our timeline defines emergence as the point at which the virus was first reported or confirmed in humans through clinical or laboratory evidence. Where retrospective studies were used, the year of emergence was taken as the year the samples were collected. Where diseases were clinically described before the 1900s but the causative virus was later isolated from a human, the year of emergence was taken to be year of virus isolation. The emergence of all viruses since 1900 were included. Serotyping based on historical relevance and current relevance/importance to public health, such as various subtypes of avian influenza or coronaviruses with human transmission were included. Where human-to-human transmission was observed, phenotypic sublineages were collected and their emergences were mapped separately.

Data analysis and visualisation

Viruses were then further categorized into the following disease categories according to their primary clinical manifestations: respiratory, exanthematous, gastrointestinal, neurological, febrile, haemorrhagic, hepatic, and other (diseases with variable primary clinical manifestation). Authors with clinical expertise also provided domain knowledge regarding diseases (Chughtai and MacIntyre). Emerging viruses were categorized according to their most prominent or initial clinical presentation, as described in first outbreak reports or initial discovery publications. While many viral infections present with fever, assigning all viruses to a general febrile illness category would have limited the utility of syndromic grouping and obscured important distinctions. Therefore, each virus was assigned to a primary syndrome category based on its dominant clinical features at the time of emergence, such as neurological, haemorrhagic, respiratory, dermatological, febrile, or gastrointestinal syndromes. Our single-label syndromic classification represents the dominant clinical syndrome at emergence and was recorded for each virus, consistent with the total of 212 viruses. This approach was designed to reduce overlap and aid in the visualization of syndromic patterns across time and geography. We acknowledge that many viruses cause multisystem illness, and that fever is a common, sometimes universal symptom. For example, arboviruses such as dengue and chikungunya often present with both rash and fever, and others may progress from nonspecific symptoms to severe complications such as encephalitis or haemorrhage. Our classification is therefore not mutually exclusive, but reflects a pragmatic framework intended to highlight the dominant syndrome at the point of emergence. This aids in interpreting early outbreak patterns and supports the development of targeted syndromic surveillance strategies. Using this data, we produced a comprehensive timeline of the emergence of human and zoonotic viruses since 1900. To best view trends in emergence over time in this large dataset, an interactive timeline of emerging pathogens in the last century was created as a Shiny App, using RStudio version 4.2.1. [24, 25], enabling users to filter and search for viruses based on primary clinical manifestation of disease, year, disease name, viruses, and predominant transmission mode, which may be accessed at: https://timelineofemergingviruses.shinyapps.io/Timeline_of_Emerging_Viruses/.

To capture a comprehensive timeline of viral emergence, two complementary search approaches were used: “search virus” and “search disease.” The “search virus” strategy focused on taxonomy-based terms, such as specific virus names or genera (e.g., Enterovirus, Filovirus, Coronavirus), and was used to trace the earliest documented human cases or identifications. In contrast, the “search disease” approach focused on clinical syndromes or disease manifestations (e.g., “hemorrhagic fever,” “viral encephalitis”) to identify outbreak reports that may not have been initially attributed to a known virus. This dual approach allowed for the inclusion of both well-characterized viral agents and syndromic presentations where the causative virus may have been identified retrospectively, with a temporal zoom feature to allowing to zoom on one period of time.

To look at the global distribution of the data collected in this study, we mapped proportional bar lengths that represent the values of transmission modes and clinical syndromes. The values were aggregated per country and were displayed side by side. Python 3.12 was used for data aggregation, and ArcGIS Pro 3.3 was used for map generation. These figures are intended to provide a high-level, visual overview of the geographic distribution of transmission modes (Fig. 3) and clinical syndromes (Fig. 4), rather than a detailed analytical comparison between countries. The proportional bars reflect aggregated values per country and are best interpreted as a global snapshot of patterns rather than precise quantitative differences. For more granular and interactive exploration of these data, users can refer to the Shiny app, where these visualizations are integrated in an interactive format.

Results

Emerging viruses in humans

Out of 698 studies identified across multiple databases, 212 met the inclusion criteria, resulting in the identification of 212 human viral pathogens that emerged between 1900 and 2024. This study identified 212 viruses affecting humans, emerging between 1905 and 2024. Most viruses were identified in the latter half of the 20th century, with 184 viruses identified, compared to 28 viruses from 1900 to 1950. The number of viruses, location and most common transmission modes identified during the following decades are summarised in Fig. 1. From 1970 to 79, 14.1% (n = 30) of viruses emerged with 13.2% between 1950 and 59 (n = 28), and 12.7% between 1960 and 69 and 2000-09 each (n = 27). From 1980 to 89, 9.9% (n = 21) of viruses emerged and between 1990 and 99, 11.8% (n = 25) of viruses emerged. Since 2000, 54 new viruses have emerged, comprising 25.5% of emerging pathogens since the 20th century. The United States (US) experienced 19.8% of new emerged viruses since the 20th century, (n = 42), followed by China, 7.1% (n = 15), and Australia, 4.7% (n = 10) (Available in the Supplementary material).

Timeline of emerging pathogens

Figure 2 shows the timeline of emerging pathogens since the 20th century extracted from the interactive Shiny app designed for this study. Highlighted are major clusters of zoonotic, vector-borne and human-human viruses. Zoonotic viruses comprised the largest proportion of viruses at 32.1% (n = 68/212), followed by vector-borne pathogens (n = 67/212 [31.6%]) and viruses with human-to-human transmission at 30.2% (n = 64/212). One virus, simian virus 40 (SV40), was associated with vaccination, as poliovirus vaccines contaminated with simian virus 40 were administered to millions of people globally between 1955 and 1963 [26], however this represents a vaccine contaminant rather than a naturally emerging pathogen. For 12 viruses (5.7%), insufficient evidence was available for confirming their primary mode of transmission to humans. An increase in zoonotic viruses was predominant in 1990-99 (n = 16/212 [7.5%]), followed by 2000-09 (n = 11/212 [5.2%]), and 1970-79 (n = 9/212 [4.2%]), whilst the emergence of vector-borne viruses were predominant between 1950 and 1959 and 1960-69 (n = 13/212 [6.1%] each), followed by 1940-49 (n = 9/212 [4.2%]). The top three decades with greatest emergence of viruses with predominantly human-to-human transmission were evident throughout 2000-09 (n = 12/212 [5.7%]), 1970-79 (n = 11/212 [5.2%]) and 1960-69 (n = 10/212 [4.7%]) (Fig. 2).

Notable zoonotic viruses included HIV, Ebola virus disease and Mers-CoV. In the instance of Ebola virus disease, the first detection occurred in humans in the 1970s. Vector-borne alphaviruses (including chikungunya and Venezuelan equine encephalitis), orthobunyaviruses (such as Oropouche and Ilesha viruses) and orthoflaviviruses (notably Murray valley encephalitis virus, Kyasanur forest disease virus and Powassan virus) dominated the 1950s. Throughout the mid-20th century, major outbreaks of various arboviruses such as yellow fever, Rift Valley fever, and West Nile fever have occurred, emphasizing the ongoing threat of vector-borne febrile diseases. The emergence of haemorrhagic and febrile disease-causing viruses was evident in the last century. In 1930, the first cases of dengue haemorrhagic fever were reported, indicating the emergence of a more severe form of dengue virus infection with higher mortality rates.

Our study shows that the most common transmission modes (Fig. 3) were vector-borne and zoonotic, each recorded 67 times, followed by human-to-human transmission (63 occurrences), unidentified transmission (12 occurrences), and vaccine-associated transmission (1 occurrence). The single instance of vaccine-associated transmission occurred in the United States.

Countries with three or more human-to-human transmission events included the United States (21), Japan (5), Hong Kong (SAR) (4), the United Kingdom (4), China (3), Australia (3), and the Netherlands (3). For vector-borne transmission, nine countries reported three or more occurrences: the United States (9), Uganda (7), Australia (5), Trinidad and Tobago (5), China (3), Italy (3), Brazil (3), Panama (3), and the Central African Republic (3). Zoonotic transmission was recorded three or more times in the United States (9), China (8), Malaysia (3), and South Korea (3). All countries and territories reported between 0 and 2 cases of unclassified transmission.

Discussion

Our findings reveal distinct periods of heightened viral emergence, notably in the 1950s, 1990s, and early 2000s, which correspond with major advances in diagnostics, surveillance, and global health infrastructure. The post-World War II era saw substantial investment in virology laboratories, cell culture techniques, and vector research, enabling the identification of a range of arboviruses and zoonotic pathogens such as Lassa and Machupo viruses [27]. Similarly, the HIV/AIDS pandemic in the 1980–1990 s catalysed global laboratory networking, strengthened blood safety protocols, and intensified pathogen discovery [28]. The introduction of PCR in 1983, with widespread adoption by the 1990s in high-resource countries, enabled rapid detection of viral genomes, contributing to the identification of hepatitis C (1989) [29], Hendra virus (1994), and Nipah virus (1999) [30, 31]. These temporal inflection points highlight the interplay between scientific advances and the detection of novel viral pathogens.

From the early 2000s onwards, molecular and digital surveillance methods expanded further. Real-time PCR [32], sequencing technologies, and bioinformatics tools [33] enabled faster pathogen discovery, while open-source platforms such as ProMED [19], HealthMap [20], and EPIWATCH® [34, 35] accelerated early warning and outbreak tracking. This was evident in the rapid identification of SARS-CoV (2002), MERS-CoV (2012), and SARS-CoV-2 (2019) [36]. These peaks in detection reflect both genuine emergence events and the increasing sensitivity and accessibility of global surveillance systems.

It is important to note that detection is not synonymous with biological emergence. The concentration of reported emergence events in the US, China, and Australia primarily reflects surveillance and reporting capacity rather than true “hotspots” of spillover. High-resource countries with robust laboratory networks and diagnostic capabilities are more likely to detect and report novel pathogens, while low-resource regions remain underrepresented. Surveillance bias likely also contributes to the predominance of influenza viruses, coronaviruses, and filoviruses in our timeline, as these families are routinely monitored and extensively studied. While our broad search strategy aimed to mitigate this bias, structural inequities in global surveillance remain an inherent limitation.

Causal interpretation of temporal clusters must be made cautiously. While the 1950s peak in arbovirus discovery coincides with post-war investment in virology, ecological changes, such as land-use modifications, shifts in vector distribution, and agricultural expansion, may have also played a role. Similarly, correlations between molecular diagnostics or urbanization and emergence events do not imply direct causation. Future studies integrating ecological and environmental datasets could strengthen causal inference and disentangle true emergence patterns from surveillance artifacts.

A One Health perspective is critical for understanding these trends. Most events in our timeline involve zoonotic or vector-borne transmission, emphasizing the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health. Anthropogenic factors, such as urbanization, deforestation, wildlife exploitation, and intensification of livestock production, particularly in Southeast Asia and West Africa, coincided with spillover events including Nipah virus, Ebola virus, and zoonotic influenza strains [8, 9]. Regional conflicts and population displacement have also been associated with weakened health systems and increased vulnerability to viral outbreaks [37]. Vector-borne and zoonotic transmission remain the most frequently recorded modes of viral emergence globally, emphasising the persistent role of animal and arthropod reservoirs in driving outbreaks.

The biological characteristics of viruses further influence emergence patterns. RNA viruses, including influenza A, coronaviruses, and filoviruses, exhibit high mutation rates, genomic reassortment, recombination, and host-jumping potential through intermediate reservoirs [8, 38–40]. This highlights their repeated emergence, as reflected in our timeline, with influenza pandemics (1918 H1N1, 1957 H2N2, 1968 H3N2, 2009 H1N1) and successive coronavirus outbreaks (SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2).

Our timeline captures recognition of viral emergence rather than the exact biological onset. Many viruses have complex natural histories, including latent or persistent infection states, variable incubation periods, and subclinical carriage, which complicate outbreak attribution. For instance, SARS-CoV-2 evolution [41] and prolonged sequelae [42, 43] illustrate how viral dynamics extend beyond initial detection) Similarly, chronic infections such as hepatitis [44, 45] may have long latency before clinical diagnosis, while Ebola virus can persist in immune-privileged sites, delaying transmission [46]. Consequently, recorded ‘emergence year’ reflects surveillance detection rather than biological onset, an important consideration for temporal clustering analysis.

Contextualizing viral emergence alongside control efforts provides additional insight. Vaccination campaigns have profoundly altered outbreak trajectories, as seen with smallpox and poliomyelitis [47, 48]. However, resurgence of measles in areas with declining coverage [49], and continued circulation of yellow fever [50] and Japanese encephalitis [51], highlight that vaccine availability alone does not guarantee control. Integrating these events into the timeline enhances understanding of emergence, persistence, and control dynamics [8, 9, 19, 20, 27–40, 47, 49–58].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design may introduce bias, particularly for early outbreaks when cases were unrecognized or misclassified. Surveillance and reporting biases likely overrepresent high-resource regions while underestimating low-resource areas. High-profile viral families may be overrepresented relative to others due to stronger surveillance, and historical records are often incomplete. Language barriers, data access, and geopolitical factors further limit global representation. All viruses in the dataset are treated equally, which does not reflect public health significance. For 12 viruses (5.7%) with unidentified transmission modes, we were unable to assign a definitive category despite additional searches, highlighting gaps in historical reporting and virus biology. Future work could incorporate multi-label syndromic classifications, severity-based tiering, and spatiotemporal analyses to provide a more nuanced understanding of pathogen dynamics.

Knowledge gap and future directions

Our timeline highlights the dynamic nature of viral emergence and emphasis the need for integrated, proactive surveillance. Incorporating a One Health perspective is essential to capture interactions between human, animal, and environmental health that drive spillover events. Future work should link epidemic intelligence with animal health, environmental, genomic, serological, and clinical datasets to enhance early warning capacity. Integration with AI and predictive modelling could facilitate earlier detection of risk signals, enabling timely interventions. Planned enhancements to the visualization tool include filtering by viral family and overlaying public health milestones, which will contextualize emergence alongside control measures. Additional modelling could prioritize high-risk viral families and strengthen preparedness strategies. These developments aim to increase the interpretive depth and utility of the tool for research, policy, and public health preparedness. Future work will also incorporate multi-label syndromic and spatiotemporal analyses, including spread metrics, to provide a more detailed understanding of pathogen dynamics.

Conclusions

This synthesis highlights how viral emergence is shaped by a convergence of biological, technological, ecological, and social factors. Temporal patterns correspond with key developments such as advances in molecular diagnostics, the expansion of global surveillance networks, and shifting environmental and demographic pressures. RNA viruses continue to dominate emergence events due to their high mutation rates and potential for cross-species transmission. While some pathogens have been successfully controlled through vaccination, others, like measles, have resurged in areas with declining immunisation coverage, underscoring persistent vulnerabilities. Our visualisation tool maps the emergence of viral pathogens over time, offering a clear, accessible representation of historical inflection points and temporal clustering. By enabling integrated analyses of past trends, this timeline can support hypothesis generation, strengthen preparedness strategies, and inform future research. Integrating One Health perspectives, enhanced surveillance data, and predictive modelling could provide earlier insights into emerging threats. Strengthening vaccination programs, maintaining robust surveillance, and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration will be essential to mitigate future epidemic and pandemic risks.
