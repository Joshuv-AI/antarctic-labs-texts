Mutual inhibition between EPFL2 and auxin extends the intervals of periodic leaf morphogenesis
Authors: Toshiaki Tameshige, Takeshi Tsuchida, Yuuki Matsushita, Yuki Doll, Kaisei Maruyama, Takemoto Agui, Mitsuhiro Aida, Hiroyuki Kasahara, Keiko U. Torii, Naoyuki Uchida, Koichi Fujimoto, Momoko Ikeuchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rules behind the spatial arrangement of repetitive modules are key principles of multicellular morphogenesis. In plant shoots, PIN-FORMED 1 (PIN1)-mediated polar auxin transport instructs periodic formation of auxin maxima, which develop into lateral organs or leaf serrations. However, it remains unknown how the intervals of regularly arising auxin maxima are controlled. Here we show that a cysteine rich peptide EPIDERMAL PATTERNING FACTOR-LIKE 2 (EPFL2) dose-dependently determines the intervals of auxin maxima during serration formation in leaf primordia. EPFL2 constitutes a mutually repressive relationship with cellular auxin response, thereby produces bistable states. Based on our computational simulations, we propose that the bistability modulates the periodicity of PIN1-mediated auxin maxima formation. Moreover, EPFL2 ensures precise phyllotactic angles, underscoring the general role of EPFL2 in auxin-mediated shoot patterning. This study illustrates that the intercoupling between EPFL2-auxin bistable module and PIN1-mediated polar auxin transport underpins versatile periodicity in auxin maxima formation during shoot development.

Introduction

How multicellular systems give rise to sophisticated morphologies stands as a central question in developmental biology. Periodic formation of structural modules is generally observed during multicellular development both in animals and in plants1–3. In seed plants, lateral organs such as leaves and floral organs repeatedly arise on the flanks of the shoot apical meristems (SAMs) at regular intervals, leading to the formation of phyllotactic patterns. Leaves emerge as oval primordia with smooth margins and are later elaborated by forming local outgrowths including serrations and leaflets along their margins4–9. Initiation sites of the lateral organs, serrations and leaflets are instructed by the localized activation of auxin response called auxin maxima. During shoot morphogenesis, an auxin transporter PIN-FORMED 1 (PIN1) is primarily responsible for the auxin maxima formation10,11. PIN1 directionally transports auxin toward incipient primordia and depletes auxin from the neighboring cells. A series of mathematical modeling studies recapitulated the spiral phyllotactic pattern with the assumption that PIN1 polarity is regulated by the auxin level of neighboring cells, so that PIN1 transports auxin toward cells with higher auxin concentration11–15. This assumption is sufficient for the formation of regularly spaced auxin maxima patterns in phyllotaxis and serration formation. PIN1-auxin module generates periodic patterns via transport-induced instability16 in a similar fashion with the diffusion-based Turing pattern, which mediates various biological pattern formation17,18. Diverse spatial patterns of auxin maxima formation were generated in silico by changing the speed of auxin diffusion and/or PIN1-mediated auxin transport6,8. However, the exact mechanisms regulating the intervals of auxin maxima in vivo remain elusive. Furthermore, it is still unknown if any PIN1-independent mechanism may be involved in the spatiotemporal regulation of auxin maxima initiation.

Serration formation in Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis) offers an excellent model system to study repetitive morphogenesis. Serration formation is a simple and predictable event occurring in the one-dimensional field along the leaf margin, which can be easily analyzed by imaging and in silico approaches. A previous study proposed a mathematical model where PIN1-mediated auxin transport reproduced the repetitive rise of auxin maxima along the leaf margin in a basipetal order, as observed in Arabidopsis leaves6. In principle, the regulatory relationships between PIN1 and auxin were modeled in a similar way as in phyllotaxis models. The only difference is that the PIN1 dynamics depend on the boundary-expressed transcription factor CUP-SHAPED COTYLEDON 2 (CUC2) in the serration model6. In addition to its role in auxin maxima initiation, CUC2 also promotes serration outgrowth19. A previous study showed that the serration outgrowth is also regulated by a cysteine-rich peptide EPIDERMAL PATTERNING FACTOR-LIKE 2 (EPFL2)20. EPFL family members regulate diverse developmental processes in plants21–25. EPFL2 negatively regulates cellular auxin response at the leaf margin, and auxin in turn represses EPFL2 expression. The reciprocal inhibition between EPFL2 and cellular auxin response was proposed to trigger localized tooth growth20. In general, mutually repressive gene regulatory relationships are able to create a toggle switch composed of bistable states26. Bistability in gene expression status shapes clear boundaries in pattern formation of multicellular systems, including early embryogenesis in Drosophila melanogaster27,28. However, whether the reciprocal inhibition between EPFL2 and auxin is involved in the spatial regulation of auxin maxima initiation remains unknown. Furthermore, it is generally unexplored whether the mutual repression modulates the intervals of diffusion-based Turing-type patterns.

Here, we report our discovery that EPFL2 regulates the interval of regularly arising auxin maxima in a dose-dependent manner during serration formation. We further show that EPFL2 and its close paralog, EPFL1, are required for the formation of sharp auxin maxima. Our experimental and computational approaches together demonstrate that the reciprocal inhibition produces bistable relationships between EPFLs and auxin thereby regulates the intervals of auxin maxima initiation.

Results

EPFL2 extends the intervals of auxin maxima along the leaf margin

We tested the potential role of EPFL2 in controlling the spatial pattern of serration initiation in leaves. Because the epfl2 mutant has shallow serrations which are often not clearly discernable, we first performed fluorescent imaging of the auxin response reporter DR5rev::GFP to visualize auxin maxima along the leaf margin, which correspond to serrations (Fig. 1a). To our surprise, the epfl2 mutant has a significantly higher number of serrations despite the absence of prominent tooth outgrowth (Fig. 1a, b). Whereas the 5th – 6th leaves of wild-type (WT) have 2.6 serrations on average per one side of the leaf, the epfl2 mutant leaves have 4.1 serrations. Noteworthy, 25.8 % (40/155) of the epfl2 mutant leaves have more than 4 serrations, which was never observed in WT in our experimental condition (n = 295). Given that the auxin maxima periodically arise in a specific developmental window at the early stage of leaf development, the increased number of auxin maxima may either be attributable to the extension of the developmental window or the shortening of the spatiotemporal interval between the periodic rise of auxin maxima. To distinguish these possibilities, we counted auxin maxima across developmental stages. Higher number of auxin maxima was observed in the epfl2 mutant already at the early stage of leaf development (Supplementary Fig. 1b), supporting the latter possibility that the periodicity is shorter in the epfl2 mutant. Consistently, we confirmed that the increase in auxin maxima in the epfl2 mutant is not associated with the increase in the overall leaf length (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

Next, we directly measured the intervals of incipient auxin maxima in leaf primordia. A previous study only measured the intervals at the later developmental stage when the intervening cell number has increased after the initiation of auxin maxima9. Our measurements on incipient auxin maxima indeed revealed that auxin maxima are formed at the regular intervals along the lateral sides of the leaf primordia (Supplementary Fig. 1d). We then compared the intervening cell numbers between the leaf tip and the first lateral auxin maximum (the first interval), the first and second lateral maximum (the second interval) and the second and the third lateral maximum (the third interval). Our measurements revealed that the intervening cells are constantly less in the epfl2 mutant than in WT (Fig. 1c, d, Supplementary Fig. 1e, f). The interval length in the absolute distance is shorter in the epfl2 mutant as well (Fig. 1e). To further confirm that the periodicity in auxin maxima formation is shorter in the epfl2 mutant, we estimated the organ size when a new auxin maximum arises, based on the observation of leaf primordia with various sizes. As expected, the second lateral maximum appears on significantly smaller leaf primordia in the epfl2 mutant (250 μm) than in WT (340 μm) (Fig. 1f). The complementation test confirmed that the observed difference in auxin maxima formation is caused by the loss-of-function of EPFL2 (Fig. 1g, h). Overall, we conclude that EPFL2 prolongs the interval of auxin maxima initiation during leaf development.

ERL1 and ERL2 negatively regulate the serration number

EPFL peptides are perceived by ERECTA-family Leucine-Rich Repeat Receptor-Like Kinases (LRR-RLKs), namely, ERECTA (ER), ERECTA-LIKE 1 (ERL1) and ERL229. To test which of these ER family receptors is responsible for the regulation of auxin maxima spacing in leaves, we analyzed their loss-of-function mutants. Considering the functional redundancy, we analyzed three combinations of double mutants and found out that only the erl1 erl2 double mutant resembles the epfl2 mutant in terms of a higher number of auxin maxima (Fig. 1i, j). This is in agreement with the previously reported peptide-receptor interaction assays using isothermal titration calorimetry, which showed that the EPFL2 peptide binds to ERL1 and ERL2 with higher affinity than to ER23. Reduced leaf serrations in er erl1 or er erl2 compared to WT is likely due to the modification in leaf shape as reported before30 rather than the difference in auxin maxima intervals. Altogether, our phenotypic analyses of ER family mutants suggest that EPFL2 primarily regulates the intervals of auxin maxima through ERL1 and ERL2.

EPFL2 inhibits cellular responsiveness to auxin

We previously reported that EPFL2 expression and cellular auxin response have mutually inhibitory relationships, where EPFL2 overexpression down-regulates cellular auxin response and exogenous application of auxin down-regulates EPFL2 expression20. EPFL2 may repress cellular auxin response as visualized by the DR5 reporter, either via the reduction in auxin content and/or the suppression of cellular responsiveness to auxin. To test whether EPFL2 reduces auxin level, we compared the endogenous level of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and its major metabolite 2-oxindole-3-acetic acid (oxIAA) in shoot apices including leaf primordia, between WT and the constitutive over-expressor of EPFL2, 35Sp::EPFL220. We found that neither the endogenous IAA nor its derivative oxIAA was reduced in the EPFL2 over-expressor (Fig. 2a). These observations suggest that EPFL2 represses cellular auxin response with minimal influence on the IAA amounts. To confirm that EPFL2 overexpression negatively regulates cellular responsiveness to auxin, we exogenously applied an excess amount of synthetic auxin, 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), and evaluated the DR5rev::GFP fluorescent signals in leaf primordia. Whereas DR5rev::GFP fluorescence was strongly enhanced after 24 h of 2,4-D application in the WT background, the induction of DR5rev::GFP fluorescence was dramatically weaker in the 35Sp::EPFL2 background (Fig. 2b, c). As 2,4-D is a synthetic auxin with different stability and transport properties compared to the natural auxin IAA31, we also tested the effects of IAA application. Consistent with the 2,4-D application (Fig. 2b, c), the IAA application also failed to intensify DR5rev::GFP fluorescence when EPFL2 is overexpressed (Supplementary Fig. 2). Next, we tested the expression pattern of EPFL2 in leaf primordia and its dependency on endogenous auxin signaling. We previously reported that EPFL2 promoter-GUS reporter displayed roughly complementary spatial patterns with DR5::GUS, although they were not simultaneously visualized20. In addition, our previous 2,4-D application experiments20 did not directly prove that the endogenous level of EPFL2 is regulated by cellular auxin signaling. To visualize EPFL2 promoter activity at higher resolution using a functional construct, we tagged EPFL2 with NLS-mCherry using translation-coupled processing of the IntF2A system32,33 and introduced the construct into the epfl2 mutant. The construct is designed to produce functional EPFL2 and nuclear-localized mCherry. As expected, EPFL2p::mCherry-IntF2A-EPFL2 complemented epfl2 phenotypes, including auxin maxima interval and serration depth (Fig. 2d–f). We observed that the EPFL2p::mCherry-IntF2A-EPFL2 signal is locally reduced at the auxin maxima (Fig. 2d). Using this reporter, we observed that the EPFL2 expression is upregulated upon inhibition of auxin signaling by the treatment of auxinole, an auxin antagonist of the TIR1 receptor complex34 (Fig. 2g, h). These data together corroborate the idea that reciprocal inhibitions between EPFL2 and auxin response establish mutually exclusive spatial patterns during serration formation.

Computer simulation supports that mutual repression between EPFL2 and auxin prolongs the intervals of auxin maxima initiation

To obtain system-level insights into the EPFL2-mediated pattern formation, we implemented a mathematical model of the regulatory relationships between EPFL2, auxin and PIN1 and performed a computer simulation of the multicellular dynamics. We formulated the PIN1-mediated polar-transport of auxin as described previously for phyllotactic pattern formation12. Cellular auxin response was calculated by multiplying the amount of auxin by the cellular responsiveness to auxin, which is negatively regulated by EPFL2. In addition, EPFL2 expression is modeled as the summation of auxin-repressed and auxin-independent production, reflecting the previous observation that the EPFL2 expression is downregulated but not completely lost upon auxin application20. We did not initially implement EPFL2 diffusion in this model and tested the effect of EPFL2 diffusion as described later. We thereby constructed the intercoupling model, where the PIN1-mediated polar auxin transport is coupled with a mutually repressive regulatory circuit between EPFL2 and auxin response (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 3a). These regulatory relationships are simulated on a one-dimensional multicellular model recapitulating the leaf margin with cell divisions occurring at the basal end of the leaf35,36 (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Movie 1). Consistent with in vivo observations, auxin maxima newly arise near the leaf base at regular intervals as cell number increases in silico (Fig. 3b). To check the robustness of our model to fluctuations in tissue growth pattern, we also performed simulations in a condition where cells in the basal region of leaf primordia stochastically divide. While most auxin maxima basipetally initiated (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Movies 2, 3), auxin maxima are infrequently formed between preexisting ones in this simulation setting (Supplementary Fig. 3c, Supplementary Movie 3), which are also occasionally observed in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 3d). As expected from the mutually inhibitory relationships between auxin response and EPFL2 expression, parameters for these two processes display bistable behavior (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Increased dosage of auxin-dependent EPFL2 expression prolongs the auxin maxima interval

To experimentally validate the predictions from the computational simulation, we increased the dosage of EPFL2 in both auxin-dependent and auxin-independent manners. We confirmed that the EPFL2 expression in the transgenic line is repressed by auxin application (Fig. 4a). As our simulations predicted that increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{E}$$\end{document}αE significantly prolongs the intervals while the effect of βE is subtle (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5), we expected to observe longer auxin maxima intervals in gEPFL2 leaves. Indeed, two independent transgenic lines of gEPFL2 out of five exhibit the reduction in leaf serration number (#2 and #3; Fig. 4b, c). The expression level of the EPFL2, the summation of the endogenous gene and a transgene, was significantly higher in these transgenic lines which displayed prominent reduction in the serration number (Fig. 4d). Transgenic lines without discernible phenotypes (#1, #4, #5) had weak EPFL2 overexpression (Fig. 4d). These data together indicate that the increased dose of EPFL2 driven by its native regulatory sequence prolongs the interval of auxin maxima. We confirmed that the second interval contains significantly more cells in gEPFL2 #2 than in WT (Fig. 4e–g). EPFL1/2 are required for the formation of sharp auxin maxima

The simulation predicted that the sharpness of each auxin maximum would be compromised in the absence of the EPFL2 function (Supplementary Fig. 4), whereas the epfl2 mutant does not display an obvious defect in peak sharpness. Hence, it is plausible that other functionally redundant EPFLs may be involved in auxin maxima formation as well. The top candidate is EPFL1, which is the closest paralog of EPFL2 and is expressed in leaf primordia20 (Fig. 5a). EPFL1 reportedly plays redundant roles in regulating shoot development with EPFL2/4/624. We found that the epfl1 mutant has shallow serrations similar to the epfl2 mutant, although the serration number is unaffected in the epfl1 mutant (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). The EPFL1 expression is only subtly downregulated by auxin, which is consistent with its broad spatial expression in leaf primordia (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 8c). When we analyzed auxin maxima formation in the epfl1 epfl2 double mutant, we found that leaf primordia fail to generate sharp auxin maxima (Fig. 5b). In the epfl1 epfl2 mutant, the normalized spatial gradient of the average fluorescence of DR5rev::GFP is broader and more variable than in WT or in the epfl2 mutant (Fig. 5c, d). We quantitatively compared the peak sharpness between in vivo measurement and in silico simulation. Consistent with the observation that auxin maxima in WT and the epfl2 mutant have comparable sharpness in vivo, sharp peaks are formed in silico in the parameter settings where WT-like or epfl2 mutant-like periodicity arises (Fig. 5e, f). Further reduction in EPFL2 activity in silico or epfl1 epfl2 mutant situation in vivo leads to the peak broadening (Fig. 5e, f). In the framework of the PIN1-auxin model, peak sharpness can be compromised by the reduction in PIN1-mediated polar auxin transport. We therefore introgressed the PIN1p::PIN1-YFP in the epfl1 epfl2 mutant to test if the expression level and/or the polarity of PIN1 along the leaf margin is affected in the mutant background. EPFL1/2 ensure stable divergence angles in phyllotaxis

It is well established that auxin maxima define the site of lateral organ primordia and hence phyllotactic patterns. To test whether EPFL1/2 generally regulate the spatial pattern of auxin maxima formation in shoots, we analyzed the phyllotactic pattern on inflorescence meristems. We found that the spatial arrangement of primordia is deviated from the stereotypical spiral phyllotaxis in the epfl2 or the epfl1 epfl2 mutant (Fig. 6a). The divergence angles of the epfl2 and the epfl1 epfl2 mutant are significantly more variable than WT, while the median divergence angles are not affected (Fig. 6b). The phyllotactic phenotype of the epfl2 and the epfl1 epfl2 mutants is reminiscent of the er erl1 erl2 triple mutant, which reportedly have irregular phyllotaxis and enlarged SAMs30. Our imaging analyses, however, revealed that SAM size was not affected in the epfl2 or the epfl1 epfl2 mutants (Fig. 6c). Nevertheless, SAMs have higher numbers of auxin maxima (Fig. 6d), suggesting that the intervals of auxin maxima are shorter in these mutants as observed for leaf serrations (Fig. 1c). Vegetative shoots also have unstable phyllotactic patterns and unaffected meristem size in the epfl2 or the epfl1 epfl2 mutants, underscoring the generality of the phyllotactic phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 9). Discussion

Spatial arrangement of newly-arising auxin maxima instructs plant morphogenesis. Ever since the in silico reconstruction of the spiral phyllotaxis and serration patterns via PIN1-mediated mechanisms6,12–14, the PIN1-auxin positive feedback model has been regarded as a central patterning principle in shoot organogenesis. In this study, we propose an intercoupling model where the mutual-inhibitory module consisting of EPFL2 and auxin response is coupled with PIN1-mediated auxin transport at the level of cellular auxin response (Fig. 7). We present genetic evidence that EPFL2 regulates the interval length of regularly-spaced auxin maxima along the leaf margin (Fig. 1). Our computer simulations based on mathematical modeling not only recapitulated the observed auxin maxima intervals in WT and the epfl2 mutant but also produced testable hypotheses (Fig. 3), which were experimentally validated by increasing the dose (gEPFL2 in WT, 35Sp::EPFL2) or further decreasing the dose (epfl1 epfl2 mutant) (Figs. 4, 5). Noteworthy, EPFL2 overexpression under the control of endogenous regulatory sequence (gEPFL2) or constitutive 35S promoter has contrasting effects on auxin maxima intervals, which matched the outcome of increasing the parameter for auxin-repressed (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{E}$$\end{document}αE) or -independent (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta }_{E}$$\end{document}βE) expression of EPFL2 in silico, respectively (Fig. 4). This experimental evidence corroborates the prediction from the simulation that the dose-dependent effect of EFPL2 expression on auxin maxima intervals is more effective if the expression is negatively regulated by auxin. Although our model, without considering EPFL2 diffusion, qualitatively reproduced the above-mentioned dependency of auxin maxima patterns on EPFL2 in silico, our simulation suggests that EPFL2 diffusion may affect auxin maxima intervals (Supplementary Fig. 7). It would be interesting to experimentally test the contribution of EPFL2 diffusion to the patterning in future studies.Fig. 7Schematic description of the intercoupling model.a Intercoupling models describing the regulatory relationships (left) and bifurcation diagrams describing the cellular states (right). We next asked how EPFL2 modulates the interval of auxin maxima formation. Our previous study reported that EPFL2 down-regulates cellular auxin response20. Here, we show that EPFL2 suppresses cellular responsiveness to auxin, rather than regulating the actual auxin amounts. Accumulating evidence shows that it is not the absolute auxin level but the signaling readout of auxin response which regulates organ initiation sites39,40. Considering that the PIN1 polarity around auxin maxima or PIN1 expression level is not disturbed in the epfl1 epfl2 mutant with broader auxin maxima, we speculate that EPFL1/2 do not directly impact the PIN1-auxin module. In support of this idea, we observed that the intervals of auxin maxima are not significantly affected in cuc2-1D mutant, which has deeper serrations due to the miR164-resistant gain-of-function mutation41 (Supplementary Fig. 10). In addition, we previously reported that CUC2 and EPFL2 independently regulate teeth outgrowth20. These results together emphasize that EPFL1/2 most likely act in parallel with PIN1-auxin-CUC2. This EPFL1/2 function is clearly distinct from other reported schemes to modify auxin maxima patterning42.

If EPFL2 does not regulate auxin transport, then how does EPFL2 modulate the spatial pattern of cellular auxin response? We propose a model that cell-intrinsic bistable relationships between EPFL2 and auxin modulates the spatial pattern of auxin response in the multicellular dimension (Fig. 7). Under the influence of high EPFL2 activity, a broad range of cells are entrained toward the low-auxin stable state, leading to the lengthening of the interval (Fig. 7). Without EPFL2 or in the presence of low-dose of EPFL2, cells around auxin maxima are entrained toward the high-auxin stable state to make auxin maxima broader, as observed in the epfl1 epfl2 mutant (Fig. 7). Intercoupling of this bistability with PIN1-dependent pattern formation likely produces diverse periodicity observed in various genetic backgrounds. Further research is awaited to see if various periodicities can indeed be found in the leaves of different plant species.

As expected from the regulatory role on auxin maxima initiation, EPFL2 generally regulates pattern formation during shoot organogenesis beyond leaf morphogenesis. We found that EPFL2 is required for the establishment of robust phyllotaxis (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. 9). The developmental roles of EPFL2 are likely shared between SAMs and leaf primordia, because auxin maxima are formed at shorter intervals in both tissues in the epfl2 mutant. In addition, EPFL2 controls constant spacing of ovule formation in the placenta23, although the effect of the epfl2 mutation on this developmental process seems different from that on serration formation. It would be intriguing to explore the similarities and differences in the regulatory mechanisms among different organs in future studies.

This study illustrates how cells couple a bistable module with transport-mediated instability, or Turing instability in general, to modulate the periodic pattern formation. Turing instability has been proposed to underlie a variety of biological and engineered pattern formation. Mutual repression conferring the system bistability allows cells to acquire distinct gene expression states27,43. The intercoupling effect of Turing instability and bistability is a previously unexplored aspect of patterning mechanisms. Considering the versatility of each mechanism in pattern formation, the intrinsic nature of the interlocked regulatory framework we identified in this study has a profound potential to be applied broadly for understanding mechanisms behind diverse pattern formation events in nature and for designing patterns at will.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Wild-type Arabidopsis plants used in this study are Columbia (Col-0). epfl2-1, er-105 erl1-2, er-105 erl2-1 and erl1-2 erl2-1, EPFL2pro::GUS, 35S::EPFL2 and gEPFL2 were described previously. cuc2-1D41 (CS16485), epfl1-124 (CS104435) were derived from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC) at Ohio State University. The reporters, DR5rev::GFP44 and PINp::PIN1-YFP45, were introgressed into mutants. Plants were grown on half-strength Murashige-Skoog (MS) medium containing 0.6% (w/v) gelzan and 1% (w/v) sucrose under constant light at 23 oC.

Generation of transgenic plants

EPFL1pro::GUS was constructed by inserting the EPFL1 upstream 2,052-bp fragment into the GUS-containing vector pGPTV at the HindIII-SmaI site. The PCR primers used to clone the fragment are 5’-CCCCCAAGCTTACCGAGATTTAAGTCATGGTTATATAC-3’ and 5’-TCCCCCGGGGTTTTGAGTTGGATTCAAGAATTACTAC-3’. EPFL2p::mCherry-IntF2A-EPFL2 was constructed as follows: mCherry-NLS and EPFL2 coding sequence, intervened by IntF2A, was synthesized in vitro (Twist bioscience) and combined with EPFL2 promoter by LR cloning into pGWB601 (Invitrogen). These resulting binary vectors were introduced to plants using Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 by the floral dip method. In addition, the gEPFL2 construct as described previously, was newly introduced into Col and the epfl2 mutant. Multiple independent lines with a single-locus insertion of the transgene with stable phenotypes were used for the following experiments.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Shoot apices of 8-day-old seedlings, including young leaves, were collected in microtubes followed by immediate freezing in liquid nitrogen. 25 seedlings were treated as a single replicate sample of each genotype. After grinding with TissueLyzer II (Qiagen, Germany), total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For mRNA quantification, 400 ng of the total RNA was used for the first-strand cDNA synthesis with PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed in technical duplicates using the THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo) with the following primers: EPFL1_F (5’-TCCAACCTCCTATTTCTCCTCA-3’) and EPFL1_R (5’-CAGTTGTTGCATCTGTTGTGG-3’) for EPFL1 (AT5G10310); EPFL2_F (5’-TCTGTCGAATTCACCAAGAGTG-3’) and EPFL2_ R (5’-TAAAGGAGAGTGAAGCTTTGTTTG-3’) for EPFL2 (AT4G37810); PIN1_F (5’-ACAAAACGACGCAGGCTAAG-3’) and PIN1_R (5’-AGCTGGCATTTCAATGTTCC-3’) for PIN1 (AT1G73590); PP2AA3_F (5’-GACCAAGTGAACCAGGTTATTGG-3’) and PP2AA3_R (5’-TACTCTCCAGTGCCTGTCTTCA-3’) for a reference gene PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 2A SUBUNIT A3 (PP2AA3, AT1G3320); ACT2_F (5’-TCCCTCAGCACATTCCAGCAGAT-3’) and ACT2_R (5’-AACGATTCCTGGACCTGCCTCATC-3’) for a reference gene ACTIN 2 (AT3G18780). The statistical tests, Dunnett T3 test and t-test, were performed on the obtained expression level after log-transformation as preprocessing, which ensures the variance equality and normality required by the statistical tests.

Auxin measurement by LC-MS/MS

Frozen shoot apices of 8-day-old seedlings (c.a. 2.5 mg FW) were homogenized using TissueLyser II. After spinning down samples, 80% acetonitrile/1% acetic acid/H2O (500 µL) was added for metabolite extraction. As internal standards, [phenyl-13C6]IAA and [phenyl-13C6]oxIAA were also added. [Phenyl-13C6]IAA was purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories. [phenyl-13C6]oxIAA was previously synthesized46. For extraction, samples were homogenized again and centrifuged at 15,000 x g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a test tube. This sequence of extraction was repeated twice, and the supernatant from each sample was collected in one test tube, respectively. The extracts were evaporated by SpeedVac (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA). Each concentrated extract was loaded onto a conditioned Oasis WAX column (1 cc; Waters), washed with 1% acetic acid/H2O (1 mL) and 80% acetonitrile/H2O (2 mL), and eluted with 80% acetonitrile/1% acetic acid/H2O (2 mL). The eluate was evaporated by SpeedVac. Each fraction was dissolved in 100% methanol and transferred to a vial. Again, each fraction was evaporated to dryness and redissolved in 1% acetic acid/H2O (20 µL). LC–MS/MS analysis was performed utilizing a 1260 Infinity LC system (Agilent Technologies, CA) coupled with an Agilent 6420 Triple Quad system (Agilent Technologies, CA). For analysis, half of the solution was injected into a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, Agilent Technologies, CA). MassHunter Workstation Software version B.06.00 was used for LC-MS data acquisition and qualitative analysis. Quantification was performed using standard curves established with analytical standards and 100 pg/µl internal standards. The HPLC separation and MS/MS analysis conditions were performed as previously described47 (Supplementary Data 1).

Stereomicroscopy

Serrations were counted on the 5th or 6th leaves at 12-day-old stage unless otherwise noted. We defined the “serration” as the site of local auxin maximum and/or morphologically discernable outgrowth along leaf lateral margins. Leaf blades of approximately 2 to 4 mm in length were imaged under an epi-fluorescence stereomicroscope MZ10F equipped with a CCD camera DFC310FX (Leica, Germany). The GFP and mCherry fluorescent images were obtained with a mercury arc lamp and filter set ET-GFP (No. 10450588, Leica) and ET-DSR (No. 10447412, Leica), respectively. The intense GFP signals along leaf margins were counted as auxin maxima. GUS staining was performed as described previously20.

Laser scanning microscopy

For the observation of leaf primordia, 5th or 6th leaves from 8- to 10-d-old seedlings were analyzed unless otherwise described. The upper thresholds of leaf size were set for each measurement to minimize the effect of cell division following auxin maxima initiation. The leaf primordia were incised from shoot apices under a stereomicroscope, stained with 80 μM FM4-64 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or 40 mg/mL Propidium Iodide (PI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged by laser scanning confocal microscope SP8 FALCON (Leica). White Light Laser with the excitation/emission of 488/494-535 nm for GFP, 488/515-556 nm for YFP, and 488/600-635 nm for FM4-64 were used for the excitation, and the HyD with a time-gating system (time window: 0.3 - 12 ns) were used for the fluorescence detection. Obtained Z-series images were projected on a single image with the maximum intensity projection method on LAS-X software (Leica) and further analyzed in ImageJ software ver. 1.54 f (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) to obtain the GFP intensity profile along the leaf margin. Auxin maximum was defined as a spot with local DR5rev::GFP signal which is more than two times higher than the basal level in the neighboring region. When more than two cells were identified within a single auxin maximum, the cell with the highest signal was chosen as the peak. All cells between two peaks were counted as the number of intervening cells. All images were captured as 8-bit depth images with pixel values ranging from 0 to 255. Raw pixel values of the GFP channel were used for the above-described quantitative analyses. In some images, signal intensity was enhanced for better visuality, where the fluorescent signal in the same Figure panel was equally enhanced. For the measurement of SAM size, 2-d-old seedlings were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution for 2 h at 4 °C. The fixed samples were washed with PBS and treated overnight in ClearSee48 solution containing 1% (v/v) Calcofluor White Stain (Sigma-Aldrich, MO) to clear the tissue and stain the cell wall. The samples were imaged by using SP8 FALCON with the excitation/emission of 405/410–485 nm. The acquired images were analyzed in ImageJ to measure the SAM size. For the imaging of auxin maxima on SAM, shoot apices from inflorescence stems shortly after bolting (< 10 cm inflorescence stems) were stained by PI (1 mg/mL) and imaged under LSM900 (Zeiss, Germany).

Application of auxin or auxin inhibitors

For auxin application before imaging analyses, the aerial part of the 11-day-old seedlings was immersed in 10 μM 2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic Acid (2,4-D, No. 040-18532, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co.), 10 μM IAA (No. 094-07121, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co.) or control solution for 24 h. For qRT-PCR analyses, 2,4-D was applied for 6 h, as previously performed to analyze endogenous EPFL2 expression. The plants were kept shaking slowly (50 rpm) under room light overnight. For auxin inhibitor treatment, seeds were sown on the MS media containing 5 μM auxinole (α-[2,4-dimethylphenylethyl-2-oxo]-IAA, No. 30-001, Bioacademia Inc.) and imaged on d9. For imaging analyses, the 5th and 6th leaves were dissected from the treated seedlings and imaged under the stereomicroscope. The obtained images were analyzed by ImageJ, where the average fluorescent level of the leaf blade in the green channel was quantified.

The divergence angles

Data from molecular biological experiments was visualized by R version 4.3.2. The auxin-maxima increasing speed was compared between the WT and epfl2 mutant as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a by fitting saturation growth curves2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y \sim \frac{{ax}}{b+x}$$\end{document}y~axb+xwhere x is leaf length as the explanatory variable, y is auxin maxima numbers as the response variable, a and b are the coefficients to fit. To estimate the appearance timing of the second lateral maximum, above LSM images from leaf primordia of various sizes were used.
