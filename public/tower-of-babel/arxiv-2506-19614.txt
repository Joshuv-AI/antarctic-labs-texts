Directional search for light dark matter with quantum sensors
Hajime Fukuda, Yuichiro Matsuzaki, Thanaporn Sichanugrist

Abstract
The presence of dark matter (DM) stands as one of the most compelling indications of new physics in particle physics. Typically, the detection of wave-like DM involves quantum sensors, such as qubits or cavities. The phase of the sensors is usually discarded as the value of the phase itself is not physically meaningful. However, the difference of the phase between the sensors contains the information of the velocity and direction of the DM wind. We propose a measurement protocol to extract this information from the sensors using quantum states. Our method does not require specific experimental setups and can be applied to any type of DM detector as long as the data from the detectors can be taken quantum mechanically. We also show that our method does not spoil the sensitivity of the DM detectors and is superior to the classical method based on the correlations of the DM signals between the detectors.



I
Introduction

Dark matter (DM) constitutes a significant fraction of the universe
Aghanim et al. 2020
, yet its fundamental properties remain elusive. Among the various candidates proposed, ultra-light particles such as axions, axion-like particles, and dark photons are particularly well motivated
Arias et al. 2012
. As their masses are sufficiently small, these particles exhibit wave-like rather than particle-like behavior.
Although their couplings to Standard Model (SM) particles are expected to be extremely weak, a wide range of experiments and observations are actively searching for signatures of such interactions.

DM comes to the Earth from all directions, but its flux is not isotropic. Due to the motion of the solar system through the galactic halo, an enhanced flux–often referred to as the “DM wind”–is expected in the direction of the solar system’s motion. This anisotropy is a key and distinctive feature of DM
Spergel 1988
. For particle-like DM, numerous experimental efforts have focused on detecting this wind by measuring the recoil of target particles
Spergel 1988
;
Lewin and Smith 1996
;
Morgan 2003
;
Morgan et al. 2005
;
Morgan and Green 2005
;
Green and Morgan 2007
; see also Refs.
Silk et al. 2010
;
Miuchi 2024
for comprehensive reviews. By observing such recoils, one can in principle determine the velocity distribution of the DM.

However, for wave-like DM, the situation is different, as the recoil of target particles is extremely small and generally undetectable. If the DM couples to SM particles via velocity-dependent interactions–such as the axion coupling to fermion spin (e.g. Ref.
Garcon et al. 2017
;
Chigusa et al. 2025
;
Chigusa et al. 2023
)–the DM wind can, in principle, be probed by changing the orientation of the apparatus. However, this approach is highly model-dependent, and the sensitivity is limited by the small DM velocity and unknown coupling strength, making independent measurement of the DM wind velocity challenging.
Alternatively, one could construct an experimental apparatus with a size comparable to the de Broglie wavelength of the DM
Irastorza and Garcia 2012
;
Knirck et al. 2018
. For example, in cavity detectors, the axion-to-photon conversion probability can vary depending on the direction of the axion wind. However, this requires specialized and often large setups, especially for lighter DM.

In this paper, we propose that if the quantum states from DM detectors can be transferred over a distance and processed quantum mechanically, it is possible to independently and simultaneously measure both the velocity of the DM wind and the coupling between DM and SM particles. Our method is broadly applicable to any DM detector capable of quantum state readout, without requiring specialized experimental setups. Furthermore, we show that, in the absence of noise, this approach does not sacrifice the sensitivity of the detector; we extract additional information from the DM detectors.

The key idea of our proposal is to use the quantum interference of the quantum sensors at distant positions. The information of the DM phase is generally encoded in the phase of the detector state. The value of the phase itself is of little physical meaning, but the difference of the phase is sensitive to the momentum of the DM wind.
The
classical
correlations of the DM signals between the detectors at distant positions have been discussed in Ref.
Derevianko 2018
, but we rather focus on the
quantum
correlations of the DM signals; we measure the interference due to the DM wind by a non-local operator. Our method outperforms the classical correlations in the weak-signal regime.

Our proposal requires transferring quantum states between distant locations.
As we will discuss, we require the distance between the detectors to be of the order of the de Broglie wavelength of the DM
and we need to transfer the quantum states even over kilometers depending on the DM mass.
However, such operations are fundamental in quantum information science
Bennett et al. 1993
and used in various applications, such as the quantum key distribution
Bennett and Brassard 2014
.
Indeed, quantum teleportation, which transfers quantum information between remote parties, has already been experimentally demonstrated by using optical photons
Boschi et al. 1998
and solid-state qubits such as trapped atoms
Nölleke et al. 2013
, rare-earth-doped crystals
Lago-Rivera et al. 2023
, superconducting qubits
Qiu et al. 2025
and the nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center in diamond
Pfaff et al. 2014
;
Hermans et al. 2022
. This capability is useful not only for quantum communication
Rozpedek et al. 2019
but also for quantum sensing
Degen et al. 2017
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, as an example, we consider a type of DM detector where the DM interacts directly with qubits, such as transmon qubits
Chen et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Chen et al. 2024b
, and review the evolution of the detector’s quantum state due to the interaction.
In Sec.
III
, we show our measurement protocol to extract the information of the DM wind from the qubits.
The comparison with the classical correlations is made in Sec.
IV
.
Sec.
V
is devoted to the conclusion and discussion.

II
Quantum mechanical signal from dark matter

In this section, we review how DM interacts with quantum detectors. For concreteness, we focus on superconducting qubits that couple directly to the DM field
Chen et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024b
, but the discussion applies equally to other two-level systems such as NV centers
Chigusa et al. 2025
and trapped ions
Ito et al. 2024
. Our approach is also applicable to other quantum sensors, including cavity detectors
Sikivie 1983a
;
Braine et al. 2020
, as long as the output is accessible quantum mechanically. We briefly comment on this at the end of the section.

Consider a qubit with ground and excited states
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
. The Hamiltonian is given as follows:

\displaystyle H_{0}=-\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_{z},

(1)

where
\omega
is the energy splitting and
\sigma_{i}
is the
i
-th Pauli matrix. The interaction with the DM field is modeled as

\displaystyle H_{1}=2\eta\sigma_{x}\Phi(t),

(2)

with the coupling of
\eta
and the DM field of
\Phi(t)
. For a monochromatic DM field,

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=\Phi_{0}\cos(Et + \varphi),

(3)

where
E=m(1+v^{2}/2)
is the DM energy,
m
is the DM mass,
v
is
its velocity,
\Phi_{0}
is
the amplitude, and
\varphi
is
the phase.

Assuming the detector is tuned to resonance (
\omega=E
), and applying the rotating wave approximation (RWA), the interaction Hamiltonian in the interaction picture becomes

\displaystyle H_{I}=\epsilon(\sigma_{x}\cos\varphi-\sigma_{y}\sin\varphi),

(4)

where
\epsilon\equiv\eta\Phi_{0}
. Starting from
|0\rangle
, after time
\tau
the state evolves to

\displaystyle|\psi(\tau)\rangle\simeq|0\rangle-i\epsilon\tau e^{-i\varphi}|1\rangle,

(5)

for
|\epsilon\tau|\ll 1
. The probability to find the qubit in
|1\rangle
is

\displaystyle p=|\langle 1|\psi(\tau)\rangle|^{2}\simeq(\epsilon\tau)^{2},

(6)

which allows measurement of the DM interaction strength
\epsilon
.

Up to this point, we have assumed that the DM field is oscillating with a single frequency. However, in reality, this may not be the case. In this paper, we assume the model adopted in Ref.
Foster et al. 2018
, where the DM field is an equal sum of many oscillating fields with random phases:

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm DM}}\cos\quantity[m\qty(1 + \frac{v_i^2}{2})t + \varphi_i],

(7)

where
N_{\rm DM}
is the number of DM particles that make up the DM field,
v_{i}
is the velocity of the
i
-th DM particle, and
\varphi_{i}
is the phase of the
i
-th DM particle.
Let the time we measure the DM field be
\tau
.
For such
\Delta v
that
mv_{i}\Delta v\tau\ll 1
, the oscillation frequencies of the DM field with velocity
v
satisfying
|v-v_{i}|\lesssim\Delta v
are almost the same, and we can add them up:

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi_{0}}{\sqrt{N_{\rm DM}}}\sum_{|v-v_{i}|\lesssim\Delta v}\cos\quantity[m\qty(1 + \frac{v_i^2}{2})t + \varphi_i]=\Phi_{0}\sum_{\text{direction}}\alpha_{i}\sqrt{f(\vec{v}_{i})\Delta v^{3}}\cos\quantity[m\qty(1 + \frac{v_i^2}{2})t + \Tilde{\varphi}_i],

(8)

where
\alpha_{i}
is a random variable obeying the Rayleigh distribution,
P(\alpha_{i})=\alpha_{i}e^{-\alpha_{i}^{2}/2}
,
f(\vec{v}_{i})
is the distribution function of the DM velocity, and
\tilde{\varphi}_{i}
is a random variable uniformly distributed in the range
[0,2\pi)
.
The sum in the right-hand side is taken over the direction of the DM velocity.
Tuning the frequency of the detector to be equal to
m\quantity(1 + \frac{\bar{v}^2}{2})
, where
\bar{v}
is the average speed of the DM, we can again use the RWA and the interaction Hamiltonian is now given by

\displaystyle H_{I}=\epsilon\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\frac{f(\vec{v}_{i})\Delta v^{3}}{2}}\quantity[\sigma_x \cos\qty(\frac{m \Delta v_i^2}{2}t + \Tilde{\varphi}_i) - \sigma_y \sin\qty(\frac{m \Delta v_i^2}{2}t + \Tilde{\varphi}_i)],

(9)

where
\Delta v_{i}^{2}\equiv v_{i}^{2}-\bar{v}^{2}
.
Then, the state of the detector after the time
\tau
is

\displaystyle|\psi(\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq|0\rangle-i\epsilon\tau\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\sqrt{\frac{f(\vec{v}_{i})\Delta v^{3}}{2}}e^{-i\quantity(\delta_i + \Tilde{\varphi}_i)}F\left(\delta_{i}\right)|1\rangle,

(10)

where
F(x)=\frac{\sin x}{x}
and
\delta_{i}=\frac{m\Delta v_{i}^{2}}{4}\tau
.
The probability of the detector to be in the state
|1\rangle
is given by

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\left|\langle\psi(\tau)|1\rangle\right|^{2}\simeq\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\frac{f(\vec{v}_{i})\Delta v}{2}F^{2}\left(\delta_{j}\right).

(11)

We have used the fact that the random phases
\tilde{\varphi}_{i}
are uncorrelated, and the cross terms with
i\neq j
average to zero. Taking the average over the random amplitude
\alpha_{i}
, we obtain
\langle p\rangle\simeq\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}
, where we assume that
m\bar{v}^{2}\tau\lesssim 1
and
F(x)\simeq 1
, and we have used the fact that the distribution function
f(\vec{v})
is normalized,
\int d^{3}vf(\vec{v})=1
. The average probability gives the same result as the case with a single frequency, Eq. (
6
).

We can take the average over the random phase
\varphi_{i}

in the density matrix formalism.
There, we may treat the detector state as an ensemble of states, each having evolved under the interaction Hamiltonian with DM field, Eq. (
7
), with random phases; the final state of the detector is, instead of
|\psi(\tau)\rangle
, given by

\displaystyle\rho(\tau)\simeq\int\quantity(\prod_i \frac{d\varphi_i}{2\pi})U(\tau)\ket{0}\bra{0}U^{\dagger}(\tau),

(12)

where
U(\tau)\equiv\text{T}\exp\left[-i\int_{0}^{\tau}dtH_{I}(t)\right]
depends on the random phases
\varphi_{i}
.
Again, we can take the partial sum over the DM velocities
v_{i}
, rewriting the integration over
\varphi_{i}
in terms of
\alpha_{i}
and
\tilde{\varphi}_{i}
. We may then perform the integration and obtain

\rho(\tau)\simeq|0\rangle\langle 0|+\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}\int d^{3}vf(\vec{v})F^{2}\left(\frac{m\quantity(v^2 - \bar{v}^2)}{4}\tau\right)|1\rangle\langle 1|.

(13)

As can be seen, averaging over the random phases removes all phase information when considering only the state of a single qubit. Consequently, no useful information from the phase of the DM can be extracted in this case. However,
the difference
of the phases can be observable, as is shown in the next section.

Up to this point, we have assumed two-level qubits as the DM detectors.
However, the information of the DM phase is also encoded in other types of DM detectors, such as resonant cavities
Sikivie 1983b
. Therefore, if we can take the data from the detectors quantum mechanically, we can apply our proposal to these types of detectors as well. As an example, we discuss the case of the cavity detectors in App.
A
.

IV
Comparison with classical correlations

In the previous section, we demonstrated that the DM wind can be measured via quantum interference between qubits with non-local operations. An alternative approach is to use classical correlations with local measurements
Derevianko 2018
. Here, we compare the two methods and show that our quantum protocol with non-local operations
offers superior sensitivity in the weak-signal regime.

The local method using classical correlations
considers detectors measuring continuous DM field values. For weak signals, the detector can be modeled as a single two-level system, with field operators
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}
. For a monochromatic DM field,
\langle\sigma_{x}\rangle
and
\langle\sigma_{y}\rangle
encode the DM amplitude and phase. The DM wind information can be extracted from the two-point correlation:

\displaystyle I\equiv\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma^{(1)}_{y}\sigma^{(2)}_{x}\rho(\tau)]\simeq\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}\int d^{3}v\,f(\vec{v})\sin(m \vec{v}\cdot\vv{\Delta r}),

where the superscripts at Pauli matrices denote the index of the detectors.
The two-point correlation is of the same order as the quantum expectation value
\overline{M}
in Eq. (
III
), taking into account that we require to pass the postselection to measure
\overline{M}
.

The key difference lies in the measurement uncertainty. For
N_{I}
repetitions, the uncertainty in
I
is

\delta I=\frac{\sqrt{\text{Tr}\quantity[\qty(\sigma^{(1)}_y \sigma^{(2)}_x)^2 \rho(\tau)]-I^{2}}}{\sqrt{N_{I}}}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{I}}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4}\tau^{4}).

(78)

The corresponding DM wind resolution is

\delta v^{(I)}_{\text{obs},i}\sim\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}\sqrt{N_{I}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left|\frac{d\overline{M}}{dv_{\text{obs},i}}\right|^{-1}\right).

(79)

To match the sensitivity of the quantum protocol with non-local operations, the local method with classical correlation requires
N_{I}\sim(\epsilon\tau)^{-4}N
measurements, where
N
is the number of postselected quantum measurements. Since the postselection probability is
p_{1}\simeq 2\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2}
, the total number of quantum measurements is
N^{(\rm total)}=N/p_{1}
. Thus, the ratio of required measurements is

\frac{N_{I}}{N^{(\rm total)}}\sim\frac{1}{(\epsilon\tau)^{2}}.

(80)

Therefore, in the weak-field limit (
|\epsilon\tau|\ll 1
), our quantum protocol requires far fewer measurements than the classical correlation method for the same sensitivity.

This scaling persists even without postselection. Measuring
M
directly, we have the expectation value
\langle M\rangle=p_{1}\overline{M}
and uncertainty
\delta M\simeq\sqrt{p_{1}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\delta v^{(M)}_{\text{obs},i}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\epsilon\tau)^{2}N_{M}}}\left|\frac{d\overline{M}}{dv_{\text{obs},i}}\right|^{-1},

(81)

where
N_{M}
is the number of measurements. Again, the method to use classical correlation requires
1/(\epsilon^{2}\tau^{2})
times more measurements. The essential advantage of our approach stems from using the non-local operator
M
;
M
directly accesses the phase difference induced by the DM wind, which is non-local as well. Indeed, as is shown in App.
C
, the scaling of the uncertainty, Eq. (
81
), in terms of
N_{M}
and
\epsilon\tau
, is optimal, as it saturates the quantum Cramér-Rao bound
Paris 2009
.

V
Conclusion and discussion

In this work, we have introduced a quantum protocol to measure the DM wind using interference between spatially separated quantum sensors. Our method enables simultaneous extraction of both the DM interaction strength and the DM wind velocity with each detection event. By optimizing the sensor separation and orientation, the protocol can probe both the solar system’s motion relative to the DM halo and the Earth’s orbital velocity. We have shown that the approach remains robust even in the presence of significant noise, and while we focused on the standard halo model, the method is adaptable to more complex DM distributions.
The scanning with various separations and orientations of qubit detectors could also help gain information about the DM distribution.

Although we assumed identical qubits for simplicity, the protocol is general: if each sensor has a different interaction strength with the DM field, the measurement outcome is simply rescaled, and the method remains valid. Even with systematic phase shifts or different detector types, as long as the phase offset is stable, the DM wind information can still be extracted.

Extending the protocol to more than two sensors with distant qubits, which is considered a quantum sensing network
Komar et al. 2014
;
Eldredge et al. 2018
;
Proctor et al. 2018
;
Ge et al. 2018
;
Kasai et al. 2022
;
Kasai et al. 2024
, offers further possibilities. For example, arranging sensors in an array with uniform phase differences allows for efficient phase estimation via quantum Fourier transform (QFT) techniques
Coppersmith 2002
. Recent work suggests that entanglement and quantum computing can enhance sensitivity to unknown-frequency signals such as DM
Allen et al. 2025
. The use of techniques such as the QFT could open up another way to use quantum computers for extracting additional information.
More sophisticated protocols, including those using entangled states like GHZ states
Greenberger et al. 1989
;
Giovannetti et al. 2011
;
Matsuzaki et al. 2011
;
Chin et al. 2012
, may further improve performance, though they may well require more sophisticated measurement strategies, such as Ref.
Sekatski et al. 2020
, since the response of the GHZ state to the DM is different
Chen et al. 2024a
. We leave the exploration of these directions to future work.

Appendix A
Cavity detector response to the DM field

In the main text, we discuss the details of the response of the qubit detector to the DM field. In this appendix, as an example, we show that a similar response is obtained from the cavity detector as well. For simplicity, here, we consider monochromatic DM, while the extension to the superposition of plane waves can be performed similarly as in the main text.

The Hamiltonian of an electromagnetic field inside a cavity in the second quantization picture is

H_{0}=\omega_{\rm c}a^{\dagger}a

(82)

with the interaction between the DM field and the cavity field given by

H_{1}=2\epsilon(a+a^{\dagger})\cos(E t+\varphi),

(83)

where
E=m\left(1+\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\right)
with
m,v
are the mass and velocity of DM, respectively, and
\varphi
is the phase of the DM field.
The operators
a,a^{\dagger}
are the annihilation and creation operators of cavity photons satisfying

[a,a^{\dagger}]=1,\quad a\ket{0}_{c}=0,

(84)

where
\ket{0}_{c}
is the vacuum state of the cavity.
We denote the interaction strength between this cavity mode and DM as
\epsilon
, which is proportional to the amplitude of the DM field. We note that the form of the interaction given by Eq. (
83
) is obtained for the cavity haloscope experiments (see, e.g., Ref.
Chen et al. 2024b
). When the interaction strength
\epsilon
is small and we focus on the Hilbert space of the two lowest-energy states
\{\ket{0}_{c},a^{\dagger}\ket{0}_{c}\}
, one can see that the system is equivalent to that of the qubit detector system interacting with DM. Therefore, the same response should be expected.

Let us consider the situation where
\omega_{c}=E
, the interaction Hamiltonian is given by

H_{I}=\epsilon(ae^{i\varphi}+a^{\dagger}e^{-i\varphi}),

(85)

where we have used the RWA.
Then, assuming that the initial state of the cavity is
\ket{0}_{c}
, we obtain the detector state at time
\tau
as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(\tau)}

\displaystyle=\text{T}\exp[-i\int_{0}^{\tau}dtH_{I}]\ket{0}_{c}

(86)

\displaystyle\simeq\ket{0}_{c}-i\epsilon\tau e^{-i\varphi}a^{\dagger}\ket{0}_{c}

(87)

where we assume a small interaction strength, i.e.,
|\epsilon\tau|\ll 1
. The response is the same as that obtained with the qubit detector, Eq. (
5
), as expected.

We also briefly comment on transferring the information of the cavity state to a qubit degree of freedom for the subsequent state transfer over distance or subsequent measurements. Let us consider the Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian
Jaynes and Cummings 1963
as the interaction between the cavity and the qubit:

\Delta H=\lambda(\ket{0}\bra{1}e^{-i\omega_{\rm q}t}+\ket{1}\bra{0}e^{i\omega_{\rm q}t})(ae^{-i\omega_{\rm c}t}+a^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{\rm c}t}),

(88)

where
\lambda
is a constant and
\omega_{\rm q}
is the (effective) qubit frequency. The frequency
\omega_{\rm q}
can be tuned, e.g. for superconducting qubits, by applying an oscillating field interacting with the qubit and inducing an AC Stark shift
Autler and Townes 1955
;
Schuster et al. 2005
. For those equipped with a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID), the frequency can be tuned by adjusting the external magnetic flux through the SQUID loop
Gao et al. 2021
. When
\omega_{\rm q}
is set to
\omega_{\rm c}
, using RWA, we obtain the interaction as

\Delta H\simeq\lambda\left(\ket{0}\bra{1 }a^{\dagger}+\ket{1}\bra{0 }a\right)

(89)

which leads to the transition;

a^{\dagger}\ket{0}_{c}\otimes\ket{0}\rightarrow\ket{0}_{c}\otimes\ket{1}.

(90)

One might apply it to transfer the cavity state, Eq. (
87
), to the qubit state as

\quantity(\ket{0}_c-i\epsilon\tau e^{-i\varphi} a^\dagger\ket{0}_c)\otimes\ket{0}\rightarrow\ket{0}_{c}\otimes\quantity(\ket{0}-i\epsilon\tau e^{-i\varphi} \ket{1}).

(91)
