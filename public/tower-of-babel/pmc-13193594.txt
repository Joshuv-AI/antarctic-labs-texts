Effects of dietary soy sauce residue on growth performance, organ development, plasma biochemical parameters, and meat quality of Pekin ducks
Authors: Zhanyue Wu, Yanhong Guo, Qimeng Wang, Junting Cao, Sen Wu, Yongbao Wu, Zhiguo Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoy sauce residue (SSR), a by-product of soy sauce production industry that is rich in protein and amino acids, was investigated for its effects on the growth and organ development, plasma biochemical parameters, meat quality, and breast muscle metabolites of Pekin ducks. A total of 280 healthy male 1-day-old ducks were randomly divided into 5 groups (7 replicates, 8 birds/replicate). The ducks were fed a corn-soybean meal supplemented with varying levels of SSR (0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8%). The study found that the addition of 4% SSR had no significant effect on growth performance, organ development, and meat quality of Pekin ducks (P > 0.05). In terms of plasma biochemistry parameters, the contents of ALT, AST, TP, ALB, DBIL, CK, CHO, and HDLC in the 6% and 8% SSR groups were significantly decreased (P < 0.05). CK showed a liner decrease with the increasing SSR level (P < 0.05). Additionally, the contents of proline, histidine, aspartic and acetic acid in the breast muscle of the 8% SSR group were significantly decreased (P < 0.05). Analysis of breast muscle metabolites revealed that there was a total of 71 differential metabolites between the control and 8% SSR groups, which were associated with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions. In summary, SSR may serve as a novel feed ingredient in meat duck production, and an inclusion level of 4% appears to be safer in this experiment.Keywords: Soy sauce residue, Pekin duck, growth performance, meat quality, breast muscle metabolites

Introduction

Duck farming is a crucial component of poultry husbandry due to its substantial economic value and a history spanning thousands of years (Chen et al. 2021). Asia stands as a dominant producer of ducks, accounting more than 70% of global output in recent years (Zhang et al. 2023). Duck meat is renowned for its nutritional richness, including polyunsaturated fatty acids, amino acids, and proteins, which makes it a preferred choice among consumers (Chartrin et al. 2005; Wołoszyn et al. 2006; Ali et al. 2007). However, the rapid expansion of the livestock and poultry industry, including meat duck production, has continuously expanded the demand for animal feed, resulting in increasing pressure on feed supply (Steinfeld et al. 2006; Davis and D’Odorico 2015). It is estimated that approximately 50% of the world’s agricultural land and 40% of arable land are dedicated to feed production or grazing, impacting overall food production negatively (Davis and D’Odorico 2015; Mottet et al. 2017).

Soy sauce is a traditional condiment brewed from soybeans and wheat, and annual production in China are about 6 million tons, constituting over three-fifths of the global output in the past decades (Zhao et al. 2013). During the soy sauce production process, a substantial amount of soy sauce residue (SSR) is generated following filtration and extraction procedures. Annually, around 450,000 tons of SSR are produced, with production increasing at a rate of 10% per year (Xiang et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2021). The concentration of crude protein in SSR is approximately 26.65%, while apparent metabolizable energy for Pekin duck is 8.59 MJ/kg and the true metabolizable energy of Pekin duck is 10.83 MJ/kg (Wu et al. 2024). It represents a significant biological resource and can be effectively utilized as animal feed (Chen et al. 2014; Cheng et al. 2022). However, there is limited research providing guidance on the optimal inclusion level of SSR in meat duck diets. Previous studies in pigs have shown that dietary supplementation with 4% or 8% SSR can positively affect growth performance (Sun et al. 2009). Moreover, because SSR contains relatively high levels of salt (Zhou et al. 2025), excessive inclusion may negatively impact the health of ducks (Thuy and Thiet 2025). Therefore, in this study, to avoid potential adverse effects on duck health caused by excessive dietary salt resulting from high levels of SSR, the SSR was included in the diet at 0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8%, with 8% representing the maximum expected inclusion based on previous studies. Since soy sauce is a grain-fermented condiment beneficial to human health (Sassi et al. 2021), we hypothesized that SSR would not have negative effects on the growth performance, organ development and meat quality of ducks, but the plasma biochemical parameters associated with nutrition and metabolism are expected to vary with the inclusion level of SSR in the diet. We also expected that the inclusion of SSR could potentially positively affect the nutritional components and metabolites of meat due to the SSR serves as a condiment rich in flavor amino acids such as Glu (2.65%) and Asp (1.90%) (Wu et al. 2024). Therefore, the evaluation of breast muscle meat quality, amino acid profile, fatty acid composition, and metabolite profiling was incorporated into the study.

Materials and methods

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences and conducted in accordance with the guidelines for animal experiments set by the National Institute of Animal Health (Approval number: CAAS-FRI-20230322).

A total of 280 1-day-old male Pekin ducks with similar body weights ([60.29 ± 0.17] g) were randomly assigned to 5 groups (seven pens per group, eight ducks per pen). The dietary levels of SSR were set at 0% (control group), 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8% group, respectively. The experimental persists to day 42. Temperature in the test room was initially set at 33 °C from 1 to 3 days of age, decreasing by 1–2 °C every 3 days, and stabilizing at 25 °C, with a relative humidity around 50–70%. Birds were exposed to continuous light (24 L:0D) throughout the experimental period. The ducks were raised in the metal battery pens. Each pen (1500 mm × 1000 mm × 600 mm; length × width × height) was equipped with a feeder and a water line with 7 drinking nipples, which can be adjusted in height, to allow ad libitum access to feed and water.

A corn-soybean basic diet was utilized, with varying concentration of SSR incorporated into different groups. The experimental diets were formulated according to China Feeding Standard for Meat Ducks (NY/T 2122-2012) of the Ministry of Agriculture of the People’s Republic of China. Measurements

Growth performance

During the experiment, the body weight and feed were weighed once per week on pen level. The average body weight (ABW), average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated.

Relative organ weight

At the 42 days of age, two ducks per pen were slaughtered by CO2 inhalation and bled. During the slaughtering process, the breast muscle, leg muscle, liver, spleen, pancreas, heart, gizzard, duodenum, ileum, jejunum, cecum, and abdominal fat were weighed to assess relative organ weight, calculated as: relative organ weight (%) = (organ weight/body weight) × 100.

Plasma biochemical parameters

At 42 days of age, two blood samples per pen (5 mL each) were collected from the jugular vein into a sodium heparin tube before slaughter. Plasma was obtained by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 15 min. The content of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), glucose (GLU), urea (UA), total cholesterol (CHO), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC) were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer (XL-200, Erba, Germany). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Meat quality measurement

Two ducks from each pen were sampled. After slaughter, samples of right breast muscle were collected and immediately placed in liquid nitrogen to measure amino acid, short-chain fatty acid, and metabolites, and whole left breast muscle were collected and stored at 4 °C for meat quality measurement. Regarding for meat quality, measurements were taken for pH, meat color (L*, a*, b*), dripping loss, cooking loss, intramuscular fat content, amino acid content, and short-chain fatty acids, and metabolites (Petracci and Baéza 2011).

The pH of each sample was measured at three different positions and averaged using Testo 206 PH2 [Testo Instruments (Shenzhen) Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China]. Muscle color parameters (L*, a*, b*) were measured using a colorimeter (3nh, NH300+, Guangdong ThreeNH Technology Co., Ltd.) with three readings taken per sample at both ends and the middle along the longitudinal direction of the breast muscle. Averaged for accuracy.

Dripping loss was determined by suspended 10 g meat slice (W1) from the center of each breast muscle inside a sealed bag, ensuring it did not reach the bag walls at 4 °C for 24 h. After 24 h, gently wipe off the moisture and weighed (W2), dripping loss (%) was calculated as [(W1 − W2)/W1] × 100.

For cooking loss, approximately 20 g of breast muscle (W3) was sealed in a bag, heated in a water bath at 80 °C for 30 min, after cooling, gently wipe off the surface moisture and weighed (W4). Cooking loss (%) was then calculated as [(W3 − W4)/W3] × 100.

Intermuscular fat content was determined from freeze-dried breast muscle using a Soxhlet Extractor (SOX 606, China) with petroleum ether at 90 °C for 8 h.

As no significant differences were detected among the intermediate levels of SSR inclusion, only the control and highest inclusion groups were chosen for further amino acid, short-chain fatty acid, and metabolites analyses which can provide the clearest assessment of treatment effects. Amino acids were analyzed following the standard procedures (AOAC 944.12). Short-chain fatty acids in the breast muscle were analyzed using GC-MS with Agilent DB-FFAP capillary column under helium carrier gas (flow rate: 1.0 ml/min) and electron ionization (EI) source at 70 eV electron energy in SCAN/SIM mode for substance detection. Muscle metabolites were assessed via LC-MS with chromatographic conditions set as follows: mobile phase A: water/acetonitrile (95/5), mobile phase B: acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (47.5/47.5/5). Mass spectrometry conditions included a mass scanning range of m/z 50–1000, positive and negative mode ion spray voltages of 5000 V and -4000 V, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3) and R Studio (version 2024.04.2). Growth performance, organ development, plasma biochemical indicators, and meat quality data were analyzed using One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), and if significant differences were found (P < 0.05), Duncan’s multiple range test was applied for multiple comparisons. Linear and quadratic regression analyses were used to evaluate the SSR dose–response trends. For the comparison of meat duck breast muscle amino acid and short-chain fatty acid content between the 0% and 8% groups, independent samples T-tests were conducted. Results are all presented as Mean ± SEM, with effects considered significant at P < 0.05. The raw metabolomics data were analyzed using Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) with the ropls package in R. Differential metabolites were determined based on Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) values (VIP >1) and P-values from Student’s t-test (P < 0.05).

Metabolic analysis

The PLS-DA model verified that the SSR effect between the control and 8% group has significant differences (Fig. 2A). The identified metabolites were primarily categorized as lipids and amino acids (Fig. 2B). Under both positive and negative ion mode, a total of 71 different metabolites were identified between the two groups, of which 49 metabolites were significantly up-regulated, and 22 metabolites were significantly down-regulated (Fig. 3A). These different metabolites were mainly enriched in signaling pathways such as ErbB and MAPK (Fig. 3B). Notably, metabolites such as Arabinofuranose, 7-Amino-4-methylcoumarin, and Indeloxazine displayed significant differences between the groups (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

Production performance

Adequate and high-quality feed resources are essential for the healthy development of the livestock industry. However, there is currently an imbalance in their availability and quality, with even instances of competition for food between humans and animals. Consequently, feed resource shortages have become a pressing issue that must be addressed. Improving feed utilization efficiency, developing new feed additives, and exploring unconventional feed resources are all key strategies for alleviating feed shortages. Among these strategies, the development of unconventional feed resources, such as by-products from agriculture, food industry, and other industrial processes, not only helps reduce resource waste and feed expenditure (Thirumalaisamy et al. 2016), but also offers a promising alternative for animal feed (Swain et al. 2014). Many of these by-products retain considerable nutritional value after processing, making them viable potential feed resources (Costa et al. 2022). SSR can be used as animal feed, and previous studies in China provide supporting evidence. For instance, incorporating dried SSR into pig diets has been shown to improve growth performance and economic efficiency, with the addition of 4% SSR reducing feed costs by approximately 5% (Sun et al. 2009). In our previous study, a nutritional evaluation was conducted before to the application of SSR, and the results demonstrated that SSR is rich in protein and amino acids (Wu et al. 2024). In the present study, limited effects of SSR on growth performance or overall organ development were observed in meat ducks. Dietary supplementation with 8% SSR markedly promoted the development of the jejunum, indicating that the SSR may enhance the intestinal development or the jejunum may have undergone adaptive changes to promote nutrient absorption (Hornick et al. 2000).

Plasma biochemical parameter

Most of the plasma parameters assessed in this study were associated with liver function and nutrient metabolism. The concentrations of ALT, AST, TP, ALB, DBIL, ALP, CK, CHO, HDLC, and LDLC in plasma were affected by SSR supplementation. Elevated content of plasma AST, ALP, and ALT can indicate tissue damage and may suggest an elevated risk of muscle tissue or liver disease (Ndrepepa, 2021; Chia et al. 2022). The DBIL content in plasma reflect the synthesis and excretion functions of the liver, and an increase in its concentration indicates liver damage (Kong et al. 2021; Liao et al. 2022). CK plays an essential role in the energy transport processes, and its concentration are notably high in tissues with significant energy demands such as the heart and neurons (Wallimann et al. 1998, 2011). The liver is a key site for lipid biosynthesis in poultry, and LDLC plays a crucial role in transporting CHO from the liver to other tissues (Ma et al. 2015). Conversely, HDLC helps to remove excess CHO to prevent its deposition (Gupta and Rajagopal 2007). These changes indicate that the liver was not adversely affected by SSR supplementation. The decrease in ALB concentration indicates either liver tissue damage (Yu et al. 2024) or an effect of nutritional status (Pain et al. 1978). TP is an indicator of growth and nutritional status and serves as a potential biomarker in the selection process for fat traits in ducks (Zhu et al. 2020; Song et al. 2023). The reduction in plasma TP concentration in ducks may be attributed to the lower digestibility of protein in SSR compared to the standard corn and soybean meal-based diets. For example, the protein digestibility of the SSR used in this study was approximately 45% (Wu et al. 2024), whereas the standardized ileal digestibility of protein from corn and soybean meal for meat ducks typically exceeds 85% (Kong et al. 2021). In summary, at 6% and 8% inclusion levels, some plasma parameters related to energy, lipid, and protein metabolism were significantly decreased following SSR supplementation. The reduction in nutrient synthesis indicators may be due to insufficient substrates caused by the low digestibility of nutrients in SSR in meat ducks. On the other hand, the synthetic processes are more easily constrained by feed level than the degradation of nutrients (Hornick et al. 2000). But the growth performance and organ development were not significantly affected with the adding of SSR means the effects are limited.

Meat quality

The color of duck meat is a critical indicator of freshness and quality, often being the primary consideration for consumers when selecting meat (Fletcher 2002). According to the study on meat color, there were no significant differences in the color of breast muscle after supplement SSR. Since growth performance, relative breast muscle weight, and meat quality were similar across all SSR inclusion levels. The amino acid composition, fatty acid profile, and metabolite content play a crucial role in determining its flavor profile, overall quality, and nutritional value (Wood et al. 2004). Feed protein is absorbed by animals and broken down into amino acids, which are subsequently absorbed by the intestine. In this study, SSR supplementation showed no significant effect on the amino acid and short-chain fatty acid content in the breast muscle of ducks, except for the histidine, proline, aspartic, and acetic acid. Some amino acids derived from SSR may be utilized by the organism through complex amino acid metabolism for the synthesis of sugars, lipids, and energy (Brosnan and Brosnan 2020). Fatty acids undergo oxidation reactions by free radicals to produce flavor compounds such as aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, and alcohols, which contribute to changes in meat flavor and serve as precursors to the Maillard reaction (Shahidi and Hossain 2022). These factors contribute to the distinctive and appealing flavors of duck meat during cooking. The addition of SSR does not significantly alter the content of most short-chain fatty acids, thereby not imparting other specific flavors to duck meat at a significant extent (Wasserman 1972; Dinh et al. 2021). The most metabolites in duck meat are steroids, lipids, predominantly amino acids and their derivatives, and nucleic acids based on the metabolomics analysis. In addition, many of the differential metabolites are enriched in the ErbB and MAPK pathways, which are largely associated with cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation (Olayioye 2001; Sun et al. 2015). Most of these metabolites have anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, hepatoprotective, and anti-hepatitis properties, such as Scymnol (Hodges et al. 2016), Ganoderenic acid B (Komoda et al. 1989), Homocarnosine (Guiotto et al. 2005), and Erythritol (Msomi et al. 2023) exhibited significant upregulation, which perhaps due to its relatively high content of soy isoflavones in SSR (Chen et al. 2014). On the other hand, metabolites related to glycolipid metabolism, such as PE (18:0/0:0), DG [14:1(9Z)/18:3 (6Z, 9Z, 12Z)/0:0], DG (21:0/0:0/TXB2), LysoPC [20:3 (8Z, 11Z, 14Z)/0:0] (Human Metabolome Database, HMDB) and UDP-D-Galactose (Frey and Hegeman 2013), were significantly downregulated. This shows a similar pattern to the decline in plasma nutrient synthesis indicators following SSR supplementation.

In conclusion, SSR can be used as a feed ingredient for meat ducks, but attention should be paid to the inclusion level. Under the conditions of the present study, supplementation with up to 4% SSR did not significantly affect any measured parameters in meat ducks. However, the nutrient synthesis may be affected at 6% and 8% group due to the low digestibility of nutrients in SSR, as reflected by changes in plasma indicators and the content of certain amino acids and fatty acids in duck meat. Although the growth rate of adipose tissue is more sensitive to nutrient intake than that of other tissues (Zhan et al. 2007), the intermuscular fat content and abdominal fat did not change following the inclusion of SSR. Also, no significant reduction in growth performance and organ development was observed at 42 days of age. Therefore, although the relatively low digestibility of SSR may have reduced the supply of substrates for nutrient synthesis, this effect appears to be limited in this study. The observed alterations in antioxidant-related metabolites in the muscle may be related to the high content of soy isoflavones in SSR, but more investigation is needed.

Conclusions

Supplementation of soy sauce residue in the diets of Pekin ducks affected plasma biochemical parameters associated with nutrient synthesis at inclusion levels of 6% and 8%. However, growth performance, organ development, meat quality, and adipose tissue content were not significantly affected, indicating that the impact of SSR on nutrient synthesis is limited at 42 days of age. Furthermore, although 8% SSR supplementation had limited effects on production performance, an inclusion level of 4% is recommended as a safer practice.
