Computational approaches to investigate the relationship between periodontitis and cardiovascular diseases for precision medicine
Authors: Sophia Duenas, Zachary McGee, Ishani Mhatre, Karthikeyan Mayilvahanan, Kush Ketan Patel, Habiba Abdelhalim, Atharv Jayprakash, Uzayr Wasif, Oluchi Nwankwo, William Degroat, Naveena Yanamala, Partho P Sengupta, Daniel Fine, Zeeshan Ahmed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriodontitis is a highly prevalent inflammatory illness that leads to the destruction of tooth supporting tissue structures and has been associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD). Precision medicine, an emerging branch of medical treatment, aims can further improve current traditional treatment by personalizing care based on one’s environment, genetic makeup, and lifestyle. Genomic databases have paved the way for precision medicine by elucidating the pathophysiology of complex, heritable diseases. Therefore, the investigation of novel periodontitis-linked genes associated with CVD will enhance our understanding of their linkage and related biochemical pathways for targeted therapies. In this article, we highlight possible mechanisms of actions connecting PD and CVD. Furthermore, we delve deeper into certain heritable inflammatory-associated pathways linking the two. The goal is to gather, compare, and assess high-quality scientific literature alongside genomic datasets that seek to establish a link between periodontitis and CVD. The scope is focused on the most up to date and authentic literature published within the last 10 years, indexed and available from PubMed Central, that analyzes periodontitis-associated genes linked to CVD. Based on the comparative analysis criteria, fifty-one genes associated with both periodontitis and CVD were identified and reported. The prevalence of genes associated with both CVD and periodontitis warrants investigation to assess the validity of a potential linkage between the pathophysiology of both diseases.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Periodontitis, Genomics, Bioinformatics, Precision medicine

Introduction

Periodontitis is one of the most prevalent inflammatory oral illnesses affecting roughly 50% of the global population [1, 2]. Poor oral health and a complex interaction between oral bacteria and the host’s immune response can lead to its development [3]. When left untreated, it results in irreversible damage to supportive tissue and can cause tooth loss [4, 5]. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is an umbrella term for diseases of the cardiac muscle and the vascular system supplying the heart, brain, and other vital organs [6]. Despite being the leading cause of death globally, current traditional methods rely on alleviating symptoms rather than treating the disease [7, 8]. Chronic inflammation and dysregulation in the inflammatory response has been studied as risk factors for atherosclerosis and resulting cardiovascular events [9].

The correlation between various inflammatory conditions and CVDs [10, 11] has encouraged the exploration of a linkage between periodontitis and CVDs, driven by the commonality of pathways that involve cytokines [12, 13]. Both diseases exhibit common systemic inflammatory mechanisms, characterized by elevated levels of inflammatory mediators, lipids, hemostatic factors, and thrombotic factors. Additionally, they share common risk factors, including smoking and genetics. Nevertheless, the precise influence of periodontitis on the initiation and progression of CVDs remains unclear [14, 15]. Periodontitis patients generally face a 19% higher risk of CVD, while individuals aged sixty-five and older may experience a relative risk as high as 44% [2]. Understanding the associated pathways can help with patient management by identifying risk factors and appropriate targets for medicine. Research consistently indicates a connection between oral health and CVD, yet causality remains unestablished. Nevertheless, numerous studies demonstrate enhancements in CVD risk factors after periodontal treatments [14–16]. Studying both in vivo and in vitro studies could further reveal more of the relationship between the two diseases [14]. Treatment of periodontitis is challenging to standardize for all patients due to various influencing factors, including aggressiveness, localization, and characteristic microbiome [17]. Similarly for CVDs, confounding factors such as age of onset of the disease, concurrent diseases, and severity of the disease can affect treatment options [18]. Viewing these diseases, not as binary factors that are either present or not, but rather, as a complex network of variables contributing to their manifestation, can aid in disease management. For instance, calcium channel blockers, such as amlodipine, are known to cause gingival hyperplasia, predisposing patients to periodontal disease [19]. Thus, standardized therapies may not prove effective for all patients [8, 12].

Precision medicine aims to target such limits and improve current healthcare systems through a personalized approach that factors an individual’s environment, genes, and lifestyle [20, 21]. Current designs for precision medicine are becoming more common in healthcare and incorporate these factors for early detection of diseases, improving diagnosis and treatment [22–24]. A crucial component of precision medicine is genetics, offering a probabilistic interpretation of heritable disease risk [25]. It can also help reveal disease-specific biomarkers, crucial for advancing preclinical and clinical treatment, ultimately improving public health [26]. These advancements reveal new possibilities for enhancing treatment strategies, including adopting a holistic approach to different stages of periodontitis, controlling the formation of aggressive periodontitis, and monitoring the microbial environment responsible for the disease’s progression [27]. Similarly, developing an understanding of the genetic components underlying CVD has helped identify targets for gene editing, early diagnosis to assist in prevention-based treatment plans, and biomarkers for drug targets [28]. As a result, collecting and assessing current periodontal biomarkers from genetic testing is indispensable for improving periodontitis and CVD treatment.

In this study, genetic biomarkers associated with both periodontitis and CVDs were reviewed, compared, and reported. The criteria must be the most up to date and authentic literature published within the last 10 years that were indexed and available from PubMed Central, the methodology, genomic dataset, and results were further analyzed to study the genetic influence on biomolecular pathways between periodontitis and CVD. This included MR analysis, gene-expression analysis, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, and meta-analysis. The literature search involved a combination of different keywords, including but not limited to: “periodontitis,” “CVD,” “stroke,” “genomic,” “genetics,” and ‘gene expression data’. Title and abstract were screened through. After, the remaining literature was read and evaluated for eligibility based on the criteria as follows: the literature must be published within the last ten years, it must be periodontitis-associated genes linked to CVD, it must use human genomic data, and it must include the genes studied and analyzed, with exclusions towards epigenetics (Fig. 1). Thirteen different peer-reviewed and published scientific studies were divided into four separate categories based on the common disease progression hypotheses: (I) establishing genetic associations, (II) investigating a possible cause-and-effect relationship, (III) exploring the molecular pathways and mechanisms and (IV) examining the bacterial role within these biological cascades and processes.

Determining genetic associations between periodontitis and CVD

In this section, the literature aims to establish a genetic association and identify key biomarkers associated with both diseases.

Shared genetic risk loci between coronary artery disease and chronic periodontitis with a focus on PLASMINOGEN and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF- β) [29]

This study investigated the shared risk loci between coronary artery disease (CAD) and chronic periodontitis by explored the role of PLASMINOGEN (PLG) and TGF- β [29]. The dataset was obtained from genotyping 46 CAD risk loci for periodontitis cases in Germany and the UK. It included 600 German aggressive periodontitis cases, and 1,448 controls genotyped with the Illumina Immunochip, as well as 283 German aggressive periodontitis cases, and 972 controls analyzed with the Affymetrix 500 K array. At the end of the study, an additional 449 German blood donors from the Biobank Popgen and 3,028 population representative German controls from the biobank Kooperative Gesundheitsforschung in der Region Augsburg were added for statistical power [29]. Specific SNPs were genotyped via TaqMan assays on 384-well plates. In silico SNP imputation and predictions were conducted. Gene expression following TGF-β exposure was assessed using human gingival fibroblast primary cells from three individuals. The study employed qRT- polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and statistical tests to analyze gene expression changes. Shared genetic susceptibility factors were identified between CAD and aggressive periodontitis, with a notable association in the PLG gene [29]. TFG-beta signaling may be associated with CAD and periodontitis, ANRIL emerged as a significant risk gene for both CAD and periodontitis, and additional associations with PLG were observed, suggesting potential collaboration in disease pathogenesis. Replication of associations of SNPs discovered at another genetic loci, FURIN, was unsuccessful. Limitations of the study included a non-diverse sample, use of imputation to achieve appropriate power for comparison, and small sample size for the TGF- β signaling study.

Meta-analysis of GWAS reveals shared genetic risk markers in periodontitis and CVD [30]

This study aimed to identify the shared genetic risk loci between CAD and generalized chronic periodontitis. Datasets were derived from the AgP-Ger (680 cases and 3,973 controls), CARDIoGRAMplusC4D CAD meta-analysis (60,801 patients and 123,504 controls), and an independent periodontitis GWAS dataset (4,415 cases and 5,935 controls); and AgP-NL (171 Dutch cases and 2,607 controls) [30]. A genome-wide association meta-analysis was conducted by first finding similarly regulated variants between datasets and pruning variants using intermediate linkage disequilibrium. To ensure the reliability of these variants, this was replicated in additional periodontitis GWAS samples using the fixed effects model with a Bonferroni correction for the multiple studies used and the variants were then annotated. ANRIL was identified to be the leading common variant but was not included in the rest of the study since it has repeatedly been shown as a risk factor for PD in previous studies. Two SNPs (rs11634042 at ADAMTS7 and rs1561198 at VAMP8) were identified as shared novel risk loci between CAD and periodontitis [30]. A limitation of this study was a small sample size for periodontitis as opposed to CAD.

Genetic polymorphisms in NLRP3 and IL-1β in periodontitis and CHD [31]

The expression of the genetic polymorphisms of NLRP3 (rs35829419) and IL-1β (+ 3954) found in.

subgingival plaque and blood samples from individuals with generalized periodontitis both with and without CHD were investigated [31]. The dataset comprised seventy individuals of South Asian descent; half had generalized periodontitis, and the remaining had generalized periodontitis and CHD. Blood samples and subgingival dental plaque samples were taken from all patients. PCR amplification of NLRP3 (rs35829419) and IL-1β (+ 3954) followed by gel electrophoresis for polymorphism visualization was done for all samples. Statistical analysis was conducted using ANOVA, t-tests, and chi-square tests [31]. Both polymorphisms were found to be significantly higher in both blood and plaque samples for the generalized periodontitis and CHD group over the generalized periodontitis group. Limitations include a small, non-diverse sample size and the cross-sectional design’s inability to establish causality between genetic polymorphisms, periodontitis, and CHD development.

Immune related genetic associations between atherosclerosis and periodontitis [32]

The purpose of this study was to identify the genetic markers underlying the immune based relationship between periodontitis and atherosclerosis and assess the efficacy of those genes in predicting the disease. Periodontitis gene expression omnibus (GEO) data sets GSE16134 and GSE10334 were used as the experimental set and validation set respectively. Atherosclerosis GEO data sets GSE100927 and GSE43292 were used as the experimental set and validation set respectively. Immune cell infiltration for twenty-two immune cell types was assessed for each group using the CIBERSORT algorithm. A weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was then done to find which genes were associated with differential immune cell infiltrates for both atherosclerosis and periodontitis models. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were then identified individually for each periodontitis and atherosclerosis using the lima package in R and compared to the results from the WGCNA and the import database for immune related genes to identify immune-related differentially expressed crosstalk genes. Fourteen immune related co-expressed genes were found using WGCNA (SLC7A7, BTK, CYTH4, BIN2, ITGAL, P2RX4, C4orf48, CMTM7, TPST2, RAC2, ARID3A, VOPP1, FUCA2, and CCDC88A) [32]. Two genes, BTK and ITGAL, were identified as important immune related crosstalk genes that were upregulated in both atherosclerosis and periodontitis. The GEO validation data sets were used to measure the predictive value of both genes for atherosclerosis and periodontitis through a clinical diagnostic model. ROC curves showed high predictive power. A limitation of this study was the lack of accounting for confounding variables, such as age, and gender.

Investigating the potential causal relationship between periodontitis and CVD

This section investigates establishing a potential causal relationship between periodontitis and CVD. MR analyses were conducted to determine association with CAD, heart failure, stroke, ischemic stroke, subclinical atherosclerosis, and hypertension.

MR analysis using periodontal and CVD GWAS data [33]

The study investigated the causality between dental caries, periodontitis, and major CVDs (CAD, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and stroke). The analysis first begins with GWAS data for dental caries and periodontitis was collected from GLIDE Consortium and UK Biobank, while GWAS data for CVDs was collected from CARDIoGRAMplusC4D Consortium, UK Biobank, Atrial Fibrillation Consortium, and Multi Ancestry Genome-wide Association Study of Stroke (MEGASTROKE) consortium [33]. Two sample MR was utilized, and effects were estimated using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method. Gene functional annotation was conducted. Two methods were used, one using HaploReg and the other using GTEx. Pathway enrichment analysis, including GO and KEGG analyses were performed using Functional Enrichment Analysis Tool These tools were used to explore common pathophysiology between CVDs and periodontal diseases. The analysis then ends with statistical analyses being conducted. Two sample MR was utilized, and effects were estimated using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method. Gene functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses were performed to explore common pathophysiology between CVDs and periodontal diseases. The two-sample MR analyses did not provide evidence that supports a causal relationship between periodontitis and CVDs. This is supported by sensitivity analyses such as MR-Egger analysis and weighted median analysis. Gene functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses indicated the presence of shared risk factors and comorbidities between CVDs and periodontal diseases. As such, observational associations between periodontal diseases and CVD may be affected by common risk factors, such as obesity. Limitations of this study include a small, non-diverse sample, pleiotropy of SNPs, and overlap in the GWAS of heart failure and atrial fibrillation, which could cause a slight bias in the MR estimations. As a result, further studies are needed to understand the specific mechanisms involved between CVDs and periodontal disease.

Periodontitis and ischemic stroke, CAD, and subclinical atherosclerosis [34]

MR analysis was used to test if periodontitis is causally associated with CAD, stroke, or subclinical atherosclerosis. The analysis began with data gathered from UK Biobank, CARDIoGRAMplusC4D, and MEGASTROKE for stroke, coronary disease, and subclinical atherosclerosis were used as the sample population. A two-sample MR approach with IVW utilized summary data from different sources, including GWAS of clinically defined periodontitis, stroke, CHD, and carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) as a marker for subclinical atherosclerosis, for the primary outcomes [34]. SNPs used were identified from a GWAS of clinically defined periodontitis patients and pleiotropy was searched for all variants. The analysis ended with sensitive analyses being performed using the MR-Egger methods and weighted median to relax some of the strict requirements and identify any outlying instrumental variables. The IVW, weighted median and MR-Egger methods failed to find evidence for causal association of periodontitis with stroke, CAD, or subclinical atherosclerosis. Some challenges associated with this study include low variance for the five periodontal disease SNPs used in the study, with selected variance being associated with the less common, more aggressive forms of periodontitis. The null findings demonstrate a possible confounder, highlighting a role for shared risk factors for both diseases.

Periodontitis, hypertension & non-surgical periodontal therapy [35]

This study investigated the association between periodontitis and hypertension. To begin the analysis data was gathered from two sources: the UK Biobank and the International Consortium of Blood Pressure (ICBP) GWAS. In the MR analysis, SNPs that were previously associated with periodontitis in GWAS were used as instrumental variables. This led to the identification of four SNPs, in LOC107984137 (rs729876), MTND1P5 (rs16870060), DEFA1A3 (rs2738058), and SIGLEC5 (rs4284742). MR analysis showed all the SNPs associated with an increased risk for both periodontitis and blood pressure (BP). Additionally, data was gathered from a randomized controlled trial of non-surgical periodontal therapy, which included participants with periodontitis and hypertension. To end this analysis a randomized controlled trial helped to investigate the effect of non-surgical periodontal therapy on blood pressure where one group received intensive periodontal treatment (IPT) in the form of supra and sub gingival scaling with topical application of 0.2% chlorhexidine gel and the control periodontal treatment (CPT) group received supragingival scaling [35]. Clinical results showed that IPT resulted in a significant reduction in BP and an increase in vascular endothelial function. Blood analysis showed significant reduction in some inflammatory markers, such as IL-6 and IFN-γ in the IPT group compared to the CPT group. The IPT group also had a significant reduction in CD8 cells compared to the CPT group. The study provides evidence that periodontitis may be a modifiable risk factor for hypertension, and that non-surgical periodontal therapy may be an effective intervention for reducing blood pressure and improving vascular health. Limitations include a small sample size for the randomized controlled trial of non-surgical periodontal therapy, and limited generalizability due to a non-diverse data set.

The link between chronic periodontitis and aggressive periodontitis with ischemic stroke [36]

Here, the focus was to investigate the relationship between chronic periodontitis and aggressive periodontitis with ischemic stroke. To start this analysis, genetic proxies for chronic periodontitis were obtained from the UK Biobank, containing 950 cases with 455,398 controls. The markers for aggressive periodontitis were obtained from a GWAS study of European ancestry containing 851 cases with 6,836 controls (replicated in 223 case and 564 controls). The ischemic stroke genetic instruments were obtained from the MEGASTROKE consortium containing 34,217 cases with 406,111 controls. The study employed the fixed-effects IVW method for MR and used MR-Egger regression and weighted median methods to ensure the reliability of the results [36]. To end this analysis, the assessment of heterogeneity, pleiotropy, and the influence of potential outlier SNPs, Cochran’s Q statistics, MR-Egger intercept, and a leave-one-out analysis were performed. The results found no substantial relationship between chronic periodontitis and aggressive periodontitis and ischemic stroke. For chronic periodontitis, there were no signs of heterogeneity, pleiotropy, or outlier SNPs. However, there was suggestive evidence of a potential causal effect of chronic periodontitis on cardioembolic stroke. Some challenges associated with this study include the non-diverse population used and the resulting limited generalizability to other populations, unaddressed biases from using various datasets, and uncertainty of whether treatments for different severities of chronic periodontitis patients can help prevent ischemic stroke.

Molecular mechanisms and pathways

Literature in this section investigates the shared molecular mechanisms and pathways between periodontitis and CVD, mainly with the inflammatory response and the immune system. Datasets were gathered from blood samples, GEO database, and GWAS. Investigations were mainly done using either protein-protein interaction (PPI) or in-vitro analysis.

Exploring gene signatures in periodontitis & carotid atherosclerosis through integrated analysis [37]

The objective of this study was to investigate the shared genes and molecular mechanisms underlying the co-pathogenesis of carotid atherosclerosis and periodontitis. The dataset used in this study was obtained from the GEO database, consisting of two different datasets: carotid atherosclerotic dataset GSE100927 (twenty-nine lesioned samples and twelve controls) and periodontitis dataset GSE10334 (183 afflicted samples and sixty-four controls). From both datasets, DEGs were identified and analyzed using the R package “Limma”. Overlapping DEGs were defined as being simultaneously up or down regulated in both data sets [37]. Functions of the overlapping DEGS were identified using gene ontology biological process (GO_BP), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Gene and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome enrichment analyses. A PPI network was created using the Search Tool for Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database and Cytoscape software and WGCNA identified key module genes. CytoHubba plugin identified PPI key genes. The genes identified in the key modules were then intersected with PPI key genes to identify key crosstalk genes. These were then subsequently validated by independent external datasets (GSE43292 [CAS microarray dataset], GSE16134 [periodontitis microarray dataset], and GSE28829 [CAS microarray dataset]). Immune cell infiltration was identified using the ssGSEA algorithm. Transcription factors (TF) associated with at least two key crosstalk genes were identified using the NeworkAnalyst 3.0 platform and mRNA expression levels of these TFs were validated in the test set and validation set using t-tests. They identified 355 overlapping DEGs of periodontitis and CAS [37]. GO_BP analysis showed inflammation, neutrophil chemotaxis, cell adhesion, and T-cell differentiation as the most enriched terms. KEGG analysis showed the DEGs were related to chemokine signaling pathways, leukocyte trans endothelial migration, rheumatoid arthritis, cytokine-receptor interaction, and hematopoietic cell lineage. Reactome analysis showed enrichment in the immune system, neutrophil degranulation, and cytokine signaling. PPI network construction followed by KEGG functional analysis showed clusters also associated with osteoclast differentiation, NK-kappa B signaling, TNF signaling, and IL-17 signaling. Reactome analysis of clusters additionally revealed IL-4 and IL-13 signaling and extracellular matrix reorganization. LCK and ITGAM were identified as key crosstalk genes. In addition, there was an increased expression of multiple immune cells in periodontitis and carotid atherosclerosis compared to controls. A possible mechanism for causal connection including the key crosstalk genes is as follows: oral pathogens, such as P. gingivalis activate T-cell receptors which are regulated by LCK which increases systemic levels of cytokines like IL-1B, TNF-α, etc. and promote arterial macrophages into foam cells, leading to plaque formation. Pathogens can also travel by the blood stream to the arteries and increase LCK expression in the vessels. ITGAM is involved in adhesive properties of immune cells and the uptake of pathogens [37]. A possible causal link through ITGAM might be macrophage activation through ITGAM activation by pathogens. It may also lead to smooth muscle cell migration to the endothelium, which can exacerbate plaque formation. SPI1 is a TF shown to be a key regulator for LCK and ITGAM (Fig. 2A). It is important to highlight that the conclusions were reached by integrating bioinformatics analysis with findings from previous relevant studies, underscoring the need for further clinical validation in the future. Moreover, the exact role of the crosstalk genes requires validation for both in vivo and in vitro settings.

Network protein interaction between stroke and periodontitis interplay [38]

The objective of this study was to investigate the key proteins and biological pathways involved in both periodontitis and stroke to understand their shared pathophysiology. Data was gathered from the National Human Genome Research Institute-European Bioinformatics Institute Catalog of Human Genome-Wide Association Studies (NHGRI-GWAS). The researchers employed a comprehensive approach combining GWAS data analysis, PPI network prediction, and gene enrichment analysis to uncover insights into the molecular mechanisms of periodontitis and stroke. The gene interaction was confirmed through a heatmap plot and the Genemania network supported the network obtained from the STRING database. The over-representation analysis identified several pathways related to fibrin clot formation, platelet activation, and signaling, among others [38]. Molecular functions such as titin binding, nuclear receptor transcription coactivator activity, and cell adhesion molecule binding were discovered to be significant. Regarding cellular components, the pseudopodium and platelet α granule lumen were among the most significant. The largest node identified with the PPI network is related to the atherosclerotic process. ACTN-1 and ACTN-2 allow leukocyte migration when endothelial dysfunction has occurred. Cytokines and MMP upregulation during this process can lead to maturation of atherosclerotic plaques. This eventually leads to the production of thrombin through the interaction of TIMP-1 and F5 and the eventual production of a thrombus that can weaken plaque deposits through the interaction of TIMP-1 and FGG with SERPINA1 (Fig. 2B). Proteins of the ADAMTS family are found to be elevated in gingival tissue after exposure to lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from P. gingivalis and can lead to reduced integrity of endothelial lining and defective vascular remodeling, which can link periodontitis with stroke. Another link is systemic inflammation brought upon by increased C5 (C5AR1) seen in periodontitis that can promote production of fibrinogen through F2 leading to the development of atherosclerosis. Upregulation of SMURF2, a ubiquitin E3 ligase, in periodontitis has also been linked to autophagy in stroke and promoting thrombus production. An important takeaway is that these identified pathways rely on an inflammatory response. Limitations of this study include reliance on previously identified genes and limited clinical predictive utility.

Associations of oxidative/nitrosative stress and endothelial dysfunction biomarker changes with CVD risk in periodontitis patients [39]

The changes in biomarkers of oxidative and nitrosative stress and endothelial dysfunction in patients with periodontitis and their association with cardiovascular risk was investigated. The dataset consisted of patients with periodontitis, CHD, or both diseases and healthy controls. The researchers collected blood from the recruited subjects to evaluate alterations in the inflammatory status. Various markers were employed, including CoQ10, Nitrotyrosine (NT), asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA), hs-CRP, and plasma lipid levels. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were also isolated for transcriptome analysis [39]. Statistical comparisons and analyses between the four groups, periodontitis, CHD, periodontitis and CHD, and control were conducted by one way ANOVA testing with fixed effects, post hoc comparisons using the HSD Tukey Test, and SPSS 22.0 for Windows software package. This study revealed a significant increase in CRP, VES, ADMA and NT levels in CHD and periodontitis patients. It was proposed that the inflammation associated with periodontitis triggers oxidative stress, causing a decrease in antioxidant defenses. This was supported by reduced CoQ10 (antioxidant) levels in the blood. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) lead to ADMA production which then leads to the creation of a greater amount of ROS through nitric oxide synthase (NOS) uncoupling. Nitrosative stress, a risk factor for CVD, is a consequence of these changes (Fig. 2C). However, this study also contained some limitations. The patient sample was small and non-diverse, meaning there is limited generalizability of the results. In addition, only moderate to severe periodontitis was assessed in this study.

Bacterial connection between periodontitis & CVD

In this section, the literature explores the relationship of bacterial pathogens and its role on the relationship between periodontitis and CVD. Genes identified were discussed in relation to periodontitis & CVD, investigating specific genes that are affected by bacterial infection.

Identifying a pivotal gene linking periodontitis to systemic diseases [40]

RNA extraction and library preparation were conducted, followed by sequencing and comprehensive data analysis, encompassing PCA, orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), differential expression analysis, and GO category analysis. DEGs were pinpointed by searching the literature for transcriptome analysis of inflammatory conditions linked to periodontitis. Gingival fibroblasts from three individuals with no periodontitis and purchased human primary gingival epithelial cells were cultured with P. gingivalis or E. coli LPS. Quantitative PCR assessed gene expression levels, focusing on PLEK, MMP7, MUC4, and GAPDH. Immunohistochemical staining was executed on biopsies to determine the degree of infiltration by inflammatory cells in gingival tissues, with CD20 expression identified in 85% of periodontitis biopsies compared to 40% in controls and CD68 expression was identified in 85% of periodontitis biopsies compared to 45% in controls [40]. PCA and OPLS-DA revealed genes MUC4, MMP7, IL19, and IGHV3-43, contributing most to the periodontitis group, while healthy subjects exhibited higher expressions of COL10A1, WISP2, and SP7. GO analysis uncovered up-regulated gene clusters associated with defense, inflammation, wounding responses, apoptosis, and cell death in periodontitis and CVD. MUC4, the most significantly up-regulated gene in periodontitis, holds promise as an early detection and monitoring biomarker, whereas MMP7, the second most up-regulated gene, is implicated in periodontitis and extracellular matrix degradation [40]. Comparative analysis across various diseases revealed common up-regulated GO categories, underscoring the gene PLEK and its role in chronic inflammation. Bacteria treated gingival fibroblasts showed increased mRNA expression of MUC4, MMP7, and PLEK. In epithelial cells, MUC4 expression was significantly changed compared to the other two. Genes that showed shared functions and signaling pathway in response to bacteria stimuli were also highlighted. RGS1, SERPINA1, and KYNU are reported to activate the mitogen‑activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, while KYNU is induced by the MAPK signaling pathway. It is implied that these genes may act together in the maintenance of chronic inflammation. These four genes are reported to be up regulated in periodontitis and CVD. Limitations included the potential for human error in test results, undiscovered genes at play, sample size constraints, and the cross-sectional design, which collected data at a specific time.

Enrichment of GWASdb genes related to CVD and periodontitis in P. gingivalis [41]

In this study, DEGs from gingival tissue afflicted with periodontitis and P. gingivalis-treated macrophages is compared to atherosclerotic plaque samples to find overlapping genes whose expression is modified by the known periodontal disease-causing pathogen. The interactome was curated using Pubmed references. Susceptibility genes were obtained from two GWAS databases: GWASdb and the NHGRI-EBI catalog. Microarray gene expression was taken from an integrated analysis of the effects of P. gingivalis, its LPS, or fimbral component on human macrophages. Atherosclerosis data was taken from a microarray study comparing stable and unstable atherosclerotic plaques from human carotid endarterectomy specimens. Overlap of genes was then tested for significance using the hypergeometric probability test and correcting for p-values using the false discovery rate. The interactome showed enrichment in GWASdb genes related to hypertension, atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, and congestive heart failure (CHF). Atherosclerotic gene enrichment was confirmed in the NHGRI-EBI database. The other diseases were not analyzed [41]. In the GWASdb database, within the interactome, 574 genes of the 3,993 genes were found to be associated with atherosclerosis and/or hypertension. The microarray study analysis revealed that upregulated and downregulated genes significantly overlapped between periodontitis and atherosclerosis. In the P. gingivalis microarrays, live P. gingivalis and the FimA component upregulated genes significantly overlapped with atherosclerotic plaques. For downregulated genes, live P. gingivalis regulated genes significantly overlapped. It is hypothesized that P. gingivalis protease (gingipains) allows bacteria to enter systemic circulation and disrupt epithelial and endothelial barriers, leading to a link between periodontitis and atherosclerosis [41]. Limitations of the study include using only P. gingivalis even though many bacteria are involved in periodontitis and the lack of standardizing for demographic factors.

DEGs in the progression of CVD and periodontitis

Despite significant advancements in CVD and periodontitis diagnostic methods, current research is still at the developing stages of deep phenotyping for identifying and providing personalized treatment for complex, heritable diseases. Recent studies focused on identifying novel loci, while many others focused on further investigation or analysis of previously reported genetic biomarkers for both CVD and periodontitis. Here, further investigation was conducted towards DEGs that were previously found to be associated with CVDs. There was a discovery of 216 CVD-related genes from previous literature review, with 190 of these genes associated with AF and twenty-six genes associated with heart failure (HF) [42]. Fifty-one genes were found to be potential biomarkers for both CVD and periodontitis (Table 1). Of those fifty-one genes, nine genes were discovered to be more significant as they have been linked to CVD and already investigated in our previous studies: TNF, MMP2, CRP, IL6, SLC2A1, CDKN2B-AS1, TAC1, FADD, and TRPV1 [43, 44]. This provides evidence for the potential significance of these genes in identifying plausible biological pathways to link CVD and periodontitis.

Inflammation is an immune system-mediated process that happens in response to trauma and the effects of disease [45]. Prolonged inflammatory reaction results in damage to cells which leads to the production of cytokines and increases permeability of vasculature and dilates capillaries. The increased permeability leads to migration of immune cells, specifically neutrophils and monocytes, into the interstitial space. These cells can release interleukins and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) which can lead to local and systemic inflammatory effects [45]. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) is an important cytokine that is produced by activated immune cells and helps mediate the immune response. TNF plays a central role in CVDs and has been shown to affect myocardial contractility and peripheral resistance. Prior studies have shown a correlation between blood levels of TNF-α and the severity of HF [46]. A previous study has conducted RNA-seq analysis as well as functional mutation, splice, distribution, and divergence analysis, and discovered TNF to be a DEG for HF [43, 44]. In periodontitis, TNF-α has been seen as an important cytokine in mediating the dysregulation of the immune response [47]. High levels of TNF-α in periodontitis patients can lead to systemic inflammation which allows increased permeability of arterial walls and the formation of foam cells from circulating monocytes [47]. Once foam cells accumulate, necrotic centers in plaque buildup can form, leading to weakened supports that can break from plaque and lead to ischemia [48].

Like TNF-α, Interleukin 6 (IL-6) is another type of cytokine which helps mediate differentiation of cells and induces production of antibodies for acquired immunity [46]. IL-6 has been linked to CVDs including but not limited to CHF [46]. Studies have found IL-6 may contribute to the progression of myocardial damage and promoting myocardial injury [49]. Proposed mechanisms for involvement in CVD include induction of vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and recruitment of other inflammatory mediators leading to plaque formation and destabilization [50]. Like TNF-α, prolonged high expressions of IL-6 can result in chronic inflammation and resulting tissue and bone loss seen in periodontitis. Increased levels of IL-6 are prevalent in patients with chronic periodontitis. The proinflammatory immune response hints at a potential pathway between chronic periodontitis and other diseases such as CVDs [51].

C-reactive protein (CRP) is a liver protein produced in response to inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and interleukins [52]. While generally a diagnostic factor, there have been some proposed mechanisms for its mediation of CVD through activation of the complement system, which is part of the innate immune system, has been thought to play a part in plaque instability and resulting ischemia [52, 53]. Long-term studies and a variety of analyses, including meta-analysis and regression analysis, of CRP concentrations have uncovered a linkage between the gene and ischemic heart disease, CAD, HF, and other CVDs [54, 55]. Furthermore, blood levels of CRP were found to be elevated in patients with periodontitis when compared to healthy patients. Patients found suffering from chronic or aggressive periodontitis consistently had higher CRP levels [56].

Matrix metalloproteinase 2 (MMP2) is another significant CVD-associated biomarker uncovered in a previous study [43]. MMP2 is an enzyme involved in the breakdown of extracellular matrix materials to allow for increased permeability and remodeling [57]. High levels of MMP2 have been linked to HF [57]. The gene has many proposed pathways for inducing CVD, including plaque rupture leading to ischemia, increased permeability leading to increased inflammatory response, and irregular cardiac remodeling [58]. High levels of MMPs have also been seen in periodontitis patients and may correlate to destruction of supporting tissue structures seen in periodontitis [59]. MMPs levels have been shown to be affected by proteins produced by TAC1 potentially being a factor for the development of periodontitis. TAC1 encodes a group of neuropeptides called tachykinins, exemplified by Substance P [60]. Substance P is a undecapeptide which belongs to the tachykinin family and has been linked to heart inflammation and other CVDs because of the important role it plays in the cardiovascular system, including regulation of blood pressure and heart frequency [61, 62]. Enrichment analysis and deep phenotyping found TAC1 to be associated with CVDs [43] and mutation analysis discovered 103 mutations were able to be mapped onto the TAC1 sequence [44]. Some hypotheses linking substance P to CVDs include its ability to activate cardiac mast cells leading to infiltration of mast cells into atherosclerotic deposits and subsequent destabilization, induction of TNF-α production [63]. Degradation of gingival fibroblasts through activation of MMPs by substance P can be a factor in the development of periodontitis. Studies focused on periodontitis have also found associations between increased concentrations of substance P and periodontitis [64]. Most of the studies have focused on non-human primates, primarily mice. In humans, substance P upregulates osteoclastogenesis leading to bone loss in human dental pulp [64]. Further research is needed for the link between TAC1 and periodontitis to determine the effect in human subjects.

Another significant gene studied previously is SLC2A1 which encodes a glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) protein whose primary function is transporting glucose into cells for energy use [65, 66]. It is found in macrophages. Immune mediation is an energetically demanding process and dysregulation of the glucose transport mechanisms for immune mediating cells can affect proper function [67]. This dysregulation can lead to senescence and resulting immune regulation failure in the heart for CVD and supporting oral tissue structures in periodontitis [68].

In this study, much of the literature focused on inflammatory pathways links, including studies with Cyclic-dependent kinase inhibitor 2B antisense RNA 1 (CDKN2B-AS1) [69]. Also known as ANRIL, CDKN2B-AS1 is another significant biomarker associated with various CVDs such as CAD. The gene is expressed in the coronary smooth muscle cells and expression levels for CDKN2B-AS1 can affect vascular cell proliferation [69]. GWAS studies have reported CDKN2B-AS1 polymorphisms and variations as genetic biomarkers for CHD [70]. Previous studies suggest periodontitis is not casually related to CVDs, but rather, similar inflammatory pathways and components in disease physiology might link both diseases [70, 71].

FADD, a type of cell signaling mediator that bridges the activation of extrinsic apoptotic signals and certain inflammatory pathways with procaspases to lead to apoptosis [72]. Studies have found FADD to play an important role in coronary events and have an association with HF [72]. Apoptosis and necroptosis have evolved as opposing forces in the defense against inflammation, a key component of periodontitis. During the progression of periodontitis, necroptosis, which leads to the release of inflammatory signals, was seen in higher levels of affected gingival tissue over apoptotic death [73].

Lastly, TRPV1, also known as transient receptor potential vanilloid subtype 1, has been found to be downregulated in CVDs [74]. TRPV-1 is a receptor that allows for detection of various imbalances, including inflammatory markers, osmotic pressure changes, tissue damage, etc. as well as certain molecules such as substance P and prostaglandins [74]. Thus, down regulation of these receptors can lead to incorrect blood pressure sensing mechanisms and increase in atherosclerotic promoting factors such as foam cell viability [72]. Through functional mutation analysis and mapping TRPV1 sequence data, there is a total of 337 mutations and functional impact scores for the eight missense mutations [44]. This suggests TRPV1 can act as a biomarker for CVDs. TRPV1 was found to be involved in the process of alveolar bone defects suggesting its effect on periodontal inflammation [75, 76]. There were conflicting conclusions as one study found upregulation of the gene prompted periodontal inflammation, whereas the second study discovered neuronal TRPV1 activation inhibited osteoclastgenesis. Nonetheless, both studies discovered TRPV1 signaling in periodontal tissue is essential and further investigation is necessary to determine the causal relationship [75, 76].

Discussion

The findings of this comparative review reveal the genetic associations and shared susceptibility factors between cardiovascular and periodontal conditions. A summary of these key findings between the studies mentioned above, including study objectives, data sources, methods used, results, and limitations of each study can be found in Table 2. Fifty-one genes have been found common for both periodontitis and CVD (Table 1). Proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF and IL-6 were found to be linked with CVD and periodontitis [51]. Increased inflammation is a common symptom among periodontitis patients; the significance of these genes for both diseases indicate inflammatory pathways may be a potential linkage between periodontitis and CVD rather than individual genes [51]. Comparative gene expression analysis across CVD and periodontitis highlights common genes and pathways underscoring shared inflammatory processes. Epidemiological studies find patients diagnosed with periodontitis exhibit higher levels of arterial stiffness, elevated arterial calcification scores, and other symptoms for CVD [77]. Several studies have found a positive association through systemic review as well as census database reports between periodontitis and CHD and AF [78].

VAMP genes, such as VAMP3 [62] and VAMP8 [30], were identified as shared risk loci for periodontitis and CVD. The VAMP genes code for components of a protein complex responsible for facilitating the docking and fusion of synaptic vesicles with the presynaptic membrane. CAD and periodontitis were known to share susceptibility loci, ANRIL and CAMTA1/VAMP3, but it was further revealed that those variants are both regulated by TGF-β regulation, strengthening the notion that TGF-β is part of the shared pathogenesis of CAD and periodontal pathologies [29]. The TGF-β signaling pathway has a critical function in cell differentiation, development, proliferation, and cell-specific or tissue-specific motility [79]. Many of the genes associated with CAD and periodontitis, such as ANRIL, ADIPOR1, and VAMP3 alter transcription levels in response to TGF-β signaling, meaning they are associated by their involvement in the regulation of that pathway [29]. Based on the level of TGF-β, there may be a greater affinity for one to develop both CAD and periodontitis symptoms [29]. ANRIL was further identified as a major risk gene since several variants within the coding region of ANRIL are risk factors shared between both diseases [29]. Most of the existing research suggests that ANRIL is the locus responsible for the link between atherosclerosis and periodontitis [80].

In addition to ANRIL, another significant gene found to be a shared genetic susceptibility factor of CAD and aggressive periodontitis was PLG, which encodes the plasmin protein [29]. PLG is involved in tissue breakdown and cell migration and is targeted by certain pathogenic bacteria such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, a bacterium of the mouth. Bacteria were discovered to bind human PLG to bacterial PLG receptors transforming them into proteolytic organisms; this can result in uncontrolled plasmin activity that is linked to both periodontal disease and CAD [29]. Interestingly, P. gingivalis can even be detected in thrombi at the sites of an acute myocardial infarction. The genetic variant rs4252120 within PLG showed a significant association with aggressive periodontitis which was found consistently across different samples [29]. Adipokine PLG activator inhibitor-1, an inhibitor of the PLG-plasmin system, is identified to be strongly upregulated in visceral adipose depots in obesity, suggesting a link between the PLG-plasmin system and obesity [29]. Overall, ANRIL or CDKN2B-AS1, PLG, VAMP genes such as VAMP3 and VAMP8, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-2, IL-8, CRP, MMP7, MMP1, MMP11, NR3C1, SLC52A2, TLR4, and TRPV1 were found to be the most noteworthy as these genes were discussed more extensively within multiple studies (Table 1). Inflammation continues to be a point of focus in establishing the relationship between periodontal disease and CVD and conducting further investigation of these genes can be utilized to explore potential links between CVD and periodontitis.

Establishing a definitive causal relationship between PD and CVD has yet to be done [77]. Many studies have hinted at possible mechanisms. One possible mechanism is the dysregulation of the immune system leading to unregulated acute responses becoming chronic and systemic responses [77]. Another possible mechanism is PD-causing bacteria-producing bacteremia that can aggravate coronary vessels leading to plaque deposits [77]. In this paper, the focus is on the genetic predisposition leading to these diseases, and thus, the question of heritability of CVD secondary to PD focused primarily on dysregulation of the immune system. Various genes associated with both PD and CVD are presented. Many of these genes are associated with the immune system, including, but not limited to, TNF, IL-6, and C-reactive protein. In this article, possible mechanisms of actions connecting PD and CVD are highlighted, and later delve deeper into certain heritable inflammatory-associated pathways linking the two. For example, increased inflammation due to periodontitis leading to oxidative stress and decreased antioxidant defenses can produce reactive oxygen species and a resultant systemic nitrosative stress, which is a risk factor for CVD [39]. Another example is increased systemic C-reactive protein levels in PD patients, leading to complement system activation in coronary arteries and resultant plaque destabilization causing ischemic heart disease [52, 53]. These examples highlight the various possible mechanisms that warrant investigation into understanding the exact pathways that lead to CVD from PD. Once the basis of the connection is made, environmental influences leading to epigenetic modifications should be investigated to understand genetic influences of common risk factors, such as smoking and obesity. Other shared risk factors were included, such as age, smoking, diabetes, obesity, and poor socioeconomic conditions [31–33, 35, 37]. With a variety of common risk factors, it can point to a likely common pathology shared between PD and CVD, such as metabolic and inflammatory pathways. With current technology, GWAS can utilize SNPS associated with these risk factors to approximate lifetime exposure risk [37]. As such, addressing modifiable risk factors can reveal common pathways and mechanisms that connects periodontitis and CVD. These can then be used to further investigate appropriate drugs and treatment protocols to combat CVD and PD as well as identify appropriate measures to prevent them in high-risk individuals.

Most of the literature for periodontitis were systemic reviews rather than analysis of SNPs or gene expression analysis. Overlapping DEGs provided evidence to support the idea of an association between both diseases. Further research of shared genetic pathways is needed to identify long-term effects and possible links of periodontitis to CVDs. It is essential to address these risk factors; any future studies seeking to investigate the relationship between periodontitis and CVD would benefit from analyzing larger, more diverse datasets to improve the generalizability of the findings. Previous studies have also discussed the benefits of AI/ML approaches when investigating complex, heritable diseases. Utilizing cutting-edge AI/ML technology to analyze and interpret genomic data, there can be a deeper understanding behind the mechanisms shared between PD and CV [81–83]. Novel ML algorithms and pipelines, such as IntelliGenes, can measure the importance of individual biomarkers and improve disease prediction [84]. This opens the possibility for earlier diagnosis and more personalized, targeted treatments [84–86].

Conclusion

Investigating the linkage between periodontitis and CVDs enables improved insight into the pathogenesis of both diseases and could increase the efficiency of their diagnosis and treatment. Advancements in the field of bioinformatics, such as publicly accessible multi-omics datasets and MR, enrichment, differential expression, and gene-regulatory analyses, have helped elucidate the connection between conditions. These findings highlight the interaction between oral and systemic health, showing that the mouth’s microbiota can stimulate pathways that affect inflammation and oxidative stress in distal tissues of the body, specifically those in the heart. While current evidence does not conclude a direct causal relationship between the two diseases, identical mechanisms stemming from dysregulation of inflammatory processes have been uncovered. Further experimental and computational studies are necessary to determine if the significant genes detailed in these findings can provide conclusive evidence of a direct association between the periodontitis and CVDs. The gene expression and variant datasets necessary to continue this investigation require the usage of AI/ML techniques and data engineering to interpret the wealth of patient-specific information available [87]. The proper utilization of these methodologies could result in genetics-based risk assessment models based for CVD based on oral health and preventative tailored treatments.
