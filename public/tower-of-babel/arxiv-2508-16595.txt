Empirical Analysis of the Model-Free Valuation Approach: Hedging Gaps, Conservatism, and Trading Opportunities
Zixing Chen 1 , Yihan Qi 1 , Shanlan Que 1 , Julian Sester 1 , Xiao Zhang 1

Abstract
. In this paper we study the quality of model-free valuation approaches for financial derivatives by systematically evaluating the difference between model-free super-hedging strategies and the realized payoff of financial derivatives using historical option prices from several constituents of the S & \& P 500 500 between 2018 2018 and 2022 2022 . Our study allows in particular to describe the realized gap between payoff and model-free hedging strategy empirically so that we can quantify to which degree model-free approaches are overly conservative. Our results imply that the model-free hedging approach is only marginally more conservative than industry-standard models such as the Heston-model while being model-free at the same time. This finding, its statistical description and the model-independence of the hedging approach enable us to construct an explicit trading strategy which, as we demonstrate, can be profitably applied in financial markets, and additionally possesses the desirable feature with an explicit control of its downside risk due to its model-free construction preventing losses pathwise. Keywords: Model-free price bounds, Hedging, Linear programming, Profitable Trading

Introduction

Building on the foundational works
Beiglböck et al. 2013
;
Beiglböck and
Juillet 2016
;
Henry-Labordère et al. 2016
, which establish the connection between solutions of the martingale optimal transport problem, model-free price bounds for financial derivatives and associated hedging strategies, the literature on model-free approaches and their application to derivative valuation has grown rapidly and still continues to grow. See, for instance,
Ansari et al. 2024
;
Cheridito
et al. 2017
;
Chiu and
Cont 2023
;
Davis 2016
;
Dolinsky and
Soner 2014
;
de Gennaro Aquino and Bernard 2020
;
Fahim and
Huang 2016
;
Hobson and
Neuberger 2012
;
Hobson and
Klimmek 2015
;
Eckstein
et al. 2021
;
Lux and
Papapantoleon 2017
, among many others.
Using only available market data of liquidly traded put- and call options, the aforementioned model-free approaches compute price bounds for less liquidly traded (so called exotic) options solely under the paradigm of no arbitrage and of consistency with observed liquid option prices but, importantly, without using any assumptions on the stochastic dynamics of the underlying assets. The resultant price bounds are then calculated as extreme prices among all calibrated arbitrage-free models, and therefore can be understood to be model-free and to respect the so called
Knightian uncertainty
(
Knight 1921
), i.e., the unquantifiable risk of having chosen a wrong model.

While appealing from a risk management perspective, not imposing model assumptions theoretically comes at the application-prohibitive cost of resultant model-free price bounds by construction being larger than the price in any calibrated model that is also consistent with liquid option prices and arbitrage-free, making the approach less appealing from a practitioner’s perspective, see also the discussions in
Eckstein and
Kupper 2021
;
Lütkebohmert
and Sester 2019
;
Dolinsky and
Neufeld 2018
.

To the best of our knowledge there exists however little to no research providing empirical evidence for the actual conservativeness of model-free approaches, i.e., research answering the question how much the corresponding prices bounds are too wide in real financial markets, and whether they indeed prevent applicability.
According to the duality theory developed in
Beiglböck et al. 2013
;
Beiglböck and
Juillet 2016
;
Henry-Labordère et al. 2016
, to study the quality of model-free upper price bounds, one can equivalently study prices of model-free super-replication strategies (or sub-replication strategies for lower price bounds). Namely, if the gap between the realized payoff of a price bound-attaining model-free super-replication strategy and the realized derivative payoff is (too) large, this indicates that the strategy’s price and hence its initial cash endowment was too large as well and could be reduced to obtain a less conservative strategy with payoff closer to the derivative’s actual payoff.

Relying on the above outlined idea, the present paper aims at closing an existing research gap by studying systematically the difference between model-free hedging strategies (corresponding to model-free price bounds) and realized payoffs of exotic derivatives. For our empirical study, we use historical option prices written on seven of the largest assets by market capitalization of the S
\&
P
500
between
2018
and
2022
. This paper is the first to empirically quantify the realized hedging performance and conservatism of model-free super-hedging strategies using real market data. Our findings challenge the prevailing common belief that model-free methods are impractically conservative, at least if the involved assets and sufficiently many call- and put options are liquidly traded in the market, as it is the case for our study.

Our analysis shows that model-free hedging strategies lead to a hedging performance lacking not much behind the ones of industry-standard models such as the Black-Scholes-, Heston- and Dupire’s local volatility model. As, in contrast to the aforementioned industry-standard models, model-free super hedging strategies do not rely on a specific probabilistic model of the underlying security, they always lead to larger payoffs than the derivative given any realization of the security. We use this robustness property to propose a trading strategy that makes use of both our statistical description of the payoff gap and the model-free construction of the hedging strategy. Trading for this strategy is triggered whenever observed market quotes for the derivative are close enough to its model-free price bounds where the degree of proximity determines the probability of a positive payoff which can be exactly described according to our statistical analysis. The strategy is robust in the sense that up to the initial investment no additional money can be lost due to its model-free construction, consequently leading to a low-risk trading strategy with an easy-to-understand risk profile.

3.
Methodology

Our methodology consists mainly of three steps. In the first step we pre-process the data, second we compute model-free price bounds and corresponding hedging strategies via linear programming, and third we compute the gap between the realized payoff of an exotic financial derivative and the payoff of the previously computed hedging strategy given outcomes of the underlying assets described in Section
2.1
. In the following we introduce the mathematical setup in Section
3.1
and detail all the computational steps in Section
3.2
and
3.3
.

3.1.
Model-free valuation of financial derivatives

We consider some financial derivative providing a payoff
\Phi(S_{t_{1}},\dots,S_{t_{n}})
at some future time
t_{n}>0
with
S_{t}
denoting the value of the underlying asset at time
t
.
To determine a range of model-free prices for
\Phi
, we compute the minimal super-replication price of
\Phi
when investing in so called semi-static trading strategies (see, e.g.
Davis and
Hobson 2007
for more details on semi-static trading), i.e., trading strategies investing once in liquidly traded European call options and adjusting dynamically its investment in the underlying security. These strategies possess a payoff at time
t_{n}
of the form

(3.1)

\displaystyle u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}:\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(S_{t_{1}},\dots,S_{t_{n}})

\displaystyle\mapsto e^{r_{(t_{n}-t_{0})}\cdot(t_{n}-t_{0})}d

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{i}}e^{r_{(t_{n}-t_{i})}\cdot(t_{n}-t_{i})}(\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,+}-\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,-})(S_{t_{i}}-K_{i,j}^{\rm call})^{+}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}H_{i}(S_{t_{1}},\dots,S_{t_{i}})(S_{t_{i+1}}-S_{t_{i}}e^{r_{(t_{i+1}-t_{i})}\cdot(t_{i+1}-t_{i})}),

where
r_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the interest rate used for discounting of a time period of length
\tau
,
d\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the cash position,
(\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,+},\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,-})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}
the holdings of the call options,
K_{i,j}^{\rm call}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
the strikes of the call options,
M_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
the number
2
2

2

Note that we do not need to distinguish between the number of strikes considered for the call options since we can simply use the larger of the two and include options multiple times if necessary to match the number of options considered.
of the call options with maturity
t_{i}
,
H_{i}(S_{t_{1}},\dots,S_{t_{i}})\in\mathbb{R}
(while
H_{0}
is a constant) denote the amount of the investment in the underlying security at time
t_{i}
.

Remark 3.1
.

(i)

As our analysis involves infrequent rebalancing of the dynamic positions
(H_{i})_{i}
, the effects of transaction costs for
(H_{i})_{i}
are expected to be rather small, and thus, even though mathematically feasible, we have decided to not include assumptions on transaction costs related to trading in the underlying asset
S
in our trading strategy. How to mathematically model semi-static trading that includes transaction costs is
S
is described, e.g., in
(
Cheridito
et al. 2017
, Section 3.1)
.

(ii)

For the sake of presentation we have decided to not include put options into (
3.1
) which is well justified by the put-call parity (
Stoll 1969
) and since the call options we consider are sufficiently liquid by our data selection process described in Section
3.3.1
.

To compute the price of the strategy with payoff
u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}
, we denote the ask and bid prices of the considered call options by
h_{i,j}^{\rm call,+}
and
h_{i,j}^{\rm call,-}
respectively. Then, to compute the largest model-free price for
\Phi
which does not allow for model-free arbitrage (in line with the arbitrage notion from
(
Acciaio et al. 2016
, Definition 1.2)
), we compute the super-hedging price by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi):=\inf_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle d+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{i}}(\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,+}h_{i,j}^{\rm call,+}-\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,-}h_{i,j}^{\rm call,-})\penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ subject to}\penalty\ u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}(S)\geq\Phi(S)\text{ for all }S\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\Bigg\}

compare also the expositions in the related literature, e.g., in
Acciaio et al. 2016
,
(
Neufeld and
Sester 2021
, Section A.1)
or
(
Neufeld and
Sester 2022
, Section II)
. Correspondingly, a sub-hedging price can be computed via

(3.3)

\underline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi):=-\overline{\operatorname{P}}(-\Phi).

By construction, market prices outside the interval
[\underline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi),\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)]
allow to pocket in a model-free arbitrage profit through investing in the corresponding semi-static sub- or super-hedging strategies with price attaining the bounds of
[\underline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi),\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)]
- independent of the realized outcome of the underlying security.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that if a financial model is calibrated to observed bid-ask prices of call options, then arbitrage-free prices of an exotic derivative
\Phi
can be computed by
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{-r_{t_{n}-t_{0}}(t_{n}-t_{0})}\Phi(S_{t_{1}},\dots,S_{t_{n}})\right]
where
\mathbb{Q}
is a martingale measure that fulfils
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{-r_{t_{i}-t_{i}}(t_{i}-t_{0})}(S_{t_{i}}-K_{i,j}^{\rm call})^{+}\right]\in\left[h_{i,j}^{\rm{call},-},\penalty\ h_{i,j}^{\rm{call},+}\right]
for all
i,j
. If we denote the set of all such probability measures by
\mathcal{Q}
, then
(
Cheridito
et al. 2017
, Section 3.1.3)
or
(
Neufeld and
Sester 2021
, Section A.1)
show that under mild assumptions

\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)=\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{-r_{t_{n}-t_{0}}(t_{n}-t_{0})}\Phi(S_{t_{1}},\dots,S_{t_{n}})\right],

i.e., the smallest super-replication price
\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)
can also be understood as the largest price among all arbitrage-free financial models calibrated to market data.

3.2.
Computation of model-free super-hedges with linear programming

For the computation of model-free super-hedging price
\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)
as defined in (
3.2
) and the associated super-hedging strategy we mainly rely on a numerical routine using linear programming (LP) as outlined in similar settings in
(
Henry-Labordere 2013
, Section 3)
and
Guo and
Obłój 2019
. Since the computation of the sub-hedging strategy follows analogously through using the relation in (
3.3
), we focus with our presentation on the computation of a super-hedging strategy at minimal price

\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)
in the two time-step case
n=2
, i.e.,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi):=\inf_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),H_{0},H_{1}}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle d+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{i}}(\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,+}h_{i,j}^{\rm call,+}-\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,-}h_{i,j}^{\rm call,-})

\displaystyle\text{ subject to}\penalty\ u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{0},H_{1})}(S)\geq\Phi(S)\text{ for all }S\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}\Bigg\}.

The above problem (
3.4
) falls in the class of infinite-dimensional linear programming problems. To solve (
3.4
) numerically, we aim at transforming (
3.4
) to a finite dimensional problem. To this end, we first consider a (sufficiently large) grid

\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{G}^{1},\mathbb{G}^{2})\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2},

where both
\mathbb{G}^{1}
and
\mathbb{G}^{2}
are one-dimensional grids with
N
meshes that discretize the values attained by the distributions of
S_{t_{1}}
and
S_{t_{2}}
, respectively. Given a function
H_{1}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, representing the dynamic investing in the underlying security, as defined in (
3.1
), for each grid point
S_{t_{1}}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{G}^{1}
, we define the value
H_{1}^{\ell}:=H_{1}(S_{t_{1}}^{\ell})
. Finally, to approximate
H_{1}
on the whole reals, we apply
a cubic spline interpolation (see, e.g.,
Ahlberg
et al. 2016
) using
(H_{1}^{\ell})_{\ell=1,\dots,N}
, and obtain a cubic spline function

\rm{spline}\left((H_{1}^{\ell})_{\ell=1,\dots,N}\right):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

which coincides with
H_{1}
on
\mathbb{G}^{1}
and interpolates smoothly the non-grid values. The above outlined procedure allows us to define an approximating finite dimensional linear programming problem
3
3

3

Note that for the formulation in (
3.5
), we do not necessarily need a smooth function approximation as only the grid points on
\mathbb{G}
are considered for the super-replication condition. However, in later sections we will use the corresponding cubic-spline function to represent the learned strategy also applied to values off the grid.

(3.5)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\overline{\operatorname{P}}}(\Phi,\mathbb{G}):=\inf_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),H_{0},(H_{1}^{\ell})_{\ell}}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle d+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{i}}(\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,+}h_{i,j}^{\rm call,+}-\theta_{i,j}^{\rm call,-}h_{i,j}^{\rm call,-})

\displaystyle\text{ subject to }\penalty\ u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{0},\rm{spline}\left((H_{1}^{\ell})_{\ell}\right)}(S)\geq\Phi(S)\text{ for all }S\in\mathbb{G}\Bigg\}.

To find numerical solutions to
\widetilde{\overline{\operatorname{P}}}(\Phi,\mathbb{G})

3.3.
Computation of the gap between the realized payoff and the payoff of the hedging strategy

This section outlines the method employed to compute the difference between the outcomes of minimal model-free super-replication strategies (as well as maximal model-free sub-replication strategies) and the underlying derivative payoff.

3.3.1.
Preprocessing of the data

From the option price data described in Section
2.1
we first select all pairs of option prices with the same underlying stock and fixed length of maturity difference
t_{2}-t_{1}>0
, where
t_{1},t_{2}
are two futures times relevant for the option payoff. To obtain sufficiently reliable prices, for each fixed maturity difference, we select the
20
most liquid call options (and associated strikes) by choosing those with the highest trading volume. Then, to avoid arbitrage among the considered options (in particular butterfly arbitrage, see
Gatheral and
Jacquier 2014
), we apply the arbitrage repair method introduced in
Cohen
et al. 2020
to the mid-prices. Using the
repaired
mid price
C^{*}
and original spread
\delta=C_{\rm ask}-C_{\rm bid}
, we obtain eventually repaired bid and ask prices
C_{\rm bid}^{*}
and
C_{\rm ask}^{*}
via the relations

\displaystyle C_{\rm ask}^{*}=C^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\delta,\qquad C_{\rm bid}^{*}=C^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\delta.

Further details of the preprocessing procedure are described in the accompanying github repository which can be found under
https://github.com/QIYIHAN/Empirical-Price-Bounds
.

3.3.2.
Computing interest rates

To model the interest rate
(r_{t})_{t}
we use throughout our study a Nelson–Siegel model (
Nelson and
Siegel 1987
) fitted to US treasury yields with the historical parameters that can be found under
https://www.federalreserve.gov/data/nominal-yield-curve.htm
.

3.3.3.
Computation of the gap

Upon computing
\widetilde{\overline{\operatorname{P}}}(\Phi,\mathbb{G})
defined in (
3.5
), by using Algorithm
1
, we obtain the optimal values of the parameters
d
,
(\theta_{i,j})_{i=1,2,\atop j=1,\dots,M_{1}}
,
H_{0}
and
(H_{1}^{\ell})_{\ell=1,\dots,N}
, and interpolate
(H_{1}^{\ell})_{\ell=1,\dots,N}
by a cubic spline interpolator
\widetilde{H}_{1}:=\rm{spline}\left((H_{1}^{\ell})_{\ell=1,\dots,N}\right)
. Denoting the realized prices of the underlying security at times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, respectively by
S\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,we compute the raw value of the gap between realized hedging strategy and realized derivative payoff by

u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{0},\widetilde{H}_{1})}(S)-\Phi(S).

Eventually, to make the gap values comparable across different underlying securities, we normalize the raw gap by the price of the underlying security at the initial time
t_{0}
denoted by
S_{0}
, i.e., we define the
normalized
hedging gap (or simply gap in the following) by

(3.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}:=\frac{u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{0},\widetilde{H}_{1})}(S)-\Phi(S)}{S_{0}}.

), prices of hedges correspond to arbitrage-free prices of the corresponding derivative, the prices produced by model-free pricing approaches turn out to be competitive and rather close to prices produced by industry standard models (and thus traded prices of OTC derivatives) - an observation that we will use in the next section to explicitly construct a profitable trading strategy.

5.
A trading strategy based on model-free price bounds

The analysis from Section
4
allows to derive a trading strategy with an easy-to-understand risk control regarding its downside risk. The idea behind the strategy is to exploit discrepancies between market prices and model-free super- or subhedging price bounds: the study from Section
4.3
shows that price bounds of model-free approaches are relatively close to model-based prices of industry standard models; if now indeed the observed price of a derivative lies
sufficiently close
to these bounds, a model-free hedge is constructed whose payoff dominates the derivative, thereby allowing the investor to lock in a non-negative payoff exceeding the limited initial investment with a high probability. By tuning a threshold parameter (that we later call
c
), the investor can control the trade-off between risk and expected return.

5.1.
Description of the strategy

To construct the strategy, let
\overline{u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}}
denote a hedging strategy of the form described in (
3.1
) attaining the upper price bound
\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)
4
4

4

We can always find such a strategy up to an arbitrarily small tolerance
\varepsilon>0
which we ignore here for our exposition.
. Then, we have by construction of the model-free super-hedge that

\overline{u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}}(S)-\Phi(S)\geq 0
for all
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, for all levels of relative hedging gaps
c\in\mathbb{R}
there exists an associated probability
\overline{q}(c)\in[0,1]
such that the empirical probability of a realized relative gap larger than
c
is
\overline{q}(c)
, i.e.,

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\overline{u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}}(S)-\Phi(S)}{S_{0}}\geq c\right)=\overline{q}(c),

where
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
denotes the empirical distribution derived from historical stock data
(S^{(i)})_{i=1,\dots,N}=(S_{t_{1}}^{(i)},S_{t_{2}}^{(i)})_{i=1,\dots,N}
of the respective stock, i.e.,

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(S_{t_{1}}^{(i)},S_{t_{2}}^{(i)})},

for
\delta_{x}
denoting the Dirac measure at point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and where
N
denotes the number of historical stock prices used for the construction of
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
.
In the same manner, we denote by
\underline{u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}}
a strategy attaining the lower price bound
\underline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)
. Then, for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
there exists some
\underline{q}(c)\in[0,1]
such that

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\Phi(S)-\underline{u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}}(S)}{S_{0}}\geq c\right)=\underline{q}(c).

If now an observed market price
\mathfrak{M}(\Phi)
for the derivative
\Phi
is close to either
\overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)
or
\underline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)
, i.e., given some
c>0
, we have

(5.1)

\min\{\mathfrak{M}(\Phi)-\underline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi),\penalty\ \penalty\ \overline{\operatorname{P}}(\Phi)-\mathfrak{M}(\Phi)\}\leq c\cdot S_{0},

then we may invest in
\overline{u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}}(S)-\Phi(S)
, or
\Phi(S)-\underline{u_{d,(\theta_{i,j}),(H_{i})}}(S)
(we choose whatever is the binding condition in (
5.1
)). For such an investment we pay less than
c\cdot S_{0}
to subsequently hold a strategy which leads under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
with a probability of either
\overline{q}(\tfrac{\overline{\rm{P}}(\Phi)-{\mathfrak{M}}(\Phi)}{S_{0}})\geq\overline{q}(c)
or
\underline{q}(\tfrac{{\mathfrak{M}}(\Phi)-\underline{\rm{P}}(\Phi)}{S_{0}})\geq\underline{q}(c)
to a positive profit. Moreover, and this makes the investment extremely attractive and fundamentally different from other
statistical arbitrage
related concepts, the maximal loss is the initial investment
c\cdot S_{0}
as the held strategy upon initialization, by its construction as a model-free hedge, never leads to negative payoffs. This means an investor can fix the level
c
which imposes an upper bound on potential future losses. We illustrate the idea in Example
5.1
and Figure
7
.

Example 5.1
.

Consider a forward-start call option on Tesla with payoff

\Phi(S_{t_{1}},S_{t_{2}})=(S_{t_{2}}-S_{t_{1}})^{+},

where
S_{0}=800

t_{0}

, are given by

\mathfrak{M}(\Phi)=42.5

\min\{\mathfrak{M}(\Phi)-\underline{\rm P}(\Phi),\overline{\rm P}(\Phi)-\mathfrak{M}(\Phi)\}=1.5\leq c\cdot S_{0},

for a threshold
c=0.02
. We thus invest
\overline{C}=\overline{\rm P}(\Phi)-\mathfrak{M}(\Phi)=1.5

\Phi(S_{t_{1}},S_{t_{2}})=(870-810)^{+}=60.

6.
Conclusion

In our study we have investigated the quality of model-independent super-hedging (and sub-hedging) strategies using data of liquidly traded options of constituents of the S&P 500 index.
Our study reveals that model-free pricing and hedging approaches lead, compared with model-based hedges based on Black–Scholes, local volatility or Heston models, (see Section
4.3
) to competitive hedging results even though, by construction as worst-case approaches they are known to be conservative.

Our quantification of the distribution of hedging gaps of model-free hedging strategies based on real data of the S&P 500 index across moneyness and different maturities shows that these model-free hedges often lead to payoffs closely aligned with actual realized payoffs of the underlying derivatives. Building on these findings, we present in Section
5
a novel highly profitable low risk trading strategy that exploits the empirical tightness of model-free bounds by trading in the model-free hedging whenever the market price is close enough to the theoretical price.

Our findings indicate that model-free methods, traditionally viewed as overly conservative, can in fact yield efficient and statistically robust trading strategies given the underlying option data is sufficiently liquid and hence informative enough. This finding opens door to broader practical adaption of robust approaches in the finance industry.

Appendix D
Benchmark Models

In this section we briefly describe the benchmark models used in Section
4.3
and the method to compute prices of forward start option and, based on this, its delta sensitivities. For all of the models, we approximate the option delta as
\Delta(S_{t},t):=\frac{{\rm P}\left(S_{t}\cdot(1+\alpha),t\right))-{\rm P}(S_{t},t)}{\alpha S_{t}}
on a daily basis, where we use
\alpha=0.001
, and where
{\rm P}(S_{t},t)
denotes the option price in the respective model at time
t
when the underlying security attains value
S_{t}
.

Black–Scholes model

The Black–Scholes model (
Black and
Scholes 1973
) models the evolution of the asset value
S_{t}
by the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}S_{t}

\displaystyle=rS_{t}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}t+\sigma S_{t}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}W_{t}

for some
r\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma>0
and some standard Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. We use the same interest rate
r
as in the model-free considerations, described in Section
3.3.2
as well as the Black–Scholes implied volatility
\sigma^{\rm BS}
, i.e., the volatility
\sigma^{\rm BS}
that makes the call option market prices match the model-based call option prices. For the Black–Scholes model, the option price
P(S,t)
is calculated as the analytical price of the forward-start option price formula with
k=1
in
(
Musiela and
Rutkowski 1997
, p. 231)
.

Heston model

As a second benchmark model we consider the stochastic volatility model introduced by
Heston 1993
where the stock price
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in the continuous-time
Heston model
is given by the stochastic differential equations

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}S_{t}

\displaystyle=rS_{t}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}t+\sqrt{\nu_{t}}S_{t}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}W_{t}^{1},

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\nu_{t}

\displaystyle=\kappa(\theta-\nu_{t}){\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}t+\sigma\sqrt{v_{t}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}W_{t}^{2},

for
r
being the interest rate,
\kappa
the volatility mean-reversion speed,
\theta
the volatility long term mean,
\sigma
the diffusion parameter associated to the volatility, and where
W_{t}^{1}
and
W_{t}^{2}
are two correlated Brownian motions. The parameters of the Heston-model are calibrated to the considered call option prices according to the methodology described in
Levendorskiĭ 2012
. The price of the forward start option (and based on this its sensitivities) can be computed as described in
Kruse and
Nögel 2005
.

Dupire’s Local Volatility model

Dupire’s local volatility model (
Dupire
et al. 1994
and
Derman and
Kani 1994
) model the asset price
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
via

\displaystyle{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}S_{t}

\displaystyle=rS_{t}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}t+\sigma(t,S_{t})S_{t}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}W_{t}

for interest rate
r
and Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as well as

(D.1)

\sigma^{2}(T,K)=2\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial T}c(T,K)+Kr\frac{\partial}{\partial K}c(T,K)}{K^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial K^{2}}c(T,K)}

with
K
and
T
being strike and maturity, respectively of the considered call option with price
c(T,K)
. The derivatives in the right-hand-side of (
D.1
) are approximated via finite differences.

The delta sensitivity is then derived from a corresponding Black–Scholes model, considering the option price as a function of stock price
S
with volatility
\sigma
,
P=P(S,\sigma(t,S))
. The option delta can then be calculated analytically as

\frac{dP}{dS}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\bigg|_{\sigma}+\frac{\partial P}{\partial\sigma}\bigg|_{S}\frac{\partial\sigma(T,K,S)}{\partial S}=\Delta_{\text{BS}}(S,\sigma)+\nu_{\text{BS}}(S,\sigma)\frac{\partial\sigma(T,K)}{\partial S}

for
\nu_{\text{BS}}
and
\nu_{\text{BS}}
denoting Delta and Vega, respectively in the Black–Scholes model.
