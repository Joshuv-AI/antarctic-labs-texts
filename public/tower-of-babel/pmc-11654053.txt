An intracranial odyssey: pediatric ganglioneuroma arising from the trigeminal ganglion: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Kaveh Ebrahimzadeh, Mohammad Mirahmadi Eraghi, Farahnaz Bidari Zerehpoosh, Hesameddin Hoseini Tavasol, Mahkameh Abbaszdeh, Adam A. Dmytriw, Fatemeh Jahanshahi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGanglioneuroma represents an uncommon benign tumor arising from the sympathetic nerves, and its development from the fifth nerve is an infrequent entity. Few ganglioneuromas arising from the fifth nerve have been discussed in literature. The authors describe the second pediatric ganglioneuroma arising from the fifth nerve.Case presentationA 7 year-old Asian male suffering from left-sided facial paresthesia, accompanied by several episodes of complex seizures over the past 2 years, presented to our clinic. The patient also complained of sudden unconsciousness and concurrent upward gaze lasting less than a minute. A brain computed tomography scan and magnetic resonance imaging revealed an isodense lesion containing focal calcifications measuring 31 × 28 mm and a solid mass extending from the pontine surface to Meckel’s cave, probably originating from the fifth nerve or its root entry zone. The lesion was mildly hypointense on T1-weighted sequences and hyperintense on T2-weighted and fluid attenuated inversion recovery sequences. Gadolinium injection revealed only minimal heterogeneous enhancement. Histopathologic and immunohistochemical findings were consistent with ganglioneuroma. A lateral approach via retrosigmoid incision and suboccipital lateral craniectomy toward the cerebellopontine angle was performed under neuromonitoring supervision, and the postoperative period was uneventful.ConclusionAlthough ganglioneuroma arising from the fifth nerve is an extremely rare entity, it should be considered when diagnosing lesions in Meckel’s cave or the cerebellopontine angle cistern.

Introduction

Ganglioneuromas represent the most noninvasive, mature form of neuroblastic tumors. Such tumors are usually associated with autonomic ganglia and manifest before the second decade of life [1]. They typically derive from neural crest cells and may originate from existing sympathetic tissue [2], including the posterior mediastinum, neck, adrenal gland, pelvis, and retroperitoneum. The most frequent locations for such entities are the retroperitoneum and posterior mediastinum. However, they may infrequently be found in the intracranial region, with sporadic reports originating from the trigeminal nerve [1, 2]. A clinical diagnosis that distinguishes such tumors from mimicking ones remains challenging owing to their rarity [3, 4]. Few reports of trigeminal ganglioneuroma are currently available in literature, most of which come from East Asia [1]. The authors describe the second case of a left-sided trigeminal ganglioneuroma extending to the cerebellopontine angle (CPA) in a pediatric patient.

Case presentation

The present patient is a 7 year-old Asian male complaining of paresthesia on the left side of his face. He has experienced episodes of complex seizures over the past 2 years, presenting as sudden unconsciousness and concurrent upward gaze lasting for less than a minute. He had been treated with topiramate and sodium valproate, which resulted in an appropriate treatment response. The patient reported no significant medical, family, or psychosocial history. Neurological examinations were also unremarkable. Owing to the new-onset paresthesia, the patient underwent a brain computed tomography (CT) scan, revealing an isodense lesion containing focal calcifications measuring 31 × 28 mm (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed, and the lesion appeared as a solid mass extending from the pontine surface to Meckel’s cave, probably originating from the trigeminal nerve or its root entry zone. It was mildly hypointense in the T1 sequence and hyperintense in the T2 and fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences. Gadolinium injection revealed only minimal heterogeneous enhancement Fig. 1. Despite the compressive effect on the brainstem, there was no evidence of hydrocephalus. Owing to the evidence of brainstem and fourth ventricle compression, surgical intervention was performed.

Under neuromonitoring supervision, we approached the patient in the lateral position via a retrosigmoid incision and suboccipital lateral craniectomy toward the CPA. Initially, the tumor appeared as a well-circumscribed encapsulated mass with an elastic consistency. It had compressed the trigeminal nerve medially. Despite a downward compression effect, no adhesion or invasion was seen in the VII–VIII cranial nerve complex. We initiated sharp debulking of the lesion. The intraoperative frozen section specimen was reported to favor schwannoma. The lesion appeared to originate from the trigeminal nerve (Fig. 1). After defining the plane between the brainstem and the tumor, the lesion was carefully dissected from the trigeminal nerve exit zone to Meckel’s cave, and gross total resection was performed.

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for a day and then to the ward for 4 days. No new neurological deficits were observed. He was discharged on postoperative day 5. Additionally, his facial paresthesia completely resolved during a 3 month follow-up.

Histopathologic examination revealed haphazardly arranged nerve bundles of bland-looking Schwann cells in a hypocellular background. Clusters of mature ganglion cells with mild pleomorphism, prominent nucleoli, and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm containing Nissl bodies were noted. Immunohistochemical analysis showed positive staining for S100, synaptophysin, and SOX10. IDH1, Olig2, EMA, CD34, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) were negative in tumor cells, and Ki67 was about 1%. Discussion

Ganglioneuroma, neuroblastoma, and ganglioneuroblastoma are categorized as neuroblastic lesions originating from the peripheral nervous system’s neural crest cells. Unlike ganglioneuroblastoma or neuroblastoma, which have more invasive behavior and typically affect younger people, ganglioneuroma represents a well-defined, benign, slow-growing lesion that clinically remains asymptomatic until it reaches a large enough size to compress nearby structures intracranially, resulting in symptoms owing to compression of adjacent structures. It is routinely found to occur in older pediatric patients and young adults, with approximately 80% of cases seen in patients not older than 30 years and no meaningful sex predisposition [3, 6–8]. The posterior mediastinum and retroperitoneum are two well-known regions for ganglioneuroma. However, it has been rarely reported in the cranial nerve ganglia, cerebellum, orbit, sella turcica, cranial vault, parapharyngeal tissue, spinal cord, mandible, tongue, gastrointestinal tract, bladder, uterus, visceral ganglia, ovary, testes, spermatic cord, prostate, bone, and skin [2–4, 7–18]. Similar intracranial regions, such as the internal auditory canal and middle ear, have also been infrequently described [14, 19–21]. The intracranial presentation of such lesions, however, remains extremely rare.

The underlying etiology of ganglioneuroma is not yet well understood. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the origin of such tumors. They might arise from a hamartomatous malformation involving ectopic sympathetic nervous tissue; the tumor may originate from neuroblastoma differentiation or may exist as multipotential precursor cells throughout the period of embryological development. From a familial predisposition perspective, no definitive evidence is available; however, some reports suggest an association with multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2B and neurofibromatosis type 1. Females with Turner syndrome are highly prone to developing neural crest-derived tumors and may have a higher likelihood of developing ganglioneuromas [3, 22, 23]. Hayes and collaborators shed more light on this, suggesting that ganglioneuroma may develop spontaneously or present secondary to chemotherapy or radiation therapy for neuroblastomas [24].

Imaging

Although the definitive diagnosis of ganglioneuroma primarily relies on histopathologic evaluations, MRI and CT scans provide information regarding the size, location, components of the lesion, and its relationship to nearby structures, which is critical in determining a surgical plan. CT scans often reveal a well-differentiated, low-density mass with punctate calcification in about 20%–42% of cases [8]. Ichikawa and collaborators [25] pointed out that the morphology of the calcifications could provide a remarkable characteristic factor to distinguish benign tumors from malignancies. While extensive patchy or irregular calcification indicates malignancy, grain-like or scattered punctate calcification implies benign lesions [8, 25]. MRI is superior to CT in demonstrating blood vessels surrounding and compressing the lesion. However, conventional MRI alone might fail to differentiate trigeminal ganglioneuroma from other extra-axial or tumor-like masses since it does not provide detailed insights regarding the actual tumor extent, cellularity, tumor type, and grading. By contrast, advanced MRI sheds more light on the histological characteristics and tumor physiological and metabolic features, including angiogenesis classification, cellularity, and mitotic indices [4].

Histopathology and immunohistochemically

Ganglioneuroma comprises single or clustered mature Schwannian stroma and giant ganglion cells. It typically lacks components of intermediate cells, neuroblasts, or mitotic figures, which would imply malignant differentiation [8]. Ganglioneuroma is classified into maturing and mature subtypes on the basis of the international neuroblastoma pathology classification of neuroblastic tumors [8]. A typical ganglioneuroma consists of Schwannian stroma and mature ganglion cells. In contrast, tumors with fully matured ganglion cells are uncommon (nearly 7%) [26]. It is characterized by reactivity for vimentin, S-100, synaptophysin, and other neuronal markers. Additionally, Ki-67 serves as an immunomarker for cellular proliferation [27].

Management

Considering that such tumors exhibit well-circumscribed benign patterns, gross total resection is regarded as curative, and radiation and chemotherapy are not helpful in treatment. Subtotal resection is considered sufficient in cases where the tumor is in close proximity to vital structures. Routine postoperative follow-up is highly recommended, as slow progression of the partially resected lesion is possible, and malignant transformation of ganglioneuroma has been rarely reported [28–30].

Conclusion

Despite the rarity of pediatric ganglioneuromas arising from the trigeminal nerve, their clinical presentation can mimic other, more common pathologies, warranting thorough imaging and histopathological evaluation. Advanced MRI is valuable for the preoperative differentiation of trigeminal ganglioneuroma from similar tumors and tumor-like masses. When imaging does not yield a definitive diagnosis, trigeminal ganglioneuroma should be considered as a possible explanation.
