Functional characterization of a novel JPH2 variant (p.R160H) associated with dilated cardiomyopathy
Authors: Kailu Jiao, Ke Gong, Yong Luo, RuiLin Liu, Lei Wang, Yao Yao, Li Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a genetic cardiac disorder characterized by ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction. Mutations in calcium-handling genes such as JPH2, which plays a critical role in maintaining cardiac dyad integrity, have been associated with DCM. Although more than 20 JPH2 variants have been reported, most lack functional validation. This study investigates the ClinVar-listed JPH2 p.R160H variant and provides initial cellular evidence related to its pathogenic potential, based on genetic data and stress-response assays.MethodsA 33-year-old male with severe DCM (LVEF 26%) and asymptomatic parents underwent trio-based whole-exome sequencing (WES). Rare variants were filtered (MAF < 0.01) and prioritized using ACMG guidelines. The JPH2 p.R160H variant was cloned and transfected into AC16 cardiomyocytes. Protein expression levels of MYH7, α-SMA, and TGF-β were assessed by Western blotting. Evolutionary conservation of R160 was evaluated using ClustalX.ResultsTrio-based WES identified a de novo JPH2 p.R160H variant absent in both parents and population controls. AC16 cells expressing the mutant construct showed increased levels of myocardial stress markers (MYH7, α-SMA, and TGF-β). JPH2 protein expression was also elevated, indicating possible dysregulation of protein turnover or stability. Evolutionary analysis confirmed that R160 is highly conserved across species. According to ACMG criteria (PS2, PS3, PM2, PP4), the variant was classified as pathogenic.ConclusionThis study provides preliminary cellular evidence supporting the pathogenic potential of the JPH2 p.R160H variant, while the direct effects on calcium handling and dyad integrity remain unknown.The combined genetic and cellular data contribute to more accurate variant classification and support the inclusion of JPH2 in cardiomyopathy genetic screening. Introduction

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a prevalent and clinically significant form of cardiomyopathy, characterized by ventricular enlargement and impaired systolic function, often culminating in heart failure [1]. As a leading contributor to global morbidity and mortality, DCM arises from complex interactions between genetic predisposition and environmental triggers [2]. Genetic studies have identified mutations in genes encoding proteins critical for cardiac structure (MYH7、TTN), contractility (TNNT2、TNNI3), and calcium homeostasis (RYR2、JPH2) as key drivers of DCM pathogenesis. Despite extensive genetic discoveries, the molecular mechanisms linking individual variants to myocardial dysfunction remain incompletely understood. Among these genes, Junctophilin-2 (JPH2) has emerged as a critical structural and regulatory protein that maintains the integrity of the junctional membrane complex and ensures proper excitation–contraction coupling in cardiomyocytes. JPH2 plays a critical role in preserving both the structural stability and functional competence of cardiac muscle cells [3], ensuring precise regulation of calcium-induced calcium release (CICR)—a cornerstone of cardiac excitation–contraction coupling [4]. By anchoring the SR to T-tubules, JPH2 facilitates rapid, synchronized calcium ion release required for efficient myocardial contraction. Approximately 20 heterozygous JPH2 variants have been linked to DCM [5, 6]; however, most reported variants—particularly missense mutations—lack direct experimental validation, thereby limiting mechanistic understanding and clinical interpretation. To address this gap, the present study aims to investigate the JPH2 p.R160H variant identified in a proband with DCM, with a focus on genetic and cellular characterization.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Prior to enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from the individual participating in the research. The DCM was diagnosed according to the 2023 ESC Guidelines [7]. Blood samples were collected from the proband and their parents for genetic analysis to determine the inheritance pattern of the identified JPH2 variant. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

DNA extraction

Peripheral venous blood samples (3–5 mL) were collected from the proband and both parents. From each sample, 600 μL was used to extract genomic DNA (gDNA) using QIAGEN kits following the manufacturer’s protocol. The remaining blood samples were cryopreserved at − 80 °C. All reference genomic sequences were obtained from the NCBI RefSeq database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov).

Whole-exome sequencing

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was conducted by the Berry Genomics Bioinformatics Institute, located in Beijing, China. Exonic regions were captured using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 Kit. The Illumina HiSeq X-10 platform was employed for high-throughput sequencing. Subsequent bioinformatic processing—comprising raw read handling, sequence mapping, variant calling, filtration, and functional annotation—was performed by the bioinformatics team at Berry Genomics.

Bioinformatics analysis of whole-exome sequencing data

Processed sequencing reads were mapped to the NCBI reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA v0.7.17). Annotation of genomic variants was conducted on VCF files with the ANNOVAR software tool. Detected variants were categorized into five tiers—pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance, likely benign, and benign—based on the standards established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). Filtration of variants involved a four-step procedure: (1) removal of variants exhibiting a minor allele frequency (MAF) exceeding 0.01 across major population databases, including the 1000 Genomes Project, the Exome Sequencing Project (ESP), the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v2.1.1), and a local in-house Novo database; (2) Retention of exonic and splice-site (± 10 bp) variants; (3) Pathogenicity prediction using computational tools including MutationTaster (disease causality), PolyPhen-2 (protein damage), SIFT (amino acid conservation), CADD (deleteriousness scoring), and LoFtool (loss-of-function tolerance); (4) Prioritization of homozygous variants through screening against a custom dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) gene panel encompassing 120 known disease-associated genes (Supplementary Table S1). Putative pathogenic variants were experimentally validated via Sanger sequencing (ABI 3730xl platform) and structurally contextualized using the SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de) to assess domain localization.

Variant validation

To validate the candidate variant identified through filtering, Sanger sequencing was performed. Primer sets were designed utilizing the web-based design tools available from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT; https://sg.idtdna.com/pages). The amplified PCR products were subsequently subjected to sequencing analysis conducted by Tsingke Biological Technology based in Beijing, China [8].

Cell culture

AC16 cardiomyocyte cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12; Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 ×; Solarbio). Cells were incubated at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. The AC16 human cardiomyocyte line was selected for its stable proliferation, reproducible transfection efficiency, and suitability for initial mechanistic screening of cardiac stress pathways. While induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs) provide a more physiologically relevant model for calcium handling and contractility studies, their generation and maintenance are technically demanding and resource-intensive. AC16 cells were selected due to their stable proliferation, high transfection efficiency, and suitability for initial screening of cellular stress responses. Importantly, we acknowledge that AC16 cells are proliferative and lack organized T-tubules and mature dyadic structures, meaning they do not fully recapitulate the physiological context in which JPH2 regulates excitation–contraction coupling. Therefore, AC16 cells serve as a feasible first-step model, whereas future mechanistic studies—particularly those assessing calcium transients and dyad integrity—will require iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes.

Cell transfection

Expression plasmids were constructed using the pcDNA3.1(+) vector under the control of a CMV promoter. A Kozak sequence (GCCACC) was inserted upstream of the start codon (ATG) of the JPH2 gene to enhance translation efficiency. A 6 × His epitope tag was fused to the C-terminus of the target gene to facilitate detection. The plasmid also carried a GFP expression cassette designed for non-fusion expression, allowing independent expression of GFP and the target gene. Wild-type (WT) and R160H-mutant (MU) JPH2 constructs were generated and sequence-verified.

AC16 cells were seeded in 60-mm dishes and transfected with 5.5 μg of plasmid DNA (WT, MU, and empty vector control) using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfection efficiency was evaluated based on GFP fluorescence, and only samples with GFP-positive cells exceeding 70% were used for subsequent experiments. The expression of JPH2 was confirmed by Western blotting using an anti-His antibody to detect the His-tagged JPH2 protein. Densitometric quantification was performed using ImageJ software, and protein levels were normalized to GAPDH.

Western blot

For protein analysis, harvested cells were resuspended and lysed on ice using RIPA buffer. Protein samples (20 µg per lane) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto 0.22 µm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies included His-tag (Proteintech, Cat#66,005, 1:1000), GAPDH (Proteintech, Cat#60,004, 1:10,000), α-SMA (Proteintech, Cat#80,008–1-RR, 1:1000), MYH7 (Proteintech, Cat#22,280–1-AP, 1:1000), and TGF-β (Proteintech, Cat#26,155–1-AP, 1:1000). After washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system [8].

Multiple sequence alignment

Protein sequences of JPH2 orthologs across multiple species were retrieved from the Ensembl database (https://asia.ensembl.org). Multiple sequence alignment was carried out using ClustalX software to evaluate evolutionary conservation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA). Quantitative data, including relative protein expression levels of HIS-JPH2, α-SMA, MYH7, and TGF-β (Fig. 2D), are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times (n = 3 independent biological replicates, performed on different days using separate cell passages).Comparisons between the wild-type (WT) and mutant (MU) groups were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test after confirming normal distribution and homogeneity of variance. Because only pairwise comparisons were conducted, no multiple-testing correction was required. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Significance levels are denoted as follows: **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Clinical data

A 33-year-old male proband presented to our cardiovascular center with symptoms of exertional dyspnea that resolved with rest, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea during sleep, and difficulty lying flat for rest. Echocardiography revealed global cardiomegaly, generalized hypokinesia of the ventricular walls, mild aortic regurgitation, mild to moderate mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary valve thickening with mild regurgitation, and a small amount of pericardial effusion. The LVED measurement of 74 mm was obtained from the apical four-chamber (A4C) view, whereas the LVIDd value of 50.7 mm shown in Fig. 1 was derived from the parasternal long-axis (PLAX) view. In patients with markedly dilated and remodeled ventricles, inter-view variability of this degree is clinically recognized. Diagnosis and clinical interpretation were based on the A4C measurement, which corresponded to the lowest LVEF and is documented in the official echocardiography report (Supplementary File). Chest radiographic examination revealed cardiomegaly and the presence of a minor right-sided pleural effusion. Clinical and radiographic evaluations of the proband’s parents showed no abnormalities indicative of dilated cardiomyopathy. Additional conditions known to affect cardiac performance were systematically ruled out. In accordance with the observed imaging features and the proband’s clinical manifestations, the findings conformed to established diagnostic criteria for dilated cardiomyopathy, leading to a preliminary diagnosis of DCM.

Genetic analysis

We performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) analysis on the proband and identified a mutation, JPH2:NM_020433.4:exon2:c.479G > A:p.R160H. Whole-exome sequencing generated approximately 8.97 Gb of data, achieving 99.9% coverage of the target regions with a mean sequencing depth exceeding 50 ×. Subsequent data filtering was conducted through the following steps: (1) removal of non-coding regions and synonymous variants not predicted to affect splicing—as evaluated by regSNP-intron (14) and dbscSNV (with ADA and RF scores < 0.6) (15)—as well as variants documented in public databases including 1000 Genomes, the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC), and gnomAD; (2) prioritization of candidate genes using in silico prediction algorithms including Polyphen-2, SIFT, MutationTaster, and CADD; and (3) experimental validation of candidate variants via Sanger sequencing [8]. Among the sequencing results, JPH2 was the only gene associated with cardiomyopathy, and no other cardiomyopathy-related genes were identified. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutation is present only in the proband and was not found in either parent (Fig. 2A). The alteration of the amino acid at position 160 in the JPH2 protein, which changes to Arginine, is evolutionarily completely conserved across various species (Fig. 2B).

Functional assessment of the jph2 p.r160h variant.

To assess the cellular effects of the variant, we constructed plasmids carrying human wild-type (WT) and mutant (MU) JPH2. After transfecting cells, we observed increased expression of MYH7 (P = 0.0019), α-SMA (P = 0.0005), and TGF-β (P < 0.0001), as well as elevated JPH2 expression (Fig. 2C, D; P = 0.001). Given that AC16 cells are proliferative and lack the mature dyadic architecture and calcium-handling properties of adult cardiomyocytes, these results should be interpreted as an initial stress-response screening rather than a direct assessment of JPH2’s canonical roles in excitation–contraction coupling. The identified JPH2 c.479G > A (p.R160H) variant was then evaluated according to the standards and guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), meeting the following criteria:

PS2 (de novo evidence):Trio-based whole-exome sequencing (WES) involving the proband and both biological parents confirmed that the variant was present only in the proband and absent in the parents, who exhibited no clinical manifestations of cardiomyopathy. This verified a de novo occurrence in the proband with confirmed parentage.

Taken together, these lines of evidence (PS2 + PS3 + PM2 + PP4) support the classification of JPH2 p.R160H as a pathogenic variant under ACMG guidelines.

Discussion

The identification of the novel JPH2 variant (p.R160H) in a proband with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) provides further evidence supporting the role of JPH2 in cardiomyopathy pathogenesis. Although this variant has been previously documented in ClinVar (RCV002337855.25) without functional validation, our study provides experimental support for its pathogenic potential. Notably, AC16 cells transfected with the mutant JPH2 plasmid exhibited upregulated expression of MYH7, α-SMA, and TGF-β, indicating activation of myocardial stress pathways. However, because AC16 cells lack the sarcomeric maturation, T-tubule organization, and coordinated calcium cycling characteristic of adult cardiomyocytes, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary cellular responses rather than direct evidence of how the p.R160H variant affects JPH2’s physiological functions in mature cardiomyocytes.

Calcium signaling is central to cardiac excitation–contraction coupling, and even subtle disturbances in intracellular calcium homeostasis can initiate maladaptive stress responses. Perturbations in calcium cycling are known to activate downstream stress signaling cascades, including the upregulation of hypertrophic and fibrotic markers such as MYH7, α-SMA, and TGF-β.

In this context, junctophilin-2 (JPH2) serves a critical structural and regulatory role by maintaining the junctional membrane complex between the sarcolemma and sarcoplasmic reticulum, thereby ensuring efficient Ca2⁺ release and reuptake during excitation–contraction coupling [9].

Interestingly, mutant cells exhibited increased JPH2 protein levels, which suggests a potential alteration in JPH2 turnover or stability. However, these findings remain correlative and do not establish whether the p.R160H variant results in loss-of-function(PLOF), gain-of-function (GOF), or dominant-negative effects. At present, no conclusions can be drawn regarding its impact on RyR2 interaction, dyadic organization, or calcium handling. Prior studies have shown that other JPH2 variants, such as E169K, can disrupt RyR2 binding and increase SR Ca2⁺ leak [10], but whether p.R160H affects similar pathways cannot be inferred from the current data. Direct mechanistic studies—such as co-immunoprecipitation, subcellular localization assays, and calcium-transient imaging in iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes—will be required to define the specific functional consequences of this variant. Importantly, as this finding is based on a single proband, the de novo p.R160H variant should be considered causal in this proband, rather than representing a generalized mechanism for JPH2-related DCM. Further familial and population-based studies are required to confirm its broader pathogenic relevance.

Analysis of Table 1 reveals several important trends. The listed variants encompass both missense substitutions and truncating or predicted loss-of-function mutations, distributed throughout the JPH2 coding sequence. Truncating variants tend to cluster toward the C-terminal half of the protein (e.g., p.Gln428Ter, p.Gln564Ter, p.Glu641Ter), and these cases are often associated with more severe clinical outcomes such as early-onset DCM, heart failure, cardiac death, or need for transplantation (HTx). In contrast, most missense variants, including p.R160H, are located in the N- to mid-regions (MORN and joining domains) and are commonly classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) due to limited functional or segregation evidence. This pattern suggests that truncating variants may cause disease through loss-of-function mechanisms, whereas missense variants require functional validation to clarify whether they disrupt or alter JPH2 function.

The high proportion of VUS among reported JPH2 variants underscores a major gap in current genetic interpretation. Most remain unclassified because of the lack of experimental data or insufficient familial evidence. Our study provides a framework for how functional validation—such as expression analysis and stress marker profiling—can facilitate the reclassification of individual variants from VUS to likely pathogenic (LP) or pathogenic (P) categories. Future work should integrate genotype–phenotype correlation, protein structural mapping, and functional assays to systematically evaluate JPH2 variants, ultimately improving their clinical interpretability.

Given the increasing evidence linking JPH2 mutations to DCM, future studies should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying these mutations and their potential as therapeutic targets. Personalized treatment strategies targeting calcium handling defects in JPH2-related DCM could significantly improve proband outcomes.

In conclusion, our study not only validates the pathogenicity of the JPH2 p.R160H variant but also emphasizes the analytical value of compiling variant data (Table 1) to reveal structural and mechanistic trends. These findings underscore the importance of including JPH2 in genetic screening for cardiomyopathies and highlight the need for ongoing functional and structural studies to reclassify uncertain variants. The p.R160H mutation may provide new insights into the mechanistic basis of JPH2-related DCM and could serve as a potential focus for future therapeutic investigation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged.

First, although the clinical presentation and echocardiographic characteristics were consistent with idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy, coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) or invasive coronary angiography was not performed. As a result, ischemic cardiomyopathy cannot be completely excluded. Additionally, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), and myocardial biopsy were not conducted, limiting our ability to definitively rule out inflammatory, infiltrative, or other non-genetic etiologies.

Second, while trio-based whole-exome sequencing (WES) confirmed the variant as de novo, this study is based on a single proband, and additional unrelated cases will be essential to further strengthen the genotype–phenotype association and clarify the broader relevance of the p.R160H variant.

Third, all functional assays were conducted in AC16 cells, which, despite their practicality and high transfection efficiency, do not fully recapitulate key features of terminally differentiated human cardiomyocytes. Specifically, AC16 cells lack organized T-tubules and do not exhibit robust, synchronous calcium cycling—both of which are fundamental to JPH2’s biological function. As a result, the functional impact of the p.R160H variant on calcium handling or dyad integrity could not be directly assessed in this model. Future studies using iPSC-derived or primary human cardiomyocytes will be required to evaluate these aspects more accurately.

Finally, this study does not assess long-term clinical outcomes or familial segregation beyond the parents, which limits broader conclusions regarding penetrance and variable expressivity.
