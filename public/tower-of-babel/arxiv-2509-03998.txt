Integral Diophantine approximation on varieties
Zhizhong Huang, Florian Wilsch

Abstract
. We study the local behavior of integral points on log pairs near a fixed rational point in the boundary by means of an integral approximation constant. In light of Siegel’s theorem about integral points on curves and McKinnon’s conjecture on rational approximation constants, we conjecture that integral points that are close to the fixed point in archimedean topology should lie on certain rational curves with at most two points at infinity on weakly log Fano varieties. We verify this conjecture for a number of examples.

14G05; 14G40, 11J99

1.
Introduction

Solving polynomial equations with integer coefficients by integers lies at the heart of Diophantine geometry. This problem comes in many qualitative and quantitative variants about the existence, number, and distribution of solutions. The goal of this article is to address the following local aspect: how well can one approximate a given point by integral solutions, that is, how quickly can the distance between integral points and a fixed point decrease while keeping their “complexity” as small as possible?

1.1.
Approximation constants

On projective varieties, rational and integral points coincide, and as the rationals are dense in the reals (and more generally, a number field is dense in any of its completions), rational points lend themselves readily to approximation problems; they are thus the original setting for
Diophantine approximation
, asking about rational numbers close to real points. Concretely, given a real number
x
, what is the supremum
\alpha
of exponents
a>0
such that

\left|x-\frac{p}{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{|q|^{a}}

has infinitely many solutions with coprime
(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
? If
x\in\mathbb{Q}
, then it is easy to see that
\alpha=1
. For irrational algebraic
x
, this question has been famously answered by K. Roth
[
22
]
:
\alpha=2
.

McKinnon
[
19
]
has proposed a geometric variant of this problem, which he studied together with M. Roth
[
18
]
:
instead of an algebraic number,
x
becomes a geometric point on a projective variety
X
over a number field
k
; the rational number
p/q
becomes a rational point
y
on
X
, the difference
|x-p/q|
becomes an appropriately constructed distance
d_{v}(x,y)
for a fixed place
v
, and
|q|
becomes the height
H_{L}(y)
, where
H_{L}
is a height function associated with an ample line bundle
L
on
X
, which is a measure of the “complexity” of rational points. Given such
x\in X(\overline{k})
and an ample line bundle
L
, the
approximation constant

\alpha_{v}(x;L)
is the supremum of positive real numbers
a
such that

d_{v}(x,y)\leq\frac{1}{H_{L}(y)^{a}}

has infinitely many solutions with
y\in X(k)
; it measures how well
x
can be approximated by rational points. K. Roth’s Theorem can then be interpreted as giving the answer for
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
McKinnon put forward a conjecture
[
19
, Conj. 2.7]
on these constants, predicting that whenever
x\in X(k)
and the approximation constant
\alpha_{v}(x;L)
is finite,
a sequence of “best approximations”
[
19
, Def. 2.5]
can be found along a rational curve
C
;
that is, there is a sequence
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C(k)
with
d_{v}(x,y_{n})\to 0
and

\frac{1}{H_{L}(y)^{\alpha_{v}(x;L)}}\ll d_{v}(x,y)\ll\frac{1}{H_{L}(y)^{\alpha_{v}(x;L)}}.

Evidence for this conjecture is underpinned by the fact
[
25
, § 7.4]
going back to Siegel
[
26
]
that the approximation constant along any curve of positive genus is infinite.

1.2.
Integral points and approximations

In the context of the classical variant of
Manin’s problem
on rational points on Fano varieties
X

[
9
]
, Peyre’s equidistribution principle
[
21
]
involves an (absolutely continuous)
Tamagawa measure
on
X(k_{v})
for each place
v
, with respect to which the set
X(k)
is equidistributed. In particular, there is an abundance of rational points that can approximate other points whenever this principle holds.
On the other hand, integral points on an affine variety
U\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
lie within the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and hence clearly cannot approximate any point in
U
with respect to the real distance.
Work on the integral variant of Manin’s problem, as initiated by Chambert-Loir and Tschinkel
[
5
]
asking about the distribution of integral points of bounded height on a log Fano pair
(X,D)
, suggests that this is the wrong point of view, however, and predicts instead an equidistribution against a certain
residue measure
supported on the boundary
D(k_{v})
as soon as
v
is archimedean. As a basic example, one may regard the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
inside
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
; this way, it ceases to be discrete, having accumulation points on the hyperplane
H\cong\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
at infinity and in fact becomes equidistributed with respect to a measure supported on
H
.
It is thus natural to investigate how well points on the boundary
D
can be approximated by integral points.
We shall study this question by defining an integral variant of McKinnon’s approximation constant. We conjecture that this constant is still governed by rational curves under suitable hypotheses (Conjecture
4.8
).

A necessary condition for a rational curve to contribute to the (integral) approximation constant is that it contains infinitely many (integral) points. Siegel’s classical theorem translates this into geometric properties of the curve.

Theorem 1.1

(Siegel (1929)
[
26
]
)
.

Let
C
be a smooth, proper curve over a number field
k
with ring of integers
\mathfrak{o}
, and let
D\subset C
be a zero-dimensional reduced subscheme.
Then there are only finitely many integral points on any
\mathfrak{o}
-model of
C\setminus D
, except possibly if
g(C)=0
and
\deg(D)\leq 2
.

An important step in our investigation is to make the converse of Siegel’s theorem explicit. Concretely, assume
g(C)=0
and write
C_{0}=C\setminus D
. If
\deg(D)\leq 1
(thus
C_{0}
is
log rational
) and
C_{0}
contains at least one integral point, then
C_{0}
admits an integral parametrization from
\mathbb{A}^{1}
. If
\deg(D)=2
, then
C_{0}
admits a parametrization by a torus. For this reason we call such curves
toroidal
. Integral points on such curves are dominated by the units
\mathfrak{o}_{K}^{\times}
of the smallest extension
K/k
over which both points in
D
are defined. In fact, if
C_{0}
contains an integral point, it contains a subgroup of
\mathfrak{o}_{K}^{\times}
of full rank. See Propositions
2.4
,
2.10
, and Corollary
2.11
for precise statements.

Based on these parametrizations of suitable subsets of integral points on rational curves, we are able to fully determine their approximation constants (Propositions
4.1
and
4.5
).

1.3.
Structure of the article

We begin by introducing the notion of

over a general Dedekind domain and studying the structure of rational curves that potentially possess infinitely many integral points in Section

.
After defining integral approximation constants in Section

2.
Log schemes and integral points on curves

Throughout this section, let
\mathfrak{o}

2.1.
Log schemes and integral points

Let
\mathfrak{U}
be a non-proper scheme that is embedded in a projective scheme
\mathfrak{X}
, all over
\mathfrak{o}
.
By blowing up the part of the complement
\mathfrak{D}=\mathfrak{X}\setminus\mathfrak{U}
that is of codimension 2 or greater, we may assume that
\mathfrak{D}
is a divisor, in which case we call
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{D})
a
log pair
.
An

on the pair
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{D})
(or on the open part
\mathfrak{U}
) is a morphism
\Spec\mathfrak{o}\to\mathfrak{U}
. The set of integral points is denoted by
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{o})
. It follows from the definition that a rational point in
X(k)
is integral if and only if the unique integral point in
\mathfrak{X}(\mathfrak{o})
that it extends to does not specialize to a point on the divisor
\mathfrak{D}
modulo any finite place. We call an integral point
generically regular
if the image of the generic point of
\Spec\mathfrak{o}
is regular — that is, if it is regular as a rational point.

Definition 2.1
.

A
log scheme
over
\mathfrak{o}
is a pair
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{D})
consisting of a separated
\mathfrak{o}
-scheme
\mathfrak{X}
of finite type and a reduced effective divisor
\mathfrak{D}
.
A log scheme
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{D})
is called

(i)

flat
if both
\mathfrak{X}
and
\mathfrak{D}
are,

(ii)

projective
if
\mathfrak{X}
(hence
\mathfrak{D}
) is, and

(iii)

generically regular
if the generic fiber
X=\mathfrak{X}_{k}
is regular and the generic fiber
D=\mathfrak{D}_{k}
of the divisor geometrically has strict normal crossings.

As a useful shorthand, a
nice log scheme
is a flat, projective, and generically regular log scheme.

Moreover, for any log scheme
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{D})
, we write
\mathfrak{X}_{0}=\mathfrak{X}\setminus\mathfrak{D}
(whenever the divisor
\mathfrak{D}
is clear from context).

Note that
\mathfrak{D}
being flat means that it is the closure of its generic fiber in
\mathfrak{X}
.

2.2.
Rational curves with infinitely many integral points

Analogously to McKinnon’s rational approximation constant, we expect that the integral approximation constant we are about to define is obtained by points on rational, possibly singular, curves. We shall thus turn our attention to curves in this more general setting for now.
As curves whose genus is at least one can never contribute to approximation constants as their set of integral points is finite by Siegel’s theorem, we need only consider rational curves.
On the other hand, any such curve that contains infinitely many integral points could, prima facie, possibly achieve the approximation constant. Our first aim is thus to determine such curves.

Recall that a
k
-rational curve on a projective
k
-variety
X
is (the image of) a non-constant morphism
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}\to X
. If the target carries a log structure.

Let

\mathfrak{o}

Remark 2.6
.

The condition that the existing integral point is regular is necessary and comparable to the necessity of a smooth rational point on a smooth geometrically rational curve to make it rational. For instance, consider the log rational curve

(1)

C\colon 2y^{2}=x^{2}\left(2x+1\right)

in
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{Z}}
. It admits the integral point
(0,0)
, but no other integral points. Indeed, for an integral solution with
y\neq 0
is integral, the
2
-adic valuation of the left-hand side of (
1
) is odd, but the one on the right cannot be.

The remaining case is that of a rational curve meeting the boundary in two points. The log genus of such curves is one, and they behave much like elliptic curves; in particular, they are algebraic groups (more precisely, tori), as soon as they possess a rational point.
Two types of embeddings are to be distinguished: those that map the two boundary points to different points and those that identify them (i.e., nodal singular curves).

Definition 2.7
.

A
toroidal curve
over
\mathfrak{o}
is a flat, projective log scheme
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
with generic fiber
(C,D)
such that

(i)

\mathfrak{C}
is birational to
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathfrak{o}}
, and

(ii)

\deg(\phi^{-1}{D})=2
, where
\phi\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\to C
is the normalization, and the residue field of every point in
\phi^{-1}(D)
is separable over
k
.

Note that if
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
is a toroidal curve over
\mathfrak{o}
, then its generic fiber
(C,D)
is a toroidal curve over the field of fractions
k
.

We say that a toroidal curve
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
with generic fiber
(C,D)
is
nodal
if
D
consists of a single point, that is, if the normalization
\phi
identifies the two boundary points on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
into a nodal singularity.

The following lemma shows that, roughly speaking, a toroidal curve
C
admits a parametrization by a torus
T=\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\phi^{-1}(D)\to C\setminus D

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
be a toroidal curve over
\mathfrak{o}
with generic fiber
(C,D)

K=k(x)
be the residue field at one of the points

\phi\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\to C

\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\phi^{-1}(D)
is isomorphic to a torus
T
, where

(i)

if
K=k

(ii)

is the norm-one-torus
R^{1}_{K/k}\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}
if
K\neq k

Proof.

A parametrization
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}\to C\setminus D
exists over the residue field
K\coloneq k(x)
— either

or a quadratic extension thereof. If
K=k
, we are done.
Now assume that
K/k
is quadratic, and denote by

the norm-one torus
R^{1}_{K/k}\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}

Proof.

Integral approximation constants

From now on,

3.1.
Preliminaries

In order to define integral approximation constants, we are in need of distances and height functions.

Distances

If

If

3.2.
Approximation over a single place

Motivated by the work of McKinnon and Roth
[
19
,
18
]
, we propose a variant of the approximation constant. It measures how well a local point

can be approximated by global points

in some set

by attempting to minimize one of the distance functions

compared to a height

.
Mostly, this set

will be the set

of integral points of a nice log scheme

, and the point

Definition 3.1
.

Let

be a projective

-variety equipped with a height function

associated with an ample line bundle

; let

be a subset. Let

be a distance function. The

(i)

If

, then

(ii)

Otherwise,

\alpha_{v}(x,W;L)=\inf\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\ \middle|\ \begin{subarray}{c}d_{v}(x,y)^{\alpha}H(y)\text{ is bounded }\\
\text{from above for all }y\in W\end{subarray}\right\}.

Remark 3.2
.

(1)

By
[
18
, Prop. 2.4]
, this definition does not depend on the choice of a particular distance

or height function

associated with

(2)

If

is an

-scheme, we write

for

; similarly if

is a subvariety,

should be read as

; in particular,

is the approximation constant studied by McKinnon–Roth
[

Before continuing with our analysis, we discuss some necessary conditions for the constant

to be finite: in particular, a point

needs to lie in the

-adic closure of

Archimedean approximation and Clemens complexes

In the absence of obstructions — specifically, the analytic and Brauer–Manin obstructions
[
24
]
*Thm. 3.13, Def. 3.15, § 5
[
27
]
*§ 2.3 — integral points of bounded log anticanonical height on log Fano schemes tend to equidistribute with respect to an archimedean place
v
towards a limit measure supported on a subset of the boundary,
D(k_{v})
encoded in the
Clemens complex
.

Let
D
be a divisor with geometrically strict normal crossings, and let
\mathcal{A}
be its set of geometric components. The
geometric Clemens complex
is the set

\mathcal{C}(D)=\left\{(A,Z)\ \middle|\ A\subseteq\mathcal{A},\ \text{$Z$ is an irreducible component of $\bigcap_{E\in A}E$}\right\},

equipped with the partial order defined via
(A,Z)<(A^{\prime},Z^{\prime})
if
A\subsetneq A^{\prime}
and
Z\supsetneq Z^{\prime}
.
In other words, it consists of a vertex
(E,E)
for every geometric component
E
of
D
. Simplices are then glued onto the vertices corresponding to components with nonempty intersection.

Let
\Gamma_{v}
be the Galois group of
k_{v}
; it acts on the set
\mathcal{A}
of geometric components of
D
.
The
v
-analytic Clemens complex
, which encodes intersections in
X(k_{v})
, is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{v}(D)=\left\{(A,Z)\ \middle|\ \begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq\mathcal{A}\text{ is $\Gamma_{v}$-invariant}\\
\text{$Z$ is an irreducible component of $\bigcap_{E\in A}E$ with $Z(k_{v})\neq\emptyset$ }\end{subarray}\right\}.

Note that for
(A,Z)\in\mathcal{C}_{v}(D)
, the subvariety
Z
is defined over
k_{v}
, in general.

Equidistribution statements for integral points of bounded log anticanonical height such as those obtained by Chambert-Loir, Tschinkel
[
6
]
, and Santens
[
24
]
now involve a
residue measure

[
5
, § 2.1]
supported on the
minimal strata

Z_{D,v}=\bigcup_{(A,Z)\in\mathcal{C}_{v}(D)\text{ maximal}}Z

as their limit.
Faces
(A,Z)
that are maximal, but not maximal dimensional only contribute to lower order terms of the counting function, hence not to the overall limit measure — restricting the counting problem to points near such a stratum still tends to result in equidistribution on
Z
, though (cf.
[
24
, p. 3]
); as only the local distribution is relevant for approximation constants, it is natural to include such maximal, but not necessarily maximal-dimensional, faces in the analysis.

In light of these phenomena, we shall mostly restrict ourselves to studying approximation constants of rational points
x\in Z_{D,v}(k_{v})\cap X(k)
, but we remain more general wherever such a restriction is not helpful.

Simultaneous approximation

Properties such as weak or strong approximation involve more than a single place at once, and indeed, approximation constants measuring how well points can be simultaneously approximated over several places are straightforward to define. For a finite set
S
of places, let

on

.

.

Let

-variety equipped with a height function

associated with an ample line bundle

,

Let

(1)

Remark 3.5
.

One can analogously define an approximation constant for

3.4.
The scope of this article

Our main focus is to understand approximation constants over a single archimedean place — over such a place, most of the phenomena that set integral points apart from rational ones are visible.

The behavior of approximation constant for nonarchimedean places differs yet again in several ways. Let
v
be a finite place. The set
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{o})
(if nonempty) is contained in the open-closed subset
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{o}_{v})
of

, so that the approximation constant is infinite for points in its complement. If strong approximation holds for
\mathfrak{U}
away from a set of places not containing

, then
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{o})
is dense in
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{o}_{v})
, so that every local integral point

lies in the

-adic closure of
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{o})\setminus\{x\}
. More generally, the integral version of Manin’s conjecture due to Santens
[
24
]
predicts equidistribution (hence density) in a closed subset of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
(
7
) explicitly described in terms of two obstructions.
In particular, if

is a global integral point, it would then automatically lie in the closure of

in
X({\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\infty})
.
In any case, a natural question seems to be how well an integral point in
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{o})
can be approximated by other integral points with respect to nonarchimedean absolute values.
We plan to address this problem more thoroughly in future investigations and content ourselves with this very brief discussion for now.

4.

The aim of this section is to compute integral approximation constants of log rational and toroidal curves, using the parametrizations obtained in Section

As in the work of McKinnon and Roth
[

, Thm. 2.16]
, they will involve the integer

r_{y,v}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $k(y)\nsubseteq k_{v}$,}\\

defined for a closed point

on a

and a place

of

Proposition 4.1

(Approximation constants for log rational curves)
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a log rational curve on a nice log scheme

We begin by observing

To get an upper bound, let

(the latter condition defines a tube in

, hence contains infinitely many elements of the integer lattice). Then, appealing to (
5
), we can estimate

d_{v}(x,\phi(a_{n}))\asymp d_{v}(\infty,a_{n})^{m_{x}}\asymp|a_{n}|_{v}^{-m_{x}},

while

H_{L}(\phi(a_{n}))=H_{\phi^{*}L}(a_{n})\asymp\prod_{v^{\prime}}\max\{1,|a_{n}|_{v^{\prime}}^{d}\}\ll|a_{n}|_{v}^{d},

so that

d_{v}(x,\phi(a_{n}))^{d/m_{x}}H_{L}(\phi(n))

is bounded, which shows that
\frac{d}{m_{x}}
is also an upper bound.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In the setting of Proposition
4.1
, it is easy to deduce the same result for the simultaneous approximation constant

\alpha_{\infty_{k}}(\Delta_{\infty_{k}}(x),\mathfrak{C}_{0};L)=\frac{\deg_{L}(C)}{\mult_{x}(C)}.

The computation of approximation constants on toroidal curves will use the following observations on the geometry of numbers.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\Lambda
be a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of full rank. Then for every
\delta>0
, there is a lattice point
x\in\Lambda\cap[\delta^{-1},\infty]\times[-\delta,\delta]^{n-1}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
be the projection onto the last coordinates. If
\ker\pi|_{\Lambda}\neq 0
, then a sufficiently large element of this kernel has the desired property. If not, then the rank of
\pi(\Lambda)
is larger than
n-1
, hence
\pi(\Lambda)
is not discrete. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
be an accumulation point and
y^{\prime}\in\pi(\Lambda)
with
|y-y^{\prime}|<\delta
. Then
y-y^{\prime}
is an accumulation point as well, and there are infinitely many points in
\pi(\Lambda)\cap[-\delta,\delta]^{n-1}
, hence so are there in
\Lambda\cap\mathbb{R}\times[-\delta,\delta]^{n-1}
. As
\Lambda
is discrete and
K=[-\delta^{-1},\delta^{-1}]\times[-\delta,\delta]^{n-1}
is compact, only finitely many of those can lie in
K
. Therefore, we can find a point
x
outside
K
, which is as claimed after multiplying with a suitable sign.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
K/k
be a quadratic extension and
U
be a subgroup of full rank of the group
\mathfrak{o}_{K}^{1}
of units of norm
1
. Let
v_{0}
be a place of
k
such that
K\subset k_{v}
; let
w_{0}
be a place above
v
. For every
\delta>0
, there is a unit
\epsilon\in U
such that
|\epsilon|_{w_{0}}<\delta
and
1/(1+\delta)<|\epsilon|_{w}<1+\delta
for archimedean places
w
with
w\nmid v_{0}
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\phi\colon\mathfrak{o}_{K}^{\times}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{\infty_{K}},

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\mapsto(\log|\epsilon|_{w})_{w\in\infty_{K}};

we shall begin by describing the image of
\mathfrak{o}_{K}^{1}
under this map by means of a linear subspace.
The set of archimedean places
\infty_{k}
is the union of splitting places
\infty_{k}^{\mathrm{s}}
and inert places
\infty_{k}^{\mathrm{i}}
.

Let
v\in\infty_{k}^{\mathrm{s}}
be one of the former for now, that is, a place that has two different places
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
lying above it; note that this case includes
v=v_{0}
by assumption, and in this case, label
w_{2}=w_{0}
.
The two places
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are swapped by the nontrivial element
\sigma
of the Galois group
\Gal(K/k)
, hence

1=|N(\epsilon)|_{w_{1}}=|\epsilon|_{w_{1}}|\sigma(\epsilon)|_{w_{1}}=|\epsilon|_{w_{1}}|\epsilon|_{w_{2}}.

Logarithmically, this translates to

f_{v}(\phi(\epsilon))=0
, where

f_{v}=x_{w_{1}}+x_{w_{2}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\infty_{K}}\to\mathbb{R}

is the sum of the two projections.
Let

b_{v}=(\mathds{1}_{w=w_{1}}-\mathds{1}_{w=w_{2}})_{w}.

Let now
v\in\infty_{k}^{\mathrm{i}}
be an inert archimedean place and
w
be the only place above
v
. Then
\mathbb{R}\cong k_{v}\subset K_{w}\cong\mathbb{C}
, and
\sigma
acts by complex conjugation, so that

This implies
f_{v}(\phi(\epsilon))=0
, where
f_{v}=x_{w}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\infty_{K}}\to\mathbb{R}
is the projection.

Let
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{\infty_{K}}
be the linear subspace cut out by the
\#\infty_{k}
linearly independent linear forms
(f_{v})_{v\in\infty_{k}}
. It is thus of dimension
\#\infty_{K}-\#\infty_{k}
with basis
(b_{v})_{v\in\infty_{k}^{\mathrm{s}}}
.
Note that
H
contains the sublattice
\phi(\mathfrak{o}_{K}^{1})
of
\phi(\mathfrak{o}_{K}^{\times})
, which is of full rank by (
2
).
In the basis
(b_{v})_{v\in\infty_{k}^{\mathrm{s}}}
, ordered so that
v_{0}\in\infty_{k}^{\mathrm{s}}
comes first, the desired properties for the absolute values translate to

\phi(\epsilon)\in[-\log\delta,\infty]\times[-\log(1+\delta),\log(1+\delta)]^{\#\infty_{k}^{s}-1}.

Now the claim follows from Lemma

.
∎

Proposition 4.5

(Approximation constants for toroidal curves)
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a toroidal curve on a nice log scheme
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{D})
such that
\#\mathfrak{C}_{0}(\mathfrak{o})=\infty
.
Let
x\in(C\cap D)(k)
,
v\in\infty_{k}
, and
L
be an ample line bundle on
X
.

(i)

If
C
is not nodal, then

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{C}_{0};L)=\frac{\deg_{L}(C)}{\mult_{x}(C)}.

(ii)

If
C
is nodal, let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be its two branches corresponding to

and
y_{2}

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{C}_{0};L)=\frac{\deg_{L}(C)}{\max_{i=1,2}r_{y_{i},v}\mult_{x}(C_{i})}.

Proof.

As
\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{C}_{0};L)\geq\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{C}_{k};L)
, all expressions on the right hold as a lower bound by
[

, so that we are only left to construct sufficiently good approximating sequences to provide upper bounds.
Let

be the splitting field of the toroidal curve

,
\phi\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\to C
be its normalization, and write
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}=\phi^{-1}(D)\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)
for the (Galois invariant) preimage of the boundary. Let
\tau\colon T=\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{y_{1},y_{2}\}\to C_{0}
be the torus of
C
.

We start with the non-nodal case, in which case
K=k
.
By applying a linear change of variables and appealing to Proposition
2.10
, we may assume that
y_{1}=0
,
y_{2}=\infty
,
\phi(y_{1})=x
, and that a subgroup
G
of
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}(\mathfrak{o})=\mathfrak{o}^{\times}
of full rank is mapped into
\mathfrak{C}_{0}(\mathfrak{o})
by
\tau
. Let
\epsilon
be an element of this subgroup of infinite order with
|\epsilon|_{v}<1
and
|\epsilon|_{v^{\prime}}>1
for all

(which exists by Dirichlet’s unit theorem), and consider the sequence
(\Phi(\epsilon^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. An argument analogous to that in the proof of
Proposition
4.1
now yields the claimed constant.

Assume from now on that
C
is nodal. Note that both points

have the same residue field

. Let

be the

-degree of

and write

. If
K=k
(and in particular,

), with the same convention

,
y_{2}=\infty
and the same choice of

while its height is

by the product formula, and

by definition, achieving the claimed constant.

We are thus only left with the case

(with
[K:k]=2
).
For now, assume that

, so that the compositum

is a quadratic extension of local fields. Then
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
lie in the open set

\mathbb{P}^{1}(k_{v})
(as else
\phi^{-1}(x)

This implies that there is a neighborhood

of

in

is bounded from below, and hence the approximation constant is infinite.

We may thus finally assume

, in which case
r_{{y_{1}},v}=r_{{y_{2}},v}=2
. There is a

such that
K=k(\sqrt{d})
and
\sqrt{d}\in k_{v}

K_{w}=k_{v}
for

. Let
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
be the two places above
v
.
By Proposition

,

is a norm one torus and that a subgroup
G
of
\mathfrak{T}(\mathfrak{o})
of full rank is mapped into
\mathfrak{C}_{0}(\mathfrak{o})
under
\tau

\lambda
be a linear change of variables of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}
mapping

to
y_{1}
,
\infty
to

and
\mathfrak{T}(\mathfrak{o})
to the norm-1-units
\mathfrak{o}_{K}^{1}\subset\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}(\mathfrak{o}_{K})\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}(K)

Composing with

we get a morphism
\psi=\lambda\circ\phi\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}\to C
with
\psi(0)=\psi(\infty)=x

\epsilon_{\delta}\in G
be a unit with

and

for all

as constructed in Lemma

\displaystyle d_{v}(x,\tau(\lambda^{-1}(\epsilon_{\delta})))

\displaystyle=d_{w_{1}}(x,\tau(\lambda^{-1}(\epsilon_{\delta})))\asymp d_{w_{1}}(\psi(0),\psi(\epsilon_{\delta}))

\displaystyle\ll|\epsilon_{\delta}|_{v}^{m_{y_{1}}}\ll\delta^{-m_{y_{1}}}.

Computing the

\displaystyle H_{L_{K}}(\tau(\lambda^{-1}(\epsilon_{\delta}^{n})))

whence

Remark 4.6
.

In stark contrast to log rational curves, the simultaneous approximation constant
\alpha_{\infty_{k}}(\Delta_{\infty_{k}}(x),\mathfrak{C}_{0};L)
can differ dramatically from the one with respect to a fixed place, depending on whether the toroidal curve
C
is nodal or not. Keep all notation from Proposition
4.5
and its proof, and let
t\in K[T]
be the nonconstant invertible regular function vanishing in
x
(hence measuring the distance to
x
). Then a multiple
f=dt
is regular on
\mathfrak{C}_{0}
, hence takes on values in
\mathfrak{o}
on integral points in
\mathfrak{C}_{0}(\mathfrak{o})
.

(i)

On nodal curves, note that the distance behaves like

d_{v}(\phi(y),x)\asymp\min\{|f(t)|_{v},|f(t)|^{-1}_{v}\},

as
x
can be approximated by approximating either
0
or
\infty
on
\mathbb{P}_{K}^{1}\supset T_{K}
, and an argument analogous to that in Proposition
4.5
shows that
\alpha_{\infty_{k}}(\Delta_{\infty_{k}}(x),\mathfrak{C}_{0};L)
is finite.

(ii)

The behavior on non-nodal curves changes, though. Since

\prod_{v\in\infty_{K}}|f(t)|_{v}=\prod_{v<\infty}|f(t)|_{v}^{-1}\geq 1,

the distance is bounded from below:

d_{\infty_{k}}(x,y)\asymp\sum_{v\in\infty_{K}}|f(t)|_{v}\gg 1.

It follows that the constant
\alpha_{\infty_{k}}(\Delta_{\infty_{k}}(x),\mathfrak{C}_{0};L)
infinite for nonnodal toroidal curves. This in particular holds true for
\alpha_{\infty}
, where
\infty
is the unique archimedean place of the rationals or an imaginary quadratic field — but note that in this case, the number of integral points on a nonnodal toroidal curve is already finite by Corollary
2.11
.

4.1.
A conjecture

Fano varieties contain an abundance of rational curves over an
algebraic closure, being geometrically rationally
connected
[
3
,
16
]
. For this geometric reason, we expect that rational points tend to be abundant on Fano varieties: a qualitative conjecture
of Colliot-Thélène
[
8
, p. 174]
implies weak weak approximation and in
particular, that the set of rational points is not thin as soon as it is nonempty.
Moreover, Manin’s conjecture
[
9
]
was proposed, measuring quantitatively how rational points of bounded anticanonical height grow.

Weak Fano varieties, whose anticanonical bundle is big and nef, but not necessarily ample,
are a slight generalization, and this property of the anticanonical bundle appears
in variants of Manin’s problem, e.g.
[
17
]
— the associated heights almost satisfy the Northcott property, except that they might stay bounded
on an infinite set of points on strict subvarieties.
The logarithmic analog to these varieties thus seems like a natural candidate to
expect approximation constants to be governed by rational curves.

Definition 4.7
.

A
weakly log Fano scheme
over
\mathfrak{o}
is a nice log scheme
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{D})
such that the log anticanonical class
-K_{X}(D)=-K_{X}-D
of its generic fiber
(X,D)
is big and nef.

It follows from
[
15
, Cor. 5.4]
that such an
X
is covered by a family of log rational or toroidal curves. Based on our previous analysis, we now propose the following analog of
McKinnon’s conjecture for integral points.
We shall restrict ourselves to schemes that indeed admit an abundance of integral points:
integral points on log Fano varieties might still be thin — but note that
a conjecture of Santens
[
24
]
expects this to be fully explained by concrete obstructions.
For the sake of simplicity, we shall directly assume that this is not the case, that is, that the
integral Hilbert property
holds:
recall that a subset
W\subset X(k)
is
thin
if there exists a generically finite rational map
f\colon Y\to X
from a
k
-variety
Y
that does not admit a section such that
W\subset f(Y(k))
, and that
\mathfrak{U}=\mathfrak{X}\setminus\mathfrak{D}
satisfies the integral Hilbert property if its set
\mathfrak{U}(\mathfrak{o})
of integral points is not thin. This latter condition is stronger than the set of integral points being Zariski dense. Moreover, the equidistribution properties discussed in previous sections imply the integral Hilbert property.

Conjecture 4.8
.

Let
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{D})
be a weakly log Fano scheme satisfying the integral Hilbert property, and let
x\in D(k)
lie on a minimal stratum of
D=\mathfrak{D}_{k}
. Let
v
be an archimedean place and
L
be an ample line bundle. Then there exists a rational curve
\mathfrak{C}
on
(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{D})
which is either log rational or toroidal such that

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U};L)=\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{C}_{0};L).

Nevertheless, it remains interesting to study approximation constants on schemes failing
these assumptions. In fact, it seems likely that rational curves still govern approximation
constants on a larger class of varieties — similarly to how Manin’s conjecture
holds for many
almost Fano
varieties as defined by Peyre
[
21
, Hyp. 3.27]
such as
all toric varieties, even though there also exist counterexamples in this more general setting
[
17
, § 5.1]
.

Remark 4.9
.

(i)

Curves achieving
\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U};L)
need not be unique.

(ii)

By Proposition

, nodal toroidal curves are more likely to contribute to a smaller approximation constant because of the factor

(iii)

Proposition

It would be interesting to find out how ‘likely’ it is that such a sparse set of points determines the approximation constant, and whether this has any implications for the geometry of the pair

.

Finally, we also ask the following question, inspired by the notion of the

The scheme

We now turn to studying integral approximation constants of the point

x=([1:1],\,[1:1])\in D(\mathbb{Q}).

Note that the restrictions of
-K_{X}(D)
to the two exceptional divisors are trivial, but as the base point
x
is away from them, they do not interfere with approximation constants.

We begin by describing the relevant height and distance functions explicitly. Any point
y
on
X
sufficiently close to
x
cannot lie on the exceptional divisors, hence can be described by homogeneous coordinates

y=([s_{0}:s_{1}],\,[t_{0}:t_{1}])

on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
, both coordinates being represented by coprime integers. If such a point is sufficiently close to

d(x,y)=d_{\infty}(x,y)\asymp\max\left(\left|\frac{s_{1}}{s_{0}}-1\right|,\left|\frac{t_{1}}{t_{0}}-1\right|\right).

We obtain a log anticanonical height function

H(y)=H_{-K_{X}(D)}(y)=\max\{|s_{0}t_{0}|,|s_{0}t_{1}|,|s_{1}t_{0}|,|s_{1}t_{1}|\}

through the standard choice of generators of
\mathcal{O}(1,1)
.
Note that the
p
-adic variant of this maximum is always

by the coprimality of the coordinates.

In order to compute the approximation constant
\alpha_{\infty}(x,-K_{X}(D))
, we shall prove Liouville-type inequalities which allow to bound it from below and construct rational curves that provide matching bounds from above.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all

In order to tackle Question

, we shall continue to construct rational curves achieving the ‘next best’ approximation constants. It turns out that three different types of curve achieve this: smooth log rational curves of degree two, nodal toroidal curves of degree four, and cuspidal log rational curves of the same degree.
We start with the first of theses: smooth curves of log anticanonical degree two passing through

such that they intersect

Proposition 5.4
.

If
a\neq 0
and
b\neq 0
, then

\alpha(x,C_{a,b}\cap\mathfrak{U};-K_{X}(D))=2.

Proof.

Indeed,
s_{0}(t_{0}-t_{1})
,
s_{1}(t_{0}-t_{1})
, and

span the two-dimensional set of all divisors containing

in

(i.e., being log rational) can be checked by computing partial derivatives and reduces the dimension by
1
; finally, the case
a=0
has to be excluded as it corresponds to a reducible divisor (the union of

and
l_{1}
), while with

, the curve coincides with the union of
D
and the exceptional curves.
All of such curves

have infinitely many integral points as they are everywhere locally soluble: for primes

, the equation simplifies to
e_{1}e_{2}(s_{0}^{\prime}t_{0}^{\prime}+s_{1}^{\prime}t_{1}^{\prime})=0

in the total coordinate ring, having nonsingular solutions with

, for instance. For primes

not dividing

, let

and
s_{1}=t_{1}=1
(such a point is automatically integral as

); the equation becomes
-a+bt_{0}=0
and has a solution modulo

that can be lifted by Hensel’s Lemma.
Using Proposition
4.1
, we deduce that
\alpha(x,C_{a,b}\cap\mathfrak{U};-K_{X}(D))=2
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

If

\alpha(x,S_{a,b}\cap\mathfrak{U};-K_{X}(D))=2.

Proof.

Each of these curves

has log anticanonical degree

, and admits the trivial integral point

∎

We remark that

.

If

is distinct from

\alpha(x,R_{a,b}\cap\mathfrak{U};-K_{X}(D))=2.

Remark 5.8
.

For any

, and

, let us define

N(W;B)\coloneq\#\{P\in\mathfrak{U}(\mathbb{Z})\cap W:H(P)\leq B\}.

Then, using the parametrizations obtained in Section
4
, it is straightforward to determine

\displaystyle N(L_{i};B)

\displaystyle\asymp B

for
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\displaystyle N(C_{a,b};B)

\displaystyle\asymp_{a,b}B^{\frac{1}{2}}

for

with

,

\displaystyle N(R_{a,b};B)

\displaystyle\asymp_{a,b}B^{\frac{1}{4}}

for

We recall the following notion that plays a key role in Batyrev’s classification of higher-dimensional toric varieties

A collection of rays

is called a

\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{\varrho_{i}}=0,

the rays
\varrho_{1},\dots,\varrho_{s}
do not generate a cone of

(in the sense of deformation theory): as elements of the kernel of

using (

See

for more details, for instance.

In order to study integral points on a log pair, we need a torus-invariant divisor. For any nonempty subset of rays
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Sigma(1)
, we denote by
D_{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{\varrho\in\mathcal{A}}D_{\varrho}
its corresponding divisor in
X
, and by
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{\varrho\in\mathcal{A}}\mathfrak{D}_{\varrho}
the corresponding divisor in
\mathfrak{X}
. Moreover, denote by
U_{\mathcal{A}}=X\setminus D_{\mathcal{A}}
the corresponding open toric subvariety and
\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathfrak{X}\setminus\mathfrak{D}_{\mathcal{A}}
the corresponding open toric subscheme.

In previous work
[
14
, Thm. 1.2]
, the first author proved that the rational approximation constant on the open orbit equals the minimal degree among all minimal rational curves. We shall compute the integral approximation constant under the following hypotheses for a family of log pairs
(X,D_{\mathcal{A}})
of a special shape:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
X
be a smooth, projective toric variety whose pseudoeffective cone is simplicial with corresponding toric
\mathfrak{o}
-scheme
\mathfrak{X}
,
L
be an ample line bundle, and
v
be an archimedean place.
Moreover, let
\mathcal{A}
consist of a single element of a central primitive collection
\mathcal{P}
, and
let
\delta_{\mathcal{P}}
be the

If
x\in D_{\mathcal{A}}^{\circ}(k)=D_{\mathcal{A}}(k)\setminus\cup_{\varrho\notin\mathcal{A}}D_{\varrho}(k)
, then

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}};L)=\delta_{\mathcal{P}}.

In particular, this confirms Conjecture
4.8
for the log pair
(X,D_{\mathcal{A}})
and the choice of
x\in D_{\mathcal{A}}^{\circ}(k)
.

Remark 6.2
.

(i)

Note that, as every ray can be contained in at most one central primitive collection
[
14
, Lem. 6.4]
, the degree
\delta_{\mathcal{P}}
is unique.

(ii)

For
\mathcal{A}
of the form in Theorem

,

U_{\mathcal{A}}
is an
\mathbb{A}^{\#\mathcal{P}-1}
-bundle over a projective toric variety of dimension
n-\#\mathcal{P}+1
(cf.
[

This immediately yields that
\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathfrak{o})
is not thin. Moreover, as

remains big, it follows from work of Santens

that
\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathfrak{o})
is equidistributed.

(iii)

That the pseudoeffective cone is simplicial has a simple combinatorial description: it holds if and only if there exists a maximal cone
\sigma_{0}\in\Sigma^{\max}
such that all the generators in
\Sigma(1)\setminus\sigma_{0}(1)
are linear combinations of those in
\sigma_{0}(1)
with negative integer coefficients
[
14
, Lem. 6.3]
.

(iv)

More generally, for every central primitive collection
\mathcal{P}
we can fix a ray
\varrho_{\mathcal{P}}\in\mathcal{P}
, and let

\mathcal{A}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{P}\text{ central primitive}}\{\varrho_{\mathcal{P}}\}\subseteq\Sigma^{(1)}.

Let
A\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be a maximal face of the Clemens complex (that is, a maximal subset of
\mathcal{A}
that generates a cone in
\Sigma
),
let
Z_{A}=\bigcap_{\varrho\in A}D_{\varrho}
, and let

x\in Z_{A}^{\circ}(k)=(Z_{A}\setminus\bigcup_{\varrho\notin A}D_{\varrho})(k).

Then a similar argument to the one that is to follow shows that

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}};L)=\min\left\{\deg_{L}(C)\ \middle|\ \begin{subarray}{c}C\text{ is minimal and has class }\mathcal{P},\\
\mathcal{P}\cap A\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}\right\}.

For simplicity of exposition, we content ourselves with the more restrictive version of Theorem
6.1
above.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

We begin by noting that only integral points on the open orbit
T=X\setminus\bigcup_{\varrho\in\Sigma(1)}D_{\varrho}
can contribute to the approximation constant, that is,

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}};L)=\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap U;L).

Indeed, the function

X(k)\setminus T\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\quad y\mapsto d(x,y)

is continuous and thus attains its minimum on the compact set
\bigcup_{\varrho\not\in\mathcal{A}}D_{\varrho}
. This minimum is nonzero as the set does not contain
x
by assumption.
Moreover,
D_{\varrho}
does not contain any integral points for
\varrho\in\mathcal{A}
; hence, integral points outside
T
cannot contribute to the approximation constant.

For the rest of the argument, we basically follow the blueprint of
[
14
, § 6]

\mathcal{A}
.
Let
\sigma_{0}\in\Sigma^{\max}
be a maximal cone as in Remark

so that
\sigma_{0}(1)=\{\varrho_{1},\dots,\varrho_{n}\}
; for the remaining rays, we obtain a relation

{\mathcal{R}}_{j}\colon n_{\varrho_{n+j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i,j}n_{\varrho_{i}}=0,\quad 1\leq j\leq r

between rays with positive coefficients

for

by the same remark.

Appealing to Salberger’s seminal work
[

, rational points on split toric varieties can be parametrized in terms of Cox coordinates of universal torsors. We shall begin by sketching some properties of this parametrization and refer the reader to
[
14
, § 3, § 6B1]
for more details. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a set of representatives of the ideal class group of

Then there exists a family

\sigma_{1}
, without the ray
\varrho_{1}
and adjacent to
\sigma_{0}
, contains the ray
\varrho_{n+1}
. It gives rise to the divisor
D(\sigma_{1})
. Let
c_{1}
be the multiplicity of
D_{\varrho_{1}}
in
D(\sigma_{1})
. Then the computation in
[
14
, (54)]
shows that

c_{1}=\delta_{\mathcal{P}}.

According to
[
23
, Lem. 8.9, Rem. 11.23]
, together with the divisor
D(\sigma_{0})
, we have

\frac{{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{1})}}{{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{0})}}=\left(\frac{X_{1}(P)}{\prod_{j=1}^{r}X_{n+j}(P)^{{b}_{1,j}}}\right)^{\delta_{\mathcal{P}}}.

Invoking (
17
), we obtain the bound

(21)

\begin{split}H_{L}(P)&\gg\prod_{v^{\prime}\in\infty_{k}\setminus\{v\}}\left|{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{1})}\right|_{v^{\prime}}\times\left|{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{0})}\right|_{v}\\
&\gg\left|\frac{{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{0})}}{{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{1})}}\right|_{v}=\left|\frac{X_{1}(P)}{\prod_{j=1}^{r}X_{n+j}(P)^{{b}_{1,j}}}\right|_{v}^{-\delta_{\mathcal{P}}}\end{split}

for the height function, where for the second inequality we use the product formula, all the implied constants being uniform. From this and (
20
) we easily deduce that

H_{L}(P)d_{v}(P,x)^{\delta_{\mathcal{P}}}\gg 1.

This shows that

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap U_{\sigma_{0}};L)\geq\alpha_{v}(x,U_{\mathcal{A}}\cap U_{\sigma_{0}};L)\geq\delta_{\mathcal{P}}.

To get an upper bound, we now lift
x\in D_{\mathcal{A}}^{\circ}(k)
to

{\boldsymbol{X}}(x)=(0,x_{2},\cdots,x_{n+r})\in\mathfrak{Y}_{\boldsymbol{c}(x)}(\mathfrak{o})\subset\mathfrak{o}^{n+r}

for a fixed
\boldsymbol{c}(x)\in\mathcal{C}^{r}
.
Let
\varepsilon\in\mathfrak{o}\setminus\{0\}
be fixed. We consider the rational curve
C
defined by

(0,0)\neq(u,v)\mapsto\pi(u\varepsilon,vx_{2},\cdots,vx_{t},x_{t+1},\cdots,x_{n},vx_{n+1},x_{n+2},\cdots,x_{n+r}).

Then by
[
14
, Thm. 2.2]
,
C
is a (smooth) minimal rational curve corresponding to
\mathcal{P}
and
C\cap D_{\mathcal{A}}=x
.
Let
C_{0}=C\cap U_{\mathcal{A}}
be the open curve.
Let
\mathfrak{C}
be the Zariski closure of
C
in
\mathfrak{X}
and let
\mathfrak{C}_{0}=C\cap\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}
.
The point

P_{x}=\pi(\varepsilon,0,\cdots,0,x_{t+1},\cdots,x_{n},0,x_{n+2},\cdots,x_{n+r})\in C_{0}(k)

lies in
\bigcap_{\varrho\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\{\varrho_{1}\}}D_{\varrho}
and admits a lift
{\boldsymbol{X}}(P_{x})=(X_{i}(P_{x}))_{1\leq i\leq n+r}\in\mathfrak{Y}_{\boldsymbol{c}(x)^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{o})
for a certain
\boldsymbol{c}(x)^{\prime}
. Then the Cox coordinates
{\boldsymbol{X}}(P_{x})
satisfy the coprimality condition (cf.
[
10
, Thm. 2.7 (iii)]
,
[
14
, Thm. 3.2]
)

X_{n+1}(P_{x})\mathbf{c(x)^{\prime}}^{-D_{\varrho_{n+1}}}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}X_{i}(P_{x})\boldsymbol{c}(x)^{\prime-D_{\varrho_{i}}}=\mathfrak{o}.

But
X_{n+1}(P_{x})=X_{2}(P_{x})=\cdots=X_{t}(P_{x})=0
.
Hence,
X_{1}(P_{x})\boldsymbol{c}(x)^{\prime-D_{\varrho_{1}}}=\mathfrak{o}
,
which implies that
P_{x}
is integral by an argument analogous to one utilized by Ortmann
[
20
, Prop. 2.4]
, resting on those by Frei and Pieropan
[
10
, Thm. 2.7]
.
This shows that
P_{x}
lifts to an integral point of
\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathfrak{o})
and hence belongs to
\mathfrak{C}_{0}(\mathfrak{o})
as a regular point. As the curve
C
meets the open orbit
T
, it follows from Proposition
2.4
that

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap U_{\sigma_{0}};L)\leq\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{C}_{0}\cap U_{\sigma_{0}};L)=\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{C}_{0};L)=\delta_{\mathcal{P}}.

We next address the case
\mathcal{A}=\{\varrho_{n+1}\}\not\subset\sigma_{0}(1)
.
We have seen that the maximal adjacent cone
\sigma_{1}
satisfies
\varrho_{n+1}\in\sigma_{1}(1)
.
That
\mathcal{P}
is central primitive means that

n_{\varrho_{1}}=-\sum_{i=2}^{t}n_{\varrho_{i}}-n_{\varrho_{n+1}};

using this, we may rewrite the relations
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}
with
2\leq j\leq r
as in (
15
) as

\displaystyle n_{\varrho_{n+j}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq n\end{subarray}}b_{i,j}n_{\varrho_{i}}+b_{1,j}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq t\end{subarray}}n_{\varrho_{i}}+n_{\varrho_{n+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq t\end{subarray}}(b_{i,j}-b_{1,j})n_{\varrho_{i}}-\sum_{t+1\leq i\leq n}b_{i,j}n_{\varrho_{i}}+b_{1,j}n_{\varrho_{n+1}}.

For any
P\in U_{\sigma_{1}}(k)
, let

(y_{2}^{\prime}(P),\cdots,y_{n}^{\prime}(P),y_{n+1}^{\prime}(P))

be the coordinates under the parametrization of
U_{\sigma_{1}}
. We then have

y_{n+1}^{\prime}(P)=\frac{\prod_{1\leq j\leq r}X_{n+j}(P)^{b_{1,j}}}{X_{1}(P)}

for any lift
{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)
.
Now
x\in U_{\sigma_{1}}(k)
and
y_{n+1}^{\prime}(x)=0
.
For any point
P
in
(U_{\sigma_{1}}\cap U_{\mathcal{A}})(k)
, we have
y_{n+1}^{\prime}(P)\neq 0
. Hence, fixing a place
v\in\infty_{k}
, a lower bound of the relevant
v
-adic distance function is given by

(22)

d_{v}(P,x)\gg\min\left(1,\left|\frac{\prod_{1\leq j\leq r}X_{n+j}(P)^{b_{1,j}}}{X_{1}(P)}\right|_{v}\right),

By (
22
), based on a variant of (
21
), we deduce that

\displaystyle H_{L}(P)

\displaystyle\gg\prod_{v^{\prime}\in\infty_{k}\setminus\{v\}}\left|{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{0})}\right|_{v^{\prime}}\times\left|{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{1})}\right|_{v}

\displaystyle\gg\left|\frac{{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{1})}}{{\boldsymbol{X}}(P)^{D(\sigma_{0})}}\right|_{v}=\left|\frac{X_{1}(P)}{\prod_{j=1}^{r}X_{n+j}(P)^{{b}_{1,j}}}\right|_{v}^{\delta_{\mathcal{P}}},

from which we again obtain

H_{L}(P)d_{v}(P,x)^{\delta_{\mathcal{P}}}\gg 1.

These all together imply that

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap U_{\sigma_{1}};L)\geq\alpha_{v}(x,U_{\mathcal{A}}\cap U_{\sigma_{1}};L)\geq\delta_{\mathcal{P}}.

Finally, we lift
x
to

{\boldsymbol{X}}(x)=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{t},x_{t+1},\cdots,x_{n},0,x_{n+2},\cdots,x_{n+r}),

and let
C
be the rational curve defined by

(0,0)\neq(u,v)\mapsto\pi(vx_{1},\cdots,vx_{t},x_{t+1},\cdots,x_{n},u\varepsilon,x_{n+2},\cdots,x_{n+r}),

in
X
, where
\varepsilon\in\mathfrak{o}\setminus\{0\}
is fixed; let
\mathfrak{C}=\overline{C}
be the Zariski closure in
\mathfrak{X}
, and let
\mathfrak{C}_{0}=\mathfrak{C}\cap\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}
. Then, as before, we verify that
C
is minimal and has class
\mathcal{P}
, meets
D_{\mathcal{A}}
precisely in
x
, and the point

\pi(0,\cdots,0,x_{t+1},\cdots,x_{n},\varepsilon,x_{n+2},\cdots,x_{n+r})

lifts to
\mathfrak{C}_{0}(\mathfrak{o})
, whence

\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{U}_{\mathcal{A}}\cap U_{\sigma_{t}};L)\leq\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{C}_{0}\cap U_{\sigma_{t}};L)=\alpha_{v}(x,\mathfrak{C}_{0};L)=\delta_{\mathcal{P}}.

The proof is thus completed.
∎
