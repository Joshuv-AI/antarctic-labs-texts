The effect of binaural pulse modulation (B.P.M) on brain state in depression and anxiety: a case series
Authors: Gerry Leisman, Joseph Wallach, Yanin Machado-Ferrer, Mauricio Chinchilla-Acosta, Abraham Gérard Meyer, Robert Lebovits, Scott Donkin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn this pilot study a binaural pulse modulator was tested to see if it leads to a change in self-reported measures of distress. This binaural pulse modulator produces two frequencies that combine to create a binaural pulse to stimulate the nervous system through a differential auditory tone presentation and the response of the user can be adjusted to the appropriate target tone for effective treatment use. Each individual calibrated the binaural pulse to increase the level of emotion experienced while imagining an experience with a similar emotional valence or while engaged in a cognitive function while also listening to the sound. “Treatment” is based on the client’s control of the binaural pulses to achieve the desired state. Training focuses on specific aspects of their psychological difficulties while listening to an auditory tone, turning a knob until the sound becomes uncomfortable. Finally, another knob is turned to cancel out the uncomfortable noise which appears to be associated with a reduction in distress.Case presentationsFour adult Hispanic participants (three females and one male) were studied with self-reported measures of distress (generalized anxiety disorder 7, Coronavirus Disease Stress Scale, posttraumatic stress disorder checklist for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, and Beck depression scale II) were completed at screening, before treatment, after treatment, 4-weeks post-treatment, and 12-weeks post-treatment. Quantitative electroencephalogram and functional magnetic resonance imaging were also performed before and after treatment.ResultsPreliminary findings indicated that at the end of treatment with binaural pulse modulator there were reported reductions in self-reported levels of distress. Quantitative electroencephalogram as well as functional magnetic resonance imaging changes in brain state were also noted when comparing pre- to post-treatment. Conclusions: binaural pulse modulator use appears to result in temporary changes in self-reported levels of distress during treatment. Limitations of the study are reviewed and directions for further research are offered.

Introduction

Psychological interventions for mental disorders are commonly validated for their clinical rather than their biological effects. However, it is increasingly recognized that a better understanding of the neural changes accompanying successful psychotherapy may have considerable benefits [1]. For example, if we can identify pathological activation patterns in relation to psychiatric symptoms, and if these patterns normalize after intervention, we may use this information in the development of new treatment protocols targeting the functional correlates of specific brain networks. To take the matter one step further, we might even be able to target these pathological networks directly through neurofeedback [2]. Several decades of feedback research with electroencephalogram (EEG) signals have shown that participants can be trained to influence the amplitude or topography of specific components of scalp electric activity [3]. However, it has been very difficult to influence specific mental states or treat psychiatric disorders with EEG-based neurofeedback, probably because of its low spatial specificity and difficulties associated with the poor signal-to-noise ratio provided by single trial-based EEG [4–11]. This phenomenon, which was initially seen in Brain–Computer Interface (BCI) research, has been shown for both healthy and unwell participants. It is known in the literature as BCI illiteracy [4, 9, 12] or BCI inefficiency [6, 13].

It is well known that a coincidence of frequencies exists between neural and musical rhythms [14–17]. Musical rhythms and low-frequency thalamocortical activity have been reported to entrain [16, 18, 19]. Much attention in the literature has been given to the synchronicity of neurodynamic processes and the physiological significance of this phenomenon. It has been demonstrated that synchronization phenomena exert a significant influence in the mechanisms of higher integrative brain functions [20]. This applies to both endogenous neurodynamic processes and neural activity evoked by external stimuli. For example, the development of conditioned reflex is possible at a certain level of synchronization (combination) of external stimuli: conditional and unconditional [21, 22].

The time factor or temporal coincidence of different activations is considered as the most important condition for long-term changes in synaptic efficiency [23–25]. An example of the importance of endogenous synchronization is the message that the activation of attention and consciously predicted arbitrary movements are accompanied by synchronized discharges of neurons of the nonspecific and motor thalamus. The processes associated with the synchronization of neural activity is considered to be one of the important mechanisms of thalamocortical integration [26, 27]. Synchronization of endogenous neural activity with external stimuli is essential for the brain information and stimulation-seeking organ [28, 29]. The literature supports the notion that afferent impulses, combined with certain phases of spontaneous neural activity can lead to a restructuring of the bioelectric activity of the brain.

A binaural pulse modulator (BPM) is a non-typical biofeedback device that, using audio input, stimulates the nervous system. Each individual calibrated the sound to increase the level of emotion (positive or negative) experienced while imagining an experience and while also listening to the sound from the device. Our objective in this pilot study was to examine the possibility of using a BPM-type device to restore the psycho-emotional state by activating the endogenous processes of self-regulation to activate the processes of recovery from anxiety and depression. In particular, we desired to demonstrate that brain networks associated with specific emotions will be regulated by BPM-type systems.

Methods and methodology

The synergetic EEG functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) analysis of cognitive activity is an effective noninvasive means showing localization of brain process, its dynamics, and the relation between the central hemodynamics and electrical activity of the brain, as well as documentation of neuroplastic changes in brain activity [30]. The synchronous EEG–fMRI study of adaptive feedback that allows direct activation of brain structures is of special interest. This method should open up new prospects in the correction of various disturbances of the higher nervous activity and in the instant case in treating depression and anxiety.

In the present work, we employed both continuous EEG (CEEG) and fMRI as well as psychometric testing to identify areas reactive to positive and negative emotional stimuli and then BPM feedback to train participants to upregulate the target areas associated with processing negative stimuli. We aim to demonstrate that brain networks associated with specific emotions will indeed be regulated employing neurofeedback.

Participants

Three female and one male adult participants were examined. Each of the participants was diagnosed with major depression and/or anxiety and evaluated by a psychiatrist and or psychologist and by scores on the Beck Depression Scale or Geriatric Depression Scale, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder test (GAD-7) [31], the Coronavirus Disease (COVID) Stress Scale, and/or the PSTD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) [32]. None of the individuals examined presented with a history of neurological disease or disorder, history of seizures, or trauma either psychological or physical. Each of the participants had physical examinations that ruled out hypothyroidism hyperthyroidism or any form of cancer. Each participant was medication-free including corticosteroids or appetite suppressants. None of the three individuals studied presented with or suffered from potentially comorbid conditions such as asthma. Inclusion criteria

Each participant presented or met the criteria for diagnosis of an anxiety disorder, symptoms of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and/or presented with a high level of distress associated with anxiety based on an objective screening measure. None of the individuals studied were taking any prescribed medication of any kind before and during the study.

Exclusion criteria

None of the individuals studied suffered from a primary psychiatric complaint that was a non-anxiety diagnosis, a diagnosis such as pervasive developmental disorder (PDD) or developmental coordination disorder (DCD), an active or inactive psychotic or thought disorder, active substance abuse or dependence excluding nicotine, caffeine or cannabis, hearing impairment, epilepsy or generalized seizure disorder, cerebral palsy, traumatic brain injury or history of brain surgery, neurologic abnormalities such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), dyslexia, metabolic illness, autoimmune disease, vascular disorder, history of cancer, and not presently breastfeeding.

Case presentations

Case 1

L.C.R. is a left-sided 19-year-old Hispanic female with a high school-level education. L.C.R. presented with significant levels of distress symptoms based on anxiety and demonstrated symptoms of distress related to the post-COVID syndrome. No developmental abnormalities were reported, and no history of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder, or significant physical impairment or disorder was evidenced. Her Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) full-scale intelligence quotient (IQ) was 123. On the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7) scale upon initial evaluation, L.C.R. scored 18, and 2 weeks after, before treatment, L.C.R. again scored 18. After 4 weeks of treatment, when retested, L.C.R. scored 18. L.C.R. scored 17 on the GAD-7 after treatment and 12 weeks later she had a score of 20. On the Beck Depression Scale II, L.C.R., upon initial evaluation, scored 63, and 2 weeks later at the outset of treatment, the score was again reported as 63. After 4 weeks of treatment, her Beck Score was 60 and after the treatment L.C.R. again scored 60.

Procedure

Depression/anxiety evaluation

Each participant was evaluated at the initial evaluation, immediately prior to the study, after 4 and again after 12 weeks of intervention by any of the following standardized instruments: Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7) [31] and/or the COVID Stress Scale and/or the PTSD checklist for the DSM-V and by the Beck Depression Scale [33].

BPM administration

Biofeedback is the process of providing individual information about one’s physiological functions in real-time, most often utilizing electronic devices that allow for continuous ongoing monitoring of the signals that represent the function, such as heart rate or skin conductance. Neurofeedback, a subtype of biofeedback, is a process of recording, monitoring, and providing information about a person’s brainwave patterns. Biofeedback utilizes the principle of classical conditioning to allow a person to be rewarded for changing the signals being monitored. Biofeedback and neurofeedback have demonstrated effectiveness in the treatment for several physiological and psychological disorders including essential hypertension [34], Raynaud’s syndrome [35], seizure disorders [36], ADHD [37], and anxiety [38]. BPM is a nontypical biofeedback device that, using audio input, stimulates the nervous system. The sound is calibrated for each individual to increase the level of emotion (positive or negative) experienced while imagining an experience and while also listening to the sound from the device.

Apparatus: BPM provides auditory stimulation in the range between 0–350 Hz. The auditory stimuli were adjusted in volume for each ear separately. The main auditory frequency adjustment control (frequency) controls ranged from 0–330 Hz and the secondary auditory frequency adjustment (disruptor) controlled an additional offset range from 0–20 Hz. The primary tone-specific level for a setting of 2 for the frequency control was estimated at 75 Hz, and the disruptor at setting of 2 yielded an offset of approximately 20 Hz difference which evokes overtones in the beta range. This difference is what was perceived/combined as a binaural pulse. Bone conduction headphones were employed.

Intervention procedure with BPM: The participants were introduced to the BPM device and were given an explanation of how it was to be used and how the volume control worked separately for each ear. The participants were instructed on how to set and adjust the frequency and disruptor controls. The participants were also instructed to put on the headphones and how to turn on the device to a setting of 2 for frequency and 2 for the disruptor. They listened to the tone for a few minutes to acclimate and become comfortable with the tone. They were able to adjust the volume and control dials until they could feel that the sound was “in their head” and until they found a frequency that they were comfortable with. Then, while they continued to wear and hear the tone, they were instructed to engage in a cognitively demanding task, such as reading or looking for details in images provided, during which they were timed at how long they were able to maintain their concentration. This could be for up to 10–15 minutes. Treatment sessions were 5 days/week for 12 weeks.

EEG

Participants were studied in our laboratory under conditions of controlled temperature (from 24 to 26 °C), noise attenuation, and dimmed lights. CEEG was recorded from 19 standard locations over the scalp according to the 10–20 systems: Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, F7, F8, T3, T4, C3, C4, P3, P4, T5, T6, O1, O2, Fz, Cz, and Pz. After careful cleansing of the skin, disc EEG tin electrodes were fixed using a conductor paste and connected to the input box of the digital continuous EEG monitoring system (Neuronic, S.A.). Monopolar leads were recorded using linked ears as a reference. Technical parameters for EEG were: gain 20,000, pass-band filters 0.1 -70 Hz, “notch” filter at 60 Hz, noise level of 2 μV (root mean squared), sampling frequency 200 Hz, and electrode–skin impedance never higher than 5 KΩ. For monitoring purposes, a 7-bipolar chest electrocardiogram (ECG) lead was simultaneously recorded with 0.5 to 30 Hz EEG filters. CEEG was recorded for 5 minutes and heart rate variability (HRV) was obtained.

EEG visual inspection and selection of samples for quantitative EEG (qEEG): Two experts visually inspected the records to select artifact-free EEG segments. EEG segments of no less than 65 seconds from the noncontaminated records were selected for each experimental session, which were later exported to an ASCII file, using the MEDICID-05 system’s facilities, containing a matrix of the original EEG values corresponding to the segments selected by the specialists for offline processing. Three ASCII files containing the EEG data corresponding to each participant in the different experimental conditions were created and stored for further quantitative analysis.

EEG analysis: EEG samples from every experimental session contained in the ASCII files previously described, were imported by a specifically tailored software tool developed with MATLAB version 7.10.0.499 R2010a (The Mathworks, Inc.). This program included different actions including preprocessing of EEG samples, estimation of the power spectral densities (PSD) for every EEG lead, computation of different spectral indices, and finally an output of these results to a database developed with Microsoft Access to dedicated files.

EEG preprocessing: The EEG values of each of the 19 leads were submitted to a previous preprocessing set of actions consisting of: (a) subtraction of the mean value of the sequence of EEG values to diminish the effect of the DC component of the time series; (b) application of a nonlinear median filter to exclude outliers or abnormally high amplitude values [39]; (c) standard linear detrending to avoid any possible drifts in the series; (d) high pass digital filtering (low cutoff frequency of 0.5 Hz); (e) low pass digital filtering (high cutoff frequency of 55 Hz) using a six order Butterworth filter. For both filtering processes, an algorithm developed by The MathWorks Inc. was applied that, after filtering the data in the forward direction, would reverse the filtered sequence and run it back through the filter producing a zero-phase distortion effect [40].

Grouping of EEG leads for coherence analysis: For short-range intrahemispheric coherence, four regions were considered: an anterior right region, including Fp2-F4, F4-C4, and F8-T4, an anterior left including values from Fp1-F3, F3-C3, and F7-T3, a posterior right region, including T3-T5, C3-P3, and P3-O1, and finally, a posterior right region with values from T4-T6, C4-P4, and P4-O2.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing: All images were acquired using a 0 T MRI scanner (Erlangen, Germany), using a standard diffusion gradient direction scheme (12 diffusion-weighted [DW]images and a b = 0 image), DW-MRI data was acquired using a single-shot Echo-Planar Imaging (EPI) sequence. Two interleaved sets of 25 slices of 6 mm thickness with a distance factor of 100% were acquired to form a volume of 50 contiguous slices of 3 mm thickness covering the whole brain for each subject.

Acquisition parameters were b = 1200 seconds/mm2, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, acquisition matrix = 128 × 128, corresponding to an “in-plane” spatial resolution of 2 × 2 mm2, TE/TR = 160 milliseconds/7000 milliseconds. The above-mentioned acquisition was repeated five times to improve signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). To improve EPI quality, magnitude and phase difference images of a T2 gradient echo-field-mapping sequence were acquired with TE = 7.71 milliseconds and 12.47 milliseconds. Also, a three-dimensional high-resolution T1-weighted Magnetization Prepared - Rapid Gradient Echo (MPRAGE) pulse sequence covering the whole brain was acquired with the following parameters: 160 contiguous sagittal slices of 1 mm thickness, in-plane Field of View (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2, and a matrix size of 256 × 256 yielding a spatial resolution of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. The echo time, repetition time, and inversion time were set to TE/TR/TI = 3.93 milliseconds/3000 milliseconds/1100 milliseconds with a flip angle (FA) = 15°. MPRAGE volumes were spatially normalized to the T1-Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template using the “unified segmentation” approach, available in Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM8) [28].

Graph-theory-based anatomical connectivity: The graph framework used has been widely described by Iturria et al. [43, 44]. In brief, the cerebral volume is represented as a nondirected weighted graph G, in which nodes N correspond to atlas-defined regions and arcs aij to the connections joining them. Atlas was registered with the gray matter volume maps in native space and segmented into 90 regions, using the anatomically labeled template corresponding to the Anterior Axillary Line (AAL). Atlas was developed by the Montreal Neurological Institute and the Individual Brain Atlases using Statistical Parametric Mapping (IBASPM). Thus, the topological properties of the brain anatomical networks were defined based on the 90 × 90 binary graph G.

An iterative streamlined fiber tractography algorithm was employed to find the most probable trajectory connecting voxels of the surfaces of the corresponding anatomical areas. Streamlines exceeding 20 mm and below 500 mm in length and a curvature threshold of ± 90° were used to generate the connectivity matrix for each subject. Statistical analysis

To obtain spatial correspondence between functional and anatomical data, a map of the EEG’s leads was registered to the rendered patient’s scalp in axial, coronal, and sagittal views, using affine transformations. Each EEG channel was labeled according to the closer atlas-defined region. Equivalent connections in anatomical and functional matrices were extracted for regression analysis. The linear correlation between the Z-transformed Coh and ACD scores were tested using pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients for each EEG’s frequency sub-band. Analyses were independently carried out for short and long-range connectivities.

Discussion

Anxiety has been steadily increasing over the last 24 years, particularly among adolescents and young adults [45]. In the 1990s, anxiety was thought to have cost the US economy $42.3 billion to $46.6 billion [46, 47]. Due to COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns, which have significantly increased the frequency of anxiety, adults in the USA were three times more likely to screen positive for anxiety disorders in April/May 2020 compared with 2019 [48]. Early stressful life experiences (ELS) that alter the functioning of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis are often associated with anxiety [49]. A person’s development can be negatively impacted by ELS in all spheres of their life, including their physical, cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and social development [50]. Given this, it is plausible that the COVID-19 pandemic acted as an early warning system (ELS) for a significant number of people during this particular period. Significant evidence pointed to the possibility that ELS may develop into anxiety in adulthood and often causes long-lasting changes to the HPA axis [49]. Specifically, increased HPA axis activity is associated with diminished inhibitory feedback and hypercortisolemia [49]. Higher cortisol levels are indeed found in those with anxiety disorders [51, 52]. This suggests that treatments targeting other components of the HPA axis and cortisol may help manage anxiety.

Many techniques are available for treating anxiety, including behavioral approaches (like exposure, cognitive behavioral therapy, and cognitive strategies), acceptance-based approaches, cognitive strategies, and anti-anxiety drugs (like benzodiazepines, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors) [52–55]. However, anti-anxiety medication response rates can be poor, and many users have severe side effects like sexual dysfunction. Furthermore, it can be difficult to predict with precision which patients will respond well to treatment and which will just exhibit a limited response [53]. Benzodiazepines are prescribed by physicians to treat more severe cases of anxiety; however, these medicines are overused, should only be used when absolutely required, and can develop a physical and psychological addiction if used repeatedly [58]. Between 1996 and 2013, there was an average annual growth of 2.5% in the number of prescriptions for benzodiazepines in the USA [54]. This suggests that there was an increase in the amount of benzodiazepines consumed per 100,000 people, from 1.1–3.6 kg. The overdose rate increased from 0.58–3.07 per 100,000 adult participants over the same time [54].

Treatment accessibility problems continue even though cognitive behavioral therapy has been effective in treating anxiety [56, 57]. Additionally, as part of the active anxiety treatment component, patients receiving cognitive behavioral therapy must face their fears head-on without turning to emotion-modulation techniques [55]. Many patients suffer greatly as a result of their inability to commit to the therapy process [55].

Many anxiety sufferers find that these traditional therapy approaches do not work for them, and many more face barriers to care, like difficulty accessing psychologists and other mental health specialists [55, 59]. It is therefore essential to identify supplementary approaches that may be useful in addition to or instead of conventional treatments. More precisely, there is a need for an anxiety treatment that is accessible, easy to use, widely deployable, and effective without having the main side effects of anti-anxiety medications like benzodiazepines, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), or serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs). Music-based sound-based anxiety treatments have the potential to address this demand as well as the many people who already use music to help manage their mental health [60].

The dynamics of spatial synchronization deserve study in treating anxiety/depression. We realize that we are here presenting a case series that limits generalizability. However, a larger-scale behavioral study has recently been completed and the data is available from a data repository. According to the neurophysiological data, the dynamics of sound synchronization have potential traction in treating anxiety/depression. According to neurophysiological data, the normal functional state is characterized by increased synchronous phasing of the frontal leads and a noticeable phase shift of the EEG of the occipital parts, that is the predominance of the level of synchronization of the frontal parts over the occipital regions of the brain. There was, as a result of BPM treatment, an increase in the synphase of the frontal parts and a decrease in the synphase of the occipital leads. Therefore, the observed dynamics of spatial synchronization can be interpreted as normalization. Changing the level of synchronization of frontal and occipital leads toward normalization may indicate the processes of restoration of thalamocortical interactions. It can be assumed that to a greater extent, these changes affect the frontothalamic system, the important role of which is shown for the implementation of complex forms of mental activity. Presentation of sounds synchronized with the bioelectric activity of the brain could activate the processes of recovery of mental activity, accelerate the recovery processes after stress and anxiety, and possibly serve as a tool in the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder.

Conclusion

After BPM treatment, there appears to be a statistically significant increment of functional connectivity (global efficiency) in the three patients. An increment of neural activity in the brain is usually coupled to increases in local cerebral blood flow, leading to increased oxygenation that generates the BOLD. Hence, a statistically significant increment of the BOLD signal after BPM intervention indicates an improvement in brain function. The improvement of functional connectivity and the increment of the BOLD signal after BPM intervention indicates an enhancement of brain function.
