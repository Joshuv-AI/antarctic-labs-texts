Navigating complex interactions between psychiatric disorders and their treatment in pregnancy: Clinical implications
Authors: Kai-Dih Juang
Published: 2026

An epidemiological study that found an association between a pharmaceutical agent and an effect may actually be confounded by indication of the agent. This type of confounding is often referred to as protopathic bias. While an underlying disease leads a patient to seek treatment and take the agent, some effects that are caused by the disease may be mistakenly attributed to the therapeutic agent. For example, in the link between sedative-hypnotic medications and dementia,1 it is suggested that the link is partially caused by protopathic bias, although the link may still be valid regardless of protopathic bias.2 Diseases and their treatment form an intriguing interaction in their effects; clinicians and scientists must interpret these data with great caution.

During pregnancy, the decision whether to treat a certain disease with a certain pharmaceutical agent becomes exceptionally complicated. There is a strong tendency to attribute adverse neonatal outcomes to pharmaceutical interventions, often leading to the avoidance of necessary medications. However, untreated maternal psychiatric illness exerts profound, independent effects on babies. Fetuses may be exposed to sustained hypercortisolemia and altered cytokine networks when their mothers experience chronic depression. Hence, maternal depression significantly increases the risk of adverse outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and neurodevelopmental disruptions.3 Differentiating the pathological impact of the untreated illness from the pharmacological impact of its treatment is therefore imperative.

The current longitudinal study by Chen et al4 in this journal offers a sophisticated methodological approach to disentangling these variables. By simultaneously evaluating the effects of maternal depressive disorders and the use of psychotropic medications (antidepressants and benzodiazepines/Z-drugs), the researchers isolated the specific hazards associated with each. Their findings elegantly show that maternal depression during pregnancy acts as an independent risk factor for the development of offspring atopic dermatitis and asthma. Crucially, prenatal exposure to antidepressants did not further elevate this risk, suggesting that previous assumptions regarding antidepressant toxicity in this context may reflect a protopathic bias, that is, a misattribution of the underlying depression’s hazards to its treatment. Conversely, benzodiazepines and Z-drugs exhibited a distinctly different profile: prenatal exposure to these GABAergic agents—particularly during the third trimester—was robustly associated with an increased risk of offspring asthma, independent of the maternal depressive state.4

Depression is very prevalent during the prenatal period. Around 10% to 27% of expectant mothers suffer from depression.5 Current findings presented by Chen et al1 yield critical implications for perinatal psychiatric care. First, maternal depression is not a benign state for the developing fetus; active screening and close monitoring of maternal psychological well-being are essential. Second, psychiatric disorders underlying sleep disturbances must be recognized. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately one-third of individuals suffering from chronic insomnia are actually suffering from depression and/or anxiety.6 Consequently, when an expectant mother presents with complaints of insomnia, clinicians must aggressively screen for underlying mood and anxiety disorders rather than prescribe hypnotics reflexively.

Finally, therapeutic interventions must be carefully stratified based on risk–benefit profiles. The current data from Chen et al.1 suggest that treating major depressive disorder with antidepressants during pregnancy does not confer additional atopic respiratory risks to the offspring, supporting their judicious use when clinically indicated. In contrast, the increased asthma risk linked to benzodiazepines and Z-drugs warrants great caution. Global clinical guidelines recommend cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as first-line treatment. It This recommendation should be followed particularly strictly when treating pregnant women. By deploying CBT-I as the primary intervention for prenatal insomnia, clinicians can effectively minimize the use of pharmacotherapy whenever possible.

As extensively reviewed by Chen et al1 in this journal, the exposure to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and other classical antidepressants is not associated with atopic illness of the offspring. Instead, it is the untreated psychiatric disorder itself that acts as the primary disruptor of fetal development. The profound physiological manifestations of major depressive disorder—characterized by hypercortisolemia and altered immune cytokine profiles—cross the placental barrier, permanently altering fetal brain architecture and sensitizing children’s immune system. Consequently, rigorous treatment of maternal depression is warranted, and use of antidepressants may be appropriate, after weighing the risks and benefits in each individual case. Conversely, prescription of benzodiazepines and Z-drugs for prenatal insomnia should be strongly discouraged, reserved only for patients with severe symptoms who do not respond to CBT-I.

In addition to these clinical implications, the current research also sheds some light on the interaction between the development of the nervous and immune systems. Neurotransmitters and hormones that mediate interactions between these two systems deserve further investigation.
