Do Improving Hepatic Biomarkers Predict Spontaneous Stone Passage? A Retrospective Analysis of ERCP Yield in Suspected Choledocholithiasis
Authors: Zehra Naseem, Renan Prado, Arjun Chatterjee, Leandro Sierra, James Scott Ramey, Stephen Firkins, Akash Khurana, Roma Patel, John McMichael, James Bena, Moises Auron, Prabhleen Chahal, Roberto Simons-Linares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCholedocholithiasis (CDL) is a common indication for endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). While guidelines use static hepatic biomarker values to estimate pre-test probability, the utility of trending these labs (bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase [ALP]) is unclear. It is unknown if improving biomarker trends reliably indicate stone passage and can help avoid unnecessary procedures.ObjectiveTo evaluate whether serial trends in hepatic biomarkers prior to ERCP can predict the presence of suspected CDL in hospitalized patients and guide decisions on ERCP referral.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of 198 patients undergoing ERCP for suspected CDL from 2002 to 2018. Patients were categorized based on biomarker trends, primarily into: Group 1 (normalized bilirubin with normalized ALP or ALP falling ≥ 50%) and Group 2 (all other patients). The primary outcome was the diagnostic yield of CDL on ERCP.ResultsAmong 198 patients, the yield of choledocholithiasis was not significantly different between Group 1 (69.5%) and Group 2 (71.2%) (p = 0.83). Even when redefining Group 1 as patients with “entirely normal hepatic biomarkers,” the yield for stones was 55%. The diagnostic accuracy of the final lab values was poor, with an area under the curve of 0.57 for bilirubin and 0.51 for ALP. Patients in Group 1 had a significantly higher rate of post-ERCP pancreatitis (8.5% vs. 0%, p = 0.012) and a lower risk of septic shock.ConclusionsNormalization or decline in hepatic biomarkers, such as bilirubin and ALP, does not reliably exclude CDL. Over half of the patients with normalized biomarkers were still found to have stones on ERCP. ERCP should be considered when suspicion remains high, regardless of laboratory trends.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10620-026-09963-x.

Introduction

Choledocholithiasis (CDL) is the most common cause of biliary obstruction encountered clinically [1]. Gallstone disease affects approximately 20 million Americans annually, resulting in costs exceeding $6 billion [2]. 10–20% of American adults with symptomatic gallstones have co-existing CDL [3]. CDL often leads to complications due to obstruction, such as cholangitis and gallstone pancreatitis. The diagnosis of CDL is typically complex, requiring a comprehensive approach that combines clinical evaluation, biochemical testing, and advanced imaging techniques. Initial imaging modalities commonly employed include transabdominal ultrasound and computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), endoscopic procedures such as endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), and intraoperative cholangiography [1]. To manage CDL, various interventional options are available, including ERCP, surgical exploration of the bile duct, and percutaneous transhepatic drainage.

ERCP is increasingly utilized for the removal of bile duct stones. However, it carries non-negligible risk, with significant complications (post-ERCP pancreatitis, bleeding, and perforation) occurring in 6–15% of cases and grave complications (death, > 10 days hospitalization) in 1–2% of cases [4]. Hence, clinicians should focus on optimizing the detection of CDL while minimizing the use of unnecessary ERCP procedures. To mitigate complications, current guidelines suggest classifying patients based on their probability of CDL, as indicated by biochemical testing and abdominal ultrasound. This classification enables the use of accurate, non-invasive techniques, such as MRCP or EUS, for appropriate patients before performing ERCP, while others may directly qualify for ERCP. The American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) and European Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) outlined such strategies in their guidelines, which categorize clinical predictors of CDL as “high,” “intermediate,” and “low” (Table 1) [5, 6]. Although studies have assessed the validity and accuracy of ASGE and ESGE guidelines in clinical settings, there is limited literature on the variations and interpretation of isolated trended hepatic biomarkers before ERCP in predicting underlying CDL [2, 7]. Many clinicians find that an elevated serum total bilirubin is highly predictive of CDL, and monitoring hepatic biomarker results over time can be highly informative [8]. Decreasing values often suggest the spontaneous passage of stones, supporting a conservative approach and potentially avoiding unnecessary procedures.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate hepatic biomarker patterns in suspected choledocholithiasis to identify patients in whom the stone had likely passed, defined by normalization or significant improvement in the bilirubin and ALP levels.

Material and Methods

We conducted a retrospective analysis of all consecutive patients who presented with suspected CDL or biliary obstruction and subsequently underwent ERCP at our institution between January 1, 2002, and September 30, 2018. The hospital’s electronic records of the suspected CDL cases were reviewed retrospectively in detail (endoscopic, clinical, and radiological records) to evaluate the presence of the eligibility criteria. Subjects (aged ≥ 18 years) had an intermediate and/or high suspicion for CDL.

A total of 940 patients were included, of whom 742 were excluded and 198 met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Exclusion criteria for the study were patients aged < 18 years, prior history of undergoing ERCP, pregnancy, those with incomplete data records, those with known, suspected, or subsequently proven primary or metastatic carcinoma into the hepatobiliary system, biliary strictures, liver transplant recipients, congenital hepatobiliary anomalies, a diagnosis of primary sclerosing cholangitis or primary biliary cirrhosis, those with a surgically altered upper gastrointestinal anatomy, and patients in whom hepatic biomarkers were not trended. Data extracted for each eligible subject included: age at the time of procedure, sex, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, hepatic biomarker trends (aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and total bilirubin). Imaging studies performed before ERCP, such as ultrasound, CT, MRCP, EUS, or intraoperative cholangiography, were recorded, and imaging findings suggestive of common bile duct (CBD) stones were noted. Daily assessments of hepatic biomarkers were performed for all patients before undergoing ERCP. All patients underwent ERCP within five days of their initial presentation. Patients were monitored for signs, symptoms, and laboratory findings indicative of post-ERCP complications (pancreatitis, bleeding, and perforation) up to 7 days post-procedure. Post-ERCP pancreatitis was defined as the onset of new or worsening abdominal pain consistent with pancreatitis, accompanied by a serum amylase level exceeding three times the upper normal limit, measured up to 7 days post-procedure [9]. Post-ERCP bleeding was classified based on its timing, occurring either immediately during or after the procedure, or within hours to 7 days following ERCP. The presence of overt gastrointestinal bleeding or a significant drop in hemoglobin levels confirmed the diagnosis. Post-ERCP perforations involved the duodenal wall, periampullary region, bile or pancreatic ducts, or the retroperitoneum. Diagnosis required both clinical signs and symptoms of perforation, along with imaging evidence of gas suggestive of a perforation within 7 days of the procedure [9]. To ensure diagnostic accuracy, we evaluated the administration of intravenous fluids and the use of enteral nutrition in patients with suspected PEP [10, 11].

We categorized study groups according to trends in hepatic biomarkers from admission to the time before ERCP. Group 1: Patients with normalized bilirubin and normal ALP, or with normal bilirubin and an ALP that has decreased by at least 50% from the difference between the initial value and the upper limit of normal (ULN), Group 2: A heterogeneous group with patients with elevated bilirubin at presentation (that can decrease but not normalize) and patients with rising hepatic biomarkers. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis in which Group 2 was further subdivided into three subgroups: Group 2a: Falling bilirubin from the value at presentation (but not normalized), and ALP falling ≥ 50%, Group 2b: Falling bilirubin from the value at presentation (but not normalized), and ALP falling ≤ 50%, and Group 2c: Rising hepatic biomarkers (Fig. 1, Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). Statistical Analysis

All available data were analyzed and presented as frequencies (percentages), stratified according to whether a CBD stone was detected on ERCP, and compared between groups using Pearson chi-square tests or Fisher exact tests. Continuous measures were described using means and standard deviations and compared using ANOVA models. The performance characteristics (sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy) of hepatic biomarkers, as well as the performance characteristics of the individual component predictors, were calculated. The association between each component predictor and the presence of CDL was examined using a multivariable logistic regression analysis.

If overall tests were significant, multiple comparisons were performed using Bonferroni corrected significance levels of 0.05/6 = 0.008. To determine the ability of bilirubin or ALP to predict the presence of a stone, we also constructed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The best cut point for discrimination was chosen based on Youden’s index or the closest proximity to perfect prediction. For all statistical analyses, a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. All data analyses were performed using the SAS software version 9.4.

Results

A total of 198 patients who underwent ERCP for suspected CDL or biliary obstruction and had hepatic biomarkers trended were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the patients was 57.2 + / − 22.4 years, and 109 patients (55.1%) were female. There were no differences in the mean age, gender, BMI, or diabetes between the groups.

Before ERCP, significant differences were observed among the groups in ALP and total bilirubin levels. Group 1 had markedly lower levels of both biomarkers compared to Group 2 (ALP: 113.8 U/L vs. 255.8 U/L; total bilirubin: 0.68 mg/dL vs. 3.6; p < 0.001). Findings suggestive of CDL on initial imaging were reported in 145 (73.2%) of patients on right upper quadrant ultrasound and in 50 (25.3%) patients on CT abdomen. However, there was no difference between the groups in the proportion of patients with findings suggestive of CLD on imaging before ERCP. 111 (56%) patients underwent advanced imaging (MRCP or EUS) before ERCP. Post-ERCP pancreatitis occurred significantly more in Group 1 compared to Group 2 (8.5% vs 0%; p value: 0.012). The incidence of other complications was 4.0% (8/198) overall, with rates of 5.1% (3/59) in Group 1 and 3.6% (5/139) in Group 2 (p = 0.68) (Tables 2 and 3). Yields of CDL identified on ERCP were 69.5% in Group 1 and 71.2% in Group 2. Yield for sludge without CDL were 10.2% and 11.5% in Groups 1 and 2, respectively. Readmission for CDL was 6.8% and 7.2%. Interestingly, redefining Group I as “entirely normal hepatic biomarker values (bilirubin, ALP, AST, ALT)” changed the yield of CDL to 55%.

Total bilirubin and ALP are heavily regarded during the clinical decision-making process of pursuing ERCP. The area under the curve (AUC) for ROC analysis of the last bilirubin measure before ERCP was 0.57 (95% CI: 0.48, 0.66) and for ALP was 0.51 (95% CI: 0.42, 0.60). A total bilirubin level of 0.9 served as the value with the best AUROC, with a sensitivity of 0.74 and a specificity of 0.41.

Discussion

Our study found that pre-procedural hepatic biomarker trends are poor predictors of CDL on ERCP. Normalization of these markers did not exclude CDL, as more than half of such patients still had stones on ERCP. Interestingly, patients with normalized biomarkers had a higher risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis and a lower risk of septic shock, indicating a potentially vulnerable group. These results suggest that additional imaging, such as EUS or MRCP, should be considered before proceeding to ERCP. Overall, laboratory trends lack diagnostic accuracy and should not guide timing or risk stratification for ERCP in suspected CDL.

Predicting the necessity for therapeutic ERCP in patients with suspected CDL continues to be challenging. Optimal clinical risk stratification tools aim to precisely identify patients who are ideal candidates for ERCP. However, the pre-test probability threshold for performing ERCP is ≥ 50%, and the accuracy of existing risk stratification guidelines is relatively low, with an overall accuracy of 62.1–63%, a sensitivity of 46.7–54.9%, and a specificity of 68.6–73% [7, 8].

The earliest study assessing laboratory trends for predicting CDL, by Roston et al. in 1997, reported increasing ERCP yields with rising hepatic biomarkers, ranging from 13% in Group 1 to 94% in Group 2c, and advised against ERCP when biomarkers normalized, except in cholangitis. In contrast, our study showed high ERCP yields even among patients with normalized biomarkers (69.7% in Group 1 and 67% in Group 2c), underscoring the poor predictive value of biomarker trends. The higher overall yield in our cohort likely reflects evolving clinical practice and broader ERCP indications over time.

Our findings align with previous studies showing that trending hepatic biomarkers or reclassifying patients by guideline-based risk categories does not improve the detection of CDL. Prior analyses by Adams et al. and Suarez et al. reported only moderate diagnostic performance for the 2010 ASGE criteria, with overall accuracy of 62–63%, sensitivity 47–54.9%, and specificity 68.6–73% [7, 12]. Similarly, He et al. demonstrated that serial biochemical trends, such as normalization or ≥ 30% reduction in bilirubin and ALT, cannot reliably exclude CDL [12, 13]. Although our data showed bilirubin ≤ 0.2 mg/dL achieved 100% sensitivity to exclude CDL, biochemical trends alone should not replace diagnostic imaging [14]. Ding et al. found that stone size, rather than hepatic markers, was the only independent predictor of spontaneous passage [14]. Collectively, these results emphasize that dynamic interpretation of ALP and bilirubin increases complexity without enhancing diagnostic accuracy.

Although ERCP is typically reserved for patients with abnormal biomarkers or severe symptoms due to the low likelihood of CDL in those with normalized values, a substantial proportion of patients with normalized or rapidly improving liver tests were still found to have stones or sludge. Readmission rates for CDL were comparable across groups [5, 6, 15]. Moreover, delaying ERCP in these cases has been linked to longer hospitalizations, higher readmission rates, greater healthcare costs, and worse clinical outcomes, including organ failure, ICU admission, and mortality [16, 17]. Therefore, in our cohort, ERCP was performed based on the overall clinical suspicion for CDL rather than hepatic biomarker trends alone. Decisions to proceed with ERCP were made by a combination of clinical presentation, imaging findings, and guideline-based risk stratification, consistent with ASGE and ESGE recommendations. As hepatic biomarkers may normalize after transient obstruction or partial stone passage, clinicians often proceeded with ERCP when the pre-test probability of choledocholithiasis remained moderate to high despite improving laboratory values. Therefore, hepatic biomarker trends should be interpreted within the broader clinical context, including imaging findings and clinical presentation, rather than being used in isolation to determine the need for ERCP.

Our analysis did not report high sensitivity, specificity, or accuracy of individual laboratory markers (ALP and bilirubin) as predictive factors for CDL. This is similar to the sensitivity of 30.1–42.5% with a specificity of 70.8–82.5% for bilirubin > 4 mg/dL (on initial presentation), and to an overall accuracy of 60.3% for bilirubin as a predictive marker for CDL in studies by Adams et al. and Magalhaes et al. [7, 18]. This reinforces the conclusion that initial laboratory values or trends lack sufficient predictive accuracy to guide clinical decision-making effectively.

In our study, imaging findings of CDL were present in 73.5% of patients on right upper quadrant ultrasound, 23.5% on CT, 32.8% on MRCP, and 5.1% on EUS, with no significant difference in imaging use across groups. Combining imaging with clinical assessment improves diagnostic accuracy, particularly for patients with low to intermediate suspicion, as recommended by ASGE and ESGE guidelines [5, 6]. EUS demonstrates superior sensitivity and specificity compared to MRCP and can be safely performed in conjunction with ERCP [19–22]. It is also the most cost-effective strategy, with a reported management cost of $1,111 versus $1,145 for MRCP-first and $1,346 for ERCP-first approaches [23]. In contrast to this, MRCP has been shown to have lower cost-effective benefits due to overall higher LOS and delay in definitive management for patients with asymptomatic CDL [24, 25]. Notably, in one cohort of 527 patients, 82% of those who underwent MRCP eventually required ERCP or laparoscopic cholecystectomy [22]. Thus, while EUS or MRCP are reasonable initial options in patients with lower pre-test probability [5, 26]. The likelihood of detecting CDL and the risk associated with retained stones remain substantial.

Post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP) is the most frequent and serious complication of gastrointestinal endoscopy, occurring in about 8% of average-risk and 15% of high-risk ERCPs [27]. Therefore, the ASGE emphasizes that the risk of PEP should be carefully considered when performing ERCP for CDL, especially in asymptomatic patients or those without cholangitis, and recommends the use of prophylactic measures such as rectal NSAIDs and pancreatic duct stenting in high-risk cases. [27] The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) also notes that ERCP should be reserved for patients with CDL who have cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction, as small (< 3–3.5 mm), distally located stones with no biliary dilation often pass spontaneously and unnecessary ERCP increases the risk of PEP [28, 29]. In our study, Group 1 showed higher post-ERCP pancreatitis rates (p = 0.014) but lower septic shock rates (p = 0.059), while other outcomes, including cholecystectomy and readmission, were similar across groups. One potential explanation for the higher incidence of PEP observed in Group 1 is that patients with normalized or improving hepatic biochemical markers may have harbored smaller or transiently obstructing choledocholithiasis that had partially or completely passed prior to intervention. In such cases, ERCP may be performed in the setting of a relatively non-dilated common bile duct or papilla, which can necessitate increased instrumentation or more technically challenging cannulation [30, 31]. These procedural factors may contribute to irritation of the pancreatic duct or inadvertent pancreatic duct manipulation, thereby increasing the risk of PEP. Additionally, the clinical improvement in hepatic biomarkers among patients in Group 1 may reflect a lower degree of persistent biliary obstruction, which could partly account for the lower incidence of severe complications such as septic shock observed in this cohort. Conversely, patients in Group 2 demonstrated persistently abnormal or rising hepatic biochemical markers, which may indicate ongoing biliary obstruction or a more advanced disease state. Persistent obstruction may predispose these patients to systemic inflammatory responses and infectious complications, including cholangitis and septic shock, thereby explaining the higher rate of severe systemic outcomes in this group. These findings reinforce the value of further imaging before ERCP, given the increased PEP risk in patients with normalized biomarkers. Prior studies have identified younger age, female sex, difficult cannulation, and asymptomatic disease as PEP risk factors, though normal bilirubin and ALP have not previously been linked to elevated risk [32–34].

While our study has inherent limitations, they are important to contextualize within the scope of its contributions. As a retrospective analysis, there was some variability in the timing and completeness of hepatic biomarker data, which limited our ability to establish precise temporal thresholds for interpreting trends. Additionally, clinical presentations and symptoms of choledocholithiasis were not systematically assessed, as the primary focus was to evaluate the utility of hepatic biomarker trends in guiding ERCP referral and timing. The single-center design may limit generalizability to other settings with different ERCP practices. Lastly, stone-related characteristics and biliary anatomical features were not systematically evaluated. Factors such as stone size, number of stones, and bile duct diameter may influence the likelihood of spontaneous stone passage and the pattern of biochemical marker changes over time. Because these variables were not collected or included in the analysis, their potential impact on the relationship between hepatic biomarker trends and the persistence of CDL could not be assessed. Future prospective studies incorporating detailed stone characteristics and biliary anatomical features may help better define predictors of spontaneous stone passage.

Despite these limitations, our study offers important insights into a relatively underexplored area. It is among the few to assess serial hepatic biomarker trends in hospitalized patients undergoing ERCP, supported by systematically collected daily data rather than isolated laboratory values. The inclusion of a diverse patient population—ranging from asymptomatic choledocholithiasis to cholangitis and biliary pancreatitis—enhances clinical relevance. Furthermore, the 16-year dataset, strict exclusion criteria, and focus on categorizing biomarker patterns provide a robust and generalizable foundation for understanding the diagnostic value of hepatic biomarker trends in the evaluation of suspected choledocholithiasis.

Conclusion

In patients with suspected CDL, it is crucial to recognize that improving or normalized hepatic biomarkers do not reliably indicate disease resolution. In our study, CDL was still detected in 55% of patients who demonstrated normalized hepatic biomarkers at the time of ERCP, underscoring a major limitation of depending solely on biochemical trends. When the pre-test probability or clinical suspicion remains high, ERCP referral remains warranted even in the absence of persistent laboratory abnormalities. Moreover, our findings revealed that patients with normalized biomarkers had a significantly higher risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis, emphasizing the importance of individualized risk–benefit evaluation before intervention. Internal medicine physicians play a pivotal role in identifying high-risk individuals, maintaining a high index of suspicion, and facilitating timely gastroenterology referral to avoid diagnostic delays and adverse outcomes.
