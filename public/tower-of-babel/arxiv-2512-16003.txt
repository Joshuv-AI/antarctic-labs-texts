Abstract
. We introduce and study a new inverse semigroup associated to a separated graph ( E , C ) (E,C) , which we call the Leavitt inverse semigroup . This semigroup is obtained as a quotient of the separated graph inverse semigroup 𝒮 ⁡ ( E , C ) \mathcal{S}(E,C) , introduced in our previous paper [ 8 ] , and it provides a canonical inverse semigroup model for the tame Leavitt path algebra ℒ K ab ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C) over a commutative unital ring K K . Our first main result describes the Leavitt inverse semigroup ℒ ​ ℐ ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{LI}(E,C) as a restricted semidirect product of the free group on the edges of E E acting partially on a certain semilattice, which is isomorphic to the semilattice of idempotents of ℒ ​ ℐ ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{LI}(E,C) . This description, given in terms of Leavitt–Munn trees, yields a normal form for the elements of ℒ ​ ℐ ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{LI}(E,C) . We obtain a normal form for elements of ℒ K ab ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C) , leading to explicit linear bases for ℒ K ab ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C) . Building on this and on the structural properties of ℒ ​ ℐ ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{LI}(E,C) , we prove that the natural homomorphism from ℒ ​ ℐ ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{LI}(E,C) to ℒ K ab ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C) is injective, so that ℒ ​ ℐ ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{LI}(E,C) embeds as the inverse semigroup generated by the canonical partial isometries in ℒ K ab ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C) . Further applications include the determination of natural bases of the kernel 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} of the natural map from the tame Cohn algebra 𝒞 K ab ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C) to the tame Leavitt path algebtra ℒ K ab ​ ( E , C ) \mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C) , the computation of the socle, and a characterization of the isolated points of the spectrum. Several examples, such as the Cuntz separated graph and free separations, are discussed to illustrate the theory.

1.
Introduction

Leavitt path algebras were introduced independently by Abrams and Aranda Pino
[
3
]
and
by Moreno, Pardo and the first author
[
14
]
,
as algebraic analogues of Cuntz–Krieger
C^{*}
-algebras. Since then, they have become a central object of study in the interplay between algebra, functional analysis, and symbolic dynamics, with deep connections to
K
-theory, classification of algebras, and noncommutative geometry, see
[
4
,
2
,
23
]
and the references therein. A particularly fruitful perspective on Leavitt path algebras arises from the theory of inverse semigroups and groupoids, following pioneering ideas of Paterson
[
38
]
and Exel
[
25
]
, which were translated to the algebraic setting independently by Steinberg
[
40
]
and by Clark, Farthing, Sims and Tomforde
[
22
]
. In this framework, inverse semigroups encode the partial symmetries generated by paths in a graph.

In recent years, separated graphs and their associated Leavitt path algebras have attracted considerable attention, see for instance
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
30
]
. Separated graphs generalize ordinary directed graphs by allowing multiple “separations” of the set of edges emitted by a vertex. This setting encompasses both the classical and the free versions of graph algebras, and it reveals a much richer structural behavior. In particular, separated graphs give rise to a hierarchy of algebras — the Cohn path algebra, the Leavitt path algebra, and their tame versions — with the tame versions admitting a natural description via inverse semigroups and groupoids.

The inverse semigroup of a directed graph was introduced by Ash and Hall in
[
17
]
. Since then, it has been studied in several papers, often in relation with the structure of Leavitt path algebras, see for instance
[
29
,
33
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
. In particular, a certain quotient
\mathcal{LI}(E)
of the graph inverse semigroup
\mathcal{S}(E)
was introduced and studied in
[
35
]
. This inverse semigroup
\mathcal{LI}(E)
, called the Leavitt inverse semigroup of
E
, is characterized by the fact that it coincides with the multiplicative subsemigroup of the Leavitt path algebra
\mathcal{L}_{K}(E)
generated by the natural copy of
E^{0}\cup E^{1}\cup(E^{1})^{*}
.

The purpose of this paper is to develop a systematic theory of
normal forms
for tame Leavitt path algebras of separated graphs, through the introduction of a new inverse semigroup, the
Leavitt inverse semigroup

\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
of the separated graph
(E,C)
. This semigroup is defined as a quotient of the separated graph inverse semigroup
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
introduced in
[
8
]
, by imposing certain additional natural relations. It can be described concretely in terms of Leavitt–Munn trees (see Section
3
for the detailed definitions). The Leavitt inverse semigroup
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
generalizes the homonymous semigroup
\mathcal{LI}(E)
described before, to which it reduces when taking the trivial separation of
E
. We show in Theorem
4.9
that
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
is naturally isomorphic to the subsemigroup of the tame Leavitt path algebra
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
generated by
E^{0}\cup E^{1}\cup(E^{1})^{*}
.

Our philosophy is that the Leavitt inverse semigroup
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
is the appropriate inverse semigroup to consider when working with tame Leavitt path algebras of arbitrary separated graphs, in the same way that the graph inverse semigroup
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
is the natural object to consider when working with tame Cohn algebras
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
. Accordingly, the consideration of the Leavitt inverse semigroup not only leads naturally to a new normal form for elements of
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
, it also leads naturally to a different description of the points of the
tight spectrum

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{tight}}
of
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
. Indeed, we directly show that the tight spectrum of
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
is equivariantly homeomorphic to the tight spectrum of
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
, and this fact leads to a new model for the
tight groupoid

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tight}}(E,C)
of the separated graph, that we call the
Leavitt model
of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tight}}(E,C)
, see Section
7
. Incidentally, this leads also to the question of whether the two semigroups
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
and
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
are
consonant
, a new concept recently introduced by Ruy Exel
[
26
]
. A proof outline is given in Remark
7.8
.

Using these tools and a recent result
[
21
]
on the socle of Steinberg algebras, we are able to completely characterize the socle of tame Leavitt path algebras
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
. The determination of the socle is an important structural problem that has been addressed for different classes of combinatorial algebras, including Leavitt path algebras of non-separated graphs
[
16
]
, Kumjian-Pask algebras associated to
k
-graphs
[
20
]
and one-sided shift algebras
[
27
]
.

The tame Cohn algebra
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
and the tame Leavitt algebra
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
are related by the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\longrightarrow 0,

where
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}(E,C)
is the ideal of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
generated by all the elements
q_{X}=v-\sum_{e\in X}ee^{*}
, where
X\in C_{v}
,
v\in E^{0}
, and
|X|<\infty
.
For Leavitt path algebras of non-separated graphs, the structure of the ideal
\mathcal{Q}
plays a central role in the study of (bivariant)
K
-theory and its applications to classification; see
[
24
,
23
]
. In that case, the ideal
\mathcal{Q}
is always contained in the socle of the Cohn path algebra, as follows from
[
2
, Propositions 1.5.8 and 1.5.11]
, but we show in the present paper that this is rarely the case for separated graphs, where the structure of the ideal
\mathcal{Q}
is much more complicated. However we are able here to compute a linear basis for the ideal
\mathcal{Q}(E,C)
for an arbitrary separated graph
(E,C)
, which is a first step in order to deepen our understanding of its structure.

We conclude the introduction with an outline of the paper. After a section of preliminaries,
our first main theorem (Theorem
3.15
) describes
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
as a semidirect product of the free group
\mathbb{F}(E^{1})
acting partially on a semilattice naturally associated to
(E,C)
, which is isomorphic to the semilattice of idempotents of
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
. This provides a transparent structural picture of
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
and a normal form for its elements.

Section
4
contains our results on the normal form for the elements of the tame Leavitt path algebra
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
.
In fact, we obtain explicit linear bases for all components of its canonical partial crossed product decomposition (Theorem
4.6
).
We observe in Example
4.7
that, for a non-separated graph
E
, our basis for
\mathcal{L}_{K}(E)
is closely related to the one obtained by Alhamadi, Alsulami, Jain and Zelmanov in
[
5
]
. As in
[
5
]
, the basis we find for
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
depends on a
choice function
, which is a function
\mathfrak{E}\colon C^{\mathrm{fin}}\to E^{1}
such that
\mathfrak{E}(X)\in X
for all
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, where
C^{\mathrm{fin}}
is the collection of sets
X\in C
such that
|X|<\infty
. Using the basis of
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
and the structure of
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
, we prove in Theorem
4.9
that
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
embeds faithfully into
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
, as the inverse semigroup generated by the canonical partial isometries.

In Section
5
, we show that for any separated graph
(E,C)
, the tame Cohn path algebra
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
is closely related to the Leavitt path algebra of another separated graph
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
, which is explicitly built from
(E,C)
. Indeed, we work more generally within the class of (tame) Cohn-Leavitt path algebras, also known as relative Cohn path algebras, which interpolate between Cohn and Leavitt algebras. This degree of flexibility is very convenient, as shown already in the non-separated situation in e.g.
[
39
]
. We use this relationship between tame Leavitt and Cohn algebras and our previous results to obtain a linear basis for the ideal
\mathcal{Q}(E,C)
, which is the kernel of the canonical projection

\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\to\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
(Theorem
6.11
).

Section
7
contains the computation of the spectrum and the tight spectrum of the Leavitt inverse semigroup

\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
. As mentioned above, we show that the tight spectrums of
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
and
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
are equivariantly homeomorphic.
As a result, we offer three different models for the tight groupoid of a separated graph: the
complete model
, the
standard model
, and the
Leavitt model
. For each specific problem, it might be that one of the three models is better suited than the others. The Leavitt model is specially useful to tackle the problem of computing the socle of a tame Leavitt path algebra.
We completely solve this problem in Section
8
, by combining our techniques with a recent result on the socle of Steinberg algebras
[
21
]
.

Finally, Section
9
discusses examples and applications. We use our techniques to explicitly compute the ideal

\mathcal{Q}(E,C)\cap\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
for any separated graph
(E,C)
and any field
K
(Theorem
9.4
).
We then consider the example of the Cuntz separated graph, endowed with the free separation (Example
9.7
) and the important example of the semigroup algebra of the free inverse monoid
\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)
on a set
X
(Example
9.9
).
For the latter, we use a representation of the free inverse monoid algebra as a full corner of the tame Cohn algebra of a certain separated graph, obtained in
[
8
, Example 4.10]
. We obtain that the socle of the semigroup algebra of the free inverse monoid on a finite set
X
is an essential ideal of the algebra, and we compute its structure. However the socle vanishes for the semigroup algebra
K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]
when
X
is an infinite set.

Our results unify and extend the existing theory of normal forms for Leavitt path algebras, showing that the separated case can be handled in a systematic way through inverse semigroups.
We expect that the Leavitt inverse semigroup will serve as a fundamental tool for future work on the structure and classification of Leavitt path algebras and of the associated
C^{*}
-algebras.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we review fundamental definitions and results from
[
8
]
. We refer the reader to
[
8
]
for further information.

2.1.
Directed graphs

A
directed graph

E
is a quadruple
E=(E^{0},E^{1},s,r)
consisting of two sets
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
and two maps
s,r:E^{1}\rightarrow E^{0}
. The elements of
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
are called vertices and edges and the maps
s,r
are called the source and range maps, respectively. We do not require our graphs to be finite or countable.

A
finite path
in
E
is a sequence of edges of the form
\mu:=e_{1}\ldots e_{n}
with
r(e_{i})=s(e_{i+1})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
. The length of
\mu
is
|\mu|:=n
and paths with length
0
are identified with the vertices of
E
(we set
s(v)=r(v)=v
). We denote by
E^{n}
the set of all finite paths with length
n
and
\text{Path}(E):=\displaystyle\cup_{n=0}^{\infty}E^{n}
denotes the set of all paths of
E
. We can extend the source and range maps to
\text{Path}(E)
in the obvious way: if
\mu=e_{1}\ldots e_{n}\in\text{Path}(E)
, then
s(\mu)=s(e_{1})
and
r(\mu)=r(e_{n})
. Given two paths
\mu,\nu\in\text{Path}(E)
with
r(\mu)=s(\nu)
, one obtains a new path
\mu\nu
by concatenation with
|\mu\nu|=|\mu|+|\nu|
.

Given a graph
E
, we define its
extended graph
(also called the
double graph
of
E
)
as the new graph
\hat{E}=(E^{0},E^{1}\cup E^{-1},r,s)
, containing
E
as a subgraph, and where we set

s(e^{-1}):=r(e)
,
r(e^{-1}):=s(e)
for all
e\in E^{1}
. This construction is standard in inverse semigroup methods, as it allows the incorporation of formal inverses of edges.

2.2.
Separated graphs

Separated graphs were introduced to interpolate between classical and free constructions, and they naturally give rise to richer inverse semigroups.
A
separated graph
is a pair
(E,C)
consisting of a graph
E=(E^{0},E^{1},s,r)
and a
separation

C=\bigsqcup_{v\in E^{0}}C_{v}
on
E
, consisting of partitions
C_{v}
of
s^{-1}(v)\subseteq E^{1}
into pairwise disjoint nonempty subsets (with
C_{v}=\emptyset
if
v
is a sink, i.e., if
s^{-1}(v)=\emptyset
). The
trivial separation
is the separation with
C_{v}=\{s^{-1}(v)\}
for all non-sinks
v\in E^{0}
; a graph with the trivial separation is also called trivially separated or a non-separated graph. The
free separation
of
E
is the finest separation, where each
s^{-1}(v)
is separated into singletons, that is,
C_{v}=\{\{e\}:e\in s^{-1}(v)\}
.

2.3.
Inverse semigroups

An inverse semigroup is a semigroup
S
endowed with an involution
s\mapsto s^{-1}
satisfying

(1)\,\,ss^{-1}s=s\quad\mbox{and}\quad(2)\,\,(ss^{-1})(tt^{-1})=(tt^{-1})(ss^{-1})\quad\mbox{for all }s,t\in S.

In this case, the pseudo-inverse
s^{-1}
of
s\in S
is uniquely determined by the relations
ss^{-1}s=s
and
s^{-1}ss^{-1}=s^{-1}
. We write
\mathcal{E}(S)
for the semilattice of idempotents of
S
.
Inverse semigroups are naturally endowed with a partial order:

s\leq t\Leftrightarrow ts^{-1}s=s\Leftrightarrow ss^{-1}t=s.

Given a semigroup
S
, we may always add a (formal) zero element
0
, getting a semigroup with zero
S_{0}=S\sqcup\{0\}
, as well as a unit
1
, obtaining a semigroup with unit (i.e. a monoid)
S_{1}=S\sqcup\{1\}
.
Our semigroups of interest will usually have already a zero, sometimes a unit. If
S
already has a unit, we shall generally write
S^{*}=S\backslash\{1\}
, and if
S
has a zero,
S^{\times}=S\backslash\{0\}
. Note that these sets are typically not subsemigroups.

We refer the reader to
[
31
]
for more information about inverse semigroups.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup with zero and
\Gamma
a group. A
partial homomorphism
from
S
to
\Gamma
is a map
\pi\colon S^{\times}\to\Gamma
such that
\pi(st)=\pi(s)\pi(t)
whenever
st\neq 0
. This notion appears frequently when describing inverse semigroups as (restricted) semidirect products.
A partial homomorphism
\pi
is
idempotent pure
if
\pi^{-1}(1)=\mathcal{E}(S)^{\times}
.
If such an idempotent pure partial homomorphism exists, we say that
S
is
strongly
E^{*}
-unitary
.
By
[
8
, Proposition 2.1]
, an inverse semigroup with
0
is strongly
E^{*}
-unitary if and only if it is a restricted semidirect product
S=\mathcal{E}(S)\rtimes_{\theta}^{r}\Gamma
, for a partial action
(D_{g},\theta_{g})
of
\Gamma
on
\mathcal{E}(S)
.

2.4.
Fundamental groupoid of a graph,
C
-separated paths and
C
-compatible trees

Recall that the free group
\mathbb{F}(X)
on a set
X
can be defined as the set of reduced words in the free monoid on
X\cup X^{-1}
, with the product given by

\alpha\cdot\beta=\text{red}(\alpha\beta)
where
\alpha\beta
is the concatenation of
\alpha
and
\beta
and
\text{red}(-)
indicates reduction of words . Given a directed graph
E
, a similar process leads to the
fundamental groupoid

\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
of
E
, which plays an important role in this work. First consider the path
*
-semigroup
\mathcal{P}(E)
, whose nonzero elements are exactly the paths on
\hat{E}
, and the product of two paths
\lambda
and
\mu
is given by its concatenation
\lambda\mu
if
r(\lambda)=s(\mu)
, or
0
if
r(\lambda)\neq s(\mu)
.
The fundamental groupoid
\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
is the set of
reduced paths
in
\mathcal{P}(E)
, which are the paths that do not contain subpaths of the form
xx^{-1}
for
x\in\hat{E}^{1}
.
The product in
\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
is defined as

\alpha\cdot\beta=\text{red}(\alpha\beta)

whenever
r(\alpha)=s(\beta)
, where again
\text{red}(-)
indicates the reduction process. This is a groupoid with set of units
E^{0}
. Note that
g\cdot g^{-1}=s(g)
and
g^{-1}\cdot g=r(g)
for all
g\in\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
. It is important to take into account that we will always use the notation
\alpha\cdot\beta
to indicate the product in
\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
, while the notation
\alpha\beta
is used for the concatenation product. Whenever we have an expression of the form
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n}
for an element of
\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
, we will generally understand that this expression is in reduced form as it stands.

General nonzero elements of
\mathcal{P}(E)
will be called
paths
, and elements of
\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
will be called
reduced paths
.

We define the
prefix order

\leq_{p}
on
\mathcal{P}(E)
by
\mu\leq_{p}\nu
if there is
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(E)
such that
\nu=\mu\gamma
. A
lower subset
of
\mathcal{P}(E)

is a subset
L
of
\mathcal{P}(E)
such that, for
\lambda\in L
, we have
\nu\in L
whenever
\nu\leq_{p}\lambda
.

We denote the Cayley graph of
\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
by
\Gamma_{E}
. We have
\Gamma_{E}^{0}=\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
and there is an edge
(g,e)
from
g
to
g\cdot e
whenever
g,g\cdot e\in\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
, for
e\in E^{1}
.
We have
\Gamma_{E}=\bigsqcup_{v\in E^{0}}K_{v}
, where
K_{v}
are the connected components of
\Gamma_{E}
, consisting of all the paths
\gamma
such that
s(\gamma)=v
.

We now introduce the notion of a
C
-separated path.

Definition 2.1
.

[
8
, Definition 3.6]

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph.
A
C
-separated path
is a reduced path
w=y_{1}\cdots y_{n}\in\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
, with
y_{i}\in\hat{E}^{1}
, that does not contain any subpath of the form
x^{-1}y
with
x,y\in X
for
X\in C
. For
v\in E^{0}
, we denote by
\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
the set of all
C
-separated paths
\gamma
such that
s(\gamma)=v
. The set
\mathbb{F}_{C}:=\bigsqcup_{v\in E^{0}}\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
is the set of all
C
-separated paths.

We now come to the crucial concept of
C
-compatibility of
C
-separated paths.

Definition 2.2
.

[
8
, Definition 3.7]

Let
v\in E^{0}
and
\gamma,\nu\in\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
. We say that
\gamma
and
\nu
are
C
-compatible
in case
\nu^{-1}\cdot\gamma=\text{red}(\nu^{-1}\gamma)\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
. Otherwise
\gamma
and
\nu
are said to be
C
-incompatible
. A subset
T
of
\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
is said to be
C
-compatible if each pair of elements of
T
is
C
-compatible.

In terms of the Cayley graph
\Gamma_{E}
of
E
, a non-empty lower subset of
\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
, for
v\in E^{0}
, is a subtree
T
of the connected component

K_{v}
of
\Gamma_{E}
, containing the base point
v
of
K_{v}
.
Such a non-empty lower subset
T
is
C
-compatible if and only if the geodesic path joining two arbitrary vertices
\gamma,\nu
of
T
is a
C
-separated path, see
[
8
, Remark 3.8]
. The set of all non-empty finite lower
C
-compatible subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
is denoted by
\mathcal{X}(v)
. As remarked above,
\mathcal{X}(v)
can be seen as a certain family of finite subtrees of
K_{v}\subseteq\Gamma_{E}
, and these play a central role in the description of
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
in Section
3
.

For a non-empty subset
A
of
\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
, we write
A^{\downarrow}=\{x\in\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E):x\leq_{p}a,\,\mbox{for some }a\in A\}
for the lower subset generated by
A
with respect to the prefix order. If
A=\{a\}
is a singleton, we shall also write
a^{\downarrow}:=\{a\}^{\downarrow}
to simplify the notation. Note that
A^{\downarrow}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{C}
whenever
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{C}
.

2.5.
Inverse semigroup, tame Cohn path algebra, and tame Leavitt path algebra of a separated graph

We start with the definition of the inverse semigroup of a separated graph, which generalizes the usual construction of the graph inverse semigroup.

Definition 2.3
.

[
8
]

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. The
inverse semigroup
of
(E,C)
is the universal inverse semigroup
\mathcal{S}(E,C)

generated by
E^{0}\cup\hat{E}^{1}=E^{0}\cup E^{1}\cup E^{-1}
subject to the following relations:

(1)

vw=\delta_{v,w}v
for all

;

(2)

s(x)x=x
for all

;

(3)

xr(x)=x
for all
x\in\hat{E}^{1}
;

(4)

e^{-1}f=\delta_{e,f}r(e)
for all

with

.

Definition 2.4
.

[
11
]

Let

be a commutative ring with an involution
*

be a separated graph. The Cohn path algebra

is the universal

-algebra over

and elements

.

The Leavitt path algebra

is the universal
*
-algebra over

generated by the same generators and relations above together with the Cuntz–Krieger relation:

(SCK2)

\sum_{e\in X}ee^{*}=v

.

Given a

-algebra

with a subset

consisting of partial isometries (i.e.

for all

, as follows.
Let

be the

-subsemigroup of

[e(x),e(y)]

, where

.
Then

is the

*
-algebra associated to

. It has the property that the
*
-subsemigroup of

generated by the canonical image of

is an inverse semigroup.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
K
be a commutative ring with an involution
*

be a separated graph. The

-algebra

, Lemma 6.15]

\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\cong\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)/\mathcal{Q}

,

. This shows that

We summarize in the next two theorems the structure of the

-algebras
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
and
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
. For an inverse semigroup

The Steinberg algebra of an ample groupoid

will be denoted by

.

Theorem 2.6
.

[
8
, Proposition 6.5 and Corollary 6.10]

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. Then we have

-isomorphisms

\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\cong K[\mathcal{S}(E,C)]\cong A_{K}(\mathcal{G}(E,C))\cong C_{K}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})\rtimes\mathbb{F},

where

is the free group on

.

Theorem 2.7
.

[
8
, Theorem 6.20]

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. Then we have

-isomorphisms

\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\cong A_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tight}}(E,C))\cong C_{K}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{tight}})\rtimes\mathbb{F},

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{tight}}
is the tight spectrum of

.

The isomorphisms

to the tight spectrum

for details.

3.
The Leavitt inverse semigroup of a separated graph

In this section we introduce a new inverse semigroup associated to a separated graph
(E,C)
, namely the
Leavitt inverse semigroup
of
(E,C)
. This generalizes the Leavitt inverse semigroup of a directed graph, introduced in
[
35
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. The
Leavitt inverse semigroup
of
(E,C)
is the universal inverse semigroup
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)

generated by
E^{0}\cup\hat{E}^{1}=E^{0}\cup E^{1}\cup E^{-1}
subject to the following relations:

(1)

vw=\delta_{v,w}v
for all
v,w\in E^{0}
;

(2)

s(x)x=x
for all
x\in\hat{E}^{1}
;

(3)

xr(x)=x
for all
x\in\hat{E}^{1}
;

(4)

e^{-1}f=\delta_{e,f}r(e)
for all
e,f\in X
with
X\in C
.

(5)

ee^{-1}=s(e)
for all
e\in E^{1}
such that
\{e\}\in C
.

In other words,
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
is the quotient of the inverse semigroup
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
of
(E,C)
(as in Definition
2.3
) by the congruence generated by the relations (5).

We show later that
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
is isomorphic to the inverse semigroup generated by
E^{0}\cup E^{1}
in the abelianized Leavitt path algebra
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
, for any commutative unital ring
K
(see Theorem
4.9
). This was shown in
[
35
]
in the non-separated case.

We start by establishing a normal form for the elements of
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
. For this, we need to recall basic notions and terminology from
[
8
]
.

We will use the notation of
[
8
]
. Recall from Definition
2.1
that for

,
\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
is the set of all the
C
-separated paths

such that

. The set

\mathcal{X}(v)
is the set of all non-empty finite lower
C
-compatible subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
, and
\mathcal{X}=\bigsqcup_{v\in E^{0}}\mathcal{X}(v)\bigsqcup\{0\}
. As in
[
8
, Definition 3.13]
, we set
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{X}/{\sim}
, where
\sim
is the congruence on
\mathcal{X}
generated by
g^{\downarrow}\sim(gx^{-1})^{\downarrow}
, where
g\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
,
x\in E^{1}
, and
gx^{-1}
is reduced as it stands.

For

, the set
\mathcal{X}_{0}(v)
is the set of those

such
that each element in
\mathrm{max}(I)
does not end in

[

, Notation 3.17]

[

.

We denote by
\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
the fundamental groupoid of

(see Subsection

).

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. For each

, write

g=g_{L}w,

where

is either

or a prefix of

ending in an edge

such that

, with

, and

,

, where for each

or

.

Observe that due to relations (4) and (5) in Definition
3.1
, we have
g_{L}g_{L}^{*}=gg^{*}
in
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
for each

Recall that an
inverse category
is a category
C
such that for each arrow
\gamma
there is a unique arrow
\gamma^{-1}
such that
\gamma=\gamma\gamma^{-1}\gamma
and
\gamma^{-1}=\gamma^{-1}\gamma\gamma^{-1}

For each graph

\hat{E}
by the congruence generated by the relations

and
(\gamma\gamma^{-1})(\nu\nu^{-1})\sim(\nu\nu^{-1})(\gamma\gamma^{-1})
whenever

, see
[

, Section 4]
.

Given an inverse category

we can form an inverse semigroup

by adjoining a zero to

and declaring any undefined product to be

. We denote by
\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)
the inverse semigroup

associated to the free inverse category

. We call

the

of

.

Let
\Gamma_{E}
be the Cayley graph of the groupoid
\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
. Hence we have

\Gamma_{E}^{0}=\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
, and there is an edge
(g,e)
from

to

whenever

,

.

The elements of

can be interpreted as certain Munn trees in

, see

and
[

Definition 3.3
.

[

, Definition 4.1(a)]

Let

be a directed graph.
A
Munn

-tree
is a pair
(T,g)
, where

is a finite connected subgraph (hence a subtree) of
\Gamma_{E}
containing the unit

of the connected component

corresponding to

, and

is a vertex of

.

We now recall two basic results from

]

Proposition 3.4
.

[
8
, Proposition 4.2]

Let
E
be a directed graph. Then the free inverse semigroup

associated to

is isomorphic to the semigroup of all Munn

-trees, together with

, endowed with the product

(T_{1},g_{1})\cdot(T_{2},g_{2})=\begin{cases}(T_{1}\cup g_{1}\cdot T_{2},\,g_{1}\cdot g_{2})&\text{ if }r(g_{1})=s(g_{2})\\
\qquad\,\,\qquad\,\,0&\text{ if }r(g_{1})\neq s(g_{2})\end{cases}.

The inverse of a Munn

-tree

is given by

(T,g)^{-1}=(g^{-1}\cdot T,g^{-1}).

Corollary 3.5
.

[
8
, Corollary 4.3]

Let
E
be a directed graph. Then
\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)
is strongly
E^{*}
-unitary and thus it is a restricted semidirect product

\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)=\mathcal{E}(\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E))\rtimes_{\theta}^{r}\mathbb{F},

where

is the free group on
E^{1}
.

For an arbitrary directed graph

, we may consider a subset

of edges in

, and impose relations
(1)-(3) from Definition
3.1
, relations
e^{-1}e=r(e)
for all edges

in

, and relations

(5)
U

for all

obtaining a certain inverse semigroup, which will be denoted here by

. As we will see in Proposition

, all these inverse semigroups follow the same pattern.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
E
be a directed graph and let
U
be a subset of
E^{1}
. For each
g\in\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
, we can uniquely write
g=g_{U}w
, where
g_{U}
is either a vertex or a prefix of

such that
g_{U}
does not end in

and does not end in an edge belonging to

, and

is a product of elements of the form

for

and elements of the form

for

.

We say that

canonical decomposition
of

with respect to

. For

, we obtain the decomposition

from
[

, Notation 3.15]

is a separated graph and

is the set of elements

such that

from Definition

Definition 3.7
.

Let
E
be a directed graph and let
U
be a subset of
E^{1}
.
We define a
Toeplitz-Munn
(E,U)
-tree
as a pair
(T,g)
where
T
is a finite
connected subgraph of
\Gamma_{E}
containing the root
v
corresponding to its connected component, and such that all maximal elements of
T
do not end in
E^{-1}
, and do
not end in an edge
e\in U
, and
g=g_{U}w
satisfies that
g_{U}\in T
.

From now on, we will identify the free inverse semigroup
\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)
on
E
with the inverse semigroup
\mathcal{M}
of Munn
E
-trees, together with
0
(see Proposition
3.4
).
We will simply refer to the first component
T
of a Munn
E
-tree
(T,g)
as an
E
-tree. The set of
E
-trees is identified with the set
\mathcal{E}(\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E))^{\times}
of nonzero idempotents of
\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)
.

Let
U
be a subset of
E^{1}
. Let
\sim_{U}
be the congruence on
\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)\cong\mathcal{M}
generated by the relations
e^{-1}e\sim_{U}r(e)
for
e\in E^{1}
and
ee^{-1}\sim_{U}s(e)
for
e\in U
. In terms of the representation as Munn

-trees,
\sim_{U}
is the congruence generated by the relations
(ge^{-1})^{\downarrow}\sim_{U}g^{\downarrow}
for all

such that
ge^{-1}\in\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
, and
(ge)^{\downarrow}\sim_{U}g^{\downarrow}
for all

such that

.

Obviously, we have

\mathcal{T}_{U}(E)\cong\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)/{\sim_{U}}\cong\mathcal{M}/{\sim_{U}}.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
U
be a subset of
E^{1}
, and set
\mathcal{M}_{U}=\mathcal{M}/{\sim_{U}}
.
For each
E
-tree
T
, the
\sim_{U}
-equivalence class
[T]_{U}
of
T
in
\mathcal{M}
contains a maximum element
T_{U}
with respect to the natural order in the inverse semigroup
\mathcal{M}
. This maximum element is the unique element
T_{U}
of
[T]_{U}
such that each maximal element of
T_{U}
does not end in
E^{-1}
and does not end in
e
, for
e\in U
.

Proof.

Let
T
be an
E
-tree. For each
g\in\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)
, we consider its canonical decomposition
g=g_{U}w
with respect to
U
(see Definition
3.6
).

Now set
A:=\{g_{U}:g\in T\}
, and
T_{U}=\mathrm{max}(A)^{\downarrow}
. Then the set of maximal elements of
T_{U}
is exactly
\mathrm{max}(A)
, and thus all maximal elements of
T_{U}
do not end in
E^{-1}
and do not end in
U
. Since
T_{U}\subseteq T
, it follows that
T\leq T_{U}
.

Also, as in the proof of
[
8
, Lemma 3.16]
, we have
T_{U}\sim_{U}T
.

We now want to show that, if
S
is an
E
-tree and
T\sim_{U}S
in
\mathcal{M}
, then
S_{U}=T_{U}
. Let
(T_{0},g_{0}),(T_{1},g_{1}),\dots,(T_{n},g_{n})
be a sequence of Munn
E
-trees, with
(T_{0},g_{0})=(T,1)
and
(T_{n},g_{n})=(S,1)
, and such that for each
0\leq i<n
, we can pass from
(T_{i},g_{i})
to
(T_{i+1},g_{i+1})
, or from
(T_{i+1},g_{i+1})
to
(T_{i},g_{i})
, by a single step either of the form

(X,h)(\{r(e)\},r(e))(Y,k)\sim_{U}(X,h)((e^{-1})^{\downarrow},r(e))(Y,k)

for
e\in E^{1}
, or of the form

(X,h)(\{s(e)\},s(e))(Y,k)\sim_{U}(X,h)(e^{\downarrow},s(e))(Y,k)

for
e\in U
.

In both cases, we have
(Z,h\cdot k)\sim_{U}(Z\cup h\cdot x^{\downarrow},h\cdot k)
, where
x\in E^{-1}\cup U
, and
Z=X\cup h\cdot Y
is an
E
-tree such that
h\in Z
. It thus suffices to show that in such a situation, one has
Z_{U}=(Z\cup h\cdot x^{\downarrow})_{U}
. If
hx
is not reduced then

h\cdot x\in Z
and we indeed have
Z=Z\cup h\cdot x^{\downarrow}
. If
hx
is reduced, then
h_{U}=(hx)_{U}
and hence
\{z_{U}:z\in Z\}=\{z_{U}:z\in Z\cup h\cdot x^{\downarrow}\}
, showing that

Z_{U}=(Z\cup h\cdot x^{\downarrow})_{U}
, as desired. Applying inductively this fact to all the consecutive pairs of elements in the sequence
\{(T_{i},g_{i})\}
, we obtain that
T_{U}=S_{U}
. Hence
S\leq T_{U}
for all
S\in[T]_{U}
,
and
T_{U}
is the largest element of
[T]_{U}
in
\mathcal{M}_{U}
. Also it is clear from this fact that

T_{U}
is the unique element in
[T]_{U}
with the property that each maximal element does not end in
E^{-1}\cup U
.
∎

The following lemma is a straightforward consequence of Lemma

. We leave its proof to the reader.

Lemma 3.9
.

With the above notation, we have, for Munn
E
-trees
(T,g)
and
(S,h)
,

(1)

(T,g)\sim_{U}(S,h)
if and only if
S_{U}=T_{U}
and
g=h
.

(2)

The
\sim_{U}
-equivalence class
[T,g]_{U}
has a largest element, which is
(T_{U}\cup g^{\downarrow},g)
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
E
be a directed graph, let
U
be a subset of
E^{1}
and set
\mathcal{M}_{U}=\mathcal{M}/{\sim_{U}}
.
Then the inverse semigroup
\mathcal{M}_{U}
is isomorphic to the inverse semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{U}\mathcal{M}
consisting of all the Toeplitz-Munn
(E,U)
-trees
(T,g)
, endowed with the product

(T_{1},g_{1})\cdot(T_{2},g_{2})=\begin{cases}\Big((T_{1}^{\prime}\cup g_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{\prime})_{U},\,\,g_{1}\cdot g_{2}\Big)&\text{ if }r(g_{1})=s(g_{2})\\
\qquad\,\,\qquad\,\,0&\text{ if }r(g_{1})\neq s(g_{2})\end{cases}

where
T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}(\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E))
are representatives of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
respectively, such that
g_{1}\in T_{1}^{\prime}
and
g_{2}\in T_{2}^{\prime}
. The inverse is given by
(T,g)^{-1}=((g^{-1}\cdot T^{\prime})_{U},g^{-1})
,
where
T^{\prime}
is any representative of
T
such that
g\in T^{\prime}
.

Moreover, the map
(T,g)\mapsto\omega(g)
determines an idempotent pure partial homomorphism
\mathcal{T}_{U}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

We define a map
\Phi\colon\mathcal{M}_{U}\to\mathcal{T}_{U}\mathcal{M}
by
\Phi([T,g]_{U})=(T_{U},g)
. Note that this map is well-defined since for
(T,g)\in\mathcal{M}
, we have
g\in T
by definition, and thus
g_{U}\in T_{U}
, and moreover
T_{U}=S_{U}
and
g=h
whenever
(T,g)\sim_{U}(S,h)
by Lemma
3.9
(1). Moreover the map
\Phi
is injective again by Lemma
3.9
(1). If
(T,g)
is a Toeplitz-Munn
(E,U)
-tree, then
(T\cup g^{\downarrow},g)
is a Munn
E
-tree, and
\Phi([T\cup g^{\downarrow},g])=(T,g)
, which shows that
\Phi
is surjective.

Since
\Phi
is a bijection, we can translate the product of
\mathcal{M}_{U}
to a product in
\mathcal{T}_{U}\mathcal{M}
, so that
\Phi
becomes a semigroup isomorphism. This leads to the product described in the statement.

Let
\omega\colon\mathfrak{F_{gr}}(E)^{\times}\to\mathbb{F}
be the obvious
idempotent pure partial homomorphism. Then the map

\pi\colon\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)^{\times}\to\mathbb{F}
,
\pi(T,g)=\omega(g)
is the canonical idempotent pure partial homomorphism of
\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)\cong\mathcal{M}
, see
[
8
, Corollary 4.3]
. It is clear that
\pi

factors through an idempotent pure partial homomorphism
\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)^{\times}/{\sim_{U}}\to\mathbb{F}
.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

Note that if
(T,g)
is a Toeplitz-Munn
(E,U)
-tree (see Definition

Let

For the next definition, recall that, whenever

Definition 3.12

Let

(a)

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph and let

be a
C
-compatible

with

-compatible.
In particular, if

is a Leavitt-Munn
(E,C)
-tree, then

is

Proof.

By Lemma

, it is enough to show that if

is an
E

is
C
-compatible, then

is also
C
-compatible.

Let
T
be an
E
-tree such that
T_{L}
is
C
-compatible. Given two vertices

and
h
in
T
, we have to show that
g^{-1}\cdot h
is a
C
-separated path.

We first observe that all elements of
T
are
C
-separated. Take

. By the construction of

we have
g_{L}\in T_{L}
, so that we can write
g=g_{L}w
, where
g_{L}\in T_{L}
and

is a product of inverse edges and of edges

such that

. Since

is
C
-compatible,

is
C
-separated, and it follows that

cannot contain a subword of the form
e^{-1}f
, where

are distinct edges in
E
such that

for

.

Consider now two vertices

. If one of them is a prefix of the other, then we deduce from the above that

is

-separated. Hence we may assume that

and

, where

and

. We need to show that

is

-separated. Since

and

are

-separated, we only have to show that

is not of the form

for

distinct edges in the same set of the partition

. If

is a prefix of

and

is a prefix of

, and

is

or

, hence

is a proper prefix of

∎

We can finally obtain our main result for the inverse semigroup

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. Then the Leavitt inverse semigroup
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
is isomorphic to the inverse semigroup consisting of all Leavitt-Munn
(E,C)
-trees, endowed with the following product

(T_{1},g_{1})\cdot(T_{2},g_{2})=\begin{cases}\Big((T_{1}^{\prime}\cup g_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{\prime})_{L},\,\,g_{1}\cdot g_{2}\Big)&\text{ if }T_{1}^{\prime}\cup g_{1}\cdot T_{2}^{\prime}\text{ is }C\text{-compatible}\\
\qquad\,\,\qquad\,\,0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

where
T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
are any representatives of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
respectively, such that
g_{1}\in T_{1}^{\prime}
and
g_{2}\in T_{2}^{\prime}
.
The inverse is given
(T,g)^{-1}=((g^{-1}\cdot T^{\prime})_{L},g^{-1})
, where
T^{\prime}
is any representative of
T
such that
g\in T^{\prime}
.
The map
(T,g)\mapsto\omega(g)
provides an idempotent pure partial homomorphism
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)^{\times}\to\mathbb{F}
, which determines a semidirect product decomposition

\mathcal{LI}(E,C)\cong\mathcal{Y}^{L}\rtimes_{\theta}^{r}\mathbb{F},

where
\mathcal{Y}^{L}=\mathcal{X}/{\sim_{L}}\cong\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{LI}(E,C))
. The partial action
\theta^{L}
, with
\theta^{L}_{g}\colon D_{g^{-1}}^{L}\to D_{g}^{L}
is defined as follows:
For each
g\in\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)\setminus\{v\}
,
D_{g}^{L}
can be identified with the set of all those
T\in\mathcal{X}_{L}(v)
such that
g_{L}\in T
, and the action

is defined by
\theta_{g}^{L}(T)=(g\cdot T^{\prime})_{L}
, where
T^{\prime}
is a representative of
T
containing

.

Proof.

The proof follows the pattern of
[
8
, proof of Theorem 4.5]
. We include details for the convenience of the reader.

If
S
is any strongly
E^{*}
-unitary inverse semigroup with an idempotent pure partial homomorphism
\theta\colon S^{\times}\to\Gamma
, and

is an ideal of

, then

is also strongly
E^{*}
-unitary with the same group
\Gamma
.
This is clear since
(S/I)^{\times}
can be identified with
S\setminus I
, and thus the restriction of
\theta
to

gives the desired idempotent pure partial homomorphism.

Now let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. Since the Leavitt inverse semigroup
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
is the Rees quotient of the inverse semigroup
\mathcal{M}_{L}=\mathfrak{F_{is}}(E)/{\sim_{L}}
by the ideal generated by all the elements of the form

,

, it follows from Proposition

and the above observation that

is a strongly
E^{*}
-unitary semigroup, with an idempotent pure partial homomorphism

. The result now follows from
[
8
, Proposition 2.1]
and Proposition
3.10
.
∎

4.
Normal form in
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)

In order to find a linear basis for
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
we will follow the route of
[
11
]
, using Diamond’s Lemma but this time for commutative algebras.

Consider the commutative subalgebra
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
generated by the idempotents of
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
. By
[
8
, Theorem 4.8]
, each idempotent in
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
corresponds uniquely to an

E
-tree
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and the idempotent of

corresponding to

\mathbf{e}(T):=\prod_{\lambda\in\mathrm{max}(T)}\lambda\lambda^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C).

Therefore
\mathcal{C}
coincides with the linear span of the family
\{\mathbf{e}(T):T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}\}
, and it is the contracted semigroup algebra

For each
g\in\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)\setminus\{v\}
, denote by
\mathcal{C}_{g}
the linear span of

, where

is the set of those
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(v)
such that

, see
[
8
, Theorem 4.5]
. Set

and
\mathcal{C}_{g}=\{0\}
if

Then the canonical structure of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
as a partial crossed product is given by

\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)=\bigoplus_{g\in\mathbb{F}_{C}}\mathcal{C}_{g}\rtimes g.

The ideal
\mathcal{Q}
is generated by homogeneous elements from the neutral component
\mathcal{C}
, hence the quotient algebra

\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
has the structure of a partial crossed product

\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)=\bigoplus_{g\in\mathbb{F}_{C}}\mathcal{L}_{g}\rtimes g,

where
\mathcal{L}_{g}=\mathcal{C}_{g}/\mathcal{Q}_{g}
, with
\mathcal{Q}_{g}=\mathcal{C}_{g}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{1}
, where
\mathcal{Q}_{1}=\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathcal{C}
is the corresponding invariant ideal of
\mathcal{C}
.

Similarly we may view
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
as a quotient of the contracted semigroup algebra

where
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
is the Leavitt inverse semigroup of

, as introduced in Section
3
.
By Theorem
3.15
, the elements of
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
can be identified with the Leavitt-Munn
(E,C)
-trees (see Definition

), and we will show below that the elements associated to a certain subfamily of those provide a linear basis for
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph, and let
C^{\mathrm{fin}}
denote the subset of

consisting of elements
X\in C
such that
|X|<\infty
. We define a

as a function
\mathfrak{E}\colon C^{\mathrm{fin}}\to E^{1}
such that
\mathfrak{E}(X)\in X
for all
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
.
We say that
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced if it does not end with
\mathfrak{E}(X)
for any

.

We say that an element

is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced
if each maximal element of

is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced. Note that, if

is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced, then

, because the maximal elements of

do not end in an edge

The above definition is suitable to find a linear basis for

\mathcal{L}_{g}=\mathcal{C}_{g}/\mathcal{Q}_{g}
. The reason for this is that the reduction process needed to find the normal form of the elements does not respect the fact that the elements belong to the linear span of

.

Observe that due to the two relations (SCK1) and (SCK2), we have

gg^{*}=g_{L}g_{L}^{*}
in
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
, indeed this holds within the Leavitt inverse semigroup. Hence, the correct domains for the partial action associated to
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
are of the form

, see Theorem
3.15
.

The following extreme example clarifies the situation.

Example 4.2
.

Let
(E,C)
be the Cuntz separated graph, consisting of a single vertex, and where all elements of
C
are singletons (see
[
7
, Example 3.4]
). Then for each

,

is trivial. More generally, if
(E,C)
is a separated graph with the free separation, that is, all elements of

are singletons, then
the only idempotents of
\mathcal{LI}(E,C)
are the vertices of

, and
D_{g}^{L}=\{\{v\}\}
whenever

.

We first abstractly describe the commutative subalgebra
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
generated by the idempotents of
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the (commutative) subalgebra of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
generated by the idempotents of
\mathcal{S}(E,C)
. Then

\mathcal{C}\cong\Pol_{K}[\mathbb{F}_{C}]/N,

where
\Pol_{K}[\mathbb{F}_{C}]
denotes the commutative polynomial algebra on the set
\mathbb{F}_{C}
, and
N
is the ideal of
\Pol_{K}[\mathbb{F}_{C}]
generated by the following elements

(1)

\lambda\cdot\mu
if
s(\lambda)\neq s(\mu)
.

(2)

\lambda\cdot\mu-\lambda
for
\mu\leq_{p}\lambda
,

(3)

\lambda\cdot\mu
if
\lambda
and
\mu
are
C
-incompatible.

(4)

\lambda e^{-1}-\lambda
where
\lambda e^{-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
and
e\in E^{1}
.

Proof.

Set
R:=\Pol_{K}[\mathbb{F}_{C}]
.
There is a
K
-algebra homomorphism

\varphi\colon R/N\to\mathcal{C}

given by
\varphi(\lambda)=\lambda\lambda^{*}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
. In view of
[
8
, Theorem 4.8]
, this map is surjective. Also, observe that, using the relations of
N
, we can bring each monomial of
R/N
to a monomial of the form

\lambda_{1}\cdot\lambda_{2}\cdots\lambda_{r},

where each
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
, all
\lambda_{i}
do not end in
E^{-1}
and
\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\}
is an incomparable
C
-compatible subset of
\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
for some
v\in E^{0}
. It follows that this set of monomials linearly spans
R/N
. Again by
[
8
, Theorem 4.8]
, the images by
\varphi
of these monomials are linearly independent in
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
, hence they are also linearly independent in
R/N
, and
\varphi
is injective.
∎

Observe that the set of monomials in
\Pol_{K}[\mathbb{F}_{C}]
appearing in the proof of Lemma
4.3
corresponds exactly to the set of elements in
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{S}(E,C))
in Scheiblich normal form. One can directly deduce that these elements form a basis of
R/N
by an application of the results in
[
19
]
, using the reduction system consisting of the pairs (1)–(4) in the next lemma. Adding a new set of pairs (5) will give the basis for the neutral component
\mathcal{L}_{1}
of
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
, as follows:

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph and let
\mathfrak{E}\colon C^{\mathrm{fin}}\to E^{1}
be a choice function. Then a
K
-basis for the neutral component
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{1}/\mathcal{Q}_{1}
of
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
is given by the following set:

\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(E,C)=\{\mathbf{e}(T):T\text{ is $\mathfrak{E}$-reduced }\}.

Proof.

We show that the set of idempotents
\{\mathbf{e}(T):T\text{ is $\mathfrak{E}$-reduced}\}
is a
K
-basis for
\mathcal{L}_{1}
.

Set
e_{X}:=\mathfrak{E}(X)\in X
for all
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
.

We apply Bergman’s Diamond’s Lemma for commutative algebras
[
19
]
, see especially
[
19
, Theorem 1.2 and Section 10.3]
. We define
\text{wt}(e_{X})=2
and
\text{wt}(x)=1
for any other
x\in E^{1}\cup E^{-1}
. We consider a weight function on
\mathbb{F}_{C}
such that the weight of
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
is the sum of the weights of the edges of
\lambda
, or
\text{wt}(\lambda)=1
if
\lambda=v\in E^{0}
. We extend this weight function to the free commutative semigroup
W:=[\mathbb{F}_{C}]
generated by
\mathbb{F}_{C}
by declaring the weight of a monomial
\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{r}
as the sum of the weights
\text{wt}(\lambda_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,r
.

For
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, set
X^{\prime}:=X\setminus\{e_{X}\}
.

We now consider the following reduction system
\mathcal{S}
on the semigroup algebra
\Pol_{K}[\mathbb{F}_{C}]=KW
:

(1)

(\lambda\cdot\mu,0)
if
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
and
s(\lambda)\neq s(\mu)
.

(2)

(\lambda\cdot\mu,\lambda)
if
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
and
\mu\leq_{p}\lambda
,

(3)

(\lambda\cdot\mu,0)
if
\lambda
and
\mu
are
C
-incompatible, where
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
.

(4)

(\lambda e^{-1},\lambda)
where
\lambda e^{-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
and
e\in E^{1}
.

(5)

(\lambda e_{X},\lambda-\sum_{e\in X^{\prime}}\lambda e)
for
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
and
\lambda e_{X}\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
.

Observe that the reduction system
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
consisting of the pairs in (1)–(4) leads to the basis of
\mathcal{C}
consisting of idempotents in Scheiblich normal form.

Define a partial order
\leq
on
W=[\mathbb{F}_{C}]
by

\lambda\leq\mu\iff\lambda=\mu\text{ or }\text{wt}(\lambda)<\text{wt}(\mu).

Then the partial order
\leq
on
W
is a semigroup partial order compatible with the reduction system
\mathcal{S}
. Moreover
\leq
satisfies the descending chain condition. In order to apply the Diamond Lemma, we have to check that all ambiguities are resolvable.
We leave to the reader to check that all ambiguities involving pairs (1)–(4) are resolvable.
We will check those concerning reduction pairs of the family (5), which are necessarily inclusion ambiguities with either (2) or (3).

Suppose we have an element of the form
\lambda e_{X}\cdot\mu
such that
\mu\leq_{p}\lambda e_{X}
. Then we have an inclusion ambiguity

\lambda e_{X}\cdot\mu\overset{(2)}{\to}\lambda e_{X},\qquad\lambda e_{X}\cdot\mu\overset{(5)}{\to}\lambda\cdot\mu-\sum_{e\in X^{\prime}}\lambda e\cdot\mu.

Applying (5) to the first, we get

\lambda e_{X}\cdot\mu\overset{(2)}{\to}\lambda e_{X}\overset{(5)}{\to}\lambda-\sum_{e\in X^{\prime}}\lambda e.

We now distinguish two cases. If
\mu\leq_{p}\lambda
then we have

\lambda\cdot\mu-\sum_{e\in X^{\prime}}\lambda e\cdot\mu\overset{(2)}{\to}\lambda-\sum_{e\in X^{\prime}}\lambda e,

and we have solved the ambiguity. If
\mu=\lambda e_{X}
, then

\lambda\cdot\lambda e_{X}-\sum_{e\in X^{\prime}}\lambda e\cdot\lambda e_{X}\overset{(2)}{\to}\lambda e_{X}-\sum_{e\in X^{\prime}}\lambda e\cdot\lambda e_{X}\overset{(3)}{\to}\lambda e_{X},

and we have solved the ambiguity too.

If
\lambda e_{X}\leq_{p}\mu
, then we can solve the corresponding inclusion ambiguity for
\lambda e_{X}\cdot\mu
just as in the case where
\mu=\lambda e_{X}
.

Finally we consider inclusion ambiguities involving (3) and (5). Suppose that

\lambda e_{X}
and
\mu
are
C
-incompatible. If
\lambda
and
\mu
are already
C
-incompatible then we have

\lambda e_{X}\cdot\mu\overset{(2)}{\to}0,\quad\lambda e_{X}\cdot\mu\overset{(5)}{\to}\lambda\cdot\mu-\sum_{e\in X^{\prime}}\lambda e\cdot\mu\overset{(2)}{\to}0-0=0.

If
\mu=\lambda e\mu^{\prime}
, where
e\in X^{\prime}
, then

\lambda e_{X}\cdot\mu\overset{(5)}{\to}\lambda\cdot\lambda e\mu^{\prime}-\sum_{f\in X^{\prime}}\lambda f\cdot\lambda e\mu^{\prime}\overset{(2),(3)}{\longrightarrow}\lambda e\mu^{\prime}-\lambda e\cdot\lambda e\mu^{\prime}\overset{(2)}{\to}\mu-\mu=0.

The irreducible forms are precisely the elements which correspond to the family
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(E,C)
, that is, the elements

\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{r},

where
\lambda_{i}
does not end in
E^{-1}
, does not end in
e_{X}
for
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, and
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}
is an incomparable
C
-compatible family of elements of
\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
, for some
v\in E^{0}
.

Hence we obtain the desired result for the neutral component.
∎

In order to obtain a linear basis for the non-neutral components of

Theorem 4.6

A basis of

We work within

5.
Relation between Cohn path algebras and Leavitt path algebras

Here we want to determine the relationship between Cohn and Leavitt algebras. It was shown in

[

that every Cohn path algebra of a non-separated graph is isomorphic to the Leavitt path algebra of another non-separated graph.
In the separated situation, this apparently cannot be achieved, but there is also a nice relation.

We proceed more generally and relate the class of Cohn-Leavitt algebras with the class of Leavitt path algebras. Cohn-Leavitt path algebras of separated graphs have been introduced in
[

]
.
Here we will recall its definition, and we will also consider the abelianized (or tame) version of these algebras.

Definition 5.1

(E,C,S)
. Observe that
CL_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C,S)=\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)/{\mathcal{Q}_{S}}
, where
\mathcal{Q}_{S}
is the ideal of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
generated by the projections
q_{X}
, for
X\in S
.

A linear basis for
CL_{K}(E,C,S)
was found in
[
11
, Theorem 2.7]
. We will now show that we can also provide a linear basis of
CL_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C,S)
by using the same method as we used for
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
in
Theorem
4.6
.

We need to extend Definition
3.12
to this new setting.

Given
S\subseteq C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, we set

S^{1}=\{e\in E^{1}:\{e\}\in S\}.

Every element
g\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
can be uniquely written as
g=g_{S}w
, where
g_{S}
is either a vertex or ends in an edge
e\in E^{1}
which does not belong to
S^{1}
, and
w
is a product of elements in
E^{-1}
and elements in
S^{1}
.

For
v\in E^{0}
, define
\mathcal{X}_{S}
as the set of those
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
such that all maximal elements of
T
do not end in an edge
e\in S^{1}
.
For
g\in\mathbb{F}_{C}\setminus E^{0}
, we denote by
D_{g}^{S}
the set of elements
T\in\mathcal{X}_{S}
such that
g_{S}\in T
. For
S=C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, we have
g_{S}=g_{L}
,
\mathcal{X}_{S}=\mathcal{X}_{L}
and
D_{g}^{S}=D_{g}^{L}
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph and let
S
be a subset of
C^{\mathrm{fin}}
.
We define a
S
-choice function
as a function
\mathfrak{E}\colon S\to E^{1}
such that
\mathfrak{E}(X)\in X
for all
X\in S
.
We say that
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced if it does not end with
e_{X}
for all
X\in S
.

We say that an element
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced
if each maximal element of
T
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced.

Let
g\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
.
We say that
T\in D_{g}^{S}
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced relatively to
g_{S}
if each element in
\mathrm{max}(T)\setminus\{g_{S}\}
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced. That is, if either
g_{S}
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced or
g_{S}
is not maximal in
T
, then
T
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced relatively to
g_{S}
if and only if
T
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced, and if
g_{S}
is not
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced and
g_{S}
is maximal in
T
, then
T
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced relatively to
g_{S}
if and only if all maximal elements of
T
except
g_{S}
are
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced.

With these definitions at hand, we can state the following result, whose proof follows the lines of the proof of Theorem
4.6
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph, and let
S
be a subset of
C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, and let
\mathfrak{E}
be an
S
-choice function for
(E,C)
. Then a linear basis
of
CL_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C,S)
is given by the elements of the form

\overline{\mathcal{B}}(E,C,S):=\{\mathbf{e}(T)\rtimes g:T\in D_{g}^{S}\text{ and }T\text{ is $\mathfrak{E}$-reduced relatively to $g_{S}$}\}.

Note that in the extreme cases where
S=\emptyset
and
S=C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, we recover the already known basis of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
and
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
, respectively.

We are now ready to study the relationship with Leavitt path algebras.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph and
S
a subset of
C^{\mathrm{fin}}
. We build a new separated graph
(E_{S},C^{S})
as follows. First we set

(E_{S})^{0}=E^{0}\sqcup\{v_{X}:X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}\setminus S\}

(E_{S})^{1}=E^{1}\sqcup\{d_{X}:X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}\setminus S\},

The source and range maps in
E_{S}
are such that the natural map
E\to E_{S}
is a graph homomorphism, and moreover

s(d_{X})=v,\quad r(d_{X})=v_{X}\,\,\text{ if }X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}_{v}\setminus S.

The partitions
C^{S}_{v}
are given by
C^{S}_{v}=\{\overline{X}:X\in C_{v}\}
, where
\overline{X}=X\cup\{d_{X}\}
if
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}\setminus S
and
\overline{X}=X
otherwise.

In the case where
S=\emptyset
, so that the Cohn-Leavitt path algebra coincides with the Cohn path algebra, we write
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
for the separated graph
(E_{\emptyset},C^{\emptyset})
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph and
S
a subset of
C^{\mathrm{fin}}
. Let
p:=\sum_{v\in E^{0}}v\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{L}_{K}(E_{S},C^{S}))
. Then we have a natural
*
-isomorphism

\varphi\colon CL_{K}(E,C,S)\cong p\mathcal{L}_{K}(E_{S},C^{S})p

such that
\varphi(v)=v
for all
v\in E^{0}
and
\varphi(e)=e
for all
e\in E^{1}
.

In particular
\mathcal{C}_{K}(E,C)\cong p\mathcal{L}_{K}(\overline{E},\overline{C})p
.

Proof.

We have a natural
*
-homomorphism
\varphi\colon CL_{K}(E,C,S)\to p\mathcal{L}_{K}(E_{S},C^{S})p
defined by
\varphi(v)=v
and
\varphi(e)=e
for all
v\in E^{0}
and
e\in E^{1}
. Note that
R:=p\mathcal{L}_{K}(E_{S},C^{S})p
is generated as
*
-algebra by
E^{0}\cup E^{1}
together with the projections
d_{X}d_{X}^{*}
, for
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}\setminus S
, with the Cohn path algebra relations amongst the elements of
E^{0}\cup E^{1}
, the Leavitt path algebra relations
v=\sum_{e\in X}ee^{*}
for all
X\in S
, and the additional relations that
d_{X}d_{X}^{*}
is a projection, and that

v=\sum_{e\in X}ee^{*}+d_{X}d_{X}^{*}

for each
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}_{v}\setminus S
,
v\in E^{0}
.
Note that these relations automatically imply that
e^{*}d_{X}d_{X}^{*}=0=d_{X}d_{X}^{*}e
for all
e\in X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}\setminus S
and that
d_{X}d_{X}^{*}\leq v
whenever
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}_{v}\setminus S
.
Note that
\varphi(q_{X})=d_{X}d_{X}^{*}
for
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}\setminus S
.

We can define a map
\psi\colon p\mathcal{L}_{K}(E_{S},C^{S})p\to CL_{K}(E,C,S)
by

\psi(v)=v
,
\psi(e)=e
for
v\in E^{0}
and
e\in E^{1}
, and
\psi(d_{X}d_{X}^{*})=q_{X}=v-\sum_{e\in X}ee^{*}
for
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}_{v}\setminus S
. Using the above observations, we see that this map preserves all the relations amongst the generators of
p\mathcal{L}_{K}(E_{S},C^{S})p
, and hence induces a
*
-homomorphism which is clearly the inverse of
\varphi
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We now extend the above result to the tame algebras.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph and
S
a subset of
C^{\mathrm{fin}}
. Let
p:=\sum_{v\in E^{0}}v\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{L}_{K}(E_{S},C^{S}))
. Then we have a natural
*
-isomorphism

\overline{\varphi}\colon CL_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C,S)\cong p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{S},C^{S})p

such that
\overline{\varphi}(v)=v
for all
v\in E^{0}
and
\overline{\varphi}(e)=e
for all
e\in E^{1}
.

In particular,
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\cong p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})p
.

Proof.

Observe that the
*
-homomorphism
\varphi
from Proposition
5.4
induces a surjective

*
-homomorphism
\overline{\varphi}\colon CL_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C,S)\twoheadrightarrow p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{S},C^{S})p
.
To show that it is injective, we prove that
\overline{\varphi}
sends a basis of
CL_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C,S)
to
a linearly independent family in
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{S},C^{S})
. Let
\mathfrak{E}
be a
S
-choice function for
(E,C)
. This means that we have a map
\mathfrak{E}\colon S\to E^{1}
such that
\mathfrak{E}(X)\in X
for all
X\in S
. We extend
\mathfrak{E}
to a choice function
\overline{\mathfrak{E}}\colon(C^{S})^{\text{fin}}\to E_{S}^{1}
by letting
\overline{\mathfrak{E}}(X)=\mathfrak{E}(X)\in X
for each
\overline{X}=X\in S
and
\overline{\mathfrak{E}}(\overline{X})=d_{X}\in\overline{X}=X\cup\{d_{X}\}
for all
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}\setminus S
.

We consider the basis
\mathcal{B}:=\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathfrak{E}}(E,C,S)
of
CL_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C,S)
given in Theorem
5.3
with respect to the
S
-choice function
\mathfrak{E}
, and the basis

\overline{\mathcal{B}}:=\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\overline{\mathfrak{E}}}(E_{S},C^{S})
of
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{S},C^{S})
with respect to the choice function
\overline{\mathfrak{E}}
given in Theorem
4.6
. If
\mathbf{e}(T)\rtimes g
is an element of the basis

, then
g_{S}\in T
and
T
is
\mathfrak{E}
-reduced relatively to
g_{S}
. But then, since neither of the edges of

and neither of the edges of

are of the forms

or
d_{X}^{-1}
for

, we see that
g_{L}=g_{S}
and that
T
is
\overline{\mathfrak{E}}
-reduced relatively to
g_{L}
. Here

is computed with respect to the separated graph
(E_{S},C^{S})
, and we remark that
|\overline{X}|\geq 2
for all
\overline{X}\in C^{S}\setminus S
. Hence
\mathbf{e}(T)\rtimes g
belongs to the basis
\overline{\mathcal{B}}
. Hence
\overline{\varphi}
restricts to an injective map from the basis
\mathcal{B}
of
CL_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C,S)
into the basis

\overline{\mathcal{B}}
of
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{S},C^{S})
, as desired.
∎

Let
(E,C)
be a bipartite separated graph, see

,

,

]
. This means that we have a decomposition

.
We then define the

.

Let
(E,C)
be a bipartite separated graph, let

be a subset of

, and let

be the bipartite separated graph constructed above. Then we have

\mathcal{C}V_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\cong\mathcal{L}V_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C}).

Let
\overline{\varphi}\colon CL_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C,S)\to p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{S},C^{S})p

6.
A linear basis of the ideal
\mathcal{Q}

Recall that
\mathcal{Q}
is the ideal of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
generated by the idempotents
q_{X}
, for all
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, so that
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\cong\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)/\mathcal{Q}
. Our goal in this section is to find a linear basis of
\mathcal{Q}
. For non-separated graphs, it is not difficult to find such a basis, see for instance
[
2
, Proposition 1.5.11]
and Example
9.6
below. However the separated case is much more involved.

We start by recalling some necessary notation and terminology from
[
8
]
.

Notation 6.1
.

For an element
g=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}\in\mathbb{F}
of length
n\geq 1
, with
x_{j}\in E^{1}\cup E^{-1}
, and
1\leq i\leq n
, we denote by
[g]_{i}
the prefix of
g
of length
i
, that is,
[g]_{i}:=x_{1}\cdots x_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
.

Notation 6.2
.

[
8
, Notation 5.10(1),(2)]

(1)

For
v\in E^{0}
, write

\mathcal{N}(v)=\{x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}^{-1}\cdots x_{n}^{-1}y\in\mathbb{F}_{C}(v):n\geq 0,x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y\in E^{1}\}.

(2)

For each
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, write

\mathcal{N}(T):=\{gg^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{C}:g\in T,\,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(r(g)),\,g[g^{\prime}]_{1}\notin T,\,\text{ and }T\cup\{gg^{\prime}\}^{\downarrow}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}\}.

We think of
\mathcal{N}(T)
as the set of neighbors of
T
. Now for each
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
and each finite subset of
\mathcal{N}(T)
, we can define idempotents in
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
, as follows:

Definition 6.3
.

[
8
, Definition 4.3]

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph and let
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
.

(1)

The idempotent
\mathbf{e}(T)
associated to
T
is

\mathbf{e}(T)=\prod_{\lambda\in\mathrm{max}(T)}\lambda\lambda^{*}.

(2)

Suppose now that
\lambda\in\mathcal{N}(T)
. Then there is a unique decomposition
\lambda=\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}
, where
\lambda_{0}\in T
,
\lambda_{1}=x_{1}^{-1}\cdots x_{r}^{-1}y
,
r\geq 0
,
x_{1},\dots,x_{r},y\in E^{1}
and
\lambda_{0}[x_{1}^{-1}\cdots x_{r}^{-1}y]_{1}\notin T
. Define

\mathbf{e}(\lambda_{0}\setminus\lambda)=\lambda_{0}\lambda_{0}^{*}-\lambda\lambda^{*}=\lambda_{0}(r(\lambda_{0})-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{1}^{*})\lambda_{0}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C).

(3)

For a finite subset
F
of
\mathcal{N}(T)
, define the idempotent
\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)
as follows:

\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)=\mathbf{e}(T)\cdot\prod_{\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}\in F}\mathbf{e}(\lambda_{0}\setminus\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1})=\prod_{\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}\in F}\mathbf{e}(T\setminus\{\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1}\}).

(4)

For
v\in E^{0}
and
e\in s^{-1}(v)
we define

\mathbf{e}(v\setminus e)=v-ee^{*}.

(5)

For
v\in E^{0}
and
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}_{v}
, define

q_{X}=\mathbf{e}(v\setminus X)=\prod_{e\in X}\mathbf{e}(v\setminus e)=v-\sum_{e\in X}ee^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C).

Observe that if
\mu=\mu_{0}\mu_{1}
with
\mu_{1}=x_{1}^{*}\cdots x_{r}^{*}
and
\mu_{0}
does not end in
(E^{1})^{*}
, where
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\in E^{1}
, and
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, then

\mu q_{X}\mu^{*}=\mathbf{e}(\mu_{0}\setminus\mu_{0}(\mu_{1}X))=\prod_{e\in X}\mathbf{e}(\mu_{0}\setminus\mu_{0}(\mu_{1}e)),

where we use the notation introduced in Definition
6.3
. Intuitively, when considering
\mathbf{e}(\mu_{0}\setminus\mu_{0}(\mu_{1}X))
, we are blocking the exit in the direction of
X
in the path
\mu=\mu_{0}\mu_{1}
. This motivates the following definition:

Definition 6.4
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph and
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
. A
blocking family
for
T
consists of a finite subset
F
of
\mathcal{N}(T)
, where

F=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{0}^{i}(\gamma_{1}^{i}X_{i})

where
\gamma_{0}^{i}\in T
, and
\gamma_{0}^{i}\gamma_{1}^{i}X_{i}=\{\gamma_{0}^{i}\gamma_{1}^{i}x:x\in X_{i}\}
for
X_{i}\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}_{r(\gamma_{0}^{i}\gamma_{1}^{i})}
, with
\gamma^{i}x=(\gamma_{0}^{i})(\gamma_{1}^{i}x)
being the standard form of the element
\gamma^{i}x\in\mathcal{N}(T)
, as described in Definition
6.3
(2).

We say that a blocking family
F
is
prime
if it consists of a single set, that is if
F=\gamma X
for a single
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
. Observe that

\mathbf{e}\Big(T\setminus\big(\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{0}^{i}(\gamma_{1}^{i}X_{i})\big)\Big)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{e}(T\setminus\gamma_{0}^{i}\gamma_{1}^{i}X_{i}).

Again, intuitively when considering
\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)
for a blocking family
F
for
T
as above, we are blocking each of the exits in the direction of the sets
X_{i}
at the paths
\gamma_{0}^{i}\gamma_{1}^{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,r
. Note that, if the
C
-separated word
\gamma^{i}_{0}\gamma_{1}^{i}
ends in
y^{-1}
, with
y\in E^{1}
, then
y\notin X_{i}
, because all the paths
\gamma_{0}^{i}\gamma_{1}^{i}x
, for
x\in X_{i}
must be
C
-separated.

We will use throughout the following expression of the elements
\mathbf{e}(T\setminus gX)
.
For
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
and
F=gX
a prime blocking family for
T
, write
g=g_{0}w
, where
g_{0}\in T
does not end in
E^{-1}
and
w
is a product of elements
x_{i}^{-1}\in E^{-1}
. Then the element
\mathbf{e}(T\setminus gX)
can be written as follows:

(6.5)

\mathbf{e}(T\setminus gX)=\mathbf{e}(T)-\sum_{x\in X}\mathbf{e}(T\cup\{gx\}^{\downarrow}).

Indeed we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}(T\setminus gX)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}(T)g_{0}(r(g_{0})-\sum_{x\in X}wxx^{*}w^{*})g_{0}^{*}=\mathbf{e}(T)(g_{0}g_{0}^{*})-\sum_{x\in X}\mathbf{e}(T\cup\{gx\}^{\downarrow})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}(T)-\sum_{x\in X}\mathbf{e}(T\cup\{gx\}^{\downarrow})

because, since
g_{0}\in T
, we have
\mathbf{e}(T)(g_{0}g_{0}^{*})=\mathbf{e}(T)
. This establishes the formula (
6.5
).

The following basic lemma will be used in the proof of Lemma
6.7
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph, let
I,J\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
and let
F\in\mathcal{N}(I)
,
G\in\mathcal{N}(J)
. Then we have

\mathbf{e}(I\setminus F)\mathbf{e}(J\setminus G)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{e}((I\cup J)\setminus(F\cup G))&\text{ if }I\cup J\text{ is $C$-compatible and }\\
&\quad F\cap J=G\cap I=\emptyset\\
\qquad\quad 0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

Proof.

This follows from the identity

\mathcal{Z}(I\setminus F)\cap\mathcal{Z}(J\setminus G)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{Z}((I\cup J)\setminus(F\cup G))&\text{ if }I\cup J\text{ is $C$-compatible and }\\
&\quad F\cap J=G\cap I=\emptyset\\
\qquad\quad\emptyset&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

which has been shown in
[
8
, Lemma 5.11]
.
∎

In the following lemma, we obtain a generating family for the ideal
\mathcal{Q}
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. Consider the set

\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}=\{\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)\rtimes g:T\in\mathcal{X}_{0},[T]\in D_{g}\text{ and }F=\gamma X\text{ is a prime blocking family for }T\}.

Then
\mathcal{Q}
is the
K
-linear span of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove that for
\mathbf{e}(I)\rtimes g,\mathbf{e}(J)\rtimes h\in\mathcal{S}(E,C)
, the element

(\mathbf{e}(I)\rtimes g)q_{X}(\mathbf{e}(J)\rtimes h)

is in the
K
-span of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}
.
We may assume that this product is nonzero. In this case we have
r(g)=v
, where
X\in C_{v}
, and thus we have

(\mathbf{e}(I)\rtimes g)q_{X}=\mathbf{e}(I\setminus gX)\rtimes g.

Now writing
((gg^{-1})\rtimes g)(\mathbf{e}(J)\rtimes h)=\mathbf{e}(J^{\prime})\rtimes(g\cdot h)
for some
J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, we get from Lemma
6.6
:

(\mathbf{e}(I)\rtimes g)q_{X}(\mathbf{e}(J)\rtimes h)=(\mathbf{e}(I\setminus gX)e(J^{\prime}))\rtimes(g\cdot h)=\mathbf{e}((I\cup J^{\prime})\setminus gX)\rtimes(g\cdot h)\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}.

∎

Remark 6.8

Lemma 6.9

Observe first that, for

Now let
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
be the separated graph associated to

, so that we have a
*
-isomorphism
\overline{\varphi}\colon\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\to p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})p

, and

In order to complete the proof, we have to check that

. Suppose first that
\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)\rtimes g\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Q})
. Then

,

,

, and all non-

-reduced. Now observe that

,
where

On the other hand, since

Conversely, suppose that

. We can write

, where

. We can write

and

, for

.

, and note that

, because no path in

, for

. Now let

Then

, and

. We now will define a suitable blocking family

for

. Take an element
\gamma_{i}d_{X_{i}}\in M_{2}
. We can write
\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{i}^{0}\gamma_{i}^{1}
where
\gamma^{0}_{i}
is a maximal prefix of
\gamma_{i}
with the property

. Note that
\gamma_{i}^{1}
might be trivial. Define

. Suppose that
\gamma_{i}^{0}\gamma_{i}^{1}x\in T

.

is

-reduced relatively to

-reduced relatively to

7.
The spectrum and the tight spectrum of the Leavitt inverse semigroup

Lemma 7.10
.

The inverse and product in

given above are well defined, and correspond to the groupoid structure on the tight groupoid of

Proof.

We first check that the inverse is well-defined, that is, for
(Z^{\prime},g,Z)\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tight}}(E,C)
, we have
that
(Z,g^{-1},Z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tight}}(E,C)
. By definition,
Z\in\mathfrak{F}_{L}
,
(g^{-1})_{L}\in Z
, and

Z^{\prime}=[g\cdot Z\cup g^{\downarrow}]_{L}
. Hence we see that
g_{L}\in Z^{\prime}
.

We claim that
Z^{\prime}\cup g^{\downarrow}=g\cdot Z
. Indeed, it is clear that
Z^{\prime}\cup g^{\downarrow}=(g\cdot Z\cup g^{\downarrow})_{L}\cup g^{\downarrow}
is contained in
g\cdot Z
. Now let

. We can write
g=x_{1}\cdots x_{r}x_{r+1}\cdots x_{n}
,

in reduced form, where
m\geq 0
and
y_{1}\neq x_{r}
if

then
g\cdot z=x_{1}\cdots x_{r}\in g^{\downarrow}\subseteq[g\cdot Z\cup g^{\downarrow}]_{L}\cup g^{\downarrow}
. If
m\geq 1

g\cdot z=x_{1}\cdots x_{r}y_{1}\cdots y_{m}
, and since

, there exists

and it follows that

and thus that

(g\cdot Z)_{L}
is a lower set. Therefore

also in this case.

We now check that the product is well-defined. Let
(Z_{1}^{\prime},g_{1},Z_{1}),(Z_{2}^{\prime},g_{2},Z_{2})

so that

is at least in

belongs to
\mathbb{F}_{C}
, which in turn is equivalent to check that

.

We will check that
(g_{2}^{-1}\cdot g_{1}^{-1})_{L}\in Z_{2}
, which in particular implies that
(g_{2}^{-1}\cdot g_{1}^{-1})_{L}\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
. Write
g_{2}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
and
g_{1}=y_{1}\cdots y_{t}x_{r}^{-1}\cdots x_{1}^{-1}
in reduced form, with

,

We distinguish two cases:

.

(g_{2}^{-1}\cdot g_{1}^{-1})_{L}=x_{n}^{-1}\cdots x_{j}^{-1}

If (2) happens, then

and thus

\displaystyle g_{1}\cdot Z_{1}\cup g_{1}^{\downarrow}

is the model introduced in

We believe that all three models have advantages and disadvantages. For a specific problem, it might be that one of the three models is better than the others. Note that the standard and the Leavitt model agree when

. We will see in the next section that the Leavitt model is specially suited to describe the socle of the tame Leavitt path algebras of separated graphs.

We first collect some basic definitions.

Recall that a point

Let

For a ring with local units

. We will use throughout that

\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{ab}}_{K}(E,C)
is isomorphic to the crossed product
C_{K}(\Omega(E,C))\rtimes\mathbb{F}\cong A_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tight}}(E,C))
. This follows from Theorem
2.7
and the fact that

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{tight}}\cong\Omega(E,C)
(Theorem
7.7
).

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. Then the algebra
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{ab}}_{K}(E,C)
is semiprime.

Proof.

By
[
41
, Theorem 4.10]
,
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\cong A_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tight}}(E,C))
is semiprime if
K\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}
is semiprime for each
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Since in our case
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}
is always a free group, the result follows.
∎

Definition 8.5
.

[
21
]

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ample Hausdorff groupoid. We say that
\mathcal{G}
satisfies condition (LP) if, for every
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,

\text{soc}(K(\mathcal{G}^{x}_{x}))\neq 0\iff\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}=\{x\}.

Theorem 8.6
.

[
21
]

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ample Hausdorff groupoid satisfying condition
(LP)
, with
X=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. The socle
\mathrm{soc}(A_{K}(\mathcal{G}))
of
A_{K}(\mathcal{G})
coincides with the ideal generated by all the characteristic functions
1_{x}
, where
x
is an isolated point in
X=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
such that
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}=\{x\}
. Moreover

\mathrm{soc}(A_{K}(\mathcal{G}))\cong\bigoplus_{[x]\in X^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}/{\mathcal{R}}}M_{|[x]|}(K),

where
X^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
is the set of elements
x\in X^{\mathrm{iso}}
such that
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}=\{x\}
, so that
X^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}/{\mathcal{R}}
is the set of orbits of
X^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
.

In the following, we will use the Leavitt model for the space
\Omega(E,C)
and the canonical action of
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}(E^{1})
on it, see Section
7
.
Recall that the notion of a Leavitt
(E,C)
-tree has been introduced in Definition
3.12
(a).

Notation 8.7
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. We denote by
\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}
the set of Leavitt
(E,C)
-trees which do not admit an exit, and we denote by
\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
the subset of
\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}
of those Leavitt
(E,C)
-trees
T
such that there is no closed path
\alpha=\beta\gamma\beta^{-1}
, where
\gamma
is a cycle,
(\alpha^{-1})_{L}\in T
and
(\alpha\cdot T\cup\alpha^{\downarrow})_{L}=T
. We will write
\Omega(E,C)^{\mathrm{iso}}
and
\Omega(E,C)^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
in case we need to specify the separated graph
(E,C)
.

We now characterize the set of isolated points in
\Omega(E,C)
, where
(E,C)
is a separated graph.

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. Then the set of isolated points of
\Omega(E,C)
coincides with the set
\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}
of all (finite) Leavitt
(E,C)
-trees which do not admit exits. Moreover the set
\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
is the set of isolated points
z\in\Omega(E,C)
with trivial isotropy.

Proof.

Suppose that
Z
is an isolated point in
\Omega(E,C)=\mathfrak{T}_{L}
. Then by Theorem
7.7
,
Z
is an element in
\mathfrak{F}_{L}
with no finite exits. Moreover, there is an open set of the form
V:=\mathcal{Z}_{L}(T\setminus F)\cap\mathfrak{T}_{L}
, where
T\in\mathcal{X}_{L}
(that is,
T
is a Leavitt
(E,C)
-tree), and
F
a finite subset of
\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{inf}}_{L}(T)
, such that
V=\{Z\}
. But if
T
admits an exit, then we can build at least two different elements
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{L}(T\setminus F)
, which then can be extended to two different elements
Z_{1}^{\prime},Z_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{L}(T\setminus F)\cap\mathfrak{T}_{L}
, and we get a contradiction. It follows that
T
has no exits, and then again by Theorem
7.7
,
T\in\Omega(E,C)
. Moreover
F=\emptyset
, because
T
has no infinite exits. It follows that
Z=T
is a Leavitt
(E,C)
-tree without exits.

Conversely, if
T
is a Leavitt
(E,C)
-tree without exits, then
T\in\Omega(E,C)
and
\mathcal{Z}_{L}(T)=\{T\}
, and so
T
is an isolated point in
\Omega(E,C)
.

The last statement follows from Lemma
8.3
.
∎

The condition that
T\in\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}
imposes serious restrictions, and
\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}=\emptyset
for many separated graphs.

Lemma 8.9
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph such that
|X|\geq 2
for all
X\in C
, and suppose that
T
is a Leavitt
(E,C)
-tree without exits. Suppose that
h\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
is such that
T\cup h^{\downarrow}\in\mathcal{X}
and
h\notin T
, and write
h=gg^{\prime}
, where
g\in T
,
g[g^{\prime}]_{1}\notin T
. Then all the edges of
g^{\prime}
are inverse edges
e^{-1}\in E^{-1}
with the property that
|C_{s(e)}|=1
.
Moreover,
T\in\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
and thus
\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}=\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
.

Proof.

If some of the edges of
g^{\prime}
is positively oriented, then we would obtain an exit of
T
, because
|X|\geq 2
for all
X\in C
. Hence all edges of
g^{\prime}
are inverse edges. Let
e^{-1}
be one of these edges, and suppose that
e\in X\in C_{s(e)}
. We then have
gg^{\prime\prime}e^{-1}\leq_{p}h
for some initial segment
g^{\prime\prime}
of
g^{\prime}
. If there is
Y\in C_{s(e)}
such that
X\neq Y
, then choosing
y\in Y
, we obtain an exit
gg^{\prime\prime}e^{-1}y
of
T
, which is a contradiction. Hence
|C_{s(e)}|=1
.

We now show that the isotropy group at
T
is trivial. Suppose, by way of contradiction that
\alpha\in F_{T}
is a non-trivial element in the isotropy group of
T
. By Lemma
8.3
, we have
\alpha=\beta\gamma\beta^{-1}
, where
\gamma
is a cycle. Moreover
\alpha^{n}=\beta\gamma^{n}\beta^{-1}
is also an element in
F_{T}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, by Lemma
8.3
. Replacing
\alpha
with
\alpha^{-1}
if needed, we may assume that
\gamma
contains an edge
e\in E^{1}
. In particular,
\beta\gamma^{n-1}\gamma_{L}=\beta\gamma^{n-1}\gamma_{0}\leq_{p}(\alpha^{n})_{L}
for all
n>0
, and thus the lengths of the paths
(\alpha^{n})_{L}
are unbounded. Since
(\alpha^{n})_{L}\in T
for all
n
, and
T
is a finite tree, this is impossible, and thus we get a contradiction. Hence
F_{T}
is trivial, and so
T\in\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
.
∎

Theorem 8.10
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. Then
\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C))
coincides with the ideal generated by all projections
\mathbf{e}(T)
, where
T\in\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
. Moreover

\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C))\cong\bigoplus_{[T]\in\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}/{\mathcal{R}}}M_{|[T]|}(K).

For a fixed
T\in\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
, we can specify a set of matrix units in the component

M_{|[T]|}(K)
of
T
by setting

e_{\alpha,\beta}:=1_{(\theta_{\alpha}(T),\alpha\cdot\beta^{-1},\theta_{\beta}(T))},

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
are such that
(\alpha^{-1})_{L},(\beta^{-1})_{L}\in T
.

Proof.

The first part follows from Theorem
8.6
and Proposition
8.8
. The second part follows easily from the multiplication rules in the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{tight}}(E,C)
given in Section
7
.
∎

With some extra hypothesis, we can obtain more precise information about the sizes of the matrices in the homogeneous components.

Proposition 8.11
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph such that
|X|\geq 2
for all
X\in C
and
|C_{v}|\geq 2
for all
v\in E^{0}
such that
C_{v}\neq\emptyset
. Then if
T\in\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}
, the size of the orbit of
T
is exactly
|T|
, the cardinality of
T
, and the elements of the orbit of
T
are exactly
\{\theta_{\alpha}(T):\alpha^{-1}\in T\}
. Moreover
r(\gamma)
is a sink of
E
for each
\gamma\in\mathrm{max}(T)
. Hence, we have

\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C))\cong\bigoplus_{[T]\in\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}/{\mathcal{R}}}M_{|T|}(K).

Proof.

Obviously, the elements
\theta_{\alpha}(T)
, where
\alpha^{-1}\in T
, belong to the orbit of
T
.
By Lemma
8.9
, if
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{C}
and
\gamma_{L}=\gamma_{0}\in T
, then
\gamma\in T
. Hence the only elements in the orbit of
T
are the ones described in the statement. It is also clear from Lemma
8.9
that
r(\gamma)
is a sink of
E
for each
\gamma\in\mathrm{max}(T)
.
∎

Hence the situation for the separated graphs satisfying the hypothesis of Proposition
8.11
is completely different from the situation in the non-separated case, in which the orbit of the points in
\Omega^{\mathrm{iso}}_{0}
can easily be infinite, as in the case of the Toeplitz algebra.

9.
Examples

In this section, we consider some examples. We first consider two easy examples, the case of a non-separated graph and the case where
E
is a finite, acyclic and connected as non-oriented graph, and
C
is the free partition.
We then consider the problem of describing the ideal
\mathcal{Q}
of an arbitrary tame Cohn algebra
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
of a separated graph, which in some cases is closely related to the socle of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)
. This is obviously related to the socle of
\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})
, where
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
is the separated graph associated to
(E,C)
in Section
5
. After this, we will consider two specific non-trivial examples.

Example 9.1
.

Let
E
be a non-separated graph. Then
\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}_{K}(E))
is the ideal generated by the line points of
E
, which are the vertices
v
of
E
such that the tree of
v
does not contain any bifurcation vertex or any cycle
[
2
, Theorem 2.6.14]
.
In the Leavitt model, a line point
v
is represented by a single vertex
\{v\}
, which does not have exits and does not have isotropy. This differs from the representation in the standard model, where the line point is represented by the path consisting of the tree of
v
, which can be finite or infinite. The third picture, that is, the complete model, also gives a different representation. The points in the orbit of a line point
v
are represented in the Leavitt model by the vertices in the tree of
v
, the line points connecting to
v
, and the finite paths
e_{1}\cdots e_{r}
in
E
such that
|s^{-1}(s(e_{r}))|\geq 2
and
r(e_{r})
is a line point connecting to
v
. Note that these are all finite trees without exits, as predicted by Proposition
8.8
. ∎

Example 9.2
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph where
E
is a finite graph which is connected and acyclic as a non-oriented graph. Suppose that
C
is the free separation, that is,
|X|=1
for all
X\in C
. Then

\mathcal{L}_{K}(E,C)=\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)=\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}_{K}(E,C))\cong M_{n}(K),

where
n=|E^{0}|
.

Indeed, each vertex is a Leavitt
(E,C)
-tree without exits, because
|X|=1
for all
X\in C
. Moreover
T_{L}
consists of a vertex for each
T\in\mathcal{X}
.
Since the graph is connected as a non-oriented graph, there is only one orbit of vertices. Since the graph is acyclic as a non-oriented graph, the isotropy of each vertex in trivial. Hence the result follows from Theorem
8.10
. ∎

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph. Recall from Section
5
that there is a natural isomorphism
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{ab}}_{K}(E,C)\cong p\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})p
. The separated graph
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
is built in Section
5
by enlarging the graph
(E,C)
. Each set
X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
is enlarged with a new edge
d_{X}
such that
r(d_{X})=v_{X}
is a sink in
\overline{E}
, and
\overline{C}^{\text{fin}}=\{\overline{X}:X\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}\}
, where
\overline{X}=X\cup\{d_{X}\}
.

We are going to describe a family of projections in the socle of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{ab}}_{K}(E,C)
. We will use the above isomorphism with
p\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})p
and the results of Section
8
for the proof of the main result of this section, Theorem
9.4
. However, the definition of the family of projections is made directly in terms of the separated graph
(E,C)
.

For the next definition, recall the notion of a
blocking family

F
for
T
, where
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, which has been introduced in Definition
6.4
.

Definition 9.3
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph, and let

T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
. We say that
T

can be completely blocked
if
\mathcal{N}(T)
is finite and non-empty. This implies that for any element
\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1}y\in\mathcal{N}(T)
, we have
y\in Y\in C^{\mathrm{fin}}
, and that there is a maximum blocking family
F=\sqcup_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{0}^{i}(\gamma_{1}^{i}X_{i})
for
T
, which contains all the elements of
\mathcal{N}(T)
.
We denote by
\mathbf{ecb}(T)
the idempotent given by

\mathbf{ecb}(T)=\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)
, where
F
is the maximum blocking family for
T
. The set of elements
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
which can be completely blocked will be denoted by
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{c}b}
.

Theorem 9.4
.

Let
(E,C)
be a separated graph.
For each
T\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{c}b}
, the idempotent
\mathbf{ecb}(T)
belongs to
\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C))
. Moreover,
\mathcal{Q}\cap\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C))
is the ideal of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)

generated by all the idempotents
\mathbf{ecb}(T)
, for
T\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{c}b}
.

Proof.

First observe that
\mathbf{ecb}(T)=\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)
belongs to the ideal
\mathcal{Q}
, because
\mathcal{N}(T)
is non-empty and finite.

Let
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
be the separated graph associated to
(E,C)
. Any
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
can be considered also as a
\overline{C}
-compatible tree in
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
. Moreover
T\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{L}
, that is,
T
belongs to the set
\mathcal{X}_{L}(\overline{E},\overline{C})
, so the concept of an exit has been defined for
T
with respect to
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
. By the isomorphism
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{ab}}_{K}(E,C)\cong p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})p
, the idempotent
\mathbf{ecb}(T)
corresponds to
\mathbf{e}(T^{\prime})
, for the Leavitt
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
-tree
T^{\prime}
, where

T^{\prime}=T\cup\big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\{\gamma^{i}_{0}\gamma^{i}_{1}d_{X_{i}}\}^{\downarrow}\Big),

with
F=\sqcup_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{0}^{i}(\gamma_{1}^{i}X_{i})
the maximum blocking family for
T
. It follows that
T^{\prime}
has no exits in
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
, so that
T^{\prime}
is an isolated point in
\Omega(\overline{E},\overline{C})
by Proposition
8.8
.

Since
|\overline{X}|\geq 2
for all
\overline{X}\in\overline{C}
, it follows from Lemma
8.9
that
\Omega^{\text{iso}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})=\Omega^{\text{iso}}_{0}(\overline{E},\overline{C})
, hence
T^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\text{iso}}_{0}(\overline{E},\overline{C})
and
\mathbf{e}(T^{\prime})\in\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C}))
by Theorem
8.10
.

Hence we get

\mathbf{e}(T^{\prime})\in p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})p\cap\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C}))=\mathrm{soc}(p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})p)\cong\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)),

and thus
\mathbf{ecb}(T)\in\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C))\cap\mathcal{Q}
, as claimed.

Finally, suppose that
e
is a minimal idempotent in
\mathcal{Q}
.

Let
\overline{\varphi}\colon\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C)\to p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}({E},\overline{C})p
be the isomorphism from Theorem
5.5
, and set
\overline{\mathcal{Q}}=\overline{\varphi}(\mathcal{Q})
.
Then
\overline{\varphi}(e)
is a minimal idempotent in the ideal

p\,\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C}))p\cap\overline{\mathcal{Q}}
of
p\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C})p
.
Now observe that

p\,\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C}))p\cong\bigoplus_{[T]\in\overline{\Omega}^{\text{iso}}_{0}/{\mathcal{R}}}M_{\delta(T)}(K),

where
\delta(T)
is the number of elements in the orbit of
T
which belong to
\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(v)
, for some
v\in E^{0}
. Indeed a system of matrix units for the component

M_{\delta(T)}(K)
is given by the elements
e_{\alpha,\beta}
as in Theorem
8.10
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{C}(v)
for some
v\in E^{0}
.
It follows that there exists some
T^{\prime}\in\overline{\Omega}^{\text{iso}}_{0}
such that
T^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}_{L}(v)
for some
v\in E^{0}
and

\overline{\varphi}(e)\sim\mathbf{e}(T^{\prime})
within
p\,\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{E},\overline{C}))p
.
Now let

T^{\prime\prime}:=T^{\prime}\setminus\{\gamma d_{X}:\gamma d_{X}\in T^{\prime}\},

which is a tree in
\mathcal{X}(v)
and set
T=(T^{\prime\prime})_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}(v)
. Then
\mathcal{N}(T)
is finite because
T^{\prime}
has no exits with respect to
(\overline{E},\overline{C})
, and
\overline{\varphi}(\mathbf{ecb}(T))=\mathbf{e}(T^{\prime})
. It follows that

e\sim\mathbf{ecb}(T)
within
\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E,C))
. In particular,
\mathbf{ecb}(T)
is a minimal idempotent in
\mathcal{Q}
.

It remains to check that
\mathcal{N}(T)
is non-empty. Set
f:=\mathbf{ecb}(T)=\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)
, where
F
is the maximum blocking family for
T
. We have to show that
F\neq\emptyset
.
Since
f\in\mathcal{Q}
, it follows from Theorem
6.11
that we can write

(9.5)

f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{e}(T_{i}\setminus F_{i}),

where
\lambda_{i}\in K^{\times}
and
F_{i}
is a non-empty blocking family for
T_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
i=1,\dots,r
. We may assume that

f\mathbf{e}(T_{i}\setminus F_{i})\neq 0
for all
i
.
With this assumption, we then have

f=f\mathbf{e}(T_{i}\setminus F_{i})
by the minimality of
f
. Hence multiplying the relation (
9.5
) by
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{e}(T_{i}\setminus F_{i})
we get

f=(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i})\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{e}(T_{i}\setminus F_{i}).

Hence
f=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{e}(T_{i}\setminus F_{i})=\mathbf{e}\Big((\cup_{i=1}^{r}T_{i})\setminus(\cup_{i=1}^{r}F_{i})\Big)
by Lemma
6.6
, and thus
T=\cup_{i=1}^{r}T_{i}
,
F=\cup_{i=1}^{r}F_{i}\neq\emptyset
, by Lemma
6.9
. Hence
F\neq\emptyset
, and the proof is complete. ∎

Example 9.6
.

If
E
is a non-separated graph, the structure of the ideal
\mathcal{Q}
is transparent, see
[
2
, Propositions 1.5.8 and 1.5.11]
. Each regular vertex
v
gives rise to an idempotent
q_{v}=v-\sum_{e\in s^{-1}(v)}ee^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}
, and we have
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{C}_{K}(E))
. The idempotents
q_{v}
determine the different homogeneous components of
\mathcal{Q}
. These facts are no longer true for a separated graph, in which case the situation is much more involved, as we will see in the following examples. Note that, in the above situation,
\{v\}
can be completely blocked, and
\mathbf{ecb}(v)=q_{v}
.

Example 9.7
.

Let
X
be any non-empty set, and let
E_{X}
be the graph with just one vertex
v
and with
E_{X}^{1}=X
. We consider the free separation
C=C_{v}=\{\{e\}:e\in X\}
on
E_{X}
. This example was considered in
[
8
, Examples 3.3 and 4.9]
. We have

\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{X},C))=\begin{cases}M_{\infty}(K)&\text{ if }|X|=1\\
\,\,\,\quad 0&\text{ if }|X|>1.\end{cases}

The case where
|X|=1
is well-known, and corresponds to a non-separated graph. In that case,
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{X},C)
is just the usual Toeplitz algebra.

Assume that
|X|>1
. Let
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
. Then
T
can be identified with a usual Munn tree over
X
, with the property that each maximal path does not end in
X^{-1}
. If
\gamma
is a maximal path of
T
, then we can consider an element
\gamma\nu^{-1}y
, where
\nu
is an arbitrary word in positive edges
x\in X
, in such a way that the word
\gamma\nu^{-1}y
is reduced. Of course this is possible because
|X|>1
. Since
\nu
has arbitrary length, we conclude that
\mathcal{N}(T)
is infinite. Therefore
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{c}b}=\emptyset
and thus
\mathrm{soc}((\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{X},C)))=0
.

Remark 9.8
.

Although
\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{X},C))=0
when
|X|>1
, one may find a related algebra which has a nonzero socle, by imposing some extra commuting relations. Indeed Bavula introduced in
[
18
]
the algebra
\mathbb{S}_{n}
as the algebra generated by
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
with the defining relations

y_{1}x_{1}=\cdots=y_{n}x_{n}=1,\quad[x_{i},x_{j}]=[y_{i},y_{j}]=[x_{i},y_{j}]=0\,\text{ for all }i\neq j.

Clearly
\mathbb{S}_{n}
is a quotient algebra of
\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(E_{X_{n}},C)
, where
X_{n}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
.
By
[
18
, Proposition 4.1]
, the socle of
\mathbb{S}_{n}
is a simple, essential ideal of
\mathbb{S}_{n}
.

Example 9.9
.

Let
X
be any non-empty set. The free inverse monoid
\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)
is realized in
[
8
, Examples 3.4 and 4.10]
as a corner of the inverse semigroup of a certain separated graph
(F_{X},D)
, as follows.
Let
F_{X}
be the graph with
F_{X}^{0}=\{v\}\sqcup X
and
F_{X}^{1}=\{e_{x},f_{x}:x\in X\}
, with
s(e_{x})=s(f_{x})=v
and
r(e_{x})=r(f_{x})=x
for all
x\in X
.
We take the free separation
D
on
F_{X}
, so that
D_{v}=\{\{e_{x}\},\{f_{x}\}:x\in X\}
and
D_{x}=\emptyset
for all
x\in X
. Then by
[
8
, Example 4.10]
we have a (unique) monoid isomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)\to v\mathcal{S}(F_{X},D)v
such that
\varphi(x)=e_{x}f_{x}^{-1}
for all
x\in X
. Consequently, we get a
*
-isomorphism

\varphi\colon K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]\to v\cdot\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(F_{X},D)\cdot v

and so we can apply the theory of separated graphs to study the monoid algebra
K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]
.
It was shown in
[
12
, Proposition 4.5]
that, in the case where
|X|=1
, the socle of
K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]
is isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}M_{i}(K)
, and it is an essential ideal of
K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]
.

We can now generalize this result for any set
X
, as follows. The bipartite separated graph
(\overline{F_{X}},\overline{D})
consists in adding sinks
v_{x},v_{x}^{\prime}
and edges
d_{x},d_{x}^{\prime}
to
F_{X}
, for each
x\in X
, such that
s(d_{x})=s(d_{x}^{\prime})=v
,
r(d_{x})=v_{x}
,
r(d_{x}^{\prime})=v_{x}^{\prime}
, and

\overline{D}_{v}=\{\{e_{x},d_{x}\},\{f_{x},d_{x}^{\prime}\}:x\in X\}.

Note that
d_{x}d_{x}^{*}=v-e_{x}e_{x}^{*}
corresponds to
1-xx^{*}
and
d_{x}^{\prime}(d_{x}^{\prime})^{*}
corresponds to
1-x^{*}x
under the isomorphism

v\cdot\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{F_{X}},\overline{D})\cdot v\cong v\cdot\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(F_{X},D)\cdot v\cong K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]

obtained from Corollary
5.6
and
[
8
, Example 4.10]
.

As shown in
[
8
, Example 4.10]
, each
T\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
corresponds uniquely with an ordinary finite tree
T
, containing
1
, in the Cayley graph of the free group
\mathbb{F}
on
X
.
We will refer to such a tree simply as a finite tree, and we will denote the set of finite trees by
\mathfrak{M}
. The elements of
\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)
correspond to Munn trees
(T,g)
, where
T\in\mathfrak{M}
and
g\in T
, see for instance
[
31
, Section 6.4]
.

Given a finite tree
T\in\mathfrak{M}
, we define the
neighborhood

\mathcal{N}(T)
of
T
as the set of all the elements
\gamma z\in\mathbb{F}
, where
\gamma\in T
,
z\in X\cup X^{-1}
, and
\gamma z\notin T
. In other words the set
\mathcal{N}(T)
of
neighbors
of
T
is the set of words which are at distance
1
of
T
. Hence
\mathcal{N}(T)
is always non-empty, and it is finite if and only if
X
is finite. We say that
\gamma\in T
is an
interior point
of
T
if all the reduced words
\gamma z
,
z\in X\cup X^{-1}
, belong to
T
. Of course
T
does not have interior points when
X
is infinite. If
\gamma z\in\mathcal{N}(T)
, we say that
\gamma z
is a neighbor of
T
at
\gamma\in T
.

Define an equivalence relation
\sim
on the set
\mathfrak{M}
of finite trees
T
over
X
by declaring that
T\sim S
if and only if
S=g\cdot T
, where
g^{-1}\in T
.

Suppose that
X
is finite, and take
T\in\mathfrak{M}
. Then the idempotent
\mathbf{ecb}(T)
corresponds to the evaluation
\mathbf{e}(T^{\prime})
, where
T^{\prime}
is the tree obtained by adding to
T
maximal elements
\gamma d_{x}
for each
\gamma x\in\mathcal{N}(T)
, and
\gamma d^{\prime}_{x}
for each
\gamma x^{-1}\in\mathcal{N}(T)
, for each
x\in X
. Moreover, by Proposition
8.11
, we have

\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{F_{X}},\overline{D}))\cong\bigoplus_{[T]\in\mathfrak{M}/\sim}M_{|T^{\prime}|}(K).

Hence we get

\mathrm{soc}(K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)])=p\cdot\mathrm{soc}(\mathcal{L}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(\overline{F_{X}},\overline{D}))\cdot p\cong\bigoplus_{[T]\in\mathfrak{M}/\sim}M_{|T|}(K).

In conclusion, we obtain the following result for
K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]\cong v\cdot\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(F_{X},D)\cdot v
:

\mathrm{soc}(K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)])\cong\begin{cases}\bigoplus_{[T]\in\mathfrak{M}/{\sim}}M_{|T|}(K)&\text{ if }|X|<\infty\\
\qquad\qquad 0&\text{ if }|X|=\infty.\end{cases}

It is also easy to show that
\mathrm{soc}(K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)])
is essential in
K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]
when
X
is finite.

We fix a non-empty set
X
and we set
A=K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]
. We denote by
\mathcal{I}
the ideal of
A
generated by all the elements
1-xx^{*}
and
1-x^{*}x
, where
x\in X
.
The ideal
\mathcal{I}
corresponds to the ideal
v\mathcal{Q}v
of
v\cdot\mathcal{C}_{K}^{\mathrm{ab}}(F_{X},D)\cdot v\cong A
. The structure of the ideal
\mathcal{I}
of
K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]
was completely determined in the case where
|X|=1
in
[
12
, Lemma 4.4 and Proposition 4.5]
.

We can easily translate our results in Section
5
to obtain a linear basis of
\mathcal{I}
. For a finite tree
T
on
\mathbb{F}
, a finite set
F\subseteq\mathcal{N}(T)
and
g\in T
, we define a
blocked Munn tree

(T\setminus F,g)
as the tree
(T^{\prime},g)
, with maximal elements
\gamma d_{x}
if
\gamma x\in F
,
\gamma d_{x}^{\prime}
if
\gamma x^{-1}\in F
, and
\gamma
if
\gamma
is a maximal element of
T
such that no neighbor of
\gamma
belongs to
F
. Note that
T^{\prime}=T\setminus F
is not a usual tree on
X
, because it has some extra paths ending in sinks
v_{x}
or
v_{x}^{\prime}
. Each blocked Munn tree
(T\setminus F,g)
has an associated element
\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)\rtimes g\in A
, where

\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)=\prod_{\lambda\in\mathrm{max}(T\setminus F)}\mathbf{e}(\lambda),

where of course for
\lambda=\gamma d_{x}
,
\mathbf{e}(\lambda)=\gamma(1-xx^{*})\gamma^{*}
and for
\lambda=\gamma d_{x}^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{e}(\lambda)=\gamma(1-x^{*}x)\gamma^{*}
. Note that the idempotents
\mathbf{ecb}(T)
correspond exactly to
\mathbf{e}(T\setminus\mathcal{N}(T))
.

With this notation, the following result follows directly from Theorem
6.11
.

Proposition 9.10
.

Let
A=K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]
, where
X
is a non-empty set. Let
\mathcal{I}
be the ideal of
A
generated by the elements
1-xx^{*}
and
1-x^{*}x
, for all
x\in X
. Then the set
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{I})
consisting of all the elements
\mathbf{e}(T\setminus F)\rtimes g
, where
(T\setminus F,g)
is a blocked Munn tree such that all the maximal elements of
T
, except possibly
g
, are blocked by
F
, is a linear basis of
\mathcal{I}
. Hence

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{I})\cup\{1\rtimes g:g\in\mathbb{F}\}
is a linear basis of
A
.

The blocked Munn trees
(T\setminus F,g)
appearing in the basis
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{I})
have the property that all maximal elements, with the possible exception of
g
, are of the form
\gamma d_{x}
or
\gamma d_{x}^{\prime}
for some
x\in X
, so they are (at least partially) blocked. Observe that this basis is closed under products. The product is computed as follows:

\displaystyle(T_{1}\setminus F_{1},g_{1})\cdot(T_{2}\setminus F_{2},g_{2})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(T_{1}\cup g_{1}\cdot T_{2})\setminus(F_{1}\cup g_{1}\cdot F_{2}),g_{1}\cdot g_{2})&\text{ if }T_{1}\cap g_{1}\cdot F_{2}=g_{1}\cdot T_{2}\cap F_{1}=\emptyset\\
\qquad\quad 0&\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

Finally, we show that when
X
is finite, the ideal
\mathcal{I}
has a filtered structure. We say that
T\setminus F
has
t
exits if
|\mathcal{N}(T)\setminus F|=t
.

Proposition 9.11
.

Let
X
be a finite non-empty set, and set
A=K[\mathfrak{F_{im}}(X)]
. Then the ideal
\mathcal{I}
of
A
has a filtered structure
\mathcal{I}=\cup_{i=0}^{\infty}B_{i}
, where
B_{i}
is the linear span of the set
\mathbf{e}(I\setminus F)\rtimes g
, where
I\setminus F
has at most
i+1
exits.
In particular
B_{0}
is a subalgebra of
A
properly containing
\mathrm{soc}(A)
.

Proof.

We have to show that
B_{i}B_{j}\subseteq B_{i+j}
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
Suppose that
T_{1}\setminus F_{1}
has
i+1
exits and
T_{2}\setminus F_{2}
has
j+1
exits, and that
T_{1}\cap F_{2}=T_{2}\cap F_{1}=\emptyset
. We have to show that
(T_{1}\cup T_{2})\setminus(F_{1}\cup F_{2})
has
\leq i+j+1
exits. If
T_{1}=T_{2}
, then

\mathcal{N}(T_{1}\cup T_{2})-|F_{1}\cup F_{2}|\leq|\mathcal{N}(T_{1})|-|F_{1}|\leq i+1\leq i+j+1,

because
j\geq 0
. If
T_{1}\nsubseteq T_{2}
, take
\gamma\in T_{1}\setminus T_{2}
. Then
\gamma=\gamma_{1}z\gamma_{2}
, where
\gamma_{1}\in T_{2}
,
\gamma_{1}z\notin T_{2}
and
z\in X\cup X^{-1}
, so that
\gamma_{1}z\in\mathcal{N}(T_{2})
and
\gamma_{1}z\notin F_{2}
since
T_{1}\cap F_{2}=\emptyset
.
Now observe that

\mathcal{N}(T_{1}\cup T_{2})\setminus(F_{1}\cup F_{2})\subseteq(\mathcal{N}(T_{1})\setminus F_{1})\cup((\mathcal{N}(T_{2})\setminus F_{2})\setminus\{\gamma_{1}z\}),

hence
|\mathcal{N}(T_{1}\cup T_{2})\setminus(F_{1}\cup F_{2})|\leq(i+1)+(j+1-1)=i+j+1
, and thus
(T_{1}\cup T_{2})\setminus(F_{1}\cup F_{2})
has
\leq i+j+1
exits. By symmetry, the same inequality holds if
T_{2}\nsubseteq T_{1}
.
∎

The subalgebra
B_{0}
of
A
has some interesting properties. We always have
\mathrm{soc}(B_{0})=\mathrm{soc}(A)
, and
B_{0}/\mathrm{soc}(A)=\mathrm{soc}(A/\mathrm{soc}(A))
holds for
|X|=1

, but it does not hold for

, or
\mathbf{e}(T_{1}\setminus F_{1})\leq\mathbf{e}(T_{2}\setminus F_{2})
, or
\mathbf{e}(T_{2}\setminus F_{2})\leq\mathbf{e}(T_{1}\setminus F_{1})
.
