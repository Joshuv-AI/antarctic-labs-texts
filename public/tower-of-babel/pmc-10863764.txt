Physician Variation and the Impact of Payment Model in Cardiac Imaging
Authors: Amity E. Quinn, Derek S. Chew, Peter Faris, Flora Au, Matthew T. James, Marcello Tonelli, Braden J. Manns
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe influence of fee‐for‐service reimbursement on cardiac imaging has not been compared with other payment models. Furthermore, variation in ordering practices is not well understood.Methods and ResultsThis retrospective, population‐based cohort study using linked administrative data from Alberta, Canada included adults with chronic heart disease (atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, and heart failure) seen by cardiac specialists for a new outpatient consultation April 2012 to December 2018. Generalized linear mixed‐effects models estimated the association of payment model (including the ability to bill to interpret imaging tests) and the use of cardiac imaging and quantified variation in cardiac imaging. Among 31 685 adults seen by 308 physicians at 136 sites, patients received an observed mean of 0.67 (95% CI, 0.67–0.68) imaging tests per consultation. After adjustment, patients seeing fee‐for‐service physicians had 2.07 (95% CI, 1.68–2.54) and fee‐for‐service physicians with ability to interpret had 2.87 (95% CI, 2.16–3.81) times the rate of receiving a test than those seeing salaried physicians. Measured patient, physician, and site effects accounted for 31% of imaging variation and, following adjustment, reduced unexplained site‐level variation 40% and physician‐level variation 29%.ConclusionsWe identified substantial variation in the use of outpatient cardiac imaging related to physician and site factors. Physician payment models have a significant association with imaging use. Our results raise concern that payment models may influence cardiac imaging practice. Similar methods could be applied to identify the source and magnitude of variation in other health care processes and outcomes.

Heart disease is one of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity, and the second leading cause of death in North America. 1 , 2 Management of heart disease places a burden on patients, caregivers, and health care systems, including direct (ie, physician visits, drugs, hospitalizations) and indirect costs due to disability or lost productivity from premature death. 3 Diagnostic imaging for heart disease is an important tool to identify heart disease; however, it is both over‐ and underused. 4

Although a constellation of factors, including patient preferences, treatment guidelines, and wait times, can influence clinical decision making, 5 evidence suggests that treatment decisions are often driven by physician, not patient, factors. 6 , 7 Diagnostic imaging and other services in which clinical judgment plays a key role often have substantial variation and are more susceptible to the influence of economic incentives. 5 In Canada, clinical services are typically publicly paid by provincial or territorial governments and privately delivered at a mix of for profit and nonprofit facilities. 8 Canadian provinces have different approaches to the delivery of cardiac imaging. Ontario and Alberta both allow delivery of echocardiography and nuclear imaging in private, community‐based settings in addition to in public, institutional settings. This financial incentive may influence variation in imaging, which in turn may influence the efficient use of public resources. Recent developments in statistical methods enable the quantification of variation due to contextual factors such as differences between physicians and delivery sites. 9 , 10

Fee‐for‐service (FFS) reimbursement is associated with higher use compared with other payment models, but the influence of physician remuneration policies on treatment decisions appears to vary by specialty and treatment type. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Among cardiology specialists paid FFS, high‐volume billing is associated with increased use of cardiac imaging by patients with coronary artery disease, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation. 15 , 16 However, the influence of FFS on cardiac imaging, and specifically the ability to bill to interpret the tests themselves, has not been compared with other payment models.

This study aimed to determine the association of payment model (including the ability to bill to interpret imaging tests) and the use of cardiac imaging for patients with diagnosed or suspected chronic heart disease seen at a new outpatient consultation as well as quantify the proportion of variation in cardiac imaging due to differences in clinical practice. We focused on echocardiography and nuclear medicine test associated with new visits because such testing is commonly considered for a new consultation and can be performed in private facilities in Alberta.

METHODS

Data Availability

Alberta Health is the custodian of the data used in this study. We cannot make data available due to our contract for secondary data use with Alberta Health. Researchers can request similar data from the Alberta Strategy for Patient‐Oriented Research Support Unit.

Setting

In 1998, Alberta provincial Diagnostic Imaging Standards and Guidelines were released that supported select imaging modalities (echocardiography, magnetic resonance imaging, nuclear medicine, and ultrasound) to be performed in private as well as public facilities and required physicians practicing in either type of facility to obtain imaging modality certification to bill for imaging services. 17 Regardless of facility ownership, cardiac imaging services are fully paid for by the Alberta Health Insurance Plan. Physicians who see patients in private, community‐based facilities are paid FFS, whereas those with additional skills and accreditation in cardiac testing interpretation, are also able to bill to interpret imaging tests. Salaried physicians predominantly work in urban, academic medical centers.

Data

We used deidentified administrative health data from April 1, 2010 to March 31, 2019 for individuals registered with the Alberta Health Care Insurance Plan. Our analysis included linked demographic information, physician claims, hospitalizations, and physician characteristics. This study was approved by University of Calgary's Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board with waiver of informed consent.

Cohort

We created a retrospective, population‐based cohort of patients with suspected or diagnosed heart disease (heart failure, coronary artery disease, or atrial fibrillation) assessed at a new outpatient consultation by a specialist (defined as a cardiologist or internist who assesses >50 patients with chronic heart disease each year and at least 30% of their total outpatient claims being for heart disease [see Table S1]) between April 1, 2012 and December 31, 2018. Chronic heart failure, coronary artery disease, and atrial fibrillation were identified from administrative codes using validated algorithms. 18 A new consultation (Table S2) was defined as the absence of any consultation with a cardiologist or a visit with an internal medicine physician for a cardiac‐related issue (Table S1) in the prior 4 years. 11 , 19 We excluded patients who received only inpatient cardiology care or had a hospital admission on the same day as their index consultation, because imaging tests received would likely be associated with inpatient rather than outpatient care. We also excluded patients who saw physicians with a low volume of consultations, including locums and those with 5 or fewer new patient consultations, because care patterns among these physicians would likely be influenced by random variation (Figure 1).

Patient cohort flow diagram.

This diagram illustrates the inclusion and exclusion criteria used to create our cohort. Atrial fibrillation is defined as 1 hospitalization or 2 claims in ≤2 years. Cardiac care visits are defined as any claims that were seen by a cardiologist or any cardiovascular claims seen by an internist. Locum physician is defined as <100 consultation claims per year.

Outcome

The primary outcome was number of echocardiography (transthoracic) and nuclear medicine (myocardial perfusion imaging and multigated acquisition scan) tests associated with the index consultation identified in claims data (Table S3). We assumed procedures that occurred from 30 days before the visit to 90 days after the visit were related to the index consultation. This window of time accounts for wait times to see specialists and access imaging services in Canada.

Payment Model

The explanatory variable encompassed both payment model and the ability to bill for interpreting cardiac imaging. This variable was created using physician claims data and included 3 categories: salary‐based, FFS physicians, and FFS interpreters. Physicians were first identified as salary‐based or FFS using 2 variables, 1 that indicates payment model and 1 that indicates the amount of money paid per claim to confirm the payment model variable (salary physicians are paid $0 per claim, but claims [shadow billing] are still required contractually). Although all physicians in this study were independent contractors paid by the provincial government, salary‐based physicians were compensated using an annual salary‐like model. Salary‐based physicians can still interpret cardiac imaging studies, but receive no additional payment for interpretation. Fee‐for‐service physicians with >100 imaging claims identified across their total patient population in physician claims data during the 7‐year study period were assumed both to have received and maintained imaging modality approvals and were defined as FFS interpreters.

Other Variables

Patient covariates included age, sex, neighborhood income quintile, rural versus urban residence, neighborhood proportion of visible minorities, and 30 comorbidities defined using validated algorithms, 18 including diabetes and stroke, using linked registry, census, claims, and hospitalization data. Physician characteristics included age, sex, specialty (cardiology or internal medicine), clinical workload, years since credential issued, and country of clinical training using claims and characteristics data. We also identified physicians' annual primary practice site as the clinic location where the largest proportion of their claims were made as well as the geographic zone of that location.

Statistical Analysis

Patient, physician, and practice site baseline characteristics were compared by physician type. We then calculated the observed number of tests per patient by physician type. Categorical and continuous variables were analyzed via χ 2 and Kruskal–Wallis tests, respectively.

For any test, as well as echocardiograms and nuclear imaging separately, we estimated the association of payment model and imaging use and quantified physician and practice site variation with general linear mixed models (GLMM) with a negative binomial distribution. The negative binomial model includes an overdispersion term that accounts for omitted patient variables. We estimated 2‐level models (including a physician random intercept to account for patients clustered within physicians) and 3‐level models (adding a site random intercept to account for physicians clustered within sites). For each outcome, we estimated a series of 4 models that sequentially added fixed effects: (1) an unconditional model including no fixed effects, (2) a model adding physician characteristics, (3) a model adding measured patient cardiovascular risk characteristics (sex, age, chronic heart disease diagnosis, diabetes, stroke), and (4) a model adding physician's site characteristics. By sequentially adding physician, patient, and site variables, we adjusted for factors that may influence the likelihood of cardiac imaging and quantified total and level‐specific explained and unexplained variation. We calculated marginal statistics (marginal expectation, marginal variance, and variance components) following Leckie et al, using sample mean values in models with covariates. 9 We also calculated variance partition coefficients, 9 which represent the unexplained variation at each level, the proportion change in variance, 20 which represents the amount of variation at each level explained by fixed effects, and a marginal R 2 GLMM, 21 which represents the total variance explained by fixed effects.

Funnel plots 22 were used to illustrate variation between physicians in observed imaging tests. These plots illustrate whether the mean number of imaging tests per patient ordered by a physician differs significantly from the mean across all physicians. The plot contains 2 funnel limits. Assuming physician differences arise randomly, the chance of the physician being within the limits is 95% for the inside funnel and 99.8% for the outer funnel.

In a sensitivity analysis, we repeated our main results in a larger cohort including all outpatient consultations to cardiologists and cardiology‐focused internal medicine physicians, regardless of diagnosis or whether the visit was a new or follow‐up visit. All P values were 2‐sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Study Cohort

We identified 31 685 adults with chronic heart disease seen by cardiac specialist physicians for a new outpatient consultation from April 1, 2012 to December 31, 2018 (Figure 1). Half of the patients had atrial fibrillation (51.8%), whereas 8.8% had coronary artery disease, and 39.4% had chronic heart failure. Over 9% of patients had >1 of these conditions (data not shown). Most patients were men (57.6%), aged ≥65 years (61.0%), and resided in urban areas (84.7%) (Table 1).

Patients were seen by 308 physicians (56 salary‐based, 180 FFS physicians, and 72 FFS interpreters) at 136 sites (50 in Urban Zone 1, 12 in Rural Zone 2, 52 in Urban Zone 3, 10 in Rural Zone 4, 12 in Rural Zone 5). FFS interpreters saw over half of the patients (n=16 799). There were significant differences in patient and physician characteristics (Table 1) among physicians by payment model.

Observed Use of Cardiac Imaging

Patients received a mean of 0.67 (95% CI, 0.67–0.68) imaging tests 30 days before to 90 days after a new consultation, including a mean of 0.42 (95% CI, 0.41–0.42) echocardiograms and 0.26 (95% CI, 0.25–0.26) nuclear tests per patient (Table 2). Imaging use was lowest for patients seeing salaried physicians and highest for patients seeing FFS interpreters. For example, the number of imaging tests among patients seeing salaried physicians, FFS physicians, and FFS interpreters was 0.23 (95% CI, 0.22–0.24), 0.55 (95% CI, 0.53–0.56), and 0.86 (95% CI, 0.85–0.87), respectively. Cardiologists ordered significantly more echocardiography tests per patient (0.43 versus 0.36, P<0.010) and fewer nuclear imaging tests per patient (0.25 versus 0.27, P<0.01) than internal medicine physicians (data not shown).

Observed Imaging by Physician Payment Model

Physician Variation in Observed Imaging Use

Observed imaging use varied substantially among physicians, ranging from a mean of 0 to 2.92 tests per patient (Figure 2). Forty‐two physicians (13.6%) ordered a significant excess of imaging tests beyond that expected in comparison with other physicians (ie, exceeded the 99.8% confidence limits), whereas 65 (21%) ordered fewer than expected. FFS interpreters tended to see more patients and were the most likely to order more tests than expected. FFS and salaried physicians had fewer new consultations, and salaried physicians ordered the fewest tests per consultation.

Impact of Physician, Patient, and Practice Site Differences and Effects on Imaging Variation

In the 2‐level unconditional model, 25% of the variation in imaging use was attributable to differences between physicians (Table 3). After adding a practice site random intercept, 10% of the variation was attributable to differences between sites and 14% to differences among physicians. Measured physician, patient, and site factors accounted for 31% of imaging variation. Adjustment for these factors reduced the unexplained variation attributable to site by 40% and that attributable to physicians by 29% variation. Over 80% of the unexplained variation was at the patient level; however, adjustment for measured physician, patient, and site factors only explained 8% of variation attributable to patients.

There was little variation in echocardiography testing, but considerable variation in nuclear imaging as indicated by the difference between the marginal variance and marginal expectation (Table 3). In the 3‐level unconditional model, 8% of unexplained echocardiography variation was due to physician and site factors, whereas 33% of unexplained nuclear imaging was due to these factors. Accounting for measured physician, patient, and site factors explained at least half of the site and physician variation in both echocardiography and nuclear imaging. These factors also explained 47% of patient‐level variation in nuclear imaging, but only 2% of patient‐level variation in echocardiography.

Association Between Payment Model and Imaging

Payment model was significantly associated with imaging use in all regression models for all outcomes. After adjusting for physician, patient, and site effects, patients seeing FFS physicians had 2.07 (95% CI, 1.68–2.54) times and FFS interpreters had 2.87 (95% CI, 2.16–3.81) times the rate of cardiac imaging than those seeing salaried physicians (Table 4).

Sensitivity Analysis

In a sensitivity analysis including all outpatient consultations to cardiologists and cardiology‐focused internal medicine physicians, regardless of diagnosis or whether the visit was a new or follow‐up visit, patients seeing FFS physicians had 2.29 (95% CI, 1.87–2.80) and FFS physicians with ability to interpret had 4.68 (95% CI, 3.81–5.76) times the rate of receiving a test than those seeing salaried physicians.

DISCUSSION

We identified substantial physician variation in cardiac imaging, and physician payment model was significantly associated with the use of cardiac imaging. After adjusting for all measured physician, patient, and site effects, FFS physicians ordered approximately 2 times and FFS interpreters ordered approximately 3 times more tests than salaried physicians. Measured patient, physician, and site effects accounted for 31% of imaging variation and reduced unexplained site‐level variation by 40% and physician‐level variation by 29%.

Recent studies using similar cohorts in Ontario found comparable imaging rates (≈0.50 echocardiograms and 0.07 myocardial perfusion imaging tests per patient in 2016 23 ) and an association between high‐volume FFS billing and higher cardiac test use, but not improved outcomes. 15 , 16 Because of the range of approaches to studying clinical variation, comparison with other research is challenging. It does appear patient, provider, and location factors may play different roles in treatment decisions depending on service type. Two studies found decisions to initiate dialysis appear to be driven by patient‐level factors, with less variation attributable to site and physician differences than we identified. 24 , 25 However, which patient characteristics are driving dialysis decisions are not well understood. 24 Local competition can also play a role in treatment decisions. For instance, the supply of cardiac catheterization laboratories in New England was associated with the rate of interventional cardiac tests. 26

There are some limitations of our work. First, our study population of patients with heart failure, coronary artery disease, and atrial fibrillation seen at new outpatient consultations limits the generalizability of our findings. We defined the cohort in the way we did to identify visits where testing would be considered and to increase the internal validity around new physician visits, because our prior work has identified differences in patient selection by physician payment model. 27 However, in a sensitivity analysis of all outpatient consultations to cardiologists and cardiology‐focused internal medicine physicians, regardless of diagnosis or whether the visit was a new visit or a follow‐up visit, we found similar and even stronger associations. Second, although we assumed tests 30 days before and up to 90 days following the consultation were associated with the visit itself, we cannot confirm who ordered a test. We examined the distribution of imaging tests 90 days before patients' initial visits and up to a year following their initial visits. The majority of tests, particularly nuclear studies, occurred during the −30‐ to +90‐day window. Given that there is a short wait time (<30 days) for cardiology imaging studies in Alberta, but often long waits for specialist consultation, we assumed that tests ordered within the 30 days before or 90 days after were related to the clinic visit. Although we cannot be certain that the tests were ordered by a specialist, if primary care physicians were ordering many of the tests when referring patients to specialists, we would expect this to dilute the observed association between payment model and imaging rate. Third, we do not know the appropriateness of the tests ordered due to lack of clinical information in administrative data. However, if all tests ordered were appropriate, we would expect the different types of physicians to order a more similar number of tests. Future work could investigate appropriateness by evaluating the relationship between practice and outcomes with our improved understanding of which patient, physician, and site factors account for clinical variation. Finally, much of the unexplained variation in practice is at the patient level. Despite observing significant patient and physician baseline differences by payment model, we did not address them in our study design using propensity score matching as in prior work. 9 Instead, we quantified these differences and investigated what was driving them. Although the patient cardiovascular risk factors that we adjusted for do not explain substantial variation in our composite outcome or echocardiography, it is possible unmeasured cardiovascular health‐related variables (eg, pain, clinical history) or other patient characteristics (eg, preferences, neighborhood) could account for clinical variation.

We identified substantial variation in the use of cardiac imaging and important physician and site differences and effects associated with imaging use. Physician payment models have a significant association with imaging use. Our results suggest the need for further studies that assess cardiac imaging practice, including decision‐making processes, to determine if policy changes are needed to improve the value of care by increasing certainty or reducing variation, as well as assessing the influence of physician and site effects. This could include changes to physician payment models, incorporating clinical decision support or clinical review in the imaging ordering process, or educating physicians (eg, audit and feedback program that compares each physician's personal or practice site use to those of their colleagues 28 ). Our methods can be applied to other areas of clinical practice to highlight where there is variation in practice and to understand what accounts for this variation. An improved understanding of what accounts for practice variation would subsequently improve our understanding of the relationship between practice and outcomes.
