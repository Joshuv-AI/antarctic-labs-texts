Strong solutions for a class of stochastic thermo-magneto-hydrodynamic-type systems with multiplicative noise
Agus L. Soenjaya, Thanh Tran

Abstract
. We study the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions, in the sense of PDEs and probability, for a broad class of nonlinear stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) on a bounded domain 𝒪 ⊂ ℝ d \mathscr{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} ( d ≤ 3 d\leq 3 ), perturbed by spatially correlated multiplicative noise. Our framework applies to many physically relevant systems, including stochastic convective Brinkman–Forchheimer equations, stochastic magnetohydrodynamics (MHD), stochastic Bénard convection in porous media, stochastic convective dynamo models, and stochastic magneto-micropolar fluids, among others. The analysis relies on Galerkin approximations and compactness arguments. Up to a suitable stopping time, we derive strong moment bounds and verify a Cauchy property for the approximate solutions, in the absence of any inherent cancellation structure. By applying a Gronwall-type lemma for stochastic processes, we establish the existence and uniqueness of maximal strong pathwise solutions, which are global in two spatial dimensions. These results provide a unified treatment of a wide class of nonlinear stochastic thermo-magneto-fluid models in the literature.



1.
Introduction

This paper investigates the existence and uniqueness of
strong
solutions (in the sense of PDEs and probability) for a class of abstract nonlinear stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) defined on a bounded domain
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d=2,3
. These problems are motivated by thermally-coupled models in magneto-hydrodynamics, which describe the interplay between thermal, magnetic, and fluid dynamic phenomena under stochastic influences. We consider stochastic evolution equations of the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi+\mathcal{A}\Phi+\mathcal{B}(\Phi,\Phi)+\mathcal{R}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=g(\Phi)\dot{W},

\displaystyle\Phi(0)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0},

which describes the time evolution of a state vector
\Phi
. Here,
\mathcal{A}
is a linear operator representing dissipative effects,
\mathcal{B}
is a bilinear form capturing convective nonlinearities,
\mathcal{R}
is a nonlinear operator accounting for other reactive physical effects. The term
g(\Phi)\dot{W}
represents a multiplicative noise, which is white in time and spatially correlated, modelling random forcing that depends on the state of the system. We impose a set of conditions on the maps
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{R}
, and
g
to cover a range of physically relevant thermo-magneto-hydrodynamical systems.

While the existence of
martingale weak
or
pathwise weak
solutions to SPDEs arising from specific magneto-hydrodynamical models, including the stochastic Navier–Stokes
[
6
,
19
]
, Boussinesq
[
15
,
52
]
, or MHD equations
[
38
,
43
]
, has been addressed in a number of separate works, these studies typically analyse each equation in isolation. In contrast, this paper develops an abstract setting that encompasses a wide range of thermally-coupled stochastic magneto-hydrodynamical-type systems. Beyond unifying the treatment of previously studied models, our formulation also captures several important stochastic nonlinear systems that have not, to our knowledge, been rigorously analysed before in either the weak or strong sense.

The existence of pathwise weak solutions for a class of SPDEs arising in fluid dynamics is established in
[
12
]
. While our work is related to that result, the contributions here are fundamentally different in both analytical strength and scope. Specifically,
[
12
]
proves the existence of pathwise solutions that are weak in the analytical sense, whereas we establish the existence of pathwise solutions that are analytically strong. In addition, our framework accommodates equations with more general nonlinearities, extending beyond the convective-type nonlinearities treated in other works. Examples of models covered by our setting, but not necessarily by that in
[
12
]
, are given in Section
3
, where some of the corresponding well-posedness results appear to be new.

Our approach also departs significantly from that in
[
12
]
. Although both proofs employ Galerkin approximations and compactness arguments, our analysis requires substantially more delicate estimates. In particular, up to a suitable stopping time, we must derive strong moment bounds and verify a Cauchy property for the approximate solutions in the absence of any inherent cancellation structure. Finally, by applying a Gronwall-type lemma for stochastic processes from
[
24
]
, we conclude the existence and uniqueness of maximal strong pathwise solutions, which are global in the case
d=2
.

This paper is organised as follows:

•

Section
2
sets up the functional framework in which the problem is posed and summarises the standing assumptions used throughout the paper.

•

Section
3
presents several nonlinear physical models that satisfy the assumptions outlined in Section

.

•

Section
4
establishes the existence and uniqueness of a local strong pathwise solution to problem (
1.1
).

•

Section
5
extends the local strong solution to a maximal (random) time interval and shows that this maximal strong solution is global when

.

•

Auxiliary results used in the paper are collected in Appendix

Our main results are the existence of a unique maximal strong pathwise solution for

(Theorem

) and the existence of a global strong pathwise solution for

(Theorem
5.3
).

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Functional setting

Let
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
d=2,3
, be an open bounded domain with
C^{2}
-smooth boundary or a convex Lipschitz domain. Let
(\mathcal{H},\left|{\cdot}\right|)
be a separable Hilbert space. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an unbounded, self-adjoint, positive linear operator on
\mathcal{H}
, defined on a domain
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{H}
, such that
\mathcal{A}
is a bijection onto
\mathcal{H}
with a compact inverse. Consequently, there exists an orthonormal basis
\{e_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
for
\mathcal{H}
consisting of eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
. The associated positive eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
form an unbounded increasing sequence. Given
\alpha>0
, let

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\alpha})=\left\{v\in\mathcal{H}:\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2\alpha}\left|{\left\langle v,e_{k}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}\right|^{2}<\infty\right\}.

For
v\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\alpha})
, we define

\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}v:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\left\langle v,e_{k}\right\rangle e_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|{v}\right|_{\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\alpha})}^{2}:=\left|{\mathcal{A}^{\alpha}v}\right|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{2\alpha}\left|{\left\langle v,e_{k}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}\right|^{2}.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{2}})
, equipped with the norm
\left\|{v}\right\|:=\left|{v}\right|_{\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{2}})}
for any
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Let
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
denote the dual of
\mathcal{V}
with respect to the inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
of
\mathcal{H}
. Thus, we have a quartet of compactly embedded Hilbert spaces
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Let
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle
denote the duality pairing between
u\in\mathcal{V}
and
v\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, such that
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle=\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
whenever
u\in\mathcal{V}
and
v\in\mathcal{H}
.

2.2.
Assumptions

We assume that the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}
satisfies the following conditions.

Condition (B)
:

•

\mathcal{B}:\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is a bilinear continuous map.

•

For any
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in\mathcal{V}
,

(2.1)

\left\langle\mathcal{B}(v_{1},v_{2}),v_{3}\right\rangle=-\left\langle\mathcal{B}(v_{1},v_{3}),v_{2}\right\rangle.

•

For any
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in\mathcal{V}
,

(2.2)

\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{B}(v_{1},v_{2}),v_{3}\right\rangle}\right|\leq C\left\|{v_{1}}\right\|\left\|{v_{2}}\right\|\left|{v_{3}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|{v_{3}}\right\|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

•

There exists
C>0
such that for any
v_{1},v_{3}\in\mathcal{V}
and
v_{2}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})
,

(2.3)

\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{B}(v_{1},v_{2}),v_{3}\right\rangle}\right|\leq\begin{cases}C\left|{v_{1}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|{v_{1}}\right\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|{v_{2}}\right\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|{\mathcal{A}v_{2}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|{v_{3}}\right|,&\text{if $d=2$},\\[4.30554pt]
C\left\|{v_{1}}\right\|\left\|{v_{2}}\right\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|{\mathcal{A}v_{2}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|{v_{3}}\right|,&\text{if $d\leq 3$}.\end{cases}

•

\mathcal{B}(v,v)\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{8}})
for all
v\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})
, and there exists
C>0
such that for any
v\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{B}(v,v)}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{v}\right\|^{3}\left|{\mathcal{A}v}\right|,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{B}(v,v)}\right|_{\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{8}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}\left|{\mathcal{A}v}\right|^{2}.

In particular, the antisymmetry property (
2.1
) implies
\left\langle\mathcal{B}(v_{1},v_{2}),v_{2}\right\rangle=0
for any
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}
. Subsequently, we will write
\mathcal{B}(v):=\mathcal{B}(v,v)
for simplicity.

We assume that the nonlinear operator
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the following conditions.

Condition (R)
:

•

\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is a continuous map defined by
\mathcal{R}(v)=\mathcal{S}(v)+\mathcal{F}(v)
satisfying
\mathcal{F}(v)\in\mathcal{H}
, such that for any
v\in\mathcal{V}
,

(2.6)

\left\langle\mathcal{S}(v),v\right\rangle\geq 0\;\text{ and }\;\left|{\mathcal{F}(v)}\right|\leq C\left(1+\left\|{v}\right\|\right).

•

There exists
C>0
such that for any
v_{1}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})
and
v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}
,

(2.7)

\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{R}(v_{1}),v_{2}\right\rangle}\right|\leq\begin{cases}C\left(1+\left|{v_{1}}\right|^{\frac{3}{2}}\left|{\mathcal{A}v_{1}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left\|{v_{1}}\right\|\left|{v_{2}}\right|,&\text{if $d=2$},\\[4.30554pt]
C\left(1+\left|{v_{1}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|{v_{1}}\right\|\left|{\mathcal{A}v_{1}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left\|{v_{1}}\right\|\left|{v_{2}}\right|,&\text{if $d\leq 3$}.\end{cases}

•

There exists
C>0
such that for any
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}
and
v_{3}\in\mathcal{V}
,

(2.8)

\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{R}(v_{1})-\mathcal{R}(v_{2}),v_{3}\right\rangle}\right|\leq C\left(1+\left\|{v_{1}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{v_{2}}\right\|^{2}\right)\left\|{v_{1}-v_{2}}\right\|\left|{v_{3}}\right|.

•

\mathcal{R}(v)\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{8}})
for all
v\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})
, and there exists
C>0
such that for any
v\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})
,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{R}(v)}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left\|{v}\right\|^{4}\right)\left\|{v}\right\|^{2},

(2.10)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{R}(v)}\right|_{\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{8}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left\|{v}\right\|^{4}\right)\left\|{v}\right\|\left|{\mathcal{A}v}\right|.

The stochastic term in (
1.1
) can be written formally as

(2.11)

g(\Phi)\dot{W}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}g_{k}(\Phi)\dot{\beta}_{k},

where
\beta_{k}
are independent standard Brownian motions. We need to impose some assumptions on
g=\{g_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. To this end, we first define the following notion: for any normed spaces
Y
and
Z
, a progressively measurable map
h:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\times Y\to Z
is said to be uniformly Lipschitz with constant
L_{Y}
if, for all
u,v\in Y
and
(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times[0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\left|{h(\omega,t,u)-h(\omega,t,v)}\right|_{Z}\leq L_{Y}\left|{u-v}\right|_{Y}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|{h(\omega,t,u)}\right|_{Z}\leq L_{Y}\left(1+\left|{u}\right|_{Y}\right).

The collection of all such mappings is denoted by
\text{Lip}(Y,Z)
.

For our analysis, we shall assume the following condition on
g
.

Condition (G)
:
A progressively measurable map
g:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\times\mathcal{H}\to\ell^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is given such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle g\in\text{Lip}\big(\mathcal{H},\ell^{2}(\mathcal{H})\big)\cap\text{Lip}\big(\mathcal{V},\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})\big)\cap\text{Lip}\big(\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}),\ell^{2}(\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}))\big).

3.
Physical models

In this section, we present several physically relevant models that fall within the scope of our framework. We note, however, that our assumptions are not limited to these thermo-magneto-fluid models and can accommodate a broader class of nonlinear stochastic PDEs arising in various contexts.

3.1.
Stochastic convective Brinkman–Forchheimer equations

The stochastic convective Brinkman–Forchheimer (or damped Navier–Stokes) equations
[
9
,
25
,
30
,
37
]
with Dirichlet (no-slip) boundary conditions read:

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\nu\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\alpha u+\beta|u|^{r-1}u+\nabla p=g(u)\dot{W}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,u=0\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.1c)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.1d)

\displaystyle u=0\;

on
\partial\mathscr{O}
,

where
r\in[2,3]
and
g
satisfies (
2.12
). Here,
u:\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the fluid velocity and

is the pressure. The positive coefficients
\nu
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
respectively denote the kinematic viscosity (Brinkman coefficient), the Darcy coefficient (permeability of porous medium), and the Forchheimer coefficient. The system (
3.1
) describes the flow of a viscous fluid at sufficiently high velocity and moderate porosity, perturbed by a stochastic forcing term
g(u)\dot{W}
, where
g
satisfies condition (G) in (
2.12
).

The existence and uniqueness of probabilistically and analytically strong solutions to (
3.1
) in the torus (or a domain with periodic boundary conditions) are established in
[
30
,
37
]
. For the case of Dirichlet boundary condition, a remark is made in
[
34
,
37
]
that the arguments presented in
[
23
,
35
]
contain a gap stemming from the use of an incorrect identity in various parts of the proof, which invalidates the corresponding existence result. In this work, we correct these mistakes and establish the existence of pathwise strong solutions for
r\in[2,3]
, including the case of Dirichlet boundary condition. When
d=2
, the solutions are shown to exist globally in time. Our results also extend to the case of Navier-type (or free) boundary conditions
[
4
,
56
]
.

Define the spaces:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle:=\{u\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,u=0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle:=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,u=0\text{ and }\left.u\cdot n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\}.

By means of the Helmholtz–Leray projector
\mathbb{P}:L^{2}(\mathscr{O})\to\mathcal{H}
, noting that
\mathbb{P}v=v
and
\mathbb{P}[g(v)\dot{W}]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[g_{k}(v)]\dot{\beta}_{k}
for

due to (

) and (

), we can project (

) onto the space of divergence-free vector fields to formulate problem (
3.1
) in the form (
1.1
), with

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}v=-\nu\mathbb{P}\Delta v,

\displaystyle\forall v\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(v_{1},v_{2})=\mathbb{P}\big[(v_{1}\cdot\nabla)v_{2}\big],

\displaystyle\forall v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(v)=\alpha v+\beta\mathbb{P}\big[|v|^{r-1}v\big],

\displaystyle\forall v\in\mathcal{V},

where
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}):=H^{2}(\mathscr{O})\cap\mathcal{V}
.
The operator

is called the

.

The map
\mathcal{B}
satisfies (
2.1
) by the definition of

and simple calculations. It also satisfies inequalities (

) and (

) by a standard argument using Hölder’s inequality and Sobolev embedding (see

]
). Furthermore, by the boundedness of

and Hölder’s inequality (with exponents

when

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{B}(v,v)}\right|^{2}\leq\left|{v}\right|_{L^{p}}^{2}\left|{\nabla v}\right|_{L^{q}}^{2}\leq C\big(\left|{v}\right|\left\|{v}\right\|\big)\big(\left\|{v}\right\|\left|{\mathcal{A}v}\right|\big)\leq C\left\|{v}\right\|^{3}\left|{\mathcal{A}v}\right|,

where in the penultimate step we used the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequalities, thus showing (
2.4

\displaystyle\left|{(v\cdot\nabla)v}\right|_{H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|{v}\right|_{L^{4}}^{2}\left|{\nabla v}\right|_{L^{4}}^{2}+\left|{v}\right|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\left|{\mathcal{A}v}\right|^{2}+\left|{\nabla v}\right|_{L^{p}}^{2}\left|{\nabla v}\right|_{L^{q}}^{2}\leq C\left\|{v}\right\|\left|{\mathcal{A}v}\right|^{3}.

In particular, this implies

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{B}(v,v)}\right|_{\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{8}})}^{2}

) and (
3.6

), (

Remark 3.1

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle:=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,u=0\text{ in $\mathscr{O}$ and }\left.u\cdot n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle:=H^{2}(\mathscr{O})\cap\mathcal{V}.

The verification of condition (B) and condition (R) proceeds in the same manner. To verify (
3.7
) and (
3.1
), the corresponding results in
[
31
]
for the Hodge–Stokes operator are used.

3.2.
Stochastic magnetohydrodynamic equations

The dynamics of turbulent motion of plasmas and liquid metals
[
7
]
can be described by the stochastic magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equations
[
38
,
42
]
, which is a coupled system of stochastic Navier–Stokes and stochastic Maxwell-type equations. Such model is important in the study of the magnetic reconnection problem in astrophysics
[
39
]
. The stochastic MHD system read:

(3.12a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\nu\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u-(B\cdot\nabla)B+\nabla\left(p+\frac{1}{2}\left|{B}\right|^{2}\right)=g_{1}(u,B)\dot{W}_{1}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.12b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}B-\kappa\Delta B+(u\cdot\nabla)B-(B\cdot\nabla)u=g_{2}(u,B)\dot{W}_{2}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.12c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,u=\mathrm{div}\,B=0\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.12d)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\;B(0,x)=B_{0}(x)\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.12e)

\displaystyle u=0,\;

on
\partial\mathscr{O}
,

(3.12f)

\displaystyle B\cdot n=0,\;\mathrm{curl}\;B\times n=0\;

on
\partial\mathscr{O}
.

The fields
u:\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
B:\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the fluid velocity and the magnetic flux density, respectively. Here,
g_{1}\dot{W}_{1}
and
g_{2}\dot{W}_{2}
are the stochastic forcing terms, where
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are independent real-valued Brownian motions. One can also add the term
\alpha u+\beta\left|{u}\right|^{r-1}u
to the left-hand side of (
3.12a
) to model a damped flow or a flow in porous media as in
[
5
,
45
]
.

The existence of martingale weak (in the sense of PDEs) solutions to the stochastic MHD systems with multiplicative noise for
d\leq 3
is shown in
[
43
,
52
]
for a bounded domain, and in
[
38
]
for a Poincaré domain. Pathwise uniqueness of the solution is also derived for
d\leq 2
, which implies the existence of pathwise solutions (weak in the sense of PDEs). The argument in this paper establishes the existence and uniqueness of probabilistically and analytically strong solutions to the stochastic MHD system (including its Brinkman–Forchheimer modification). Our results also extend to the case of perfect slip boundary conditions for the fluid (
3.11
) or periodic boundary conditions.

The appropriate functional setting for this problem will now be described. Define the spaces:

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{u\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,u=0\},

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,u=0\text{ in $\mathscr{O}$ and }\left.u\cdot n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\},

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{B\in H^{1}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,B=0\text{ in $\mathscr{O}$, }\left.B\cdot n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\,\text{ and }\left.\mathrm{curl}\,B\times n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\},

\displaystyle H_{2}

\displaystyle:=H_{1}=\{B\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,u=0\text{ in $\mathscr{O}$ and }\left.u\cdot n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle:=V_{1}\times V_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle:=H_{1}\times H_{2}.

As before,
\mathbb{P}:L^{2}(\mathscr{O})\to H_{1}
is the Helmholtz–Leray projector. Let
A_{1}=-\nu\mathbb{P}\Delta
be the Dirichlet–Stokes operator and
A_{2}=-\kappa\Delta
be the Hodge Laplacian operator. Let
\mathcal{A}=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}
with domain
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{V}\cap\left[H^{2}(\mathscr{O})\right]^{2}
acting on
\Phi=(u,B)
. The map
\mathcal{B}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{P}\big[(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)u_{2}-(B_{1}\cdot\nabla)B_{2}\big]\\[2.15277pt]
(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)B_{2}-(B_{1}\cdot\nabla)u_{2}\end{pmatrix}

for
\Phi_{i}=(u_{i},B_{i})\in\mathcal{V}
, where
i=1,2
. The stochastic term is
g(\Phi)\dot{W}=(g_{1}\dot{W}_{1},\,g_{2}\dot{W}_{2})^{\top}
. In this case,
\mathcal{R}(\Phi)=0
.

The map
\mathcal{B}
satisfies (
2.1
), as well as inequalities (
2.2
) and (
2.3
); see
[
38
]
. The verification of (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) proceeds in the same manner as in (
3.5
) and (
3.7
).

3.3.
Stochastic Boussinesq model for the Bénard convection

A model for convection in a fluid due to Bénard consists of a coupled system of Navier–Stokes and heat equations, employing Boussinesq approximation (see
[
2
,
20
,
22
]
and references therein). One can also use the Brinkman–Forchheimer equation instead for the fluid flow, as in
[
3
,
41
,
46
]
. Let
e
be a given vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and suppose that the non-homogeneous boundary temperature data is fixed. By employing a transformation procedure
[
26
]
, one can convert the problem into a system with homogeneous Dirichlet data, which reads:

(3.13a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\nu\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\alpha u+\beta|u|^{r-1}u+\nabla p+\theta e=g_{1}(u,\theta)\dot{W}_{1}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.13b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta-\kappa\Delta\theta+(u\cdot\nabla)\theta+(u\cdot\nabla)\phi=g_{2}(u,\theta)\dot{W}_{2}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.13c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,u=0\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.13d)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.13e)

\displaystyle u=0,\;\theta=0\;

on
\partial\mathscr{O}
.

In the above system,
u:\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\theta:\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the fluid velocity and the temperature, respectively. The function
\phi\in H^{2}(\mathscr{O})
is assumed to be given. The non-negative coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
are, respectively, the Darcy and the Forchheimer coefficients. Perfect slip boundary conditions for
u
as defined by (
3.11
) can also be used.

The existence of probabilistically strong, analytically weak solutions to the problem (
3.13
) is established in
[
12
]
. The argument in this paper proves the existence and uniqueness of probabilistically and analytically strong solutions to this problem for
d\leq 3
(which is global when
d\leq 2
).

We now describe the functional setting for this system. Define the spaces:

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,v=0\},

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{v\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,v=0\;\text{ and }\left.v\cdot n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\},

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle:=H^{1}_{0}(\mathscr{O}),

\displaystyle H_{2}

\displaystyle:=L^{2}(\mathscr{O}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle:=V_{1}\times V_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle:=H_{1}\times H_{2}.

Let
A_{1}=-\nu\mathbb{P}\Delta
be the Dirichlet–Stokes operator and
A_{2}=-\kappa\Delta
be the Dirichlet Laplacian operator. Let
\mathcal{A}=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}
with domain
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{V}\cap\left[H^{2}(\mathscr{O})\right]^{2}
acting on
\Phi=(u,\theta)
. The map
\mathcal{B}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{P}\big[(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)u_{2}\big]\\[2.15277pt]
(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)\theta_{2}\end{pmatrix}

for
\Phi_{i}=(u_{i},\theta_{i})\in\mathcal{V}
, where
i=1,2
. The map
\mathcal{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\Phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\theta e+\alpha u+\mathbb{P}\big[|u|^{r-1}u\big]\\[2.15277pt]
(u\cdot\nabla)\phi\end{pmatrix},

where
\Phi=(u,\theta)
. The stochastic term is
g(\Phi)\dot{W}=(g_{1}\dot{W}_{1},\,g_{2}\dot{W}_{2})^{\top}
.

By similar argument as in Section
3.1
, we infer that
\mathcal{B}
fulfils condition (B). Next, we verify condition (R) for the map
\mathcal{R}
. Firstly, (
2.6
) is satisfied with
\mathcal{R}(\Phi)=\mathcal{S}(\Phi)+\mathcal{F}(\Phi)
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\Phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha u+\mathbb{P}\big[|u|^{r-1}u\big]\\[2.15277pt]
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{F}(\Phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\theta e\\[2.15277pt]
(u\cdot\nabla)\phi\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\mathcal{S}
has the same form as (
3.4
), it remains to verify (
2.7
), (
2.8
), (
2.9
), and (
2.10
) for the map
\mathcal{F}
. By Hölder’s inequality and Sobolev embedding,

\displaystyle\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1}),\Phi_{2}\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left|{\theta_{1}}\right|\left|{u_{2}}\right|+C\left|{u_{1}}\right|_{L^{4}}\left|{\nabla\phi}\right|_{L^{4}}\left|{\theta_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|{u_{1}}\right\|+\left\|{\theta_{1}}\right\|\right)\left(\left|{u_{2}}\right|+\left|{\theta_{2}}\right|\right)\leq C\left\|{\Phi_{1}}\right\|\left|{\Phi_{2}}\right|,

which implies (
2.7
). Inequality (
2.8
) can be shown similarly. Next, we will prove (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) for the map
\mathcal{F}
. By Hölder’s inequality and Sobolev embedding,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{F}(\Phi)}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left|{\theta}\right|^{2}+C\left|{u}\right|_{L^{4}}^{2}\left|{\nabla\phi}\right|_{L^{4}}^{2}\leq C\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}.

By interpolation and Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequalities,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{F}(\Phi)}\right|_{\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{8}})}^{2}\leq\left|{\mathcal{F}(\Phi)}\right|_{\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{4}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left|{\mathcal{F}(\Phi)}\right|\left\|{\mathcal{F}(\Phi)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\Phi}\right\|\big(\left\|{\theta}\right\|+\left|{\nabla u}\right|_{L^{4}}\left|{\nabla\phi}\right|_{L^{4}}+\left|{u}\right|_{L^{\infty}}\left|{\phi}\right|_{H^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\Phi}\right\|\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi}\right|.

These inequalities imply (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), thus completing the verification of condition (R).

3.4.
Stochastic convective dynamo system

The dynamo theory
[
16
]
is a widely accepted mechanism for the creation and the maintenance of planetary magnetic field. It asserts that the cosmic magnetic field is generated and maintained by the turbulent motion of conducting fluid.

Let
\mathscr{O}=\mathbb{T}^{d-1}\times[0,h]
be a cylindrical domain, where
h>0
and
d=2,3
. Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We define the following fields:

(i)

u:[0,T]\times\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the fluid velocity, i.e.
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})
,

(ii)

B:[0,T]\times\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the magnetic flux density, i.e.
B=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{d})
,

(iii)

\theta:[0,T]\times\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}
is the temperature profile.

The dynamics of the fluid motion, the magnetic field, and the temperature profile are described by the stochastic convective dynamo system
[
10
,
11
,
47
]
:

(3.14a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\nu_{1}\Delta u+\sigma e_{d}\times u+(u\cdot\nabla)u-(B\cdot\nabla)B+\nabla\left(p+\frac{1}{2}\left|{B}\right|^{2}\right)+\theta e_{d}=g_{1}\dot{W}_{1}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.14b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}B-\nu_{2}\Delta B+(u\cdot\nabla)B-(B\cdot\nabla)u=g_{2}\dot{W}_{2}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.14c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta-\nu_{3}\Delta\theta+(u\cdot\nabla)\theta+e_{d}\cdot u=g_{3}\dot{W}_{3}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.14d)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,u=0,\;\mathrm{div}\,B=0\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.14e)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\;B(0,x)=B_{0}(x),\;\theta(0,x)=\theta_{0}(x)\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.14f)

\displaystyle u_{d}=B_{d}=0,\;\theta=0\;

on
\Gamma_{d}
.

The system (
3.14
) is equipped with a periodic boundary condition in the lateral parts. Here,
\Gamma_{d}:=\{x:x_{d}=0\text{ or }h\}
is the top and the bottom boundaries. The constant
\sigma
is the Coriolis force parameter, which is zero when
d=2
.

The functional setting for this system will be described next. Let
H^{1}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathscr{O})
denote the closure in
H^{1}(\mathscr{O})
of the space of compactly supported smooth functions that are periodic in the lateral direction. Define the spaces:

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=V_{2}:=\{v\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,v=0\},

\displaystyle V_{3}

\displaystyle:=H^{1}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathscr{O})

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=H_{2}:=\{v\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,v=0\;\text{ in $\mathscr{O}$ and }\left.v\cdot n\right|_{\Gamma_{d}}=0\},

\displaystyle H_{3}

\displaystyle:=L^{2}(\mathscr{O}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle:=V_{1}\times V_{2}\times V_{3},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle:=H_{1}\times H_{2}\times H_{3}.

For
i=1,2
, let
A_{i}=-\nu_{i}\mathbb{P}\Delta
be the (periodic-Dirichlet) Stokes operator, and let
A_{3}=-\nu_{3}\Delta
be the Laplacian operator with the same boundary conditions. Let
\mathcal{A}=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{3}
with domain
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{V}\cap\left[H^{2}(\mathscr{O})\right]^{3}
acting on
\Phi=(u,B,\theta)
. More generally, it is known that
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{s}{2}})=\big[H^{s}(\mathscr{O})\big]^{3}
for
s\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
and
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1}{2}})=\mathcal{V}
; see
[
27
]
for further properties of these function spaces and operators. The map
\mathcal{B}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{P}\big[(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)u_{2}-(B_{1}\cdot\nabla)B_{2}\big]\\[2.15277pt]
(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)B_{2}-(B_{1}\cdot\nabla)u_{2}\\[2.15277pt]
(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)\theta_{2}\end{pmatrix}

for
\Phi_{i}=(u_{i},B_{i},\theta_{i})\in\mathcal{V}
, where
i=1,2
. The map
\mathcal{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\Phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma\mathbb{P}\big[e_{d}\times u\big]+\theta e_{d}\\[2.15277pt]
0\\[2.15277pt]
e_{d}\cdot u\end{pmatrix},

where
\Phi=(u,B,\theta)
. The stochastic term is
g(\Phi)\dot{W}=(g_{1}\dot{W}_{1},\,g_{2}\dot{W}_{2},g_{3}\dot{W}_{3})^{\top}
. To show that condition (B) and condition (R) in the assumptions are satisfied, we can proceed in a similar manner as in Section
3.1
and Section
3.3
.

3.5.
Stochastic micropolar and magneto-micropolar fluid systems

The theory of micropolar fluids
[
17
]
describes the behaviour of fluids that exhibit rotational effects and micro-rotational inertia, which include blood and liquid crystal. Taking the electromagnetic effects into consideration, we obtain the stochastic magneto-micropolar fluid system
[
28
,
50
,
51
]
, which reads:

(3.15a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-(\mu+\chi)\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u-(B\cdot\nabla)B+\nabla\left(p+\frac{1}{2}\left|{B}\right|^{2}\right)-\chi\,\mathrm{curl}\,w=g_{1}\dot{W}_{1}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.15b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w-\gamma\Delta w-(\alpha+\beta)\nabla(\mathrm{div}\,w)+2\chi w+(u\cdot\nabla)w-\chi\,\mathrm{curl}\,u=g_{2}\dot{W}_{2}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.15c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}B-\nu\Delta B+(u\cdot\nabla)B-(B\cdot\nabla)u=g_{3}\dot{W}_{3}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.15d)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,u=\mathrm{div}\,B=0\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.15e)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\;w(0,x)=w_{0}(x),\;B(0,x)=B_{0}(x)\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.15f)

\displaystyle u=w=0,\;

on
\partial\mathscr{O}
,

(3.15g)

\displaystyle B\cdot n=0,\;\mathrm{curl}\;B\times n=0\;

on
\partial\mathscr{O}
.

Here,
u,w,B
are vector fields representing the fluid velocity, the micro-rotational vector of the fluid, and the magnetic flux density, respectively. For physical reasons, the coefficients
\mu,\chi,\gamma,\alpha,\beta,\nu
are assumed to satisfy
\min\{\mu,\chi,\gamma,\nu,\alpha+\beta+\gamma\}>0
. This system can also be further coupled with an equation of the form (
3.14c
) describing the temperature dynamics
[
29
,
48
]
.

The existence of strong solutions to the determinisitic magneto-micropolar fluid system is shown in
[
40
,
49
]
. In the stochastic case, the existence of martingale weak solutions to the system is proven in
[
50
,
51
]
. This paper establishes the existence and uniqueness of local pathwise strong solutions to the stochastic magneto-micropolar fluid system for
d\leq 3
. This pathwise strong solution is shown to be global when
d\leq 2
.

The functional setting for this problem will be described next. Define:

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,v=0\},

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle:=H^{1}_{0}(\mathscr{O}),

\displaystyle V_{3}

\displaystyle:=\{v\in H^{1}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,u=0\text{ in $\mathscr{O}$, }\left.u\cdot n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\,\text{ and }\left.\mathrm{curl}\,u\times n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\},

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{v\in L^{2}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,v=0\;\text{ and }\left.v\cdot n\right|_{\partial\mathscr{O}}=0\},

\displaystyle H_{2}

\displaystyle:=L^{2}(\mathscr{O})

\displaystyle H_{3}

\displaystyle:=H_{1}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle:=V_{1}\times V_{2}\times V_{3},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle:=H_{1}\times H_{2}\times H_{3}.

Let
\mathbb{P}:L^{2}(\mathscr{O})\to H_{1}
be the Leray–Helmholtz projection,
\Delta_{D}
be the Dirichlet Laplacian, and
\Delta_{H}
be the Hodge Laplacian. Define the operators:

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle:=-(\mu+\chi)\mathbb{P}\Delta_{D},\text{ where }\mathrm{D}(A_{1})=V_{1}\cap H^{2}(\mathscr{O}),

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle:=-\gamma\Delta_{D}-(\alpha+\beta)\nabla\mathrm{div}\,,\text{ where }\mathrm{D}(A_{2})=V_{2}\cap H^{2}(\mathscr{O}),

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle:=-\nu\Delta_{H},\text{ where }\mathrm{D}(A_{3})=V_{3}\cap H^{2}(\mathscr{O}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle:=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus A_{3},\text{ where }\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})=A_{1}\times A_{2}\times A_{3}.

The map
\mathcal{B}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{P}\big[(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)u_{2}-(B_{1}\cdot\nabla)B_{2}\big]\\[2.15277pt]
(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)w_{2}\\[2.15277pt]
(u_{1}\cdot\nabla)B_{2}-(B_{1}\cdot\nabla)u_{2}\end{pmatrix}

for
\Phi_{i}=(u_{i},w_{i},B_{i})\in\mathcal{V}
, where
i=1,2
. The map
\mathcal{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\Phi)=\begin{pmatrix}-\chi\,\mathrm{curl}\,w\\[2.15277pt]
2\chi w-\chi\,\mathrm{curl}\,u\\[2.15277pt]
0\end{pmatrix},

where
\Phi=(u,w,B)
. The stochastic term is
g(\Phi)\dot{W}=(g_{1}\dot{W}_{1},\,g_{2}\dot{W}_{2},g_{3}\dot{W}_{3})^{\top}
. As before, we can verify that our standing assumptions are valid for the operators defined above.

3.6.
Stochastic diffusive tropical climate model

A tropical climate model of the equatorial atmosphere is proposed by Frierson–Majda–Pauluis in
[
21
]
by performing a Galerkin truncation to the hydrostatic Boussinesq equation. Since then, several physically reasonable modifications of the model have been proposed, including adding diffusion or damping terms to some or all of the equations
[
33
,
53
]
. Here, we consider the stochastic diffusive tropical climate model:

(3.16a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\nu_{1}\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p+\mathrm{div}\,(v\otimes v)=g_{1}\dot{W}_{1}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.16b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\nu_{2}\Delta v+(u\cdot\nabla)v+\nabla\theta+(v\cdot\nabla)u=g_{2}\dot{W}_{2}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.16c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta-\nu_{3}\Delta\theta+(u\cdot\nabla)\theta+\mathrm{div}\,v=g_{3}\dot{W}_{3}\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.16d)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,u=0\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.16e)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\;v(0,x)=v_{0}(x),\;\theta(0,x)=\theta_{0}(x)\;

in
\mathscr{O}
,

(3.16f)

\displaystyle u=v=0,\;\theta=0,\;

on
\partial\mathscr{O}
,

where
\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3}>0
are diffusivity constants, and

(i)

u:[0,T]\times\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the barotropic mode,

(ii)

v:[0,T]\times\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the first baroclinic mode of the velocity vector,

(iii)

\theta:[0,T]\times\mathscr{O}\to\mathbb{R}
is the temperature profile.

In the deterministic case, the system (
3.16
) is widely studied. Global existence of strong solutions for
d=2
is established in
[
33
]
, even assuming
\nu_{3}=0
. Global existence of solutions for
d=2
with
-\nu_{1}\Delta u
replaced by the fractional Laplacian is shown in
[
53
]
; see also
[
13
,
14
]
. For
d=3
, the system with additional damping terms is studied in
[
55
]
. In the stochastic case, local existence and uniqueness of strong solutions for the problem when
d=2
is shown in
[
54
]
. In this paper, we establish the local existence and uniqueness of pathwise strong solutions for
d\leq 3
, which is global when
d=2
.

We describe the functional setting for this problem as follows. Define:

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathscr{O}):\mathrm{div}\,v=0\},

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle=V_{3}:=H^{1}_{0}(\mathscr{O}),

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle\Phi(t\wedge\tau)+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\big(\mathcal{A}\Phi+\mathcal{B}(\Phi)+\mathcal{R}\Phi\big)\,\mathrm{d}s=\Phi_{0}+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}g(\Phi)\,\mathrm{d}W\quad\text{in $\mathcal{V}^{\prime}$}.

(local strong pathwise solution)

Definition 4.3

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\Phi_{0}}\right\|^{2}-2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{n}),\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n}\right\rangle}\right|\mathrm{d}t\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{n}),\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n}\right\rangle}\right|\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|{P_{m}g_{k}(\Phi^{n})}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{r\in[0,S\wedge\tau]}\left|{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{r}2\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi^{n}),\Phi^{n}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}}\right|\right].

(4.22)

\displaystyle=:\left\|{\Phi_{0}}\right\|^{2}+J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}+J_{4}.

We need to estimate each term on the last line. For the term
J_{1}
, by (
2.3
) for
d\leq 3
and Young’s inequality, we have

(4.23)

\displaystyle\left|{J_{1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n}}\right|^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{6}\mathrm{d}t\right]+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right].

Next, for the term
J_{2}
, applying (
2.7
) and Young’s inequality yields

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left(1+\left|{\Phi^{n}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n}}\right|\mathrm{d}t\right]

(4.24)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{10}\mathrm{d}t\right]+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right].

For the term
J_{3}
, we use the Lipschitz assumptions on
g
to obtain

(4.25)

\displaystyle J_{3}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right].

Finally, for the term
J_{4}
, we apply the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality to have

\displaystyle J_{4}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|{g_{k}(\Phi^{n})}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.26)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,S\wedge\tau]}\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{2}\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]

Substituting (
4.23
), (
4
), (
4.25
), and (
4
) back into (
4
), then rearranging the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,S\wedge\tau]}\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\Phi_{0}}\right\|^{2}+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{10}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right],

where
C
is independent of
\tau,S
, and
n
. Therefore, by applying Chebyshev’s inequality, for the set
A_{n}(\tau,S)
defined in (
4.6
) we have for any
T>0
and
M>1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[A_{n}(\tau,S)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[C\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{10}\right)\mathrm{d}t>(M-1)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(M-1)^{-2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{S\wedge\tau}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi^{n}}\right\|^{10}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq CS,

where

. Taking supremum over

, then over
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and taking the limit as

, we deduce (
4.8
). This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

We now prove the existence of a local strong pathwise solution to (
1.1
).

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\Phi_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
be a given initial data. Then there exists a local strong solution
(\Phi,\tau)
to the problem (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition (
4.2
).

Proof.

We proceed with a compactness argument by applying Theorem
A.1
with
X=\mathcal{V}
and
Y=\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A})
, noting the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{M,T}
in (
4.5
). Since the hypotheses of this theorem have been shown to hold in Proposition
4.5
, we infer the existence of a subsequence of the Galerkin solutions
\{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
to (
4.4
), a strictly positive stopping time
\tau\leq T
, and a process
\Phi(\cdot)=\Phi(\,\cdot\wedge\tau)
which is continuous in
\mathcal{V}
, such that

(4.27)

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(t)-\Phi(t)}\right\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\tau}\left|{\mathcal{A}\big(\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(t)-\Phi(t)\big)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)=0\;\text{ a.s.}

Furthermore, (
A.4
) implies for any
p\in[1,\infty)
,

(4.28)

\displaystyle\Phi(\,\cdot\wedge\tau)\in L^{p}\big(\Omega;C([0,T];\mathcal{V})\big)\;\text{ and }\;\Phi\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\in L^{p}\big(\Omega;L^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}))\big),

while (
A.5
) confers the existence of a collection of measurable sets
\{\Omega_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
with

(4.29)

\displaystyle\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(t)}\right\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\tau}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(t)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]<\infty.

Thus, given (
4.27
) and (
4.29
), by Lemma
A.2
we have

(4.30)

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\Phi^{n_{\ell}}\rightharpoonup\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\Phi\quad\text{in }L^{p}\big(\Omega;L^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{A}))\big),

(4.31)

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(\,\cdot\wedge\tau)\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\Phi\quad\text{in }L^{p}\big(\Omega;L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{V})\big).

Next, we consider the convergence of the first nonlinear term
P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})
. For any
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|{\left\langle P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})-\mathcal{B}(\Phi),v\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}}-\Phi,\Phi^{n_{\ell}}),P_{n_{\ell}}v\right\rangle}\right|+\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Phi,\Phi^{n_{\ell}}-\Phi),P_{n_{\ell}}v\right\rangle}\right|+\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Phi),Q_{n_{\ell}}v\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}-\Phi}\right\|\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right\|\left\|{v}\right\|+C\left\|{\Phi}\right\|\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}-\Phi}\right\|\left\|{v}\right\|+C\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\left|{Q_{n_{\ell}}v}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|{Q_{n_{\ell}}v}\right\|^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}-\Phi}\right\|\left\|{v}\right\|\left(\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right\|+\left\|{\Phi}\right\|\right)+C\lambda_{n_{\ell}}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\left\|{v}\right\|,

where in the penultimate step we used (
2.2
), while in the last step we applied (
A.7
). This estimate, together with (
4.27
), implies that given any
v\in\mathcal{V}
, for a.e.
(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]
,

(4.32)

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\left\langle P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}}),v\right\rangle\to\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Phi),v\right\rangle\;\text{ as $\ell\to\infty$}.

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left|{P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right\|^{3}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right|\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,\tau]}\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(s)}\right\|^{2}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)\right]

(4.33)

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,\tau]}\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(s)}\right\|^{4}+\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}\right)\right]<\infty,

where we applied Young’s inequality and (
4.29
) in the last line. Altogether, (
4.32
), (
4
), and Lemma
A.2
imply that

(4.34)

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})\rightharpoonup\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\mathcal{B}(\Phi)\quad\text{in }L^{2}\big(\Omega;L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})\big).

Now, we study the convergence of the second nonlinear term
P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})
. For any
v\in\mathcal{V}
, by the triangle and the Hölder inequalities we have

\displaystyle\left|{\left\langle P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})-\mathcal{R}(\Phi),v\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})-\mathcal{R}(\Phi),P_{n_{\ell}}v\right\rangle}\right|+\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\Phi),Q_{n_{\ell}}v\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\right)\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}-\Phi}\right\|\left|{v}\right|+C\left(1+\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\right)\left\|{\Phi}\right\|\left|{Q_{n_{\ell}}v}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\right)\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}-\Phi}\right\|\left\|{v}\right\|+C\lambda_{n_{\ell}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\right)\left\|{\Phi}\right\|\left\|{v}\right\|.

Here, in the penultimate step we utilised (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), while in the last step we applied (
A.7
). Similarly to (
4.32
), the above estimate, together with (
4.27
), implies that for a.e.
(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]
,

(4.35)

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\left\langle P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}}),v\right\rangle\to\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\Phi),v\right\rangle\;\text{ as $\ell\to\infty$}.

Moreover, noting (
4.29
), we have the estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left|{P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right\|^{4}\right)\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

(4.36)

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,\tau]}\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(s)}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(s)}\right\|^{6}\right)\right]<\infty.

Thus, (
4.35
), (
4
), and Lemma
A.2
imply that

(4.37)

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})\rightharpoonup\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\mathcal{R}(\Phi)\quad\text{in }L^{2}\big(\Omega;L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H})\big).

Finally, we consider the stochastic term. Noting the assumptions on
g
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{P_{n_{\ell}}g_{k}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})-g_{k}(\Phi)}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{P_{n_{\ell}}g_{k}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})-P_{n_{\ell}}g_{k}(\Phi)}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{Q_{n_{\ell}}g_{k}(\Phi)}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}-\Phi}\right\|+\lambda_{n_{\ell}}^{-1}(1+\left\|{\Phi}\right\|)\right).

This implies for a.e.
(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]
, we have
\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}P_{n_{\ell}}g(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})\to\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}g(\Phi)
in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. Furthermore, again by the assumptions on

and (
4.29
),

\displaystyle\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{P_{n_{\ell}}g_{k}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq C+C\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left\|{\Phi^{n_{\ell}}}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]<\infty,

from which we infer that

(4.38)

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}P_{n_{\ell}}g(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})\rightharpoonup\mathds{1}_{t\leq\tau}g(\Phi)\quad\text{in }L^{2}\big(\Omega;L^{2}(0,T;\ell^{2}(\mathcal{H}))\big).

Thus, from (
4.30
), (
4.34
), (
4.37
), and (
4.38
), we deduce for any fixed
v\in\mathcal{H}
the following weak convergences in
L^{2}\big(\Omega;L^{2}(0,T)\big)
:

(4.39)

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n_{\ell}},v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle\mathcal{A}\Phi,v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}}),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle B(\Phi),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle P_{n_{\ell}}g_{k}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}}),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}.

Finally, we apply the following standard argument. Let
K\subset\Omega\times[0,T]
be a measurable set. Then by (
4.31
) and (
4.39
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{K}\left\langle\Phi(t),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\Phi^{n_{\ell}}(t\wedge\tau),\mathds{1}_{K}v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{K}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\left\langle P_{n_{\ell}}\Phi_{0},v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{K}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\left(\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle\mathcal{A}\Phi^{n_{\ell}}+P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}})+P_{n_{\ell}}\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}}),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{K}\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{\ell}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle P_{n_{\ell}}g_{k}(\Phi^{n_{\ell}}),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{K}\left(\left\langle\Phi_{0},v\right\rangle-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle\mathcal{A}\Phi+\mathcal{B}(\Phi)+\mathcal{R}(\Phi),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{1}_{K}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}\right)\mathrm{d}t\right].

Since
v
and
K
are arbitrary, we infer that
\Phi
satisfies (
4.2
) as an equation in
\mathcal{H}
. The required regularity for a strong solution is conferred by (
4.28
). This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

The following theorem establishes the pathwise uniqueness of local strong solution obtained previously.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\tau>0
be a stopping time such that
(\Phi^{(1)},\tau)
and
(\Phi^{(2)},\tau)
are local strong solutions to (
1.1
) with the same initial data
\Phi_{0}
. Then

(4.40)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\Phi^{(1)}(t\wedge\tau)=\Phi^{(2)}(t\wedge\tau),\;\forall t\in[0,\infty)\right]=1.

Proof.

Let
\Psi:=\Phi^{(1)}-\Phi^{(2)}
. Then
\Psi
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Psi+\left(\mathcal{A}\Psi+\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{(1)})-\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{(2)})+\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{(1)})-\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{(2)})\right)\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(g_{k}(\Phi^{(1)})-g_{k}(\Phi^{(2)})\right)\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}.

Applying Itô’s lemma to
\left|{\Psi}\right|^{2}
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\left|{\Psi}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\left\|{\Psi}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t-2\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{(1)})-\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{(2)}),\Psi\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t-2\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{(1)})-\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{(2)}),\Psi\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{g_{k}(\Phi^{(1)})-g_{k}(\Phi^{(2)})}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi^{(1)})-g_{k}(\Phi^{(2)}),\Psi\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}.

For any stopping time
\sigma\leq\tau
, we integrate the above expression over
[0,\sigma]
and apply the expected value to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\Psi(\sigma)}\right|^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma}\left\|{\Psi(t)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle=-2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma}\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{(1)})-\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{(2)}),\Psi\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma}\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{(1)})-\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{(2)}),\Psi\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\right]

(4.41)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma}\left|{g_{k}(\Phi^{(1)})-g_{k}(\Phi^{(2)})}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right],

where we also used the martingale property of stochastic integral. Note that we have the following estimates. Firstly, by (
2.1
) and (
2.2
),

\displaystyle\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{(1)})-\mathcal{B}(\Phi^{(2)}),\Psi\right\rangle}\right|=\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Psi,\Phi^{(1)}),\Psi\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\Psi}\right\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\left\|{\Phi^{(1)}}\right\|\left|{\Psi}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}

(4.42)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\Psi}\right\|^{2}+C\left\|{\Phi^{(1)}}\right\|^{4}\left|{\Psi}\right|^{2},

where in the last step we used Young’s inequality. Furthermore, by (
2.8
), we have

\displaystyle\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{(1)})-\mathcal{R}(\Phi^{(2)}),\Psi\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle=C\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{(1)}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi^{(2)}}\right\|^{2}\right)\left\|{\Psi}\right\|\left|{\Psi}\right|

(4.43)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\Psi}\right\|^{2}+C\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{(1)}}\right\|^{4}+\left\|{\Phi^{(2)}}\right\|^{4}\right)\left|{\Psi}\right|^{2}.

Now, for any
R>0
, define the stopping time

\displaystyle\sigma_{R}:=\inf\left\{t>0:\left\|{\Phi^{(1)}(t)}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi^{(2)}(t)}\right\|^{2}>R\right\}\wedge\tau.

Noting this stopping time and Lipschitz assumptions on
g
, we employ estimates (
4
) and (
4
) in (
4
) and rearrange the terms to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\Psi(\sigma_{R}\wedge t)}\right|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma_{R}\wedge t}\left\|{\Psi(s)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma_{R}\wedge t}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi^{(1)}}\right\|^{4}+\left\|{\Phi^{(2)}}\right\|^{4}\right)\left|{\Psi(s)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(1+R^{2})\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma_{R}\wedge t}\left|{\Psi(s)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right].

By the Gronwall lemma, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\Psi(\sigma_{R}\wedge t)}\right|^{2}\right]=0,

which implies
\Phi^{(1)}(\sigma_{R}\wedge t)=\Phi^{(2)}(\sigma_{R}\wedge t)
a.s. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\Phi^{(1)}(t\wedge\tau)\neq\Phi^{(2)}(t\wedge\tau)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\{\sigma_{R}<\tau\}\cap\{\Phi^{(1)}(t\wedge\tau)\neq\Phi^{(2)}(t\wedge\tau)\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\sigma_{R}<\tau\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,\tau]}\left(\left\|{\Phi^{(1)}(s)}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi^{(2)}(s)}\right\|^{2}\right)>R\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{R}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,\tau]}\left(\left\|{\Phi^{(1)}(s)}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\Phi^{(2)}(s)}\right\|^{2}\right)\right],

which tends to
0
as
R\to\infty
. Therefore for any
t\geq 0
, we have
\Phi^{(1)}(t\wedge\tau)=\Phi^{(2)}(t\wedge\tau)
on a set of full measure which may depend on
t
. By taking the intersection of such sets corresponding to positive rational times, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\Phi^{(1)}(t\wedge\tau)=\Phi^{(2)}(t\wedge\tau),\;\forall t\in[0,\infty)\cap\mathbb{Q}\right]=1.

By the continuity in time property (
4.3
) of the solution, we deduce (
4.40
), completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.
Existence of a maximal strong solution

In this section, we establish the existence of a maximal strong solution
(\Phi,\xi)
to (
1.1
). Furthermore, we show that this maximal strong solution is global if
d=2
. We begin with the following estimate on a local weak solution of (
1.1
) which will be used on several occasions.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
(\Phi,\tau)
is a local weak solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
4.1
corresponding to an initial data
\Phi_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
. Then for any
T>0
and
p\geq 4
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]}\left|{\Phi(t)}\right|^{p}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge T}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\leq C_{p}\left(1+\left|{\Phi_{0}}\right|^{p}\right),

where
C_{p}
is a constant which depends on
p
and
T
, but is independent of
\tau
.

Proof.

For any stopping times
\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}
such that
0\leq\sigma_{a}\leq t\leq\sigma_{b}\leq\tau\wedge T
, by applying Itô’s lemma to
\left|{\Phi}\right|^{p}
, where
p\geq 4
, we have

\displaystyle\left|{\Phi(t)}\right|^{p}+p\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\mathrm{d}s+p\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\left|{\Phi(\sigma_{a})}\right|^{p}+\frac{p}{2}\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left|{g_{k}(\Phi(s))}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{p(p-2)}{2}\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-4}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+p\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}.

Noting assumption (
2.6
) on
\mathcal{R}
, we then have

\displaystyle\left|{\Phi(t)}\right|^{p}+p\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\Phi(\sigma_{a})}\right|^{p}+p\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{F}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle}\right|\mathrm{d}s+\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left|{g_{k}(\Phi(s))}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+p\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}.

Using the triangle inequality, taking supremum over
t\in[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]
, and applying the expected value, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]}\left|{\Phi(t)}\right|^{p}\right]+p\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\Phi(\sigma_{a})}\right|^{p}\right]+C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{F}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle}\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\quad+C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left|{g_{k}(\Phi(s))}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\quad+C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]}\left|{\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}}\right|\right]

(5.1)

\displaystyle=:C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\Phi(\sigma_{a})}\right|^{p}\right]+I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

We will estimate each term on the last line. Firstly, note that by (
2.6
), we have

\displaystyle\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{F}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|\right)\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-1}+C\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{\frac{p}{2}}

(5.2)

\displaystyle\leq C+C\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p}+\frac{p}{4}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2},

where in the last step we applied Young’s inequality. Next, by the assumptions on
g
and Young’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left|{g_{k}(\Phi(s))}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left(1+\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}s

(5.3)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}s\right).

Similarly, by the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality, we also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]}\left|{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{t}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|{\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi(s)),\Phi(s)\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{2(p-2)}\mathrm{d}s\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\left(1+\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{2}\right)\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{2(p-1)}\mathrm{d}s\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

(5.4)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]}\left|{\Phi(t)}\right|^{p}\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[1+\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}s\right],

where in the last step we again used Young’s inequality. Altogether, applying (
5
), (
5
), and (
5
) in (
5
), and rearranging the terms, we infer

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[\sigma_{a},\sigma_{b}]}\left|{\Phi(t)}\right|^{p}\right]+p\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p-2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[1+\left|{\Phi(\sigma_{a})}\right|^{p}\right]+C_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\sigma_{a}}^{\sigma_{b}}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}s\right].

The required inequality then follows from Lemma
A.4
.
∎

5.1.
Maximal strong solution for
d\leq 3

The existence of a maximal strong pathwise solution in the sense of Definition
4.3
will be shown in this section. The argument follows closely the idea in
[
8
, Section 5.3.4]

Theorem 5.2
.

There exists a unique maximal strong pathwise solution
(\Phi,\xi)
to (
1.1
) and a sequence of stopping times
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
announcing
\xi
. Moreover,
(\Phi,\xi)
is a local weak pathwise solution.

Proof.

With the aim of extending the solution to a maximal time of existence, we define the set

\mathcal{L}:=\{\tau\text{ is a stopping time}:\exists\Phi\text{ such that }(\Phi,\tau)\text{ is a local strong pathwise solution}\}.

The set
\mathcal{L}
is non-empty by Theorem
4.6
. More precisely, every stopping time
\tau_{n}
defined in Remark
4.4
belongs to
\mathcal{L}
and
\xi=\sup_{n}\tau_{n}
is a stopping time.
For such
\tau_{n}
and each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Phi_{n}
denote the process for which
(\Phi_{n},\tau_{n})
is a local pathwise strong solution. For
\tau_{k}<\tau_{l}
define

\Omega_{k,\ell}:=\{\omega\in\Omega:\Phi_{k}(t\wedge\tau_{k})=\Phi_{\ell}(t\wedge\tau_{k}),\;\forall t\in[0,\infty)\}

By uniqueness (Theorem
4.7
), we have
\mathbb{P}\big[\widetilde{\Omega}\big]=1
, where
\widetilde{\Omega}:=\bigcap_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{k,\ell}
. For a fixed
\omega\in\widetilde{\Omega}
and every
t>0
, the sequence
\{\Phi_{k}(t\wedge\tau_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy in
\mathcal{V}
. Let

\widetilde{\Phi}(t):=\lim_{k\to\infty}\Phi_{k}(t\wedge\tau_{k})\,\text{ a.s.}

By Lemma
5.1
and the monotone convergence theorem, we have for any
T>0
,

(5.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\xi\wedge T]}\left|{\widetilde{\Phi}(t)}\right|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\xi\wedge T}\left\|{\widetilde{\Phi}(s)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]<\infty.

Therefore, noting (
5.5
), we can define
\Phi(t)
for any
t>0
by

(5.6)

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi(t),v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Phi(0),v\right\rangle-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\xi}\left\langle\mathcal{A}\widetilde{\Phi}+\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{\Phi})-\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{\Phi}),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\xi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\langle g_{k}(\widetilde{\Phi}),v\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}

for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Note that
\Phi(t,\omega)=\widetilde{\Phi}(t,\omega)
for all
t<\xi
and
\Phi(\cdot)
is weakly continuous a.s. in
\mathcal{H}
. As such, (
5.6
) implies that
(\Phi,\xi)
is a local weak pathwise solution in the sense of Definition
4.1
.

In fact, by construction of
\tau_{n}
in Remark
4.4
,
(\Phi,\tau_{n})
is a local strong solution for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Next, we observe that, by repeating the construction of a local strong pathwise solution, a solution on
[0,\tau_{n}]
can be extended to a solution on
[0,\tau_{n}+\hat{\tau}]
for an a.s. strictly positive stopping time
\hat{\tau}
. Indeed, we can construct a new solution starting from
\Phi(\tau_{n})
as the initial data as follows. Define a stochastic basis
(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\mathcal{F}},\{\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\hat{\mathbb{P}}):=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}+t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
together with a sequence of independent Brownian motions
\{\hat{\beta}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\hat{\beta}_{k}(t):=\beta_{k}(\tau_{n}+t)-\beta_{k}(\tau_{n})
. Then by the local existence theory, there exists a local strong solution
(\hat{\Phi},\hat{\tau})
relative to
\{\hat{\beta}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
on the stochastic basis
(\hat{\Omega},\hat{\mathcal{F}},\{\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\hat{\mathbb{P}})
. Since solutions are unique, setting

\Phi_{\mathrm{e}}(t):=\begin{cases}\Phi(t),&\text{if $t\leq\tau_{n}$,}\\
\hat{\Phi}(t),&\text{if $\tau_{n}<t\leq\tau_{n}+\hat{\tau}$.}\end{cases}

yields a local strong pathwise solution
(\Phi_{\mathrm{e}},\sigma)
, defined up to the stopping time
\sigma:=\tau_{n}+\hat{\tau}
, relative to the original stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
.

Finally, it remains to show that
\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{n}\wedge T=\xi\right]=0
. Suppose otherwise, that for some sufficiently large
n
and
T
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{n}\wedge T=\xi\right]>0
. The argument in the previous paragraph then confers the existence of a stopping time
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\sigma>\tau_{n}\wedge T
a.s., contradicting the maximality of
\xi
by the definition of
\xi=\sup\mathcal{L}
. We also note that
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
announces
\xi
. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.2.
Global strong solution for
d=2

The existence of a global strong pathwise solution to (
1.1
) for
d=2
will be established in this section.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
d=2
. The maximal strong pathwise solution
(\Phi,\xi)
of (
1.1
) corresponding to an initial data
\Phi_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
is global in the sense of Definition
4.3
. In particular, there exists an increasing sequence of strictly positive stopping times
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\tau_{n}\uparrow\xi
, with
\mathbb{P}\left[\xi=\infty\right]=1
.

Proof.

Let
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a strictly increasing sequence of stopping times announcing
\xi
. Note that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\{\xi<\infty\}=\bigcup_{T=1}^{\infty}\{\xi\leq T\}=\bigcup_{T=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\{\tau_{n}\leq T\}.

Since
\tau_{n}
is increasing, we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\{\tau_{n}\leq T\}\right]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{n}\leq T\right].

Therefore, to show that
\mathbb{P}\left[\xi<\infty\right]=0
, by (
5.7
) and (
5.8
), it suffices to show that for any
T\geq 1
,

(5.9)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{n}\leq T\right]=0.

To this end, for any
M>0
, define the stopping time

(5.10)

\displaystyle\sigma_{M}:=\inf_{t\geq 0}\left\{\int_{0}^{t\wedge\xi}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{6}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s>M\right\}\wedge 2T.

Note that

(5.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{n}\leq T\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\sigma_{M}\leq T\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\left\{\tau_{n}\leq T\right\}\cap\left\{\sigma_{M}>T\right\}\right]=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

The term
I_{1}
can be bounded by the Chebyshev inequality as follows:

(5.12)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T\wedge\xi}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{6}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\geq M\right]\leq\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T\wedge\xi}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{6}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right].

Note that the last term tends to zero as
M\to\infty
by Lemma
5.1
. Next, by definition, for the term
I_{2}
in (
5.11
), we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{n}\wedge T]}\left\|{\Phi(t)}\right\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi(s)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\geq n\right\}\cap\left\{\sigma_{M}>T\right\}\right]

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{n}\wedge\sigma_{M}]}\left\|{\Phi(t)}\right\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}\wedge\sigma_{M}}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi(s)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\geq n\right].

We need to estimate this last probability. Let
T,M
, and
n
be fixed. Let
\tau_{a},\tau_{b}
be a pair of stopping times such that
0\leq\tau_{a}\leq\tau_{b}\leq\tau_{n}\wedge\sigma_{M}
. Applying Itô’s lemma to
\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}
and following the same argument as in (
4
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[\tau_{a},\tau_{b}]}\left\|{\Phi(t)}\right\|^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi(s)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\Phi(\tau_{a})}\right\|^{2}\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{B}(\Phi),\mathcal{A}\Phi\right\rangle}\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left|{\left\langle\mathcal{R}(\Phi),\mathcal{A}\Phi\right\rangle}\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|{g_{k}(\Phi)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[\tau_{a},\tau_{b}]}\left|{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\tau_{a}}^{t}2\left\langle g_{k}(\Phi),\Phi\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\beta_{k}}\right|\right].

(5.14)

\displaystyle=:\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\Phi(\tau_{a})}\right\|^{2}\right]+J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}+J_{4}.

For the term
J_{1}
, we use (
2.3
) for
d=2
and Young’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left|{\Phi}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|{\Phi}\right\|\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi}\right|^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathrm{d}s\right]\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left|{\Phi}\right|^{2}\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{4}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right].

Next, we apply (
2.7
) for
d=2
and Young’s inequality to estimate
J_{2}
as

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left(1+\left|{\Phi}\right|^{\frac{3}{2}}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left\|{\Phi}\right\|\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi}\right|\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}+\left|{\Phi}\right|^{6}\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{4}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right].

By the assumption on
g
, we also have

\displaystyle J_{3}\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}s\right].

For the last term, by the Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality, we have

\displaystyle J_{4}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[\tau_{a},\tau_{b}]}\left\|{\Phi(t)}\right\|^{2}\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}s\right].

Substituting these estimates into (
5.2
) and rearranging the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[\tau_{a},\tau_{b}]}\left\|{\Phi(t)}\right\|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi(s)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]

(5.15)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\Phi(\tau_{a})}\right\|^{2}\right]+C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}\left(1+\left|{\Phi}\right|^{6}\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\right)\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right].

Note that by the definition of
\sigma_{M}
in (
5.10
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sigma_{M}}\left|{\Phi}\right|^{6}\left\|{\Phi}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq M,\;\text{ a.s.}

We apply the Gronwall lemma for stochastic processes (Lemma
A.4
) to (
5.2

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{n}\wedge\sigma_{M}]}\left\|{\Phi(t)}\right\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}\wedge\sigma_{M}}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi(s)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\leq C_{T,M}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi_{0}}\right\|^{2}\right),

where
C_{T,M}
depends on
T
and
M
, but is independent of
N
. Now, continuing from (
5.2
), by the Chebyshev inequality we have

(5.16)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{n}\wedge\sigma_{M}]}\left\|{\Phi(t)}\right\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}\wedge\sigma_{M}}\left|{\mathcal{A}\Phi(s)}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]\leq\frac{C_{T,M}}{n}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi_{0}}\right\|^{2}\right).

Altogether, from (
5.11
), (
5.12
), and (
5.16
), we deduce that for any
T\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{n}\leq T\right]\leq\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T\wedge\xi}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{6}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right]+\frac{C_{T,M}}{n}\left(1+\left\|{\Phi_{0}}\right\|^{2}\right).

First, we let
n\to\infty
to obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{n}\leq T\right]\leq\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T\wedge\xi}\left|{\Phi(s)}\right|^{6}\left\|{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right].

Since this is true for any

, we conclude that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{n}\leq T\right]=0
, thus

, as required in (
5.9
).
∎

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[\tau_{a},\tau_{b}]}u(s)+\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}v(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\leq C_{0}\mathbb{E}\left[u(\tau_{a})+\int_{\tau_{a}}^{\tau_{b}}(r(t)u(t)+w(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\right].
