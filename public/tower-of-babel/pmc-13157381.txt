Nano TiO2 and direct black marking system for precise localization in intraoperative pathological diagnosis of small tumors
Authors: Zhenkun Zhu, Huan Shi, Zijian Han, Xingyue Wang, Tao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcordance between intraoperative frozen section (FS) diagnosis and permanent section diagnosis is essential for quality assurance in pathology. However, in cases involving small lesions (≤ 1 cm), tissue dehydration following FS processing often reduces visibility and hinders localization, leading to diagnostic discrepancies between FS and permanent sections. This study introduces nano-titanium dioxide (nano-TiO2) and Direct Black (DB) for intraoperative tissue marking to enhance tumor identification. Surgical specimens from lung, thyroid, and breast cancers (January 2024–March 2025) with tumors ≤ 1 cm were included. Following FS diagnosis, tissues were marked using methylene blue, carbon nanoparticle suspension, nano-TiO2, and DB. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunohistochemistry, and reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction were applied to evaluate visibility, stability, and diagnostic impact. Nano-TiO2 and DB exhibited excellent visibility and tissue adherence across all three tumor types without interfering with H&E, immunomarkers (PD-L1, TTF-1, and HER2), or molecular pathology assays (EGFR and BRAF). Compared with unmarked specimens, marked tissues demonstrated higher concordance between FS and permanent diagnosis: Lung cancer (95% versus 84.38%), thyroid cancer (100% versus 81.25%), and breast cancer (100% versus 85.71%) (all p < 0.05). These findings indicate that nano-TiO2 and DB are safe, effective, and significantly improve intraoperative localization and diagnostic accuracy of small lesions. Introduction

Intraoperative pathological consultation provides surgeons with critical information and directly influences clinical treatment planning. Intraoperative frozen section (FS) diagnosis is widely applied in lung [1, 2], breast [3], and thyroid cancers [4]. However, tumors < 1.1 cm often present diagnostic challenges with FS, requiring confirmation and subtyping through subsequent examination of permanent Sect. [5]. Notably, discrepancies between FS and permanent sections may result from omission of deeper tumor tissue, tissue shrinkage post-dehydration, and embedding errors that hinder lesion sampling in the permanent Section [6, 7]. These limitations contribute to inconsistencies between FS and the final diagnosis. According to the 2015 World Health Organization classification of lung adenocarcinoma, FS and permanent section concordance is only about 80% [8, 9], highlighting the need for improved diagnostic reliability.

FS diagnosis is susceptible to discrepancies when compared with permanent paraffin sections due to sampling bias, tissue shrinkage during dehydration, orientation changes during embedding, and potential loss of small tumor foci during block trimming. These factors are particularly critical in tumors ≤ 1 cm, where minimal structural displacement may result in diagnostic discordance. In the present study, diagnostic discrepancy was evaluated across two complementary dimensions: (1) localization stability during permanent section processing, defined by the need for additional trimming or deeper sectioning to relocate the target focus, and (2) overall diagnostic concordance between FS and permanent sections. This framework was designed to systematically assess factors contributing to intraoperative–definitive diagnostic inconsistency.

Accurate intraoperative localization is especially critical for patients with pulmonary nodules < 1 cm [10], which are frequently invisible to the naked eye. Current preoperative marking techniques, such as methylene blue (MB) injection guided by electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy for nonpalpable nodules [11], or carbon nanoparticle suspension (CNS) for sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy in breast cancer [12], assist surgeons but offer no diagnostic benefit for pathology and may even obscure interpretation. Consequently, novel tissue-marking strategies are urgently required to enhance diagnostic concordance between FS and permanent sections.

Tissue marking play an important role in pathology by enhancing specimen visibility and stability. In earlier studies, Direct Red 227, Direct Blue 80, and Direct Violet 66 improved the visibility of small biopsies without interfering with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or immunohistochemical (IHC) staining [13]. Building on these findings, we screened additional agents and identified nano-titanium dioxide (nano-TiO2) and Direct Black (DB) as promising options for labeling small tumors. Both are relatively safe, inexpensive, and readily available with distinct coloration. DB is a direct dye widely used in the textile industry, while nano-TiO2 is a common nanomaterial applied as a whitening agent in food such as cheese, milk, ice cream, and pastries [14]. It is valued for its exceptional stability and corrosion resistance. Leveraging these properties, we applied nano-TiO2 and DB to mark tumor tissues after FS confirmation. The marked areas remained clearly visible both before and after fixation and dehydration, allowing technicians to localize tumors accurately during the embedding and sectioning for permanent section. This is the first study to evaluate nano-TiO2 and DB for intraoperative tissue marking in permanent section diagnosis. Their effects on H&E staining, IHC markers, and molecular genetic tests were examined in lung, thyroid, and breast cancer tissues, demonstrating feasibility and potential clinical utility in pathological diagnosis.

Methods

Sample source

Tissue samples were obtained from the Department of Pathology, Xiangyang Central Hospital, between January 2023 and December 2024. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Xiangyang Central Hospital, Hubei Province (Approval No.: 2024 − 161). The cohort included 52 partial lung cancer resections, 31 thyroid cancer surgeries, and 28 breast cancer resections. Only tumors ≤ 1 cm in diameter were enrolled. All underwent FS diagnosis, tissue marking, and subsequent permanent section evaluation. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Tumor diameter ≤ 1 cm; (2) tissue fixed for 12–48 h; (3) sectioning into 1–1.5 cm tissue blocks.

Tissue marking

After frozen section diagnosis, the pathologist microscopically identified the tumor region and correlated its location with the corresponding area on the frozen specimen holder. Based on this correlation, nano-TiO2 or DB was applied circumferentially around the tumor region on the specimen surface to delineate a predefined localization range. In our study, frozen and permanent sections were derived from the same tissue block. This application is based on the correlation between the microscopic tumor location and the gross morphology of the excised specimen, nano-TiO2 (CI77891) (UsoIf, China), DB (CI19), MB (Jumpcan Pharmaceutical Company, Jiangsu, China), and CNS (Lummy Pharmaceutical Company, Chongqing, China) were used to mark tumor regions. Nano-TiO2 and DB were proportionally blended with the acrylic solution. Each tumor type was divided into two groups: One processed without marking, and the other labeled with one of the four agents after FS diagnosis. Following application, samples were dried for 5 min to secure surface adherence and prevent deep diffusion. Tissue morphology was photographed, and specimens were then embedded in paraffin using standard plastic cassettes. The cassette base carried a 1 mm square grid as a reference scale for tissue size. As shown in Scheme 1, prior to routine microtomy (block trimming/facing), the marked region was grossly identifiable on the surface of the paraffin block, allowing confirmation of the predefined target tumor sampling location without requiring additional block trimming, deeper sectioning, or re-embedding.

H&E staining

Marked tumor tissue was placed into embedding cassettes and fixed in 10% neutral formalin for 10–12 h. The fixed tissue cassettes were then processed for 12 h in a fully enclosed automatic tissue processor (DAKE WE, China). After completion of the program, the tissue was embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks were sectioned with a microtome (Leica, Germany), and the sections were examined by the same technician to identify the marked tumor regions. Tumor tissue sections were mounted onto slides; unlabeled tissues were collected empirically. The thickness of all sections was 3–5 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Baso, China) was performed, and the slides were examined under a microscope to evaluate morphological changes and to locate the marked tissue.

IHC staining

To assess whether tissue marking interfered with IHC markers’ expression, representative markers were analyzed in lung, thyroid, and breast cancers (Supplementary Table 1). For lung cancer, the markers examined included Ki-67, p53, TTF-1, Napsin A, and PD-L1. In thyroid cancer, the markers Galectin-3, TPO, CD56, BRAF, and V600E were evaluated. For breast cancer, the markers assessed were ER, PR, and HER2.

Immunohistochemical staining was evaluated using a semiquantitative intensity × proportion scoring method for Ki-67, p53, TTF-1, ER, PR, Napsin A, Galectin-3, TPO, CD56, and BRAF V600E. Staining intensity in tumor cells was graded on a 4-point scale as follows: 0 (no signal), 1 (weak signal), 2 (medium signal), and 3 (strong signal). The proportion of positive tumor cells was categorized into 4 levels: 1 (0–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (76–100%). The final immunoreactivity score was calculated as the product of staining intensity and positive proportion (range, 0–12). HER2 expression was assessed based on membranous staining and interpreted according to the standard 0/1+/2+/3 + diagnostic criteria. PD-L1 expression was assessed using tumor proportion score (TPS), defined as the percentage of viable tumor cells showing membranous staining. TPS were categorized according to clinical relevance (< 1%, 1–49%, and ≥ 50%). All slides were independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists, and discrepant interpretations were resolved by consensus review. One representative paired case per tumor type was selected for illustrative presentation in Fig. 6.

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Molecular analysis was performed using the ABI 7500 quantitative PCR system. In 10 lung cancer cases, expression of 11 genes (EGFR, HER2, KRAS, BRAF, ALK, ROS1, RET, NTRK1, NTRK2, NTRK3, and MET exon 14 skipping) was assessed. In 10 thyroid cancer cases, expression of 9 genes (BRAF, TERT, KRAS, NRAS, HRAS, CCDC6, NCOA4, PAX8, and ETV6) was analyzed. RT-PCR kits were obtained from Xiamen AmoyDx Biopharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Lung cancer 11-gene mutation detection kit, Catalog No.: 8.01.0126) and Shanghai RIGEN-BIO Co., Ltd. (Thyroid multi-gene detection kit, Catalog No.: 240901). The reaction protocols are provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Continuous variables, including visibility scores and semi-quantitative IHC intensity × proportion scores for paired tissue samples (control vs. dye-labeled), were compared using the nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables, including HER2 and PD-L1 expression categories as well as frozen section (FS)–permanent section diagnostic concordance between labeled and unlabeled groups, were analyzed using the Fisher exact test. The permanent section diagnosis was considered the reference standard. Diagnostic discrepancy was defined as disagreement between FS and permanent section diagnoses at a predefined clinically actionable diagnostic level (benign vs. malignant and, where applicable, tumor type); deferred/indeterminate FS interpretations later resolved on permanent sections were also classified as discordant. All tests were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Visibility of marked tumors before dehydration

After FS diagnosis, the tumor was marked with MB, CNS, nano-TiO2, and DB (Fig. 1). Post-labeling, the tissues were allowed to air dry. The number of visibly marked tumors varied based on the marking material and cancer type (Fig. 1). In lung cancer, visible labeling was achieved in MB (9/20), CNS (10/20), nano-TiO2 (20/20), and DB (18/20). For thyroid cancer, the results were as follows: MB (7/15), CNS (9/15), nano-TiO2 (15/15), and DB (13/15). In breast cancer, the visibility was as follows: MB (13/14), CNS (13/14), nano-TiO2 (8/14), and DB (14/14). Accordingly, nano-TiO2 exhibited the best visibility in lung and thyroid cancers, whereas MB, CNS, and DB performed better in breast cancer tissues.

Visibility of marked tumors after dehydration

Macroscopic assessment after dehydration and paraffin embedding (Fig. 2), revealed lung—MB (3/20), CNS (6/20), nano-TiO2 (20/20), and DB (16/20); thyroid—MB (2/15), CNS (4/15), nano-TiO2 (15/15), and DB (11/15); breast—MB (2/14), CNS (10/14), nano-TiO2 (3/14), and DB (14/14). Nano-TiO2 maintained superior visibility in lung and thyroid cancers, while DB revealed the best performance in breast cancer. Conversely, MB and CNS exhibited poor retention.

Paraffin-embedded lung (A), thyroid (B), and breast (C) cancer tissues. Red arrows indicate marked tumor regions

Stability of marked tissues

In paraffin-embedded H&E sections, residual staining persisted in CNS, nano-TiO2 and DB, while MB was decolorized during dehydration, suggesting its poor stability. In breast cancer, CNS spread into the surrounding tissue, indicating instability. Conversely, stable labeling was retained in CNS (10/14), nano-TiO2 (15/15), and DB (11/15) groups, without fading (Fig. 3). On microscopic examination, nano-TiO2 and DB were predominantly confined to the predefined labeled surface region and showed no evident extension into the adjacent normal tissue in the examined sections. Similar findings were observed in both unstained and H&E-stained slides, indicating that the marking signals remained mainly surface-localized under the conditions used in this study.

Unstained sections of lung (A), thyroid (B), and breast (C) cancer tissues. Red arrows highlight the marked regions (×200)

H&E staining analysis

None of the marking materials (MB, CNS, nano-TiO2, and DB) adversely affected morphology or H&E staining quality in lung and thyroid cancer. In breast cancer, MB, nano-TiO2, and DB revealed no interference, whereas CNS caused marked staining artifacts (Fig. 4).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections of lung (A), thyroid (B), and breast (C) cancer tissues. Red arrows indicate the marked regions (×200)

Diagnostic concordance rates

In lung cancer, unlabeled tissues demonstrated 5/32 discrepancies (84.38%) between FS and permanent section diagnoses, while the nano-TiO2-labeled group exhibited 1/20 discrepancy (95%). For thyroid cancer, unlabeled tissues 3/16 (81.25%) versus nano-TiO2-labeled 15/15 (100%). For breast cancer, unlabeled 2/14 (85.71%) versus DB-labeled 14/14 (100%). McNemar’s test confirmed significantly higher concordance in labeled groups compared to unlabeled groups across all cancer types: Lung (p = 0.0066), thyroid (p = 0.0075), and breast (p = 0.0042). Tissue marking thus markedly enhanced intraoperative diagnostic accuracy (Fig. 5).

Diagnostic concordance between unlabeled and marked tissues in lung, thyroid, and breast cancers

IHC analysis

Compared with the control group, marked sections demonstrated accurate signal localization without evident antigen attenuation, diffusion, or nonspecific background staining (Fig. 6). For categorical therapeutic markers, HER2 interpretation in breast cancer tissues remained unchanged between control and marked sections. PD-L1 expression, assessed using tumor proportion score (TPS) and categorized into clinically relevant strata (< 1%, 1%–49%, and ≥ 50%), showed complete categorical concordance between paired sections. The remaining markers (TTF-1, Napsin A, p53, Ki-67, Galectin-3, TPO, CD56, BRAF V600E, ER, and PR) were scored using the semiquantitative intensity × proportion method. The staining patterns and scores were comparable between control and marked tissues, without evidence of systematic alteration in staining intensity or distribution. No statistically significant differences were observed (P > 0.05).

Molecular pathological testing

Nano-TiO2-labeled lung and thyroid cancer tissues were analyzed using RT-PCR for mutations in 11 lung cancer genes (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, RET, HER2, KRAS, BRAF, NTRK1/2/3, and MET exon 14 skipping) and 10 thyroid cancer genes (BRAF, TERT, KRAS exon 2/3, NRAS exon 3, HRAS exon 3, CCDC6-RET, NCOA4-RET, PAX8-PPARG, and ETV6-NTRK3). In lung cancer, mutations were detected in EGFR (9/10, 90%) and KRAS (1/10, 10%) (The specific mutation sites are demonstrated in Table 1). In thyroid cancer, BRAF mutations were present in 4/10 cases (40%), NRAS in 1/10 (10%), and HRAS in 1/10 (10%) (the specific mutation sites are listed in Table 2). The consistency of molecular results between labeled and unlabeled tissues was 100%. Results for additional genes are provided in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5.

Discussion

Intraoperative FS examination is a vital tool for rapid pathological diagnosis, guiding immediate surgical decisions. However, its diagnostic accuracy is limited to about 80% [15], and in some cases, pathologists defer final interpretation until permanent sections are available, results in ensuring diagnostic precision. Differences in tissue handling between FS and permanent sections can cause diagnostic discrepancies, which may influence treatment outcomes. While intrinsic histological processing differences between frozen and permanent sections cannot be eliminated, reducing localization instability during specimen handling may mitigate workflow-related sampling variability. By preserving spatial continuity of small tumor foci within the same tissue block, the proposed strategy targets a modifiable source of diagnostic discordance rather than the biochemical effects of processing itself. Consequently, improving consistency between FS and permanent section diagnoses remains an important challenge in pathology. This study is the first to reveal that intraoperative marking with nano-TiO2 and DB can effectively trace small tumor foci during permanent sectioning. The approach significantly enhanced tissue visibility, stability, and diagnostic consistency. By standardizing intraoperative marking, this strategy may improve both diagnostic speed and accuracy, ultimately supporting optimized surgical management.

Several factors may contribute to inconsistencies observed between FS and permanent section diagnoses. Prior studies report a higher error rate in mucinous and borderline tumors, often due to sampling bias, technical artifacts, or inadequate intraoperative communication [16, 17]. During the dehydration process, the architecture of frozen tissues may undergo shrinkage and discoloration to a dark brown hue, and tumor regions—particularly those measuring between 0.5 and 1 cm—may shift due to structural deformation. During paraffin embedding, the tumor-bearing side is typically downward oriented in the mold to ensure diagnostic slicing, yet small lesions are often indistinguishable, complicating specimen orientation. This may lead to improper slicing planes, over-trimming, and even tumor tissue loss, all of which increase the risk of diagnostic errors. Hence, precise localization of tumor regions is essential for accurate histopathological evaluation.

Marking lesions intended for permanent sectioning provides a practical strategy to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Clinically, MB and CNS are widely applied in SLN biopsies for breast cancer. MB is inexpensive and readily available and has also been used to localize nonpalpable breast lesions [18]. However, its high-water solubility promotes rapid diffusion within glandular tissues and fast metabolic clearance [19]. CNS, developed through nanotechnology, offers greater stability and diffusibility and has been validated for gastrointestinal, thyroid, and breast cancer surgeries [20, 21]. Although these marking materials improve intraoperative visibility, their tendency to diffuse can complicate pathological interpretation. Conversely, custom-developed—nano-TiO2 and DB—demonstrated superior visibility, stability, and tissue compatibility when utilized for marking during intraoperative permanent sectioning. While not designed as a formal comparative superiority study, the observed differences in diffusion behavior and surface retention suggest potential practical advantages in specific workflow contexts. The proposed method has been filed as a Chinese invention patent application (Application No. 202510660942.0). The innovation of the proposed system lies not in the chemical novelty of its components, but in their structured integration into a workflow-compatible tissue-marking strategy that addresses a practical unmet need in intraoperative pathology.

The selection of nano-TiO₂ and Direct Black (DB) was guided by physicochemical considerations relevant to interfacial retention and optical contrast. Titanium dioxide is known for its high refractive index (> 2.4) and high chemical stability, properties that support strong visual contrast and material robustness during handling [22]. In addition, nanoscale particles exhibit a high surface-to-volume ratio and distinct surface properties compared with larger counterparts, which may facilitate surface interaction and retention on biological substrates [23].

DB is a direct dye with relatively high molecular weight, which provides high optical density and supports superficial, surface-dominant deposition rather than deep penetration [24]. When formulated in an acrylic-based carrier, particulate pigments embedded in acrylic polymer emulsions have been shown to yield durable markings with minimal seepage into underlying tissue and without compromising routine section quality or staining. Consistent with these considerations, in our workflow nano-TiO2/DB markings remained confined to the tissue surface and were retained throughout fixation, dehydration, and embedding.

Both nano-TiO2 and DB adhered strongly to tissue surfaces and retained distinct markings throughout fixation, dehydration, and embedding, without appreciable diffusion. Histological evaluation confirmed that labeling was confined to the predefined tumor surface region without extension into adjacent normal tissue.

Conversely, MB faded rapidly, failing to provide distinct markings. CNS demonstrated significant spread within breast cancer tissue, consistent with prior reports [25], reducing boundary definition. The water solubility of MB and CNS is attributed to their aqueous solubility. To enhance stability, acrylic has been recognized as a safe, cost-effective, and readily accessible solvent for labeling both fixed and unfixed specimens [26]. When combined with nano-TiO2 and DB, the acrylic-based solution produced durable, intensely pigmented markings resistant to environmental changes. Notably, these markings localized exclusively to the tissue surface, avoiding deep penetration, thereby preserving structural clarity during embedding and sectioning. During microtomy, the marking gradually faded without interfering with subsequent procedures. Furthermore, lung and thyroid tissues typically appear dark brown after dehydration, while breast tissues appear grayish white, allowing the bright contrast of nano-TiO2 and DB to stand out prominently. These findings support the preferential use of nano-TiO2 for lung and thyroid cancers, and DB for breast cancer in intraoperative tissue marking.

H&E staining remains the cornerstone of pathological diagnosis for evaluating cellular morphology, while IHC detects specific protein expressions and RT-PCR assesses DNA or mRNA alteration. These methodologies are essential for diagnostic and clinical decision-making processes. To determine whether nano-TiO2 and DB interfere with these workflows, we examined their effects on H&E staining, IHC, and molecular diagnostics. Both marking revealed no adverse impact on H&E staining, protein marker expression (PD-L1, TTF-1, Napsin A, ER, PR, and HER2), or mutation analysis (EGFR, BRAF, KRAS, and RET) in lung, thyroid, and breast cancer tissues. Furthermore, diagnostic concordance was significantly higher in labeled tissues compared to controls (95% versus 84.38% in lung cancer, 100% versus 81.25% in thyroid cancer, and 100% versus 85.71% in breast cancer; all p < 0.05). Improved tumor visibility and localization stability were technical outcomes related to specimen handling. These factors may have reduced workflow-related sampling variability, which in turn could influence diagnostic concordance. Importantly, diagnostic discrepancy in this study was defined strictly as disagreement between frozen and permanent section diagnoses, independent of visual boundary clarity. These findings confirm that nano-TiO2 and DB improve diagnostic consistency between FS and permanent section without disrupting subsequent testing.

Compared with MB and CNS, nano-TiO₂ and DB showed several practical advantages for intraoperative tissue marking, including better gross visibility, stronger retention after fixation and dehydration, less surface diffusion, and good compatibility with routine histological, immunohistochemical, and molecular analyses. These combined features made small tumor foci easier to recognize and follow during paraffin processing, which may help reduce workflow-related sampling deviation and contribute to the improved concordance between frozen and permanent section diagnoses observed in this study.

It is important to clarify that the nano-TiO2/DB marking system proposed in this study is not intended for surgical margin assessment. In routine pathology practice, dedicated margin inks are applied circumferentially to the resection boundary to enable evaluation of tumor involvement at the specimen edge. In contrast, the present marking strategy is applied focally to the tumor surface for intra-specimen localization during permanent section processing. The spatial application pattern therefore differs fundamentally from margin-marking protocols. Because the marking is confined to tumor surfaces and does not extend to specimen boundaries, it does not obscure or alter the evaluation of inked surgical margins.

In this study, nano-TiO2 was incorporated into an acrylic-based liquid matrix and applied to ex vivo specimens via localized spot coating. No dry powder handling, aerosolization, or spray procedures were involved, thereby substantially reducing the potential for airborne particle generation. It is noteworthy that occupational concerns regarding TiO2 primarily relate to chronic inhalation of fine particulate matter. Given the liquid-phase formulation and controlled laboratory handling in the present setting, exposure risk is expected to be minimal under standard safety practices. Nevertheless, implementation of appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE), ventilation measures, and institutional safety protocols is recommended prior to broader clinical application. In addition, nano-TiO2 labeling was compatible with routine paraffin processing, microtomy, H&E staining, immunohistochemistry, and molecular assays in the present study, with no observable workflow disruption or staining artifacts detected. These findings suggest practical compatibility with standard pathological procedures. However, although no procedural adverse effects were identified under the conditions evaluated, the long-term safety of sustained routine laboratory use—particularly regarding cumulative occupational exposure and material disposal—has not been systematically assessed and warrants further investigation.

Despite these advantages, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was modest, underscoring the need for larger studies to validate the stability and reproducibility of this technique across a broader range of tumor types. Second, our research focused on lung, thyroid, and breast cancers, and future investigations should extend to other tumor types, such as cutaneous, oral, gynecologic, and lymphoid neoplasms. Third, variations in technical protocols across different hospitals and laboratories may influence implementation, highlighting the necessity of multicenter validation to establish feasibility and reproducibility in diverse settings. An improvement in diagnostic concordance between frozen and permanent sections was observed following tissue marking. However, as this was not a randomized or blinded study design, the observed effect cannot be attributed exclusively to the marking itself. Potential influences of sampling variability, tissue heterogeneity, and operator-related factors cannot be entirely excluded. To mitigate these sources of bias, frozen and permanent sections were derived from the same tissue block, all specimens were processed within a single pathology department under standardized protocols, and predefined criteria for successful localization were applied. Nevertheless, as a single-center study with a limited sample size, residual confounding related to sampling or operator variation remains possible. Advanced microscopic and imaging technologies indeed offer superior resolution for detecting minute pathological changes. However, the objective of the present study is not to enhance microscopic detection sensitivity. Rather, the proposed marking strategy addresses a distinct workflow-related challenge—maintaining spatial continuity of small tumor foci during the transition from frozen section diagnosis to permanent paraffin processing. Therefore, this approach should be regarded as complementary to, rather than competitive with, advanced imaging modalities, as it operates at the macroscopic localization level within routine pathological workflows.

Compared with conventional marking materials, nano-TiO2 and DB demonstrated superior visibility and stability, significantly enhancing diagnostic concordance between frozen and permanent sections’ diagnoses, while preserving compatibility with IHC and molecular analyses. The present findings are limited to lung, thyroid, and breast cancer specimens. Although the surface deposition mechanism of the marking materials is not inherently organ-specific, tissue architecture, consistency, and surface characteristics may influence labeling performance. Therefore, the proposed method should currently be regarded as validated within the evaluated tumor types, and further studies are required to determine its generalizability across additional organs and pathological settings.
