Impaired PGC-1α-pAMPK signaling in postmenopausal women undergoing cardiac surgery and the role of nicotinamide in its reversal: Insights from a murine model
Authors: Mark Robitaille, Usman Ahmed, Juan Valencia, Adnan Khan, Adil al-Karim Manji, Elizabeth Russ, Louis Chu, Khamal Khabbaz, Feroze Mahmood, Robina Matyal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostmenopausal women undergoing cardiac surgery face disproportionately worse outcomes compared with men, a disparity linked to mitochondrial dysfunction following estrogen loss. We investigated sex-specific metabolic responses to cardiac stress in surgical patients and in a murine model of postmenopausal dysfunction, with a focus on proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) signaling and the therapeutic potential of nicotinamide (NAM), a precursor of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+). In a prospective cohort of 102 patients (51 men, 51 women) undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, women exhibited a significantly higher incidence of diastolic dysfunction and longer hospital stays despite comparable baseline characteristics. Molecular analysis of atrial tissue revealed that men demonstrated increased postoperative PGC-1α and phosphorylated AMP-activated protein kinase (pAMPK), while women had reduced expression and also displayed a significant decline in myocardial NAD+ levels. To mechanistically model these findings, ovariectomized mice on a high-fat diet exhibited reduced myocardial PGC-1α and pAMPK expression, suppressed antioxidant defenses, and increased fibrosis. NAM supplementation restored myocardial NAD+, increased PGC-1α expression, and significantly improved systolic and diastolic cardiac function. These findings indicate that loss of estrogen impairs responses to oxidative stress in postmenopausal women, predisposing to adverse outcomes after cardiac stress. NAM may represent a promising non-hormonal strategy to restore mitochondrial function and improve cardiac function. This supports further evaluation of NAD+-boosting therapies as targeted interventions for postmenopausal women at risk of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.Keywords: Postmenopausal women, Cardiac surgery, Mitochondrial, PGC-1α/AMPK signaling, Nicotinamide supplementation, NAD+ metabolism

Introduction

Sex differences in the response to ischemia–reperfusion injury during cardiac surgery are well documented, with women experiencing disproportionately higher morbidity and mortality [1]. Following menopause, the loss of estrogen is associated with diminished cardioprotective mechanisms, which may contribute to a higher incidence of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and poorer surgical outcomes, including prolonged hospital stays [2,3]. Mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a central mechanism linking estrogen loss to adverse cardiovascular outcomes [4]. Estrogen normally supports mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism through the upregulation of Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 alpha (PGC-1α), a key regulator in the response to cellular stress [5,6]. PGC-1α dysregulation has been implicated in diseases like obesity, diabetes and metabolic syndrome via alterations of various protective pathways [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. In the setting of menopause, reduced estrogen signaling further impairs these pathways, predisposing the myocardium to oxidative stress.

The use of hormone replacement therapy with estrogen supplementation in postmenopausal women has declined due to safety concerns [11,12]. More recently, SGLT-2 inhibitors have been shown to provide cardiovascular benefits, but their effectiveness appears attenuated in postmenopausal women compared with men [13,14]. These limitations have spurred growing interest in therapies that act more proximally on cellular energy metabolism. Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is a fundamental redox cofactor and signaling molecule, essential for oxidative phosphorylation and a required substrate for sirtuins, a family of NAD+-dependent deacetylases that regulate mitochondrial biogenesis [15]. In this context, NAD+ supplementation has emerged as a promising strategy to restore mitochondrial function and attenuate oxidative stress across cardiovascular and metabolic disease models [16]. Here, we investigate sex-specific mitochondrial responses to cardiac stress in patients and a murine model of postmenopausal dysfunction and evaluate whether NAD+ supplementation can restore mitochondrial function and improve cardiac function in the postmenopausal cohort.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All experimental protocols involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC). Animal studies were conducted under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at BIDMC, in compliance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Animal Welfare Act, and reported following ARRIVE guidelines. Human and animal studies described herein were conducted in accordance with relevant regulatory standards and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Human study design

The study cohort consisted of 102 adult patients (51 males and 51 females) undergoing cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), specifically coronary artery bypass grafting and aortic valve replacement, enrolled between August 2019 and August 2024. Patients under 18 years of age, those undergoing emergent procedures, and those with symptomatic heart failure (Stage C or D) were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion. Demographic, procedural and outcome variables were obtained through retrospective chart review. The patients underwent cardiac surgery under standardized perioperative protocols at our institution, including uniform anesthetic management, cardiopulmonary bypass technique, myocardial protection strategy, and postoperative care pathways. There were no sex-specific variations in intraoperative or perioperative management across the cohort. As part of our preoperative institutional protocol, all patients underwent a pre-defined echocardiography protocol based on the 2016 American Society of Echocardiography Guidelines for the assessment of diastolic dysfunction. Diastolic dysfunction was defined as 2 more abnormal left ventricular filling indices based on e′, E, their ratio (E/e′), LA volume index (LAVI), and peak tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity [17]. In accordance with the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure, this pattern was classified as Stage B heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), reflecting structural heart disease in the absence of overt symptoms [18].

Sample collection and processing

Preoperative blood (10 mL) was obtained from radial arterial line prior to incision. Right atrial tissue and epicardial fat were harvested intraoperatively during cannulation and prior to initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass, and again postoperatively during venous cannula removal immediately after the patient was placed off cardiopulmonary bypass. Serum was isolated by centrifugation at 2000 ×g for 15 min. Tissue samples (50–100 mg) were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Western blotting and immunohistochemistry

Western blot analysis was performed on homogenized atrial tissue lysates using RIPA buffer. Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg) were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes, and probed with primary antibodies specific to phosphorylated AMP-activated protein kinase (pAMPK), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC1α), Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), Cytochrome C (CytC), cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4 (COX4), and superoxide dismutase (SOD). Signals were visualized using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies and chemiluminescent substrates. Densitometric analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD), with normalization to housekeeping proteins glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) or β-actin. We calculated a lane-specific normalization factor for each blot by dividing the GAPDH intensity of each lane by the highest GAPDH intensity on that membrane. Experimental band intensities were then normalized by dividing the raw signal by this lane-specific factor [19]. NAD quantification in atrial tissue lysates was performed via an enzymatic cycling assay kit (ab65348 NAD/NADH Assay Kit (Colorimetric)). Immunohistochemical staining was performed on formalin fixed, paraffin embedded mice cardiac tissue sections. Slides were stained for fibrosis with Direct Picrosirius stain after slide section (Sigma, Cat # 365548). Slides were separated into four distinct regions and analyzed at 10× magnification using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD) for quantification of areas of fibrosis.

Mouse study design

Two separate experiments were conducted. In the first experiment, thirty (30) female C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA) were randomized into three groups: control (C, n = 10), ovariectomized (OVX, n = 10), and ovariectomized + high-fat diet (OHFD, n = 10). Mice were fed either a low-fat diet (10 % kcal from fat) or a high-fat diet (60 % kcal from fat) for 25 weeks. OVX was performed at 8 weeks of age under sterile conditions. In the second experiment, 20 female C57BL/6 mice were ovariectomized at 8 weeks of age and then fed a high-fat diet (60 % kcal from fat) for 25 weeks. Between weeks 33 and 34 half of the mice (n = 10; OHFD+N) received daily intraperitoneal injections of nicotinamide (400 mg/kg) [20] for 7 days, while the remaining mice (n = 10) received saline. Abbreviations: NAM, nicotinamide; EchoMRI, echo magnetic resonance imaging; TTE, transthoracic echocardiography

At the conclusion of both experiments, all mice were euthanized. Plasma was collected via terminal cardiac puncture, and cardiac tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Tissue lysates were prepared by homogenizing whole hearts in RIPA buffer. Serial fat mass, lean mass, and total body weight were measured in OHFD+N mice using EchoMRI 3-in-1 (Echo Medical Systems, Houston, TX).

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography (Vevo 2100 Imaging system) was performed on the OHFD+N group of mice under isoflurane (2.5 %) anesthesia at 3 time points: baseline, and before and after nicotinamide treatment. Systolic function was assessed using B-mode and M-mode echocardiography from the parasternal short-axis view to measure left ventricular (LV) dimensions and wall thickness. Fractional shortening (FS) and ejection fraction (EF) were determined to evaluate cardiac function. Diastolic function was assessed by recording transmitral inflow velocities, the Doppler waveforms provided measurements of early (E wave) and late (A wave) diastolic velocities, which were used to calculate the E/A ratio. Tissue Doppler imaging allowed measurement of the early-diastolic myocardial relaxation velocity (e'), which was used to calculate the E/e' ratio for further evaluation of diastolic performance.

Protein quantification in mice cardiac lysates

Protein expression in murine cardiac tissue was assessed by Western blot, following procedures similar to those used for human samples. Primary antibodies targeting phosphorylated AMP-activated protein kinase (pAMPK), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC1α), B-cell lymphoma-extra large (BCL-XL), manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) were used. Quantification of signal intensity was performed by densitometric analysis, as described previously.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA (v18) and GraphPad Prism (v9.0). Normality of continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on distribution. Group comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-tests, Pearson's chi-squared test or one-way ANOVA test with post hoc comparisons where appropriate. A two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Our study cohort included 102 patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, evenly distributed by sex (51 males, 51 females). Baseline demographic and anthropometric characteristics, including age and body mass index (BMI), were similar between groups (Table 1). The mean age was 67.1 ± 8.5 years in men and 64.8 ± 9.5 years in women (p = 0.21), and BMI did not differ significantly (29.7 ± 5.7 kg/m2 vs. 30.0 ± 6.0 kg/m2, p = 0.81). The type of surgery performed was comparable between sexes, with the majority undergoing isolated CABG (92.1 % of men vs. 88.2 % of women), and smaller proportions receiving valve surgery or combined procedures. The prevalence of comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, renal failure, and transient ischemic attack, did not differ significantly between groups. Operative variables, including mean bypass time (94.4 ± 29.2 min vs. 89.0 ± 27.8 min, p = 0.35) and cross-clamp time (78.3 ± 26.1 min vs. 72.1 ± 23.3 min, p = 0.20), were not significantly different between sexes.

Women exhibited a significantly higher incidence of diastolic dysfunction compared with men (51.0 % vs. 25.5 %, p = 0.014). Mitochondrial markers in human atrial tissue

In male patients, PGC-1α expression significantly increased following cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) (p < 0.05), whereas in female patients, postoperative levels were reduced (Fig. 1A). Consistently, phosphorylated AMPK (pAMPK) expression, an upstream regulator of PGC-1α, increased in men but decreased in women after CPB (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). Sirtuin-1 (SIRT1) expression was elevated in both sexes postoperatively (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). Postoperative cytochrome C (CytC) expression was significantly increased in males compared to females (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). Similarly, postoperative levels of cytochrome oxidase subunit IV (COXIV) were reduced in females compared with males (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1F). Postoperative SOD1 expression increased in females (p < 0.05) but remained unchanged in males (Fig. 1E). Importantly, NAD+ levels declined significantly in females after CPB (p < 0.05), while no significant decline was observed in men (Fig. 1G).

Animal studies

To model postmenopausal metabolic stress, female mice underwent ovariectomy (OVX) and were fed a high-fat diet (OHFD) [21]. Western blot analyses revealed that PGC-1α expression was significantly decreased in OHFD mice compared with controls (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). OHFD also suppressed pAMPK (Fig. 2C), reduced MnSOD levels (Fig. 2D), and downregulated the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-XL (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2E), consistent with heightened susceptibility to oxidative stress and apoptosis. PPAR-γ expression (Fig. 2B) was significantly increased in OVX and OHFD groups compared to controls (p < 0.05). Histological analysis of cardiac tissue demonstrated significantly greater interstitial fibrosis in OHFD hearts compared with controls (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2F).

Effect of NAM treatment

To test whether NAD+ repletion could reverse these maladaptive changes, a subset of OHFD mice were treated with nicotinamide (NAM). NAM supplementation significantly restored myocardial NAD+ levels, PGC-1α and pAMPK expression compared with untreated OHFD mice (p < 0.05) (Figs. 3D-F). Echocardiographic evaluation revealed significant improvements in both systolic and diastolic function: ejection fraction (EF) was increased, while the E/e' ratio was reduced (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3G-I). Left ventricular volumes remained unchanged, indicating that the functional improvements were not driven by structural dilation.

Beyond mitochondrial markers, NAM also favorably altered body composition. NAM-treated OHFD mice demonstrated reduced body mass, fat mass, and lean mass after treatment (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3A–C).

Discussion

Sex-specific metabolic responses to cardiac stress

In our surgical cohort, we observed sex-specific differences in the incidence of stage B heart failure, hospital length of stay, and molecular responses to cardiac stress, particularly in markers of fatty acid metabolism such as PGC-1α and its upstream regulator, pAMPK. This aligns with well-documented disparities in cardiovascular disease between sexes [22,23]. There were no significant postoperative differences in the expression of SIRT1, an NAD+-dependent deacetylase. However, in women, the postoperative increase in SIRT1 was insufficient to sustain PGC-1α levels, likely due to reduced NAD+ availability, which may have limited SIRT1 activity and its downstream regulatory effects. SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of PGC-1α is essential for maintaining mitochondrial biogenesis and fatty acid oxidation, and reduced activity of this pathway may worsen clinical outcomes following cardiac stress [[24], [25], [26]].

Estrogen plays a key role in maintaining mitochondrial function by regulating the AMPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway [5,27]. It activates AMPK through its receptors ERα and ERβ, which in turn promotes the phosphorylation and activation of PGC-1α via SIRT1 [28]. Estrogen-related receptor alpha (ERRα) also supports this process by directly promoting mitochondrial biogenesis through PGC-1α [29,30]. The reduced expression of PGC-1α observed in women may be driven by the loss of estrogen signaling, especially after menopause. This may underlie the attenuated metabolic response to cardiac stress in postmenopausal women and contribute to poorer clinical outcomes following surgery [31].

Mouse model replicates human postmenopausal cardiac dysfunction

We confirmed our molecular findings in a mouse model of ovariectomy and high-fat diet, which showed decreased expression of markers of fatty acid metabolism, indicating decreased mitochondrial biogenesis. This was accompanied by a marked reduction in anti-apoptotic proteins, indicating increased susceptibility to apoptosis under metabolic and hormonal stress. The upregulation of PPARγ in OHFD mice likely reflected a compensatory attempt to restore lipid metabolism. Given the established role of AMPK in attenuating interstitial fibrosis and preserving diastolic function in metabolic disease and aging models [32], the observed suppression of AMPK and PGC-1α signaling, loss of antioxidant capacity, reduced anti-apoptotic defense, and increased fibrosis in the OHFD group provide mechanistic insight into the increased vulnerability of the postmenopausal myocardium to maladaptive remodeling. The link between estrogen signaling and cardiomyocyte survival in the context of metabolic and hormonal stress is well established. Patten et al. demonstrated that 17β-estradiol replacement in ovariectomized mice significantly reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis after myocardial infarction by enhancing PI3K–Akt, with downstream inhibition of caspase-3 activation and preservation of cell viability [33]. Similar protective effects have been reported in vitro, where estrogen rapidly activated Akt and suppressed apoptosis induced by oxidative stress, effects abolished by ER antagonism or PI3K inhibition [34]. This aligns with our present findings, suggesting that loss of estrogen may compromise anti-apoptotic defenses. Impairment of the estrogen–PGC-1α axis reduces mitochondrial turnover, increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and promotes pathological cardiac remodeling [35].

Nicotinamide restores mitochondrial function and cardiac performance

Treatment with NAM, an NAD+ precursor known to enhance SIRT1 activity and improve mitochondrial function, significantly increased cardiac PGC-1α and pAMPK levels and improved body composition. Echocardiographic analysis revealed significantly improved systolic and diastolic function. NAD+ supplementation has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy across cardiac, neurologic, renal, and metabolic disorders [16,[36], [37], [38]]. Both acute and chronic stress conditions, including myocardial infarction, acute kidney injury, aging, and metabolic syndrome, are associated with NAD+ depletion [37,38]. Restoring NAD+ levels through supplementation or precursor administration enhances mitochondrial bioenergetics, improves oxidative phosphorylation, and promotes fatty acid oxidation [[39], [40], [41]]. These effects are mediated in part by activation of NAD + -dependent sirtuins, which regulate mitochondrial protein acetylation and protect against oxidative stress and hypertrophy in the heart [15,42].

Notably, trials of SGLT-2 inhibitors have suggested attenuated cardiovascular benefits in women compared with men [43,44]. In contrast, GLP-1 receptor agonists have demonstrated consistent cardiovascular protection across sexes [45,46]. As postmenopausal women with HFpEF and metabolic syndrome represent a relatively small and underrepresented subgroup within these trials [47,48], the specific effects in this high-risk population remain uncertain. Given the disproportionate burden of HFpEF and metabolic dysfunction in postmenopausal women, dedicated trials focusing on sex-specific mechanisms and therapeutic responses are warranted to guide precision therapies and address this unmet need.

Postmenopausal women exhibit altered lipid metabolism and face a disproportionately high risk of metabolic syndrome and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), a syndrome marked by diastolic dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and impaired energetic reserve [2,49]. Since diastole is an energy-dependent process of the cardiac cycle [50], energy insufficiency is a key contributor to disease progression in this population. Emerging preclinical evidence further supports the model that NAD+-boosting therapies act through the SIRT1/PGC-1α axis to preserve mitochondrial function. In a recent study, Zhao et al. showed that intraperitoneal nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) administration increased myocardial NAD+/NADH ratio, upregulated SIRT1 and PGC-1α expression, enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, and improved cardiac performance in SIRT3-deficient mice [51]. NAD+ repletion restores mitochondrial function, improves fatty acid metabolism, and enhances diastolic performance in models of HFpEF [20,38,52,53]. Given the impact of metabolic stress in postmenopausal women, NAD+-boosting strategies may be particularly effective in reactivating the SIRT1/PGC-1α axis, strengthening antioxidant defenses, and regulating apoptosis. Collectively, these findings highlight NAD+ supplementation as a promising non-hormonal therapeutic approach to mitigate metabolic dysfunction and HFpEF progression in postmenopausal women [54,55].

This study has several limitations. The patient cohort was limited to a single center and relatively small, which may restrict generalizability. NAD+ levels and mitochondrial protein expression were measured at discrete perioperative time points, which may not capture dynamic changes over the full course of the procedure. The murine model, while replicating key features of postmenopausal metabolic stress through ovariectomy and high-fat diet, does not fully capture the complexity of human menopause, comorbidities, or surgical stress. Moreover, nicotinamide administration was limited to a short treatment window, and long-term effects on cardiac remodeling, fibrosis, and functional outcomes remain unclear. While our data are consistent with a model in which NAM-mediated NAD+ repletion improves cardiac function by activating SIRT1 and augmenting PGC-1α–dependent mitochondrial function, causal validation and detailed assessment of the mechanistic pathways will be necessary in future work. Confirming that NAD+ repletion is the primary mediator of cardio-protection would require additional mechanistic studies, such as direct SIRT1 activity assays, mitochondrial bioenergetic profiling, or genetic modulation of NAD+ metabolism. Further in-vitro studies will be needed to clarify whether NAD+ restoration itself drives the cardioprotective effects or acts through secondary downstream mechanisms. Although nicotinamide is a clinically relevant NAD+ precursor, other NAD+-boosting strategies (such as nicotinamide riboside or nicotinamide mononucleotide) may have distinct pharmacologic profiles and therapeutic potential that were not assessed in this study. While the current design focused on establishing translational feasibility, formal dose–response studies and independent replication cohorts are warranted to refine the therapeutic range and reproducibility of these findings in future studies. Although our study demonstrates early improvements in mitochondrial signaling and cardiac function following nicotinamide supplementation, assessment of long-term outcomes, including survival, cardiac remodeling, and HF readmissions will require multicenter randomized trials with extended follow-up. Such studies will be essential to determine the chronic therapeutic potential of NAD+-boosting strategies in postmenopausal cardiac dysfunction.

Conclusion

Postmenopausal women exhibit a blunted compensatory response to cardiac oxidative stress due to decreased PGC-1α activation and NAD+ depletion, predisposing them to fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction. NAM-induced restoration of the SIRT1–PGC-1α axis leads to improved mitochondrial biogenesis and cardiac function, offering a promising therapeutic target. Unlike estrogen replacement therapy, which carries cardiovascular risks, NAM represents a non-hormonal alternative with promising safety and metabolic benefits. These results justify further investigation into NAM as a perioperative adjunct or chronic therapy to improve outcomes in postmenopausal women at risk of cardiac dysfunction.
