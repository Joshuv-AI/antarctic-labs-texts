Radiofrequency Ablation of the Distal Vein of Marshall Prior to Ethanolization
Authors: Huaxiu Cai, Gang Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case SummaryA woman with atrial fibrillation underwent radiofrequency ablation combined with vein of Marshall (VOM) ethanol infusion. To prevent ethanol-induced sinus node injury via the distal VOM, we performed targeted radiofrequency ablation to occlude the distal VOM before ethanol infusion. No sinus arrest or atrioventricular block occurred. Pulmonary vein isolation was successfully achieved, and atrial fibrillation terminated during the procedure.Take-Home MessagesSinus node and atrioventricular node injury are rare but critical complications of VOM ethanol infusion. Preemptive occlusion of the distal VOM by radiofrequency ablation before ethanol infusion can reduce the risk of conduction system injury.

The patient was a 75-year-old woman with long-standing persistent atrial fibrillation (AF), who suffered recurrent palpitations for more than a year. She had a history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia. Holter showed AF with rapid ventricular rate.

She underwent transcatheter radiofrequency ablation plus ethanolization of the vein of Marshall (VOM). A JR4.0 guiding catheter was positioned inside the coronary sinus through an 8.5-F SL1 fixed curved sheath. The Marshall vein was visible through coronary sinus angiography. A guide wire and a balloon (2.5∗15 mm) were sent to the distal Marshall vein. Selective VOM angiography was performed. Direct injection of ethanol may cause sinus node damage. A guide wire with an insulating coating, which could conduct electricity at both the head and end, was placed in the distal end of the Marshall vein. The coronary balloon catheter was inserted into the Marshall vein along the guide wire. The end of the guide wire was in contact with the head of the ablation catheter (Figure 2). Radiofrequency ablation was performed, temperature control mode 10W 55 °C, to occlude the distal segment of the Marshall vein (Figure 3). Then 2 mL of ethanol was slowly injected, and after a pause for 2 minutes, 4 mL of ethanol was slowly injected. The contrast showed obvious myocardial staining (Figure 4). No sinus arrest or atrioventricular block was found. Pulmonary vein isolation followed, during which the AF terminated. After successful pulmonary vein isolation, isoproterenol and adenosine were used. After a period of observation, no arrhythmia was found. The operation was successful. Discussion

European and American electrophysiology experts published a study in Circulation: Arrhythmia and Electrophysiology in 2025.1 This study is an international survey of ethanolization of the VOM. It included a total of 5,579 patients (from 2008 to 2024) from 26 centers in Europe and the United States. Temporary or permanent second- or third-degree atrioventricular block was observed in 5 patients (0.09%), of whom 1 patient (0.02%) required permanent pacemaker implantation. Two patients (0.04%) developed sick sinus syndrome, 1 of whom required pacemaker implantation. Kawashima2 summarized the anatomical variations of the sinoatrial node and atrioventricular node arteries. Similar anatomical variations may also occur in veins, a finding supported by published case reports. Liu et al3 reported a case in 2025 of atrioventricular block lasting for 15.8 seconds after ethanol infusion of VOM. Jiwani et al4 also reported a case in 2025 in which sinus node damage occurred after ethanolization of the VOM and required implantation of a permanent pacemaker. This modified approach appears safe and feasible for avoiding conduction injury in ethanolization of the VOM.
