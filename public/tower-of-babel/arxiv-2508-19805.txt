Abstract
Understanding the computational power of mobile robot systems is a fundamental challenge in distributed computing. While prior work has focused on pairwise separations between models, we explore how robot capabilities, light observability, and scheduler synchrony interact in more complex ways. We first show that the Exponential Times Expansion ( ETE ) problem is solvable only in the strongest model— fully-synchronous robots with full mutual lights ( ℒ ​ 𝒰 ​ ℳ ​ ℐ F {\mathcal{LUMI}}^{F} ). We then introduce the Hexagonal Edge Traversal ( HET ) and TAR( d d )* problems to demonstrate how internal memory and lights interact with synchrony: under weak synchrony, internal memory alone is insufficient, while full synchrony can substitute for both lights and memory. In the asynchronous setting, we classify problems such as LP–MLCv , VEC , and ZCC to show fine-grained separations between ℱ ​ 𝒮 ​ 𝒯 ​ 𝒜 {\mathcal{FST\!A}} and ℱ ​ 𝒞 ​ 𝒪 ​ ℳ {\mathcal{FCOM}} robots. We also analyze Vertex Traversal Rendezvous ( VTR ) and Leave Place Convergence ( LP–Cv ), illustrating the limitations of internal memory in symmetric settings. These results extend the known separation map of 14 canonical robot models, revealing structural phenomena only visible through higher-order comparisons. Our work provides new impossibility criteria and deepens the understanding of how observability, memory, and synchrony collectively shape the computational power of mobile robots.

Background and Motivation

The computational power of autonomous mobile robots operating through

-
\mathit{Compute}
-
\mathit{Move}

The weakest standard model,
{\mathcal{OBLOT}}

]

A widely studied enhancement is the luminous robot model,

To understand the individual roles of memory and communication, two submodels of

External assumptions such as synchrony and activation schedulers also play a crucial role. The

activates an arbitrary non-empty subset of robots in each round, who perform one atomic

In contrast, the

Weaker variants of

Our Contributions

This paper goes beyond pairwise comparisons and explores

, revealing new structural properties in the model hierarchy.

•

In Section
4
, we show that some problems are solvable only in the strongest model
{\mathcal{LUMI}}^{F}
(equivalent to
{\mathcal{FCOM}}^{F}
), but not in either
{\mathcal{FST\!A}}^{F}

{\mathcal{LUMI}}^{R}
, highlighting the role of synchrony in enabling luminous computation.
We also introduce the problems

These results reveal asymmetries and complex trade-offs between memory, communication, and synchrony that are not captured by pairwise analysis. They underscore the need for a global landscape perspective on distributed robot models.

Models and Preliminaries

2.1
Robots

We consider a set
R_{n}=\{r_{0},\ldots,r_{n-1}\}
of
n>1
mobile computational entities, called
robots
, which move and operate in the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where they are viewed as points.
We simply write
R
instead of
R_{n}
, except when it is necessary to emphasize the number of robots.
The robots are
autonomous
(i.e., they operate without central control or external supervision),

identical
(i.e., they are indistinguishable by appearance and do not have unique identifiers), and

homogeneous
(i.e., they execute the same program).
Throughout this paper, we denote the position of a robot
r\in R
at time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by
r(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Each robot is equipped with a local coordinate system (in which it is always at its origin), and it is able
to observe the positions of the other robots in its local coordinate system.
The robots are
disoriented
(i.e., that is, there might not be consistency between the coordinate systems of different robots at the same time, or of the same robot at different times
1
1

1

The disorientation is said to be
static
if it is assumed that each local coordinate system always remains the same.
).
The robots however have
chirality
; that is, they agree on the same circular orientation of the plane (e.g., “clockwise” direction).

A robot is endowed with motorial and computational capabilities. When active, a robot performs a
{\mathit{Look}}
-
\mathit{Compute}
-
{\mathit{Move}}{}
(
\mathit{LCM}
) cycle:

1.

{\mathit{Look}}
: The robot obtains an instantaneous snapshot of the positions occupied by the robots, expressed in its local coordinate system. It cannot detect whether multiple robots are at the same location, nor how many (i.e., there is no
multiplicity detection
).

2.

\mathit{Compute}
: The robot executes its built-in deterministic algorithm, identical for all robots, using the snapshot as input. The result of the computation is a destination point.

3.

{\mathit{Move}}
: The robot moves continuously in a straight line until it reaches the computed destination (i.e., movements are
rigid
).
2
2

2

Movements are said to be
non-rigid
if they may be interrupted by the adversary.

If the destination is the current location, the robot stays still.

In the standard model,
{\mathcal{OBLOT}}
, the robots are also
silent
:
they have no means of direct
communication of information to other robots;
furthermore, they are
oblivious
: at the start of a cycle, a robot has no
memory of observations and computations performed in previous cycles.

In the other common model,
{\mathcal{LUMI}}
,
each robot
r
is equipped with a persistent register
\textit{Light}[r]
, called a
light
,
whose value—referred to as its
color
—is drawn from a constant-sized set
C
and is visible to all robots.
The color can be set by
r
at the
\mathit{Compute}
operation, and it is not automatically reset at the end of a cycle.
In
{\mathcal{LUMI}}
, the
{\mathit{Look}}
operation returns a colored snapshot; that is, it returns the set of distinct pairs
(\textit{position},\textit{color})

representing the states of the other robots at that time.
Note that if
|C|=1
, this case corresponds to the
{\mathcal{OBLOT}}
model.

Two submodels of
{\mathcal{LUMI}}
have been defined and investigated,
{\mathcal{FST\!A}}
and
{\mathcal{FCOM}}
, each offering only one
of its two capabilities, persistent memory and
direct means of communication, respectively.
In
{\mathcal{FST\!A}}
, a robot can only see the color of its own lights; thus, the color merely encodes an internal state. Therefore, robots are
silent
, as in
{\mathcal{OBLOT}}
, but they are
finite-state
.
In
{\mathcal{FCOM}}
, a robot can only see the colors of the lights of the other robots;
thus, it can communicate the color of its own lights to others,
but it cannot remember its own state (i.e., its own
k
colors).
Hence, robots are enabled with
finite-communication
but are
oblivious
.

2.2
Schedulers, Events

In this subsection, we define several schedulers—
Fsynch
,
Rsynch
,
Ssynch
,
Asynch
,
and several subclasses of
Asynch
—in terms of the activation schedule of the robots and the duration of their
\mathit{LCM}
cycles.
These schedulers impose different constraints on the adversary, as we shall see,
but they all share a common constraint called
fairness
:
every robot must perform the
{\mathit{Look}}
,
\mathit{Compute}
, and
{\mathit{Move}}
operations infinitely often.

The time it takes to complete a cycle is assumed to be finite and the operations
{\mathit{Look}}

and
\mathit{Compute}
are assumed to be instantaneous.

We assume that each of the
{\mathit{Look}}
,
\mathit{Compute}
, and
{\mathit{Move}}
operations completes in finite time.
Moreover, the
{\mathit{Look}}
and
\mathit{Compute}
operations are assumed to be instantaneous.
In the literature, the
\mathit{Compute}
operation has also been modeled as having some nonzero duration,
at the end of which a robot changes its color (in the
{\mathcal{LUMI}}
and
{\mathcal{FCOM}}
models).
However, we can assume without loss of generality that the
\mathit{Compute}
operation is instantaneous:
since the robot’s color always changes exactly at the end of the
\mathit{Compute}
operation,
its duration has no effect on the subsequent execution.

For any robot
r\in R
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, let

t_{L}(r,i)
,
t_{C}(r,i)
,
t_{B}(r,i)
, and
t_{E}(r,i)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

denote the times at which
r
performs its
i
-th Look, Compute, Move-begin, and Move-end operations, respectively.
These satisfy:

t_{L}(r,i)<t_{C}(r,i)<t_{B}(r,i)<t_{E}(r,i)<t_{L}(r,i+1)
.
If
r
decides to move from
p_{B}
to
p_{E}
at
t_{C}(r,i)
, it moves continuously along
[p_{B},p_{E}]
during
[t_{B}(r,i),t_{E}(r,i)]
, with
r(t_{B}(r,i))=p_{B}
,
r(t_{E}(r,i))=p_{E}
, and variable speed: for any
t_{1}<t_{2}
in
[t_{B}(r,i),t_{E}(r,i)]
,

|p_{B}-r(t_{1})|<|p_{E}-r(t_{2})|
if
p_{B}\neq p_{E}
.

In the
{\mathcal{LUMI}}
and
{\mathcal{FCOM}}
models, if
r
changes its light from
c_{1}
to
c_{2}
at time
t
and another robot
s
performs Compute at
t
, then
s
observes
c_{2}
.

In the the
synchronous
setting (
Ssynch
), also called
semi-synchronous
and first studied in
[
24
]
, time is divided into discrete
intervals, called
rounds
; in each round, a non-empty set of robots is activated and they simultaneously perform
a single
{\mathit{Look}}
-
\mathit{Compute}
-
{\mathit{Move}}
cycle in perfect synchronization.
The particular synchronous setting, where every robot is activated in every round
is called
fully-synchronous
(
Fsynch
).

We define

as the semi-synchronous scheduler in which,
after an optional finite prefix of fully synchronous rounds,
the remaining rounds activate non-empty subsets of robots

In the
asynchronous
setting (
Asynch
), first studied in
[
13
]
, we do not have any assumption—except for the fairness mentioned before—on the timing of each
{\mathit{Look}}
,
\mathit{Compute}
, and
{\mathit{Move}}
operation and the duration of each
{\mathit{Move}}
operation.

t_{i}<t_{i+1},i\in{\rm I\kern-2.20001ptN}
.

In the rest of this subsection, we introduce subclasses of

•

M

•

{\mathit{CM}}
-

Under this scheduler, each robot performs its consecutive

{\mathit{Look}}
,

A

A

Each location is given with respect to a fixed global coordinate system (which is unknown to the robots).
Recall that the color set

{\mathcal{OBLOT}}

\gamma:\mathcal{R}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

A problem to be solved (or task to be performed) is described by a set of

temporal geometric predicates

valid

as well as restrictions (if any) on the size

Given a model

3.1
RDV
(Rendezvous)
[
12
]

Two robots are assembled at a single point that is not predetermined from an arbitrary initial configuration.

Lemma 1

\textit{RDV}\notin\mathcal{FSTA}^{A_{M}}
.

Proof

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{FSTA}^{A_{M}}
that solves
RDV
.
Consider two robots
r
and
q
that share the same unit of distance and initially face each other along the positive
X
-axis direction.
Assume that the local coordinate system does not change during the execution of
\mathcal{A}
. We make the following observations:

1.

To solve
RDV
, the robots must move toward each other. Even if they use a finite number of lights to recognize their own activation count and perform moves toward or away from the other robot in an arbitrary order, the distance between them must decrease after one full cycle of such moves. Here, the distance moved by a robot in one activation is treated as the distance moved after one epoch (the number of rounds required for one full cycle).

2.

Each time a robot is activated and executes
\mathcal{A}
, it must move. If it were defined not to move at some distance
d
, then if both robots are activated synchronously, they would never move and never rendezvous.

3.

The amount of movement
f(d)
computed when robot
r
sees
q
at distance
d
is the same as when
q
sees
r
at distance
d
.

Consider the next execution
\varepsilon
under the asynchronous model (
M^{A_{M}}
). Initially,
r
and
q
are activated simultaneously at distance
d
.
Robot
r
finishes its computation and immediately starts moving while
q
continues computing.

From observation 1, each movement by
r
reduces the distance between the two robots. More precisely, from observation 3, after
k\geq 1
moves, the distance decreases from
d
to
d_{k}
as

d_{0}=d,\quad d_{k}=d_{k-1}-f(d_{k-1})=d-\sum_{0\leq i<k}f(d_{i}).

Lemma 2

After finitely many moves by
r
, the distance between the two robots becomes less than
f(d)
.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
r
never gets closer to
q
than
f(d)
, i.e., for all
k>0
,
d_{k}>f(d)
holds.

Next, consider the execution
\varepsilon
of
\mathcal{A}
under a RoundRobin synchronous scheduler, where robots are activated alternately starting at distance
d
.
Since
\mathcal{A}
is correct under AM, it should also be correct under RoundRobin. This means that starting from initial distance
d
, the two robots eventually get arbitrarily close to each other for any fixed distance
d^{\prime}>0
.

Let
m(d^{\prime})
be the number of rounds until the distance becomes less than
d^{\prime}
. Then after
m(f(d))
rounds, the distance is less than
f(d)
, and therefore,

d_{m(f(d))}=d-\sum_{0\leq i<m(f(d))}f(d_{i})<f(d),

contradicting the assumption that
d_{k}>f(d)
for all
k>0
.

Finally, consider the execution
\varepsilon
at the point where the distance is less than
f(d)
.
If
q
finishes computation at this point and moves by length
f(d)
toward
r
, a collision occurs, contradicting the correctness of
\mathcal{A}
.

Hence,
\textit{RDV}\notin\mathcal{FSTA}^{A_{M}}
holds.

3.2
CYC
(Cyclic Circles)
[
5
]

Let
n\geq 3
,
k=2^{n-1}
, and
d:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-invertible function. The problem is to form a cyclic sequence of patterns
C,C_{0},C,C_{1},C,C_{2},\ldots,C,C_{k-1}
where
C
is a pattern of
n
robots forming a “circle” occupied by
n-1
robots with one in the center, and
C_{i}
(for
0\leq i\leq k-1
) is a configuration where the
n-1
circle robots are in the exact same position, but the center robot occupies a point at distance
d(i)
from the center on the radius connecting the center to the missing robot position on the circle. In other words, the central robot moves to the designed position at distance
d(i)
and comes back to the center, and the process repeats after the
2^{n-1}
configurations
C_{i}
have been formed.

3.3
CGE
(Center of Gravity Expansion)
[
5
]

Let
R
be a set of robots with
|R|\geq 2
. Let
P=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
be the set of their positions and let
c=(c_{x},c_{y})
be the coordinates of the
CoG
(Center of Gravity) of
P
at time
t=0
. The
CGE
problem requires each robot
r_{i}\in R
to move from its initial position
(x_{i},y_{i})
directly to
(f(x_{i},c_{x}),f(y_{i},c_{y}))
where
f(a,b)=\lfloor 2a-b\rfloor
, away from the Center of Gravity of the initial configuration so that each robot doubles its initial distance from it and no longer moves.

3.4
TAR(
d
)
(Triangle Rotation)
[
17
]

Let
a,b,c
be three robots forming a triangle
ABC
, let
C
be the circumscribed circle, and let d be a value known to the three robots. The
TAR(
d
)
problem requires the robots to move so to form a new triangle
A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
with circumscribed circle C, and where
dis(A,A^{\prime})=dis(B,B^{\prime})=dis(C,C^{\prime})=d
.

3.5
OSP
(Oscillating Points)
[
17
]

Two robots,
a
and
b
, initially in distinct locations, alternately come closer and move further from each other. More precisely, let
d(t)
denote the distance of the two robots at time
t
. The
OSP
problem requires the two robots, starting from an arbitrary distance
d(t_{0})>0
at time
t_{0}
, to move so that there exists a monotonically increasing infinite sequence time instant
t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},\ldots
such that:

1.

d(t_{2i+1})<d(t_{2i}),\quad\text{and}\quad\forall h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}\in[t_{2i},t_{2i+1}],\;h^{\prime}<h^{\prime\prime}\implies d(h^{\prime\prime})\leq d(h^{\prime})
,

2.

d(t_{2i})>d(t_{2i-1}),\quad\text{and}\quad\forall h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}\in[t_{2i-1},t_{2i}],\;h^{\prime}<h^{\prime\prime}\implies d(h^{\prime\prime})\geq d(h^{\prime})
.

4.1
The Most Difficult Problem (ETE)

Definition 1

ETE (Exponential Times Expansion)

Let
n\geq 3
,
k=2^{n-1}
, and
d:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-invertible, meaning that for a given
i
,
d(i)
is uniquely determined, but the inverse is not.The problem is to construct a sequence of patterns
C_{0},C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
, each formed at respective times
t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}
. Pattern
C_{0}
is a circle formed by
n-1
robots, with one robot located at the center (see Figure
4
). Let
R
be the set of robots excluding the central one
r_{0}
, and let
g_{i}=(g_{x},g_{y})
be the coordinates of the center of gravity at time
t_{i}
. For each
i\neq 0
, pattern
C_{i}
is formed by moving each robot
r\in R
from position
(x,y)
to directly position
(f(x,g_{x}),f(y,g_{y}))
, where the function
f(a,b)
is defined as follows:

f(a,b)=\left\lfloor b+d(i)\cdot(a-b)\right\rfloor

In other words, each robot is scaled by a factor of
d(i)
from the center of gravity.

Fig. 4:
Movement of the
ETE
problem.

Lemma 3

Let
n\geq 3
.
\textit{ETE}\notin{\mathcal{LUMI}}^{F^{\prime}}
, where
F^{\prime}
is any synchronous scheduler such that the first activation does not contain all robots.

Proof

Let
k
(
1\leq k<n
) be the number of activated robots in the first round. By contradiction, suppose that the problem is solvable from an arbitrary initial configuration. Consider two scenarios with
R=\{r_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
.

In scenario A, the global coordinates of the robots are given by
r_{i}=(c_{i},0)
for
1\leq i\leq n
, where
c_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq n-1
) are distinct negative integers,
c_{n}
is a natural number, and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=0,

thus the center of gravity is at
(0,0)
.

In scenario B, the global coordinates of the robots are
r_{i}=(c_{i}+\varepsilon,0)
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, and
r_{n}=(c_{n},0)
, where
c_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq n-1
) are distinct negative integers,
c_{n}
is a natural number, and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=0,\quad\text{with }1>\varepsilon>0,

so the center of gravity is at
\left(\frac{(n-1)\varepsilon}{n},0\right)
.

Consider an execution
\varepsilon
where the scheduler activates
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{k}
at time
t_{0}
, with
1\leq k\leq n-1
. In this execution, in scenario A, each
r_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq k
) moves to

(f(x_{i},g_{x}),f(y_{i},g_{y}))=(f(c_{i},0),f(0,0))=(d(i)\cdot c_{i},0),

possibly changing color. The new configuration at time
t_{0}+1
is

r_{i}=(d(i)\cdot c_{i},0)\quad(1\leq i\leq k),\quad r_{i}=(c_{i},0)\quad(k+1\leq i\leq n-1),\quad r_{n}=(c_{n},0).

Observe that the same configuration would result from the same execution in scenario B, where the center of gravity is
\left(\frac{(n-1)\varepsilon}{n},0\right)
. Each
r_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq k
) moves to

\left(f\left(c_{i}+\varepsilon,\frac{(n-1)\varepsilon}{n}\right),f(0,0)\right)=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)\varepsilon}{n}+d(i)\cdot\left(c_{i}+\left(1-\frac{n-1}{n}\right)\varepsilon\right)\right\rfloor,0\right),

assuming
\frac{(n-1)\varepsilon}{n}<1
.

Thus, the new configuration at time
t_{0}+1
is:

r_{i}=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)\varepsilon}{n}+d(i)\cdot\left(c_{i}+\left(1-\frac{n-1}{n}\right)\varepsilon\right)\right\rfloor,0\right)\quad(1\leq i\leq k),

r_{i}=(c_{i}+\varepsilon,0)\quad(k+1\leq i\leq n-1),\quad r_{n}=(c_{n},0).

At time
t_{0}+1
, let the scheduler activate
R_{t_{0}+1}
(possibly all of
R
), and let
r\in R
. Robot
r_{n}
may be able to determine that
r_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq k
) have reached their destinations by observing their colors; i.e., it may know their positions were computed using the target function
f
. However, by lemma
5
, the inverse
f^{-1}
corresponds to an infinite set of possible origins, which includes both
(c_{i},0)
and
(c_{i}+\varepsilon,0)
for
1\leq i\leq k
.

In scenario A, the correct destination for
r_{n}
would be:

(f(c_{n},0),f(0,0))=(d(i)\cdot c_{n},0).

In scenario B, to solve the problem,
r_{n}
should instead move to:

\left(f\left(c_{n}+\varepsilon,\frac{(n-1)\varepsilon}{n}\right),f(0,0)\right)=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)\varepsilon}{n}+d(i)\cdot\left(c_{n}+\left(1-\frac{n-1}{n}\right)\varepsilon\right)\right\rfloor,0\right).

Since robot
r_{n}
observes the same configuration at time
t_{0}+1
in both scenarios, it cannot distinguish between them. This leads to a contradiction.

Lemma 4

\textit{ETE}\notin{\mathcal{FST\!A}}^{F}
.

Proof

To reach a contradiction, assume that there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that solves the
ETE
problem. The robots following this algorithm
\mathcal{A}
must form the sequence of configuration patterns
C_{0},C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
. Assuming
Variable Disorientation
, the adversary can force the observed distances in the snapshot taken during each robot’s
{\mathit{Look}}
operation to always appear as 1. In this case, there will exist configurations
C_{i}=C_{j}
for some
i\neq j
. Thus, there will be at least two different ways to proceed to the next configuration, but the robots have no means to distinguish between them. Therefore, a number of colors equal to the number of configuration patterns would be required. However, to distinguish other robots and recognize
d(i)
, a logarithmic amount of memory is necessary. There is no way to make a decision using only a constant amount of memory. Since robots can only use a constant number of colors, no robot
r
can recognize
d(i)
using only its own light. As a result, the robots cannot move to the positions
(f(x,g_{x}),f(y,g_{y}))
, defined by
f(a,b)=\left\lfloor b+d(i)\cdot(a-b)\right\rfloor
, and cannot form the configuration patterns
C_{0},C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
. This contradicts our initial assumption.

Lemma 5

For a set of points
P=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
there exist infinite sets of points
Q_{i}\quad(1\leq i\leq n)
such that
(x_{i},y_{i})\in Q_{i}\quad(1\leq i\leq n)
and
\forall(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})\in Q_{i}\quad
:
f(x_{i},g_{x})=f(x^{\prime},g_{x}^{\prime})
, and
\quad f(y_{i},g_{y})=f(y^{\prime},g_{y}^{\prime})
, where
(c_{x}^{\prime},c_{y}^{\prime})
is the coordinates of the ETE of
\{(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
.

Proof

Given
(x_{i},y_{i})\in P

A_{i}=f(x_{i},g_{x})=\lfloor g_{x}+d(i)\cdot(x_{i}-g_{x})\rfloor

and

B_{i}=f(y_{i},g_{y})=\lfloor g_{y}+d(i)\cdot(y_{i}-g_{y})\rfloor

that is :

d(i)\cdot(x_{i}-g_{x})=A_{i}+1-\delta\quad(0<\delta\leq 1)

d(i)\cdot(y_{i}-g_{y})=B_{i}+1-\delta^{\prime}\quad(0<\delta^{\prime}\leq 1)

Consider the points
(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})\quad(1\leq i\leq n)
with
x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}+\varepsilon\quad(0<\varepsilon<\delta)
and,
y_{i}^{\prime}=y_{i}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\quad(0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\delta^{\prime})
. We now show that
f(x_{i},g_{x})=f(x^{\prime},g_{x}^{\prime})
and,
f(y_{i},g_{y})=f(y^{\prime},g_{y}^{\prime})
.

Let us first compute
g_{x}^{\prime}
:

g_{x}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(g_{x}+\varepsilon)=g_{x}+\varepsilon

Then

f(x_{i}^{\prime},g_{x}^{\prime})=\left\lfloor(g_{x}+\varepsilon)+d(i)\cdot\big((x_{i}+\varepsilon)-(g_{x}+\varepsilon)\big)\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor d(i)\cdot(x_{i}-g_{x})+(g_{x}+\varepsilon)\right\rfloor

Since, by definition,
d(i)\cdot(x_{i}-g_{x})+g_{x}=A_{i}+1-\delta
and
\varepsilon<\delta
,

d(i)\cdot(x_{i}-g_{x})+(g_{x}+\varepsilon)<A_{i}+1
