Predictive modeling of pest spread in tea plants using an intelligent computational approach
Authors: Hamad Jan, Muhammad Sulaiman, Muhammad Fawad Khan, Ghaylen Laouini, Izaz Ur Rahman, Mohammed Abdullah Salman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTea is a popular beverage prepared from the leaves of the Camellia sinensis plant, embraced by a good share of the world’s population. However, pests and predators threaten its production, which must be controlled without adversely affecting the natural resources. This research presents a mathematical framework for a predatory tri-trophic system that captures the growth of tea plants, pests, and predators. Bayesian Regularization Backpropagation Neural Network (BR-BNN), an intelligent computational approach, is used to derive the output solutions of the presented mathematical model. The Fourth Order Runge–Kutta Method (RKM-4) is utilized to find the target solutions, and different scenarios and parameter variations are employed to determine the model’s solutions and assess the stability of BR-BNN. Furthermore, the comparison graphs of BR-BNN solutions with the target solutions also exhibit how well the BR-BNN performs with a minimal percentage of error in all the results. The findings can also help address challenges related to increasing yield, protecting the environment, and increasing the sustainability of tea production.Keywords: Tri-trophic model, Tea plants, Mathematical modeling, Machine learning, Optimization technique, Bayesian regularization backpropagation neural networkSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Ecology, Ecology, Mathematics and computing, Plant sciences

Introduction

The Camellia sinensis shrub or tree belongs to the family Theaceae. The harvested leaves are used to produce tea. This alternate-branching plant has elliptical, matte-green leaves with a serrated border and a leathery texture. Due to pruning and maintenance, tea plants never grow to their full capacity as bowl-shaped trees. Tea plants cultivated for leaf production rarely produce flowers. When allowed to blossom, tea plants produce fragrant white flowers in single or clusters. Tea trees may grow up to 15 m (49 ft) tall and can live for 30-50 years in natural conditions1. With increasing awareness of the environmental impacts of pesticides, the usage of pesticides in the tea industry is declining. Beneficial insects, known as predators, eat dangerous mites and insects that harm the production of tea. In 1610, tea was introduced to Europe and immediately gained popularity.

Tea is one of the most widely consumed beverages worldwide. It is the second most consumed beverage worldwide, just behind water, and many people consider it more than just a beverage. The three Asian countries of China, Sri Lanka, and India produce most of the world’s tea2. Camellia sinensis is the only plant of tea capable of producing all types of tea. The province of Assam is the source of over 52% of the tea produced in the country3. Tea is cultivated in a variety of soil types and climates in tropical and subtropical regions, with annual rainfall ranging from 938 to 6000 mm, relative humidity from 30% to 90%, and temperatures between 12 °C to 40 °C4.

Since tea farmers harvest the crops between June and September, when the insect population is at its highest, the plants are particularly susceptible to pests at this time (see Fig. 1). Pest populations grow between June and September, when tea planters cut their crops, making them more sensitive to pests. Pests play a significant role in crop damage by reducing plant growth, causing structural damage, and decreasing overall yield5. Insects consume a toxic substance from their saliva when sucking sap from leaves, buds, and terminal branches. Young, healthy leaves may twist and curl over time due to pest feeding. Under extreme conditions, plants may die starting from the tips of roots or leaves. Female insects lay 500-600 eggs per female in sensitive plant tissues6. Many methods may be employed to measure crop loss7,8. The economic stability of many nations depends significantly on their tea production. Arthropod pests, such as insects and mites, can cause anywhere from 5% to 55% of the impact. Numerous techniques can be used to manage pests. Most of these components and tactics come from or are used in conjunction with nature. Predators, parasites, geography, and other natural controls are all important. Various strategies have been developed to control pest populations, including mechanical methods (traps, barriers), biological methods (use of natural enemies), physical methods (e.g., heat or light treatments), and cultural approaches (crop rotation, sanitation). To keep pests under control during the early stages of tea growing, synthetic pesticides and acaricides were often utilized. When applied usually and in large amounts, chemical pesticides harm the environment and human health9. Natural enemy extinction, death of domestic animals and poisoning, harm to neighboring crops, pesticide resistance developing, groundwater contamination, pollination and honeybee decline, and extinction of fish and birds are some detrimental effects of pesticides. Toxic pesticides produce residual problems, disturb the natural enemy complex, and kill microorganisms, which in turn causes pests to develop resistance to the pesticides and serious health problems, since the products are formed by chemical change10. Pesticides are not always effective; thus, alternative methods of pest management should be examined11,12. Biological control is considered one of the most effective and environmentally sustainable pest management strategies, which shields plants from harmful organisms. Biological control utilizes natural enemies to suppress pest populations, reducing reliance on chemical pesticides. The utilization of natural enemies to control pests is referred to as “Biological Control”13. Biological control is the most eco-friendly and helpful strategy for preserving species diversity and biodiversity in agroecosystems. The attacks of a natural enemy on plant-damaging pests preserve ecosystem stability and pest control14.

Damages and pests in tea plants.

In nature, many species destroy pests such as parasitoids, diseases, and predators. Parasitoids, predators, and pathogens are examples of natural enemies of pests15. Biological control methods offer several benefits over chemical control. These are (i) The long-term impact of natural enemies on pest management and (ii) The prevention of insect resistance development uses biological control methods. Pesticides attack all pests, whether they are helpful or harmful to people. Natural enemies are a valuable tool for controlling some pests since they only attack and destroy their target pests. The fact that biological control is extensively employed is a positive and useful outcome of a significant study. Researchers have developed methods to cultivate and release natural enemies in tea plantations as part of biological control strategies, while also conducting surveys to identify local natural enemies.

Recent advancements in neural network-based computational methods have significantly improved the analysis of complex nonlinear and dynamic systems16–18. Fan et al.19 suggested coevolutionary neural dynamics with multiple approaches to solving nonconvex optimization problems, and showed superior convergence and strength. Liu et al.20 suggested coevolutionary neural dynamics with multiple approaches to solving nonconvex optimization problems, and showed superior convergence and strength. Liu et al.21 developed a momentum recurrent neural network framework to address the sparse motion planning problem, and their framework is focused on generating efficient optimization via the advanced neural architectures. Also, Jin et al.22 introduced a gradient-based differential neural strategy of time-dependent nonlinear optimization, which renders precise and steady reactions of dynamic systems. Such experiments illustrate the efficiency of intelligent computational algorithms, but these have been made little use of in biological dynamic systems, especially in pest modeling of tea fields23–25.

Motivation

Tea is one of the most popular drinks in the world, and its sustainable production has a great economic and ecological value. Control of pests is also another key issue that can have a great influence on lowering yield and quality in tea production. The customary approaches of pest management typically involve the use of chemical pesticides that may result in environmental destruction, the generation of resistance in the pest, and have an impact on species other than the intended target. It is, therefore, highly motivated to develop predictive, environmentally friendly, and intelligent online decision support systems that can predict the behaviors of the pests and help in coming up with sustainable pest management methods.

Mathematical modeling provides an effective approach to the dynamics of complex interactions among plants, pests, and their natural predators in a tri-trophic tea ecosystem26–28. However, classical numerical techniques may be limited by their computational efficiency, their sensitivity to parameter variations, and their applicability only to complex nonlinear processes. On the other hand, intelligent computing algorithms29, in particular, machine learning-based solutions, offer desirable behavior in generalization and noise-tolerant solutions. This leads to the joint application of mathematical modeling and intelligent learning algorithms to enhance the accuracy and stability in prediction in the construction of the spread dynamics of pests in tea plantations.

Novelty of the study

The novelty of the study is the implementation of a tri-trophic mathematical model of tea plants, pests, and predators alongside an intelligent BR-BNN to obtain the right and steady numeric solutions. This study applies the RKM-4 to produce credible target solutions and the BR-BNN to learn and recreate the system dynamics with high precision, unlike traditional studies that rely solely on classical numerical solvers or purely data-driven machine learning. The Bayesian regularization (BR) process boosts the resistance of the neural network to variations in parameters and scenarios that the neural network uses to generalize. The near correspondence of the BR-BNN solutions and the RKM-4 results, as shown by comparison plots and low error measures, makes it clear that the proposed intelligent computational framework is effective. In comparison to the current literature where most researchers are involved with generic ecological model, or general neural network models, the work specifically applies a BR-based neural network to a tea tri-trophic ecosystem. The stability and robustness when parameters vary is a focus of the proposed framework, and a difference between the traditional hybrid modeling methods.

Methodology

Artificial neural networks

Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are a type of machine learning (ML) algorithm that is inspired by the composition and operations of the human brain. Artificial neurons, sometimes called layers of linked nodes, make up ANNs38. Each neuron in the network utilizes a mathematical function to generate an output after gathering data from other neurons. After that, subsequent neurons take the output of one neuron as input until the output of the last layer is produced. Each neuron employs a mathematical function known as the activation function, which can be selected depending on the specific issue being addressed39. Data set

The field of artificial intelligence, known as machine learning, involves building algorithms and models that can recognize patterns and make judgments or predictions without the need for direct or explicit programming40. Otherwise, ML makes it possible for computers to gain information from data and enhance their efficiency by repeatedly modifying their settings in response to input. The numerical solver RKM-4, combined with the ND-solve function in Mathematica, is utilized in this work to generate the dataset for the supervised learning of the ML algorithm. For every value of time t in the range [0 200], a data collection of 1001 points is generated. The generated set is fed into the MATLAB neural network model produced with nftool.

Conclusion

This work analyzes a tri-trophic mathematical model to gain insight into the complicated relationships among tea plants, pests, and natural predators. The RKM-4 approach was used to come up with credible target solutions that well represent the dynamic behavior of the system in different situations and parameter changes.

This work was founded on BR-BNN, which was very competent in learning and replicating the complicated dynamics of the model. The accuracy and stability of the presented intelligent approach to computation are high, as the correspondence of the BR-BNN output solutions to the RKM-4 target solutions is close.

In general, the work is significant due to its great and accurate predictive value of tea pest control. The findings not only inform the ecologists and agricultural planners practically but also give a conceptual framework of how the integrated pest management would be carried out. This work opens the way to increasing the yield of tea, decreasing the use of harmful chemical fertilizers, and ensuring environmental sustainability by allowing one to predict and manage pest populations more effectively. In addition, a successful integration of both numerical and machine learning approaches provides an acceptable foundation to add to future investigations into complicated modeling of biological systems.

In the future, the performance, accuracy, and computational efficiency of the suggested BR-BNN model will be further evaluated by contrasting it with other deep learning and machine learning models, including CNN, LSTM, and regression-based models.

Limitations of the research

The proposed model assumes constant parameters and does not incorporate environmental factors such as climate variability, soil conditions, or seasonal changes. Additionally, spatial dynamics and pest migration are not considered. Future extensions of this work will aim to incorporate these factors to better represent real-world agricultural ecosystems.
