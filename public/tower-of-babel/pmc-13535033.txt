Thyrotoxic Periodic Paralysis Following a 75-g Oral Glucose Load Revealing Previously Undiagnosed Graves’ Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Amogh Verma, Sourav Sudan, Mudit Singla, Tarun Kumar Suvvari, Tweheyo Ronald, Shubham Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThyrotoxic periodic paralysis (TPP) is an uncommon endocrine emergency caused by rapid intracellular potassium redistribution during thyrotoxicosis. Carbohydrate exposure is a recognized precipitant, but direct observation of a first paralytic attack developing during supervised diagnostic glucose testing is unusual.Case PresentationA 29-year-old Indian man underwent a supervised oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) for borderline fasting dysglycemia and episodic postprandial tremulousness. Introduction

Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis (TPP) is an acquired form of hypokalemic periodic paralysis in which thyrotoxicosis creates a reversible state of skeletal-muscle membrane instability. 1 The disorder is uncommon even among patients with excess thyroid hormone, yet its demographic distribution is striking: young men are disproportionately affected despite the greater overall prevalence of Graves’ disease among women. 2 Contemporary cohorts and reports from Asian populations continue to demonstrate a strong male predominance, frequent previously unrecognized Graves’ disease, and preferential involvement of proximal and lower-limb musculature.3,4

The biochemical disturbance in TPP differs fundamentally from potassium-depletion states. Total-body potassium is generally not reduced in proportion to the severity of the measured hypokalemia. Instead, extracellular potassium is rapidly sequestered within skeletal muscle. 5 Thyroid hormone increases tissue responsiveness to catecholamines and promotes sodium-potassium adenosine triphosphatase (Na+/K+-ATPase) activity. 6 Insulin provides an additional stimulus for cellular potassium uptake, explaining the long-recognized relationship between attacks and carbohydrate-rich meals. 7

This relationship is nevertheless neither universal nor sufficient by itself. In a prospective series of 135 patients with TPP, identifiable precipitants were present in only a minority, and deliberate glucose loading reproduced hypokalemia with paralysis in only a subset of susceptible patients. 8 These observations imply that the magnitude of insulin release alone cannot explain the phenotype. Reduced outward potassium conductance and susceptibility involving skeletal-muscle inwardly rectifying potassium channels provide a complementary mechanism by which increased potassium influx can become electrophysiologically destabilizing.9,10

A standard 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) is routinely used in India and internationally to clarify dysglycemia. 11 The investigation is generally considered metabolically safe, and serum electrolytes are not routinely monitored during testing.12,13 In a patient with clinically unrecognized TPP susceptibility, however, a standardized glucose load could theoretically create the same insulin-mediated conditions associated with a spontaneous carbohydrate-triggered attack.14,15 Unlike retrospective attribution of paralysis to a preceding meal, development of weakness during supervised testing offers an opportunity to document the temporal relationship among glucose exposure, endogenous insulin release, electrolyte redistribution, and neuromuscular failure. 16

Following the CARE guidelines for case reporting, 17 we describe a young Indian man in whom a first recognized episode of acute hypokalemic paralysis developed during a clinically ordered 75-g OGTT and led to the diagnosis of previously unrecognized Graves’ disease.

Case Presentation

A 29-year-old man from northern India was referred for borderline fasting hyperglycemia detected during occupational screening. During the preceding seven weeks, he reported weight loss despite preserved appetite, palpitations, sweating, heat intolerance, hand tremor, and intermittent postprandial shakiness, without previous muscle weakness. There was no personal or family history of periodic paralysis or thyroid disease, gastrointestinal potassium loss, or relevant medication use.

A previously scheduled 75-g oral glucose tolerance test with extended serial glucose and insulin sampling was undertaken for evaluation of fasting dysglycemia and recurrent postprandial symptoms. At baseline, he was ambulatory without weakness, hemodynamically stable, and had no known thyroid disease or previous paralytic episodes. Because the accompanying hyperadrenergic symptoms raised the possibility of thyroid dysfunction, thyroid-function tests were obtained concurrently; results were not available at the time of glucose administration. Baseline serum potassium was 4.1 mmol/L, and pre-test electrocardiography demonstrated sinus tachycardia without significant repolarization abnormality or arrhythmia.

During post-load observation, bilateral thigh heaviness progressed to difficulty standing and shoulder-girdle weakness, prompting clinical evaluation. Examination demonstrated symmetrical flaccid quadriparesis with predominant proximal lower-limb involvement and reduced lower-limb reflexes, while sensation, cranial nerve function, swallowing, and respiration remained preserved. Serial testing demonstrated progressive hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia, and a marked insulin response (Table 1). At peak weakness, electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia, flattened T waves, and prominent U waves without sustained arrhythmia. The biochemical and urinary evaluation supported transcellular potassium redistribution, while thyroid testing and ultrasonography established Graves’ disease (Table 2).

Diagnostic Evaluation of Acute Paralysis and Thyrotoxicosis

The patient was transferred to a monitored acute-care area. Dextrose-containing fluids were avoided, and cautious potassium replacement, intravenous magnesium sulfate, and oral propranolol were administered. Motor power recovered completely within 12 hours without rebound hyperkalemia (Table 3). Carbimazole 15 mg twice daily was started approximately 24 hours after the acute episode, on hospital day 2, after baseline complete blood count and liver-function tests were confirmed to be normal.

Discussion

The distinctive feature of this case is the occurrence of a first recognized episode of TPP during a supervised 75-g OGTT, with normal serum potassium documented before glucose ingestion. Although carbohydrate-rich meals are established precipitants of TPP,14,27 glucose loading reproduces paralysis in only a subset of susceptible patients. 8 Here, serial sampling demonstrated a temporal sequence from standardized glucose exposure to a marked insulin response, progressive hypokalemia and hypophosphatemia, and neuromuscular weakness, providing detailed temporal documentation of the metabolic evolution of the attack.

TPP results primarily from rapid intracellular potassium redistribution rather than total-body potassium depletion. 28 Thyroid hormone increases adrenergic responsiveness, while catecholamines and insulin stimulate skeletal-muscle Na+/K+-ATPase activity and promote intracellular potassium uptake.29,30 Reduced outward potassium conductance, including susceptibility associated with Kir2.6 variants, may further predispose affected individuals.31-33 Thus, the insulin response observed during the OGTT should be regarded as a precipitating factor acting on thyrotoxic skeletal-muscle susceptibility rather than an independently sufficient cause.

Normal potassium between attacks does not exclude TPP. 5 In this patient, low urinary potassium excretion and normal acid-base status supported transcellular redistribution and argued against renal or gastrointestinal potassium loss.34,35 The absence of previous or familial attacks and the presence of Graves’ thyrotoxicosis also favored an acquired rather than familial periodic paralysis. 36 Recognition of redistribution is therapeutically important because excessive potassium administration may produce rebound hyperkalemia as the intracellular shift reverses. 37 Cautious potassium replacement and propranolol resulted in complete recovery without rebound hyperkalemia; β-adrenergic blockade can counter the adrenergically mediated intracellular potassium shift in TPP. 14

This case also highlights a potential diagnostic pitfall when thyrotoxic symptoms coexist with apparent dysglycemia. Thyrotoxicosis itself can alter glucose metabolism. 38 Although routine thyroid screening before every OGTT is not warranted, unexplained weight loss, tachycardia, tremor, heat intolerance, or goiter should prompt consideration of thyrotoxicosis before substantial carbohydrate loading.39,40 Deliberate glucose provocation should not be used to diagnose suspected TPP. Prevention ultimately depends on control of thyrotoxicosis, 5 and this patient remained normokalemic without recurrent paralysis during 24 weeks of treatment.

This report has limitations. As a single case, it cannot establish the frequency or predictive value of OGTT-associated TPP. Early electrolyte measurements were obtained retrospectively from stored specimens and should therefore be interpreted with consideration of potential preanalytical effects. Neither insulin-mediated causality nor skeletal-muscle channel susceptibility was directly assessed.

Conclusion

A first recognized episode of TPP developed during a supervised 75-g OGTT in a patient with previously unrecognized Graves’ disease, with documented transition from normal baseline potassium to severe redistribution hypokalemia. TPP should be considered when acute flaccid weakness follows carbohydrate exposure, even when serum potassium was recently normal. Symptoms suggestive of thyrotoxicosis should prompt thyroid evaluation before glucose provocation, while acute management should emphasize cardiac monitoring, cautious potassium replacement, adrenergic blockade when appropriate, and definitive control of thyrotoxicosis.
