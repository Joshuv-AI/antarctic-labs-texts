Classroom air purifiers for reducing school absence: study protocol for a pragmatic, cluster-randomised, parallel, two-arm, group sequential superiority trial
Authors: Runar B Solberg, Sverre B Holøs, Ingeborg Hess Elgersma, Petter Elstrøm, Christopher James Rose, Arnfinn Helleve, Unni Gopinathan, Atle Fretheim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRespiratory infections like influenza and SARS-CoV-2 pose significant global health risks due to their high transmissibility and severity. SARS-CoV-2 has caused over 7 million deaths worldwide, and the Lancet Commission estimates a >20% chance of a similar respiratory virus pandemic within a decade. Schools, often poorly ventilated, are high-risk settings for transmission. While COVID-19 school closures may have reduced transmission, they likely caused learning loss, mental health issues and increased burdens on parents and caregivers. Air purifiers with High Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filters may offer a non-disruptive mitigation strategy, but the evidence to support their effectiveness in reducing viral transmission is weak. This protocol describes a cluster-randomised, parallel, two-arm, group sequential superiority trial with an interim analysis—to allow early stopping for efficacy or futility—to estimate the effect of portable air purifiers with HEPA filters in primary school classrooms on student absenteeism.Methods and analysisThis group sequential trial will randomise schools (clusters) 1:1 to intervention or control arms in two stages: winter 2025/2026 (N=32 schools; ~736 students) and winter 2026/2027 (N=30 schools; ~690 students). The study setting is Norway. Eligible schools must have classrooms suitable for portable air purifiers, >10 students in grades 5–7 (typically aged 10–13 years) and principal consent. Intervention and control classrooms will each receive two portable air purifiers with HEPA filters operating at a performance equivalent to 3.0 and 0.3 air changes per hour, respectively, with control purifiers acting as shams. Outcomes will be measured during and at the end of a 12-week period. The primary outcome is student absenteeism, measured as full child days of absence aggregated at the class level. An interim analysis is planned at the end of the first stage, with error-spending O’Brien-Fleming stopping boundaries that are binding for efficacy and non-binding for futility. The primary estimand is the marginal incidence rate ratio of student absences, estimated using generalised estimating equations with a negative binomial model to account for overdispersion. Prespecified stopping boundaries will determine stopping, with efficacy boundaries being binding. Treatment effects will be estimated using cluster-bootstrapped CIs adjusted to provide strong control on overall type I and II error probabilities, and a bias correction will be applied if the trial is stopped early for efficacy. All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle. The primary question is whether installing and operating air purifiers with HEPA filters (intervention) reduces student absenteeism due to sickness (primary outcome) compared with sham air purifiers (control). Secondary questions examine whether the intervention reduces teacher absenteeism due to respiratory infections, rate and 12-week risk of self-reported respiratory infections among teachers, and teachers’ perceptions of air quality, compared with sham air purifiers. If the trial estimates a statistically significant effect for the primary outcome, a cost-consequence analysis will evaluate the direct and indirect costs associated with operating air purifiers against the potential benefits of reduced student and teacher absenteeism. A process evaluation will explore mechanisms of effect.Ethics and disseminationThe trial has been approved by the Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics and the National Research Ethics Committee (ref. 737650). Absence data, routinely collected and fully anonymised at the class level, will serve as the primary outcome and does not require informed consent. Informed consent will be obtained from teachers participating in weekly surveys or interviews. Results will be disseminated to stakeholders, participants and the public through peer-reviewed journals, scientific meetings and social media.Trial registration numberNCT07119333.

Introduction

Respiratory infections, such as influenza and SARS-CoV-2, pose a significant global health threat due to their high transmissibility and potential severity. SARS-CoV-2, the virus responsible for the COVID-19 pandemic, primarily spreads through droplets and aerosols.1 As of April 2025, it has caused around 7 million deaths globally and significantly disrupted daily life before vaccines became available. A recent Lancet Commission estimated a >20% likelihood of another respiratory pandemic caused by viruses in either the influenza or coronavirus families within the next decade, with possible mortality rates comparable to COVID-19.2 This underscores the urgent need for effective public health and social measures (PHSM) to control airborne transmission and mitigate the impact of future outbreaks.

One of the earliest PHSM introduced to protect people from COVID-19 was school closures, which by April 2020, had been implemented in over 180 countries.3 Schools are high-risk settings for respiratory infections due to high occupancy density and prolonged student presence in often poorly ventilated rooms.46 While school closures may have reduced transmission, they may also have caused learning loss, mental health issues and increased burdens on parents and caregivers, including work disruptions.79 Strategies that keep schools open while minimising transmission risk are therefore important.

School absenteeism is a key outcome for evaluating the burden of respiratory infections and the effects of PHSM in a school setting: it is well-defined, routinely measured by schools and reflects both the direct impact of infections and the broader societal and economic disruptions they cause. While respiratory infection incidence might appear to be a more direct measure of transmission, it is difficult to measure in children and does not provide direct information about educational, societal and economic disruptions.

Studies suggest that air purifiers with High Efficiency Particulate air (HEPA) filters can reduce airborne particles, including those carrying SARS-CoV-2 in classroom and other indoor settings.1015 Modelling studies support their potential for lowering infection risk.16 17 However, evidence on their effectiveness for reducing infection transmission and absenteeism in schools is limited. Hence, randomised trials are needed to better estimate the effects of air purifiers on disease transmission and absenteeism in schools.18

To date, only one randomised trial, the class-Air Cleaning Technologies (ACT) trial in the UK (ISRCTN46750688), has been conducted, though its results have yet to be published. Additional trials have recently commenced in Massachusetts (NCT05953233) and Italy (AEARCTR-0011960), but further randomised trials are essential to establish a robust evidence base.

We conducted a pilot study to assess the feasibility and acceptability of deploying portable air purifiers in classrooms (submitted for publication), which confirmed that such a trial would be practical and provided preliminary evidence that the devices reduce indoor particulate matter (PM). Building on these results, we are initiating a cluster-randomised, parallel, two-arm, group-sequential superiority trial to estimate the effect of classroom air purifiers on absenteeism among students and teachers.

Research questions

The primary question is whether installing and operating air purifiers with HEPA filters (intervention) reduce student absenteeism (primary outcome) compared with sham air purifiers (control). Secondary questions examine whether the intervention reduces teacher absenteeism due to respiratory infections, rate and 12-week risk of self-reported respiratory infections among teachers and teachers’ perceived air quality compared with sham air purifiers. Methods and analysis

Trial design and setting

We developed this protocol following the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidance19 (online supplemental file 2). The trial is designed as a cluster-randomised, parallel, two-arm, group sequential superiority trial with one interim analysis that is binding for efficacy and non-binding for futility. The unit of randomisation is school (cluster). Schools will be randomised 1:1 to intervention or control arms. This trial will be conducted by the Centre for Epidemic Interventions Research, at the Norwegian Institute of Public Health (NIPH) and the trial setting is Norway.

Trial populations and eligibility criteria

There are two general trial populations. The first population is primary school children in Norway. Primary school children in primary schools meeting the following criteria will be eligible:

Schools with more than 10 students in either fifth, sixth or seventh grade (typically aged 10–13 years).

Schools with at least one classroom of sufficient dimensions to accommodate the installation of two portable air purifiers.

The school principal accepts the installation of air purifiers in at least one classroom in the fifth, sixth or seventh grade that meets the first two inclusion criteria.

For logistical and administrative reasons, we will try to limit the sampling frame to schools in municipalities located close to our offices in Oslo. These municipalities include several large towns with populations of around 30 000–100 000 000 people, as well as sparsely populated rural areas, and are therefore reasonably representative of Norway and many other countries. We will enlarge the sampling frame as necessary.

Intervention and control

The intervention is the installation and operation of two portable air purifiers with HEPA filters (Mill Silent Pro Air Purifier, Mill International AS, 1361 Østerås, Norway) per intervention school classroom. Their placement will be tailored to classroom layout and ventilation characteristics, aiming to maximise air purification. The choice to use portable air purifiers rather than wall-mounted or ceiling-mounted units was based on pilot study findings, which demonstrated that portable air purifiers are easier to instal, well-accepted by teachers and students and provide an effective solution for reducing PM in classrooms. They were considered more practical and potentially superior to ceiling-mounted alternatives. Air purifiers with HEPA filters can remove over 99.5% of particles of sizes that are most difficult to filter from the air current passing the filter complying with class C in NS 8175: 2019 (Lp, A,T≤35 dB). Each air purifier will operate at a level corresponding to 3.0 air changes per hour, providing a combined capacity corresponding to a Clean Air Delivery Rate (CADR) of at least 486 m3/hour.

The control is the installation and operation of two sham air purifiers per control classroom. These will be identical in appearance and placement to the intervention units and will also include an HEPA filter, but they will be configured by a senior researcher (SH) following a detailed protocol provided by the manufacturer to operate at the lowest fan setting permitted by the motor. This setting results in minimal airflow and negligible air-cleaning capacity but may also produce less sound than the intervention units. The sham air purifiers will operate at a performance equivalent to 0.3 air changes per hour, providing a combined CADR of at least 48 m3/hour. We decided to use sham air purifiers rather than no air purifiers for the control group to account for a possible placebo effect. We considered using air purifiers running at the same fan speed as in the intervention arm, but without HEPA filters, but this was deemed unethical due to the potential risk of particle resuspension, which could theoretically increase infection risk. We will instruct the schools to run the intervention and sham air purifiers continuously during school hours over the 12-week trial period.

The treatment ‘installing and operating’ means that we will instal air purifiers and shams, and ‘operate’ them by instructing a contact person at each school (eg, a school janitor) to ensure that they are running as intended, according to a typical maintenance schedule. Here, ‘operating’ does not mean the air purifiers or shams will be operational (running) at all times, nor that we will account for non-operational (out-of-order) periods as intercurrent events. This reflects real-life conditions and supports the external validity of the trial findings. We do not anticipate any systematic differences in the operation of the air purifiers or shams between trial arms.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the number of student-days of absence per classroom, measured each week. We will define a student day of absence as a day for which a student is absent for the entire day. We will not distinguish between the specific students who are absent (ie, 2 days of absence will be counted for one student who is absent on 2 days, and for two students absent for 1 day each).

Secondary outcomes

Self-reported absence due to respiratory infections among teachers

We will measure the number of teacher-days of work absence (sick leave) due to self-reported respiratory infection each week. We will ask teachers, ‘Did you experience symptoms of a cold, COVID-19 or the flu last week?’. Teachers who answer ‘Yes’ to this question will be asked to specify the symptoms they experienced (sneezing, nasal obstruction, nasal discharge, sore throat, cough, headache, fever or general discomfort) and rate each symptom from 0 to 3, where 0=absent, 1=mild, 2=moderate, 3=severe, in accordance with the Jackson index.20 21 They will then report the number of days they experienced these symptoms (ranging from 1 to 7 days), before answering ‘How many days were you absent from work last week due to respiratory infection?’; their response to this question will be used to measure number of days of absence. A count of 0 days will be assigned to teachers answering ‘No’ to the first question.

Incidence of self-reported respiratory symptoms consistent with respiratory infections among teachers

We will assess incidents of respiratory symptoms consistent with respiratory infections among teachers using self-reported data collected weekly. We define an incident as a period during which a teacher reports symptoms for at least two consecutive calendar days, preceded and followed by at least seven symptom-free calendar days. We define symptoms of respiratory infection as meeting all four of the following criteria:

Answering ‘yes’ to the question ‘Did you experience symptoms of a cold, COVID-19 or the flu last week?’.

Answering ‘yes’ to the question ‘Did you experience symptoms of a cold, COVID-19 or the flu last week?’.

Reporting at least one of the following symptoms: nasal obstruction (plugged or congested), nasal discharge (runny nose), sneezing or sore throat, in the last week.

Reporting at least one of the following symptoms: nasal obstruction (plugged or congested), nasal discharge (runny nose), sneezing or sore throat, in the last week.

Scoring at least four points on the Jackson scale, based on symptom severity during the preceding week (where symptoms are rated as absent (0), mild (1), moderate (2) or severe (3)).

Scoring at least four points on the Jackson scale, based on symptom severity during the preceding week (where symptoms are rated as absent (0), mild (1), moderate (2) or severe (3)).

Reporting that they have experienced at least one of the reported symptoms for at least two consecutive days in the last week.

This case definition largely mimics the Wisconsin Upper Respiratory Symptom Survey definition.22

We will not distinguish between the specific days for which teachers experienced symptoms—that is, 2 days of symptoms will be counted as one event both for a teacher who reports symptoms on two consecutive days, and for a teacher who reports symptoms on two non-consecutive days (if there are periods of at least seven symptom-free calendar days before and after the period with symptoms).

Teachers reporting respiratory infections

We will assess respiratory infection as a dichotomous outcome for each teacher, based on whether they meet the case definition at any point during the 12-week follow-up. See above for details and definitions.

Teacher perceptions of air quality in classrooms

We will assess teachers’ perceptions of classroom air quality at the end of follow-up using the MM040 questionnaire:23 24 teachers will report whether they have experienced drafts, high room temperature, low room temperature, fluctuating room temperature, stuffy or ‘bad’ air, dry air, unpleasant odours, noise or dust in the air during follow-up. The response options will be: ‘Yes, often (every week)’, ‘Yes, occasionally’ or ‘No, never’.

We will also assess symptoms experienced by teachers during follow-up using the same response options.

Adverse events

We define an adverse event as any significant disruption to lessons, or any accident or injury, that is attributable to the intervention or control. We will instruct school principals and teachers to report such events.

Cost-consequence analysis

If the trial finds that the intervention is superior to the control, we will report a cost-consequence analysis in a separate paper. The analysis will evaluate both the direct and indirect costs associated with operating air purifiers in school classrooms against the potential benefits of reduced student and teacher absenteeism. Costs will include the purchase, installation, maintenance and energy consumption of air purifiers, while benefits will consider the potential reduction in illness-related absences. This analysis will adopt a societal perspective, incorporating data from school records, public health sources and national statistics to estimate the implications of the intervention. We will publish a separate analysis plan for the cost-consequence analysis.

Interviews and focus groups

We will conduct a process evaluation to explore barriers and facilitators to the implementation of air purifiers in classrooms. This will use qualitative methods, including group and potentially individual interviews, as well as focus groups, with teachers and other school staff. Interviews with teachers will explore their experiences regarding potential barriers and facilitators, the acceptability of air purifiers and their perspectives on having air purifiers in classrooms. To ensure a comprehensive understanding, we also plan to invite staff from schools that were eligible for the trial but chose not to participate, to explore their reasons for declining participation. All interviews will be conducted in Norwegian, recorded and transcribed, except in cases where recording is not feasible.

Adherence

Each participating school will designate a contact person (eg, a school janitor) who will be responsible for ensuring that the air purifiers are operated in accordance with the trial protocol. This individual will monitor and maintain the functionality of the devices according to a maintenance schedule and will be instructed to promptly report any deviations or issues to the research team. The research team may then take appropriate remedial actions, such as replacing a defective device, in a way that is similar to how such remedial actions could be taken if air purifiers were used outside of the trial context (eg, during an epidemic or pandemic). By establishing this role, the trial aims to maintain protocol adherence and address potential disruptions. Additionally, the weekly teacher questionnaire will include the item: ‘During the past week, have you noticed that the air purifiers were not operating according to the trial protocol?’ If respondents answer ‘Yes’, they will be asked to estimate the duration of any disconnection. This measure is intended to monitor protocol adherence and reinforce compliance by reminding teachers to ensure continuous operation of the purifiers.

Cost-consequence analysis

The relevant data for the cost-consequence analysis will be collected through various sources.

Direct costs

Data on the cost of purchasing, installation, maintenance and energy consumption will be obtained directly from the supplier and manufacturer.

Indirect costs

Time spent by the contact person responsible for ensuring that the air purifiers operate in accordance with the trial protocol will be estimated through records. This time will be monetised based on average wage rates.

Contextual data

Air quality measurements

Classrooms in both the intervention and control arms will be equipped with wall-mounted air quality monitors. These devices will collect data at 5 min intervals, measuring the following parameters:

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) concentration: measurement range of 0 ppm to 5000 ppm, with an accuracy of ±40 ppm.

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) concentration: measurement range of 0 ppm to 5000 ppm, with an accuracy of ±40 ppm.

Air temperature and humidity: recorded continuously to provide additional environmental context.

Air temperature and humidity: recorded continuously to provide additional environmental context.

Indoor CO₂ concentrations, which are higher in human exhaled breath compared with ambient air, are commonly used as a proxy for ventilation effectiveness. Data on PM) levels can be used to assess the air-cleaning performance of the air purifiers equipped with HEPA filters.

The air quality measurement period will begin a minimum of 2 weeks before the start of the intervention and will continue throughout the entire intervention period.

Loss to follow-up

Students who change schools due to relocation will be classified as lost to follow-up for outcome measurement, as records from the original enrolment schools will no longer capture their data. However, the change in schools will reduce the total number of observed student-days for exposure, so the analysis will naturally account for the loss to follow-up. Teachers who withdraw from the trial by revoking their consent will also be considered lost to follow-up.

If, despite repeated requests, a school does not make any outcome data available to us, we will assume the school has withdrawn consent and will exclude the school from analysis. We will, however report if any schools are excluded for this reason.

Sample size

We designed this cluster-randomised group sequential trial using Stata’s gsdesign command and the sample size formula published by Li et al,28 which we identified from the scoping review of Zhan et al.29 The trial is designed to detect an incidence rate ratio (IRR) of 0.85 at a two-sided overall alpha of 0.05 and overall power of at least 90% using the following assumptions, which were informed by data from our 6-week pilot study in five schools: 12 weeks of follow-up; 20 school days per month; a mean of 23 students per classroom; a mean of 1.36 classrooms per school (cluster size), with an SD of 0.63; a 5.56% probability of a control student being absent on a given school day; an overdispersion factor of 2; an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.3; a 20% probability of school (cluster) dropout; a 1% probability of student dropout (eg, transfer to another school) one interim analysis at an information fraction of 0.5; and error-spending O’Brien-Fleming-style boundaries for binding efficacy and non-binding futility.30 31 Under these assumptions, we expect a total of 76 and 65 student-days of absence per control and intervention classroom, respectively.

The group sequential design, including the cumulative sample sizes and stopping boundaries, is shown in table 3. We will recruit and randomise a total of 32 schools (N=~736 students) for the first look (interim analysis) during winter 2025/2026 and, if the trial continues to the second look during winter 2026/2027, an additional 30 schools (N=~690 students) will be recruited and randomised for a maximum total of 62 schools if the trial continues after the interim analysis. We also used simulation to verify the trial’s operating characteristics (see the SAP, (online supplemental file 2). Simulation results indicate 85% power—slightly below the nominal 90%—to detect the target effect (IRR=0.85), and strong control over type I error probability. Group sequential design

Recruitment

As the responsibility for primary schools (grades 1–7, students typically aged 10–13 years) in Norway lies with the municipalities, the municipalities will be informed and potentially assist with recruiting schools to the trial.

We will invite primary schools in Norway to participate with classrooms preferably from the seventh grade. However, schools may also participate with fifth or sixth grade classrooms if this is preferred by the principal. The principal will further decide the number of classrooms from the selected grade that will participate in the trial.

Recruitment will follow a group sequential approach. The first wave of recruitment will begin in the summer of 2025, and the first trial stage is planned for the winter months of 2025/2026, defined as January through April. If the trial continues after the interim analysis, the second wave of recruitment will begin in the spring of 2026, with the second stage planned for the winter months of 2026/2027.

Randomisation

The randomisation method is described in full in the SAP. Following consent from school principals, schools (clusters) will be randomised 1:1 to intervention or control by the trial statistician (CJR) who will not be involved in school recruitment, treatment implementation or outcome assessment.

All participating schools will be randomised concurrently using covariate-constrained randomisation at each randomisation point (immediately after the baseline period for winter 2025/2026 and similarly for winter 2026/2027 if the trial is not stopped at the interim analysis). Constraints will include baseline classroom CO₂ concentration, student absence rate, the subtype of mechanical ventilation already used by the schools (mixing vs displacement ventilation), median after-tax household income in the school district (a proxy for socioeconomic status) and the number of students in seventh grade. These covariates were selected to ensure approximately balanced distributions of baseline factors presumed to be prognostic for the primary and secondary outcomes.24 32 33 Randomisation will use a prespecified random number generator seed that is documented in the statistical analysis plan and was chosen prior to school recruitment to prevent interference with randomisation.

Because concurrent rather than sequential randomisation will be used, there is no allocation sequence and there can be no concerns about allocation concealment (that knowledge of previous allocations might influence subsequent allocations).

Blinding/masking

School staff, participating teachers and students will be blinded to treatment allocation via the use of sham air purifiers in the control group. The researchers responsible for installing the air purifiers will also be blinded. To ensure this, all air purifier units will be configured and labelled with serial numbers indicating whether they are sham or functional devices, with one identifier placed in a clearly visible location and another in an inaccessible location. Prior to installing, the visible identifier will be replaced with a label displaying the school and classroom names according to an allocation key, which will be securely stored and accessible only to a single designated researcher (SH). This researcher will configure the air purifiers assigned to the control and intervention arms and will be the sole individual with knowledge of treatment allocation until the point of unblinding.

We will blind the statistician to treatment allocation. When presenting results, the statistician will blind the research group by applying arbitrary labels to trial arms and withholding event counts and exposures, thereby minimising the risk of unblinding. While blinded, the research group will discuss and agree how the interim analysis result for the primary outcome can be interpreted under the two possible treatment allocations. We will publish a short document on Zenodo describing these interpretations. In the event of futility at the interim analysis (table 2), the decision to stop or continue the trial will be made blinded. If the trial continues, the same blinding procedures will be followed at the final analysis to ensure unbiased interpretation of the primary outcome, including blinded review by the research group and public posting of the corresponding interpretations. Further details are provided in the SAP (online supplemental file 2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will follow a prespecified SAP (online supplemental file 1), adhere to the intention-to-treat principle, and will be reported following Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines.34 School, classroom, student and teacher baseline characteristics will be summarised using descriptive statistics. Population-averaged treatment effects will be estimated using generalised estimating equations (GEEs). For count outcomes—including the primary outcome—IRRs will be estimated using negative binomial GEEs with a log link. Exposures will be defined as student-days or teacher-days using class scheduling and absence data. For repeated measures outcomes, we will use a first-order autoregressive working correlation matrix to account for serial correlation between repeated measurements within classrooms and report between-period intraclass correlation coefficients.35 To account for anticipated temporal variation in baseline rates due to changing community infection pressure, models will include indicator variables for trial week.

Goodness of fit and model selection for covariate adjustment will be based on quasi-likelihood information criteria,36 37 selecting from models adjusted for all combinations of the covariates used to constrain randomisation. Effect estimates and z-statistics for group sequential testing will be derived from selected models. If the trial proceeds beyond the interim analysis, we will apply the same strategy to also allow adjustment for season (2025/26 vs 2026/27). We will use cluster-bootstrapping (resampling at the school level) to account for cluster-randomisation when computing two-sided 95% CIs—adjusted for the group sequential design—and p values.

A decision to stop will be made if the z-statistic crosses one of the prespecified group sequential boundaries for efficacy. Interpretation of treatment effect magnitude will be based on point estimates and CIs. If the trial stops early, bias-corrected estimates will be reported.38

Missing data will be assessed using a prespecified strategy to classify the missing mechanism as completely at random, missing at random or missing not at random, with complete case analysis, inverse probability weighting39 or Manski-like bounds40 41 used, respectively. All analyses will be performed using Stata V.19 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) or later.

Interim analysis

First look (interim analysis)

Data will be collected for the first look (interim analysis) over a 3-month post-randomisation period during winter 2025/2026. Analysis will be performed for the primary estimand once data for all estimands are finalised. The stopping boundaries for futility are non-binding to allow us to stop the trial early and redeploy NIPH resources if necessary, or to continue the trial to obtain a more definitive result. If the trial is stopped for efficacy, or a decision is made to stop for futility, the remaining analyses will be performed. If the trial continues to the second look, we will not perform any other analyses at the first look. Irrespective of whether we stop the trial early, we will report and justify the decision to stop or continue at the interim analysis.

Second look

If the trial continues after the interim analysis, data will be collected over a 3-month post-randomisation period during winter 2026/2027. Analysis will be performed for all estimands once all data are finalised.

The choice of these variables is based on the hypothesis that treatment effect may differ by geographical region, school size, building age, ventilation system type and household income, potentially influencing the effectiveness of installing and operating air purifiers in different settings. For example, schools in urban areas may have higher baseline air pollution levels than rural schools, leaving more room for improvement with air purifiers. Similarly, larger schools with more students may experience higher levels of airborne contaminants, potentially leading to a greater treatment effect. Older school buildings may have poorer ventilation and higher pollution accumulation, which could make air purifiers more impactful compared with newer buildings. Lastly, schools with mechanical ventilation may already have better air quality, leaving less room for improvement compared with naturally ventilated classrooms. Children from lower-income households may have higher baseline respiratory vulnerability due to socioeconomic health disparities and greater cumulative exposure to environmental stressors at home and in their neighbourhoods, potentially making them more responsive to school-based air quality improvements.

We will estimate treatment effects in subgroups by restricting the estimation sample by subgroup (factor variables) or including interaction terms (continuous variables) and using the same analysis methods as described above, modifying the model specification as necessary. We will report p values for treatment×subgroup and treatment×covariate interactions via interaction terms.

Participant and public involvement

Our trial has incorporated feedback from teachers and students and insights gathered by the trial team through a pilot study focused on acceptability and feasibility of installing air purifiers in school classrooms. The pilot study allowed us to refine the intervention approach based on teachers’ and students’ input and experiences, ensuring that the trial aligns with their preferences and practical needs.

Ethics and dissemination

This trial has received approval from the REK and the NEM (ref. 737650). Given the nature of the intervention—installing and operating portable air purifiers in schools—it is not feasible to obtain informed consent from each individual teacher, student or legal guardian. Instead, the decision to participate will rest with the school principal, who may accept or decline the installation of air purifiers on behalf of the school.

Absence data, which is routinely collected by schools, will be used as the primary outcome measure. These data will be obtained only at the group level and will be fully anonymised and aggregated, containing no personal identifiers. As such, consent for the use of absence data is not required, as it is anonymised, routinely collected and not gathered specifically for the purposes of this trial.

For teachers providing data via surveys, as well as teachers or other school staff participating in interviews, informed consent will be obtained prior to data collection. All identifiable data from surveys and interviews will be handled in accordance with data protection regulations to ensure participant confidentiality and privacy.

By structuring data collection to rely primarily on anonymised, aggregated absence data and obtaining explicit consent only from individuals contributing sensitive personal information, this trial seeks to minimise privacy risks and uphold ethical standards while effectively addressing the research objectives and facilitating publication of anonymised trial data.

Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, press releases via the NIPH, social media, newspapers, television and scientific conferences, both nationally and internationally. The advisory board of our research centre, which includes representatives from civil society organisations, will be invited to contribute to dissemination efforts. Findings will also be shared with relevant participants and public organisations, and all participating schools will receive a summary of the trial results by email. We will report all protocol amendments, if any, in the final paper under the subheading ‘Protocol amendments’.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of effective infection control measures in schools, as they are high-risk settings for respiratory infections due to prolonged indoor exposure and often inadequate ventilation. While air purifiers with HEPA filters have shown promise in reducing airborne particles that may carry viruses like SARS-CoV-2, real-life evidence from randomised trials of their effectiveness in schools is limited, with only a few randomised trials underway globally.42 Recognising both the potential benefits and practical challenges of using air purifiers in classrooms—such as costs, maintenance and acceptability—we conducted a pilot study to assess feasibility and gather preliminary data. The pilot demonstrated that air purifiers were well-accepted by teachers and students and may be effective in reducing PM in classrooms. Building on these findings, our larger randomised trial aims to determine whether air purifiers with HEPA filters can effectively reduce absenteeism and respiratory infection risk in schools, providing evidence to guide future public health policies in educational settings.

While primary, secondary and high schools were considered as potential locations for this trial, primary schools were selected as the most suitable environment for evaluating the effect of air purifiers. Secondary and high school students typically move between multiple classrooms throughout the day, meaning they would spend a significant portion of their school hours in spaces without air purifiers, thereby reducing the potential impact of the intervention. Additionally, older students are more likely to engage in larger social gatherings outside of school, which could increase the risk of transmission through other exposures. Focusing on primary schools minimises these external influences, making it easier to isolate and demonstrate any effect of air purifiers. For clarity, in this study, ‘primary schools’ refer to grades 1–7 (ages 6–13), and ‘secondary schools’ refer to grades 8–10 (ages 13–16), consistent with the Norwegian school system.

Within primary schools, we chose to focus on fifth to seventh graders rather than younger students in first to fourth grade. Younger students in Norway frequently attend after-school programmes (‘Aktivitetsskole’ or ‘Skolefritidsordning’) where they spend time in different rooms outside their regular classrooms. It is economically and practically infeasible to instal air purifiers in all these additional spaces, precluding the inclusion of younger students. Furthermore, we anticipate that schools may be more diligent in recording absences for older students, especially in the final year of primary education.

We chose to use portable air purifiers based on insights from our pilot study, as well as considerations regarding cost and ease of implementation. Portable units are more practical for temporary infection control measures, such as during a pandemic or seasonal respiratory outbreaks, because they are affordable, easily installed and relocated and do not require modification to school buildings or classroom infrastructure. Although ceiling-mounted units offer benefits such as saving floor space and potentially providing more even air distribution, they require professional installation and integration with existing systems, making them more costly and complex to implement. While portable air purifiers occupy floor space and may be more accessible to students, which could lead to adverse events, our pilot study indicated that student interference was minimal.

During the pilot study, we explored the effectiveness, feasibility and acceptability of both ceiling-mounted and portable air purifiers. Preliminary findings suggest that there was no substantial difference in PM 2.5 reduction between the two types, making portable units a cost-effective choice. Additionally, the flexibility of portable purifiers aligns well with the need for adaptable infection control solutions during periods of increased respiratory disease transmission.

A key limitation is the lack of long-term follow-up evaluations, precluding evaluation of long-term effects. Another limitation is the lack of objective outcome or based on immunological biomarkers. For example, an outcome based on a PCR test result would have provided more specific information about infections. We will explore the feasibility of measuring airborne viruses. Briefly, measurements will be performed in a standardised way at the participating schools and be analysed in relation to venue properties and recommended threshold values for viruses. If feasible, details will be described in a separate protocol amendment.

This trial has the potential to shape future policies and guidelines for respiratory infection prevention, promoting the well-being and safety of students, teachers and the broader school community.
