Saphenous Vein Graft Kinking Secondary to Left Atrial Appendage Clip Causing Type 5 Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Rashid Alhusain, Mohamed Ibrahim, Artur Schneider, Abdallah El Sabbagh, John Haney, Riyaz Bashir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft atrial appendage (LAA) clip placement is increasingly used for stroke prophylaxis in atrial fibrillation during cardiac surgery. Rare mechanical interactions with adjacent grafts may cause graft failure and type 5 myocardial infarction.Case SummaryA 75-year-old man underwent coronary artery bypass grafting with left internal mammary artery to left anterior descending artery and saphenous vein grafts to posterior descending artery and second obtuse marginal branch, with LAA clip placement. On postoperative day 2, he suffered pulseless electrical activity arrest and cardiogenic shock. Angiography revealed graft kinking from clip-related distortion. Percutaneous coronary intervention to the graft failed, and revascularization was redirected to native circumflex artery, restoring normal flow. The patient recovered, with discharge on postoperative day 18.DiscussionClip-related complications are rare but important causes of early graft failure. Recognition is critical in postoperative ischemia.Take-Home MessageWhen graft kinking occurs, native-vessel intervention may be a safer strategy.Key Words: left atrial appendage clip, type 5 myocardial infarction, vein graft kinking

History of Presentation

A 75-year-old man underwent a positive stress test as part of a preoperative evaluation and was subsequently referred for coronary angiography, which revealed multivessel coronary artery disease. He proceeded to coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). His early postoperative course was initially uneventful; however, on postoperative day 2, he developed pulseless electrical activity cardiac arrest complicated by cardiogenic shock.

Past Medical History

His medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and peripheral arterial disease. He underwent CABG with a left internal mammary artery graft to the left anterior descending artery, a saphenous vein graft (SVG) to the posterior descending artery, and an SVG to the second obtuse marginal branch (OM2), as well as left atrial appendage (LAA) ligation using an epicardial clip.

Differential Diagnosis

The leading consideration was Type 5 myocardial infarction, potentially due to graft spasm, technical or anastomotic failure, or acute thrombosis.

Investigations

Urgent coronary and graft angiography revealed a significant stenosis of the SVG to OM2, consistent with a mechanical kink, secondary to the LAA clip (Figures 1A and 1B).

(A and B) Straight projection (A) and right anterior oblique (B) angiographic views demonstrating a focal mechanical kink (white arrow) of the saphenous vein graft to second obtuse marginal branch caused by the left atrial appendage clip (asterisk).

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated akinesis of the inferolateral and lateral walls and dyskinesis of the apical septum, with a 2-dimensional biplane ejection fraction of 32%. Calculated left ventricular cardiac output was 2.61 L/min, with a cardiac index of 1.48 L/min/m2 (Videos 1A to 1D).

Transesophageal echocardiography performed pre- and post-bypass showed a visually normal left ventricular chamber size and visually estimated ejection fraction of 55% to 60% pre-bypass and 65% post-bypass. No regional wall-motion abnormalities were noted.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

Urgent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) to the SVG–OM2 was initially attempted. A 6-F sheath was placed, and the lesion was crossed; however, multiple attempts at stent delivery were unsuccessful (Figure 2). The procedure was aborted, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit in critical condition. An intra-aortic balloon pump was placed the following day to provide hemodynamic support.

Coronary Angiography Demonstrating Buddy Wire Crossing of SVG Kink With Failed Balloon Delivery

Deployment of a buddy wire successfully crossing the kink; however, balloon delivery across the lesion was not possible (white arrowhead). LAA Clip (Star). LAA = left atrial appendage clip.

Despite intra-aortic balloon pump augmentation and multiple inotropes, the patient remained unstable. A second attempt at SVG stenting was performed. The lesion was crossed with a workhorse wire, but stent delivery remained unsuccessful despite Guideliner support.

Given repeated failure to treat the graft, intervention was redirected to the heavily calcified native circumflex artery (Video 2). Rotational atherectomy was performed using 1.5-mm and 1.25-mm burrs in the proximal and distal circumflex, respectively.

Following atherectomy, the lesion was predilated with 2.5-mm noncompliant balloons and further treated with coronary intravascular lithotripsy. A 3.0 × 32-mm Synergy drug-eluting stent was successfully delivered and deployed at nominal pressure. Postdilatation with a 3.0-mm noncompliant balloon achieved excellent angiographic results with adequate stent expansion and TIMI flow grade 3 (Video 3).

The patient was maintained on aspirin, P2Y12 inhibition, and intravenous heparin. Hemostasis was achieved with a TR band, and he was transferred back to the intensive care unit for continued care.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient showed steady clinical improvement, with successful weaning from inotropic support and removal of the intra-aortic balloon pump without complications. Laboratory values stabilized, and he was extubated, transitioned to nasal cannula, and then to room air.

A P2Y12 assay demonstrated suboptimal response to clopidogrel (P2Y12 value 330), prompting transition to ticagrelor. An infectious workup for leukocytosis (white blood cell count 20.2 × 109/L) was initiated, and broad-spectrum antibiotics were started; no infectious source was identified, and the leukocytosis resolved. He was discharged home in stable condition on postoperative day 18.

At 10-day follow-up, he remained asymptomatic and recovered well without any further complications.

Discussion

Graft kinking is an uncommon but clinically consequential complication after CABG, capable of precipitating acute graft failure, myocardial ischemia, or type 5 myocardial infarction. Although SVGs are well known to be susceptible to thrombosis, torsion, and anastomotic issues, external mechanical compression is a rare and often underrecognized cause of graft dysfunction.

The increasing use of epicardial LAA clip in patients undergoing CABG introduces an anatomic consideration not reflected in current guidelines. The LAAOS III (Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion Study III) trial demonstrated that concomitant LAA occlusion in patients with atrial fibrillation reduces the risk of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism without increasing myocardial infarction; however, most closures in that trial were performed using cut-and-sew techniques (≈56%), with device-based occlusion used in only ∼15% of patients.1

Ailawadi et al2 demonstrated the safety and feasibility of clip-based LAA occlusion with 95% success rate and a generally safe profile, although rare complications including coronary compression of native left main coronary artery have been reported,3, 4, 5 highlighting the potential for mechanical interference with adjacent structures.

Two mechanisms may contribute to graft kinking in settings of LAA clip placement. First, graft redundancy or suboptimal routing may predispose angulation and kinking. Second, external compression or geometric distortion may occur when the clip alters local anatomy, particularly near the left atrioventricular groove where the left circumflex artery travels near the LAA, and grafts to the OM branches traverse this same region (Figure 3). In this case, although graft redundancy may have contributed, the angiographic appearance and anatomic relationship strongly suggested external geometric distortion from the LAA clip as the dominant mechanism.

In our case, the distal anastomosis to the OM branch was completed and followed by graft flushing confirming initial patency. The LAA was subsequently ligated at its base using a 40-mm clip before completion of the remaining proximal graft anastomoses. The patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty, and postoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated preserved ventricular function without regional wall-motion abnormalities. Intraoperative visual inspection and TEE did not suggest graft compromise, raising the possibility that distortion developed after chest closure or evolved during the early postoperative period.

The literature emphasizes meticulous conduit handling, avoidance of excessive tension or angulation, and careful graft routing to promote long-term patency.6 However, they do not address the potential for iatrogenic external compression or distortion from adjacent cardiac structures or surgical devices. Hołda et al7 demonstrated that a distance of <2 mm between the left circumflex artery and the LAA landing zone may represent a high-risk configuration for mechanical compression. These findings suggest that careful preoperative planning and intraoperative inspection are particularly important. In patients with high-risk anatomy, alternative LAA management strategies, such as suture ligation or surgical excision, may be considered to reduce the risk of graft compromise.

PCI of SVG lesions has historically been associated with worse outcomes8,9 This is reflected in the 2021 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions guidelines for coronary artery revascularization, which offers a class 2a recommendation to choose PCI of the native over diseased SVG.10 More recent evidence from de Winter et al11 suggests that PCI to SVGs may achieve improved 1-year outcomes compared with native-vessel PCI. Nevertheless, kinking poses unique procedural challenges, including difficulty crossing the lesion and delivering equipment. In this case, surgical revision of the graft or repositioning/removal of the LAA clip was considered; however, given the patient's early postoperative status, hemodynamic instability, and the high risk associated with reoperation, a repeat surgical approach was deemed unfavorable. Consequently, PCI was initially attempted to the SVG, but inability to cross the lesion prompted redirection toward the native circumflex artery. Utilization of adjunctive tools, such as rotational atherectomy and intravascular lithotripsy, enabled successful PCI and was aligned with contemporary interventional practice, particularly in the absence of specific guideline recommendations for graft-kinking scenarios.

Conclusions

This case illustrates a rare but important mechanism of early graft failure: mechanical distortion of an SVG by an epicardial LAA clip. As device-based LAA occlusion becomes more common during CABG, its potential to alter local anatomy and interfere with graft routing warrants increased awareness. Importantly, intraoperative assessment, including visual inspection and TEE, may fail to detect evolving geometric distortion that manifests after chest closure or during the early postoperative period. Careful preoperative planning, attention to graft courses near the left atrium, and early postoperative surveillance are essential. When graft kinking occurs, native-vessel PCI may offer a safer and more feasible revascularization strategy. This case illustrates an anatomic consideration not currently addressed in revascularization guidelines and highlights the need for further investigation into the technical success and outcomes of PCI for kinked SVGs.
