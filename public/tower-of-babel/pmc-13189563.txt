Matched-Pair Analysis of Patients With Large-Vessel Occlusions Undergoing Thrombectomy Using Single-Plane Versus Biplane Angiography
Authors: Diwas Gautam, Sarah E. Nguyen, Sarah Dabb, Erin Peterson, Jackson Aubrey, Julian Brown, Philipp Taussky, Robert C. Rennert, Craig Kilburg, Ramesh Grandhi, Karol P. Budohoski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The Mechanical Thrombectomy Global Access for Stroke study demonstrated that access to mechanical thrombectomy (MT) is <3% of demand, with severe disparities across urbanization and income levels. Although biplane angiography is the standard, single-plane systems may offer a cost-effective alternative. We conducted a propensity-matched analysis comparing the procedural and clinical outcomes of MT performed in single-plane versus biplane angiography suites.METHODS:All consecutive stroke MT cases performed at 3 centers (2 single plane and 1 biplane) between 2018 and 2024 were included. Outcomes assessed included rates of first-pass effect (modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction score ≥2b on first pass), successful reperfusion (final modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction score ≥2b), time to reperfusion, complication rates, postprocedural symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, and good clinical outcome (modified Rankin Scale score 0–2) at discharge, 30 days, and 90 days.RESULTS:A total of 51 thrombectomies were performed in single-plane suites, and 367 were performed in biplane suites. After propensity matching, 100 patients (50 in each group) were included. In a multivariable analysis, the type of angiography suite was not significantly associated with successful reperfusion (odds ratio, 3.84 [95% CI, 0.22–68.4]; P=0.36) or good clinical outcome at 90 days (odds ratio, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.18–4.88]; P=0.94). There were no significant differences in reperfusion rates and clinical outcomes in the unmatched comparison. In the unmatched cohort, higher numbers of patients with medium- or distal-vessel occlusions and posterior circulation strokes were treated in the biplane suite.CONCLUSIONS:This propensity score–matched analysis comparing MT for large-vessel occlusion in single-plane versus biplane angiography suites demonstrated no statistically significant differences in procedural and clinical outcomes or safety measures. These results indicate that MT using single-plane angiography suites can serve as a viable alternative for treating proximal large-vessel occlusions within the anterior circulation in areas where establishing a full biplane suite is not feasible.

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is among the leading causes of death globally.1,2 In 2019, strokes were responsible for an estimated 6.5 million deaths and the loss of 143 million disability-adjusted life years.1,2 Approximately 87% of disability-adjusted life years lost to stroke, and 86% of stroke-related deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries.2 Since the landmark stroke trials in 2015 that demonstrated clear evidence supporting mechanical thrombectomy (MT) as first-line treatment for AIS caused by large-vessel occlusion (LVO), its indications and demand have continued to expand globally.3–7 Currently, only 56% of the US population has access to a thrombectomy-capable center within a 60-minute ground transport window.8 These disparities are even more pronounced internationally.8–10 The Mechanical Thrombectomy Global Access for Stroke study demonstrated that access to MT is <3% of demand, with severe disparities noted in lower-income countries.10 The global barriers to accessing MT range from health care system limitations and resource unavailability to the absence of trained personnel and necessary equipment.

The unavailability of state-of-the-art imaging with biplane angiography constitutes a major hurdle to developing MT programs in low-resource settings. Although all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) supporting the efficacy of MT were performed in biplane systems,3–7 many centers are performing MT in single-plane angiography suites. Previous studies have demonstrated the safety of MT on single-plane systems.11–16 Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis of MT performed on single-plane versus biplane angiography reported better outcomes in patients whose procedures were performed using single-plane systems.16 A limitation of most prior studies is the potential for selection bias, where patients with proximal LVO in the anterior circulation without significant comorbidities undergo MT in centers with single-plane machines, while those with more complicated lesions (tandem occlusions, distal occlusions, and posterior circulation lesions) are transferred to centers equipped with biplane angiography.17 In the absence of RCTs, we conducted a propensity score–matched analysis to compare the procedural and clinical outcomes of MT performed in single-plane versus biplane angiography systems for AIS.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective review of electronic medical records and imaging of patients who underwent MTs at 3 stroke centers in the same metropolitan area between February 2018 and February 2024. Pediatric patients (≤18 years) were excluded from the final analysis. Institutional review board approval was provided with a waiver of patient consent. STROBE guidelines (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology; Supplemental Material) were followed for the reporting of this study. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

All MTs were performed by 1 of 5 endovascular neurosurgeons from the same practice group. MT procedures using biplane angiography (Artis Q biplane, Siemens) were performed at an academic tertiary care comprehensive stroke center. The single-plane MTs (Allura, Phillips) were performed at 2 community stroke centers.

Data Extraction

We recorded patient demographics, stroke risk factors, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at presentation, last-known-well (LKW)-to-puncture time, presence of tandem occlusion, affected hemisphere (left versus right), vessel(s) occluded, Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score (ASPECTS), use of thrombolysis (alteplase and tenecteplase), procedural details (including approach, anesthesia type, use of stent retriever, time to reperfusion, number of passes, and first-pass effect), final modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) score, procedural complications, postprocedural hemorrhage based on European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study classification, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), need for postprocedural decompressive craniectomy, and modified Rankin Scale score at discharge, 30 days, and 90 days after the procedure. Where source images were available for review, the study team independently verified all scoring system calculations.

Successful reperfusion was defined as mTICI score ≥2b and a good clinical outcome as modified Rankin Scale score ≤2. Time to reperfusion was calculated as the interval from access to successful reperfusion (mTICI score ≥2b). First-pass effect was defined as a mTICI score of 2c or 3 on first pass, whereas modified first-pass effect (mFPE) included mTICI score of 2b, 2c, or 3.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the mFPE rate and good clinical outcome at 30 and 90 days. mFPE was selected as a primary outcome because it reflects procedural efficiency and has been shown to correlate with functional outcome.18,19 Secondary outcomes included time to reperfusion, successful reperfusion (mTICI score ≥2b), complication rates, and postprocedural sICH. In addition, the demographics and case characteristics of unmatched patients were compared between single-plane and biplane centers to better understand the types of cases performed at each type of center.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.1) and SPSS Statistics, version 20 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Propensity score matching was done to pair MT procedures performed at single-plane and biplane angiography suites using the MatchIt package in R with a 1:1 nearest-neighbor approach and a standard caliper of 0.2 to ensure tight matching. Matching covariates included patient sex, age, history of prior stroke, LKW-to-puncture time (categorized into ≤6, 6–24, or >24 hours), NIHSS score on admission, occluded vessel, use of both aspiration catheter and stent retriever, and presence of tandem occlusion. ASPECTS was not included as a variable in the propensity score matching because of missing source images and a lack of independent verification for some patients in the single-plane cohort. After matching, the Pearson χ2 test was used to assess categorical variables, and the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous data. Parametric data were assessed using the Student t test and nonparametric data with the Mann-Whitney U test. Where appropriate, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed for primary and secondary outcomes. Variables that were statistically significant on univariable analysis, as well as those considered clinically relevant, were included in the multivariable model. Given the inability to independently verify the ASPECTS in some patients, we did not include it in the propensity score matching; however, it was included in the multivariable regression models examining radiographic and clinical outcomes. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Clinically distinct covariates in multivariable analysis were prespecified based on clinical relevance. In addition, all covariates were evaluated for collinearity using variance inflation factors. No significant collinearity was observed (all variance inflation factors <2; all tolerances>0.1). In addition, a secondary propensity-matched sensitivity analysis including ASPECTS as a matching variable was performed among patients with available ASPECTS data.

Results

We identified 51 MT cases performed in the single-plane angiography suites and 371 cases performed in the biplane center. Four pediatric cases from the biplane cohort were removed, and 1 case from the single-plane cohort was removed because the NIHSS score was missing (Figure 1). The 50 cases from the single-plane centers were 1:1 propensity score matched with cases from the biplane center. After matching, 50 single-plane center cases and 50 biplane center cases were included in the final analysis.

In the matched cohorts, there were no significant differences in demographic or comorbidity variables, mean NIHSS score, occlusion location and laterality, percentage of patients receiving thrombolysis, and percentage of tandem occlusions (Tables 1 and 2). Similarly, no statistically significant difference was found in the time window distribution of LKW-to-puncture times between single-plane and biplane groups (P=0.785). However, general anesthesia was used more frequently in the biplane group (2% versus 16%; P=0.014).

MT Procedure Characteristics

In both groups, the combined stent retriever and manual aspiration technique was used in most cases. The differences between the single-plane and biplane groups, respectively, in the number of passes required (1.96 versus 2.52; P=0.153), rate of mFPE (66% versus 58%; P=0.410), and overall rate of successful recanalization (90% versus 94%; P=0.461) were not statistically significant in the single-plane and biplane groups, respectively (Table 3). There were no significant differences in the rates of good clinical outcomes (modified Rankin Scale score 0–2) at discharge (30% versus 26%; P=0.656), 30 days (49% versus 38%; P=0.270), and 90 days (61.2% versus 42.9%; P=0.069). The rates of embolization to new territory (2% versus 2%, P>0.999) and sICH (26.8% versus 16.3%; P=0.224) were comparable between the 2 groups. Similarly, no statistically significant difference was observed in the median puncture to successful reperfusion times (23 versus 33.5 minutes; P=0.320). Importantly, 10% of cases treated initially at a single-plane center were subsequently transferred to the tertiary academic center for further monitoring and treatment after MT. In a multivariable analysis adjusted for age, sex, admission NIHSS score, ASPECTS, use of thrombolysis, occlusion location, presence of tandem occlusion, and use of stent retriever and aspiration catheter, the type of angiography suite was not significantly associated with successful reperfusion (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 3.84 [95% CI, 0.22–68.4]; P=0.360) or good clinical outcome at 90 days (aOR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.18–4.88]; P=0.943; Table 4).

Patient and Stroke Characteristics in Single-Plane Versus Biplane Centers

To better understand the demographics, clinical characteristics, and stroke pathology of patients treated at single-plane and biplane centers, we compared patients in our study population before propensity score matching (Table 5). A significantly higher percentage of patients who self-identified as White were treated in the biplane angiography suite (83.1% versus 72.0%), whereas a higher percentage of those who self-identified as Hispanic were treated in single-plane angiography suites (16.0% versus 5.2%; P=0.026); however, admission NIHSS score, presence of tandem occlusion, and pretreatment ASPECTS were not statistically different between the 2 groups. Notably, a significantly higher percentage of patients treated at a single-plane center received intravenous thrombolysis (60.0% versus 39.5%; P=0.006). The distribution of primary occluded vessels suggests that a greater variety of cases were treated at the center with a biplane angiography suite compared with those with a single-plane suite. In the single-plane angiography group, 80.0% of occlusions involved the internal carotid artery and middle cerebral artery branch M1 segments, and 16.0% involved the proximal or dominant middle cerebral artery branch M2. There were only 2 patients with stroke in the posterior circulation among the patients treated in a single-plane suite. In the biplane angiography group, occlusions of the internal carotid artery and middle cerebral artery branch M1 segments still comprised the majority of cases (69.0%), with another 17.7% involving the proximal or dominant middle cerebral artery branch M2. However, there was a higher percentage of cases involving the posterior circulation or distal-vessel occlusion in the biplane cohort (Table 5; Figure 2).

In the full study population, patients treated in single-plane angiography suites were more likely to achieve mTICI score ≥2B in the first pass (66% versus 47.7%; P=0.015) and had higher final mTICI scores (P=0.004; Table S1). None of the patients treated with single-plane angiography had a complication, but they did have a higher rate of sICH postoperatively (26.8% versus 11.8%; P=0.008); other outcomes were not statistically significantly different between groups, including the percentage of patients who required craniotomy.

Similarly to the matched cohort, in a multivariable model controlling for age, sex, admission NIHSS score, ASPECTS, use of thrombolysis, occlusion location, presence of tandem occlusion, and use of stent retriever and aspiration catheter, the use of biplane angiography was not associated with successful reperfusion (aOR, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.25–1.92]; P=0.475), mFPE (aOR, 0.53 [95% CI, 0.27–1.02]; P=0.062), or good clinical outcome at 90 days (aOR, 1.74 [95% CI, 0.91–3.41]; P=0.086), but it was significantly associated with lower odds of having sICH (aOR, 0.32 [95% CI, 0.13–0.76]; P=0.014; Table S2).

A second propensity-matched analysis, including ASPECTS as a matching variable, yielded a total of 20 cases (10 single plane versus 10 biplane). Although limited by a small sample size, outcomes remained consistent with the primary analysis and did not demonstrate any statistically significant difference between the 2 groups (Table S3).

Discussion

In our propensity score–matched analysis, MT for AIS secondary to LVO performed on single-plane angiography and biplane angiography machines demonstrated comparable procedural and clinical outcomes without compromising safety in the 2 groups. Importantly, our results demonstrate that centers with single-plane angiography typically treated patients with technically uncomplicated LVO involving the internal carotid artery or proximal middle cerebral artery, namely, those within the currently accepted guidelines for MT.20 On the other hand, the center with biplane angiography more often treated patients with technically challenging cases involving the posterior circulation and medium- or distal-vessel occlusions.21 These findings support that under resource constraints and in an era of significantly increasing demand for MT, it is safe for experienced neurointerventionalists to perform MT for technically uncomplicated LVO in single-plane angiography suites when biplane angiography suites are unfeasible.

In 6 prior studies, including a recent meta-analysis, good outcomes have been achieved after MT procedures at sites with single-plane systems.11–16 Moreover, in their recent meta-analysis, Orscelik et al16 demonstrated better outcomes for MT performed on single-plane angiography systems. However, the prior studies share a significant potential for selection bias whereby younger, healthier patients, particularly those with proximal vessel LVOs of the anterior circulation, constitute the majority of patients treated with single-plane angiography.17 On the other hand, patients with more complicated lesions (eg, tandem lesions, posterior circulation lesions, and medium- or distal-vessel occlusions) are often referred to comprehensive stroke centers equipped with biplane angiography.17 This is also supported by studies that report higher case volumes and a broader case mix of thrombectomies performed using biplane angiography systems.11–15 In response, we aimed to reduce this bias by using propensity score matching to compare patients with similar baseline characteristics, including clinical and radiological stroke burden. Our results reinforce the conclusion that for patients who fit globally recognized guidelines for treatment of AIS secondary to LVO, comparable outcomes can be achieved when performing MT using single-plane and biplane angiography machines.

Biplane systems offer several advantages over single-plane systems, including improved visualization of occlusions and potentially easier and faster navigation through tortuous anatomy14,22; however, these benefits may be more relevant in complex cases. In our cohort, 96% of patients had occlusion in the internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery branch M1, or proximal/dominant middle cerebral artery branch M2 vessels. Notably, RCT data demonstrating the benefit of MT for basilar artery stroke have only been available since 202223,24 and have only recently been included in societal guidelines.25,26 Before 2022, major RCTs, such as the Basilar Artery Occlusion Endovascular Intervention Versus Standard Medical Treatment and Basilar Artery International Cooperation Study trials, failed to show a definitive benefit of MT over medical therapy for posterior circulation strokes.27,28 Similarly, although earlier studies demonstrated outcome benefits of MT for medium- and distal-vessel occlusions, more recent trials, such as the Distal Ischemic Stroke Treatment With Adjustable Low-Profile Stentriever and Endovascular Treatment to Improve Outcomes for Medium Vessel Occlusions studies, have not confirmed these findings.29–32 Therefore, it is important to recognize that high-volume comprehensive stroke centers tend to treat more diverse and complicated lesions, whereas centers using single-plane angiography, including those in the current study and prior studies, have a tendency to operate only within the established guidelines. Our findings have significant implications for expanding access to MT, particularly in rural regions where rapid access to stroke centers is difficult and in resource-limited regions, where access to MT is only being established. Indeed, in low- and middle-income countries, the high cost of biplane systems poses a significant barrier to MT implementation. In such a context, initial investment in single-plane systems may be a cost-effective and practical solution for establishing more MT-capable stroke programs that can treat proximal occlusions in the anterior circulation, where MT consistently demonstrates the strongest benefit.4–6,20

Interestingly, a significantly higher percentage of patients treated at biplane centers self-identified as White, while a greater proportion of Hispanic patients were treated at single-plane centers. This may reflect regional patient populations surrounding the centers; however, we did not have access to detailed socioeconomic or geographic data to explore these disparities further. In addition, a significantly higher percentage of patients at single-plane centers received intravenous thrombolysis. Because intravenous thrombolysis is indicated for AIS within 4.5 hours of symptom onset,20,33 this finding suggests that single-plane centers may be geographically closer to patients, allowing for faster presentation and treatment initiation, including intravenous thrombolysis. This is further supported by significantly shorter LKW-to-puncture time in the single-plane cohort in the unmatched analysis. Shorter LKW-to-puncture times are generally associated with better outcomes. Our propensity matching accounted for this, with LKW-to-puncture times not significantly different between the single-plane and biplane groups in the matched analysis. Finally, ≈10% of patients initially treated at single-plane centers were subsequently transferred to tertiary academic centers for postthrombectomy care. Although this could have artificially improved outcomes in the single-plane group, it also highlights that single-plane centers can provide rapid access to thrombectomy, which is a major contributor to favorable outcomes. Finally, our results demonstrate that a proportion of patients were transferred either for MT or after the procedure from the hospitals with single-plane systems to the tertiary referral hospital with biplane angiography capabilities. Importantly, the reviewed transfer decisions in the described cohorts were made on a case-by-case basis by the operator, and we were not able to provide reliable quantitative information about them. However, the common indications were patients with medium- or distal-vessel stroke, tandem occlusion, posterior circulation stroke, low ASPECTS, significant comorbidities, and clinical instability. Furthermore, although the difference was not statistically significant, there was a higher rate of sICH in the single-plane group (26.8% versus 16.3%). This may reflect postprocedural management in nonneuro-specific intensive care because patients treated at the single-plane centers were managed in nonneuro-specific intensive care units, whereas patients at the biplane center, an academic tertiary institution, were managed in neuro specific intensive care units. Our data do not allow for definitive statements as to the cause of this finding. Notably, the proportion of patients requiring decompressive craniotomy did not differ significantly between the 2 groups.

Collectively, these findings suggest that expanding the availability of single-plane thrombectomy suites in areas without existing biplane-equipped centers could meaningfully improve access to stroke care in underserved and resource-limited settings when establishing full biplane-equipped centers is not feasible. Although biplane technology can be advantageous in complex cases, single-plane systems can play a crucial role in improving accessibility.

Limitations

There are several limitations to our study. Our data are limited by the accuracy of electronic medical records, and our study’s relatively small sample size may limit statistical power. Although propensity score matching helps mitigate selection bias, it cannot fully replace the rigor of an RCT. There were also residual differences between the cohorts, including median ASPECTS and anesthesia type, which may have influenced outcomes. ASPECTS was not included in the propensity score matching (hence the observed differences in the matched cohorts) because there was a substantial portion of patients with missing source images for independent ASPECTS value verification. This was a result of a transition in electronic medical records and imaging systems during the study period. However, in a multivariable model comparing outcomes between single-plane and biplane angiography systems in the matched cohorts, ASPECTS was not an independent predictor of reperfusion or outcomes. Importantly, although the matched groups did show a difference in median ASPECTS, the score in both groups fell well within the standard acceptable ASPECTS for patients undergoing MT (10 versus 8 in the single-plane and biplane groups, respectively). In addition, a secondary propensity-matched analysis incorporating ASPECTS yielded findings consistent with the primary analysis although the reduced sample size limits definitive statistical inference.

The higher rate of general anesthesia in the biplane group likely represents ease of access to general anesthesia in the academic center with the biplane system. Anesthesia type was not included in the propensity match because current evidence has not demonstrated a consistent benefit of general anesthesia over conscious sedation/local anesthesia for thrombectomy outcomes34; however, we acknowledge that anesthesia type may influence procedural workflow.

Interventionalist experience and the composition of the procedural teams (ie, the availability of neuro-specific technologists and nurses) were not captured in this study and may represent additional unmeasured variability across the centers. Although operator experience was consistent across these centers because of shared call coverage, the experience of the support staff and resource availability are likely greater at the tertiary academic center with a biplane suite. These factors may influence workflow efficiency but could not be reliably quantified in this retrospective database.

Furthermore, the study period (2018–2024) also introduces potential temporal bias because thrombectomy techniques and outcomes have evolved over this timeframe. In addition, with the limited sample size, the study is not powered to formally assess equivalence or noninferiority between angiography systems. Future studies with larger sample sizes and prospective randomized designs are needed to validate these findings and further explore the role of single-plane systems in expanding global access to MT.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that MT for AIS secondary to anterior circulation LVO can be performed safely and effectively using single-plane angiography systems. These results should not be generalized as evidence of equivalence between single-plane and biplane systems across all clinical scenarios. However, when biplane systems are not feasible because of resource constraints, the findings suggest that single-plane centers may provide a practical means of delivering thrombectomy for proximal LVO, achieving comparable outcomes in select patients. These findings have important implications for expanding access to MT in resource-limited and rural settings, both within the United States and globally.
