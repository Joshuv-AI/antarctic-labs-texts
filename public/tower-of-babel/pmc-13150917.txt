Mentoring Trauma Nurses: A Global Perspective on Leadership and Resilience in Conflict Zones
Authors: Kathleen Martin, Zakk Arciaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKathleen Martin (KM) is an internationally renowned trauma nurse, mentor, and humanitarian whose career has impacted trauma nurses. As the keynote speaker for this year’s Sharon M Henry Maryland Committee on Trauma State-of-the-Art Lecture, no one is better qualified to illuminate the vital—but too often overlooked—role of trauma nursing in austere and conflict-affected settings than KM. Her discussion helped illuminate that trauma nursing plays a pivotal role in global conflict zones, yet the unique challenges and contributions of nurses are often underrepresented in trauma literature. This keynote, transcribed and shared, reflects on a multidisciplinary mentorship initiative supporting trauma care in Ukraine, emphasizing leadership, cross-cultural collaboration, and the development of sustainable nursing practices (see supplemental file).Through direct fieldwork and partnership with Ukrainian hospitals during the war, the author led a volunteer nursing mission integrated with the Global Surgical and Medical Support Group. Structured education, bedside mentorship, and infection control protocols were implemented in Lviv’s 3000-bed hospital. Cross-cultural and logistical barriers were addressed through adaptive teaching strategies and remote follow-up.Key advancements included initiation of multidisciplinary intensive care unit (ICU) rounds, infection surveillance using custom-built tools, introduction of evidence-based protocols, and integration of nurses into medical planning. A national burn ICU and rehabilitation center was supported through international donations. Nurse engagement and empowerment markedly improved, and training programs were expanded through virtual platforms and NGO partnerships.Mentorship across borders can catalyze systemic improvements in trauma care, even amid war. This experience underscores the necessity of including nurses in trauma system development and illustrates the power of international collaboration in advancing trauma nursing practice in austere and conflict-affected settings.Keywords: nursing, Public Health, Mass Casualty Incidents, War-Related Injuries

Introduction

This section discusses mentorship and what it has meant to me. I started my trauma journey in Philadelphia and had some of the best mentors, Dr C. William Schwab and Dr Michael Rotondo. In the military setting, Dr (retired Colonel) Warren Dorlac. These mentors helped me integrate a nursing model with the medical model. I was somewhere in between. This section discusses mentoring, but then focuses on the work that we did in Ukraine (slides 1–8).

Maryana Svirchuk is a nurse and is the CEO of the Lviv Hospital Union, a 3000-bed hospital in western Ukraine. And she has been transformational in elevating nursing practice in Ukraine. She worked closely with the six nurses who volunteered their time to come to Ukraine to assist in elevating trauma nursing practice. Of the 100 nurses that I asked, only five others volunteered to go. The others wanted to participate, but their families objected. I had to focus not only on their experience but on a positive global perspective and appreciating how different trauma care is in other countries.

Mentoring—I have had some of the best mentors in my career. Dr C William Schwab was a visionary and was always able to plant the seed, and then I would put tactics in place. He inspired me. That is what I try to do for nurses globally. Supporting them, coaching them, inspiring them, and jointly figuring out what direction we should take. How are we going to get this done? There’s a great book, if you haven't read it, A Message to Garcia. It basically says, take initiative without hesitation.1 Not, why me? Not, am I going to be paid extra to do this? Am I going to be promoted? You just have to do it.

I had trauma nurse mentors in my life. I was at the University of Pennsylvania, and I called Jori Klein at Parkland, in Dallas, Texas, a huge trauma center. I told her that I just didn’t understand trauma performance improvement. And she said, ‘well, come down to Dallas, stay at my house, spend the week’. I did not realize I was doing performance improvement; I just did not know that it was packaged in a certain way. The focus was how we improve the patient’s outcome. Probably the best mentor I had was my US Navy nurse mother, LtJG Edith Hall, who inspired me to become a nurse but also how to be organized. I have had the privilege of working with dynamic trauma surgeons, as I mentioned, Bill Schwab, Mike Rotondo, Warren Dorlac, John Holcomb, Tom Scalea and Sharon Henry. All inspirational mentors, many of whom were visiting professors who volunteered their time for 2 weeks at a time and came to Landstuhl, Germany. Dr Scalea, Dr Rotondo, and I went to Afghanistan together to complete a Trauma Systems assessment in the conflict zone. My other mentor is Sergeant David Doran, US Marine Corps, my father, who influenced me.

There are many global challenges in mentoring nurses in other countries. There are cultural, language, and communication difficulties. Thank goodness for Google Translate—you can not only type into it, but you can talk into it, and it talks back to them in Ukrainian or whatever language that you are working with. The other challenge is the Cyrillic vocabulary, the letters, they're nothing I have ever seen before. Being able to use the Google Lens to read signs, directions, and communicate was valued.

I have collaborated with nurses in countries where hierarchy, male supremacy, or patriarchy is prominent. I had a challenging time with that. You have to look at the culture of a country in advance and realize that you cannot easily change culture. Another consideration is differences in mechanisms of injury. Who would have thought in South Korea that the number one mechanism of injury for children is hot ramen noodle burns? It is vital to look at the different mechanisms which may be related to the local environment.

When you are mentoring, you are not always going to be on site. Another consideration is that there are 38 different time zones, or perhaps they do not have internet connectivity. Maybe when I translated it in Google Translate to give them a document, it did not match up—it was not appropriate. You have to look at all these aspects in order to be able to continue to mentor but give them independence. Sustainability is important, and how will you communicate with all these people at all different hours of the day?

The countries outlined in this slide are the countries that I have traveled to and had the honor to mentor nurses globally. I primarily volunteer my time now and have traveled to Australia and New Zealand, the UK and Europe, Ukraine, Afghanistan, Peru, Brazil, Chile, and I have most of the US states. It is vital that you focus on the relationships that you build, and when aspects are virtual, keeping the time of day and events going on in their country at that time foremost.

The Call to Ukraine

In 2006, I went to the US Army base in Landstuhl Regional Medical Center, Germany. There were numerous people who were critical of why I would get involved in the war, and my response was ‘it is not about the war, it is about the warrior’. It’s about focusing not on the conflict, but on the patient and their injuries. We do not make judgments when patients come into our trauma resuscitation bays, not who was at fault, but how can I save their life. Being a warrior is not about the act of fighting. It’s about the ability and the courage and the commitment to continue doing what you're doing until aspects are right (slides 8–13).

Warren Dorlac said ‘the Ukrainian people are just us; they're just like anyone else in the world. They want to live in peace, be part of building their country, and be something better. If you believe in a free world and justice, it is important that you try to do something to help. There are plenty of ways people can help, but you need to do something. If you roll over to tyrants and aristocrats, aspects will only get worse’. He said this, 3 years ago, ‘The longer it goes on, the harder and more painful it is to reverse it’. I think that is what we went to Ukraine to try to do. This impacted me. I never imagined that I could go into Ukraine and help. I was able to do that in person, and I continue remotely.

Picture of a mother. These are the civilian injured patients that we took care of. Quite different than when I worked at Landstuhl Regional Medical Center, where I took care of Soldiers, Airmen, and Marines. But here, we were taking care of civilians.

Picture of a child. Multiple fractures with external fixators. There is no internal fixation of any kind due to the expense. If a family wants internal fixation, they must purchase the hardware, which is approximately US$5000 American, which may be half of their annual salary. So, everything was external fixation.

Picture of a teenager: this (pediatric) patient was taught to drive by her mother by driving farming equipment. When their town was being attacked, she got her grandmother, her mother, and two sisters into the car, and they were being shot at by the Russians, and she continued to drive 30 km with her legs full of bullet holes until the car stalled, and the Ukrainian soldiers picked them up, she was transported by train to Lviv Hospital.

The Trauma Care/Warzone Challenges

This is a train station of people trying to leave the country and were shot to death at the train station. These were regular civilians with their rolling suitcases and backpacks, and they were shot to death at the train station, and all their belongings were then looted (slides 15–27).

500 000 military have died, but close to 13 000 civilians have died. Eight million people left the country. As of last month, 2200 children have died or been injured, and each of those strollers in this picture represents one of those children. Bomb shelters are everywhere, such as the basement of the Lviv Hospital. Alarm sirens went off at least once a day, and that is when it is necessary to move to the bomb shelter. There was an app that I used that would tell me exactly where the bombings were happening, how close they were to the hospital, and how many were intercepted. There were not a lot of bombings near Lviv, but there definitely were some. The attacks on healthcare have been outrageous. 996 attacks on destroyed hospitals and 267 health workers killed, and many more injured.

These slides depict what we would know as a forward operating base, where we usually have numerous cleaner tents. They use bombed-out buildings, usually with flashlights to give the area the lights for the operating room. Many soldiers did not get to leave the war zone, despite their amputations. They continued in the fight and had to stay there, because there was no way to transport them anywhere else.

In the US military, it took about 45 days in Vietnam to get from injury to Japan or Hawaii, but you had your buddy care, your field hospital, your Landstuhl evacuation hospital, and then definitive care back in the USA. During the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, we were moving patients in one to 3 days, and probably closer to 1 day later in the war. But in Ukraine, they don’t own the airspace; their airspace is closed. When I travel there, I fly into Poland and then go by ground across the border to Ukraine. Ukraine airspace does not allow them to transfer patients via air, and they certainly do not have a Landstuhl Role IV center to transfer to, until recently, when Lviv started to develop into being a true trauma center. Because there is no air transport, there is no medevac, and civilians are transported from the east combat zone to Lviv in the west by trains. Doctors Without Borders from France takes care of transporting the civilians. Sometimes transport takes up to 24 hours, and many may have been injured days before and not had their wounds cared for. The Doctors Without Borders renovated train cars and transformed them into operating rooms, ICUs, and a one for ward patients.2 An entire train car was used for generating electricity, and another train car was used for generating oxygen. As the trains got close, Lviv would get a call and list the incoming wounded, their injuries, and their status. Then the Lviv staff would work with prehospital and arrange for ambulances to come to the Lviv train station to bring them to Lviv Hospital.

Lviv Hospital

This is a photo of the city of Lviv. It is breathtakingly beautiful. I thought it looked like Paris with the fountains in front of the opera house and so much history. Life looked fairly normal in Lviv compared with the eastern part of the country (slides 28–33).

The Global Surgical Medical Support Group was the non-governmental agency that John Holcomb and our teams worked with to arrange our safe transport and accommodations. We set up 10 teams to go into Ukraine, which consisted of an orthopedic surgeon, a burn surgeon, a trauma surgeon, sometimes a vascular surgeon or pediatric surgeon, and a trauma nurse. The first team did not include a nurse. I was asked to coordinate the nurses who would go with the subsequent teams.

Many patients have been evacuated to other countries where there were resources for amputations, traumatic brain injury, and reconstruction. This slide depicts the movement of Ukrainians going to Romania, Hungary, Germany, Poland, and many of them up to the UK. We were traveling in the opposite direction; we flew into Rzeszów, Poland, and crossed the border to Ukraine by ground. It is about a 90-min ride by car to Lviv, but it took us 10 hours to cross. This is when it started to look different, more like a war zone.

This is a picture of many of the dynamic team members: Drs John Holcomb, Warren Dorlac, Chuck Fox, Roman Hayda, and Sandy Wanek. I worked with several of them at Landstuhl Regional Medical Center. Many were retired military and were cognizant of what was needed, what to wear, how to keep safe, and how to always have a ‘go bag’ ready at all times. The nurses were trauma leaders; many were Society of Trauma Nurses presidents or members of the board of directors. Mario Rivera-Barbosa, Jorie Klein, Sarah Mattocks, Tracy McDonald, and Pam Woods were all career trauma nurses, all diplomatic and with a worldly perspective.

Trauma Care Enhancements

This slide is a picture of the Lviv Hospital, which consists of an emergency hospital, a burn center, and a children’s hospital for a total of 3000 beds. I was the first nurse of our group to go into Lviv and did not know what to expect and was told to go in, collaborate, do a needs assessment, and mentor the nurses (slides 34–63).

Maryana wanted to elevate nursing practice, to advance nursing practice to be like the rest of Europe. Nursing practice in Ukraine was different; they are taught medication administration, IV administration, and dressing changes. We created a process for bedside rounds that included nurses. I had everything translated on reference cards we could talk through care and prevention of complications. I went around taping foleys to the thigh, ensuring patients were turned, heads of beds were up, using prevention bundles for catheter-associated urinary tract infection, central line-associated bloodstream infections, and ventilator-associated pneumonia. They said, ‘What are you trying to do, make us into little doctors?’ It was a much different culture. I felt I should pull back. But Maryana said, ‘no’, just keep on going.

They had a stated 80% infection rate in the ICU, with the average time to get cultures back being 10 days. Therefore, everybody had Clostridioides difficile from being on antibiotics for extended times and developed resistance. I then worked with the labs. They had one microbiologist for the 3000 patients. I worked closely with her staff and assisted in creating an Excel spreadsheet to track the infections, organisms, and unit the patient was in. Their data collection was in its infancy, and they were just starting to track this information. They started calling me the ‘infection control nurse’, which doesn't sit well with a trauma nurse, right? Preventing complications then became my major area of focus during the time that I was there. Anybody heard of FAST-HUGS BID (Feeding, Analgesia, Sedation, Thromboembolic, Head of bed up, Ulcer prophylaxis, Glycemic control, Spontaneous breathing trial, Bowel regimen, Indwelling lines/catheters removed, De-escalation of antibiotics)?3 It’s a real-time rounding tool for the ICU to ensure prophylaxis to prevent complications. I gave this lecture to the nurses and created reference cards for them in Ukrainian, empowering the bedside nurse to prevent complications. Subsequently, the physicians wanted to hear the lecture as well, and the teams are now collaborative.

Dressing changes were a daily occurrence in a separate room at the end of the hall. They were using full-strength betadine on all the dressings. Dr Dorlac initiated the use of a Dakin’s solution. We asked them to start using sterile technique instead of just clean technique.

This is the red zone of the Emergency Department, which is for the most critical patients on the left—Resuscitation Bay. Doesn't look much different (than in the USA). And when I got there, Dr Holcomb had been there, before my arrival, and he recommended a blood refrigerator which was donated.

ICU daily rounding with a critical care team was the norm for my day, and the nurses appreciated being included. This nurse is seen wearing a Society of Trauma Nurses pin on her lapel.4 And I told the nurse who followed me, Mario. If he could bring condom catheters. They leave Foley’s in forever, and he had an entire suitcase of condom catheters that got stopped at customs and asked, what is this? In the ICU, there were three patients on one side of the room, then a small area for the staff, and then three additional patients on the other side. The ICU nurse–patient ratio would be one nurse to those six patients.

Your family did your bathing, went to the pharmacy to fill your prescription, went across the street to buy lunch, and came back. And there was little. The ward patients were either five or 6 patients to a room. In addition, family members are at the bedside. It was difficult, and only one of the rooms had oxygen. I assisted in caring for a man whose mechanism was a motor vehicle crash, with pulmonary contusion, ruptured bladder, and multiple maxillofacial lacerations. I suggested perhaps he should be on supplemental oxygen, and they listened, and we put him on 2 L NC. I taught them how to do maxillofacial care, etc. I had to work with them to make sure that I was not intimidating them.

The nursing station was non-existent, just a desk in the hall. There was little documentation, no monitors, no computers, just me and paper. Many of you would probably love that. The doctors would write out the medications that they were expected to be given in the morning report. And then they would put a line on the hours that transcribed that. And the nurse gave it. She just put the arrow across on it to say it was given.

The doctor’s meeting room is where they did their morning report. The nurses did not attend, but they do now. This picture is of nurses from the trauma ward; they attend rounds every day now. This is how they plan the care now; it is not a locked room anymore where nurses were not allowed in there. It is encouraging to see these advances.

The Society of Trauma Nurses donated the electronic library of trauma lectures. And these are all the lectures. Every day at 3 o’clock, I would go to the auditorium to present lectures. I was constantly amazed at how many people showed up. There were the microbiologists, nurses, and doctors. And then they would have someone translate for me. After my return to the USA, I would then do Zoom education for them. Mario, who came after me, brought the advanced burn life support course for the first time in Ukraine.5 Others brought the fundamentals of critical care, ultra-talented fundamentals of critical care from the Society of Critical Care Medicine.6 They were now elevating not only the physician practice but also including the nurses in all of this. Then and now, you see at the top of the screen, the bar of the app and the sink. That was the sink. That was the only way to wash your hands in the ward patient’s room with five or six patients and family members in that room. Now, we have got hand hygiene, sanitizer, masks, gloves, and hats. Most of it has been donated, and now that is outside of a single room.

They built a new burn intensive care center, which was much needed. The city of Vilnius in Lithuania donated US$800 000, and the government spent several million to build a rehabilitation center. So while I was there, they had built the foundation of Unbroken, which is now open.7 They expect to see about 50 000 injured a year. This is a diagram of the Unbroken complex now, where nearly 300 000 Ukrainians sought psychological care. The equipment is enhanced now, with an Angio suite and high-slice CAT scanners. Unbroken includes housing where family members can stay. They have an art therapy center. One of the biggest advances was a three-dimensional printer that is making all their prostheses. It is the only rehabilitation hospital in the country.

Drs. John Holcomb and Warren Dorlac received a grant from the DOD the following year to train and conduct multiple educational symposiums and ASSET courses. There were symposiums focused on trauma registries, another on blood use, another on trauma nursing, and rehabilitation. All the training is being done in Warsaw.

After the 10 rotations, the nurses in our group compiled all of the recommendations, which are listed here. There were many, but the focus was on: administration, emergency department, infection prevention and control, nursing competencies, leadership, ICU, and performance improvement. They now have an infection prevention and control department. They have eight nurses now who are working in this department.

Young Couple

(A young couple), they are 22. ‘So, we laugh all the time. Young and in love. He is mine. We always have something to talk about. Got married in the hospital. Both have wedding rings on their left hands (Ukrainians wear wedding rings on their right hand) because he does not have a right one. He was a Ukrainian soldier, a sniper. He signed the contract 2 years ago. In February, he was transferred nearby Kyiv. ‘My boys and I were everywhere, starting from Kyiv on February 24th; Irpin, Bucha, Moschun, Vymedivny, Boromali. Next, the Luhansk region. May 29, the day of the injury, was supposed to be special for him and his significant other. It was our anniversary. I wanted to take a little vacation, and I was given a task. I was nervous about it, that I forgot to wish her a happy anniversary in the morning. During the task, the Russians began firing heavy weapons. When a mine exploded near San Pakhi, none of the comrades were around. I was lying down. I could not scream. I could not call anyone. I could not grab the phone because I had a broken shoulder blade, and my right arm was missing. I understood that I have two legs, and they are wet. They are punctured. And a torn lung. So, he knew if he panicked, his heart would stop eventually. All he could do was wait. I was just lying there, waiting for it to be over, because I was in much pain. It was a little scary that my body would not be found. Then a friend just flies by in a pickup truck. My condition is extremely severe’. Once when he came to consciousness, he decided to break up with her. I thought that was all. She should live her life. She did not need me. When he got injured, I only realized more that I did not want to lose him. About where to find a loved one who did not get in touch, she was told by his siblings. And she came to Lviv. When she arrived at the Lviv hospital, the first thing I did was just burst into tears. I was happy. And she said, marry me. Of course, I said yes. Got married right in the hospital room. And then, with her support, he began to learn to live with one hand, to put on socks with his left hand, and to fasten his shirt with one hand. For example, to cook kebabs, I could not cut the meat. There’s simply nothing to hold it and cut it with. I wanted a bionic prosthesis that I can live with a wife. Now the couple is in Lviv, in the National Rehabilitation Center ‘Unbroken’. They came here when they heard that the bionic prosthesis program had been launched and they had already given the first bionic arm to a soldier. A team of surgeons, prosthetists, rehabilitation specialists, and psychotherapists gets together to examine whether He is ready for a prosthesis. The nerves on the left hand are damaged. It is losing strength. The hand atrophies. The strength disappears. I exercise and I keep it that way, but it still disappears. In order to maintain the strength and not lose hope, rehabilitation specialists and psychotherapists work with him. A prosthesis is an additional opportunity for a person who is missing a limb. And a bionic one has additional capabilities for him and dozens of other Ukrainian defenders. They are part of the new generation of Ukrainians—strong, resilient, and determined to heal.

Conclusion

Mentorship is more than guidance—it is legacy. It is how we transform individuals, systems, and nations. As trauma nurses, we are uniquely positioned to lead from the frontlines—whether at home or abroad, in peace or in conflict. Together, each achieves more. Together, we heal.
