Assessing flares in patients with psoriatic arthritis using questionnaires
Authors: Nicholas J Chronis, Daniel Pereira, Shangyi Gao, Vinod Chandran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe psoriatic arthritis (PsA) FLARE Questionnaire was developed to study flares in PsA. We aimed to validate the FLARE Questionnaire and compare it with the Psoriatic Arthritis Impact of Disease 12-item Questionnaire (PsAID-12) to determine whether PsAID-12 was sufficient for assessing disease flares.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 150 patients aged ≥18 years. Data collection using a standard protocol consisted of demographic characteristics, patient-reported outcomes (including PsAID-12), cutaneous/musculoskeletal examination findings, routine labs, FLARE Questionnaire and a stand-alone question asking participants whether they believed they were experiencing a flare. Internal consistency of the FLARE Questionnaire was measured using Cronbach’s α. Test-retest reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) by administering the questionnaire twice to 51 patients, 1 week apart. Agreement between flare definition using the FLARE Questionnaire and the stand-alone question was determined using Cohen’s κ. Associations between clinical features and flares self-reported by the stand-alone question were assessed using logistic regressions. The FLARE Questionnaire and PsAID-12 were compared against the stand-alone question using receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsThe FLARE Questionnaire displayed good internal consistency (α = 0.88, 95% CI 0.85 to 0.90), good test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.76, p<0.001, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.87), and moderate agreement between flare definition using the cut-off and stand-alone question (κ = 0.59, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.72). However, ROC analysis demonstrated that the FLARE Questionnaire (area under the curve (AUC) = 0.90, 95% CI 0.85 to 0.95) and PsAID-12 (AUC = 0.83, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.90) performed similarly. A PsAID-12 cut-off of ≥3.13 achieved the best balance between sensitivity (0.72) and specificity (0.79).ConclusionsPsAID-12 may be used for defining PsA flares.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory arthritis that is associated with psoriasis (PsO), a common skin disease.1 In Ontario, Canada, the crude incidence of PsA was 15.3 per 100 000 population and the crude prevalence of PsA was 0.173% as of 2015.2 Within the musculoskeletal system, PsA typically affects the synovium, tendons, ligaments, entheses and articular capsules of joints in an asymmetrical fashion.1 3 Joints subsequently undergo destructive (erosive and proliferative) changes after the initial onset of symptoms, necessitating early detection and treatment. Patients with PsA may also experience symptoms that can profoundly impact their quality of life, including fatigue and a high emotional burden.4 5 The diverse clinical presentations and heterogeneous nature of PsA render diagnosis and management challenging.1

In inflammatory diseases, a flare is defined as the worsening of disease activity, particularly in duration and intensity, which would usually require initiating or changing therapy.6 However, characterising flares in PsA has been challenging due to the heterogeneity of the disease and the broad range of symptoms exhibited by patients. Hence, there is a paucity of data on flares in patients with PsA. Development of the concept of PsA flare through patient and physician consensus exercises has culminated in the creation of the FLARE Questionnaire intended to be used by patients to self-report PsA flares.7

A recent study by Helliwell et al evaluated and validated the FLARE Questionnaire in Leeds, UK.8 They demonstrated good internal consistency among the questionnaire items (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) and test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) = 0.87; 95% CI 0.72 to 0.94). Importantly receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve analysis using Youden Index calculated an optimal cut-off score for PsA flares of ≥4 (area under the curve (AUC) 0.85, sensitivity 0.82 and specificity 0.76). Cohen’s κ of 0.59 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.72) indicated moderate agreement between flare definition using the cut-off and a stand-alone question asking patients whether they thought they were experiencing a flare.

We aimed to evaluate the performance of the FLARE Questionnaire in a cohort of Canadian patients with PsA and compare it to the application of the Psoriatic Arthritis Impact of Disease 12-item Questionnaire (PsAID-12) as an already well-established patient-reported measure of disease impact to the assessment of PsA flares. PsAID-12 was hypothesised to perform similar to the FLARE Questionnaire in identifying PsA flares, given the extensive evidence already supporting its robustness as a patient-reported measure.

Methods

Study population

In this cross-sectional study, we recruited 150 patients with PsA from the Toronto Western Hospital Psoriatic Arthritis Clinic. Eligibility criteria included those who were at least 18 years of age, able to understand and complete questionnaires in English and fulfilled the Classification Criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis.9 All patients enrolled provided their written informed consent.

Demographic and disease characteristics

We collected the date of birth, sex, family history of PsA and PsO, smoking and alcohol consumption status. Patients self reported race and ethnicity through a fixed set of categories and open-ended questions, respectively. We also collected information related to disease status including the dates of PsA and PsO diagnoses, psoriatic nail involvement, presence of axial arthritis, active joint count (AJC, consisting of tender and swollen joints according to the American College of Rheumatology 66 swollen joint count (SJC) and 68 tender joint count (TJC)), damaged joint count (DJC, defined as the number of joints out of 68 with a reduction in the range of movement by >20% of the normal in the absence of joint swelling, deformed, ankylosed, flail joints or joints having been surgically replaced),10 physician global assessment (Numerical Rating Scale 0–10),11 and the current or past treatments for PsA using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (cDMARDs) and targeted disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (tDMARDs). We calculated the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) as a measure of psoriasis severity.12 PASI scores range from 0 to 72 where higher scores indicate greater skin involvement. Routine lab tests included measurements for high-sensitivity C reactive protein (hsCRP). We have previously demonstrated the reliability of these assessments.13 14

Patient-reported outcomes

We administered the following questionnaires at the time of assessment. The patient global assessment evaluated patients’ perceptions of their own health (Numerical Rating Scale 0–10).15 The Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ) assesses five health-related dimensions: disability, pain, effects of medication, costs of care and mortality.16 HAQ scores range from 0 to 3 where higher scores represent worse health. The Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy (FACIT)-Fatigue Scale consists of a general 27-item functional assessment questionnaire with additional 13 fatigue-related questions for the measurement of fatigue perception and severity.17 FACIT-Fatigue scores range from 0 to 52 where lower scores indicate greater fatigue. The Medical Outcomes Survey 36-Item Short-Form (SF-36) assesses mental health, pain, vitality, general health perceptions and limitations in physical, social, or role activities due to physical or emotional problems.18 SF-36 scores range from 0 to 100 where higher scores represent better health status. Pain intensity is measured via self-assessment based on a Numerical Rating Scale from 0 to 10, with higher scores representing worse pain.19 The Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) is a 10-item questionnaire that assesses quality-of-life impact of the disease and its treatment in dermatology patients.20 DLQI scores range from 0 to 30 where higher scores represent worse impact of disease on quality of life. We also asked the study subjects to complete the PsAID-12 Questionnaire that measures disease impact specifically in patients with PsA across 12 domains.21 PsAID-12 scores range from 0 to 10 where higher scores represent a higher disease impact.

Composite disease activity measures

As measures of PsA disease activity, we also measured the following composite indices—the Disease Activity Index for Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA), which is the sum of the number of tender and swollen joints and patient measurements of pain and global disease activity (range 0–10), CRP,22 and the Psoriatic Arthritis Disease Activity Score (PASDAS), a weighted index combining patient and physician measurements of global disease activity, as well as assessments of function, joints, acute-phase response and quality of life. PASDAS ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores representing higher PsA disease activity.22

FLARE instrument

All patients were administered the PsA FLARE Questionnaire, consisting of 10 items that assessed domains such as skin involvement, joint involvement, fatigue and emotional impact.8 Each question was answered ‘yes’ or ‘no’, and all positive answers were tallied to determine the final score.

All patients were also asked the following stand-alone question: ‘Do you think you are having a flare of your disease? Please answer ‘yes’ or ‘no’ to this question. If yes, how long have you been experiencing this flare? Please estimate the duration of the flare’.

Physician assessment

Patients completed all of the necessary questionnaires, including the PsAID-12 and FLARE Questionnaire, prior to their appointment, either online before they arrived at the clinic or in person. The physician then completed their history, physical examination and assessment of the patient without having reviewed the questionnaire scores or the results of the routine labs. The physician assessing the patient was also responsible for creating their management plan.

Statistical analyses

The sample size of 150 patients was consistent with the study by Helliwell et al. We first evaluated the FLARE Questionnaire to compare its performance in a Canadian population with the metrics reported by Helliwell et al. We assessed the internal consistency of the 10 FLARE Questionnaire items using Cronbach’s α. We determined the test-retest reliability using the ICC (mixed model, average measures) by re-administering the FLARE Questionnaire electronically to 51 patients 1 week after the initial visit. We determined the agreement between flare definition using the FLARE Score and stand-alone question using Cohen’s κ.

Associations between clinical features and the self reporting of flares using the stand-alone question were assessed using univariate logistic regressions to determine whether there were significant differences between those with and without self-reported flares. The FLARE Questionnaire and PsAID-12 were then compared against the stand-alone question using ROC curve analysis. AUC was calculated for both questionnaires. Sensitivity and specificity for the FLARE Questionnaire were calculated based on the previous ≥4/10 cut-off determined by Helliwell et al. Sensitivity and specificity for the PsAID-12 were determined for the optimal threshold value for defining flares, which was determined with and without the Youden Index.

All logistic regression and ROC analyses were accomplished with missing data imputed. Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio V.2025.09.2+418.

Results

We recruited 150 patients with PsA. Their demographic and disease characteristics are provided in table 1. A large proportion of patients was receiving disease-modifying therapy, a third using cDMARDs and two-thirds using tDMARDs. The mean DAPSA and PASDAS indicated patients generally had low disease activity. At the initial visit, 47 patients out of 150 believed they were experiencing a flare based on the stand-alone question. Of those, 4% reported a flare duration of <1 week, 11% with 1–2 weeks, 26% with >2–4 weeks, 23% with >4–12 weeks and 26% with >12 weeks. Fifty-five patients self reported having a PsA flare based on the ≥4/10 FLARE cut-off.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the FLARE Questionnaire, using the ≥4/10 cut-off score previously identified by Helliwell et al, and subsequently compare it to the PsAID-12, with its own optimal cut-off score calculated with ROC analyses. The stand-alone question acted as the variable to which both questionnaires could be compared. The goal was to determine whether PsAID-12, being a pre-existing and extensively validated questionnaire, performs similarly to the FLARE Questionnaire.

Results for internal consistency, test-re-test reliability, and agreement between the FLARE Questionnaire and stand-alone question were very similar to those reported by Helliwell et al and results from another study that similarly evaluated the FLARE Questionnaire in Italy.8 23 These similarities suggest that the FLARE Questionnaire performs equally well in a Canadian population of patients with PsA.

In table 2, there was a significant association of TJC with stand-alone question flares. Accordingly, 83% of patients with TJC >4, as an arbitrary threshold for joint involvement, perceived a flare. SJC was significantly associated with stand-alone question flares, although patients experienced swollen joints much less frequently than tender joints as no patient exhibited SJC >4. AJC, which includes both TJC and SJC, was significantly associated with stand-alone question flares. The proportion of patients perceiving a flare with AJC >4 was also 83%, reflecting the predominance of tender joints over swollen joints contributing to AJC. hsCRP was not associated with stand-alone question flares, and only 40% of patients with hsCRP >3, generally considered high, perceived a flare. DJC demonstrated no association with stand-alone question flares, which may have been because DJC is more representative of damage rather than disease activity. The lack of association of PASI with stand-alone question flares may suggest the predominance of joint symptoms as the driving factors for flares. PsA is differentiated from PsO in a similar fashion. Correspondingly, skin irritation was one of the least frequently reported items of the questionnaire.

When comparing the ability of the FLARE Questionnaire and the PsAID-12 to predict flares using the stand-alone question as the reference, both demonstrated high AUC values but there was no statistically significant difference between them. This result suggests both questionnaires demonstrate excellent discrimination between self-reported flare and non-flare states.

The ideal PsAID-12 cut-off may be determined either by maximising the Youden Index or by selecting the most appropriate balance between sensitivity and specificity. Recent literature has suggested that maximising specificity rather than Youden Index is more ideal for determining PsAID-12 thresholds as it is more clinically useful and minimises false positives.24 Furthermore, previous studies investigating the PsAID-12 have identified thresholds typically between 3 and 4 as being indicative of clinically significant disease worsening.21 25 Therefore, this study recommends using the PsAID-12 cut-off of ≥3.13 for flare definition since it maximises specificity rather than being constrained by the Youden Index, maintains a good balance between sensitivity and specificity, and more closely aligns with reports of clinically significant disease worsening in the literature.

Compared with the FLARE Questionnaire, PsAID-12 is already an extensively validated questionnaire with strong evidence supporting its ability to assess impact of disease, which has rendered it a widely accepted patient-reported outcome measure.21 PsAID-12 assesses similar domains of health compared with the FLARE Questionnaire, including physical impact, emotional impact and quality of life. However, PsAID-12 is broader in scope because it includes questions that target the social impact of disease, representing another potential factor contributing to PsA flares. PsAID-12 is also frequently administered to patients enrolled in PsA clinical studies, alongside other routine surveys that are often lengthy. Each additional questionnaire that patients must complete increases the burden of participation in clinical studies; therefore, scrutinising which questionnaires are added into the routine research protocol and eliminating redundant items is necessary. This study demonstrates that the FLARE Questionnaire may be a redundant questionnaire when the PsAID-12 is instead able to assess flare status.

Since patient perception of flares may be multifactorial, there is a possibility that adverse effects of current medications, such as DMARDs, may play a role in self reporting flares. Although this factor was not further investigated in this study, it is presumed to have minimal impact on flare perception, as these medications are typically prescribed long-term and are adjusted on a case-by-case basis with the goal of managing disease. As part of the stand-alone question, patients were asked to estimate the duration of their flare if they believed they were experiencing one. A duration of at least 4 weeks was self reported in approximately 50% of patients self reporting a flare, which was similarly observed by Helliwell et al. The long duration of flare symptoms experienced by patients highlights the need to further evaluate flares over time in a future longitudinal study using the FLARE and PsAID-12 questionnaires. A longitudinal study will further be able to assess changes in flare status over time, which would address the limitation of the cross-sectional design of this study. Furthermore, the use of mobile health applications that would allow patients to complete flare assessments remotely in between clinic visits represents another avenue for future research that would explore the ability of these questionnaires to track and identify new flares over time. These future directions represent the next steps towards better understanding PsA flares, with the goal of improving disease management for patients with PsA.

In conclusion, this study has demonstrated that PsAID-12 with a ≥3.13 cut-off may be used as an instrument for assessing PsA flares because of its high sensitivity and broad scope of questionnaire items. The subjective patient perception of flare duration warrants further investigation of both questionnaires in a longitudinal study to further compare their ability to define as well as better characterise PsA flares.
