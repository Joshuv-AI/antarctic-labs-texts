Decoding SDCBP’s role in tumor progression and immune cells infiltration: from databases to macrophage validation
Authors: Chanyuan Zhao, Chenli Zhang, Xiaoyu Zhang, Cunpu Du, Wei Liu, Quan Zhou, Jiaming Dong, Feng Yang, Yun Dang, Aijun Yang, Min Wang, Chenyu Wang, Min Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The search for highly sensitive and specific tumor biomarkers is essential for the evaluation of prognosis and new therapeutic targets. Immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) have dual roles in promoting and suppressing tumor growth, which is crucial for the development and treatment of cancer. According to previous studies, syndecan binding protein (SDCBP) participated in biological processes like epithelial-mesenchymal transformation, angiogenesis, and exosome formation and secretion during cancer metastasis, but whether SDCBP affects immune cells infiltration in the TME remains unclear. Here, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of multiple databases to explore the expression and prognosis of SDCBP in pan-cancer and focused on the effect of SDCBP on the immune cells, particularly on macrophages. The study found that SDCBP was expressed at different levels in most tumor tissues and cells. Upregulation of SDCBP was associated with malignant progression, poor patient prognosis and treatment non-response in certain tumors. Gene set enrichment analysis suggested that SDCBP was mainly involved in regulating immunity and inflammation-related pathways, which may be closely associated with the high levels of immune cells infiltration. Further experiments confirmed that SDCBP is highly expressed in macrophages, and knockdown of SDCBP in THP-1-derived macrophages reduces their chemotaxis but does not affect their polarization and phagocytic function. Our study indicated that SDCBP involves in tumor progression and macrophages infiltration, providing novel perspectives for cancer, particularly in combination with immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04316-y.

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022, the global cancer burden is further increasing, with nearly 20 million new cancer cases reported worldwide in recent years, and about half of these cases being cancer-related deaths [1]. Despite ongoing breakthroughs in cancer prevention, diagnosis and treatment, clinical outcomes remain unsatisfactory, mainly due to the complexity of cancer evolution and the emergence of therapeutic resistance [2]. Pan-cancer studies could help us to discover both similarities and differences in genomic and cellular alteration across various tumor types, and to find certain universal patterns providing new perspectives for improving cancer diagnostic and treatment.

Syndecan binding protein (SDCBP), also known as syntenin, is a scaffold protein containing two PDZ domains. It was first discovered in melanoma by Fisher et al. in 1996 [3], and was also referred to as melanoma differentiation related protein 9 (MDA-9) due to its crucial role in melanoma differentiation. Under physiological conditions, SDCBP participates in various important cellular processes and signal pathways, such as exosome biogenesis, immune regulation, neurodevelopment modulation, and cell adhesion [4]. In addition, elevated levels of SDCBP in various tumor cells have been associated with increased cell proliferation, stemness and treatment resistance [5, 6]. The primary mechanism involves the interaction of SDCBP’s PDZ domain with various oncoproteins, including FAK, c-Src, EGFR, IGF-1R, TGF-βR, Slug and VEGFR, activating the signaling cascades of p38/MAPK, STAT3, NF-κB, PI3K/Akt and TGF-β/Smad [7], thereby influencing multiple steps in tumor metastasis, such as epithelial-mesenchymal transformation (EMT), angiogenesis, exosome formation and secretion, and tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling [8].

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are key components of the TME, playing multifaceted roles in tumor progression, metastasis and therapeutic resistance [9]. Originating primarily from circulating monocytes, TAMs are recruited to the tumor site by various chemokines, including CCL2, CCL5, and CSF-1 [10]. Unlike classically activated (M1) macrophages, which exhibit pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor properties, M2-like TAMs promote tumor growth, angiogenesis, immune suppression and metastasis [11]. Emerging evidence highlights TAMs as both prognostic markers and therapeutic targets, with high TAMs infiltration often correlating with poor clinical outcomes, emphasizing their significance in tumor biology [12]. Ongoing research is investigating strategies to reprogram TAMs from a pro-tumor M2 to an anti-tumor M1 phenotype, or selectively deplete TAMs, as potential approaches to improve cancer therapies [13].

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of SDCBP by using multiple bioinformatics tools and methods. Our investigation focused on the mRNA and protein expression levels of SDCBP, as well as its potential as a prognostic and diagnostic biomarker, and its role in therapeutic response and immune-related signatures. Additionally, we examined the expression patterns and functional roles of SDCBP in macrophages, aiming to uncover its potential applications and clinical significances as a novel therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

SDCBP expression in tumor cell lines and tissues

Information on the databases used in the manuscript was presented in Table S1. Tumor cell lines expression profiling data were obtained from CCLE. Normalized pan-cancer data in TCGA and GTEx were downloaded from the UCSC database. Protein expression data were acquired from the CPTAC module of UCLAN. SDCBP protein analysis in pan-cancer tissues were obtained from the HPA database. Differences in SDCBP expression between tumor and normal tissues, pathologic stages, histologic grades, and tumor, normal and metastatic tissues were achieved using SangerBox. The single-cell sequencing data of tumor datasets were analyzed via the TISCH2 website.

Analysis of prognosis and diagnostic value of SDCBP

Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves were visualized using the “survival” R package to demonstrate the effect of SDCBP expression on OS and DSS of patients. The optimal cutoff value of SDCBP was calculated using the “maxstat” R package, with the minimum group sample size set at greater than 25% and the maximum group sample size at less than 75%, and patients were divided into high and low groups based on the final optimal cutoff value. The effect of SDCBP expression levels on patient prognosis were verified using Kaplan-Meier plotter database. The diagnostic value of SDCBP was assessed by plotting the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve using the “pROC” R package, and the diagnostic accuracy was measured by the area under curve (AUC).

Predictive potential of SDCBP in therapeutic outcomes

The relationship between SDCBP expression levels and drug response was determined by ROC plotter. The correlation of SDCBP with tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) were demonstrated by radar plotting with the “fmsb” R package. Heatmaps of the correlation between SDCBP expression and mismatch repair genes (MMRs), DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) were achieved by Pearson’s correlation analysis. The predictive power of SDCBP with seven established biomarkers in immunotherapy efficacy across various cancer types were achieved via the “Biomarker Evaluation” module within the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) platform.

Correlation of SDCBP with tumor immunity signatures

The correlation of SDCBP expression with Immune scores, Stromal scores and ESTIMATE scores were evaluated using the “estimate” R package. The TIMER database was used to analyzed the infiltration of immune cells or immunosuppressive cells in each tumor and were validated by the QUANTISEQ and MCPCOUNTER algorithm. Heatmaps of the correlation between SDCBP expression and immune checkpoint genes were achieved by Pearson’s correlation analysis.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of SDCBP

All cancer samples were categorized into high and low expression groups based on the median expression level of SDCBP. Differential gene expression (log2 fold change) was calculated using the “limma” R package, and genes were ranked accordingly. GSEA was performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package with KEGG and HALLMARK gene sets. The analysis was conducted with 1000 permutations, and the false discovery rate (FDR) value was used to adjust p-values. Pathways with |NES| > 1, p-value < 0.05, and FDR < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched.

Double Immunofluorescence labeling

The 4 μm stomach cancer sections were dewaxed, hydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval with citrate buffer (pH 8.0) in a microwave oven. After natural cooling, the slides were washed three times in PBS for 5 min each time and blocked with 10% goat serum at room temperature for 30 min. The sections were incubated with Syntenin-1 antibody (Genetex, #GTX108470, 1:250) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Goat Anti Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594 (Immunoway, #RS3611, 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with anti-CD68 antibody (Abcam, #ab213363, 1:500), followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody, Goat Anti Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 (Immunoway, #RS3211, 1:200). After each incubation, the slides were washed three times in PBS for 5 min each time. Finally, the slides were mounted with a medium containing DAPI and scanned using a Nikon fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan) to capture images.

Cell lines and cell culture

MKN45 and MGC803 stomach cancer cells were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and stored in our lab. THP-1 monocytes were resourced from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Stomach cancer cells were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, while THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium under the same conditions at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2.

THP-1 cells were differentiated into M0 macrophages using 100 ng/mL PMA and then polarize to M1 with LPS (100 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL), or to M2 with IL-4 (20 ng/mL) and IL-13 (20 ng/mL) for 48 h. M0 macrophages were co-cultured with stomach cancer cells (1 × 106) in 0.4 μm Transwell system (Corning, USA) for 48 h to obtain TAMs.

Western blot

Cell lysate was prepared using a RIPA buffer with phosphatase inhibitor and PMSF, lysed on ice and sonicated. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected for protein quantification by BCA assay. Proteins were mixed with loading buffer (4:1) and denatured at 95 ℃, and the protein samples were electrophoresed on a polyacrylamide gel and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk, incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, and visualized using the BIO Imaging System (VILBER, France). β-tubulin and GAPDH served as loading controls.

Lentivirus transfection

Lentiviral vectors targeting SDCBP were generated by Genechem Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). THP-1 cells (2 × 105) were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with GV493-SDCBP-shRNA or control vectors at an MOI of 30. The efficiency of SDCBP knockdown was evaluated by RT-qPCR and western blot analysis in the transfected cells.

Transwell chemotaxis assay

THP-1 monocyte suspension was treated with 200 ng/mL PMA (1:1 ratio) for 48 h to differentiate into M0 macrophages with 8 μm pore size Transwell inserts (Corning, USA). After medium replacement with serum-free conditions, the inserts were placed in wells containing 600 µL 20% FBS medium and co-cultured for 72 h. At the end of the experiment, the inserts were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Five random fields were imaged, and cell counts were determined using Image J software (NIH, USA).

Immunofluorescence

50,000 cells/well were plated in 24-well plates on slides. After fixation with 4% PFA for 15 min and permeabilization with 0.1% Triton-X 100 for 10 min, cells were blocked with 3% BSA. Primary antibodies for Syntenin-1 (Proteintech, #22399-1-AP, 1:100), NOS2 (Proteintech, #18985-1-AP, 1:200) and CD163 (Proteintech, #16646-1-AP, 1:200) were applied respectively and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Goat Anti Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594 (Immunoway, #RS3611, 1:200) was used as secondary antibody and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Slides were mounted with DAPI-containing medium and imaged using a Nikon fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Flow cytometry

A million macrophages were harvested in staining buffer and blocked with Human TruStain FcX™ (Biolegend, #422301, USA). They were then stained with APC anti-CD86 (Biolegend, #374207, USA) and PE anti-CD163 (Biolegend, #333605, USA) on ice for 30 min. After washing, the cells were resuspended in staining buffer and analyzed by the NovoCyte Quanteon Flow Cytometry (Agilent, Singapore).

Macrophage phagocytosis assay

1.5 × 106 macrophages were subjected to serum starvation for 4 h. 2 μm fluorescent latex beads (Sigma-Aldrich, #L3030, USA) were diluted in medium at a ratio of 1:100 and added to macrophages. After 24 h incubation, the cells were washed and harvested in PBS, and detected by NovoCyte Quanteon Flow Cytometry (Agilent, Singapore).

Statistical analysis

Differential in gene expression between different cancer cell lines were performed using the Kruskal Wallis test. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was utilized to analysis differences in gene expression between tumor and normal tissues. One-way ANOVA was performed for three or more groups comparisons. OS and DFS survival curves were computed by Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log rank test. p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlations analyses were performed using either Spearman or Pearson’s correlation depend on the data distribution, and the Benjamini-Hochberg method was utilized for multiple testing correction, with adjusted p-value < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

SDCBP expression varied across cancer types and correlated with clinical features

We first analyzed the mRNA expression of SDCBP across tumor cell lines and TCGA data. Our results revealed that SDCBP was widely highly expressed in 21 kinds of tumor cell lines, with the highest in skin tumor cells and the lowest in hematopoietic and lymphoid tissue tumor cells (Fig. 1A). Analysis of TCGA data further suggested that SDCBP expression was upregulated in 7 types of cancer and downregulated in 8 types (Fig. 1B). By integrating normal tissue expression data from the GTEx database, we found that SDCBP was upregulated in 11 tumor types, including CHOL, COAD, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KIRP, LGG, LIHC, PAAD, PRAD and STAD, while downregulated in 9 tumor types, such as BLCA, BRCA, KICH, LUAD, LUSC, OV, READ, THCA and UCEC (Fig. 1C). The protein expression patterns in CPTAC samples were generally consistent with the mRNA expression, with the except of HNSC (Fig. S1A).

Further exploration of the correlation between SDCBP expression and clinicopathological features suggested that high SDCBP expression was significantly correlated with advanced pathologic stages in KIPAN (KICH + KIRC + KIRP), STAD, THYM and KICH, with the lowest levels in stage I and the highest in stage IV (Fig. S1B). Additionally, SDCBP overexpression correlated with higher histologic grades of GBM and LGG, HNSC and PAAD (Fig. S1C). SDCBP expression was upregulated in metastatic samples from CHOL, ESCA, KIRP and STAD, but downregulated in metastatic samples from BRCA, LUAD, LUSC and THCA (Fig. S1D).

SDCBP served as a biomarker for cancer prognosis, diagnosis, and therapy response in multiple cancers

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that increased SDCBP expression were risk factors for OS (overall survival) in patients with HNSC, KICH, LGG, LUSC, PAAD, STAD and UVM, as well as for DSS (disease specific survival) in patients with HNSC, KICH, KIRP, LGG, LUSC, PAAD, UCEC and UVM (Fig. S2A-B). Analysis using the Kaplan-Meier plotter database confirmed that patients with elevated SDCBP expression experienced shorter OS and RFS (relapse-free survival) in HNSC, LUAD, LUSC, KIRP and PDAC (Fig. S2C-D).

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that SDCBP exhibited excellent diagnostic value for 12 tumor types (CESC, CHOL, GBM, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, PAAD, SKCM, TGCT, THCA), with an AUC greater than 0.8, and an acceptable diagnostic value for 8 tumor types (BLCA, BRCA, HNSC, KICH, KIRP, OSCC, STAD, THYM), with AUC values greater than 0.7 but less than 0.8 (Fig. S3).

Histograms and ROC curves for predictive biomarkers of treatment response revealed that patients with elevated SDCBP expression were less likely to respond to immunotherapy with PD-1 or CTLA-4 inhibitors, whereas no significant difference was observed with PD-L1 inhibitors (Fig. S4A). Especially, patients with lower SDCBP expression experimented more favorable response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in melanoma, glioblastoma and stomach cancer cohorts (Fig. S4B). Additionally, in breast cancer patients receiving anti-HER2 treatments, as well as in chemotherapy cohorts of glioblastoma and colorectal cancer, SDCBP expression levels were higher in the non-response groups (Fig. S4C).

In assessment of SDCBP’s predictive value by comparing it with established biomarkers for treatment response, we found that SDCBP expression was positively correlated with TMB in COAD, ESCA, LGG, PRAD, SKCM and STAD, MSI in COAD, KIRC, READ and UVM, and five DNA mismatch repair genes in BRCA, CESC, COAD, HNSC, KIRP, LGG, LIHC, PAAD, PRAD, READ, THCA and UCEC. Tumors with high SDCBP expression exhibited elevated DNMTs, except in UCS and ESCA (Fig. S5A-D). Moreover, SDCBP had an AUC value greater than 0.5 in 12 ICB cohorts, with a predictive power comparable to MSI scores and superior to TMB, T or B. Clonality, but inferior to CD274, TIDE, IFNG, and CD8 (Fig. S5E). These results suggested that SDCBP has the potential to serve as a biomarker for the prognosis, diagnosis, and therapy response in certain tumors.

SDCBP regulated immune and inflammation-related signaling pathways in tumors

GSEA was performed to identify biological pathways enriched in SDCBP-associated differential genes within the KEGG and HALLMARK datasets. Table S2 and S3 listed the top 20 representative pathways associated with SDCBP expression, respectively. The top 3 biological pathways enriched in each dataset were visualized in Fig. 2. Specifically, KEGG analysis indicated that elevated SDCBP expression was correlated with apoptosis, Toll-like receptor and chemokine signaling pathways (Table S2). HALLMARK analysis revealed that upregulation of SDCBP mainly affected the complement pathway, KRAS pathway, inflammatory response, IL2_STAT5, and IL6_JAK_STAT3 pathway (Table S3).

SDCBP modulated immune cells infiltration in the TME

Functional enrichment analysis suggested that SDCBP potentially contributed to the regulation of TME by modulating immune and inflammation-related pathways. We calculated the correlation between SDCBP expression and immune, stromal and ESTIMATE scores, and found that SDCBP was significantly correlated with these three scores (Fig. S6A). The highest correlation with stromal scores were observed in KIRC, LGG and PCPG, while the highest correlation with immune scores were found in LGG, BRCA and OV, the highest correlation with ESTIMATE scores were seen in DLBC, LGG and BRCA (Fig. S6B).

Further analysis of the correlation between SDCBP and immune cells infiltration demonstrated that SDCBP was positively correlated with six immune cells infiltration in BLCA, BRCA, CHOL, COAD, KIRC, LGG, LUAD, LUSC, OV, PCPG, PRAD, and THYM (Fig. 3A). The top three tumor types with the highest correlation with immune cells infiltration were DLBC, BRCA and CHOL (Fig. S6C). Notably, the correlation between SDCBP and immune checkpoint genes shown that SDCBP was mainly positively correlated with the expression of NRP1, LAIR1, HAVCR2, CD80 and CD86 (Fig. S7). Further analysis found a significantly positively correlation between SDCBP and the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells, including Tregs, M2-TAMs and CAFs infiltration in multiple tumor types (Fig. 3B).

To elucidate the impact of SDCBP expression on macrophage functions, we employed lentiviral vectors to knockdown SDCBP in THP-1-derived macrophages (Fig. S10A-D). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that SDCBP knockdown had no significant effect on the polarization of M0, M1, or M2 macrophages, as evidenced by the expression levels of M1 marker CD86 and M2 marker CD163 (Fig. 6A-B). Observations of cell morphology and immunofluorescence analysis of the NOS2 and CD163 markers also revealed no significant differences (Fig. S10E-F). Western blot results further confirmed that SDCBP knockdown did not affect the expression of M1 polarization-related markers p-STAT1 and IRF3 (Fig. 6C). Additionally, phagocytosis assays with 2 μm fluorescent latex beads showed no significant differences between macrophages with and without SDCBP knockdown (Fig. 6D-E). However, Transwell migration assays revealed that SDCBP knockdown reduced chemotactic ability of M0 macrophages (Fig. 6F-G). RT-qPCR detection revealed a significant down-regulation of several chemokines implicated in macrophage chemotaxis, such as CCL2, in SDCBP-knockdown macrophages (Fig. S11). These results suggested that SDCBP expression in macrophages may affect their chemotactic capacity via chemokine signaling, but does not influence their polarization state and phagocytosis ability.

Discussion

Pan-cancer analysis facilitates more comprehensive investigation into cancer by elucidating similarities and differences across multiple tumors, helping to identify new biomarkers and therapeutic targets [14]. SDCBP has been implicated in epithelial-mesenchymal transformation, angiogenesis, stemness, chemoresistance, and exosomes biogenesis in tumor progression [8], but its diagnostic and prognostic significance, as well as its role in immune infiltration warrant further investigation. This paper presented a systematic analysis based on multiple databases such as TCGA to provide an overview of SDCBP’s role in cancers. SDCBP exhibited differential expression across various cancer types, which associated with malignant progression and poor prognosis of patients. The ROC curves and AUC values suggested SDCBP had considerable diagnostic sensitivity in distinguishing tumor patients from healthy individuals. SDCBP expression also affected the efficacy of anti-tumor therapy and the infiltration of immune cells, suggesting its potential as a molecular marker for tumor diagnostic, prognostic assessment and treatment guidance.

Analysis of SDCBP expression features showed that SDCBP was generally overexpressed in various tumor cell lines and tissues (Fig. 1). This overexpression was correlated with pathological grades, histologic stages, and metastasis status in several tumor types (Fig. S1). Survival prognosis analysis demonstrated that SDCBP served as a risk factor, associated with poorer prognosis in HNSC, KIRP, LGG, PAAD and STAD (Fig. S2). Similarly, previous studies have reported upregulation of SDCBP in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [15], pancreatic cancer [5], colorectal cancer [16], and hepatocellular cancer [17], and high SDCBP was correlated with malignant features. Nevertheless, our findings were contrary to those reported in lung cancer [18] and breast cancer [19], where SDCBP expression were found to be upregulated in tumor tissues, while our analysis of BRCA, LUAD and LUSC exhibited lower SDCBP expression in both tumor and metastatic tissues (Fig. 1 & Fig. S1). This was despite the fact that high SDCBP predicted worse survival outcomes. This discrepancy may reflect the heterogeneity in gene expression across different tumor subtypes and within different regions of the tumor tissues, sample selection from specific subtypes or different regions may exhibit distinct patterns of SDCBP expression. Future research needs to further verify the specific expression and regulation of SDCBP in different tumor subtypes.

TME is a complex ecosystem, comprising not only tumor cells but also a variety of immune and stromal cells, all contributing to tumor growth and immune escape, and playing pivotal roles in modulating therapeutic responses and dictating the success of cancer treatments [20]. GSEA analysis implied that SDCBP was mainly involved in the regulation of immune and inflammation-related pathways (Fig. 2 & Table S2&S3). Further analysis of the correlation between SDCBP and immune cells infiltration, as well as immune checkpoint genes revealed that high expression of SDCBP was not only positively associated with the infiltration of six common immune cells, but also with the infiltration of M2-TAMs, Tregs, and CAFs, as well as the expression of NRP1, LAIR1, HAVCR2, CD80, and CD86 (Fig. 3 & Fig. S7). These findings suggested that overexpression of SDCBP in tumors may not impede the infiltration of antigen presenting cells and T cells, but could instead suppress their tumoricidal activities [21]. This is because high expression of NRP1, LAIR1, HAVCR2, CD80, and CD86 could lead to T cell dysfunction and exhaustion, induce immune escape, and ultimately affect the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor in immunotherapy [22–24]. Meanwhile, M2-TAMs, Tregs, and CAFs collaboratively contribute to tumor immune suppression through various mechanisms, including the secretion of immunosuppressive factors, recruitment and activation of immunosuppressive cells, and inhibition of effector T cell functions [25]. These results offered supportive evidence that SDCBP promoted the formation of tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. Our findings were consistent with a triple-negative breast cancer study, which demonstrated that SDCBP upregulated PD-L1 and induced apoptosis of CD8+ T cells [26].

Macrophages, particularly TAMs, have been shown to play crucial roles in shaping the TME by producing pro-tumorigenic cytokines and promoting tumor progression through immunosuppression and angiogenesis [27]. We found that SDCBP expression was predominantly localized in macrophages, providing new insights into its role in immune regulation. Previous studies have indicated that SDCBP is one of the co-expressed genes in M2 macrophages in renal clear cell carcinoma, but its function has not been investigated [28]. We observed that knockdown of SDCBP in THP-1-derived macrophages significantly affected their chemotactic ability, but not their polarization levels or phagocytic capacity (Fig. 6). This aligns with the role of SDCBP in cellular migration [29, 30]. D. Manna [17] and J. Yu [31] et al. also found that inhibiting SDCBP in tumor cells could significantly reduce macrophage infiltration, especially M2 macrophages. Furthermore, chemokines and their receptors are crucial in directing the migration of macrophages [32, 33]. Our RT-qPCR results preliminarily suggested that SDCBP regulates the expression of multiple chemokines, especially CCL2, but the exact molecular mechanisms by which SDCBP regulates macrophage chemotaxis remain to be fully elucidated. GSEA analysis suggested that SDCBP may regulate key signaling pathways such as JAK/STAT3 or TNF-α/NF-κB signaling pathway, which are known to regulate CCL2 expression [34, 35]. Future studies involving signaling pathway or rescue experiments are warranted to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the role of SDCBP in macrophage chemotaxis.

In terms of therapeutic implications, our findings observed that patients with high expression levels of SDCBP exhibited a poorer response to ICIs. Previous studies have found that SDCBP inhibitor combined with anti-PD1 had exhibited considerable potential to enhance immunotherapy efficacy in colorectal cancer [31]. Elevated expression of biomarkers such as TMB and MSI can be used to predict the response to various treatments, particularly immunotherapy [36]. Our results revealed a weak positive correlation between SDCBP expression and TMB or MSI, whereas the expression of SDCBP was significantly positively correlated with MMRs and DNMTs (Fig. S5), which meant that tumors with high SDCBP expression were mostly microsatellite stability or low instability, which were insensitive to anti-tumor therapy [37]. SDCBP’s role in DNA methylation dysregulation may silence tumor suppressor genes or activate oncogenes, thereby influencing tumor progression and treatment response [38]. TIDE analysis suggested SDCBP’s predictive power for immunotherapy outcomes was superior to standardized biomarkers like TMB in certain cohorts (Fig. S5E), but it underperformed compared to biomarkers like CD274 (PD-L1), IFNG, CD8. This positions SDCBP as a complementary rather than standalone biomarker, but a direct head-to-head clinical cohort comparison would substantiate this claim more convincingly in the future. On the other hand, TAMs express immunosuppressive factors, such as PD-1 and PD-L1, as well as FcγRI, leading to T-cell dysfunction and exhaustion and significantly affecting the immunotherapeutic efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [39]. Given SDCBP’s role in promoting macrophage recruitment, therapeutic strategies centered on inhibiting the recruitment of monocyte-derived macrophages may synergize with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors to reverse the TME, this approach warrants further investigation in preclinical models. Similar to other PDZ-domain proteins, PDZK1 is a gene highly associated with HBV infection, and tissues with high PDZK1 expression exhibit increased infiltration of macrophages and Treg cells [40]. NHERF1 has also been found to be expressed in the cytoplasm of tumor cells and in most lymphocytes present in the tumor and lymphoid stroma, and it is involved in the functional regulation of immune cells, including macrophages [41]. The roles of these two proteins in regulating immune evasion will further provide novel insights into the roles of PDZ-domain proteins in immunity. Future research should explore the combination of PDZ-domain inhibitors with immunotherapy to enhance antitumor effects.

Nevertheless, there are some limitations to this study. In this study, we conducted a preliminary exploration of the role of SDCBP in regulating macrophages through bioinformatics analysis and THP-1 cell line model. In the future, we will conduct in-depth research on the mechanism by which SDCBP regulates macrophages through chemokine signal transduction or cytoskeleton reorganization. Primary patient-derived TAMs will be used to verify the differences in SDCBP-mediated macrophage functions across different cancer types. This will confirm SDCBP’s role in macrophage chemotaxis and immune cell infiltration across different TME, thereby enhancing its clinical translational potential.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that SDCBP plays a critical role in regulating tumor progression, immune cells infiltration, and response to immunotherapy. Targeting SDCBP may offer a novel approach for modulating the TME and enhancing the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.
