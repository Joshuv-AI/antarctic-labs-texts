NaHS protects brain, heart, and lungs as remote organs from renal ischemia/reperfusion-induced oxidative stress in male and female rats
Authors: Shadan Saberi, Hamid Najafipour, Mohammad Amin Rajizadeh, Abbas Etminan, Elham Jafari, Maryam Iranpour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute Kidney Injury (AKI) is frequently observed in hospitalized patients in intensive care units, often caused by renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI). IRI disrupts the function of various ‘remote organs’ such as the lungs, pancreas, intestine, liver, heart, and brain through inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, leukocyte infiltration, and increased urea and creatinine levels. Gender differences in renal IRI-induced injury are noted. H2S, an endogenous gaseous modulator, shows potential in vasodilation, bronchodilation, and hypotension and can regulate apoptosis, inflammation, angiogenesis, metabolism, and oxidative stress. This study aims to investigate the protective effects of NaHS on brain, heart, and lung injuries following renal IR and to assess the oxidative system status as a potential mechanism in male and female rats.Forty-eight Wistar rats were randomly divided into eight groups (n = 6): Control/Saline, Sham/Saline, IR/Saline, and IR/NaHS in both sexes. Forty-five minutes of bilateral renal ischemia followed by 24-hour reperfusion was induced in the IR groups. NaHS (100µM/Kg, IP) was administered 10 min before clamp release in treated groups. BUN, SCr, BUN/SCr, albuminuria, histopathology, and oxidative stress biomarkers of the brain, heart, and lung were assessed as remote organs. IR increased serum markers of renal function, albuminuria, malondialdehyde levels, and tissue injury scores while reducing nitrite levels and superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase activities. NaHS treatment reversed the adverse effects of IR in remote organs in both sexes, although it showed limited improvement in renal function. Our findings demonstrate that NaHS has a beneficial effect on remote organ injury following renal IR by mitigating oxidative stress, with noted tissue-specific and gender-specific differences in response. These findings suggest NaHS as a potential therapeutic agent for mitigating multi-organ injury after renal IR, with effects varying by tissue and gender.Keywords: Renal IRI, Remote organ damage, Oxidative stress, Sodium Hydrosulfide, Gender differences

Introduction

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) is a common clinical syndrome observed in hospitalized patients in intensive care units, with mortality rates reaching up to 50% [1]. Renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) is a major cause of AKI, arising from events such as myocardial infarction, sepsis, organ transplantation, and trauma, which elevate reactive oxygen species production and inflammatory cytokines and activate leukocytes [2]. Researches indicate that AKI resulting from renal IRI disrupts the function of various ‘remote organs’ like the lungs, pancreas, intestine, liver, heart, and brain due to inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, leukocyte infiltration, and increased levels of urea and creatinine [3, 4]. Studies have shown decreased Bcl2 expression and increased Bax and NF-κB expression, indicating apoptosis and inflammation as mechanisms of brain injury one hour after renal IR; brain damage becomes more pronounced with extended ischemia periods [5]. In mice, 60 min of renal ischemia followed by 24 h of reperfusion leads to increased brain microgliosis, pyknosis, and locomotor dysfunction [6]. Additionally, renal IR can cause lung and heart damage through inflammatory processes [7, 8].

There is ongoing debate regarding gender differences in renal injury induced by ischemia-reperfusion [9, 10]. Evidence suggests that sex steroids or the testosterone-to-estrogen ratio contribute to observed differences in kidney dysfunction due to renal IR [11]. Azarkish et al. reported more significant brain injury from renal IRI in male rats compared to females [12].

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) is a gaseous modulator with several beneficial biological effects, produced endogenously in the body. Its vasodilatory, bronchodilatory, and hypotensive functions are well-documented [13, 14]. H2S influences the expression and activation of signaling pathway proteins involved in apoptosis, inflammation, angiogenesis, metabolism, and oxidative stress by regulating kinases, reactive oxygen scavengers, and transcription factor activity [15]. Improvement in myocardial injury induced by renal IR has been reported in diabetic rats via activation of the H2S production pathway [16]. Previous studies have confirmed the neuroprotective effect of the H2S donor NaHS against traumatic brain injury-induced memory impairment [17]. However, the protective effects of H2S on brain, heart, and lung injuries associated with renal IR are not well understood. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether NaHS has a protective effect on brain, heart, and lung damage following renal IR and to assess the oxidative system status as a possible mechanism in male and female rats.

Materials and methods

Animals

This research is an experimental study. 48 male and female Wistar rats (weight: 200–250 g) were purchased from the Kerman University Medical Sciences animal farm (Ethic No. IR.KMU.REC.1399.311(. The animals were kept in standard condition (23℃±2 room temperature, 12 h:12 h light-dark cycle started at 7 am, and free access to water and food ad libitum), then divided into eight groups: male control (Control/Saline), male sham (Sham/Saline), male renal ischemia/reperfusion (IR/Saline), male renal ischemia/reperfusion treated by NaHS (IR/NaHS) (100µM/Kg, NaHS was purchased from Sigma Aldrich Co, USA) [18, 19], and four female groups similar to the male groups.

Study design

After one week of acclimatization, the animals of sham-operated and I/R groups under anesthesia by ketamine (50 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) underwent surgery [20]. The right and left planks were opened to access the kidneys, and then renal pedicles were clamped for 45 min, followed by a 24-hour clamp release as reperfusion. Animals of sham groups received the same intervention except for the clamping of renal vessels. NaHS was administered intraperitoneally 10 min before the reperfusion phase. Finally, albuminuria was measured to confirm renal injury by collecting 24-hour urine samples from individual animals placed in metabolic cages. Then the animals were sacrificed with a lethal dose of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (80 mg/kg) and blood samples were collected by heart puncture, and the left kidney, left hemisphere of the brain, lung, and heart tissues were removed from the body of rats and fixed in 10% formalin for histopathologic evaluations. For biochemical studies, the right hemisphere of the brain, right kidney, right lung, and half of the heart were stored in a -80℃ freezer.

Histopathologic evaluations

Tissue samples were harvested, washed with normal saline, and then fixed in 10% buffered formalin. The fixed tissues were embedded into the paraffin wax and sliced using a microtome into the tiny Sects. (3–5 μm). H&E-stained sections were evaluated. In the brain tissue, the percentage of cortical apoptotic neurons and brain edema (Brain edema was determined based on the spaces observed in extracellular spaces and, separately, in parenchymal cells. It was graded in the following manner: mild (when edema was < 10%), moderate (when edema was 10–50%), severe (when edema was > 50%), and brain vascular congestion (0 = none, 1 = minimal, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, 4 = marked, and 5 = severe) were measured based on the previous research [21, 22]. In the heart tissue, inflammation and cardiomyocyte necrosis were all scored according to the following scale: 0 = absent, 0.5 = rare, 1.0 = 1–10% of the myocardial area affected, 2.0 = 10 − 25% area affected, 3.0 = 25–50% area affected, and 4.0 = 50% area affected and total injury (0 = none, 1 = minimum, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, and 4 = severe) were assessed [23, 24].The degree of lung injury, alveolar collapse, and neutrophil infiltration ((0 = very mild, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe, and 4 = extremely severe) were investigated in lung tissues through other study methods [25, 26]. Kidney tissue damage score (KTDS) was explained from 1 to 4, and the presence of acute tubular damage, such as tubular dilation and degeneration, tubular cell swelling and necrosis, tubular casts, glomerulosclerosis, and intraluminal cell debris with inflammatory cell infiltration was considered. Score of zero was assigned to normal tissue [27].

Biochemical analysis

The serum levels of BUN (blood urea nitrogen), Cr (SCr) (serum creatinine) and urine albumin were measured by an auto-analyzer (Selectra-XL, Vital Science, Netherlands) using commercial kits (Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran). The tissues were sonicated on ice, and the Bradford method was used to determine the total protein in all homogenate tissues. Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured using the thiobarbituric acid (TBARS) method [28]. , and superoxide dismutase (SOD) and Glutathione peroxidase (GPX) activities and nitrite levels were determined by specific kits and spectrophotometry methods (Navand Salamat, Iran).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed through Prism 8 software. The normality of data was checked by the Shapiro-Willk test. The data were compared by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey test as post hoc. The non-parametric data were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H and followed by Mann-Whitney tests as post hoc. All data were represented as mean ± SD, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The effects of NaHS on oxidative stress following renal IR

The brain

Our findings revealed that the levels of MDA in both males and females were elevated in IR/Saline compared to the Sham/Saline group (P < 0.01 for males and P < 0.001 for females). NaHS administration decreased the MDA levels in both sexes (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1A).

Our results showed that nitrite levels in both males and females were reduced in IR/Saline compared to the Sham/Saline groups (P < 0.001). NaHS administration increased the nitrite in both sexes (P < 0.01 for males and P < 0.05 for females) (Fig. 1B).

Our results showed that the levels of GPX activity in both males and females were reduced in IR/Saline compared to the Sham/Saline groups (P < 0.001). NaHS administration increased the GPX activity in both sexes significantly (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1C).

Our observations disclosed that the levels of SOD activity in both males and females were reduced in IR/Saline compared to the Sham/Saline groups (P < 0.001). NaHS administration increased the SOD activity in both sexes, but this increase was not significant (Fig. 1D).

There was no significant difference between male and female groups regarding oxidative status in the Brain tissues.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effects of NaHS on remote organ (brain, heart, and lung) injuries resulting from renal ischemia-reperfusion at both histopathological and oxidative levels in male and female rats. Our findings revealed that renal ischemia-induced renal dysfunction (elevated urine albumin and serum BUN, SCr BUN/SCr ratio), and caused damage to the brain, heart, and lungs through increased oxidative stress, marked by elevated MDA and reduced SOD and GPX levels. Histological analysis also showed pathological changes, including apoptosis and congestion. NaHS administration mitigated oxidative stress in the tissues of both sexes.

Renal ischemia-reperfusion is a major cause of AKI, which has a high mortality rate largely due to dysfunction in remote organs [29, 30]. During ischemia, the kidney releases factors such as free radicals, inflammatory agents, and apoptotic factors that are transferred to other tissues during reperfusion, causing damage through increased inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and autophagy [2, 31]. Elevated ROS production during IR exacerbates local tissue damage and affects remote organs [32]. ROS induces cellular damage via lipid peroxidation and DNA degradation and triggers apoptosis as a late-phase response [33]. Apoptosis has been reported in remote lung, liver, and myocardial injuries after AKI [34]. The endogenous antioxidants SOD and catalase, as well as GPX, which are responsible for defense against ROS production, have an important role in reducing IR injury [33].

Our findings indicated that renal IR elevated MDA levels and decreased SOD and GPX activities across all organs. These results align with previous studies reporting similar oxidative alterations in the kidney, serum, and hippocampus following renal IR [35–39]. Although NaHS treatment led to an overall increase in antioxidant enzyme activity, this increase was particularly pronounced in GPX activity within the heart and brain across all animals and in the lungs of female subjects. According to the literature, the activity and expression of antioxidant enzymes are tissue-specific and exhibit differential responses under pathophysiological conditions. For example, hypoxia has been shown to elevate GPX activity in the liver while decreasing it in the brain, with no significant changes observed in the heart, lung, or uterus. Additionally, pregnancy has been reported to enhance GPX activity in the brain, heart, and lungs but to reduce it in the liver [40]. It has also been established that females possess a higher intrinsic antioxidant capacity than males. Furthermore, the adaptation to altered antioxidant capacity induced by physical activity appears to be influenced by gender differences [41]. Exogenous H2S has been shown to enhance antioxidant activities and increase the protein expression of antioxidant enzymes [42].

Exogenous NaHS has been shown to protect against global cerebral IR injury by reducing oxidative stress, evidenced by decreased MDA and increased SOD activity [43]. Moreover, NaHS protects against IR-induced acute kidney injury by reducing oxidative stress through decreased MDA and increased SOD activity in the kidney [18, 35, 44]. However, a study demonstrated that NaHS accelerates the recovery of kidney tubules after renal IR through the mitigation of oxidative stress [45]. Our previous study confirmed the antioxidant effect of NaHS in the renal tissue of ischemic male and female rats. However, renal function and tissue damage did not recover and were even exacerbated in females [35].

The role of NO pathway in renal IR pathogenesis is contentious, with its alterations appearing to be tissue-specific. Some studies have shown that NO has a protective effect on renal IR, and its inhibition exacerbates renal damage [46, 47]. Conversely, other studies have found that inhibiting inducible nitric oxide synthase and reducing NO levels can mitigate injuries resulting from renal IR [48, 49]. Our findings demonstrated decreased nitrite levels in heart and brain tissues and increased lung tissue nitrite levels following renal IR. To elucidate the observed variations in nitrite levels post-IR, it is essential to discuss the relationship between nitric oxide (NO), nitrite, and ischemia.

During normoxia, NO produced by nitric oxide synthase (NOS) is thought to be stored in a nitrite reservoir. This reservoir can be reactivated during hypoxia and ischemia when NOS activity is impaired. In this context, nitrite functions as an “ischemic NO buffer,” helping to maintain NO homeostasis under hypoxic-ischemic conditions [50]. Previous studies have highlighted nitrite’s protective role in brain, heart, and lung injuries following IR [51–53]. Moreover, Faassen et al. reviewed nitrite’s role in mammalian physiology under hypoxia, noting that nitrite, a stable NO metabolite, remains inactive under normoxic conditions but is converted back to NO when tissue oxygen levels fall below a critical threshold [54].Tissue ischemia-induced acidification facilitates this conversion of nitrite to NO [55]. Several mammalian enzymes, including xanthine oxidase (XO), cytochrome c oxidase, and even endothelial NOS, have been shown to reduce nitrite under hypoxic conditions. However, their tissue distribution and reaction rates vary significantly [56–59].

Xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) is a key enzyme in reducing nitrite to NO, protecting against cardiac IR injury [58]. For decades, increased protein abundance and enzymatic activity of XOR under hypoxic or inflammatory conditions have been linked to organ damage and vascular dysfunction [60]. Additionally, heme-globins such as hemoglobin, myoglobin, and neuroglobin, which vary in their oxygen affinity, also play an important role in nitrite reduction to NO, thereby protecting tissues across different levels of hypoxia [61, 62].

The decline in nitrite levels observed in our study following ischemia can be attributed to nitrite reduction to NO, which helps mitigate tissue IR injury. It is important to consider the role of the microenvironment, including pH and oxygen tension, as well as enzyme activity, in this reaction. The differences in these parameters between lung, heart, and brain tissues may explain the observed variations in nitrite levels.

Furthermore, hydrogen sulfide has been shown to confer significant vascular benefits through nitrite-mediated mechanisms independent of NOS under hypoxic/acidic conditions, in which NO production via NOS is diminished [63]. It has also been demonstrated that hydrogen sulfide stimulates the conversion of xanthine oxidoreductase to nitrite reductase, leading to NO formation [64]. Park et al. reported that baseline NOS activity is higher in females than in males in a rat model of renal IR injury and that estrogen administration further increases NOS activity, whereas testosterone decreases it [11]. This is consistent with our findings, which show higher nitrite levels in female NaHS-treated animals. Additionally, a study in breast cancer cells indicated that nitrite competes with estradiol for binding to the estrogen receptor alpha (ERα), which suggested that nitrite anions directly interact with the ligand-binding domain of ERα [65].

In this study, serum BUN and Cr, BUN/SCr ratio, and urine albumin levels increased after renal IR in both sexes, indicating successful model induction and aligning with other studies [35, 66, 67]. Also, renal histopathology assessment revealed increased kidney damage scores in IR groups. NaHS treatment could not decrease BUN and serum Cr in IR-treated male rats, while these parameters were elevated in IR-treated female rats. BUN/SCr levels increased in male and female animals. Moreover, NaHS treatment exacerbated renal tissue injury in females. In our previous research, we compared the response of IR male and female rats to NaHS in renal damage and function and reported the effects in kidney tissue showed similar changes [37]. We hypothesize that this unexpected result may be due to the time or dose of NaHS administration, as its effects are dose- and time-dependent and exhibit a U-shaped pattern [68]. Although the dose used in this study demonstrated significant antioxidant effects in remote organs, the results related to kidney injury necessitate a re-examination of other doses and administration times of NaHS in future studies.

In line of other studies showing gender differences in inflammatory biomarkers following renal IR [69, 70], our study found considerable differences between male and female rats in antioxidant defense following NaHS treatment in renal IR rats, in addition to tissue differences. Overall, NaHS has a beneficial effect on remote organ injury following renal IR. Future studies should explore different doses and administration times of NaHS to optimize its therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

This study explores the protective effects of NaHS on brain, heart, and lung injuries induced by renal IR in male and female rats. NaHS administration significantly reduced oxidative stress by decreasing MDA levels and increasing GPX activity, indicating reduced lipid peroxidation and enhanced antioxidant defense. Histological improvements were observed, with reductions in neuronal apoptosis, brain edema, vascular congestion, myocyte necrosis, and inflammation. Lung tissue analysis showed decreased alveolar collapse and neutrophil infiltration with NaHS treatment. Both male and female rats exhibited these protective effects, although some sex-specific differences were noted. These findings suggest NaHS is a promising therapeutic agent for mitigating oxidative damage and histological injury in multiple organs following renal IR.
