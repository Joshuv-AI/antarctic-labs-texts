Dynamic variations of metal-O bonding in electro- and thermo-catalytic activation processes
Authors: Ronghui Zhu, Shunzheng Zhao, Kai Chen, Qingjun Yu, Fengyu Gao, Guang Mo, Yunpeng Liu, Shaobin Wang, Honghong Yi, Xiaolong Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDynamically tracking the variations in electronic configurations and molecular binding of a catalyst under operando conditions remains a significant challenge. Herein, we report an in-situ millisecond-resolved wide-angle X-ray scattering technique to capture lattice distortion within a catalyst in low-temperature oxidation under electric- and thermal-induced catalysis and reveal the intrinsic mechanism of metal-O bonding on oxygen activation and the electro promotion. The c-directional lattice spacing of MnO2 loaded on Ni foam is observed to undergo contraction and recovery processes with and without a direct electric field, respectively. Hard and soft X-ray absorption fine structure spectra, density functional theory and temperature-programmed reduction analyses confirm that an external current can effectively modulate the electronic configuration of manganese and oxygen atoms in the MnO2 catalyst and alter the covalent bonding between Mn and O atoms by weakening the Mn-O bond, thereby enhancing the activity of lattice oxygen in the catalyst. This study provides scientific insights into the dynamic evolution of lattice distortion in externally field-induced catalytic reactions.Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Thermoelectric devices and materials, Pollution remediation, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

Lattice oxygen activation plays a pivotal role in catalytic reactions by supplying reactive oxygen species (e.g., O2- or O-) for redox cycles and facilitating electron transfer between catalysts and reactants, thereby influencing reaction pathways1,2. This activation process controls catalytic efficiency, side reactions, and long-term activity and stability in many reactions for chemical synthesis3, pollutant degradation4, photo/electrocatalytic CO2 reduction5, and water splitting6. Traditional lattice oxygen activation relies on high temperature to enhance the thermal motion of oxygen atoms, helping them detach from lattice positions or react with molecules, thus boosting catalytic oxidation7,8. However, high temperatures can cause side reactions toward catalyst deactivation, leading to high costs. In contrast, external fields like electric9,10, optical11, and magnetic12 stimulus offer an alternative approach13. Via altering the electronic and bond structures of catalysts, these external fields facilitate low-temperature lattice oxygen activation14,15.

When a voltage is applied to a catalyst, free electrons of a current are accelerated under the electric field, enabling the catalyst to carry the current. The free electrons can collide with atoms and molecules in the lattice structure of current-carrying catalysts. Electron injection strengthens the built-in electric field at the catalyst interface, increasing the electron density near the Fermi level, which weakens the metal–oxygen bond and facilitates lattice oxygen activation and migration16. Thus, more O2 can be activated by the catalyst via capturing additional electrons to produce electrophilic •O2- species and promote complete oxidation of intermediates17. This auxiliary action during the reaction process can improve a rapid response for the material’s long-term stability18. Hence, using a catalyst with current-carrying capability for redox reactions has a unique operational advantage.

Mei et al.17 proposed a concept of electrically driven lattice oxygen release for carbon soot treatment. Using conductive oxide catalysts in contact with soot and applying a voltage for rapid ignition, they achieved 53% soot conversion on potassium-supported antimony-tin oxides (K/ATO) at below 75 °C under close contact. In contrast, traditional thermal catalysis reached only 50% conversion at 300 °C. In-situ optical microscopy revealed that a reverse electron flow between K/ATO and soot particles increased collisions and enhanced contact efficiency. However, this optical microscopy technique is not applicable in catalytic gaseous oxidation. Li et al.19 used an electric field to boost oxygen release from the lattice, promoting Co3O4 formation on the catalyst surface. Infrared spectroscopy tracked surface functional group changes during the reaction, but lattice structure and charge transfer changes could not be determined. Though these works have confirmed that an electric field can promote lattice oxygen release and surface reactions, key changes in lattice structure and charge transfer pathways have not been directly observed. Direct evidence of these changes is essential to understand the electric–thermal–chemical coupled catalysis in current-carrying systems, and can offer a theoretical support for enhancing catalyst performance, increasing conversion efficiency, and expanding application scenarios. The structural evolution, such as crystal structures, chemical valence states and component proportions in current-carrying catalysis, needs to be monitored by advanced characterization techniques. In this work, we set up a system for current-carrying catalysis tests and investigated the Joule heat-driven catalysis of acetone oxidation (Supplementary Figs. S1–2).

To directly capture these transient structural dynamics, we employed in-situ millisecond-resolved wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) at the fourth-generation High Energy Photon Source (HEPS) synchrotron20,21. This technique provides a key advantage over conventional X-ray diffraction (XRD) through its millisecond temporal resolution, enabling real-time monitoring of lattice changes under operando conditions that is crucial for probing fast catalytic processes. It was used to capture lattice distortion of a MnO2 catalyst in catalytic oxidation. Results show that the c-directional lattice spacing of MnO2 loaded on Ni foam (NF) undergoes contraction and recovery processes with and without a direct electric field, respectively. Hard and soft X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra, density functional theory (DFT) and temperature-programed reduction (TPR) analyses reveal that an external current can effectively modulate the electronic configuration of manganese and oxygen atoms in the catalyst, which alters the chemical bonding between Mn and O atoms, weakens the Mn-O bond, and thereby enhances the activity of lattice oxygen in the catalyst. This study provides insights into the dynamic evolution of lattice distortion in externally field-induced catalytic reactions.

Results

Morphology and phase structure of MnO2/NF (Mn/NF) catalysts

In current-carrying catalysis, the key is the design of a conductive support–active site composite catalyst. Metallic substrate composites overcome oxide limitations, such as conductivity, strength, and current distribution, while leveraging metal/oxide interface synergies to enhance catalytic performance in current-carrying reactions22,23. Manganese oxides, in particular, show potential in the catalytic oxidation of typical volatile organic compounds such as acetone and benzene24. In this study, MnOx was loaded onto nickel foam via a one-step method to fabricate a catalyst (designated as Mn/NF), as shown in Supplementary Fig. S3. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) mapping images (Fig. 1a–d and Supplementary Fig. S4) clearly show uniform dispersion of manganese elements on the support surface, indicating effective integration of MnOx with the carrier. BET analysis (Supplementary Table S1) indicates that the catalyst possesses a mesoporous structure, which is beneficial for reactant mass transport and exposure of active sites. XRD, cyclic voltammetry (CV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were then employed to characterize the crystal structure, oxidation capacity, and electron transfer kinetics of the Mn/NF catalyst, respectively.

Figure 1e displays the XRD patterns, where MnO2 powder shows diffraction peaks at 12.36°, 24.64°, 37.18°, and 65.74°, corresponding to the (001), (002), (1¯11), and (020) planes of δ-MnO2 (JCPDS 80-1098). The Mn/NF catalyst exhibits peaks at 12.32° and 25.04°, attributed to the (001) and (002) planes of δ-MnO225, while peaks at 44.44°, 51.80°, and 76.32° correspond to the (111), (200), and (220) planes of Ni (JCPDS 04-0850)26. The XRD results indicate that MnO2 is successfully loaded onto NF in the form of δ-MnO2. From electrochemical CV measurements, it was observed that Mn/NF exhibited a larger closed area at the same scan rate of 0.1 V/s, indicating that it may expose more active sites and possess stronger redox activity (Supplementary Fig. S5). EIS measurements (Supplementary Fig. S6) showed that Mn/NF had a smaller semicircle diameter in the high-frequency region, suggesting that the Mn/NF catalyst has lower charge transfer resistance and a faster electron transfer rate. Additionally, the Mn/NF catalyst had a higher straight-line slope in the low-frequency region, indicating a lower diffusion limitation, which is beneficial for the migration of surface reactive species27.

We investigated the effects of an electric current treatment on the synthesized Mn/NF catalyst through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Fig. 1f), which revealed two distinct peaks in the Mn 2p3/2 region at 642.14–642.21 and 643.26–643.39 eV, corresponding to Mn3+ and Mn4+ species, respectively28,29. A quantitative analysis (Supplementary Table S1) confirms an increase in the Mn3+ population on the sample after electrical activation (EPMN). This elevation in Mn3+ content is associated with an increase in lattice disorder, which will in turn promote the formation of oxygen vacancies to enhance the adsorption of atmospheric oxygen30,31. From an empirical formula32, the average oxidation state (AOS, AOS = 8.956–1.126 × ΔE) was calculated based on the energy difference (ΔE) between the Mn 3 s peaks (Supplementary Fig. S7). The results from Mn 3s and Mn 2p (Supplementary Table S1) are in agreement, indicating a higher Mn3+ concentration induced by electric activation. Furthermore, the O 1s spectra (Fig. 1g) display peaks at 529.78–529.79, 530.98–531.04, and 533.81–533.85 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen (Olatt), surface-adsorbed oxygen species (such as O2-, O-, and OH groups), and adsorbed water molecules, respectively. The surface of EPMN exhibits a higher proportion of adsorbed oxygen (Oads) compared to the original Mn/NF. The surface-adsorbed oxygen is characterized by a more rapid and efficient electron transfer rate than Olatt, and is a critical factor contributing to enhanced catalytic oxidation performance facilitated by the current-carrying catalyst33.

In-situ Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 1h, i) of samples at varying voltages reveals two characteristic peaks at 572 (symmetric Mn-O stretching) and 644 cm⁻¹ (asymmetric Mn-O stretching) in MnO234. The progressive spectral attenuations and redshifts with increasing power are attributed to current-induced lattice distortion to elevate concentrations of surface oxygen vacancies. This structural evolution is corroborated by complementary in-situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis (Supplementary Fig. S8). The attenuation of the peak at 572 cm⁻¹ (associated with Mn-O vibrations facilitating lattice oxygen departure35) aligns with Raman observations, while broader bands at 400–800 cm⁻¹ confirm characteristic Mn-O-Mn and Mn-O vibrations within [MnO6] octahedral frameworks. These results demonstrate that the introduction of an electric current induces lattice distortion, thereby increasing the Mn3+ content on the catalyst surface and enhancing the number of surface oxygen vacancies. This process is represented by Eq. (1)29, where VO denotes an oxygen vacancy.

To further investigate the impact of lattice distortion on oxidative performance, we conducted temperature programed reduction with hydrogen (H2-TPR) analysis (Fig. 1j), revealing two distinct reduction peaks at 243.5 and 301 °C under external heating, assigned to the MnO2 → Mn2O3 phase transition36,37. However, the peaks appearing at lower temperatures of 228.4 and 246.7 °C during the current-carrying process indicate a narrower temperature window for reduction. This suggests that an electron influx homogenizes the Mn-O bond energy, enhancing the reactivity of Olatt with H2 at lower temperatures and facilitating the reduction process38. Moreover, the current-carrying oxidation process commences at a lower temperature of 114 °C, compared to the traditional external heating at 130 °C. The increased influx of electrons enhances the catalytic oxidation activity in the low-temperature range (100–200 °C), as evidenced by a higher integrated area ratio compared to that in external heating (Supplementary Fig. S9), pointing to improved low-temperature activity.

The combined results from these multi-faceted characterizations elucidate the regulatory mechanism of electron influx on the oxygen activation capability of the Mn/NF catalyst. Current injection primarily induces lattice distortion and Jahn-Teller effects (as evidenced by in-situ Raman spectroscopy), leading to an increased Mn3+/Mn4+ ratio and the formation of oxygen vacancies (Eq. 1, XPS). The resulting surface disorder and charge redistribution enhance the proportion of highly reactive surface-adsorbed oxygen species (Oads) while consuming lattice oxygen (Olatt). Crucially, this electronic restructuring synergistically activates both oxygen species. The weakened Mn–O bonds facilitate the release and migration of lattice oxygen, which is directly reflected in the downward shift of the reduction peaks in H2-TPR, indicating a lowered energy barrier for lattice oxygen participation. Concurrently, the enriched Oads species provide a reservoir of active oxygen with faster electron transfer kinetics. This dual activation mechanism-reduced activation energy for lattice oxygen involvement and increased concentration and activity of surface oxygen-leads to enhanced low-temperature acetone oxidation activity observed under current-carrying conditions.

Catalyst performance analysis

Acetone (C3H6O) degradation activity

A reaction apparatus was designed in this study to accommodate the catalytic process of the current-carrying catalyst (as shown in Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Fig. S10). K-type thermocouples were inserted from one end for contacting the catalyst to measure the material’s temperature. The temperature readings from the K-type thermocouples basically matched those from infrared thermography. The infrared thermometer indicated a relatively uniform temperature distribution across the catalyst. Within a short period of time (20 s), the temperature could rise directly from 30 to 230 °C (Supplementary Fig. S11). Moreover, the temperature showed a linear rise with the increase of input power (Supplementary Fig. S12). The catalytic performances in acetone removal by current-carrying and external heating processes have revealed that current-carrying catalysis is more effective than the external heating process (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. S13). Specifically, the temperature in the current-carrying process at T10 point is 123 °C, which is lower than that (175 °C) of external heating. Figure 2e illustrates that current-carrying catalysis can be attributed to thermal catalysis and electronic effects. The temperature for 90% conversion in current-carrying catalysis is 165.9 °C, compared to 238.6 °C in thermal catalysis, indicating improved low-temperature catalysis. At 165.9 °C, the thermal catalytic efficiency is approximately 5%, and the catalytic activity is increased by 85% in the current-carrying process at an electrical power of 4.9 W.

In current-carrying catalytic reactions, introduction of an electrical current allows the catalyst and acetone to react at 120 °C, which is lower than the temperature required in conventional external heating catalysis, suggesting that this strategy broadens the operational temperature window of catalysts toward lower temperatures39. To investigate the role of electrons in current-carrying catalysis, we calculated the difference in acetone conversion between the current-carrying and external heating processes at the same temperature, yielding a curve of conversion versus temperature (Supplementary Fig. S14). This curve enables the differentiation and quantification of the respective contributions of the electronic and thermal effects, which were derived from the conversion difference. The analysis reveals that the electronic contribution intensifies rapidly within the low-temperature regime (below 170 °C), becoming the dominant factor. For instance, it accounts for over 80% of the total conversion at 123 °C. This directly verifies the pivotal role of the electronic effect in initiating the reaction at low temperatures. As the temperature rises above 170 °C, the thermal effect increases exponentially. Although the absolute activity enhancement from the electronic effect persists, its relative contribution gradually diminishes. This dynamic evolution illustrates a clear synergistic relationship: the electronic effect is crucial for kick-starting the reaction at low temperatures by activating reactants and lowering the apparent activation energy, while the thermal effect progressively takes over at higher temperatures to drive complete conversion. Thus, the synergy between Joule heating and the non-thermal electronic effect is a temperature-dependence correlation.

To further investigate the roles of electronic and thermal effects in the catalytic reaction, we applied varying currents (1, 2, 3, 4 A) to the catalyst under programed thermal fields (90–300 °C with 30 °C increments). Results demonstrate that higher currents yield better catalytic performance at identical temperatures (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. S15), where current-carrying catalysis without external heating exhibits optimal efficiency. Kinetic analyses via the Arrhenius equation (testing at four data points within 20% removal rate40) reveal that the activation energy for current-carrying catalysis is 95.1 kJ·mol-1, lower than that (108.5 kJ·mol-1) for external heating (Fig. 2f), suggesting acetone molecules are more readily activated under current-carrying conditions. Moreover, the apparent pre-exponential factors for external heating and current-carrying catalysis (2.06 × 1011 vs. 1.04 × 1012 mol·g-1·s-1) indicate that flowing electrons activate more surface active sites41. Collectively, we conclude that, while a portion of electrons in Joule heating generate thermal energy, the remaining electrons directly drive the catalytic reaction, enabling interconversion between energy forms22,42. This manifests a distinct synergistic effect where Joule heating enhances low-temperature activity. Furthermore, an enhanced current not only accelerated the heating rate but also reduced energy consumption, with savings exceeding 70–76%, demonstrating higher energy efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S16).

The sustainability and stability of the current-carrying catalytic process are critical for performance assessment. A continuous catalytic oxidation of acetone at 5 W for 3 days (Fig. 2h) maintained over 90% catalytic conversion and above 80% CO2 yield, demonstrating good long-term activity stability. Furthermore, the catalyst exhibits strong operational robustness, as evidenced by its stable performance over eight start-stop cycles within 8 h43 (Supplementary Fig. S17a). To directly corroborate this stability at the structural level, we performed post-reaction characterizations. The XRD patterns (Supplementary Fig. S17b) confirm the complete retention of the δ-MnO2 crystalline phase with dominant (001) facet exposure after the 72-h test. Correspondingly, the SEM images (Supplementary Fig. S17c) show that the nanoflower-like morphology remains intact without observable degradation. Integrating these findings, current-carrying catalysis was found to decrease energy usage and increase catalyst activity. Compared to existing catalysts, Mn/NF exhibits effective activity among cost-effective transition metal catalysts at a 90% conversion rate and 165.9 °C (Fig. 2i, Supplementary Table S2), realizing energy conservation in the catalytic process and retaining high activity at low-temperature, which is of a practical value.

Mechanism of current-carrying catalysis

To delve into the catalytic mechanisms of Mn/NF catalyst at the atomic level under external heating and current-carrying catalysis for acetone oxidation, synchrotron radiation in-situ WAXS patterns and XAFS spectra (Fig. 3a) were collected to investigate the changes in crystal structures, chemical valence states and component proportions under distinct heating modalities. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. S18, a custom-designed current-carrying catalytic reaction cell was employed, where air or acetone was introduced into the reactor, and the catalyst was heated to achieve catalytic oxidation by modulating the external current. In the fourth-generation synchrotron with high-brightness, 300-millisecond-resolved in-situ WAXS data was obtained to quickly capture the changes in the catalyst’s crystal structure during the reaction. The distance from the sample to the detector was calibrated using the WAXS pattern of a MnO2 standard sample as 148.67 mm.

As shown, external heating in air caused no significant change in q, whereas q increased significantly at 3 s in the current-carrying catalysis, with a shortened spacing along the c-axis. At this time, the temperature of the current-carrying catalyst was around 70 °C, while the catalyst remained unchanged in terms of q at external heating. Around 10 s after disconnecting the current at 180 s, q exhibited a blue shift, indicating that the current induces rapid lattice distortion or surface defects45. To further verify this, we introduced acetone into both reaction systems. Figure 3f, g show that the catalyst’s unit-cell structure remained unchanged below 100 °C under external heating, indicating no lattice-oxygen participation in the reaction. In contrast, the characteristic peak shifted from 8.84 to 9.60 and gradually disappeared over time at the current flow. This suggests that lattice distortion made MnO2 prone to reduction to Mn, leading to the disappearance of the (001) diffraction peak of MnO2. The in-situ WAXS results demonstrate that current-carrying catalysis accelerates lattice contraction, reduces interplanar spacing, and induces lattice distortion in MnO2, thereby weakening oxygen bonds and facilitating their dissociation.

To explore the chemical states and component proportions in the catalytic reactions under external heating and current-carrying operations, we collected X-ray absorption near-edge spectra (XANES) of the Mn K-edge for the Mn/NF catalyst (Fig. 3h, i). The valence state of Mn decreases progressively with increasing temperature (Supplementary Fig. S19). The maximum of the first derivative of the Mn L3-edge (Supplementary Fig. S20) shifts towards lower energy as the reaction proceeds, indicating a gradual reduction in the chemical valence of Mn. Linear combination fitting (LCF) analysis46 was applied to the normalized XANES spectra in the range of 6520–6620 eV to refine the proportions of MnO2 and MnO (Supplementary Figs. S21–S22). The fitting results (Fig. 3i, k and Supplementary Table S4) reveal that the proportion of MnO2 decreases linearly from 96.1 to 85.9% during external heating catalysis. Under current-carrying catalysis, the proportion of MnO2 drops from 96.1 to 0%. The XANES results indicate that the reaction rate is nearly threefold of that in the thermal catalysis at power levels below 6 W, and that MnO2 begins to react at lower temperatures at certain currents. Beyond 6 W, the transition of MnO2 to MnO is accelerated, suggesting that the electronic effect accelerates the reduction of MnO2, enhancing low-temperature activity. Besides, the extended XAFS (EXAFS) oscillations of Mn K-edge (Supplementary Fig. S23b) show a gradual weakening under current-carrying. A faster decay in the wavelet shape indicates an increase in material disorder47. At 8.45 W, a shift in the oscillation occurs, consistent with the WAXS results.

The corresponding Fourier transform (FT) of Mn K-edge EXAFS oscillations (Supplementary Fig. S24) show peaks around 1.5 Å, associated with Mn-O bonds within the basic MnO6 octahedral framework, and 2.5 Å due to Mn shared edges within the MnO6 octahedra48. In external heating catalysis from 30 to 150 °C, no significant change occurs in peak position or intensity, indicating that the catalyst maintains stable local structure during this stage, consistent with the low catalytic activity observed below 150 °C. In current-carrying catalysis, the intensities of Mn-O and Mn-Mn bonds show a marked decrease above 90 °C, suggesting reduced coordination numbers or enhanced structural disorder49, while further pronounced attenuation and peak shifts beyond 210 °C clearly indicate a phase transformation. To further confirm the type and length of the coordinated bond around Mn, we employed wavelet transform analysis, offering high resolution in both R and K space. Supplementary Fig. S25 shows that the intensities of the peaks around 6 Å for Mn-O and 8 Å for Mn-Mn decrease, which is consistent with the FT spectra. It is also found that the peak intensity is decreased at 90 °C under current-carrying. With the increasing power, the Mn-Mn bond not only weakens but also shifts, due to the escape of lattice oxygen and a tendency towards the formation of MnO. This result shows that the introduction of currents affects the coordination environment of Mn, increasing the electronic state density, accelerating the breaking of Mn-Mn and Mn-O bonds, and enhancing disorder, thereby promoting catalytic oxidation.

Theoretical analysis of current-induced lattice distortion

To investigate the electronic configuration variations of Mn species in Mn/NF during external heating and current-carrying catalysis, we employed quasi in-situ soft X-ray absorption spectra (sXAS). The characteristics of the O K-edge arise from electronic transitions from O 1s to O 2p antibonding orbitals, which are associated with the interactions with surrounding cations. This edge is highly sensitive to the local chemical environment of oxygen atoms bonded to cations, offering an in-depth understanding of the oxygen atoms and their adjacent cations50. The peak intensities in O K-edge spectra observed between 530 and 535 eV (Fig. 4a) correspond to the densities of the hybridized states of O(2p)-TM(3d-t2g) and O(2p)-TM(3d-eg), respectively51. In external heating, a decrease in the intensity of the unoccupied t2g states with increasing temperature suggests diminished hybridization between O 2p and Mn t2g orbitals52. In current-carrying catalytic process, the material exhibits a more pronounced reduction in t2g state intensity at the same temperature, indicating increased Jahn-Teller distortion in the MnO6 octahedra. At 8.45 W, it can be observed that the orbitals shift to higher energy levels, and the energy of the O(2p)-TM(3d-eg) orbitals decreases due to the changes in the molecular structure.

Furthermore, similar conclusions can be drawn from the analysis of the Mn L3-edge spectra (Fig. 4b) and the corresponding d-orbital fitting results (Supplementary Table S5). An increase in temperature promotes the population of the eg orbitals. Notably, in the current-carrying system, the occupancy of the eg orbitals also increases, with the most significant enhancement observed in the dx2-y2 orbital. The schematic diagram of the crystal field splitting and orbital occupancy is presented in Fig. 4c. Therefore, compared with the external heating catalytic approach that inhibits the O(2p)–TM(3d-t2g) molecular orbital interaction, the current-carrying catalysis demonstrates greater efficacy in enhancing the O(2p)-Mn(3d-eg) molecular orbital interaction. The current-carrying process facilitates electron accumulation on the d orbitals, enhances electron transfer kinetics, and consequently promotes the activation of lattice oxygen.

To confirm spin-related d-electron occupation states and verify the impact of electron addition on lattice oxygen, we conducted DFT simulations. The catalyst configuration was determined by synchrotron radiation fitting (Supplementary Fig. S26a, b and Table S6) combined with XRD data. The optimized catalyst structure is shown in Supplementary Fig. S27. The stability of the slab model under the applied electric field is confirmed by the absence of charge spill-out in the planar-averaged electron density analysis (Supplementary Fig. S26c). Crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (COHP) analysis was utilized to assess the interaction between Mn and O atoms in Mn/NF under varying electric field strengths (Fig. 4d, f and Supplementary Table S7). The ICOHP value for the Mn-O bond at 0 V/Å (−3.683) is lower than that at 2 V/Å (−3.291), indicating a shift towards antibonding character and a weakened bonding nature of the Mn-O bond under certain electric field strengths, which favors the escape of lattice oxygen53,54. Additionally, Bader charge analysis indicates an enhanced electron-accepting capability of oxygen atoms, facilitating the formation of adsorption sites on the surface (Supplementary Table S8). The electron-accepting ability of oxygen atoms in Mn/NF is 0.35 e- without an electric field and will be increased to 0.50 e- under an electric field strength of 2 V/Å. It suggests that the electric field enhances the electron-accepting ability of oxygen atoms, which is beneficial for the formation of surface oxygen adsorption sites. Concurrently, an increase in surface Mn-O bond length is observed. The charge density difference (CDD) of oxygen indicates a stable, isolated atomic state in the absence of an electric field. Upon the introduction of an electric field, hybrid molecular orbitals are formed between O and adjacent atoms to favor the bonding adsorption of small molecules (Fig. 4e).

Analysis of the electron localization function reveals a spontaneous tendency for surface O to couple and form bonds under increased electric fields, facilitating bidentate adsorption similar to acetone molecules and the formation of a stable four-membered ring structure (Supplementary Fig. S28). It is observed that the 2p energy level of oxygen atoms is elevated by 0.04 eV compared to the field-free state (-1.49 eV) under an external electric field of 2 V/Å (Fig. 4g, i), indicating enhanced activity of valence electrons for rapid chemical reactions55. With increasing electric field strength, both the p band center of oxygen atoms and the d band center of manganese atoms shift towards antibonding orbitals, leading to reduced stability of Mn-O bonds for their cleavage. Furthermore, the energy levels of Mn’s 3 d orbitals and O’s 2p orbitals approach the Fermi level (Supplementary Table S9), facilitating electron transition and promoting redox reactions. Additional analysis (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. S29–S30) indicates that the spin-up density of states of the dz2, dx2-y2 orbitals of Mn atoms and the pz orbital of O atoms increases under external electric fields, suggesting an increase in the material’s deprotonation ability56. The weakening of bonding orbitals accelerates the reaction process. Significant changes in bandwidth occur, with band broadening directly correlated with increased electron mobility57,58. Notably, the spin-up density of states for the pz orbital of oxygen atoms increases under an external electric field, indicating an enhanced electron cloud density, which may play a more significant role in the hybridization of Mn-O bonds.

In some studies, modulating the covalency of Mn-O bonds has been shown to enhance catalyst activity, as it promotes the formation of oxygen vacancies and oxygen migration, thereby increasing the material’s oxidative activity59. The introduction of an electric field is considered to be an effective strategy to improve the performance of Mn catalysts, as the electric field plays a crucial role in optimizing the adsorption of reactants and key intermediates and has a positive impact on interfacial behavior60. By combining the analysis of the charge changes of the Mn center (Supplementary Table S8 and Fig. 4e) and the d-band center (Supplementary Tables S9–S10 and Fig. 4h), we found that a unidirectional electric field not only enhanced the electron loss of Mn in MnO2 but also shifted the d-band center of Mn upward61. Among the five d orbitals, the shift of the dx2-y2 band center was the most significant, indicating that the application of an electric field had the greatest impact on the dx2-y2 orbital in the eg orbitals, which is consistent with the experimental results observed by XAFS. Additionally, the combination of ICOHP revealed that the electric field weakened the Mn-O bonding orbitals and caused the electrons to deviate towards the oxygen atoms, which is beneficial for the activation of lattice oxygen (Supplementary Fig. S31). In addition, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were conducted to investigate the effect of electric field on the material at different temperatures, and the results are presented in Supplementary Figs. S32–S34 and Table S11. Both heating and electric field promote an increase in the average Mn-O bond length and a weakening of bonding orbitals, which collectively facilitate the activation of lattice oxygen. This observation is consistent with the WAXS analysis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S35, compared with the case without a current, the ICOHP value exhibits a more dramatic change after applying the current, indicating that the application of the current plays a positive role in suppressing the Mn-O bonding state.

Discussion

In this study, we established a characterization protocol comprising transient in-situ millisecond-resolved WAXS, in-situ XANES, DFT analysis, and H2-TPR for mechanistic investigation of catalyst’s bonding variations in catalytic redox reactions under electric and conventional heating conditions. We found a current-carrying catalytic system achieved rapid response kinetics and 76% energy-saving efficiency relative to external heating catalysis. Mechanistic investigations confirm that an electron influx induces MnO2 lattice distortion, increases Mn3+ content, and facilitates oxygen desorption for low-temperature acetone oxidation while maintaining catalyst stability, reusability, and selectivity. Furthermore, we elucidate current-induced Jahn-Teller distortion dynamics: Electron influx shifts the Mn spin-up d-band center toward the Fermi level and broadens spectral bands (directly correlating with enhanced charge mobility). Subsequent occupation of Mn-d and O-p antibonding orbitals destabilizes Mn-O bonds, facilitating lattice oxygen activation.

It is worth noting that the electron injection → orbital modulation → lattice distortion → bond weakening mechanism revealed in this work may possess broader applicability. For other transition metal oxides with analogous electronic structures (e.g., Co3O4, Fe2O3, CuO), the covalency of the metal–oxygen bond and the activity of lattice oxygen are likewise influenced by the d-electron configuration of the metal centers. External electric fields or current injection may similarly modulate orbital occupancy and induce electronic redistribution, leading to structural distortion and weakened metal–oxygen bonds, thereby generally enhancing the migration and reactivity of lattice oxygen. This work not only provides fundamental insights into electric field-modulated lattice distortion mechanisms but also offers a viable strategy for the rational design of efficient and energy-saving electro-thermal-chemical coupled catalytic systems and reactors.

Methods

Materials

C2H5OH (95%), HCl (37%), KMnO4 and C3H6O were of an analytical grade and were purchased from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd.

Activation of nickel foam

Nickel foam was cut in sizes of 100 mm × 20 mm × 1.5 mm and was sonicated in 6 M HCl for 30 min to remove the oxide film on the surface. Then the nickel foam was washed several times with ethanol solution and deionized water to remove the oil on the surface. Later, it was dried in an oven at 100 °C, and recorded as NF.

Preparation of Mn/NF catalyst

A Mn/NF catalyst was prepared by one-step hydrothermal synthesis as follows: KMnO4 (7.5 mmol) was dissolved in 75 mL of deionized water, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min. The above solution and NF were placed into a 100 mL Teflon lined high-pressure reactor and heated for 24 h in a constant temperature of 160 °C. The solution was centrifuged, and the catalyst was washed with ethanol and deionized water, and vacuum dried at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain a monolithic MnO2/NF catalyst, denoted as Mn/NF. A current of 6 W is applied to the Mn/NF for 1 h, referred to as an electrically pre-treated Mn/NF catalyst (EPMN).

Characterizations

XRD was carried out on a Panalytical Empyrean, and Cu Kα radiation (40 kV and 40 mA) was used to determine the XRD of the catalyst in a range of 5–90° at a rate of 2°/min. The morphology and structure were performed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Gemini 500) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. XPS was performed on a Thermo Scientific spectrometer (USA) equipped with Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV), with the carbon 1 s peak (BE = 284.6 eV) used as the standard to calibrate all binding energies. Raman spectra were acquired using a DXR2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) instrument equipped with a 532 nm semiconductor laser. In CV and EIS, a working electrode, immersed in the solution, had an area of 1 cm2 coated with Mn/NF catalyst. The tests were completed at a scan rate of 0.1 V/s. For TPR measurements, the H2-TPR were performed on a Microtrac BELCAT II instrument. The infrared thermometer used is the Wuhan Gaode company’s equipment model PL210, with an infrared resolution of 256*192@12 μm. FTIR was carried out on an iS10 FT-IR spectrometer of Negoli. WAXS patterns were obtained at the beamline of High Energy Photon Source (HEPS-ID08). The 2D WAXS patterns were further converted to 1D I-q curves using the ScatterX62 software. XAFS spectra were measured at the XRD station of beamline 1W2B and 4B9A of Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) in a transmission mode or fluorescence mode. sXAS of Mn L-edge and O K-edge were collected from the XMCD-B beam lines at the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory (NSRL), Hefei, China.
