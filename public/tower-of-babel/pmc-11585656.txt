Ecological status and type of alteration determine the C-balance and climate change mitigation capacity of Mediterranean inland saline shallow lakes
Authors: Daniel Morant, Carlos Rochera, Antonio Picazo, Javier Miralles-Lorenzo, Alba Camacho-Santamans, Antonio Camacho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSaline shallow lakes may play an important role in carbon exchange with the atmosphere, but their alteration may change carbon balance and greenhouse gas emissions patterns. This study investigated the relationship between carbon metabolic processes and environmental factors, focusing on alterations in salinity, hydroperiod length, and trophic status. The results revealed that disruptions to natural salinity patterns, driven by hydrological changes and ecological degradation, enhanced carbon greenhouse gas emitting metabolisms. In contrast, well-preserved lakes demonstrated significantly higher carbon retention and climate mitigation capacities (–188 ± 412 g C m−2 yr−1) compared to lakes with hydrological and trophic disturbances (–30 ± 141 g C m−2 yr−1). These findings emphasize the relevance of the metabolic activity of saline shallow lakes and highlight the need for targeted management and restoration efforts to maximize their climate regulation potential. Introduction

Saline lakes are aquatic ecosystems widely distributed across the globe, with a greater presence in arid and semi-arid regions and endorheic basins. In terms of total volume, these lakes are comparable to freshwater lakes1. However, saline lakes experience particularly high rates of alteration2–4, as their ecological characteristics—high salinity and, in many cases, temporary hydroperiods5—make them especially vulnerable to anthropogenic disturbances and climate change.

Climate change not only alters the hydrological patterns of many of these lakes3 but also contributes to rising surface water temperatures6. This phenomenon can have even more pronounced effects on shallow lakes across diverse latitudes. Additionally, anthropogenic disturbances are leading to a global decline in water quality7, negatively impacting biodiversity and biogeochemical processes8,9.

Saline shallow lakes are recognized as active participants in carbon exchange with the atmosphere10,11; however, uncertainties remain regarding the impact of global change on their carbon balances and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.

Carbon (C)-related ecosystem metabolic processes, basically photosynthesis and respiration, are intrinsically linked with the lakes’ ecological features. The rates of these processes can be determined by salinity, hydroperiod features, and temperature12. These, among multiple factors, may finally influence the net ecosystem metabolism (NEM), a proxy of the total C-balance13, which determines the net C-capturing or C-releasing capacity of the ecosystems14. Hydroperiod, a key ecological characteristic of lentic ecosystems, is of paramount importance for the annual C-balance15 and for GHG emissions16. Differences in salinity influence other physical-chemical features and have an impact on the biological communities17,18, thus affecting the C-processes19,20. Lower salinity and the extent of the hydroperiod length towards warmer months can also enhance methane emissions in saline lakes21, with huge implications for the ecosystem C-balance22 and, especially, for its global warming potential (GWP) effect23. The trophic status is also decisive for the primary production and respiration rates of aquatic ecosystems24,25, thus influencing C-GHG exchanges with the atmosphere.

The Mediterranean region, with its semi-arid climate, is highly diverse in terms of wetlands and saline lake types. In the Iberian Peninsula, there are many lake complexes and large endorheic basins that contain a significant number of inland saline shallow lakes. Like many other regions globally, saline lakes in this area have been highly altered, particularly due to land reclamation, pollution, and water overexploitation8. Furthermore, historical management practices have favoured intensive agriculture, neglecting the ecological importance of these lentic aquatic ecosystems26.

Hydrological alterations are among the most common impacts in these areas. The natural hydrological regimes of many lakes have been modified, often receiving excess freshwater inputs even including pollution sources such as urban wastewater or agricultural runoff26. Consequently, both the hydroperiod and salinity have been altered, often accompanied by excessive nutrient loading27. More recently, management interventions have additionally hydrologically altered some lakes by maintaining continuous freshwater inflows to ensure water permanence for recreational activities and waterfowl habitat during the summer months26. All these additional freshwater inputs have degraded the key ecological feature of these ecosystems—their saline character2. Today, ongoing restoration efforts aim to recover the natural characteristics of highly degraded or even extinct shallow lakes.

The widespread ecological degradation suggests that the carbon balance and GHG fluxes from saline shallow lakes may have been significantly altered. Recent data indicate a rapid increase in methane emissions from some types of ecosystems, with at least one-third of global methane emissions now attributed to changes in the balance of natural sources28, among which saline lakes are included. Therefore, understanding the role of hydrological alterations, salinity changes, and increases in the trophic status over carbon balances in saline lakes is crucial for understanding the functioning of these ecosystems, and this knowledge is essential for effective conservation and proper management.

Given the importance of saline lakes as unique ecosystems as well as in global carbon and GHG balances, and the limited understanding of how alterations affect these systems, this study aimed to explore the link between ecological disruptions and carbon metabolic rates. We examined common alterations in Mediterranean saline shallow lakes, such as hydrological and trophic changes, along with shifts in salinity levels, across a diverse set of sites with contrasting environmental characteristics and management strategies. Our main hypothesis was that ecological degradation, including eutrophication and hydrological changes, negatively impacts carbon-GHG retention, reducing the climate change mitigation potential of these lakes. In some cases, these alterations may even promote GHG emissions, contributing to warming.

Methods

Study site

Nineteen representative inland saline shallow, mostly temporary, lakes (Fig. 1, Table 1), located within the Iberian Peninsula, were selected for this study (Supplementary Table 1). The selection of sites was based on an own inventory of saline lakes in Spain. The 19 representative sites were selected based on criteria that would spatially represent the main saline lake districts in the Iberian Peninsula, covering a wide range of conditions, both ecological (hydroperiod and salinity) and known levels of characteristic and frequent alterations. The selection was made based on previous studies and projects, where the main disturbances were known, and sampling logistics were feasible. Within the lake districts where these systems are located, a representative number of lakes were selected according to their relative abundance. Most of the selected lakes, with the largest occupied surface area, are currently protected areas. Very small lakes, many of which are ephemeral or have disappeared, were not included in this study.

All the selected lakes are endorheic, generally shallow, with maximum depths of a few cm to less than 1.5 m, becoming dry in summer in most cases. Furthermore, the only permanent deep saline lake of Western Europe, Salada de Chiprana, was also surveyed in our study. Both the length of the flooding period and the lake depth were mediated by the annual precipitation-evapotranspiration balance, which also determined, together with the watershed lithology, the seasonality of water salinity. The main dissolved salts were magnesium sulphate, sodium chloride or calcium bicarbonate, depending on lithology of the catchment area and the type of lakes9.

Limnological characteristics of the studied lakes

The study was carried out a covering two hydrological years (2016–2017 and 2017–2018). Studied lakes were sampled three times per hydrological year coinciding with the filling season (fall-winter), the ecological optimum (spring) and the drying season (summer). For the most ephemeral lakes (Salicor, Tirez, Alcahozo, Longar, and Camino de Villlafranca) sampling was carried out bi-monthly during one hydrological year (2013–2014). Average depth at the different sampling points, maximum lake surface, and current flooded area at each sampling, as well as the marginal vegetation coverage (helophytes and/or halophytes, estimated by orthophotos), were also considered for the carbon balance extrapolations (Table 1). Some physical and chemical water variables were measured in situ at a water depth of 5–10 cm avoiding sediment resuspension, considering that the depth of most of these shallow lakes was not deeper than that in some seasons. A multiparameter WTW Multi 3410 logger, connected to a WTW Tetracon®, holding a 925 IDS probe was used for measuring water electrical conductivity, and a WTW FDO® 925 IDS optical probe for oxygen and temperature (WTW, Germany) measurements. The pH was measured with a Crison Basic 20 portable pH-meter. Alkalinity was estimated from unfiltered samples by titration with HCl using a pH shift indicator (phenolphthalein) of the equivalence end point pH (method 2320; 29). Analyses of nitrogen and phosphorus compounds were conducted following spectrophotometric methods using a Beckman DU-7 UV-Visible spectrophotometer29. Total suspended solids (TSS) and volatile solids (% organic matter within the TSS) were determined by the gravimetric method after drying and later burning, respectively, of the seston retained in Whatman glass microfiber filters, grade 934-AH (method 2540, 29). The Chl-a concentrations were measured by HPLC after filtering water onto GF/F glass microfiber filters (Whatman, England)30. Sediments were also sampled at the same time and location than the water, to a depth of 5–10 cm from its surface. The organic matter content of the sediment (in % of dry weight) was obtained by the loss on ignition (LOI%-sed) method at 460 ˚C/6 h (LOI460)31.

Lake grouping according to the water salinity, ecological (trophic) status, and type of alteration

The 19 studied lakes were grouped into 5 ecological classes, four of them corresponding to temporary lakes, related to their electrical conductivity (EC) as a surrogate of the salinity. Hyposaline lakes were considered as those showing an average EC of < 15 mS cm−1. Lakes were considered as mesosaline when they showed average EC within the range 15–45 mS cm−1, whereas hypersaline lakes had average EC of > 45 mS cm−1 (Table 1)32. Considering their naturally different water chemical composition, dominated by sodium carbonate, and their permanently high pH, both Caballo Alba and Iglesia were classified in a separated group as soda lakes. Finally, the fifth class was represented by the only permanent hypersaline inland lake currently existing in Spain, Salada de Chiprana. This lake was considered as naturally permanent, as historic alterations, linked to the development of agriculture in the area a millennium ago, have sustained permanent flooding for centuries33. This lake was included in the study as a representative site of the diversity of saline lakes. However, only its shallower areas were surveyed.

For the grouping in ecological status classes, the abundance of phytoplankton, using chl-a concentration as a metric, was the indicator used. Thus, the ecological status was then related to the trophic status, The used thresholds of chl-a metric in the ecological status classification (Good, Moderate, Poor and Bad status) were retrieved from the Spanish regulation for each lake type (Supplementary Table 1) according to their classification as lake waterbodies for the European Water Framework in Spain34. For the permanent lake Salada de Chiprana, the chl-a concentration of mid-spring and mid-summer samplings were used. For temporary lakes, chl-a values were obtained not before than one month after the beginning of filling and around mid-spring (optimum season), following the procedures established by Spanish regulations35.

Finally, lakes were classified according to their conservation/alteration status. The well-preserved group (11 lakes) included those saline lakes that lacked acute hydrological alteration, keeping its temporary natural hydroperiod pattern, also lacking significant trophic alterations. In addition, other parameters were considered to rule out other types of alterations affecting water quality, mainly chl-a. to assess the level of trophic alterations. Two of the well-preserved sites (lakes Tollos and Musco) were actually not unaltered but instead restored, but had recovered their natural ecological features, so they are then grouped with the well-preserved lakes. The hydrologically altered (HA) group (2 lakes) includes those lakes that received significant extra freshwater inputs, which modified the hydroperiod and salinity values until they became semi-permanent or permanent, though these extra water inputs did not provide significant nutrients amounts. Such way, the alteration was only hydrological and did not increase the tropic status. Chl-a values were also checked to rule out trophic alteration. Finally, hydrologically plus trophic altered (HTA) lakes (8 sites) consisted of systems with known external water inputs, mainly from WWTP, that altered the hydroperiod, and also increased nutrient inputs, with average chl-a concentrations above 15 mg m−3.

Carbon metabolic study

The metabolic rates of the main ecosystem C-related processes, primary production (by plankton, benthos and marginal vegetation) capturing atmospheric carbon, and respiration (separating aerobic respiration from the CH4 emissions resulting from the balance between methanogenesis and methanotrophy), releasing carbon, were studied seasonally, following the methods described by Morant et al.36. These rates were further extrapolated and integrated as aggregated annual rates.

Plankton and benthos gross primary production (GPP) and respiration rates were measured to estimate their relative contribution to the Net Ecosystem Metabolic (NEM) balance, using the oxygen-exchange method37. The oxygen concentration was measured before and after incubation in dark and clear Winkler bottles for plankton, and similarly in bell-jars for benthos, as described in36. With the respirometry method the anaerobic CO2-flux generated by anaerobic respirations other than methanogenesis was not assessed.

Net production of the marginal vegetation, composed of helophytes and/or halophytes, depending on the lake (see Table 1), was obtained by the harvest method, estimating the net biomass production and the growth rate between sampling dates38.

CH4 emissions were estimated as the measured balance between methanogenesis and methanotrophy, following the method described by Camacho et al.21. CH4 emissions were measured in 6–12 replicated cores containing, from the bottom to the top, a layer of undisturbed lake sediment, a lake water layer (when flooded), and an air chamber. CH4 was measured using an Aeroqual Gas AQ-MT sensor equipped with a gas-sensitive semiconductor (Aeroqual, New Zealand) calibrated by Gas-Chromatography21.

All measured metabolic rates were converted to carbon units and integrated as seasonal and annual rates, the latter by extrapolation within the periods between sampling events. A C-balance estimated from the metabolic approach (NEM-balance) was calculated by comparing carbon uptake by C-assimilating processes (GPP –gross photosynthetic rates- by plankton and benthos, as well as helophytes/halophytes net production) with C-releasing processes (plankton and benthos aerobic respiration, and methane net emissions resulting from the methanogenesis/methanotrophy balance). For the overall lake calculation of the final balance from each of these processes, the relative surface of water flooding and marginal vegetation coverages were considered, obtained from satellite images and using the calculation tools of Google Earth, together with field observations39–41.

Considering the warming capacity of the C-GHG involved in the metabolic processes measured, a global warming potential (GWP) balance was also estimated. To do this, carbon units were converted into CO2-eq. in terms of the greenhouse potential effect, being 1 the factor conversion for C-CO2, and 28 the factor conversion for C-CH4, as reported by the IPCC42. Both the NEM and GWP balances were estimated seasonally as well as annually.

Statistical analyses

For the comparison of the rates of the metabolic activities among the different lake types and status, both seasonal and annual integrated rates relative to the surface per lake (g C m−2 yr−1) were used. One-way ANOVAs and HDS Tukey post-hoc tests were applied to explore the differences in the metabolic rates between the lake types, their ecological (trophic) status, and alterations. A PERMANOVA (9999 permutations) based on the Bray-Curtis similarity matrix was applied to the annual metabolic rates, considering the lake type, their ecological status, and alterations. A pair-wise test PERMANOVA (9999 permutations) was additionally applied to check significant differences between the groups defined (ecological lake types, ecological status, and alterations). Data were transformed by square root. Finally, a distance-based redundancy multivariate analysis, (dbRDA) was applied to explore the relationship and gradients between the environmental features (indicators of the lake type, the ecological status, and alterations) and the annual carbon metabolic rates. The software used to carry out the statistical analyses were PRIMER 6 and IBM SPSS Statistics 24.

Results

Physical-chemical characteristics

The studied set of saline lakes (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1) showed the ecological variability existing within similar shallow lake types, especially given the huge range of salinity covered. Average electrical conductivities (EC), as a surrogate of lake water salinity, ranged from 3 to 124 mS cm−1 (Fig. 2a). In particular, the hypersaline lakes located in La Mancha Húmeda Biosphere Reserve, such as Longar, Tirez, Salicor and Alcahozo, displayed the highest salinity-range rank, most of them being temporary lakes lacking hydrological alterations. On the other hand, the soda lakes, Caballo Alba and Iglesia, as well as other hyposaline lakes, such as Grande de Villafranca, El Hito, Musco and Veguilla, showed average EC below 10 mS cm−1 (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Table 2). Hydrologically altered lakes, including those where the non-natural freshwater inputs also cause trophic alterations, showed, on average, lower EC than well-preserved lakes of a similar saline type, given the excess of freshwater inputs. Seasonal variability in EC within each lake was also evident, due to seasonal differences in the hydrological balance, reaching maximum EC in periods close to drying, evolving from lower salinities in colder months. Thus, maximum values of EC could be more than 300 mS cm−1, as in lakes Salicor and Tirez, and even close to 500 mS cm−1 in the brine of Lake Longar, associated with puddles at the time of lake drying (Fig. 2a).

Chl-a concentrations differed among the studied lakes, with higher average values, between 24 and 185 µg L−1, for the trophic altered sites (HTA), where part of water inputs often came from sewage in the past, though now they are from treated urban wastewater (Fig. 2b). On the other hand, well-preserved lakes, such as Caballo Alba, and the restored lakes Musco and Tollos, showed chl-a values below 1 µg L−1.

When comparing chl-a concentrations with the availability of soluble inorganic nutrients, high chl-a values, above 15 µg L−1, usually coincided with higher Soluble Reactive Phosphorus (SRP) concentrations, from 2.7 µmol L−1 in Manjavacas, to more than 17 µmol L−1 in Veguilla, the latter currently receiving treated wastewater although sewage was poured in the past. A similar pattern was found for inorganic nitrogen, both NO3−, and NH4+, as well as for the percentage of organic matter of the TSS, which were also higher on average in the trophic altered lakes (Supplementary Table 2). Other water features, such as alkalinity and pH, showed much higher values in soda lakes, both Caballo Alba and Iglesia (Supplementary Table 2), which averaged a pH of 9–10 and an alkalinity between 26 and 31 meq. L−1, in contrast with values mostly below 10 meq. L−1 for the other studied lakes.

Sediment features also differed between the studied lakes. Organic matter content in sediment was highly variable, being higher in lakes such as Salada de Chiprana, Gallocanta, as well as in the altered lakes Grande de Villafranca, Manjavacas, Longar and Yeguas, with values above 20% LOI% in sediment. The restored sites, lakes Musco and Tollos, were those with lower LOI% in sediment, with values around 5–6% (Supplementary Table 2).

Temporal patterns of carbon metabolic processes

The rates of the ecosystem metabolic processes fixing (photosynthesis, GPP for plankton and benthos, and net primary production –NPP– for emerged plants – helophytes and/or halophytes) or releasing (respiration, both aerobic respiration and methane balance) carbon, showed temporal differences, as well as differences between lakes with contrasting status. A temporal pattern could be identified, with the drying season when still flooded, with higher temperatures, showing higher metabolic rates of plankton and benthos, and higher lake net methane emissions. In the ecological optimum season (i.e., spring), helophyte/halophyte productivity peaked (Fig. 3a), whereas the rates of all carbon processes during the filling period (autumn/winter) were the lowest. In general, both GPP and aerobic respiration by plankton and benthos, as well as methane emissions, behaved similarly, including the peaking pattern in each of the different studied lakes.

On average the balance of plankton and benthos aerobic metabolisms was slightly heterotrophic, meaning a net carbon release, during the filling and optimum seasons, reaching an equilibrium, nearly neutral in terms of carbon balance, during the drying season. However, when including CH4 emissions (C-releasing) and marginal vegetation production (C-capture), the NEM-balance turned to a C-retention capacity both in the optimum and drying seasons (Fig. 3b). Looking to the balances per site (Fig. 3c), most lakes behaved generally as heterotrophic (C-emitters) during the coldest months, in the filling season, when the emergent vegetation was dormant. From the optimum season to drying, net C-sequestration was observed, on average, for the studied lakes, though with high variability. Some of them, such as Larga de Villacañas, which is fed by great amounts of treated wastewater from a nearby WWTP that has transformed this naturally temporary lake into a permanent one, followed the opposite pattern, acting as an autotrophic C-retention system during the filling (fall-winter) and optimum seasons (–263 ± 868 and –1,001 ± 2,126 mg C m−2 d−1 , respectively), then becoming a strong C-emitter during the warmest season (+ 7,932 mg C m−2 d−1), as a result of very high plankton respiration and much lower primary production compared to respiration during this period. Other lakes, such as Veguilla, permanently behave as autotrophic, displaying C-retention capacity regardless of the season. Meanwhile, lake Longar maintained its net C-release balance during the different studied seasons.

Annual rates of C-metabolic processes

Looking at the integrated annual rates per site, some patterns arise when grouped according to their conservation/alteration status (Fig. 4). Hydrologically and trophic altered sites, the latter characterized by higher chl-a concentrations, showed much higher plankton metabolic rates and methane emissions, whereas well-preserved (including restored) lakes have generally higher relative benthos rates.

Positive values mean C-release and warming generation, negative values mean C-retention and warming mitigating capacity. Lake codes as given in Table 1.

Lakes such as Larga de Villacañas, Camino de Villafranca, Veguilla, and Iglesia, which are strongly eutrophic (Fig. 4) and present trophic and hydrological alterations as having received wastewater inputs, showed the highest rates of plankton activity, both for GPP (360-1,100 g C m−2 yr−1) and aerobic respiration (260-1,190 g C m−2 yr−1). Benthos metabolic rates were one order of magnitude lower for both GPP and respiration (30–120 g C m−2 yr−1). Lakes Manjavacas and Longar, despite being trophic altered lakes, presented lower, but still high, annual plankton GPP and respiration rates (70–230 g C m−2 yr−1), and slightly lower benthos rates. Methane emissions were relatively higher in all these trophic altered lakes, all of them above 1 g C m−2 yr−1, reaching higher rates in lakes Larga de Villacañas and Longar, with 8.4 and 9.2 g C m−2 yr−1, respectively. Among the trophic altered lakes, except for lakes Veguilla and Manjavacas, where helophyte production was more relevant and methane emissions were lower, as well as for Salicor, all the other trophic altered lakes acted as heterotrophic systems on the yearly balance, being net metabolic C-emitters (+ 30 to + 110 g C m−2 yr−1, Fig. 4a), with the GWP showing a warming generating capacity (+ 200 to + 700 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1, Fig. 4b) strongly enhanced by the heavier weighting of CH4 emissions.

Lakes Grande de Villafranca and Carralogroño, two hydrologically altered sites without trophic alterations, showed lower GPP and respiration plankton rates compared to the trophic altered lakes, but relatively more important GPP and respiration benthos rates (between 30 and 150 g C m−2 yr−1). In Lake Grande de Villafranca, helophyte net production rates were among the highest of all the studied lakes, which resulted in a highly C-retention metabolic capability (–330 g C m−2 yr−1).

Among the well-preserved and restored lakes (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2), hypersaline systems, such as lakes Gallocanta, Tirez, and Alcahozo, showed relatively low GPP and respiration rates, ranging between 10 and 50 g C m−2 yr−1 in the plankton compartment and 1–25 g C m−2 yr−1 in the benthic compartment. CH4 emissions were also low (0.1 to 0.5 g C m−2 yr−1), and halophyte production hardly achieved 60 g C m−2 yr−1. On average, the rates of C-assimilation metabolisms were higher than those of C-releasing metabolisms. The single hypersaline permanent inland lake in Western Europe, Salada de Chiprana, showed relatively higher rates. The benthic compartment in Salada de Chiprana was especially relevant, where a biofilm and submerged charophytes, adapted to these high salinities, played a relevant role in C-fixation. Carbon balances in all these hypersaline lakes were usually near the equilibrium, varying in a narrow range of slight C-retention and mitigating capacities (–8 to –40 g C m−2 yr−1 for the NEM, and –20 to –150 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1 for the GWP).

Other well-preserved or restored lakes, though with lower salinity (mesosaline), such as lake Salada de Zorrilla, and the restored lake Tollos, showed slightly higher rates, on average (45–110 g C m−2 yr−1 for plankton GPP and respiration, and 37–90 g C m−2 yr−1 for benthos GPP and respiration), when compared with the studied hypersaline lakes. On the other hand, well-preserved and restored hyposaline lakes showed a high variability of C-related metabolic rates and balances. Some of these lakes, such as Musco and El Hito, displayed very low metabolic rates, though both had a shorter hydroperiod length than expected during the sampling years. Methane emissions never exceeded 1 g C m−2 yr−1 for the well-preserved and restored lakes, except for the soda lake Caballo Alba (4.3 g C m−2 yr−1). Marginal vegetation net production was generally higher in the well-preserved and restored lakes with lower salinity (hypo- and mesosaline), when it is made up of helophytes, compared to the hypersaline lakes, where halophytes which are the only marginal vegetation, with rates ranging from 90 to 200 g C m−2 yr−1 for the helophyte net production of mesosaline and hyposaline lakes. In fact, lake Musco, a restored site, was completely covered with helophytes (Table 1), which was by far the dominant compartment for C-fluxes in the total NEM balance. This is why lake Musco reached an integrated annual balance of –1,350 g C m−2 yr−1, while the other lakes showed balances of around –30 to –110 g C m−2 yr−1. Overall, among the well-preserved and restored meso- and hyposaline lakes, a strong C-retention and mitigating capacity was observed. Only the soda Lake Caballo Alba showed lower, but even mitigating capacity, with a GWP balance of –10 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1.

Response of carbon metabolic rates and balances to alterations

When comparing the rates of the C-related processes of the lakes based on their conservation status, well-preserved or restored, hydrologically altered, and hydrologically and trophic altered, significant differences arose. Hydrologically and trophic altered displayed statistically significant higher rates than well-preserved sites for plankton GPP (Fig. 5a, F = 5.32, p = 0.017), and plankton respiration (Fig. 5b, F = 4.23, p = 0.034). Meanwhile, significant differences in CH4 emissions were found in hydrologically altered lakes with respect to well-preserved lakes (Fig. 5e, F = 4.54, p = 0.027), as confirmed by ANOVA and post-hoc tests. Although some differences are noticeable for both GPP and respiration for benthos, as well as for marginal vegetation net production (Fig. 5c, d and f), these were not statistically significant.

Looking at the balances studied per salinity type (Fig. 6a), hyposaline lakes, on average, clearly showed a net C-sequestration balance (–509 ± 574 g C m−2 yr−1) and provided a net GWP mitigation effect (–1680 ± 2202 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1). A reduction of the C-retention and warming mitigating capacity was observed with increases in salinity. Mesosaline lakes reduced their average C-sequestration balance to –90 ± 76 g C m−2 yr−1, and their GWP-balance to –249 ± 383 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1. The hypersaline permanent Salada de Chiprana, was close to a NEM and GWP neutral condition, whereas the temporary hypersaline lakes, on average, had balances slightly leaning towards some C-release and warming generation (+ 8 ± 54 g C m−2 yr−1, and + 103 ± 316 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1). The soda lakes showed an even higher C-release balance on average (+ 21 ± 90 g C m−2 yr−1), as well as a higher GWP balance and, thus, a greater warming capacity as well (+ 216 ± 321 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1).

However, the results found for the lake types according to their salinity might be biased by the differences in the ecological status and the alteration/conservation degree in each of the salinity types. To deal with this, we performed a similar analysis where the comparisons were made considering just the well-preserved and altered lakes, separately (Fig. 7). The results of this analysis showed how well-preserved and restored lakes maintained a C-retention and mitigating capacity, reaching the NEM equilibrium when increasing the salinity to hypersaline levels, whereas, among the altered lakes, only hyposaline lakes still maintained some C-retention and mitigating capacity on average, strongly reducing the C-retention and turning into C-releasers and a net generation of warming capacity with increases in salinity.

A similar analysis was performed with the ecological status categories given by the European WFD using chl-a concentrations as an indicator26, as shown in Fig. 6b. This showed a clear C-retention NEM balance and mitigating GWP capacity in lakes at Good ecological status, with trophic conditions close to those of reference unaltered lakes (–210 ± 390 g C m−2 yr−1 and –671 ± 1435 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1, respectively). Although reducing their C-retention and warming mitigation capacity, Moderate and Poor status lakes, slightly maintained these capacities. Conversely, Bad status lakes (the worst category), with chl-a concentrations corresponding to eutrophic conditions, turned to C-release (+ 31 ± 61 g C m−2 yr−1) and warming-generating systems (+ 286 ± 356 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1).

Finally, the differences between well-preserved and restored, hydrologically altered, and hydrologically and trophic altered lakes (Fig. 6c) were contrasted. NEM-balance maintained a C-retention effect of –188 ± 412 g C m−2 yr−1 on average for well-preserved and restored lakes, similar to hydrologically altered lakes (–219 ± 167 g C m−2 yr−1), whereas the C-retention capacity was greatly reduced for hydrologically and trophic altered lakes (–30 ± 141 g C m−2 yr−1). Concerning the GWP, the mitigating effect shown by well-preserved and restored lakes (–651 ± 1524 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1) as well as by hydrologically altered lakes, (–448 ± 127 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1), turned into a warming effect for hydrologically and trophic altered lakes (+ 20 ± 601 g CO2-eq m−2 yr−1), mainly associated with their higher methane emissions.

Discussion

Inland saline lakes, one of the most characteristic lentic ecosystems in the Mediterranean and many other regions of the world43,44, have specific ecological features that largely determine their ecological functioning and, particularly, their metabolic rates, C-balances and climate change mitigation potential. Shallow lakes and wetlands have been shown to be active in C-exchange with the atmosphere10,11, sometimes showing high C-burial rates45,46. However, there are multiple interrelated factors that can drive carbon fluxes and can be related with alterations, including changes in the length of the flooding period, salinity, and trophic status12,14. In this manuscript the impact of these alterations on their functioning was deeply investigated in saline shallow lakes.

According to our results, overall, hydrological and, especially, trophic alterations, reduced the C-retention and GWP mitigation capacity of inland saline Mediterranean lakes. This trend has also been observed in other regions and wetland types47–49. These alterations impact other nutrient cycles as well, promoting phosphorus mobilization9 and increasing pollutant concentration8. In addition to disrupting biogeochemical cycles, these alterations have adverse effects on other ecosystem functions, biodiversity, and the services provided by saline lakes4. Conversely maintaining a natural lower trophic status and natural hydroperiod, as demonstrated by our findings, supports a better balance between the planktonic and benthic compartments This balance, in turns, enhances C-retention and improves the lake’s capacity to mitigate warming.

Delving into the drivers of the C-exchange and metabolic rates in saline lakes, ecological factors such as temperature and salinity were found to play a key role in C-processes. Seasonal patterns in C-metabolic rates and balances suggested that temperature influences aerobic respiration and photosynthesis (e.g.,50), but most notably affects CH4 emissions21. Our results indicate that during warmer months, anaerobic pathways like methanogenesis increase more than aerobic processes, given their stronger response to temperature14,21, and may be further enhanced by lower salinity21,51. Consequently, hydrological alterations—such as increased freshwater inputs extending the hydroperiod of temporary saline lakes and reducing salinity—could lead to a substantial increase in CH4 emissions during warmer months. Under unaltered conditions, these lakes should be dry in summer and C-metabolic rates should be much lower52, even though dry aquatic ecosystems may continue to emit CO2 to the atmosphere53. Modelling scenarios predict that CH4 emissions, and thus warming potential, would increase in future climate change scenarios in these altered systems54.

Our study also confirmed that high salinity reduces C-metabolic activities, as the hypersaline temporary lakes showed the lowest metabolic rates. However, when comparing the well-preserved temporary hypersaline lakes (Alcahozo, Tirez and Gallocanta), mostly lacking submerged macrophytes when sampled, with the only permanent inland hypersaline lake in Western Europe, Salada de Chiprana33, the latter showed the highest C-fixation capacity due to extensive benthic coverage by the halophytic charophyte Lamprothamnium papulosum, and microbial mats adapted to hypersaline conditions55. This highlights the importance of permanent flooding for the development and C-retention capacity of halophytic submerged charophytes and biofilms. In many other among the studied lakes, benthic communities played a significant role in the overall C-balance. Though benthic C-fixation rates were lower than plankton, they often helped to offset C-releasing planktonic contributions to the NEM-balance, especially in well-preserved and restored lakes with submerged macrophyte coverage—an indicator of good ecological quality56.

Focusing on the integrated net metabolism (NEM), in our study, most well-preserved lakes exhibited a net carbon retention, functioning as net autotrophic ecosystems with strong GWP mitigation capacities. Lakes like Alcahozo, Carravalseca, and Zorrilla clearly displayed carbon retention and a significant ability to mitigate warming. However, soda lakes, characterized by lower salinity and sodium bicarbonate dominance, behaved differently. They showed higher CH4 emissions, these contributing relatively more to their GWP. Despite this, the well-preserved Caballo Alba lake maintained an autotrophic balance with low plankton activity and significant benthic contributions, with the contribution of submerged macrophytes. In contrast, the degraded Iglesia soda lake displayed a net heterotrophic behaviour, releasing carbon into the atmosphere and driving warming. This highlights again how the degradation of trophic status reduces the climate mitigation capacity of saline and soda lakes, potentially turning them into net carbon sources, contributing to global warming through GHG emissions.

Hydrological alterations disproportionally impacted C-process rates, particularly when combined with trophic changes, as many of these freshwater inflows came from partially sewage or wastewater9. These wastewater inputs are often managed to keep the lake flooded during the drying season for different purposes. Altering the hydrological regime with wastewater, even when treated, degrades water quality and likely reduces C-sequestration capacity while increasing GHG emissions57,58, as observed in lakes Camino de Villafranca, Larga de Villacañas, and Iglesia. Land uses in the watershed may also have significant influences on water pollution59, as found for Lake Salicor. Hydrological alterations not associated with trophic alterations also modulated the C-balance, enhancing significantly CH4 emissions, and promoting the net marginal helophyte production, like in Grande de Villafranca.

As seen in other studies (e.g.,24,25,38), our results showed how eutrophication primarily impacted plankton C- processes. In the multivariate analysis, plankton GPP and respiration differed significantly from benthic metabolisms, with higher rates of plankton processes linked to trophic altered lakes. A similar pattern was also shown in coastal marshes38, where increases in phytoplankton biomass inhibited the growth of submerged macrophytes and degraded water quality.

Many trophic altered lakes exhibited higher organic matter content in their sediments, leading to higher methane emissions. However, some lakes currently lacking trophic alterations, like Gallocanta or Salada de Chiprana, experienced past nutrient and organic matter discharges, resulting in the accumulation of sediment rich in organic matter. In hypersaline lakes, higher organic matter content did not result in large CH4 emissions, given the inhibitory effect of salinity favouring sulphate-reducers over methanogens21,60,61. Other studies also confirm that salinity significantly reduces CH4 emissions in aquatic ecosystems62. In contrast, restored lakes, such as Tollos and Musco, displayed low levels of organic matter in the sediment, as the upper organic-rich layers were removed during restauration, resulting in relatively low methane emission rates. This highlights the role of sediment organic content and salinity in regulating anaerobic metabolisms.

Trophic altered lakes generally shifted towards heterotrophy, with plankton respiration exceeding primary production and declining benthic activity. High nutrient levels can boost plankton production, but this is often offset or surpassed by increased respiration due to organic matter degradation25,63. However, some lakes, such as Manjavacas and Veguilla, maintained high autotrophic rates and some C-retention capacity, contributing to warming mitigation. This is largely due to helophyte production, enhanced by reduced salinity from freshwater inputs.

Despite the key role of the marginal vegetation in the NEM-balance, differences among sites emerged based on salinity. Faster growing helophytes, like Phragmites australis, Juncus acutus, and Scirpus holoschoenus dominated the marginal vegetation in hypo- and mesosaline lakes, regardless of the cause of the low salinity (natural or exceeding freshwater inpouts). In contrast, slow-growing halophytes, mainly Salicornia sp., were more prevalent in hypersaline lakes (Table 1). Helophytes strongly contributed to C-retention, fixing more carbon than the halophytes38, though halophytes still play a role the NEM-balance in hypersaline systems64. Halophytes, adapted to saline environments, may help regulate the C-balance, especially under future climate scenarios in the Mediterranean region. By forming organic carbon deposits, these species could enhance the resilience of C-cycling processes in lakes, which are likely to face worsening fluctuations and extreme conditions65.

Another key issue from our study is the use of chl-a thresholds, customized by lake type according to the WFD, to assess ecological status and provide insights into C-retention and warming mitigation capacity of inland saline lakes. Spanish regulation, aligned within the WFD, have developed a metric based on chl-a concentration that establishes reference values and boundaries specific to each lake type34. This allows for a more accurate assessment, taking into account factors such as salinity and water residence time. In our study, this evaluation classified lakes by trophic condition, significantly aligning environmental variables and metabolic rates in the multivariate analysis. Assessing the ecological status of waterbodies is a legal obligation within the European Union, serving as a basis for management and restoration criteria66. Given the relationships we identified between ecological status, carbon retention, and GHG emission reduction, these findings could help incorporate a climate perspective into water policies and management practices, in line with WFD criteria.

Understanding how salinity, hydroperiod, and ecological status influence the ecosystem metabolism is crucial for developing effective management policies and plans, as well-preserved and restored saline shallow lakes can play a role as climate allies. Particularly now, with ecosystem restoration strategies being implemented in Europe following the approval of the EU Nature Restoration Law, findings such as those here described can support decision-making when restoring or managing ecosystems by also considering their functioning. In our study, for instance, anthropogenic disturbances, and the resulting changes in a lake’s ecological condition, were shown to play a key role in its ability to store C and mitigate GHG emissions, as suggested by other authors in different wetland types and regions (e.g.,47–49).

Therefore, management and restoration plans based on this type of research could strengthen the climate change mitigation and C-retention capacities through the conservation/restoration of natural ecological features. Hydrological restoration is an emerging approach for mitigating and adapting to climate change, while also preserving water quality and biodiversity67,68. Linked with hydrology, maintaining or recovering salinity levels according to the ecological type, along with the seasonal variability and temporality of the hydroperiod, is key to ensure the proper functioning of these ecosystems. Additionally, controlling external nutrient loads is essential to reduce eutrophication and promoting the establishment of submerged macrophytes, which can enhance C-retention and mitigate warming, ultimately supporting the lake’s resilience to climate change. An example would be using the outflow water from the WWTP of the municipalities near the lakes for crop irrigation instead of discharging them directly into the lake, as is currently common in the study area. This, in addition to avoiding hydrological and trophic alteration, would also reduce the overexploitation of the aquifers, which are exhausted in the area.

Conclusions

This study identified the impact of hydrological and trophic alterations, as well as salinity, on C-metabolic processes and GHG emissions in saline shallow lakes, until now poorly studied in these aquatic ecosystems. Focusing on the Mediterranean region, this research provides a basis for understanding the functioning of saline shallow lakes globally. Natural ecological characteristics and the ecological status were found to significantly influence C-metabolisms and the regulation of the net ecosystem metabolism balance. Variations in salinity were largely driven by hydrological changes due to anthropic freshwater inputs, which extended the flooding period. The patterns observed indicated increased C-metabolic rates as salinity decreased. Helophyte carbon fixation, but also CH4 emissions were higher in less saline lakes. The presence of water in temporary lakes during the warmest months further activated C-metabolisms, particularly CH4 emissions. Trophic status influenced the NEM-balance and the relative contribution of the different lake compartment. In well-preserved (including restored) lakes the benthic compartment played a more prominent role, whereas in altered lakes, plankton-mediated carbon fluxes and anaerobic C-processes became more dominant. Marginal vegetation, particularly helophytes, played a key role in carbon balances. NEM balance tended towards greater C-retention in hyposaline lakes, and in those with naturally low trophic status, while soda lakes and lakes experiencing eutrophication tended to function more as C-sources. Lakes with hydrological alterations and bad ecological status displayed a higher warming potential.

Assessing ecological status using the WFD thresholds for each lake type could be useful to link legally reclaimed ecological conditions and conservation policies with a climatic perspective. Knowing that this study is carried out in a specific region, and in medium-size lakes (not ponds nor big endorheic areas), further research on the effects of other alterations and climate change in saline lakes functioning is needed, particularly across different regions and latitudes, and more lake subtypes, to establish global patterns. These insights would support the development of management and restoration strategies to improve the ecological integrity and functionality of saline lakes, thereby enhancing their role in climate mitigation.
