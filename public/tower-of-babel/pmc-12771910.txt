Effects of remimazolam versus propofol on the optic nerve sheath diameter in patients undergoing laparoscopic hysterectomy: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Huiman Ji, Gaojie Yu, Huifan Huang, Zhenyi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have suggested that propofol can reduce intracranial pressure (ICP). However, the effects of remimazolam, a new intravenous anesthetic, on ICP remain unknown. This study aimed to compare the effects of remimazolam and propofol on the ICP in patients undergoing laparoscopic hysterectomy by measuring the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) using ultrasound.MethodsA total of 60 patients scheduled for elective laparoscopic hysterectomy were randomly assigned to remimazolam group (0.3 mg/kg induction, 1–2 mg/kg/h infusion) and propofol group (2 mg/kg induction, 4–8 mg/kg/h infusion). The ONSD was measured 5 min after induction of anesthesia in the supine position (T1); 15 min (T2), 30 min (T3), and 60 min (T4) following the establishment of pneumoperitoneum and the Trendelenburg position; and at 5 min after desufflation in the supine position (T5). Respiratory and hemodynamic variables, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and headache were also recorded.ResultsCompared with the ONSD measured at T1, the ONSD at T2-T5 were significantly increase in both groups (P<0.05), whereas the extent of change in ONSD at T2-T5 showed no significant differences between the propofol group and remimazolam group (7.30% vs. 8.11%, 9.45% vs. 10.41%, 9.37% vs. 10.31%, 4.26% vs. 4.25%, P>0.05).ConclusionsThe extent of the increase in ONSD during the laparoscopic hysterectomy has no significantly difference between remimazolam and propofol anesthesia, suggesting that remimazolam and propofol may have similar effects on ICP.Trial registrationThis trial was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on April 18, 2025 (Registration Number: ChiCTR 2500101025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03525-w.

Introduction

Laparoscopic hysterectomy is widely utilized due to its advantages of minimal trauma, reduced bleeding, rapid rehabilitation [1]. This surgical approach requires the use of carbon dioxide (CO2) pneumoperitoneum combined with steep Trendelenburg position to optimize the surgical exposure. However, the combination of these factors can lead to an increase in cerebral blood volume and consequently elevated ICP [2, 3]. Patients with impaired cerebral autoregulation, such as those with systemic hypertension and cerebrovascular disease, are vulnerable to elevated ICP, which may affect cerebral homeostasis [4]. Additionally, an increase in ICP may contribute to postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and postoperative headaches, which are closely related to patient satisfaction, length of hospital stay, and costs [5].Therefore, monitoring ICP during laparoscopic surgery facilitates anesthetic management in anesthetized patients.

Ultrasound measurement of the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) has been suggested as a simple, non-invasive, and reliable bedside technique for assessing ICP, exhibiting a good correlation with invasive ICP measurements [6]. This method can effectively and rapidly evaluate the changes in ICP [7, 8]. As demonstrated in previous studies, there is a considerable increase in ONSD during laparoscopic surgery performed in the Trendelenburg position [9–11].

Anesthetics can influence ICP by affecting cerebral metabolic rate and cerebral blood flow. Propofol, for instance, can cause a dose-dependent reduction in cerebral blood flow and metabolic rate, leading to decreased ICP [12]. In contrast, inhalational anesthetics, as their dosage increases, predominantly cause vasodilation, resulting in increased cerebral blood flow and elevated ICP [13]. A meta-analysis has demonstrated that propofol anesthesia is superior to inhalational anesthesia in attenuating the increase of ONSD during laparoscopic surgery in the Trendelenburg position [14]. Remimazolam, a novel benzodiazepine agonist, offers advantages such as hemodynamic stability and a rapid onset/offset [15, 16]. Park et al. [17] observed that, compared to sevoflurane, remimazolam can attenuate the increase of ONSD in patients undergoing gynecological laparoscopic surgery. However, further research is needed to compare the effects of remimazolam and propofol on ONSD.

This study aimed to explore the changes in ONSD in relation to remimazolam and propofol used in laparoscopic hysterectomy.

Methods

Patient selection

This randomized controlled clinical study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University ((Review Board Number: [2023] No. 120), and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Patients scheduled for elective laparoscopic hysterectomy between October 2023 and October 2024 were included, with the following inclusion criteria: age 18 to 65 years; BMI 18 to 28 kg/m²; American Society of Anesthesiology (ASA) physical status I or II. Exclusion criteria included allergies to anesthetics; drug addiction; cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal dysfunction; central nervous system disorders; psychiatric conditions, or consciousness disturbances; and ophthalmological diseases. Additionally, patients with a change in surgical methods and those with serious adverse events or accidents during the course of the study were excluded.

Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the propofol group (Group P) or the remimazolam group (Group R) using a computerized randomization sequence generated by Microsoft Excel 2010. Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened only after the patient arrived in the operating room. Except for the anesthesiologists who conducted this study, the participants and postoperative outcome assessors were unaware of the group assignment.

Anesthesia protocol

The patients did not receive any preoperative drugs and were routinely monitored for electrocardiogram, noninvasive blood pressure, pulse oxygen saturation, and Bispectral Index (BIS).

Group P received propofol 2 mg/kg and group R received remimazolam 0.3 mg/kg, both groups also received cisatracurium 0.2 mg/kg and fentanyl 4 µg/kg for anesthesia induction. Anesthesia was maintained with propofol 4–8 mg/kg/h in the Group P and remimazolam 1–2 mg/kg/h in the Group R. Both groups received a continuous infusion of remifentanil 0.1–0.2 µg/kg/min and additional boluses of cisatracurium as required at the discretion of the clinician. Mechanical ventilation was initiated in volume controlled mode, with settings including tidal volume of 8–10 ml/kg, respiratory rate of 12–15 breaths/min. During the surgery, ventilation parameters were adjusted to maintain end-tidal CO₂ (EtCO₂) of 35–40 mmHg and peak airway pressures below 30 cmH₂O.

The infusion rate of propofol or remimazolam was modified to maintain the BIS between 40 and 60. In the event of hypotension (MAP<60 mmHg or a decrease of more than 30% from baseline), fluid resuscitation or a bolus of ephedrine (6 mg) was administered. If bradycardia (HR<45 bpm) occurred, atropine (0.3 mg) was given intravenously.

The angle of the Trendelenburg position adopted in the operation was 30°, and the CO2 pneumoperitoneum pressure was maintained at 13mmHg. Thirty minutes before the end of surgery, discontinued additional cisatracurium and administered intravenous dezocine 0.1 mg/kg, flurbiprofen 2 mg/kg and tropisetron 5 mg. The pneumoperitoneum was released and the patient was returned to a supine position at the time of skin closure. All anesthetics were discontinued at the end of the surgery, and patients were awakened in the recovery room.

Ultrasound measurement of ONSD

A disposable transparent patch was applied over the patient’s closed eyelids. The 6–14 Hz high-frequency linear ultrasound probe, coated with ultrasound-coupling agent, was gently placed above each eye and slightly adjusted to obtain the clearest ONSD image. The ONSD was measured vertically 3 mm behind the optic disc in the transverse planes in both eyes (Fig. 1). Each eye was scanned twice, and the average of the 4 ONSD values was recorded per time point for analysis. All ultrasound scanning and image measurements were performed by a trained anesthesiologist who was blinded for group allocation.

Observation indicators

The primary outcome was the extent of change in ONSD at various time points in two groups. The time points included 5 min after tracheal intubation in the supine position (T1), 15 min (T2), 30 min (T3), and 1 h (T4) after the induction of CO2 pneumoperitoneum combined with the Trendelenburg position, and 5 min after the desufflation of pneumoperitoneum and return to the supine position (T5). The ONSD value measured at T1 serves as the baseline. The percentage change in ONSD at T2-T5 relative to the baseline was calculated using the formula: ∆ONSD% = (ONSD - baseline value)/baseline value × 100%, which reflected the extent of change in ONSD at each time point from T2 to T5.

Secondary outcomes included the recording of mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR) at T0 (before anesthesia induction) and T1-T5. Additionally, EtCO2 was recorded at T1-T5. The incidence of postoperative complications such as headache and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) within 24 h after surgery were also documented for both groups.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was performed using PASS 15.0 software. Based on the ONSD measurements of 14 patients in the preliminary experiment, the maximum extent of increase in ONSD was at T3, with values of 9.23±1.90% in group P and 10.68±2.21% in group R. To achieve an alpha of 0.05 with a power of 0.8, the required sample size was calculated as 28 patients per group. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, 32 patients were included for each group.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean±standard deviation (x̅ ± s), and comparisons of general data between groups were performed using the independent samples t-test. Repeated measures analysis of variance was used for comparisons of the same indicator at different time points within and between groups. Non-normally distributed continuous data were presented as the median and interquartile range (M [P25, P75]), with group comparisons conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as frequencies (%), and comparisons between groups were made using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 64 patients were assessed for eligibility in this study, and 2 patients were excluded according to the exclusion criteria. A total of 62 patients were randomly allocated to the two groups in a 1:1 ratio. Due to the change in surgical methods and intraoperative bleeding of more than 800 ml, 30 patients in each group were finally analyzed (Fig. 2). There was no significant difference in the demographic and perioperative data of the two groups (P>0.05, Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, we have compared the effects of remimazolam and propofol on the ONSD in patients undergoing laparoscopic hysterectomy. The results showed that the ONSD increased significantly in both groups during the establishment of CO2 pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg position, while there was no difference in the extent of the increase in ONSD between the two groups, and no difference was observed in the incidence of adverse outcomes such as headache and PONV between the two groups in the postoperative 24 h.

The study demonstrated that in patients undergoing laparoscopic hysterectomy, a significant increase in ONSD occurred with the establishment of CO2 pneumoperitoneum and the Trendelenburg position, which was consistent with the previous findings [18, 19]. However, the extent of the increase in the ONSD differed. The maximum increase in the mean ONSD was 9.45% in the propofol group and 10.41% in the remimazolam group. Kim et al. [9]observed an ONSD increase of about 12.5% during robot-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy, noting that their study involved elderly male patients maintained on desflurane anesthesia with a pneumoperitoneum pressure of 15 mmHg. In contrast, our study involved young female patients maintained on total intravenous anesthesia with a pneumoperitoneum pressure set at 13 mmHg, which may account for the differences in ONSD increase between the two studies. Furthermore, in a study [20] on robotic gynecological laparoscopic surgery, patients maintained on propofol anesthesia exhibited a maximum increase of ONSD was 7.17%, but their intraoperative EtCO2 was maintained between 30 and 35 mmHg, whereas our study regulated EtCO2 within the range of 35–40 mmHg. It is well known that arterial CO2 partial pressure regulates cerebral blood flow and hypercapnia can results in cerebral vasodilation and increased cerebral blood flow and may increase intracranial pressure [21]. Thus, factors such as age, pneumoperitoneum pressure, arterial CO2 partial pressure, and anesthetics may have contributed to the differences in the extent of change in ONSD during surgery.

During the establishment of CO2 pneumoperitoneum and the Trendelenburg position, the Group R exhibited higher ONSD values compared to the Group P, possibly due to differences in the effects of anesthetics on cerebral circulation. Studies [22, 23]have shown that propofol can reduce cerebral blood flow velocity by 36%−45% and cerebral blood volume by 15%. Soejima et al. [24] used near-infrared spectroscopy to measure cerebral blood volume and transcranial Doppler to measure middle cerebral artery blood flow velocity, observing that remimazolam reduced cerebral blood flow velocity by 11% while cerebral blood volume remained largely unchanged. Given that cerebral blood volume is a key factor influencing ICP, patients maintained on remimazolam anesthesia in this study exhibited higher ONSD values during pneumoperitoneum and the Trendelenburg position.

Tracking the trend and the extent of changes in the ONSD is crucial, rather than just focusing on ONSD values. The ∆ONSD% directly reflects the magnitude of change and controls for individual baseline variability. Although statistically significant differences in ONSD values were observed during the maintenance period, the very small standard deviations meant that even minor mean differences could be detected. The clinical relevance of such a small difference remains uncertain and warrants further investigation in larger studies. Therefore, we consider the ∆ONSD% a more robust metric. According to the Monro-Kellie doctrine, within the cranial cavity, brain tissue, cerebral blood flow, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) are in a state of equilibrium [25]. When the volume of one component increases, initial compensation can occur through increased cerebral venous return or reduced cerebral blood flow, as well as the transfer or reduction of CSF. However, once these regulatory mechanisms are exhausted, ICP will rapidly increase [4]. Blecha et al. [26] found an age-related difference in the changes of ONSD during robot-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy in steep Trendelenburg position, with the ONSD decreased in patients aged < 63 years but further increased in older patients, from an already widened baseline, suggesting that younger patients have better intracranial compliance and adaptability of the ICP. Therefore, the extent of increase in ONSD was not different and the tendency for ONSD change to stabilize during pneumoperitoneum and the Trendelenburg position in this study may be attributed to the inclusion of young female patients without comorbid cranial diseases, who possess good ICP regulation capabilities.

We observed an increase in ONSD as well as MAP following pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg position in both groups. Whiteley et al. [27] propose that the increase in ONSD is associated with increasing MAP. The concomitant rise in MAP may appears to serve as a compensatory mechanism that helps maintain adequate cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) despite the elevated ICP induced by pneumoperitoneum and steep Trendelenburg position. This physiological adaptation may explain the clinically observed low incidence of neurological complications associated with these surgical conditions.

Yilmaz et al. [5]found a significant correlation between the extent of the increase in ONSD and PONV, as well as headaches occurring within three hours postoperatively, in patients undergoing laparoscopic hysterectomy, which proposed an ONSD threshold of 5.85 mm for predicting PONV occurrence. However, we found no differences in postoperative headaches and PONV between the two groups, possibly because the extent of increase in the ONSD was similar in both groups and the mean ONSD was less than the 5.85 mm threshold proposed by Yilmaz. Additionally, differences in anesthetics may play a role, as our study used propofol and remimazolam for anesthesia maintenance, while Yilmaz used sevoflurane inhalation and the use of volatile anesthetics is a risk factor for PONV [28].

This study has several limitations. Firstly, its single-center design and the small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations and diverse healthcare settings. Secondly, the inclusion of patients with comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes, which can affect cerebrovascular compliance, may have influenced the absolute ONSD values. However, the balanced distribution of these conditions between groups minimizes their impact on the comparative analysis of the two anesthetic agents. Finally, the absence of transcranial Doppler sonography to measure middle cerebral artery flow velocity, which could provide complementary cerebral hemodynamic data, is a limitation of this study.

Conclusion

The extent of the increase in ONSD during the laparoscopic hysterectomy has no significantly difference between remimazolam and propofol anesthesia, suggesting that remimazolam and propofol have similar effects on ICP.
