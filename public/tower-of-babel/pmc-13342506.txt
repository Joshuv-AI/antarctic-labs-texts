A hybrid VMD-CNN-autoencoder approach for speed-invariant fault detection of parallel and angular misalignment in rotating machinery
Authors: N Chandran, K Sathish Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly identification of unbalance and shaft misalignment is essential for averting unforeseen failures and minimizing maintenance expenses in rotor-bearing systems. This paper delineates an experimental examination succeeded by an intelligent condition monitoring system for the identification of healthy, parallel misalignment, and angular misalignment states. A custom-built Machinery Fault Simulator was utilized to get vibration responses from multi-axis accelerometers at speeds of 1200, 1500, and 1800 rpm. First, time- and frequency-domain analysis were done to look at the vibration characteristics that were associated to faults. After the vibration signals were collected in the lab, they were broken down using Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) to get noise-robust intrinsic mode functions. A convolutional autoencoder and a one-dimensional convolutional neural network were then used to get statistical and deep features. Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers with early stopping were used to avoid overfitting and classify the faults. An ensemble learning technique was then employed to obtain the final fault classification. The suggested framework achieved an overall classification accuracy of 95.25%. The F1-scores for healthy, parallel misalignment, and angular misalignment conditions were 0.99, 0.88, and 0.87, respectively. The experimental findings indicated that parallel misalignment is defined by predominant and speed-invariant 2X harmonic components exhibiting radial energy concentration, while angular misalignment displays impulsive broadband responses characterized by substantial axial energy dominance and increased kurtosis. The results demonstrate that the proposed hybrid technique, which combines experimental and data-driven methods, provides a reliable and scalable solution for intelligent condition monitoring of variable-speed rotor-bearing systems.Keywords: Rotor-bearing system, Shaft misalignment, Variational mode decomposition, Convolutional neural network, Speed-invariant fault diagnosisSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Rotating machinery such as rotor–bearing systems are pervasive in power generation, aerospace, manufacturing, and process industries. Among the most common and damaging faults in these systems are unbalance and shaft misalignment, which lead to increased vibration, reduced bearing life, noise, energy losses, and catastrophic failures if left undetected. Condition monitoring, the systematic surveillance of machinery health using real-time sensor data, has thus become indispensable for predictive maintenance strategies that aim to address faults before they escalate to functional failures, minimizing downtime and maintenance costs. Rich datasets with labelled healthy and faulty states spanning a wide range of operation speeds, loads, and induced defect types, including unbalance and misalignment, have provided a robust foundation for data-driven fault diagnostics. The ability to systematically introduce and validate specific fault conditions, as well as the trend towards open and comprehensive datasets, has accelerated the benchmarking and validation of emerging algorithms. In this evolving context, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Transformer Encoders have emerged as next-generation solutions capable of capturing both the spatial relationships among sensors and components and long-range temporal dependencies within sequential vibration data. GNNs represent machinery as graphs, enabling relational learning between interacting parts under variable loads and speeds, while Transformer Encoders utilize dynamic attention mechanisms to discern salient fault signatures across large-scale or multi-modal datasets. These models, validated on increasingly complex experimental and synthetic datasets, offer a pathway toward truly predictive diagnostics, identifying unbalance and misalignment not just after they occur, but before they reach fault thresholds. This research leverages extensive experimental datasets, including those from a Machinery Fault Simulator and other validated sources, combining healthy and faulty states to train GNN- and Transformer-based predictors. By focusing on early-stage anomaly detection for unbalance and misalignment, this work aims to push the frontier of condition monitoring from reactive classification to proactive fault prognosis in rotor–bearing systems, setting the stage for the next generation of intelligent and autonomous maintenance systems. Traditional signal processing methods such as Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and wavelet transforms may fail to extract meaningful features from non-stationary or noise-contaminated vibration signals. To overcome these limitations, this study adopts a hybrid framework integrating signal decomposition, machine learning, and deep learning techniques. Specifically, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Autoencoders, Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD), Random Forest (RF), and XGBoost classifiers, together with early stopping strategies, are employed to classify operating conditions into three categories: Healthy, Parallel Misalignment, and Angular Misalignment.

Proposed methodology

The proposed methodology introduces a hybrid speed-adaptive fault diagnostic framework for identifying healthy, parallel misalignment, and angular misalignment states in rotor–bearing systems using vibration signals recorded at rotational speeds of 1200, 1500, and 1800 rpm9. The dual-speed control approach, which is intended to improve fault characterization in both the temporal and frequency domains, is the foundation of the selection of multiple operational speeds (1200, 1500, and 1800 rpm). The excitation forces that regulate the vibrational reactions of rotating machinery are theoretically dependent on harmonic generation and speed. When the system operates at a reduced speed (such as 1200 rpm), the dynamic conditions are more stable, and the centrifugal effects are diminished. This facilitates the observation of time-domain characteristics such as amplitude modulation and waveform consistency. This keeps fault signatures consistent, which is especially important for early-stage problems, when going too fast could hide small changes.

When the speed of rotation is increased, such as 1800 rpm, the centrifugal forces are more intense. This facilitates the identification of the harmonic components (1X, 2X, and higher-order frequencies) in the frequency domain. Enhanced signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and reduced background noise are additional advantages of increased velocities. This simplifies the identification of frequency components that are the result of issues. It is at the middle speed (1500 rpm) that the low-speed stability and high-speed spectral amplification stay in sync. According to vibration theory for rotor systems, fault signatures need to be checked in a variety of working conditions to make sure they are strong and can find faults at any speed. This two-speed method fits with that theory.Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) is first employed to normalize and decompose the raw non-stationary vibration signals into noise-robust intrinsic mode functions (IMFs)5. The IMFs with the highest energy contributions are then selected to preserve fault-sensitive information19. A convolutional autoencoder is subsequently used to compress the selected IMFs into low-dimensional latent representations, effectively removing redundant information while retaining discriminative features associated with fault conditions7,15. These encoded features are further processed using a one-dimensional Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN) to learn hierarchical temporal patterns related to harmonic components and impulsive responses induced by shaft misalignment48. An ensemble classification strategy combining Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers is employed to categorize the extracted deep features into corresponding fault classes22. To enhance robustness and generalization, the predictions from both classifiers are integrated using a majority voting scheme6. An early stopping mechanism is incorporated during model training to prevent overfitting and improve generalization performance. The proposed framework demonstrates reliable and speed-invariant fault identification capability, achieving an overall classification accuracy of 95.25%.

Experimental method

The experimental setup consists of a rotor model incorporating two rigid discs supported by single-row deep-groove ball bearings. One disc is mounted near the left bearing, while the other is positioned near the right bearing. The rotor speed is controlled using a variable frequency drive (VFD), and power is transmitted from a three-phase induction motor to the rotor shaft through a flexible coupler16,27. The motor specifications are summarized in Table 1. The rotor shaft is fabricated from aluminium, with a total length of 1 m and a diameter of 16 mm. Each disc has a diameter of 153 mm, and the axial distance between the two discs is maintained at 200 mm. To simulate various operating and fault conditions, unbalanced mass is mounted on the discs. The detailed specifications of the proposed rotor model, including material properties and geometric parameters, are presented in Table 2. The experimental test rig comprises a motor, coupler, rotor–bearing assembly, accelerometer sensors, and a data acquisition system16. As illustrated in the schematic of the experimental setup, two accelerometers are mounted on the test bearing to measure vibration responses in the horizontal (x) and vertical (y) directions. These sensors capture the rotor system’s responses by measuring their displacements along different axes. A piezoelectric accelerometer sensor with a sensitivity of 105 mV/g is attached to the test bearing. This sensor is designed to convert mechanical vibrations into electrical signals. The signal responses are accurately recorded due to the high sensitivity of these sensors. A data acquisition system (DAQ) is used to collect these responses. It comprises three main components: a piezoelectric sensor, an amplifier, and an analyzer. The sensors serve as the input that receives the electrical signals generated by the accelerometer (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). These signal responses are subsequently processed and analyzed using DAQ. This process includes sampling and recording the signal responses at specific rates, enabling further analysis of these signals43. Variational mode decomposition (VMD)

We employed Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) as the initial step in analyzing the raw vibration data because it wasn’t stable or linear. VMD is a non-recursive, adaptive decomposition approach that divides a signal into a set of intrinsic mode functions (IMFs), known as , each with its unique sparsity characteristics. VMD is employed to decompose each vibration signal into a finite set of band-limited intrinsic mode functions:

where represents the IMF. VMD solves a constrained variational problem that minimizes the total bandwidth of all modes while maintaining signal reconstruction fidelity50. Compared with Empirical Mode Decomposition, VMD provides superior resistance to mode mixing and noise contamination. In this work, the IMFs with the highest energy contribution are selected for subsequent feature extraction19. This is different from Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD), which is prone to mode mixing and noise. The VMD approach defines the decomposition as a restricted variational problem, seeking to minimize the aggregate of the estimated bandwidths for each mode. This technology uses AM-FM, which causes IMFs to detect specific frequency bands of the data that vibrate within them. VMD’s two primary tasks in this scenario are to reduce noise: By separating high-frequency noise into patterns that might otherwise go unnoticed, VMD increases the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)5. Additional practical features Dividing complex vibration spectra into smaller components makes it easier to identify frequency errors that may be hidden in the raw time-domain data. To obtain the features, we select the IMFs with the highest energy. In contrast to conventional methods like EMD, VMD presents significant advantages, including diminished mode mixing, enhanced noise resilience, and superior applicability for non-stationary signal processing. These benefits allow VMD to derive noise-resistant and physically significant IMFs, greatly enhancing feature quality for subsequent machine learning and deep learning models. Consequently, VMD improves the identification of fault-related attributes, including harmonic components and impulsive signatures. This study selects IMFs with the highest energy contributions to retain fault-sensitive information for subsequent processing.

Autoencoder for feature extraction

After VMD, an Autoencoder (AE) was used to get strong low-dimensional features from the chosen IMFs10,11. The Autoencoder is a deep learning neural network that doesn’t need any help to learn how to code the input data in a way that works well. It comprises two essential components namely Encoder and Decoder. The Encoder converts the high-dimensional VMD-decomposed signals into a condensed representation within a latent space, eliminating superfluous information while preserving essential structural details of the vibration signature. The Decoder seeks to recreate the original input from the latent representation to verify that the encoded features retain adequate information. To complete this task, a multi-layer dense autoencoder incorporating a bottleneck layer was developed. The bottleneck layer generates the latent vector, which is a short list of properties indicating the rotating device’s “health signature”. This unsupervised pre-training approach significantly reduces the number of dimensions in the data before the supervised classification step15.

Convolutional neural network (CNN)

A one-dimensional Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN) was trained with the Autoencoder’s encoded features to learn hierarchical features10,20. The CNN should look for patterns and consistent local time correlations in the signal. The Autoencoder, on the other hand, makes the data smaller. You can make CNNs in a lot of different ways. The convolutional layers apply a series of trainable filters (kernels) on the input characteristics to generate feature maps. These maps show local patterns, such as rapid changes or effects that repeat over and over again. Rectified Linear Units were employed to make things non-linear. This allows the network model demonstrate how complicated interactions function. We used max-pooling to make the feature maps smaller in space. This made the characteristics less expensive to compute and made sure they stayed stable even when the input changed little. The next layers combined together the flattened output from the convolutional blocks, making the features into a high-level representation that could be used for classification48. The amalgamation of the Autoencoder and CNN facilitates effective noise reduction and dimensionality reduction via latent feature learning, while permitting the CNN to extract hierarchical and discriminative patterns from the compressed representations, thus enhancing fault classification performance and diminishing reliance on handcrafted features.

Ensemble machine learning: random forest and XGBoost

We used an ensemble learning technique instead of just one classifier to do the final fault diagnosis8,22. This made the classification more accurate and reliable. Two strong tree-based algorithms employed the high-level properties that the CNN found as inputs. A group of decision trees, each of which is trained on a different part of the data and features. The last forecast is made by having all the trees vote on it. Random Forest is very good at lowering variance and lowering the chance of overfitting. A version of gradient boosting that can be used on a large scale that creates trees one at a time, with each new tree fixing the mistakes of the preceding ones. XGBoost is very efficient and has regularization options that help keep it from overfitting even further. The framework uses the variance-reduction features of Random Forest and the bias-reduction features of XGBoost to make a very robust and accurate diagnostic system by integrating the predictions of these models6.

Early stopping

We employed Early Stopping during training to make sure that the CNN and Autoencoder deep learning models could generalize and didn’t fit too well. At the end of each training epoch, you need to assess how well the model fares on a different validation dataset to use this method of regularization. Training finishes automatically if the validation loss doesn’t grow better for a given number of epochs in a row (the patience parameter). This makes sure that the model weights are saved at the time where the model is most able to generalize, not after it starts to remember noise in the training data15,21.

Results and discussion

Time responses and frequency spectrum at test bearing with a rotor speed of 1200 rpm

The Machinery Fault Simulator (MFS) rotor-bearing-shaft mechanism was fully operational at all three rotational speeds to collect vibration baseline data for the experiment. The healthy system had a clean frequency spectrum at 1200 RPM, a 1X running speed harmonic at 20 Hz, and an amplitude of 0.07 mm/s RMS. Since it was below 0.008 mm/s RMS, the 40 Hz 2X harmonic was almost invisible. This demonstrated no misalignment-induced forcing mechanisms. The wideband noise level in the 0–500 Hz band was always approximately 0.05 mm/s RMS. This indicates an excellent signal-to-noise ratio (>10 dB) and a well-balanced, undamaged rotor assembly. When parallel misalignment was applied and tested at 1200 RPM, the frequency spectrum altered dramatically. The offset coupling design increased bearing stress, raising the 1X operating speed harmonic at 20 Hz to 0.30 mm/s RMS (4.3 times the healthy baseline). The abrupt development of a massive 2X harmonic peak at 40 Hz was the most evident symptom of a problem, reaching 3.7 mm/s RMS. This was 463 times stronger than the missing 2X component when the system worked normally. The amplitude ratio was 12.3, or 2X:1X, indicating misalignment. A complete succession of even harmonics followed the 2X peak. Peaks were clearly visible at 4X (1.7 mm/s RMS at 80 Hz), 6X (0.8 mm/s), and 8X harmonics. As harmonic order increased, peak amplitude fell. The frequency spectrum changed dramatically when angular misalignment was applied at 1200 RPM. This matched failure procedures. Polyharmonic complexity beyond 1X and 2X was evident in the spectra, with noticeable 1.5X (30 Hz, 0.17 mm/s), 2.5X (50 Hz), and 3X (60 Hz, 0.12 mm/s) components and sidebands. Compared to parallel misalignment, the broadband noise floor from 0–100 Hz increased to 0.15–0.20 mm/s RMS. Impact frequencies and structure resonance stimulation enhanced the 200–400 Hz noise floor (0.05–0.10 mm/s RMS). Since the shaft wasn’t coaxial, the bearing points were angularly misaligned, causing moment connections. The NDEH and DEH regions show oscillatory activity with peak amplitudes of g. Radial components typically function with amplitudes below g. The RMS is 0.12 g, peak amplitude 0.35 g, crest factor 2.9, kurtosis 3.2, and energy distribution is balanced (35% radial, 32% vertical, and 33% axial). Parallel misalignment raises 1200 RPM baseline vibration. A 2.0 amplitude ratio (AR = 2.0) is observed at the same speed compared to the healthy baseline. The measurement channels show peak amplitudes of g, with horizontal displacement components being significantly higher than vertical and axial components. The time-domain data exhibits quasi-periodic modulation patterns with a half-rotation period. This supports the 2X harmonic idea of misalignment-induced force. The directional energy analysis shows that 65% is radial (NDEH + DEH), 13% is axial (NDEA + DEA), and 22% is vertical (NDEV + DEV). Radial predominance (65% vs. 35% baseline) is the best parallel misalignment indicator. It shows that misaligned shaft centers cause radial forcing. Peak amplitude is 0.90 g, RMS is 0.32 g, Crest Factor is 2.8, Kurtosis is 3.8 (up from 3.1 at baseline), Radial energy is 68%, and Amplitude ratio is 2.25. Angular misalignment induces vibrations with peak amplitudes of g at 1200 RPM, resulting in an AR of 2.3 relative to a healthy baseline. The frequency content distribution is substantially different from parallel misalignment at the same speed, although the amplitude ratio is similar. Most angular misalignment stimulates axially. The directional energy analysis at 1200 RPM shows that axial energy (NDEA + DEA) is 50% (compared to 33% at baseline), radial energy is 28% (compared to 35%), and vertical energy is 22%. An angular misalignment fault is shown by this substantial axial displacement (50% vs. 33%) from baseline. The time-domain signature shows that NDEA and DEA components have more variance and transient-like characteristics, with impulsive traits: Crest Factor is 2.9 (similar to parallel) and Kurtosis is 4.1 (higher than the healthy baseline of 3.2, indicating that the data is not Gaussian). The high Kurtosis and mild Crest Factor suggest that angular misalignment spreads tiny impulses instead of forming a big peak.

Precision (positive predictive value)

Precision measures how reliable the model’s favourable predictions are. It is the number of accurately anticipated positive observations divided by the total number of predicted positives. The model got a Precision of 0.96 for the Healthy class, which means that 96% of the samples that were predicted to be “Healthy” were indeed healthy. Both types of misalignments had a Precision of 0.89, which means that the number of false positives was low. It is very important for the model to not mix a healthy state with a problem condition, which is what keeps maintenance from being needed.

Recall (sensitivity)

Recall informs you how well the model can find all the important cases (actual positive samples) in a dataset. With a Recall of 0.99, the Healthy class shows that the system almost always knows what typical working conditions are. The 0.89 (Parallel) and 0.86 (Angular) recalls show that most of the problems were found. The somewhat lower recall for Angular misalignment means that there is a small overlap in feature space with Parallel misalignment. This is a common problem in vibration analysis because the 1X and 2X rpm harmonic responses are similar.

F1-score

The F1-Score is a single number that balances both Precision and Recall. It is the harmonic mean of the two. This is very helpful for figuring out how well someone did on datasets that aren’t balanced. The F1-Score of 0.99 for the Healthy class shows that the model is very good at separating baseline data. The fact that the fault classes got scores of 0.88 and 0.87 shows that the hybrid framework strikes a good compromise between finding defects (Recall) and reducing false alarms (Precision).

Overall accuracy

The proposed framework achieved an Overall Accuracy of 95.25%. The hybrid technique works because it is quite accurate. The system could learn discriminative features even though vibration signals are quite sophisticated. It accomplished this by employing VMD to decompose the signals, utilizing a CNN-Autoencoder to extract deep features, and implementing ensemble classification to integrate them. This method based on deep learning is better at discovering problems with misalignment than prior methods that solely use raw signal FFTs.

Conclusion

This study extensively tested and analyzed unbalance-induced parallel and angular misalignment in a rotor bearing shaft system at varied speeds. All nine test cases showed obvious and consistent diagnostic patterns using a custom Machinery Fault Simulator and multi-axis vibration measurements at 1200, 1500, and 1800 rpm. Discovering the speed-invariant diagnostic signal 2X:1X harmonic amplitude ratio is this study’s key contribution. Angular misalignments created a narrow band of 0.56–0.66, while parallel misalignments caused very high ratios of 9.0 to 13.0. Healthy operations always had ratios below 0.15. This big disparity allowed a simple and effective threshold-based categorization approach. A 2X:1X ratio > 6 indicates parallel lines, 0.5–1.5 indicates slanted lines, and < 0.2 indicates a healthy operation. This rule-based discrimination eliminates speed-dependent recalibration and works well for real-time monitoring. Parallel misalignment induced superlinear vibration development with speed, with peak amplitudes increasing 57% from 1200 to 1800 rpm, since centrifugal force increases. However, angular misalignment only increased amplitude by 31%. Failure severity was measured by increasing impulsiveness, not amplitude. Kurtosis values remained near to Gaussian (3.0–3.2) under healthy conditions, increased slightly for parallel misalignment (3.5 4.2), and significantly increased for angular misalignment (4.1 5.3). Kurtosis (K > 4.0) is a speed-independent angular misalignment indicator, according to these studies.

Using directional energy analysis made defect identification easier. Radial, vertical, and axial energy distribution was balanced in healthy functioning. Radial energy was more relevant (65–70%) in parallel misalignments and axial energy (50–60%) in angular ones. In healthy and parallel conditions, 200–400 Hz broadband energy was minimal. However, angular misalignment increased it significantly, indicating moment-driven impulses created the stresses. A qualitative difference was that healthy and parallel states produced clean, sparse spectra with discrete harmonics, while angular misalignment produced intricate polyharmonic patterns with sidebands at half-integer multiples of running speed. The hybrid VMD–CNN–Autoencoder–ensemble system achieved 95.25% classification accuracy, demonstrating that high-fidelity defect diagnosis may be done with low processing power and feature dimensionality. The results reveal that directional energy ratios, kurtosis evolution, and 2X:1X amplitude ratios are a modest but valuable set of features that can detect problems in real time without signal preprocessing. Overall, this study provides a solid experimental foundation for predictive maintenance in variable-speed rotor-bearing systems and shows that powerful, low-cost, multi-channel vibration analysis can distinguish parallel and angular misalignment. The technology is suitable for industrial condition monitoring systems and adaptive, autonomous machinery health management platforms.

To test model generalization in more realistic industrial situations, future research will apply the suggested methodology to complex fault scenarios with simultaneous imbalance, bearing faults, and misalignment. The approach will also benefit from graph neural networks and transformer-based temporal models. These models will identify sensor spatial correlations and long-term temporal dependencies in changing operating situations. Multimodal diagnostics in noisy environments would benefit from motor current and acoustic emission signals added to vibration data. Finally, we will examine real-time deployment on embedded edge-computing platforms to simplify adaptive predictive maintenance systems. In the future, we will test the proposed framework on real-world industrial prototypes, like centrifugal pumps and rotating fans, to see how well the extracted fault features hold up in situations with a lot of background noise, changing load conditions, and real-world operational environments. This will help connect theoretical modeling with real-world engineering applications.
