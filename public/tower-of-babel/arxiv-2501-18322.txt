A Unified Perspective on the Dynamics of Deep Transformers
Valérie Castin, Pierre Ablin, José A. Carrillo, Gabriel Peyré

Abstract
Transformers, which are state-of-the-art in most machine learning tasks, represent the data as sequences of vectors called tokens. This representation is then exploited by the attention function, which learns dependencies between tokens and is key to the success of Transformers. However, the iterative application of attention across layers induces complex dynamics that remain to be fully understood. To analyze these dynamics, we identify each input sequence with a probability measure and model its evolution as a Vlasov equation called the Transformer PDE, whose velocity field is nonlinear in the probability measure. Our first set of contributions focuses on compactly supported initial data. We show that the Transformer PDE is well-posed and is the mean-field limit of an interacting particle system, thus generalizing and extending previous analyses to several variants of self-attention: multi-head attention, ℓ 2 \ell^{2} attention, Sinkhorn attention, Sigmoid attention, and masked attention—leveraging a conditional Wasserstein framework. In a second set of contributions, we are the first to study non-compactly supported initial conditions, by focusing on Gaussian initial data. Again for different types of attention, we show that the Transformer PDE preserves the space of Gaussian measures, which allows us to analyze the Gaussian case theoretically and numerically to identify typical behaviors. This Gaussian analysis captures the evolution of data anisotropy through a deep Transformer. In particular, we highlight a clustering phenomenon that parallels previous results in the non-normalized discrete case.

Introduction

Transformers, introduced in
[
64
]
, are extremely successful deep learning models, which have reached the state of the art in a wide variety of tasks, from natural language processing
to computer vision.
A key feature of Transformers is that each data point (image, sentence…) is mapped to a
sequence of vectors

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
, called
tokens
, before being processed by the model.
Each sequence of tokens is then processed by a succession of layers containing a self-attention block and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), interleaved with a layer normalization (LayerNorm) operation.
The core component of the Transformer architecture is really self-attention: contrary to the MLP and LayerNorm, which are applied token-wise, self-attention makes all tokens interact, which allows the model to learn complex dependencies between them.
In this work, we aim to model the evolution of tokens as they go through a deep Transformer, and to identify typical behaviors to improve our understanding of how Transformers process data.
We consider a simplified model with only attention blocks, i.e., without MLPs and layer normalizations; note that adding LayerNorm in the analysis has been studied in
[
37
,
12
]
and leads to very different dynamics, as tokens are constrained to evolve on a sphere.

Variants of self-attention

The most widespread version of self-attention is the one introduced in
[
64
]
, which we call Softmax self-attention, but several variants have been proposed over the years to improve specific behaviors of self-attention, such as its regularity
[
44
,
59
]
or its computational complexity
[
43
,
71
,
58
]
.
We adapt our analysis to each of the following variants, which we present in more detail in Section
2
:
\ell^{2}
self-attention
[
44
]
, self-attention without Softmax
[
62
]
, ReLU
[
71
]
, Sigmoid
[
58
]
self-attention and Sinkhorn self-attention
[
59
]
.
Sinkhorn attention can be interpreted as a way to restore a Wasserstein gradient flow structure through symmetrization—in a related spirit,
[
1
]
exploits this observation in the opposite direction, viewing Transformers with a finite number of layers as a novel discretization of Wasserstein gradient flows and studying the convergence properties of the resulting scheme.
Finally, several self-attention blocks are typically linearly combined to obtain multi-head attention, and possibly masked in Transformer decoders
[
64
]
—we include these two cases in our analysis, building on the framework introduced in
[
24
]
for masked self-attention.

Transformers as interacting particle systems

Transformers are deep models: several residual self-attention layers are stacked (typically
10
to
100

[
50
]
) and interleaved with non-linear operations, which results in an involved architecture whose properties are only partially understood.
To uncover the mechanisms by which Transformers process data, recent papers
[
59
,
36
,
37
]
have proposed to model the behavior of tokens going through the Transformer architecture as an interacting particle system, ruled by a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs)—thus adapting the theory of neural ODEs
[
25
,
31
,
39
]
to Transformers.
This allows for a mathematical study of the dynamics of tokens
(x_{1}(t),\dots,x_{n}(t))
as they are processed by a Transformer, where the time variable
t
corresponds to layer position in the model.
Let us focus on the simplest of these models, introduced in
[
59
]
, where only residual single-head self-attention layers are stacked, and assume that the parameters of the attention layers are continuous functions of time.
Denoting
(x_{1}(0),\dots,x_{n}(0))\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
the input tokens, the dynamics of tokens as they go through the model read

\phantom{1\leq i\leq n}~~~~~~~~~~~~\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\Gamma_{t,X(t)}(x_{i}(t))~~~~~~~~~~~~1\leq i\leq n

(1)

where
X(t)\coloneqq(x_{1}(t),\dots,x_{n}(t))
and
\Gamma_{t,X}
is the attention vector field, defined for
X\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
and three time-dependent matrices
Q(t),K(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
V(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
k\leq d
as

\Gamma_{t,X}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}(t,x)V(t)x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

where

p_{j}(t,x)\coloneqq\exp(Q(t)x\cdot K(t)x_{j})/\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\exp(Q(t)x\cdot K(t)x_{\ell})

for Softmax (i.e., vanilla) self-attention (see Section
2
for the other types of attention),
\cdot
denoting the Euclidean scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The authors in
[
36
]
provide a thorough study of the dynamics (
1
) in the case of time-independent matrices
Q,K,V
, showing the emergence of clusters after properly rescaling tokens. Clustering is also investigated in the context of low-rank adaptation in
[
45
]
.

The Transformer PDE

The dynamics (
1
) can be generalized as the following PDE on probability measures
[
59
,
73
,
36
,
12
]
:

\partial_{t}\mu+\mathrm{div}(\mu\Gamma_{\mu})=0,

(2)

where tokens are now represented by a probability measure
\mu\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\Gamma_{\mu}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\int Vy\,\kappa_{\mu}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

with
Q(t),K(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
,
V(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\kappa_{\mu}(x,y)\coloneqq\exp(Qx\cdot Ky)/\int\exp(Qx\cdot Ky)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)
for Softmax self-attention (see Section
2
for the other types of attention).
We do not write explicitly the dependence of
\kappa_{\mu}
on
t
to lighten notation.
The case (
1
) of discrete initial data
(x_{1}(0),\dots,x_{n}(0))
corresponds to plugging the empirical measure
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}(0)}
in Equation (
2
).
This viewpoint allows us to take possibly continuous probability measures as initial data, thus generalizing the Transformer model to inputs with infinitely many tokens.
The well-posedness of the evolution (
2
), which is a Vlasov-type equation
[
67
,
30
]
or an aggregation-type equation
[
38
,
7
]
, is a non-trivial problem, as the velocity field
\Gamma_{\mu}
is typically non-linear in
\mu
and associated with a kernel
\kappa_{\mu}(x,y)
that is unbounded, since we do not normalize dynamics as opposed to
[
12
]
, and cannot be rewritten as a function of
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert
.
This rules out more traditional approaches, presented for instance in
[
30
,
13
,
23
,
54
]
.
In the case of Softmax self-attention, when the initial data is constrained in a compact set and
Q,K,V
are constant over time (i.e., across layers), Equation (
2
) is the mean-field limit of the dynamics (
1
)
[
36
]
, in the sense that Equation (
2
) is well-posed for compactly supported initial data, and satisfies a stability estimate of the form

W_{2}(\mu(t),\nu(t))\leq C(t,R_{0})W_{2}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})

where
R_{0}
is the radius of any compact set containing the support of
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
, and
W_{2}
is the Wasserstein distance between measures
[
66
,
61
]
.
In the first part of this work (Section
3
), we extend this result to time-dependent parameters and to the variants of self-attention mentioned above, including masked self-attention.
We are also the first to study Equation (
2
) for non-compactly supported initial data, by focusing on the case of Gaussian probability measures (Section
4
).
The Gaussian case is special, as we show that Equation (
2
) preserves Gaussians for several self-attention variants mentioned above.
This property is observed in several algorithms in sampling and optimization
[
35
,
18
,
16
]
.
This allows us to summarize the evolution on Gaussians as two matrix ordinary differential equations (ODEs) connecting the expectation and the covariance matrix of the Gaussian data.
This explicit characterization of the dynamics then allows us to identify a variety of possible behaviors, and highlight a
clustering
phenomenon, in addition to studying well-posedness.

To exemplify this and gain intuition on the possible behaviors of solutions to (
2
), consider the case where the initial condition

\mu_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{0})
is a centered Gaussian with covariance
\Sigma_{0}\succ 0
, and assume for simplicity that

Q=K=\mathrm{Id}
and
V=\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Id}
. As shown in Section
4
, the Gaussian structure is preserved along the flow and
\mu(t)=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma(t))
, where the covariance satisfies the Riccati ODE
\dot{\Sigma}=2\varepsilon\,\Sigma^{2}
in the case of Softmax self-attention.
Its closed form solution is
\Sigma(t)=(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}-2\varepsilon t\,\mathrm{Id})^{-1}
.
If
\varepsilon<0
, the solution is global in time and
\Sigma(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
In contrast, if
\varepsilon>0
, the maximal eigenvalue of the covariance blows up in finite time
t_{\max}=(2\varepsilon\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{0}))^{-1}
.
These two behaviors are illustrated in Figure
1
.
For general
Q,K,V
, possibly depending on
t
, the covariance dynamics become more complex, and a more detailed analysis is carried out in Section
4
.

Figure 1:
Covariance evolution of a centered Gaussian measure processed by the Softmax Transformer PDE, with
Q=K=\mathrm{Id}
and
V=\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Id}
, for
\varepsilon=-0.4
(upper plot) and
\varepsilon=0.1
(lower plot) with the same initial measure. The covariance matrix follows the ODE
\dot{\Sigma}=2\varepsilon\Sigma^{2}
. Choosing
\varepsilon<0
leads to
\Sigma(t)\to_{t\to+\infty}0
, i.e., all the mass clusters at a single point, while
\varepsilon>0
leads to a finite-time divergence of the maximal eigenvalue of
\Sigma(t)
.

Gradient flow structure of the PDE

To equip a partial differential equation with a gradient flow structure can be a useful tool to prove convergence and properties of the limit.
Several works have tackled this question for the Transformer PDE.

[
59
]
show that Equation (
2
) is not a Wasserstein gradient flow, whereas the Sinkformer PDE is, allowing them to highlight a diffusive behavior when the parameter
\varepsilon
associated to Sinkhorn tends to zero.

[
37
]
study an alternative to the Transformer PDE where tokens are of unit norm, thus modelling the effect of LayerNorm, and leverage the gradient flow structure of this projected dynamics to prove the emergence of one single cluster when
t\to\infty
.
They also introduce a modified metric on
(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{n}
, which equips the original dynamics (
1
) with a non-Euclidean gradient flow structure, and is a particular case of the more general framework introduced in
[
49
]
.
In Section
5
, we propose a generalization of this metric from tokens to probability measures, formally showing that it equips the Transformer PDE (
2
) associated with Softmax self-attention with a gradient flow structure.
Adapting metrics to convert similar nonlocal PDEs to gradient flows has been a very fruitful approach in different settings
[
17
,
20
,
11
]
. In fact, this has very recently been developed for the normalized dynamics similar to (
2
) associated to (
1
) on the sphere in
[
12
]
.
In the case of Gaussian initial data (Section
4
), the gradient flow structure of the Sinkformer PDE
[
59
]
leads to a Bures-Wasserstein gradient flow.
The Bures-Wasserstein distance, first introduced in the field of quantum information geometry
[
10
]
, appears in several recent works in machine learning
[
47
,
27
,
48
]
.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows.

i)

If
\Gamma_{\mu}
is the velocity field of one of the following variants of self-attention, in its single-head
or
multi-head form, and in its masked
or
unmasked form: Softmax self-attention,
\ell^{2}
self-attention, Sinkhorn self-attention, Sigmoid self-attention, then we show that for any compactly supported initial data
\mu\in{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the evolution (
2
) is well-posed.
The masked self-attention case leverages the conditional optimal transport framework introduced in
[
24
]
, and requires that the position marginal of the initial distribution has a Dirac mass at 0.
We also derive a stability estimate with respect to the initial data, which proves that Equation (
2
) is the mean-field limit of the dynamical system (
1
). (Section
3
)

ii)

For Softmax,
\ell^{2}
, Sinkhorn and linear self-attention, we show that initial data that are Gaussians stay Gaussian along the dynamics (
2
). This allows us to derive explicit ODEs on the mean and the covariance matrix of these Gaussian solutions. For Softmax self-attention, we demonstrate under certain assumptions on the parameters that the limiting covariance of the solution is rank-deficient, which mimics the clustering phenomenon observed in
[
36
]
. (Sections
4.1
,
4.3
and
4.4
).

iii)

With a numerical study of the covariance ODEs in the Gaussian case, we point out that rank-deficiency of the limiting covariance generically holds even without the assumptions of our theoretical analysis, for Softmax self-attention as well as
\ell^{2}
self-attention. Moreover, we identify a range of typical behaviors in dimension 2, divided in three categories: (i) convergence, (ii) divergence at
t\to+\infty
and (iii) divergence in finite time. We prove that
\ell^{2}
self-attention cannot lead to finite-time divergence, contrary to Softmax self-attention. (Section
4.5
)

iv)

As a side contribution, we observe that Gaussian-kernel drifting models
[
28
,
14
]
are driven by the difference of two normalized Gaussian-kernel fields, each of which is the field that appears in
\ell^{2}
self-attention for a suitable choice of parameters. The Gaussian analysis therefore yields closed ODEs for drifting between Gaussian distributions and clarifies the mechanism in this simple regime. (Appendix
B
)

v)

Finally, we generalize results in
[
37
]
by introducing a twisted metric on the space of probability measures, which equips the Softmax Transformer PDE (
2
) with a gradient flow structure. We prove the non-geodesic convexity of the associated functional for the non-normalized dynamics. This complements very recent results in
[
12
]
for the normalized dynamics. We also reformulate the covariance ODE for Sinkhorn attention in the Gaussian case as a Bures-Wasserstein gradient flow. (Section
5
)

github.com/vcastin/2025-transformers-PDEs
.

Some Variants of Self-Attention

Several variants of self-attention and masked self-attention
[
64
]
have been introduced over the years to improve some properties of the attention map, such as its regularity
[
44
,
59
]
or its computational complexity
[
43
,
71
,
58
]
.
Our approach provides a unified framework to compare the behavior of a Transformer model for each variant: we model any Transformer model (Encoder-only or Decoder-only), which is the composition of several residual attention layers interleaved with projections and multi-layer perceptrons—that we do not include here, by a PDE of the form

\partial_{t}\mu+\mathrm{div}(\mu\Gamma_{\mu})=0

(3)

where the velocity field
\Gamma_{\mu}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\delta}\to\mathbb{R}^{\delta}
depends on the data
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{\delta})
and on the chosen variant of self-attention.
For unmasked self-attention,
\delta
is equal to the dimension
d
of each token.
In the case of masked self-attention, we have
\delta=d+1
following
[
24
]
, as explained in Subsection
2.6
.
This approach generalizes the case of a finite number of tokens, which corresponds to
\mu
being an empirical measure
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
.
Indeed, casting this formula for
\mu
in Equation (
3
) leads back to the discrete ODE system (
1
).

Here, we list all the attention variants covered by our framework.
Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with
k\leq d
and matrices
Q,K\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
We denote
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
.

2.1
Softmax (Single-Head) Self-Attention

We call Softmax self-attention the original definition of self-attention in
[
64
]
.
For any integer
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and any vectors
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, Softmax self-attention with parameters
(Q,K,V)
maps the sequence
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
to

f^{(\mathrm{SM})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\coloneqq\Big(V{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}}P_{ij}x_{j}\Big)_{1\leq i\leq n}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n},

(4)

\text{with}\quad P_{i}\coloneqq\softmax\left((Qx_{i}\cdot Kx_{j})_{1\leq j\leq n}\right),

where

\softmax(w)\coloneqq(\exp(w_{i})/{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(w_{j})})_{1\leq i\leq n}.

Note that, as we do not consider training, we absorbed the usual normalizing factor
1/\sqrt{k}
in
Q
and
K
.
Now, Equation (
4
) can be rewritten as

f(X)=(\Gamma_{X}^{(\mathrm{SM})}(x_{1}),\dots,\Gamma_{X}^{(\mathrm{SM})}(x_{n})),

(5)

with

\Gamma_{X}^{(\mathrm{SM})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{Qx\cdot Kx_{i}}Vx_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{Qx\cdot Kx_{i}}}.

Equation (
5
) naturally leads to the PDE viewpoint (
3
) by defining, for any probability measure
\mu\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mu}[\lvert y\rvert e^{Qx\cdot Ky}]<+\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the velocity field

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{SM})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\frac{\int Vy\,e^{Qx\cdot Ky}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}{\int e^{Qx\cdot Ky}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}.

This generalization of Softmax self-attention to probability measures appears first in
[
69
]
, and later, in this specific form, in
[
59
]
.
Note that Softmax self-attention does not depend separately on
Q
and
K
, but only on the product
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Remarkably,
\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{SM})}
is well-defined when
\mu
is Gaussian, and even has a closed form (see Section
4.1
).

2.2
Linear Self-Attention

Several variants of self-attention, called
linear
variants, reduce the computational complexity of attention from quadratic to linear in the number of tokens
n

[
43
,
26
,
57
,
63
,
62
]
.
The simplest version of linear attention replaces the Softmax by the identity in Equation (
4
), and is widely used in the field of in-context learning
[
32
,
68
,
51
,
60
]
.
It is defined as

f^{(\mathrm{lin})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\coloneqq(\Gamma_{X}^{(\mathrm{lin})}(x_{1}),\dots,\Gamma_{X}^{(\mathrm{lin})}(x_{n})),

with

\Gamma_{X}^{(\mathrm{lin})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto V\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}x_{j}^{\top}Ax,

which corresponds to the velocity field

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{lin})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\int Vy(Qx\cdot Ky)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

Let us point out that a slight modification of this attention without Softmax
[
62
]
can be obtained as a second-order expansion of Softmax self-attention in the regime where
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
is very small.
Denoting
f_{\varepsilon}^{(\mathrm{SM})}
the Softmax attention map with parameters
(\varepsilon Q,K,V/\varepsilon)
, we have for any
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
that

f^{(\mathrm{SM})}_{\varepsilon}(X)_{i}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Vx_{j}+V\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}x_{j}^{\top}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}x_{\ell}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}x_{\ell}^{\top}\right)Ax_{i}+o_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\varepsilon),

with
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
.
We denote

f^{(\mathrm{lin,\varepsilon})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\coloneqq(\Gamma_{X}^{(\mathrm{lin,\varepsilon})}(x_{1}),\dots,\Gamma_{X}^{(\mathrm{lin,\varepsilon})}(x_{n})),

with

\Gamma_{X}^{(\mathrm{lin,\varepsilon})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\frac{1}{n\varepsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Vx_{j}+V\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}x_{j}^{\top}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}x_{\ell}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}x_{\ell}^{\top}\right)Ax

the variant of linear attention obtained by expanding the Softmax attention map.
The second sum corresponds exactly to attention without Softmax.
Note that this attention variant has the following simple expression for any
compactly supported or Gaussian
probability measure
\mu
of expectation
\alpha
and covariance
\Sigma
:

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{lin,\varepsilon})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\frac{1}{\varepsilon}V\alpha+V\Sigma Ax.

This expression is the same as for Softmax self-attention up to a rescaling of
\alpha
(see Subsection
4.1
).
Therefore, all the results we state for Softmax self-attention in the Gaussian case also hold for this particular choice of linear self-attention.

2.3
\ell^{2}
Self-Attention

For any integer
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and vectors
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\ell^{2}
self-attention with parameters
(Q,K,V)
maps the sequence
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
to

f^{(\mathrm{\ell^{2}})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\coloneqq\Big(V{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}}P_{ij}x_{j}\Big)_{1\leq i\leq n}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n},

with

\quad P_{ij}\coloneqq e^{-\left\lvert Qx_{i}-Kx_{j}\right\rvert^{2}}/\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}e^{-\left\lvert Qx_{i}-Kx_{\ell}\right\rvert^{2}}.

This
\ell^{2}
self-attention was introduced in
[
44
]
for its Lipschitz continuity.
When
Q=K=V=I_{d}
, it is akin to a mean-shift algorithm step
[
33
]
.
Following the same approach as for traditional self-attention, we define the velocity field associated with
\ell^{2}
self-attention, for any compactly supported probability measure
\mu\in{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, as

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{\ell^{2}})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\frac{\int Vy\,e^{-\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}{\int e^{-\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}.

Note that
\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{\ell^{2}})}
depends on
Q,K
and not only on their product
A=K^{\top}Q
.
Like Softmax self-attention, it can be computed in closed form when
\mu
is Gaussian (Section
4.3
).

2.4
Sinkhorn Self-Attention

Sinkhorn self-attention has been introduced in
[
59
]
.
We only give its definition on probability measures for conciseness.
For any compactly supported or Gaussian probability measure
\mu
, the velocity field associated to Sinkhorn self-attention with the parameter
\varepsilon>0
and the cost
c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}
is defined as

\Gamma_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(\mathrm{sink})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int Vy\,\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

where
\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}
is obtained by performing the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm on
\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{0}\coloneqq e^{-c_{\varepsilon}}
, i.e.
\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x,y)
is the limit of the following sequence:

\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\ell+1}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\ell}(x,y)}{\int\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\ell}(x,y^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y^{\prime})}\mbox{ if $\ell$ is even,}\\
\frac{\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\ell}(x,y)}{\int\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\ell}(x^{\prime},y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x^{\prime})}\mbox{ if $\ell$ is odd.}\end{cases}

(6)

This algorithm—which, in its discrete version, normalizes the kernel matrix
\kappa
iteratively row-wise and column-wise—outputs a bistochastic kernel:
\int\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}(z,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)=\int\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x,z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)=1
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The Sinkhorn algorithm has deep connections with entropic optimal transport (EOT).
Indeed, we have the following reinterpretation of Sinkhorn self-attention.
Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, either supported in a compact set or Gaussian.
Keeping the notation
c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}
, consider the entropic optimal transport problem

OT_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)+\KL(\pi\|\mu\otimes\nu),

(7)

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
and
\KL(\alpha\|\beta)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\left(\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\,\mathrm{d}\beta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, which is infinite if
\alpha
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\beta
.
Assuming that
\mu=\nu
and denoting
\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{*}(x,y)\coloneqq\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}(\mu\otimes\mu)
where
\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}
is obtained as above with Sinkhorn, it is well-known
[
55
,
41
]
that the coupling
\pi^{*}
is the unique solution of the entropic optimal transport problem (
7
).
Therefore,
\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}
is the density of the optimal coupling
\pi^{*}
with respect to the measure
\mu\otimes\mu
.
Note that, like for Softmax self-attention,
\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}
only depends on the product
A=K^{\top}Q
(see
[
59
]
).
We also study the Gaussian case for Sinkhorn self-attention in Section
4.4
.

2.5
Unnormalized Self-Attention

A natural family of self-attention variants consists of suppressing the normalization factor in the definition of
\Gamma
, and possibly changing the exponential that appears in the Softmax for a smoother function such as identity
[
62
]
(which falls back on linear attention, Section
2.2
), ReLU
[
71
]
or Sigmoid
[
58
]
.
In Section
3
, we mention the following variants:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{exp})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\int Vy\ e^{Qx\cdot Ky}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{ReLU})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\int Vy\ \mathrm{ReLU}(Qx\cdot Ky)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{\sigma})}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\int Vy\ \mathrm{\sigma}(Qx\cdot Ky)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y),

where
\mathrm{ReLU}\colon x\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\max(x,0)
and
\sigma\colon z\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
is the Sigmoid function.
It is easy to see that none of these attention variants preserve the Gaussianity of the data, as
\Gamma_{\mu}
is not an affine function when
\mu
is Gaussian.

2.6
Masked Self-Attention

Softmax self-attention, introduced at the beginning of Section
2.1
, is used in Encoders, which appear in Encoder-only
[
29
]
and Encoder-Decoder Transformers
[
64
]
.
In Decoders, however, what is used is masked self-attention, which takes into account the sequential nature of the inputs.
In its discrete version, masked self-attention maps the sequence
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
to
(f^{(\mathrm{m})}(X)_{1},\dots,f^{(\mathrm{m})}(X)_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
such that

f^{(\mathrm{m})}(X)_{i}\coloneqq f^{(\mathrm{SM})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i}),

(8)

where
f^{(\mathrm{SM})}
is Softmax self-attention, defined in Equation (
4
).
Similarly, one can define a masked attention map for any discrete self-attention variant.
Masked self-attention is crucial for next-token prediction tasks in NLP and time series: at test time, it is used to generate data in an autoregressive fashion, i.e., each newly generated token contributes to the computation of the next one; and during training, it prevents the model from cheating in its next-token prediction task by making use of tokens it has to predict.

Equation (
8
) cannot be directly generalized to probability measures, as masked attention makes use of the order of tokens, which is lost when representing a sequence
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
by the associated empirical measure
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
.
To include masked self-attention in our unified framework, we leverage a contribution in
[
24
]
, which extends masked self-attention to probability measures by adding a
position coordinate
in
[0,1]
to the input space: this coordinate specifies the order of tokens.
Then, an input to masked self-attention becomes a probability measure
\bar{\mu}
on the product space
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
Q,K\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
The velocity field associated with masked Softmax self-attention is defined, for any compactly supported probability measure
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\bar{\mu}(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})>0
, as

\Gamma_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\coloneqq\left(0,\frac{\int_{[0,\sigma]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}Vy\,e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)}{\int_{[0,\sigma]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)}\right)

(9)

with
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
.
In what follows, we call
position marginal
the first marginal of
\bar{\mu}
, defined as

\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\sigma)\coloneqq\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\sigma,x).

(10)

\theta
is therefore a probability measure on
[0,1]
.
The assumption
\bar{\mu}(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})>0
is equivalent to saying that
\theta
has a Dirac mass at zero.
Adding a Dirac mass at zero together with a null token vector is equivalent to the off-by-one attention correction and the attention sink mechanism
[
53
,
72
,
2
]
.
Note that these techniques absorb a portion of the attention probability mass, preventing pathological concentration of attention on spurious tokens and improving robustness to outliers and long-context degradation.

We call
space marginal
and denote
\mu
the marginal of
\bar{\mu}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined as

\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\coloneqq\int_{\sigma\in[0,1]}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\sigma,x).

\mu
is therefore a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark 1
.

Note that the assumption
\bar{\mu}(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})>0
ensures that
\Gamma_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)
is well-defined for
\sigma=0
. It can be relaxed to the weaker assumption that
\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\sigma,x)
disintegrates as
\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}^{\sigma}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\sigma)
for
(\bar{\mu}^{\sigma})_{\sigma\in[0,1]}
a weak-
\star
continuous family of probability measures—which is for example the case when
\bar{\mu}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
34
]
.
However, our proof of well-posedness for masked self-attention (Theorem
6
) only holds under the assumption
\bar{\mu}(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})>0
, which is why we keep this assumption in the definition, for simplicity.

For a finite number of tokens, Equation (
8
) can be cast in the framework (
9
) by choosing
\bar{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{((i-1)/n,x_{i})}
, for instance.
Equation (
9
) then provides a natural generalization of masked Softmax self-attention to continuous distributions of tokens.
Moreover, this idea, originally applied to Softmax self-attention, can be used for any of the attention variants mentioned above.

Note that with this choice of velocity field, the position marginal of
\bar{\mu}(t)
stays constant along the dynamics (
3
).
However, Equation (
9
) does not allow for a study of the Gaussian case—e.g., when the space marginal is Gaussian—as the masked Transformer PDE does not preserve this structure.

2.7
Multi-Head Self-Attention

The attention functions presented above provide different ways of learning dependencies—for example, semantic dependencies—between tokens.
In practice, to increase the expressive power of attention and learn dependencies at different scales in the text or image, several
attention heads
with different parameters are linearly combined to obtain what is called multi-head attention.
If
\Gamma
is the velocity field of a (possibly masked) attention function—Softmax, linear,
\ell^{2}
, Sigmoid…, the associated multi-head velocity field takes the form

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{MH})}\coloneqq\sum_{h=1}^{H}\Gamma_{\mu}^{(h)}

for
H
the number of heads, which must divide
d
, and where the parameters of
\Gamma_{\mu}^{(h)}
are
Q^{(h)},K^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d/H\times d}
and
V^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Note that multi-head attention usually involves matrices
W^{(h)}
that multiply each term
\Gamma_{\mu}^{(h)}
for
1\leq h\leq H
.
As we do not consider training, we absorb
W^{(h)}
in
V^{(h)}
, so that our matrices
V^{(h)}
are
d\times d
.
Moreover, it is practical to represent the list of matrices
(Q^{(h)},K^{(h)})_{1\leq h\leq H}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d/H\times d})^{H}
by two square matrices
Q,K\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, where
Q
(resp.
K
) is obtained by stacking the
Q^{(h)}
(resp.
K^{(h)}
) row-wise:

Q\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}Q^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
Q^{(H)}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad K\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}K^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
K^{(H)}\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

As multi-head attention is a
linear
combination of single attention heads, we show that well-posedness and Gaussian preservation, when they hold for a single-headed variant of self-attention, also hold for its multi-headed version.

3
Well-Posedness for a Compactly Supported Initial Condition

We first investigate the behavior of the Transformer PDE

\partial_{t}\mu+\mathrm{div}(\mu\Gamma_{\mu})=0

(12)

when the initial condition is compactly supported, for all variants of self-attention presented in Section
2
.
For any
p\geq 1
, we will be looking for solutions of Equation (
12
), as curves
\mu\in{\cal C}([0,T],{\cal P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, continuous with the topology induced by the Wasserstein distance
W_{p}
on
{\cal P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
—which makes it a complete space.
We say that
\mu\in{\cal C}([0,T],{\cal P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is a (weak) solution of Equation (
12
) with initial data
\mu_{0}
if for any
{\cal C}^{\infty}
function
\psi\in{\cal C}_{0}^{\infty}([0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
that tends to zero at infinity we have

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\Gamma_{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(T,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(T)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(0,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}.

We focus on proving the well-posedness of the PDE (
12
), i.e., existence and uniqueness of a global solution, and deriving a stability estimate.
The case of Softmax self-attention has already been studied in the particular case of constant parameters, while the other cases are completely new.
Our proof, which relies on a fixed-point argument, is different and technically simpler than the one in
[
36
]
since it relies on classical Dobrushin type estimates
[
30
,
13
]
.

3.1
Unmasked Self-Attention

Let us first study Equation (
12
) for our attention variants in their unmasked form.
We have the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
d,k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with
k\leq d
and
p\geq 1
.
Let
Q,K\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
V\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be three integrable maps, modeling the evolution of parameters
Q,K,V
across layers of the Transformer.
We set
\varepsilon=1
for Sinkformer attention, by absorbing the
\varepsilon
in
Q,K,V
.
Let
\mu_{0}\in{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a compactly supported initial condition.
Then, for any choice of velocity field in
\{\Gamma^{(\mathrm{SM})},\Gamma^{(\mathrm{\ell^{2}})},\Gamma^{(\mathrm{sink})},\Gamma^{(\mathrm{\sigma})}\}
in its single-head
or
multi-head version, and denoting
\Gamma_{\mu}(t,\cdot)\coloneqq\Gamma_{\mu(t)}(\cdot)
the attention map associated to
Q(t),K(t),V(t)
(with the convention (
11
) and
k=d
for multi-head attention), the Transformer PDE

\partial_{t}\mu+\mathrm{div}(\mu\Gamma_{\mu})=0

(13)

with initial condition
\mu_{0}
has a unique global weak solution
\mu\in{\cal C}([0,+\infty),{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, with
{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the
p
-Wasserstein distance
W_{p}
.
Moreover, let
R_{0}
be the smallest radius such that
\supp\mu_{0}\subset B_{R_{0}}
, and define

R(t)\coloneqq\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert V(s)\right\rVert_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)R_{0}

for
t\geq 0
, where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
is the spectral norm.
Then, the solution
\mu
satisfies

\supp\mu(t)\subset B_{R(t)}

for all
t\geq 0
.
Finally, we have the following stability estimate.
For all
R_{0}>0
and
t>0
, there exists a constant
C(t,R_{0})
depending only on
t
,
R_{0}
and
Q,K,V
(and on the choice of
\Gamma
), such that for any initial conditions
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
supported in
B_{R_{0}}
, and denoting
\mu
and
\nu
the associated global solutions of Equation (
13
), we have

W_{p}(\mu(t),\nu(t))\leq C(t,R_{0})W_{p}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}).

Therefore, for compactly supported initial data, the Transformer PDE is well-posed: it has a unique global solution on
[0,T]
with initial data
\mu_{0}
, whose support typically grows exponentially with time.
Moreover, it generalizes the discrete system (
1
), which corresponds to the initial data
\mu_{0}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}(0)}
, and
is the mean-field limit of this interacting particle system on any compact time interval
[0,T]
: if
(\mu^{\eta}(0))_{\eta\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is a sequence of empirical measures such that
W_{p}(\mu^{\eta}(0),\mu(0))\to_{\eta\to+\infty}0
, then the distance
W_{p}(\mu^{\eta}(t),\mu(t))
tends to
0
as well when
\eta\to+\infty
for any
0\leq t\leq T
; and this holds for Softmax,
\ell^{2}
, Sinkhorn and Sigmoid self-attention and their multi-head version.
However, the constant
C(t,R_{0})
is exponential in
R(t)
, which is itself exponential in
t
.
When
t
becomes large, the stability estimate is therefore very loose.

Proof.

Let us detail the proof of Theorem
2
in the simpler case where the parameters
Q,K,V
are constant over time.
The general case is deferred to Appendix
C.4
.

Step 1: defining the flow
\phi_{t}(\mu)(x)

For all
T>0
, denote
{\cal C}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,T],{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
the set of equi-compactly supported curves, i.e., of continuous curves
\mu\in{\cal C}([0,T],{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
such that for any compact time interval
[s,t]\subset[0,T]
, there exists a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
verifying:
\forall\tau\in[s,t],\ \supp(\mu(\tau))\subset K.

Let
\mu\in{\cal C}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,T],{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and consider the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\dot{r}(t)=\Gamma_{\mu}(t,r(t))\mbox{ in }0\leq t\leq T\\
r(0)=x.\end{cases}.

(14)

We prove the following estimates for each single-headed self-attention type (see Appendix
C.1
)—multi-head estimates, stated in Lemma
47
, are the same up to changing the constant factors, so that the arguments still hold.
If
\supp\mu\subset B_{R}
and
\supp\nu\subset B_{R}
, then

(i)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}(x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}R
,

(ii)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert D_{x}\Gamma_{\mu}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R^{2}
,

(iii)

\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}(x)-\Gamma_{\nu}(x)\right\rvert\leq c(\left\lvert x\right\rvert,R)W_{p}(\mu,\nu),

where
c(\left\lvert x\right\rvert,R)
is a continuous function that depends on
\left\lvert x\right\rvert,R
and
Q,K,V
.
Then, traditional Cauchy theory tells us that problem (
14
) has a unique global solution in
[0,T]
.
Indeed, denote
R>0
a radius such that
\supp\mu(t)\subset B_{R}
for all
0\leq t\leq T
.
Such a radius exists because
\mu
is equi-compactly supported.
Equations
(ii)
and
(iii)
ensure that the map
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\Gamma_{\mu(t)}(x)
is continuous.
Thanks to Equation
(ii)
, we also have, for all
t\in[0,T]
:

\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu(t)}(x)-\Gamma_{\mu(t)}(y)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R^{2}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert.

Finally, Equation
(i)
ensures that
\Gamma_{\mu(t)}
grows less than linearly with
\left\lvert x\right\rvert
—in fact, it is bounded with respect to
\left\lvert x\right\rvert
—so that there is a unique global solution.
We can therefore define the associated flow
\phi_{t}(\mu)(x)
, such that the solution
r
of Problem (
14
) satisfies
r(t)=\phi_{t}(\mu)(x)
.

Step 2: local in-time fixed point argument

Let us set any initial condition
\bar{\mu}_{0}\in{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and a time
T>0
to be chosen later.
Define the map

\displaystyle{\cal F}\colon{\cal C}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,T],{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

\displaystyle\to{\cal C}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,T],{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\mapsto\phi_{t}(\mu)_{\sharp}\bar{\mu}_{0}

and equip the space
{\cal C}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,T],{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with the distance

{\cal D}_{p,T}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\max_{0\leq t\leq T}W_{p}(\mu(t),\nu(t)).

We look for a complete space
X
preserved by
{\cal F}
and such that
{\cal F}
is a contraction on
X
, to apply a fixed point argument and show the existence and uniqueness of a solution to Equation (
13
) in
[0,T]
.
Let
X
be the set of curves
\mu\in{\cal C}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,T],{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
satisfying

\supp\mu(t)\subset\,\overline{\!{B}}(0,e^{\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}t}R_{0})

for all
0\leq t\leq T
, where
R_{0}
is the smallest radius such that
\supp\bar{\mu}_{0}\subset B_{R_{0}}
.
We show (see Appendix
C.2
) that
{\cal F}(X)\subset X
, that
(X,{\cal D}_{p,T})
is complete and that

{\cal D}_{p,T}({\cal F}(\mu),{\cal F}(\nu))\leq f(T,R_{0}){\cal D}_{p,T}(\mu,\nu)

for any
\mu,\nu\in X
, where
f(T,R_{0})
is a continuous function such that
f(T,R_{0})\to_{T\to 0}0
.
Let us then choose
T>0
small enough to have
f(T,R_{0})<1
, so that
{\cal F}
becomes a strict contraction.
According to Banach fixed-point theorem,
{\cal F}
has a unique fixed point
\mu
.
Equivalently, Equation (
13
) with initial data
\bar{\mu}_{0}
has a unique solution in
[0,T]
.

Step 3: constructing a global solution

By repeating Step 2 with the updated initial condition
\phi_{T}(\mu)_{\sharp}\bar{\mu}_{0}
, we can extend
\mu
to further times, and so on.
However, the time interval that we add at each step depends on
R_{0}
, which grows as we iterate the argument—and the length of the time interval shrinks accordingly.
Assume by contradiction that this method does not allow us to extend
\mu
beyond some finite limiting time
T_{\mathrm{lim}}
.
It is easy to check that
\mu
satisfies, for all
t\in[0,T_{\mathrm{lim}})
:

\supp\mu(t)\subset\,\overline{\!{B}}(0,e^{\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}t}R_{0}),

where
R_{0}
is the smallest radius such that
\supp\bar{\mu}_{0}\subset B_{R_{0}}
.
Denote
R(t)\coloneqq e^{\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}t}R_{0}
for all
0\leq t\leq T_{\mathrm{lim}}
, and choose
T^{\prime}>0
small enough so that

f(T^{\prime},R(T_{\mathrm{lim}}))<1.

By using the same arguments as above, we can extend the restriction
\mu_{\lvert[0,T_{\mathrm{lim}}-T^{\prime}/2]}
to the time interval
[0,T_{\mathrm{lim}}+T^{\prime}/2]
, which is a contradiction.
Therefore,
\mu
can be extended to arbitrarily large times by iterating the fixed point argument, and so there exists a unique global solution to Problem (
13
) with initial condition
\bar{\mu}_{0}
.

Step 4: stability estimates

We derive the stability estimates in Appendix
C.3
adapting the traditional Dobrushin’s method
[

,
38
,
23
]
, building on Equations
(i)
,
(ii)
,
(iii)
.

Remark 3
.

Theorem
2
does not cover the unnormalized attentions
\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{exp})}

and

Indeed, for these three types of attention and assuming that

We rather have that

\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{exp})}(x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}Re^{\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R^{2}}
,

\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{lin})}(x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R^{3}
,

\sup_{x\in B_{R}}\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{ReLU})}(x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R^{3}
,
and when
V=I_{d}
, each bound is reached for a suitable Dirac measure.
Therefore, Lemma
50
does not hold, and we have no guarantee that
\mu
stays compactly supported across the Transformer PDE dynamics, so that our proof technique is not applicable.

3.2
Masked Self-Attention

The case of masked self-attention is special: indeed, with the framework introduced in Section
2.6
, no upper bound of the form

\left\lvert\Gamma_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)-\Gamma_{\bar{\nu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\right\rvert\leq C(x)W_{2}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})

can hold (see Appendix
C.5
), even when the supports of
\mu
and
\nu
are constrained in a compact set and have the same position marginal (defined in Equation (
10
)).
A key remark to circumvent this issue is to notice that if
\bar{\mu}(t)
is the solution of Equation (
3
) initialized at
\bar{\mu}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then the position marginal of
\bar{\mu}(t)
is constant over time.
For any probability distribution
\theta\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])
, let us denote
{\cal P}^{\theta}_{c}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of compactly supported probability measures whose position marginal is equal to
\theta
.
The idea is to equip this space with an alternative to the Wasserstein distance, the conditional Wasserstein distance.

Definition 4
(Conditional Wasserstein distance
[
40
]
).

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}^{\theta}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\theta(\{0\})>0
.
The conditional Wasserstein distance between
\bar{\mu}
and
\bar{\nu}
is defined as

d(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau})\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)

where we have written

\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,x)=:\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}^{\tau}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu}(\tau,x)=:\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu}^{\tau}(x)

with the disintegration theorem.

Remark 5
.

One can also define the conditional
p
-Wasserstein distance for
p\geq 1
as

d(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})\coloneqq\left(\int_{0}^{1}W_{p}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau})^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)\right)^{1/p}

with the same notation as in Definition
4
.
All our proofs adapt directly to this case, as the estimates rely on a
W_{1}
estimate.

The idea of Definition
4
is to constrain the transport plans so that they preserve the position marginal.
This allows us to prove the following well-posedness result.

Theorem 6
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Let
Q,K\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
V\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be three integrable maps, modeling the evolution of parameters
Q,K,V
across layers of the Transformer.
Let
\bar{\mu}_{0}\in{\cal P}_{c}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a compactly supported initial condition. Denote
\theta
its position marginal, defined as
\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\sigma)=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}_{0}(\sigma,x),

and assume that
\theta(\{0\})>0
, i.e.,
\theta
has a Dirac mass at
0
.
Let
\Gamma\in\{\Gamma^{(\mathrm{SM})},\Gamma^{(\mathrm{\ell^{2}})},\Gamma^{(\mathrm{sink})},\Gamma^{(\mathrm{\sigma})}\}
be an unmasked, single-headed or multi-headed velocity field associated with the parameters
Q(t),K(t),V(t)
(with the convention (
11
) and
k=d
for multi-head attention), and denote
\Gamma_{\bar{\mu}}(t,(\sigma,x))\coloneqq\Gamma_{\bar{\mu}(t)}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)
the corresponding masked attention map.
Then, the masked Transformer PDE

\partial_{t}\bar{\mu}+\mathrm{div}(\bar{\mu}\Gamma_{\bar{\mu}})=0

(15)

with initial condition
\bar{\mu}_{0}
has a unique global weak solution
\bar{\mu}\in{\cal C}([0,+\infty),{\cal P}_{c}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, with
{\cal P}_{c}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the conditional Wasserstein distance
d
.
Moreover, let
R_{0}
be the smallest radius such that
\supp\mu_{0}\subset B_{R_{0}}
, where
\mu_{0}
is the space marginal of
\bar{\mu}_{0}
, and define

R(t)\coloneqq e^{\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert V(s)\right\rVert_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s}R_{0}

for
t\geq 0
, where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
is the spectral norm.
Then, the space marginal
\mu
of the solution
\bar{\mu}
satisfies

\supp\mu(t)\subset B_{R(t)}

for all
t\geq 0
.
Finally, we have the following stability estimate.
For all
R_{0}>0
and
t>0
, there exists a constant
C(t,R_{0},\theta(\{0\}))
depending only on
t
,
R_{0}
,
\theta(\{0\})
and
Q,K,V
such that for any initial conditions
\bar{\mu}_{0}
and
\bar{\nu}_{0}
supported in
[0,1]\times B_{R_{0}}
with the same position marginal
\theta
, and denoting
\bar{\mu}
and
\bar{\nu}
the associated global solutions of Equation (
15
), we have

d(\bar{\mu}(t),\bar{\nu}(t))\leq C(t,R_{0},\theta(\{0\}))d(\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\nu}_{0}).

We defer the proof to Appendix
C.5
.

Remark 7
.

The assumption that the position marginal
\theta
has a Dirac mass at zero is crucial for the proof to hold. This is due to the following bound:

\left\lvert\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)-\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\nu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\right\rvert\leq\frac{c(\left\lvert x\right\rvert,R)}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)}d(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}),

for any
\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}\in{\cal P}([0,1]\times B_{R})
, which is sharp up to a constant factor independent of
\theta
. When
\theta(\{0\})=0
, the right-hand side diverges—and, for suitable
\bar{\mu}
and
\bar{\nu}
, the left-hand side as well, which is an obstruction to proving the contractivity of
{\cal F}
.
A way to ensure this assumption is always satisfied is to put an artificial and arbitrarily small Dirac mass at
(0,0_{d})
, and encode actual tokens in
(0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, this slightly changes the computation of masked attention—relaxing this assumption is left for future work.

4
Clustering For Gaussian Initial Data

Let us now tackle the particular case where the initial data is a Gaussian measure.
This is an oversimplified model for real data; still, it provides a simple example of anisotropic data, which allows us to study the evolution of the anisotropy across the dynamics.
Indeed, the Gaussian case has the nice property that several types of unmasked self-attention introduced in Section
2
have a closed form when
\mu
is Gaussian—more precisely,
\Gamma_{\mu}
becomes an affine function.
This implies that Gaussian input measures stay Gaussian during the dynamics
\partial_{t}\mu+\mathrm{div}(\mu\Gamma_{\mu})=0
, so that we can summarize the evolution as a system of ordinary differential equations linking the expectation and the covariance matrix of
\mu
, which can then be studied more easily than the initial PDE.
This viewpoint is not limited to self-attention layers: Appendix
B
records a closely related application to the Gaussian-kernel drifting field introduced in
[
28
]
. This field is the difference between the target and current normalized Gaussian-kernel attention fields, so the Gaussian computations below also provide closed mean and covariance dynamics for drifting between Gaussian measures.

4.1
Softmax Self-Attention

Let us start with Softmax self-attention.
We have the following closed form for the velocity field
\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{SM})}
when
\mu
is a Gaussian probability measure, which is key for proving the stability of Gaussians and deriving ODEs on their expectation and covariance.
Recall that
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mu={\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma)
be a Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{SM})}(x)=V(\alpha+\Sigma Ax).

Note that this covers the case of linear attention as well by absorbing the
\varepsilon
in the matrix
A
(see Subsection
2.2
).
When the initial data is Gaussian, we can then rewrite the dynamics as follows.

Proposition 9
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Let
Q,K\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
V\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be three continuous maps, modeling the evolution of parameters
Q,K,V
across layers of the Transformer.
Consider a Gaussian initial condition
\mu_{0}={\cal N}(\alpha_{0},\Sigma_{0})
with
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
positive definite.
Then, the Transformer PDE

\partial_{t}\mu+\mathrm{div}(\mu\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{SM})})=0

associated to Softmax self-attention with parameters
Q(t),K(t),V(t)
has a unique maximal solution
\mu
on
[0,t_{\mathrm{max}})
, such that
\mu(t)
is Gaussian for all
t\in[0,t_{\mathrm{max}})
.
Moreover, denoting
\mu(t)\eqqcolon{\cal N}(\alpha(t),\Sigma(t))
and
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
, we have

\dot{\Sigma}=V\Sigma A\Sigma+\Sigma A^{\top}\Sigma V^{\top}.

(16)

and

\dot{\alpha}=V(I_{d}+\Sigma A)\alpha.

The proof is in Appendix
D.1
.
In the rest of the section, let us assume to simplify the problem that
Q,K,V
are constant over time. Under a commutation assumption on these matrices, we have the following classification of possible behaviors of
\Sigma
.

Proposition 10
.

Consider the matrix-valued differential equation

\dot{\Sigma}=V\Sigma A\Sigma+\Sigma A^{\top}\Sigma V^{\top}.

(17)

Assume that the matrices
V
and
V^{\top}
commute with
\Sigma_{0}
and
VA+A^{\top}V^{\top}
.
Then, Equation (
17
) with initial condition
\Sigma_{0}\succ 0
has a unique maximal solution, defined as

\Sigma(t)=\left(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}-t(VA+A^{\top}V^{\top})\right)^{-1}

for all
t\geq 0
such that the matrix
\Sigma_{0}^{-1}-t(VA+A^{\top}V^{\top})
is invertible.
The behavior of the solution therefore depends on the sign of the eigenvalues of
VA+A^{\top}V^{\top}
as follows.

1.

If
VA+A^{\top}V^{\top}\preceq 0
, then Equation (
17
) has a global solution. Moreover, the matrix
\Sigma(t)
converges to a limit
\Sigma^{*}\succeq 0
satisfying

\lambda_{i}(\Sigma^{*})=0
for all
i
such that
\lambda_{i}(VA+A^{\top}V^{\top})<0
.
Therefore, the mass concentrates on an affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, of dimension equal to the multiplicity of 0 as an eigenvalue of
VA+A^{\top}V^{\top}
.

2.

If
VA+A^{\top}V^{\top}
has a positive eigenvalue, then the largest eigenvalue of
\Sigma(t)
goes to
+\infty
in finite time.

Remark 11
.

Note that in the even simpler case where
\Sigma_{0}
and
VA+A^{\top}V^{\top}
commute, the limit of
\Sigma(t)
becomes explicit. Indeed, as these matrices are simultaneously diagonalizable, we get

\lambda_{i}(\Sigma(t))=\left(\lambda_{i}(\Sigma_{0})^{-1}-t\lambda_{i}(VA+A^{\top}V^{\top})\right)^{-1}.

In this case, if
VA+A^{\top}V^{\top}\preceq 0
then
\Sigma(t)
converges to the matrix
\Sigma^{*}\succeq 0
defined as

\lambda_{i}(\Sigma^{*})=\begin{cases}0~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\mbox{if }\lambda_{i}(VA+A^{\top}V^{\top})<0,\\
\lambda_{i}(\Sigma_{0})~~~~~~~~~~~\mbox{if }\lambda_{i}(VA+A^{\top}V^{\top})=0.\end{cases}

Hence, under the assumption of Proposition
10
and for
VA+A^{\top}V^{\top}\neq 0
, any Gaussian solution of the Transformer PDE either i) converges when
t\to+\infty
to a degenerate Gaussian measure, i.e., with a low-rank covariance matrix, or ii) leaves the finite-covariance Gaussian regime in finite time.
Case i) corresponds to a collapse of the mass along the axes associated to zero eigenvalues of the limiting covariance
\Sigma^{*}
, and can be seen as the continuous parallel of the clustering for discrete tokens highlighted in
[
64
,
36
]
.
The proof of Proposition
10
is in Appendix
D.1
.
We complement this analysis with numerical experiments (Section
4.5
), which show that this behavior goes beyond the assumption of Proposition
10
.

Remark 12
.

As a more intuitive illustration of Proposition
10
, let us look at Equation (
16
) in dimension 1, writing
(s,a,v)
for
(\Sigma,A,V)
in this case.

Proposition 13
.

Let
a,v\in\mathbb{R}
and
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
.
Consider Equation (
17
) in dimension 1, which reads

\dot{s}=2avs^{2}

with initial condition
s(0)=s_{0}
.
There is a unique maximal solution to this equation, defined as

s(t)=(s_{0}^{-1}-2vat)^{-1}

for
t\in[0,t_{\mathrm{max}})
, where
t_{\mathrm{max}}\coloneqq(2vas_{0})^{-1}
if
av>0
, and
t_{\mathrm{max}}\coloneqq+\infty
if
av\leq 0
.

In dimension 1, the dynamics on Gaussians are therefore simple: the covariance matrix either i) goes to zero or ii) blows up, i.e., becomes infinite, in finite time.
Translating these behaviors in terms of the mass distribution induced by the Gaussian measure gives either i) clustering of the mass towards a single Dirac when
t\to+\infty
or ii) escaping of the mass to infinity in finite time.

To complement the analysis, the following paragraphs showcase two properties of Equation (
16
).
Note that in the particular case where
V=I_{d}
, Equation (
16
) takes the form of a Riccati equation
[
70
]
.

Rank is preserved across the dynamics

We show in Appendix
59
that Equation (
16
) preserves the rank of
\Sigma
.
In particular, the case where the initial covariance matrix is of rank 1, which corresponds to a maximally anisotropic distribution of tokens, leads to a simpler differential equation.

Lemma 14
.

Let
A,V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then the maximal solution
\Sigma(t)
of the equation

\dot{\Sigma}=V\Sigma A\Sigma+\Sigma A^{\top}\Sigma V^{\top}

with initial data
\Sigma_{0}\coloneqq u_{0}u_{0}^{\top}

is of rank 1 for all times where it is defined.
Moreover, denoting
\Sigma(t)\eqqcolon u(t)u(t)^{\top}
with
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\dot{u}=(u^{\top}Au)Vu.

(18)

The evolution of
u(t)
is characterized by a non-linear coupling between the radial dynamics and the angular dynamics: while
V
determines the flow’s direction, the scalar field
u^{\top}Au
acts as a non-uniform time-scaling factor. Therefore, unless
u_{0}
is a joint eigenvector of
V
and
A
, or these matrices share a specific algebraic relationship (like commutativity), finding a closed form for
u(t)
seems a difficult problem.
Still, up to an implicit time rescaling, one easily checks that the behavior of the solution is the following.

Proposition 15
.

Let
A,V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Denote
u(t)
the solution of Equation (
18
).
We have

u(t)=e^{\tau(t)V}u_{0},

where
\tau(t)
is the time rescaling that is solution of
\dot{\tau}=u^{\top}Au
such that
\tau(0)=0
.

Moreover, from Lemma
14
we can easily characterize rank-1 stationary points of the dynamics.

Lemma 16
.

Let
A,V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
The rank-1 matrix
uu^{\top}
with
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a stationary point of Equation (
16
) if and only if
u\in\ker V
or
u^{\top}(A+A^{\top})u=0
.

Note that the set
\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:u^{\top}(A+A^{\top})u=0\}
is the isotropic cone associated to the quadratic form
A+A^{\top}
.
Its geometry depends on the signature of
A+A^{\top}
.
It is non-trivial if and only if
A+A^{\top}
has zero eigenvalues or eigenvalues of the opposite sign.

Stationary points have low rank when
V
is identity and
A
is symmetric

Another interesting case is to look at stationary points of Equation (
16
) when
V=I_{d}
and
A
is symmetric.
We have seen in Proposition
10
that when
A+A^{\top}\preceq 0
, limiting points
\Sigma^{*}
must satisfy

\mathrm{rk}\Sigma^{*}\leq\mathrm{rk}A.

We generalize this result to any symmetric matrix
A
as follows (see Appendix
D.1
for the proof).

Proposition 17
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a symmetric matrix and assume that
V=I_{d}
.
If a symmetric matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is solution of

V\Sigma A\Sigma+\Sigma A^{\top}\Sigma V^{\top}=0,

then

\mathrm{rk}\Sigma\leq\dim\ker A+\min(\sharp\{\mathrm{positive\ eigenvalues\ of\ }A\},\sharp\{\mathrm{negative\ eigenvalues\ of\ }A\}).

Therefore, when
A
is symmetric and
V=I_{d}
, the stationary points of Equation (
16
) have low rank—smaller than
d/2
when
A
is invertible, for instance.
Note that when
A
has a positive eigenvalue, we have seen in Proposition
10
that the dynamics with a positive definite initialization blow up in finite time. In that case, stationary points are never reached.

4.2
Multi-Head Softmax Self-Attention

Lemma
8
allows us to derive the following result for multi-head Softmax self-attention.

Proposition 18
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
For
1\leq h\leq H
, let

Q^{(h)},K^{(h)}\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d/H\times d}

and

be

of the Transformer.

\mu_{0}={\cal N}(\alpha_{0},\Sigma_{0})
with
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

Then, the multi-head Transformer PDE

\partial_{t}\mu+\mathrm{div}(\mu\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{MH})})=0

associated to multi-head self-attention with parameters
(Q^{(h)},K^{(h)},V^{(h)})_{1\leq h\leq H}
via Equation (
11

\mu
on
[0,t_{\mathrm{max}})
, such that

4.3
\ell^{2}
Self-Attention

In the case of
\ell^{2}
self-attention, we have the following closed form for the velocity field when
\mu
is Gaussian.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\mu={\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma)
be a Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{\ell^{2}})}(x)=V(\Sigma^{-1}+2K^{\top}K)^{-1}(\Sigma^{-1}\alpha+2K^{\top}Qx).

As
\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{\ell^{2}})}
is an affine function when
\mu
is Gaussian, Gaussianity is preserved by the
\ell^{2}
Transformer PDE, so we can rewrite it as two coupled matrix ODEs, with the same method as for Softmax self-attention.

Proposition 20
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Let
Q,K,V\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be continuous functions.
Consider a Gaussian initial condition
\mu_{0}={\cal N}(\alpha_{0},\Sigma_{0})
with
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
positive definite.
Then, the Transformer equation

\partial_{t}\mu+\mathrm{div}(\mu\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{\ell^{2}})})=0

associated to
\ell^{2}
self-attention has a unique maximal solution
\mu
, such that
\mu(t)
is Gaussian for all
t\in[0,t_{\mathrm{max}})
.
Moreover, denoting
\mu(t)\eqqcolon{\cal N}(\alpha(t),\Sigma(t))
and
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
, we have

\dot{\Sigma}=2V\Sigma(I_{d}+2K^{\top}K\Sigma)^{-1}A\Sigma+2\Sigma A^{\top}(I_{d}+2K^{\top}K\Sigma)^{-1}\Sigma V^{\top}.

(19)

and

\dot{\alpha}=V(\Sigma^{-1}+2K^{\top}K)^{-1}(\Sigma^{-1}+2A)\alpha.

Remark 21
.

Note that a similar result as Proposition
20
has been stated in a completely different context in
[
46
, Page 229]
, only in dimension 1, assuming
Q=K
and with a slight modification of the velocity field:
\Gamma_{\mu}(x)=\nu x+\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{\ell^{2}})}(x)
.

Contrary to what happens with Softmax self-attention, we show (see Appendix
D.1
) that the dynamics (

) cannot blow up in finite time.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}\succ 0
.
The matrix-valued differential equation

\dot{\Sigma}=2V\Sigma(I_{d}+2K^{\top}K\Sigma)^{-1}A\Sigma+2\Sigma A^{\top}(I_{d}+2K^{\top}K\Sigma)^{-1}\Sigma V^{\top}

initialized at
\Sigma_{0}
has a unique global solution.

In this sense,
\ell^{2}
self-attention is therefore smoother than Softmax self-attention.
This nicely complements—and is connected to—the result in
[
44
]
showing that
\ell^{2}
self-attention is globally Lipschitz continuous, contrary to Softmax self-attention.
As the equation on
\Sigma
is more involved in the
\ell^{2}
case than in the Softmax self-attention case, let us focus only on dimension 1.
A clustering phenomenon appears as for Softmax self-attention.

Proposition 23
.

Let
q,k,v\in\mathbb{R}
and
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
.
Denote
a\coloneqq qk
and consider the differential equation

\dot{s}=\frac{4avs^{2}}{1+2k^{2}s}

with initial condition
s(0)=s_{0}
.

1.

If
av>0
, then
s(t)\to+\infty
when
t\to+\infty
.

2.

If
av<0
, then
s(t)\to 0
when
t\to+\infty
.

Proof.

If
av>0
, then
\dot{s}>0
so
s
is increasing. Hence, it converges in
(s_{0},+\infty]
, and it cannot converge to a finite value as
0\notin[s_{0},+\infty)
is the only stationary point of the equation.

It
av<0
, then
s
is decreasing, so it converges in
[-\infty,s_{0})
. The only stationary point in this interval is
0
, and
s_{0}>0
, which proves the claim as
s(t)
is continuous.

In Proposition
23
, case 1 corresponds, in terms of the Gaussian measure of covariance
s(t)
, to the mass spreading to infinity, while case 2 induces a clustering of the mass into one single Dirac.
The 1-dimensional case for
\ell^{2}
self-attention is therefore close to Proposition
13
(dimension 1 for Softmax self-attention), where the finite-time blow-up of

is replaced by a divergence when
t\to+\infty
.
Following this remark, we point out numerically in Section
4.5
that the Softmax and
\ell^{2}
self-attention dynamics are very close except when the former blows up.
We also show in Section
4.5
that the clustering behavior observed with Softmax self-attention also occurs with
\ell^{2}
self-attention.

4.4
Sinkhorn Self-Attention

Let us finally focus on Sinkhorn self-attention.
We start by computing a closed form for the velocity field
\Gamma_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(\text{sink})}
when
\mu
is a Gaussian probability measure.

Lemma 24
.

Let
\mu={\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma)
be a Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
Q,K\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
be two matrices, denote
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
and assume that
A
and
\Sigma
are invertible.
Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds

\Gamma^{(\mathrm{sink})}_{\mu,\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}V(I_{d}-A^{-\top}\Sigma^{-1}C)\alpha+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}VA^{-\top}\Sigma^{-1}Cx,

where

C\coloneqq\Sigma^{1/2}(\Sigma^{1/2}A^{\top}\Sigma A\Sigma^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d})^{1/2}\Sigma^{-1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}.

The proof is in Appendix
D.1
.
Building on this result, we obtain with the same method as for Proposition
9
two matrix ODEs summarizing the Sinkformer PDE on Gaussian measures.

Proposition 25
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Let
Q,K,V\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be continuous functions.
Consider a Gaussian initial condition
\mu_{0}={\cal N}(\alpha_{0},\Sigma_{0})
with
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
positive definite.
Then, the Sinkformer PDE

\partial_{t}\mu+\mathrm{div}(\mu\Gamma_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(\mathrm{sink})})=0

associated to Sinkhorn self-attention has a unique maximal solution
\mu
, such that
\mu(t)
is Gaussian for all
t\in[0,t_{\mathrm{max}})
.
Moreover, denoting
\mu(t)\eqqcolon{\cal N}(\alpha(t),\Sigma(t))
and
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
, we have

\dot{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}VA^{-\top}\Sigma^{-1}C\Sigma+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Sigma C^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}A^{-1}V^{\top},

with
C\coloneqq\Sigma^{1/2}(\Sigma^{1/2}A\Sigma A^{\top}\Sigma^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d})^{1/2}\Sigma^{-1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d},

and

\dot{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}V\alpha.

Remarkably, in this case, and contrary to
\ell^{2}
and Softmax self-attention, the expectation
\alpha
and the covariance matrix
\Sigma
evolve independently.
We have

\alpha(t)=e^{tV/\varepsilon}\alpha_{0}.

Remark 26
.

The fact that the expectation and the covariance evolve independently is not that surprising.
Indeed,
[
59
]
show that, under certain assumptions on the parameters, the Sinkformer PDE corresponds to the Wasserstein gradient flow of a functional that takes the form
\phi(\alpha)+\psi(\Sigma)
on Gaussians, where
\alpha
and
\Sigma
are respectively the expectation and the covariance matrix of the Gaussian measure (see Equation (
31
)).
Then, the Wasserstein gradient flow of
\phi(\alpha)+\psi(\Sigma)
, which stays in the space of Gaussian measures, corresponds to following a Euclidean gradient flow on
\phi(\alpha)
and a Bures-Wasserstein gradient flow on
\psi(\Sigma)
(see Section
5.3
).

Contrary to the expectation equation, the covariance equation is much more challenging to be analyzed theoretically.
We only consider the 1-dimensional case.

Proposition 27
.

Let
q,k,v\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\coloneqq qk
.
Assume that
\varepsilon=1
, as it can be absorbed in

and
v
.
The covariance equation associated with Sinkhorn self-attention in dimension 1 reads

\dot{s}=\frac{v}{a}(\sqrt{4a^{2}s^{2}+1}-1).

For any initial data

, this equation has a global solution

, then

,

2.

if

, then
s(t)\to 0
when

.

The proof is the same as for Proposition

.
Therefore, in dimension 1, Sinkhorn self-attention is smoother than Softmax self-attention, and induces the same clustering phenomenon when

5
Viewing the Dynamics as a Gradient Flow

This section aims to connect the Transformer PDE with gradient flows in measure spaces.
Let us first stress that the derivations in this section are only formal, the goal being to gain some intuition on the gradient flow structure of the different Transformer evolutions considered so far, and to highlight, in particular, the structure of the Gaussian case. Making these calculations rigorous is beyond the scope of this paper.
We start with some background on gradient flows with respect to a geodesic distance, which generalize Wasserstein gradient flows.

5.1
Limiting Minimizing Schemes for Geodesic Distances

We consider a distance
{\cal D}(\mu,\nu)
on the space
{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For some function
{\cal F}\colon{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
, one can define minimizing evolutions on the metric space
{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
starting from
\mu_{0}\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, following
[
3
]
, by considering implicit stepping with step-size
\tau>0
:

\mu_{t+\tau}\in\underset{\mu\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}{\operatorname{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\tau}{\cal D}(\mu_{t},\mu)^{2}+{\cal F}(\mu)\right\}.

(20)

Under suitable regularity properties on
{\cal F}
, it is possible to take the limit
\tau\to 0
and consider the continuous-time evolution
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
, which solves a PDE.
The aim of this subsection is to formally derive this PDE, called the
gradient flow
of
{\cal F}
for the distance
{\cal D}
, and which takes the form of a continuity equation for some velocity field that depends on
{\cal F}
.
Note that if
{\cal D}
is equal to the Wasserstein distance
W_{2}
, this is by now a textbook computation which corresponds to the JKO flow construction
[
42
,
3
,
61
]
of Wasserstein gradient flows.
We consider the more general case where
{\cal D}
is a geodesic distance with the following dynamical formulation:

{\cal D}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{\rho,v}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\!\int\mathcal{G}_{\rho_{s}}(v_{s})\cdot v_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s:\partial_{s}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\,\mathcal{G}_{\rho}(v))=0,\mbox{ }\begin{array}[]{c}\rho_{0}=\mu\\
\rho_{1}=\nu\end{array}\right\},

(21)

where
\mathcal{G}_{\rho}
is a linear invertible
L^{2}_{\rho}
-self-adjoint operator on vector fields. The classical distance
{\cal D}=W_{2}
corresponds to the choice
\mathcal{G}_{\rho}=\text{Id}
. We here follow a similar duality strategy to
[
9
,
17
]
to formally rewrite the distance as a saddle point problem for which optimality conditions are easier to obtain. We first observe that we can dilate the time variable
s
and scale
v
such that

\frac{1}{\tau}{\cal D}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{\rho,v}\left\{\int_{0}^{\tau}\!\!\!\int\mathcal{G}_{\rho_{s}}(v_{s})\cdot v_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s:\partial_{s}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\,\mathcal{G}_{\rho}(v))=0,\!\begin{array}[]{c}\rho_{0}=\mu\\
\rho_{\tau}=\nu\end{array}\!\!\right\},

(22)

Using a Lagrangian dual formulation of
{\cal D}
, we rewrite

\displaystyle\mu_{t+\tau}\in\underset{\mu}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ 2{\cal F}(\mu)

\displaystyle+\inf_{\rho,v}\sup_{\psi}\int_{0}^{\tau}\!\!\!\int\mathcal{G}_{\rho}(v)\cdot v\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\tau}\!\!\!\int(\partial_{s}\psi+\mathcal{G}_{\rho}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi)\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}s+\int\!\psi(\tau,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int\!\psi(0,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}.

Here, the test functions
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,\tau]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
play the role of Lagrange multipliers to rewrite the distance as an inf-sup problem without constraints. This approach is classical going back to the case of the classical Wasserstein distance
W_{2}
, see
[
6
,
9
]
.
The optimality condition on
\mu_{t+\tau}
is

2\delta{\cal F}(\mu_{t+\tau})+\psi(\tau,\cdot)=0,

(23)

where
\delta{\cal F}
is the first variation (Fréchet derivative) of
{\cal F}
, i.e.,

{\cal F}(\mu+h\nu)={\cal F}(\mu)+h\int\delta{\cal F}(\mu)\,\mathrm{d}\mu+o(h).

Equation (
23
) formally gives

\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{x}\psi=-\nabla_{x}\delta{\cal F}(\mu),

(24)

when
\tau\to 0^{+}
.
Moreover, we find the following optimality condition for
v
given by

v=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{x}\psi,

(25)

by taking Fréchet derivatives with respect to
v
in (
22
) and using that
\mathcal{G}_{\rho}
is a linear invertible
L^{2}_{\rho}
-self-adjoint operator on vector fields. Combining Equations (
24
) and (
25
), one can rewrite
\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\,\mathcal{G}_{\rho}(v))=0
as the following non-linear advection equation

\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\,\Gamma_{\rho})=0.

(26)

with
\Gamma_{\rho}\coloneqq-\mathcal{G}_{\rho}(\nabla_{x}[\delta{\cal F}(\mu)])
. In this sense, we say that the PDE (
26
) is the gradient flow of
{\cal F}
for the distance
{\cal D}
. Notice that for the classical case with
{\cal D}=W_{2}
so that
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}=\text{Id}
, then
\Gamma_{\rho}
is the standard Wasserstein gradient.

Remark 28
.

Classical free energy functionals are of the form

{\cal F}(\rho)=\int U(\rho)\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\int V(x)\,\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int W(x-y)\,\rho(x)\,\rho(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

(27)

where
U\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is a density of
internal energy,
V\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a confinement potential and

W\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is an interaction potential, see for instance
[
22
,
66
]
. The corresponding variation is given by

\delta{\cal F}(\rho)=U^{\prime}\left(\rho\right)+V+W\ast\rho.

Without interaction potential
W=0
, this general family of PDEs, with
{\cal D}=W_{2}
so that
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}=\text{Id}
, contains well-known models in mathematical physics such as the heat equation,
U(s)=s\log s
and
V=0
, the porous-medium
and fast-diffusion equations
[
65
]
,
U(s)={s^{m}}/{(m-1)}
,
m>0
and
V=0
and their Fokker-Planck counterparts,

U(s)=s\log s
and
V(x)={|x|^{2}}/{2}
, and
U(s)={s^{m}}/{(m-1)}
and
V(x)={|x|^{2}}/{2}
respectively, see

[
56
]
for instance. With nontrivial interaction potentials, it includes many important PDEs in mathematical biology and mathematical physics, such as the Keller-Segel model, see
[
19
]
and the references therein, or nonlocal McKean-Vlasov equations, see
[
21
]
and the references therein.

5.2
Restriction to the Subspace of Gaussians

In Section
4
, we have seen that the Transformer PDE with a Gaussian measure as initial data stays in the space of Gaussians over time, for several variants of self-attention.
For Transformer PDEs that have a gradient flow structure as introduced in Subsection
5.1
, the evolution of the Gaussian case can be inferred from the gradient flow structure, and follows a Riemannian gradient flow.
This subsection details that connection, which allows us to check the Gaussian evolutions obtained in Section
4
.

Let
{\cal D}
be a geodesic distance.
We assume that
{\cal D}
is translation invariant, in the sense that:

{\cal D}(\mu,\nu)^{2}={\cal D}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})^{2}+\lvert m(\mu)-m(\nu)\rvert^{2},

where
\lvert\cdot\rvert
is the Euclidean norm,
m(\mu)\coloneqq\int x\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
is the mean of
\mu
, and
\mu_{0}=T_{\#}\mu
with
T(x)=x-m(\mu)
(so that
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
have zero mean). This is the case for the Wasserstein distance and the twisted distance (
33
) considered below.
The metric
{\cal D}
descends to a finite-dimensional metric
D
on the cone
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{++}
of covariances:

D(\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime})\coloneqq{\cal D}(\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{\prime})),

where
\mathcal{N}(\alpha,\Sigma)
is the Gaussian measure with covariance
\Sigma
and mean
\alpha
. If the initial data
\mu_{t=0}
is Gaussian and the vector field
\Gamma_{\mathcal{N}(\alpha,\Sigma)}
is affine, solutions of Equation (
26
) are Gaussian for all time, as seen in Section
4
: we can write
\mu_{t}\eqqcolon\mathcal{N}(\alpha(t),\Sigma(t))
, and
t\mapsto(\alpha(t),\Sigma(t))
follows a Riemannian gradient flow of the finite-dimensional function

F(\alpha,\Sigma)\coloneqq{\cal F}(\mathcal{N}(\alpha,\Sigma)),

where the Riemannian structure is induced by
D
as follows.

Similarly to Equation (
20
), the Riemannian flow induced by the gradient flow on the space of Gaussians can be defined via an implicit stepping:

(\alpha(t+\tau),\Sigma(t+\tau))\in\underset{\alpha,\Sigma}{\operatorname{argmin}}\,D(\Sigma,\Sigma(t))^{2}+\lvert\alpha-\alpha(t)\rvert^{2}+2\tau F(\alpha,\Sigma),

where the minimization is restricted to positive semidefinite matrices.
Taking the limit
\tau\to 0
yields a continuous-time trajectory
t\mapsto(\alpha(t),\Sigma(t))
. This flow satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\alpha}F(\alpha(t),\Sigma(t)),

(28)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-M_{\Sigma}^{-1}\nabla_{\Sigma}F(\alpha(t),\Sigma(t)),

where the Riemannian metric
M_{\Sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is defined as follows:

D(\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime})^{2}=\langle M_{\Sigma}(\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}),\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}+o(\|\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}).

(29)

The following two subsections detail the gradient flow structure of the Transformer PDE respectively for Softmax and Sinkhorn self-attention, in the general case and the Gaussian case, building on what has been introduced above.

5.3
Sinkformer: Wasserstein Flow

As shown in
[
59
]
, for the specific case of Sinkhorn attention layers, and under the assumption that
A=A^{\top}=-V
(with
A\coloneqq K^{\top}Q
), the Sinkformer PDE is a Wasserstein gradient flow, i.e., it satisfies Equation (
26
) for
{\cal D}=W_{2}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}=\text{Id}
), and for the functional:

{\cal F}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\int\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}\log\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}}{\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{0}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}(\mu\otimes\mu)+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\int(\left\lvert Qx\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert Kx\right\rvert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

(30)

Remark 29
.

The second term in Equation (
30
) does not appear in
[
59
]
, because the authors define
\kappa^{0}(x,y)\coloneqq\exp(Qx\cdot Ky/\varepsilon)
, while we choose
\kappa^{0}(x,y)\coloneqq\exp(-\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}/2\varepsilon)
.
These two choices lead to the same
\kappa^{\infty}
.

As seen in Section
4.4
, in this case, for a Gaussian
\mu
, the vector field
\Gamma_{\mu}
is an affine function. Consequently, one can also consider this evolution as a Riemannian gradient flow over the mean and covariance space, according to Subsection
5.2
. The corresponding Riemannian metric is the so-called Bures-Wasserstein metric, whose inverse reads
[
52
]
:

M_{\Sigma}^{-1}:A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\mapsto A\Sigma+\Sigma A^{\top}.

When restricted to the space of Gaussian distributions, we show in Appendix
E.1
that the Sinkformer evolution (
2
) corresponds to the Riemannian flow ODE (
28
) for the function:

F_{\varepsilon}(\alpha,\Sigma)=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\left(-\mathfrak{B}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\Sigma,\Sigma)+\tr(Q\Sigma Q^{\top})+\tr(K\Sigma K^{\top})+\alpha^{\top}(Q^{\top}Q+K^{\top}K)\alpha\right),

(31)

where
\mathfrak{B}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})
is the entropy-regularized Bures distance, defined as

\mathfrak{B}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})\coloneqq 2\varepsilon OT_{\varepsilon}({\cal N}(\alpha_{1},\Sigma_{1}),{\cal N}(\alpha_{2},\Sigma_{2}))-\left\lvert\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\right\rvert^{2},

(32)

where
OT_{\varepsilon}
is defined in Equation (
7
).
Note that these Bures flows have several favorable properties. In particular, the low-rank manifold is closed, meaning that the rank cannot increase during evolution. However, it is possible for the rank to decrease, and even if the initial covariance is full-rank, it typically becomes rank-deficient at the final time, as illustrated numerically in Section
4.5
.

5.4
Softmax Transformer: A Twisted Wasserstein Flow

For a finite number of tokens, the Transformer evolution can be recast as a gradient flow for a particular non-Euclidean metric
[
36
]
.
We generalize this construction to probability measures by defining a twisted Wasserstein distance denoted
d_{A,V}
, associated with:

\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(v)\colon x\mapsto\frac{Bv(x)}{\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)},

(33)

with the notation of Equation (
21
), and where
B\coloneqq-VA^{-\top}
.
The twisted Wasserstein distance

d_{A,V}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{\rho,v}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\!\int\mathcal{G}_{\rho_{s}}(v_{s})\cdot v_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s:\partial_{s}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\,\mathcal{G}_{\rho}(v))=0,\mbox{ }\begin{array}[]{c}\rho_{0}=\mu\\
\rho_{1}=\nu\end{array}\right\}

therefore mimics the dynamical formulation of the Wasserstein distance, but changing the mobility, in the spirit of
[
17
,
49
]
.

Using this metric, we obtain with the computation of Subsection
5.1
that when
B
is symmetric and positive definite, the Transformer PDE becomes the gradient flow PDE (
26
) for the quadratic interaction functional:

{\cal F}(\mu)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

When restricted to the space of Gaussian distributions, the Transformer evolution (
2
) corresponds to the Riemannian flow ODE (
28
) for the function:

F(\alpha,\Sigma)\coloneqq\frac{e^{\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\top}((A+\Sigma^{-1})^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1}-A\Sigma A^{\top})^{-1}(A+\Sigma^{-1})-\Sigma^{-1})\alpha}}{2\lvert\det(I_{d}-A\Sigma A^{\top}\Sigma)\rvert^{1/2}}

and the Riemannian metric induced by
d_{A,V}
on the space of Gaussians via Equation (
29
).
The computation is in Appendix
E.2
.

An important open question is to know whether the energy functional
{\cal F}
is geodesically convex for the twisted Wasserstein distance.
We provide a first answer by showing that this is not the case under natural sign and commutation assumptions on the parameters.
In future work, it could be interesting to investigate the geodesic convexity of
{\cal F}
restricted to smaller classes of measures, or subject to some assumptions on the parameters.

We have the following characterization of the geodesics of
d_{A,V}
(see Appendix
E.2
).

Lemma 30
.

Let
A,V
be such that
A
is symmetric and
B\coloneqq-VA^{-1}
is symmetric positive definite.
Denote
G(y,z)\coloneqq e^{Ay\cdot z}
, and
G*\mu(y)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(y,z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
for any probability measure
\mu
.
The geodesics
\rho
of
d_{A,V}
are characterized by

\partial_{s}\rho+\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{B\nabla_{x}\psi}{2G*\rho}\rho\right)=0

for
\psi
solving

\partial_{s}\psi+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot B\nabla_{x}\psi}{G*\rho}-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\nabla_{y}\psi\cdot B\nabla_{y}\psi}{(G*\rho(y))^{2}}G(y,x)\rho_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0.

Building on this computation, we prove the following negative result (see Appendix
E.2
).

Proposition 31
.

Let
A,V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that
A
is symmetric and
B\coloneqq-VA^{-\top}
is symmetric positive definite. Assume that
A
commutes with
B
and that
V
has at least one positive eigenvalue.
Then
{\cal F}
is not geodesically convex for the distance
d_{A,V}
.

The absence of geodesic convexity rules out the usual methods for analyzing gradient flows and their long-term behavior. It often signals complex, long-term dynamics—such as metastability or a variety of equilibrium states—similar to what is observed in aggregation equations
[
5
,
4
]
. In fact, the non-geodesic convexity is proved nearby Dirac concentrations at single points, suggesting that these points may be saddle points or unstable equilibria.

Remark 32
.

Proposition
31
and Proposition
10
part 2, which can both be seen as negative results, and whose assumptions have a nonempty overlap (for instance
V=I_{d},A=-I_{d}
), capture complementary aspects of the geodesic geometry of the functional: the finite-time blow-up reflects a lack of upper control on the second variation along Wasserstein geodesics (a smoothness issue), whereas the failure of geodesic convexity corresponds to the absence of a lower bound.

6
Conclusion

This work studies the Transformer PDE, which models the evolution of data that goes through the layers of a deep Transformer model.
For several variants of self-attention, we show that the Transformer PDE is well-posed when the initial data is compactly supported, and we derive a stability estimate with respect to the initial condition. Our framework includes in particular masked self-attention.
We also consider the case of a Gaussian initial condition, which has the useful property of staying Gaussian across the dynamics for several attention variants.
Building on this remark, we show both theoretically and numerically that when the Gaussian evolution converges, the covariance of the limiting Gaussian measure is rank-deficient, which parallels the clustering phenomenon observed with discrete tokens.
Finally, we draw a connection between our framework and gradient flows, by introducing formally a distance on probability measures that equips the Transformer PDE associated with Softmax self-attention with a gradient flow structure.

Background on Entropic Optimal Transport

A.1
Dual Formulation of EOT

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two compactly supported or Gaussian probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Recall the entropic optimal transport problem

OT_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)+\KL(\pi\|\mu\otimes\nu),

(34)

introduced in Section
2
, where
c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}
.
It is well-known
[
41
]
that problem (
34
) admits the following dual formulation

OT_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in{\cal L}^{1}(\mu)\\
g\in{\cal L}^{1}(\nu)\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu+1-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{f(x)+g(y)-c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y),

and that a pair of dual potentials
(f,g)
is optimal if and only if it satisfies the following optimality conditions respectively
\nu
- and
\mu
-almost everywhere:

\begin{cases}f(x)&=-\log\int e^{g(y)-c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\\
g(y)&=-\log\int e^{f(x)-c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).\end{cases}

(35)

There always exist solutions
f
and
g
that satisfy Equation (
35
) for
every

(x,y)\in{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}

[
55
]
.
Let us focus on such pairs.
Then,
(f,g)
is unique modulo the equivalence relation

(f_{1},g_{1})\sim(f_{2},g_{2})\Leftrightarrow\exists\eta\mbox{ s.t. }f_{1}=f_{2}+\eta\mbox{ and }g_{1}=g_{2}-\eta.

Let then
(f,g)
be any couple in the equivalence class.
We call
f
and
g
Schrödinger potentials or dual potentials.
They inherit the regularity of
c
, and satisfy the following useful relation:

\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x,y)=e^{f(x)+g(y)-c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}.

We can then rewrite
OT_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)
as follows:

OT_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)+\int\log(\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty})\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y).

(36)

Assume now that
\mu
and
\nu
are compactly supported, with a support included in the ball
B_{R}
.
The dual potentials are both Lipschitz continuous.

Lemma 33
.

Assume that
\supp\mu
,
\supp\nu\subset B_{R}
.
Let
c\colon(x,y)\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}\mapsto\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}
and
(f,g)
a couple of dual potentials satisfying Equation (
35
) everywhere.
Then

\sup_{x,y\in B_{R}}f(x)+g(y)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2})^{2}R^{2}.

(37)

Moreover,
f
and
g
are both Lipschitz continuous, with a Lipschitz constant bounded by
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2})^{3/2}R
.

Proof.

Equation (
37
) derives from
[
55
]
, Lemma 4.9, noticing that
c_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2})^{2}R^{2}
for
x,y\in B_{R}
.
Then, according to
[
55
]
Lemma 4.11, the dual potentials are Lipschitz continuous with the same Lipschitz constant as
c_{\varepsilon}
.
It is then straightforward to check that
c_{\varepsilon}
is
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2})^{3/2}R
-Lipschitz continuous in both variables.

Finally, we have the following stability result.

Lemma 34
(
[
15
]
).

For any compactly supported distributions
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, consider the entropic optimal transport problems
OT_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\mu)
and
OT_{\varepsilon}(\nu,\nu)
for the cost
c(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}
, and denote
(f^{\mu},g^{\mu})
and
(f^{\nu},g^{\nu})
associated Schrödinger potentials.
Then, there exists a function
C_{\mathrm{stab}}(R)>0
depending on
R,Q,K,\varepsilon
such that for all compactly supported probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\inf_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert f^{\mu}-f^{\nu}-\eta\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert g^{\mu}-g^{\nu}+\eta\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{\mathrm{stab}}(R)W_{2}(\mu,\nu).

for

We have the following Lemma.

\displaystyle g_{n+1}=T_{\mu,Q,K}\left(f_{n}\right)

paradigm in which the model distribution is evolved by a data-dependent drifting
field, and recent work relates this field to Wasserstein gradient flows of
KDE-approximated divergences
[
14
]
.
Although drifting models are not the focus of this paper, the Gaussian-kernel
case has a direct connection with the Transformer PDE studied above: its
velocity uses the same normalized Gaussian-kernel vector field as

\ell^{2}
self-attention, so the Gaussian calculations of Section

4
can be reused directly.

For simplicity, we write the unit-bandwidth Gaussian kernel

k(x,y)\coloneqq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{2}\right).

For a probability measure
\mu\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, define the normalized
Gaussian-kernel field

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{G})}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(x,z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)}

and the associated mean-shift field

a_{\mu}(x)\coloneqq\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{G})}(x)-x=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(y-x)k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(x,z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)}.

The drifting PDE from a current distribution
\rho_{t}
toward a fixed target
distribution
\nu
reads

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{t}\left(a_{\nu}-a_{\rho_{t}}\right)\right)=0.

(46)

Since the two
-x
terms cancel, this is equivalently

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{t}\left(\Gamma_{\nu}^{(\mathrm{G})}-\Gamma_{\rho_{t}}^{(\mathrm{G})}\right)\right)=0.

(47)

Moreover, with the convention of Section
2
,

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{G})}
is exactly the
\ell^{2}
attention field with

Q=K=I_{d}/\sqrt{2}
and
V=I_{d}
.
Thus, drifting with a Gaussian kernel is governed by the same normalized
Gaussian-kernel mechanism as one of the attention variants analyzed in this
paper, with the only difference that the velocity is the target field minus the
self-field of the current distribution.

This observation makes the Gaussian case explicit.

Proposition 40
(Gaussian drifting dynamics).

Assume that

\nu={\cal N}(m_{\nu},\Sigma_{\nu})
and

\rho_{0}={\cal N}(m_{0},\Sigma_{0})
, with

m_{\nu},m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\Sigma_{\nu},\Sigma_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}^{++}
.
Then, for all times for which the solution of
(
46
) exists, it remains Gaussian:

\rho_{t}={\cal N}(m_{t},\Sigma_{t})
.
Moreover,

\dot{m}_{t}=(\Sigma_{\nu}+I_{d})^{-1}(m_{\nu}-m_{t})

(48)

and

\dot{\Sigma}_{t}=B_{t}\Sigma_{t}+\Sigma_{t}B_{t}^{\top},\qquad B_{t}\coloneqq(\Sigma_{t}+I_{d})^{-1}-(\Sigma_{\nu}+I_{d})^{-1}.

(49)

Proof.

Let
\mu={\cal N}(m,\Sigma)
.
Completing the square in the Gaussian reweighting gives

\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{G})}(x)=m+\Sigma(\Sigma+I_{d})^{-1}(x-m)=x-(\Sigma+I_{d})^{-1}(x-m).

Hence

a_{\mu}(x)=-(\Sigma+I_{d})^{-1}(x-m)
.
If
\rho_{t}={\cal N}(m_{t},\Sigma_{t})
, then the velocity in
(
46
) is affine:

a_{\nu}(x)-a_{\rho_{t}}(x)=B_{t}(x-m_{t})+(\Sigma_{\nu}+I_{d})^{-1}(m_{\nu}-m_{t}),

with
B_{t}
defined in (
49
).
This affine form closes the dynamics on Gaussian measures.
Integrating the velocity against
\rho_{t}
yields
(
48
); after subtracting this mean motion, one obtains

a_{\nu}(x)-a_{\rho_{t}}(x)-\dot{m}_{t}=B_{t}(x-m_{t}),

and differentiating the covariance gives (
49
).

In dimension
d=1
, writing
\Sigma_{t}=s_{t}
and
\Sigma_{\nu}=s_{\nu}
, the covariance
ODE becomes

\dot{s}_{t}=2s_{t}\left(\frac{1}{s_{t}+1}-\frac{1}{s_{\nu}+1}\right).

Thus
s_{\nu}
is the stable stationary variance: if
s_{t}>s_{\nu}
, then

\dot{s}_{t}<0
, while if
s_{t}<s_{\nu}
, then
\dot{s}_{t}>0
.
More generally, when
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Sigma_{\nu}
are simultaneously
diagonalizable, the dynamics decouple into these scalar equations along their
common eigenbasis.
This gives a simple interpretation of the drifting mechanism in the Gaussian
setting: the target field contracts or expands each covariance direction until
the current kernel-smoothed conditional mean matches the target one.

Let us start with the following useful result.

Lemma 41
.

Let
R>0
.
Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
supported in
B_{R}
.
Denote
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the expectation of
\mu
.
Then

\left\lVert\var\mu\right\rVert_{2}\leq R^{2},

where

\var\mu\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}(X-\alpha)(X-\alpha)^{\top}.

Proof.

Let
X
be a random variable distributed according to
\mu
.
Using the triangle inequality, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}(X-\alpha)(X-\alpha)^{\top}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left\lVert(X-\alpha)(X-\alpha)^{\top}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert X-\alpha\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert X\right\rVert^{2}-\left\lVert\alpha\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left\lVert X\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq R^{2},

as
\mu
is supported in
B_{R}
.

Notations are the same as in Section

We start with Softmax self-attention.

Lemma 42
(Estimates for Softmax self-attention).

Let
p\geq 1
and
R>0
.
Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures supported in
B_{R}
.
We have the following estimates.

(i)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{SM})}\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}R
,

(ii)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert D_{x}\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{SM})}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R^{2}
,

(iii)

\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{SM})}(x)-\Gamma_{\nu}^{(\mathrm{SM})}(x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}(1+2\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R\left\lvert x\right\rvert)e^{2\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R\left\lvert x\right\rvert}W_{p}(\mu,\nu)
.

Proof.

Equation
(i)
is straightforward. Equation
(ii)
relies on the following remark:

D_{x}\Gamma_{\mu}=V\var\left(\frac{e^{Ax\cdot y}}{\int e^{Ax\cdot z}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)A.

As the probability measure
\frac{e^{Ax\cdot y}}{\int e^{Ax\cdot z}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)
is supported in
B_{R}
, its variance is bounded by
R^{2}
, according to Lemma
41
, which proves
(ii)
.
Finally, let us derive
(iii)
, with a similar method as in the proof of Lemma 6.5 in
[
36
]
.
By adding and subtracting
\frac{\int e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)}{\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}
, we bound

\displaystyle\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}(x)-\Gamma_{\nu}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lvert\int e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)(y)\rvert}{\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}

\displaystyle\quad+\lvert\int e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\rvert\frac{\lvert\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)(y)\rvert}{\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lvert\int e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)(y)\rvert}{\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}+\lvert\Gamma_{\nu}(x)\rvert\frac{\lvert\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)(y)\rvert}{\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)}.

We bound each component separately. By the duality formula for
W_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert\int e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)(y)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{B_{R}}(y\mapsto e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy)W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert}\lVert V\rVert_{2}(1+\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert)W_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\lvert\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)(y)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{B_{R}}(y\mapsto e^{Ax\cdot y})W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert}\lVert A\rVert_{2}\lvert x\rvert W_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\int e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\geq e^{-\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert},

and the same bound holds for
\nu
. Putting everything together and using
(i)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}(x)-\Gamma_{\nu}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert}\lVert V\rVert_{2}(1+\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert)W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\quad+\lVert V\rVert_{2}Re^{2\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert}\lVert A\rVert_{2}\lvert x\rvert W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}(1+2\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R\left\lvert x\right\rvert)e^{2\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R\left\lvert x\right\rvert}W_{p}(\mu,\nu)

as
W_{1}\leq W_{p}
in
B_{R}
.

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{MH})}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left\lVert V^{(h)}\right\rVert_{2}R
,

(ii)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert D_{x}\Gamma_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{MH})}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left\lVert V^{(h)}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A^{(h)}\right\rVert_{2}R^{2}
,

(iii)

\left\lvert\Gamma^{(\mathrm{MH})}_{\mu}(x)-\Gamma^{(\mathrm{MH})}_{\nu}(x)\right\rvert\leq c(\left\lvert x\right\rvert,R)W_{p}(\mu,\nu)
,

where
c(\left\lvert x\right\rvert,R)
is a continuous function that depends on
\left\lvert x\right\rvert,R
and on the parameters
(Q^{(h)},K^{(h)},V^{(h)})_{1\leq h\leq H}
.

Finally, we derive estimates for single-head Softmax masked self-attention. Similar estimates can be obtained for multi-head Softmax masked self-attention, and
\ell^{2}
, Sinkhorn and Sigmoid masked self-attention in their single-head or multi-head version.

Lemma 48
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
R>0
. Let
\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}\in{\cal P}_{c}([0,1]\times B_{R})
, with the same position marginal
\theta
. Assume that
\theta(\{0\})>0
. Let
\Gamma^{(\mathrm{m})}
be Softmax masked self-attention, and let
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\mathrm{m})}
be defined as
\Gamma^{(\mathrm{m})}\eqqcolon(0,\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\mathrm{m})})
.
We have the following estimates.

(i)

\sup_{(\sigma,x)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}R
,

(ii)

\sup_{(\sigma,x)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert\partial_{x}\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}(R^{2}+1)
,

(iii)

\left\lvert\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\mathrm{m})}_{\bar{\mu}}(\sigma,x)-\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\mathrm{m})}_{\bar{\nu}}(\sigma,x)\right\rvert\leq\frac{c(\left\lvert x\right\rvert,R)}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)}d(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in[0,1]
.
Recall that
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Vye^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)}
. For estimate
(i)
, we bound

\displaystyle\left\lvert\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\right\rvert\leq\frac{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert Vy\right\rvert e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)}\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}R.

Estimate
(ii)
follows from:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)

\displaystyle=V\mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\tau\in[0,\sigma]}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)}{\int_{[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{\omega\in[0,\sigma]}e^{Ax\cdot z}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\omega,z)}\right)A.

Hence, with Lemma
41
,
\left\lVert\partial_{x}\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}(R^{2}+1)
, as the measure
\mathbf{1}_{\tau\in[0,\sigma]}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)
is supported in
[0,1]\times B_{R}
.
Finally, let us prove estimate
(iii)
.

\lvert\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)-\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\nu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\rvert\leq\frac{\lvert\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy\,\mathrm{d}(\bar{\mu}-\bar{\nu})(\tau,y)\rvert}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)}\\
\quad+\lvert\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu}(\tau,y)\rvert\frac{\lvert\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}(\bar{\mu}-\bar{\nu})(\tau,y)\rvert}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,y)\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\nu}(\tau,y)},

which implies

\lvert\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)-\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\nu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\rvert\leq\frac{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy\,\mathrm{d}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau}-\bar{\nu}^{\tau})(y)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}^{\tau}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)}\\
+\lvert\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\nu}}(\sigma,x)\rvert\frac{\lvert\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau}-\bar{\nu}^{\tau})(y)\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)\rvert}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}^{\tau}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)}

with the triangle inequality and the disintegration theorem
\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(\tau,x)=\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}^{\tau}(x)
.
We bound each component separately. By the duality formula for
W_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy\,\mathrm{d}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau}-\bar{\nu}^{\tau})(y)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{B_{R}}(y\mapsto e^{Ax\cdot y}Vy)W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau})

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert}\lVert V\rVert_{2}(1+\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert)W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau}).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau}-\bar{\nu}^{\tau})(y)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{B_{R}}(y\mapsto e^{Ax\cdot y})W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau})

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert}\lVert A\rVert_{2}\lvert x\rvert W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau}).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{Ax\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\tau}(y)\geq e^{-\lVert A\rVert_{2}R\lvert x\rvert}.

Putting everything together and using
(i)
, we obtain

\lvert\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\mu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)-\tilde{\Gamma}_{\bar{\nu}}^{(\mathrm{m})}(\sigma,x)\rvert\leq e^{2\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R\left\lvert x\right\rvert}(1+\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R\left\lvert x\right\rvert)\frac{\int_{0}^{\sigma}W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau})\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)}\\
+\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}Re^{2\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}R\left\lvert x\right\rvert}\frac{\int_{0}^{\sigma}W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau})\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)}{\int_{0}^{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)}.

We conclude noticing that
\int_{0}^{\sigma}W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau})\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)\leq\int_{0}^{1}W_{1}(\bar{\mu}^{\tau},\bar{\nu}^{\tau})\,\mathrm{d}\theta(\tau)=d(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})
.

Lemma 49
.

Let
u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
v\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
b\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be integrable functions such that, for all
t\in[0,T]
, it holds

u(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}L(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Then, we have the following upper bound on
u
:

u(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}L(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

Denote
v(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}L(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
.
Then

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=L(t)u(t)+b(t)

\displaystyle\leq L(t)v(t)+b(t),

so

v^{\prime}(t)-L(t)v(t)\leq b(t).

Multiplying both sides by
e^{-\int_{0}^{t}L(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}
and integrating between 0 and
t
gives

e^{-\int_{0}^{t}L(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}v(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\int_{0}^{s}L(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

We conclude noticing that
v(t)\geq u(t)
and multiplying both sides by
e^{\int_{0}^{t}L(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}
.

Lemma 50
.

Let
\mu_{0}\in{\cal P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an initial condition, and assume that the associated problem (

For any

By taking a supremum over

Lemma 53

X

C.3
Stability estimates

As for the stability estimate, let
t\geq 0
.
We have

\displaystyle W_{p}(\mu(t),\nu(t))

\displaystyle=W_{p}(\phi_{t}(\mu)_{\sharp}\mu_{0},\phi_{t}(\nu)_{\sharp}\nu_{0})

\displaystyle\leq W_{p}(\phi_{t}(\mu)_{\sharp}\mu_{0},\phi_{t}(\mu)_{\sharp}\nu_{0})

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+W_{p}(\phi_{t}(\mu)_{\sharp}\nu_{0},\phi_{t}(\nu)_{\sharp}\nu_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\lip(x\mapsto\phi_{t}(\mu)(x))W_{p}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})+\left\lVert\phi_{t}(\mu)-\phi_{t}(\nu)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\supp\nu_{0})}.

Let us bound the Lipschitz constant
\lip(x\mapsto\phi_{t}(\mu)(x))
.
For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\phi_{t}(\mu)(x)-\phi_{t}(\mu)(y)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert+\int_{0}^{t}\left\lvert\Gamma_{\mu}(s,\phi_{s}(\mu)(x))-\Gamma_{\mu}(s,\phi_{s}(\mu)(y))\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert+C_{2}R(T)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lvert\phi_{s}(\mu)(x)-\phi_{s}(\mu)(y)\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s

by Lemma
42
, with
C_{2}=\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}
.
Then, Grönwall’s inequality entails that

\left\lvert\phi_{t}(\mu)(x)-\phi_{t}(\mu)(y)\right\rvert\leq e^{C_{2}R(T)^{2}t}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert.

Plugging this into our previous bound for
W_{p}(\mu(t),\nu(t))
, together with Equation (
50
), we get:

W_{p}(\mu(t),\nu(t))\leq e^{C_{2}R(T)^{2}t}W_{p}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})+C_{1}(R(T))\int_{0}^{t}e^{C_{2}R(T)^{2}(t-s)}W_{p}(\mu(s),\nu(s))\,\mathrm{d}s.

We can then apply Grönwall’s inequality to
t\mapsto e^{-C_{2}R(T)^{2}t}W_{p}(\mu(t),\nu(t))
to obtain

W_{p}(\mu(t),\nu(t))\leq e^{\left(C_{1}(R(T))+C_{2}R(T)^{2}\right)t}W_{p}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}),

which concludes the proof, recalling that
R(T)=e^{\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}T}R_{0}
.

Proof of Lemma

.

Consider the map

\varphi(M)=\left(M^{-1}+2K^{\top}K\right)^{-1}M=(I_{d}+2K^{\top}KM)^{-1}M^{2},

defined on the set
\{M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:M\mbox{ symmetric s.t. }M\succeq 0\mbox{ and }MK^{\top}K=K^{\top}KM\}
of nonnegative matrices that commute with

, which is a Banach space when equipped with Frobenius norm.
This map is locally Lipschitz continuous, so Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem gives us the local existence and uniqueness of the considered Cauchy problem.

To recover global existence (and uniqueness), let us show that
\left\lVert\varphi(M)\right\rVert_{F}
grows at most linearly with
\left\lVert M\right\rVert_{F}
.
Using that the squared Frobenius norm of a matrix is equal to the sum of its squared eigenvalues, we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(M^{-1}+2K^{\top}K\right)^{-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}(M^{-1}+2K^{\top}K)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\left(\lambda_{i}(M^{-1})+\lambda_{\sigma(i)}(2K^{\top}K)\right)^{2}}

for some permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
, where
\lambda_{i}
denotes the
i
-th eigenvalue, as the matrices
M^{-1}
and
2K^{\top}K
commute.
Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(M^{-1}+2K^{\top}K\right)^{-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\left(\lambda_{i}(M)^{-1}+\lambda_{\sigma(i)}(2K^{\top}K)\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{i}(M)^{2}}{\left(1+\lambda_{\sigma(i)}(2K^{\top}K)\lambda_{i}(M)\right)^{2}}.

Now, the function

x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\frac{x^{2}}{(1+\lambda_{\sigma(i)}(2K^{\top}K)x)^{2}}

is bounded on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, so that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left\lVert\left(M^{-1}+2K^{\top}K\right)^{-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq C^{2}

for all nonnegative symmetric matrices
M
that commute with
K^{\top}K
.
Finally, as the Frobenius norm is sub-multiplicative:

\left\lVert\varphi(M)\right\rVert_{F}\leq\left\lVert\left(M^{-1}+2K^{\top}K\right)^{-1}\right\rVert_{F}\left\lVert M\right\rVert_{F}\leq C\left\lVert M\right\rVert_{F},

E.1
The Bures-Wasserstein Gradient Flow Induced by Sinkformers

We have seen in Section
5.2
that the Sinkformer PDE initialized with a Gaussian probability measure induces a Bures-Wasserstein gradient flow in the space of Gaussian measures, associated with the energy functional

F_{\varepsilon}(\alpha,\Sigma)={\cal F}_{\varepsilon}({\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma))

where
{\cal F}
is defined in Equation (
30
).
Let us compute a more explicit expression for
F_{\varepsilon}
.
First, for any compactly supported or Gaussian
\mu\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

{\cal F}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\int\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}\log\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}}{\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{0}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}(\mu\otimes\mu)+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\int(\left\lvert Qx\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert Kx\right\rvert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

and, according to Equation (
36
) we have

OT_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\mu)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+\int\log(\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty})\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

Therefore, it holds, recalling that
\kappa_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{0}(x,y)=e^{-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left\lvert Qx-Ky\right\rvert^{2}}
:

{\cal F}_{\varepsilon}(\mu)=-\frac{1}{2}OT_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\mu)+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\int(\left\lvert Qx\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert Kx\right\rvert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Now, by definition of the entropy-regularized Bures distance (
32
) we have

OT_{\varepsilon}({\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma),{\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma))=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\mathfrak{B}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\Sigma,\Sigma),

so that

F_{\varepsilon}(\alpha,\Sigma)=-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\mathfrak{B}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\Sigma,\Sigma)+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}\int(\left\lvert Qx\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert Kx\right\rvert^{2})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Finally, writing
\left\lvert Qx\right\rvert^{2}=\tr(Qxx^{\top}Q^{\top})
we compute

\int\left\lvert Qx\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma)(x)=\tr(Q\int xx^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}{\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma)(x)Q^{\top})=\tr(Q\Sigma Q^{\top})+\alpha^{\top}Q^{\top}Q\alpha.

The Transformer PDE as a
d_{A,V}
Gradient Flow

Energy Functional On Gaussians

We have seen in Section
5.4
that the Transformer PDE associated with Softmax self-attention is a gradient flow for the twisted distance
d_{A,V}
, the energy functional being

{\cal F}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\int e^{Qx\cdot Ky}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

Let us compute the expression of
F(\alpha,\Sigma)\coloneqq{\cal F}({\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma))
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma
a
d\times d
covariance matrix.
First, we have

x^{\top}A^{\top}y-\frac{1}{2}(x-\alpha)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\alpha)=-\frac{1}{2}(x-\alpha-\Sigma A^{\top}y)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\alpha-\Sigma A^{\top}y)\\
+\alpha^{\top}A^{\top}y+\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}A\Sigma A^{\top}y.

This gives

\displaystyle\int e^{x^{\top}A^{\top}y}\,\mathrm{d}{\cal N}(\alpha,\Sigma)(x)

\displaystyle=\int e^{x^{\top}A^{\top}y-\frac{1}{2}(x-\alpha)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\alpha)}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\left\lvert\det\Sigma\right\rvert^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha^{\top}A^{\top}y+\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}A\Sigma A^{\top}y}.

In view of computing the integral of this term in
y
, we write

-\frac{1}{2}(y-\alpha)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(y-\alpha)+\alpha^{\top}A^{\top}y+\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}A\Sigma A^{\top}y=\\
-\frac{1}{2}(y-(\Sigma^{-1}-A\Sigma A^{\top})^{-1}(A+\Sigma^{-1})\alpha)^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1}-A\Sigma A^{\top})(y-(\Sigma^{-1}-A\Sigma A^{\top})^{-1}(A+\Sigma^{-1})\alpha)\\
+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\top}(A^{\top}+\Sigma^{-1})(\Sigma^{-1}-A\Sigma A^{\top})^{-1}(A+\Sigma^{-1})\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\alpha.

A similar computation as for the integral in

leads to

\displaystyle F(\alpha,\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\top}((A+\Sigma^{-1})^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1}-A\Sigma A^{\top})^{-1}(A+\Sigma^{-1})-\Sigma^{-1})\alpha}}{2\lvert\det(\Sigma^{-1}-A\Sigma A^{\top})\rvert^{1/2}\left\lvert\det\Sigma\right\rvert^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\top}((A+\Sigma^{-1})^{\top}(\Sigma^{-1}-A\Sigma A^{\top})^{-1}(A+\Sigma^{-1})-\Sigma^{-1})\alpha}}{2\lvert\det(I_{d}-A\Sigma A^{\top}\Sigma)\rvert^{1/2}}.

Geodesics of
d_{A,V}

We look for a characterization of geodesics for
d_{A,V}
.
We reformulate it via a Lagrange multiplier
\psi(t,x)
:

d_{A,V}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{(\rho,v)}\sup_{\psi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{v\cdot Bv}{G*\rho}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}s\\
-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\partial_{s}\psi(s,x)+\frac{Bv(s,x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi(s,x)}{G*\rho(x)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}s\\
+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(1,x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(0,x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

where
(\rho,u)\in{\cal C}([0,1],{\cal P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\times{\cal C}([0,1],{\cal C}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}))
in the infimum is now unconstrained.
The optimality condition on
v
is

v=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{x}\psi.

Incorporating this in the equation, the problem becomes

d_{A,V}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{\rho}\sup_{\psi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}-\left(\partial_{s}\psi(s,x)+\frac{1}{4}\frac{B\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi}{G*\rho}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}s\\
+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(1,x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(0,x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Then, the optimality in
\rho
gives

\partial_{s}\psi(s,x)+\frac{1}{4}\frac{B\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi}{G*\rho(x)}=\int\frac{1}{4}\frac{B\nabla_{y}\psi\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi}{(G*\rho(y))^{2}}G(y,x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(y).

We thus obtain the following equations characterizing geodesics for
d_{A,V}
:

\begin{cases}\partial_{s}\rho+\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{B\nabla_{x}\psi}{2G*\rho}\rho\right)=0\\
\partial_{s}\psi+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot B\nabla_{x}\psi}{G*\rho}-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\nabla_{y}\psi\cdot B\nabla_{y}\psi}{(G*\rho(y))^{2}}G(y,x)\rho(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0.\end{cases}

Proof of Proposition
31
.

If
\rho
is a geodesic for
d_{A,V}
, associated with the test function
\psi
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\cal F}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\int\nabla_{x}G(x,y)\cdot(B\nabla_{x}\psi)\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi\,\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

In the whole proof, we write
\,\mathrm{d}\rho(y)=\rho(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
for simplicity—this is only a formal computation.
In order to compute
\partial^{2}_{t}{\cal F}(\rho)
, let us compute

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\Gamma_{\rho}(x))_{i}

\displaystyle=\int(Vy)_{i}G(x,y)\left(\frac{\partial_{t}\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)}-\frac{\rho(y)}{(G*\rho(x))^{2}}\int G(x,z)\partial_{t}\rho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\right)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\nabla_{y}((Vy)_{i}\cdot G(x,y))\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle~~~~-\frac{1}{2}\int(Vy)_{i}G(x,y)\frac{\rho(y)}{(G*\rho(x))^{2}}\int\nabla_{z}G(x,z)\cdot(B\nabla_{z}\psi)\frac{\rho(z)}{G*\rho(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(L_{i}(V)G(x,y)+(Vy)_{i}G(x,y)Ax\right)\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(y)G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle~~~~-\frac{1}{2}\int(Vy)_{i}G(x,y)\frac{\rho(y)}{(G*\rho(x))^{2}}\int G(x,z)(Ax)\cdot(B\nabla_{z}\psi)\frac{\rho(z)}{G*\rho(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
L_{i}(V)
is the
i
-th row of
V
, seen as a column vector.
Then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Gamma_{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,y)\left(VB\nabla_{y}\psi+(Ax)\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)Vy\right)\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(y)G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle~~~~-\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,z)(Ax)\cdot(B\nabla_{z}\psi)\frac{\rho(z)}{G*\rho(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z\int VyG(x,y)\frac{\rho(y)}{(G*\rho(x))^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,y)\left(VB\nabla_{y}\psi-x^{\top}V^{\top}\nabla_{y}\psi Vy\right)\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(y)G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle~~~~+\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,z)x^{\top}V^{\top}\nabla_{z}\psi\frac{\rho(z)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z\Gamma_{\rho}(x)

as
AB=-V^{\top}
.
Finally,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Gamma_{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,y)VB\nabla_{y}\psi\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(y)G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle~~~~+\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,y)(Vx)\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi(\Gamma_{\rho}(x)-Vy)\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,y)\left(VB+(\Gamma_{\rho}(x)-Vy)(Vx)^{\top}\right)\nabla_{y}\psi\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y.

We use this formula to compute
\partial_{t}^{2}{\cal F}(\rho)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}{\cal F}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int(\nabla_{x}\partial_{t}\psi)\cdot\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\mathrm{(1)}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\int(\nabla_{x}\psi)\cdot(\Gamma_{\rho}(x))\partial_{t}\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\mathrm{(2)}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\int(\nabla_{x}\psi)\cdot(\partial_{t}\Gamma_{\rho})\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.\quad\mathrm{(3)}

Let us compute (1). We have, using symmetry of
B
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}(\partial_{t}\psi)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot(B\nabla_{x}\psi)}{G*\rho(x)}+\frac{1}{4}\int\frac{\nabla_{y}\psi\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)}{(G*\rho(y))^{2}}G(x,y)\rho(y)\,\,\mathrm{d}y\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}B^{-1}\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\frac{\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot(B\nabla_{x}\psi)}{G*\rho(x)}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{D_{x}^{2}\psi B\nabla_{x}\psi}{G*\rho(x)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\int\frac{\nabla_{y}\psi\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)}{(G*\rho(y))^{2}}G(x,y)Ay\rho(y)\,\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\frac{(B^{-1}\Gamma_{\rho}(x)(\nabla_{x}\psi)^{\top}+2D_{x}^{2}\psi)B\nabla_{x}\psi}{G*\rho(x)}\quad\mathrm{(a)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\int\nabla_{y}\psi\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)Ay\frac{G(x,y)}{(G*\rho(y))^{2}}\rho(y)\,\,\mathrm{d}y.\quad\mathrm{(b)}

We inject separately each term inside the integral that defines (1):

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{(a)}\cdot\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\int(B\nabla_{x}\psi)^{\top}\left[B^{-1}\Gamma_{\rho}(x)(\nabla_{x}\psi)^{\top}\!+2D_{x}^{2}\psi\right]^{\top}\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\frac{\rho(x)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\int\left[(B\nabla_{x}\psi)\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi\ \Gamma_{\rho}(x)\cdot(B^{-1}\Gamma_{\rho}(x))+2(\nabla_{x}\psi)^{\top}B(D_{x}^{2}\psi)\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\right]\frac{\rho(x)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

and

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{(b)}

\displaystyle\cdot\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8}\int\left(\int\nabla_{y}\psi\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)Ay\frac{G(x,y)}{(G*\rho(y))^{2}}\rho(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\cdot\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Finally,

\displaystyle\mathrm{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int(\nabla_{x}\partial_{t}\psi)\cdot\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\int\left[\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\cdot(B^{-1}\Gamma_{\rho}(x))\nabla_{x}\psi+2D_{x}^{2}\psi\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\right]\cdot(B\nabla_{x}\psi)\frac{\rho(x)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{8}\iint\nabla_{y}\psi\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)(Vy)\cdot(B^{-1}\Gamma_{\rho}(x))\frac{G(x,y)}{(G*\rho(y))^{2}}\rho(x)\rho(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

Let us now compute (2). We have, using that
A=-B^{-1}V=-V^{\top}B^{-1}
by symmetry of
B
:

\displaystyle D_{x}\Gamma_{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int Vyy^{\top}A\,G(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)}-\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\frac{\int y^{\top}A\,G(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\int Vyy^{\top}V^{\top}G(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)}B^{-1}+\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\Gamma_{\rho}(x)^{\top}B^{-1}.

Then

\displaystyle(2)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int(\nabla_{x}\psi)\cdot(\Gamma_{\rho}(x))\partial_{t}\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\int\nabla_{x}(\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot\Gamma_{\rho}(x))\cdot(B\nabla_{x}\psi)\frac{\rho(x)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\int\Bigg(D_{x}^{2}\psi\Gamma_{\rho}(x)+B^{-1}\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\Gamma_{\rho}(x)^{\top}\nabla_{x}\psi

\displaystyle\quad\quad-B^{-1}\frac{\int Vyy^{\top}V^{\top}G(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)}\nabla_{x}\psi\Bigg)\cdot(B\nabla_{x}\psi)\frac{\rho(x)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\int(D_{x}^{2}\psi\Gamma_{\rho}(x))\cdot(B\nabla_{x}\psi)\frac{\rho(x)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{4}\int(\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot\Gamma_{\rho}(x))^{2}\,\frac{\rho(x)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\iint(\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot Vy)^{2}\,G(x,y)\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{(G*\rho(x))^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

Finally, we compute (3). We have, using symmetry of
A
, so that
A^{\top}B=-V^{\top}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Gamma_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,y)\left[VB\nabla_{y}\psi+(Ax)\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)Vy\right]\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(y)G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,z)(Ax)\cdot(B\nabla_{z}\psi)\frac{\rho(z)}{G*\rho(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z\int VyG(x,y)\frac{\rho(y)}{(G*\rho(x))^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}G(x,y)\left[VB\nabla_{y}\psi-x^{\top}V^{\top}\nabla_{y}\psi Vy\right]\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,z)x^{\top}V^{\top}\nabla_{z}\psi\frac{\rho(z)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z\ \Gamma_{\rho}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,y)VB\nabla_{y}\psi\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,y)(Vx)\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi(\Gamma_{\rho}(x)-Vy)\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int G(x,y)\left[VB+(\Gamma_{\rho}(x)-Vy)(Vx)^{\top}\right]\nabla_{y}\psi\frac{\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{(3)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot\partial_{t}\Gamma_{\rho}\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\int\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot\int G(x,y)[VB+(\Gamma_{\rho}(x)-Vy)(Vx)^{\top}]\nabla_{y}\psi\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\iint\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot(VB\nabla_{y}\psi)G(x,y)\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{4}\iint(\nabla_{x}\psi)\cdot(\Gamma_{\rho}(x)-Vy)(Vx)\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

Putting everything together leads to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}{\cal F}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\int\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot(B\nabla_{x}\psi)\Gamma_{\rho}(x)\cdot(B^{-1}\Gamma_{\rho}(x))\frac{\rho(x)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle~~~+\frac{1}{8}\iint\nabla_{y}\psi\cdot(B\nabla_{y}\psi)(Vy)\cdot(B^{-1}\Gamma_{\rho}(x))G(x,y)\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{(G*\rho(y))^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle~~~-\frac{1}{4}\int(\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot\Gamma_{\rho}(x))^{2}\frac{\rho(x)}{G*\rho(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle~~~+\frac{1}{4}\iint(\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot Vy)^{2}G(x,y)\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{(G*\rho(x))^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle~~~-\frac{1}{4}\iint\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot(VB\nabla_{y}\psi)G(x,y)\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle~~~-\frac{1}{4}\iint\nabla_{x}\psi\cdot(\Gamma_{\rho}(x)-Vy)(Vx)\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{G*\rho(x)G*\rho(y)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

Let us now compute
\partial_{t}^{2}{\cal F}(\rho)
when
\rho=\delta_{z}
is the Dirac measure at
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We obtain, as
\Gamma_{\delta_{z}}(x)=Vz
, that

\partial_{t}^{2}{\cal F}(\delta_{z})=\frac{1}{4}e^{-Az\cdot z}(\nabla_{z}\psi)^{\top}\left((z^{\top}V^{\top}B^{-1}Vz)I_{d}-V\right)B\nabla_{z}\psi.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
31
,
V=-BA
is symmetric and commutes with
B
. Hence

\partial_{t}^{2}{\cal F}(\delta_{z})=\frac{1}{4}e^{-Az\cdot z}(\nabla_{z}\psi)^{\top}B^{1/2}\left((z^{\top}V^{\top}B^{-1}Vz)I_{d}-V\right)B^{1/2}\nabla_{z}\psi.

As
V
has a positive eigenvalue, take
z=0
and choose
\nabla_{z}\psi
so that
B^{1/2}\nabla_{z}\psi
belongs to a positive eigenspace of
V
.
Then
\partial_{t}^{2}{\cal F}(\delta_{0})<0
.
Now, consider any (for example compactly supported) measure
\mu
, and denote
\rho
the geodesic between
\delta_{0}
and
\mu
for
d_{A,V}
.
As
\partial_{t=0}^{2}{\cal F}(\delta_{0})<0
, the functional
{\cal F}
is not convex along
\rho
, which proves the claim.
