Fine-tuned feelings, focused attention: how emotional granularity shapes our attention to national symbols
Authors: Lu Fan, Bingping Zhou, Xinyi Zhu, Yingying Yan, Jiajia Jin, Yidan Lu, Shuhui Xu, Yu Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmotional granularity—the ability to make fine-grained distinctions between emotional states—has been linked to adaptive emotion regulation and attentional control. However, its influence on attentional bias toward culturally salient stimuli, such as national symbols, remains underexplored.MethodsUsing a dot-probe paradigm, this study investigated how positive emotional granularity (PEG) modulates attentional bias toward Chinese versus Non-Chinese images among 107 Chinese university students. Reaction times were analyzed across cue congruency (congruent vs. incongruent) and stimulus presentation durations (200 ms, 500 ms, and 1000 ms). Both categorical (high vs. low PEG) and continuous-variable analyses were performed to examine convergent effects.ResultsAcross the overall sample, participants exhibited attentional vigilance toward China-related images at 200 ms and 1000 ms. Group-based analyses revealed distinct temporal patterns: individuals with high PEG showed early vigilance at 200 ms, attentional avoidance at 500 ms, and a return to baseline at 1000 ms, whereas low-PEG individuals displayed delayed vigilance emerging only at 1000 ms. Continuous-variable analyses yielded convergent trends, with higher PEG predicting reduced vigilance at 200 ms and stronger attentional engagement at 1000 ms.ConclusionsPositive emotional granularity dynamically modulates attention to patriotic cues. High PEG supports flexible, stage-specific attentional allocation, while low PEG reflects slower, delayed engagement. These findings extend the scope of attentional bias research beyond negative stimuli to culturally meaningful imagery and highlight emotional granularity as a key trait underlying context-sensitive attentional control.

Introduction

Patriotic emotions are multifaceted affective experiences that are deeply embedded within cultural and national identity frameworks. These emotions are often triggered by exposure to national symbols and imagery—such as flags, landmarks, or culturally significant imagery—which serve as highly salient cognitive-affective cues imbued with symbolic meanings [21, 22]. Understanding how such emotionally laden national stimuli capture attention is of considerable significance in contemporary social psychology, as these processes shape not only identity development but also social cohesion and civic engagement [14, 23].

Despite their social importance, empirical research on attentional engagement with patriotic stimuli remains scarce. The majority of studies on attentional bias have focused on general emotional stimuli such as threatening faces [7, 18], aversive words [35], or negative body language [40]. Much less is known about how individuals allocate attention toward stimuli tied directly to national identity, even though such stimuli evoke particularly strong and contextually relevant emotional responses. This gap is important, as it limits our understanding of how patriotic affect is processed in real time and how it may contribute to the construction of social identity.

Emotional granularity (EG), also termed emotion differentiation, refers to the ability to represent one’s affective experiences with precision and specificity [2, 9, 30]. High-EG individuals can distinguish fine-grained differences between similar emotional states, whereas low-EG individuals tend to classify feelings in broad, undifferentiated terms such as simply “good” or “bad.”

Accumulating evidence suggests that emotion granularity (EG) has broad consequences for emotion regulation, psychological resilience, and attentional control. Individuals high in EG show greater flexibility in selecting context-appropriate regulation strategies [8], more adaptive responses to anger and frustration, and better recovery from stress [32]. At the level of attentional selection, high-EG individuals engage in information-seeking visual exploration rather than avoidance when confronted with negative stimuli [26], a pattern consistent with more precise early perceptual discrimination of affective cues. Cognitively, high EG is associated with greater working-memory capacity [13], which supplies the resources needed to maintain and manipulate affective information during appraisal, converging evidence links higher working memory to more flexible reappraisal [36], whereas lower working memory in adolescence constrains regulation efficiency [17]. Neuro physiologically, high EG is linked to enhanced neural sensitivity to emotional input, including larger N2 and late positive potential (LPP) amplitudes in ERP tasks [13]. Together, these findings indicate that EG scaffolds both early-stage attention/perception and later-stage regulatory control in emotion–attention interactions—by promoting information-directed exploration and providing the cognitive resources required for effective, goal-consistent regulation.

While most research has examined EG for broadly positive or negative affect, its relevance to patriotic emotions—identity- and value-laden affects such as collective pride, gratitude, or elevation elicited by national symbols—remains underexplored. This is a critical gap because patriotic cues require value-based appraisal and identity linking, precisely the processes EG supports. Building on the mechanisms reviewed above, high positive emotional granularity (PEG) should enable individuals to differentiate and label nuanced positive states (e.g., pride vs. gratitude) elicited by patriotic symbols, yielding clearer appraisals, stronger approach motivation, and thus greater attentional engagement with those cues. Conversely, higher negative EG may allow more nuanced parsing of ambivalent reactions (e.g., pride mixed with concern), promoting information-seeking attention rather than avoidance. Consequently, EG provides a principled account of how patriotic affect is formed and regulated. We therefore predict that PEG will be associated with (i) enhanced selective attention to patriotic symbols (e.g., shorter orienting latencies, longer dwell time; larger late positive potential), and (ii) finer discrimination among patriotic positive states; these effects should be stronger when national identity is central and the cues are perceived as authentic and value-congruent.

Attentional bias refers to the systematic tendency to preferentially allocate attentional resources to certain types of stimuli [4, 27]. Classic models distinguish between three bias processes: accelerated orienting, avoidance, and difficulties in disengagement [10]. Historically, research has emphasized biases toward negative or threatening stimuli [19, 33], but recent work has begun to highlight the importance of biases toward positive emotional cues [25, 34]. However, little is known about how attentional biases manifest in response to emotionally salient, identity-relevant stimuli such as national symbols.

Crucially, attentional biases are temporally dynamic. The time window in which a stimulus is presented can fundamentally alter attentional responses. Short exposure durations (e.g., ~ 200 ms) typically capture rapid, automatic vigilance, whereas intermediate durations (e.g., ~ 500 ms) are more likely to reveal avoidance or attentional shifting, and longer durations (e.g., ~ 1000 ms) allow for more controlled, top-down regulatory processes [4, 6]. The dot-probe paradigm is widely used to capture these temporal dynamics, offering a direct behavioral measure of attentional bias by comparing reaction times to probes replacing congruent versus incongruent stimuli [24].

Building on this literature, the present study deliberately selected three presentation times—200 ms, 500 ms, and 1000 ms—to map early orienting, intermediate avoidance, and late-stage controlled attention, respectively. This design aligns with dual-stage models of emotional attention [19, 33] and ensures that the temporal dynamics of attentional bias toward patriotic stimuli can be systematically assessed.

Although EG has been extensively studied in relation to emotion regulation and psychopathology, its role in attentional processing of nationally relevant stimuli remains underexplored. This research therefore seeks to address two interrelated questions: (1) How do individuals allocate attentional resources toward patriotic stimuli across different temporal stages? (2) How does positive EG shape these attentional processes? Grounded in existing theories, we advance the following hypotheses: H1: Participants will demonstrate attentional vigilance toward patriotic (China-related) images, reflected in faster responses under congruent versus incongruent conditions, particularly at early exposure durations (200 ms). H2: Individuals with high PEG will exhibit attentional bias at earlier time points (200 ms), consistent with their superior capacity for precise emotional identification and regulation. H3: At longer durations (500 ms, 1000 ms), attentional responses will reflect avoidance and/or controlled regulation processes, with high-PEG individuals showing more flexible adaptation compared to low-PEG individuals.

These hypotheses are theoretically motivated by dual-stage models of attention and the demonstrated role of EG in emotion regulation, thereby clarifying the rationale for both the design and the expected findings.

By integrating the constructs of emotional granularity, attentional bias, and patriotic emotion, this study contributes to both theoretical and applied domains. Theoretically, it extends EG research into the domain of national identity, offering a novel framework for understanding how fine-grained emotional processing shapes attentional engagement with culturally significant stimuli. Methodologically, it employs a temporally sensitive dot-probe paradigm to disentangle different stages of attentional bias, thereby providing a more comprehensive account of how patriotic emotions are processed. Practically, the findings offer potential implications for civic education and interventions aimed at fostering adaptive emotional engagement with national symbols among youth populations.

Methods

Participants

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software, which indicated that a total of 36 participants (18 in each group) was required for the three-factor mixed design, with an effect size of f = 0.25, power = 0.95, and α = 0.01. A total of 132 college students aged 17 to 21 were randomly selected as subjects from a university in Zhejiang province. Of these, 10 participants were excluded due to lack of seriousness in their responses or low accuracy, and 15 were excluded for having intraclass correlation (ICC) values less than 0. The final valid sample consisted of 107 participants (75 females), selected based on their responses to the Emotion Differentiation Task (EDK). Participants were sorted by positive emotion granularity scores, with the top 27% and bottom 27% selected to form the high and low positive emotion granularity groups, respectively, resulting in 28 participants in each group (21 females in the low granularity group and 16 females in the high granularity group). An independent samples t-test was conducted to analyze the data. Chi-square test indicated that there was no significant difference between the two groups regarding age and gender. However, the difference in the level of positive emotional granularity was significant (p < 0.001). All participants had normal vision (with correction if necessary), were right-handed, had no history of mental illness or color vision disorders, and lacked prior experimental experience.

Materials

The experimental materials for the dot-probe task comprised 110 images collected from online sources, divided equally into Chinese and Non-Chinese categories (e.g., "Chinese Flag" vs. "American Flag"), with 55 images in each group. All images were standardized using Adobe Photoshop 2022 to a resolution of 1040 × 650 pixels, ensuring congruency in brightness and contrast. To validate the stimuli, 30 college students were randomly recruited to rate each image on a 7-point Likert scale across four dimensions: “Belonging to China,” “Sense of Patriotism,” “Typicality,” and “Familiarity.” In this context, "typicality" refers to how representative an image is of its respective category, "familiarity" reflects how recognizable the image is to participants, and "patriotism" captures the degree to which the image evokes patriotic emotion.

Based on these evaluations, 30 images from each category were selected for use in the formal experiment, yielding a final set of 60 stimuli. Statistical analyses demonstrated the effectiveness of the selected images in differentiating between Chinese and Non-Chinese content. Chinese images were rated significantly higher than Non-Chinese images on the dimensions of “Belonging to China” [t(29) = 70.06, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 11.83] and “Sense of Patriotism” [t(29) = 38.82, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 6.56]. No significant differences were observed between the two categories on the dimensions of “Typicality” [t(29) = 1.70, p = 0.100] or “Familiarity” [t(29) = 2.04, p = 0.051], indicating that the two sets of images were well-matched in terms of general representativeness and recognizability. Given that emotional granularity reflects the extent to which individuals differentiate between emotional experiences, a precise one-to-one mapping between emotion words and images was not required, rather, a general thematic match was considered sufficient. The ten emotion terms included: pride, pleasure, excitement, contentment, relaxation, happiness, awe, gratitude, hope, and challenge. In the experiment, participants rated how well ten positive-emotion words matched each image on a 7-point scale, following the procedure established in Lian et al. [15].

Participants were asked to rate, on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = "not at all", 7 = "very strongly"), the extent to which each emotion was elicited by each image. The level of positive emotional granularity was operationalized by computing the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) across the ten emotion ratings for each participant. As higher ICC values reflect greater covariation across emotion terms—indicative of lower emotional differentiation—emotional granularity scores were calculated as 1 minus the Fisher Z-transformed ICC value. Thus, higher scores represent greater positive emotional granularity, reflecting more differentiated and nuanced emotional experiences in response to positive stimuli.

Procedure

A three-factor mixed experimental design was employed: 2 (Positive Emotional Granularity: High vs. Low) × 2 (Cue Congruency: Congruent vs. Incongruent) × 3 (Stimulus Presentation Time: 200 ms, 500 ms, 1000 ms). Positive emotional granularity was treated as a between-subjects factor, while cue congruency and stimulus presentation time were within-subjects factors. The probability of each cue congruency condition and stimulus duration was equally distributed. Emotionally relevant image pairs were presented in a randomized order, with each pair appearing three times (excluding practice trials). The dependent variables were response accuracy (%) and reaction time (ms) to the probe stimuli.

Participants first completed a positive emotional granularity assessment, followed by a dot-probe task examining attentional bias toward country-related imagery. All tasks were programmed and administered using E-Prime 2.0.

As illustrated in Fig. 1(A), the positive emotional granularity task involved presenting a positive emotion image at the center of the screen, with an emotion word displayed beneath it. Participants rated the extent to which the emotion word reflected their emotional experience upon viewing the image on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “not at all”, 7 = “very strongly”). The task consisted of 10 blocks with 10 trials each, totaling 100 trials. Each block featured a single image paired with 10 randomly ordered emotion words. Block order was randomized and counterbalanced across participants.

The dot-probe task, depicted in Fig. 1(B), comprised three blocks of 30 trials (totaling 90 trials). In each block, stimuli with presentation durations of 200 ms, 500 ms, or 1000 ms were randomly interleaved. A one-minute rest period was provided between blocks. During each trial, participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible to the location of a probe using designated keys (“F” for left, “J” for right). Trials in which the probe and the China-related image appeared on the same side were classified as congruent; when presented on opposite sides, the trial was considered incongruent.

Statistical analysis

Preprocessing

Response errors and invalid trials—defined as reaction times (RTs) < 200 ms or > ± 3 SD from a participant’s own mean—were excluded. After cleaning, 97.82% of trials remained; mean accuracy on the retained trials was 99.02%. All analyses were conducted in SPSS 25.0 and JASP 0.17.1.

Reliability and validity checks

To index the robustness of the emotion granularity (EG) measure, we report the participant-level intraclass correlation (ICC) derived from emotion ratings (M = 0.154, SD = 0.123, range = 0.000–0.775). Following prior work, EG scores were computed as 1 − Fisher-z(ICC), with higher scores indicating finer differentiation [2]. Because Cronbach’s α is a special case of ICC under common assumptions, and our design targets within-person differentiation across emotion categories, we use ICC as the internal-consistency–like index and therefore do not compute α on these ratings. For construct validity, we cite prior evidence linking ICC-based EG to adaptive regulation and well-being [2, 9] and note convergent findings from an independent sample collected by our group [15], where EG correlated positively with cognitive reappraisal and negatively with social anxiety.

For the dot-probe task, we assessed reliability in two ways. First, we computed split-half reliability for the cue validity index (CVI = RT_incongruent − RT_congruent) at each stimulus duration (200, 500, 1000 ms) using odd–even splits with Spearman–Brown correction. Second, we calculated Cronbach’s α for trial-level RTs within congruent and incongruent conditions (collapsed across durations). These indices indicate that both CVI and RT measures capture stable attentional patterns rather than random variation.

Main analyses

We analyzed the data at both the RT level and the CVI level, and we used both extreme-groups and continuous approaches to EG. (1) RT-level mixed ANOVA (full sample, N = 107). A 3 (Duration: 200/500/1000 ms) × 2 (Congruency: congruent/incongruent) repeated-measures ANOVA tested the dot-probe effect. Mauchly’s test was used to assess sphericity; Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied when violated. (2) RT-level mixed ANOVA with EG groups. For the extreme-groups comparison (top/bottom 27%; n = 28 per group), we ran a 3 (Duration) × 2 (Congruency) within-subjects × 2 (EG group: high/low) between-subjects mixed ANOVA to test whether congruency and duration effects differ by EG group.(3) CVI-level analyses. For each participant and duration we computed CVI = RT_incongruent − RT_congruent. We then conducted a 1-factor repeated-measures ANOVA with Duration (3 levels) as the within-subjects factor in the full sample, and a 2 (EG group) × Duration mixed ANOVA for the extreme-groups comparison. One-sample t-tests compared each duration’s CVI against zero (positive CVI = attentional bias toward China-related images; negative CVI = avoidance). Multiple comparisons were FDR-adjusted. (4) Continuous PEG analyses. To avoid information loss, positive EG (PEG) was also treated as a continuous predictor. We ran linear regressions predicting CVI (separately at 200, 500, 1000 ms) from PEG, and parallel regressions predicting raw RTs (with Congruency coded and Duration entered as factors). This complementary approach tests whether individual-difference effects are consistent across analytic strategies.

Results

Reliability of the dot-probe task

Split-half reliability analyses revealed acceptable stability of CVI scores across stimulus durations. Specifically, the Spearman–Brown corrected split-half coefficients were r = 0.72 for the 200-ms condition, r = 0.68 for the 500-ms condition, and r = 0.75 for the 1000-ms condition. These results suggest that attentional bias indices showed moderate reliability across different exposure times.

In addition, internal consistency analyses demonstrated that reaction times were stable across congruent and incongruent trials. Cronbach’s α for congruent trials was 0.84, while for incongruent trials it was 0.81, indicating good internal consistency for both trial types. Together, these indices provide evidence that the dot-probe task produced sufficiently reliable measures of attentional bias in the current sample.

Overall results of the dot-probe task

The analysis revealed a significant main effect of cue congruency, F(1, 106) = 14.26, p = 0.0003, η2ₚ = 0.12. CVI Cue Validity Index, calculated as RT_incongruent – RT_congruent

To assess attentional bias toward China-related stimuli, cue validity indices (CVI = RT—incongruent − RT_congruent) were calculated for each stimulus presentation duration (200 ms, 500 ms, and 1000 ms). One-sample t-tests were conducted to determine whether CVI values significantly differed from zero. The results revealed that CVI was significantly greater than zero in the 200-ms (t(106) = 2.56, p = 0.012, Cohen’s d = 0.25) and 1000-ms condition (t(106) = 4.151, p < 0.001 (exact value = 6.72 × 10⁻5), Cohen’s d = 0.40), indicating that participants responded significantly faster to probes that appeared in the same spatial location as China-related images compared to probes in the opposite location. This result reflects an attentional vigilance effect emerging during shorter and longer stimulus exposure durations. In contrast, CVI values at 500-ms did not significantly differ from zero, suggesting that attentional bias was not evident under medium presentation times.

Results based on emotional granularity grouping

To further explore the effect of emotional granularity on attentional bias, a 2 (positive emotional granularity: high, low) × 2 (cue congruency: congruent, incongruent) × 3 (stimulus presentation time: 200 ms, 500 ms, 1000 ms) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted. Positive emotional granularity served as a between-subjects factor, while cue congruency and stimulus presentation time were within-subjects factors.

The analysis revealed a significant main effect of cue congruency, F(1, 54) = 5.37, p = 0.024, η2ₚ = 0.09, indicating that participants responded more quickly in the congruent cue condition compared to the in congruent condition. There was also a significant main effect of stimulus presentation time, F(1.85, 99.89) = 69.41, p < 0.0001(exact value: p = 3.0 × 10⁻1⁰), η2ₚ = 0.56, with post hoc comparisons showing decreasing reaction times as presentation time increased: 200 ms > 500 ms > 1000 ms (all p < 0.001).

A significant interaction was observed between cue congruency and stimulus presentation time, F(2, 53) = 3.59, p = 0.035, η2ₚ = 0.12. Simple effects analyses indicated that in the congruent cue condition, RTs at 200 ms were slower than at 500 ms, F(1, 54) = 11.26, p = 0.0012, η2ₚ = 0.17, and slower at 500 ms than at 1000 ms, F(1, 54) = 16.48, p = 2.1 × 10⁻4, η2ₚ = 0.23. In the incongruent condition, RTs at 200 ms were slower than 500 ms, F(1, 54) = 21.72, p = 6.0 × 10⁻5, η2ₚ = 0.29, and slower than at 1000 ms, F(1, 54) = 24.35, p = 4.0 × 10⁻5, η2ₚ = 0.31. The difference between 500 and 1000 ms was nonsignificant, F(1, 54) = 2.03, p = 0.160.

Moreover, a significant three-way interaction among cue congruency, stimulus presentation time, and emotional granularity group was observed, F(2, 53) = 3.25, p = 0.047, η2ₚ = 0.109 (see Fig. 3). Further simple effects analyses showed that for the low PEG group, RTs under the congruent condition varied significantly across durations: 200 ms > 500 ms > 1000 ms, F(1, 26) = 8.92, p = 0.006, η2ₚ = 0.26; F(1, 26) = 12.14, p = 0.002, η2ₚ = 0.32. For the high PEG group, congruency had a pronounced effect. At 200 ms, participants responded significantly faster in the congruent condition compared to the incongruent condition, t(27) = 2.54, p = 0.017, Cohen’s d = 0.48, reflecting early attentional vigilance. In contrast, at 500 ms, responses were significantly slower in the congruent condition than in the incongruent condition, t(27) = − 2.43, p = 0.022, Cohen’s d = 0.46, indicating attentional avoidance. To further investigate attentional bias dynamics as a function of emotional granularity, a 2 (positive emotional granularity: high, low) × 3 (stimulus presentation time: 200 ms, 500 ms, 1000 ms) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted using the cue validity index (CVI = RT_incongruent – RT_ congruent) as the dependent variable.

The analysis revealed a significant main effect of stimulus presentation time, F(2, 53) = 3.59, p = 0.035, η2ₚ = 0.12. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons indicated that CVI values differed significantly only between the 500 ms (M = −5.07, SD = 3.62) and 1000 ms (M = 6.68, SD = 2.40) conditions (p = 0.021). There were no significant differences between the 200 ms condition (M = 9.49, SD = 4.73) and the other two durations (p ≥ 0.05).

To further examine attentional patterns across different stimulus presentation durations, one-sample t-tests were conducted to compare cue validity index (CVI) scores with zero within each group and condition. In the 200-ms condition, participants in the high positive emotional granularity group exhibited significantly higher CVI values than zero, t(27) = 2.87, p = 0.008, Cohen’s d = 0.54, indicating faster responses to probes at congruent locations and suggesting attentional vigilance. Continuous variable analyses

To further validate the robustness of the findings, we additionally treated positive emotional granularity (PEG) as a continuous variable. This approach allows us to capture more subtle individual differences and provides a complementary test of whether the observed attentional dynamics are consistent across analytic strategies. Regression models were estimated with PEG predicting both attentional bias indices (CVI) and raw reaction times (RTs) across the three stimulus presentation durations (200, 500, and 1000 ms).

Treating positive emotional granularity (PEG) as a continuous variable, regression analyses showed a marginally positive association between PEG and CVI at 200 ms (β = 5.48, p = 0.065, R2 = 0.03), indicating a trend for higher PEG to predict stronger attentional vigilance. No significant effects were observed at 500 ms (β = − 4.00, p = 0.171) or 1000 ms (β = − 0.65, p = 0.705).

Analyses of reaction times further indicated that PEG was marginally related to slower responses in the incongruent condition at 200 ms (β = 10.44, p = 0.111, R2 = 0.02) and in the congruent condition at 1000 ms (β = 7.21, p = 0.151, R2 = 0.02). Taken together, these results provide convergent though modest evidence that higher emotional granularity is linked to subtle shifts in early attentional vigilance and later attentional maintenance, consistent with the group-based analyses.

Discussion

Attentional bias toward nation-related stimuli

The present study investigated the role of positive emotional granularity (PEG) in modulating attentional bias toward nation-related stimuli using a dot-probe paradigm. The findings revealed a congruent attentional bias toward China-related images across participants, as evidenced by significantly faster reaction times in cue-congruent trials compared to incongruent trials. The present findings revealed that attentional bias toward nation-related stimuli emerged at both early (200 ms) and late (1000 ms) stimulus durations, but not at the intermediate duration (500 ms). The bias observed at 200 ms suggests that patriotic cues are capable of rapidly and automatically capturing attention, reflecting a bottom-up process of attentional orienting to emotionally and socially salient stimuli (cf. [25]). By contrast, the absence of attentional bias at 500 ms may indicate a transitional phase characterized by attentional regulation or avoidance, consistent with the vigilance–avoidance model of emotional attention [4]. At 1000 ms, the reemergence of attentional bias suggests sustained or renewed engagement with patriotic cues, highlighting the role of top-down control processes that allocate cognitive resources to stimuli of social and identity relevance [29]. Taken together, this dynamic pattern underscores that national symbols not only capture attention rapidly but also maintain their salience during prolonged processing, supporting their unique role in emotion–attention interactions.

The role of emotional granularity in attentional dynamics

The present study demonstrated that emotional granularity plays a critical role in shaping attentional allocation to nation-related stimuli. Group-based analyses revealed that individual differences in positive emotional granularity (PEG) systematically modulate attentional responses to patriotic cues across time. Specifically, individuals with high emotional granularity exhibited early attentional vigilance at 200 ms, followed by a strategic shift toward attentional avoidance at 500 ms, and a return to baseline (no significant bias) at 1000 ms. In contrast, individuals with low emotional granularity showed delayed attentional vigilance, emerging only at 1000 ms. This temporal pattern suggests that high PEG facilitates flexible modulation of attention across different stages of stimulus exposure, whereas low PEG is associated with slower and less adaptive engagement.

Complementing the group-based results, the continuous-variable analyses revealed a marginally positive association between PEG and attentional vigilance at 200 ms (β = 5.48, p = 0.065), indicating that individuals higher in PEG tended to display stronger early attentional bias toward national cues. At 1000 ms, PEG showed a marginal positive association with slower responses in congruent trials (β = 7.21, p = 0.151), suggesting sustained attentional engagement during prolonged exposure rather than disengagement. Although these effects did not reach conventional significance thresholds, the pattern converges with the categorical findings, underscoring that emotional granularity influences attentional bias in a stage-specific and dynamic manner—facilitating early vigilance toward salient national cues and promoting later-stage attentional maintenance.

Taken together, both analytic approaches highlight the adaptive function of positive emotional granularity. High-PEG individuals appear capable of rapidly orienting to emotionally salient patriotic cues, strategically disengaging to prevent overinvestment of attention, and flexibly re-engaging when deeper or context-relevant processing is required. Such flexibility aligns with theoretical models linking emotional granularity to enhanced emotion regulation, psychological resilience, and context-sensitive attentional control [2, 5]. Importantly, these findings extend traditional attentional-bias research—largely centered on threat or negative stimuli—by demonstrating that trait-level differences in emotional differentiation also modulate responses to culturally symbolic, identity-relevant cues.

Although flexible attentional control is often assumed to manifest at longer stimulus durations, the present findings suggest that for individuals with high positive emotional granularity, such flexibility reflects a dynamic regulation process rather than a sustained attentional bias. These individuals demonstrated early vigilance toward nation-related stimuli at 200 ms, strategic avoidance at 500 ms, and a neutral pattern at 1000 ms, indicating the deployment of adaptive regulation mechanisms across time. One plausible interpretation is that high-EG individuals process emotionally salient stimuli more efficiently, reducing the need to maintain attentional prioritization over time. Another explanation draws on inhibition-of-return (IOR) mechanisms, in which attention is actively withdrawn from previously attended locations to facilitate exploration of novel information [38]. In this context, the absence of bias at 1000 ms does not signal attentional failure but reflects the flexible reallocation of cognitive resources—engaging early and releasing once processing demands are met.

By contrast, individuals with low emotional granularity displayed a delayed vigilance effect, evident only at 1000 ms. This pattern suggests that insufficient differentiation of emotional states may hinder early attentional capture, leading to less efficient and less flexible attentional control. From a psychological standpoint, positive emotional granularity refers to the capacity to make fine-grained distinctions among similar positive emotions [15]. This ability has been linked to better emotion regulation, greater resilience, and more effective coping strategies [5]. The present study extends this literature by showing that emotional granularity also governs attentional allocation to nationally symbolic stimuli, implying that high-PEG individuals are better equipped to navigate complex socio-emotional environments.

By contrast, individuals with low emotional granularity showed a delayed vigilance effect, emerging only at 1000 ms. This suggests that insufficient differentiation of emotional states may hinder early attentional capture, leading to less efficient and less flexible attentional dynamics.

Finally, these findings contribute to the emerging field of research examining emotional responses to patriotic symbols and national identity. While most attentional-bias research has traditionally emphasized negative or threat-related cues [3, 20], little is known about how individuals allocate attention to culturally or identity-laden stimuli. The current study fills this gap by demonstrating that attentional biases toward patriotic imagery can emerge under specific temporal conditions and are meaningfully shaped by individual differences in emotional granularity. This insight opens a promising avenue for future research at the intersection of emotion, attention, and identity.

Implications for emotional granularity and national symbol processing

While prior research has focused predominantly on attentional biases toward negative emotional stimuli such as threat-related faces or body expressions [3, 20], the current study extends this inquiry to state-related imagery, a relatively underexplored emotional domain. Our findings indicate that patriotic cues, when emotionally encoded, elicit distinct attentional patterns modulated by individual differences in emotion differentiation. This opens new avenues for understanding how trait-level emotional capacities affect attentional prioritization of socially and politically meaningful stimuli.

Media exposure plays a pivotal role in reinforcing culturally shaped emotional patterns. Repeated encounters with emotionally salient national symbols—such as flags [1], monuments [28, 31], and national heroes [39]—across various channels including news media, educational content, and public campaigns, may increase individuals’ emotional specificity toward country-related stimuli [12, 16, 37]. This process not only fosters enhanced positive emotional granularity but also strengthens attentional prioritization of patriotic cues. Over time, such exposure may serve as a mechanism of emotional socialization, shaping how individuals interpret and respond to culturally relevant affective information.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the stimulus database for patriotic imagery was limited to Chinese and non-Chinese image pairs, which restricts generalizability. Future work could expand the range of stimuli and include direct measures of patriotic affect to better capture emotional salience. Second, the absence of a neutral–neutral control condition limited the ability to calculate more specific attentional indices, such as orienting and disengagement scores [10]. Accordingly, the current design cannot clearly distinguish “attentional disengagement” from other avoidance-related processes, and CVI values should be interpreted with caution. Third, the study relied solely on behavioral measures,incorporating cognitive neuroscience methods (e.g., ERP, fMRI) could reveal the neural chronometry of granularity-related attentional biases. Fourth, the sample consisted only of Chinese university students, which limits generalizability to broader populations and across cultures. Finally, although the ICC-based index of positive emotional granularity has been widely used and validated in prior research [2, 15], the present study did not include additional convergent measures (e.g., self-report scales or ecological momentary assessments). Future studies should adopt multi-method approaches to enhance the reliability and construct validity of emotional granularity measurement and examine its cross-situational and cross-cultural stability.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that positive emotional granularity (PEG) modulates attentional dynamics toward patriotic, identity-relevant stimuli. The dot-probe task revealed early (200 ms) and late (1000 ms) vigilance effects toward China-related images. Results from both group-based and continuous analyses converged: high-PEG individuals exhibited early vigilance followed by mid-stage avoidance, reflecting flexible attentional allocation, whereas low-PEG individuals showed delayed vigilance emerging only at 1000 ms. These findings extend attentional-bias research beyond negative stimuli, highlighting PEG as a key factor in context-sensitive attentional control. Future research should further validate PEG, test its cross-cultural generalizability, and integrate neural measures to clarify the underlying mechanisms.
