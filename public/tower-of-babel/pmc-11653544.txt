The role of CEUS in diagnosing tuberculous abscess of the right diaphragm
Authors: Wenzhi Zhang, Xiulei Yu, Jun Li, Lingling Wang, Jie Chu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the diagnostic value of contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) in detecting tuberculous diaphragmatic abscess (TDA) of the right diaphragm and provide a reference for its clinical diagnosis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 33 patients with right TDA confirmed via surgery and biopsy. The diagnostic accuracy of CEUS and routine ultrasound for detecting right TDA was compared.ResultsAmong the 33 right TDA cases, the lesions primarily exhibited mixed and low echogenicity, with an orientation parallel to the diaphragmatic surface. Eighteen patients (54.5%) demonstrated the characteristic “crocodile mouth sign.” Heterogeneous enhancement was observed in most cases (24 patients) compared to homogeneous or no enhancement. The diagnostic accuracy of routine ultrasound for right TDA was 54.50% (18/33), whereas CEUS demonstrated a significantly higher accuracy of 78.78% (26/33) (χ2 = 4.364, P = 0.037).ConclusionCEUS provides valuable insights into the vascular characteristics of right TDA, with most cases showing mixed echogenicity and heterogeneous enhancement. The “crocodile mouth sign” was a distinctive feature, highlighting the utility of CEUS in improving diagnostic accuracy for right TDA.

Introduction

Tuberculous diaphragmatic abscess (TDA) is a chronic tuberculous infection of the diaphragm and a rare form of extrapulmonary tuberculosis. In 2018, extrapulmonary tuberculosis accounted for 15% (range: 8–24%) of the 7.0 million incidental cases [1]. TDAs can rupture into the chest or abdominal cavity. Due to the atypical clinical symptoms of TDA, it is often confused with diaphragmatic tumors, subdiaphragmatic abscesses, or hepatic capsule tuberculosis during imaging, making diagnosis challenging. Reports on diaphragmatic abscesses are rare, and those specifically evaluating TDA using contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) are even rarer. This study aimed to summarize the CEUS findings of TDA cases treated in our hospital and provide a reference for the diagnosis of this condition.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Red Cross Hospital, and all patients provided written informed consent to participate. From January 2014 to April 2022, 33 patients with pathologically confirmed TDA were examined using routine ultrasound and CEUS at the Affiliated Hangzhou Chest Hospital of Zhejiang University. These cases were retrospectively reviewed and analyzed. Pathological findings of lesions on the right side of the diaphragm were obtained. The study included patients diagnosed with TDA and those with positive diaphragmatic findings on ultrasound and CEUS. Other inclusion criteria were the availability of complete medical records, normal blood clotting function, and the absence of severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction. Patients were excluded if they had diaphragmatic lesions that could not be identified via ultrasound, severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction preventing surgery, no pathological confirmation of TDA, or contraindications for CEUS.

Ultrasound and CEUS examinations

A Philips ultrasonic diagnostic instrument (iU22, Philips Healthcare, Bothell, WA, USA) equipped with a high-frequency linear array probe (L12-5, frequency 5–12 MHz; L9-3, frequency 3–9 MHz) and a convex low-frequency probe (C5-1, frequency 1–5 MHz) was used for the examinations. The routine ultrasound and CEUS characteristics of the lesions were recorded, including lesion size, location, relationship with the diaphragm, echogenicity, presence of calcifications, and color blood-flow signal features. To minimize interference from lung gas during the examination, patients were instructed to control their breathing amplitude, allowing observation of the relative movement between the lesion and the liver capsule.

Pulse-inversion harmonic CEUS was performed using a low mechanical index (0.06). The ultrasonographic contrast agent SonoVue (Bracco SpA, Milan, Italy) was administered via an initial injection of 2.4 mL into the elbow vein, followed by a 5-mL saline flush. Dynamic observation of the enhancement of diaphragmatic lesions was conducted, with continuous observation for 2 min.

The enhancement patterns of CEUS were analyzed, including rapid enhancement in the arterial phase, regression in the venous phase, and uniform enhancement within the lesion. All findings were videographed for subsequent analysis. Lesions that demonstrated asynchronous enhancement compared to the liver were considered diaphragmatic in origin. Intrahepatic cystic or solid foci were excluded. Hepatic malignancies near the diaphragmatic apex typically exhibit septal and/or nodular hypo-enhancement in the portal–sinusoidal phase, with or without arterial enhancement on CEUS [2]. Hepatic abscesses in the fornix generally show non-enhancement of the liquefied portions combined with arterial rim enhancement on CEUS [3]. To minimize subjective errors, routine ultrasound and CEUS assessments were performed by two attending physicians with 5 years of experience in ultrasound diagnosis. Both physicians were blinded to the pathological results. The data were independently reviewed by the two sonographers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to unify the results.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data were analyzed using the χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ultrasound examination

In 18 patients, a “crocodile mouth sign” was observed between the TDA and the normal diaphragm (Fig. 1). The lesion divided the diaphragm into two layers, with the layered diaphragms surrounding the lesion’s edges, resembling an open crocodile mouth. The long axis of the lesions was typically parallel to the diaphragmatic surface, and calcifications were identified within the lesions. Mixed echogenicity (54.54%, 18/33) was the most commonly observed echogenic feature of the lesions (Fig. 2), followed by hypoechogenicity (42.42%, 13/33) and isoechogenicity (6.06%, 2/33). Discussion

The diaphragm forms between the 4th and 12th weeks of embryonic development. In the 4th week, the coelom develops as a horseshoe-shaped cavity in the cardiogenic and lateral mesoderm. This cavity later serves as the origin of the pleural and peritoneal cavities. By the 6th week, the pleural pericardium extends medially and fuses with the esophageal mesentery and septum, separating the pleural cavity from the peritoneal cavity [4, 5]. The diaphragm is the primary muscle responsible for breathing [6]. Due to its unique anatomy, diaphragmatic lesions can extend into both the thoracic and abdominal cavities, providing a large space for growth. Clinical symptoms typically manifest only after the lesion has reached a significant size or when vital organs are affected [7, 8].

The clinical symptoms of TDA are often not prominent. In this study, fever and upper abdominal discomfort were each observed in one case, whereas fatigue and night sweats were reported in five cases each; the remaining cases were asymptomatic. This lack of symptoms may be related to the size of the lesion or the anatomical structure of the diaphragm. TDA is generally small in size; however, in one patient in this study, the lesion was notably large, with a maximum dimension of 7.0 × 2.6 cm. Due to the favorable imaging clarity and range of ultrasound on the right side of the diaphragm, only patients with right-sided TDA were included in this study.

TDA is often mistaken for diaphragmatic tumors or hepatic capsule tuberculosis in imaging studies. Diaphragmatic tumors can be classified into primary and secondary types. Primary diaphragmatic tumors, which are less common than secondary ones, include schwannoma, sarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, and germ cell tumors [9, 10]. Secondary diaphragmatic tumors are more frequently observed and typically include lung cancer, malignant mesothelioma, thymoma, and germ cell tumors [6, 11, 12]. The central region of the diaphragm is primarily composed of central tendons, whereas the surrounding muscle fibers migrate toward the center. This anatomical structure suggests that diaphragmatic tumors predominantly originate from the diaphragmatic horn. In this study, TDAs were most commonly observed in the lateral part of the diaphragm, followed by the anterior and posterior regions. The lesions were primarily located near the diaphragm’s edge, a pattern similar to that of tumor distribution. This predilection for peripheral locations may be related to the difficulty in detecting central diaphragm lesions due to interference from pulmonary gas during ultrasound imaging. However, this hypothesis requires further confirmation through additional imaging modalities.

Tuberculous granuloma, liquefactive necrosis, fibrosis, and calcification occur simultaneously in the pathological progression of TDA [13], leading to diverse ultrasound findings. In this study, 13 cases of TDA were hypoechoic, whereas 2 cases were isoechoic. Conventional ultrasound often resulted in misdiagnosis as a tumor or an unclear diagnosis. CEUS, however, provided detailed visualization of the lesion’s blood supply and accurately identified necrotic areas within the lesion. Among the cases, seven showed uneven enhancement, consistent with an abscess. CEUS demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy than conventional ultrasound in this study, likely due to its ability to precisely delineate the internal blood supply of the lesion.

TDA is often misdiagnosed as hepatic capsule tuberculosis, a specific type of hepatic tuberculosis. Hepatic tuberculosis is classified into miliary, nodular, and mixed types. The ultrasound manifestations of tuberculosis located in the hepatic capsule are distinctive but often require histopathological or microbiological confirmation for diagnosis [13–17]. In this study, two patients with heterogeneous enhancement on CEUS were misdiagnosed with hepatic capsule tuberculosis. This misdiagnosis likely occurred because the lesions were located near the diaphragmatic foot, close to the liver capsule, leading the diagnostician to incorrectly attribute the lesions to hepatic capsule tuberculosis. Upon reviewing the imaging data, it was observed that the diaphragmatic lesions were clearly separated from the liver capsule, and their relative movement with respiration was visible. This highlights the importance of improving diagnosticians’ understanding of this condition. We recommend that when localizing the lesion proves challenging, a high-frequency probe should be used to better identify the lesion and its relationship with surrounding organs. This approach can enhance diagnostic accuracy and reduce the likelihood of misdiagnosis.

In this study, the shape of the lesions was primarily fusiform, and in 30 patients, the long axis of the lesions was often parallel to the diaphragmatic plane. Lesions located at the lateral margin of the diaphragm often clearly displayed the abscess wall and whether it was ruptured, whereas those near the diaphragm center frequently failed to show the abscess wall on the pleural side, resulting in an unclear view of the lesions and the lungs. In this study, 13 patients exhibited an unclear boundary with the lung, 10 patients were confirmed to have a clear boundary with the lung, and 3 patients were confirmed to have a ruptured thorax with an unclear boundary with the lung. We suggest that when TDA is suspected, the boundary between the lesion and the lung is usually unclear. Therefore, it is insufficient to simply state that the boundary is unclear; instead, the lesion’s location should be specified, and it is more accurate to note that the pleural edge of the lesion is unclear due to gas interference. Computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging is necessary to confirm the relationship between the lesion and the lungs.

In 18 cases in this study, the “crocodile mouth sign” was observed, characterized by the surrounding normal diaphragms being locally stratified by lesions, which is typically the characteristic ultrasound appearance of diaphragmatic abscesses, especially in cases with mixed echogenicity. Due to the significant difference in acoustic impedance between the pus and the diaphragm, the greater the contrast between the echogenicity of the two tissues, the more pronounced the “crocodile mouth sign.” When tuberculous abscesses rupture into the thorax [18–20], the “crocodile mouth sign” can present in various forms.

Among the 33 patients in this study, 27 exhibited non-enhancement and heterogeneous enhancement, and the volumes of the lesions in these patients were larger than those in patients with homogeneous enhancement. Thus, the larger the volume of the tuberculous abscess, the higher the liquefaction necrosis rate, and diaphragmatic mass imaging shows no enhancement and septal enhancement. TDA should be considered in conjunction with a history of tuberculosis.

TDAs that show homogeneous enhancement are difficult to distinguish from diaphragmatic tumors. In this study, six cases were confirmed as TDA via surgery and showed homogeneous enhancement on CEUS, with two cases clearing earlier than the liver. When CEUS suggests a high possibility of a tumor, needle biopsy should be performed to confirm the diagnosis. TDA should also be differentiated from inflammatory pseudotumor (IPT) of the liver fornix. TDA is closely associated with the diaphragm, with the lesion moving relative to the liver during respiration, and is often accompanied by a history of tuberculosis or tuberculosis at other sites. Conversely, IPT is closely associated with the liver, with the edge of the lesion forming an acute angle with the liver capsule, though IPT in the liver exhibits diverse manifestations on CEUS. A clear diagnosis is challenging to achieve through imaging alone and typically requires a puncture biopsy for confirmation [21, 22].

Due to national policy, our hospital serves as a regional referral medical institution, with many suspected tuberculosis patients referred for evaluation. Diagnosing TDA is critically important; ultrasound provides locational diagnostic information, whereas CEUS offers detailed vascular insights into the lesion, providing essential data for further diagnosis. This study has certain limitations. The small number of cases impacted the statistical power, and as a single-center study, its findings require validation through multicenter studies to confirm their accuracy.

Conclusion

Features of right TDA primarily include mixed echogenicity, lesion movement relative to the liver capsule, and the “crocodile mouth sign.” CEUS revealed the blood supply characteristics of right TDA, demonstrating heterogeneous enhancement and no enhancement. Our findings indicate that CEUS is highly valuable for diagnosing TDA on the right side.
