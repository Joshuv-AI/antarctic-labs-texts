Feature-Wise Mixing for Mitigating Contextual Bias in Predictive Supervised Learning Corresponding author email: tomar4@purdue.edu
Yash Vardhan Tomar 1

Abstract
Bias in predictive machine learning (ML) models is a fundamental challenge due to the skewed or unfair outcomes produced by biased models. Existing mitigation strategies rely on either post-hoc corrections or rigid constraints. However, emerging research claims that these techniques can limit scalability and reduce generalizability. To address this, this paper introduces a feature-wise mixing framework to mitigate contextual bias. This was done by redistributing feature representations across multiple contextual datasets. To assess feature-wise mixing’s effectiveness, four ML classifiers were trained using cross-validation and evaluated with bias-sensitive loss functions, including disparity metrics and mean squared error (MSE), which served as a standard measure of predictive performance. The proposed method achieved an average bias reduction of 43.35% and a statistically significant decrease in MSE across all classifiers trained on mixed datasets. Additionally, benchmarking against established bias mitigation techniques found that feature-wise mixing consistently outperformed SMOTE oversampling and demonstrated competitive effectiveness without requiring explicit bias attribute identification. Feature-wise mixing efficiently avoids the computational overhead typically associated with fairness-aware learning algorithms. Future work could explore applying feature-wise mixing for real-world fields where accurate predictions are necessary.

Most critically, current techniques lack mechanisms to address contextual bias—systematic performance differences that emerge when models trained in one geographic, demographic, or socioeconomic context are deployed in another. This form of bias is particularly relevant for global AI deployments, where models must maintain fairness across diverse operational environments.

I-A

Contributions: Feature-Wise Mixing for Contextual Bias Mitigation

This work introduces feature-wise mixing, a novel data-centric approach that addresses the limitations of existing bias mitigation techniques while specifically targeting contextual bias. My approach contributes to the field through four key innovations:

Dataset-Centric Architecture Independence:
Unlike in-processing techniques that require algorithm-specific modifications
[
15
]
or post-processing methods that adjust outputs
[
9
]
, feature-wise mixing operates exclusively at the dataset level. This preprocessing approach transforms data distributions before model training, eliminating computational overhead during inference while maintaining compatibility with any classifier architecture. The method directly addresses implementation complexity barriers identified in fairness literature
[
1
]
.

Attribute-Agnostic Operation:
While reweighting methods require explicit identification of protected attributes
[
1
]
, feature-wise mixing achieves bias reduction through stochastic feature redistribution without requiring sensitive attribute specification. This capability enables deployment in domains where protected characteristics are unknown, legally restricted, or ethically problematic to collect—overcoming a fundamental limitation noted across comprehensive fairness surveys
[
1
,
2
]
.

Contextual Bias Disruption:
Existing techniques primarily address class imbalance
[
10
]
or demographic discrimination
[
5
]
, but lack mechanisms to mitigate context-specific biases that emerge from geographic, institutional, or temporal differences. Feature-wise mixing uniquely disrupts spurious correlations between features and contextual factors by blending feature distributions across heterogeneous datasets, forcing models to learn context-invariant relationships.

Theoretical Foundation in Distributional Moments:
Unlike standard mixup techniques
[
16
]
that interpolate individual samples, feature-wise mixing operates on distributional statistics:

\mathbb{E}[x_{\text{mixed}}]=\sum_{r}\alpha_{r}\mu_{r},\quad\text{Var}(x_{\text{mixed}})=\sum_{r}\alpha_{r}^{2}\sigma_{r}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{noise}}^{2}

(1)

where
\mu_{r}
and
\sigma_{r}^{2}
represent region-specific statistical moments. This formulation explicitly disrupts context-feature covariances
\text{Cov}(x,c)
that traditional sample-level mixing approaches leave intact.

I validate feature-wise mixing through comprehensive experimentation across four classifier architectures (Decision Trees, Random Forest, SVM, K-Nearest Neighbors) using socioeconomic datasets from three geographic regions (Kenya, India, Sri Lanka). The technique achieves an average 43.35% reduction in mean squared error when models transition from single-context training to mixed-dataset approaches. Individual regional improvements are particularly striking: 78.30% bias reduction for Decision Trees in Indian contexts, 78.40% for Random Forests, and 78.40% for K-Nearest Neighbors.

Benchmarking against established techniques reveals important trade-offs in the bias mitigation landscape. While reweighted dataset approaches achieve superior performance (47.58% improvement over feature-wise mixing,
p=1.35\times 10^{-11}
), they require explicit bias attribute identification and impose 52% longer training times. Feature-wise mixing consistently outperforms SMOTE oversampling while maintaining computational efficiency and regulatory compliance through its attribute-agnostic operation.

These results position feature-wise mixing as a practical solution for organizations seeking to deploy fair AI systems under real-world constraints where protected attributes may be unavailable, computational budgets are limited, and regulatory requirements prohibit explicit demographic profiling. The remainder of this paper details my methodology (Section 2), presents comprehensive experimental validation (Section 3), and discusses implications for equitable machine learning deployment (Section 4).

II
Methodology

In this study, I compiled socio-economic datasets from three distinct regions: Mombasa, Kenya; Kolkata, India; and Colombo, Sri Lanka, spanning from 1960 to 2023. These regions were selected due to their economic significance in the global tea trade and their diverse socio-economic conditions, which allow for a robust analysis of contextual bias in predictive modeling. The primary objective of this study is to forecast tea prices (in $/kg), which serve as the target variable. The key independent variables influencing tea price predictions include GDP per capita (in USD) and inflation rates (in percentage terms). These indicators were chosen based on their well-documented impact on economic markets and commodity pricing trends.

To ensure authenticity and cross-region comparability, all data was sourced from The World Bank
[
3
]
, a globally recognized and widely used database for economic and development research. Standardizing data sources minimizes inconsistencies and ensures that differences in data distributions across regions reflect genuine socioeconomic variations rather than artifacts of inconsistent data collection methodologies.

For each region, the dataset is mathematically defined as follows:

D_{r}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

(2)

where:

•

D_{r}
represents the dataset for a given region
r
, where
r\in\{\text{Mombasa, Kolkata, Colombo}\}
.

•

x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the feature vector containing socio-economic indicators for the
i^{th}
observation.

•

y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
represents the observed tea price at the corresponding time step.

•

n
denotes the total number of observations within the dataset.

Each dataset was carefully structured to maintain feature consistency in all three regions, ensuring that machine learning models trained on these datasets could be meaningfully compared without requiring additional feature engineering. This consistency is crucial when implementing bias mitigation techniques, as any variation in feature representation could introduce unintended biases unrelated to regional differences.

II-A

Dataset Augmentation and Feature-Wise Mixing

II-A
1
Data Augmentation via Noise Addition

The original dataset contained only 765 monthly data points per region, which was insufficient to train robust machine learning models. To address this, data augmentation techniques were used to artificially expand the dataset. I increased the number of samples to enhance the stability of model training. Specifically, the dataset was expanded to a daily frequency from a monthly frequency, effectively increasing the number of observations from 765 to 23,000 per region (approximately a 31x expansion factor).

To prevent overfitting while preserving the statistical properties of the original dataset, Gaussian noise was systematically added to each feature using the following transformation:

x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}+\epsilon_{i},\quad\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})

(3)

where:

•

x_{i}^{\prime}
represents the augmented feature value after noise addition.

•

\epsilon_{i}
is a noise term drawn from a Gaussian (normal) distribution with mean 0 and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

•

\sigma^{2}
is the empirically calibrated variance of the added noise, ensuring that the augmented dataset remains realistic.

This augmentation technique serves multiple purposes. First, it regularizes the dataset, preventing machine learning models from memorizing specific data points and thus improving generalization. Second, it simulates natural variations that may occur in real-world socio-economic conditions, making the model more robust to slight deviations in input data. Gaussian noise augmentation is widely recognized as an effective approach in machine learning, particularly in cases where data scarcity poses a challenge
[
17
]
. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests also confirmed distributional fidelity (
D=0.032,p>0.05
) between original and augmented data.

II-A
2
Feature-Wise Mixing for Contextual Bias Mitigation

One of the central challenges in cross-regional predictive modeling is the presence of contextual bias: systematic discrepancies in socioeconomic indicators and pricing trends across different regions. This brings us to the proposed method to mitigate this bias: a feature-wise mixing approach was employed, which blends the datasets from each region into a single, contextually balanced dataset.

If
D_{r}^{\text{aug}}
represents the augmented dataset for region
r
, then the final mixed dataset
D_{\text{mix}}
is defined as:

D_{\text{mix}}=\bigcup_{r=1}^{3}\alpha_{r}D_{r}^{\text{aug}}

(4)

where:

•

D_{\text{mix}}
is the synthesized dataset that integrates socio-economic distributions across the three regions.

•

\alpha_{r}
is a scaling coefficient ensuring that each region contributes equally to the final dataset.

Given that each region’s dataset was expanded to 23,000 observations, the computational complexity of the feature-wise mixing process is
O(n\log n)
, arising from the sorting and weighted averaging operations necessary to merge the datasets.

II-A
3
Theoretical Mechanism of Bias Reduction Through Feature-Wise Mixing

The effectiveness of feature-wise mixing stems from its ability to disrupt spurious correlations that emerge from contextual homogeneity within individual regional datasets. When a model trains exclusively on data from a single region, it inadvertently learns region-specific patterns that may not generalize to broader contexts, effectively encoding contextual bias into its decision boundaries.

Mathematically, consider a feature vector
x_{i}
from region
r
that exhibits strong correlation with a contextual bias factor
b_{r}
. The model learns a mapping
f_{r}(x_{i})=\theta^{T}x_{i}+\epsilon_{r}
, where
\epsilon_{r}
represents region-specific bias. Feature-wise mixing breaks this dependency by creating a composite feature space:

x_{\text{mixed}}=\sum_{r=1}^{3}\alpha_{r}x_{r}+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})

(5)

where
\alpha_{r}
are region weighting coefficients from Eq.(4).

This transformation forces the model to learn relationships that are robust across multiple contextual environments, effectively learning
f_{\text{mix}}(x_{\text{mixed}})=\theta^{T}x_{\text{mixed}}
where the bias terms
\epsilon_{r}
are averaged out, resulting in more generalizable decision boundaries.
The bias reduction mechanism can be quantified through the variance decomposition:

\text{Bias}_{\text{total}}=\text{Bias}_{\text{algorithmic}}+\text{Bias}_{\text{contextual}}

(6)

Where feature-wise mixing specifically targets
\text{Bias}_{\text{contextual}}
by ensuring that:

\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{r}|\text{mixed data}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\epsilon_{r}\approx 0

(7)

This mathematical foundation explains why mixed models demonstrate lower MSE: they learn feature relationships that are invariant to contextual variations, resulting in more robust predictive performance.

II-B

Machine Learning Classifier Model Training

To develop an effective predictive framework for tea prices, four distinct regression models were implemented using scikit-learn
[
15
]
:

1.

Support Vector Regression (SVR)
– Effective for handling nonlinear relationships.

2.

K-Nearest Neighbors Regression (KNN)
– Useful for capturing local trends.

3.

Decision Trees (DT)
– Provides interpretable decision rules.

4.

Random Forest (RF)
– An ensemble model that improves prediction accuracy.

Each model was trained using an 80/20 train-test split, ensuring a fair assessment of predictive performance.The primary metric for assessing model performance was the Mean Squared Error (MSE), calculated as:

\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}

(8)

where:

•

y_{i}
represents the actual tea price.

•

\hat{y}_{i}
is the predicted tea price generated by the model.

•

N
is the total number of test samples.

Loss functions such as Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) were also considered. However, MSE was chosen as the primary loss-function metric due to its strong penalization of large, more extreme errors, making it more suitable for bias detection and subsequent mitigation.

II-C

Classifier Model Result Generation

To quantify the impact of feature-wise mixing on bias reduction, a relative MSE reduction metric was defined as:

\Delta_{\text{bias}}=\frac{\text{MSE}_{D_{r}}-\text{MSE}_{D_{\text{mix}}}}{\text{MSE}_{D_{r}}}\times 100

(9)

where:

•

\text{MSE}_{D_{r}}
is the mean squared error for models trained on individual regional datasets.

•

\text{MSE}_{D_{\text{mix}}}
is the mean squared error for models trained on the mixed dataset.

•

\Delta_{\text{bias}}
quantifies the percentage reduction in bias due to feature-wise mixing.

In terms of computational trade-offs:

•

Linear models (SVR, KNN):
Faster but limited in capturing nonlinear relationships.

•

Tree-based models (DT, RF):
Balance between interpretability and accuracy.

•

Neural Networks (NN):
Computationally expensive but capable of modeling complex interactions.

While these metrics provide a theoretical framework for evaluating feature-wise mixing effectiveness, the practical validation of bias reduction claims requires rigorous empirical testing across multiple model architectures and statistical validation techniques. To ensure that observed performance improvements are statistically significant rather than artifacts of random variation, and to demonstrate the generalizability of feature-wise mixing across different classifier types, I implemented a comprehensive benchmarking framework that systematically evaluates model performance under controlled experimental conditions.

II-D

10-Fold Cross Validation & Statistical Differentiation of Model Performances

To ensure the robustness and reliability of the predictive models, I implemented a rigorous 10-fold cross-validation procedure. In this approach, the entire training dataset, denoted as
D_{\text{train}}
with
N
observations, is divided into 10 equal partitions (folds):

D_{\text{train}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{10}D_{i}

(10)

For each fold
i
(where
i=1,2,\dots,10
), the model is trained on:

D_{\text{train}}^{(i)}=D_{\text{train}}\setminus D_{i}

(11)

and validated on
D_{i}
. The error for fold
i
is denoted as
\text{MSE}_{i}
, and the overall performance is computed as the mean of these errors:

\overline{\text{MSE}}=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}\text{MSE}_{i}

(12)

To statistically differentiate the performances of models trained on the mixed versus unmixed datasets, error bars are derived using two key metrics:

Standard Error of the Mean (SEM):

\text{SEM}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{10}},

(13)

where
\sigma
is the standard deviation of the
\text{MSE}_{i}
values from the 10 folds. Error bars are then set as:

\text{Error Bar}=2\times\text{SEM}.

(14)

This 2SEM approach provides a conservative estimate of variability across the folds.

IV
Broader Impact Statement

While feature-wise mixing demonstrates substantial bias reduction capabilities (43.35% average improvement), the technique’s deployment raises important considerations regarding potential misuse and unintended consequences. The method’s attribute-agnostic operation, while enabling deployment in privacy-constrained environments, could inadvertently mask legitimate performance differences that reflect genuine skill or qualification disparities rather than unfair bias. Organizations might inappropriately apply feature-wise mixing to contexts where observed performance gaps stem from addressable systemic inequalities rather than algorithmic bias, potentially perpetuating injustice under the guise of fairness. Additionally, the technique’s effectiveness in disrupting context-feature correlations could be exploited to obscure discriminatory practices by making biased decision-making less detectable through traditional auditing mechanisms.

The global deployment implications merit particular scrutiny given my cross-regional validation across Kenya, India, and Sri Lanka. Feature-wise mixing’s capacity to blend socioeconomic distributions across geographic contexts, while beneficial for reducing contextual bias, could inadvertently impose dominant economic paradigms on developing regions through dataset homogenization. This risk is amplified in domains like healthcare and financial services, where my findings suggest broad applicability: diagnostic models trained on mixed datasets might systematically underperform for populations with unique genetic, environmental, or cultural characteristics not adequately represented in blended distributions. Similarly, credit scoring applications could disadvantage communities whose economic behaviors differ from mixed-dataset norms, potentially restricting financial access for already marginalized populations.

The computational efficiency advantages I demonstrated (52% faster training than reweighted approaches) could accelerate widespread deployment without adequate consideration of contextual appropriateness. Organizations seeking cost-effective bias mitigation might prioritize feature-wise mixing over more targeted interventions that address root causes of inequality, treating algorithmic fairness as a technical problem rather than a systemic issue requiring comprehensive social solutions. Furthermore, the technique’s success in achieving statistical parity across diverse classifier architectures might create false confidence in algorithmic neutrality, potentially reducing vigilance for other forms of discrimination not addressed by the distributional mixing approach. These concerns underscore the necessity for careful deployment protocols, continuous monitoring for unintended consequences, and recognition that technical bias mitigation techniques complement rather than replace broader efforts toward social equity and justice.

V
Conclusion

The deployment of machine learning systems across diverse geographic and socioeconomic contexts reveals a persistent challenge: contextual bias that systematically degrades performance for certain populations while remaining largely undetectable through traditional fairness metrics. This study addresses this fundamental limitation by introducing feature-wise mixing, a data-centric intervention that transforms how machine learning systems learn from contextually heterogeneous data sources.

My comprehensive empirical evaluation demonstrates the effectiveness of feature-wise mixing across diverse classifier architectures. The technique achieved an average 43.35% reduction in MSE when models transitioned from single-region training to mixed dataset approaches, with individual regional improvements reaching 78.30% for Decision Trees, 78.40% for Random Forests, and 78.40% for K-Nearest Neighbors in Indian tea market predictions. Statistical validation through 10-fold cross-validation and 2-SEM error bar analysis confirmed that these improvements exceed random variation, with non-overlapping confidence intervals establishing significance at
p<0.001
across regional comparisons.

The benchmarking analysis reveals important trade-offs within the bias mitigation landscape. While reweighted dataset approaches achieved statistically superior performance (p =
1.35\times 10^{-11}
), they require explicit bias attribute identification and impose 52% longer training times. Feature-wise mixing consistently outperformed SMOTE oversampling while maintaining computational efficiency through its preprocessing-based operation, positioning it strategically for deployment scenarios where protected attributes are unavailable or legally restricted.

From a theoretical perspective, feature-wise mixing advances data-centric machine learning by operating on distributional moments rather than individual samples. The mathematical framework disrupts context-feature covariances
\text{Cov}(x,c)
through strategic dataset blending, forcing models to learn relationships robust across diverse contextual environments. This approach addresses a gap in existing bias mitigation techniques, which primarily target demographic discrimination rather than context-specific biases emerging from geographic or institutional differences.

The practical implications extend beyond tea price prediction to domains requiring cross-institutional data integration. Healthcare systems can leverage feature-wise mixing to combine patient data across hospitals without compromising privacy, reducing diagnostic disparities between rural and urban populations through contextual bias disruption rather than demographic profiling. Financial services can mitigate geographic bias in credit scoring models, expanding access for underserved communities without explicit demographic analysis that might raise regulatory concerns. Global development initiatives can facilitate cross-country economic data collaboration while preventing neocolonial biases that historically disadvantage developing nations.

My methodology included dataset expansion from 765 monthly observations to 23,000 daily observations per region through calibrated Gaussian noise addition. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests confirmed distributional fidelity (D=0.032,
p>0.05
) between original and augmented data, ensuring that observed improvements reflected genuine bias mitigation rather than artifacts of synthetic data generation. However, this augmentation approach may limit generalizability to domains where authentic temporal data is available, suggesting future work should validate feature-wise mixing using naturally occurring high-frequency datasets.

The technique’s attribute-agnostic operation represents both a strength and limitation. While enabling deployment in privacy-constrained environments, this approach may not achieve the performance levels possible when explicit bias attributes are available and their use is ethically permissible. The 47.58% performance gap with reweighted approaches highlights this trade-off, suggesting that optimal bias mitigation strategy selection depends on deployment constraints rather than simple performance rankings.

The empirical findings illuminate several promising research directions. Theoretical optimization could develop mathematical frameworks for determining optimal mixing coefficients based on dataset characteristics and bias severity metrics. High-dimensional scalability represents another critical direction, extending feature-wise mixing to deep learning contexts where traditional bias mitigation approaches face computational and interpretability challenges. Hybrid architecture development could combine feature-wise mixing with complementary techniques to achieve superior bias reduction without requiring explicit attribute identification.

Additionally, my work suggests investigating feature-wise mixing’s effectiveness across different bias types beyond contextual bias. The technique’s success in disrupting spurious feature-context correlations indicates potential applications for temporal bias in longitudinal studies, institutional bias in multi-site research, and measurement bias arising from inconsistent data collection protocols.

This research contributes to the growing recognition that dataset composition fundamentally determines algorithmic fairness outcomes. Feature-wise mixing demonstrates that preprocessing transformations can achieve substantial bias reduction (43.35% average improvement) while maintaining computational efficiency and regulatory compliance. The validation across diverse classifier architectures—from SVMs achieving 35.18% bias reduction to Random Forests reaching 46.68% improvement—establishes the technique’s value as an infrastructure for generalizable and efficient machine learning. This establishes the technique’s value as model-agnostic infrastructure for equitable machine learning.

The broader implications align with data-centric AI principles that emphasize transforming datasets rather than modifying algorithms to improve model behavior. As machine learning systems increasingly influence high-stakes domains, techniques like feature-wise mixing that preemptively address bias through data transformation will become essential infrastructure for ethical AI deployment.

My findings suggest that the future of algorithmic fairness lies not in choosing between competing techniques, but in understanding their strategic applications and deploying them where their unique advantages create maximum benefit. Feature-wise mixing’s combination of effectiveness, efficiency, and ethical flexibility positions it as essential infrastructure for organizations seeking to deploy fair AI systems under real-world constraints where protected attributes may be unavailable, computational budgets are limited, and regulatory requirements prohibit explicit demographic profiling.

The empirical evidence supporting consistent bias reduction across multiple architectures, validated through rigorous statistical analysis, establishes feature-wise mixing as a practical solution for advancing algorithmic equity in diverse application domains. Future developments building on this foundation promise to advance the data-centric fairness paradigm toward greater impact in promoting algorithmic justice across global machine learning deployments.
