On a Trace Formula for Counting Eulerian Cycles
Ye Luo

Abstract
. Counting Eulerian cycles in undirected graphs is a classical problem in enumerative combinatorics and is #P-complete in general. We connect this problem with homological spectral graph theory and prove an explicit trace formula for the number of Eulerian cycles. For an Eulerian graph G G with m m edges and genus g g , the number ec ⁡ ( G ) \ec(G) is expressed as a signed average of traces of the m m -th powers of twisted vertex adjacency matrices A γ A_{\gamma} and twisted edge adjacency matrices B γ B_{\gamma} , indexed by H 1 ​ ( G , ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ ) H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}) . The formula yields a fixed-parameter tractable exact algorithm with parameter g g : after the 2 g 2^{g} twists are fixed, the remaining work is polynomial in the graph size. Since #P-completeness rules out a polynomial-time exact algorithm for arbitrary graphs unless FP = # ​ P \mathrm{FP}=\#\mathrm{P} , this confines the unavoidable complexity to the homological parameter in a precise sense. Finally, we develop symmetry reductions coming from spectral antisymmetry and graph automorphisms, often decreasing the number of distinct twisted spectra that must be evaluated.

1.
Introduction

An Eulerian circuit, or tour, is a closed traversal of a graph
G
that uses every edge exactly once. This concept originates in Euler’s solution of the Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem and is one of the starting points of graph theory. A connected graph is Eulerian if and only if every vertex has even degree. This existence criterion is accompanied by Hierholzer’s linear-time algorithm, which finds an Eulerian circuit in time
O(|E|)
when one exists.

The enumeration problem is much harder. We count Eulerian cycles, i.e., Eulerian circuits up to cyclic translation. For directed graphs, the counting problem is governed by the BEST theorem
[
18
]
(Theorem
2.5
), named after de Bruijn, van Aardenne-Ehrenfest, Smith, and Tutte. It reduces the number of directed Eulerian cycles to rooted arborescence counts, which are computable in polynomial time by the directed matrix-tree theorem.

For undirected graphs, however, no such efficient method is known. By reduction to the problem of counting Eulerian orientations
[
11
]
, Brightwell and Winkler
[
1
]
showed that the problem of counting Eulerian cycles for general graphs is #P-complete. Later on, the same counting problem was proved to be #P-complete for planar graphs by Creed
[
4
]
and for 4-regular graphs by Ge and Štefankovič
[
6
]
. The class #P, introduced by Valiant
[
17
]
, is the counting analogue of NP; in particular, a polynomial-time algorithm for a #P-complete counting problem would imply polynomial-time algorithms for the corresponding NP-complete decision problems. Consequently, unless
\mathrm{FP}=\#\mathrm{P}
, one cannot expect a general exact formula whose direct evaluation is polynomial-time on all graphs. A realistic goal is therefore to identify natural parameters that contain the hardness and to make the remaining computation polynomial.

Given this #P-completeness result, exact counting has mainly progressed in restricted regimes: asymptotic formulas for dense graph families, exact formulas or algorithms for structured graph classes, and general upper and lower bounds. Asymptotic formulas have been found for complete graphs by McKay and Robinson
[
10
]
, for complete bipartite graphs
[
7
]
, and for graphs with large algebraic connectivity by Isaev
[
8
]
. Chebolu, Cryan, and Martin gave a polynomial-time algorithm for generalized series-parallel graphs
[
2
]
. More recently, Punzi, Conte, Grossi, and Rizzi established upper and lower bounds for general graphs
[
14
,
13
]
.

Exact enumeration is also useful as a quantitative tool in structured applications. In genome assembly, de Bruijn-type graphs model sequence reconstruction from local read data
[
12
,
3
]
; the number of Eulerian cycles can therefore measure reconstruction ambiguity. In routing and inspection problems, Euler tours underlie the classical Chinese postman framework
[
5
]
, so exact counts measure the redundancy of complete traversals using every edge once. Exact values on tractable families also provide benchmarks for approximation, sampling, and heuristic counting methods
[
4
]
. These applications do not contradict the #P-completeness barrier; rather, they motivate exact formulas that are effective on graph classes with additional structure or small topological complexity.

This paper develops a homological spectral approach to exact Eulerian enumeration. The framework of Luo and Roy
[
9
]
counts circuits in specified homology classes by applying character sums to twisted adjacency matrices. The key observation here is that an Eulerian circuit in an undirected graph is exactly a closed walk of length
m
whose mod-
2
abelianization is the all-one circulation. This converts Eulerian enumeration into a homology-class counting problem and leads to an explicit trace formula.

2.3.
BEST theorem

An

If

Theorem 2.5

(BEST theorem)
.

Let

be an Eulerian graph and
{\mathfrak{o}}
an Eulerian orientation on

be the number of arborescences rooted at

compatible with

. Then

is independent of the root vertex

and

\ec(G,{\mathfrak{o}})=\In_{w}(G,{\mathfrak{o}})\cdot\prod_{v\in V(G)}(\outdeg_{\mathfrak{o}}(v)-1)!

where

is the out-degree of

The BEST theorem provides an efficient method of counting Eulerian cycles on an Eulerian digraph

.

need not be a quotient of
H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
, as required there. For example,
M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G))=C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
is generally not a quotient of
H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Moreover, the proofs in this section are self-contained, not relying on the comprehensive framework established in
[
9
]
.

We first define counting functions based on two types of submodules of the
{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}}
-module
C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})=M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G))
, i.e.,
M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})
for some
{\mathbf{F}}\subseteq{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)
and the first homology group
H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
.

For
\alpha=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)}a_{\mathbf{e}}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}\in C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
, we will use the notation
\alpha_{[t]}:=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)}a^{\prime}_{\mathbf{e}}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}\in C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
where
a^{\prime}_{\mathbf{e}}=a_{\mathbf{e}}\mod t
.

Definition 3.1
.

We define the following counting functions of circuits:

(i)

For a subset
{\mathbf{F}}
of
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)
and
\alpha\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})
,
N_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l)
(respectively
\widetilde{N}_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l)
) is defined to be the number of circuits (respectively closed walks)
C
of length
l
such that
C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}=\alpha
.

(ii)

For a special case
{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)
of (i), we also write
N_{t}(\alpha,l):=N_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l)
and
\widetilde{N}_{t}(\alpha,l)=\widetilde{N}_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l)
for each
\alpha\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})=C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
.

A quick result about Definition
3.1
(ii) is shown in the following lemma, which is simply a restatement of the fact that
C^{\ab}\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
for all
C\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}(G)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
\alpha\in C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})\mathbin{\backslash}H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
and any length
l
,
N_{t}(\alpha,l)=\widetilde{N}_{t}(\alpha,l)=0
.

The main results of this section are stated in the following two theorems: Theorem
3.3
provides a trace formula for
N_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l)
and
\widetilde{N}_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l)
with
\alpha\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})
(Definition
3.1
(i)), and Theorem
3.4
provides a trace formula for
N_{t}(\alpha,l)
and
\widetilde{N}_{t}(\alpha,l)
with
\alpha\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
(Definition
3.1
(ii)).

Theorem 3.3
.

For a subset
{\mathbf{F}}
of
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)
and
\alpha\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})
, we have

N_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l)=\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)

and

\widetilde{N}_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l)=\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)

for any length
l
.

Theorem 3.4
.

For a graph
G
of genus
g
, a spanning tree
T
of
G
and
\alpha\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
, we have

N_{t}(\alpha,l)=\frac{1}{t^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)

and

\widetilde{N}_{t}(\alpha,l)=\frac{1}{t^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)

for any length
l
.

Remark 3.5
.

For
t=2
, the character sums in Theorem
3.4
are ordinary Fourier transforms on the elementary abelian
2
-group
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
. Thus, after the trace values
\trace(B_{\gamma}^{l})
or
\trace(A_{\gamma}^{l})
have been computed for all
\gamma
, the counts in all homology classes can be recovered together by applying the inverse Fast Walsh–Hadamard transform. This post-processing step costs only
O(g2^{g})
arithmetic operations and does not change the dominant cost, which is the computation of the
2^{g}
trace values.

Before proving these theorems, we will first present a counting lemma (Lemma
3.6
) which provides a trace characterization of the counting problem of circuits and closed paths, and then show how the twisting of the adjacency matrices affects the trace functions.

Again, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{l}
(respectively
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{l}
) be the set of all circuits (respectively all closed walks) of length
l
on
G
. Then the following lemma states a standard fact, saying that the cardinality of
{\mathcal{C}}_{l}
(respectively
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{l}
) is essentially the trace of the
l
-th power of the edge (respectively vertex) adjacency matrix of
G
. For a square matrix
M
, denote by
\spec M
the
spectrum
of
M
, i.e., the multi-set of all eigenvalues of
M
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
A
and
B
be the vertex and edge adjacency matrices of graph
G
respectively. For any length
l
, we have

(a)

|{\mathcal{C}}_{l}|=\trace\left(B^{l}\right)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec B}\lambda^{l}
;

(b)

|\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{l}|=\trace\left(A^{l}\right)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec A}\lambda^{l}
.

Proof.

Let
A=(a_{ij})_{n\times n}
and
B=(b_{ij})_{2m\times 2m}
.

The diagonal entries of
B^{l}=(b^{(l)}_{ij})_{2m\times 2m}
can be written as

b^{(l)}_{ii}=\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{l-1}}b_{ik_{1}}b_{k_{1}k_{2}}\cdots b_{k_{l-1}i}.

Note that
b_{ik_{1}}b_{k_{1}k_{2}}\cdots b_{k_{l-1}i}=1
if and only if
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{k_{1}}\cdots{\mathbf{e}}_{k_{l-1}}
is a circuit; otherwise
b_{ik_{1}}b_{k_{1}k_{2}}\cdots b_{k_{l-1}i}=0
. Therefore,
\trace\left(B^{l}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{2m}b^{(l)}_{ii}
exactly counts the number of all circuits of length
l
on
G
, i.e.,
\trace\left(B^{l}\right)=|{\mathcal{C}}_{l}|
.

The diagonal entries of
A^{l}=(a^{(l)}_{ij})_{n\times n}
can be written as

a^{(l)}_{ii}=\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{l-1}}a_{ik_{1}}a_{k_{1}k_{2}}\cdots a_{k_{l-1}i}.

Note that by definition of vertex adjacency matrix,
a_{ik_{1}}a_{k_{1}k_{2}}\cdots a_{k_{l-1}i}
is the number of closed walks of length
l
going through the vertices
v_{i},v_{k_{1}},\cdots,v_{k_{l-1}},v_{i}
consecutively. Therefore,
\trace\left(A^{l}\right)=\sum_{v\in V}a^{(l)}_{vv}
exactly counts the number of all closed walks of length
l
on
G
, i.e.,
\trace\left(A^{l}\right)=|\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{l}|
. Analogously, we can show that
\trace\left(B^{l}\right)=|{\mathcal{C}}_{l}|
. Note that in general
\trace\left(A^{l}\right)\geq\trace\left(B^{l}\right)
, since the former counts all the closed walks (allowing backtracks and tail) while the latter only counts the circuits, i.e., the closed paths without backtracks and tail.

It remains to show that
\trace\left(A^{l}\right)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec A}\lambda^{l}
and
\trace\left(B^{l}\right)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec B}\lambda^{l}
. But this follows from a standard fact in linear algebra, i.e.,
\trace(M^{l})=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec M}\lambda^{l}
for a square matrix
M
(a simple proof can be given based on the Jordan canonical form of
M
).
∎

For
{\mathbf{F}}\subseteq{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}
, let
M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})
be naturally identified as a submodule of
M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}})=C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
. For each
\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})
and
C\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}
, we let
\chi_{\gamma}(C):=\chi_{\gamma}(C^{\ab})=\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,C^{\ab}_{[t]}\rangle}=\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}\rangle}
. The following lemma is a “twisted” version of Lemma
3.6
.

Lemma 3.7
.

We have the following identities:

(a)

\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)
is real, and
\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)=\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{l}}\chi_{\gamma}(C)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec B_{\gamma}}\lambda^{l}
;

(b)

\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)
is real, and
\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)=\sum_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{l}}\chi_{\gamma}(C)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec A_{\gamma}}\lambda^{l}
;

Proof.

Let
A_{\gamma}=(a_{ij})_{n\times n}
and
B_{\gamma}=(b_{ij})_{2m\times 2m}
.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.6
, the diagonal entries of
B_{\gamma}^{l}=\left(b^{(l)}_{ij}\right)_{2m\times 2m}
can be written as

b^{(l)}_{ii}=\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{l-1}}b_{ik_{1}}b_{k_{1}k_{2}}\cdots b_{k_{l-1}i}.

Again
b_{ik_{1}}b_{k_{1}k_{2}}\cdots b_{k_{l-1}i}
is nonzero if and only if
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{k_{1}}\cdots{\mathbf{e}}_{k_{l-1}}
is a circuit. However, the contribution from such a circuit is not necessarily
1
as in Lemma
3.6
. Actually, by the definition of twisted edge adjacency matrix (Definition
2.4
), one may verify that the contribution of
C
is exactly
\chi_{\gamma}(C)
.
Therefore,
\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)=\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{l}}\chi_{\gamma}(C)
.

The diagonal entries of
A_{\gamma}^{l}
can be written as

a^{(l)}_{vv}=\sum_{u_{1},\cdots,u_{l-1}\in V}a_{vu_{1}}a_{u_{1}u_{2}}\cdots a_{u_{l-1}v}.

Analogously,
a^{(l)}_{vv}
can be interpreted as the sum of
\chi(C)
with
C
running through all closed paths with base vertex
v
. Therefore, we have
\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)=\sum_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{l}}\chi_{\gamma}(C)
.

The identities
\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec A_{\gamma}}\lambda^{l}
and
\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec B_{\gamma}}\lambda^{l}
also follow from the standard linear algebraic fact that
\trace(M^{l})=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec M}\lambda^{l}
for a square matrix
M
.

It remains to show that
\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)
and
\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)
are all reals. Note that the eigenvalues of
A_{\gamma}
are all reals since
A_{\gamma}
is Hermitian. This implies that
\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)
must also be real for all
l
. For
\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)
, we note that circuits always come in opposite pairs, i.e., if
C
is a circuit, then reversing the traveling direction of
C
, we always get another circuit
C^{-1}
of the same length. Moreover, it is straightforward to verify that
\chi_{\gamma}(C^{-1})
is always the complex conjugate of
\chi_{\gamma}(C)
. Therefore, the identity
\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)=\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{l}}\chi_{\gamma}(C)
shows that
\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)
must always be real.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

For all
{\mathbf{F}}\subseteq{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)
,
\alpha\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})
and
C\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}
, we have the following orthogonal relation:

\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\chi_{\gamma}(C)=\begin{cases}t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|},&\text{if}\ C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}=\alpha;\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

By definition,
\chi_{\gamma}(C)=\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,C^{\ab}_{[t]}\rangle}=\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}\rangle}
. Therefore,

\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\chi_{\gamma}(C)=\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}-\alpha\rangle}.

Clearly if
C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}=\alpha
, then
\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}-\alpha\rangle}=|M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})|=t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}
.

Now suppose
C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}\neq\alpha
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
{\mathbf{F}}=\{{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{e}}_{f}\}
where
f\leq m
. Let
\alpha=a_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+\cdots+a_{f}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{f}
and
C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}=b_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+\cdots+b_{f}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{f}
. We may also assume there exists
1\leq k\leq f
such that
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
and
a_{i}=b_{i}
for
k+1\leq i\leq f
. This implies
C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}-\alpha=c_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{k}
where
c_{i}=b_{i}-a_{i}\neq 0
for
1\leq i\leq k
.

For
\gamma=\gamma_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+\cdots\gamma_{f}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{f}\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})
, we have

\langle\gamma,C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}-\alpha\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma_{i}c_{i}
. Note that
\sum_{\gamma_{i}=0}^{t-1}\epsilon_{t}^{\gamma_{i}c_{i}}=0
for each
1\leq i\leq k
, since
\epsilon_{t}^{c_{i}}
is a primitive
t/\gcd(t,c_{i})
-th root of unity. This means

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{\mathbf{F}}-\alpha\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma_{i}c_{i}}

\displaystyle=t^{f-k}\left(\sum_{\gamma_{1}=0}^{t-1}\epsilon_{t}^{\gamma_{1}c_{1}}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{\gamma_{k}=0}^{t-1}\epsilon_{t}^{\gamma_{k}c_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

and the claim is proved.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.4
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Then by Lemma
3.7
(a) and Lemma
3.8
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{l}}\chi_{\gamma}(C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{l}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\chi_{\gamma}(C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{l},C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{{\mathbf{F}}}=\alpha}t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}=N_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l).

Analogously, by Lemma
3.7
(b), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\sum_{C\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{l}}\chi_{\gamma}(C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{C\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{l}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{F}})}\epsilon_{t}^{-\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle}\chi_{\gamma}(C)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}}\sum_{C\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}_{l},C^{\ab}_{[t]}|_{{\mathbf{F}}}=\alpha}t^{|{\mathbf{F}}|}=\widetilde{N}_{t,{\mathbf{F}}}(\alpha,l).

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

As shown in Lemma
2.2
,
H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
is isomorphic to
M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))
by the restriction map
\alpha\mapsto\alpha|_{{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T)}
. This means that for all
\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))
and
\alpha\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
, we have
\langle\gamma,\alpha\rangle=\langle\gamma,\alpha|_{{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T)}\rangle
,

N_{t}(\alpha,l)=N_{t,{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T)}(\alpha|_{{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T)},l)
and
\widetilde{N}_{t}(\alpha,l)=\widetilde{N}_{t,{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T)}(\alpha|_{{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T)},l)
.
Then Theorem
3.4
is implied by Theorem
3.3
.
∎

4.
Proof of the main theorem and its directed version

By Remark
2.3
, when considering
C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
, the orientation
{\mathfrak{o}}
does not matter, and we write
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))=M_{2}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))
for a spanning tree
T
of
G
. Now let
\mathbbm{1}=1\cdot e_{1}+\cdots+1\cdot e_{m}\in C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
and
\mathbbm{1}_{T}=1\cdot e_{1}+\cdots+1\cdot e_{g}\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
where
E^{c}(T)=\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{g}\}
. As shown in Lemma
2.2
, let
\rho_{T}:H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})\xrightarrow{\sim}M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
be the restriction map which is an isomorphism.

Proposition 4.1
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

G
is an Eulerian graph;

(b)

The degree of every vertex of
G
is even;

(c)

\mathbbm{1}\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
;

(d)

\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{T})=\mathbbm{1}
for all spanning trees
T
of
G
.

(e)

G
is bridgeless (i.e.,
G\mathbin{\backslash}e
is still connected for all
e\in E
) and
\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{T})
is identical for all spanning trees
T
of
G
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (a) and (b) is actually the statement of the Euler’s Theorem.

Now suppose for each vertex
v\in V
, the degree of
v
is even. Equivalently, this means
\sum_{e\in E\text{ and }e\text{ is adjacent to }v}1=0\mod 2
for all
v\in V
, i.e., the balancing condition for
\mathbbm{1}
to be a
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
-circulation is satisfied. Hence we have the equivalence of (b) and (c).

By Lemma
2.2
, the restriction map
\rho_{T}
is an isomorphism. Then if
\mathbbm{1}\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
, then
\rho_{T}(\mathbbm{1})=\mathbbm{1}_{T}
and hence
\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{T})=\mathbbm{1}
. This means that (c) implies (d), and the converse is obviously true.

Since Eulerian graphs are clearly bridgeless, it is straightforward to see that (d) implies (e). Now suppose (e) is satisfied. Let
\alpha
be the identical inverse image of
\mathbbm{1}_{T}
for all spanning trees of
G
. Note that an edge
e\in E
is a bridge if and only if
e
appears in all spanning trees of
G
. Since
G
is bridgeless, each edge
e
must appear in some spanning trees
T
of
G
. By the nature of the restriction map
\rho_{T}
, this means that the coefficient of
e
in
\alpha
must be
1
. As a result, we see
\alpha=\mathbbm{1}
and (d) holds.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a graph with
m
edges. Then
\ec(G)=N_{2}(\mathbbm{1},m)/m=\widetilde{N}_{2}(\mathbbm{1},m)/m

Proof.

It follows from the fact that a closed walk
C
on
G
is an Eulerian circuit if and only if
\mathbbm{1}=C^{\ab}_{[2]}\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
and the length of
C
is
m
.
∎

Now let us prove the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The above lemma gives
\ec(G)=N_{2}(\mathbbm{1},m)/m=\widetilde{N}_{2}(\mathbbm{1},m)/m
. Since
G
is Eulerian by assumption,
\mathbbm{1}=\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{T})
for all spanning trees
T
of
G
by Proposition
4.1
.

Using the formula in Theorem
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle N_{2}(\mathbbm{1},m)=N_{2}(\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{T}),m)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))}(-1)^{-\langle\gamma,\mathbbm{1}\rangle}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))}(-1)^{-\langle\gamma,\mathbbm{1}_{T}\rangle}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))}(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{m}\right),

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}_{2}(\mathbbm{1},m)=\widetilde{N}_{2}(\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{T}),m)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))}(-1)^{-\langle\gamma,\mathbbm{1}\rangle}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))}(-1)^{-\langle\gamma,\mathbbm{1}_{T}\rangle}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))}(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{m}\right).

∎

Remark 4.3
.

If
G
is not Eulerian, then Theorem
1.1
is not true in general. Actually, in this case, we have
\ec(G)=N_{2}(\mathbbm{1},m)=\widetilde{N}_{2}(\mathbbm{1},m)=0
. But for each spanning tree
T
, we may still compute
N_{2}(\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{T}),m)
and
\widetilde{N}_{2}(\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{T}),m)
which are not necessarily zero. The reason is that
\mathbbm{1}\notin H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
(Proposition
4.1
) and the identity
\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{T})=\mathbbm{1}
does not hold anymore. See Example
6.1
for an example in such a case.

Now let
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
be an Eulerian digraph, which means
{\mathfrak{o}}
is an Eulerian orientation on the Eulerian graph
G
. We can also use Theorem
3.4
to count the number
\ec(G,{\mathfrak{o}})
of Eulerian cycles on
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
. In particular, for
t\geq 3
, each Eulerian orientation
{\mathfrak{o}}
induces uniquely a circulation in
H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
in a natural way. Therefore, we may prove analogously the following theorem on an explicit formula for
\ec(G,{\mathfrak{o}})
, which can be considered as a directed version of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 4.4
.

For an Eulerian graph
G
of genus
g
with
m
edges, let
T
be a spanning tree of
G
and
{\mathfrak{o}}
an Eulerian orientation of
G
. For each
t\geq 3
, we have the following trace formula for the number of Eulerian cycles on
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
:

\displaystyle\ec(G,{\mathfrak{o}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\cdot t^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))}\epsilon_{t}^{-\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\cdot t^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))}\epsilon_{t}^{-\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{m}\right),

where
\sigma(\gamma)=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T)}c_{\mathbf{e}}
for
\gamma=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T)}c_{\mathbf{e}}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}\in M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T)=\{{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{e}}_{g}\}
and
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(T)=\{{\mathbf{e}}_{g+1},\cdots,{\mathbf{e}}_{m}\}
. Let
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{o}}:=1\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+\cdots+1\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{m}\in C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
and
\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathfrak{o}},T}:=1\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+\cdots+1\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{g}\in M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))
.

Then a closed walk
C
on
G
is an Eulerian circuit compatible with the orientation
{\mathfrak{o}}
(which means
{\mathfrak{o}}
must be an Eulerian orientation) if and only
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{o}}=C^{\ab}_{[t]}\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
and the length of
C
is
m
. Hence, we must have
\ec(G,{\mathfrak{o}})=N_{t}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{o}},m)/m=\widetilde{N}_{t}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{o}},m)/m
. Let
\rho_{T}:H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})\xrightarrow{\sim}M_{t}({\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}^{c}(T))
be the restriction map which is an isomorphism (Lemma
2.2
). If
{\mathfrak{o}}
is Eulerian, then
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{o}}=\rho_{T}^{-1}(\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathfrak{o}},T})\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
, and we can complete the proof using the formulas in Theorem
3.4
as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

5.
Algorithmic consequences

We now make explicit the algorithmic content of the trace formula. This section records the complexity interpretation suggested by the character-sum formula; it is not meant to replace specialized polynomial-time algorithms for particular graph classes such as generalized series-parallel graphs. Because exact counting is #P-complete in general, the purpose of the analysis is not to claim a uniform polynomial-time algorithm, but to show that the only exponential term in the general trace-sum method is the number
2^{g}
of homological characters.

Algorithm 5.1

(Baseline trace-sum algorithm)
.

Given a connected graph
G=(V,E)
:

(1)

Check whether all vertices have even degree. If not, return
\ec(G)=0
.

(2)

Compute a spanning tree
T
and list the cotree
E^{c}(T)=\{f_{1},\cdots,f_{g}\}
.

(3)

For each
\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
, construct the twisted vertex matrix
A_{\gamma}
and compute
\tau_{\gamma}=\trace(A_{\gamma}^{m})
.

(4)

Return

\frac{1}{m2^{g}}\sum_{\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))}(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\tau_{\gamma}.

The cost of the baseline procedure is easy to read from the formula. For a graph with
n
vertices,
m
edges, and genus
g
, there are
2^{g}
twists to evaluate. Constructing a twisted vertex matrix
A_{\gamma}
only requires scanning the edges and inserting the corresponding signs or phases, so this part costs
O(m)
arithmetic operations per twist. If
\trace(A_{\gamma}^{m})
is obtained from an eigendecomposition of the Hermitian matrix
A_{\gamma}
, the dominant cost is the usual dense linear-algebra cost
O(n^{3})
per twist. Thus the vertex-matrix implementation has the indicative cost
O(2^{g}n^{3}+2^{g}m)
arithmetic operations and can store the graph and one twisted matrix at a time, using
O(n^{2}+m)
working space. The same reasoning applied directly to the edge matrices
B_{\gamma}
gives the analogous, but usually larger, cost
O(2^{g}m^{3})
and
O(m^{2})
working space. In this sense the trace formula is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to the genus: for fixed
g
, the remaining computation is polynomial in the graph size, while the unavoidable exponential dependence is isolated in the homological parameter.

Remark 5.2
.

The symmetry reductions in Theorems
7.2
–
7.15
turn the preceding bounds into bounds with fewer trace evaluations. Spectral antisymmetry replaces
2^{g}
by
2^{g-1}
for non-bipartite Eulerian graphs. If a subgroup
H\leq\Aut(G)
is used and
r_{H}
denotes the number of relevant orbits of
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
, then the orbit-reduced formula requires one trace computation per orbit representative, up to the cost of finding or supplying those representatives.

7.
Reduction of computation

The computations in Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
3.3
, and Theorem
3.4
can be reduced based on certain symmetries of the spectra distribution of the twisted adjacency matrices over
C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
.

7.1.
Reduction based on spectral antisymmetry of twisted graph adjacency

As discovered in
[
9
]
, spectral antisymmetry of twisted graph adjacency is a phenomenon that the spectra of the twisted vertex and edge adjacency matrices of a graph
G
have an antisymmetric distribution over the character group
{\mathcal{X}}(G)
of
G
with two poles being the trivial character and a special character called the
canonical character
(see Theorem 3.6(SA1) in
[
9
]
for a precise statement). The canonical character is always a
2
-torsion of
{\mathcal{X}}(G)
, and in this section, our discussion will be focused on the
2
-torsion subgroup of
{\mathcal{X}}(G)
, which is non-canonically isomorphic to
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
with
T
being any spanning tree of
G
. The canonical character has a representative in
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
, which we call the canonical element in
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
defined as follows.

Definition 7.1
.

For a spanning tree
T
of
G
, we say
\gamma_{T}=\sum_{e\in E^{c}(T)}\gamma_{e}\cdot e\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
is the
canonical element in
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
if
\gamma_{e}=0
whenever the length of the unique path on
T
connecting the adjacent vertices of
e
is odd, and
\gamma_{e}=1
otherwise.

The above definition of the canonical element in
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
is essentially the same as Construction 3.3 in
[
9
]
, and spectral antisymmetry in this special setting follows from Lemma 5.3 there. The result is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
G
be a non-bipartite Eulerian graph of genus
g
with
m
edges, and
T
a spanning tree of
G
. Let
\gamma_{T}
be the canonical element in
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
, and
\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
any partition of
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
such that for any
\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
,
\gamma\neq\gamma+\gamma_{T}
and if
\gamma\in S_{1}
, then
\gamma+\gamma_{T}\in S_{2}
. Then the number of Eulerian cycles on
G
can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\ec(G)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\cdot 2^{g-1}}\sum_{\gamma\in S_{i}}(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\cdot 2^{g-1}}\sum_{\gamma\in S_{i}}(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{m}\right)

for
i=1,2
.

Before proving Theorem
7.2
, we will first prove a series of lemmas. For each
\gamma=\sum_{e\in E^{c}(T)}\gamma_{e}\cdot e\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
, the
support
of
\gamma
is defined as
\supp(\gamma):=\{e\in E^{c}(T)|\gamma_{e}=1\}
.

Lemma 7.3
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

\gamma_{T}=0
;

(ii)

\supp(\gamma_{T})=\emptyset
;

(iii)

G
is bipartite.

Proof.

The equivalence of (i) and (ii) directly follows from definition.
Now note that the spanning tree
T
is itself a bipartite graph, meaning that we can partition
V(G)
into the disjoint union of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
such that the length of unique path on
T
connecting two vertices
v
and
w
is even if and only if both
v
and
w
belong to one of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. Therefore, an edge
e
belongs to the support of
\gamma_{T}
if and only if the adjacent vertices of
e
belong to one of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. This implies the equivalence of (ii) and (iii).
∎

Lemma 7.4
.

For a graph
G
and a spanning tree
T
of
G
, let
G^{\prime}=G\mathbin{\backslash}\supp(\gamma_{T})
, i.e.,
G^{\prime}
is derived by deleting the edges in
\supp(\gamma_{T})
from
G
. Then
G^{\prime}
is the maximal bipartite subgraph of
G
containing
T
as a subgraph.

Proof.

This is an immediate corollary of Lemma
7.3
.
∎

Lemma 7.5
.

For an Eulerian graph
G
and a spanning tree
T
of
G
, the cardinality of the support of
\gamma_{T}
has the same parity as the number of edges of
G
.

Proof.

Let
G^{\prime}=G\mathbin{\backslash}\supp(\gamma_{T})
. Then by Lemma
7.4
,
G^{\prime}
is bipartite and
E(G)=\supp(\gamma_{T})\sqcup E(G^{\prime})
. Hence we just need to show
|E(G^{\prime})|
must be even. Suppose for contradiction that
|E(G^{\prime})|
is odd. Partition
V(G)
into
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
such that each edge of
G^{\prime}
has one adjacent vertex in
V_{1}
and the other in
V_{2}
. Since the sum of the degrees in
V_{1}
is
|E(G^{\prime})|
, the graph
G^{\prime}
has an odd number of vertices in
V_{1}
with odd degree. Every edge in
\supp(\gamma_{T})
joins two vertices in the same part of this bipartition. Adding such an edge either changes no degree in
V_{1}
or changes the degrees of two vertices in
V_{1}
, so the parity of the number of odd-degree vertices in
V_{1}
is preserved. Thus
G
has an odd-degree vertex and cannot be Eulerian, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 7.6

(Spectral antisymmetry)
.

For a graph
G
, a spanning tree
T
of
G
, the canonical element
\gamma_{T}
in
M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
, and any
\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
, we have

(i)

\spec B_{\gamma+\gamma_{T}}=-\spec B_{\gamma}
and
\spec A_{\gamma+\gamma_{T}}=-\spec A_{\gamma}
, and

(ii)

for any length
l
,
\trace\left(B_{\gamma+\gamma_{T}}^{l}\right)=(-1)^{l}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)
and
\trace\left(A_{\gamma+\gamma_{T}}^{l}\right)=(-1)^{l}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)

Proof.

(i) is the specialization of Lemma 5.3 in
[
9
]
to the 2-torsion subgroup of the character group of
G
.

(ii) follows from (i) and the fact that
\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{l}\right)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec B_{\gamma}}\lambda^{l}
and
\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{l}\right)=\sum_{\lambda\in\spec A_{\gamma}}\lambda^{l}
(Lemma
3.7
).
∎

Now we can prove Theorem
7.2
.

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

By Lemma
7.3
, since
G
is non-bipartite,
\gamma_{T}\neq 0
, which means
\gamma\neq\gamma+\gamma_{T}
for all
\gamma\in M_{2}(E^{c}(T))
. Consequently, for all
\gamma\in S_{1}
,
\gamma+\gamma_{T}\in S_{2}
and vice versa. Moreover, by Lemma
7.5
and Lemma
7.6
,

(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma+\gamma_{T})}\trace\left(B_{\gamma+\gamma_{T}}^{m}\right)=(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)+\sigma(\gamma_{T})+m}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{m}\right)=(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{m}\right)

and

(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma+\gamma_{T})}\trace\left(A_{\gamma+\gamma_{T}}^{m}\right)=(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)+\sigma(\gamma_{T})+m}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{m}\right)=(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{m}\right).

This implies
\sum_{\gamma\in S_{1}}(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{m}\right)=\sum_{\gamma\in S_{2}}(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(B_{\gamma}^{m}\right)

and
\sum_{\gamma\in S_{1}}(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{m}\right)=\sum_{\gamma\in S_{2}}(-1)^{\sigma(\gamma)}\trace\left(A_{\gamma}^{m}\right)
, and the theorem follows.
∎

The following lemma provides a choice of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
for effectively applying Theorem
7.2
for computation.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
G
be a non-bipartite Eulerian graph with a spanning tree
T
. Choose arbitrarily
e\in\supp(\gamma_{T})
. Let

7.2.
Reduction based on graph automorphisms

Definition 7.9
.

An
automorphism

\tau
of
G
is composed of a permutation of
V(G)
and a permutation of
E(G)
(both permutations denoted by
\tau
by an abuse of notation) such that the adjacency relations are preserved, i.e., for all
v\in V(G)
and
e\in E(G)
,
v
is adjacent to
e
if and only if
\tau(v)
is adjacent to
\tau(e)
. All automorphisms of
G
form a group which is called the
automorphism group
of
G
, denoted by
\Aut(G)
.

If an orientation
{\mathfrak{o}}
of
G
is fixed, let
\Aut(G,{\mathfrak{o}})
denote the subgroup of
\Aut(G)
consisting of automorphisms which send every positively oriented edge with respect to
{\mathfrak{o}}
to a positively oriented edge with respect to
{\mathfrak{o}}
. Equivalently,
\Aut(G,{\mathfrak{o}})
is the automorphism group of the directed graph
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
.

Remark 7.10
.

The actions of an automorphism
\tau
of
G
on
V(G)
and
E(G)
can be naturally extended to actions on
{\mathbf{E}}(G)
and
C_{1}(G,R)
as follows:

(i)

For each pair of oppositely oriented edges
{\mathbf{e}},{\mathbf{e}}^{-1}\in{\mathbf{E}}(G)
with the same underlying edge
e\in E(G)
,
\tau({\mathbf{e}})
and
\tau({\mathbf{e}}^{-1})
are the oppositely oriented edges with the same underlying edge
\tau(e)
such that their initial vertices are
\tau({\mathbf{e}}(0))
and
\tau({\mathbf{e}}^{-1}(0))
respectively. Clearly such an action on
{\mathbf{E}}(G)
is a permutation. However, it does not preserve orientation in general. That is to say a positively oriented edge
{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)
with respect to an orientation
{\mathfrak{o}}
can possibly be mapped to a negatively oriented edge
\tau({\mathbf{e}})\in{\mathbf{E}}(G)\mathbin{\backslash}{\mathbf{E}}_{{\mathfrak{o}}}(G)
and vice versa. In this case, we say the action of
\tau
changes the orientation of
{\mathbf{e}}
with respect to
{\mathfrak{o}}
; otherwise, we say
\tau
preserves the orientation of
{\mathbf{e}}
with respect to
{\mathfrak{o}}
.

(ii)

For each
\gamma=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)}c_{{\mathbf{e}}}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}\in C_{1}(G,R)
, the action of
\tau
on
\gamma
is defined to be
\tau(\gamma)=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)}c_{{\mathbf{e}}}\cdot\tau({\mathbf{e}})
. Alternatively, we can write
\tau(\gamma)=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)}b_{{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}
where for each
{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)
,
b_{{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}}=c_{{\mathbf{e}}}
if
{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}=\tau^{-1}({\mathbf{e}})
(i.e.,
\tau
preserves the direction of
{\mathbf{e}}
) and
b_{{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}}=-c_{{\mathbf{e}}}
if
{\mathbf{e}}^{\prime}=(\tau^{-1}({\mathbf{e}}))^{-1}
(i.e.,
\tau
changes the direction of
{\mathbf{e}}
). One can also easily verify that such an action of
\tau
on
C_{1}(G,R)
is also an automorphism of
C_{1}(G,R)
.

The adjacency matrix is a graph isomorphic invariant (more precisely, for labelled graphs). The following lemma shows that the conjugacy of twisted vertex and edge adjacency matrices is invariant under automorphisms.

Lemma 7.11
.

Let
\tau
be an automorphism of
G
. For each
\gamma\in C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
,
A_{\gamma}
is similar to
A_{\tau(\gamma)}
and
B_{\gamma}
is similar to
B_{\tau(\gamma)}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathfrak{o}}
be an orientation of
G
.
Index the vertices and oriented edges of
G
as
V(G)=\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}\}
,
{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(G)=\{{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{e}}_{m}\}
and
{\mathbf{E}}(G)=\{{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{e}}_{m},{\mathbf{e}}_{m+1}={\mathbf{e}}_{1}^{-1},\cdots,{\mathbf{e}}_{2m}={\mathbf{e}}_{m}^{-1}\}
. Let
\gamma=c_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}_{m}\in C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/t{\mathbb{Z}})
. Then
\tau(\gamma)=c_{1}\cdot\tau({\mathbf{e}}_{1})+\cdots+c_{m}\cdot\tau({\mathbf{e}}_{m})
. This also means
\langle\gamma,{\mathbf{e}}\rangle=\langle\tau(\gamma),\tau({\mathbf{e}})\rangle
for each
{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}(G)
.

By definition, the twist vertex adjacency matrices are
A_{\gamma}=(a_{ij})_{n\times n}
with
a_{ij}=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}(G),{\mathbf{e}}(0)=v_{i},{\mathbf{e}}(1)=v_{j}}\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,{\mathbf{e}}\rangle}
, and
A_{\tau(\gamma)}=(a^{\prime}_{ij})_{n\times n}
with
a^{\prime}_{ij}=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}(G),{\mathbf{e}}(0)=v_{i},{\mathbf{e}}(1)=v_{j}}\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\tau(\gamma),{\mathbf{e}}\rangle}
.

Let
v_{i^{\prime}}=\tau(v_{i})
and
v_{j^{\prime}}=\tau(v_{j})
. Then since the action of
\tau
on
{\mathbf{E}}(G)
is a permutation respecting the incidence relations and the identity
\langle\gamma,{\mathbf{e}}\rangle=\langle\tau(\gamma),\tau({\mathbf{e}})\rangle
holds for all
{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}(G)
, we have

\displaystyle a^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}(G),{\mathbf{e}}(0)=\tau(v_{i}),{\mathbf{e}}(1)=\tau(v_{j})}\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\tau(\gamma),{\mathbf{e}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}(G),\tau({\mathbf{e}})(0)=\tau(v_{i}),\tau({\mathbf{e}})(1)=\tau(v_{j})}\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\tau(\gamma),\tau({\mathbf{e}})\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}(G),{\mathbf{e}}(0)=v_{i},{\mathbf{e}}(1)=v_{j}}\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,{\mathbf{e}}\rangle}=a_{ij}.

This means that the matrix
A_{\tau(\gamma)}
can be derived from
A_{\gamma}
by a permutation of rows and columns with respect to the permutation of
\tau
on
V(G)
. Hence
A_{\gamma}
is similar to
A_{\tau(\gamma)}
.

For the twisted edge adjacency matrices,
B_{\gamma}=(b_{ij})_{2m\times 2m}
(respectively
B_{\tau(\gamma)}=(b^{\prime}_{ij})_{2m\times 2m}
) such that
b_{ij}=\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rangle}
(respectively
b^{\prime}_{ij}=\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\tau(\gamma),{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rangle}
) if
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
feeds into
{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
, and
b_{ij}=0
(
b^{\prime}_{ij}=0
) otherwise.

Let
{\mathbf{e}}_{i^{\prime}}=\tau({\mathbf{e}}_{i})
and
{\mathbf{e}}_{j^{\prime}}=\tau({\mathbf{e}}_{j})
.
\tau
being an isomorphism guarantees that
{\mathbf{e}}_{i^{\prime}}
feeds into
{\mathbf{e}}_{j^{\prime}}
if and only if
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
feeds into
{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
. Then
b^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\tau(\gamma),{\mathbf{e}}_{i^{\prime}}\rangle}=\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\tau(\gamma),\tau({\mathbf{e}}_{i})\rangle}=\epsilon_{t}^{\langle\gamma,{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\rangle}=b_{ij}
if
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
feeds into
{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
, and
b^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=b_{ij}=0
otherwise. Consequently, the matrix
B_{\tau(\gamma)}
can be derived from
B_{\gamma}
by a permutation of rows and columns with respect to the permutation of
\tau
on
{\mathbf{E}}(G)
. Hence
B_{\gamma}
is similar to
B_{\tau(\gamma)}
.
∎

Concluding remarks

The formula in Theorem
1.1
should be read both as an exact enumeration formula and as a Fourier filter over the mod-
2
homology of the graph. This viewpoint explains why the genus is the natural parameter: the number of twists is the size of
H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
. Since exact counting is #P-complete in general, the value of the formula is not that it turns the whole problem into an unrestricted polynomial-time computation. Rather, it identifies a concrete source of exponential complexity and confines it to the homological parameter
g
, while the remaining trace computations are polynomial for fixed
g
and can be reduced further by the symmetries in Section
7
.

The trace form also separates two computational questions. The first is how to reduce the number of twists; this is addressed here by spectral antisymmetry, automorphism orbits, and their combined action. The second is how to evaluate each trace efficiently. For this purpose, the vertex formulation is especially useful because
A_{\gamma}
is an
n
by
n
Hermitian matrix, whereas the edge matrix
B_{\gamma}
has size
2m
and is generally nonnormal. Characteristic-polynomial trace recovery, sparse linear algebra, and determinant identities of Bass–Ihara type are natural ways to implement the individual trace evaluations without changing the homological character-sum structure.

Several further directions remain open. One direction is to sharpen the reduction step by finding canonical representatives for the combined antisymmetry-automorphism action, especially for graph families with large symmetry groups. Another is to compare the trace formula with existing exact methods on restricted classes, such as generalized series-parallel graphs, where polynomial-time algorithms are already known. Randomized trace estimation or sparse Fourier methods may also be useful for approximation on large graphs, but the alternating character sum requires careful control of cancellation errors. Applications to assembly graphs
[
12
,
3
]
, route-redundancy questions
[
5
]
, and other low-genus networks are promising because the exact formula is practical when
g
is small; however, such applications require domain-specific modeling of how graph simplification affects the Eulerian-cycle count.
