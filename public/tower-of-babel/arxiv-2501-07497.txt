Abstract

Most varieties in the geometry of tensors, e.g. the variety of d d -way tensors of slice rank at most r r , are of this form. We prove that the singular locus of a 𝐕𝐞𝐜 \mathbf{Vec} -variety is a proper closed 𝐕𝐞𝐜 \mathbf{Vec} -subvariety, analogously to the situation for ordinary finite-dimensional varieties. 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main result

A crucial observation in the geometry of tensors is that many varieties of
tensors depend functorially on the choice of a finite-dimensional vector
space. The goal of this paper is to show that the singular locus of such
a variety exhibits the same functoriality, and can hence be described in
finite terms. Our main result is a vast generalisation of the following
well-known example.

Example 1.1
.

The singular locus of the variety of
n\times n
-matrices of rank at most
r

is the variety of matrices of rank at most
r-1
. This statement is
independent of
n
, provided that
n>r
.

Let
K
be a field of characteristic zero and let
\mathbf{Vec}
denote the
category of finite-dimensional vector spaces over
K
. The varieties
that we consider are certain functors
X
from
\mathbf{Vec}
to the category of
affine schemes of finite type. For
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
, the variety
X(V)
is
a closed subvariety of an affine space
P(V)
, where

P
is a direct sum of Schur functors. Thus
P(V)
can be thought of as a
space of tensors of fixed type whose size varies with
V
, and
X(V)
is
a closed subvariety, functorial in
V
; see Section
2

for details.

In the example above,
P(V)
equals
V\otimes V
, and
X
sends

V
to the variety of tensors in
P(V)
of rank at most
r
. Note that for a
linear map
\varphi:V\to W
, the linear map
P(\varphi)=\varphi\otimes\varphi

maps
X(V)
into
X(W)
, so that
X
is, indeed, a functor from

\mathbf{Vec}
to the category of varieties over
K
.

Given a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety
X
, we look at the behaviour of
\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
,
the singular locus of
X(V)
, as
V
varies. The following is our main result.

Theorem 1.2

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
X
be a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety. Then there exists a unique closed

\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety
Y
of
X
such that
Y(V)=\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
for
all
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
with
\dim(V)\gg 0
. This
Y
satisfies
Y(V)\supset\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
for all
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
.

We will denote this
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety
Y
by
X^{\text{sing}}
. In the example
above,
X^{\text{sing}}(V)
is the variety of matrices in
V\otimes V
of rank at
most
r-1
. Note that for all
V
of dimension at most
r
,
X(V)
equals

P(V)
and is hence smooth. This shows that the inclusion
X^{\text{sing}}(V)\supset\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
can indeed be strict for small
V
.

1.2.
Local equations for
X
away from
X^{\text{sing}}

Let
X
be a closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety of a polynomial functor
P
. By
topological Noetherianity (see Theorem
2.32
below)
there exists a
U\in\mathbf{Vec}
such that for all
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
we have

X(V)=\{p\in P(V)\mid\forall\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}(V,U):P(\varphi)(p)\in X(U)\}.

In particular, all pullbacks of defining equations in
K[P(U)]
for

X(U)
, along the linear maps
P(\varphi)
, together define
X(V)
as a
set. However,
it is
not known
whether the corresponding statement holds at the
ideal-theoretical level: do those pullbacks generate the ideal of
X(V)

for all
V
, if
U
is taken sufficiently large? The following result
shows that at least outside singularities these pullbacks do define a
reduced variety.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X
be a closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety of a polynomial functor
P
.
Then there exists a
U\in\mathbf{Vec}
such that for all
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
the ideal

I_{V}\coloneqq(f\circ P(\varphi)\mid f\in I(X(U)),\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}(V,U))

of
K[P(V)]
defines
X(V)
as a set, and the reduced locus of
\operatorname{Spec}(K[P(V)]/I_{V})
contains

X(V)\setminus\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
.

In fact, by the Jacobian criterion the second statement is equivalent to the following. If
p\in X(V)
is nonsingular and
I_{V}
is generated by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
, then the evaluation of some
c\times c
-minor of the Jacobian matrix
(f_{i}/x_{j})
at
p
is nonzero, where
c=\dim P(V)-\dim_{p}X(V)
and
x_{j}\in P(V)^{*}
.

1.3.
Semi-functorial resolution of singularities

Let
X
be a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety. By functoriality, for every
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
, we have an algebraic group action of
\operatorname{GL}(V)
on
X(V)
.
Hironaka’s theorem guarantees that for every
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
,
there exists a resolution of singularities of
X(V)
. In fact, for each
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
such a resolution can be chosen to be
\operatorname{GL}(V)
-equivariant (see e.g.
[
Vil92
]
), in the sense that
\operatorname{GL}(V)

acts algebraically on the resolution space and that the resolution map is

\operatorname{GL}(V)
-equivariant.
An interesting question is whether it is possible
to find a functorial way to assign to each vector space
V\in\mathbf{Vec}

a resolution of singularities
\Omega(V)
of
X(V)
that is functorial
in
V
. Some lead comes from the following example.

Example 1.4
.

For every
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
, denote with
S^{2}(V)
the vector subspace
of
V\otimes V
of symmetric tensors (matrices), and observe that for every linear map
\varphi:V\to V^{\prime}
we get a map
S^{2}(\varphi):S^{2}(V)\to S^{2}(V^{\prime})
given as the restriction to
S^{2}(V)
of the map
\varphi\otimes\varphi
.
Let
X
be the
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety inside
S^{2}
of symmetric matrices of rank at most
r
.
Then

\Omega(V)\coloneqq\{(p,U)\mid p\in U\otimes U\}\subset S^{2}(V)\times\operatorname{Gr}(r,V)

together with the projection
\pi_{V}
on the first coordinate is a
\operatorname{GL}(V)
-equivariant resolution of singularities of
X(V)
for any vector space
V
of dimension bigger than
r

[
Wey03
, page 189]
.
Moreover, for every injective linear map
\varphi
from
V
to
V^{\prime}
, we get a map
\Omega(\varphi)
from
\Omega(V)
to
\Omega(V^{\prime})
defined by sending
(p,U)
to
(S^{2}_{\varphi}(p),\varphi(U))
, and the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega(V^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(V^{\prime}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega(\varphi)}

The above example scales back expectations. First, we need to work
with Grassmannians that behave functorially only with respect to
injective linear maps
\varphi:V\to V^{\prime}
. Second, the dimension of
X(V)

of Example
1.4
grows linearly in
\dim(V)
, so this is a resolution for one of the “smallest”

\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties only. Third, the variety
\Omega(V)
is not an affine
variety, so even if it were functorial in
V
, it would not be a

\mathbf{Vec}
-variety in our sense.

We plan to return to these issues in future work on not necessarily
affine varieties equipped with an action of the infinite general linear
group
\operatorname{GL}
. The hope is that that setting is broad enough to allow for

\operatorname{GL}
-equivariant resolutions of singularities.

In the current paper, we will restrict ourselves to the following
situation. We say that a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety
X
is of
linear type

if
\dim(X(V))
is eventually a linear function of
\dim(V)
; see
Section
4
for details. Roughly speaking, these are
the varieties that can be parameterised by finitely many vectors.
The varieties in Examples
1.1

and
1.4
are of linear type. In
Section
5
we construct an assignment
\Omega

from
\mathbf{Vec}
to finite-type schemes over
K
that behaves functorially
with respect to suitable linear maps.
We further construct a transformation
\pi
with the property that for every
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
the map
\pi_{V}:\Omega(V)\to X(V)
is a
weak
resolution
of singularities such that for most linear maps
\varphi:V\to V^{\prime}

the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega(V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega(V^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(V^{\prime}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega(\varphi)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{V}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{V^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X(\varphi)}

The adjective “weak” refers to the fact that the map
\pi_{V}
is not in general birational on the whole smooth locus of
X(V)
: smooth points can have strictly positive-dimensional fibres.

1.4.
Relation to the existing literature

Infinite-dimensional and
\operatorname{GL}
-equivariant algebraic geometry
have recently gained much attention on account of their applications
to problems in commutative algebra such as Stillman’s conjecture

[
PS09
, Problem 3.14]
. This conjecture was
first proved by Ananyan-Hochster
[
AH20
]
and later proved and
generalised using infinite-dimensional methods by Erman-Sam-Snowden

[
ESS19
,
ESS21
]
; see
[
ESS19a
]
for an introduction to these
ideas. Similar ideas have found application in tensor decomposition.
For instance, Karam’s insight that many notions of bounded rank can be
detected in small subtensors
[
Kar22
]
was made more effective
for partition rank using ideas from infinite-dimensional geometry

[
DK23
]
;
[
BDES23
]

shows that tensors in the boundary of a tensor (de-)composition map can
be approximated in a uniform manner; and
\operatorname{GL}
-equivariant geometry
also plays a crucial role in many of the open problems on tensors in

[
Ges23
]
.

These developments have led to a systematic study of
infinite-dimensional varieties with an action of the infinite
general linear group; see
[
BDES22
]
in
characteristic zero and
[
BDS24
]
in positive characteristic. The
general question is whether behaviour of finite-dimensional
algebraic varieties persists for infinite-dimensional ones if one
imposes
\operatorname{GL}
-symmetry. Successes were obtained for topological
Noetherianity
[
Dra19
,
BDD22
]
,
Chevalley’s theorem
[
Bik20
,
BDES22
]
,
and ring-theoretic Noetherianity certain polynomial representations
of degree
\leq 2

[
SS16
,
SS19
,
NSS16
,
SS22
]
.
Excitingly, ring-theoretic Noetherianity was recently disproved in
positive characteristic
[
Gan24
]
; in characteristic zero it
remains open.

Theorem
1.2
fits in this line of research. It concerns
the singular loci of varieties depending functorially on the choice of a
vector space
V
. We are certainly not the first to study the singular
loci of such varieties; here is a well-known example from the literature.

Example 1.5
.

Fix a positive integer
d\geq 3
, let
P
be the polynomial functor
V\mapsto V^{\otimes d}
, and let
Y
be the closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety in
P

defined by

Y(V)=\{v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{d}\mid v_{i}\in V\}.

Define
X
as the first secant variety of
Y
, i.e.,

X(V)=\overline{\{p+q\mid p,q\in Y(V)\}}.

So
Y
is the
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety of tensors of rank
\leq 1
, and
X

is the
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety of tensors of border rank
\leq 2
. Then,
by
[
MOZ15
, Corollary 7.17]
,
X^{\text{sing}}
has
\binom{d}{2}

irreducible components, namely,

X_{12}(V)=\{A_{12}\otimes v_{3}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{d}\mid A_{12}\in V\otimes V\text{ of rank }\leq 2\text{ and }v_{3},\ldots,v_{d}\in V\}

and its orbit under the symmetric group on
d
letters. Note that here,
too,
X^{\text{sing}}(V)
is strictly larger than
\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
if
V
is too
small. For instance, if
d=3
, then
X(K^{2})
is all of
K^{2}\otimes K^{2}\otimes K^{2}
and hence smooth, while
X^{\text{sing}}(K^{2})
is nonempty. The results from
[
MOZ15
]
were generalised from Segre
varieties to Segre-Veronese varieties in

[
KMZ20
]
.

Our Main Theorem establishes the existence of such a uniform description
of the singular locus for any
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety.
Furthermore, follow-up work
by Chiu-Danelon-Snowden relates our result to the more subtle notions
of singularity and smoothness in the realm of infinite-dimensional
varieties
[
CDS
]
.

2.
Background

2.1.
Polynomial functors and
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties

Let
K
be a field of characteristic zero and recall that
\mathbf{Vec}
is the category of finite-dimensional
K
-vector spaces with
K
-linear maps.
Let
V,W\in\mathbf{Vec}
. A
polynomial map

from
V
to
W
is an element of
\operatorname{Sym}_{K}^{\bullet}(V^{*})\otimes W
,
where
\operatorname{Sym}_{K}^{\bullet}(V^{*})
denotes the symmetric
K
-algebra on the dual space of
V
.

More concretely, let
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be the dual of a basis
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
of
V
, and let
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\}
be a basis for
W
.
An element
f
of
\operatorname{Sym}^{\bullet}_{K}(V^{*})\otimes W
is of the form:

f\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\otimes w_{i},

where the
p_{i}
are polynomials in
K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
Then
f
defines the (polynomial) map
V\to W
mapping the vector
v=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}v_{j}
with
\lambda_{i}\in K
to the vector
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})w_{i}
.
The
degree
of
f
is the maximum among the degrees of the
p_{i}
(and
-\infty
if
f=0
) and
f
is said
homogeneous of degree
d
if all the
p_{i}
are homogeneous of degree
d
.
As we are working over an infinite field, the assignment
\operatorname{Sym}^{\bullet}_{K}(V^{*})\otimes W\to\operatorname{Map}(V,W)
—the maps from
V
to
W
—is injective.

Definition 2.1
.

A polynomial functor is a functor
P
from the category
\mathbf{Vec}

to itself such that for all vector spaces
U,V\in\mathbf{Vec}
the assignment

P_{U,V}:\operatorname{Hom}(U,V)\to\operatorname{Hom}(P(U),P(V))

is a polynomial map whose degree is bounded from above by some element in

\{-\infty,0,1,\ldots\}
independent of
U,V
; the smallest such element
is called the degree of
P
and denoted
\deg(P)
. If for every

U,V
the map
P_{U,V}
is homogeneous of a fixed degree
d
, we say
that the polynomial functor
P
is homogeneous of degree
d
.
To simplify notation we write
P(\varphi)
instead of
P_{U,V}(\varphi)

when the domain
U
and the codomain
V
of a map
\varphi
are clear from
the context.

Many texts on polynomial functors allow that the degree of
P_{U,V}

goes to infinity for
\dim(U),\dim(V)
tending to infinity, so that
for instance

V\mapsto\bigwedge^{1}V\oplus\bigwedge^{2}V\oplus\cdots

is a polynomial functor. But we are only interested in polynomial functors
for which this degree can be bounded uniformly in
U,V
,
as required in the definition above.

.

Let
P
and
Q
be polynomial functors.
A polynomial transformation
\alpha:P\to Q
of polynomial functors is given by a polynomial map
\alpha_{V}:P(V)\to Q(V)
for every
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
such that for every
\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}(U,V)
the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P(U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q(U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P(V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q(V)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P(\varphi)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{U}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q(\varphi)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{V}}

commutes.

Example 2.3
.

Many varieties of tensors are (unions of) images (or image closures)
of polynomial transformations. For instance, consider the functors
Q:V\mapsto V^{\otimes d}
and
P:V\mapsto(V\oplus V^{\otimes d-1})^{r}
. Fix an
r
-tuple
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})\in[d]^{r}
, and consider the polynomial transformation
\alpha:P\to Q
given by

\alpha_{V}((v_{1},T_{1}),\ldots,(v_{r},T_{r}))\coloneqq\sigma_{1}(v_{1}\otimes T_{1})+\cdots+\sigma_{r}(v_{r}\otimes T_{r})

where
\sigma_{k}:V^{\otimes d}\to V^{\otimes d}
is the linear map
induced by

u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{d}\mapsto u_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{i_{k}}\otimes u_{1}\otimes u_{i_{k}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{d}.

A tensor in the image of
\alpha
is a sum of
r
tensors that factor
as a vector in one copy of
V
times a tensor in the remaining

(d-1)
-fold tensor power of
V
. Taking the union over all
r
-tuples

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})
, we obtain the variety of tensors with slice
rank at most
r

[
TS16
]
.

Remark 2.4
.

Polynomial functors with homogeneous polynomial transformations of
degree one form an Abelian category
\mathbf{PF}
. On the other hand, the
category
\mathbf{PF}^{\text{pol}}
of polynomial functors equipped with
all polynomial transformations is not Abelian; e.g., the image of such a
transformation may no longer be a polynomial functor.
For us, a subfunctor
Q
of a polynomial functor
P
is a subobject of
P
in the category
\mathbf{PF}
.

Remark 2.5
.

In
\mathbf{PF}
, a polynomial functor
P
is the direct sum of its homogeneous components defined by

P_{i}(V)\coloneqq\{\,p\in P(V):P(\lambda\operatorname{id}_{V})(p)=\lambda^{i}p\text{
for every }\lambda\in K\,\}.

Indeed, each
P_{i}
is a homogeneous polynomial functor of degree
i
,
and we have
P=\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}P_{i}
, where only finitely many of the
P_{i}

are nonzero (
[
FS97
, Section 2]
). We note that
P_{0}
is a constant polynomial functor, which assigns a fixed vector space
P(0)\in\mathbf{Vec}
to all
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
and the identity map to each linear map.
We call
P
pure if
P_{0}=\{0\}
.

Definition 2.6
.

We say that a polynomial functor
P
is irreducible if
P

has precisely one nonzero subobject in
\mathbf{PF}
(namely,
P

itself).

Remark 2.7
.

A polynomial functor
P
is irreducible if and only if for every

V\in\mathbf{Vec}
we have that
P(V)
is either zero or an irreducible

\operatorname{GL}(V)
-representation; this follows from

[
FS97
, Lemma 3.4]
.

2.1.1.
A well-founded order

(Isomorphism classes of) polynomial functors are partially
ordered by the relation
\prec
defined by
Q\prec P
if
Q\not\cong P
and for the largest
e
with
Q_{e}\not\cong P_{e}
the former
is a quotient of the latter. This partial order is well-founded; see

[
Dra19
, Lemma 12]
for a proof.

2.1.2.
Connection to Schur functors

We refer to
[
FH91
]
for background on
Schur functors.
A
partition

\lambda
of
length
n
is a sequence
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n})
of integers such that
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}>0
.
It corresponds to the Young diagram where the
i
-th row has
\lambda_{i}
boxes.
To each
\lambda
one associates an irreducible representation
U_{\lambda}
of the symmetric group
\operatorname{Sym}([d])
with
d=|\lambda|\coloneqq\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
. The
Schur functor

\mathbb{S}_{\lambda}
is then defined as
\mathbb{S}_{\lambda}(V)\coloneqq\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{Sym}([d])}(U_{\lambda},V^{\otimes d})
.

Example 2.8
.

If
\lambda
is the partition
(1,\dots,1)
of length
d
, then

\mathbb{S}_{\lambda}=\Lambda^{d}
, the
d
-th skew-symmetric power. At the
other extreme, if
\lambda
is the partition
(d)
of length
1
,
then
\mathbb{S}_{\lambda}=S^{d}
, the
d
-th symmetric power. General
Schur functors are constructed via a combination of symmetrisation
and skew-symmetrisation.

Remark 2.9
.

Since
K
has characteristic zero, the Schur functors are the simple
objects in
\mathbf{PF}
, and moreover every polynomial functor is a finite direct
sum of Schur functors. This follows from the corresponding statement for polynomial representations of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
and
[
FS97
, Lemma 3.4]
.

2.1.3.
Shifting

Definition 2.10
.

Given a finite-dimensional vector space
U
, we define the shift functor
\operatorname{Sh}_{U}:\mathbf{Vec}\to\mathbf{Vec}
by assigning to each
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
the vector space

, and to each map
\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)
the map
\operatorname{id}_{U}\oplus\varphi
. For a polynomial functor

the composition

and call it
the shift of

by

the vector space

Remark 2.11
.

Let

be a polynomial functor. Then

\operatorname{Sh}_{U}P\cong P\oplus P^{\prime}

with

a polynomial functor of strictly lower degree than

has
as a left inverse the natural transformation
\operatorname{Sh}_{U}P\to P
given by

P(0_{U\to V}\oplus\mathrm{id}_{V}):P(U\oplus V)\to P(V);

and for the
statement about the degree see
[
Dra19
, Lemma 14]
.

Remark 2.12
.

Let
\lambda
be a partition,
U,V
finite-dimensional vector spaces, and consider the Schur functor
\mathbb{S}_{\lambda}
.
Then we have:

(\operatorname{Sh}_{U}\mathbb{S}_{\lambda})(V)=\mathbb{S}_{\lambda}(U\oplus V)=\bigoplus_{\mu,\nu}\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mu}(U)\otimes\mathbb{S}_{\nu}(V)\right)^{N_{\mu\nu\lambda}},

where the direct sum ranges over all partitions

and

and the symbol

is the Littlewood-Richardson
coefficient: the number of copies of

in

[

.
We will use later that

and moreover the lengths of

.
Furthermore, we have

, in accordance
with Remark
2.11
.

2.1.4.
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties

Let
\mathbf{Sch}
denote the category of schemes over
K
. A polynomial functor

gives rise to a functor

\mathbf{Vec}\to\mathbf{Sch}
that sends

to the spectrum of the symmetric algebra
on

. We write
P
for this functor
\mathbf{Vec}\to\mathbf{Sch}
, as well,
and we write

for said symmetric algebra.

Remark 2.13
.

For every

the polynomial map

extends naturally to a morphism of affine schemes, which we will still denote by

. If

is any

-algebra, then on

-points

is given by a map

Via the embedding
M\in\mathbf{Mod}_{R}\mapsto\operatorname{Spec}(\operatorname{Sym}_{R}^{\bullet}(M^{*}))\in\mathbf{Sch}
maps on the right hand side
are identified with

-morphisms
P(U)\times_{K}\operatorname{Spec}R\to P(V)\times_{K}\operatorname{Spec}R
.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
X,Y:\mathbf{Vec}\to\mathbf{Sch}
be functors.
Let
\alpha:X\to Y
be a natural transformation.
We say that
\alpha
is a closed embedding if
\alpha_{V}:X(V)\to Y(V)
is a closed embedding for every

.
We say that a functor

admitting a closed
embedding
\alpha:X\to P
in some polynomial functor

is an affine
\mathbf{Vec}
-scheme.
The category of affine
\mathbf{Vec}
-schemes is the full subcategory (whose objects are affine
\mathbf{Vec}
-schemes) in the functor category
\mathbf{Sch}^{\mathbf{Vec}}
.

The following is our main object of study: it is our notion of variety in polynomial functors.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
X
be an affine
\mathbf{Vec}
-scheme.
If

is a reduced affine scheme for every

, then we say that

is a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety.

We can think of a

-variety

in

as a functor

such that
X(V)\subset P(V)
is a closed subvariety and

for every
\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}(U,V)
.

Let
X
and

be
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties.
A

of
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties is a natural transformation

\alpha:X\to Y
, i.e. given by the data of a morphism
\alpha_{V}:X(V)\to Y(V)
of affine varieties for every

such that for every

.
The category of
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties is the full subcategory, in the category of affine
\mathbf{Vec}
-schemes, whose objects are
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties.
The following proposition is immediate.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
X
be a closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety in a polynomial
functor
P
and let
\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)

(1)

if
\varphi
is injective, then
P(\varphi)
restricts to a

X(V)\to X(W)
;

(2)

if
\varphi
is surjective, then

restricts to a

; and

(3)

if

and

is a linear isomorphism, then

restricts to an automorphism

—and indeed, the map
\operatorname{GL}(V)\times X(V)\to X(V),(\varphi,p)\mapsto P(\varphi)p
is an algebraic group

Proof.

We prove the first item; the rest is proved in a similar fashion. If

\varphi
is injective, then let
\psi\in\operatorname{Hom}(W,V)
be such that

\psi\circ\varphi=\operatorname{id}_{V}
. Then
P(\psi)\circ P(\varphi)=P(\psi\circ\varphi)=P(\operatorname{id}_{V})=\operatorname{id}_{P(V)}
by functoriality. It follows that

is an injective linear map, hence defines a closed embedding

, and this restricts to a closed embedding

.
∎

Lemma 2.22
.

In the setting of Lemma

is
the minimal subspace promised by that Lemma,

is an extension

Proof.

By Lemma

. Then for

2.2.
The embedding Theorem and its consequences

We collect several results about
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties. The following theorem
is implicit in
[
Dra19
]
, and it was made explicit in the infinite-dimensional setting in
[

]
.

Theorem 2.30

(Embedding Theorem)
.

Let
P
be a polynomial functor and let
X
be a proper
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety of
P
.
Let
R
be an irreducible subfunctor of
P
of degree
>0
and let
\pi:P\to P/R
be the projection transformation.
Let
X^{\prime}
be the closure of the projection of
X
along
\pi
.
Then one of the following holds:

(1)

X=\pi^{-1}(X^{\prime})\cong X^{\prime}\times R
; or

(2)

there exists a finite-dimensional
vector space
U
and a function
h\in K[P(U)]
that does not
vanish identically on
X(U)
such that the projection
\operatorname{Sh}_{U}P\to(\operatorname{Sh}_{U}P)/R
restricts to a closed embedding

(\operatorname{Sh}_{U}X)[1/h]\to((\operatorname{Sh}_{U}P)/R)[1/h].

Furthermore,
h
can be chosen as the directional derivative

\frac{f}{r}
for some vector

and
some function

that vanishes identically on
X(U)
,
and for any
h
constructed in this manner the map above is a closed

In the last statement, it may be that the partial derivative does vanish

Remark 2.31
.

In case (1) the isomorphism comes from the fact that

as polynomial functors (since

In case (2) we identify

We recap below some of the applications of the Embedding Theorem.

2.2.1.
Topological Noetherianity of polynomial functors.

Theorem 2.32

(
[
Dra19
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
X
be a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety. Then every descending chain of
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvarieties

stabilises, that is, there exists
N\geq 0
such that for each
n\geq N
we have
X_{n}=X_{n+1}
. Equivalently, for any closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety

of

there exist finitely many
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}\in\mathbf{Vec}
and

such that for all

we have

Y(V)=\{p\in X(V)\mid\forall i\;\forall\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}(V,U_{i}):f_{i}(X(\varphi)p)=0\}.

In the latter setting, we say that the
f_{i}
define the
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety
Y
in
X
.

Definition 2.33
.

A
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety

is called irreducible if it is non-empty and
if

for closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvarieties

of

implies
that

or
X=Z
.

Irreducibility of

is equivalent to the condition that

is
irreducible for all

. By Noetherianity, every
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety
can be written in a unique manner as
X_{1}\cup\cdots\cup X_{k}
where
the
X_{i}
are irreducible and none is contained in any other; the
X_{i}

are the

of

.

2.2.2.

Next we recall the shift theorem in

]
.
We rephrase it here in the language of polynomial functors and

For a proof in the functorial setting, see

.

If

2.2.3.
Dimension

The following is well-known.

Proposition 2.36
.

Let

in

of degree

We call the polynomial

the dimension function of

Definition 2.37
.

Let

be a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety. We define the dimension function

of

f_{X}(n)\coloneqq\dim(X(K^{n})).

Proposition 2.38
.

If
X
is a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety, then there exists a polynomial
p_{X}
with
rational coefficients such that for all

.

Proof.

By Noetherianity, we may assume that

is irreducible. By

2.34
, for some nonnegative integer

we have,
for all

\dim(X(K^{m}\oplus K^{n}))=\dim((\operatorname{Sh}_{K^{m}}X)(K^{n})[1/h])=\dim(B)+\dim(Q(K^{n})),

so the result follows from Proposition

applied to

.
∎

There are alternative proofs of this statement, see for example

[
Dan23
, Proposition 2.4.7]
—indeed, even a more general

in
[

, Proposition 86]
.

3.
Proof of the main theorem

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
. That is, given a

\mathbf{Vec}
-variety

, we have to show that there exists a unique closed

\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety
Y
of

such that
Y(V)=\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
for all
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
of sufficiently large dimension; and that this

satisfies

for all
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
.

Along the way, following the same inductive approach as
for Theorem
1.2
, we will also establish
Theorem
1.3
.

3.1.
Uniqueness of
Y

The uniqueness of the
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety
Y\subset X
such that
Y(V)=\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
for all
V
of sufficiently high dimension follows
immediately from the fact that
Y(V)
for
\dim(V)\gg 0
determines

Y(V)
for small

by Proposition
2.16
.

3.2.
Singular points remain singular

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X
be a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety,
U\in\mathbf{Vec}
, and
p
a singular point
in
X(U)
. Then for any
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
and any injective linear map

\iota:U\to V
, the point
X(\iota)(p)
is singular in
X(V)
.

Proof.

Let
\pi:V\to U
be a linear map such that
\pi\circ\iota=\mathrm{id}_{U}
.
Then
X(\pi)\circ X(\iota)=\mathrm{id}_{X(U)}
, and it follows that for any
p\in X(U)
, the map
T_{X(\iota)(p)}X(\pi):T_{X(\iota)(p)}X(V)\to T_{p}X(U)
is surjective. Then the claim follows by e.g.
[
CFD22
, Remark 6.8]
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
Y
be a closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety of
X
with
Y(V)=\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))

for all
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
with
\dim(V)\gg 0
. Then
Y(V)\supset\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))

for all
V
.

Proof.

Let
p\in\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
and let
W
with
\dim(W)\gg 0
, so that
there exists an injective linear map
\iota:V\to W
and moreover

\operatorname{Sing}(X(W))=Y(W)
. Then
X(\iota)
maps
p
into
\operatorname{Sing}(X(W))=Y(W)

by Lemma
3.1
. Hence, with
\pi
as in the proof of
Lemma
3.1
, we have
p=X(\pi)X(\iota)\in Y(V)
.
∎

In what follows, we will therefore restrict ourselves to the existence
of
Y
with the property that
Y(V)=\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
for all
V
with

\dim(V)\gg 0
.

3.3.
Reduction to
K
algebraically closed

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be a
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety and let
\overline{K}
denote an algebraic closure of
K
. Write
X_{\overline{K}}
for the base change of
X
, see Remark
2.17
. If Theorem
1.2
holds for the
\mathbf{Vec}_{\overline{K}}
-variety

, then it holds for
X
.

Proof.

For
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
write
V_{\overline{K}}\coloneqq V\otimes_{K}\overline{K}
. Then the morphism

\pi_{V}\colon X_{\overline{K}}(V_{\overline{K}})=X(V)\times_{K}\operatorname{Spec}\overline{K}\to X(V)

is closed. Hence, given any closed
\mathbf{Vec}_{\overline{K}}
-subvariety
\widetilde{Y}
of
X_{\overline{K}}
, setting

defines a closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety of

.

Conversely, we have that the image of
\operatorname{Sing}(X_{\overline{K}}(V_{\overline{K}}))
under
\pi_{V}
equals
\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
. For example, if
X(V)
is integral, then its singular locus is given by the Fitting ideal
F^{d}_{V}\coloneqq\operatorname{Fitt}^{d}(\Omega_{X(V)/K})
, where

. But then
X(V)\times_{K}\operatorname{Spec}\overline{K}
is equidimensional of dimension

, so its singular locus is given by the pullback of

. Since
\pi_{V}
is flat, the image of the closed subset defined by the pullback of

equals that of
F^{d}_{V}
itself.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety of a polynomial functor
P
.
If Theorem
1.3
holds for
X_{\overline{K}}\subseteq P_{\overline{K}}
,
then it also holds for

.

Proof.

Let

have the property of

in

, but for

. Without loss of

, where

. Then the ideal
of

in

by taking the tensor product with

. Hence if

for

∎

Until the end of this section, we assume that

. By point we mean a closed (

-rational) one.

3.4.
Reduction to the irreducible case

Lemma 3.5
.

If Theorem
1.2
holds for irreducible

\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties, then it holds for all
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties.

Proof.

By Noetherianity (Theorem
2.32
), the
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety

admits
a unique decomposition

with

a nonnegative integer and each

an irreducible
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety that is not
contained in the union
\bigcup_{j\neq i}X_{j}
.
This means that
X_{i}(V)\not\subset\bigcup_{j\in[s]\setminus\{i\}}X_{j}(V)
for all vector spaces

of sufficiently large

with

, we have

\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))=\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}\operatorname{Sing}(X_{i}(V))\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[s];\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}(X_{i}(V)\cap X_{j}(V))\right).

By assumption,
X_{i}
has a closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety

such that

\operatorname{Sing}(X_{i}(V))=Y_{i}(V)
for all

. Now the closed

\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety

has the property that
\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))=Y(V)
for all

with

\dim(V)\gg 0
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

If Theorem
1.3
holds for irreducible
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties,
then it holds for all
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties.

Proof.

Let
X=X_{1}\cup\cdots\cup X_{s}
denote the (non-redundant) decomposition
of

into irreducible
\mathbf{Vec}
-varieties, and let

be

for all

is contained

with

for

.

First of all, if a point

is

for

, so that

of pullbacks

as a set.

Next let

and let

be a nonsingular point. We may
assume that

, since otherwise the pullbacks of the

generate a radical ideal in

. It then

is contained in any

with

, so
that

,

.
Let

. By the irreducible case of Theorem

, there exist

Note that for fixed

locally at

.

∎

3.5.
The irreducible case

Proof of Theorems
1.2
for irreducible
X
.

By definition, the irreducible
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety

is a closed
\mathbf{Vec}
-subvariety of a polynomial functor

using the well-founded order from Section

P(V)=P(0)

Suppose next that

of

. We write

\mathbf{Vec}

The previous paragraph applies as long as there exists an irreducible

More precisely, we define a closed

of

Proof.

Since polynomial functors in characteristic zero form a semisimple

is the sum of its irreducible subfunctors.
Therefore, the directional derivative

We construct the Jacobi-matrix for

as follows: the rows

Proposition 4.2

-variety and let

(1)\implies(2)

(2)\implies(3)

and by the construction we have

\displaystyle\dim(\Omega(V))=\dim(\operatorname{Hom}^{0}(K^{d},V))+\dim(Z)-\dim(\operatorname{GL}_{d})

\displaystyle=d\dim(V)+\dim(X(K^{d}))-d^{2}=\dim(X(K^{d}))+d(\dim(V)-d).\qed

This concludes the proof of Theorem
5.3
.

5.4.
Examples

We now illustrate some strengths and weaknesses of the weak
resolution
\rho_{V}:\Omega(V)\to X(V)
through a number of examples. We
start with an example where
\rho_{V}
agrees with an earlier example.

Example 5.8

(Example
1.4
revisited)
.

Let
X\subset S^{2}
be the variety of symmetric matrices of rank

\leq r
. Then
d=r
, since any
p\in X(V)
of rank equal to
r

lies in
X(U)
for unique
r
-dimensional
U\subset V
. We have

X(K^{r})=S^{2}(K^{r})
, which is smooth, and hence
Z=X(K^{r})
and
\rho

is the identity. Now, for
V
of dimension
\geq r
, we have

\Omega(V)=(\operatorname{Hom}^{0}(K^{r},V)\times S^{2}(K^{r}))/\operatorname{GL}_{r}

and
\rho_{V}([\varphi,p])=S^{2}(\varphi)p
. This is readily seen to
be isomorphic, even functorially in
V
, to the resolution of
Example
1.4
.

Next an example that shows that
\rho_{V}
is, in general, not an actual
resolution of singularities.

Example 5.9
.

Consider the
\mathbf{Vec}
-variety
X=S^{1}
. We have

\dim(X(K^{n}))=\dim(K^{n})=n
, so
d=1
. The variety
X(K^{1})
is the
affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1}
, hence smooth, so
Z=X(K^{1})=\mathbb{A}^{1}
, and
\rho
is
the identity map. Now, for all
V
of dimension at least
1
, we have

\Omega(V)=(\operatorname{Hom}^{0}(K^{1},V)\times\mathbb{A}^{1})/\operatorname{GL}_{1}\cong((V\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{A}^{1})/\operatorname{GL}_{1},

and under this isomorphism we have
\rho_{V}([v,z])=zv
. So
\rho_{V}

is the blow-up of the affine space
V
in the origin.

Example 5.10

(Example
5.1
revisited)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\leq r}\subset T^{2}
be the variety of rank
\leq r
-matrices. The smallest subspace
U
for which a rank-
r
element of
V\otimes V
lies in
U\otimes U
is typically
2r
-dimensional, so
d=2r
.
As in Example
5.1
we define

Z\coloneqq\{(p,U,W)\in(K^{2r}\otimes K^{2r})\times\operatorname{Gr}(r,K^{2r})\times\operatorname{Gr}(r,K^{2r})\mid p\in U\otimes W\}

and let
\rho:Z\to X(K^{2r})
be the projection to the first factor. We
have

\Omega(V)=(\operatorname{Hom}^{0}(K^{2r},V)\times Z)/\operatorname{GL}_{2r}

and
\rho_{V}([\varphi,(p,U,W)]=T^{2}(\varphi)(p)
. We find that the map
\rho_{V}

has positive-dimensional fibres over all matrices
p\in X(V)
whose
row space and column space together span a space of dimension

<2r
. This includes all singular points of
X(V)
, whose row and column
space each are of dimension
<r
, but many more. On the other hand,

\rho_{V}
is an isomorphism over the open subscheme ov
X(V)
where the
row and column space together span a space of dimension equal to
2r
.

Remark 5.11
.

In the setting of Proposition
5.5
, a point
p\in X(V)
for which
U_{p}
has dimension
d
is nonsingular in
X(V)
if
and only if it is nonsingular in
X(U_{p})
. “Only if” is the content
of Lemma
3.1
(and was used above). For “if”,
we observe that the morphism defined in Remark
5.6
is actually
defined on all points for which
X(U_{p})
is nonsingular.

Recall that in Section
4
we established the existence of a
bound
F(d,c)
such that for all
V\in\mathbf{Vec}
of dimension
d+k
with

k\geq F(d,c)
we have
X^{\text{sing}}(V)=\operatorname{Sing}(X(V))
. It would be nice to
have a much more explicit bound, e.g. linear in
d
. The main obstacle
to obtaining such a bound is that for nonsingular points
p\in X(K^{d})

with
\dim(U_{p})<d
we do not yet have a more explicit bound on
k
where
we need to test whether
p
is still nonsingular in
X(K^{d}\oplus K^{k})
.
