The Spectrum of Bacterial Infection in a Large Cohort of Chinese Pediatric Patients with Inborn Errors of Immunity: A Nine-year, Retrospective, Single-center Study
Authors: Xiaodan Zhu, Pan Fu, Wenjie Wang, Wenjing Ying, Bijun Sun, Jia Hou, Xiaoying Hui, Jinqiao Sun, Chuanqing Wang, Qinhua Zhou, Xiaochuan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the spectrum of bacterial infections in children with inborn error of immunity (IEIs).MethodsPediatric patients with IEIs and positive for bacteria considered to be pathogenic were included in this retrospective study.ResultsIn this study, 1811 medical records of IEI inpatients were reviewed, and 243 IEI patients with 290 hospitalizations were enrolled. A total of 361 strains were detected, of which, 83 (22.99%) were gram-positive bacteria, and 278 (77.01%) were gram-negative bacteria. The main bacteria isolated from different IEI classifications were different. Patients with combined immunodeficiencies were more likely to have Klebsiella pneumoniae (12.68%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (12.68%) isolated. Patients with predominant antibody deficiencies were more prone to the isolation of Haemophilus influenzae (31.82%) and Moraxella catarrhalis (13.64%). Patients with congenital defects of phagocytes were more frequently associated with the isolation of K. pneumoniae (16.84%) and Escherichia coli (11.58%). Patients with different classifications of IEI were susceptible to specific bacteria. Salmonella was often isolated from patients with defects in intrinsic and innate immunity (4.23%), and Staphylococcus aureus was often isolated from patients with combined immunodeficiencies with syndromic features (5.52%). The percentages of methicillin-resistant S. aureus, carbapenem-resistant E. coli, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii in IEI patients were55.57%, 38.10%, 25.71%, 25.81%, and 70.59%, respectively, and these values were greater than those in non-IEI patients.ConclusionChildren with IEIs exhibit a unique spectrum of bacterial infections. Bacteria isolated from children with IEIs have high antimicrobial resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10875-025-01964-1.

Introduction

Inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) are a group of rare genetic diseases that affect the function of the immune system [1]. The presence of IEIs of the host defense system involving one or more components increases susceptibility to infectious diseases, and the risk of other immune-related diseases, including allergies, immune dysregulation, lymphocyte proliferation, and malignant tumors [2]. The frequency and severity of bacterial infections have improved with advances in the diagnosis, treatment, and bacterial detection of IEIs. However, because infections in patients with IEIs are usually due to common pathogens, difficult to eradicate, and prone to spread, infection remains the most common complication in these patients.

There are two types of infection scenarios. Some IEIs lead to broad susceptibility to pathogens, whereas other IEIs have a narrow spectrum. Multiple immune components are impaired in Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)patients, resulting in susceptibility to a wide range of pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi [3]. X-linked agammaglobulinemia (XLA) patients are susceptible to capsulated bacteria, including Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus [4]. Elastase, and neutrophil-expressed (ELANE) mutations cause severe congenital neutropenia and are prone to bacterial infections, including S. aureus, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [5]. Patients with chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) are susceptible to catalase-positive microorganisms, including S. aureus and Salmonella, due to the absence or dysfunction of Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate (NADPH) oxidase [6, 7].

Some IEIs have a relatively narrow microbial susceptibility spectrum and are thought to be associated with single gene defects that affect immunity [8]. In these rare cases, approximately half of the Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) can be explained by single-gene lesions [9]. The mutated genes found in MSMD are mainly involved in interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) signaling, and these patients are susceptible to Mycobacterium, Salmonella, and Candida infection [8]. STAT3 mutations lead to defects in TH17 cell differentiation and reduced production of IL17A and IL-22, reducing the levels of anti-staphylococcal host factors in keratinocytes and bronchial epithelial cells [10]. It has recently been reported that OTULIN haploinsufficiency affects innate immunity and is associated with staphylococcal disease [11]. Currently, studies on the spectrum of susceptible bacteria have focused mainly on certain types of diseases, such as CGD, XLA, and MSMD. However, there are many types of IEIs and bacteria, and many unfamiliar bacterial spectra remain to be elucidated.

In addition to the spectrum of bacterial infections in patients with IEIs, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is also an important concern. Relevant studies have shown that the epidemic characteristics and AMR of bacteria in children differ from those in adults, and multidrug-resistant organisms exhibit increased antibiotic resistance [12]. It has been reported that the AMR in IEI patients is greater than that in patients with normal immune function [13]. At present, there is a lack of relevant studies on AMR in large samples of IEI children.

This study retrospectively reviewed the spectra of bacterial infection and AMR in a large cohort of IEI children in a tertiary referral center, which is one of the major IEI treatment centers in China. This study also aimed to further broaden the spectrum of bacterial infection in IEI and characterize the AMR features of bacterial infection in IEIs.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study collected data from patients admitted to the Department of Immunology at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University from April 2014 to December 2022. Patients were under 18 years of age. In this cohort, IEI was diagnosed according to clinical manifestations, immunological indicators, and genetic tests. The enrolled patients with IEIs were required to have a positive bacterial etiology assessment (bacterial culture and/or C. difficile toxin test), and the detected bacteria were pathogenic.

Patient Enrollment

A total of 1,811 electronic medical records of IEI patients hospitalized at the Department of Clinical Immunology. Overall, 290 hospitalizations of 243 patients with IEIs who were considered to have bacterial pathogen infections were included in this study (The detailed procedure was provided in Fig. 1). A total of 114 non-IEI patients with positive bacterial cultures who were hospitalized during the same period at the Department of Clinical Immunology were included as controls. Detailed information on the definitions and classifications of IEI, clinical data collection, study definitions, bacterial identification and the AMR test were available in the supplementary materials.

Results

Demographic characteristics, Isolated Bacterial sites, and Classification of IEI in Children from 2014 To 2022

The cohort of patients with IEI was predominantly male (228 males, 62 females), with a median age of onset at 0.33 years, a median age of diagnosis at 1.46 years, and a median age of admission at 2.25 years. The primary sites of bacterial isolation included the respiratory tract (57.06%), digestive tract (14.68%), and skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs) (17.17%). The major types of IEI demonstrated stability over the years, combined immunodeficiencies (17.93%), predominantly antibody deficiencies (33.45%), and congenital defects of phagocytes (24.83%). Notably, the predominant type of bacterial infection observed in this study was community-acquired infections (Table 1). The immunological features and genetic mutations of patients with IEI were also analyzed (Table S1−4). The majority of non-IEI patients were male (75 males and 39 females), and the median age at admission was 1.08 years. The main site of bacteria isolated from non-IEI patients was the respiratory tract (78.52%) (Table S5).

Total and Main Infection Sites of the Bacterial Spectrum in IEI Patients

A total of 361 strains of bacteria were isolated from 290 IEI hospitalized patients, of which 83 strains were gram-positive and 278 strains were gram-negative. The bacteria isolated from IEI patients were mainly gram-negative bacteria (77.01%), among which H. influenzae (18.71%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (12.59%) and Salmonella 12.23%) were the main bacteria. Among the isolated gram-positive bacteria (22.99%), S. aureus (33.73%), S. pneumoniae (28.92%), and C. difficile (15.66%) were the primary bacteria. The main gram-positive bacteria isolated from the respiratory tract were S. pneumoniae and S. aureus, and the main gram-negative bacteria were H. influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, and P. aeruginosa. The digestive tract isolates were mainly Salmonella. The bacteria isolated from the skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs) were mainly S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli. The gram-positive bacteria isolated from the bloodstream were mainly E. faecium, and gram-negative bacteria were mainly P. aeruginosa and Salmonella. The urinary tract isolates were mainly E. coli. The predominant pathogens causing infections at different body sites were summarized in Fig. 2. A total of 135 pathogenic bacteria were isolated from 114 hospitalizations with non-IEI, of which 60 strains (44.44%) were gram-positive, and 75 strains (55.56%) were gram-negative (Fig. S1).

Different IEI Classifications Have Different Susceptible Bacterial Spectra

The respiratory tract was the primary site of infections in patients with combined immunodeficiencies, followed by SSTIs. The most isolated bacteria were K. pneumoniae (12.68%), P. aeruginosa (12.68%), and Acinetobacter baumannii (9.86%), all of which were mainly derived from the respiratory tract (Fig. 3A). The respiratory tract was the leading infection site in patients with combined immunodeficiencies with syndromic features, followed by the SSTIs. The main bacteria species isolated from the SSTIs was S. aureus (25.71%) (Fig. 3B). The most prominent infection site for children with predominant antibody deficiencies was the respiratory tract, and the total bacterial spectrum was highly coincident with the spectrum of the respiratory tract (Fig. 3C). The most isolated bacteria included H. influenzae (31.82%), M. catarrhalis (13.64%), and P. aeruginosa (11.82%), all of which were mainly derived from the respiratory tract. The respiratory tract was the main site of infection in patients with congenital defects of phagocytes, followed by the SSTIs (Fig. 3D). The most isolated bacteria were K. pneumoniae (16.84%), E. coli (11.58%), and salmonella (10.53%), K. pneumoniae and E. coli were mainly isolated from the SSTIs, whereas Salmonella was isolated from the digestive tract. The susceptible sites of intrinsic and innate immunity defects were the respiratory tract, followed by the intestinal tract. Salmonella (21.62%) and K. pneumoniae (13.51%) were the most isolated bacteria. Salmonella mainly originated from the intestinal tract, whereas K. pneumoniae primarily originated from the respiratory tract (Fig. 3E). This study also summarized the common specific types of IEIs, including SCID, HIES, XLA, congenital neutropenia, and CGD (Fig. 3F and J). In addition, this study further analyzed the types of infections in SSTIs. Superficial skin infections were more common in patients with combined immunodeficiencies, with S. aureus being the predominant pathogen. In contrast, abscesses were more frequently observed in patients with congenital defects of phagocytes, where K. pneumoniae was the primary causative agent (Figure S2).

Specific Bacteria Define Certain IEIs

The isolation rates for specific bacteria in different classifications of IEIs were summarized, and the findings indicated bacterial infection susceptibility (Fig. S3). H. influenzae was often isolated in patients with predominantly antibody deficiencies (6.36%), followed by those with combined immunodeficiencies (2.7%). K. pneumoniae was often isolated from patients with combined immunodeficiencies (3.47%) and congenital defects of phagocytes (3.51%). Salmonella easily distinguished patients with defects in intrinsic and innate immunity (4.23%) and congenital defects of phagocytes (2.19%). P. aeruginosa was often isolated from patients with combined immunodeficiencies (3.47%) and predominantly antibody deficiencies (2.00%). S. aureus infection was common in patients with combined immunodeficiencies with syndromic features (5.52%) and combined immunodeficiencies (1.93%). M. catarrhalis infection was common in patients with predominantly antibody deficiencies (2.73%). S. pneumoniae was often isolated in patients with predominantly antibody deficiencies (2.00%) and autoinflammatory diseases (3.13%). E. coli was often isolated from patients with combined immunodeficiencies (1.93%) and congenital defects of phagocytes (2.19%). A. baumannii was often isolated from patients with combined immunodeficiencies (2.70%) and combined immunodeficiencies with syndromic features (1.84%). C. difficile infection occurred in patients with combined immunodeficiencies with syndromic features (1.84%). Next, the genotypes of IEI infected with specific pathogens were summarized (Fig. S4). Children without genetic testing or with negative genetic testing were included in the unknown category.

Common drug-resistant Bacteria in Children with IEIs Exhibit Have High AMR

This study summarized the AMR of common drug-resistant bacteria in IEI patients(15, 25–31). For H. influenzae and M. catarrhalis, the β-lactamase production rates were 68.63% and 100%, respectively. According to the penicillin susceptibility test, only one strain of S. pneumoniae was moderately resistant, and the other strains were sensitive. The PNSP rate was 4.35% (noncentral nervous system, [non-CNS]). The rate of MRSA was 53.57%. Three strains of K. pneumoniae did not have extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production data. The EBSL production rates of K. pneumoniae and CRKP detection rates were 78.13% and 25.71%, respectively. The EBSL production rate of E. coli and CREC detection rates were 71.43% and 38.10%, respectively. Two P. aeruginosa strains were cultured in other hospitals and had no initial drug susceptibility results, so the AMR assessment was not included. The CRPA and CRAB detection rates were 25.81% and 70.59%, respectively (Table 2).

Discussion

Using data from one of the major IEI referral centers in China, this retrospective study summarized bacterial infection spectrum of a large cohort of IEI patients over a 9-year period. Patients with IEIs have a specific bacterial infection spectrum; some have a broad spectrum, and some have a narrow spectrum. Immune genetics have a very strong impact on bacterial infection. This study also showed that AMR was also a severe problem for IEI patients.

IEI patients had a very specific spectrum of bacterial infections. The isolated bacteria were primarily from the respiratory tract, and gram-negative bacteria were predominant. This finding is consistent with previous reports from non-IEI [13]. This study assessed pathogenic bacteria, and the composition of gram-negative bacteria was greater than that previously reported and our non-IEI group. Several kinds of opportunistic bacteria dominate the spectrum, such as P. aeruginosa, C. difficile, and A. baumannii, due to the weakened immunity of IEI patients.

Different isolation sites had different susceptible bacteria. The bacterial species isolated from the respiratory tract of IEI patients were similar to those isolated from non-IEI patients and were mainly gram-negative bacteria. The composition of conditional pathogens isolated from the respiratory tract of IEI patients, including P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii and S. maltophilia, was greater than that of non-IEI patients, and this finding is possibly related to impaired immune function and long-term antibiotics [14]. The intestinal strains isolated in this study were similar to those in other studies of non-IEI patients, with Salmonella and Campylobacter identified as the main pathogenic bacteria [15, 16]. However, E. coli was the primary fecal isolate in normal children. However, this bacterium may be related to the different degrees of immune problems in the included patients. C. -difficile intestinal infections were also common in this study and differed from those in normal children. Bacteria isolated from SSTIs of children with IEIs are common skin isolates, but the isolation rate of gram-negative bacteria seems to be greater than that of the general population [17, 18].

Different types of IEIs have different features of bacterial infection in terms of infection site and bacterial spectrum. The respiratory tract infection was the main bacterial infection site, which was consistent with previous reports [19–21]. The bacterial spectrum in the respiratory tract differed among major types of IEI, whereas H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis, and S. pneumoniae were the top three bacteria for IEI patients with predominantly antibody deficiency. S. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii were the major bacteria for the combined immunodeficiency IEI classification. The SSTIs also represent prominent sites of infection in patients with combined immunodeficiencies with syndromic features and congenital defects of phagocytes. The bacterial infection spectrum of relatively common IEIs was also summarized, and different types of IEIs had quite different bacterial infection spectra.

IEI profiles are susceptible to specific pathogens. Several studies have shown that H. influenzae is a susceptible bacterium that is common in children with predominantly antibody deficiencies, and our study also supported this point [22–24]. It has been reported that the B cell immune response stimulated by H.influenzae extract in vitro is impaired in patients with common variable immunodeficiency [23]. Regarding intracellular bacteria, such as Mycobacterium and Salmonella, macrophages recognize these microorganisms and remove pathogens by activating the IFN-γ pathway [25]. In this study, children with intrinsic and innate immunity, most of whom were MSMD patients, were susceptible to Salmonella infection. The basic immune mechanisms of anti-S. pneumoniae include the opsonization of antibodies or complement agents and the phagocytosis of splenic macrophages [26]. In our study, IEI patients with predominantly antibody deficiencies were susceptible to S. pneumoniae. The bacteria-related susceptibility IEI spectrum in this study is similar to that in previous reports, but there are also different features. This study included P. aeruginosa susceptible XLA patients, and we found that children with IEIs characterized by combined immunodeficiency were also susceptible to this pathogen. S. maltophilia is not common in patients with IEIs, but its susceptibility pattern is unclear. A recent study suggested that the S. maltophilia infection rate in CGD patients was greater than that in patients with other types of immunodeficiency [27]. However, no CGD patients had S. maltophilia infection in our study. In addition, our study provides a relatively complete summary of the IEI spectra of some bacteria that are not often reported, including E. coli, A. baumannii, C. difficile, and Campylobacter. Specific bacterial susceptibility genotypes were also analyzed in this study, but the representativeness of the conclusions of this part of the study may be insufficient.

In contrast to previous AMR reports of IEI patients, this study focused mainly on common multidrug resistant bacteria. Our study revealed that the β-lactamase production rate of H. influenzae and M. catarrhalis, the EBSL production rate of K. pneumoniae and E. coli, and the detection rates of MRSA, CRKP, CREC, CRPA, and CRAB were greater than those in normal children as demonstrated from previous reports [12, 14, 28–33]. Compared with our non-IEI group, PNSP and the EBSL production rates of K. pneumoniae and E. coli were lower in the IEI group, but the detection rates of MRSA, CRKP, CREC, CRPA, and CRAB was greater. In our study, PNSP was significantly lower in both the IEI group and the non-IEI group compared with the ordinary group. This finding provides some guidance for clinicians in the selection of antibiotics for children with IEIs. The AMR of some isolates in non-IEI patients in this study was also greater than that previously reported in ordinary children because most of the non-IEI patients in our group had some immune abnormalities and this group had a small sample size. Of concern is the surprisingly high antibiotic resistance observed in children with IEIs. The causes of high AMR in children with IEIs include difficulty removing pathogens, long-term use of antibiotics, cross-infection, and nosocomial infection. Patients with IEIs may be more susceptible to low-virulence strains due to abnormal immune function, which makes it difficult to remove the bacteria; thus, these patients may remain in a long-term carrier state [34]. During the course of chronic infection with bacteria carried by the host for a long time, the bacteria undergo adaptive mutations, which may explain the high AMR of bacteria in IEI patients [35, 36].

The limitations of the study should be noted. As this study was a retrospective analysis over a long period of time, some diagnostic criteria for bacterial infections, such as C. difficile, did not adhere to the latest guidelines. This study used bacterial culture results as the main inclusion criterion. However, due to its limited sensitivity, some patients with bacterial infections may have been missed. Although our research center is a major research center for patients with IEIs in China, this is a single-center study with a limited number of participants. Thus, it is difficult to further discuss the IEI subcategories. Therefore, the representativeness and extrapolation of the conclusions of this study are limited.

In conclusion, patients with different IEI classifications exhibit unique bacterial infection spectra. Different IEI classifications and genotypes are susceptible to different bacteria. These findings might be helpful for clinicians when etiological results are not available or when the IEI classification is not clear. The AMR of isolated bacteria in children with IEIs was generally significantly greater than that in normal children, this finding should be further explored in future study.
