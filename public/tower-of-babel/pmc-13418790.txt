Lnc00892 enhances cisplatin sensitivity by inducing apoptosis through the BTAF1/MDM2/STAT5B/XIAP axis in bladder cancer cells
Authors: Yixin Chang, Ning Sun, Yijie Liu, Zihui Jin, Peipei Zhang, Sijia Wang, Jie Chen, Jianting Liu, Zhenni Lin, Yongyong Lu, Haishan Huang, Chuanshu Huang, Honglei Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChemotherapy is the primary mode of treatment for patients with advanced bladder cancer. However, because of their non-specificity, chemotherapy drugs induce many notable side effects in patients. Recent studies have shown that the expression of certain long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) is closely related to the sensitivity of tumors to chemotherapeutic drugs. Thus, lncRNAs can be exploited as markers of tumor chemotherapeutic sensitivity to improve the efficacy of chemotherapy. Here, we investigated the role of the lnc00892 in the response of bladder cancer cells to cisplatin.ResultsMechanistically, both RNA pull down assay and RIP experiment showed lnc00892 bound to BTAF1, which accelerated the dissociation of TFIID from the MDM2 promoter region in cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells. This process reduced the transcription of MDM2, promoting STAT5B protein degradation through the ubiquitination-mediated pathway. Ultimately, these events attenuated XIAP transcription and promoted bladder cancer cell apoptosis.ConclusionLnc00892 promoted bladder cancer cells apoptosis by binding to BTAF1 which further reduced MDM2/STAT5B/XIAP levels, and ultimately promoted the sensitivity of bladder cancer cells to cisplatin. Lnc00892 could be used as a marker of cisplatin chemosensitivity in the clinic to improve the outcomes of patients with bladder cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00842-z.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is a common malignant tumor of the urinary system, among which muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) and metastatic bladder cancer are highly malignant and have poor prognosis, making them the focus of clinical treatment [1–3]. Currently, the treatments for metastatic or advanced bladder cancer include platinum-based chemotherapy, the combination of enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab (EV+P) and targeted therapy [4]. Among these, cisplatin-based chemotherapy is the most widely used and classic first-line treatment. Cisplatin binds to tumor cell DNA, which disrupts the DNA structure and function, induces cell apoptosis, and consequently suppresses tumor proliferation [5, 6]. However, the clinical application of cisplatin is limited by chemotherapy resistance and significant toxic side effects. Approximately 35% of patients with metastatic bladder cancer exhibit primary resistance to cisplatin, and its adverse reactions including nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity and gastrointestinal toxicity further restrict its clinical efficacy. Thus, screening patients for more effective molecular targets or markers of drug sensitivity before chemotherapy initiation would improve the efficacy of chemotherapy regimens while minimizing side effects. This individualized approach to chemotherapy promises to improve cancer treatment outcomes in the future.

LncRNAs are RNA molecules > 200 nucleotides long, with relatively conserved secondary structures, splicing forms, and subcellular localization [7]. Because lncRNAs do not encode proteins, they were initially considered useless; however, researchers were baffled why these seemingly useless molecules had not been eliminated over the course of Darwinian evolution. Despite their low abundance compared to rRNA and mRNA, lncRNAs play critical roles in regulating gene expression. In recent years, lncRNAs have been shown to play important roles in key cellular processes such as chromatin modification, transcription and post-transcriptional regulation, and epigenetic regulation [8–10]. LncRNAs are also involved in the regulation of various tumor cell processes. In recent years, the expression of various lncRNAs has been linked to chemotherapy sensitivity or resistance. For instance, the expression of the lncRNA ARA is significantly up-regulated in doxorubicin-sensitive breast and liver cancer cell lines [11]. Meanwhile, the high expression of lncRNA DMTF1v4 in gastric adenocarcinoma cells significantly reduces their sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs [12]. Many studies have shown that tumor metastasis also underlies the drug resistance of tumors; thus, inhibiting tumor cell metastasis may increase the sensitivity of tumors to chemotherapy drugs.

In humans, the genetic sequence encoding the lncRNA lnc00892 is located in band 6 and within the three sub-bands of the second region of the long arm of the X chromosome and produces a 701bp transcript. According to existing literature, the high expression of lnc00892 is associated with the prolonged overall survival of patients with bladder cancer [13]. In accordance, we previously showed that the expression of lnc00892 was typically low in bladder cancer [14]. Moreover, lnc00892 expression specifically inhibited bladder cancer cell metastasis in vitro and in vivo. Given that lnc00892 appeared to have multiple roles in bladder cancer, we wondered whether it was involved in the sensitization of bladder cancer cells to chemotherapy. To test this hypothesis, we explored whether and how lnc00892 affected the response of bladder cancer cells to chemotherapy, with a future goal of exploiting this lncRNA in the clinic to improve the outcomes of patients with bladder cancer.

The X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis (XIAP) is a member of the inhibitor of apoptosis protein (IAP) family [15]. XIAP is often abnormally expressed in cancer cells, where it inhibits apoptosis and the ubiquitination ligase, therefore contributing to cell transformation and tumor formation. In our previous study, we found XIAP is a key regulator of YTHDC1, which promotes the ubiquitination of YTHDC1 and positively regulates the expression of MMP-2, facilitating the metastasis of bladder cancer [16]. Not only that, XIAP is the most potent and effective caspase-inhibiting protein in the IAP family. In this study, we explored the role lnc00892 played in the cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells, and preliminarily uncovered the mechanism by which lnc00892 enhances apoptosis in bladder cancer cells treated with cisplatin through XIAP.

Thus, we investigated how lnc00892 modulates the sensitivity of bladder cancer cells to cisplatin. We found that lnc00892 bound to BTAF1, which accelerated the dissociation of TFIID from the MDM2 promoter region in cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells. This process reduced the transcription of MDM2, promoting STAT5B protein degradation through the ubiquitination-mediated pathway. Ultimately, these events attenuated XIAP transcription and promoted bladder cancer cell apoptosis. Our findings lay a foundation for enhancing cisplatin sensitivity of bladder cancer in the future.

Materials and methods

Reagents, antibodies, and plasmids

The information of lnc00892 plasmids is as described in our previous study [14]. HA-XIAP plasmid was constructed in PLVX-puro vector with EcoRI and BamHI restriction endonucleases sites (forward, 5′-GAT TAC GCT GAA TTC ATG ACT TTT AAC AGT TTT GAA GGA T-3′; reverse, 5′-AGA GTC GCG GGA TCC TTA AGA CAT AAA AAT TTT TTG CTT G-3′). The XIAP promoter-driven luciferase reporter plasmid was purchased from Tsingke Biotechnology. XIAP promoter-driven luciferase reporter with a mutation in STAT5B binding sites was constructed using point mutation primers (forward, 5′-CCT TCC CGG ATT GGA AAC AGA TCC G-3′; reverse,5′-CGG ATC TGT TTC CAA TCC GGG AAG G-3′) XIAP promoter plasmid mentioned before. HA-STAT5B plasmid was purchased from Miaoling Biotechnology. HA-MDM2 plasmid was constructed in the PLVX-puro vector with EcoRI and BamHI restriction endonucleases sites (forward, 5′-GAT TAC GCT GAA TTC ATG GTG AGG AGC AGG CAA ATG TGC AAT-3′; reverse, 5′-AGA GTC GCG GGA TCC CTA GGG GAA ATA AGT TAG CAC AAT CAT-3′). The human MDM2 promoter was cloned into the PGL3-Basic luciferase reporter using KpnI and HindIII restriction enzyme cutting sites (forward, 5′-CTA TCG ATA GGT ACC TTC TTA AAA CTT CTT CTT GAC T-3′; reverse, 5′-CGG AAT GCC AAG CTT ATT CCA CTC TCC GGG CCA G-3′). The truncated human MDM2 promoter and all the three mutant fragments (the binding sites of TFIID were mutated) were constructed by MDM2 promoter plasmid mentioned above (TFIID MUT1: forward, 5′-CTG TCT CTG AAC TTT GCC GGC ACA AAC CCA TTC G-3′; reverse, 5′-TGG GTT TGT GCG CCG AAA GTT CAG AGA CAG AAT T-3′; TFIID MUT2: forward, 5′-GGA AGG ATA TAA CTT GCC GAA AAA AAA AAA GCT GCA GAA GGG AA-3′; reverse, 5′-CAG CTT TTT TTT TTT GCC GAA GTT ATA TCC TTC CCT TCT CCC GC-3′; TFIID MUT3: forward, 5′-GGA AGG ATA TAA CTT GCC GAA AAA AGG TCC TTG TTC ACA AGG AGC TA-3′; reverse, 5′-TGA ACA AGG ACC TTT TTT GCC GAA GTT ATA TCC TTC CCT TCT GCA GC-3′). BTAF1-shRNA and control plasmids were purchased from the Public Protein/Plasmid Library. Plasmids were managed using the plasmid preparation/extraction kit from QIAGEN. The drug cisplatin was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Antibodies specific against caspase-3 (9665), cleaved caspase-3 (9661), Bax (5023), Bcl-2 (4223), survivin (2808,), c-IAP1 (7065), c-IAP2 (3130), HA (3724), SP1 (9389), MDM2 (86934), USP14 (11931), RXRα (3085) STAT4 (2653), STAT5 (94205) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology, and the dilution ratio is 1:1000 for the Western blotting. Antibodies specific against c-Jun (sc-44), MAFG (sc-166548), STAT5A (sc-271542), STAT5B (sc-1656), CBL (sc-1651), TFIID (sc-421), XIAP (610763) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, and the dilution ratio is 1:500 for the Western blotting. Antibodies specific against FOXP3 (122228-1-AP, 1:1000) and β-actin (66009–1-lg, 1:10000) were purchased from Proteintech. Antibodies specific against TFII-I (ab129025, 1:1000) were purchased from Abcam.

Cell culture and transfection

Human malignant bladder cell lines J82 and UMUC3 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, as previously described [14]. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination. PolyJetTM DNA In Vitro Transfection Reagent were used for stable cell transfection. Puromycin (J593, Amresco Inc) was used for the stable cell filtration.

Lentivirus infection

293T cells are seeded in a 6-well plate, and cell transfection is performed when the cell density reaches about 70%-80%. Replace fresh culture medium for cells 30 min in advance, and temporarily place cells at 37 °C. Mix 2 μg of target plasmid, 1.2 μg of plasmid psPAX2, 1.2 μg of plasmid pMD2.G and 6 μL of transfection reagent PolyJetTM into 50 μL of DMEM without serum thoroughly. Then got the mixture incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Dropped the mixture into cell culture medium and incubated in the incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 6–8 h. Discard the cell culture medium and replace it with fresh one containing 10% FBS. Collected the virus 48 h later and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 30 min, then passed through a 0.45 μm filter using a syringe. Every well was added 1 mL virus supernatant to the cell culture medium. Stable cell lines were selected after the infection.

Cisplatin-resistant cell line establishment assay

Cisplatin resistance was induced by stepwise increasing concentrations of the drug. Tumor cells were initially exposed to a low concentration of cisplatin (recommended at 1/10 to 1/5 of the IC50 of the parental cell line) for 24 hours. Subsequently, the cisplatin-containing medium was discarded and replaced with drug-free medium, they were passaged and treated with a low concentration of cisplatin for 24 h. Cisplatin concentrations were gradually increased, with 6–8 cycles of stimulation at each level. The IC50 of cisplatin-resistant cell lines and control cells was determined by cell proliferation assay (Fig. S1).

Cell proliferation assay

CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit (G7570) was used to exam cell vitality under the condition of cisplatin treatment. 3000 cells were seeded into a 96-well plate according to the specific experimental situation. Cells were cultured in 0.1% FBS medium for 12 hours, and 8, 16, 32, and 64 µM of cisplatin were added respectively. Cell culture was terminated after 24 h in 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2 and cell viability was detected by Centro LB 960 luminometer (Berthold Technologies, Berthold, Germany).

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis assay was performed using flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter Inc). Cells were collected and suspended in 500 μL binding buffer, then incubated in room temperature with 7-AAD and APC in the dark for 15 min before testing. The results were analyzed with the CytExpert software.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (15596018, Invitrogen), and cDNAs were synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (RR037A, TAKARA). qRT-PCR assays were performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme) in the Q6 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The primers used in this assay were as follows: human lnc00892 and XIAP were described before [14, 17], human STAT5B (forward, 5′-GCC ACT GTT CTC TGG GAC AAT G-3′; reverse, 5′-ACA CGA GGT TCT CCT TGG TCA G-3′), human MDM2 (forward, 5′-ACC TCA CAG ATT CCA GCT TCG-3′; reverse, 5′-TTT CAT AGT ATA AGT GTC TTT TT-3′).

Western blot analysis

The cells were lysed with lysis buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM Na3VO4, and 1% SDS (pH 7.4) on ice. The samples were then collected and heated at 100 °C for 5 min, and nucleic acids were broken with ultrasound with suitable amplitude. Protein samples were separated on SDS-PAGE gels, followed by transfer to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and probed with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with AP-conjugated secondary antibody. The ECF western blotting system (RPN5787; GE Healthcare, PA, USA) was used to detect the protein signal, and images were captured using a phosphorimager (Typhoon FLA 7000, GE Healthcare). Grayscale values were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For IHC staining, antibodies specific were used anti-Ki67 (1:1000, 62548, CST). And the experiment was performed using the kit from Boster Bio-Engineering Company (SA1022, Wuhan, China). The images were captured by Nikon Eclipse Ni microsystem (DS-Ri2). Image-Pro Plus version 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, MD, USA) was used to analyze the protein expression by calculating the integrated optical density per stained area (IOD/area).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Bladder cancer cells and the vector control were co-transfected with the promoter-driven luciferase reporter and pRL-TK before the treatment of cisplatin. Cells were collected and then lysed with passive lysis buffer for 10 min at room temperature. Add 20 μL cell lysate into 96-well plate and 20 μL Luciferase Assay Reagent II for the detection of promoter activity (Promega, USA). 20 μL Stop & Glo Buffer was added to the same well for the detection of TK activity (Centro LB 960 luminometer, Berthold). All the results were normalized by internal TK signal.

Protein degradation assay

Cells were collected according to the specified time point of STAT5B protein under the treatment of CHX(50 μg/mL) for indicated time. Then the protein stability was evaluated by western blot analysis.

Xenograft model in nude mice in vivo

Animal experiments were performed in the animal institute of Wenzhou Medical University according to the protocols approved by the Laboratory Animal Center of Wenzhou Medical University and the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University. Female BALB/c-nude mice (3 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech LLC (license no. SCXK, Jiangsu 2018–0008). After a week of acclimatization, 2.5 × 106 indicated bladder cancer cells were injected into the right side of the back. About 5 days later, the mice were grouped into four and intraperitoneal injection with cisplatin (3 mg/kg) or PBS. After 3 weeks, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were surgically removed, imaged, and weighed. A part of tumor was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for IHC, and the remaining was frozen at −80 °C.

ChIP assay

ChIP assay was performed using a SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads, #9003, CST) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Amplification of the region-containing putative responsive elements of the human MDM2 promoter was done using PCR and the primers (forward, 5′-TGG TAG GCT GGG AAG CGG GAG AAG GGA A-3′; reverse, 5′-TGA TGC GGT CTC ATA AAT TGA GGA CAT A-3′). Resulting PCR products were separated over 2% agarose gels that were in turn stained with ethidium bromide. Final images were then generated using Image Lab (ChemiDoc XRS+, Bio-Rad).

RNA pull down assay

RNA pull down assay was performed using a PureBindingTM RNA-Protein pull-down Kit (P0202, GENESEED) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RIP assay

A RIP assay was performed using an RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit (P0102, GENESEED) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers used to amplify lnc00892 were mentioned above.

GDSC database analysis

Gene expression profiles and the corresponding drug sensitivity data of cell lines were extracted from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database. A drug sensitivity prediction model was constructed using the oncoPredict R package (v1.2), which was subsequently applied to samples from the TCGA-BLCA cohort to predict their sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents including cisplatin.

Bioinformatic analysis

The potential transcription factors binding to the promoter region were predicted by PROMO database. E3 ligases were screened by UbiBrowser database.

Statistical analysis

All data were plotted as mean±SD of triplicate assays. Student’s t-test was applied for the significance of any differences between the various groups, and statistical significance was defined as follows: *p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; **** p < 0.0001. All experiments were performed in triplicate. All analyses were conducted using Prism 5.0 Software.

Results

Lnc00892 enhances cisplatin sensitivity of bladder cancer cells in vivo and in vitro

The clinical application of molecular targets in chemotherapy represents a cornerstone of precision oncology, aiming to maximize antitumor efficacy while minimizing damage to healthy tissues. Our previous studies have shown that the overexpression of lnc00892 inhibits the metastasis of bladder cancer cells [14]. To further clarify the biological function of lnc00892 in promoting the sensitivity of bladder cancer cells to cisplatin-based chemotherapeutic regimens, we predicted drug response in bladder cancer patients using the GDSC database (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer) and found that those with high expression of lnc00892 had lower predicted score for cisplatin compared to patients with low expression of lnc00892. Additionally, the expression of lnc00892 was negatively correlated with the predicted score of cisplatin chemotherapy in bladder cancer patients. This indicates that, in bladder cancer patients, the expression of lnc00892 is associated with sensitivity to cisplatin chemotherapy (Fig. 1A, B). Next, we detected the expression of lnc00892, and found that lnc00892 showed significantly lower expression in CDDP-resistant cell line (J82/CDDP) (Fig. 1C). We subsequently transfected these cell lines with the pcDNA3.1 and pcDNA3.1-lnc00892 plasmids to construct stable cell lines [14]. The real-time PCR results showed that lnc00892 was successfully overexpressed in the J82 and UMUC3 cells (Fig. 1D, E). We used the ATP assay to compare the activities of cisplatin-treated lnc00892-overexpressing and control cells. We found that the ATP level of cells was dose-dependent on the cisplatin concentration. It is notable that cells overexpressing lnc00892 are more sensitive to cisplatin (Fig. 1F, G), indicating that lnc00892 reduced the viability of bladder cancer cells treated with cisplatin. To confirm these findings in vivo, we constructed a subcutaneous tumor model of lnc00892 overexpression in nude mice and subjected it to cisplatin treatment. We found the tumor volume of the lnc00892+cisplatin group was significant smaller than that of the vector+cisplatin group. However, there was no difference in the control treatment group (Fig. 1H, I). The immunohistochemical staining results showed that in the cisplatin treatment group, the expression level of Ki67 in the overexpression lnc00892 group was significantly lower than control group, while there was no difference in the PBS treatment group (Fig. 1J, K). Lnc00892 expression promotes the apoptosis of bladder cancer cells after cisplatin treatment

Cisplatin, as a widely used chemotherapy drug, exerts its core mechanism of action by inducing DNA damage response and activating the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway, ultimately leading to cell apoptosis [18]. Caspases are cysteine-containing, aspartate-specific proteases, which orchestrate apoptosis. The expression of caspase-3, and its activated form cleaved caspase-3 in particular, is associated with increased rates of apoptosis [19]. In this study, we treated the bladder cancer cell lines 6 or 12 hours with 32 µM cisplatin and measured cleaved caspase-3 expression levels. We found that the expression of cleaved caspase-3 in lnc00892-overexpressing bladder cancer cells increased significantly at 12 hours and that the expression of cleaved caspase-3 in control cells was significantly lower than that in lnc00892-overexpressing cells (Fig. 2A). We then treated the bladder cancer cells with 16 or 32 µM cisplatin for 12 hours. We found that treating the cells with 32 µM cisplatin for 12 hours significantly increased the expression of cleaved caspase-3 in the lnc00892 overexpression group versus the control group (Fig. 2B). The above results indicated that lnc00892 promoted the apoptosis of bladder cancer cells treated with cisplatin. In addition, we detected the apoptosis of bladder cancer cells by flow cytometry. The results showed that the apoptosis of lnc00892-overexpressing bladder cancer cells was significantly higher than control cells (Fig. 2C–F). Lnc00892 inhibits the expression of XIAP to increase cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer cells

We next set out to clarify how lnc00892 regulates the apoptosis of cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells. The Bcl-2 protein family, which includes the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, regulates apoptosis by maintaining a balanced Bcl-2 to Bax ratio [20]. However, western blot analysis showed that the ratio of Bcl-2 to Bax did not change significantly in cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells (Fig. 3A). Considering that the IAP protein family is a group of anti-apoptotic proteins, which are structurally homologous, but function independently of the Bcl-2 family. The BIR2 domain of the IAP family proteins can directly bind to and inhibit the activity of caspase-3 [21]. XIAP is the most functionally important and potent apoptosis-inhibiting member of the IAP family. It precisely regulates cell survival and death through multiple mechanisms. c-IAP1 and c-IAP2, both belonging to the IAP family of proteins, can also bind to caspase proteins and inhibit apoptosis, but their inhibitory potency is far lower than that of XIAP [22]. Survivin, another member of the IAP family, exhibits a more selective pathway in inhibiting apoptosis [23]. We therefore tested the level of the IAP family members in lnc00892-overexpressing and control cells. We found that the XIAP protein level in lnc00892-overexpressing versus control cells was significantly lower following cisplatin treatment but did not significantly change in the absence of cisplatin. This suggests that lnc00892 may promote the apoptosis of cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells by reducing the expression of XIAP. To validate the above hypothesis, we generated J82/UMUC3-lnc00892 cells with stable XIAP expression (Fig. 3B). The ATP and flow cytometry assay results showed that XIAP overexpression inhibited the apoptosis of bladder cancer cells during cisplatin treatment (Fig. 3C–E). Lnc00892 enhances cisplatin sensitivity by inhibiting STAT5B levels and reducing XIAP transcription in bladder cancer

Despite the above results, the molecular mechanism underlying the regulation of XIAP by lnc00892 was still unclear. First, we used the real-time PCR to detect XIAP mRNA levels during cisplatin exposure (Fig. 4A). We found that lnc00892 significantly reduced XIAP mRNA levels in cisplatin-treated cells, suggesting that lnc00892 regulates XIAP protein expression in bladder cancer cells at the mRNA level. The level of XIAP mRNA is mainly affected by the rate of transcription and transcript stability. Thus, we constructed a plasmid containing the XIAP promoter and detected its transcriptional activity via dual-luciferase reporter assay. We found that the overexpression of lnc00892 inhibited the transcriptional of the XIAP in cisplatin-treated cells (Fig. 4B). The above results indicate that lnc00892 decreases the protein expression of XIAP by inhibiting its transcription during cisplatin treatment. Transcription factors bind to the promoter region of a gene to regulate transcription. We next analyzed the transcription factors in the XIAP promoter region through published literature and prediction websites (Fig. 4C), and used western blotting tests. The results reveal that lnc00892 significantly inhibited the expression of STAT5B in cisplatin-treated cells (Fig. 4D). To confirm that STAT5B associated with the XIAP promoter and regulated its transcription, we scanned through the literature and learnt that STAT5B recognizes the TTCNNNGAA sequence on the XIAP promoter [24]. Given that the core part of this binding sequence is TTC, we mutated it to GGATTGGAA (Fig. 4E), before co-transfecting the mutant plasmid and wild type (TK) plasmid into lnc00892-overexpressing or control J82 and UMUC3 cells. The experimental cells were then treated with 32 µM cisplatin, whereas the control cells were left untreated. The XIAP promoter activity was detected in the cells 12 hours later. The results demonstrated that the STAT5B binding site activity on the mutated XIAP promoter was partially restored compared to the wild-type promoter (Fig. 4F, G), indicating that STAT5B regulates XIAP expression in cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells through binding to the XIAP promoter region.

To verify whether the lnc00892/STAT5B/XIAP axis is involved in cisplatin-induced apoptosis, we next transfected the STAT5B plasmid and its control into J82/UMUC3-lnc00892 cells (Fig. 4H). We performed functional experiments, which revealed that the overexpression of STAT5B reduced the apoptosis of lnc00892-overexpressing cells receiving cisplatin treatment (Fig. 4I–K). At the same time, we found that XIAP protein expression, promoter activity, and mRNA expression were restored in UMUC3-lnc00892 (HA-STAT5B) compared to UMUC3-lnc00892 (HA) cells treated with 32 µM cisplatin for 12 hours (Fig. 4L–N). These results indicate that lnc00892 inhibits the expression of XIAP by reducing the expression of STAT5B in bladder cancer cells during cisplatin treatment.

To further elucidate the mechanism of lnc00892/STAT5B/XIAP axis, we used real-time PCR to measure the level of STAT5B mRNA in bladder cancer cells. The results showed that lnc00892 did not significantly regulate the level of STAT5B mRNA in bladder cancer cells exposed to cisplatin (Fig. 5A). Therefore, we ruled out the possibility that lnc00892 regulates STAT5B in bladder cancer cells at the mRNA level and speculated whether lnc00892 regulated the expression of STAT5B at the protein level. To measure STAT5B protein degradation, we added CHX at different time points and then detected the degradation rate of STAT5B protein in bladder cancer cells exposed to cisplatin. We found that lnc00892 significantly promoted the degradation of STAT5B protein following cisplatin treatment (Fig. 5B, C). To determine whether STAT5B was degraded by the proteasome, we treated bladder cancer cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 for 8 hours, and the results showed that the protein level of STAT5B was increased (Fig. 5D). To investigate the mechanism underlying protein degradation, we first predicted the E3 ubiquitin ligases targeting STAT5B (Fig. S2). Furthermore, based on literature review, MDM2 can competitively bind to STAT5 with CBL, thereby preventing CBL-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of STAT5, which in turn enhances STAT5 stability [25, 26]. To clarify the specific mechanism by which lnc00892 promotes the degradation of STAT5B, we detected the CBL and MDM2 for STAT5B by western blotting. The results showed that overexpression of lnc00892 could significantly inhibit the expression of MDM2 under cisplatin treatment (Fig. 5E). To delineate the mechanistic role of MDM2 in lnc00892-driven cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer, we overexpressed MDM2 in J82/UMUC3-lnc00892 cells (Fig. 5F). Subsequent, functional experiments showed that the overexpression of MDM2 restored cell viability and apoptosis of cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells overexpressing lnc00892 (Fig. 5G–I). We found that MDM2 overexpression restored the expression of STAT5B and XIAP during cisplatin treatment, while promoting STAT5B protein degradation (Fig. 5J, K). These results suggest that lnc00892 down-regulates the expression of STAT5B by inhibiting the expression of MDM2 in bladder cancer cells treated with cisplatin.

Lnc00892 and BTAF1 disrupt TFIID binding to the MDM2 promoter in cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells

To further explore the molecular mechanism of MDM2-mediated STAT5B inhibition, we measured the level of MDM2 mRNA. We found that overexpressing lnc00892 in J82 and UMUC3 cells significantly reduced the level of MDM2 mRNA during cisplatin treatment (Fig. 6A). Next, the dual-luciferase reporter assay results showed that lnc00892 overexpression significantly inhibited MDM2 promoter activity in bladder cancer cells treated with cisplatin (Fig. 6B). These results indicated that lnc00892 reduced the expression of MDM2 by inhibiting the promoter activity of MDM2 during cisplatin treatment. To clarify how lnc00892 inhibited the activity of the MDM2 promoter in cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells, we analyzed the transcription factors and binding sites of the MDM2 promoter region and constructed MDM2–P1(−1984–0) and MDM2–P2(−1072–0) promoter plasmids (Fig. 6C). However, removing half of the original MDM2 promoter did not impact its transcriptional activity (Fig. 6D), thus, we focused on the −1984 to −1072 region of the promoter in subsequent experiments. Since TFII‑I and SP1 had been excluded in Fig. 4D, we further tested the transcription factors TFIID, FOXP3 and RXRα. Western blotting results indicated that the expression of FOXP3 and RXRα exhibited differential changes in J82 and UMUC3 cells, whereas TFIID showed no significant alteration under cisplatin treatment (Fig. 6E). Considering that lncRNAs may function as molecular decoys or scaffolds, this mechanism acts independently of regulating the expression of their binding partners [27, 28]. Thus, we preliminarily concluded that lnc00892 did not regulate MDM2 promoter activity in cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells by regulating the expression of transcription factors. We hypothesized that lnc00892 might modulate the binding of TFIID to the MDM2 promoter, despite observing no alterations at the protein level. We constructed three mutant plasmids of the TFIID binding site (−1752 to −1745, −1133 to −1126, −1093 to −1086), then each one was co-transfected with the TK plasmid into UMUC3-vector cells and UMUC3-lnc00892 cells (Fig. 6F). The results showed that TFIID-MUT3 could restore the activity of MDM2, while TFIID-MUT1 and TFIID-MUT2 showed no difference from the wild type (Fig. 6G). These findings suggest that lnc00892 affected the activity of the MDM2 promoter by inhibiting the binding of TFIID to the −1093 to −1086 promoter region of MDM2. Considering that TFIID is a complex, composed of the TATA box binding protein (TBP) and TBP-associated factors (TAFs), and lncRNA can directly affect protein interactions [29, 30]. Thus, we speculated that lnc00892 affected the binding of TFIID to MDM2 by changing the spatial localization of TFIID. We used ChIP to determine the effect of cisplatin treatment on TFIID and MDM2, and found that lnc00892 reduced the binding of TFIID to the MDM2 promoter (Fig. 6H). The RNA pull-down assay revealed that BTAF1 directly bound to lnc00892 (Fig. 6I), furthermore, we demonstrated that cisplatin treatment further enhances the binding between lnc00892 and BTAF1 (Fig. 6J). Therefore, we speculate that lnc00892 disrupt the structure of TFIID by binding to BTAF1, thus reducing TFIID binding to the MDM2 promoter in bladder cancer cells exposed to cisplatin. To validate the above hypothesis, we established a BTAF1-knockdown cell line in lnc00892-overexpressing bladder cancer cells and examined MDM2 expression. The protein expression of MDM2 increased to some extent after BTAF1 knockdown, and this increase became more pronounced following cisplatin treatment (Fig. 6K). This also indicates that after cisplatin treatment, lnc00892 binds to more BTAF1 and accelerates the dissociation of TFIID from the MDM2 promoter region, ultimately inhibiting MDM2 promoter activity. Discussion

Chemotherapy is a powerful tumor eradication strategy. However, it is also associated with notable adverse events. Moreover, due to inter-patient variation and tumor heterogeneity, chemotherapy cannot achieve 100% therapeutic efficacy. It is not uncommon for patients with tumors to be insensitive to or develop resistance to chemotherapy drugs; these are the ongoing challenges facing clinical cancer chemotherapy. Increasing the sensitivity of tumor to chemotherapy would certainly improve treatment outcomes and patient survival; besides, it would lessen the possibility of disease progression. Improving tumor sensitivity to chemotherapy would, as a complementary strategy, also improve the outcomes of other modes of cancer treatment such as surgery. Drug resistance arises mainly from dysregulation of drug uptake and efflux, enhanced DNA damage repair, and impaired apoptosis. Alterations in these key pathways allow cancer cells to evade the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapeutic drugs, ultimately leading to resistance in patients. Overexpression of ABC transporters (e.g., MDR1, MRP) increases drug efflux and reduces intracellular drug accumulation, while decreased drug transporter expression impairs drug uptake. Upregulation of key DNA repair genes, including ERCC1, XRCC1, and BRCA1, enhances the repair of chemotherapy-induced DNA damage and consequently reduces drug sensitivity [31–35]. Specifically, ERCC1 is primarily involved in nucleotide excision repair to remove chemotherapy-induced DNA adducts, XRCC1 participates in base excision repair and single-strand break repair, while BRCA1 plays a critical role in homologous recombination repair to resolve DNA double-strand break. Furthermore, dysregulation of apoptotic pathways, characterized by the inhibition of pro-apoptotic proteins such as Bax and Bak or the overexpression of anti-apoptotic proteins including Bcl-2 and XIAP, compromises chemotherapy-induced cell death and contributes to drug resistance. It is worth noting that the research has found that lncRNAs have also been shown to be involved in drug efflux, DNA replication and apoptosis may be involved in the process of drug resistance. Our study provides a more detailed explanation of the mechanism by which lnc00892 promotes apoptosis in bladder cancer cells following cisplatin treatment. It offers a deeper analysis of how lncRNAs enhance tumor cell sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs and supplements potential new mechanisms. This research contributes new strategies for addressing clinical chemotherapy resistance in the future.

High expression of the lncRNA lnc00892 has been shown to prolong the OS of bladder cancer patients and inhibit the invasion and metastasis of bladder cancer cells. Here, we found for the first time that lnc00892 increased the sensitivity of bladder cancer to cisplatin both in vitro and in vivo. We showed that high levels of lnc00892 promoted the apoptosis of cisplatin-treated bladder cancer cells and increased their sensitivity. Based on these encouraging results, we envisage that lnc00892 may be used as a marker of cisplatin sensitivity before the initiation of chemotherapy. Mechanistically, our study showed that during cisplatin treatment, lnc00892 reduced the binding of TFIID to the MDM2 promoter by binding to BTAF1, which further inhibited the transcription of MDM2 and reduced its expression. These events reduced the stability of the STAT5B protein and promoted its degradation, which in turn inhibited the transcription of XIAP. A decrease in XIAP expression eventually increased the sensitivity of tumor cells to cisplatin and promoted their apoptosis.

LncRNAs play important roles in the occurrence and development of tumors. Recent studies have shown that lncRNAs may participate in the sensitivity of tumor to cisplatin and drug resistance via a variety of pathways, which can be roughly divided into four categories: drug efflux, cell cycling, apoptosis, and EMT [36–40]. LncRNAs can also regulate the drug sensitivity of tumor cells at the epigenetic level. For instance, the high expression of the lncRNA TUG1 was shown to epigenetically regulate the expression of PDCD4 through EZH2, which reduced the sensitivity of tumor cells to cisplatin and ultimately led to the resistance of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma to this form of chemotherapy [41].

As a member of the IAP family, XIAP inhibits apoptosis by binding to caspase-3 and preventing its activation and expression. XIAP is highly expressed in a variety of cancers, including in bladder cancer, where its BIR domain inhibits the expression of miR-200a through the PP2A/MAPKs/c-Jun pathway. This promotes the translation and anchorage-independent accumulation of the EGFR protein in bladder cancer cells and drives their proliferation [42]. Moreover, the RING domain of XIAP mediates the expression of miR-4295, thereby inhibiting the translation of p63a and promoting the transformation, invasion, and metastasis of bladder epithelial cells [43]. Recent studies of ovarian cancer have found that XIAP is also closely related to chemotherapeutic drug resistance. The protein levels of XIAP were reduced in cisplatin-sensitive ovarian cancer cells, which limited XIAP binding to caspase-3 and further promoted cancer cell apoptosis. An analysis of clinical tumor samples revealed that XIAP expression was significantly higher in melanoma cells than in other tumor cell types, which may explain why melanoma is especially resistant to chemotherapy. Therefore, using drugs to target and degrade XIAP could sensitize tumor cells to chemotherapy. Our results showed that lnc00892 regulates the transcription of XIAP in bladder cancer cells undergoing cisplatin treatment, inhibits the expression of the XIAP protein, increases cell sensitivity to cisplatin, and induces apoptosis.

The humans, the STAT5B gene is located in the 17q21.2 chromosome gene domain. STAT5B shares 96% of its amino acid identity with STAT5A; thus, the two proteins are generally considered to be interchangeable and collectively referred to as STAT5. However, there are some differences between STAT5A and STAT5B; crucially, STAT5A cannot make up for the depletion of STAT5B [44]. STAT5B expression and activation are significantly increased in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma tissues and cell lines. Moreover, antisense nucleotides targeting STAT5B inhibit tumor proliferation by reducing the expression of downstream genes [45]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), STAT5B activation induce hepatocyte migration and invasion, and is associated with HCC stage, venous invasion, and poor prognosis [46]. Our results suggest that: 1) STAT5B promotes XIAP expression; and 2) lnc00892 inhibits STAT5B expression in bladder cancer cells treated with cisplatin, reducing XIAP promoter activity.

MDM2 is one of three highly amplified genes originally identified in the Balb/c3T3 fibroblast cell line of tumor-bearing mice; the other two genes being MDM1 and MDM3 [47, 48]. Studies have reported that only MDM2 protein is implicated in human malignancies and is found in patients with primary resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors [49, 50]. The N-terminus of MDM2 protein contains the I region, which binds to p53 to form the MDM2-p53 complex; the formation of this complex inhibits the transcriptional activation of p53. Meanwhile, the ring finger structure of MDM2 can act as E3 ubiquitination ligase to mediate the ubiquitination and degradation of p53 [51]. Studies have shown that MDM2 expression in tumors can also promote their chemoresistance by inducing EMT through p53-independent pathways. In addition, some studies have shown that targeting MDM2 and down-regulating its expression promotes the apoptosis of tumor cells, thereby inducing the sensitivity of tumor cells to chemotherapeutic drugs [52]. In addition to the above pathways, recent studies have shown that MDM2 can also compete with c-Cbl for binding to STAT5 to stabilize the STAT5 protein in T cells [25]. Moreover, MDM2 was shown to increase the half-life of E2F1 by displacing its E3 ubiquitin ligase [53]. Our study showed that MDM2 had a similar effect in bladder cancer cells. The overexpression of lnc00892 inhibited MDM2 expression, thus reducing STAT5B protein stability and promoting its degradation. This finding enriches our understanding of the biological role of MDM2 in tumors and provides theoretical support for MDM2 as a molecular target in cancer therapy.

General transcription factors are essential transcription factors which bind to specific DNA regions to activate transcription. These transcription factors include TFIIA, TFIIB, TFIID, TFIIE, TFIIF, and TFIIH. During gene transcription, TFIID is the first to bind to the TATA box of the promoter and initiate transcription [54]. TFIID itself is also a complex, consisting of TBP, which plays a major role in promoter transcription, and TAFs [55]. In this study, we showed that lnc00892 bound to BTAF1 (also known as TATA-binding protein factor 172) and interacted with TBP. It has been reported that BTAF1 can use the energy of ATP hydrolysis to dissociate TBP from the TATA box, thus playing a role in transcriptional repression [56]. The present study showed that, during cisplatin treatment, lnc00892 promoted the dissociation of TBP from the promoter region by binding to BTAF1 in bladder cancer cells. There are some limitations in this study. First, our findings are derived from only two bladder cancer cell lines. While these cell lines are representative of the disease and were carefully chosen for this investigation, the generalizability of our conclusions to all bladder cancer contexts requires further validation in a broader panel of cell models and, ultimately, in clinical samples. Second, this study is primarily mechanistic, and a key limitation is the lack of data from patient specimens. Although the use of well-characterized cell lines (UMUC3 and J82) allows for controlled experimental dissection of the proposed mechanism, it remains uncertain whether this pathway operates identically within the complex tumor microenvironment of human cancers.

Therefore, in the future, our next critical step will be to validate these results in a clinical context. This includes analyzing lnc00892 expression in archived tumor tissues from patients treated with cisplatin to determine if its levels can serve as a predictive biomarker for drug sensitivity or resistance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that lnc00892 is highly expressed in cisplatin-sensitive bladder cancer cells and correlates with a more favorable prognosis. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments indicate that lnc00892 overexpression in bladder cancer cells enhances tumor cell apoptosis under cisplatin treatment. Mechanistically, in the presence of cisplatin, lnc00892 binds to BTAF1, thereby reducing the binding of TFIID complex to the promoter region of MDM2, inhibiting the transcriptional activity and protein expression of MDM2. Consequently, the binding of MDM2 to STAT5B and the stabilization of STAT5B expression are diminished. As a result, more STAT5B undergoes ubiquitination and degradation mediated by c-Cbl. Given that STAT5B functions as a positive regulator of XIAP promoter activity, the reduced expression of STAT5B further suppresses the activity of the XIAP promoter, leading to decreased XIAP protein levels and ultimately promoting the apoptosis of bladder cancer cells. Taken together, we have identified the functional role of lnc00892 in bladder cancer upon cisplatin treatment and uncovered a novel mechanism by which lnc00892 regulates apoptosis. These fundings indicate that lnc00892 could be used as a marker of cisplatin sensitivity in the clinic to improve the outcomes of patients with bladder cancer.
