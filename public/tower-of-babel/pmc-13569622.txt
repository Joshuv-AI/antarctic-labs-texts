MAIT Cells Suppress IgE‐Mediated Asthma via IFNγ‐Dependent B Cell Regulation
Authors: Angela M. Cannata, Jaclyn W. McAlees, Julie M. Hargis, Archana Shankar, Shouxiong Huang, Senad Divanovic, Ian P. Lewkowich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMucosal‐associated invariant T (MAIT) cells are innate‐like lymphocytes enriched in mucosal tissues, but their role in allergic asthma pathogenesis remains poorly defined. In this study, we sought to elucidate the role of MAIT cells in a T cell‐dominant model of allergic asthma.MethodsWe used a murine model of house dust mite–induced asthma and a selective MAIT cell antagonist (Acetyl‐6‐formylpterin, A6FP) to inhibit MAIT cell activation in vivo. Airway hyperresponsiveness, lung inflammation, serum IgE, and cytokine profiles were assessed. In vitro co‐culture experiments evaluated the direct impact of MAIT cells on B cell IgE production.ResultsPharmacologic inhibition of MAIT cells exacerbated airway hyperresponsiveness and elevated circulating IgE without altering airway eosinophilia or T helper type 2/type 17 cytokine production. MAIT cell antagonism failed to increase AHR in IgE‐deficient mice. In vitro, activated MAIT cells directly suppressed B cell IgE production through IFNγ signaling. IL‐4 was sufficient to enhance IFNγ production by MAIT cells, suggesting that allergic inflammation may induce a counter‐regulatory response from MAIT cells to limit IgE‐mediated pathology.ConclusionMAIT cells limit airway hyperresponsiveness by suppressing IgE production through IFNγ–dependent B cell regulation. These findings define a previously unrecognized immunoregulatory function of MAIT cells in allergic asthma and suggest that enhancing MAIT cell activity may represent a therapeutic strategy for IgE‐mediated diseases.

Introduction

Asthma affects > 300 million individuals and is a leading cause of lung morbidity [1]. Asthma is driven by aberrant allergen‐specific immune responses characterized by eosinophilia, mucus overproduction, airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), and reversible airflow obstruction [2, 3]. Immunoglobulin E (IgE), produced by B cells, is a central mediator of asthma as it sensitizes mast cells and basophils, amplifying airway inflammation and precipitating symptom exacerbations [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. While TH2 cells contribute to IgE production, other innate‐like cell populations (ILC2, iNKT, and MAIT) have also been implicated [4, 9]. Although multiple strategies for asthma treatment exist, asthma patients still experience uncontrolled symptoms, underscoring the need for a deeper understanding of the underlying immunopathological mechanisms.

Mucosal‐associated invariant T (MAIT) cells are tissue‐resident innate‐like T lymphocytes located at mucosal sites, bridging innate and adaptive immunity [10, 11, 12, 13]. MAIT cells have described roles in diverse immunological processes, including delayed type hypersensitivities [14], atopic dermatitis [15], autoimmune diseases [16], and mycobacterial infection [17]. MAIT cells are activated following antigen presentation on the MHC class‐I‐related protein (MR1) or through direct cytokine stimulation that enables rapid responses to inflammatory signals [12, 18, 19]. Upon activation, MAIT cells produce IFN‐γ (MAIT1) or IL‐17A (MAIT17) and have therefore been attributed varied roles in asthma pathogenesis [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Some studies highlight a pathogenic role for MAIT cells (particularly MAIT17 cells) in promoting asthma exacerbations [20, 22, 26, 27, 28] whereas others suggest a protective role, noting that reduced MAIT cell numbers correlate with worse asthma and higher frequencies of MAIT1 cells are associated with reduced disease risk [29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. In Alternaria alternata ‐induced, ILC2‐dependent animal models, MAIT cells suppress ILC2 activity and limit asthma symptom development [34, 35].

In addition to their well‐described roles in cytokine production and regulation of innate immune responses, emerging evidence suggests that innate‐like T cells may influence adaptive immune processes, including B cell responses, through cytokine‐mediated mechanisms [36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. MAIT cells express effector cytokines such as IFN‐γ, which is known to modulate class‐switch recombination and IgE production. However, whether MAIT cells directly regulate humoral immunity in allergic asthma has not been explored. Given the described plasticity of MAIT cells in regulation of asthma, a clearer understanding of MAIT cell function in T cell‐dependent asthma models is an unmet need. Here, we define a novel immunoregulatory role for MAIT cells in T cell‐driven allergic asthma. Following pharmacologic inhibition of MAIT cells in an HDM–induced model, we observe increased AHR and IgE levels. MAIT cell antagonism failed to increase AHR in IgE‐deficient mice and, in vitro, activated MAIT cells suppressed IgE production via an IFNγ‐dependent mechanism. These findings identify MAIT cells as key regulators of B cell function in allergic asthma and extend their functional repertoire beyond microbial defense. More broadly, this work highlights the potential of tissue‐resident innate‐like T cells to limit maladaptive type 2 immunity and suggests new strategies for targeting IgE‐driven disease.

Materials and Methods

Mice

Female C57BL/6 and BALB/c (Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME, USA), IgE‐deficient C57BL/6 (provided by Mitch Grayson, Nationwide Children's Hospital) and IgE‐deficient BALB/c (provided by Dr. Fred Finkelman, Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center [CCHMC]) mice were used. All mice were housed at CCHMC, maintained on sterile chow and water ad libitum, and used at 8–12 weeks of age. Procedures were approved by the CCHMC Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC2024‐0021).

House Dust‐Mite (HDM)‐Induced Allergic Asthma and MAIT Cell Antagonism

Allergic airway inflammation was induced via intratracheal (i.t.) administration of 10 μg HDM (Greer Laboratories; 195.8 EU/mg protein) as previously described [41, 42, 43]. Mice were sacrificed 72 h after final exposure (day 24). A MAIT cell inhibitor (Acetyl‐6‐formylpterin A6FP; 50 nM; Cayman Chemicals) was administered i.t. with each HDM exposure and intraperitoneally on days 3, 6, 9, 13, 15, 18, 20, 22, and 23. All i.t. exposures were given in a final volume of 40 μL per mouse, and all i.p. exposures were given in a final volume of 100 μL per mouse.

Assessment of AHR and Inflammation

Seventy two hours after the final HDM exposure lung function was assessed via FlexiVent (EMKA) as previously described [41, 42, 43]. Mice were anesthetized with ketamine (150–175 mg/kg) and xylazine (20–25 mg/kg), tracheotomized, and ventilated (150 breaths/min, PEEP 3 cm H2O). Pancuronium bromide (1 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered for muscle relaxation. Methacholine (0–25 mg/mL; Sigma‐Aldrich) was delivered via ultrasonic nebulizer. Following resistance measuremengs, lungs were lavaged with 1 mL ice‐cold HBSS. Cells were pelleted (300 g, 6 min), erythrocytes lysed (ACK buffer), and spun onto glass slides (400 rpm, 4 min). Slides were stained with Diff‐Quik (RAL Diagnostics), and at least 500 cells per sample were morphologically classified. Serum levels of total IgG1, total IgE levels were measured using commercial ELISAs (Invitrogen); IgA (clone C10‐3) and IgM (clone R6‐60.2) levels were determined by in‐house ELISA.

Lungs were minced and digested in RPMI 1640 containing Liberase (Sigma‐Aldrich Inc.; 0.5 mg/mL), DNase I (0.5 mg/mL), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin/glutamine at 37°C for 45 min. Suspensions were filtered (70 μm), red blood cells lysed and counted via hemacytometer. Lung cells (2.5 × 105 per well) were restimulated with HDM (30 μg/mL) in complete RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin/glutamine, and 0.01% β‐mercaptoethanol. Supernatants were harvested after 72 h, and IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, and IL‐17A were quantified using in‐house ELISA.

Flow Cytometry

Single‐cell suspensions (4 × 106 cells/well) were stimulated for 4 h at 37°C in IMDM containing 10% FBS, and supplemented with PMA (50 ng/mL), and ionomycin (500 ng/mL). To block protein secretion, both Brefeldin (BioLegend) and Monensin (BioLegend) were included according to the manufacturer's direction (1000‐fold dilution of a 1000X solution) for the duration (4 h) of the cultures. To block Fc receptors, cells were incubated in the undiluted supernatant of an in‐house generated anti‐mouse CD16/CD32 hybridoma (clone 2.4G2) for 15 min at room temperature. Following blockade of Fc receptors, cells were stained for 15 min at 4 degrees with viability dye (fixable viability dye (eFluor506); eBioscience), stained with cell surface markers (30–45 min at room temperature), fixed and permeabilized for 60 min at 4 degrees using eBioscience Foxp3 Transcription Factor Fixation/Permeabilization Concentrate and Diluent kit (Invitrogen), followed by overnight intracellular staining in presence of saponin at 4°C. Antibodies used to identify mouse pulmonary lymphocyte populations included anti‐CD90.2‐BV605 (clone: 53–2.1; BioLegend), anti‐TCRβ‐APC‐Cy7 (clone: H57‐597; eBioscience), anti‐CD3‐AlexaFluor700 (clone: eBio500A2; eBioscience), anti‐CD4‐BV711 (clone: RM4‐5; BioLegend), anti‐CD8α‐BUV737 (clone: 53–6.7; BD Biosciences), anti‐CD44‐PE‐eFluor610 (clone: IM7; eBioscience), anti‐TCRδ‐PE‐Cy7 (clone: GL3; BioLegend), anti‐NK1.1‐APC (clone: PK136; BioLegend), anti‐IL‐13‐eFluor450 (clone: eBio13A; eBioscience), anti‐IL‐17A‐PE (clone: eBio17B7; eBioscience), anti‐IL‐22‐PerCP‐eFluor710 (clone: 1H8PWSR; eBioscience), and anti‐IFNγ‐FITC (clone: XMG1.2; eBioscience). Data were acquired on BD LSRFortessa.

MAIT‐B Cell co‐Cultures

MAIT cells and B cells were isolated from lungs and spleens, respectively, of C57BL/6 mice using Buoyancy Activated Cell Sorting (Akadeum BACS; B cell isolation kit catalog #12210–110; T cell isolation kit catalog #11210–120) isolation kits. MAIT cells were further isolated via FACS (CD90.2+CD4−CD8−NK1.1−Vβ6+/8+). Purity of isolated B cell and MAIT cell populations was generally between 95%–98% and 99%–100%, respectively. Purified cells were plated in round‐bottom 96‐well plates in complete RPMI 1640. 5 x104 B cells ±1.5 × 104 MAIT cells were cultured in the presence of LPS (5 μg/mL), IL‐4 (5 ng/mL), anti‐IFNγ (1 and 10 μg/mL), and/or anti‐CD3/CD28 beads (1:1 bead:T cell ratio; Invitrogen) and incubated for 5 or 7 days.

Histology

Left lung lobes were fixed, sectioned, and stained with H&E or PAS by the CCHMC Integrated Pathology Research Core. Inflammatory scores were assigned as follows: (1) No immune cell infiltration seen. (2) Immune cell infiltration from blood vessels apparent but migration to airways is minimal or absent. (3) Infiltrating immune cells surrounding larger airways more apparent with some possible thickening of larger airway epithelium and/or basement layer. Distal airways may have immune cell migration from blood vessels. (4) Infiltrating immune cells surrounding both large and distal airways, and thickening of airway epithelium and basement membranes apparent.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v10 (GraphPad Software). Normality of datasets was tested (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test; GraphPad Prism 10). Once normality was established, comparisons between relevant groups were done using an unpaired Student's t‐test (in vivo studies comparing HDM and HDM + A6FP), one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test (in vitro studies comparing effects of MAIT cells on B cell IgE production), or two‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction (in vivo studies of airway hyperreactivity). Where indicated, a Mann–Whitney test or Kruskal Wallis test was used to test datasets that did not follow a normal distribution. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Antagonizing MAIT Cells Aggravates Airway Hyperresponsiveness

Prior investigations exploring the role of MAIT cells in asthma demonstrate a protective role for MAIT cells following exposure to Alternaria alternata [34, 35, 44, 45], a highly ILC2‐dependent model of allergic asthma [46, 47]. To dissect the contributions of endogenous MAIT cells in a T cell‐driven model, we pharmacologically inhibited MAIT cells during pulmonary house dust mite (HDM) administration using the MAIT cell inhibitor Acetyl‐6‐formylpterin (A6FP) [48, 49, 50, 51] (Figure 1A). A6FP binds to and stabilizes MR1 on the surface of the APC and allows it to engage with the MAIT‐cell expressed TCR. In contrast to other ligands, MR1: A6FP complexes fail to activate the TCR of the MAIT cell, therefore acting as a competitive inhibitor [48, 49]. Thus, administration of A6FP allowed for inhibition only during the period of allergen exposure and limited potential effects of lacking MAIT cells through development. A6FP (or vehicle) was administered i.t. with each HDM dose, and intraperitoneally (i.p.) between exposures ensuring consistent inhibition. Seventy two hours after the final HDM challenge, we assessed airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) and lung inflammation. PBS‐treated mice showed minimal AHR, which was unaffected by A6FP (Figure 1B). HDM exposure increased AHR, which was further enhanced by A6FP co‐treatment (Figure 1B), confirming a previously observed protective role for MAIT cells in airway function [27, 34]. While the differences observed in the magnitude of AHR in HDM versus HDM + A6FP are comparatively small in magnitude, similar changes in magnitude of AHR are observed in mice undergoing a mild Th2‐dominated immune response versus those undergoing a mixed Th2/Th17 immune response more reminiscent of severe asthma [41, 43]. Similar findings were seen in BALB/c mice, a more “Th2‐biased” strain (Figure S1). Thus, endogenous MAIT cells inhibit T cell dependent, HDM‐induced AHR, regardless of genetic background.

To determine if A6FP inhibited MAIT cells, we assessed pulmonary MAIT cell cytokine expression by flow cytometry (gating strategy Figures 1C and S2). As some MAIT cells may not bind to MR‐1:5‐OP‐RU tetramers [52, 53], an alternative approach to identify MAIT cells, regardless of antigen specificity, was used. Consistent with a previous report highlighting that lung MAIT cells from C57BL/6 mice do not express CD4, CD8, or NK1.1, and express specific TCR β chains (Vβ6, or Vβ8) [54], MAIT cells were identified as CD90.2+TCRδnegTCRβ +CD3+CD4negCD8negTCRVβ6/8+ NK1.1neg cells (Figure 1C). Neither HDM nor HDM + A6FP exposure affected the number or frequency of MAIT cells in the lungs (Figure 1D). Despite similar frequencies of cytokine‐producing MAIT cells (IFNγ+, IL‐17A+, IL‐22+) in PBS and HDM‐treated mice, HDM increased the absolute number of cytokine‐producing MAIT cells (Figure 1E–G), likely reflecting HDM‐driven immune cell infiltration. Importantly, the frequency and number of IL‐17A+ MAIT cells were significantly reduced in HDM + A6FP‐treated mice (Figure 1E). A6FP also reduced IFNγ+ and IL‐22+ MAIT cells (Figure 1F,G), although this did not reach statistical significance.

As our MAIT cell targeting regimen included both local (i.t) and systemic (i.p.) administration of A6FP, we also examined the impact of HDM and HDM + A6FP administration in the spleen in a small number of animals. As shown in Figure S3, HDM had little impact on the frequency of MAIT cells in the spleen (Supplementary Figure S3A) or the frequency of MAIT cells producing IL‐17A, IL‐22, or IFNγ (Figure S3B–D). Mice treated with HDM + A6FP demonstrated a decreased frequency of total MAIT cells, as well as a decrease in the frequency of MAIT cells producing IL‐17A or IFNγ (Figure S3). These data suggest that administration of A6FP was sufficient to limit MAIT cell activity in multiple compartments. Together, these findings demonstrate that A6FP reduces systemic MAIT cell activity and increases HDM‐induced AHR.

Increased AHR Observed in HDM + A6FP Treated Animals Is Not Associated With Increased Production of Th2 or Th17‐Associated Cytokines

MAIT cells have previously been shown to limit Alternaria alternata ‐induced asthma through effects on ILC2s [34, 35]. To determine if cytokine production by non‐T cells was reduced in A6FP‐treated mice, cytokine‐producing CD90.2+ TCRβneg CD3neg cells (which include ILCs—hereafter called non‐T cells) were quantified by flow cytometry (see gating, Figure S2). Although HDM exposure increased total (Figure 2A) and cytokine producing non‐T cells (Figure 2B–D), A6FP did not alter numbers or frequency of total, IL‐13+, IL‐17A+, or IL‐22+ non‐T cells (Figure 2A–D). In contrast to the observation of elevated ILC2‐derived cytokine production in models of Alternaria alternata‐induced asthma [34, 35], these data suggest that increased AHR observed in models of HDM‐induced asthma is not associated with increased production of cytokines by pulmonary non‐T cells, including ILCs.

To better understand how A6FP increased HDM‐induced AHR, we examined the frequency of other cytokine‐producing lymphocytes (CD4+, γδ T cell) by flow cytometry. As expected, HDM exposure increased the number of IL‐13+ cells (Figure 2E), IL‐13+ CD4+ T cells (Figure 2F), and IL‐13+ γδ T cells (Figure 2G). Importantly, A6FP did not alter the frequency of any IL‐13‐producing cells indicating that A6FP‐induced increases in AHR were independent of type 2 cytokine production. As IL‐17A can also influence AHR [23, 24, 51, 55, 56, 57, 58], we also assessed IL‐17A production (Figure 2H–J). Although HDM exposure increased total IL‐17A+ cells (Figure 2H), IL‐17A+ CD4+ T cells (Figure 2I), and IL‐17A+ γδ T cells (Figure 2J), frequencies of IL‐17A+ CD4+ and γδ T cells were unchanged in HDM + A6FP treated mice. Similarly, A6FP did not alter IFNγ+ or IL‐22+ T cell populations in the lungs (Figure S4). As an alternative approach to assess HDM‐induced cytokine production, cytokine levels were assessed in HDM‐restimulated lung cell cultures. Cytokine production increased from HDM treatment but was not affected by A6FP (Figure 2K–M), consistent with flow cytometry findings.

MAIT Cell Antagonism via A6FP Does Not Augment HDM‐Induced Lung Inflammation

We next determined if A6FP altered HDM‐induced pulmonary inflammation. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid (BALF) was collected and differential cell counts were performed. HDM exposure increased numbers of eosinophils (Figure 3A), neutrophils (Figure 3B), and lymphocytes (Figure 3C), with smaller effects on macrophages (Figure 3D). Notably, HDM + A6FP treatment did not alter granulocyte or lymphocyte counts compared to HDM alone (Figure 3A–D), suggesting that the increased AHR observed in the presence of MAIT cell antagonism by A6FP is not due to enhanced granulocytic inflammation. Histological assessment of lung inflammation revealed that HDM treatment induced robust inflammation (histology scores 3–4), but that A6FP treatment did not increase HDM‐induced inflammation (Figure 3E,F). Similarly, assessment of mucus production demonstrated that both HDM and HDM + A6FP groups exhibited marked, but comparable, increases in PAS+ cells (Figure 3G,H). Together, these data suggest that MAIT cell protection from HDM‐induced AHR occurs independent of airway inflammation, cytokine production, and mucus secretion.

MAIT Cell Antagonism via A6FP Enhances AHR Through Increased IgE Production

As IgE plays a central role in driving allergic asthma symptoms [4, 5, 6], we determined whether A6FP enhanced HDM‐induced immunoglobulin levels in serum by ELISA. HDM exposure elevated serum IgE and IgG1, with modest changes in IgA or IgM (Figure 4A–D). Notably, A6FP further increased HDM‐induced IgE levels (Figure 4A) without affecting levels of IgA (Figure 4B), IgM (Figure 4C), or IgG1 (Figure 4D). Enhancement of IgE was also observed in BALB/c mice (Figure S5), suggesting that MAIT cells similarly regulate IgE responses across genetic backgrounds.

To determine whether IgE production was required for A6FP‐mediated enhancement of AHR, we utilized IgE‐deficient (IgE KO) mice. IgE KO and C57BL/6 mice were exposed to PBS, HDM, or HDM + A6FP and AHR was assessed. HDM exposure induced significant AHR in IgE KO mice; however, unlike in WT mice, A6FP co‐treatment did not aggravate this response. A similar pattern was observed in IgE KO BALB/c mice (Supplemental Figure S6A) and C57BL/6 mice lacking B cells (μMT mice; Supplemental Figure S6B), reinforcing the IgE dependency of A6FP‐induced AHR enhancement. As in WT controls, A6FP did not significantly alter HDM‐induced increases in BAL eosinophils (Figure 4F), neutrophils (Figure 4G), lymphocytes (Figure 4H), monocytes/macrophages (Figure 4I), or cytokine production (IL‐5, IL‐13, IL‐17A; Figure 4J–L). Collectively, these studies demonstrate that IgE production is required for increased AHR observed after inhibition of MAIT cells in HDM‐induced models.

MAIT Cells Restrict IgE Production In Vitro

To determine whether MAIT cells influenced B cell IgE production, C57BL/6 MAIT cells and B cells were co‐cultured at a 1:3 ratio in the presence of LPS + IL‐4. IgE production was assessed by ELISA, and the frequency of IgE‐producing cells was assessed by flow cytometry (gating strategy: Figure S7). Culture of B cells with LPS + IL‐4 induced IgE secretion (Figure 5A) and class switch to IgE (Figure 5B,C). Co‐culture of B cells with MAIT cells suppressed IL‐4 + LPS‐induced IgE production (Figure 5A) and the frequency of IgE+ B cells (Figure 5B,C). While MAIT cells exhibited baseline IgE‐suppressive activity, stimulation via αCD3/CD28 amplified this effect, resulting in near‐complete abrogation of IgE production, demonstrating that activated MAIT cells strongly inhibit IgE production. Since TCR‐dependent MAIT cell activation requires MR1–TCR interactions [18, 59, 60, 61, 62, 63], we investigated the dynamics of B cell MR1 expression in our co‐cultures. Flow cytometric analysis showed that B cells express MR1 at baseline, but this expression was reduced in the presence of IL‐4, MAIT cells, or both (Figure 5D–F). No significant differences in the frequency of MR1+ B cells were observed across conditions. These observations suggest that IgE inhibition by MAIT cells is not likely due to MR1–TCR signaling.

MAIT‐Cell Derived IFNγ Suppresses IgE Production

As IFNγ is a common MAIT‐cell‐derived cytokine and inhibitor of IgE [8, 32, 33, 60], we assessed IFNγ production in co‐cultured MAIT cells via flow cytometry (Figure 6A–C). The lowest IFNγ production was observed in cultures with MAIT cells and B cells alone, with modest increases upon αCD3/CD28 stimulation. IL‐4 significantly enhanced IFNγ production by MAIT cells, paralleling their IgE‐suppressive effect (Figure 6B). Similar trends were seen when IFNγ+ MAIT cells were quantified (Figure 6C), supporting the hypothesis that MAIT cell stimulation increases IFNγ production. To confirm that IFNγ was required for IgE inhibition, MAIT cells and B cells were co‐cultured with anti‐IFNγ (Figure 6D). IL‐4 stimulation induced IgE production and MAIT cells suppressed IL‐4‐induced IgE. Importantly, suppression was reversed by IFNγ blockade in a dose‐dependent manner—partial abrogation at 1 μg/mL anti‐IFNγ and complete inhibition at 10 μg/mL anti‐IFNγ (Figure 6D). These results indicate that MAIT cell‐derived IFNγ is a key mediator of IgE suppression in B cells.

Discussion

Herein, we demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of MAIT cells aggravates HDM‐induced AHR in C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice. Contrary to prior studies where MAIT cells repressed ILC2‐mediated immune responses in Alternaria alternata ‐induced asthma models through anti‐inflammatory molecules like IL‐4‐Induced 1 (IL4I1) [34, 35], we found that MAIT cell inhibition in HDM‐driven asthma did not alter pulmonary inflammation or Th2 cytokine production by non‐T cells or conventional T cells. Rather, enhanced AHR was associated with elevated serum IgE levels, and aggravation of AHR was abolished in IgE‐deficient mice, suggesting that increased AHR observed in the context of MAIT cell antagonism requires IgE. In vitro, MAIT cells suppressed IgE production, and this inhibition was reversed in the presence of anti‐IFNγ. Thus, we present a novel immunoregulatory axis through which MAIT cells constrain allergic inflammation and suggest that MAIT cells possess allergen‐specific inhibitory mechanisms.

A central pathway revealed by this study is the inhibition of B cell–mediated IgE responses by MAIT cells. Through their capacity to secrete factors like IL‐21, IL‐6, IL‐18, MIP‐1α/β, MIF, and CXCL12, MAIT cells are typically regarded as positive regulators of B cell activation, differentiation, and/or antibody production [38, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70]. However, our data reveal a contrasting role in allergic inflammation. In this setting, MAIT cells suppress IgE production, thereby constraining IgE‐mediated immunopathology and AHR. These findings highlight the context‐dependent nature of MAIT cell‐B cell interactions, which may shift from helper to regulatory modes based on the surrounding inflammatory milieu.

Our in vitro experiments revealed that MAIT activation via αCD3/CD28 unleashed the strongest capacity for IFNγ production. IFNγ is well‐documented to antagonize B cell IgE production [8, 25], serving as a key immune regulator and protector against airway hyperreactivity. However, the mechanism by which IFNγ is produced by MAIT cells in HDM‐driven asthma remains unresolved. Notably, our in vitro data also reveals IL‐4 was sufficient to increase MAIT cell IFNγ production. MAIT cells have been reported to express Il4Ra [71, 72]. Supporting this, analysis of publicly available RNA‐Seq datasets from MAIT cells and CD4+ T cells [34] demonstrated readily detectable expression of both IL‐4Rα and γc on MAIT cells (Figure S8). As IL‐4 is a classical inducer of IgE production, this highlights a naturally antagonistic relationship between MAIT cells and IgE. Consistent with this, despite the predominantly TH2‐skewed response in HDM‐exposed mice, we observed a marked increase in IFNγ+ MAIT cell numbers in response to HDM in vivo. Although induction of IFNγ responses during type 2 responses cytokines seems counterintuitive, IL‐4 (but not IL‐13) induces IFNγ responses in mouse lung [73] suggesting that IL‐4 may prime MAIT cells to suppress IgE production via IFNγ, potentially creating a feedback loop that limits allergic inflammation and maintains immune homeostasis. Alternatively, HDM may activate MAIT cells through the TCR. While our in vitro experiments did not display shifts in MR1 expression between groups, HDM extract can activate MAIT cells, and co‐treatment with HDM and 5‐OP‐RU (a MAIT stimulatory ligand) synergistically enhances MAIT cell activation [33]. Thus, although the identity of the HDM‐derived MAIT cell TCR ligands remains unclear, we cannot rule out TCR stimulation completely. Indeed, given the nature of HDM as a complex allergen containing both microbial and non‐microbial components, it is possible that both TCR and cytokine signaling pathways contribute to MAIT cell activation in HDM‐induced models of asthma.

The anatomical site at which MAIT cells: B cell interactions occur is unclear. The lung‐draining lymph node is a key site for antigen presentation and initiation of adaptive immune responses in the respiratory tract, so it is plausible that lung MAIT cells migrate to these nodes and influence B cell class switching and/or IgE production therein. Indeed, within the lymph node, MAIT cells reside primarily in the B cell zones, whereas conventional T cells reside in the T cell zones [65]. Recent evidence also suggests that lung‐resident IgG1+ memory B cells can act as precursors to IgE‐producing cells following allergen rechallenge, generating IgE locally in the lung [74]. Decreased frequencies of circulating MAIT cells following microbial or allergen exposures have been observed—potentially reflecting their recruitment to inflamed tissues [28, 29, 75, 76, 77]. Thus, MAIT cells may be recruited from the circulation to the lung and converge with B cells in the lung parenchyma, particularly within inducible bronchus‐associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT) structures that emerge in response to allergic inflammation [78]. Future studies are needed to characterize differences between circulating and tissue‐resident MAIT cells in HDM‐driven asthma and map the spatial distribution of MAIT cells, B cells, and IgE production within the lung and mediastinal lymph nodes.

MAIT cells act as sentinels bridging innate and adaptive immunity and participate in microbial infections, tissue repair, and cancer. Our study reveals a role in allergic disease as regulators of B cell responses. MAIT cell involvement in B cell maturation and memory has also been described [38, 64, 69, 79] suggesting MAIT cells play context‐dependent roles—supporting or restraining humoral immunity—depending on the immune environment. By demonstrating that MAIT cells suppress IgE through IFNγ, we identify a novel immunological axis with therapeutic potential. Enhancing or restoring MAIT cell function in allergic lungs could reduce IgE‐mediated inflammation and asthma exacerbations. This is supported by studies on allergic rhinitis where restored IFNγ‐producing MAIT cells correlated with decreased IgE during allergen immunotherapy [33]. Pharmacologic strategies like MR1‐binding agonists to boost MAIT cell activity show promise for asthma treatment. Although our studies demonstrate that MAIT cell antagonism during acute allergen sensitization and challenge can worsen asthma outcomes, it remains unclear whether MAIT cell modulation would have an impact after allergen sensitization, and thereby have the ability to alter more disease activity in more chronic forms of disease. Further investigation into the timing of relevant MAIT cell–MR1–ligand interactions is crucial for developing more potent and selective therapeutics.

Although our study was completed in mice, it is important to point out that MAIT cells are significantly more abundant in humans than in common laboratory mouse strains. In humans, MAIT cells account for up to 10% of peripheral blood T cells and are abundant at mucosal surfaces like the lung [39]. Further a deficiency in these cells correlates with increased asthma severity [29, 32, 34]. There is also substantial evidence that human lung MAIT cells are potent producers of IFNγ through both TCR‐dependent recognition of microbial metabolites and TCR‐independent activation by cytokines such as IL‐12 and IL‐18 [36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Crucially, recent human studies have demonstrated that restoring IFNγ‐producing MAIT cells via immunotherapy in allergic rhinitis patients is associated with decreased allergen‐specific IgE levels [33], aligning with our finding that MAIT‐derived IFNγ acts as a direct negative regulator of B cell IgE production.

In conclusion, our results define a previously unappreciated function for MAIT cells in restraining IgE‐mediated responses and limiting asthma severity. These findings support the MAIT–B cell axis as a critical regulatory pathway and offer a potential target for future therapeutic development in allergic disease. Although our study highlights the importance of MAIT cells in regulation of allergic asthma, our findings have important implications for other IgE mediated diseases (e.g., food allergy, atopic dermatitis). Further, by highlighting a previously unappreciated inhibitory role for MAIT cells in the regulation of immunoglobulin production, these findings have broader implications on other areas of immunity where immunoglobulins play an important role.
