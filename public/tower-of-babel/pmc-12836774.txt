Sepsis related multi-organ dysfunction: a global perspective on lung and gastrointestinal mortality and management framework
Authors: Yang Liu, Zekun Wei, Weihong Liu, Hao Hao, Tejin Ba, Shuanglin Zhang, Bagenna Bao, Cunyang Li, Li Kong, Feihu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent-day’s sepsis is reported as a life-threatening global health problem responsible for 11 million deaths and 50% of them were associated with lung and gastrointestinal infections. The present study aims to analyse available data from WHO mortality database for the assessment of worldwide lung and gastrointestinal sepsis mortality statistics. The study covers 106 countries and territories from 7 different geographical regions from different income groups. A total of 218 causes related to lung and 146 causes related to gastrointestinal sepsis mortality from 2017 to 2021 were analyzed. Gender and age wise specific analysis was also done covering two genders and 23 age categories. High income countries were found with the highest mortality rates at 1,066.76 per 1,00,000 population suppressing the rates in developing nations. Further, mortality rates were found to be higher in European countries than in their Asian counterparts. During age standardized mortality analysis, males were found with high vulnerabilities during early and middle age whereas females were found to be more susceptible in older age groups. For lung-related sepsis male deaths (1,30,195) were found to be higher than female deaths (9,58,08) in the early neonatal stage. However, this trend was found to be reversed in the elderly population. This comprehensive analysis showed the need for targeted age and gender-specific approaches for prevention and management of this critical health issue.Keywords: Sepsis-related mortality, Lung and gastrointestinal infections, Global health disparities, Age and gender vulnerability, Critical care management

Introduction

Sepsis is a global health challenge that contributes significantly to worldwide morbidity and mortality, affecting nearly 50 million individuals annually, resulting in ~ 11 million fatalities [1]. Among different types of sepsis respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts are reported as frequently impacted organs. Further, during sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is reported as a severe complication, impacting up to two-fifths of patients with a mortality risk of 40–50% [2]. ARDS is characterised by damage to the endothelial lining, vascular leakage, and abnormal inflammatory responses [3]. Alongside, for the gastrointestinal system sepsis is associated with intestinal motility, low nutrient absorption, along with increased bacterial penetration through the gut wall. It is also reported that 50–80% of patients during critical care experience gastrointestinal complications [4]. The breakdown of the intestinal barrier during sepsis can create systemic inflammation by bacterial translocation [5]. Current days, available therapies for sepsis generally focus on early antibiotic administration, fluid resuscitation, and organ support, goal-directed therapy etc. However, limitations still exist in addressing the complex mechanism of sepsis-induced organ failure with antibiotic resistance [6]. Strategies like fluid resuscitation strategies are sometimes associated with overload and lung injury in sepsis-induced ARDS .

Sepsis mortality rate is reported to be high, particularly in low and middle-income where access to intensive care resources is challengingThe heterogeneity of sepsis is associated with the lack of targeted therapies for organ specific-dysfunctions contribute to sepsis-related mortality globally. Nowadays, best practice guideline, such as surviving sepsis campaign, promotes antibiotic initiation and fluid administration within the first hour of sepsis diagnosis. However, proper implementation of these recommendations still remains challenging. Furthermore, the long-term effect of sepsis on survivors is also challenging as a wide number of patients who overcome sepsis face readmission to hospitals within three months, often due to repeated infections or deterioration on pre-existing health conditions [7]. This underscores the vital need for advances in post-sepsis care strategies to address this global issue.

With this context, there is a gap in understanding of the global mortality dynamics of lung and gastrointestinal sepsis. It is crucial for getting the clinical context of this condition. In the present study, this knowledge gap was analysed by considering data from the Global Burden of Diseases (GDB) and WHO mortality datasets (2017–2021) on global and national scale, covering 106 countries and territories with 218 causes related to lung and 146 causes related to gastrointestinal sepsis mortality with both genders and 23 age categories.

Methodology

Lung and gastrointestinal sepsis classification

In the present study, cause identification behind lung and gastrointestinal sepsis was done considering fortwo types of causes including, explicit (with direct ICD codes) and implicit (with infection and dysfunction code) [8]. Classification was also done for sepsis cases and deaths based on their root causes including infections, injuries, and non-communicable diseases [9]. Further, through root cause analysis of the codes were done for classifying the lung and gastrointestinal sepsis mortality causes [10]. It was considered that, acute lung injury caused by sepsis involves severe inflammatory response, alveolar wall damage, and impaired gas exchange [11]. Gastrointestinal sepsis was considered as systemic illness caused by microbial invasion of the GI tract, resulting in inflammation and possible organ dysfunction [12].

Data mining and categorization

Using the data mining technique detailed analysis was done on the World Health Organization’s mortality database, specifically the ICD-10 (part 5/5) section for 106 countries and territories over a six-year period (2017–2022). This analysis provided yearly statistics on mortality, neatly categorized by age, sex, and cause [13].

Clear inclusion and exclusion criteria were well-known for the present analysis to assure data quality and cross-regional comparability. This analysis was carried out for the period (2017–2021). The analysis included all sepsis-associated deaths identified by explicit or implicit codes for gastrointestinal and lung infections under ICD-10, during the period 2017–2021, all sepsis- associated deaths that integrated open or hidden finding codes for gastrointestinal and lung infections as considered under ICD-10 were incorporated. The age groups 23 and populations of all genders were thought to offer a systematic representation and provide a widespread overview. We measured counties having completed and accessible WHO mortality database for the designated years. Records with missing demographic data, duplicate entries, unclear coding that did not satisfy the diagnostic threshold for sepsis, or mortality occurrences in which sepsis was not established as causal cause of death were among the segregation criteria [14, 15]. By implementing these standards, the dataset minimized impartiality from conflicting or missing data while capturing accurate representations of sepsis mortality associated to the gastrointestinal and respiratory disorders. Further, the data was categorised into seven geographical areas including East Asia and Pacific, Europe and central Asia, South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, Middle East and North Africa, Latin America and Caribbean and North America for analysis of the geographical region wise analysis. Further, a comprehensive investigation was done for analysis of sepsis-related mortality trends across diverse populations and healthcare systems. The data was categorized into seven distinct geographical regions including East Asia and Pacific, Europe and Central Asia, Latin America and Caribbean, Middle East and North Africa, North America, South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa for the analysis of geographical characterization [16]. In subsequent steps analysis was carried out covering different income groups, viz., lower –income, lower middle income, upper middle income and high income economies for investigating the socio-economic influence on the lung and gastro intestinal sepsis related mortality [17].

Statistical approach

During the statistical analysis the direct method and age-specific population estimation was used to compute age-adjusted mortality rates for lung and gastrointestinal sepsis [18]. Annual percent change was calculated by fitting a regression line to the natural logarithm of rates over the last six years of available data [19]. Analyses was also done separately for men and women, as well as for specific age categories starting with three neonatal categories (early neonatal, late neonatal, and post neonatal), followed by 5-year intervals from age 5 to 94, and finally one category for those 95 and above), for understanding potential differences in lung and gastrointestinal sepsis-related mortality across different demographic groups [20].

Age standardised lung and gastrointestinal-related sepsis mortality patters across different sex

Lung related sepsis mortality

The results of our study on sepsis-related deaths associated with lung conditions revealed significant patterns across age groups and sexes. In the neonatal period, we observed the highest mortality rates, with male deaths (130,195) surpassing female deaths (95,808) in the early neonatal stage. This disparity persisted, albeit to a lesser extent, in late neonatal and post-neonatal periods. Childhood and adolescence (ages 1–19) showed a marked decrease in deaths, reaching the lowest levels between ages 5–14 for both sexes, with males consistently experiencing slightly higher mortality. As age progressed into adulthood (20–64 years), we noted a gradual increase in deaths for both sexes, with males consistently showing higher mortality rates. This trend continued until a significant shift occurred in the elderly population (65 + years). Female deaths surpassed male deaths starting from the 70–74 age groups, with the disparity becoming more pronounced in advanced age. Notably, in the 85–89 age groups, female deaths peaked at 165,064 compared to 101,925 for males. Analysis of mortality rates per 100,000 populations revealed further insights. Male mortality rates were highest during the neonatal period (approximately 57,000 per 100,000) and remained relatively stable until middle adulthood. After age 55, male mortality rates showed a steady decline, reaching their lowest levels in the oldest age groups In contrast; female mortality rates began to rise sharply after age 75, eventually surpassing male rates, while remaining consistently lower than male rates in younger and middle-aged groups. The highest female mortality rate was observed in the 95 + age group, reaching approximately 71,690 per 100,000 populations. Further, Linear regression analysis reveals opposing trajectories between genders. The male population exhibits a declining trend (y = -780.16x + 62397), while the female population shows an increasing pattern (780.16x + 37603). These findings underscore a pronounced sex- and age-based disparity in sepsis-related lung mortality. Males exhibited higher vulnerability during early life and middle adulthood, while females faced disproportionately higher risks in older age groups. This pattern likely reflects a combination of biological factors, including differences in immune responses and the impact of longer life expectancy in females.

Gastrointestinal-related sepsis mortality

A comprehensive analysis of gastrointestinal sepsis mortality patterns reveals significant age and gender-specific variations in both absolute deaths and population-adjusted mortality rates. The neonatal period exhibits concerning mortality rates, particularly among male infants. In most of the areas where high newborn infants mortality, mainly in low- and middle-income countries, the newborn male infants have mortality rates is higher 140 per 100,000 whereas female infants have relatively lower rates at about 100 per 100,000 population. This difference also seen into the late neonatal and post-neonatal phases, showing that male newborns are biologically influence to problems from gastrointestinal sepsis. In adulthood, the mortality scenario changes significantly. The 3rd phase of life is where mortality rates to rise remarkably, with male mortality rates rising between the ages of 20 to 54. It was observed that the age group between 45 and 54 has the highest male mortality rate, which is nearly 180 per 100,000 people. For male patients, physiological and environmental factors are played crucial role that may be impacted.

Female mortality rates demonstrate a steady increase with advancing age, ultimately surpassing male rates in the population aged 85 and above. The highest female mortality rates are observed in the 95 + age group, approaching 70 per 100,000 population. This pattern suggests age-related changes in susceptibility to gastrointestinal sepsis may be gender-dependent. Further, linear regression analysis reveals opposing trajectories between genders. The male population exhibits a declining trend (y = -676.55x + 67563), while the female population shows an increasing pattern (y = 676.55x + 32437). These findings underscore the importance of age and gender-specific approaches in the prevention and management of gastrointestinal sepsis, particularly in high-risk populations identified through this epidemiological analysis (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Management of lung and gastrointestinal related sepsis mortality is a global issue in current days with distinct economic groups, age and gender specific variations. During the present study high rates were found in Europe and Central Asia. In case of Europe, it was reported that sepsis occurrence is high with 54.1% of study population and depicting significant ICU and hospital mortality rates. The reason may be influenced by demographics, antimicrobial stewardship and allocation of resources during critical care [21, 22]. The global mortality rate if sepsis was reported to be declining still the reported areas need special attention. Further, the result revealed that the high income nations have higher mortality rates comparing with the developing countries which depict potential systemic disparities during sepsis recognition or reporting practices and therapeutic recognition. It is fact that sepsis is more common in low-and middle-income overall, high-income countries (HICs) remarkably have higher fatality rates from pulmonary and gastrointestinal sepsis as compare to low-and middle-income (LMICs). Various factor are responsible for sharing to this scenario inclusive the fact that older HIC populations are more likely to have chronic respiratory and gastrointestinal disease, which increase their weakness to severe organ-specific problem that could lead to sepsis.Timely diagnoses and accurate identification and their classification of the source of sepsis, made more beneficial by the complex healthcare practice of HICs, also help to higher reported fatality rates in these categories. These numbers may be minimize due to under-reporting and insufficient diagnostic equipment, even though sepsis has a higher incidence and mortality in LMICs.The general variation were also seen between the minimum rate in the occupied Palestinian Territory and France, further more emphasizes the previously mentioned elements of acquired pathogen resistance and the durability of the healthcare markdown infrastructure [23, 24]. As per recent research reported sepsis is also linked to neighborhood-level variables like park proximity and housing expense burden [25]. The timing of sepsis-related mortality is also important to have a major impact on mortality rates; for example, a 29% reduction in risk was reported in the source nation within 6 h interval of sepsis [26] Genetic variances and other Hormonal difference may have an impact on gender-wise gap in sepsis outcomes. The estrogen hormone found in females may give better outcomes, as it has an anti-inflammatory effect [27]. There is a clear hepatic correlation are seen between intestinal sepsis, cirrhosis and liver disease, which are important resign of death. Patients having Intestinal diseases like gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), where symbiosis increases mainly due to illness, focused the role of the gut and liver in the development of sepsis. The main pulmonary sepsis cases are caused by streptococcal infections, which are also often the, major cause of acute lung injury (ALI) rates above 40% 46, 47 [28, 29]. Pulmonary embolism (PE), which is very common in patients having septic shock significantly increases in the death rate from sepsis [30]. Sepsis in newborn infants remains a basic problem with 153,078 deaths, underscoring the important for required prenatal care procedures and early-life immunological susceptibility [31]. Further, the results indicate the need for thorough strategies for management of sepsis considering patient’s unique conditions such as gender and socioeconomic status. However, older women are more at risk after the age of 70 [32]. In case of gastrointestinal sepsis males aged 45–54 have the highest fatality rate. Current days, several impactful management initiatives include alternate vasopressors, conservative fluid management in particular populations, and customized antibiotic treatments based on quick microorganism identification etc. during the first 24h of care. Autologous cryo-shocked neutrophils are also used current days to neutralize endotoxins and pro-inflammatory cytokines [33]. The clinical possibility and outcome of sepsis are extensively influenced by its microbial constituent. Whereas lung-related cases are commonly linked to Gram-positive pathogens including streptococcus pneumonia and Staphylococcus aureus, gastrointestinal sepsis is often linked to Gram-negative bacteria like Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumonia, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [34]. Additionally, fungi like Candida species are becoming recognized as vital contributors, particularly in patients with weakened immune systems and those receiving extensive critical care management [35, 36]. Since delayed commencement of sufficient antibiotic medication is considerably linked with increased mortality, the global rise of multidrug-resistant pathogens makes management even more difficult [37, 38]. Variations in antibiotic use, infection control measures, and the resilience of the healthcare system are reflected in regional variances in microbial profiles [39]. Therefore, it is crucial to use initial microbiological diagnostics, such as culture-based sensitivity testing and quick molecular assays, to direct targeted therapy, reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure, and improve sepsis outcomes [40, 41]. Additionally, machine learning models including logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost are also reported with promising outcomes in anticipating sepsis occurrence and optimizing treatment options [42]. In case of low- and middle-income nations reporting with delayed diagnosis and antibiotic resistance, pediatric sepsis is being addressed through the use of telemedicine and collaborative care approaches. These diverse approaches suggested by the present work seek to address the complex interactions between variables that contribute to lung and gastrointestinal sepsis-related mortality and will ultimately lower the worldwide burden of sepsis.

Management framework

Based on the above results and available literature reports a multidimensional framework could be followed that will address issues in various income group countries including regional specificity (Fig. 6). During analysis it was found that countries with high income groups are reported with high incidences which may be because of aging populations and lifestyle [43]. Suggestions like effective interventions (Upper-middle-income); fundamental health care infrastructure improvement (lower-middle-income economies); early recognition and maternal and paediatric management strategies (Low-income economies) may be implemented for management of lung and gastrointestinal sepsis [44–46]. Thereafter, the framework also contains regional strategies like investigation of high mortality rates in Europe and Asia followed by Middle East and North Africa [47, 48]; management of hospital-acquired infections in Latin America and the Caribbean [49]. In the final stage, the framework also encompasses cross cutting planning relevant to all regions and income groups viz., prompt recognition and diagnosis [50, 51], appropriate intervention [52], enhancements in healthcare infrastructure [53], collaborative care [54], data-driven improvements [55], and public health initiatives. The strategies seek to mitigate the complicated relationships among economic status, regional variables, and healthcare practices to decrease the worldwide incidence of sepsis-related mortality.

Comprehensive framework for management of lung and gastrointestinal sepsis related mortality in world

Both immediate clinical intervention and long-term infection control should be given main concern in an absolute management arrange for pulmonary and gastrointestinal sepsis. To reduce antimicrobial resistance, it is essential to establish using broad-spectrum antibiotics that are specific to probable infection sites as soon as possible. This should be followed by de-escalation depending on culture sensitivity data [6, 56]. A key component of therapy is helpful source management, which lowers mortality considerably and can be achieved by removing necrotic lung tissue, draining abscesses, or repairing the colon. The possibility of multi-organ failure is reduced by supportive treatments such as lung-protective mechanical ventilation, vasopressor adjustment, and conservative but prompt fluid recovery [57]. Prone positioning for ARDS patients can extensively lower mortality in pulmonary cases, while enhanced recovery procedures with early mobilization and nutritional support improve results in gastrointestinal sepsis [58]. To stabilize hyper inflammatory reactions, new adjunctive treatments such immunomodulatory medications, extracorporeal blood purification, and modern biologics are being investigated in addition to conventional treatments [59] Last but not least, systematic sepsis bundles, frequent ICU team training, and hospital-wide quality enhancement initiatives have incessantly demonstrated improvements in survival and resource efficiency. Enhancing the addition of these organizational and therapeutic variables guarantees that management delivers patient-centred, evidence-based outcomes by going beyond demographic trends.

Conclusion

Lung and gastrointestinal sepsis related mortality are global health issues faced by patients with critical health conditions. During the present study, global pattern of the conditions was analysed across different geographical regions, income groups, age and gender categories. Significant outcomes of the work include high occurrence of mortality in high income group countries compared with developing nations may be governed by issues including lifestyle and intensified reporting of organ specific disorders. Further, findings of the study also highlights, high occurrence in male during early and middle adulthood and female are more impacted during old age. Overall findings of the work highlights specialised need of multifaceted management approaches for the management of lung and gastrointestinal sepsis.
