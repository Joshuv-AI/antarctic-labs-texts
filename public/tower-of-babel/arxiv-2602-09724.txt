Targum – A Multilingual New Testament Translation Corpus


Abstract
Many European languages possess rich biblical translation histories, yet existing corpora—in prioritizing linguistic breadth—often fail to capture this depth. To address this gap, we introduce a multilingual corpus of 657 New Testament translations, of which 352 are unique, with unprecedented depth in five languages: English (208 unique versions from 396 total), French (41 from 78), Italian (18 from 33), Polish (30 from 48), and Spanish (55 from 102). Aggregated from 12 online biblical libraries and one preexisting corpus, each translation is manually annotated with metadata that maps the text to a standardized identifier for the work, its specific edition, and its year of revision. This canonicalization empowers researchers to define "uniqueness" for their own needs: they can perform micro-level analyses on translation families, such as the KJV lineage, or conduct macro-level studies by deduplicating closely related texts. By providing the first resource designed for such flexible, multilevel analysis, our corpus establishes a new benchmark for the quantitative study of translation history. Keywords: Corpus, Digital Humanities, Multilinguality, Translation Studies, Text Mining

1.  Introduction

In the data-scarce era of early Natural Language Processing, the Bible was a widely used parallel corpus. Its utility for methods such as statistical machine translation (SMT) was based on the assumption that the verses between translations were semantically equivalent
10
. The current landscape of NLP, characterized by terabyte-scale datasets and large language models, redefines this utility. The Bible’s volume is negligible for training modern foundation models, and its specific thematic and stylistic registers are not representative of general language use. This shift reframes the main application of the corpus: from being data
for
AI systems to be an object of study
by
AI systems, enabling large-scale quantitative analysis of textual choices that reflect its cultural and theological history.

To facilitate this new analytical paradigm, we introduce
targum
, a corpus designed to prioritize vertical depth over linguistic breadth.
targum
provides coverage for five languages with extensive translation histories. English, French, Italian, Polish, and Spanish. This level of depth makes it possible to computationally analyze a wide spectrum of historical periods and confessional traditions, treating translation variants that were previously discarded by heuristic methods as a primary object of study.

The name of the corpus is a tribute to this methodological focus on the full spectrum of translation styles. A “Targum” (Aramaic/Hebrew:
תרגום
) was an ancient Aramaic translation of the Hebrew Bible
2
. The name itself means "translation" with roots traced to the Hittite tarkummaj ("to proclaim, explain, or translate"). The Targumic method was defined by a unique duality: it often combined passages of high literalness with dynamic additions and expansions to make the text accessible to its audience
13
. Our corpus is named for this principle, as it is designed to contain a massive collection of translations that spans this same spectrum — from the most formal, literal renderings to the most dynamic paraphrases.

The main contributions of this paper are two-fold. First, we introduce
targum
, a new multilingual corpus of 657 New Testament translations (352 unique) with deep coverage in English (208 unique from 396 total), French (41 from 78), Italian (18 from 33), Polish (30 from 48), and Spanish (55 from 102). Second, we manually verify metadata for each translation, including its publication history. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We first position our work with respect to existing corpora. We then detail the methodology for corpus construction and annotation, followed by a statistical analysis of the resulting resource. We conclude by discussing new research directions and plans for future work.

2.  Related Work

The Bible is a frequently used text for creating multilingual corpora due to its verse structure, which provides de facto parallel sentence-level alignment in a large number of languages
10
. Research using these corpora has followed two main paradigms. The most common has been
instrumental
, using the text as a convenient dataset to bootstrap NLP tools for low-resource languages, such as training machine translation systems
7
or creating part-of-speech taggers via annotation projection
12
;
11
. In contrast, a more recent paradigm, often aligned with the digital humanities, treats the biblical text as the
primary object of study
. This line of research applies computational methods to perform a diachronic analysis of language change or to study stylistic variation between translations. Our work is situated within this second paradigm.

A significant body of work has focused on creating biblical corpora that maximize linguistic breadth. Over time, a series of progressively larger collections were introduced, starting with foundational resources such as those from
6
and
8
. This trend culminated in the Johns Hopkins University Bible Corpus, which expanded coverage to over 1,600 languages to support tasks such as typological analysis and low-resource NLP
9
;
3
. The state of these digital resources, however, is often dynamic; for instance, while some corpora like that of
8
are actively maintained and have grown since their initial publication, the Johns Hopkins corpus is no longer publicly available. A more recent effort, the eBible Corpus, provides another standardized and accessible resource
1
. While these collections offer unparalleled breadth, their depth for any single language remains limited in comparison to our work. We provide a detailed statistical comparison in Section
4
, which highlights this gap.

Although most work has focused on breadth, some projects have curated collections with depth for specific scholarly questions. A prime example is the EDGeS Diachronic Bible Corpus, a resource containing 36 translations across four Germanic languages designed to enable the “longitudinal and contrastive study of complex verb constructions”
4
. Similarly,
5
compiles 34 English versions for the specific task of evaluating the transfer of prose style. Our work seeks to
extend and generalize this depth-focused approach
. Although these valuable prior corpora were built to answer a predefined research question,
targum
is designed as a
general-purpose resource for comparative analysis
. Its aim is to allow researchers to dynamically create a wide variety of bespoke subcorpora tailored to their own specific inquiries across a diverse set of languages and traditions.

3.  Methodology

3.1.  Data Acquisition and Processing

The construction of the corpus began with the aggregation of translations from a diverse set of sources, including 12 online biblical libraries and one preexisting digital corpus (eBible). Our selection strategy was twofold: we identified well-known, large-scale aggregators (e.g., Bible Gateway, Bible Hub) to ensure broad coverage, and supplemented these with language-specific repositories (e.g.,
bibliepolskie.pl
for Polish,
laparola.net
for Italian) to achieve deep coverage in our target languages. The acquisition pipeline consists of three stages.

First, an
indexer
inventories all available translations on each source website, capturing metadata such as source-specific identifiers and translation names. This creates a stable, timestamped snapshot of each site’s collection, providing a clear basis for the subsequent steps.

Second, a
scraper
uses this index to download the full text of each translation, caching the raw source files (e.g., HTML or JSON) for each of the New Testament’s 260 chapters without modification. This separation ensures that parsing logic can be iteratively refined offline, which prevents repeated requests to the source websites and guarantees reproducibility.

Third, a
parser
processes the cached files to extract verse-level text. A key challenge at this stage is the structural variation in verse notation. While many translations align to a simple one-to-one mapping, more dynamic versions often introduce non-standard segments (e.g., 6a, 6b) or consolidated ranges (e.g., 6–8). Our parser is designed to handle this complexity. The final output is stored in two formats: a simple TSV file for rapid processing, and a structured JSON file that preserves the detailed verse segmentation.

3.2.  Canonicalization and Metadata Annotation

The scraped data is inconsistent across sources; the same translation often appears with slightly different names and inconsistent metadata (e.g. publication year). To resolve this, we manually mapped each scraped instance to a canonical entry. This metadata is stored in YAML files, where each translation is assigned a standardized
canonical_id
and
canonical_revision_year
. The
canonical_id
identifies a distinct translation work, meaning any version presented under a new name (e.g., the American Standard Version relative to the King James Version) receives its own unique identifier. The
canonical_revision_year
specifies the exact edition. Determining these values required extensive external research. While we initially explored using AI assistants for automated metadata extraction, this approach proved unreliable under manual review, often failing to distinguish between closely-related revisions or misidentifying publication details. Consequently, the final canonicalization was performed entirely manually. This manual process is supported by the lexical and semantic similarity analysis described in the following section, which acts as a safeguard to verify that translations grouped under the same identifier are indeed textually consistent. The rationale for each classification is documented to ensure transparency and to allow for future reassessment, making it possible to track the original reasoning for a decision and re-evaluate it in light of new evidence.

3.3.  Quality Control via Cross-Source Validation

The aggregation process naturally yields multiple copies of the same canonical text, particularly for widely-distributed versions. Instead of viewing these as duplicates to be discarded, we treat their presence as a central feature of our quality control methodology.

Once a group of scraped translations are assigned the same
canonical_id
and
canonical_revision_year
, we conduct a systematic, side-by-side comparison of their texts using lexical similarity metrics. These are sensitive to the single-character variations that distinguish editions or reflect transcription errors. This process is highly effective at spotting discrepancies, which reliably expose one of two issues: a subtle error in our parsing logic, or a pre-existing data error on the source website, such as a typo or a missing verse. To resolve these differences and identify the correct edition, we employ several verification techniques, including comparing the text against scanned historical documents and using search engines to find quotations of a specific divergent phrase, which can often be traced back to a particular publication. This iterative process allows us to correct our parsers and progressively enhance the textual accuracy of the entire collection.

3.4.  Final Corpus Structure and Philosophy

The final output of our methodology is not a single, pre-filtered dataset where duplicates have been removed. Instead, we deliver the complete archive of all collected translations, accompanied by a comprehensive metadata index. This index file contains all the canonical information for every translation instance, allowing researchers to understand the provenance and specific edition of each text.

This structure embodies the core philosophy of the resource: to empower researchers, not to impose a pre-defined notion of what constitutes a "duplicate." The rich metadata allows users to define uniqueness on their own terms, tailored to their specific research question. For example, a scholar studying Polish translations might choose to prioritize copies from the language-specific
bibliepolskie.pl
source. A researcher analyzing the evolution of the KJV family would treat each revision as a distinct entity. Conversely, a user performing a broad, cross-lingual study might choose to deduplicate aggressively, keeping only one instance per
canonical_id
. By providing the complete data alongside the structured metadata to navigate it,
targum
is designed as a flexible platform.

4.  Corpus Analysis

In this section, we present a statistical analysis of the compiled corpus to demonstrate its scale and analytical potential. We begin by quantifying its comparative depth against existing resources, then examine its diachronic and linguistic properties, and conclude by analyzing its internal structure through similarity clustering. This analysis showcases the new avenues for research that the corpus enables.

4.2.  Diachronic Distribution

The depth of the corpus is not only numerical but also historical. Figure
1
illustrates the temporal distribution of the unique translations based on their revision year. Across all five languages, the distribution is skewed to the right, with a significant concentration of translations published in the last 50 years. The historical scope of the corpus begins in the 16th-century Reformation and Counter-Reformation period, with English showing the most continuous and extensive history from that point forward. The plot also reveals notable historical gaps. For Polish and Spanish, there is a long period of silence between a small cluster of early 17th-century translations and a renewed burst of activity in the second half of the 19th century. Similarly, while our Italian collection includes a few early 17th-century texts, this period remains scarce, and the earliest French translation in the corpus is from the latter half of the 17th century.

4.3.  Verse Length Distribution

To analyze the linguistic properties of the corpus, we examined the distribution of verse lengths aggregated at the chapter level. For each of the New Testament’s 260 chapters, we calculated the median and interquartile range (IQR) of verse lengths (in characters) across all unique translations. The results, visualized in Figure
2
, reveal structural patterns in the biblical narrative. The Gospels and Acts, which constitute the first five books and roughly 45% of the chapters, show similar characteristics, with Acts generally exhibiting longer verses. In contrast, the subsequent Epistles are composed of fewer chapters each, and their median verse lengths are more variable than those in the narrative books. The book of Revelation, the final book, contains the longest verses on average. Across the entire New Testament, the IQR remains remarkably consistent, typically spanning around 80 characters, which suggests a stable level of stylistic disagreement among translations.

4.4.  Translation Similarity and Clustering

Given that the corpus contains numerous translations for each language, many of which are near-duplicates, we sought to quantify their similarity. To do this effectively at scale, we limited our analysis to the Gospel of Matthew and devised two comparison methods: semantic and lexical.

For the semantic comparison, we generated a single vector embedding for each chapter using the Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B model
1
1

1

https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B
, selected for its state-of-the-art multilingual performance and long-context capabilities
14
. We then calculated the pairwise cosine similarity for each shared chapter between two translations and averaged the results to produce a final similarity score.

While effective for capturing broad stylistic differences, semantic similarity can fail to register minor textual variations. To capture these, we also computed a lexical similarity score. This was calculated in the same chapter-wise manner, but using the Levenshtein similarity, defined as
1-\frac{LD(x_{1},x_{2})}{\textrm{max}\left(\textrm{len}(x_{1}),\textrm{len}(x_{2})\right)}
, where
LD(x_{1},x_{2})
is the Levenshtein distance.

The pairwise similarity matrices in Figure
3
reveal several key features of the corpus’s internal structure. A primary observation, particularly from the semantic matrices, is the high degree of overall similarity, with most translation pairs scoring above 0.8 in cosine similarity. This reflects their shared source text and narrative content. Against this cohesive backdrop, two types of clusters are visible. The most prominent are the tight, high-similarity blocks along the diagonal, which represent near-identical editions or minor revisions of the same translation work. Additionally, we observe distinct clusters of translations that are internally cohesive but markedly dissimilar from the main group. These often correspond to more dynamic or paraphrastic translations that employ a different stylistic register. While these clusters appear in both matrices, their distinctiveness is far more pronounced in the lexical view. This divergence highlights how the lexical metric, with its sensitivity to specific word choices and sentence structures, is particularly effective at quantifying the unique stylistic strategies of these more dynamic versions, revealing differences that are partially smoothed over by the semantic embeddings.

5.  Discussion

5.1.  New Research Directions

The
targum
corpus enables computational analysis of research questions that were previously difficult or impossible to address on a scale. These new avenues can be broadly grouped into
micro-
and
macrolevel
analyses.

5.1.1.  Micro-Level Analysis

First,
targum
facilitates
micro-level analyses
by allowing researchers to select and compare focused subsets of the corpus based on specific metadata criteria. For example, scholars can:

•

Conduct diachronic studies
by tracing linguistic changes across decades of revisions within a single translation family (e.g., the King James Version).

•

Perform synchronic comparisons
of contemporary versions to isolate the effects of confessional tradition or individual translator style.

•

Track translation lineage
by following the propagation of theological and stylistic choices through indirect translation chains, such as those mediated by a Latin Vulgate source or, in modern institutional cases (e.g., Jehovah’s Witnesses, Biblica), by an English pivot translation.

Furthermore, the corpus can serve as a
quantitative complement to traditional qualitative evaluation
. While an expert might label a translation as “controversial” or “overly literal” based on key passages,
targum
allows researchers to measure the pervasiveness of these features across the entire New Testament. This provides a more objective and comprehensive context for such judgments, framing them within the full scope of the text.

5.1.2.  Macro-Level Analysis

Second, on a
macro-level
,
targum
can function as a comprehensive
benchmark and analytical testbed
for any new translation. By generating a vector embedding for a new version, whether produced by humans or machines, it can be situated within the semantic space of the entire corpus. This allows for its stylistic and theological profile to be quantitatively compared against hundreds of historical precedents. It makes it possible to answer questions such as: “Is this new translation stylistically closer to the KJV or the NIV family?” or “Does its interpretation of key theological terms align more with historical Catholic or Protestant traditions?”

5.2.  Future Work

The work presented in this paper establishes a foundation that can be expanded in several key directions. First, we plan to enrich the metadata for the existing translations. This will involve annotating details such as the source text (e.g., Textus Receptus, Nestle-Aland), the confessional tradition of the translators, and the known textual lineage of each version. Second, the most direct extension is to broaden the linguistic scope of targum to include other languages with rich translation traditions, such as German, Portuguese, or Welsh. A third avenue is to expand the corpus textually beyond the New Testament to also include the Old Testament. Together, these enhancements will enable an even more comprehensive analysis of translation practices and provide a richer dataset for the computational study of translation history.

6.  Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce
targum
, a multilingual New Testament corpus focused on the depth of translation within each language to address a key gap in existing biblical resources. By providing extensive coverage for five European languages and enriching 352 unique translations with manually-verified metadata,
targum
offers the necessary infrastructure for a new class of computational research. This resource makes it feasible to address long-standing questions about the interplay of theology, style, and culture in translation using scalable quantitative methods.

7.  Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations in the current version of the
targum
corpus.

First, the corpus is necessarily constrained by the availability of digitized translations on public websites. This introduces a potential “digitization bias,”, which means that the corpus reflects the history of translations that have been made digitally accessible, which may not perfectly align with the complete publication history. For instance, German was initially considered for inclusion due to its rich translation tradition but was ultimately excluded because of the relative scarcity of online versions compared to the five languages that were selected. Therefore, the distribution of translations in
targum
should be understood as representative of the digital landscape, not the complete landscape.

Second, the manual annotation process has inherent limitations. Although our methodology is robust, the final classification is still subject to human error. A key challenge arose when trying to pinpoint the exact revision year for certain translations. This difficulty was most pronounced not with historical texts, which often have well-documented print editions, but with modern, digital-native translations distributed primarily online. Such versions can be revised multiple times per year without formal versioning, and their release history is often undocumented. In these cases, we made a best-effort judgment based on the available textual and contextual evidence (e.g., copyright dates on the webpage) to determine the edition.

8.  Ethics Statement

Data for the
targum
corpus was aggregated from publicly accessible websites. The authors do not claim copyright on any of the translated texts. Our distribution plan is designed to respect the intellectual property rights of the copyright holders. The code for creating the corpus, along with the subset of translations that are in the public domain, will be made publicly available. For translations under active copyright, the full text will not be publicly distributed but will be made available to researchers upon reasonable request for noncommercial research purposes. Furthermore, we are committed to responsible data stewardship and will remove any translation from the corpus upon a direct request from a verified copyright holder.

In the preparation of this work, AI assistants were used as productivity tools. Specifically,
Cursor
with
Gemini 2.5 Pro
and standalone
Gemini 2.5 Pro
were used for tasks such as generating boilerplate code for web scraping and helping with the editing and formatting of this article.
