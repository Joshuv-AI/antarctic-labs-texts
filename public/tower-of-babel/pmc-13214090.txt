M. canadensis and its active component salvianolic acid B overcome paclitaxel resistance in gastric cancer via ROS–ER stress signaling
Authors: Bo Ram Kim, Heerim Yeo, Sang-Min Park, Seol Jang, Hye Mi Yun, Youn-Hwan Hwang, Sun Il Lee, Sang Cheul Oh, Han Na Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPaclitaxel (PTX) is widely used for gastric cancer treatment; however, PTX resistance (PTXR) significantly limits clinical efficacy.PurposeTo investigate the potential of Mentha canadensis and its bioactive component, salvianolic acid B, to overcome PTX resistance in gastric cancer.MethodsThe cytotoxic effects of M. canadensis and salvianolic acid B, either alone or in combination with PTX, were assessed by cell viability assays. Total RNA sequencing was conducted on M. canadensis–treated PTXR cells to identify differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways. Expression levels of NOXA, PERK, ATF4, and other apoptotic markers were assessed using western blotting. ROS accumulation further contributed to M. canadensis–induced apoptosis. Salvianolic acid B acted synergistically with PTX by decreasing cell viability and promoting caspase-dependent apoptosis.ConclusionsM. canadensis and its active component salvianolic acid B sensitize PTXR gastric cancer cells to apoptosis by inducing ROS accumulation and ER stress–mediated NOXA upregulation. Introduction

Gastric cancer has a high incidence and mortality worldwide, and patients with advanced or drug-resistant disease have limited treatment options [1]. The various treatment approaches include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, among which chemotherapy has become the main treatment for advanced gastric cancer. Paclitaxel (PTX) is an effective treatment option for several solid tumors, including gastric cancer. It inhibits tumor growth through a mechanism that arrests cell division. However, PTX resistance remains a major clinical challenge, leading to poor treatment outcomes and limited long-term efficacy [2]. Therefore, novel therapeutic strategies are required to enhance PTX efficacy and restore chemotherapeutic sensitivity in resistant cancer cells to improve patient outcomes.

Disrupted apoptotic pathways, oxidative stress reactions, and changes in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress signaling are frequently associated with chemotherapy resistance [3, 4]. Furthermore, cancer cells frequently avoid apoptosis by downregulating pro-apoptotic proteins, such as NOXA, and adjusting to ER stressors to enhance survival [5, 6]. NOXA, a pro-apoptotic member of the Bcl-2 family, is critical for apoptosis induction, and its downregulation has been linked to drug resistance. Targeting NOXA expression may be a promising approach to overcome PTX resistance.

Natural products have attracted attention as potential therapeutic agents in cancer research because of their ability to modulate multiple signaling pathways and low toxicity and side effects [7, 8]. Natural products have the potential to be developed as single anticancer agents or to exhibit synergistic effects in combination with existing compounding regimens. Medicinal plants with well-established anti-inflammatory and anticancer properties are good options for overcoming resistance to chemotherapy.

Mentha canadensis L. (M. canadensis), a medicinal plant with anti-inflammatory and anticancer effects, influences stress-related pathways and apoptosis [9]. M. canadensis extracts can modulate oxidative stress responses and cellular apoptotic pathways, indicating their potential utility in cancer treatment [10, 11]. These reports indicate that M. canadensis may help overcome PTX resistance by regulating apoptosis-related processes. However, this aspect has not yet been studied.

In this study, we examined whether M. canadensis alters apoptosis-related mechanisms to restore PTX sensitivity in PTX-resistant (PTXR) gastric cancer cells. Using transcriptome analysis, we identified key pathways affected by M. canadensis extract and potential molecular targets. We investigated the regulatory function of NOXA and the role of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and ER stress in M. canadensis–induced apoptosis in vitro.

Our study suggests that M. canadensis and its active component, salvianolic acid B, may be effective treatments for PTX-resistant gastric cancer. By elucidating the molecular mechanisms involved in overcoming resistance, this study may provide potential therapeutic strategies to enhance the chemotherapeutic efficacy of PTX in the treatment of gastric cancer and address drug resistance.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human gastric cancer cell line AGS was purchased from the Korea Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Korea). AGS PTX-resistant AGS was constructed by growing AGS cells with 200 nM paclitaxel for more than 6 months. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (GenDEPOT, LLC) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), 1 mM L-glutamine, 26 mM sodium bicarbonate, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). All cells were cultured at 37 ˚C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Mycoplasma infection was ruled out using a mycoplasma detection kit (BIOMAX, SMD0173). Reagents and antibodies used in the study and their sources are listed in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

UHPLC-Q-orbitrap-MS analytical conditions

An ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography/quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap-MS) system was used to identify the components of M. canadensis. Details of the equipment and method are given in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Cell survival assay and apoptosis

Cell viability was determined using a Cell Viability Assay Kit (EZ-Cytox; DOGEN, Daejeon, Korea). AGS and AGS PTXR cells were seeded at a density of 6 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates and treated with PTX (100 and 200 nM) and M. canadensis (200 and 300 µg/mL) alone or in combination for 24 h. In certain experiments, AGS PTXR cells were treated with PTX (100 and 200 nM) and salvianolic acid B (100 and 200 µM) alone or in combination. The cells were then treated with EZ-Cytox reagent and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SPECTRA190 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

To detect apoptosis, cells were stained using a FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, to observe phosphatidylserine translocation. The analysis was performed using a Beckman Coulter Navios EX BC34612 flow cytometer.

Immunoblotting assay

As previously described [12], cells were prepared, and western blotting was performed. Signals were detected on X-ray films. The GangNam-Stain protein ladder (Intron Bio, Seoul, Korea) was used to measure the protein size.

Immunofluorescence staining

Detailed procedure for immunofluorescence staining is given in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Transfection

AGS PTXR cells were transfected with NOXA and ATF4 small interfering RNA (siRNA) purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). The siRNA was mixed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The resulting siRNA mixture was added to the cells, which were incubated for 6 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Subsequently, the cells were seeded and treated with PTX and M. canadensis the following day.

Mitochondrial cytosolic fractionation

Mitochondria and cytosol fractionation were performed using Mitochondria Isolation Kit for Cultured Cells (Thermo Scientific™, 89874) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, AGS PTXR cells were harvested, and the cytosolic fraction was isolated using the cytosolic extraction buffer included in the kit, followed by sequential extraction of the mitochondrial fraction using the mitochondrial extraction buffer. Fraction purity was validated using VDAC as a mitochondrial marker and β-actin as a cytosolic marker.

After centrifugation steps as specified in the protocol, the cytosolic and mitochondrial fractions were collected separately and quantified using the BCA protein quantification method. Equal amounts of protein from each fraction were collected and analyzed for cytochrome c distribution by immunoblotting.

Measurement of intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H₂DCFDA; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The procedure for immunofluorescence staining is detailed in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Measurement of mitochondrial superoxide

MitoSOX Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to measure the mitochondrial superoxide production. The detailed procedure for immunofluorescence staining is given in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

ChIP assay

The ChIP assay was performed as previously described [13]. Overall, 1.5 × 106 AGS PTXR cells were seeded. DNA was analyzed by PCR using primers listed in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Details regarding qRT-PCR are given in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

RNA library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA concentration was calculated using Quant-IT RiboGreen (Invitrogen, #R11490). To assess the integrity of the total RNA, samples were run on a TapeStation RNA screentape (Agilent, #5067–5576). Only high-quality RNA preparations (RIN greater than 7.0) were used for the RNA library construction. A library was independently prepared with 0.5 µg of total RNA for each sample by Illumina TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA, #20020599). Steps involved in cDNA library creation have been detailed in Supplementary Materials and Methods. Libraries were quantified using KAPA Library Quantification kits for Illumina Sequencing platforms according to the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (KAPA BIOSYSTEMS, #KK4854) and qualified using a TapeStation D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies, # 5067–5582). Indexed libraries were then submitted to Illumina NovaSeqX (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and paired-end (2 × 100 bp) sequencing was performed by Macrogen Inc.

Data processing and analysis

Paired-end sequencing reads were generated using an Illumina NovaSeqX sequencing platform. Before starting the analysis, Trimmomatic v0.38 was used to remove adapter sequences and trim bases of poor base quality. The cleaned reads were aligned with Homo sapiens (GRCh38) using HISAT v2.1.0 [14]. Reference genome sequences and gene annotation data were downloaded from the NCBI Genome Assembly and NCBI RefSeq databases, respectively. The aligned data (SAM file format) were sorted and indexed using SAM tools v 1.9. After alignment, the transcripts were assembled and quantified using StringTie v2.1.3b [15, 16]. Gene- and transcript-level quantification were calculated as the raw read count, Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) and Transcripts Per Million (TPM).

Transcriptome analysis

RNA-Seq data were analyzed using R software (v.4.3.2). Principal component analysis (PCA) and differential expression analyses were performed using the DESeq2 package (v1.42.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were visualized using the Enhanced Volcano package (v1.20.0). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted on ranked lists based on differential expression using the fgsea package (v1.28.0) with the following parameters: minSize = 15, maxSize = 500, and nperm = 10,000. Gene sets were sourced from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [17] using the msigdbr package (v.7.5.1). Changes in gene expression were compared using log2-transformed TPM + 1 values and visualized as box plots using the ggplot2 package (v3.5.1).

Network pharmacology

The Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) of bioactive compounds in M. canadensis were retrieved from the PubChem [18]. The known targets of M. canadensis compounds were retrieved from the HIT 2.0 [19] and SymMap [20] databases, whereas the predicted targets were identified using the SwissTargetPrediction [21] and BATMAN-TCM 2.0 [22] databases. As a criterion for selecting relevant targets associated with the compound of interest, a probability score > 0 was applied for SwissTargetPrediction, whereas a confidence score cutoff > 0.68 (LR = 23) was used for the BATMAN-TCM. The keywords “Apoptosis,” “Reactive oxygen species” and “Endoplasmic reticulum stress” were used to gather the relevant targets from the GeneCards [23] database. A Venn diagram was generated using the ggvenn package (v0.1.16) to visualize the overlap between compound targets and mechanism-related targets. Network construction was performed using the Cytoscape software (v3.10.1) [24]and pathway enrichment analysis was performed using Enrichr [25] to identify significantly enriched pathways associated with the target genes of salvianolic acid B.

Combination index (CI) and statistical analysis

The Chou–Talalay CI method was used to assess the cytotoxic effects of PTX and M. canadensis on AGS PTXR cells to determine whether the interactions were synergistic, additive, or antagonistic.

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad InStat 8 (GraphPad Software Inc.; Dotmatics). Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Unpaired t-tests were used to compare two groups. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Synergistic induction of apoptosis by M. canadensis and paclitaxel in PTX-resistant gastric cancer cells

AGS PTXR cells exhibited greater resistance to paclitaxel than AGS cells (Fig. 2A). To evaluate whether M. canadensis enhances PTX-induced cytotoxicity in AGS PTXR cells, the combined effect was evaluated using CI. A strong combination effect was observed at PTX 200 nM and M. canadensis 300 µg/mL (Fig. 2B). Further, PTX or M. canadensis alone were found to induce minimal morphological changes; however, the combination treatment resulted in a significant decrease in cell density and an increase in the number of floating cells (Fig. 2C). Flow cytometry results also showed that combination treatment significantly increased the percentage of dead cells compared to the individual treatments (Fig. 2D). To confirm whether this combined effect was mediated by apoptosis, we investigated the expression of apoptosis-related proteins. Combination treatment of PTX and M. canadensis caused significantly increased levels of cleaved PARP, caspase-8, caspase-9, and caspase-3. Furthermore, pretreatment with the pan-caspase inhibitor, z-VAD-fmk, inhibited caspase and PARP cleavage, confirming that the combined effect of PTX and M. canadensis was apoptosis-dependent (Fig. 2F). These results suggest that M. canadensis can overcome PTX resistance.

M. canadensis induced apoptosis via ER stress and oxidative stress to overcome PTX resistance

We performed transcriptomic analysis by comparing gene expression profiles between PTXR cells and M. canadensis–treated PTXR cells. Clustering analysis revealed a distinct segregation between the two groups (Fig. 3A), with a significant number of differentially expressed genes identified in the M. canadensis–treated group (Fig. 3B).

Consistent with the in vitro results, GSEA demonstrated significant activation of apoptosis, ROS signaling, and ER stress pathways (Fig. 3 C). Specifically, the expression of pro-apoptotic regulators, including NOXA, PUMA, BAD, and ARAF1, was upregulated, indicating the activation of the mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathway (Fig. 3D). Meanwhile, the ROS-scavenging and cytoprotective enzymes PRDX4 and GPX1 were downregulated. Additionally, the increased expression of oxidative stress regulators, including FOXO3 and CYP1A1, suggests enhanced ROS signaling, which contributes to oxidative stress accumulation. Furthermore, ER stress–induced apoptosis regulators, including ATF4 and DDIT3, were upregulated, whereas ER stress sensors, ERN1 and ATF6, were downregulated. These findings indicate that M. canadensis induces ER and oxidative stress while compromising cytoprotective mechanisms and ultimately triggering apoptotic cell death, thereby overcoming PTX resistance and facilitating its anti-cancer effects.

M. canadensis induces NOXA expression via ATF4-mediated ER stress signaling

Based on the results of total RNA sequencing, we examined the expression of apoptosis-related proteins in AGS PTXR cells to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying M. canadensis–induced apoptosis. M. canadensis treatment (300 µg/mL) significantly increased NOXA expression, accompanied by a reduction in MCL-1 levels (Fig. 4A). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that M. canadensis treatment significantly increased NOXA expression in AGS PTXR cells (Fig. 4B). Further, we demonstrated that M. canadensis–induced cell death was inhibited by siRNA-mediated NOXA knockdown, indicating that NOXA is important for M. canadensis–induced cell death (Fig. 4C). Because NOXA is known to be regulated by ER stress [28], we investigated the involvement of the ER stress pathway in M. canadensis treatment. The ER stress pathway was activated by M. canadensis treatment by increased phosphorylation of PERK, eIF2α, and ATF4 (Fig. 4D). Additionally, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that ATF4 accumulated in the nucleus upon M. canadensis treatment (Fig. 4E). Further, western blot analysis showed that siRNA-mediated ATF4 knockdown dramatically decreased NOXA expression and c-PARP cleavage, suggesting that ATF4 is necessary for NOXA-mediated apoptosis induced by M. canadensis (Fig. 4F). Finally, as no increase in CHOP levels was noted on M. canadensis treatment, we hypothesized that ATF4 directly regulates NOXA. ChIP analysis revealed that ATF4 directly binds to BS2 in the NOXA promoter upon M. canadensis treatment (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these results suggest that M. canadensis induces NOXA expression via ATF4-mediated ER stress signaling to overcome resistance in AGS PTXR cells.

M. canadensis induces mitochondrial ROS accumulation and oxidative stress–mediated apoptosis

ER stress is regulated by ROS [29]. Flow cytometry revealed that M. canadensis treatment (300 µg/mL) of AGS PTXR cells significantly increased total cellular (Fig. 5A) and mitochondrial ROS levels (Fig. 5B) compared with the control. Further, TMRE fluorescence intensity was significantly reduced upon M. canadensis treatment, indicating mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 5C). To determine whether ROS generation contributes to M. canadensis-induced cell death, we pretreated cells with the mitochondrial ROS scavenger MitoTEMPO. Pretreatment with MitoTEMPO, a mitochondrial ROS scavenger, decreased the expression of NOXA, ATF4, and c-PARP, suggesting that mitochondrial ROS play a key role in inducing cell death (Fig. 5D). Further, M. canadensis treatment suppressed the expression of the antioxidant enzyme catalase (Fig. 5E). However, RT-PCR analysis showed no significant changes in catalase mRNA levels, suggesting that M. canadensis decreased catalase protein levels (Fig. 5 F). To investigate the mechanism of catalase reduction in more detail, we evaluated whether proteasome degradation contributes to this effect. However, catalase protein levels were not restored even after pretreatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Supplementary Fig. 1B), indicating that catalase reduction is not mediated by proteasomal degradation. Overall, these findings suggest that M. canadensis induces mitochondrial ROS, resulting in oxidative stress, NOXA upregulation, and cell death in AGS PTXR cells.

Salvianolic acid B is the potential bioactive compound in M. canadensis that is critical to overcoming PTX resistance

We performed a network pharmacology-based target prediction analysis for the 22 compounds identified in M. canadensis (Fig. 1).

Multiple targets of all 22 compounds were retrieved from the databases, resulting in 885 unique targets. Among these, 183 overlapped with apoptosis-related genes, 49 with ROS signaling, and 35 with ER stress–related genes (Fig. 6A-C), suggesting that these compounds exert multi-target modulation across apoptosis, ROS signaling, and ER stress. To further illustrate these interactions, shared targets were visualized as a compound–target network (Fig. 6A-C).

To evaluate the target specificity of each compound in modulating these mechanisms, we calculated the proportion of mechanism-related targets among the total number of targets for each compound (Fig. 6D). Among the 22 compounds identified, salvianolic acid B showed the highest proportion of mechanism-related targets, suggesting that it is the most selective regulator of these mechanisms among the bioactive compounds in M. canadensis. The interaction network of salvianolic acid B revealed that it targets multiple genes associated with apoptosis, including CASP9 and CASP3; reactive oxygen species signaling, such as CAT and HIF1A; and ER stress, including SIRT1 and HMOX1 (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, enrichment analysis of the target genes of salvianolic acid B confirmed its significant regulatory role in these mechanisms. (Fig. 6 F). Collectively, these findings suggest that salvianolic acid B is an important bioactive compound in M. canadensis, contributing to its potential to overcome PTX resistance.

Salvianolic acid B enhances PTX-induced apoptosis via NOXA and ATF4 upregulation

Further, we investigated whether salvianolic acid B could overcome PTX resistance. Co-treatment with salvianolic acid B and PTX (100 nM) significantly decreased survival of AGS PTXR cells. CI analysis indicated a synergistic interaction between salvianolic acid B and PTX (CI < 1), suggesting that salvianolic acid B enhanced the cytotoxicity of PTX against PTXR cells (Fig. 7A). Western blotting indicated that co-treatment with salvianolic acid B and PTX significantly increased c-PARP levels and the cleavage of caspase-9, caspase-8, and caspase-3, indicating activation of the apoptotic pathway (Fig. 7B). Western blot analysis showed that salvianolic acid B upregulated NOXA, ATF4, p-eIF2α, and p-PERK expression (Fig. 7C). In addition, flow cytometry using MitoSOX showed that ROS levels within the mitochondria were significantly increased by salvianolic acid B treatment, suggesting that oxidative stress may contribute to the induction of apoptosis (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

PTX resistance remains a major challenge in gastric cancer treatment and novel therapeutic strategies are required. Here, we showed that M. canadensis and its physiologically active constituent, salvianolic acid B, efficiently overcame PTX resistance by inducing PTXR gastric cancer cells to undergo apoptosis via the ROS–ER stress–NOXA pathway.

Transcriptome analysis showed that M. canadensis treatment markedly upregulated pathways associated with ER stress, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Consistent with these findings, in vitro validation confirmed that M. canadensis induces apoptosis by upregulating NOXA, a member of the pro-apoptotic BCL-2 family. In addition, subcellular fractionation analysis demonstrated cytosolic translocation of cytochrome c following M. canadensis treatment, providing functional evidence consistent with mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) (Supplementary Fig. 1A). This finding further supports the engagement of intrinsic apoptotic signaling downstream of stress-induced BH3-only protein upregulation. The downregulation of Mcl-1 was mediated by proteasomal degradation, evidenced by the recovery of protein levels with MG132 (Supplementary Fig. 1C). This rapid turnover is likely facilitated by the robust induction of NOXA, a known mediator of Mcl-1 degradation. In contrast, although the total protein level of Bcl-2 showed only a marginal decrease following treatment, siRNA-mediated ATF4 knockdown resulted in a dramatic three-fold increase in Bcl-2 mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 1D). This suggests that while M. canadensis does not further reduce Bcl-2 from its already low basal state, ATF4 serves as a critical constitutive transcriptional repressor that prevents Bcl-2 recovery in AGS PTXR cells. Furthermore, elevated expression of ER stress markers, such as PERK and ATF4, indicated that ER stress signaling contributes to apoptotic execution. Time-course analysis showed that M. canadensis transiently increased CHOP expression at early time points (Supplementary Fig. 2A). However, CHOP protein induction was modest and not sustained. Consistent with this observation, time-resolved qPCR analysis revealed that CHOP mRNA exhibited an early transient peak that declined at later time points (Supplementary Fig. 2B), explaining the limited protein accumulation. These findings suggest that CHOP functions as an early stress-responsive marker rather than a sustained mediator of apoptosis in our system. In contrast, ATF4 activation was sustained, supporting a model in which NOXA expression is directly regulated by ATF4 rather than CHOP [30]. Among the three canonical branches of the unfolded protein response, activation was strongly biased toward the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 axis. Although phosphorylation of IRE1α was detectable, it was modest and declined over time, and no sustained activation of ATF6 was observed. Such PERK-dominant signaling is consistent with cellular contexts in which mitochondrial ROS and redox imbalance constitute the primary stress input, as PERK is particularly sensitive to oxidative perturbation. This selective engagement of PERK provides a mechanistic framework linking mitochondrial ROS to ATF4-dependent NOXA induction and intrinsic apoptosis.

In addition, the markedly low basal NOXA expression observed in PTX-resistant cells may reflect reduced apoptotic priming, potentially involving epigenetic repression of the PMAIP1 locus. Although chromatin-level regulation was not directly examined in this study, the robust inducible activation of NOXA following ATF4 engagement indicates that the gene remains transcriptionally competent under stress conditions.

ROS promotes cell death by inducing oxidative and ER stress responses [29]. Our findings demonstrate that M. canadensis induces apoptosis by drastically increasing the intracellular ROS levels, indicating that mitochondrial ROS acts as a central upstream driver of ER stress and apoptotic commitment in PTXR cells.

Mitochondrial ROS generation emerged as an early and critical event in our model. Importantly, ATF4 knockdown did not attenuate M. canadensis–induced mitochondrial ROS accumulation, as assessed by MitoSOX fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 3A). These results indicate that mitochondrial ROS accumulation occurs upstream of ATF4 activation rather than being sustained by ATF4 signaling.

To further define the origin of ROS, we examined the effect of site-specific electron transport chain (ETC) inhibitors. Pretreatment with rotenone or antimycin A did not suppress, but rather markedly potentiated mitochondrial ROS accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 3B). This synergistic enhancement suggests that M. canadensis perturbs mitochondrial respiratory function broadly, leading to electron leakage and superoxide amplification rather than isolated inhibition of a single ETC complex. Collectively, these findings support a model in which mitochondrial ROS serves as the initiating stress signal, activating the PERK–ATF4 axis and subsequently promoting NOXA-mediated mitochondrial apoptotic commitment.

Salvianolic acid B, a component of M. canadensis, acts synergistically with PTX, significantly decreasing cell viability and promoting caspase-dependent apoptosis. Previous studies have shown that natural compounds can improve anticancer treatment efficacy by regulating apoptosis-related pathways. Our study revealed the anticancer mechanism of salvianolic acid B, demonstrating its potential as a therapeutic agent to overcome PTX resistance in gastric cancer. Interestingly, salvianolic acid B treatment resulted in a sustained increase in ATF4 and NOXA expression, implying that its anticancer effects may be mediated by persistent ER stress activation.

Network pharmacology analysis further identified HIF1A as a potential hub associated with salvianolic acid B. Although oxidative stress can stabilize HIF-1α through hydroxylation-dependent mechanisms, our current data primarily support a ROS-driven PERK–ATF4–NOXA axis. Further studies will be required to determine whether HIF-1α stabilization contributes functionally to the salvianolic acid B response in PTX-resistant gastric cancer cells.

Although we aimed to further validate our findings in an in vivo model, we were unable to proceed because of the failure of AGS PTXR cells to form tumors despite multiple attempts. Parental AGS cells have been reported to form subcutaneous xenograft tumors in immunodeficient mice [31, 32]. However, in our hands, AGS PTXR cells failed to establish tumors despite multiple inoculation attempts, suggesting reduced in vivo tumorigenicity associated with the acquired resistant phenotype [33]. This limitation highlights the need to explore alternative in vivo models, such as patient-derived xenografts, to further evaluate the therapeutic potential of M. canadensis and salvianolic acid B. Given the complexities of drug resistance in the tumor microenvironment, future research should examine the effects of salvianolic acid B on immune cell populations and tumor–stroma interactions. Salvianolic acid B’s bioavailability should be investigated in future studies to increase its clinical applicability. Liposomal formulations and nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems may improve the stability and targeted delivery of salvianolic acid B, thereby increasing its therapeutic efficacy.

Collectively, our findings delineate a hierarchical model in which mitochondrial ROS initiates PERK-biased ER stress signaling, activates ATF4, and induces NOXA-mediated intrinsic apoptosis. By restoring apoptotic susceptibility through oxidative and ER stress modulation, M. canadensis and salvianolic acid B represent promising candidates for overcoming PTX resistance in gastric cancer.
