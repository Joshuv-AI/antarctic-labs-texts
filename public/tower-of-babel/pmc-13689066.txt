Predicting acute kidney injury after radical cystectomy for muscle-invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Kays Chaker, Ahmed Chaabouni, Wafa Elleuch, Aida Khadhar, Khouloud Ouertani, Ali Chakroun, Afif Limem, Yasmine Laabidi, Wassim Ben Chedly, Yassine Ouanes, Mokhtar Bibi, Mahdi Marrak, Ines Chelly, Mourad Hadj Slimene, Yassine Nouira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRadical cystectomy (RC) with urinary diversion remains the gold standard for the treatment of muscle invasive tumors. RC is a major surgery associated with a high morbidity rate. Postoperative acute kidney injury (AKI) is associated with severe morbidity and mortality. In this study we attempted to identify risk factors for postoperative AKI in patients undergoing RC.Patients and methodsA multicenter retrospective analysis was conducted among patients treated with radical cystectomy for muscle-invasive bladder cancer between 2015 and 2024. AKI was defined by Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO), within 90 days of surgery, with the occurrence of 1 of the 3 items: (1) increase in serum creatinine by ≥ 26.5 µmol/L within 48 h; (2) increase in serum creatinine ≥ 1.5-time baseline, which is known or presumed to have occurred within the prior seven days; (3) urine volume < 0.5 ml/kg/h during 6 h, KDIGO score were also recorded. Data were compared between two groups of patients: occurrence of AKI within the first 30 days of the post-operative period (AKI group) or no AKI during this period (no AKI group).ResultsWe included 520 patients. The incidence of postoperative AKI was 40.38% (n = 210). Patients with AKI were active smoker (p = 0.03), significantly less often anemic (p = 0.01), had a higher BMI (p = 0.002), preoperative anemia (p = 0.01). Duration of surgery was longer in AKI patients (p < 0.001). AKI patients were more likely to need vasoactive agents (p = 0.04). Independent factors associated with AKI were intraoperative blood loss (OR = 4.8 [95%CI: 3.06–6.43]; p = 0.01), duration of surgery (OR = 3.36 [95%CI: 2.9–11.19]; p = 0.0014); Preoperative anemia (OR = 2.69 [95%CI: 1.06–5.2]; p < 0.001); use of vasoactive agents (OR = 6.36, [95%CI: 6.01–36.87]; p < 0.001). Postoperative AKI was not associated with 2-year mortality (p = 0.71).ConclusionA significant number of patients experienced AKI after RC. Recognizing pre- and perioperative risk factors can identify patients where close surveillance and early intervention may help minimize renal function decline following RC.

Introduction

Bladder tumors infiltrating the TVIM muscle are characterized by a more aggressive nature, with a 5-year survival rate of around 60%. In metastatic forms, however, this rate falls to less than 10% [1]. The standard treatment for TVIM is radical cystectomy. However, this procedure is associated with significant morbidity in the postoperative period, with complication rates of between 40% and 60%, and a mortality rate of up to 9% in the first three months after surgery [2]. RC is a major surgery associated with a high risk of both short- and long-term postoperative morbidity and mortality. Renal impairment in cancer patients, particularly following surgery, is associated with longer hospital stays, increased morbidity, and higher rates of both overall and cancer-specific mortality [3]. Multiple preoperative variables contribute to the occurrence of long-term renal insufficiency following radical cystectomy, such as advanced age, baseline renal dysfunction, hypertension, and prior chemotherapy. In the postoperative setting, ureteral strictures and recurrent urinary tract infections have been identified as additional risk factors [3, 4]. In a recent study from our institution, we reported that the absolute risk of developing long-term renal insufficiency after radical cystectomy reached 17% at 5 years of follow-up. We recently reported that patients undergoing RC at our institution had a 17% absolute risk of developing long-term renal insufficiency within 5 years after surgery [4, 5].

The current study aimed to describe the incidence of AKI in a cystectomy population and identify patients at increased risk of developing AKI after RC.

Patients and methods

Patient selection

A multicenter retrospective analysis was conducted among patients treated with radical cystectomy for muscle-invasive bladder cancer between 2015 and 2024. Patients aged between 18 and 85 years who underwent radical cystectomy, had complete medical records, and had a minimum follow-up of 24 months were eligible for inclusion. Patients with one or more of the following criteria were not included in the study: Received salvage cystectomy, presence of concomitant neoplasia and metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis on imaging. The study population included patients with a preoperative eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m² who had at least one serum creatinine assessment performed more than 90 days after surgery. Patient demographic and disease characteristics were recorded. All patients underwent radical cystectomy and urinary diversion. All surgical specimens were examined by specialized genitourinary pathologists, and both tumour T stage and nodal N stage were determined. Following radical cystectomy, patients underwent regular follow-up consisting of clinical examinations and radiological imaging. AKI was defined by Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [6], within 90 days of surgery, with the occurrence of 1 of the 3 items: (1) increase in serum creatinine by ≥ 26.5 µmol/L within 48 h; (2) increase in serum creatinine ≥ 1.5-time baseline, which is known or presumed to have occurred within the prior seven days; (3) urine volume < 0.5 ml/kg/h during 6 h, KDIGO score were also recorded. Anemia was defined according World Health Organization (WHO) classification (male < 13 g/dl, female < 12 g/dl).

Study outcomes

Data were compared between two group of patients: occurrence of AKI within the first 30 days of the post-operative period (AKI group) or no AKI during this period (no AKI group). Following radical cystectomy, patients underwent regular follow-up consisting of clinical examinations and radiological imaging. Long-term renal insufficiency was defined as estimated glomerular function < 45 mL/min. The occurrence of AKI within 90 days after RC was assessed, along with the identification of preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative factors associated with its development.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Descriptive data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) for typically distributed continuous variables, and as median and interquartile range (IQR) for disposed variables. Data were presented as frequency and percentages (%) for categorical data. For normally distributed variables, the t test was used to compare two means, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare several means. For categorical variables, chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used in independent samples. Variables showing statistical significance or a trend toward significance (p < 0.1) in univariate analyses, along with clinically relevant factors identified in the literature, were subsequently entered into a multivariable regression model using backward stepwise selection. We performed a logistic regression analysis to identify independent variables associated with the development of postoperative AKI, as measured by the estimated odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval [95%CI]. The association between postoperative AKI and 2-year overall survival was assessed using the log-rank test. All p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

We included 520 patients. The mean age was 67 ± 12 years. Of these, 470 (90.38%) were male and 60 (11.53%) were female. All patients underwent an open radical cystectomy. Among the cases studied, 51.92% were active smokers. The mean tumor size was 62 ± 12 mm. Transurethral resection of bladder tumor was completed in 398 (76.53%) patients, while it was not completed in 122 cases (23.46%). Variant histology was observed in 35 (6.6%) patients. A total of 178 (34%) patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Anemia was present in 250 patients (48.07%) before surgery. Anemia was attributed to hematuria in 188 patients (75.2%) and to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in 62 patients (24.8%). Preoperative packed red blood cell transfusions were administered in 220 patients (42.3%). Iron therapy was prescribed to correct anemia in 180 patients. Ileal conduit was the most common form of urinary diversion, performed in 450 patients (86.5%), while cutaneous ureterostomy was chosen in 62 cases (11.92%), while an enterocystoplasty was performed in 8 patients (1.53%). The incidence of postoperative AKI was 40.38% (n = 210). Patients with AKI were active smoker (p = 0.03), significantly less often anemic (p = 0.01), had a higher BMI (p = 0.002), preoperative anemia (p = 0.01). Duration of surgery was longer in AKI patients (p < 0.001). AKI patients were more likely to need vasoactive agents (p = 0.04). Independent factors associated with AKI were intraoperative blood loss (OR = 4.8 [95%CI: 3.06–6.43]; p = 0.01), duration of surgery (OR = 3.36 [95%CI: 2.9–11.19]; p = 0.0014); Preoperative anemia (OR = 2.69 [95%CI: 1.06–5.2]; p < 0.001); use of vasoactive agents (OR = 6.36, [95%CI: 6.01–36.87]; p < 0.001) (Table 3). The 2-year overall survival rate was 86.0% in patients without postoperative AKI following cystectomy, compared with 79.9% in those who developed AKI. This difference was not statistically significant (log-rank test, p = 0.71) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Acute kidney injury (AKI) represents a frequent postoperative complication following radical cystectomy, with incidence rates varying considerably among studies according to the diagnostic criteria applied and the period of evaluation. Using the Acute Kidney Injury Network (AKIN) classification, Joung identified AKI in 38.2% of 238 patients treated with radical cystectomy [7]. In addition, Furrer reported that early postoperative AKI occurred in approximately 11% of patients after radical cystectomy and urinary diversion [8]. AKI after radical cystectomy remains a significant postoperative complication associated with increased morbidity and prolonged hospitalization. AKI is generally associated with a substantial risk of short- and long-term cardiovascular events, as well as chronic kidney disease [8]. Therefore, optimizing renal recovery following AKI is of paramount importance, as impaired renal function may adversely affect the expected postoperative recovery after radical cystectomy and urinary diversion. Several studies have investigated predictive factors for AKI following radical cystectomy. Reported risk factors include advanced age, prolonged operative time, increased intraoperative blood loss, preexisting renal dysfunction, and elevated preoperative serum uric acid levels [9]. In a cohort of 238 patients undergoing radical cystectomy, Joung et al. identified elevated preoperative serum uric acid concentration and prolonged operative time as independent predictors of postoperative AKI [7]. Osman [10] in a large series of 988 patients, reported that older age, higher body mass index, preoperative hydronephrosis, and perioperative blood transfusion were significantly associated with the occurrence of AKI after radical cystectomy and urinary diversion. More recently, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Liu [9] demonstrated that advanced age, robotic surgical approach, and perioperative nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use were significant predictors of AKI after radical cystectomy. (AKI) following radical cystectomy is associated with substantial postoperative morbidity and adverse long-term outcomes. Reported consequences include increased rates of major postoperative complications, prolonged hospital stays, higher intensive care unit admission rates, progression to chronic kidney disease, end-stage renal disease, and increased mortality. Early identification of patients at risk of AKI is therefore essential to optimize perioperative management and improve long-term renal outcomes [8, 10, 11]. Several studies reported that postoperative AKI or acute kidney disease (AKD) after radical cystectomy significantly increases the risk of long-term renal impairment and CKD development [10]. Acute kidney injury following radical cystectomy has been linked to higher perioperative mortality and poorer long-term overall survival. Postoperative renal impairment may promote the progression of chronic kidney disease, increase cardiovascular morbidity, and reduce tolerance to adjuvant treatments, thereby negatively affecting survival outcomes [10, 12]. A recent prospective cohort study demonstrated that impaired postoperative renal function after radical cystectomy was independently associated with significantly worse overall survival on Kaplan–Meier and multivariate analyses [12]. Furthermore, Osman [10] identified AKI as a significant postoperative complication following radical cystectomy, associated with a higher incidence of severe postoperative adverse events, which are recognized predictors of poorer overall survival. Early postoperative AKI has also been associated with increased short-term mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and persistent renal function deterioration across several surgical populations, including patients undergoing radical cystectomy [13]. Several studies have identified multiple predictors of acute kidney injury (AKI) after radical cystectomy, including advanced age, pre-existing chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, anemia, and impaired baseline renal function. Perioperative factors such as prolonged operative time, significant blood loss, blood transfusion, postoperative sepsis, urinary leakage, and the need for intensive care admission have also been associated with an increased risk of AKI following radical cystectomy [14, 15]. Furthermore, postoperative urinary tract obstruction, hydronephrosis, and complications related to urinary diversion have been reported as important contributors to renal dysfunction, highlighting the multifactorial nature of AKI in this setting [16, 17]. Prevention of acute kidney injury after radical cystectomy relies on careful perioperative optimization and early identification of high-risk patients. Several studies emphasized the importance of maintaining adequate intravascular volume, minimizing intraoperative blood loss and operative time, and avoiding prolonged hypotension to preserve postoperative renal function [18]. Measures to prevent postoperative AKI after radical cystectomy should be discussed in accordance with current evidence and clinical practice [18, 19]. Preventive strategies include optimization of perioperative hemodynamics, adequate fluid management, avoidance of nephrotoxic medications, early treatment of infectious complications, and prompt management of urinary obstruction or leakage [15]. Incorporating these evidence-based measures and discussing the available literature would enhance the clinical implications of the study and provide practical guidance for reducing the risk of AKI after radical cystectomy.

Conclusion

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent postoperative complication after radical cystectomy and is associated with adverse perioperative outcomes. Beyond its immediate clinical impact, postoperative AKI appears to negatively influence long-term oncological outcomes. Early identification of high-risk patients and optimization of perioperative management may help reduce the incidence of AKI and improve both renal and oncological prognosis.
