Strategic Collusion of LLM Agents: Market Division in Multi-Commodity Competitions
Ryan Y. Lin * Siddhartha M. Ojha * Kevin Cai * Maxwell F. Chen

Abstract
Machine-learning technologies are seeing increased deployment in real-world market scenarios. In this work, we explore the strategic behaviors of large language models (LLMs) when deployed as autonomous agents in multi-commodity markets, specifically within Cournot competition frameworks. We examine whether LLMs can independently engage in anti-competitive practices such as collusion or, more specifically, market division. Our findings demonstrate that LLMs can effectively monopolize specific commodities by dynamically adjusting their pricing and resource allocation strategies, thereby maximizing profitability without direct human input or explicit collusion commands. These results pose unique challenges and opportunities for businesses looking to integrate AI into strategic roles and for regulatory bodies tasked with maintaining fair and competitive markets. The study provides a foundation for further exploration into the ramifications of deferring high-stakes decisions to LLM-based agents.

Introduction

With artificial intelligence technologies becoming commonplace, machine-driven pricing agents and algorithms have found widespread adoption. As pricing decisions are increasingly placed in the hands of these agents, concerns have been raised regarding whether such agents may be susceptible to engaging in illegal or unethical business practices, often to the detriment of the consumer
[
1
,
2
]
. This practice is referred to as
algorithmic collusion
.

Early work of both theoretical
[
3
,
4
]
and empirical
[
5
,
6
]
nature has demonstrated the risk of machines and algorithmic approaches engaging in unethical business practices, triggering an interest among regulatory bodies
[
2
,
1
]
. Large language models (LLMs) offer general-purpose language generation capabilities, enabled by their pre-training on massive datasets
[
7
]
. Soon, businesses may begin feeding key business metrics into LLM-based pricing agents. Recent work has found that LLM-based agents are capable of autonomous collusion in an oligopolistic setting
[
8
]
. Furthermore, they find success in maximizing profits at the detriment of the consumer without explicit instruction or means of direct communication, suggesting a troublesome prognosis for consumer welfare in the future of AI pricing agents.

In this work, we examine the risk of collusion in a multi-commodity variant of
Cournot
models of competition. Cournot competition is a classical economic model in which firms choose output quantities, and prices are determined by aggregate supply. Whereas previous works in this area have focused on anti-competitive "price fixing", we focus on the more overt anti-competitive practice of "market division". Our work also marks the first application of LLM-based agents in the Cournot competition model, as well as the first empirical study of LLM-based agents in the multi-commodity variant of Cournot competition. We show that LLM-based agents do indeed display cooperative behavior and divide up the market in this problem setting.

2
Related Works

O’Sullivan and Sheffrin [9]
define collusion in multi-agent settings as a self-benefiting agreement, tacit or explicit, among firms or individuals to divide a market, set prices, limit production, or limit opportunities, often at the expense of the consumer. We will adopt this definition for the remainder of this work, focusing specifically on market division and consumer welfare. Research on AI-based pricing agents often examines their potential for collusion using various market scenarios. Take, for example, the Bertrand oligopoly, which uses a logit demand model to dictate the quantity of the product sold as a function of price
[
10
,
8
]
. In this setting, agents produce a homogeneous product and set
prices
simultaneously, with the Nash equilibrium serving as a benchmark for empirical comparisons.

Another frequently studied model is Cournot competition, where agents choose production
quantities
as opposed to setting prices as in the Bertrand setting. In Cournot competition, an inverse demand model is calculated, where the price of a good is calculated based on the quantity of the good in the market (See Equation
1
). Recent work explores multi-market Cournot settings where firms with symmetric cost functions decide which markets to enter under resource constraints
[
11
,
12
]
. Auction-based models, particularly two-bidder first-price auctions, are also used to study collusion, with agents acting simultaneously over multiple rounds and known equilibrium outcomes
[
8
,
13
]
.

The study of collusion in games pre-dates the deployment of autonomous agents in such settings. In the context of game theory, the Folk Theorem suggests that repeated interaction can result in virtually any average payoff in a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium (SPE) if agents maintain enough patience
[
14
]
. This patience is quantified with the "discount factor", typically denoted as
\delta
in economic literature. In effect, the Folk Theorem shows that there is always an SPE for which agents cooperate for long-term payoff. However, it is not immediately obvious that a particular agent possesses the reasoning capability to arrive at these cooperative equilibria.

In several of the above problem settings, Q-learning agents have demonstrated tacit collusive behavior, learning to charge supra-competitive prices in Bertrand competition games even without direct communication
[
10
]
. Collusion persists under imperfect monitoring, where agents lack access to others’ prices
[
5
]
. Similar outcomes are observed in Cournot competition with policy gradient reinforcement learning
[
11
]
. In auctions, Q-learning agents collude only under specific conditions, such as asynchronous bidding in first-price auctions, but this behavior diminishes when bid history is introduced
[
13
]
.

Collusive behavior is not limited to Q-learning agents. Recent works explore the behavior of LLM-based agents in multi-agent market settings, and empirical experiments demonstrate that these agents can exhibit collusive pricing behavior even without explicit instructions to collude or channels to communicate with each other
[
8
]
.

This work highlights collusion between LLM-based agents in the Cournot market setting. More specifically, we show that such agents effectively divide sales territories among each other and tacitly collude to discourage competition at the expense of the consumer. This act of "market division" is considered illegal by various regulatory bodies, such as the
Federal Trade Commission [15]
.

3
Experiments

In Cournot competition, each firm decides the quantity of a
homogeneous
, or indistinguishable, good to produce
[
16
]
. Using the aggregate quantity supplied to the market, the market-clearing price is determined through the inverse demand function. Previous works have identified Cournot-like competition in markets for commodities, such as crude oil
[
17
]
and coal
[
18
]
, as well as wholesale electricity markets
[
19
]
.

While there has been significant work done in characterizing the original model and various derivatives, including but not limited to multi-market Cournot competition
[
20
]
and Cournot competition in networked markets
[
21
]
, there has been limited work in understanding the model as a learning game and even less attention given to better understanding the risk of collusive game-play under such a model. Existing works exploring machine-learning methods in the context of Cournot competition have been limited exclusively to using traditional reinforcement learning (RL) techniques
[
11
,
22
,
23
]
.

In this work, we present a novel application of LLM-based agents to a multi-commodity variant of the Cournot competition model. We run experiments with six commercially available models: OpenAI’s o4-mini, GPT-4.1, and GPT-4.1-mini
[
24
]
, DeepSeek’s DeepSeek-V3
[
25
]
, Anthropic’s Claude-3.7-Sonnet
[
26
]
, and Google’s Gemini-1.5 Pro
[
27
]
. In each experiment, we only pair models of the same type with each other (i.e., within the same experiment, both agents are identical). This decision was made to ensure that observed behaviors and outcomes are attributable to the intrinsic characteristics of the model architecture and not to cross-model dynamics. It also ensures fairness, as heterogeneous match-ups may conflate model performance with strategic mismatches rather than inherent decision-making quality. By isolating each model in symmetric settings, we can more reliably evaluate how each model internally reasons about market dynamics in a competitive multi-agent scenario.

3.1
Problem Setting

We now present the general set-up of our multi-agent, multi-commodity Cournot game. Let there be
n
firms and
m
commodity markets, with
F=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
and
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{m}\}
denoting the sets of firms and commodities, respectively. For the market of commodity
\gamma_{j}
, let
q_{i,j}
denote the quantity that firm
f_{i}
produces. Then, there exists a linear inverse demand function of the form

p_{j}^{*}(Q_{j})=\alpha_{j}-\frac{Q_{j}}{\beta_{j}}\text{ where }\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}\text{ and }Q_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i,j}

(1)

Firm
f_{i}
faces a marginal production cost
c_{i,j}
to produce commodity
j
, drawn from the cost set
C=\{c_{1},\ldots c_{n}\}
. We argue that potentially differentiated production costs are more representative of real-world inequities in the distribution of scarce resources, such as human capital and natural materials.

We run the market for a finite number of rounds. However, because the total number of rounds is unknown to firms, and firms are told to maximize long-run profits, we effectively simulate an infinitely repeated game. Every round, each firm
f_{i}\in F
chooses a strategy
s_{i}\in S_{i}
from its strategy space,
S_{i}=(q_{i,1},\ldots,q_{i,m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
.

After each round, we compute market-clearing prices for each good
\gamma_{j}\in\Gamma
,
p^{*}_{j}(s)
using the collective strategy profile for all firms,
s=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n})\in S=S_{1}\times S_{2}\times\cdots\times S_{n}
, and the inverse demand function for commodity
\gamma_{j}
. We then compute the profit of each firm according to the formula,

\Pi^{i}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(p^{*}_{j}(s)-c_{i,j})\cdot q_{i,j}

(2)

Our simulation of an infinite-horizon game represents a deliberate and consequential design choice, as it reshapes the strategic landscape away from finitely repeated or single-period Cournot games.

In finitely repeated games, traditional game theory predicts the collapse of cooperation through backward induction: a rational agent, anticipating defection by the opponent in the final round, has no incentive to cooperate in the penultimate round. This logic propagates backward, ultimately eliminating the viability of cooperation from the outset.

In the absence of sustained cooperation, profit-maximizing agents must act myopically. In other words, they have no knowledge of or coordination with the other firms’ future actions. This lack of strategic linkage between periods effectively reduces to a single-period game, in which the firms are expected to repeatedly play the single-period Cournot-Nash. As such, the single-period Cournot-Nash serves as a suitable baseline for a market environment in which the agents are
not
colluding.

Interestingly, behavioral economics research has shown that human subjects can deviate from these theoretical predictions, sustaining cooperation even in finitely repeated games
[
28
,
29
]
. This behavior has been attributed to human behavioral biases, absent from classical models of strategic interaction, but that previous works have shown to emerge in LLMs
[
29
]
.

For infinitely repeated games, the Folk Theorem establishes that cooperative outcomes can be sustainable as equilibria if agents sufficiently value future payoffs
[
14
]
. We argue that an infinite-horizon game more accurately reflects the deployment context for LLM-based pricing agents, where firms seek to optimize revenue indefinitely rather than toward some known terminal event, such as bankruptcy or acquisition.

A key challenge in deploying LLM-based agents in strategic environments is that, unlike the well-defined, patient agents of economic theory, or even the human subjects studied in behavioral research, we lack a clear understanding of their inductive biases or how they discount future payoffs. Such effective “patience" is a necessary (though not sufficient) condition for sustaining cooperation and may depend not only on the foundation model but also on the prompts and reward structures that shape agent behavior. Critically, the potential for these agents to engage in harmful or illegal collusion may not be fully anticipated, or even understood, by the firms that deploy them.

We posit that our infinite-horizon setting provides a compelling testbed where both previous theoretical and empirical works support the possibility of sustained collusion, but where the dynamics of LLM-based agents remain underexplored and potentially risky.

While the repeated Cournot duopoly is a textbook example of an infinite-horizon game, its multi-commodity variant is relatively underexplored in the literature
[
11
,
12
]
. Despite modeling semi-realistic and well-understood strategic interactions, this variant’s limited representation in publicly available texts makes it especially well-suited for evaluating the strategic behavior of LLM-based agents, offering a scenario more likely
to fall outside their training distribution.

3.2
Quantifying Market Division

Market division is the practice of dividing a market among a set of competitors to avoid competition. To quantify this, we use the common Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI)
[
30
]
:
\text{HHI}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{2}
, where
n
is the number of firms in a market, and
s_{i}
represents the market share of firm
i
. This statistic ranges from
[0,1]
where
0
indicates a highly-competitive market and
1
indicates a pure monopoly. In practice, one typically considers any HHI over
0.25
to be "highly concentrated". However, with only two firms, the minimum possible value is
0.5
.

3.3
Quantifying Collusion

Collusion, by contrast, is a profitable agreement between firms that typically harms consumers. For example, dividing the market or restricting output can increase prices for consumers. To quantify such behavior, we use the consumer surplus across repeated iterations of the game. A general expression for the aggregate consumer surplus across both product markets at a discrete time step is given by

\sum_{j}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{j}-p_{j})\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\right)

(3)

More specifically, we use the ratio of the current consumer surplus to that under the single-period Cournot-Nash equilibrium (see Appendix
B.2
for optimization formulation), which corresponds to the case of no cooperation. We will refer to this metric as the Consumer Surplus Ratio (CSR).

3.4
Environment Configuration

For our experiments, we restrict ourselves to the case of
n=2
firms simultaneously competing in
m=2
commodity markets. Both firms face a set of fixed marginal costs drawn from the
cost set

C=\{40,50\}
. We choose the marginal cost set
C
to reflect potential modest cost heterogeneity that may arise from slight but impactful differences in production efficiency or proximity to marketplaces, while allowing us to test both symmetric and asymmetric cost scenarios.

Each agent is tasked with maximizing total firm profits over 50 rounds (a number which is unknown to the agents) where the market dynamics are governed by identical inverse demand functions for both products, denoted by
\alpha=100
and
\beta=2
. These are scaling parameters that effectively ensure that supplying more than 100 units to a particular market yields negative profits and that profits are not always guaranteed, encouraging exploration of the strategy space. Further, choosing parameters specific to our experiments limits the likelihood of memorization in the training data. As a reference point, we solve for expected market outcomes under duopoly and monopoly (full collusion), the formulations for which are presented in Appendix
B.2
.

Agents are provided with historical market data, including quantities sold, prices, market shares, and profits from the previous 15 rounds. The experiment prompts are intentionally vague. Each firm is unaware of its competitor’s payoff. We note that this level of competitor detail is less than that of
Fish et al. [8]
, which also used 300 rounds compared to our 50 rounds. The general flow of the experiment is depicted in Figure
1
.

Previous works demonstrate that allowing agents to communicate with their future selves ensures continuity and evolution of strategy
[
8
,
31
]
. Our experiment builds off these findings, allowing for communication between different temporal instances of each agent through "Plans and Insights" documents, which are updated each round. The full prompts can be found in Appendix
B.3
.

[
32

Notably, despite the absence of any explicit communication channel, agents never re-enter a market once they have exited. From a myopic profit-maximization perspective, unilateral re-entry would yield short-term gains. However, agents consistently avoid this behavior, suggesting an implicit understanding of the long-term consequences. This indicates emergent collusive dynamics: agents appear to understand that re-entering a previously conceded market would trigger reciprocal retaliation by the opponent, reducing overall payoffs. Their behavior aligns with a form of tacit coordination resembling subgame-perfect collusion.

5
Discussion and Conclusions

This research explores the potential for LLM-based agents to engage in anti-competitive behaviors, such as market division and monopolization, within a multi-commodity market setting. By simulating agents making strategic pricing and production decisions in multi-commodity Cournot competition models, our study reveals the risks of algorithmic collusion and its significant impact on markets, consumers, and communities.

Importantly, the concern is not that every agent deployment will result in collusion, but rather that even seemingly benign prompts can give rise to such behavior. The threshold for regulatory or ethical concern is not universal coordination, it is the existence of any realistic scenario in which LLM-based agents can learn to collude at consumers’ expense. Such emergent outcomes could have serious real-world consequences, from restricting access to essential goods to prompting major business decisions like layoffs.

The above results suggest that LLMs possess the reasoning capability to reach cooperative equilibria, the existence of which is supported by the Folk Theorem from traditional game theory. Our experiments identify that LLM-based agents do indeed engage in market division, resulting in an effectively monopolistic control over specific commodities. This behavior is particularly surprising given the absence of explicit coordination channels and the potential short-term profits available from market re-entry.

These findings underscore the legal and ethical concerns of delegating high-impact decisions to AI agents, especially when such decisions risk violating antitrust laws or adversely affecting workers and consumers.

The study is limited by its scope, focusing only on two agents and two products. Expanding to scenarios with more agents and products could yield further insights into market dynamics where direct market division is more complex. Agents’ decision-making capabilities were also constrained by simplified actions, such as limited context windows, which affected their learning and decision retention.

Future work could address these limitations by exploring more complex market scenarios. For example, agents could be deployed in a more open market setting, where they are not limited to producing a set quantity of goods or have the option to make decisions about long-term business investments. Exploring these mechanisms more fully is a promising avenue for future work.

Our findings call for the development of comprehensive regulatory frameworks and the implementation of robust guardrails to mitigate the risk of market manipulation by autonomous agents. Our work aims to inform policymakers, researchers, and industry leaders about the potential perils of unregulated deployment, encouraging proactive measures to ensure fair and competitive market dynamics. Further investigation in these areas could provide a deeper understanding of the potential and pitfalls of LLM-based agents in economic settings. We release our code at
https://github.com/smojha/collusive-llm-agents
.

B.2
Optimization Formulations

B.2.1
Cournot Duopoly Optimization Formulation

For completeness, we provide a formulation of the optimization solved numerically to identify the unique single-period Cournot-Nash equilibrium for the game setting used in our experiments. We employ an iterative best response dynamics algorithm
[
33
]
. This method alternates between optimizing each firm’s strategy via the agent’s profit function (Equation
2
) while holding the other firm’s strategy fixed, until convergence within a specified tolerance, for which we use
\epsilon=10^{-8}
, or until the maximum number of iterations,
T=100
, has been reached, reflecting an unsuccessful optimization. We initialize
q_{i}^{0}
at 100 for each
i
. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

Algorithm 1

Iterative Best Response for Cournot Duopoly

1:

Initialize
q_{i}^{0}=(q_{iA}^{0},q_{iB}^{0})
,
q_{j}^{0}=(q_{jA}^{0},q_{jB}^{0})

2:

Set convergence threshold
\epsilon=10^{-8}
, maximum iterations
T=100

3:

for

t=1
to
T

do

4:

Solve for firm
i
’s best response:

5:

q_{i}^{t}\leftarrow\argmax_{q_{i}}\Pi^{i}(q_{i},q_{j}^{t-1})

6:

Solve for firm
j
’s best response:

7:

q_{j}^{t}\leftarrow\argmax_{q_{j}}\Pi^{j}(q_{j},q_{i}^{t})

8:

if

\max(|q_{f}^{t}-q_{f}^{t-1}|)<\epsilon
for
f\in\{i,j\}

then

9:

return

q_{iA}^{t},q_{iB}^{t},q_{jA}^{t},q_{jB}^{t}

10:

end

if

11:

end

for

12:

Raise convergence failure exception

Each firm’s optimization problem is solved using the Sequential Least Squares Quadratic Programming (SLSQP) method using the
scipy
library
[
34
]
, which is well-suited for constrained nonlinear optimization problems. The objective function for each firm is the negative of its profit function (Equation
2
), as we perform minimization. The algorithm converges when the change in quantities for both firms between iterations is below the specified tolerance
\epsilon
. If convergence is not achieved within the maximum number of iterations, an exception is raised. This numerical approach is particularly effective for our Cournot duopoly model, as it can handle the nonlinear nature of the profit functions and the capacity constraints efficiently. Moreover, it allows for easy modification of model parameters, facilitating sensitivity analyses and extensions to more complex market structures. The algorithm cannot converge to multiple solutions, as our problem setting satisfies the assumptions required for a unique Cournot equilibrium (as outlined in
[
35
]
).

B.2.2
Full Collusion Optimization Formulation

In the case of full collusion, which we denote in our figures as the "Monopoly" lines, the two firms simply act together as a single firm seeking to maximize its profits. Still, they face the production capacity constraints of the individual firms. Thus, they seek to maximize the following profit function,

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{M}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\{A,B\}}\left[p_{m}^{*}(q_{1,m}+q_{2,m})\cdot\left(q_{1,m}+q_{2,m}\right)-\sum_{f\in\{1,2\}}c_{f,m}q_{f,m}\right]

(4)

As in the Cournot-Nash optimization, we solve the firm’s problem using Sequential Least Squares Quadratic Programming (SLSQP). As we need only solve the firm’s problem in a single shot, we rely on
scipy

[
34
]
to return when a feasible solution has been identified, which we verify by inspecting the returned result.

B.3.1

Your task is to assist a user in allocating production resources between

firm, and you will be provided with previous quantity and profit data
from a user who is selling these products, as well as files that will
help inform your allocation strategy. You will receive market data for up
to the last 15 rounds. Also, in addition to the selling prices for each

Product A information:
- The cost to produce each unit is $40.

Product B information:
- The cost to produce each unit is $50.

You are also provided your market share. There is no difference between
products of the same category (i.e. Product A) sold by different firms.

Producing more than 100 units of aggregate product will result in negative
profits, but you are allowed to produce less than this aggregate amount.
