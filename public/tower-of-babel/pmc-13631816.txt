Potential Role of the Urokinase-Type Plasminogen Activator Receptor–Integrin β3 Axis in Podocyte Adhesion Instability and Loss
Authors: Ji-Hee Kim, Bao TN Dang, Junyoung Kim, Minseob Eom, Seung-Kuy Cha, Jaeseok Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPodocyte detachment is a critical determinant of irreversible podocyte loss and progression of proteinuric glomerular diseases. Although altered glomerular hemodynamics are a hallmark of kidney diseases, it remains unclear how specific fluid mechanical forces contribute to podocyte adhesion failure and detachment in disease-relevant contexts.MethodsRenal biopsy specimens from patients with minimal change disease (MCD), membranous nephropathy (MN), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), and control kidneys were analyzed for urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR), integrin β3, and fibronectin (FN1) expression. Differentiated human podocytes were exposed to controlled fluid shear stress (FSS) using an orbital shear system. Podocyte retention, detachment, and F-actin cytoskeletal remodeling were quantified alongside biochemical assessments of downstream mechanotransduction signaling pathways through immunoblotting and quantitative image analysis. Integrin β3 inhibition and neutralizing antibodies were used as mechanistic probes.ResultsBiopsy specimens showed disease-specific profiles—uPAR and integrin β3 were markedly upregulated in active MN and MCD glomeruli but attenuated and chronic lesion-like in FSGS. In vitro, FSS induced time-dependent upregulation of uPAR, integrin β3, and FN1, with disruption of parallel F-actin stress fibers and phosphorylation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK). Integrin β3 returned toward baseline by Day 3, consistent with a selection effect; its inhibition blunted FAK/JNK phosphorylation, restored F-actin alignment, and preserved podocyte adhesion.ConclusionsImmunologic or mechanical injury may induce the uPAR–integrin β3 axis, whereas sustained FSS amplifies it through FAK/JNK signaling, F-actin disruption, and detachment. Integrin β3 inhibition attenuated this cascade, supporting integrin-associated mechanotransduction as a strategy for preserving podocyte adhesion under altered glomerular hemodynamics.Keywords: fibronectin, fluid shear stress, integrin αvβ3, mechanotransduction, podocytes, urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor

Podocytes are highly specialized epithelial cells that form the final barrier of the glomerular filtration apparatus. Owing to their terminal differentiation and limited proliferative capacity, podocyte loss is largely irreversible and is a strong predictor of glomerulosclerosis and kidney function decline.1 Even modest podocyte depletion exposes segments of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), triggering maladaptive extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and promoting FSGS. Although podocyte depletion can result from either cell death or physical detachment, the predominant mechanism remains unclear. Most evidence for apoptosis comes from in vitro studies, whereas the presence of viable podocytes in the urine of patients with kidney diseases strongly suggests that the detachment of living podocytes is a major contributor to podocyte loss in vivo.2

Recent studies have underscored the central role of biomechanical forces in regulating podocyte behavior. Podocytes inhabit a mechanically dynamic microenvironment and experience repetitive cycles of shear stress, tensile strain, and filtration drag generated by pulsatile blood flow and capillary wall deformation.3 Under physiologic conditions, podocyte adhesion to the GBM is primarily mediated by integrin α3β1, which provides stable anchoring and maintains foot process architecture. In contrast, injury states are marked by a shift toward αvβ3 integrin engagement, which promotes motility and destabilizes podocyte anchoring, thereby increasing susceptibility to detachment.4,5 Enhanced expression of the urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) and its soluble form, soluble uPAR (suPAR), further accelerates αvβ3 integrin induction, leading to cytoskeletal remodeling, lamellipodia formation, and foot process effacement.6,7 Mechanical cues intensify these responses; mechanical stretch induces conformational activation of αvβ3 integrin,8 and mechanical load increases FN1 production in podocytes, thereby reshaping the ECM to favor integrin-mediated motility.9

Despite these insights, the role of the uPAR–integrin β3 axis in inducing podocyte adhesion instability under FSS remains incompletely understood. Although altered hemodynamics are a key feature of proteinuric kidney diseases, it is not established whether fluid shear forces alone are sufficient to induce podocyte adhesion instability and detachment.3,10 Notably, most mechanobiology studies of podocytes have focused on tensile or stretch-based models, leaving the specific contribution of shear stress, a fundamental component of glomerular flow, largely unexamined.

To address this critical gap, we examined human kidney biopsy specimens to investigate changes in the expression of uPAR and integrin β3, key molecules involved in podocyte adhesion, in disease states. Furthermore, we exposed differentiated human podocytes to controlled FSS and assessed its effects on podocyte adhesion to the GBM and on downstream signaling pathways that regulate actin cytoskeleton remodeling. In particular, the FSS magnitude applied in our orbital culture system (∼1.0–2.5 dyne/cm2 in the peripheral high-shear zone at 200 rpm) was strategically selected to model pathologically elevated, supraphysiological mechanical forces. This specific range directly aligns with previous validated in vitro podocyte studies designed to simulate hyperfiltration-associated hemodynamic loading, including the studies by Srivastava et al.11 (2.0 dyne/cm2) and Friedrich et al.12 (spanning 0.015–0.25 dyne/cm2 to capture physiologic-to-early pathologic transitions).

Finally, we used an integrin β3 inhibitor to evaluate the role of the uPAR–integrin β3 axis in podocyte adhesion and its potential to prevent podocyte loss. We aimed to investigate the role of the uPAR–integrin β3 axis as a key pathway underlying podocyte adhesion instability and to provide pathophysiological insight into how altered glomerular hemodynamics may contribute to podocyte loss in proteinuric kidney diseases.

Methods

Human Tissues

Paraffin-embedded human kidney tissue samples were analyzed from a total of 52 individuals, including pathologically confirmed normal controls (n = 15), MN (n = 14), MCD (n = 13), and FSGS (n = 10). Normal kidney tissues were obtained from nephrectomy specimens resected because of traumatic injury and showed no histologic abnormalities. MN, MCD, and FSGS samples were obtained and used in accordance with institutional and ethical guidelines.

All biopsy specimens were obtained at the time of initial diagnosis, before the initiation of any immunosuppressive therapy. The immunosuppressive treatments summarized in Table 1 were administered after the diagnostic biopsy during subsequent clinical follow-up. During follow-up, most patients with MCD and MN achieved remission, whereas approximately half of the patients with FSGS progressed to end-stage renal disease.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed for uPAR, integrin β3, and FN1. A Ventana Benchmark Ultra automated immunostaining system (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, German) was used for all staining procedures. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections (4 μm thickness) were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated through graded alcohols, and pretreated with CC1 (formalin-fixed solution; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). The sections were washed with reaction buffer, then incubated with antibodies at dilutions of 1:200 (uPAR), 1:1000 (integrin β3), and 1:200 (FN1). Bound antibodies were detected using the UltraView Universal DAB (3,3′-diaminobenzidine) Detection Kit (760-500, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Appropriate positive and negative control tissues were included in each staining run. After immunohistochemical staining, the staining intensities of uPAR, integrin β3, and FN1 were quantified using ImageJ (version 1.54p; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD), and comparisons were performed across experimental groups.

Human Podocyte Cell Culture

An immortalized human podocyte cell line, kindly gifted by Prof. Heon Yung Gee (Yonsei University), was cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium Liquid Media Supplement (I3146, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Cells were grown under permissive conditions at 33 °C, subcultured, and subsequently shifted to 37 °C to induce differentiation. After 14 days, fully differentiated cells were seeded around a Pyrex cloning cylinder (CLS316610, Corning Inc., Corning, NY) that was positioned at the center on each well of a 6-well plate. The cloning cylinder was used only during cell seeding and was removed before FSS exposure for Western blot analysis, whereas crystal violet staining was performed without the cloning cylinder.

FSS Exposure and Pharmacologic Intervention

FSS was applied by placing differentiated podocytes cultured in 6-well plates on an orbital shaker (ORBi-Shaker CO2; BMSBT4500EUK, Benchmark Scientific, Sayreville, NJ) at 200 rpm inside a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. On the basis of our computational fluid dynamics simulation, this orbital profile generated a radial shear gradient across each well, yielding a pathologically elevated wall shear stress of approximately 1.0–2.5 dyne/cm2 within the peripheral region (60%–100% radius), whereas the central region (0%–40% radius) experienced minimal mechanical load.11, 12, 13 This peripheral range purposely exceeds the estimated physiological baseline of podocytes (∼0.3 dyne/cm2) to recapitulate hyperfiltration-induced mechanical loading. These levels correspond to elevated mechanical shear forces that mimic altered glomerular hemodynamics beyond physiological baselines.

For targeted intervention studies, cells were pretreated with either cilengitide, a pharmacologic inhibitor of β3/αvβ5 integrins, or a specific integrin β3 neutralizing antibody before FSS onset, and fresh culture medium containing the respective agent was replenished and maintained throughout the 3-day FSS exposure period. To eliminate potential vehicle effects, the final concentration of dimethyl sulfoxide used to dissolve cilengitide was strictly controlled at less than 0.1% (v/v) in the culture medium, and an equivalent volume of vehicle-only medium was added to the FSS-alone and static control groups.

Western Blot

Cells were rinsed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline and lysed in ice-cold radio-immunoprecipitation assay lysis and extraction buffer (89901, ThermoFisher Scientific Inc., Rockford, IL) supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails (05892791001, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Lysates were centrifuged at 17,000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for Western blot analysis. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecylsulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto 0.2-μm nitrocellulose membranes (88018, ThermoFisher Scientific Inc., Rockford, IL). Membranes were blocked with 6% skim milk and incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies against rabbit (31460) or mouse (31450) IgG (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Select Western Blotting Detection Reagent (XLS075-0100, Cyanagen, Bologna, Italy) and imaged using a ChemiDoc XRS system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software (version 6.0.1; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA).

Crystal Violet Assay

Human podocytes were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1500 cells/cm2 and exposed to FSS by rotating the plates on an orbital shaker at 200 rpm. After FSS exposure, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (ST4090-PP, SupremeTech, Chuncheon, Korea) for 15 minutes, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (CV1040, SupremeTech, Korea) for 20 minutes. Excess dye was removed with distilled water, and plates were air-dried.

Instead of measuring total absorbance, crystal violet–stained wells were analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.54p). Two concentric regions were defined on the basis of radial distance—a central region (0%–40% of the radius) and a peripheral region (60%–100% of the radius). For each region, area-normalized cell counts were calculated from thresholded images.

Drugs

Cilengitide (CS-1211, ChemScene, Monmouth Junction, NJ), an inhibitor of αvβ3/αvβ5, was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was isolated from cells using RiboEx (301-902, GeneAll Biotechnology, Seoul, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (FSQ-301, Toyobo, Japan). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction was performed using sequence-specific human primers and Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (4367659, Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA) on a QuantStudio™ 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (4485691, Applied Biosystems, ThermoFisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA). The primer sequences were as follows: 18S, 5′-AACCCGTTGAACCCCATT-3′ (forward) and 5′-CCATCCAATCGGTAGTAGCG-3′ (reverse); Synaptopodin, 5′-CCGCAAATCCATGTTTACTT-3′ (forward) and 5′-GCTTCTCATCCGCTGTCTGT-3′ (reverse).

Phalloidin Staining

Differentiated human podocytes seeded onto the confocal dish were treated with integrinβ3 or IgG antibody (sc-2025, Santa Cruz; 1 μg/mL) for 30 minutes before applying FSS. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline for 15 minutes at 37 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor 594 phalloidin staining (1:40 dilution, A12381, Invitrogen, CA) for 20 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescence images were obtained using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (LSM800, Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Fluorescent images were quantified using ImageJ 1.53c (NIH, Bethesda, Maryland).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). All experiments were conducted with ≥3 independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with individual data points shown in scatter dot plots. Comparisons among > 2 groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference. For pairwise comparisons between static and FSS conditions, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was performed. p > 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Altered Glomerular Expression of uPAR, Integrin β3, and FN1 in Patients with Proteinuric Glomerular Diseases

The baseline clinical and demographic characteristics of the study cohort, comprising healthy controls and patients diagnosed with MCD, MN, and FSGS, are summarized in Table 1. Notably, patients across all disease groups exhibited significant proteinuria at the time of biopsy, providing an appropriate clinical context for studying the dynamic mechanisms of podocyte injury.

To investigate the clinical relevance of the uPAR–integrin β3 pathway in proteinuric kidney diseases, we first evaluated the glomerular expression levels of uPAR, integrin β3, and FN1 using IHC staining on human kidney biopsy specimens. Representative glomerular images from patients with MN and MCD revealed a robust and significant increase in the immunoreactivity of uPAR, integrin β3, and FN1 within the glomeruli compared with those from healthy controls (Figure 1a, c, and e). Quantitative morphometric analysis confirmed that the staining intensities of all three proteins were significantly elevated in both the MN and MCD groups (Figure 1b, d, and f).

Given the well-established role of uPAR in FSGS, we further expanded our cohort to include biopsy samples from patients with FSGS (Supplementary Figure S1). Interestingly, IHC staining of FSGS specimens showed distinct alterations; although glomerular FN1 expression was markedly increased compared with controls (Supplementary Figure S1e and f), the immunoreactivities of uPAR and integrin β3 were unexpectedly lower than those observed in the active phases of MN and MCD, showing a more heterogeneous or attenuated pattern (Supplementary Figure S1 A-D). These clinical findings indicate that the uPAR–integrin β3–FN1 axis is differentially induced across various podocytopathies, potentially serving as a prominent tissue signature during the active or early remodeling phases of podocyte injury.

Establishment of an In Vitro FSS Model and Its Impact on Podocyte Adhesion and Actin Cytoskeleton

To model the altered glomerular hemodynamics observed in proteinuric kidney diseases, conditionally immortalized human podocytes were differentiated by shifting the culture temperature from 33 °C to 37 °C for 14 days before FSS exposure (Figure 2a). The successful maturation of podocytes was verified by a marked upregulation of synaptopodin, at both the protein and mRNA levels, alongside the acquisition of an elongated cellular morphology with mature processes (Figure 2b–d).

To model mechanical load, differentiated podocytes were exposed to orbital FSS at 200 rpm using a 6-well culture system. Computational fluid dynamics simulation showed a characteristic radial shear gradient across the well, yielding an elevated wall shear stress of approximately 1 to 2.5 dyne/cm2 in the peripheral region (60%–100% radius), whereas the central region (0%–40% radius) experienced minimal shear stress (Figure 2e).

Consistent with this computational profile, crystal violet staining and subsequent threshold-based quantitative image analysis revealed a region-specific and time-dependent pattern of podocyte loss (Figure 2f). Intriguingly, exposure to FSS for 1 day did not induce immediate cell detachment, showing a preserved area-normalized cell density in the peripheral zone (Figure 2g and h). However, prolonged and sustained exposure to FSS for 3 days triggered a significant reduction in area-normalized cell density exclusively within the high-stress peripheral zone (Figure 2h), whereas the low-stress central zone remained largely unaffected even after 3 days of shear stress (Figure 2g). These findings indicate that podocyte detachment does not occur as an acute mechanical stripping; rather, it is a progressive process driven by sustained hemodynamic overload, preferentially disrupting podocyte anchorage in high-stress zones over time.

To further investigate the structural alterations underlying this shear-induced detachment, we examined the organization of the podocyte actin cytoskeleton. Alexa594–phalloidin staining showed that under static conditions, differentiated podocytes exhibited robust, well-aligned longitudinal F-actin stress fibers (Figure 2i). In contrast, prolonged exposure to FSS led to a progressive disruption of these actin networks, characterized by fiber rearrangement, dissolution, and a significant, time-dependent decrease in total F-actin fluorescent intensity (Figure 2i and j).

Collectively, these data confirm that our orbital shear system effectively recapitulates the biomechanical stress that triggers actin cytoskeleton destabilization and subsequent cell detachment in vulnerable podocytes.

FSS Induces uPAR-Integrin β3 Axis and Engages Downstream Pathways Involved in Actin Cytoskeleton Remodeling

To determine whether mechanical loading triggers specific molecular adaptations and injury signaling in podocytes, we examined the expression profiles of uPAR, integrin β3, and FN1 after FSS exposure (Figure 3a). Western blot analyses revealed that exposure to FSS (1 or 3 days) markedly increased the protein expression levels of uPAR, integrin β3, and FN1 compared with static controls (Figures 3b, d, and f). Quantitative densitometry confirmed the significant upregulation of all three markers (Figures 3c, e, and g). Intriguingly, the induction of these proteins exhibited a dynamic, time-dependent transition, characterized by a sharp peak at day 1 followed by a slight decline at day 3 (Figure 3c, e, and g). This pattern aligns with our chronological cell density data (Figure 2h), likely reflecting a selection effect wherein highly motile podocytes with strong uPAR–integrin β3 induction preferentially detach by day 3, leaving behind a surviving subpopulation with lower expression profiles.

To further elucidate whether this mechanical induction of the uPAR–integrin β3 axis translates into functional signaling cascades that drive the previously observed actin disruption (Figure 2i), we evaluated key downstream regulators of mechanotransduction and cytoskeletal remodeling. To establish a functional link, podocytes were exposed to FSS in the presence or absence of cilengitide, a β3 integrin inhibitor.

Western blot analyses first showed that pharmacologic blockade of integrin β3 by cilengitide markedly and significantly suppressed the FSS-induced upregulation of integrin β3 itself, confirming its targeted inhibitory efficacy under mechanical stress conditions (Figure 4a and b). Concurrently, we assessed whether this upstream blockade effectively blunts downstream enzymatic activation. As shown in Figure 4c–f, FSS significantly enhanced the phosphorylation levels of FAK and c-Jun N-terminal kinase compared with static controls. Notably, cilengitide treatment significantly attenuated the mechanical phosphorylation of both FAK and JNK, effectively restoring their activities to levels comparable with static baseline conditions (Figure 4c–f).

Taken together, these findings show that pathologically elevated FSS does not merely upregulate selected structural components; rather, it orchestrates a functional mechanosensitive program that cascades down to activate the phosphorylation levels of FAK and c-Jun N-terminal kinase pathways through the uPAR–integrin β3 axis, ultimately driving actin cytoskeleton destabilization and rendering podocytes highly susceptible to physical detachment.

Targeted Inhibition of Integrin β3 Breaks the Maladaptive Mechanosensitive Cascade, Restores F-Actin Architecture, and Preserves Podocyte Adhesion Under FSS

To systematically investigate the functional and causal relationships within the shear-induced pathways, we first conceptualized the dynamic, time-dependent transition of the upstream components on the basis of our chronological observations (Figure 5a). As shown in Figure 3, uPAR and FN1 expression is rapidly induced and robustly sustained through day 3 of FSS, whereas integrin β3 expression peaks early on day 1 and characteristically declines by day 3 because of the selective detachment of highly vulnerable cells (Figure 5a). On the basis of this molecular timeline, we designed an intervention strategy in which differentiated podocytes were pretreated with either cilengitide (a pharmacologic inhibitor of αvβ3/αvβ5 integrins) or a specific integrin β3-neutralizing antibody before the initiation of FSS, followed by continuous exposure to pathologically elevated FSS for 3 days (Figure 5b).

We first evaluated whether this targeted intervention could mitigate physical cell loss under sustained mechanical stress. A concentration–response analysis showed that cilengitide progressively reduced integrin β3 expression in podocytes exposed to 24-hour FSS, establishing the effective dose used for subsequent experiments (Supplementary Figures S2A and S2B). Crystal violet staining and subsequent radial thresholding analysis showed that a 3-day exposure to FSS markedly decreased podocyte density in the peripheral high-stress zone (Figure 5c and d). Remarkably, pharmacologic blockade with cilengitide substantially and significantly preserved the adherent cells, effectively preventing shear-induced podocyte detachment (Figure 5c and d).

To correlate this physical preservation with downstream molecular stabilization, we examined the feedback effects of integrin β3 inhibition on the axis components through Western blot analysis after 3 days of FSS. Consistent with the time-dependent resolution observed in Figure 3, the integrin β3 expression level after 3 days of FSS exposure did not show a marked increase relative to static controls, returning to near baseline levels (Figure 5f and i). In contrast, uPAR and FN1 levels remained robustly elevated even at day 3 of FSS (Figure 5e, g, h, and j). Notably, despite the baseline-like state of integrin β3 at this prolonged time point, pharmacologic intervention with cilengitide significantly attenuated the mechanical induction of uPAR and FN1 compared with the FSS-alone group (Figure 5e, g, h, and j). Concurrently, although it did not reach statistical significance, cilengitide treatment showed a clear trend toward reducing integrin β3 protein levels to, or slightly below, the static control baseline (Figure 5f and i). This targeted intervention effectively assisted in disrupting the self-amplifying injury loop and downregulating the entire mechanosensitive axis (Figure 5e–j).

Finally, to visually confirm that the rescue of podocyte anchorage was driven by the preservation of cellular structural frameworks, we analyzed the spatial organization of the actin cytoskeleton following integrin β3 antibody-mediated neutralization (Figure 5k). Under prolonged FSS conditions, the robust, parallel longitudinal F-actin stress fibers observed in static controls were severely disrupted, resulting in a fragmented, disorganized peripheral network (Figure 5k). In contrast, pretreatment with the integrin β3 neutralizing antibody successfully reversed this mechanical destabilization, restoring well-aligned, parallel F-actin fiber networks and maintaining total F-actin fluorescent intensity profiles comparable with static baselines (Figure 5k and l). Collectively, these robust functional and structural data show that blocking the uPAR–integrin β3 axis successfully halts the maladaptive mechanosensitive program, protecting both cytoskeletal architecture and anchoring stability in vulnerable podocytes against pathologic hemodynamic stress.

Discussion

The interaction between podocytes and GBM determines podocyte adhesion stability.14 Moreover, podocyte adhesion instability can directly cause podocyte detachment and loss, which affects podocyte survival.3,12 In this study, we show that FSS, a physiologically relevant mechanical force within the glomerulus, induces a uPAR–integrin β3–FN1 signaling program that destabilizes podocyte adhesion and promotes detachment. By integrating analyses of human kidney biopsy specimens with a controlled in vitro shear stress model, we provide evidence that this adhesion-associated pathway is upregulated in proteinuric glomerular diseases and can be directly triggered by mechanical loading. These findings offer mechanistic insight into how altered glomerular hemodynamics may contribute to podocyte loss, a key determinant of disease progression in nephrotic syndromes.

The podocyte-GBM interaction is formed through integrin molecules on the basal surface of podocytes, which bind to ECM components of the GBM. Integrins, also known as transmembrane receptors, mediate cell-ECM or cell-cell interactions and play key roles in cell anchorage, migration, and signaling. Integrin molecules are composed of α- and β-subunits; in the kidney, the predominant forms are integrin α3β1 and integrin αvβ3. There are various types of integrin-ECM interactions, and the interaction between podocytes and GBM is mainly determined by integrin α3β1-laminin or integrin αvβ3-FN1.4

Previous studies have shown that suPAR-mediated induction of integrin αvβ3 causes podocyte injury and proteinuria.15 Another observational study showed that variants in the apolipoprotein L1 (APOL1) gene, which are closely related to the development of chronic kidney disease, increase the risk of proteinuria and progression of chronic kidney disease by activating integrin αvβ3 in a suPAR-dependent manner.7 Elevated integrin αvβ3 expression has been observed in proliferative glomerulonephritis, including IgA nephropathy, suggesting a pathologic role for integrin αvβ3.5 Collectively, these findings suggest a pathogenic role for integrin αvβ3 in kidney disease. Furthermore, accumulating evidence indicates that integrin αvβ3 is closely linked to both uPAR and FN1. It has been established that uPAR signaling enhances integrin αvβ3 induction, thereby leading to podocyte foot process effacement and proteinuria.6 Another study reported that mechanical stress induces FN1 expression in podocytes.9 Collectively, these research results suggest that immunological injury and mechanical stress can synergistically induce the uPAR-integrin αvβ3 axis in podocytes.

Previous studies have established that integrin αvβ3 is involved in tumor metastasis, with 1 report showing that this integrin increases tumor cell adhesion and spreading.16 In addition, podocytes exposed to high glucose showed an upregulation of integrin β3 that enhanced podocyte motility, and spironolactone reduced cell motility by decreasing integrin β3.17 Another study reported that increased integrin αvβ3 expression after TGF-β administration enhanced podocyte adhesion and migration.18 Although cell adhesion and cell spreading (or migration) may appear to be opposing processes, earlier work suggests that integrin αvβ3-dependent adhesion and migration represent different phases of a single dynamic process.

Indeed, 1 study showed that in migrating cells, the density and turnover of integrin αvβ3 vary spatially within the same cell, depending on whether the cell is adhering or migrating. In particular, the migrating area within the cell exhibited high-density integrin αvβ3 that formed focal adhesions, and the high turnover of integrin αvβ3 facilitated cell movement through the continuous renewal of contact sites.19 In addition, another study showed significant changes in integrin αvβ3-FN1 binding according to the intensity of FSS. In an experimental system using FN1-coated microspheres flowing over an integrin αvβ3-coated plate, integrin αvβ3-FN1 adhesion was strengthened in the catch bond region, where shear stress was low, and the microspheres showed a “stop” movement. However, in the slip bond region, where the shear stress was high, the adhesion weakened, and a “rolling” movement was observed. These results showed that integrin αvβ3–ligand binding is dynamically regulated in response to force and indicated that cell migration is initiated if the cell is exposed to mechanical stress beyond the tolerable range.20 Similarly, another study reported that glucose exposure increased the adhesion of human glomerular epithelial cells in association with elevated integrin αvβ3 levels, whereas high-glucose-induced integrin αvβ3 overexpression decreased cell adhesion.10

On the basis of these dynamic changes in integrin αvβ3, we propose a biphasic adaptive response in podocytes. During the acute phases of immunologic or mechanical podocyte injury, the induction of the uPAR–integrin β3 axis and increased FN1 expression may initially enhance podocyte adhesion and stabilize attachment to the underlying matrix under mechanical loads.20 However, if the injury is severe, cumulative, or prolonged, podocytes may initiate an avoidance or escape response to persistent stress by increasing their motility. This increased motility inevitably destabilizes stable podocyte anchoring, making podocytes highly prone to detachment. In this context, hyper-motility represents a maladaptive response. Ultimately, sustained induction of the uPAR–integrin β3 axis may increase podocyte instability, making podocytes more vulnerable to shear-stress–induced detachment and loss.

Notably, this study revealed disease-specific changes in the expression of uPAR, integrin β3, and FN1 within human glomerulopathies. In human kidney tissue analysis, the active proteinuric disease groups, MN and MCD, showed a prominent upregulation of uPAR and integrin β3. In contrast, the FSGS group showed a distinct expression profile characterized by a marked increase in FN1 but an unexpected decrease in uPAR and integrin β3 levels compared with healthy controls.

Clinical data indicated that, unlike the typical nephrotic-range proteinuria observed in our MN and MCD groups, the FSGS patients in this study exhibited significantly lower 24-hour proteinuria levels, suggesting a more chronic or secondary etiology. Given that the uPAR–integrin β3 pathway is intricately linked to active nephrotic syndrome, our results suggest that this axis serves as a prominent molecular signature primarily during the active, early remodeling stages of podocyte injury, rather than in advanced, chronic sclerotic lesions.

Supporting this interpretation, our in vitro studies provided formal causal validation by linking upstream axis induction with downstream functional consequences. Exposure of human podocytes to pathological FSS activated key downstream enzymatic cascades involved in mechanotransduction and cytoskeletal remodeling, as evidenced by the robust phosphorylation of FAK and JNK. Concurrently, high-resolution confocal microscopy revealed a severe, time-dependent disruption of parallel F-actin stress fiber networks. Crucially, blockade of integrin β3 significantly blunted the mechanical phosphorylation of FAK and JNK, effectively reversed the fragmentation of F-actin filaments, and partially preserved podocyte anchorage. These results suggest that the uPAR–integrin β3 axis is not merely upregulated but is functionally engaged in the signaling cascade contributing to shear-induced cytoskeletal destabilization.

Our in vitro studies further showed that uPAR and integrin β3 protein levels varied over time. Although uPAR and FN1 remained strongly elevated up to day 3, integrin β3 expression peaked sharply on day 1 but characteristically returned to near-baseline levels by day 3. This temporal resolution reflects a prominent selection effect under continuous hemodynamic loading: podocytes undergoing aggressive cytoskeletal remodeling driven by intense integrin β3 upregulation are highly vulnerable and preferentially detach by day 3. Consequently, the surviving adherent subpopulation collected at day 3 exhibits a baseline-like integrin β3 status. Intriguingly, this day 3 molecular phenotypes perfectly mirror the IHC pattern observed in our chronic FSGS biopsy specimens. When interpreted together, these shared in vitro and in vivo findings strongly indicate that the uPAR–integrin β3 signaling program operates as a transient yet critical gateway during the early, active phase of actin cytoskeleton dismantling.

Taken together, our study suggests that podocytes with enhanced uPAR–integrin β3 axis are particularly vulnerable to mechanical stress, such as shear stress. Major mechanical stresses in the in vivo glomerular microenvironment include tensile stress at the podocyte base and filtration-induced FSS across the filtration slits. Furthermore, structural alterations in the filtration barrier under proteinuric conditions, such as foot process effacement, are likely to accelerate filtration-related fluid velocity and, in turn, shear stress. The localized mechanical force exerted in our system (1–2.5 dyne/cm2) deliberately exceeds the estimated physiologic FSS experienced by podocytes in normal murine glomeruli (∼ 0.3 dyne/cm2).12 Instead, it closely approximates the 1.5- to 3.0-fold pathologic acceleration observed under clinical hyperfiltration states, such as secondary to nephron reduction.21 At these supraphysiological thresholds, hemodynamic shear forces are known to dismantle parallel transversal stress fibers, induce a defensive shift toward cortical actin networks,12 and activate proinflammatory COX-2/PGE2 mechanotransduction cascades,11 triggering cell detachment in regions lacking adequate mesangial structural anchors.22 Our findings expand this established biophysical paradigm by showing that supraphysiological FSS concurrently induces the localized uPAR-integrin β3-FN1 axis, which propagates downstream signals through FAK and JNK phosphorylation (Figure 4) to drive podocyte anchoring instability.

Thus, the findings of this study provide mechanistic insights into how altered glomerular hemodynamics, such as glomerular hyperfiltration or glomerular hypertension, directly contribute to accelerated podocyte loss and irreversible glomerular damage in proteinuric kidney disease.

From this perspective, targeted integrin αvβ3 inhibitors hold strong promise as potential renoprotective agents.23,24 Cilengitide, a cyclic Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) pentapeptide, is a well-characterized small-molecule inhibitor of integrin αvβ3. Although extensively investigated as an antitumor agent to prevent metastasis, its clinical efficacy in oncology has been limited.25,26 Our study showed that cilengitide markedly downregulates integrin β3 and prevents podocyte loss under shear stress. These results highlight that integrin αvβ3 inhibitors may represent a promising therapeutic strategy against progressive chronic kidney disease by protecting podocyte anchoring stability.

This study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. Although the antibody used to detect integrin β3 in this study targeted extracellular epitopes, it remains unclear whether it distinguished between the active and inactive conformations of integrin β3. Therefore, we were unable to assess the activation state of integrin β3 or determine whether the observed effects of the uPAR–integrin β3 axis were mediated by conformational activation of integrin β3. In addition, we were unable to evaluate other focal adhesion–associated signaling molecules, including paxillin, talin, and vinculin, which may function as downstream mediators of the uPAR–integrin β3 axis.

The in vitro orbital shear stress model, although capturing key elements of mechanical loading, cannot fully replicate the complex 3-dimensional microarchitecture and multidirectional capillary wall strain present in vivo. Lastly, although our patient cohort included MN, MCD, and FSGS specimens, further investigations are required to determine whether this mechanosensitive cascade operates in other etiologies of proteinuric kidney disease, such as diabetic nephropathy or lupus nephritis. Future studies using in vivo animal models and longitudinal clinical data will be important for further validating the translational value of uPAR–integrin β3 signaling in disease progression.

In conclusion, the findings of this study support a model in which induction of the uPAR–integrin β3 axis represents an adaptive response to immunologic or mechanical podocyte injury. However, when mechanical stress is severe or sustained, this pathway promotes a maladaptive program associated with hypermotility. Consequently, increased podocyte motility destabilizes podocyte anchoring, driving structural disassembly of the actin cytoskeleton and triggering cell detachment under hemodynamic FSS, ultimately leading to irreversible podocyte depletion. By establishing a direct functional link between altered glomerular hemodynamics and tissue-level adhesion instability, this study clarifies a key mechanism underlying podocyte loss and highlights integrin β3-associated pathways as central therapeutic targets in podocyte mechanobiology (Figure 6).

Proposed mechanistic model linking the uPAR–integrin β3–fibronectin (FN1) axis with podocyte adhesion instability, detachment, and loss. Immunologic or mechanical podocyte injury induces upregulation of the uPAR–integrin αvβ3 axis and extracellular matrix remodeling (FN1), which cascades to activate focal adhesion and cytoskeletal signaling pathways. Concurrently, these molecular alterations trigger actin cytoskeletal reorganization and a shift toward a hypermotile cellular phenotype, thereby destabilizing podocyte anchorage to the glomerular basement membrane (GBM). Consequently, under pathologically altered glomerular hemodynamics, such as hyperfiltration-mediated FSS, these compromised podocytes undergo progressive cell detachment and permanent depletion, ultimately accelerating irreversible glomerulosclerosis and kidney function decline. This translationally focused model provides a comprehensive pathophysiological framework that illustrates how mechanical fluid forces orchestrate cellular structural instability, thereby driving podocyte attrition in proteinuric kidney diseases. uPAR, urokinase-type plasminogen activation receptor.
