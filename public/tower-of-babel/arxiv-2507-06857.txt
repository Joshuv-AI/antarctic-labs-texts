Nonparametric Bayesian Inference for Stochastic Reaction-Diffusion Equations
Randolf Altmeyer & Sascha Gaudlitz

Abstract
We consider the Bayesian nonparametric estimation of a nonlinear reaction function in a reaction-diffusion stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE). The likelihood is well-defined and tractable by the infinite-dimensional Girsanov theorem, and the posterior distribution is analysed in the growing domain asymptotic. Based on a Gaussian wavelet prior, the contraction of the posterior distribution around the truth at the minimax optimal rate is proved. The analysis of the posterior distribution is complemented by a semiparametric Bernstein–von Mises theorem. The proofs rely on the sub-Gaussian concentration of spatio-temporal averages of transformations of the SPDE, which is derived by combining the Clark-Ocone formula with bounds for the derivatives of the (marginal) densities of the SPDE.

1
Introduction

For a time horizon
T>0
and a bounded spatial domain
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
consider the semi-linear SPDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}(y)=\Updelta X_{t}(y)\,\mathrm{d}t+f(X_{t}(y))\,\mathrm{d}t+\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}(y),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,y\in\Lambda,

(1.1)

with a continuous and deterministic initial value
X_{0}
, a Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and a cylindrical Wiener process
W
defined on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
. We endow the SPDE with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, such that
\varDelta=\partial_{y}^{2}
is the Neumann-Laplacian. We are interested in the nonparametric Bayesian inference on the function
f
from a single observation of
(X_{t}(y)\colon(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\Lambda)
solving (
1.1
).

Semi-linear SPDEs of type (
1.1
) are known as
stochastic reaction-diffusion equations
. They are of fundamental importance in various fields, such as cell biology
(
Alonso et al. 2018a
;
Altmeyer et al. 2022a
)
, biodiversity
(
Grošelj et al. 2015a
)
, climate modelling
(
Debussche et al. 2013a
;
Franzke et al. 2015a
)
, phase segregation and interface modelling
(
Spohn 1993a
;
Funaki 1995a
;
Kharchenko et al. 2009a
;
Bertini et al. 2017a
)
, optics
(
Caraballo et al. 2006a
;
Kamel et al. 2024a
)
and spatial statistics
(
Roques et al. 2022a
)
. The Laplacian describes diffusion in a medium and the nonlinear
reaction function

f
captures local state-dependent reactions. For example, if
X_{t}(y)
models to heat diffusion at a time
t
and location
y
, then the function
f
corresponds to temperature-dependent reactions. The choice
f(x)=x(x-a)(x-b)
yields the Allen-Cahn equation in phase field modelling with stable points
a,b\in\mathbb{R}

(
Berglund 2022a
)
. The
“space-time white noise”

\mathrm{d}W_{t}(y)
represents neglected effects arising from the absence of detailed knowledge about the physical setting
(
Benzi et al. 1989a
)
.

Inference for semi-linear SPDEs is well-studied with respect to the linear part, in particular for the diffusivity in a stochastic heat equation
(
Huebner & Rozovskii 1995a
;
Kříž & Maslowski 2019a
;
Cialenco et al. 2020a
;
Chong 2020a
;
Altmeyer & Reiß 2021a
;
Hildebrandt & Trabs 2021a
;
Altmeyer et al. 2024a
)
. Much less is known about estimating the reaction function
f
. References for the classical noiseless inverse problem of inferring
f
from boundary or interior measurements of
\Lambda
(with
\mathrm{d}W_{t}(y)=0
) can be found in
(
Cannon & DuChateau 1998a
)
. In our measurement model, maximum likelihood estimation for a finite-dimensional reaction parameter is studied by
Ibragimov & Khas’minskii 1999a
;
Ibragimov & Khas’minskii 2000a
;
Ibragimov & Khas’minskii 2001a
;
Ibragimov 2003a
in the small noise limit, by
Gaudlitz & Reiß 2023a
for vanishing diffusivity and by
Goldys & Maslowski 2002a
as
T\to\infty
. Nonparametric procedures were considered by
Hildebrandt & Trabs 2023a
, who obtain
L^{2}
convergence rates for least-squares estimators in the large time limit, and by
Gaudlitz 2023a
using kernel-type estimators when diffusivity and noise level are coupled and both tend to zero. Bayesian posteriors were only studied when
f
does not depend on the state
X_{t}(y)
and the solution to (
1.1
) is Gaussian, see
(
Bishwal 2002a
;
Yan 2020a
)
.

In this paper, the log-likelihood function for the observation
(X_{t}(y)\colon(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\Lambda)
is obtained from an infinite-dimensional Girsanov theorem. It is a quadratic form in
f
such that a conjugate Gaussian process prior gives rise to a Gaussian posterior. This allows for efficient posterior sampling techniques, see Section
2.7
for numerical examples and the approximation of the posterior from discrete observations. The posterior mean provides a point estimator for the reaction function
f
, and coincides with the Bayesian maximum-a-posterior (MAP) estimator in our Gaussian setting. Uncertainty quantification with posterior credible sets is computationally feasible.

We provide theoretical guarantees for this approach relative to a data generating
“truth”

f_{0}
in the large domain limit: With the time horizon
T
fixed and
\Lambda=\lambda\bar{\Lambda}
for an open interval
\bar{\Lambda}\subset\mathbb{R}
, we assume that
\lambda\to\infty
. Large domain asymptotics are common in spatial statistics
(
Mardia & Marshall 1984a
;
Zhang & Zimmerman 2005a
;
Giordano et al. 2023a
;
Guan 2008a
)
, but our analysis of the posterior hinges on establishing that the solution to (
1.1
) is
spatially ergodic
with respect to spatial averages of
X_{t}(y)

(
Chen et al. 2021a
)
and on identifying the (spatially) invariant measure. In fact, one of our main results shows for a density
p_{f_{0}}
and a large class of functions functions
g
the convergence in probability of

\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\lambda\bar{\Lambda}}g(X_{t}(y))\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(z)p_{f_{0}}(z)\mathrm{d}z,\quad\lambda\to\infty.

(1.2)

Following the established test-based approach of Bayesian nonparametrics
(
Ghosal et al. 2000a
)
and by exploiting the spatial ergodicity, we obtain posterior contraction rates as
\lambda\to\infty
with
T
fixed. Consequently, we deduce frequentist minimax optimal convergence rates for the posterior mean in
L^{2}
-loss over Hölder balls. Using the methodology introduced by
Castillo & Nickl 2013a
(see also the recent extensions by
Nickl & Ray 2020a
;
Giordano & Ray 2025a
to diffusion processes), we subsequently show that the posterior satisfies a nonparametric Bernstein–von Mises theorem. It demonstrates that the rescaled posterior, centred at the posterior mean, corresponds asymptotically as
\lambda\to\infty
to the law of a Gaussian process (defined on suitable negative Sobolev spaces) with a covariance operator that is
“minimal”
in an information-theoretic sense. In particular, linear functionals of the posterior mean provide efficient estimators attaining the parametric rate for the corresponding linear functionals of the reaction function. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first nonparametric Bayesian contraction results and the first Bernstein–von Mises theorem for semi-linear SPDEs.

The test-based approach relies on relating what can be considered the
“natural distance”
in our statistical model, namely the path-dependent
Hellinger semi-metric

h_{\lambda}(f,g)^{2}\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\lambda\bar{\Lambda}}(f(X_{t}(y))-g(X_{t}(y)))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t,

to the
L^{2}
distance, which was first done for (temporal) semi-martingales by
van der Meulen et al. 2006a
. Since classical techniques such as local times, martingale approximations or spectral properties (cf.
Kutoyants 2013a
;
van der Meulen et al. 2006a
;
Nickl & Ray 2020a
;
Aeckerle-Willems & Strauch 2021a
;
Dalalyan & Reiß 2007a
;
Trottner et al. 2023a
;
Comte & Marie 2019a
) are not available for the process
y\mapsto X_{t}(y)
, we develop uniform sub-Gaussian concentration inequalities for the spatio-temporal averages in (
1.2
). Such results have recently been obtained in several works starting with
(
Chen et al. 2021a
)
, but these are suboptimal for our purposes as they depend on the Lipschitz constant of the function
g
in (
1.2
). Instead, building on ideas from
Gaudlitz 2023a
, we use tools from Malliavin Calculus to rewrite the spatial averages as martingales. Suitably integrating by parts and upper bounding the marginal densities of
X_{t}(y)
allows us to obtain sharp sub-Gaussian concentration inequalities in terms of the
L^{1}
-norm of
g
.

Let us briefly mention that our measurement model is equivalent to the small-diffusivity setting of
Gaudlitz & Reiß 2023a
;
Gaudlitz 2023a
. Using Lemma A.2 of
Gaudlitz 2023a
, the rescaled process
Y_{t}(y)\coloneq X_{t}(\lambda y)
,
y\in\bar{\Lambda}
,
0\leq t\leq T
solves

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}(y)=\nu\Updelta Y_{t}(y)\,\mathrm{d}t+f(Y_{t}(y))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}(y),

(1.3)

with known diffusivity
\nu\coloneq\lambda^{-2}
and noise level
\sigma\coloneq\lambda^{-1/2}
. It follows that the Bayesian approach applies equivalently to
Y
and that all results derived for
X
as
\lambda\to\infty
transfer to
Y
as
\nu,\sigma\to 0
. The latter is physically relevant, cf.
(
Altmeyer et al. 2022a
)
. The specific coupling
\nu/\sigma^{4}\asymp 1
ensures that the spatially ergodic averages in (
1.2
) do not degenerate as
\nu,\sigma\to 0
. This is closely related to the well-known scaling assumption in the Fluctuation-Dissipation theorem
(
Pavliotis 2014a
)
on the drift and volatility coefficients of a finite-dimensional diffusion process, which guarantees the existence of a temporal invariant measure. For extensions of the SPDE model (
1.1
) to multivariate domains, linear operators different from the Laplacian and coloured noise, as well as further comparisons to other asymptotic regimes see Section
2.8
.

The paper is structured as follows. We introduce the basic notations and the statistical model in Sections
2.1
-
2.3
. Spatial ergodicity of
X
is explained in Section
2.4
. In Sections
2.5
and
2.6
, we state the posterior contraction and the Bernstein–von Mises Theorem for the posterior. Section
2
is concluded by a numerical illustration in Section
2.7
and a discussion in Section
2.8
. Section
3
contains the proof of the ergodic properties stated in Section
2.4
, and the proofs of the posterior contraction and the Bernstein–von Mises Theorem are carried out in Section
4
. The remaining technical proofs are included in the Appendices
A
and
B
.

2.1
Basic notation

For a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
and a function
U\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the supremum and Lipschitz norms

\displaystyle\lVert U\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}\coloneq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\lvert U(x)\rvert,\quad\lVert U\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip(\mathcal{X})}}\coloneq\lVert U\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}+\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{X},x\neq y}\frac{\lvert U(x)-U(y)\rvert}{d(x,y)},

and let
B(a,d,L)
denote the ball in
(\mathcal{X},d)
with centre
a\in\mathcal{X}
and radius
L>0
. We denote
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}\coloneq\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip(\mathbb{R})}}
. For an open or closed set
O\subset\mathbb{R}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
0<p\leq\infty
, we denote by
L^{p}(O)
and
H^{s}(O)
the usual Lebesgue and
L^{2}
-Sobolev spaces equipped with the norms
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{L^{p}(O)}
and
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{H^{s}(O)}
. For
s\geq 0
and open
O\subset\mathbb{R}
let
C^{s}(O)
be the Hölder spaces with

\lVert f\rVert_{C^{s}(O)}\coloneq\max_{k=0,\dots,\lfloor s\rfloor}\lVert f^{(k)}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(O)}+\sup_{x,y\in O,x\neq y}\frac{\lvert f^{(\lfloor s\rfloor)}(x)-f^{(\lfloor s\rfloor)}(x)\rvert}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{s-\lfloor s\rfloor}}<\infty,

where
f^{(k)}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, denotes the
k
-th derivative of
f
. If
O
is closed, then the derivatives are taken one-sided. Note that functions belonging to
C^{s}(O)
have bounded derivatives up to order
\lfloor s\rfloor
, even if
O
is unbounded. We write
C(O)=C^{0}(O)
. For brevity, we set

\mathcal{F}_{s,K}\coloneq B(0,\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{C^{s}(\mathbb{R})},L),\quad s\geq 0.

We write
a\lesssim b
(or
b\gtrsim a
) if there exists a constant
0<C<\infty
depending only on nonasymptotic quantities such that
a\leq Cb
and
a\sim b
if
a\lesssim b
and
a\gtrsim b
.
We highlight the dependency of the constant
C
on a particular parameter
\xi
by
a\lesssim_{\xi}b
.
We write
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{P}}
for convergence in probability and
\xrightarrow{d}
for convergence in distribution of random variables.
For equality in distribution under a law
\mathrm{Q}
we write
\overset{\mathrm{Q}}{=}
.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
let
a\wedge b\coloneqq\min(a,b)
and
a\vee b\coloneqq\max(a,b)
.
For a set
M\subset\mathbb{R}
and a point
a\in\mathbb{R}
we define their distance as
\operatorname{dist}(a,M)\coloneqq\inf\{\lvert a-m\rvert\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}m\in M\}
.
The Lebesgue-measure of a Borel-set
M\subset\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
\lvert M\rvert
and its indicator function by
\mathbb{1}_{M}
.

2.2
SPDE and likelihood

Consider the semi-linear SPDE (
1.1
) with a Lipschitz-continuous reaction function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a continuous and deterministic initial condition
X_{0}
. Suppose that
\Lambda
has length
\lambda=\lvert\Lambda\rvert\geq 1
, such that
\Lambda=\lambda\bar{\Lambda}
for an open interval
\bar{\Lambda}\subset\mathbb{R}
of unit length. The time horizon
T>0
is fixed throughout, and we sometimes write
F(z)\coloneq f\circ z
for
z\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
.

The classical mild solution concept of
Da & Zabczyk 2014a
shows that (
1.1
) has a unique strong solution
X
in the probability sense taking values in
C([0,T],L^{2}(\Lambda))
, which is also a mild solution in the analytical sense, see Theorem 7.5 of
Da & Zabczyk 2014a
. Equivalently, as discussed by
Dalang & Quer-Sardanyons 2011a
, the mild solution can be obtained pointwise at
0\leq t\leq T
and
y\in\Lambda
as the solution to the integral equation

X_{t}(y)=\int_{\Lambda}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)X_{0}(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Lambda}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)\mathcal{W}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}s)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Lambda}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)f(X_{s}(\eta))\,\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}s.

(2.1)

Here,
G^{\lambda}
denotes the Neumann heat kernel on
\Lambda
, and
\mathcal{W}
(formally understood as
\mathrm{d}W_{t}/\mathrm{d}t
) is an isonormal Gaussian process on
L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(\Lambda))
.

A process satisfying the SPDE (
1.1
) in the sense of (
2.1
) is called
random field solution

(
Walsh 1986a
)
. The representation (
2.1
) is convenient for our purposes as we frequently rely on pointwise properties of
X_{t}(y)
. Moreover, the random field solution provides a useful framework for studying the marginal (Lebesgue-) density
p_{f,\lambda}(t,y,\>\cdot\>)
of
X_{t}(y)
, which exists for every
0<t\leq T
and
y\in\Lambda
according to
(
Bally & Pardoux 1998a
)
. We summarise the key probabilistic properties of the random field solution in Subsection
B.1
.

Let us write
X^{\lambda}\coloneq(X_{t}(y)\,|\,(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\Lambda)
and denote by
\mathrm{P}_{f}
the law of
X^{\lambda}
on the path space
C([0,T],L^{2}(\Lambda))
. In particular,
\mathrm{P}_{0}
is the law of the solution to (
1.1
) with
f\equiv 0
. Our goal is to estimate the reaction function
f
from a single realisation of
X^{\lambda}\sim\mathrm{P}_{f}
. As
f
is Lipschitz-continuous, the Girsanov theorem (e.g. Theorem 10.18 of
Da & Zabczyk 2014a
) implies that the laws
\mathrm{P}_{f}
and
\mathrm{P}_{0}
(with the same initial condition) are equivalent on
C([0,T],L^{2}(\Lambda))
. Their Radon-Nikodym derivative is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{f}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{0}}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\langle F(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}X_{t}-\Updelta X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert F(X_{t})\rVert_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right),

where we
define

\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle F(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}X_{t}-\Updelta X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}
as the
L^{2}(\mathrm{P}_{0})
-limit of

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle F(X_{t}),e_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\left(\left\langle e_{k},\mathrm{d}X_{t}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}-\left\langle\Updelta e_{k},X_{t}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)

(2.2)

for an orthonormal basis
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
L^{2}(\Lambda)
contained in the domain of the Neumann Laplacian. In particular, for two Lipschitz-continuous functions
f,f_{0}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the log-likelihood function relative to
\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}
is equal to

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{f}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneq\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{f}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}}\right)=\sqrt{\lambda}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}-\frac{\lambda}{2}h_{\lambda}(f,f_{0})^{2},

(2.3)

with
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}\coloneq\lambda^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{T}\langle F(X_{s})-F_{0}(X_{s}),\mathrm{d}W_{s}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}
and with the squared
Hellinger semi-metric
defined as

h_{\lambda}(f,f_{0})^{2}\coloneq\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}(f(X_{t}(y))-f_{0}(X_{t}(y)))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t.

(2.4)

Since the Hellinger semi-metric can be understood as the quadratic variation of the (temporal) martingale
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
, it characterises the log-likelihood and should be viewed as the natural distance between
f
and
f_{0}
.

The analysis of the posterior distribution defined in the next section relies on finding upper and lower bounds on the expected Hellinger semi-metric
#1[h_{\lambda}(f,f_{0})^{2}]
in terms of the standard (squared)
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-metric. As
X_{t}(y)
is supported on all of
\mathbb{R}
for all
0<t\leq T
, cf. the Gaussian-type density lower bound from Proposition
B.3
, we consider reaction functions supported on a known compact set. Moreover, we restrict to initial conditions satisfying a scaling property.

Assumption 2.1
.

For a fixed compact interval
\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}
the parameter set is

\Theta\coloneq\{f\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R})\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\operatorname{supp}(f)\subset\Xi\}.

There exists a function
\chi\in H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})\supset C(\bar{\Lambda})
for some
\tilde{s}>3/2
such that the initial condition satisfies
X_{0}(y)=\chi(y/\lvert\Lambda\rvert)
for all
y\in\Lambda
.

Restricting to compactly supported regression functions is common when considering random (unbounded) design as in our case
(
van der Meulen et al. 2006a
;
Comte et al. 2007a
;
van der Vaart & van Zanten 2008a
)
. As the Hellinger semi-metric depends on
f
and
f_{0}
only through the difference
f-f_{0}
, we could remove the support assumption if
f_{0}
was known outside of
\Xi
. Note that any
f\in\Theta
is Lipschitz, such that the random field solution (
2.1
) always exists. The additional regularity of
f
is required to control the marginal densities of
X_{t}(y)
, see Proposition
3.1
below. The scaling assumption on the initial conditions ensures the well-behavedness of
X
in the large domain asymptotic
\lambda\to\infty
.

2.3
A Bayesian posterior for the reaction function

We consider a Bayesian approach for inference on the unknown reaction function
f
belonging to the parameter set
\Theta
. We begin by defining a prior distribution on
\Theta
based on a finite-dimensional truncated Gaussian series expansion on
L^{2}(\Xi)
. Such priors are a common choice in Bayesian nonparametrics
(
van der Vaart & van Zanten 2008a
)
and they are conjugate in our setting, see below. For details on Gaussian processes and the prior measures constructed from them see, e.g., Section 11.2 of
Ghosal & van der Vaart 2017a
.

We describe the series expansion in terms of an orthonormal wavelet basis
(\psi_{\mu})_{\mu}
of
L^{2}(\Xi)
, where following the notational convention from
Cohen 2003a
;
Gobet et al. 2004a
each
\mu=(j,k)
is a multiindex and
\lvert(j,k)\rvert\coloneq j
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
k=0,\dots,\max(0,2^{j}-1)
. We assume throughout that the
\psi_{\mu}
are
S
-regular,
S\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, boundary-corrected Daubechies wavelets, see Section 3.9 of
Cohen 2003a
or Section 4.3.5 of
Giné & Nickl 2015a
for their construction. In particular, the
\psi_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are
S
-times continuously differentiable and supported in
\Xi
. Taking
S\geq s\geq 0
large enough, recall (e.g. from Equation (4.139) of
Giné & Nickl 2015a
) the wavelet characterisation of the Sobolev spaces on
\Xi
given by

H^{s}(\Xi)=\bigg\{f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\,\Big|\,f|_{\Xi^{c}}=0,\,\lVert f\rVert_{H^{s}(\Xi)}^{2}=\sum_{\mu}2^{2s\lvert\mu\rvert}\langle f,\psi_{\mu}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}^{2}<\infty\bigg\}.

(2.5)

In the following, we always assume that
S\geq\beta
is large enough depending on the known regularity of a data generating
“true”
reaction function
f_{0}\in C^{\beta}(\Xi)
. For a maximal frequency
M\in\mathbb{N}
, which may depend on
\lambda
, and some
\beta_{0}\geq 0
we take as prior
\Pi_{\lambda}=\mathcal{L}(f)
the law of the random function

f=\sum_{\lvert\mu\rvert\leq M}2^{-\beta_{0}\lvert\mu\rvert}Z_{\mu}\psi_{\mu},\quad Z_{\mu}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(0,1).

(2.6)

This induces a centred Gaussian probability measure on
C^{S}(\mathbb{R})
supported on the finite-dimensional approximation spaces

\displaystyle V_{M}

\displaystyle\coloneq\operatorname{span}(\psi_{\mu}\,\colon\,\lvert\mu\rvert\leq M)\subset\Theta.

Since the
\psi_{\mu}
are compactly supported,
V_{M}
contains for each
j
only finitely many
\psi_{\mu}
with
\mu=(j,k)
and by standard wavelet theory
V_{M}
has dimension
\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})\lesssim 2^{M}
.

Denoting by
\ell_{\lambda}(f)\coloneq\log(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{f}/\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}})
the log-likelihood function of (
2.3
), the posterior distribution arises from Bayes’ theorem and takes the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\lambda}(f\,|\,X^{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\ell_{\lambda}(f)}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\lambda}(f)}{\int_{V_{M}}{e^{\ell_{\lambda}(\bar{f})}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\lambda}(\bar{f})}},\quad f\in C^{S}(\mathbb{R}).

(2.7)

Practical implementation of the posterior (
2.7
) is straightforward as the Gaussian prior (
2.6
) is conjugate noting the quadratic form of log-likelihood (
2.2
). To see this, let us write a candidate reaction function
f\in V_{M}
relative to the first
\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})
wavelet basis functions as
f=\mathbf{f}^{\intercal}\mathbf{\Psi}
with coefficients
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})}
and with a vector of basis functions
\mathbf{\Psi}=(\psi_{\mu}\,\colon\,\lvert\mu\rvert\leq M)^{\intercal}
. Setting

\mathbf{G}\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\mathbf{\Psi}(X_{t}(y))\mathbf{\Psi}(X_{t}(y))^{\intercal}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t,\quad\mathbf{a}\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathbf{\Psi}(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}X_{t}-\Updelta X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}

the log-likelihood function equals
\ell_{\lambda}(f)=\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\mathbf{f}-(1/2)\mathbf{f}^{\intercal}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{f}
. Denote furthermore by
\mathbf{\Sigma}
the diagonal covariance matrix of the Gaussian prior
\Pi_{\lambda}
with entries
2^{-2\beta_{0}|\mu|}
,
|\mu|\leq M
, on the diagonal according to (
2.6
). Setting
\mathbf{Q}\coloneq(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{\Sigma}^{{{-1}}})^{{{-1}}}
,
\bm{\mu}\coloneq\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{a}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\log\left(\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\lambda}(f\,|\,X^{\lambda})\right)

\displaystyle\sim\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\mathbf{f}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{f}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1})\mathbf{f}\sim-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{f}-\bm{\mu})^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}^{{{-1}}}(\mathbf{f}-\bm{\mu}),

such that the posterior distribution of the parameter
\mathbf{f}
is multivariate normal with mean
\bm{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{Q}
. This allows for direct sampling methods instead of computationally more demanding MCMC algorithms, see Section
2.7
for an illustration. Even though there is no analytical formula for higher order Daubechies wavelets, they can be efficiently approximated using the cascade algorithm (Section 6.5 of
Daubechies 1992a
). Gaussian conjugacy holds similarly for posteriors of continuously observed (finite-dimensional) diffusion processes with a Gaussian prior on the drift vector, cf.
(
Nickl & Ray 2020a
;
Giordano & Ray 2022a
)
.

2.4
Limit process and spatial ergodicity

Our first two main results study the statistical properties of the model in the spatially ergodic setting in the large domain limit
|\Lambda|=\lambda\to\infty
. For
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
define the process
(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}})_{t\in[0,T]}
as the random field solution to the SPDE

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)=\Updelta Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)\,\mathrm{d}t+f(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y))\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{t}(y),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,y\in\mathbb{R},

(2.8)

on
\mathbb{R}
with spatially constant initial condition
Z_{0}^{\bar{y}}(y)=\chi(\bar{y})
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
. By Lemma 7.1 of
Chen et al. 2021a
the process
y\mapsto Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)
is stationary for every
t\geq 0
and
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
in the sense that
Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)\overset{d}{=}Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0)
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

We begin by showing that the process
(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}})_{t\in[0,T]}
is the natural pointwise limit of the rescaled process
X_{t}(\lambda\bar{y})
, and that ergodic spatial averages of
X
converge as
\lambda\to\infty
. While the Neumann boundary conditions disappear in the large domain limit,
Z^{\bar{y}}
still depends on
\bar{y}
due to the initial condition
X_{0}(\lambda\bar{y})=\chi(\bar{y})
being constant and independent of
\lambda
. In particular, when
X_{0}\equiv 0
,
Z^{\bar{y}}
does not depend on the specific point
\bar{y}
.

For the case
f\equiv 0
and
X_{0}\equiv 0
, the convergence of
X
to the global solution
Z
has been obtained by
Altmeyer & Reiß 2021a
;
Pasemann & Reiß 2025a
using the convergence of the associated heat semi-groups. Since we are interested in pointwise convergence for both
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
and
0\leq t\leq T
held fixed, our proof is based on comparing the heat kernels on
\Lambda
with Neumann boundary conditions and on
\mathbb{R}
, respectively. The statement for general
f
and
X_{0}
follows using a Gronwall-type expansion and by tracing the impact of the Neumann heat semi-group for a short time
\lambda^{-2}t\to 0
as
\lambda\to\infty
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Grant Assumption
2.1
and let
f_{0}\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R})
.

(a)

For every
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
and
0\leq t\leq T
we have
X_{t}(\lambda\bar{y})\xrightarrow{d}Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0)
,
\lambda\to\infty
.

(b)

For any
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
the spatial ergodicity property holds, i.e.

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\lambda\bar{\Lambda}}g(X_{t}(y))\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}#1[g(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0))]\,\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\mathrm{d}t\xrightarrow{\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}}0,\quad\lambda\to\infty.

Proof.

Part (
a
) follows from Proposition
B.6
(below). Part (
b
) follows from Theorem
2.3
(below).
∎

While it is generally not true that the pointwise convergence in part (
a
) of Theorem
2.2
implies convergence of the respective densities, this is true here. Indeed, the convergence of the densities can be deduced from the converse Scheffé Theorem (Theorem 1 of
Sweeting 1986a
) by combining part (
a
) of the theorem with the bound on the first derivative of the marginal densities of
X_{t}(y)
from Proposition
3.1
(below).

The following sub-Gaussian concentration inequality makes the convergence in part (
b
) of Theorem
2.2
quantitative. This is the key technical tool for controlling the Hellinger semi-metric (
2.4
) in the proof of Theorem
2.4
. The proof of the concentration inequality is nonstandard since the process
y\mapsto X_{t}(y)
is neither Gaussian nor are techniques for semi-martingales directly applicable. The main steps of our proof rely on Malliavin Calculus. They are reviewed in Section
3
.

Theorem 2.3

(Subgaussian concentration of
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)
)
.

Grant Assumption
2.1
, let
f_{0}\in\Theta
and
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. There exists a constant
0<C<\infty
, depending only on
\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{C^{3}(\mathbb{R})}
,
T
and
\lVert\chi\rVert_{H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})}
, such that the following hold:

(a)

We have the sub-Gaussian concentration

#1(\lvert\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)-#1[\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)]\rvert\geq\lambda^{-1/2}x)\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{x^{2}}{2C\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}}\bigg),\quad x\geq 0.

(2.9)

(b)

We can bound the expectation

\lvert#1[\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)]\rvert\leq\lambda^{-1}C\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}.

Proof.

The proof of the sub-Gaussian concentration in (
a
) is carried out in Section
3
. Part (
b
) follows from Corollary
B.8
taking
k=1
.
∎

2.5
Posterior contraction

We proceed with a contraction result for the posterior distribution (
2.7
) using the Gaussian process prior (
2.6
) for
\beta_{0}\geq 0
in the large domain limit
\lambda\to\infty
. We rely on the test-based approach of
Ghosal et al. 2000a
and the concentration results to first establish posterior contraction in the expected random Hellinger semi-metric (
2.4
), which is equivalent to the
L^{2}(\Xi)
metric. Borrowing ideas from
Giordano & Nickl 2020a
, we then show that the posterior concentrates on sets of bounded higher-order smoothness if
\beta_{0}>3/2
, cf. Equation (
2.11
) (below). Exploiting such additional regularisation of the posterior has recently been instrumental for dealing with different Bayesian nonlinear inverse problems, see
(
Nickl 2023a
)
for an overview. In our case, this allows us to use Theorem
2.3
(
b
) to relate the expected Hellinger semi-metric to the norm induced by the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{0}\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}#1[f(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0))g(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0))]\,\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(z)g(z)p_{f_{0},\bar{y}}(t,z)\,\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\mathrm{d}t,

where
p_{f_{0},\bar{y}}(t,\>\cdot\>)
for
t>0
and
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
denotes the density of
Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0)
. We note that by the density bounds from Corollary D.2 of
Gaudlitz 2023a
, there exist constants
0<c,C<\infty
depending on
\Xi
,
\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}
and
T
satisfying the norm equivalence

c\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{0}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)},\quad f\in\Theta.

(2.10)

Theorem 2.4

(Posterior contraction)
.

Grant Assumption
2.1
and suppose
f_{0}\in C^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})\cap\Theta
for
\beta\geq 3
. Consider the Gaussian prior
\Pi_{\lambda}
from (
2.6
) with
0\leq\beta_{0}<\beta
and cut-off
M=M_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2^{M}\sim\lambda^{1/(2\beta+1)}
.

(a)

Then for every
K_{\lambda}\to\infty

\Pi_{\lambda}(f\in V_{M}\colon\lVert f-f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}\geq K_{\lambda}\lambda^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)}\log(\lambda)\,|\,X^{\lambda})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}}0,\quad\lambda\to\infty.

(b)

If
3/2<\beta_{0}\leq\beta
we have for any large enough
L>0

\Pi_{\lambda}(f\in V_{M}\,\colon\,\lVert f\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\geq L\,|\,X^{\lambda})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}}0,\quad\lambda\to\infty,

(2.11)

and for every
K_{\lambda}\to\infty

\Pi_{\lambda}(f\in V_{M}\colon\lVert f-f_{0}\rVert_{0}\geq K_{\lambda}\lambda^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)}\log(\lambda)\,|\,X^{\lambda})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}}0,\quad\lambda\to\infty.

Proof.

See Section
4
.
∎

The minimal smoothness assumption
f_{0}\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R})
is required to bound the derivative of the density
p_{f_{0},\lambda}(t,y,\>\cdot\>)
of
X_{t}(y)
in Proposition
3.1
, which plays a crucial role for the sub-Gaussian concentration of the Hellinger semi-metric
h_{\lambda}
around the
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{0}
-norm using Theorem
2.3
. See Remark
3.2
for a discussion on the necessity of this regularity assumption.
Moreover, the restriction of
f_{0}\in\Theta
to have compact support is not required for the spatial ergodicity results in Section
2.4
, but it is necessary for the posterior contraction in Theorem
2.4
to ensure that the expected (squared) Hellinger semi-metric
#1[h_{\lambda}(f,f_{0})^{2}]
and
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{0}^{2}
are equivalent to the (squared)

-metric.

Contraction of the posterior immediately yields a frequentist convergence rate for the posterior mean
\hat{f}=#1[f\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}X^{\lambda}]
. Since the posterior is Gaussian, the posterior mean equals the maximum-a-posteriori estimator

\displaystyle\hat{f}=\operatorname{argmax}_{f\in V_{M}}\mathrm{d}\Pi(f\,|\,X^{\lambda})=\operatorname{argmax}_{f\in V_{M}}\left(\ell_{\lambda}(f)-\frac{1}{2}\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{H}^{\lambda}}\right),

where
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{\lambda}}
is the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) norm with respect to (
2.6
). Lemma 11.43 of
Ghosal & van der Vaart 2017a
shows that the RKHS is given by
(V_{M},\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{\lambda}})
and

\lVert h\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{\lambda}}\coloneq\lVert h\rVert_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\Xi)},\quad h\in V_{M}.

(2.12)

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\hat{f}=#1[f\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}X^{\lambda}]
be the posterior mean. Under the conditions of Theorem
2.4
we have for every
K_{\lambda}\to\infty

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{f}-f_{0}\rVert_{0}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}}\left(K_{\lambda}\lambda^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)}\log(\lambda)\right),\quad\lambda\to\infty.

Proof.

The posterior is a Gaussian measure on
V_{M}
with centring
\hat{f}\in V_{M}
. By Anderson’s Lemma (Theorem 2.4.5 of
Giné & Nickl 2015a
), this means that the function
Q(g)\coloneq\Pi_{\lambda}(C+g\,|\,X^{\lambda})
,
g\in V_{M}
, with
C\coloneq\left\{f\in V_{M}\,\colon\,\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}\leq K_{\lambda}\lambda^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)}\log(\lambda)\right\}
is maximal at

. Theorem 2.5 of
Ghosal et al. 2000a
and Theorem
2.4
therefore imply the rate of convergence
\lVert\hat{f}-f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}}(K_{\lambda}\lambda^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)})\log(\lambda)
. This transfers to the
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{0}
-norm by (
2.10
).
∎

The rate of convergence
\lambda^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)}\log(\lambda)
is minimax-optimal over
\beta
-regular Hölder balls up to a log-factor.

.

, let

and

. Then

where
\inf_{T_{\lambda}}
denotes the infimum over all estimators

for

based on

solving the semi-linear SPDE (

) with domain size

, and where the constant

depends on

.

Proof.

See Section
4
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The rate of contraction in Theorem
2.4
hinges on the optimal choice of the cut-off
M
. Using standard arguments (e.g., Section 2.2.3 of
Giordano & Nickl 2020a
), posterior contraction at the same rate extends to adaptive priors, where
M
is randomised independent of the unknown smoothness
\beta
. In this case, however, the conjugacy for the prior is lost and sampling needs to be done using computationally more demanding MCMC algorithms. Moreover, similar to Section 2.2.2 of
Giordano & Nickl 2020a
it can be shown by modifying the proof of Lemma

(below) that the posterior contracts at the minimax-optimal rate for a rescaled Gaussian process prior

, where
\bar{f}=\lambda^{-1/(4\beta+2)}f
with

from (
2.6
) and with
\beta_{0}=\beta
. In the case of rescaled Gaussian priors, however, achieving adaptation is an open problem.

2.6
A nonparametric Bernstein–von Mises theorem

In classical parametric statistical models the Bernstein–von Mises theorem (e.g.,
van der Vaart 1998a
) states that in the large data limit the posterior distribution approximately equals a normal distribution centred at an efficient estimator (usually the posterior mean) with the variance attaining the Cramér-Rao bound in the underlying statistical model. Several works such as
(
Cox 1993a
;
Freedman 1999a
)
have shown that such a theorem cannot hold in nonparametric statistical models.

Following the semiparametric ideas introduced by
Castillo & Nickl 2013a
;
Castillo & Nickl 2014a
, which were recently extended to the finite-dimensional diffusion setting by
Nickl & Ray 2020a
;
Giordano & Ray 2025a
, we show that a Bernstein–von Mises-type theorem for the posterior, centred at the posterior mean, holds in function spaces with norms weaker than
L^{2}
. Here, we consider the negative Sobolev spaces
(\mathcal{X},d)=(H^{-\rho}(\Xi),\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{H^{-\rho}(\Xi)})
for
\rho>3/2
to ensure that Lemma
2.8
can be applied, cf. (
A.7
) (below). Since weak convergence in
H^{-\rho}(\Xi)
corresponds by duality to uniform weak convergence of linear functionals, it suffices to analyse the posterior by the action
\gamma\mapsto\langle f-\hat{f},\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}
,
\gamma\in H^{\rho}(\Xi)
.

Weak convergence of probability measures on
(\mathcal{X},d)
is metrised by the bounded Lipschitz metric, see Chapter 18 of
Dudley 2002a
. For two probability measures
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
on
(\mathcal{X},d)
it is defined by

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathcal{X}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\sup_{F\colon\lVert F\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip(\mathcal{X})}}\leq 1}\mathopen{\left\lvert\vphantom{\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau-\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau-\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau-\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}}\right\rvert}.

The limiting distribution of the posterior will be given below by a generalised Wiener process
\mathbb{W}=(\mathbb{W}(\gamma):\gamma\in H^{\rho}(\Xi))
on
H^{\rho}(\Xi)
with covariances

\displaystyle#1[\mathbb{W}(\gamma)\mathbb{W}(\bar{\gamma})]=\int_{\Xi}\gamma(z)\bar{\gamma}(z)/p_{f_{0}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,\quad f_{0}\in\Theta.

(2.13)

Here, we set
p_{f_{0}}(z)\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}p_{f_{0},\bar{y}}(t,z)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\mathrm{d}t
such that

\lVert h\rVert^{2}_{0}=\lVert hp_{f_{0}}^{1/2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{2}(z)p_{f_{0},\bar{y}}(t,z)\,\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\mathrm{d}t.

The numerical example in Section
2.7
illustrates the role of the density
p_{f_{0}}
appearing in the limiting covariance. Note that for
\rho>3/2
, the Wiener process
\mathbb{W}
indeed yields a tight Gaussian measure on
H^{-\rho}(\Xi)
, see Theorem 3.1 of
Nickl & Ray 2020a
for details.

At the heart of the analysis in this section is the following LAN (
“local asymptotic normality”
) expansion with the associated LAN-norm
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{0}
. Consequently, well-known arguments from semiparametric statistics such as in Chapter of
van der Vaart 1998a
imply that the asymptotic variances appearing in Theorem
2.9
and Corollary
2.10
below are optimal in an information-theoretic sense.

Lemma 2.8

(LAN expansion)
.

Grant Assumption
2.1
and let
h\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz-continuous. Set
W_{\lambda}(h)\coloneq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle h(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}W_{t}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}
,
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(h)\coloneq\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert h(X_{t})\rVert_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t
. Then we have the likelihood expansion

\ell_{\lambda}\left(f_{0}+\frac{h}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)-\ell_{\lambda}(f_{0})=W_{\lambda}(h)-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(h),\quad f_{0}\in\Theta

(2.14)

along with the convergences
W_{\lambda}(h)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\lVert h\rVert_{0}^{2})
,
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(h)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}}\lVert h\rVert_{0}^{2}
as
\lambda\to\infty
.

Proof.

The likelihood expansion (
2.14
) follows from (
2.3
). By Theorem
2.3
with
f=f_{0}
and
g=h^{2}
, we find
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}/\lVert h\rVert_{0}^{2}\to 1
in
\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}
-probability as
\lambda\to\infty
. The convergence in distribution of the (time-) martingale
W_{\lambda}
follows by noting that its quadratic variation is given by
\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}
. Since the latter converges to
\lVert h\rVert_{0}^{2}
in
\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}
-probability, we conclude by a standard martingale central limit theorem (Theorem 5.5.4 of
Liptser & Shiryayev 1989a
).
∎

Besides weak convergence on
H^{-\rho}(\Xi)
, we also have convergence in total variation distance

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\sup_{F\colon\lVert F\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}\leq 1}\mathopen{\left\lvert\vphantom{\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau-\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau-\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau-\int_{\mathcal{X}}F\,\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}}\right\rvert}

(2.15)

for the posterior distribution acting on a single linear functional.

Theorem 2.9

(Semiparametric Bernstein–von Mises)
.

Grant Assumption
2.1
and suppose
f_{0}\in C^{\beta}(\mathbb{R})\cap\Theta
for

. Let

and let

See Section

.
∎

Example 2.11

(Test for polynomial degree)
.

Of particular interest in applications are stochastic reaction-diffusion equations with polynomial reaction functions
(
Cristofol et al. 2012a
)
where the steady states correspond to the roots of the polynomials. Such models are compatible with Assumption
2.1
if we suppose that
f_{0}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{q}a_{k}x^{k}
for
x\in\bar{\Xi}
,
a_{1},\dots,a_{q}\in\mathbb{R}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
\bar{\Xi}\subset\Xi
is a compact interval, and that
f_{0}
extends outside of
\bar{\Xi}
smoothly to zero with support in
\Xi
.

Suppose we want to derive a statistical test for the null hypothesis that
f_{0}
is a polynomial of degree at most
q^{\ast}\geq 0
on
\bar{\Xi}
. Let
\gamma
be a sufficiently smooth function with compact support in
\bar{\Xi}
that is orthogonal to all polynomials up to degree
q^{\ast}
such that
\langle(\>\cdot\>)^{\bar{q}},\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}=0
for all
0\leq\bar{q}\leq q^{\ast}
. Such functions are constructed by
Tsybakov 2009a
, or we may take suitable
“mother”
wavelets, cf. Proposition 4.2.7 of
Giné & Nickl 2015a
. Under the null hypothesis the inner product
\langle f_{0},\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}
vanishes, such that by Corollary
2.10
(
a
) the test statistic
\tau\coloneq\sqrt{\lambda}\langle\hat{f},\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}
is for large
\lambda
approximately normally distributed with variance
\lVert\gamma/p_{f_{0}}\rVert^{2}_{0}
. If an upper bound on the variance is known (e.g. using the density bounds from Corollary
B.4
), we can derive approximate critical values for the test statistic
\tau
relative to the standard normal distribution.

Example 2.12

(Estimating polynomial coefficients)
.

Continuing Example
2.11
, suppose that
f_{0}
is a polynomial of degree
q^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}
on
\bar{\Xi}
. If
\gamma
is orthogonal to all polynomials of degree
q^{\ast}-1
and
\langle(\>\cdot\>)^{q^{\ast}},\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}\neq 0
, then
\langle f_{0},\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}=a_{q^{\ast}}\langle x^{q^{\ast}},\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}
and using Corollary
2.10
(
a
) we can estimate
a_{q^{\ast}}
by
\langle\hat{f},\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}/\langle(\>\cdot\>)^{q^{\ast}},\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}(\Xi)}
with the rate of convergence
\sqrt{\lambda}
. Using this approach, we can estimate all coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{q^{\ast}}
.

Example 2.13

(Estimating power functions)
.

Besides linear functionals, as discussed in Section 2.3.2 of
Castillo & Nickl 2013a
, the Bernstein–von Mises theorem
2.9
also yields asymptotic normality for smooth nonlinear functionals
\Psi(f_{0})
as long as
\Psi\colon L^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable in a functional sense near
f_{0}
with a sufficiently small remainder term. Skipping details, we can argue as
Giordano & Ray 2025a
in their Section 2.2 and show that this yields asymptotic normality for the plug-in estimator
\Psi(\hat{f})
for
\Psi(f_{0})
and functionals of the form
\Psi(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x
,
q\geq 2
. In particular, this allows one to estimate the
L^{2}
norm of
f_{0}
.

2.8
Discussion

2.8.1
The Bayesian approach for more general SPDEs

A natural extension to (
1.1
) is the semi-linear SPDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=AX_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+F(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+B\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(2.18)

defined on a bounded domain
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
A\colon\operatorname{dom}(A)\subset L^{2}(\Lambda)\to L^{2}(\Lambda)
generates a
C_{0}
-semi-group on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
,
B\colon L^{2}(\Lambda)\to L^{2}(\Lambda)
is linear bounded operator and
F\colon L^{2}(\Lambda)\to L^{2}(\Lambda)
is not necessarily of Nemytskii type as in (
1.1
). Typical examples for
A
include
A=\nu\Updelta
(diffusivity
\nu>0
),
A=-(-\Updelta)^{\rho}
(fractional Laplacian with
\rho\geq 1/2
) and
A=\partial_{y}(a(y)\partial_{y}\>\cdot\>)
(divergence form), while
B
can for example have the form
B=\sigma\mathbb{I}
(space-time white noise with intensity
\sigma
) or
B=-(-\Updelta)^{-\kappa}
with
\kappa>0
, the latter inducing higher spatial smoothness for the solution. Sufficient conditions for the wellposedness of (
2.18
) as a (unique) mild and weak solution on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
are provided in Theorems 7.2 and 7.5 of
Da & Zabczyk 2014a
and Proposition G.0.5 of
Liu & Röckner 2015a
. If

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}#1[\exp(\delta\lVert B^{{{-1}}}F(X_{t})\rVert_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}^{2})]<\infty

for some
\delta>0
, Theorem 10.18 of
Da & Zabczyk 2014a
shows that the log-likelihood with respect to the nonlinearity
F
is given by

\ell(F)=\int_{0}^{T}\langle B^{{{-1}}}F(X_{t}),B^{{{-1}}}[\mathrm{d}X_{t}-AX_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t]\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert B^{{{-1}}}F(X_{t})\rVert_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Analogously to (
2.2
), we define the first integral as the
L^{2}(\mathrm{P}_{0})
-limit

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle F(X_{t}),(B^{{{-1}}})^{\ast}e_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\left(\left\langle(B^{{{-1}}})^{\ast}e_{k},\mathrm{d}X_{t}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}-\left\langle A^{\ast}(B^{{{-1}}})^{\ast}e_{k},X_{t}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)

for an orthonormal basis
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
L^{2}(\Lambda)
such that
e_{k}\in\operatorname{dom}(A^{\ast}(B^{{{-1}}})^{\ast})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

This shows that the likelihood is well-defined and the Bayesian procedure is applicable under mild assumptions. The likelihood is quadratic in the operator
F
, so Gaussian conjugacy is preserved (assuming a suitable Gaussian prior). The computation of the likelihood, however, requires knowledge of the operators
A
and
B
. By adjusting the likelihood, the analysis presented in this paper transfers to the setting
A=\nu\Updelta
and
B=\sigma\mathbb{I}
for known
\nu,\sigma^{2}>0
.

2.8.2
Spatial ergodicity for more general SPDEs

The analysis of the posterior distribution in the general setting (
2.18
) is delicate. On the one hand, by following the proofs of Propositions
3.1
and Theorem
2.3
, the concentration results for the averages
\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\lambda\bar{\Lambda}}g(X_{t}(y))\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t
can be generalised beyond
A=\Updelta
and
B=\mathbb{I}
, provided that there exists some
0<\alpha<1
such that

\int_{0}^{t}\lVert BG_{t-s}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\sim t^{-\alpha},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

for all
\lambda\geq 1
where
G^{\lambda}
is the Green function of
A
on
\Lambda
. On the other hand, if
B^{{{-1}}}
is an unbounded operator on
L^{2}(\Lambda)
or
F
is not of Nemytskii-type, the Hellinger semi-metric
h_{\lambda}
no longer takes the form of an average, since

h_{\lambda}(F,G)^{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert B^{{{-1}}}(F(X_{t})-G(X_{t}))\rVert_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Whereas
B^{{{-1}}}
is in general a local operator and spatial ergodicity is expected, the analysis requires a precise understanding of the joint action of
B^{{{-1}}}
and any candidate functions
F,G
on
X_{t}
and lies beyond the scope of this work.

2.8.3
Small noise asymptotics

Suppose we observe
Y^{\sigma}=(Y_{t}(y)\,\colon\,(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\bar{\Lambda})
solving the SPDE (
1.3
) on a domain
\bar{\Lambda}\subset\mathbb{R}
with known diffusivity
\nu>0
and known noise level
\sigma>0
. Provided that
f
is Lipschitz-continuous, the log-likelihood reads

\ell(f)=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle F(Y_{t}),\mathrm{d}Y_{t}-\nu\Updelta Y_{t}\rangle_{L^{2}(\bar{\Lambda})}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert F(Y_{t})\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{\Lambda})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Analogously to Subsection
2.3
, Gaussian conjugacy holds, and the posterior distribution can be analysed in the small noise asymptotic
\sigma\to 0
with diffusivity
\nu>0
held constant. Using Theorem 3 of
Ibragimov 2003a
and the proof strategy of Proposition 5.2 of
Van 2005a
, it can be shown that the appropriate limiting metric is given by

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}\left(g(Y_{t}^{\operatorname{det}}(y))-\tilde{g}(Y_{t}^{\operatorname{det}}(y))\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t,\quad g,\tilde{g}\in\Theta,

where
Y^{\operatorname{det}}
satisfies the deterministic PDE (
1.3
) with
\sigma=0
and
f=f_{0}
. Posterior contraction in this metric can be shown along the lines of Subsection 3.1 of
van der Meulen et al. 2006a
.

3
The Malliavin calculus and the proof of Theorem
2.3

The Malliavin calculus was initiated by
Malliavin 1978a
to obtain a probabilistic proof of Hörmander’s Theorem and has since experienced significant extensions and new areas of application. The interested reader is referred to the monographs
Sanz-Solé 2005a
;
Nualart 2006a
;
Bogachev 2010a
;
Nualart & Nualart 2018a
;
Ikeda & Watanabe 1984a
;
Nourdin & Peccati 2012a
;
Malliavin & Thalmaier 2006a
;
Malliavin 1997a
and references therein. Its interpretation as an
“infinite-dimensional differential calculus on the Wiener space”

(
Nualart 2006a
)
makes the Malliavin calculus appealing in our setting, since we are aiming to control quantities, e.g.
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
, which are nonlinear transformations of the Gaussian process
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. If
X
is the solution to the semi-linear SPDE (
1.1
) and
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then the Malliavin derivative
(\tau,z)\mapsto\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}X_{t}(y)\in L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(\Lambda))
of
X_{t}(y)
for any time point
0<t\leq T
and location
y\in\Lambda
exists. It satisfies the linear PDE with random coefficients

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}X_{t}(y)=\partial_{y}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}X_{t}(y)+f^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}X_{t}(y),&0\leq\tau<t\leq T,\quad z\in\Lambda\\
\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}X_{\tau}(y)=\delta_{\tau,z}(y),&z\in\Lambda,\end{cases}

(3.1)

and
\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}X_{t}(y)=0
for
0<t<\tau\leq T
,
z,y\in\Lambda
. Assume that
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. We apply the Malliavin calculus at two instances in the proof of the sub-Gaussian concentration of Theorem
2.3
. The first instance is the well-known Clark-Ocone formula in the form given by Proposition 6.3 in
(
Chen et al. 2021a
)
, which implies

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}-#1\left[\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\right]=\mathcal{M}_{T}\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}#1\left[\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\middle|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}{F}_{\tau}\right]\,\mathcal{W}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}\tau),

(3.2)

almost surely, where
({F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the natural filtration generated by
\mathcal{W}
.
The stochastic integral
\mathcal{M}
is a martingale (in time) with quadratic variation

\langle\mathcal{M}\rangle_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}#1[\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\bigg(\int_{\tau}^{T}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\Lambda}#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}X_{t}(y)\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\tau.

This representation allows us to control deviations of
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
by bounding the quadratic variation
\langle\mathcal{M}\rangle_{T}
and applying a (martingale) Bernstein inequality.
Lemma 4.6 of
Gaudlitz 2023a
shows that
\langle\mathcal{M}\rangle_{T}
can be controlled once we can bound the conditional expectation

\lvert#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]\rvert.

At this point, a second application of the Malliavin calculus allows us to control the conditional expectation via density estimates. Importantly, the subsequent bound on the derivatives of the density of
X_{t}(y)
does not depend on the initial condition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X_{0}\in C(\Lambda)
be any deterministic initial condition,
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
K>0
and
f\in\mathcal{F}_{N+2,K}
. Then there exists a constant
0<p_{\max}^{(N)}<\infty
, depending on
T
,
K
and
N
, such that we have for all
0\leq n\leq N
,
0<t\leq T
and
\lambda\geq 1

\lvert\partial_{x}^{n}p_{f,\lambda}(t,y,x)\rvert\leq p_{\max}^{(N)}t^{-(N+1)/4},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},y\in\Lambda.

Proof.

The proof follows the classical approach for proving the smoothness of densities using the Malliavin calculus of
Watanabe et al. 1984a
;
Ikeda & Watanabe 1984a
, see also Section 2.1.4 of
Nualart 2006a
and Section 7.2 of
Nualart & Nualart 2018a
for modern expositions. It is carried out in Subsection
B.2
.
∎

Remark 3.2

(Assuming
f\in\mathcal{F}_{N+2,K}
)
.

Proposition
3.1
shows that assuming
f\in\mathcal{F}_{N+2,K}
in Theorem
2.3
allows us to control the derivatives of the density up to the order
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The gap of 2 between the regularity of
f
and the regularity of the density is commonly found when proceeding similarly to Theorem 2.1.4 of
Nualart 2006a
, see
(
Carmona & Nualart 1988a
;
Millet & Sanz-Solé 1999a
;
Márquez-Carreras et al. 2001a
;
Dalang & Nualart 2004a
;
Sanz-Solé 2005a
;
Nualart & Quer-Sardanyons 2007a
;
Dalang et al. 2009a
;
Marinelli et al. 2013a
)
. The gap has been reduced by
Nourdin & Viens 2009a
;
Romito 2018a
, but their results do not apply to our required case
N=1
. Providing a tighter connection between the regularity of
f
and of the density poses an intricate question for future research.

We proceed with the proof of the sub-Gaussian concentration for spatio-temporal averages in Theorem
2.3
(
a
) in three steps.
When
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we combine the integration-by-parts formula with the bounds for
\partial_{x}p_{f,\lambda}(t,y,x)
from Proposition
3.1
to obtain a bound for
\lvert#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]\rvert
in terms of
\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
in Lemma
3.3
.
Subsequently, when
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
we apply Lemma 4.6 of
Gaudlitz 2023a
to obtain the claimed sub-Gaussian concentration for
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)
in Lemma
3.4
.
We conclude by an approximation argument for
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Step 1: Bounding the conditional expectation

We denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of all functions
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that are of at most polynomial growth.
The subsequent lemma bounds the conditional expectation
\lvert#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]\rvert
in terms of
\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
by combining the integration-by-parts formula with the bound for the derivative of the density
\partial_{x}p_{f,\lambda}(t,y,x)
from Proposition
3.1
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f_{0}\in\Theta
and
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{P}
. Then

\lvert#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]\rvert\leq\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}p_{\max}^{(1)}(t-\tau)^{-1/2},\quad y\in\Lambda,\quad 0\leq\tau<t\leq T.

Proof.

Using the Markov property of
t\mapsto X_{t}
we can write

#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]=#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}X_{\tau}]=\left.#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t-\tau}(y))]\right|_{X_{0}=X_{\tau}}

Let
\xi=X_{\tau}
. In the next step, we rewrite the expectation on the right-hand-side as an integral with respect to the density
p_{f_{0},\lambda}(s,y,\>\cdot\>)
of the process
(X_{s}(y)\,|\,y\in\Lambda,0<s<T-\tau)
conditioned to start at the initial condition
\xi
, which is treated as deterministic due to the conditioning.
Noting that the density
p_{f_{0},\lambda}(s,y,\>\cdot\>)
depends on initial condition
\xi\in C(\Lambda)
, we find

#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]=\left.\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{\prime}(x)p_{f_{0},\lambda}(t-\tau,y,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|_{X_{0}=\xi}

for all
y\in\Lambda
and
0\leq\tau<t\leq T
.
Proposition
3.1
with
f=f_{0}
shows that
p_{f_{0},\lambda}(t,y,\>\cdot\>)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for any deterministic and continuous initial condition.
Furthermore, Part (
c
) of Corollary
B.4
(below) shows that
p_{f_{0},\lambda}(t,y,\>\cdot\>)
has Gaussian tails for any deterministic initial condition.
Since
g^{\prime}
grows at most polynomially, we integrate by parts without boundary terms to obtain

\lvert#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]\rvert=\mathopen{\left\lvert\vphantom{\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)\partial_{x}p_{f_{0},\lambda}(t-\tau,y,x)\,\mathrm{d}x}\kern-1.2pt\right.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)\partial_{x}p_{f_{0},\lambda}(t-\tau,y,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathclose{\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)\partial_{x}p_{f_{0},\lambda}(t-\tau,y,x)\,\mathrm{d}x}\right\rvert}.

Applying the
\xi
-independent upper bound for
\lVert\partial_{x}p_{f_{0},\lambda}(t,y,\>\cdot\>)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
from Proposition
3.1
with
f=f_{0}
, we find

\lvert#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]\rvert\leq\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}p_{\max}^{(1)}(t-\tau)^{-1/2}.

∎

Step 2: Subgaussian concentration for
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})

Lemma 3.4

(Subgaussian concentration of
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)
–
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
-version)
.

Let
f_{0}\in\Theta
and
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathcal{P}
. Then there exists a constant
C
, depending on
\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{C^{3}(\mathbb{R})}
and
T
, such that we can bound

#1\left(\lvert\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)-#1[\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)]\rvert\geq\lambda^{-1/2}x\right)\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{x^{2}}{2C\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}}\bigg),\quad x\geq 0.

(3.3)

Proof.

Using the Clark-Ocone formula, we can write

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)-#1[\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)]=\mathcal{M}_{T}\coloneq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}#1\left[\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(g)\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\middle|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\right]\,\mathcal{W}(\mathrm{d}z,\mathrm{d}\tau),

which is a martingale in time under
\mathrm{P}_{f_{0}}
evaluated at time
T
with quadratic variation

\langle\mathcal{M}\rangle_{T}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\bigg(\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathcal{D}_{\tau,z}X_{t}(y)\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\tau.

The bound
\lvert#1[g^{\prime}(X_{t}(y))\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}]\rvert\leq\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}p_{\max}^{(1)}(t-\tau)^{-1/2}
from Lemma
3.3
implies that we can use Lemma 4.6 of
Gaudlitz 2023a
with
\kappa(t,\tau)=\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}p_{\max}^{(1)}(t-\tau)^{-1/2}
to obtain

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{M}\rangle_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}(p_{\max}^{(1)})^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(2(T-\tau)^{1/2}+e^{\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}T}\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}\frac{4}{3}(T-\tau)^{3/2}\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}(p_{\max}^{(1)})^{2}\left(2T^{2}+\frac{16}{9}T^{3}e^{\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}T}\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}+\frac{4}{9}T^{4}e^{2\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}T}\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}^{2}\right).

Therefore, we can almost surely bound the quadratic variation by

\langle\mathcal{M}\rangle_{T}\leq C^{2}\frac{1}{\lambda}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

with a suitable constant
C
depending on
T
and
\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{C^{3}(\mathbb{R})}

(a)

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

(a)

We have
\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})\lesssim 2^{M}\lesssim\lambda^{1/(2\beta+1)}\leq\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda}
. So, for
L
large enough
\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})\leq(L^{2}/2)\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda}
. For this
L
, we find with the random variables
Z_{\mu}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(0,1)
,
|\mu|\leq M
, from (
2.6
) that

\Pi_{\lambda}(f\in V_{M}:\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\Xi)}>L^{2}\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda})=#1(\sum_{|\mu|\leq M}Z^{2}_{\mu}>L^{2}\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda})\leq#1(\sum_{|\mu|\leq M}(Z^{2}_{\mu}-1)>(L^{2}/2)\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda}),

where the last inequality holds since there are at most
\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})
many
Z_{\mu}
in the sum. The random variables
Z_{\mu}^{2}-1
are independent centred chi-squared random variables. A standard subexponential inequality (e.g., Equation 3.29 of
Giné & Nickl 2015a
) implies for the last probability the upper bound

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(-\frac{(L^{2}/2)^{2}(\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda})^{2}}{4\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})+(L^{2}/2)\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda}}\bigg)\leq\exp(-(L^{2}/10)\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda}).

(b)

Note
\lVert f\rVert_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\Xi)}\leq 2^{M\beta_{0}}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}
for
f\in V_{M}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\log\left(N\left(V_{M}\cap B(0,\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\Xi)},L\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}),\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)},\varepsilon_{\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\leq\log\left(N\left(B(0,\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})}},L2^{M\beta_{0}}\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}),\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})}},\varepsilon_{\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})\log(3L2^{M\beta_{0}}\sqrt{\lambda})\lesssim\lambda^{1/(2\beta+1)}\log(\lambda)\lesssim\lambda\varepsilon_{\lambda}^{2},

using a metric entropy estimate for finite-dimensional Euclidean balls (Proposition 4.3.34 of
Giné & Nickl 2015a
).

(c)

For
f_{0}\in H^{\beta}(\Xi)
we have
\lVert f_{0}-P_{M}f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}\lesssim 2^{-M\beta}\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{H^{\beta}(\Xi)}\lesssim\varepsilon_{\lambda}
, so for
L^{\prime}
large enough with
C=L^{\prime}/2

\displaystyle\Pi_{\lambda}(f\in V_{M}:\lVert f-f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}\leq L^{\prime}\varepsilon_{\lambda})\geq\Pi_{\lambda}(f\in V_{M}:\lVert f-P_{M}f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}\leq C\varepsilon_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\quad\geq\Pi_{\lambda}(f\in V_{M}:\lVert f-P_{M}f_{0}\rVert_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\Xi)}\leq C\varepsilon_{\lambda})\gtrsim\Pi_{\lambda}(f\in V_{M}:\lVert f\rVert_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\Xi)}\leq C\varepsilon_{\lambda}),

concluding with Corollary 2.6.18 of
(
Giné & Nickl 2015a
)
, noting
P_{M}f_{0}\in V_{M}
and
\lVert P_{M}f_{0}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{\lambda}}\leq\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{H^{\beta}(\Xi)}
, cf. (
2.12
). Using the notation from (a) and letting
Z\overset{d}{\sim}N(0,1)
, the last expression is equal to

\displaystyle#1(\sum_{|\mu|\leq M}Z^{2}_{\mu}\leq C^{2}\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda})\geq#1(\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})\max_{|\mu|\leq M}Z^{2}_{\mu}\leq C^{2}\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\quad=#1(\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})Z^{2}\leq C^{2}\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda})^{\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})}\geq#1(|Z|\leq C^{\prime}\lambda^{-1/2})^{\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})}

for some
C^{\prime}>0
, using again that
\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})\lesssim\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda}
. Since the standard normal density is uniformly bounded away from zero near the origin, the last probability is up to a constant lower bounded by
\lambda^{-\operatorname{dim}(V_{M})/2}\gtrsim\exp(-c\lambda^{1/(2\beta+1)}\log(\lambda))=\exp(-c\lambda\varepsilon^{2}_{\lambda})
for some

. Adjusting the constant for multiplicative factors yields the desired small ball lower bound.

(d)

Suppose
f\in V_{M}
with
\lVert f-f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}\leq L^{\prime\prime\prime}\varepsilon_{\lambda}=L^{\prime\prime\prime}\lambda^{-\beta/(2\beta+1)}\log(\lambda)
. Then, by the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\Xi)}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f-P_{M}f_{0}\rVert_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\Xi)}+\lVert P_{M}f_{0}\rVert_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\Xi)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{M\beta_{0}}\lVert f-P_{M}f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}+\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{H^{\beta}(\Xi)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{M\beta_{0}}\lVert f-f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}+2^{M\beta_{0}}\lVert f_{0}-P_{M}f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}+\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{H^{\beta}(\Xi)}

\displaystyle\leq 2L^{\prime\prime\prime}\lambda^{(\beta_{0}-\beta)/(2\beta+1)}\log(\lambda)+\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{H^{\beta}(\Xi)},

which is bounded by a constant uniformly in
\lambda
for
\lambda\to\infty
as long as
\beta>\beta_{0}
. This proves the claim taking
L^{\prime\prime}>\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{H^{\beta}(\Xi)}
large enough, depending on
L^{\prime\prime\prime}
.

∎

d_{2}(\gamma,0)\lesssim\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}2^{-M\rho}\lVert\gamma\rVert_{H^{\rho}(\Xi)}\lesssim\lambda^{-(\rho-1/2)/(2\beta_{0}+1)}
. Together with the tail bound in (

implies the moment bound

\displaystyle\sup_{\gamma\in\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J}}#1[\lvert G_{\lambda}(\gamma)\rvert\mathrel{}\mathclose{}|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}X^{\lambda}]\lesssim\sup_{\gamma\in\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J}}(d_{1}(\gamma,0)+d_{2}(\gamma,0))\lesssim 2^{-J(\rho-1)}+\lambda^{-(\rho-1/2)/(2\beta_{0}+1)}.

We then obtain the claimed maximal inequality from Theorem 3.5 of
Dirksen 2015a
by proving the following two entropy integral bounds:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log(2N(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J},d_{1},\varepsilon))}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon&\lesssim 2^{-J(\rho-1)},\\
\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(2N(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J},d_{2},\varepsilon))\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon&\lesssim\lambda^{-(\rho-3/2)/(2\beta_{0}+1)}.\end{split}

(A.16)

For this, let
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{J}=\{P_{M}\gamma_{0}:\gamma\in\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J}\}
. We have
N(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J},d_{1},\varepsilon)=N(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{J},\bar{C}\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Xi)},\varepsilon)
. Recalling (
A.15
), we have
\lVert P_{M}(\gamma-P_{J}\gamma)_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Xi)}\lesssim 2^{-J(\rho-1)}
if
\lVert\gamma\rVert_{H^{\rho}(\Xi)}\leq 1
, such that
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{J}\subset B(0,\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{H^{1}(\Xi)},c2^{-J(\rho-1})
for a constant

depending only on
p_{f_{0}}
and
\Xi
. From Corollary 4.3.38 of
Giné & Nickl 2015a
we get
N(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J},d_{1},\varepsilon)\lesssim\exp(c2^{-J(\rho-1)}/\varepsilon)
, and (
A.12
) gives
\sup_{\gamma,\bar{\gamma}\in\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J}}d_{1}(\gamma,\bar{\gamma})\lesssim 2^{-J(\rho-1)}
. The first integral bound in (
A.16
) follows as in (
b
). Regarding the second bound, note
\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J}\subset B(0,\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{H^{\rho}(\Xi)},1)
and
d_{2}(\gamma,\bar{\gamma})\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}\lVert\gamma-\bar{\gamma}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)}
we have by Corollary 4.3.38 of
Giné & Nickl 2015a

\displaystyle N(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J},d_{2},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq N(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J},\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{L^{2}(\Xi)},\varepsilon/(\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}))\lesssim\exp{((C\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}/\varepsilon)^{1/\rho})}

for a constant
C>0
. The second integral bound is obtained from

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(2N(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{J},d_{2},\varepsilon))\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\bar{c}\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}2^{-M\rho}}(\log(2)+\exp{((C\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}/\varepsilon)^{1/\rho})}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}2^{-M\rho}+(\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda})^{1/\rho}(\sqrt{\lambda}\varepsilon_{\lambda}2^{-M\rho})^{1-1/\rho}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-(\rho-3/2)/(2\beta_{0}+1)}.

(d)

Let
\langle h\rangle_{t}=\lambda^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert h(X_{s})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\mathrm{d}s
denote the quadratic variation of the continuous martingale

,
t\geq 0

\displaystyle=#1(\lvert W_{\lambda}(\gamma)\rvert\geq x,\langle\gamma\rangle_{T}\leq T\lVert\gamma\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Xi)})\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2T\lVert\gamma\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Xi)}^{2}}\right)

from (

B.2

This subsection contains the upper bound for the derivative of the density

Lemma B.5
.

For any

and

Proof.

Define

. Then we have

\lVert G_{t-\>\cdot\>}^{\lambda}(y,\>\cdot\>)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Lambda}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(\lambda\bar{y},\eta)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}s=\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(\lambda\bar{y},\lambda\bar{\eta})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\eta}\mathrm{d}s.

Using the rescaling of the Green function
G_{t}^{\lambda}(\lambda\bar{y},\lambda{\bar{\eta}})=\lambda^{-1}G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}(\bar{y},\bar{\eta})
for

\lVert G_{t-\>\cdot\>}^{\lambda}(y,\>\cdot\>)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}=\lambda^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}G_{\lambda^{-2}(t-s)}^{1}(\bar{y},\bar{\eta})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\eta}\mathrm{d}s.

Applying the bound
G_{t}^{1}(\bar{y},\bar{\eta})\leq c(t^{-1/2}\vee 1)\exp(-(\bar{y}-\bar{\eta})^{2}/(\tilde{c}t))
for some constants

from Theorem 3.2.9 of

, we find

\lVert G_{t-\>\cdot\>}^{\lambda}(y,\>\cdot\>)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}\leq c^{2}\tilde{c}^{1/2}\lambda^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}((\lambda^{-2}(t-s))^{-1/2}\vee 1)^{2}\lambda^{-1}(t-s)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}s\lesssim_{T}t^{1/2}.

∎

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\Lambda}\lVert\mathcal{D}^{n}X_{t}(y)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\Lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Lambda}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)\sum_{l=2}^{n}\sum_{P_{l}}c_{l}\lVert f^{(l)}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\prod_{i=1}^{l}\lVert\mathcal{D}^{\lvert p_{i}\rvert}X_{s}(\eta)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes\lvert p_{i}\rvert}}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{y\in\Lambda}\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Lambda}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)\lVert\mathcal{D}^{n}X_{s}(\eta)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\Lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Lambda}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)\sum_{l=2}^{n}\sum_{P_{l}}c_{l}\lVert f^{(l)}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\prod_{i=1}^{l}C_{\lvert p_{i}\rvert}(s)\,\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\sup_{y\in\Lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{z\in\Lambda}\lVert\mathcal{D}^{n}X_{s}(z)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}\int_{\Lambda}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=2}^{n}\sum_{P_{l}}c_{l}\lVert f^{(l)}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\prod_{i=1}^{l}C_{\lvert p_{i}\rvert}(t)t+\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{z\in\Lambda}\lVert\mathcal{D}^{n}X_{s}(z)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

An application of Gronwall’s inequality shows

\sup_{y\in\Lambda}\lVert\mathcal{D}^{n}X_{t}(y)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}\leq\sum_{l=2}^{n}\sum_{P_{l}}c_{l}\lVert f^{(l)}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\prod_{i=1}^{l}C_{\lvert p_{i}\rvert}(t)te^{\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}t}=C_{n}(t),\quad 0<t\leq T,

uniformly in the domain size
\lambda\geq 1
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Step 2 (Controlling
\lVert\gamma_{t,y}^{-1}\rVert_{n,r}^{n}
).

The upper bound for
n=1
from (
B.4
) and the lower bound from the proof of Lemma C.1 of

show

\gamma_{t,y}=\lVert\mathcal{D}X_{t}(y)\rVert_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}\sim t^{1/2},

uniformly in
\lambda\geq 1
,
0<t\leq T
and
y\in\Lambda
. The recursion (2.24) of
(

)
together with (
B.4
) reveal that
\lVert\gamma_{t,y}^{-1}\rVert_{n,r}^{r}
,

, is dominated by its zeroth component when no derivative is taken, such that

\lVert\gamma_{t,y}^{-1}\rVert_{n,r}^{r}\lesssim_{T}#1[\lvert\gamma_{t,y}^{-1}\rvert^{r}]\lesssim t^{-r/2},\quad 0<t\leq T,\quad y\in\Lambda,

and thus

\lVert\gamma_{t,y}^{-1}\rVert_{n,r}^{n}\lesssim_{T}t^{-n/2}

uniformly in
\lambda\geq 1
,
0<t\leq T
and

.

Step 3 (Conclusion).

The claim follows from (
B.3
), (
B.5
) and (

): There exists a constant

and

,

,

B.3
Convergence to the global solution

This section explores the behaviour of the semi-linear stochastic heat equation driven by space-time white noise on the growing spatial domain
\Lambda=\lambda\bar{\Lambda}
with
\lambda\to\infty
. Without loss of generality we assume
0\in\bar{\Lambda}
such that
\Lambda=\lambda\bar{\Lambda}\to\mathbb{R}
as
\lambda\to\infty
. The goal is to show the weak convergence
X_{t}(\lambda\bar{y})\xrightarrow{d}Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0)
as
\lambda\to\infty
for every
0\leq t\leq T
and
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
and proving Theorem
2.2
(
a
). Denote by
\star
the operation

(u\star v)(y)\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(y,\eta)v(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta,\quad y\in\mathbb{R},

for any functions
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,
v\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
u(y,\>\cdot\>)v(\>\cdot\>)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\varphi_{t}
be the heat kernel on
\mathbb{R}
, that is

\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y-\eta)^{2}}{4t}\right),\quad t>0,\quad y,\eta\in\mathbb{R}.

Recall that the process
(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)\,|\,y\in\mathbb{R},0\leq t\leq T)
solving the semi-linear heat equation (
2.8
) can be written as

Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)=\int_{0}^{t}(\varphi_{t-s}\star f(Z_{s}^{\bar{y}}))(y)\,\mathrm{d}s+\bar{Z}_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y),\quad\bar{Z}_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)\coloneq\chi(\bar{y})+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{t-s}(y,\eta)\mathcal{W}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}s),

(B.9)

where
\mathcal{W}
is understood as spatially homogeneous (space-time white) noise
\mathcal{W}
on
\mathbb{R}
, compare
(
Dalang & Quer-Sardanyons 2011a
)
.
Denote by
G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)
for
t>0
and
y,\eta\in\Lambda
the Neumann heat kernel on
\Lambda
. Without further mentioning we will extend
G_{t}^{\lambda}
by zero to a function on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. Define the process

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{X}_{t}(y)&\coloneq(G_{t}^{\lambda}\star X_{0})(y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)\mathcal{W}(\mathrm{d}\eta,\mathrm{d}s),\\
X_{t}(y)&\coloneq\int_{0}^{t}(G_{t-s}^{\lambda}\star f(X_{s}))(y)\,\mathrm{d}s+\bar{X}_{t}(y),\end{split}

(B.10)

for
0\leq t\leq T,y\in\mathbb{R}
, driven by the same noise
\mathcal{W}
as
Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}
. Note that the process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
defined in (
B.10
) and the process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
defined in (
1.1
) have the same marginal laws for every
0\leq t\leq T
and
y\in\Lambda
.
Since we are only aiming to prove convergence in distribution
X_{t}(\lambda\bar{y})\xrightarrow{d}Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0)
as
\lambda\to\infty
for every
0\leq t\leq T
and
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
, we keep the notation
X
for both processes for notational simplicity.

For a standard Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
define the stopping time

\tau_{\lambda}\coloneq\inf\{t\geq 0\,\colon\,B_{2t}\not\in\lambda\bar{\Lambda}\}.

For any location
y\in\Lambda
and time
t\geq 0
, the probability of the event
\{\tau_{\lambda}\leq t\}
, given that
B_{0}=y
, is denoted by
#1(\tau_{\lambda}\leq t)
. Lemma
B.11
implies
#1(\tau_{\lambda}\leq t)\to 0
as
\lambda\to\infty
for every
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
and
t>0
. Recall that for
A\subset\mathbb{R}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
we define their distance as
\operatorname{dist}(y,A)\coloneq\inf\{\lvert y-\eta\rvert\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\eta\in A\}
. The main result of this section is the following (pointwise) convergence of
X_{t}(\lambda\bar{y})
to
Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(\lambda\bar{y})
.

Proposition B.6
.

Assume that
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz-continuous and that
\chi\in H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})
for some
\tilde{s}>1/2
. For any
p\geq 1
we have convergence

#1[\lvert Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(\lambda\bar{y})-X_{t}(\lambda\bar{y})\rvert^{p}]\to 0,\quad\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

as
\lambda\to\infty
for the process
X
defined in (
B.10
). In particular, the process
X
defined in (
1.1
) satisfies
X_{t}(\lambda\bar{y})\xrightarrow{d}Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0)
for all
0\leq t\leq T
and
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
. More precisely, for any exponent
p\geq 1
and location
y\in\Lambda
define

K(y,\lambda,p)\coloneq\exp\left(-\frac{\operatorname{dist}(y,\partial\Lambda)^{2}}{8cTp}\right)\begin{cases}(1+\log(\lambda)+\operatorname{dist}(y,\partial\Lambda)^{2}),&p=2\\
2,&p\neq 2,\end{cases}

where
c>1
is arbitrary. Then, for all
0\leq t\leq T
,
y=\lambda\bar{y}\in\Lambda
and
0<\varepsilon<\tilde{s}-1/2
,

\displaystyle#1[\lvert Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)-X_{t}(y)\rvert^{p}]\leq C\left(#1(\tau_{\lambda}\leq t)^{p/2}+K(y,\lambda,p)+K(y,\lambda,2p)+\lambda^{-p\varepsilon}\lVert\chi\rVert_{H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})}^{p}\right),

(B.11)

where the constant
C
depends only on
T
,
\lVert f\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}
and
p
.

Before proving Proposition
B.6
, a few remarks are in order.

Remark B.7

(On
K(y,\lambda,p)
)
.

(a)

For any location
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
, we have
K(\lambda\bar{y},\lambda,p)\sim\exp(-\lambda^{2}/(8cTP))\to 0
as
\lambda\to\infty
. This convergence is not uniform in
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
due to the effect of the boundary conditions.

(b)

By integration we find for all
1\leq\lambda<\infty
and
r>0
the bound

\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\Lambda}K(y,\lambda,p)^{r}\,\mathrm{d}y\lesssim_{T,p}\lambda^{-1}\begin{cases}\log(\lambda)^{r},&p=2,\\
1,&p\neq 2.\end{cases}

(B.12)

Corollary B.8
.

Assume that
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz-continuous and that
\chi\in H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})
for some
\tilde{s}>3/2
. Let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be either the process defined in (
1.1
) or in (
B.10
). For any Lipschitz-continuous function
h\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\bigg\lvert#1\Big[\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}h(X_{t}(y))^{k}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t\Big]-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}#1\Big[h(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0))^{k}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\mathrm{d}t\bigg\rvert\leq C\lVert h\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}\lambda^{-1},

where the constant
0<C<\infty
depends on
T
,
\lVert f\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}
,
k
and
\lVert\chi\rVert_{H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})}
.

Proof.

Note that
Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0)\overset{d}{=}Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)
by the stationarity of the process
y\mapsto Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)
. This implies

\displaystyle\bigg\lvert#1\Big[\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}h(X_{t}(y))^{k}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t\Big]-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}#1\Big[h(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0))^{k}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\mathrm{d}t\bigg\rvert\hskip-180.00027pt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigg\lvert#1\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}h(X_{t}(y))^{k}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t\Big]-#1\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}h(Z_{t}^{y/\lambda}(y))^{k}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t\Big]\bigg\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}#1\Big[\Big\lvert\left(h(X_{t}(y))-h(Z_{t}^{y/\lambda}(y))\right)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}h(X_{t}(y))^{k-i}h\Big(Z_{t}^{y/\lambda}(y)\Big)^{i-1}\Big)\Big\rvert\Big]\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t.

Applying Hölder’s inequality with
r>1
,
r\neq 2
and
1/r+1/\tilde{r}=1
we find

\displaystyle\bigg\lvert#1\Big[\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}h(X_{t}(y))^{k}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t\Big]-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}#1\Big[h(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0))^{k}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\mathrm{d}t\bigg\rvert\hskip-210.00032pt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\Big(#1\Big[\left(h(X_{t}(y))-h(Z_{t}^{y/\lambda}(y)\right)^{r}\Big]^{1/r}#1\Big[\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}h(X_{t}(y))^{k-i}h(Z_{t}^{y/\lambda}(y))^{i-1}\Big)^{\tilde{r}}\Big]^{1/{\tilde{r}}}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t.

By Lemma
B.2
and Theorem 25 of
Dalang & Quer-Sardanyons 2011a
, we can deduce for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the bounds

\sup_{\lambda\geq 1,y\in\Lambda,0\leq t\leq T}#1[\lvert X_{t}(y)\rvert^{n}]\leq c,\quad\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R},0\leq t\leq T,\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}}#1[\lvert Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(y)\rvert^{n}]\leq\tilde{c},

where the constants
0<c,\tilde{c}<\infty
depend on
T
,
\lVert f\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}
,
n
and
\lVert\chi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Lambda})}
. Using the Lipschitz-continuity of
h
, Proposition
B.6
with
p=r\neq 2
and finally the bounds (
B.12
) and (
B.19
) we find

\displaystyle\bigg\lvert#1\Big[\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}h(X_{t}(y))^{k}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t\Big]-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}#1\Big[h(Z_{t}^{\bar{y}}(0))^{k}\Big]\,\mathrm{d}\bar{y}\mathrm{d}t\bigg\rvert\hskip-130.0002pt

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T,\lVert f\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}},k,\lVert\chi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Lambda})}}\lVert h\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}#1\left[\Big\lvert X_{t}(y)-Z_{t}^{y/\lambda}(y)\Big\rvert^{r}\right]^{1/r}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T,\lVert f\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}},r}\lVert h\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Lambda}\sqrt{#1(\tau_{\lambda}\leq t)}+K(y,\lambda,r)^{1/r}+K(y,\lambda,2r)^{1/r}

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{-\varepsilon}\lVert\chi\rVert_{H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})}\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T}\lVert h\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}\left(\lambda^{-1}+\lambda^{-1}+\lambda^{-1}+\lambda^{-\varepsilon}\lVert\chi\rVert_{H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\lVert\chi\rVert_{H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})}}\lVert h\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}}\lambda^{-1},

where we chose
\varepsilon\geq 1
in the last line.
∎

B.3.1
Proving Proposition
B.6

The proof of the bound (
B.11
) is based on two ingredients. First, it requires the convergence of the stochastic integrals
\bar{X}
to
\bar{Z}^{\bar{y}}
in (
B.9
) and (
B.10
), which is proved using the convergence of the corresponding Green functions. The impact of the varying initial conditions can be controlled by understanding the action of the heat semi-group
t\mapsto G_{t}\star\>\cdot\>
for short times, as the subsequent Lemma shows. To this end, a convenient tool is the scaling property of the Neumann heat kernel
G_{t}^{\lambda}
on
\Lambda
in terms of the Neumann heat kernel
G_{t}^{1}
on
\bar{\Lambda}
such that

G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)=\lambda^{-1}G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}(\lambda^{-1}y,\lambda^{-1}\eta),\quad t>0,y,\eta\in\Lambda.

(B.13)

Second, we perform a Gronwall argument to extend the convergence to the semi-linear case.

Lemma B.9
.

Assume that
\chi\in H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})
for some
\tilde{s}>1/2
and let
0<\varepsilon<\tilde{s}-1/2
. Then there exists an absolute constant
0<C<\infty
such that

\lVert X_{0}-G_{t}^{\lambda}\star X_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Lambda)}=\lVert\chi-G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}\star\chi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Lambda})}\leq Ct^{\varepsilon/2}\lambda^{-\varepsilon}\lVert\chi\rVert_{H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})},\quad 0<t\leq T.

Proof.

Using the scaling of the Green kernel from (
B.13
), we deduce for every
y\in\Lambda

\displaystyle(G_{t}^{\lambda}\star X_{0})(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)X_{0}(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta=\lambda\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\lambda\bar{\eta})\chi(\bar{\eta})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\eta}=\int_{\bar{\Lambda}}G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}(y/\lambda,\bar{\eta})\chi(\bar{\eta})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\eta}

\displaystyle=(G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}\star\chi)(y/\lambda),

which shows the claimed equality. Since
\tilde{s}-\varepsilon>1/2
, the Sobolev embedding (Theorem 4.12 of
Fournier & Adams 2003a
) yields

\lVert\chi-G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}\star\chi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Lambda})}\lesssim\lVert\chi-G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}\star\chi\rVert_{H^{\tilde{s}-\varepsilon}(\bar{\Lambda})}.

By Theorem 16.6 of
Yagi 2010a
, the interpolation norm
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{\gamma}\coloneq\lVert(-\Updelta)^{\gamma}\>\cdot\>\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{\Lambda})}
of the Neumann Laplacian on
\bar{\Lambda}
is equivalent to the Sobolev norm
\lVert\>\cdot\>\rVert_{H^{2\gamma}(\bar{\Lambda})}
,
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
. Since
\chi\in\operatorname{dom}(-\Updelta)^{(\tilde{s}-\varepsilon)/2}
and powers of
(-\Updelta)
commute with its semi-group
t\mapsto G_{t}^{1}\star\>\cdot\>
, we find

\lVert\chi-G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}\star\chi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Lambda})}\lesssim\lVert(-\Updelta)^{(\tilde{s}-\varepsilon)/2}\chi-G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}\star(-\Updelta)^{(\tilde{s}-\varepsilon)/2}\chi\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{\Lambda})}.

Since the Neumann Laplacian generates an analytic semi-group
t\mapsto G_{t}^{1}\star\>\cdot\>
on
L^{2}(\bar{\Lambda})
, we can apply Theorem 6.13 (d) of
Pazy 1983a
to deduce

\lVert\chi-G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}\star\chi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Lambda})}\lesssim(\lambda^{-2}t)^{\varepsilon/2}\lVert(-\Updelta)^{\tilde{s}/2}\chi\rVert_{\varepsilon/2}\lesssim\lambda^{-\varepsilon}t^{\varepsilon/2}\lVert\chi\rVert_{H^{\tilde{s}}(\bar{\Lambda})}.

∎

Step 1: Preliminaries on Green functions.

In the following, we will make frequent use of the fact that the Green functions
\varphi_{t}
and
G_{t}^{\lambda}
can be represented as transition probabilities of Markov processes.
Since the Neumann heat kernel
G_{t}^{\lambda}
can be interpreted as the transition probabilities of a Brownian motion
(B_{2t}^{\leftrightarrow})
, which is reflected when hitting the boundary of
\Lambda
(see Section 2 of
Kendall 1989a
), it is natural to expect that
G_{t}^{\lambda_{1}}(y,\eta)\leq G_{t}^{\lambda_{2}}(y,\eta)
whenever
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}
since then
\lambda_{1}\bar{\Lambda}\supset\lambda_{2}\bar{\Lambda}
. In contrast to the Dirichlet heat kernel, however, the domain monotonicity property for Neumann boundary conditions is not true in general and counter examples are given by
Bass & Burdzy 1993a
.
Kendall 1989a
proves that

G_{t}^{\lambda_{1}}(y,\eta)\leq G_{t}^{\lambda_{2}}(y,\eta),\quad 0<t\leq T,\quad y,\eta\in\lambda_{2}\bar{\Lambda},\quad\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2},

(B.14)

whenever there exists a ball
B
between
\lambda_{1}\bar{\Lambda}
and
\lambda_{2}\bar{\Lambda}
such that
\lambda_{2}\bar{\Lambda}\subset B\subset\lambda_{1}\bar{\Lambda}
. Since
0\in\bar{\Lambda}
, we find
\Lambda\uparrow\mathbb{R}
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Consequently, (
B.14
) holds for
\lambda_{1}\gg\lambda_{2}
sufficiently large.

Lemma B.10
.

Let
1\leq\lambda<\infty
,
0<t\leq T
,
y\in\Lambda
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)-G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle\leq 2#1(\tau_{\lambda}\leq t),

(B.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)-G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{-1/2}#1(\tau_{\lambda}\leq t),

(B.16)

where the constant
0<C<\infty
depends only on
T
.

Proof.

The domain monotonicity (
B.14
) implies for any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
, location
y\in\Lambda
and time
0<t\leq T
the bound

\displaystyle\int_{A}\lvert G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)-\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle=\int_{A\cap\Lambda}(G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)-\varphi_{t}(y,\eta))\,\mathrm{d}\eta+\int_{A\cap\Lambda^{c}}\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle=\int_{A\cap\Lambda}G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta-\int_{A\cap\Lambda}\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta+\int_{A\cap\Lambda^{c}}\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

Recalling that
\varphi_{t}
and
G_{t}^{\lambda}
are the transition kernels of
B_{t}
and
B_{2t}^{\leftrightarrow}
, respectively, we find

\displaystyle\int_{A}\lvert G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)-\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle=#1(B_{2t}^{\leftrightarrow}\in A\cap\Lambda)-#1(B_{2t}\in A\cap\Lambda)+#1(B_{2t}\in A\cap\Lambda^{c})

\displaystyle=#1(B_{2t}^{\leftrightarrow}\in A\cap\Lambda)-#1(B_{2t}\in A)+2#1(B_{2t}\in A\cap\Lambda^{c})

\displaystyle\leq#1(B_{2t}^{\leftrightarrow}\in A\cap\Lambda)-#1(B_{2t}\in A)+2#1(\tau_{\lambda}\leq t).

Choosing
A=\mathbb{R}
yields (
B.15
). Since
G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)=\lambda^{-1}G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}(\lambda^{-1}y,\lambda^{-1}\eta)
by (
B.13
), where the latter is the Green kernel on
\bar{\Lambda}
, we can apply Theorem 3.2.9 of
Davies 1990a
to obtain

G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)=\lambda^{-1}G_{\lambda^{-2}t}^{1}(\lambda^{-1}y,\lambda^{-1}\eta)\lesssim\lambda^{-1}((\lambda^{-2}t)^{-1/2}\vee 1)\lesssim_{T}t^{-1/2},\quad y,\eta\in\Lambda.

(B.17)

This implies the bound

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)-\varphi_{t}(y,\eta))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\max(G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta),\varphi_{t}(y,\eta))\lvert G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)-\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T}t^{-1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert G_{t}^{\lambda}(y,\eta)-\varphi_{t}(y,\eta)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{-1/2}#1(\tau_{\lambda}\leq T).

∎

The following Lemma quantifies the convergence
#1(\tau_{\lambda}\leq t)\to 0
as
\lambda\to\infty
for any fixed
\bar{y}\in\bar{\Lambda}
and follows by classical arguments.

Lemma B.11
.

For all
y\in\Lambda
and
