Reply to: Utilizing foundation models for developing clinical tools
Authors: Juzhao Zhang, Senlin Lin, Haidong Zou, Yingyan Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis reply addresses Chan et al.’s comments on our previous study, clarifying the use of foundation models (e.g., RETFound), commercial data sources/anonymity, and DeLong test results. We highlight that the SDEDS-fine-tuned RETFound outperforms commercial models and acknowledge the need for further testing, while referencing an early benchmark value.Subject terms: Eye diseases, Diagnosis, Disease prevention, Health care economics

We appreciate Chan et al. for their attention and valuable comments on our study1. The primary goal of our research was to explore the applicability of foundation models like RETFound in real-world eye disease screening. We fully agree that further studies are needed to validate the advantages of foundation models in such screening efforts.

Regarding the predictions from the two commercial products, they were derived from real-world business databases, with no direct involvement from the companies. Due to confidentiality agreements, it is not appropriate to disclose the companies’ identities without explicit consent, and the companies have not published the training processes of their commercial models. Prospective studies remain a priority in our future plans. To provide a more transparent comparison, we included RETFound, EfficientNet, and ResNet models trained on publicly available datasets in our study. Given that traditional commercial models primarily use convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures, this comparison mitigates potential validation challenges posed by anonymization.

We also agree with Chan et al.’s observation that the Youden index is often used to compare models in determining screening thresholds. Once a model’s diagnostic threshold is set, the Youden index can serve as a measure of accuracy. We further concur with the use of the DeLong test to assess differences in model performance prior to threshold determination2. We performed DeLong tests following Sun et al.’s method3, though the results were not included in the main text. The results showed that, in internal validation, the differences between the RETFound and ResNet50 models (z = −4.540, p < 0.001) and between EfficientNetB3 and ResNet50 (z = −3.537, p < 0.001) were statistically significant, while the difference between RETFound and EfficientNetB3 was not significant (z = −1.388, p = 0.165). In external validation, significant differences were observed between RETFound and EfficientNetB3 (z = −4.350, p < 0.001) and between RETFound and ResNet50 (z = −4.715, p < 0.001), but not between EfficientNetB3 and ResNet50 (z = −1.221, p = 0.222). All comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction.

We believe that the paradigm shift toward fine-tuning pretrained foundation models for downstream tasks represents a crucial transition in medical AI research. Traditional medical AI approaches have focused on developing general models trained on large-scale datasets and applying them across different regions and populations. While this method has achieved notable progress, generalization remains a significant challenge. Foundation models in ophthalmology pave the way for population- or region-specific AI model calibration, thereby enabling the creation of personalized eye disease screening models, which is a unique advantage in ophthalmic applications4. Our primary objective was to quickly and effectively train a localized model tailored for the Shanghai community, rather than a generalized model—even when working with community-based images, which differ markedly from clinical datasets in equipment type, image quality, disease prevalence, and artifact frequency. We did not consider fine-tuning on an in-house dataset and subsequently validating on public datasets, even though some of these datasets (e.g., APTOS-2019) contain images of varying quality. This approach is contrary to our initial intent. Our results demonstrate that the RETFound model, fine-tuned with Shanghai Digital Eye Disease Screening Program (SDEDS) dataset, outperforms commercial models in both sensitivity and specificity, making this strategy potentially viable for implementation. Recent emerging models5, along with advanced vision-language foundation models6, further validate the feasibility of this technical pathway.

In summary, our study may serve as an early benchmark for the application of fine-tuning pretrained foundation models in real-world eye disease screening. We agree with Chan et al. that future research involving more extensive application testing will provide further evidence and help optimize the use of foundation models, such as RETFound, in the development of clinical tools.
