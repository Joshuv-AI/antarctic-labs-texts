Streaming quantum state purification for general mixed states
Daniel Grier, Debbie Leung, Zhi Li, Hakop Pashayan, Luke Schaeffer

Abstract
Given multiple copies of a mixed quantum state with an unknown, nondegenerate principal eigenspace, quantum state purification is the task of recovering a quantum state that is closer to the principal eigenstate. A streaming protocol relying on recursive swap tests has been proposed and analysed for noisy depolarized states with arbitrary dimension and noise level. Here, we show that the same algorithm applies much more broadly, enabling the purification of arbitrary mixed states with a nondegenerate principal eigenvalue. We demonstrate this through two approaches. In the first approach, we show that, given the largest two eigenvalues, the depolarized noise is the most difficult noise to purify for the recursive swap tests, thus the desirable bounds on performance and cost follow from prior work. In the second approach, we provide a new and direct analysis for the performance of purification using recursive swap tests for the more general noise. We also derive simple lower bounds on the sample complexity, showing that the recursive swap test algorithm attains optimal sample complexity (up to a constant factor) in the low-noise regime.

The first proof, in

Proof 1 – Reduction from general spectrum to depolarizing noise

In our first proof, we argue that for given

, the worst case setting in recursive swap test purification corresponds to the depolarizing noise spectrum (where

We wish to ensure generality of our result for all

\Delta(\Lambda)\geq\Delta(\Lambda^{\prime})

Here, the dimensions (lengths) of

It is

It is a partial order on

Lemma 11

For

1+\sum q_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=1+\lambda_{1}^{2}+\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{2}}\lambda_{2}^{2}=1+\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}=1+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{1}\Delta=1+\lambda_{1}-(1-\lambda_{1})\Delta.

Substituting this into

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(F(\Lambda))

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{1}^{2}}{1+\lambda_{1}-(1-\lambda_{1})\Delta},

\displaystyle\Delta(F(\Lambda))

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta(1+2\lambda_{1}-\Delta)}{1+\lambda_{1}-(1-\lambda_{1})\Delta}.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\lambda_{1}(F(\Lambda))}{\partial\lambda_{1}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\lambda_{1}(F(\Lambda))}{\partial\Delta}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Delta(F(\Lambda))}{\partial\lambda_{1}}

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Delta(F(\Lambda))}{\partial\Delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\Delta)^{2}+(3-2\Delta-\Delta^{2})\lambda_{1}+2\lambda_{1}^{2}}{(1+\lambda_{1}-(1-\lambda_{1})\Delta)^{2}}\geq 0\,.

In the last inequality, we use the fact that

Therefore

.
∎

Lemma 12

Due to

and

and
\lambda_{2}

This holds since

Lemma 13

\forall k\in\mathbb{N}

3.2.3
Upper Bound on Iteration Levels

For a
d
-dimensional mixed state
\rho
, we associate a generalized spectrum as

\Lambda(\rho)=((1,\cdots,1),(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d})),

(31)

where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
\rho
in descending order.
Recall from

\rho

\textsc{Swap}(\rho^{\otimes 2})=\frac{\rho+\rho^{2}}{\Tr(\rho+\rho^{2})}.

(32)

Therefore,

\Lambda(\textsc{Swap}(\rho^{\otimes 2}))=F(\Lambda(\rho)).

(33)

For purification, we care about how
\lambda_{1}(F^{(k)}(\Lambda(\rho)))
converges to 1 as

increases.
Lemma

says that
F^{(k)}(\Lambda)\succeq F^{(k)}(S(\Lambda))
, so
\lambda_{1}(F^{(k)}(\Lambda(\rho)))

Operationally, this signifies that

\rho
is easier to purify than a "depolarized state with generalized spectrum" having the same largest and second largest eigenvalues.

A generalized spectrum
\Lambda=(\textbf{q},\bm{\lambda})\in\mathcal{S}
is determined by two independent parameters.
We make a change of variable to
d_{\text{eff}}
and
\delta
by

\displaystyle d_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{2}}=\frac{1-\Delta}{\lambda_{1}-\Delta},

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=d_{\text{eff}}\lambda_{2}=1-\Delta.

(35)

Note that if
\Lambda
comes from a physical mixed state

where the first equality holds if and only if

takes the form of depolarized states.
Using these variables, we have

q_{2}=d_{\text{eff}}-1,~~\lambda_{1}=1-\delta+\frac{\delta}{d_{\text{eff}}},~~\lambda_{2}=\frac{\delta}{d_{\text{eff}}}.

(37)

We may think of

as a (possibly non-integer) dimension, and

as the noise parameter.
In fact, when

, the spectra of

The function

\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle d_{\text{eff}}(F(\Lambda))

\displaystyle=d_{\text{eff}}(\Lambda),

(38)

\displaystyle\delta(F(\Lambda))

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta+\delta^{2}/d_{\text{eff}}}{2-2(1-\frac{1}{d_{\text{eff}}})\delta+(1-\frac{1}{d_{\text{eff}}})\delta^{2}}.

This is exactly the same as

with

.
Therefore,

undergoes the same dynamics as in the depolarizing noise case.
Thanks to

Theorem 15

\begin{cases}\frac{9(1-\lambda_{1})}{\epsilon},&\text{if~}\Delta\geq\frac{2}{3}\,,\\[4.30554pt]

Here,

3.3
Proof 2 – New direct proof

In our second proof, we present a new and more direct analysis of the same algorithm.
In our analysis, we divide the iterations into three stages according to the progression of intermediate states’ purity.
Starting from a highly noisy initial state, where the principal and second eigenvalues may be quite close, the algorithm progresses through the following three stages:

•

First stage: in a worst-case state, the gap
\Delta:=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
can be arbitrarily small. Initially, progress is made by increasing
\Delta
while there is little change in
\lambda_{1}
. For technical reason, progress is measured by a sum of eigenvalue ratios, and the first stage ends when

\sum_{j=2}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{(0)}},

(55)

where
\lambda_{1}^{(0)}
is the initial value of
\lambda_{1}
.
3
3

3

In principle we could use the contemporaneous value of
\lambda_{1}
here. It makes no significant difference for worst-case (depolarized) states, but we use the initial value for simplicity of analysis later on.

•

Second stage: at this point, the principal eigenvalue (
\lambda_{1}
) begins to grow and different ideas are required since
\lambda_{1}
is changing. Surprisingly, the same sum can be used to measure progress, and the second stage ends when

\sum_{j=2}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(56)

•

Third stage: the algorithm continues until the desired purification is achieved, i.e.,
1-\lambda_{1}\leq\epsilon
. We switch to working with
\eta:=1-\lambda_{1}
as a measure of progress.

We emphasize that the algorithm itself remains unchanged across all three stages.
The division into stages is solely for analytical convenience, enabling the use of different techniques and progress measures at each step.
Moreover, if the initial state is already somewhat pure, certain stages may be skipped.

3.3.1
First Stage

For the first two stages, we introduce the quantity

\gamma_{j}:=\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})=\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}},

(57)

which we most often use in a summation:

E(\bm{\lambda}):=\sum_{j=2}^{d}\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})=\sum_{j=2}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}.

(58)

The quantity
E
serves as an important measure of progress in the algorithm: it can be arbitrarily large initially and we will show that it decreases (see, e.g.,
Corollary

19
and
Lemma

22
) from one level of the recursive swap test algorithm to the next. Recall that stage 1 runs until
E(\bm{\lambda})\leq\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{1}}
. We will show that
E
is bounded between

\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}-1\leq E(\bm{\lambda})\leq\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\Delta}.

(59)

The intuition for the threshold between stages is that we can bound the progress in
E
as a function of
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}
, or use
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\leq E+1
. Towards the upper end of this interval, it is better to use
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}
(
Corollary

19
), but eventually we need to use
E
(
Lemma

22
) because
E
itself is changing.

We begin with a lemma showing that
\gamma_{j}
decreases geometrically, by a factor that is some function of
\lambda_{1}
.

Lemma 17
.

For all
\bm{\lambda}\in V
,

\frac{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})}=\frac{1+\lambda_{j}}{1+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{j}}\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{1}{1/\lambda_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}}}}\right\rparen,

(60)

where
\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}=F(\bm{\lambda})
.

Proof.

Recall from
eq.

9
that for all
j
,

\lambda_{j}^{\prime}=\frac{\lambda_{j}(\lambda_{j}+1)}{1+\lVert\bm{\lambda}\rVert^{2}}.

(61)

In
\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})
, the
1+\lVert\bm{\lambda}\rVert^{2}
denominators cancel out and we have

\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})=\frac{\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{j}^{2}}=\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}\frac{1+\lambda_{j}}{1+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{j}}=\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})\frac{1+\lambda_{j}}{1+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{j}}.

(62)

A standard inequality on logarithms says
\frac{2}{2+x}\leq\frac{\ln(1+x)}{x}
for all
x>-1
. In particular, we have
\frac{2x}{2+x}\leq\ln(1+x)
for all
x>0
. Substituting
x=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{1+\lambda_{j}}
gives

\frac{\lambda_{1}}{1+\lambda_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{1}}=\frac{2x}{2+x}\leq\ln(1+x)=\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen\frac{1+\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{1}}{1+\lambda_{j}}}}\right\rparen.

(63)

We rearrange this into

\frac{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})}=\frac{1+\lambda_{j}}{1+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{j}}\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{1+\lambda_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{1}}}}\right\rparen\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{1}{1/\lambda_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}}}}\right\rparen,

(64)

where the rightmost inequality comes from setting
\lambda_{j}
to its upper bound,
\lambda_{1}
. This completes the proof.
∎

While it is not strictly necessary for the proof, we also can bound
\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})
on the other side by a similar function of
\lambda_{1}
.

Lemma 18
.

For all
\bm{\lambda}\in V
,

\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\lambda_{1}}}\right\rparen\leq\frac{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})},

(65)

where
\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}=F(\bm{\lambda})
.

Proof.

We have

\ln\frac{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})}{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}=\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{j}}{1+\lambda_{j}}}}\right)<\frac{\lambda_{1}}{1+\lambda_{j}}\leq\lambda_{1},

(66)

where in the first inequality we apply the basic inequality
\ln(1+x)\leq x
for
x=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{1+\lambda_{j}}
.
The lemma is proved by exponentiating both sides and then taking their reciprocals.
∎

In other words,
\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})
decreases by a factor of approximately
\exp(-\lambda_{1})
for all
j
. Since
\lambda_{1}
is at least
\frac{1}{d}
, we can use this to lower bound the progress we make even when the gap is tiny. Since the same bounds hold for
\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})/\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})
for all
j
, we can get a similar bound on
E(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})/E(\bm{\lambda})
quite easily.

Corollary 19
.

For all
\bm{\lambda}\in V
,

\frac{E(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{E(\bm{\lambda})}\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{1}{1/\lambda_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}}}}\right\rparen,

(67)

where
\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}=F(\bm{\lambda})
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{E(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{E(\bm{\lambda})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=2}^{d}\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{\sum_{j=2}^{d}\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})}

by (
58
),

(68)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{j}\frac{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})}

(69)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{1}{1/\lambda_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}}}}\right\rparen

\displaystyle\text{by \lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{lem:gamma_ratio}}.\qed

(70)

Lemma 20
.

For any initial state
\bm{\lambda}\in V
, we have
E(\bm{\lambda})\leq\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{1}}
already or achieve
E(F^{(l_{1})}(\bm{\lambda}))\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}

4
4

4

Note that this is the initial value of
\lambda_{1}
,
not

F^{(l_{1})}(\bm{\lambda})_{1}
, the value at step

.
.

Proof.

Take

\frac{E(F^{(t)}(\bm{\lambda}))}{E(\bm{\lambda})}=\prod_{k=0}^{t-1}\frac{E(F^{(k+1)}(\bm{\lambda}))}{E(F^{(k)}(\bm{\lambda}))}\leq\prod_{k=0}^{t-1}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{1}{1/(F^{(k)}(\bm{\lambda}))_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}}}}\right\rparen

Observe that
\exp(-\tfrac{1}{1/x+3/2})
is decreasing as a function of

and since

is always at least

, it follows that

\frac{E(F^{(t)}(\bm{\lambda}))}{E(\bm{\lambda})}\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{t}{1/\lambda_{1}+\frac{3}{2}}}}\right\rparen.

At

we have

E(F^{(l_{1})}(\bm{\lambda}))\leq E(\bm{\lambda})\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lceil(1/\lambda_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2})\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen E(\bm{\lambda})\lambda_{1}}}\right\rparen}}\right\rceil}{1/\lambda_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}}}}\right\rparen\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},

(73)

and since

∎

3.3.2
Second Stage

The second stage begins when

. We will show in a moment that
\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}-1\leq E
, so

Proposition 21
.

\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\leq 1+E(\bm{\lambda})
for all
\bm{\lambda}\in V
.

Proof.

The proof is a one-liner:

\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}}\leq 1+\sum_{j=2}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}=1+\sum_{j=2}^{d}\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})=1+E(\bm{\lambda}).\qed

Conveniently,

to bound the rate of decay for

Lemma 22
.

For all
\bm{\lambda}\in V
and
\lambda^{\prime}=F(\bm{\lambda})

\frac{E(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{E(\bm{\lambda})}\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{1}{E(\bm{\lambda})+\tfrac{5}{2}}}}\right\rparen.

(74)

Proof.

Bound the ratio with
Corollary

and then use

21
inside the exponential.
∎

Lemma

says that the rate of decay of

to decrease by a constant factor

Corollary 23

Fix

\bm{\lambda}\in V

for some

Proof.

We telescope (much like the proof of

\frac{E(F^{(t)}(\bm{\lambda}))}{E(\bm{\lambda})}=\prod_{k=0}^{t-1}\frac{E(F^{(k+1)}(\bm{\lambda}))}{E(F^{(k)}(\bm{\lambda}))}\leq\prod_{k=0}^{t-1}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{1}{E(F^{(k)}(\bm{\lambda}))+\tfrac{5}{2}}}}\right\rparen\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen-\frac{t}{E(\bm{\lambda})+\tfrac{5}{2}}}}\right\rparen\,.

(77)

Lemma 24
.

For
\bm{\lambda}\in V

Let us use

where

(83)

∎

3.3.3
Third Stage

In the final stage, we boost
\lambda_{1}
up to our desired level of fidelity,

Lemma 25
.

For all
\bm{\lambda}\in V
,
\eta(\bm{\lambda})\leq E(\bm{\lambda})
.

Proof.

Proposition

\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\leq 1+E(\bm{\lambda})

\eta(\bm{\lambda})=1-\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}}\leq E(\bm{\lambda}).\qed

(84)

As in the second regime,

Lemma 26
.

For all
\bm{\lambda}\in V
and
\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}=F(\bm{\lambda})

\frac{E(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{E(\bm{\lambda})}\leq\frac{1+\eta(\bm{\lambda})}{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp(\eta(\bm{\lambda})).

(85)

Proof.

The proof is a simple calculation and shares some similarity with the proof of

\displaystyle\frac{E(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{E(\bm{\lambda})}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{j}\frac{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})}{\gamma_{j}(\bm{\lambda})}

\displaystyle=\max_{j}\frac{1+\lambda_{j}}{1+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{j}}

We finally bound this with

Lemma 27

Let

\displaystyle E(F^{(t)}(\bm{\lambda}))

\displaystyle=\frac{E(\bm{\lambda})}{2^{t}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\eta(F^{(k)}(\bm{\lambda}))}}\right\rparen

Finally, let

Proposition 28
.

For
\bm{\lambda}\in V

(95)

Proof.

The cost per swap test is
2

\frac{4}{1+\lVert\bm{\lambda}\rVert^{2}}\leq\frac{4}{1+\lambda_{1}^{2}}=\frac{2}{1-\eta(\bm{\lambda})+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta(\bm{\lambda})^{2}}

It is a standard result in calculus that the difference between a smooth function (

(1-x+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2})-\exp(-x)=\tfrac{1}{6}\xi^{3}\geq 0,

Let

3.3.4

We have an analysis for each of the three stages with

\begin{cases}\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\epsilon}}}\right\rparen&\text{if $\lambda_{1}\geq\tfrac{2}{3}$,}\\

For stage 2, we apply

l_{2}\leq 2\ln 2\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\Delta}+(\frac{5}{2}+\frac{1}{\ln 2})\ln\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\Delta}+O(1).

4
Sample Complexity Lower bound

In this section, we derive three lower bounds (collected into two theorems) for the sample complexity to purify general noisy states.
In each case, very few parameters about the noisy states are known, and the lower bound holds for the worse case spectra satisfying the constraints.
Our derivations follow an approach inspired by, and similar to, that described in Section 5 of
[
CFL+25
]
. In each case, the general purification protocol is applied to solve a purification problem with known optimal sample complexity.

The first two bounds are built upon an optimality result from

[
CEM99
]
. It is shown that, to perform the purification for a single qubit,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1-\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|+\delta\frac{I}{2}}}\right)^{\!\!\otimes N}\to~\sigma

(119)

such that
F(\sigma,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|)\geq 1-\epsilon
,
the sample complexity is lower bounded by

N\geq\frac{\delta}{2(1-\delta)^{2}\epsilon}-o_{\delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}\right).

(120)

Here
o_{\delta}(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
denotes a term that grows asymptotically slower than
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
for any fixed
\delta
.

From the above, we obtain the following sample complexity lower bounds for general purification.

Theorem 31
.

1.

To purify a general mixed state
\rho
with dimension
d
and
\lambda_{1}>\tfrac{1}{2}
, so that the fidelity to its principal eigenvector becomes at least
1-\epsilon
, the sample complexity in the worst case is at least

N\geq\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{(2\lambda_{1}-1)^{2}\epsilon}-o_{\lambda_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}\right).

(121)

2.

To purify a general mixed state
\rho
with dimension
d
and gap
\Delta
, so that the fidelity to its principal eigenvector becomes at least
1-\epsilon
, the sample complexity in the worst case is at least

N\geq\frac{1-\Delta}{2\Delta^{2}\epsilon}-o_{\Delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}\right).

(122)

The lower bounds from the above theorem can be compared with the upper bounds derived for the recursive swap test purification protocol

eqs.

51
and
54
, or
eq.

104
.
We can see that if
\lambda_{1}
is bounded away from
1/2
(for example,
\lambda_{1}\geq 2/3
), then our protocol is optimal up to an absolute constant factor in the sense that it works for general mixed state with dimension
d
and principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
and matches the lower bound in the worst case.
Similarly, if
\Delta
is bounded away from 0 (and the distance is independent of
d
, for example,
\Delta\geq 1/3
), then our protocol is optimal up to an absolute constant factor in the sense that it works for general mixed state with dimension
d
and gap
\Delta
and matches the lower bound in the worst case.

Proof.

Given a general state purification protocol, we can design a qubit purification protocol as follows:

\rho_{2}^{\otimes N}\xrightarrow{\text{embedding}}\rho_{d}^{\otimes N}\xrightarrow{\text{purification}}\sigma_{d}\xrightarrow{\text{reduction}}\sigma_{2},

(123)

introducing quantum channels which embed a qubit into a
d
-dimensional qudit, and extract a qubit from a qudit.

More precisely, we suppose that the qubit is the subspace of the qudit spanned by the first two basis vectors,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|1}}\right\rangle
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|2}}\right\rangle
. Then an arbitrary single-qubit mixed state
\rho_{2}=(1-\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|+\delta\frac{I}{2}
can be embedded into the qudit by inclusion:

\rho_{2}\mapsto\rho_{d}=\rho_{2}+0\sum_{i=3}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|i}}\right\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle i}}\right|.

(124)

Here,

\lambda_{1}(\rho_{d})=1-\frac{\delta}{2},~~\Delta(\rho_{d})=1-\delta.

(125)

Then we perform the purification protocol
\mathcal{P}
for
\rho_{d}^{\otimes N}
:

\rho_{d}^{\otimes N}\mapsto\sigma_{d}=\mathcal{P}(\rho_{d}^{\otimes N}).

(126)

Then we reduce
\sigma_{d}
back to a qubit state as follows:

\sigma_{d}\mapsto\sigma_{2}=P\sigma_{d}P+\Tr((1-P)\sigma_{d})\frac{I}{2},

(127)

where
P
is the projector onto the subspace spanned by
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|1}}\right\rangle
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|2}}\right\rangle
. That is, we make the two-outcome measurement with projectors
P
and
I-P
, and replace the state with a maximally mixed qubit for the
I-P
outcome (and keep the
P
outcome state).

Note that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|\sigma_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|\sigma_{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle+\Tr((1-P)\sigma_{d})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|\tfrac{I}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|\sigma_{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle.

(128)

Therefore, to purify
\rho_{2}
so that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|\sigma_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle\geq 1-\epsilon
, it suffices to purify
\rho_{d}
so that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|\sigma_{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle\geq 1-\epsilon
.
Therefore, to purify
\rho_{d}
to
\epsilon
infidelity, we also need
the sample complexity according to
eq.

120
:

N\geq\frac{\delta}{2(1-\delta)^{2}\epsilon}-o_{\delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}\right).

(129)

Substituting
\delta
with
\delta=2(1-\lambda_{1}(\rho_{d}))
and with
\delta=1-\Delta(\rho_{d})
(immediate from
eq.

125
) gives statements 1 and 2 of the theorem respectively.
∎

Note that
eq.

125
requires
\lambda_{1}>1/2
for allowing a solution with
\delta<1
. Furthermore, while our proof is similar to that employed in Section 5 of
[
CFL+25
]
, applying it here to the more general input state significantly simplifies the proof, and strengthens the bound to be dimension independent.
In the current derivation, the state after the embedding need not be a depolarized state, which enables us to tune the embedding parameter more favorably.

Our third lower bound is built on the more recent optimality result from

[
LFIC24
]
, wherein
eq.

120
is generalized to qudits.
It is shown that to perform

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen(1-\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|+\delta\frac{I}{d}}}\right\rparen^{\!\!\otimes N}\to~\sigma

(130)

such that
F(\sigma,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|)\geq 1-\epsilon
,
the sample complexity is
asymptotically
lower bounded by

N\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{d}}}\right)\frac{\delta}{(1-\delta)^{2}\epsilon}-o_{\delta,d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}\right).

(131)

Here
o_{\delta,d}(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
denotes a term that grows asymptotically slower than
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
for any fixed
\delta
and
d
.

With this in mind, we can prove the following result.

Theorem 32
.

To purify a general mixed state with dimension
d
, principal and second principal eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, so that the fidelity to its principal eigenvector becomes at least
1-\epsilon
, the sample complexity in the worst case is at least

N>\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{4\Delta^{2}\epsilon}-o_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}\right).

(132)

Compare the above theorem with
eqs.

46
and
104
, we see that if
\Delta
is bounded away from 0 (and the distance is independent of
d
), then our protocol is optimal up to an absolute constant factor in the sense that it works for general mixed state with given dimension
d
, principal and second principal eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
and matches the lower bound in the worst case.

Proof.

We use a similar proof strategy as before, except that now we embed
d^{\prime}
-dimensional qudits, with
d^{\prime}
to be determined later. More precisely, given
\rho_{d^{\prime}}=(1-\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\psi}}\right\rangle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\psi}}\right|+\delta\frac{I}{d^{\prime}}
, we embed it into a
d
-dimensional space as follows:

\rho_{d^{\prime}}\mapsto\rho_{d}=\mu\rho_{d^{\prime}}+(1-\mu)\frac{I_{d-d^{\prime}}}{d-d^{\prime}},

(133)

where
0\leq\mu\leq 1
and
I_{d-d^{\prime}}/(d-d^{\prime})
is the maximally mixed state in the space orthogonal to
\rho_{d^{\prime}}
.
For this state, we have

\lambda_{1}(\rho_{d})=\mu(1-\frac{d^{\prime}-1}{d^{\prime}}\delta),~~\lambda_{2}(\rho_{d})=\frac{\mu\delta}{d^{\prime}},

(134)

assuming

\mu\delta/d^{\prime}\geq(1-\mu)/(d-d^{\prime}).

(135)

Solving
\mu
and
\delta
, we get

\delta=\frac{d^{\prime}\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}+(d^{\prime}-1)\lambda_{2}},~~\mu=\lambda_{1}+(d^{\prime}-1)\lambda_{2}.

(136)

The requirement
eq.

135
is then automatically satisfied, since it is equivalent to
\lambda_{1}+(d-1)\lambda_{2}\geq 1
.
Also, we need
0\leq\delta\leq 1
, and
0\leq\mu\leq 1
.
The former is automatically satisfied since
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
, and the latter is achieved by choosing

d^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{2}}}}\right\rfloor+1.

(137)

The above choice for
d^{\prime}
also implies
d^{\prime}\leq d
, hence this is a valid choice.
To complete the analysis, we substitute

eqs.

136
and
137
into
eq.

131
and note that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{d^{\prime}}}}\right)\frac{\delta}{(1-\delta)^{2}\epsilon}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lparen 1-\frac{1}{d^{\prime}}}}\right\rparen\frac{d^{\prime}\lambda_{2}(\lambda_{1}+(d^{\prime}-1)\lambda_{2})}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{d^{\prime}}}}\right)\frac{(1-\lambda_{1})(1-\lambda_{2})}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}\geq\frac{1-\lambda_{1}}{4\Delta^{2}\epsilon}.

(138)

∎
