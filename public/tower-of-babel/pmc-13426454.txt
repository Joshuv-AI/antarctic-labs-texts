Mechanistic insights into water transport in electrified desalination: Osmosis and electroosmosis decoupled
Authors: Lijing Xu, Zhiwei Wang, Menachem Elimelech, Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-salinity wastewaters pose a serious challenge for conventional desalination technologies that are fundamentally constrained by the high osmotic pressure. Electrified desalination can overcome this limitation as it is driven by an electric field rather than hydraulic pressure. However, undesired water transport through ion exchange membranes, arising from both osmosis and electroosmosis, remains a key bottleneck to their efficiency. To date, the underlying mechanisms of water transport in ion exchange membranes remain largely unexplored. In this study, we determine the water transport number of ions via streaming potential measurements, based on the framework of irreversible thermodynamics, establishing a theoretical basis for quantifying electroosmosis. We then demonstrate excellent agreement between experimental measurements of water transport and theoretical predictions, validating the robustness and accuracy of our theoretical framework. We also decouple the respective contributions of osmosis and electroosmosis to the overall water transport. Guided by these insights, we propose a previously unidentified strategy to mitigate osmotic water loss by leveraging concentration polarization to reduce the effective osmotic pressure gradient, thereby suppressing osmotic water flux and improving energy efficiency. Overall, this work advances the fundamental understanding of water transport in ion exchange membranes and provides practical guidance for optimizing electrified desalination systems for high-salinity wastewater treatment.

INTRODUCTION

The growing discharge of high-salinity wastewater from industrial activities poses major environmental and sustainability challenges, and increasingly stringent regulations are accelerating the adoption of water reuse and zero liquid discharge strategies. To address these issues, industries are increasingly implementing water reuse and zero liquid discharge strategies (1, 2). Reverse osmosis (RO) plays a central role in these efforts, but its operating pressure limit constrains water recovery. Thus, RO is usually integrated with downstream thermal brine concentration and crystallization processes to maximize overall water recovery (3, 4). However, the prohibitively high energy consumption of such RO–thermal crystallizer configurations (20 to 70 kWh m−3 of treated water) (1, 4), together with severe challenges of mineral scaling and corrosion problems, limits the economic viability of this approach. While high pressure RO, low-salt-rejection RO, and osmotically assisted RO can extend treatable brine concentration (5–8), they also raise capital costs due to the need for extensive pretreatment to prevent mineral scaling, specialized membranes, and pressure-resistant equipment (9, 10).

Electrified desalination technologies, driven by electrical voltage rather than hydraulic pressure, avoid the limitations imposed by osmotic pressure and can potentially enable high water recovery with reduced mineral scaling risks (11–17). Representative electrified technologies include electrodialysis (ED), membrane capacitive deionization (MCDI), and flow-electrode capacitive deionization (FCDI). These systems rely on transporting ions through ion exchange membranes (IEMs) under an applied electric field, demonstrating high efficiency and effectiveness, particularly in treating brackish water (18–23).

Nevertheless, the effectiveness of these electro-driven desalination technologies declines when treating high salinity waters. In such cases, undesired water transport through the IEMs, from the desalination (dilute) chamber to the concentrated chamber, reduces overall water recovery and hinders brine concentration (24–27). This water crossover is driven by two mechanisms: osmosis and electroosmosis. Osmosis is caused by the salt concentration difference or osmotic pressure difference across the membrane (28–30), whereas electroosmosis primarily results from the electromigration of hydrated ions under an applied electric field (31, 32). Both effects become more pronounced at higher salinities, exacerbating water loss (13, 25, 33, 34), lowering freshwater yield, and increasing energy consumption. These challenges limit the applicability of electrified technologies for high-salinity water treatment.

Despite the critical role of water transport through IEMs under an applied electric field, the underlying mechanisms and the relative importance of the two effects remain poorly understood. To date, increasing attention has been devoted to investigating water transport across IEMs (25–27, 35–42), yet a mechanistic understanding of this process remains largely lacking. In particular, quantifying and distinguishing between osmotic and electroosmotic water transport is challenging as both water molecules and salt ions transport simultaneously under the combined influences of both osmotic pressure and electrical potential. Although the water permeance of IEMs has been characterized, osmotic water flux (i.e., a product of water permeance and osmotic pressure difference) under varying conditions remains insufficiently explored (37). Electroosmotic transport has received relatively little attention in the desalination field, despite its recognized importance in many other fields such as microfluidic and nanofluidic pumping (43), osmotic diodes (44, 45), and microfluidic transistors (46).

Recent studies have attempted to quantify electroosmotic flow by incorporating empirical electroosmotic coefficients into phenomenological models. However, these reported coefficients vary by several orders of magnitude across studies (table S1) (26, 27, 47), highlighting the inherent complexity of the phenomenon. This variability also emphasizes a critical knowledge gap in the mechanistic understanding of the coupled transport of ions and water molecules through IEMs, which limits accurate prediction and regulation of water flux in electrified desalination processes. Bridging this gap is essential for optimizing system design and advancing the development of efficient, scalable electrified technologies for high-salinity water treatment and brine management.

In this study, we elucidate the fundamental mechanisms governing water transport in electrified desalination systems. Using irreversible thermodynamics, we first establish the relationship between membrane potential and ionic water transport number and, subsequently, develop a method to experimentally quantify both parameters. Afterward, we calculate the total water transport including osmosis and electroosmosis and validate the results against experimental measurements, showing excellent agreement. With this validated approach, we decouple the contributions of electroosmosis and osmosis to the overall water transport, identifying the influences of key operating parameters. We propose a strategy to mitigate osmotic water loss by adhering a carbon cloth layer onto ion-exchange membranes, which enhances concentration polarization and suppresses undesired osmotic water flux. Our results demonstrate that reducing osmotic water loss markedly improves the energy efficiency of electrified desalination. The findings of this work provide a deeper understanding of water transport mechanisms in electrified desalination systems and offer practical insights for improving their performance in high-salinity wastewater treatment.

RESULTS

Irreversible thermodynamics reveals ionic water transport numbers

Electroosmosis is proportional to the water transport number of salt ions. Determining the water transport number is, therefore, critical for quantifying electroosmotic water flux. In electrified desalination processes, two primary driving forces influence the simultaneous transport of water molecules and salt ions: the osmotic pressure difference (Δπ) and the electrical potential difference (Δφ) across the membrane. These coupled transport phenomena can be described using the framework of irreversible thermodynamics (Fig. 1A) (48–50). Within the framework of linear irreversible thermodynamics, the resulting fluxes, namely, volumetric water flux (Jv) and electrical current (I), are linearly related to their corresponding thermodynamic driving forces, the osmotic pressure difference (Δπ) and the electric potential difference (Δφ), respectively. Here, z is the ion valence and F is the Faraday constant. Therefore, under the condition of zero current, the water transport number (n) can be readily calculated once the transmembrane potential difference (Δφ), i.e., the streaming potential measured across the charged membrane porous channels under an imposed osmotic pressure difference (Δπ), is determined. We note that the water transport number is related to ion hydration number and corresponds to the effective number of water molecules cotransported with ions, including both hydration shell water and water molecules carried by hydrodynamic coupling.

To measure the transmembrane electrical potential (Δφ), we used an electrochemical cell with two reservoirs separated by an IEM (Fig. 1A). Both reservoirs were filled with the same electrolyte solution (i.e., the tested electrolyte). To induce water flux without generating a net current, glucose was added to the receiving reservoir to increase its osmotic pressure. The resulting water flux with the coupled ion transport generates an electrical potential difference across the charged membrane pores. In a cation exchange membrane (CEM), this potential increases along the direction of cation migration. The rising electrical potential suppresses cation migration while accelerating anion migration to maintain zero-current condition. Conversely, in an anion exchange membrane (AEM), the potential decreases along the direction of anion transport, producing an opposite electrical profile. Therefore, the electrical potential difference is expected to be positive and negative for cations and anions, respectively (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, because salt concentrations were maintained equal in both reservoirs, the Donnan potential differences at the interfaces between the salt solution and the membrane had equal magnitudes but opposite signs. Thus, the measured electrical potential difference represents the potential drop across the membrane porous channels alone, which is directly related to the ion water transport number (Eq. 3).

Ag/AgCl reference electrodes housed in salt bridges were placed in the receiving (working) and feed (counter) reservoirs to monitor the potential. These electrodes also served to close the electrical circuit and compensate for the charge imbalance generated by selective counterion transport through the membrane. This configuration is therefore distinct from a classical concentration-driven salt diffusion setup. In addition, to account for the interferences of the background environment, we subtract the measured electrical potential without adding glucose to yield the transmembrane electrical potential (Δφ).

The measured potential difference was positive for cations and negative for anions (Fig. 1B), consistent with theoretical predictions (Fig. 1A). Notably, as ion transport continued, diffusion boundary layers formed on both sides of the membrane, which increased the magnitude of the observed potential difference (Fig. 1B). The diffusion layer thickness scales as t1/2, consistent with classical semi-infinite diffusion behavior, with detailed derivations and calculation provided in texts S1 and S2. To obtain the intrinsic transmembrane potential (Δφ), we extrapolated the fitted straight lines to the intercept on the vertical axis (Fig. 1B and fig. S1B), thereby minimizing the influence of diffusion boundary layers. This method has previously been applied to determine ion water transport numbers through peptide and protein channels (48, 51, 52).

Using this method, we measured the transmembrane electrical potentials of Li+, Na+, and K+ across three types of CEMs (fig. S2A) and those of Cl−, Br−, and I− across three types of AEMs (fig. S2B). The corresponding water transport numbers were calculated using Eq. 3 (Fig. 1, C and D), with detailed calculations provided in text S3. Notably, the determined water transport number may reflect the combined contributions of both cations and anions, with their relative contributions governed by their respective transference numbers (text S4). Nevertheless, because the IEMs exhibit high permselectivity (fig. S3), counterions dominate ion transport. Therefore, the measured water transport numbers still capture the relative trends among the ions.

Overall, for cations, the water transport number follows the order Li+ > Na+ > K+ across various types of CEMs, in line with their corresponding hydration free energies (table S2). Similarly, for anions, the trend Cl− > Br− > I− is also consistent with their hydration free energy order. These findings confirm that smaller, more strongly hydrated ions exhibit greater transmembrane potential differences. Notably, water transport numbers showed negligible variation across different types of IEMs, likely due to their similar pore sizes (53). Moreover, the measured water transport numbers closely matched the hydration numbers in bulk solution, in agreement with previous experimental and molecular dynamics studies (table S2). Previous studies have shown that IEMs typically have effective pore sizes on the order of a few nanometers, which exceed the hydrated diameters of most common inorganic ions (54–56). Hence, it is reasonable to expect that ion transport across IEMs involves little dehydration of the hydration shell. This also means that the amount of water carried along by momentum transfer is insignificant.

We further investigated the dependence of ion water transport number on electrolyte concentrations (Fig. 1E). Our results demonstrate that the water transport number remains relatively constant across salt concentrations ranging from 100 to 600 mM. Although increased salt concentration has been demonstrated to introduce deswelling in polymeric membranes, the resulting pore sizes are still believed to be larger than the hydrated ion size, leading to negligible influences of ion concentration on ion hydration behavior. This finding is particularly relevant for electrified desalination processes as it suggests that the hydration behavior of ions can be considered independent of concentration under typical operating conditions. This simplification aids in the modeling and analysis of ion transport in practical scenarios.

Water transported through the membrane is contributed by osmosis and electroosmosis

To validate our measurements of ion water transport number through IEMs, we quantified water transport in a representative electrified desalination technology, FCDI (30, 57). In addition to electroosmotic flow, water transport across the membrane can also be driven by osmotic pressure difference (Fig. 2A). In this system, mobile suspended carbon flow electrodes enable the electrode discharging outside the desalination water channel, eliminating the need for intermittent switching between charging and discharging steps, typical of conventional electrified adsorption processes.

We systematically performed constant-voltage FCDI desalination experiments using sodium chloride (NaCl) solutions of varying concentrations in a semibatch mode. In this setup, water in the desalinated channel was continuously recirculated, and the flow electrode slurries circulated between anode and cathode channels (i.e., short-circuited closed-cycle operating mode). As desalination progressed, the salt concentration in the desalination channel decreased, whereas it increased in the electrode channel (Fig. 2B). To enable consistent comparisons, all experiments were performed to achieve an effluent concentration of 8.6 mM (i.e., NaCl at 500 mg liter−1), consistent with guideline values for drinking-water salinity or total dissolved solids recommended by international and national regulatory agencies (58, 59). Under constant-voltage operating mode, increasing the feed salinity led to an enhanced current density (Fig. 2C), primarily due to the increased ionic conductivity in the FCDI system. As a result, the average salt removal rate (ASRR; Fig. 2D) increased. The charge efficiency (CE) remained consistently around 95% across all tested conditions, indicating well-controlled operation with minimal side reactions (Fig. 2D).

We further evaluated the osmotic pressure difference across the IEMs using the van’t Hoff equation, focusing on a representative case with a 600 mM NaCl feed (Fig. 2E). Salt concentrations in the desalination tests were continuously monitored using an in-line conductivity meter, whereas those in the electrode channel were calculated on the basis of the cumulative salt removal. Based on prior studies (60, 61), the removed salt predominantly resides in the aqueous phase of the flow electrode rather than the carbon.

Because osmotic water flux is proportional to the osmotic pressure difference and the membrane water permeance, we quantified the water permeance of various IEMs (Table 1). Given that osmotic water transport occurs through both the CEM and AEM, we used their average water permeance to calculate osmotic water flux. In parallel, the electroosmotic water flux was separately calculated by multiplying the ionic flux with the water transport number of each ion species. Detailed example calculations for both osmotic and electroosmotic fluxes are provided in text S3.

The total theoretical water flux was then obtained by summing the osmotic and electroosmotic components and compared against experimental measurements (Fig. 2F). The latter was quantified by measuring the mass difference before and after the desalination period in the desalination channel. Theoretical predictions closely matched the experimental determined values, with only slight deviations observed at higher feed salinities. These deviations likely stem from prolonged operation at high salinity, which triggers changes in membrane transport and potential water dissociation, both of which contribute to additional experimental water loss. Despite these slight deviations, the strong agreement validates the accuracy and robustness of our method for quantifying osmotic and electroosmotic water transport.

Water loss substantially influences the specific energy consumption (SEC) of FCDI (Fig. 2G), particularly at high salinities. The transported water leads to a decreased yield of desalinated freshwater, therefore decreasing the SEC. For example, at a feed concentration of 600 mM, the SEC increased more than 45% when accounting for the water loss (i.e., 28.8 vs 41.9 kWh m−3). This substantial increase highlights the critical influence of water transport on the overall energy efficiency of FCDI, especially when treating high-salinity waters.

Our robust theoretical framework enabled the breakdown of total water transport into osmotic and electroosmotic components (Fig. 2H). As feed salinity increased, the transported water flux from the desalination channel to the electrode channel increased. Specifically, when the feed concentration increased from 50 to 600 mM, the osmotic water flux rose markedly from 0.09 to 1.03 liters m−2 hour−1, whereas the electroosmotic water flux increased from 0.25 to 0.75 liters m−2 hour−1. Both contributions to water fluxes showed a substantial enhancement with increasing salinity, and a more detailed explanation of these relative contributions will be discussed in the following section.

Osmosis and electroosmosis are influenced by operating conditions

Unraveling the contribution of osmosis and electroosmosis to the overall water transport is of paramount importance for process optimization and membrane design. In this section, we explore the osmotic and electroosmotic water fluxes under varying operating conditions, including cell voltage, membrane types, and salt types.

ASRR depends not only on the cell voltage but also on the IEM materials (Fig. 3A). The thinnest IEM pair (M III) exhibited a higher ASRR than M I, likely due to its shorter pathways for ion transport. Although M II was the thickest IEM pair, it showed the fastest ion transport rate among the three pairs, which may be attributed to its high membrane ion exchange capacity and thus ion conductivity.

For the same IEM pair, salt type also influenced ASRR. Specifically, potassium chloride (KCl) exhibited the highest ASRR, followed by NaCl, with lithium chloride (LiCl) exhibiting the lowest. This order mirrors the diffusion coefficients of cations in free solution, suggesting that a similar trend is preserved within the IEM pores. From another perspective, K+ experiences the least ion-water friction inside the membrane pores, consistent with its smallest water transport number observed in our measurements (Fig. 1C). Overall, the ion diffusion behavior inside the membrane resembles that in free solution, supporting the notion that the pores of the IEMs are too large to impose substantial confinement effects.

By comparing the theoretically predicted transported water volume with the measured experimental water loss across all experiments under various conditions, we found an excellent agreement between those two values (Fig. 3B). This demonstrates that the theoretical model accurately described the water-salt migration behavior in actual electrified systems, thereby validating the reliability and correctness of the proposed theory. With this validated theory, we can evaluate the contribution of osmosis and electroosmosis to the total water transport.

Our experiments were designed to achieve the same separation conditions (i.e., identical feed and effluent concentrations for a given salt), thus the amount of salt removed is identical across different experiments. As a result, the electroosmotic water loss (i.e., a product of ion water transport number and ion removal) remained the same across voltages and IEM pairs (Fig. 3C). However, the volumetric water loss varied when the salt type changed due to differences in ion water transport numbers. Among the studied salts, despite identical molar salt removal, the high water transport number of LiCl resulted in 36% more electroosmotic water transport than KCl (0.53 versus 0.39 ml), corresponding to the difference in water transport numbers between K+ and Cl−.

In contrast, the osmotic water loss—a function of osmotic pressure difference, membrane water permeance, and desalination duration—varies considerably among different operating conditions even when achieving the same separation. When the applied voltage increased from 1.2 to 2.1 V, osmotic water loss decreased from 0.39 to 0.23 ml (Fig. 3C), resulting from the reduced desalination duration, which decreased from 82 min to 50 min (fig. S5). Membrane water permeance (Aw) exhibited marked variations (Table 1), consequently leading to substantial differences in osmotic water flow. For instance, the M III pair (Nafion NR 211/FAS 30) exhibited the highest average Aw (0.123 liters m−2 hour−1 bar−1) and thus the largest osmotic water loss (0.68 ml), whereas the M I pair (FKS 75/FAS 75, average Aw of 0.005 liters m−2 hour−1 bar−1) reduced the water loss to 0.04 ml. Furthermore, when treating different salt waters, variations in ASRR result in different desalination duration, which, in turn, affect the osmotic water loss.

Overall, osmosis and electroosmosis occur simultaneously yet respond disparately to system parameters. Osmosis is governed by membrane water permeance, osmotic pressure difference (concentration difference), and desalination duration, whereas electroosmosis is ion dependent, primarily influenced by the ion water transport number and the amount of ion removal. The interplay between feed concentration, applied voltage, membrane properties, and salt type governs osmosis and electroosmosis (Fig. 3D).

At the same target effluent concentrations, higher feed salinity prolongs the desalination time, increasing osmotic water loss. Simultaneously, it requires greater total salt removal, which enhances electroosmotic transport. Thus, feed concentration amplifies both osmotic and electroosmotic water loss (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, voltage predominantly influences osmosis by changing the desalination duration. Specifically, higher voltage accelerates ion migration, shortening operational time and consequently reducing osmotic water flow, while electroosmotic water transport remains intrinsically tied to the amount of removed ions. IEM type mainly influences osmotic water transport as water permeance directly dictates osmosis. Salt type influences electroosmosis via ion water transport number, although its effect is minor compared with other parameters (Fig. 3D).

Minimizing osmosis substantially enhances energy efficiency

Given that electroosmosis is proportional to the salt ions that are removed from the feed electrolyte solution, regulating osmosis presents a more achievable approach to enhance energy efficiency. Because osmotic water flow is driven by the osmotic pressure difference across the IEMs, reducing such a difference appears to be a reasonable solution. Inspired by the previous studies on forward osmosis where water flow is solely driven by the osmotic pressure difference, internal concentration polarization in the porous support layer of a composite membrane plays a critical role in determining water flux (62, 63). Thus, we incorporated a carbon cloth that is closely attached to the IEM to leverage the concentration polarization in the flow-electrode chamber (Fig. 4A). To ensure a firm attachment, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (1 ml) was sprayed between the IEM and carbon cloth. The carbon cloth layer serves two purposes. First, it magnified the effect of concentration polarization and, thus, reduces the effective osmotic pressure difference (∆πeff). Second, the high conductivity of carbon cloth can improve the overall electrical conductivity of the system.

To ensure a direct and controlled comparison, experiments were conducted under conditions where the average current density remained consistent between systems with and without carbon cloth. As a result, the time required to desalinate the feed (600 mM NaCl) to the target salt concentration was nearly identical in both cases (Fig. 4B), and the measured average current density is about the same (Fig. 4C). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy revealed that the incorporation of carbon cloth accelerated charge transfer in the electrode chamber and substantially reduced the overpotential at the flow electrode surface (fig. S10). Consequently, the applied voltage required to achieve the target desalination level was reduced from 1.80 to 1.02 V (Fig. 4C).

We also investigated the impact of carbon cloth integration on water transport, which reduced the total water flux from 1.76 (without integrating carbon cloth) to 1.27 liters m−2 hour−1. Notably, for both the carbon cloth–integrated system and the regular system, the total water fluxes were determined experimentally in the same manner, based on the mass change in the desalination channel before and after the experiments. However, the presence of the carbon cloth reduced the effective osmotic pressure difference across the membrane. As a result, the osmotic water flux in the carbon cloth–integrated system can no longer be directly calculated from the bulk osmotic pressure difference using the van’t Hoff relationship, as was done for the regular system (i.e., without integrating carbon cloth).

Given that both systems removed the same amount of salt at an identical ASRR, the electroosmotic water flux (i.e., a product of salt flux and water transport number) should be the same (Fig. 4D). Therefore, for the carbon cloth–integrated system, the osmotic water flux was calculated by subtracting the calculated electroosmotic contribution from the experimentally measured total water flux. This approach allows the effect of carbon cloth integration on reducing the effective osmotic driving force and associated water transport to be quantitatively isolated.

Our results show that, when the feed was 600 mM NaCl, the osmotic water flux decreased nearly 50% from 1.02 to 0.52 liters m−2 hour−1, yielding a 30% reduction in total water loss with the incorporation of carbon cloth (Fig. 4D). This result demonstrates that carbon cloth effectively reduces the osmotic pressure difference across the IEM by approximately half. Accordingly, the SEC is considerably reduced. This results from two effects of incorporating carbon cloth. First, the operating voltage was substantially reduced from 1.80 to 1.02 V (Fig. 4C), corresponding to a ∼43% decrease in applied voltage. Second, a ∼7% increase in product water yield (relative to total feed) was achieved, primarily attributed to the osmotic pressure being halved by the incorporation of carbon cloth. Collectively, the SEC is reduced by 50% upon incorporation of carbon cloth (Fig. 4E).

We further compare our system with literature-reported FCDI systems. In particular, we are interested in desalinating highly saline waters where water loss is appreciable. Unfortunately, there are limited reported data on those separations. To fairly compare the treatment among drastically different scenarios, we report the thermodynamic energy efficiency (TEE) (11, 64, 65). It reflects the extent to which a desalination process approaches its thermodynamic limit. A higher the TEE means that it is more thermodynamically efficient. In addition, TEE can be used for cross-system comparisons. Herein, we calculated the SEC and the specific Gibbs free energy of separation (∆g) based on literature-reported data (Fig. 4F).

Notably, many prior studies that reported SEC did not account for the contributions of osmosis and electroosmosis. In contrast, in this study, energy calculations were performed following the methodology outlined in this work, explicitly considering the effects of osmosis and electroosmosis to ensure a more accurate assessment of system performance. For published studies, we first summarized the key operational parameters, including influent concentration, effluent concentration, flow electrode solution volume, and IEM water permeance. These parameters were then used to calculate both the osmotic pressure difference and total desalination amount during the desalination process following the same methodology used to evaluate the performance of our work. The detailed data collected from the literature are provided in table S3.

We then compare SEC (fig. S11 and table S3) of this work with previously reported FCDI studies. The electrified desalination developed here outperforms other alternatives by achieving a higher TEE and enabling a greater degree of salt-water separation (Fig. 4F). In contrast, many previously reported FCDI systems hardly achieve effective seawater desalination, as reflected by the small ∆g in the surveyed literature (table S3) (11, 65).

These results demonstrate that incorporating carbon cloth can substantially improve the desalination performance, resulting from the enhanced electrical conductivity and reduced osmotic water transport. It is also worth emphasizing that the energy efficiency and separation capability can be further improved if we could minimize electroosmotic water transport. This may be achievable through materials advancements, such as reducing the pore size of IEMs. However, such exploration lies beyond the scope of the current study and warrants future investigation.

DISCUSSION

This study systematically quantified ion water transport numbers via membrane potential measurements derived from the irreversible thermodynamics. As electroosmosis is proportional to ion water transport number, these values enable theoretical calculation of electroosmosis for a given separation. We validated these calculations with the experimentally determined water transport in FCDI, demonstrating an excellent agreement across a range of operating conditions. Then, we used this robust method to decouple electroosmosis and osmosis and quantified their contributions to the total water transport across the membrane.

When varying operating conditions to achieve a target effluent concentration, our results reveal distinct influences of system parameters on water transport mechanisms. The feed salt concentration influences both osmosis and electroosmosis, whereas the cell voltage primarily affects osmosis by changing the duration of the desalination process. The type of IEM also influences osmosis due to differences in membrane water permeance. In contrast, the salt type affects electroosmosis through variations in ion water transport number, although this effect is minor compared to the other factors.

Because electroosmosis remains constant for a target separation, we minimized osmosis by inducing salt concentration polarization using carbon cloth. This modification markedly reduced water loss, enhanced energy efficiency, and successfully achieved seawater desalination to drinking water level with record-high energy efficiency for FCDI systems.

Fundamentally, our approach provides a quantitative analysis of ion water transport numbers using transmembrane electrical potential measurements. This method can be extended to investigate how membrane-specific properties modulate ion transport. The findings contribute to a deeper understanding of ion-membrane interactions, which is essential for optimizing IEMs and other separation membranes. Notably, the experimentally measured water transport number can be incorporated into classical transport models to predict electroosmotic fluxes in electrified desalination processes.

From a process perspective, our results highlight the importance of evaluating osmotic and electroosmotic contributions to water loss. The established interplay between water transport number, membrane properties, and water transport provides a foundation for the rational design of membranes and operational strategies. More broadly, these insights extend beyond FCDI to other membrane-based electrified separation technologies, including ED, reverse ED, MCDI, and hybrid electrochemical systems, advancing sustainable, high-efficiency desalination solutions for high-salinity wastewater in an area of intensifying water and energy challenges.

Membrane potential measurements

Membrane potential experiments were conducted using an H-cell. The commercial IEM (specific parameters listed in Table 1) was clamped between two chambers. Both chambers were filled with 50 ml of salt solutions of identical concentration. To establish an osmotic driving force, the right chamber was perfused with a hyperosmotic solution achieved by the addition of 1 M impermeant glucose. Magnetic stirs (speed of ∼1000 rpm) were added to both chambers to promote the homogeneity of the solution. The potential was measured with a potentiostat (Gamry, Interface 1010E, USA) connected to Ag/AgCl electrodes placed in L-shaped salt bridges positioned closely on both sides of the membrane. It is particularly important to eliminate systematic errors or unavoidable perturbations by first measuring the transmembrane electrical potential ∆φ1 in the absence of glucose, serving as a background measurement that inherently accounts for baseline contributions from the electrodes, salt bridges, and other aspects of the measurement system, followed by the measurement electrical potential ∆φ2 after glucose addition. The difference between these two values represents the true transmembrane electrical potential (∆φ=∆φ2−∆φ1) induced by ion transport across the membrane. The cell voltage curves as a function of time1/2 were fitted to derive the transmembrane potential difference at time zero. The intercept of the fitted line was then used to calculate the ion water transport number (n), with detailed procedures provided in text S3.

FCDI system and performance measurements

The self-designed FCDI cell consisted of a spacer made of a polyester sheet (thickness of ∼500 μm), a pair of ion-exchange membranes, and two graphite current collectors (100 mm by 90 mm by 7 mm) with carved serpentine flow channels (2 mm wide and 2 mm deep). The effective contact area (Aeff) between the flow electrode and the IEMs was ∼14.0 cm2. All components were held together with a pair of acrylic endplates (thickness of 10 mm). The specific device digital diagram is shown in fig. S12.

For the preparation of flow electrode, 100 g of carbon slurry was prepared by mixing 8 wt % activated carbon, 0.8 wt % Ketjen black, a salt concentration identical to that of the feed water, and appropriate deionized water. The mixture was stirred for 24 hours to ensure homogeneous dispersion. All FCDI experiments were carried out in short-circuited closed-cycle batch mode, with 30 ml of feed stream (to be desalinated) continuously circulated at a flow rate of 20 ml min−1. Flow electrodes (100 ml) were recirculated between the FCDI cell and a stirred reservoir using a peristaltic pump (BT 100-2J, Longer Precision Pump Co. Ltd., China) at a constant flow rate of 50 ml min−1. The cell voltage was provided by a dc power. The effluent conductivity was measured using an inline conductivity meter (isoPod EPU357, eDAQ, Australia) installed at the exit of the FCDI cell and was further converted to salt concentration based on a preestablished calibration curve. During all desalination experiments, the effluent concentration was maintained roughly 500 parts per million (8.5 mM for NaCl). In particular, the mass of both the feed and effluent solutions before and after desalination were precisely recorded and denoted as m0 and md, respectively.

IEM modifications

A thin layer of DMSO (∼1 ml) was sprayed onto the effective projected area between the IEM and the current collector. A piece of carbon cloth (W0S1011, CeTech), matching the projected area of the IEM, was tightly adhered to the surface of the IEM, resulting in an IEM covered with carbon cloth. Both cation and AEMs were treated in this way. Under the same operating conditions as described above, and with an influent concentration of 600 mM, the applied voltage was continuously adjusted to achieve the same average current density as that of the system without the carbon cloth-covered IEM.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy of the half-cell FCDI system

The half-cell consisted of a flow electrode compartment separated from the electrolyte chamber by an IEM. To mimic the electrochemical environment of the full FCDI system, we compared the performance with and without a carbon cloth modification. The compositions and concentrations of the flow electrode suspension were identical to those used in the desalination experiments, and the electrolyte solution was 600 mM NaCl, with graphite electrodes used to probe the impedance response. The impedance measurements were carried out using a potentiostat (Gamry, Interface 1010E, USA) in the frequency range of 0.01 Hz to 100 kHz with a sine-wave perturbation voltage (10 mV) at a dc voltage of 1.2 V.
