Effect of modeling liquids on surface roughness, gloss, and color stability of a nanohybrid resin composite: an in vitro study
Authors: Metin Bakir, Savaş Sağmak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of different modeling resins on the surface roughness, surface gloss, and color stability of a nanohybrid resin composite.MethodsForty disk-shaped specimens (10 mm diameter × 2 mm thickness) were fabricated from a nanohybrid resin composite (Estelite Asteria). The samples were divided into four groups (n = 10): control (no modeling liquid), Modeling Resin (Bisco Inc., Schaumburg, IL, USA), GC Modeling Liquid (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and Composite Wetting Resin (Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA). After polymerization, all specimens were polished using a standardized Sof-Lex Polishing System. Color stability was evaluated using a spectrophotometer after immersion in a coffee solution for 1, 3, and 7 days. Color change was calculated using the CIELAB color system (ΔE) formula. However, the surface roughness values showed significant differences between the groups (p < 0.05), with the GC modeling liquid presenting significantly higher roughness values than the control and Ultra groups. Background

Surface quality is a key parameter for the long-term clinical success of resin composite restorations. Increased surface roughness may promote plaque accumulation, tooth discoloration, secondary caries, and gingival inflammation. Roughness values exceeding approximately 0.2 μm represent a critical threshold for bacterial adhesion, which can directly affect the esthetic and biological performance of restorations [1].

The surface characteristics are influenced by several factors, including the organic matrix composition of the composite, filler particle size and distribution, application technique, and finishing–polishing procedures [2, 3]. However, during clinical application, resin composites tend to adhere to placement instruments, which may complicate manipulation for the operator.

To reduce this stickiness, clinicians have attempted to moisten instruments with substances such as isopropyl alcohol, acetone, dentin/enamel adhesive systems, and commercial lubricants [4]. Recently, manufacturers have introduced materials specifically designed to facilitate composite handling. These products are referred to in the literature as modeling liquids (ML), modeling resins, composite lubricants, or composite primers [4].

Modeling liquids are commonly used to reduce composite adhesion to instruments and facilitate anatomical contouring. They are typically applied using wetting instruments, such as spatulas, pluggers, condensers, or brushes, between composite increments or over the final layer. Clinically, this approach may help achieve smoother surface shaping and potentially reduce the finishing and polishing time [4].

Chemically, MLs generally consist of methacrylate-based monomers, such as urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA), bisphenol-A glycidyl methacrylate (Bis-GMA), and triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA), often with little or no filler content [5]. Due to their low filler concentration, these materials may create a resin-rich, superficial layer on the composite surface.

The composition of the resin matrix plays an important role in determining the polymerization behavior and mechanical properties. Therefore, the addition of an extra resin phase to the composite surface may induce clinically relevant changes in the surface properties of the composite [6].

While methacrylate monomers in MLs are analogous to those in composite matrices, research indicates that restorations formed using lubricated instruments may experience chemical or mechanical modifications. Consequently, some manufacturers recommend caution when combining materials from different resin systems [6].

Previous studies have investigated the effects of lubricants on properties such as microhardness, bond strength, and the surface characteristics of resin-based composites. However, the findings remain inconsistent, and clear clinical guidelines, particularly regarding esthetic outcomes, have not yet been established [4].

Most existing studies have focused on a single parameter (e.g., microhardness or bond strength), while investigations evaluating the simultaneous effects of modeling resins on surface roughness, gloss, and color stability remain limited [4, 7, 8].

Because MLs are largely unfilled resins, they may increase the resin matrix content at the composite surface. This superficial resin-rich layer may alter the surface topography after polishing, influence the light reflection properties, and increase water sorption. Theoretically, these changes can lead to increased surface roughness, reduced gloss, and compromised long-term color stability. However, the clinical significance of these effects remains unclear. However, the clinical implications of modeling liquid use, particularly regarding their combined effects on surface roughness, gloss, and color stability, remain insufficiently clarified [7, 8].

Accordingly, the aim of the present in vitro study was to evaluate the effect of modeling resin application on the surface roughness, surface gloss, and color stability of conventional resin composites.

The null hypotheses tested were that the application of modeling resin would not significantly affect the surface gloss (H01), surface roughness (H02), or color stability (H03) of the resin composite.

Methods

This in vitro experimental study aimed to evaluate the effects of different modeling liquids on the surface roughness, surface gloss, and color stability of a nanohybrid resin composite under standardized laboratory conditions.

Three commercially available modeling liquids were evaluated: Bisco Modeling Resin (Bisco Inc., Schaumburg, IL, USA), GC Modeling Liquid (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and Composite Wetting Resin (Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA). The supra-nanohybrid resin composite Estelite Asteria (Tokuyama Dental, Tokyo, Japan) was selected as the restorative material owing to its high esthetic performance and superior polishability. Experimental groups, main composition and manifacturer

Forty disk-shaped specimens were fabricated using a resin composite. All specimens were prepared and polished by a single operator to minimize variability. Composite increments were standardized using a calibrated mold, and modeling liquid application was performed using a consistent microbrush technique. The specimens were approximately 10 mm in diameter and 2 mm in height and were prepared using a calibrated circular Plexiglas mold. A clean glass slab was placed beneath the mold to provide support and ensure the adequate condensation of the material.

The first group was prepared without the application of modeling liquid (control group). The remaining three groups were prepared using one of the three modeling liquids. The composite was placed incrementally in approximately 0.5-mm-thick layers. After the placement of each layer, a thin layer of modeling liquid was applied by moistening the instrument. Composite placement and modeling liquid application were repeated twice per increment, resulting in a restoration consisting of four composite layers and three modeling liquid applications, simulating an incremental restoration similar to that performed clinically in anterior teeth.

After placement of all layers, the specimens were light-cured from both the top and bottom surfaces for 20 s using a light-emitting diode (LED) curing unit (Radii; SDI, Bayswater, Australia) with an irradiance of 900 mW/cm², which was continuously monitored to ensure consistent light output [9].

Following polymerization, all specimens were removed from the mold and visually inspected for defects. The prepared specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h in an incubator prior to polishing [10, 11].

All specimens were polished using a Sof-Lex disc system (3 M ESPE Dental Products, St. Paul, MN, USA). Sequential discs with decreasing abrasiveness were used: coarse (100 μm), medium (29 μm), fine (14 μm), and super fine (8 μm). To ensure standardization, polishing was performed by a single operator using a low-speed handpiece at 15,000 rpm with uniform, dry, and intermittent pressure for 20 s per disc [12]. A new disc was used for each specimen in the study. After polishing, the specimens were rinsed and ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 5 min.

Thermocycling

Following the polishing procedure, all specimens were subjected to thermocycling to simulate oral aging. The samples were thermocycled for 5000 cycles between 5 °C and 55 °C, with a dwell time of 30 s and a transfer time of 10 s, using a thermocycling device. This protocol has been widely used in laboratory studies to simulate approximately one year of intraoral temperature fluctuations and thermal aging of restorative materials [13].

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) analysis

Surface roughness measurements were performed using atomic force microscopy (AFM) (MFP-3D, Asylum Research, Santa Barbara, CA, USA). Measurements were conducted in tapping mode under ambient conditions using silicon probes with a nominal spring constant of approximately 10 N/m and a resonance frequency of approximately 250 kHz. For each specimen, surface topography images were obtained from a scanning area of 10 μm × 10 μm at a resolution of 512 × 512. Three different regions of each specimen were randomly selected and scanned to minimize the local surface variations. The images were processed using the manufacturer’s analysis software, and the average surface roughness (Ra) values were calculated from height data. The mean Ra value obtained from the three scans was used for the statistical analysis.

Gloss measurement

Surface gloss measurements were performed for all groups using a glossmeter (ETB6-6 Glossmeter; Shanghai Total Industrial Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) with a 60° angle. The results were expressed in terms of gloss units (GU). For each specimen, measurements were taken from three randomly selected points, and the mean value was used for the statistical analysis. To ensure proper specimen positioning and eliminate the influence of ambient light, a custom-made black polytetrafluoroethylene mold with a thickness of 10 mm was placed over the specimens during the measurements.

Color measurement

Color measurements were performed using a digital spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade V; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) designed for precise and reliable shade determination of natural teeth and restorative materials. The color parameters (L*, a*, and b*) were analyzed according to the standards defined by the Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage (CIE). Additional color parameters, including the hue angle (h°) and chroma (C*), were calculated from these values. The hue angle represents the color position on the color wheel, where 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270 °correspond to red, yellow, green, and blue, respectively. Chroma (C*) represents color saturation or intensity relative to brightness.

In this study, the color changes caused by immersion in black coffee were evaluated using colorimetric analysis. The coffee solution was prepared using a standard infusion ratio of 5 g of coffee in 150 mL of boiled water (Nespresso, Nestlé Romania SRL, Bucharest, Romania). The specimens were immersed in the solution for 7 days and allowed to dry in air after their removal. Color measurements were performed at baseline and repeated on days 1, 3, and 7. The CIE L*, a *, and b *parameters were recorded before and after immersion. Color differences were calculated using the classical CIELAB formula (ΔEab), which remains widely used in dental materials research and allows comparison with previous studies.

Statistical analysis

A priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1 software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on an effect size of 0.8, significance level of 0.05, and statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 10 specimens per group. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the surface gloss data showed a normal distribution, comparisons among groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test was applied to determine pairwise differences between groups. Because the surface roughness data did not show a normal distribution, intergroup comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons.

Changes in color difference (ΔE) over time and between groups were evaluated using a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (Repeated Measures ANOVA – General Linear Model). In this model, time (days 1, 3, and 7) was considered a within-subject factor, and group (control, Bisco, GC, and Ultra) was considered a between-subject factor. Multiple comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction. Descriptive statistics are presented in tables as means, standard deviations, medians, minimums, and maximums.

AFM analysis

AFM analysis revealed noticeable differences in the surface topography among the groups. The control group exhibited a relatively homogeneous surface with shallow micro-irregularities. In contrast, the specimens treated with the modeling liquids demonstrated more pronounced surface features and irregularities. In particular, the GC modeling liquid group presented a more heterogeneous surface morphology, consistent with the higher surface roughness values obtained. Discussion

In this in vitro study, the effects of different modeling resins on the surface roughness, surface gloss, and color stability of a nanohybrid resin composite were evaluated. According to the findings of this study, the modeling resin did not have a statistically significant effect on surface gloss or color stability, whereas a significant increase in surface roughness was observed in some groups. Accordingly, the null hypothesis tested in this study was accepted for surface gloss and color stability but rejected for surface roughness.

Surface gloss is an important parameter that influences the optical performance and esthetic success of restorative materials and is largely associated with the surface topography. In the present study, the application of the same finishing and polishing protocol to all specimens may have standardized the surface topography and minimized the potential influence of the modeling resins. Previous studies have reported that finishing and polishing procedures are among the most critical factors determining the surface characteristics of composite materials, and that appropriate polishing systems can significantly improve surface gloss [2, 3]. Therefore, the thin resin layer produced by the modeling resins was largely removed during the polishing procedure.

Gloss is closely related to the surface smoothness and light reflection properties of composite materials. Previous studies have suggested that modeling resins have a limited influence on the optical properties of resin composites. Paolone et al. [31] reported that the use of modeling liquids did not significantly affect translucency or surface-related optical properties. Similarly, Bayraktar et al. [7] found no significant differences in surface characteristics after the use of modeling resins. These findings support the results of the present study, in which no significant differences in gloss values were observed between the groups.

Although statistically significant differences in surface roughness were observed in specific groups, these differences did not result in measurable differences in surface gloss. Additionally, the high polishability and spherical filler structure of the composite material may have contributed to the enhanced light reflection and surface uniformity. The thin resin layer introduced by the modeling liquids is unlikely to significantly alter the optical behavior of the polished surface.

Surface roughness is a critical parameter that influences both esthetics and plaque accumulation. Previous studies have reported varying effects of modeling resins on the surface roughness of resin composites. Maia et al. [30] reported that modeling liquids did not significantly alter surface roughness after polishing procedures, whereas other studies suggested that resin-rich superficial layers formed by modeling agents may increase surface irregularities depending on the material composition and polishing protocol [8, 22]. In the present study, a statistically significant increase in surface roughness was observed in the GC group compared with the control and Ultra groups. This finding suggests that the effect of modeling liquids on surface topography may depend on the chemical composition and filler characteristics of the material used.

Surface roughness is a critical parameter that influences both esthetics and plaque accumulation. Previous studies have reported varying effects of modeling resins on the surface roughness. Maia et al. [30] found that the use of modeling liquids did not significantly alter surface roughness values after polishing procedures. Similarly, Sedrez-Porto et al. [23] reported that modeling resins minimally influenced the surface characteristics of composite materials when appropriate finishing techniques were applied. These findings are consistent with those of the present study, in which no significant differences were observed among the groups.

The lack of significant differences in surface roughness among the groups may be attributed to the polishing protocol used in this study. Proper finishing and polishing procedures can effectively remove the superficial resin-rich layer formed during composite placement, thereby standardizing surface topography. In addition, the spherical nanosized fillers of the composite used in this study may have contributed to achieving a smoother surface after polishing. The limited thickness and low viscosity of the modeling resins may also explain their negligible effect on surface roughness.

Another potential consequence of the formation of a resin-rich superficial layer is the reduction in the mechanical resistance at the composite surface. Because the resin matrix phase has a lower wear resistance than the filler particles, greater material loss may occur in these regions during polishing or mechanical wear. This phenomenon may contribute to increased surface roughness and potentially affect the long-term surface integrity of restorations [14–17].

Surface roughness values are also clinically relevant. It has been reported in the literature that surface roughness values exceeding approximately 0.2 μm represents a critical threshold for bacterial plaque retention [1, 18]. In the present study, the mean roughness values of some modeling resin groups approached or slightly exceeded this threshold. Importantly, the surface roughness values observed in the GC group exceeded the clinically critical threshold of 0.2 μm, which has been associated with increased plaque accumulation and potential biological complications. This situation may represent a potential risk for biofilm accumulation and surface staining in the clinical environment. However, it should be considered that the polishing protocol used in this study may differ from clinical practice and that restorations may behave differently under intraoral conditions involving mechanical wear, thermal fluctuations, and biofilm formation [19–21].

The surface morphology findings obtained from AFM analyses also supported the roughness results. More heterogeneous surface morphologies and localized irregularities were observed in some specimens treated with modeling resins. These findings suggest that modeling resins may produce microstructural differences on the composite surface and influence surface behavior after polishing. In particular, resin matrix–rich regions may undergo different wear patterns during polishing, contributing to the formation of micro-irregularities in surface topography [22–24] Although AFM analysis provided qualitative support for the roughness findings, future studies incorporating quantitative topographic parameters would strengthen these observations.

When the color stability results were evaluated, ΔE values increased over time in all groups; however, no statistically significant differences were found between groups with and without modeling resins. Although no significant differences were observed between groups, several ΔE values exceeded the clinically acceptable threshold (3.3), indicating that discoloration may still be perceptible in clinical conditions. This finding suggests that color changes in resin composites are largely related to factors such as the organic matrix structure of the material, water sorption, and pigment penetration [25, 26]. These findings suggest that the clinical use of modeling resins does not adversely affect the evaluated properties under the conditions tested.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that chromogenic beverages such as coffee can significantly affect the color stability of resin composite materials. In an in vitro study by Jrady et al. [27], the authors evaluated the combined effects of different polishing systems and coffee staining on bleach-shade resin composites and reported that prolonged exposure to coffee led to clinically perceptible discoloration, with surface finishing playing a critical role in stain susceptibility. Similarly, Ersoz et al. [28] compared the staining effects of hot and cold coffee on resin-based composites and found that temperature influenced discoloration, with hot coffee causing greater color changes due to increased pigment penetration and matrix softening. Furthermore, a recent systematic review by Alenezi et al. [29] concluded that coffee, particularly due to its high chromogen content and low polarity, has a strong staining potential compared to other beverages such as tea, leading to significant ΔE changes over time. In line with these findings, the increase in ΔE values observed in the present study indicates that immersion in coffee solution enhanced pigment absorption by the composite material.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that chromogenic beverages such as coffee can significantly affect the color stability of resin composite materials. In the present study, ΔE values increased over time in all groups following coffee immersion, indicating progressive pigment absorption by the composite material. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies evaluating coffee-induced discoloration in resin composites [27–29].

Despite the staining effect of coffee, the use of modeling resins did not significantly influence color stability in the present study. This finding is consistent with previous investigations reporting that modeling liquids have minimal or no clinically significant effect on the optical properties of resin composites after appropriate finishing and polishing procedures [7, 23, 30, 31]. The limited influence of modeling liquids may be explained by the extremely thin resin layer formed during application, which is likely removed during polishing. In addition, the high filler loading and spherical filler structure of the supra-nanohybrid composite used in this study may have contributed to reduced pigment adsorption and improved color stability. Furthermore, the relatively hydrophobic resin matrix may have limited water sorption and staining susceptibility.

One clinically important finding of this study is that modeling resins did not produce a significant adverse effect on the surface gloss or color stability of composite restorations. In clinical practice, modeling resins facilitate the manipulation of composite materials by reducing their adhesion to instruments and enabling easier anatomical shaping of restorations [4]. Nevertheless, considering that certain products may increase surface roughness, careful finishing and polishing procedures are recommended.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted under in vitro conditions and therefore cannot fully replicate the complex biological and mechanical environment of the oral cavity. In addition, only a single composite material was evaluated, and long-term aging factors such as mechanical wear or biofilm formation were not investigated. Furthermore, the thickness of the modeling liquid layer was not quantified, which may have introduced variability and should be considered a limitation. Only one composite material was evaluated, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other resin composite systems. Future studies including different composite systems, longer aging protocols, and biofilm interactions may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of modeling resins on restorative materials. However, future studies should also include delivery systems as alternative unit agents for increased clinical change. However, the ΔEab formula was used to allow comparison with previous studies; however, future studies may benefit from using the CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀) formula due to its improved perceptual accuracy.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, modeling resins did not significantly affect surface gloss or color stability of the tested supra-nanohybrid resin composite. However, certain modeling liquids resulted in a statistically significant increase in surface roughness. Although discoloration increased over time following immersion in a chromogenic solution, the use of modeling liquids did not significantly influence color change. Considering that increased surface roughness may be associated with plaque accumulation, careful finishing and polishing procedures are recommended when using modeling agents.
