A deep-learning framework for brain tumor segmentation via three-dimensional mass-preserving geometric transformation
Authors: Tsung-Ming Huang, Kai-Qian Zheng, Wen-Wei Lin, Tiexiang Li, Shing-Tung Yau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article presents a robust and efficient framework for brain tumor segmentation based on deep learning. We introduce a novel three-dimensional (3D) mass-preserving geometric transformation (MPGT) that employs a homotopy method to transform irregular brain magnetic resonance (MR) images into standardized solid cubes. This transformation preserves local mass ratios while maintaining global structural integrity, providing a structured input for deep learning models. Furthermore, we propose a modified two-phase segmentation strategy to minimize inference time and a postprocessing technique to enhance lesion-wise performance. Extensive validation on the Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) Challenge 2023 dataset demonstrates that our method, when integrated with nnU-Net, achieves competitive Dice scores of 0.9282 (Whole Tumor), 0.8812 (Tumor Core), and 0.8527 (Enhanced Tumor). These results are superior to or comparable with top-ranking competition entries.

Introduction

Gliomas are the most common type of malignant brain tumor in adults. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), brain tumors can be classified into four grades, from 1 to 4, on the basis of their histological characteristics and molecular features. Grade 1 tumors are typically slow-growing and benign, whereas grade 4 tumors, including glioblastomas (GBMs), are the most aggressive and malignant forms. GBMs are among the deadliest types of cancer due to their location and invasive nature. The mutation status of isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) is considered the most critical marker for glioma diagnosis [1]. Deep learning techniques [2–4] have been shown to accurately and objectively predict the status of IDH mutations on the basis of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data. However, in most studies involving IDH mutations and deep learning, glioma regions must be segmented along the lesion edges [5]. Thus, detecting and segmenting brain tumors on the basis of MRI data are essential tasks.

The Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) Challenge [6–10] serves as a robust platform that offers a publicly available dataset for model development. The objective of this competition is to evaluate the latest and most advanced methods for segmenting diffuse gliomas in the brain. The BraTS Challenge 2023 includes 2040 baseline 3D MR images of adult gliomas, with the aim of segmenting the whole tumor (WT), tumor core (TC), and enhanced tumor (ET) regions. Each MR image features four modalities: native T1, post-contrast T1-weighted (T1Gd), T2-weighted (T2) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR). Each modality is represented as a tensor image with dimensions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$240 \times 240 \times 155$$\end{document}.

In the past decade, U-Net-based deep learning architectures, such as ResUNet [11], SegResNet [12], nnU-Net [13], and MedNeXt [14], have emerged as crucial and effective methods for brain tumor segmentation. Luu et al. [15] utilized nnU-Net as a baseline, integrating BraTS-specific optimizations from [16] along with their own enhancements to accurately segment tumor regions. Similarly, [17] introduced an ensemble approach combining DeepSeg [18], the improved nnU-Net [15], and DeepSCAN [19] for the automatic detection of glioma boundaries in preoperative MR images.

Building upon these foundational approaches, the field has seen rapid progress, with more sophisticated models and strategies emerging, particularly evident in works from 2024–2025. Noteworthy recent contributions include enhanced multi-modal multi-scale contextual aggregation with attention fusion for improved segmentation [20], the application of YOLO-based deep learning for automated brain tumor segmentation and classification [21], continued advancements in U-Net based methods through transfer learning for segmentation enhancement [22], and comprehensive deep learning frameworks that address both 3D segmentation and survival prediction [20]. For instance, Ferreira et al. [23], a top-performing entry in the BraTS 2023 challenge, utilized generative adversarial networks and registration techniques to significantly expand training data for nnU-Net and Swin UNETR [24] algorithms. Their approach involved proposing three methods for synthetic data generation and combining all inferred results to predict tumor regions. Maani et al. [25], another strong participant in BraTS 2023, leveraged SegResNet and MedNeXt with deep supervision and sophisticated post-processing to achieve competitive results. Kasliwal et al. [26] recently introduced ReFuSeg, a regularized multi-modal fusion model specifically designed to achieve precise brain tumor segmentation even with missing modalities and in the presence of imaging artifacts. These works collectively highlight the continuous drive for more robust and generalizable segmentation methods.

However, limitations in GPU memory remain challenging owing to the large size of each BraTS image, with dimensions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$240 \times 240 \times 155$$\end{document}. To address this issue, random cropping of the raw brain images is commonly employed; however, this approach may not capture all relevant information from the original data. To better preserve the global features of the MRI data, a two-stage optimal mass transport (OMT) method with the mass-preserving parameterization was proposed in [27]. In the first stage, the irregular brain domain in the MR image is transformed into a solid unit sphere using the mass-preserving OMT \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_b$$\end{document}. In the second stage, the cubic domain is mapped to a unit sphere using the volume-preserving OMT \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_c$$\end{document}. The combined OMT map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f = f_c^{-1} \circ f_b$$\end{document} can subsequently be used to convert the irregular brain domain into a cubic form.

Building on this OMT approach, Lin et al. [28] introduced a novel two-phase residual U-Net algorithm for detecting tumor regions. In Phase I, the authors employed the residual U-Net with OMT tensors to accurately predict the WT region. In Phase II, they generated a smooth density function based on the predicted WT region, thereby enlarging the potential tumor region in the new OMT tensors, which serve as inputs for the residual U-Net to effectively train the model.

The geometric transformation achieved through the mass-preserving OMT approach serves as a critical framework for data preprocessing in deep learning applications for brain tumor segmentation. The aim of this paper is to enhance (i) the efficiency of the mass-preserving geometric transformation (MPGT) and the two-phase segmentation method and (ii) the lesion-wise Dice scores through a proposed postprocessing step. Ultimately, the goal is to establish a more robust and efficient deep learning framework for brain tumor segmentation. The three main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows. Furthermore, a higher value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma $$\end{document} results in a larger tumor volume within the MPGT tensor, potentially increasing sensitivity but also distortion. Specifically, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma =0$$\end{document}, the transformation is purely volume-preserving. As \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma $$\end{document} increases, regions with higher histogram intensity (typically tumors) are expanded in the target cubic domain. We empirically limit \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma $$\end{document} to [0, 2] based on previous studies [28, 31], which showed that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma > 2$$\end{document} leads to excessive distortion of non-tumor tissues, degrading the boundary definition of the tumor core. In Sect. 4.3, we perform a sensitivity analysis to justify this selection and examine its impact on segmentation performance.

In the training data, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_w$$\end{document} in (8) represents the labeled WT region. In this case, it is straightforward to define the density function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{\gamma }$$\end{document} in (9) to construct the MPGT tensors and subsequently train the model. However, the region \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_w$$\end{document} is not available for the online validation or testing data. To address this challenge, Lin et al. [28] proposed a two-phase OMT algorithm to construct the associated density function.

In Phase I of the two-phase OMT algorithm, the value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _\gamma (v) = \exp (\gamma \mathbb {H}_{\text {e}}(v))$$\end{document} at each voxel v in the brain region is used to estimate the potential WT region \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_w$$\end{document} for the online validation and testing data. Once this region \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_w$$\end{document} is established, in Phase II, the MPGT tensors are constructed using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_w$$\end{document}, and the inference results are obtained from the trained model. This approach requires constructing two MPGT tensors with different density functions for each inference dataset.

To streamline the inference process, we replace the MPGT tensors with the raw data as training inputs in Phase I, eliminating the need to construct MPGT tensors for the testing data in this phase. To address the observed difference between the Dice and lesion-wise Dice scores in (10)-(11), we establish volumetric thresholds for the WT, TC, and ET regions. If the number of voxels in a disconnected WT/TC/ET region in the inference data is below the specified threshold, we classify this region as a false positive and relabel it as a non-tumor region. Importantly, in the BraTS 2023 challenge, a volumetric threshold of 50 voxels is used, and lesions smaller than this threshold are not evaluated as true lesions [36].

Our analysis of the training data (Fig. 8) shows that there are only 32 disconnected WT lesions with tumor voxels in the interval (50, 250], 50 TC lesions with tumor voxels in (50, 450], and 90 ET lesions with tumor voxels in (50, 1450]. On average, our method achieves a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.31\times $$\end{document} speedup. This improvement arises because computing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(f^{*}_{1})^{-1} \circ f^{*}_{\rho _\gamma }: \mathcal {M} \rightarrow \mathcal {C}$$\end{document} in the volumetric OMT method is time consuming for the large meshes involved (more than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^5$$\end{document} vertices in both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {M}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {C}$$\end{document}; see Subsection 2.3). In contrast, our homotopy-based approach transforms the interior vertices of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {M}$$\end{document} into the interior of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {C}$$\end{document} more efficiently by restricting the expensive computations to the spherical boundary and propagating the deformation inward. This makes our geometric transformation more practical for large 3D medical images.

In terms of wall-clock time, the average runtimes per case for (i) constructing the MPGT tensor, (ii) inference in Phase I, (iii) inference in Phase II, and (iv) inverting the labels from the MPGT tensor back to the raw MRI space and ensembling the final prediction are 87.08, 8.17, 4.37, and 1.48 s, respectively. Thus, the total average time required by our complete pipeline to obtain a final inference result per case is approximately 101.11 s.

The Dice and lesion-wise Dice scores in each row of Table 3 show noticeable differences. As indicated in (10)-(11), these discrepancies suggest the presence of false negative or false positive lesions. To mitigate the impact of small false positives and improve lesion-wise Dice scores, we apply volumetric thresholds after ensembling Phase I and Phase II results, thereby filtering out small disconnected lesions. On the basis of the analysis in Subsection 3.3, we use thresholds of (450, 150, 80) voxels for (WT, TC, ET) for nnU-Net, MedNeXt, and SegResNet. Furthermore, Ferreira et al. [23] used thresholds of 250 and 1450 voxels for the WT region. To facilitate comparison with [23], we also apply thresholds of (250, 150, 80) and (1450, 150, 80) for nnU-Net.

In [23], the authors used generative adversarial networks and registration techniques to generate 23049 training samples for their three deep-learning models. This data generation process took approximately two weeks. In contrast, our method generates only 5004 training MPGT tensors in Phase II using four different values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma $$\end{document}, while still preserving the global MRI features in each tensor. Moreover, the “All" ensemble in [23] combines nine models (three architectures with three synthetic data schemes), each trained via five-fold cross-validation, leading to an ensemble of 45 inference passes for the final prediction. In our case, we ensemble only ten inference results (5 folds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times $$\end{document} 2 phases) using nnU-Net.

Despite using less training data and fewer inference passes than the winner in [23], our results in Table 6 show that our method achieves superior lesion-wise Dice scores and HD95 metrics, except the lesion-wise Dice score for WT at the thresholds (1450, 150, 100). These findings highlight the effectiveness of the MPGT representation with hyperparameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma $$\end{document} and our ensemble strategy.

The same dataset were used for training and online validation in BraTS 2021, BraTS 2022, and BraTS 2023. The results in Tables 6 and 7 indicate that our method consistently outperforms, or is competitive with, the approaches in [15, 17, 23, 25] in terms of both Dice and lesion-wise Dice scores.

Conclusion

The two-stage optimal mass transport (OMT) method with a mass-preserving map was proposed by Lin et al. [27] to transform irregular brain MR images into cubes, with the generated 3D tensor used as the input to a 3D U-Net model. Building on this OMT framework, Lin et al. [28] introduced a two-phase residual U-Net algorithm. In Phase I, MPGT tensors are used to accurately predict the WT region, whereas in Phase II, a new MPGT tensor is constructed based on this prediction, requiring two MPGT tensors per case.

In this article, we propose a modified mass-preserving geometric transformation that uses a homotopy method to reduce computational cost in the two-stage process. We further develop a modified two-phase segmentation approach to minimize inference time for testing data. By replacing MPGT tensors with randomly cropped raw data in Phase I, we only need to compute MPGT tensors in Phase II.

We also introduce postprocessing steps that ensemble the Phase I and Phase II results and apply volumetric thresholds for tumor detection to improve lesion-wise performance on the testing data. Our numerical results show that using SegResNet, nnU-Net, and MedNeXt within the proposed framework yields comparable Dice scores and lesion-wise Dice scores to state-of-the-art models during online validation, demonstrating the robustness of the proposed approach. Among these models, nnU-Net achieves the highest Dice scores (0.9282, 0.8812, 0.8527) and lesion-wise Dice scores (0.9043, 0.8692, 0.8558), outperforming or showing comparable performance to the models in [15, 17, 23, 25].

Regarding generalizability and external validation, we acknowledge that, although our method demonstrates high performance on the well-curated BraTS 2023 dataset, the lack of validation on independent external cohorts is a current limitation for assessing robustness in real-world scenarios. The BraTS dataset is highly curated, whereas real-world clinical data often contains artifacts, varying resolutions, and diverse scanner protocols that are not fully represented here, which can lead to significant domain-shift issues. The hyperparameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma $$\end{document}, used to enlarge tumor regions in the MPGT space, was empirically optimized for gliomas, and its applicability to other tumor types (e.g., meningiomas or metastases) requires further validation. Furthermore, the postprocessing volume thresholds (450, 150, 80) voxels were derived from the training set statistics. Therefore, future work will focus on adaptive thresholding techniques and extensive cross-dataset validation on external cohorts (e.g., the Erasmus Glioma database (EGD) dataset [37], the LUMIERE dataset [38], the University of Pennsylvania glioblastoma (UPenn-GBM) dataset [39], The Cancer Genome Atlas Glioblastoma Multiforme (TCGA-GBM) dataset, and the Cancer Genome Atlas Low Grade Glioma (TCGA-LGG) dataset [40]) to ensure robustness against such domain shifts and to further improve the method’s generalizability.
