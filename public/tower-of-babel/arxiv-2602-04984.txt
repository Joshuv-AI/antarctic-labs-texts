Branch-and-price strikes back for the k k -vertex cut problem
Fabio Ciccarelli, Fabio Furini, Christopher Hojny, Marco Lübbecke

Abstract
Given an undirected graph, the k k -vertex cut problem ( k k -VCP) asks for a minimum-cost set of vertices whose removal yields at least k k connected components in the resulting graph. The k k -VCP is an important problem in network optimization, with applications in infrastructure protection and epidemic containment. We present a new extended integer linear programming (ILP) formulation that unifies and strengthens existing models and serves as the foundation for a new branch-and-price algorithm for the k k -VCP. An in-depth theoretical study enables us to devise algorithmic components such as tailored branching rules that preserve the structure of the pricing problems, as well as valid inequalities and symmetry-handling techniques. We also show that our new model dominates all previous ILP formulations of the k k -VCP in terms of their linear relaxations, which theoretically justifies the computational effectiveness of our approach. Extensive computational experiments against state-of-the-art methods demonstrate substantially improved performance, both in terms of instances solved to proven optimality and running times. On the full benchmark of 608 instances, our algorithm consistently outperforms all competitors and is able to solve 73 previously unsolved instances within the time limit of one hour.

Introduction

Graph disconnection problems via critical vertex deletion are fundamental in network optimization, due to their theoretical significance as well as their broad applications in telecommunication and transportation systems, computational biology, network vulnerability assessment, and immunization against epidemics
(
Lalou et al. (2018)
)
.
As connectivity underlies the behavior, function, and resilience of complex networks, understanding the mechanisms through which it can be altered, influenced, or reorganized is important for both graph theory and real-world applications.
Within this broad family of
critical vertex detection problems
(CVDPs), a prominent class is concerned with fragmenting the graph through the deletion of vertices.

In this paper, we focus on the
k
-vertex cut problem
(
k
-VCP), an important CVDP that has received considerable attention in recent years; see, e.g.,
Marx (2006)
,
Berger et al. (2014)
,
Cornaz et al. (2019)
,
Furini et al. (2020)
.
Formally, let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a simple undirected graph, where
\mathcal{V}
is the set of vertices and
\mathcal{E}
the set of edges.
For any subset of vertices
S\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, the induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}[S]=(S,\mathcal{E}[S])
consists of the vertices in
S
together with the edges

\mathcal{E}[S]=\{\,\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{E}:u,v\in S\,\},

i.e., all edges of
\mathcal{G}
whose endpoints lie in
S
.
A
connected component
is an inclusion-wise maximal connected induced subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
.
A
vertex cut
is a subset of vertices
\mathcal{V}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
whose removal disconnects the graph, i.e., the induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{0}]
contains at least two connected components.
If the induced subgraph contains at least
k
connected components, then
\mathcal{V}_{0}
is called a
k
-vertex cut
.
The (weighted)
k
-VCP can therefore be formally defined as follows:

Definition 1.1

(
k
-vertex cut problem)

Given a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, a nonnegative cost
c_{v}
for each vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
, and an integer
k\geq 2
, the
k
-vertex cut problem
(
k
-VCP) consists in finding a minimum-cost set
\mathcal{V}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
such that the induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{0}]
contains at least
k
connected components.

An equivalent structural characterization
of
k
-vertex cuts that we use throughout the paper is as follows.
A subset of vertices
\mathcal{V}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
is a
k
-vertex cut if and only if the
induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{0}]
admits a partition of its vertex
set into
k
nonempty subsets

\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{k}

that are pairwise disconnected, in the sense that no edge of
\mathcal{E}
has endpoints
in two different subsets.
Each subset may contain one or more connected components; what matters is that the

k
subsets are mutually disconnected in the induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{0}]
.
For ease of reference, in the remainder of the paper, we will refer to each subset

\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{k}
as a
cluster
of the graph.
From a complementary perspective, the
k
-VCP can also be viewed as finding a
maximum-cost set of vertices
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{V}_{i}
such that
the induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}\!\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{V}_{i}\right]
contains at least

k
connected components.

The
k
-VCP plays a central role in applications where one aims to disrupt communication, isolate subnetworks, or assess the robustness and structural vulnerability of complex systems, see e.g.,
Furini et al. (2020)
.
The computational complexity of the
k
-VCP depends critically on the value of
k
.
When the deletion budget is taken as parameter, the decision version of the problem is
W[1]-hard
(
Marx (2006)
)
.
If
k
is part of the input, the problem is already
\mathcal{NP}
-hard,
as follows from the hardness of the
k
-way vertex cut problem
(
Berger et al. (2014)
)
,
whose decision version coincides with the decision version of the
k
-VCP.
Moreover, even when
k
is fixed, the problem remains
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
for every
k\geq 3

(
Cornaz et al. (2019)
)
.
On the other hand, the case
k=2
is polynomially solvable, as it reduces to
computing
O(|\mathcal{V}|^{2})
minimum
s
–
t
cuts via vertex-splitting
over all pairs of vertices, which in the unit-cost case coincides with
computing the vertex connectivity of the graph.

Three main approaches currently represent the most effective exact methods for solving the
k
-VCP.
A first, off-the-shelf option consists in using a general-purpose
integer linear programming
(ILP) solver applied to a compact ILP formulation with a polynomial number of variables and constraints, as proposed in
Cornaz et al. (2019)
.
Beyond this generic strategy, two dedicated exact algorithms have been introduced in the literature:
a
branch-and-price
(BP) algorithm
(
Cornaz et al. (2019)
)
, based on an ILP formulation with an exponential number of variables,
and a
branch-and-cut
(BC) algorithm
(
Furini et al. (2020)
)
, based on an ILP formulation with an exponential number of constraints.
Although these exact methods are quite effective, they still fail to solve many benchmark instances to optimality.
Motivated by these limitations, the main methodological contribution of this paper is to introduce a new exact algorithm for the
k
-VCP that markedly improves upon the BP approach and enables the solution of significantly larger and more challenging instances.

1.1
ILP models for the
k
-VCP from the literature

In this section we recall the three ILP formulations that have been proposed in the literature for the
k
-VCP.
For each model, we outline its modeling structure and the exact solution method originally associated with it.
These models, together with their corresponding algorithms, will be included in the computational experiments of this article and used as benchmarks against the new BP algorithm proposed in this paper.

1.1.1
The compact ILP model

For each vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
and each index
i\in\mathcal{K}=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
,
let
y_{iv}
be a binary variable taking value
1
if
v
is assigned to the
i
-th cluster of the graph and value
0
otherwise.
Vertices assigned to at least one cluster are retained in the graph, whereas vertices
not assigned to any cluster belong to the set
\mathcal{V}_{0}
and form the

k
-vertex cut.
Using these variables,
the
compact
ILP formulation for the
k
-VCP, introduced by
Cornaz et al. (2019)
and
denoted by
\text{ILP}_{\text{C}}
, reads as follows:

(
\text{ILP}_{\text{C}}
)

\displaystyle\max\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c_{v}\,y_{iv}

(1a)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}y_{iv}

\displaystyle\leq 1,

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{V},

(1b)

\displaystyle y_{iu}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{i\}}y_{jv}

\displaystyle\leq 1,

\displaystyle i\in\mathcal{K},\,\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{E},

(1c)

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}y_{iv}

\displaystyle\geq 1,

\displaystyle i\in\mathcal{K},

(1d)

\displaystyle y_{iv}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle i\in\mathcal{K},\ v\in\mathcal{V}.

(1e)

This formulation is closely related to the one by
Borndörfer et al. (1998)
for the
k
-separator
problem and uses the same assignment variables.
The objective function (
1a
) maximizes the total weight of vertices
assigned to the clusters of the graph.
Constraints (
1b
) ensure that each vertex is assigned to at most one
cluster.
Constraints (
1c
) prevent adjacent vertices from being assigned to
different clusters, thereby ensuring mutual disconnection of the clusters.
Constraints (
1d
) require each cluster to be nonempty, enforcing the
presence of at least
k
connected components.
The compact formulation involves a polynomial number of binary variables and
constraints, and can therefore be solved directly by a general-purpose ILP solver.

1.1.2
The extended ILP model

Let

\mathscr{S}=\{S\subseteq\mathcal{V}:S\neq\emptyset\}

be the family of all nonempty subsets of vertices. Furthermore, for any

U\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, let
\mathscr{S}(U)=\{S\in\mathscr{S}:S\cap U\neq\emptyset\}

denote the subsets in
\mathscr{S}
that contain at least one vertex of
U
.
For singleton sets
\{v\}
,
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we write
\mathscr{S}(v)
instead of
\mathscr{S}(\{v\})
, i.e.,
\mathscr{S}(v)=\{S\in\mathscr{S}:v\in S\}
denotes the subsets in
\mathscr{S}
that contain vertex
v
.
For each subset
S\in\mathscr{S}
, let
\lambda_{S}
be a binary variable taking value
1
if
S
is selected as one of the
k
clusters of the solution and value
0
otherwise.
Denote by
\mathscr{C}
the family of all cliques in
\mathcal{G}
.
Let
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathscr{C}
be an
edge-covering
family of cliques of
\mathcal{G}
,
that is, a family such that for every edge
\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{E}
there exists at least one
clique
C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
containing both
u
and
v
. Using the
\bm{\lambda}
-variables, the
extended
ILP formulation for the
k
-VCP, introduced by

Cornaz et al. (2019)
and denoted by
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
, reads as follows:

(
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
)

\displaystyle\max\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\bigg(\sum_{v\in S}c_{v}\bigg)\,\lambda_{S}

(2a)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle=k,

(2b)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(v)}\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle\leq 1,

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{V},

(2c)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(C)}\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle\leq 1,

\displaystyle C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime},

(2d)

\displaystyle\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle S\in\mathscr{S}.

(2e)

The objective function (
2a
) maximizes the total cost of the
k
clusters
selected in the solution. It generalizes the original unweighted version—which
maximizes the cardinality
|S|
of each chosen subset—by incorporating vertex
costs.
Constraint (
2b
) ensures that exactly
k
subsets are selected, and thus
that the solution consists of
k
clusters.
Constraints (
2c
) require that each vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
appears in
at most one selected subset, guaranteeing that the chosen subsets are vertex-disjoint
and therefore represent distinct clusters.
Constraints (
2d
) enforce that the selected clusters are mutually
disconnected. Since
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
is an edge-covering family of cliques, requiring
that at most one subset in
\mathscr{S}(C)
can be selected for each
C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}

ensures that no two chosen subsets contain adjacent vertices. Consequently, the
k

selected clusters of vertices are pairwise disconnected in the graph. In
Cornaz et al. (2019)
, a family of cliques of polynomial size with respect to the number of edges of the graph is considered.

The extended formulation
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
contains an exponential number of variables.
As a consequence, solving the model requires a
column generation
(CG)
approach embedded within a BP algorithm. At each node of the
branching tree, the LP relaxation of Formulation (
2
) is solved through
a CG procedure, where new variables are generated by solving the so-called
pricing
problem
(PP). We refer the interested reader to
Barnhart et al. (1998)
,
Lübbecke & Desrosiers (2005)

for further details on CG and BP algorithms, as well as to the book by

Desaulniers et al. (2005)
and the two recent online monographs
Desrosiers et al. (2024)
and
Uchoa et al. (2024)
.
In what follows, we briefly describe the two principal components of the BP algorithm of

Cornaz et al. (2019)
: the pricing problem and the
branching scheme.

At the root node of the BP algorithm,
Cornaz et al. (2019)
show that the pricing problem can be solved via solving a sequence of minimum cut problems on an auxiliary network, enabling the use of efficient max-flow/min-cut algorithms.
Although the pricing problem is originally described for unit vertex costs, the same approach extends naturally to the weighted setting by simply adjusting the vertex profits in the reduced-cost computation, and both the complexity and the solution methods remain unchanged.
We refer the reader to Section
3.1.2
for further details.

A dedicated branching scheme is proposed in
Cornaz et al. (2019)
, operating on two
levels. At the first level, a “partially” assigned vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
is identified,
namely one for which the sum of the variables over subsets containing
v
is
fractional. Two branches are created: one forcing
v
into the vertex cut and the
other forcing
v
to belong to exactly one cluster. This branching preserves the
structure of the pricing problem: in the first branch,
v
is simply forbidden in
newly generated subsets, while in the second the pricing formulation is unchanged.
Since this is not sufficient to guarantee integrality, a second branching
level, akin to Ryan-Foster branching
(
Ryan & Foster (1981)
)
, is applied to vertex pairs. Here, two
vertices
u\in\mathcal{V}
and
v\in\mathcal{V}
outside the vertex cut that are “partially” assigned to the
same cluster (i.e., the sum of variables over subsets containing both is
fractional) trigger two additional branches: enforcing that they belong to the same
cluster or to different clusters.
In the
“same-cluster” branch, the pricing problem is preserved by contracting

\{u,v\}
into a single supervertex, whereas in the “different-clusters” branch
an incompatibility constraint forbidding subsets containing both vertices must be
enforced. The latter destroys the min-cut structure of the pricing problem, which
must then be solved using an ILP formulation.

1.1.3
The natural ILP model

A
subgraph
of
\mathcal{G}
is any graph

\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{F}),\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{F}))
such that

\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, with every edge
of
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{F})
having both endpoints in
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{F})
.
For any subgraph
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{G}
, we denote by
\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(v)
the degree
of vertex
v
in
\mathcal{F}
, i.e., the number of edges of
\mathcal{F}
incident to
v
.
A subgraph
\mathcal{F}
is
spanning
if
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{V}
, and it is

cycle-free
if it contains no cycles (equivalently, if
\mathcal{F}
is a
forest
).
We denote by
\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{G}}
the family of all
spanning cycle-free subgraphs

of
\mathcal{G}
, that is,

\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{G}}=\{\,\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{F})):\mathcal{F}\text{ is acyclic}\,\}.

For each vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we introduce a binary variable
x_{v}
that takes value 1
if
v
belongs to the
k
-vertex cut and 0 otherwise.
Using the
\bm{x}
-variables,
the
natural
ILP formulation for the
k
-VCP, introduced by
Furini et al. (2020)

and denoted by
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
, reads as follows:

(
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
)

\displaystyle\min\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c_{v}\,x_{v}

(3a)

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\big(\deg_{\mathcal{F}}(v)-1\big)x_{v}

\displaystyle\geq k-|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{F})|,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\in\mathscr{F}_{\mathcal{G}},

(3b)

\displaystyle x_{v}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{V}.

(3c)

The objective function (
3a
) minimizes the total weight of the vertices
removed in the
k
-vertex cut. Constraints (
3b
) form an exponential family
of inequalities derived from a bilevel interpretation of the
k
-VCP and from the key
property that a graph has at least
k
connected components if and only if every
acyclic subgraph contains at most
|\mathcal{V}|-k
edges. We refer to

Furini et al. (2020)
for further details.

The natural formulation
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
contains an exponential number of constraints.
As a consequence, solving the model requires a
cut generation
approach
embedded within a branch-and-cut algorithm. At each node of the branching tree,
the LP relaxation of Formulation (
3
) is solved by iteratively
generating violated inequalities from the family (
3b
). These cuts are
produced by solving a separation problem that searches for a maximum-weight
acyclic subgraph of the current residual graph. As shown by
Furini et al. (2020)
,
this separation can be performed in polynomial time for both integer and
fractional solutions, allowing the exponential family of constraints to be
added only when violated.

1.2
Paper outline and contributions

This section reviews the main contributions of our work and specifies the sections in which they are presented.
Section
2
presents our main theoretical contribution: a new extended ILP formulation that unifies and extends both the natural and the previous extended formulations by simultaneously making use of the binary variables from both models.
Crucially, we prove that integrality needs to be enforced only on the variables inherited from the natural formulation, whereas the variables coming from the extended formulation can be safely relaxed to continuous without affecting optimality—an essential property that greatly simplifies the design of the new branch-and-price algorithm.
The
linear programming
(LP) relaxation of the new extended model strictly dominates the LP relaxations of both classical models, thereby providing a substantially tighter foundation for exact solution methods.
Building on the new formulation, Section
3
introduces our main methodological contributions, namely a branch-and-price algorithm that incorporates a tailored column generation procedure, a branching strategy fully compatible with pricing problem, and dedicated symmetry-handling and primal-heuristic components that improve the computational performance.
Section
4
reports computational results on standard benchmark instances from the literature, including comparisons with state-of-the-art exact methods and ablation tests designed to assess the contribution of each main component of our new approach.
The experiments show that our method establishes a new state-of-the-art for the
k
-VCP, outperforming all existing exact algorithms and solving 73 open instances within the time limit of one hour.
Finally, Section
5
summarizes the contributions of the paper and outlines directions for future research.

2
A new extended ILP model for the
k
-VCP

In this section, we introduce a new extended ILP formulation for the
k
-VCP that
simultaneously exploits the vertex-cut variables
\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{V}}
of

\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
and the cluster-selection variables
\bm{\lambda}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathscr{S}}
of

\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
. By combining the strengths of both formulations within a unified model,
we obtain an enhanced representation of the
k
-VCP given by
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}
below.
We will show, through a detailed theoretical study, that the variables inherited from
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
can in fact be treated as continuous.
The new model therefore does not only shed light on hidden properties of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
, but has also important algorithmic consequences for solving the
k
-VCP as we will discuss in detail in Section
3
. As in
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
, variable
x_{v}
indicates whether vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
is contained in the vertex cut, and, analogously to
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
, variable
\lambda_{S}
indicates whether
S\in\mathscr{S}
forms one of the
k
mutually disconnected clusters of the remaining graph.

Using these variables,
the new
extended
ILP formulation for the
k
-VCP
and denoted by
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}
, reads as follows:

(
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}
)

\displaystyle\min\,\,\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c_{v}\,x_{v}

(4a)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle=k,

(4b)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(v)}\lambda_{S}+x_{v}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{V},

(4c)

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(C)}\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle\leq 1,

\displaystyle C\in\mathscr{C},

(4d)

\displaystyle\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle S\in\mathscr{S},

(4e)

\displaystyle x_{v}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{V}.

(4f)

The objective function (
4a
) coincides with the one of
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
and minimizes the total cost of the the selected
k
-vertex cut.
Constraints (
4b
) and (
4d
) serve the same purpose as in
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
, i.e., they guarantee to select exactly
k
mutually disconnected clusters and enforce adjacent vertices in the remaining graph to be contained in the same cluster, respectively.
To explain Constraint (
4c
), observe that (
2c
) in
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
takes left-hand side value 1 if
v\in\mathcal{V}
is not removed from the graph and of 0 otherwise.
That is,
x_{v}
in (
4c
) can be interpreted as a slack variable in Constraint (
2c
).
The correctness of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}
immediately follows from the correctness of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
.

We now discuss the main theoretical contribution of this section.

Theorem 2.1

Let

2.1
Partially relaxing integrality in
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}

To prove Theorem

First, we derive an alternative formulation of the

2.1.1

2.2
Strength of the LP relaxations

In the previous section, we have derived
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}
for the
k
-VCP, thus complementing the existing models
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
and
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
from the literature.
At first glance, however, it is unclear which model is best suited for solving the
k
-VCP.
One common means to compare the strength of an ILP model is to assess the objective value of its LP relaxation:
the closer the LP objective value is to the objective value of an optimal mixed-integer solution, the arguably more powerful the bounding mechanism of branch-and-bound (or branch-and-price) is.
We therefore compare the objective values of the LP relaxations of the different methods in the following.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be an undirected graph that is endowed with vertex costs
\bm{c}\in\mathds{R}^{\mathcal{V}}
, and let
k
be a positive integer.
We then denote by
\zeta_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathcal{G},\bm{c},k)
,
\zeta_{\mathrm{E1}}(\mathcal{G},\bm{c},k)
, and
\zeta_{\mathrm{E2}}(\mathcal{G},\bm{c},k)
the optimal objective value of the LP relaxations of
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
,
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
, and
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}
, respectively. These LP models are called:
\text{LP}_{\text{N}}
,
\text{LP}_{\text{E1}}
, and
\text{LP}_{\text{E2}}
.

Theorem 2.8

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be an undirected graph, let
\bm{c}\in\mathds{R}^{\mathcal{V}}
, and let
k
be a positive integer such that the
k
-vertex cut problem is feasible.
Then,

\zeta_{\mathrm{N}}(\mathcal{G},\bm{c},k)\leq\zeta_{\mathrm{E2}}(\mathcal{G},\bm{c},k)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\zeta_{\mathrm{E1}}(\mathcal{G},\bm{c},k)\leq\zeta_{\mathrm{E2}}(\mathcal{G},\bm{c},k).

As a consequence,
\text{LP}_{\text{E2}}
is not weaker than both
\text{LP}_{\text{N}}
and
\text{LP}_{\text{E1}}
.
In the remainder of this section, we provide a proof of this theorem.

Lemma 2.9

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be an undirected graph and let

.
Let

.
Then,

\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{F})}\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}^{\subseteq}(e)}\lambda_{S}\leq|\mathcal{V}|-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}-k.

Proof 2.10

Proof
We observe

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{F})}\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}^{\subseteq}(e)}\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}|\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{F})[S]|\;\lambda_{S}\leq\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}(|S|-1)\;\lambda_{S}=\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}|S|\lambda_{S}-\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\sum_{v\in S}\lambda_{S}-\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\lambda_{S}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(v)}\lambda_{S}-\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\lambda_{S}\overset{\eqref{eq:zig-base:vertex_cover}}{=}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}(1-x_{v})-\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\lambda_{S}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{V}|-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}-\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}}\lambda_{S}\overset{\eqref{eq:zig-base:cardinality}}{=}|\mathcal{V}|-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}x_{v}-k,

\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(\{u,v\})}\lambda_{S}\leq 1

Since the clique constraints (

) are valid for the augmented model by Corollary

To conclude the proof, we show that we can relax equality in (

Let

be an optimal solution of (
10
).
The assertion follows if we can establish that

satisfies
\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(v)}\lambda_{S}+x_{v}=1
.
This is indeed the case, since every vertex

is contained in a clique
C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
.
Hence, (

) and non-negativity of the
\bm{\lambda}
-variables imply
\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(v)}\lambda_{S}\leq 1
.
If there was a

such that

,
we could improve the objective value by decreasing

because

.
This, however, contradicts optimality of

.
∎

To construct the edge-covering family of cliques

3.1.2
The pricing problem

When solving the LP relaxation of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
via the RMP, the integrality
constraints (
10f
) are relaxed to non-negativity
constraints, and only a subset
\overline{\mathscr{S}}\subseteq\mathscr{S}
is
considered.
Note that we do not impose the upper bound
x_{v}\leq 1
,
v\in\mathcal{V}
, in the RMP, because in every
optimal
solution
x_{v}\leq 1
will hold, which is a consequence of the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

To find an optimal solution of the LP relaxation of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
, it might not be enough to solve the RMP for the selected set
\overline{\mathscr{S}}
.
Given a solution
(\bm{x},\bm{\lambda})
of the RMP, we therefore need to decide whether there exists a variable
\lambda_{S}
for some
S\in\mathscr{S}\setminus\overline{\mathscr{S}}
with negative reduced cost.
This problem is called the pricing problem and coincides with the separation problem of a dual solution associated with
(\bm{x},\bm{\lambda})
.
Let
\sigma\geq 0
be the dual variable associated with (
10b
), let
\mu_{v}\geq 0
,
v\in\mathcal{V}
, be the dual variables associated with (
10c
), and let
\pi_{C}\geq 0
,
C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
, be the dual variables associated with (
10d
).
The dual constraints associated to the
\bm{\lambda}
-variables are

\sigma+\sum_{v\in S}\mu_{v}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}:\\
S\cap C\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\pi_{C}\leq 0,~~~~S\in\overline{\mathscr{S}}.

Given a dual solution
(\sigma,\bm{\mu},\bm{\pi})
, the pricing problem for
\bm{\lambda}
-variables is to decide if there exists
S\in\mathscr{S}\setminus\overline{\mathscr{S}}
such that

\sigma+\sum_{v\in S}\mu_{v}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}\colon\\
S\cap C\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\pi_{C}>0\qquad\iff\qquad\sum_{v\in S}\mu_{v}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}:\\
S\cap C\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\pi_{C}>-\sigma.

Deciding whether such
S
exists is therefore equivalent to solving the optimization problem

\max_{S\subseteq\mathcal{V}}\left\{\sum_{v\in S}\mu_{v}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}\colon\\
S\cap C\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\pi_{C}:|S|\geq 1\right\}.

(11)

Indeed, there exists a violated dual constraint if and only if the maximum takes a value that is larger than
-\sigma
.

In the following, we discuss how to solve the pricing problem.
To better understand its structure, it is useful to express it as an ILP.
To model the selected set
S
, let
\varphi_{v}
,
v\in\mathcal{V}
, be a binary variable
that takes value 1 if and only if
v
belongs to
S
.
Moreover, for every clique
C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
, let
\psi_{C}
be a binary variable that
takes value 1 whenever the generated subset
S
intersects clique
C
(though the
converse does not necessarily hold).
An ILP model of the pricing problem, for a given dual
solution
(\sigma,\bm{\mu},\bm{\pi})
, is then

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\varphi}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{V}}\\[0.75346pt]
\bm{\psi}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathscr{C}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\left\{\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mu_{v}\,\varphi_{v}\;-\;\sum_{C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}}\pi_{C}\,\psi_{C}:\quad\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\varphi_{v}\geq 1,\quad\varphi_{v}\leq\psi_{C},\;C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime},~v\in C\right\}.

(12)

The objective function in (
12
) maximizes the violation of the dual constraint.
The first constraint (
cardinality constraint
) prevents selecting the empty set,
while the second family of constraints (
precedence constraints
) ensures that

\psi_{C}
takes value 1 whenever the chosen set
S
contains a vertex belonging to the
clique
C
.

The pricing problem (
11
) can be solved in polynomial time.
As shown by
Cornaz et al. (2019)
, if the cardinality constraint is removed from (
12
),
the resulting ILP has a totally unimodular constraint matrix.
Indeed, the precedence constraints define a bipartite
incidence structure between vertices and cliques, while all remaining conditions
are simple (integer) bounds. Consequently, the LP relaxation of the reduced model is
integral and can be solved efficiently.
However, removing the cardinality constraint may yield the trivial solution

S=\emptyset
. To enforce
S\neq\emptyset
, one can solve the LP relaxation of the
reduced model once for each vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
, adding the fixing constraint

\varphi_{v}=1
. Total unimodularity is preserved, so each resulting LP again
admits an integral optimal solution, which corresponds to a valid nonempty set

S_{v}
. Among all such sets, one achieving the largest objective value is
selected as the solution of the pricing problem.
Since a polynomial number of LPs is solved and each LP can be solved in
polynomial time, the pricing routine is polynomial as well. By the classical
equivalence between optimization and separation

(
Grötschel et al. (1981)
)
, this also implies that the LP relaxation of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
can be solved in polynomial time.

Complementing the LP techniques for solving the pricing problem, we discuss a combinatorial algorithm that is faster in practice.
A variant of this algorithm was already used by
Cornaz et al. (2019)
for
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
.
To make this paper self-contained and since their explanation of this approach omits many details, we provide a detailed explanation. Moreover, we provide some important algorithmic improvements that fully preserve the use of the combinatorial algorithm for the pricing phase.
We exploit a result by
Barahona & Jensen (1998)
who describe how to optimize a linear objective subject to precedence constraints (like the one in (
12

Their idea is to solve the maximization problem by computing a minimum cut in an auxiliary network
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{N}),\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{N}))
.
Note that this approach neglects the cardinality constraint and we will discuss how to enforce it at the end of the section.

The vertex set of the network is given by

\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{N})=\{s,t\}~\cup~\mathcal{V}~\cup~\mathscr{C}^{\prime},

where
s
and

are the source and sink of the network.
The arc set
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{N})
consists of (i) the arcs
(s,v)
with capacity
\mu_{v}
for all

(C,t)
with capacity
\pi_{C}
for all

(v,C)
with infinite capacity for all

and

An

is a partition

of

such that

and

The arcs crossing a cut
(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})
are the arcs
\varUpsilon(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})=\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{N}):u\in S^{\prime},\;v\in T^{\prime}\}

{\{v\in\mathcal{V}:(s,v)\notin\varUpsilon(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})\}}

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus S}\mu_{v}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}\colon\\

3.1.3
The branching scheme

A central component of a branch-and-price algorithm is the branching scheme.
For
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
,
Cornaz et al. (2019)
suggest at two-step branching procedure.
In
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
, however, the
\bm{\lambda}
-variables are continuous and it is not necessary to branch on them.
Instead, we only branch on the
\bm{x}
-variables. We use the reliable pseudo cost branching rule
(
Achterberg (2007)
)
.

When solving the RMP at a node of the branching tree that is different from the root node, then also the branching decisions need to be respected by the pricing problem.
If there exists a vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
such that
x_{v}=1
holds due to branching, then
v
is contained in the vertex cut and, therefore, cannot be included in the generated set
S
.
This decision can be incorporated into the pricing problem by adding the arc
(v,t)
of infinite capacity to the network used for solving the pricing problem.
Due to the infinite capacity, this arc will never be contained in a minimum cut, thus representing the branching decision
x_{v}=1
.

To model the branching decision
x_{v}=0
, we need to make sure that
v
is not contained in the neighborhood

N(S)=\{w\in\mathcal{V}:\exists u\in S\text{ with }\{u,w\}\in\mathcal{E}\}

of the set
S
that is generated by the pricing problem.
Indeed, since
x_{v}=0
, vertex
v
needs to be contained in some cluster.
But if
v\in N(S)
this would mean that
v
is not contained in
S
and the cluster
S^{\prime}
containing
v
is connected with
S
—a contradiction to a
k
-vertex cut.
To incorporate the neighborhood condition into the pricing problem, we can add arcs
(w,v)
with infinite capacity for all
w\in\mathcal{V}
with
\{v,w\}\in\mathcal{E}
.
Thus, in a minimum cut, it is impossible to include a neighbor of
v
into
S
while excluding
v
from
S
.

3.1.4
The separation problem for the clique -partitioning constraints (
4d
)

In principle, it is possible to strengthen formulation
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
by explicitly separating violated constraints of type (
10d
), rather than restricting ourselves to the inequalities associated with the initial edge-covering family of cliques
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
. Given a fractional solution
(\bm{x},\bm{\lambda})
of the LP relaxation, the corresponding separation problem asks whether there exists a clique
C\subseteq\mathcal{V}
such that
\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(C)}\lambda_{S}>1
,
and, if so, to identify a clique with maximum violation.
In order to fully assess the strength of formulation
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
, we also implemented a branch-and-price-and-cut algorithm in which the clique partitioning constraints (
10d
) are separated exactly at the root node. This setting allows us to exploit the full potential of these inequalities and to benchmark the best bound that can be obtained from
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
. The computational results reported in Appendix
E
indicate that, for the instances considered, a comparable dual bound can already be achieved by adding only the constraints associated with the edge-covering family of cliques
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
introduced in Section
3.1
. In other words, the initial edge-covering family of cliques
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
suffices to obtain a bound of very high quality, which is in most cases almost indistinguishable from that obtained with exact separation at the root. Since the corresponding separation problem is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard and adds a computational overhead, our final algorithm therefore does not include the exact separation of clique partitioning constraints and relies exclusively on the inequalities generated from
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
. Further details, including the branch-and-price-and-cut performance and the proof of
\mathcal{NP}
-hardness of the separation problem, are provided in Appendix
E
.

3.2
Symmetry handling techniques

To further enhance solving
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
, we observe that the
k
-vertex cut problem admits structurally equivalent solutions if the underlying graph is symmetric.
Consider, for example, the minimum 2-vertex cut problem on a cycle of length
n\geq 4
, and assume its vertices are consecutively labeled by
1,\dots,n
.
Then, a family of solutions is given by
\{1,3\},\{2,4\},\dots,\{n-2,n\},\{1,n-1\}
, i.e., one removes two vertices which are at distance 2 from each other.
Although these solutions are structurally identical, they look differently to a solver because they are encoded by different variables.
In the MIP literature, it is well-known that, when not handled appropriately, the presence of such symmetries can considerably hinder solving integer programs by branch-and-bound
(
Pfetsch & Rehn (2019)
)
.
For this reason, a wealth of techniques has been developed to handle symmetries within branch-and-bound in the last two decades.
The main goal of these techniques is to prune parts of the search tree when it is known that another part contains symmetric information, which arguably accelerates branch-and-bound.
The branch-and-price literature knows problem specific symmetry breaking and, of course, elimination of model symmetry by aggregation of identical subproblems. However, we may additionally have symmetry in the input instance. This can be exploited for pruning the branch-and-price tree using the generic symmetry handling machinery built into solvers.
In the following, we do exactly this when solving
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
. To the best of our knowledge, our application of symmetry handling (on what can be called the original variables) is the first of its kind in a branch-and-price context.

To formally introduce our approach, we require some notation.
A permutation
\gamma
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
acts on an
n
-dimensional vector
\bm{y}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
by permuting its indices, i.e.,
\gamma(\bm{y})\coloneqq(y_{\gamma^{-1}(1)},\dots,y_{\gamma^{-1}(n)})
.
Such a permutation
\gamma
defines a
symmetry
of the integer program
\min\{{\bm{c}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}:\bm{A}\bm{y}\leq\bm{b},\bm{y}\in\mathds{Z}^{n}\}
, where
\bm{A}
is a matrix and
\bm{b}
and
\bm{c}
are vectors of suitable dimensions, if for all
\bm{y}\in\mathds{Z}^{n}
we have that
\bm{A}\bm{y}\leq\bm{b}
holds if and only if
\bm{A}\gamma(\bm{y})\leq\bm{b}
holds, and
{\bm{c}}^{\top}{\gamma(\bm{y})}={\bm{c}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}
.
That is,
\gamma
preserves both feasibility and the objective value of a solution.
In the following, we want to derive a class of symmetries for
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
.
As mentioned above, a natural class of symmetries is given by symmetries of the underlying graph.
To formally define graph symmetries, let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be an undirected graph with vertex costs
\bm{c}\in\mathds{R}^{V}
.
An
automorphism
of
\mathcal{G}
is a map
{\pi\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}}
such that
c_{v}=c_{\pi(v)}
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
and
\{\pi(u),\pi(v)\}\in\mathcal{E}
for all
\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{E}
.
That is,
\pi
can be considered a relabeling of the vertices of
\mathcal{G}
that preserves vertex costs and adjacency.

Proposition 3.6

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be an undirected graph with vertex costs
\bm{c}\in\mathds{R}^{V}
, let
\gamma
be an automorphism of
\mathcal{G}
, and let
k
be a positive integer.
Let
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{S})_{S\in\mathscr{S}}
.
Then,
\gamma
defines a symmetry of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
, where
\gamma(\bm{x})=(x_{\gamma^{-1}(1)},\dots,x_{\gamma^{-1}(n)})
, and
\gamma(\bm{\lambda})=(\lambda_{\gamma^{-1}(S)})_{S\in\mathscr{S}}
, where
\gamma^{-1}(S)=\{\gamma^{-1}(v):v\in S\}
.

For a proof, we refer the reader to Appendix
A
.

Knowing a class of symmetries of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
, we can apply state-of-the-art methods for handling them.
A common feature of most existing symmetry handling techniques is to define a lexicographic order on the variable space and to enforce that only those solutions can be computed that are lexicographically maximal in their class of symmetric solutions
(
van Doornmalen & Hojny (2025)
)
.
In a branch-and-price context, however, the challenge arises that not all variables are known explicitly.
We therefore suggest to define the lexicographic order only on the
\bm{x}
-variables and ignore the
\bm{\lambda}
-variables for symmetry considerations.

We have experimented with two approaches for handling symmetries.
For both approaches, we assume that we are given access to a set
\Gamma
of automorphisms of
\mathcal{G}
.
To explain the differences between these approaches, let us assume
{\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,n\}}
.

The first approach defines a global lexicographic order
\geq_{\mathrm{lex}{}}
, where
\bm{x}\geq_{\mathrm{lex}{}}\bm{y}
holds for vectors
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
if
\bm{x}=\bm{y}
or, for the first position
i
at which
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
differ, we have
x_{i}>y_{i}
.
Given an automorphism
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we enforce
\bm{x}\geq_{\mathrm{lex}{}}\gamma(\bm{x})
by the lexicographic reduction algorithm
(
van Doornmalen & Hojny (2025)
)
.

The second approach defines for every node of the branch-and-bound tree a possibly different partial lexicographic order.
To define the order at a node
b
of the branch-and-bound tree, let
x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{j}}
be the ordered sequence of branching variables from the root node to node
b
.
Two vectors
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
then satisfy the partial lexicographic order
\geq_{\mathrm{lex}{,b}}
at node
b
if
(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{j}})\geq_{\mathrm{lex}{}}(y_{i_{1}},\dots,y_{i_{j}})
.

Ostrowski (2009)
has shown that a valid symmetry handling approach is to enforce
\geq_{\mathrm{lex}{,b}}
at every node
b
of the branch-and-bound tree.
We enforce this by the lexicographic reduction algorithm and the orbital fixing method
(
Margot (2003)
,
Ostrowski et al. (2011)
)
, which are both shown to be compatible by
van Doornmalen & Hojny (2025)
.

Preliminary experiments have shown that the second approach yields better reductions of running time.
For this reason, we will only discuss the impact of the second approach in our computational study.

3.3
Further algorithmic enhancements

Let
\Xi(\mathcal{G},\bm{c})
be the minimum total cost of a vertex set whose removal disconnects one connected component of
\mathcal{G}
. It is worth noting that, if
\mathcal{G}
is connected and
c_{v}=1
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
, then
\Xi(\mathcal{G},\bm{c})
coincides with the vertex connectivity of
\mathcal{G}
.
If
\mathcal{G}
is instead disconnected, then
\Xi(\mathcal{G},\bm{c})
equals the minimum (possibly weighted) vertex connectivity among the connected components of
\mathcal{G}
.

Then, every optimal solution of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
satisfies

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}c_{v}x_{v}\geq\Xi(\mathcal{G},\bm{c}),

(13)

and inequality (
13
) can be added to
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
to strengthen its LP relaxation.
We refer to (
13
) as
connectivity cut
.
The value
\Xi(\mathcal{G},\bm{c})
can be computed in polynomial time by solving a maximum-flow problem on an auxiliary network.

Preliminary computational experiments indicate that the effectiveness of the connectivity cut depends on the value of
k
.
For moderate values (up to
k=15
), the cut typically strengthens the LP relaxation and improves the overall performance, while incurring only a limited computational overhead.
For larger values of
k
, the cut may instead become less beneficial and interfere with the internal branching mechanisms of the solver.
For this reason, in our computational study we activate the connectivity cut only for
k\leq 15
.

Next to strengthening the LP relaxation, having access to a good initial feasible solution for the
k
-VCP allows to prune nodes of the BP tree already at an early stage of the branching process.
To this end, we employ a constructive heuristic, which we call
Iterative Disconnection Heuristic
(IDH).

Starting from
\mathcal{V}_{0}\leftarrow\emptyset
, IDH repeatedly considers the induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{0}]
and, as long as it has fewer than
k
connected components, proceeds as follows. For each connected component
H\subseteq\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{0}
in the residual graph which is not a clique, IDH evaluates
\Xi(\mathcal{G}[H],\{c_{v}\}_{v\in H})
, i.e., the minimum total cost of vertices whose removal disconnects the induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}[H]
.
It then selects a component
H^{\star}
for which this cost is smallest and adds the minimum-cost vertex set that disconnects
\mathcal{G}[H^{\star}]
to
\mathcal{V}_{0}
.
The heuristic terminates once
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{0}]
has at least
k
connected components, or reports failure if every connected component of
\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{0}]
is a clique, since in this case no vertex removal can split a component into multiple connected components. Details are provided in Appendix
C
.

4
Computational experience

Our computational study pursues three main objectives.
The first goal is to assess the impact of the main algorithmic enhancements introduced in the new BP algorithm based on
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
, described in Section
3
, denoted by BP
⋆
, and implemented in
SCIP
, a solver for ILP models which allows the inclusion of additional variables at the nodes of the branching tree.
The second is to compare the performance of BP
⋆
with that of the BP algorithm based on
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
proposed by
Cornaz et al. (2019)
and denoted by BP
†
.
The third goal is to benchmark BP
⋆
against state-of-the-art exact approaches from the literature, namely those based on
\text{ILP}_{\text{C}}
and
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
, both tackled using
CPLEX
, a commercial solver for ILP models.
Throughout this section,
COMP
denotes the approach that uses
CPLEX
to solve
\text{ILP}_{\text{C}}
, strengthened by a symmetry-breaking preprocessing as proposed in
Cornaz et al. (2019)
.
We denote by
HYB
the BC algorithm based on an enhanced version of
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
, implemented within
CPLEX
, which allows the inclusion of additional constraints at the nodes of the branching tree.
This model is further strengthened by incorporating a coefficient downlifting procedure for Constraints (
3b
) together with additional valid inequalities, as proposed by
Furini et al. (2020)
.

All experiments are conducted on the benchmark instances for the
k
-VCP introduced by
Furini et al. (2020)
.
The benchmark contains 304 instances, each considered in two variants: an unweighted version, where all vertices have unit cost, and a weighted version obtained by assigning integer vertex costs in the interval
[1,10]
.
The instances cover values of
k\in\{5,10,15,20\}
.
A detailed description of the full benchmark set of instances is provided in Section
D
.

All experiments comparing BP
⋆
against BP
†
, HYB, and COMP were executed single-threaded with a time limit of one hour on the compute cluster of the Chair of Operations Research at RWTH Aachen University, running Debian 11. The implementations of HYB and COMP are publicly available at
https://github.com/paoloparonuzzi/k-Vertex-Cut-Problem
, while the code for BP
†
was obtained through personal communication with the authors.
The cluster nodes are equipped with Intel Xeon L5630 quad-core processors operating at
2.13\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
and
16\text{\,}\mathrm{GB}
of DDR3 RAM.

The remainder of this section is organized as follows.
Section
4.1
describes the computational setup and the implementation details of BP
⋆
.
Section
4.2
presents an ablation study aimed at quantifying the contribution of each algorithmic component to the overall performance of BP
⋆
and at comparing BP
⋆
with BP
†
.
In Section
4.4
reports a detailed performance comparison between BP
⋆
and the other state-of-the-art exact approaches, namely
COMP
and
HYB
. Finally, in Section
4.5
, we compare the strength of the LP relaxations of the different ILP models.

4.1
Computational setup and implementation details of BP
⋆

BP
⋆
has been implemented in
C++
using on the BP framework
SCIP
9.2.3
(
Bolusani et al. (2024)
)
, which we extended through custom plugins to support the tailored column generation scheme.
LP relaxations within
SCIP
are solved using the LP solver
SoPlex
7.1.5.
Maximum flow problems arising in the pricing subproblem and in the initial IDH are solved using the
LEMON
graph library
(
Lemon (2014)
)
.
The complete
C++
source code, together with all benchmark instances and detailed computational results, is publicly available at
https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17897865
.
This implementation is intended to facilitate reproducibility and to serve as a reference platform for the development and fair comparison of future exact algorithms for the
k
-VCP and related problems.

4.2
Evaluation of components of BP
⋆
and comparison with BP
†

Extensive preliminary experiments showed that the construction of the edge-covering family of cliques used in Constraints (
10d
) has a significant impact on the performance of BP
⋆
. For this reason, in addition to the constructive algorithm described in Section
3.1.1
, we test two alternative strategies.
The first alternative uses a modified version of the constructive algorithm in which, during the construction of a clique, a vertex is added only if it is connected to all vertices currently in the clique by uncovered edges. This modification produces an
edge-partitioning
family of cliques, meaning that each edge belongs to exactly one clique. We denote the corresponding variant of BP
⋆
by BP
{}^{\star}_{a}
.
The second alternative uses the edge set itself as an edge-partitioning family of cliques. The corresponding variant of BP
⋆
is denoted by BP
{}^{\star}_{b}
.

A second key factor in the performance of BP
⋆
is the relaxation of Constraints (
4b
) and (
4c
) from equalities to inequalities, as in (
10b
) and (
10c
). We therefore test a third variant of BP
⋆
, denoted as BP
{}^{\star}_{c}
, which uses the equality constraints.

\mathscr{C}^{\prime}

. This confirms that using Constraints (
10b
) and (
10c
) yields a significant performance gain.

†
uses an edge-partitioning family of cliques for Constraints (
2d
), as in BP
{}^{\star}_{a}
. BP
†
solves 165 instances and is therefore substantially outperformed by BP
⋆
and all its variants except BP
{}^{\star}_{b}
. Interestingly, the comparison between BP
†
and BP
{}^{\star}_{a}
shows that, even when using the same type of clique family, the new algorithm achieves a marked improvement.

In the following, we highlight the main differences between BP
⋆
, based on
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
, and BP
†
, based on
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
, and discuss the reasons we think are behind their performance gap. We first observe that the construction of the clique family
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
has a significant impact on the strength of the LP relaxation of both ILP formulations (see also Section
4.5
). In particular, the edge-covering family of cliques used in BP
⋆
induces stronger constraints than the edge-partitioning family adopted in BP
†
.

Another important difference concerns Constraints (
2b
) and (
10b
). BP
†
enforces the equality constraint (
2b
), which requires selecting exactly
k
clusters, whereas BP
⋆
uses the inequality constraint (
10b
), which only requires selecting at least
k
clusters. This difference can significantly affect the column generation process. In particular, the inequality constraint does not force the algorithm to generate columns corresponding to clusters that merge multiple connected components, if these are associated with already existing columns.
For instance, suppose that columns associated with two clusters, each containing a single connected component, have already been generated: if an optimal solution of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
requires a single cluster obtained by merging these two components, BP
†
must continue the column generation process until the corresponding merged cluster is produced. In contrast, BP
⋆
may terminate earlier, since the two connected components can be selected separately and still satisfy Constraint (
10b
).

The column generation process of the two algorithms is also affected by an additional element of difference between them. BP
†
enforces Constraints (
2c
), which require that each vertex belongs to at most one cluster in any feasible solution of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
. As introduced in Section
1.1.2
, BP
†
adopts a bilevel branching scheme: at the first level, a partially covered vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
(i.e., one for which
\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(v)}\lambda_{S}
is fractional) is selected, and two child nodes are created by forcing
v
either to belong to the cut (
\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(v)}\lambda_{S}=0
) or to a cluster (
\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(v)}\lambda_{S}=1
). Such branching decisions may require the generation of new columns in order to restore feasibility of the RMP.
In contrast, BP
⋆
uses Constraints (
10c
), which impose that a vertex not assigned to the cut must be covered by a selected cluster, and vice versa. These constraints are formulated as inequalities, allowing a (fractional) LP solution to simultaneously assign a vertex to a cluster and to the cut. This is particularly advantageous during column generation. For instance, when branching forces a vertex into the cut, BP
⋆
does not necessarily require the generation of new columns associated with clusters that explicitly exclude that vertex: indeed, the restricted master problem can remain feasible by reusing existing cluster columns.

Furthermore, as detailed in Section
1.1.2
, first-level branching on individual vertices in BP
†
is not sufficient to enforce integrality. As a consequence, BP
†
applies a second branching level based on a Ryan–Foster-type scheme on pairs of vertices: when two vertices
u,v\in\mathcal{V}
are partially assigned to the same cluster, two child nodes are generated by enforcing that
u
and
v
must either belong to the same cluster or to different clusters.
This additional branching level may significantly enlarge the search tree, as fractional solutions can persist even when all vertices are integrally assigned. Moreover, it complicates the pricing subproblem, since newly generated columns must respect pairwise compatibility decisions. In contrast, BP
⋆
does not require any additional branching on vertex pairs, leading to a smaller search tree and keeping the pricing problem unchanged.

Finally, on the implementation side, the code by
Cornaz et al. (2019)
uses a custom branch-and-bound framework to implement BP
†
, where the branching tree is explored in a depth-first fashion, and first-level branching decisions are based on the lowest-index vertex
v
for which
{\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(v)}\lambda_{S}\notin\mathds{Z}}
in an LP solution.
On the other hand, BP
⋆
makes use of the branch-and-bound framework of
SCIP
.
Next to more sophisticated tree exploration and branching rules,
SCIP
also implements advanced methods such as strong branching
(
Applegate et al. (1995)
)
which help to reduce the size of the branching tree.
Moreover, the implementation of BP
⋆
benefits from the primal heuristics that are available in
SCIP
, whereas the implementation of BP
†
requires LP solutions to be integral to improve the primal bound.

4.2.1
Evaluation of additional components of BP
⋆

We also evaluated the impact of three additional features: the symmetry breaking techniques detailed in Section
3.2
, providing BP
⋆
with the initial primal solution returned by IDH (see Section
3.3
) and adding the connectivity cut (
13
) to
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
. Symmetry handling on the
\bm{x}
-variables has only a modest visible impact on the running time for most instances, since the corresponding branching trees are relatively small and the techniques detailed in Section
3.2
are particularly effective on large search trees.
Nonetheless, their inclusion allows BP
⋆
to solve four additional instances within the time limit compared with the variant of the algorithm without symmetry handling, which proves useful in terms of the overall number of instances solved and motivates keeping these techniques in the final version of the algorithm.
Providing BP
⋆
with the initial solution returned by IDH improves the performance of the algorithm as well: with an initial primal solution, BP
⋆
solves eight more instances within the time limit than the variant started without any initial solution. Similarly, the connectivity cut is generally beneficial and is therefore included in our default configuration. Enabling it for moderate values of
k
(
\leq 15
), as discussed in Section
3.3
, allows BP
⋆
to solve three additional instances within the time limit compared with the variant without it.

(

, see)
, and let

Overall, BP

The size of the initial edge-covering family of cliques remains moderate (about 195 cliques on average, with a mean of around 7 vertices per clique), with an increase in the number of cliques –and an associated decrease in the number of vertices per clique– as the value of
k
increases.
Average total running times for solved instances are on the order of one to two minutes (104.3 seconds on average), with pricing accounting for about one third of the time, which confirms that column generation is not a bottleneck component from the computational perspective.
The number of generated columns is in the low thousands (about 2,760 on average), with a substantial fraction already produced at the root node and a relatively small number of Farkas columns.
The branch-and-bound tree remains shallow and compact on the solved instances: on average, fewer than 50 nodes are explored, with depth around 5, which is consistent with a strong LP relaxation and effective branching.
Finally, the average integrality gaps are very small: on instances whose optimal value is known and for which BP

4.5
Strength of the LP relaxations

In this section, we compare the strength and computational cost of the LP relaxations associated with the different formulations considered in this paper. The goal of this analysis is twofold: on the one hand, to quantify how the choice of the initial clique family in
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
(edge-covering family of cliques, edge-partitioning family of cliques, or single edges) affects the quality of the root-node bound; on the other hand, to position the resulting relaxations with respect to previously proposed models, namely the enhanced version of
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
introduced in Section
4
and detailed in
Furini et al. (2020)
(denoted as
\text{ILP}_{\text{H}}
), the natural formulation
\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}
, and the compact formulation
\text{ILP}_{\text{C}}
with the symmetry breaking enhancement mentioned in Section
4
(denoted as
\text{ILP}_{\text{C}}^{\star}
).

\text{ILP}_{\text{E2, b}}^{\star}
is much weaker, with gaps between 52.3% and 23.6%, comparable to those of the natural formulation.
This shows that the initial clique family is a decisive modeling aspect: moving from an edge-covering family of cliques to an edge-partitioning family of cliques, and further to single edges, quickly weakens the LP relaxation down to the level of the natural model.

As a computational counterpart to the theoretical results in Section
2.2
, we observe that
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2, b}}^{\star}

\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}

and
k=10

Such overhead can be explained by the high number of columns generated at the root node (roughly 50% more than
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}

The hybrid model
\text{ILP}_{\text{H}}

\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}

\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}

Finally, the compact formulation
\text{ILP}_{\text{C}}^{\star}
has by far the weakest relaxation, with gaps close to 90% across all values of

Overall, the table illustrates that the edge-covering family of cliques used in
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
is key to obtaining a much stronger LP bound than all other formulations considered.
Although computing the LP relaxation of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}

\text{ILP}_{\text{H}}

\text{ILP}_{\text{N}}

\text{ILP}_{\text{C}}^{\star}

This tighter relaxation of
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}^{\star}
is a promising indication of the effectiveness of BP

and is likely to translate, in the full branch-and-price algorithm, into fewer explored nodes and a larger number of instances closed within the time limit compared with the other existing approaches.

Conclusions

Despite its practical relevance for numerous areas such as network design or vulnerability assessment, existing methods for exactly solving the
k
-VCP are not powerful enough to solve medium sized instances.
This article therefore took a fresh look into the problem and identified unused potential in an existing branch-and-price framework:
while all variables in the previous extended formulation were required to take binary values, our in-depth study revealed that the integrality requirement on those variables can be dropped when introducing the binary variables of the natural formulation.
This new perspective on the extended model allowed us to significantly simplify the underlying branching scheme and to enhance the new branch-and-price algorithm by novel components.
While results by
Furini et al. (2020)
suggest that branch-and-price was not the right technique for tackling the
k
-VCP, we demonstrate in an extensive numerical study that, when properly set up, the new branch-and-price substantially improves on the state of the art.
Our algorithmic framework thus not only provides a new benchmark for solving the
k
-VCP to proven optimality, but also changes the perspective on how to solve the
k
-VCP: branch-and-price strikes back by solving for the first time 73 open instances.

Moreover, although our algorithm can be considered state of the art, there are still instances in our test set that remain unsolved. This suggests several directions for further improvement. One promising line of research is the development of more powerful presolving routines aimed at simplifying particularly difficult instances. Another potential enhancement is the incorporation of connectivity constraints within the subsets associated with the variables of the extended formulation. Considering such constraints could further strengthen the LP relaxation, but this would also have to be taken into account in the pricing problem, which may significantly increase its complexity. A careful study of this trade-off between bound quality and pricing difficulty can therefore be an interesting topic for future work.

The theoretical insights developed in this work may indicate a more general pathway for deriving extended formulations from natural bilevel models (see, e.g.,
Furini et al. (2020)
,
Furini et al. (2022)
). Such models arise frequently in graph partitioning problems, where a leader removes vertices or edges and a follower solves a subproblem that captures a structural property of the modified graph. As discussed in Section
2.1.1
, if the follower’s problem admits a polyhedral description with a totally unimodular constraint matrix, then dualization and subsequent linearization yield an extended formulation of the original problem with an exponential number of variables which can then be solved by means of a branch-and-price algorithm. For the
k
-VCP, this procedure led to formulation
\text{ILP}_{\text{E2}}
, in other settings the same construction may give rise to new formulations and, in turn, potentially efficient branch-and-price algorithms. Among others, this concerns the
k
-way vertex cut problem
(
Berger et al. (2014)
)
, whose optimization version aims at maximizing the number of connected components of the residual graph under a budget on the cardinality of the vertex cut. We believe that our branch-and-price algorithm can be easily adapted and used to tackle this problem as well. More generally, the same approach might apply to other critical vertex detection problems that seek to fragment the graph by deleting vertices.

Appendix B
Solution analysis of the Zachary’s karate club network

The
karate

(

)

In this graph, vertices correspond to members of a university karate club, and edges connect pairs of individuals who were observed interacting outside formal activities. A conflict between the instructor (vertex 1) and the club administrator (vertex 34) eventually led to the split of the club into two factions, a structural characteristic that makes this instance particularly suitable for illustrating graph disconnection phenomena.
The optimal solutions of the
k

k

k
-vertex cut shifting from a single highly central vertex to a larger set of influential ones. For

As observed by
Cornaz et al. (2019)
, to obtain a valid formulation, it is sufficient to consider a family of clique-partitioning inequalities associated with a clique cover of the edge set. This is the approach adopted in formulation
\text{ILP}_{\text{E1}}
, where a collection of maximal cliques is used to partition all edges.
The quality of the LP relaxation, however, strongly depends on the family of clique-partitioning constraints that is selected, cf. Section
4.5
.

A natural question is to understand which dual bound can be obtained when we explicitly separate violated clique-partitioning inequalities, instead of restricting ourselves to those associated with a fixed family of cliques.
To address this question, we introduce and analyze the separation problem for the clique-partitioning inequalities.
Given a vector
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathds{R}^{\mathscr{S}}
, the separation problem of clique-partitioning inequalities is to decide whether there exists a clique
C\in\mathscr{C}
such that
\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(C)}\lambda_{S}>1
.
In the following, we prove that the separation problem is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard for solutions of a restricted master problem.

Proposition E.1

The separation problem for clique-partitioning inequalities in
\mathcal{NP}
-hard.

Proof E.2

Proof
We prove the claim by a reduction from the
\mathcal{NP}
-hard Maximum Clique Problem (MCP).
That is, given an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
and a positive integer
\ell\geq 2
, one needs to decide whether there exists a clique of size at least
\ell
in
\mathcal{G}
.
We construct an instance of the separation problem as follows.

Let
\mathscr{S}
be the family of all singleton subsets of
\mathcal{V}
, i.e.,
\mathscr{S}=\{\{v\}:v\in\mathcal{V}\}
.
For each
S=\{v\}\in\mathscr{S}
, set
\lambda_{S}=\frac{1}{\ell-1}
.
On the one hand, if there exists a clique
C
of size at least
\ell
in
\mathcal{G}
, then
\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(C)}\lambda_{S}=\sum_{v\in C}\lambda_{\{v\}}\geq\frac{\ell}{\ell-1}>1
, i.e., there exists a violated clique-covering inequality.
On the other hand, if there exists a violated clique-covering inequality, there exists a clique
C
in
\mathcal{G}
with
\sum_{v\in C}\lambda_{\{v\}}=\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(C)}\lambda_{S}>1
.
Since all
\bm{\lambda}
-variables have the same value
\frac{1}{\ell-1}
, this means that the left-hand side of this inequality evaluates to
\frac{|C|}{\ell-1}>1
.
That is,
C
has size at least
\ell
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Due to the separation problem being
\mathcal{NP}
-hard, we cannot expect to find a polynomial time algorithm for separating clique-covering inequalities.
We therefore suggest to solve the separation problem by means of integer programming.
To this end, observe that there is a clique-covering inequality that is violated by a non-negative vector
\bm{\lambda}
if and only if

\max_{C\in\mathscr{C}}\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}(C)}\lambda_{C}>1.

We can find a maximizing clique
C
by solving an integer program.
Let us define a variable
\beta_{v}\in\{0,1\}
, for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
, taking value
1
if vertex
v
is in
C
, and 0 otherwise; we also define a variable
\gamma_{S}\in\{0,1\}
for all
S\in\mathscr{S}
, equal to
1
if clique
C
intersects
S
, and
0
otherwise. An ILP formulation for the separation problem reads as follows:

(SEP)

where
\overline{\mathcal{E}}=\{\{u,v\}\,:\,u,v\in\mathcal{V}\text{ and }\{u,v\}\notin\mathcal{E}\}
is the set of non-edges of
\mathcal{G}
. The objective function (
14a
) maximizes the total weight of the subsets in
\mathscr{S}
that are intersected by the selected clique, where the weight of each subset
S
is given by the value
\lambda_{S}
of the corresponding variable in the current fractional solution. Constraints (
14b
) ensure that, for each non-edge
\{u,v\}\in\overline{\mathcal{E}}
, at most one of its endpoints can be part of the selected clique, thus guaranteeing that any subset of vertices
C=\{v\in V\,:\,\beta_{v}=1\}
is a clique. Finally, Constraints (
14c
) enforce that, for each subset of vertices
S\in\mathscr{S}
, its associated variable
\gamma_{S}
can take value
1
only if at least one of the vertices in
S
is part of the solution clique
C
. We remark that, since
\lambda_{S}\geq 0
for all
S\in\mathscr{S}
, any solution
(\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma})
to (

\gamma_{S}<\min\{1,\,\sum_{v\in S}\beta_{v}\}
for some
S\in\mathcal{S}
cannot be optimal. This is because setting
\gamma_{S}=\min\{1,\,\sum_{v\in S}\beta_{v}\}
we obtain a solution with a greater objective function value, without violating any constraint.
It follows that the integrality of the
\bm{\beta}

\bm{\gamma}
variables are integral.
As a consequence, the integrality of the latter set of variables can be relaxed.

We now turn to a computational assessment of the effectiveness of the exact separation procedure described above. Given the
\mathcal{NP}
-hardness result of Proposition
E.1
, we restrict the use of Model (
14

In this way, we can evaluate the impact of optimizing over the closure of the clique-partitioning constraints at the root node, without incurring an excessive computational overhead.
In our experiments, we compare two settings on the entire set of unweighted instances.

⋆

The initial family of maximal cliques used in both settings is constructed by means of the greedy edge-cover procedure detailed in Section

The resulting family of maximal cliques provides an edge cover of
\mathcal{E}
and is used to define the initial set of clique-partitioning constraints in both BP
⋆

-C.
