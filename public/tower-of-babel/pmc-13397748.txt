Molecular convergence in gallbladder cancer: MEK–ERK signalling at the crossroads of oncogenic hubs and pathway cross-talks
Authors: Deeptima Jaiswar, Manjusha Dixit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGallbladder cancer (GBC) is a highly aggressive malignancy with late presentation, limited therapeutic options, and dismal survival rates. Despite recent advances in molecular profiling, our understanding of GBC remains fragmented, with most studies focusing on isolated genetic or epigenetic events rather than integrating the broader molecular interplay. Frequently altered genes—including TP53, KRAS, ERBB2, and CDKN2A—impact critical signaling cascades such as Ras/MAPK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Wnt/β-catenin, and p53, with aberrant ERK pathway activation emerging as a pivotal driver. Epigenetic modifications, dysregulated non-coding RNAs, and immune microenvironmental factors add further layers of complexity but remain underexplored in a unified context. Currently, no comprehensive framework links these alterations into a generalized molecular mechanism that could accelerate biomarker discovery and therapeutic innovation. This review integrates genomic, epigenomic, and immunologic data, complemented by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, to present a consolidated molecular landscape of GBC. By mapping converging oncogenic hubs and signaling cross-talk, we aim to identify actionable targets—particularly within the ERK axis—that could inform early detection, guide precision therapy, and direct future translational research.Keywords: Gallbladder cancer, Epigenetic modification in GBC, Mutational profile of GBC, Immune landscape of GBC, Targeted therapies in GBC

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC), while ranking only 22nd in global cancer incidence [1], is the most lethal malignancy of the biliary tract and one of the most aggressive gastrointestinal tumors. Its distribution is geographically heterogeneous, with marked “hot spots” in the Andean belt, North and Northeast India, East Asia, and Eastern Europe [2]. Known etiologic factors include chronic inflammation from gallstones, Salmonella carriage, and anatomical variants such as anomalous pancreaticobiliary duct junction (APBDJ) [3, 4, 5, 6]. Unfortunately, most patients are present at advanced stages due to asymptomatic early disease and the absence of population-level screening strategies, resulting in poor prognosis [7, 8, 9].

Therapeutic progress in GBC has been slow [10, 11], partly due to its frequent exclusion from large clinical trials and the reliance on data extrapolated from other gastrointestinal malignancies [9, 12]. Molecular studies over the past decade have uncovered recurrent alterations in TP53, KRAS, RAD51, ARID1A/2, COX-2, SMAD4, and ERBB2 [13, 14, 15], along with copy number changes, loss of heterozygosity [16], microsatellite instability [17], and dysregulated non-coding RNAs [18, 19, 20]. However, most reports focus on individual genes or pathways [21, 22], without integrating these findings into a coherent molecular framework that reflects the true complexity of GBC pathogenesis. Similarly, while epigenetic modifications [23, 24, 25] and immune microenvironmental interactions [26, 27, 28] are recognized contributors, they remain insufficiently connected to genetic and transcriptomic alterations in current literature [29, 30].

These gaps hinder the identification of generalized molecular mechanisms and delay the translation of genomic insights into clinically relevant biomarkers or therapeutic strategies. The scope of this review is to bridge this knowledge gap by synthesizing data across mutational profiles, epigenetic alterations, and immunologic landscapes, and by applying KEGG/DAVID pathway analysis [31, 32, 33] to highlight converging oncogenic hubs. By presenting a “bigger molecular picture,” we aim to provide a foundation for future research directions, improve the rational design of targeted therapies, and accelerate the development of early detection tools for this neglected but deadly malignancy.

In the sections that follow, we first provide an overview of the epidemiology and clinical presentation of GBC, followed by a detailed synthesis of its genetic and transcriptomic alterations. We then examine epigenetic dysregulation and the role of non-coding RNAs, before discussing the tumor immune microenvironment and its therapeutic implications. Finally, we integrate these layers of molecular data using KEGG/DAVID pathway enrichment to propose a unified model of GBC pathogenesis, highlighting key signaling hubs and identifying opportunities for biomarker discovery, drug development, and future translational research.

Overview of the epidemiology and clinical presentation of gallbladder cancer

GBC represents the most aggressive malignancy of the biliary tract and is predominantly diagnosed at advanced stages due to its deep anatomical location and the absence of specific early symptoms [12]. The early course of the disease is typically silent, with symptomatic manifestations arising only in later stages. When present, clinical features may include right upper quadrant abdominal pain, jaundice, unexplained weight loss, and, in rare cases, a palpable abdominal mass [3]. Notably, a substantial proportion of GBC cases are discovered incidentally during cholecystectomy [2, 12] performed for presumed benign conditions such as gallstone disease.

Diagnosis relies on a combination of imaging and histopathological confirmation. Ultrasound remains the first-line diagnostic tool, offering accessibility and utility for initial assessment [35]. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging/magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRI/MRCP) provide superior staging accuracy and detailed visualization of the biliary tract [6]. Endoscopic ultrasound further enhances evaluation by delineating local invasion and enabling targeted tissue acquisition. Although serum tumor markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA 19 − 9) may be elevated, their limited sensitivity and specificity preclude their use as screening tools [8, 34].

Population-wide screening for GBC is not recommended; however, targeted surveillance is justified in well-defined high-risk groups [5]. These include individuals with porcelain gallbladder [35, 36], gallbladder polyps larger than 1 cm [6], primary sclerosing cholangitis, anomalous pancreaticobiliary duct junctions, and chronic gallstone disease—particularly among older women [37, 38]. Elevated risk is also observed in specific ethnic and geographic populations such as Native Americans [39], South Americans [40, 41], and North Indians [6].

Preventive strategies focus on early cholecystectomy in high-risk settings [9, 42, 43], modification of known risk factors, and adoption of healthy lifestyle measures [44, 45, 46, 47]. Nevertheless, despite these preventive efforts, early detection remains rare.

Given these diagnostic and epidemiological challenges, understanding the molecular underpinnings of GBC becomes essential. Molecular profiling not only offers the potential to identify biomarkers for early detection but also provides a framework for targeted therapies that could improve outcomes in this otherwise fatal disease. The next section explores the mutational, epigenetic, and immunologic landscapes of GBC, integrating them into a broader molecular perspective that may inform both clinical practice and future research.

I.Mutational landscape of gallbladder cancer

Inherited genetic predisposition plays a significant role in GBC risk, as evidenced by familial clustering and associations with several hereditary cancer syndromes. Peutz–Jeghers syndrome, characterized by mucocutaneous pigmentation and gastrointestinal polyps, has been linked to GBC, with gallbladder polyps reported in a notable subset of affected individuals [47]. Population studies, such as those from Sweden, also support a familial incidence of the disease [48, 49]. Similarly, Lynch syndrome increases lifetime GBC risk [50], while Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP), driven by germline APC mutations, predisposes patients to biliary tract tumors, including gallbladder neoplasms [51, 52]. These patterns highlight the importance of defining the specific genetic alterations and pathways involved in GBC pathogenesis, as such insights can reveal causative mutations, clarify disease mechanisms, and inform targeted prevention and treatment strategies.

Somatic alterations in gallbladder cancer

While hereditary syndromes and familial clustering highlight the role of germline susceptibility in GBC, most cases arise sporadically and are driven by acquired somatic genetic alterations—DNA changes that occur in non-germline cells. These alterations encompass base substitutions, copy number variations, chromosomal rearrangements, loss of heterozygosity, and, in some cases, microsatellite instability. By disrupting key tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes, they impair critical regulatory pathways controlling cell cycle progression, DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, and inflammatory signaling. The resulting dysregulation promotes uncontrolled proliferation, evasion of apoptosis, and metastatic potential.

The diversity and complexity of these alterations contribute to significant tumor heterogeneity, influencing prognosis and treatment response. Advances in high-throughput genomic technologies, particularly next-generation sequencing, have enabled comprehensive profiling of these genetic changes, deepening our understanding of GBC biology and revealing potential molecular vulnerabilities [53]. Recent large-scale multi-omics studies, including whole-exome sequencing and transcriptomic profiling of biliary tract cancers, have further refined this landscape by identifying recurrent driver alterations and clinically actionable targets, while also highlighting substantial intertumoral heterogeneity across patient populations. Single-cell analyses additionally reveal distinct cellular subpopulations and tumor–microenvironment interactions that contribute to disease progression and therapeutic resistance [54]. The following sections detail the major categories of somatic alterations in GBC—base substitutions, copy number alterations, loss of heterozygosity, and microsatellite instability—and their implications for disease pathogenesis and therapeutic targeting.

Base substitutions: Mutations in genes such as p53, ARID1A, CTNNB1, ERBB2, KMT2D, KRAS, PIK3CA, SMAD4, TERT, ZNF521, TTN, ELF3, and various mucins are among the most commonly reported in the literature and observed in analyses on cBioPortal (Gallbladder Cancer - MSK 2022; MSK 2018 and Shanghai 2014 datasets) [13, 14] [Fig. 1A]. However, the prevalence of these mutations varies across global populations. Studies indicate that US patients exhibit mutations in all these genes except ZNF521, while Chinese and Japanese patients show the highest mutation rates in KMT2D and the lowest in KRAS. Notably, TP53, SMAD4, and ARID1A were the most frequently mutated genes across all populations [13]. A separate study utilizing next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified targetable mutations in 89.91% of GBC cases, including mutations in SMAD4, NOTCH1, ERBB2, PIK3CA, MET, PTEN, EGFR, KRAS, and BRAF. Mutations in the TP53 gene were significantly associated with histopathological differentiation (p = 0.0001), while ERBB4 and ALK mutations correlated with necrosis (p = 0.012 and 0.027, respectively). Furthermore, the EGFR mutation was linked to mucin status (p = 0.023), and ERBB2 mutations related to T stage (p = 0.036), with metastatic disease showing increased mutations in CTNNB1, KRAS, and NRAS [20]. Functionally, mutations in TP53 result in uncontrolled cell growth and loss of tumor suppression, whereas KRAS mutations enhance cell survival and proliferation. Additionally, alterations in SMAD4, ARID1A, MUC16, TTN, ZNF521, ELF3, and PIK3CA often disrupt critical cellular processes, contributing to the development of GBC [56]. These findings together with recent integrative genomic analyses further reinforce these findings, demonstrating that recurrent mutations in TP53 [56, 57], KRAS [58], ERBB2 [59], and chromatin-remodeling genes converge on a limited number of oncogenic signaling hubs—most prominently the MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways. Emerging single-cell studies additionally reveal that these alterations are heterogeneously distributed across tumor cell populations, defining distinct functional states and highlighting the complexity of GBC evolution.

b.Copy Number Alterations (CNAs): Changes in the copy number of a gene, which can occur through amplification (the increase in the number of copies [Fig. 1B] or deletion-the decrease in the number of copies [Fig. 1C]), can profoundly influence both the expression of the gene itself and the amounts of its downstream interacting proteins, as these alterations have a significant impact on the functional dynamics of the gene. CNAs associated with GBC include key genes such as CDKN2A, CDKN2B, MDM2, ERBB2, ERBB3, and KRAS, among others [13, 22, 60]. A study by Shioi et al. examined copy number changes in 36 patients (11 with GBC and 25 with bile duct cancer) using a single nucleotide polymorphism array. They discovered CNA gains in over 40% of patients at specific chromosomal regions: 14q32.33, 8q11.1-q24.3, 17q11.2-q12, and 1q21.1-q44, with decreasing frequency in GBC. Some of these CNAs may serve as potential therapeutic targets [28]. Another study involving 491 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples of advanced GBC found potentially targetable structural copy number alterations (SCNAs) like ERBB genes, FGFRs, MET, and the CCND1-FGF3/4/19 amplicon in 21% of patients14. Instances of oncogenic rearrangements involving BRAF, FGFR2/3, or ALK were noted in 1.4% of cases. Additionally, 7.8% of patients showed germline alterations in BRCA2 or ATM, while 0.8% had INI1 loss, suggesting that PARP or EZH2 inhibitors might be advantageous61. More investigation is required to determine if these CNAs can be targeted for treatment. Future studies should therefore focus on the potential therapeutic applications of these CNAs.

c.Loss of Heterozygosity: Loss of heterozygosity (LOH) is a prevalent genetic alteration observed in the cancer genome, including GBC. This phenomenon arises from mechanisms such as the deletion of one allele from a heterozygous pair or the duplication of a parental chromosome followed by the loss of the corresponding allele [29, 62]. In GBC, at least 21 chromosomal locations show recurrent allele losses, indicating the potential silencing of several tumor suppressor genes throughout tumor progression [63]. The most frequent LOH events (≥ 60%) in GBC occur in specific chromosomal regions: 3p, 6q, 7q,8p, 9p, 9q, 11q, 12q, 17q, 18q, 19p and 22q [38]. Notably, LOH has also been reported in gallbladder dysplasias on chromosome 5q (55%) and 17p (40%), and in gallbladder carcinomas on chromosomes 3p (52%), 5q (66%), 9p (52%), and 17p (58%). Furthermore, LOH at loci 13q and 18q is exclusively found in advanced-stage gallbladder carcinomas (stages III and IV), with frequencies of 40% and 31%, respectively. Additionally, LOH on chromosome 17p correlates with the intranuclear accumulation of the p53 protein, suggesting its role in GBC pathogenesis [15, 30, 64]. Besides these, the FHIT gene, a tumor suppressor on chromosome 3p14.2, is frequently inactivated in human cancers via LOH, homozygous deletions, and abnormal expression [42]. Wistuba et al. linked Fhit protein expression to FHIT allele loss in GBC, noting reduced FHIT expression in 79% of GBC cases and allelic loss in 76% of samples. 94% patients showed either decreased immunostaining or allelic loss, with a 70% correlation to LOH in invasive GBC (P < 0.05) [65]. Tanaka et al. found that this transcriptional silencing of the FHIT gene in tumors is caused by methylation of the 5ʹ CpG Island, which has been confirmed in lung and breast cancer [66]. Importantly, while these early studies established LOH as a recurrent feature of GBC, recent genomic and multi-omics analyses indicate that LOH events occur alongside somatic mutations, copy number alterations, and epigenetic dysregulation. This integrated landscape suggests that LOH contributes to tumorigenesis not as an isolated event, but as part of a broader network of molecular alterations. Mechanistically, LOH-driven loss of tumor suppressor genes—including TP53 and negative regulators of RAS signaling—removes critical inhibitory constraints on proliferative pathways [67]. This loss facilitates sustained activation of downstream signaling cascades, particularly the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK axis. In this context, LOH acts synergistically with activating mutations (e.g., KRAS, ERBB2) and epigenetic deregulation to amplify ERK signaling output, promoting uncontrolled proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and tumor progression. Collectively, these findings position LOH as an important contributor to the convergence of oncogenic signals on the MEK–ERK pathway in GBC.

d.Microsatellite Instability: Microsatellite instability (MSI) in GBC is relatively uncommon, largely due to infrequent mutations in genes associated with the mismatch repair (MMR) pathway. Typically, GBC samples exhibit low microsatellite instability (MSI-L) [30, 63]. In a study by P. Moy et al., MSI was evaluated in 77 GBC patient samples using multiplex PCR, revealing MSI in only six cases (7.8%). Notably, the study found that MSI status did not correlate with Lynch syndrome, tumor grade, the presence of extracellular mucin, or tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Furthermore, there was no significant difference in mean survival rates between patients with and without MSI [16]. While early studies established the prevalence of LOH and MSI in GBC, recent genomic analyses suggest that these alterations coexist with broader mutational and epigenetic changes, reinforcing a multi-layered model of tumorigenesis [67]. Despite challenges such as the difficulty in obtaining histological specimens, especially in unresectable cases, and the limited number of specimens from biopsies, there are promising aspects of MSI assessment in biliary tract cancers. Kai et al. demonstrated that MSI testing and subsequent pembrolizumab treatment can be effective for patients with unresectable or recurrent MSI-positive biliary tract cancers. MSI can lead to the production of multiple mutations that code for tumor neoantigens, making these tumors more immunogenic. As a result, immunotherapies, such as immunosuppressants, show potential in treating MSI-positive malignancies [69].

II. Epigenetics and RNA modifications in gallbladder cancer

Epigenetic regulation encompasses heritable alterations in gene activity and expression that occur without changes to the underlying DNA sequence, yet profoundly influence transcriptional dynamics [23]. Key mechanisms include DNA methylation, chromatin remodeling through covalent histone modifications, nucleosome repositioning, and post-transcriptional control mediated by non-coding RNAs and microRNAs (miRNAs) [69, 70]. These processes act in an interconnected manner to orchestrate gene expression programs that govern tumor initiation, progression, metastatic potential, and treatment response.

In addition to these classical epigenetic mechanisms, reversible RNA modifications—particularly N⁶-methyladenosine (m⁶A)—have emerged as important regulators of RNA fate and protein synthesis in cancer. Together, these regulatory layers contribute to the molecular heterogeneity of GBC and represent a promising frontier for biomarker discovery and therapeutic innovation. This section examines the role of DNA methylation and m⁶A RNA modification in GBC pathogenesis, underscoring their diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic potential.

DNA methylation: DNA methylation—the enzymatic addition of a methyl group to cytosine residues in CpG dinucleotides—is a fundamental epigenetic process that shapes chromatin architecture and regulates gene transcription. In normal cells, it safeguards genomic integrity and maintains tissue-specific gene expression patterns. In GBC, methylation patterns are frequently disrupted, manifesting as promoter hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes and global hypomethylation, the latter contributing to chromosomal instability. Hypermethylation-induced transcriptional silencing has been documented in genes governing cell cycle regulation (CDKN2A/p16) [70], apoptosis (DAPK) [71], DNA repair (MLH1), and signal transduction (RASSF1A, APC) [72]. These alterations correlate with advanced tumor stage, higher histologic grade, and poorer clinical outcomes, and are detectable in circulating tumor DNA—supporting their potential utility as non-invasive biomarkers for early diagnosis and prognostication [24]. From a therapeutic perspective, DNA methylation is reversible, making it an attractive drug target [54, 55]. DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors such as azacitidine and decitabine, which have shown efficacy in other malignancies [73,74], warrant exploration in GBC—particularly in combination with chemotherapy or immunotherapy. Integrating methylation profiles with genomic and transcriptomic datasets may enable the refinement of GBC molecular subtypes and guide personalized therapy. However, current clinical evidence in GBC remains limited, emphasizing the need for larger, ethnically diverse cohorts and robust preclinical models.

m6A RNA modification: N⁶-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most prevalent internal modification of eukaryotic mRNA, influencing key post-transcriptional processes including RNA stability, alternative splicing, nuclear export, and translational efficiency [75]. The dynamic regulation of m6A marks is orchestrated by three functional protein classes: “writers”—methyltransferases such as METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP [58]; “erasers”—demethylases including FTO and ALKBH5; and “readers”—m6A-binding proteins such as YTH domain-containing proteins and IGF2BPs [77, 78]. In GBC, dysregulation of m6A machinery contributes to tumorigenesis by altering the expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. For example, METTL3 overexpression has been shown to enhance proliferative signaling [75], whereas reduced ALKBH5 expression correlates with aggressive disease features and poor patient prognosis [79]. Aberrant m6A patterns have been implicated in the activation of oncogenic pathways including PI3K/AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby promoting tumor growth, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy [76, 80, 81]. Importantly, m6A modification is reversible, rendering it an attractive target for therapeutic intervention. Small-molecule inhibitors of METTL3 and activators of ALKBH5 are currently under investigation in preclinical models. Furthermore, m6A-based molecular signatures hold promise as prognostic biomarkers and tools for patient stratification. However, the clinical utility of these markers in GBC remains to be validated through larger, multi-center studies.

miRNA: MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are 18–25 nucleotide, single-stranded noncoding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally and influence nearly one-third of all genes. Aberrant miRNA expression is a hallmark of many malignancies, including GBC [82, 83, 84, 85], where profiling studies have revealed distinct upregulated and downregulated miRNA signatures [86, 87]. Ueta et al. identified 39 miRNAs upregulated in GBC, while Yang et al. reported 24 upregulated and 22 downregulated miRNAs [9, 43]. Oncogenic miRNAs linked to GBC progression and EMT include miR-31-3p, miR-1246, miR-451a, and miR-29b-1-5p. Exosomal miRNAs such as miR-552-3p, miR-581, miR-4433a-3p, miR-496, and miR-203b-3p may contribute to disease risk. Functionally, miR-20a promotes tumor growth via the SMAD7/β-catenin axis, miR-155 is associated with invasion and lymph node metastasis, and miR-182 enhances cell migration and invasion [44]. In contrast, tumor-suppressive miRNAs—including miR-218-5p, miR-335, miR-34a, miR-130a, miR-135a-5p, miR-26a, miR-146b-5p, miR-1 and miR-145—are frequently downregulated [44]. Collectively, the miRNAs summarized in Table 1 indicate that miRNA dysregulation in GBC is not merely a random epigenetic event, but reflects a functionally coordinated layer of post-transcriptional regulation converging on a restricted set of oncogenic signaling networks. Among these, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK/ERK pathways emerge as the most recurrently affected, with multiple oncogenic and tumor-suppressive miRNAs modulating key components either directly (e.g., PTEN, KRAS, EGFR) or indirectly through regulators of EMT, apoptosis, and cell-cycle progression. This repeated convergence underscores the role of miRNAs not only as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, but also as active determinants of signaling plasticity in GBC.

miRNA: MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are 18–25 nucleotide, single-stranded noncoding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally and influence nearly one-third of all genes. Aberrant miRNA expression is a hallmark of many malignancies, including GBC [82, 83, 84, 85], where profiling studies have revealed distinct upregulated and downregulated miRNA signatures [86, 87]. Ueta et al. identified 39 miRNAs upregulated in GBC, while Yang et al. reported 24 upregulated and 22 downregulated miRNAs [9, 43]. Oncogenic miRNAs linked to GBC progression and EMT include miR-31-3p, miR-1246, miR-451a, and miR-29b-1-5p. Exosomal miRNAs such as miR-552-3p, miR-581, miR-4433a-3p, miR-496, and miR-203b-3p may contribute to disease risk. Functionally, miR-20a promotes tumor growth via the SMAD7/β-catenin axis, miR-155 is associated with invasion and lymph node metastasis, and miR-182 enhances cell migration and invasion [44]. In contrast, tumor-suppressive miRNAs—including miR-218-5p, miR-335, miR-34a, miR-130a, miR-135a-5p, miR-26a, miR-146b-5p, miR-1 and miR-145—are frequently downregulated [44]. lncRNA: lncRNAs are endogenous RNAs lacking long open reading frames and the Kozak sequence, primarily exerting regulatory functions in transcription, epigenetic control, cell cycle, stress responses, and immune regulation [19, 114, 115]. Their dysregulation is linked to many cancers, where oncogenic lncRNAs drive proliferation, migration, and EMT, while tumor-suppressive lncRNAs inhibit these processes [19, 114, 116, 117]. In GBC, lncRNAs influence aggressiveness, chemoresistance, and prognosis [116] [Table 2]. AFAP1-AS1 is elevated in tumors and correlates with larger size and poor survival [116]; ANRIL enhances proliferation [118]; CCAT1 is linked to higher grade, lymph node invasion, and metastasis [119]; and DGCR5 promotes EMT by downregulating E-cadherin/ZO-1, upregulating N-cadherin/vimentin/MMPs, and activating JNK/p38 MAPK and MEK/ERK1/2 pathways [120]. AFAP1-AS1, MALAT1, and ROR correlate with tumor size; LET, LINC00152, and HEGBC with high T status; LINC00152, HEGBC, MALAT1, and ROR with lymph node metastasis; while GCASPC, MEG3, LET, and UCA1 act as tumor suppressors. Elevated HEGBC, PAGBC, PVT1, and UCA1 are associated with advanced TNM stages, whereas LET, GCASPC, and MEG3 are enriched in early stages [121–128]. LncRNAs thus hold promise as biomarkers for early detection, prognosis, and therapy in GBC. Other prognostic markers include HMGA2, CD9, CD146, ERCC1, EZH2, PTEN, ERK1/2, PI3K, SHH, PTCH1, GLI1, RKIP, REGIV, MK1, PEG10, TSG101, VHL, and HIF1α [128]. Collectively, the lncRNAs summarized in Table 2 suggest that their dysregulation in GBC is not merely a by-product of malignant transformation, but rather contributes to disease progression through coordinated regulatory effects. A substantial proportion of these dysregulated lncRNAs converge on core oncogenic signaling networks, most prominently ERBB, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, JAK/STAT, and MAPK/ERK, through which they influence proliferation, invasion, EMT, metastatic progression, and therapeutic resistance. Several oncogenic lncRNAs, including LINC01410, PAGBC, DGCR5, and HGBC, exert their effects through pathway cross-talk that ultimately converges on PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK signaling, reinforcing these cascades as dominant downstream effectors in GBC. Thus, beyond their biomarker potential, lncRNAs likely constitute an additional regulatory axis through which transcriptomic dysregulation amplifies the core signaling circuitry of gallbladder tumorigenesis.

III. Molecular pathways associated with frequently mutated genes

Cancer is characterized by the ability of cells to undergo uncontrolled proliferation and survival, often due to genetic alterations in the genes regulating signaling pathways related to cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis. The mechanisms, and interactions of these alterations can differ significantly between various tumors and types of cancer. Thus, gaining insight into the specific processes and pathways involved in the onset and progression of a particular cancer can aid researchers and healthcare professionals in identifying targetable molecules for drug development or recognizing biomarkers for improved cancer detection.

Recent advancements in DNA sequencing and computational analysis have greatly enhanced research focused on the molecular pathways associated with GBC. Numerous genetic alterations and signaling mechanisms relevant to the development and progression of GBC have been identified, hypothesized, and studied. Among the most frequently observed and well-researched signaling pathways are [Fig. 2]:

TP53: TP53, Known as the “gauardian of the genome” is a critical tumor suppressor gene essential for apoptosis, DNA repair, cell cycle regulation and genomic stability [129]. In GBC, roughly 58.6% of patients have TP53 mutations, which hinder its ability to suppress tumors, resulting in uncontrolled cell growth, genomic instability, and potential resistance to treatment [130, 131, 132]. Studies also show that the abnormal expression of mutant TP53 in knockout mice intensifies tumor characteristics [133]. Genomic investigations of GBC patients reveal frequent TP53 changes, especially missense mutations in exons 5 and 7 during dysplasia [134]. Immunohistochemical studies indicate that 50% to 92% of GBC patients exhibit elevated TP53 levels, predominantly in less differentiated tumors [134, 135, 136, 137]. This overexpression activates proteins that promote tumor growth, such as c-Myc and MDM2, and inhibits apoptotic signaling through modulation of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), correlating with a poorer prognosis [138, 139]. Additionally, research suggests that in early-stage GBC, p53 mutations may contribute to the development of flat-type carcinoma, whereas polypoid types may follow a different pathway, influencing cancer-related processes such as VEGF regulation and HSP70 complex formation [139, 140, 141].

KRAS: KRAS, a member of the RAS oncogene family, encodes a small GTPase that regulates key cellular processes through activation of downtstream signaling pathways, particularly PI3K-AKT-mTOR and RAF-MEK-ERK [142, 143–147]. In GBC, KRAS mutations are relatively frequent, with reported prevalence rates of approximately 15% in hyperplastic lesions, 60% in stage I carcinoma, and approaching 100% in stage II–IV carcinoma [145, 146, 148, 149, 150]. The most characteristic alteration involves codon 12 (GGT→GAT; glycine→aspartic acid), detected exclusively in malignant tissue [138, 139]. Additional hotspot mutations occur at codons 13, 59, and 61 [150, 151, 152]. These substitutions impair GTP hydrolysis, effectively locking KRAS in its active, GTP-bound conformation and driving constitutive MAPK pathway activation [147, 153]. The downstream consequences include persistent RAF activation, phosphorylation of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2, and transcriptional reprogramming that enhances cellular proliferation, differentiation, migration, invasion, EMT, and metastatic spread [154, 155, 156]. The aggressive phenotype of KRAS-driven GBC is attributable to this sustained oncogenic signaling, which frequently confers resistance to standard cancer treatments [156, 157, 158, 159, 160]. Therapeutic strategies currently under investigation include direct inhibition of mutant KRAS, blockade of critical downstream effectors, and combination regimens targeting multiple nodes within the KRAS pathway. Early preclinical and clinical studies suggest that such multi-target approaches may help overcome adaptive resistance and improve patient outcomes.

ERBB2: ERBB2 (HER2), a member of the epidermal growth factor receptor family, functions through heterodimerization with other receptor tyrosine kinases, leading to activation of downstream signaling pathways, particularly PI3K–AKT and MAPK [161]. While variants at amino acid sites 654/655 (isoform A) and 624/625 (isoform B) have been reported in several cancer types [162, 163, 164] with Ile654/Ile655 being the most common [181, 182] the specific role and prevalence of ERBB2 alterations in GBC remains an area of ongoing investigation. Studies have explored the landscape of ERBB2 alterations in biliary tract cancers, including GBC. Mondaca et al. (2019) observed ERBB2 gene alterations in 5.4% of biliary tract cancer patients, with 2.7% showing gene amplification, 2.3% having mutations, and 0.4% exhibiting both. Importantly, their findings suggested that ERBB2 alterations were more frequent in GBC compared to other biliary malignancies such as intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [164]. Research in different populations has provided further insights into the role of ERBB2 in GBC [164]. Iyer et al. (2018) found ERBB2 mutations in 40% of early-stage, rare gallbladder tumors within an Indian population [168]. Overexpression of ERBB2 was observed in 24% of these malignancies (n = 25), while recurrent mutations were present in 14% (n = 44) [165, 166, 167, 168]. Their in vitro studies showed that ERBB2 activation, via heterodimerization with EGFR, sustains MAPK signaling. This promotes cell growth, adhesion, migration, and invasion, effects that are reversed when ERBB2 is suppressed68.Furthermore, Li et al. reported that ERBB2 and ERBB3 mutations occurred in 7-8% of Chinese GBC cohorts and were linked to poorer outcomes [169]. These mutations were shown to increase cellular proliferation and migration, along with elevated PD-L1 expression, which inhibited T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity via the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby supporting GBC growth and progression in vivo [169, 170].

ARID1A/2: The ARID gene family comprises eukaryotic DNA-binding proteins with a conserved helix–turn–helix domain [171]. Its 15 human members regulate development, cell fate, the cell cycle, gene expression, and chromatin remodeling [172, 173, 174]. Among them, ARID1A and ARID2 are most frequently mutated in cancer, affecting cell cycle control and genomic stability [175, 176]. Loss of ARID1A disrupts cell cycle regulation—enhancing proliferation via c-Myc activation, reduced p21, and deregulated E2F targets—and impairs DNA repair (double-strand break and mismatch repair), leading to genomic instability and cancer progression [177, 178, 179, 180, 181]. In GBC, particularly in areas where the disease is more common, mutations in ARID1A and ARID2 are frequently observed [181]. Research by Giraldo et al., using the MSK-IMPACT platform, shows that loss-of-function mutations in ARID1A and ARID2 occur in 18% and 6% of GBC cases, respectively, identifying them as important targets for therapy [182]. This loss of function in ARID1A and ARID2 likely interferes with SWI/SNF complexes, promoting tumor development. Additionally, ARID1A inactivation is linked to worse outcomes in biliary cancers, possibly due to impaired immune surveillance [183, 184, 185]. Reduced ARID1A expression may alter PD-L1 levels, affecting immune checkpoint regulation and contributing to poorer prognosis in GBC patients with ARID1A mutations [186]. However, more research is necessary to fully elucidate the molecular pathways involving ARID1A and ARID2 in GBC, underscoring ARID1A's potential as a marker for prognosis and how well the cancer responds to treatment.

COX-2: Cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes convert arachidonic acid into prostaglandins. Of the three isoforms (COX-1, COX-2, COX-3), inducible COX-2 is closely linked to inflammation and cancer, promoting angiogenesis, tumor invasion, and resistance to apoptosis [195, 196], while suppressing antigen presentation and immune activation [187, 188, 189]. Therefore, COX-2 and the prostaglandin pathway are significant factors in the inflammation associated with cancer. In a study conducted in 2005, Moon et al. discovered that COX-2, c-MET, and β-catenin were excessively expressed in tissue samples of invasive GBC. Additionally, they observed co-expression of COX-2, c-MET, β-catenin, and c-ERBB2 in 42% of these samples, particularly in cancer cells forming the invasive front [92]. Building on their previous research demonstrating that PGE2, generated by COX-2, rapidly induces EGFR transactivation leading to activation of the ERK2-mitogenic signaling pathway in a colon cancer cell line [190], they also found that PGE2 enhances colon cancer cell invasiveness by trans-activating c-MET and increasing tyrosine phosphorylation of β-catenin [191]. Moreover, their earlier work showed that HGF stimulates the activation of the COX-2 gene in gastric epithelial cells through c-MET phosphorylation [191]. Pennacchietti et al. demonstrated that the MET receptor expression, encoded by the MET proto-oncogene, is induced under conditions of low oxygen tension, which triggers an invasive growth program. Such low oxygen conditions are typically found at the invasive front of cancer, where it interfaces with the matrix [192]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the coordinated expression of COX-2, c-MET, and β-catenin, along with their local interactions, significantly contribute to the growth and invasion of human GBC [193].In addition, research has shown a link between environmental factors and GBC development, revealing that exposure to cadmium can increase the expression of PI3K and MAPK pathways in a manner dependent on dosage. This exposure also enhances COX-2 expression, contributing to chronic inflammation, and leads to elevated Akt/Erk pathway activity in GBC [194].

SMAD4: The TGF-β/SMAD4 signaling pathway impacts key cellular functions like proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, migration, and cancer development [195, 196, 197]. TGF-β exhibits a dual role in cancer, initially suppressing tumor formation by inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [196, 197]. However, as tumors progress, cells become resistant to TGF-β, which then promotes immunosuppression, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis. SMAD4, the core component of this pathway, is vital for the functional shift of TGF-β during tumorigenesis [195, 198, 199]. While relatively straightforward, the TGF-β/SMAD4 pathway is modulated by pathways like MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and Wnt/β-catenin, creating a complex regulatory network [195, 200].SMAD4 deactivating mutations are common in many cancers. However, loss of SMAD4 function alone is generally insufficient to trigger tumor formation. Instead, it often speeds up tumor progression when combined with other genetic alterations, such as KRAS activation in pancreatic cancer or APC inactivation in colorectal cancer. In contrast, SMAD4 loss in skin cancer can initiate tumor development by disrupting DNA damage response and repair processes, resulting in genomic instability [96]. This illustrates that SMAD4's role varies across different cancer types. In GBC, SMAD4 is frequently mutated, with reported rates between 20% and 60% depending on the population [20, 21]. Despite this prevalence, the specific molecular mechanisms of SMAD4 in GBC remain unclear. Given SMAD4's known functions in other cancers and its interactions with signaling pathways like MAPK, ERK, JNK, p38, PI3K/AKT, and Wnt/β-catenin [200], it represents a promising avenue for investigating the development and progression of GBC.

SMAD4: The TGF-β/SMAD4 signaling pathway impacts key cellular functions like proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, migration, and cancer development [195, 196, 197]. TGF-β exhibits a dual role in cancer, initially suppressing tumor formation by inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [196, 197]. However, as tumors progress, cells become resistant to TGF-β, which then promotes immunosuppression, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis. SMAD4, the core component of this pathway, is vital for the functional shift of TGF-β during tumorigenesis [195, 198, 199]. While relatively straightforward, the TGF-β/SMAD4 pathway is modulated by pathways like MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and Wnt/β-catenin, creating a complex regulatory network [195, 200].SMAD4 deactivating mutations are common in many cancers. However, loss of SMAD4 function alone is generally insufficient to trigger tumor formation. Instead, it often speeds up tumor progression when combined with other genetic alterations, such as KRAS activation in pancreatic cancer or APC inactivation in colorectal cancer. In contrast, SMAD4 loss in skin cancer can initiate tumor development by disrupting DNA damage response and repair processes, resulting in genomic instability [96]. This illustrates that SMAD4's role varies across different cancer types. In GBC, SMAD4 is frequently mutated, with reported rates between 20% and 60% depending on the population [20, 21]. Despite this prevalence, the specific molecular mechanisms of SMAD4 in GBC remain unclear. Given SMAD4's known functions in other cancers and its interactions with signaling pathways like MAPK, ERK, JNK, p38, PI3K/AKT, and Wnt/β-catenin [200], it represents a promising avenue for investigating the development and progression of GBC.

RAD51: RAD51 is an essential protein for DNA repair through homologous recombination, a process critical for maintaining genomic stability and the survival of tumor cells. Specifically, RAD51 is important for repairing double-strand breaks (DSBs) in DNA, a frequent type of damage in fast-growing cancer cells exposed to harmful agents. Dysregulation of RAD51, including abnormal expression, can impair DNA repair, resulting in genomic instability and potentially promoting cancer development and progression [201, 202]. While many cancers show increased levels of RAD51, decreased levels have also been reported [203, 204, 205]. Too little RAD51 results in unrepaired DNA damage and genomic instability, predisposing cells to non-hereditary breast cancers and certain types of renal cell carcinomas. Conversely, increased RAD51 levels are seen in cancers such as pancreatic, melanoma, breast, non-small cell lung, prostate, and glioblastoma. Overexpression of RAD51 in cancer cells boosts homologous recombination activity, potentially causing resistance to standard cancer treatments, leading to lower survival rates and poorer clinical outcomes [204]. In GBC, although a study conducted by Lin et al. recognized RAD51 among most frequently altered genes through NGS [205], the exact role of RAD51 expression and activity is largely unexplored. Data from similar cancers like cholangiocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma [206] also suggests RAD51 to be a promising area for future research in GBC. Changes in RAD51 expression or function could affect how tumors respond to chemotherapy or radiation, since these treatments depend on functional RAD51-mediated repair pathways to manage DNA damage [201, 202].

IV. Understanding the immunological landscape of GBC

GBC is an aggressive malignancy influenced by various immunological pathways [Figure 3]. The following outlines key immunological aspects and pathways involved in GBC:

Immune Evasion and Tolerance Pathways: Tumors frequently exploit immune checkpoint pathways to evade the immune system. Key checkpoints include programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4). In GBC, cancer cells express programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), which binds to PD-1 on T cells, inhibiting their immune response. Emerging evidence suggests that activation of oncogenic signaling pathways, particularly the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK cascade, contributes to immune evasion by regulating PD-L1 expression. ERK signaling can enhance PD-L1 transcription through downstream effectors such as AP-1 and STAT3, thereby promoting T-cell exhaustion and immune escape [207]. FasL expression by gallbladder carcinoma cells enables tumors to escape immune surveillance by inducing apoptosis in infiltrating lymphocytes [208]. Recent research indicates elevated levels of polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 3 (PTBP3), a splicing factor, in GBC. PTBP3 facilitates exon skipping of interleukin-18 (IL-18), leading to the production of ΔIL-18, which downregulates FBXO38 transcription in CD8+ T cells, reducing PD-1 degradation and aiding immune evasion. H3K36 trimethylation recruits PTBP3 through MRG15 to promote IL-18 exon skipping, linking gene transcription with alternative splicing. The H3K36 methyltransferase SETD2 interacts with heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein L (hnRNPL), preventing PTBP3's binding to IL-18 pre-mRNA209. Additionally, regulatory T cells (Tregs) induce immune tolerance by suppressing anti-tumor immune responses. High infiltration of FoxP3+ Tregs is associated with reduced overall survival in gallbladder carcinoma patients [210].

Conditions like chronic cholecystitis and infections as well as lifestyle factors including heavy chemicals and metal exposure, dietary intake like excessive palm and mustard oil consumption and altered lipid profile leading to lipid raft formation can cause continuous immune activation, potentially leading to carcinogenesis [212]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, not only promote tumor growth and metastasis but also directly interface with oncogenic signaling networks. These cytokines activate MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways, thereby linking inflammatory stimuli to core proliferative signaling [211]. Bacterial infections may contribute to GBC etiopathogenesis through inflammatory pathways involving production of cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-ɑ. Furthermore, these infections also activate pathways including NF-κB and MAPK inducing cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis and fostering a tumor conducive environment in GBC [212].

Antigen Presentation and Immune Cell Infiltration Pathways: Effective anti-tumor immune responses depend on tumor antigen presentation by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II molecules, activating CD8+ and CD4+ T cells. Cancer cells may evade immune recognition by downregulating MHC molecules. Tumor budding (TB) and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are prognostic indicators reflecting tumor-host interactions and aggressiveness [209]. High levels of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells are generally associated with improved prognosis, while dysfunctional or exhausted T cells can negatively impact patient outcomes [213, 214]. High CD8+ T cell infiltration is linked to prolonged patient survival [210]. Increased CD3+ T cell infiltration correlates with better prognostic outcomes in GBC patients [215]. High TIL infiltration is associated with an earlier tumor stage and lower histological grades in GBC, and a significant correlation exists between TILs and lymphovascular invasion [216]. Studies indicate that high levels of infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells are associated with reduced tumor invasion, while increased dendritic cells (DCs) correlate with decreased lymph-node metastasis in GBC [216, 217, 218]. High levels of infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and DCs are linked to improved survival outcomes. Unlike T cells and DCs, natural killer cells (NKCs) did not show any correlation with clinicopathological factors [218]. Identifying specific tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) in GBC is essential for developing targeted immunotherapies. Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) is a promising target for GBC immunotherapy.

Immunotherapy and Clinical Implications: Therapeutic agents that target immune checkpoints, such as anti-PD-1/PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies, are being investigated for their potential use in treating GBC. Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating the safety and efficacy of these treatments. Currently, two key drugs used in the management of GBC are Pembrolizumab, which targets PD-1, and Durvalumab, which targets PD-L1 [28]. Preliminary findings suggest that these drugs may have enhanced effectiveness when used in combination therapies. Consequently, there is a compelling need to identify more effective targets for immunotherapy in GBC [28]. Additionally, there is promising research focused on personalized cancer vaccines that specifically target tumor antigens. These vaccines aim to activate the immune system to better recognize and combat cancer cells. However, there has been limited research into their application for GBC [218, 219]. Moreover, while still in experimental stages for GBC, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has demonstrated potential in treating other types of cancers, presenting possible future therapeutic options for patients with GBC [220, 221].In conclusion, the interplay between GBC and the immune system is intricate, involving various immunological pathways that affect tumor development and treatment responses. A comprehensive understanding of these pathways is essential for formulating effective immunotherapeutic strategies and enhancing patient outcomes.

V. Current and future treatment options

GBC is the most common biliary tract malignancy, accounting for approximately two-thirds of cases. The prognosis for GBC is generally poor, with a 5-year survival rate of only about 5%–15%, due to late-stage diagnosis and high recurrence rates (60%–70%) after surgery [220].

Surgical treatment is the primary approach for early-stage GBC, with radical resection being the only curative option [221]. While radical surgical resection remains the primary treatment for early-stage GBC, gemcitabine and platinum-based chemotherapies are typically used for unresectable, locally advanced, and metastatic GBC. Chemotherapy plays a vital role, particularly for advanced or unresectable GBC, serving as a primary treatment, adjuvant therapy, or for symptom management in metastatic cases [221, 222]. Common chemotherapy regimens include gemcitabine and cisplatin (a standard combination), and FOLFOX (Leucovorin, Fluorouracil, and Oxaliplatin).

Targeted therapy, which has evolved since its initial development in 1988, aims to selectively inhibit molecular targets linked to tumor cell processes, utilizing small molecules and therapeutic antibodies to disrupt signaling pathways [222]. Recent advances in sequencing technology and molecular profiling are facilitating the development and clinical application of novel targeted and immunotherapeutic drugs, refining therapeutic strategies for GBC [223]. The most recent comprehensive review of targeted therapies for GBC was published in 2020 [224]. Tables 3 and 4 present the updated status of these therapies, highlighting ongoing clinical trials at various stages worldwide and FDA approved targeted therapies. Due to the rarity of GBC, some targeted therapies listed are approved for broader biliary tract cancers rather than GBC specifically. These agents are included based on shared molecular alterations and their clinical relevance to GBC. However, the clinical translation of these approaches requires a more integrated understanding of the molecular drivers of disease and their therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Recent insights into GBC biology indicate that diverse oncogenic alterations converge on a limited number of signaling pathways, particularly the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK axis, providing a unifying framework for therapeutic intervention. HER2-targeted therapy has shown promise in patients with ERBB2 amplification or overexpression, with trastuzumab-based combinations demonstrating clinical activity [225]. Given that HER2 signaling activates MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways, its inhibition may suppress ERK-driven tumor growth. In contrast, KRAS-mutant GBC remains therapeutically challenging due to constitutive MEK–ERK activation and resistance to upstream receptor-targeted therapies [60]. This highlights the need for downstream targeting strategies, including MEK and ERK inhibitors, to overcome resistance mechanisms. Combining ERK pathway inhibition with immunotherapy represents a promising approach. ERK signaling contributes to immune evasion through regulation of PD-L1 and the tumor microenvironment [223], while its inhibition may enhance tumor immunogenicity and improve responses to immune checkpoint blockade. Biomarker-driven stratification is essential for optimizing therapy. Genomic alterations (e.g., KRAS, ERBB2), epigenetic regulators, and immune markers such as PD-L1 and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes may guide personalized treatment. Collectively, these advances underscore a shift toward mechanism-based, precision oncology in GBC, with the MEK–ERK pathway serving as a central convergence node and therapeutic target.

MEK–ERK signaling as a central convergence hub in GBC

Our pathway analysis revealed that numerous mutated or aberrantly expressed genes in GBC influence diverse signaling pathways that ultimately converge on the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK axis (MAPK pathway), identifying it as one of the most consistently altered signaling node. Consistent with prior studies and KEGG pathway mapping, this axis emerges not simply as one of several dysregulated pathways, but as a central signaling hub through which diverse oncogenic inputs are integrated to drive GBC initiation and progression [Figure 4B]. At the genomic level, recurrent alterations in KRAS and ERBB2 directly activate upstream MAPK signaling, while loss of tumor suppressors such as TP53 and CDKN2A removes critical constraints on proliferation and survival, indirectly reinforcing ERK-dependent oncogenic output [154, 155, 156]. Likewise, alterations in chromatin-remodeling and DNA repair regulators, including ARID1A/2 and RAD51, promote genomic instability and further sustain aberrant downstream signaling [186, 201].

This convergence is reinforced at the epigenetic and post-transcriptional level. DNA methylation, m6A modification, and non-coding RNA-mediated regulation collectively reshape MAPK pathway activity through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Multiple miRNAs discussed in this review, including miR-145-5p, miR-181b-5p, and miR-575, regulate key signaling intermediates such as KRAS, EGFR, PTEN, and MAPK-associated effectors, while lncRNAs such as LINC01410, PAGBC, HGBC, and DGCR5 amplify oncogenic signaling through ERBB, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK/ERK pathway cross-talks [Tables 1 and 2]. Notably, these regulatory interactions do not act in isolation; rather, they repeatedly reinforce upstream receptor signaling, PI3K–MAPK cross-talk, and ERK-linked transcriptional output, highlighting non-coding RNAs as an additional regulatory layer that stabilizes MEK–ERK signaling in GBC. Importantly, convergence on MEK–ERK is further reinforced by the tumor microenvironment. inflammatory cytokines, stromal interactions, and immune-derived signals can sustain ERK pathway activation independently of canonical driver mutations, thereby extending MAPK signaling beyond tumor-intrinsic genetic alterations [187, 207, 212]. Cytokine-driven signaling, particularly through IL-6- and TNF-α-associated inflammatory circuits [211], may further potentiate ERK activity through cross-talk with STAT and PI3K-linked pathways, while oncogenic ERK signaling contributes to immune escape through regulation of PD-L1 expression and broader remodeling of the tumor immune milieu [207, 211]. This positions MEK–ERK not only as a tumor-intrinsic signaling dependency, but also as a key interface linking oncogenic signaling, stromal adaptation, and immune evasion in GBC.

The translational implications of this convergence are substantial. HER2-directed therapies may attenuate upstream ERK activation in ERBB2-amplified tumors, whereas KRAS-mutant GBC, often resistant to receptor-level inhibition, may be more effectively addressed through MEK- or ERK-directed strategies, particularly in rational combination with epigenetic modulators or immune checkpoint blockade. Together, these observations support a model in which MEK–ERK signaling functions not merely as a downstream effector, but as the dominant integrative node linking genetic, epigenetic, and microenvironmental determinants of GBC. This convergence framework represents a central mechanistic insight of the present review and provides a biologically coherent rationale for prioritizing MEK–ERK-centered therapeutic strategies in GBC.

Conclusion

Gallbladder cancer remains one of the most aggressive and therapeutically challenging biliary tract malignancies, owing to its late clinical presentation, marked molecular heterogeneity, and limited treatment options. Molecular profiling has uncovered a complex and interconnected pathogenic landscape in which recurrent genetic alterations, including TP53, KRAS, ERBB2, and CDKN2A, interact with broad epigenetic and transcriptomic deregulation, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, miRNAs, lncRNAs, and m6A-associated regulation [Figure 5]. However, the evidence synthesized in this review suggests that this complexity is not biologically random. Rather, these diverse molecular alterations repeatedly converge on a restricted set of signaling networks—most notably Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK, PI3K–AKT–mTOR, WNT, and p53—that collectively govern proliferation, apoptosis, EMT, metastatic progression, and immune evasion.

Among these, the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK axis emerges as the most consistent central signaling node integrating upstream oncogenic signals from receptor tyrosine kinases, PI3K pathway cross-talk, inflammatory mediators, and non-coding RNA–driven regulatory programs. By integrating genomic, epigenetic, transcriptomic, and microenvironmental alterations into a unified signaling framework, this review moves beyond descriptive cataloguing to propose a mechanistic model of GBC centered on signaling convergence. In this context, KEGG pathway mapping further supports the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK axis as a dominant oncogenic hub, highlighting it as the most recurrent point of molecular convergence across the pathways and regulatory networks examined in this review.

Importantly, this convergence-based framework has direct translational relevance. The identification of MEK–ERK-centered signaling as a dominant molecular dependency provides a biologically coherent rationale for biomarker development, patient stratification, and rational combination therapies in GBC. Strategic combinations of targeted inhibitors, epigenetic modulators, and immune-directed therapies may therefore offer greater clinical benefit than single-agent approaches. In parallel, the emerging ability to detect epigenetic regulators and circulating RNA signatures offers promising opportunities for minimally invasive diagnostics and earlier intervention.

However, while this review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the molecular landscape of GBC, the available evidence remains derived largely from heterogeneous studies with variable cohort sizes, geographic representation, and methodological approaches, which may limit the generalizability of current findings. Although integrative analyses highlight convergence on key signaling pathways, particularly the MEK–ERK axis, direct functional validation in GBC-specific models remains necessary. The continued expansion of multi-omics and single-cell datasets further underscores the evolving nature of this field. Future efforts should prioritize large-scale integrative studies, functional validation of key signaling nodes, and the development of clinically actionable biomarkers and rational combination therapies to advance precision oncology in GBC. Thus, by uniting these molecular insights into a cohesive framework, the field stands poised to transform GBC management—from late-stage palliative therapy toward earlier detection, personalized therapy, and ultimately improved survival outcomes.
