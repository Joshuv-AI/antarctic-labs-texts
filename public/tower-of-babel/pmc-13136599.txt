Characteristics of Cognitive Function and Brain Alterations in Pneumoconiosis: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Xuanyu Huang, Yilin Hu, Jun Cheng, Hailong Lin, Xiaochun Chen, Erhan Yu, Jiawei Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesAirborne particulate matter (PM) exposure may induce brain alterations and increase risks of cognitive impairment. Pneumoconiosis patients are exposed to harmful levels of PM for an extended period, providing an opportunity and a paradigm to study the impacts of PM and its interactions with the brain and cognition. The characteristics of cognitive function and brain alterations in pneumoconiosis, as well as their associations, remain poorly understood.MethodsDemographic, structural, and resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging, along with neurocognitive data, were collected from a cross‐sectional cohort of pneumoconiosis workers (PWs) and gender‐ and age‐matched healthy controls. Pneumoconiosis was clinically diagnosed and staged by experienced pulmonologists based on radiological findings and comprehensive occupational histories. Cognitive function was assessed using a standardized neuropsychological battery, with domain‐specific analyses. Brain alterations were quantified through measurements of gray matter volume (GMV), mean fractional amplitude of low‐frequency fluctuation (mfALFF), and functional connectivity (FC). Correlational analyses were performed to examine associations between neuroimaging markers and specific cognitive domains.ResultsThe PW group exhibited significantly poorer global cognitive performance, with pronounced deficits in registration memory, verbal cognitive ability, and visuospatial cognitive ability (all p < 0.05). Compared with the normal control group, the PW group showed significantly altered clusters of GMV, mfALFF and FC in specific brain regions, exhibiting mixed patterns. The significant brain alterations were correlated with specific cognitive domains.ConclusionPneumoconiosis patients exhibit worse cognitive function and significant brain alterations. These brain alterations are associated with specific cognitive domains.

Introduction

Air pollution, driven by rapid urbanization and industrialization, has emerged as a critical global environmental issue, which is associated with over 6.5 million deaths each year and imposes over 1.6 trillion dollars of economic losses globally [1, 2, 3]. As one of the main components of air pollution, airborne particulate matter (PM) is categorized as coarse (PM 10, 2.5–10 µm), fine (PM 2.5, 0.1–2.5 µm), or ultrafine (PM 0.1, less than 0.1 µm) based on its aerodynamic diameter size [4]. Long‐term PM exposure has been widely associated with increased risks of infectious, allergic, metabolic, neoplastic, respiratory, cardiovascular and mental illnesses, suggesting its negative impacts on human bodies and the urgent need to intervene this significant public health threat [5].

Besides the aforementioned effects, emerging evidence have demonstrated the potential impacts of PM exposure in brain through multiple mechanisms [6, 7], as well as associations with significantly increased risks of cognitive impairment and dementia [8, 9, 10]. Observational studies have suggested that long‐term exposure to PM is linked to cognitive impairment and brain structure alterations, including volume reduction in regions such as the cortex and hippocampus [11, 12]. Meanwhile, PM exposure has also been correlated with alterations in brain functional activities, such as abnormal decreases or increases in connectivity, as well as changes in network integration and activity patterns [13, 14]. The underlying mechanisms are thought to include the translocation of PM to the brain via either the circulatory system or the olfactory nerve, the initiation of oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades, damage to the blood–brain barrier, or changes in the expression levels of microRNAs such as miR‐6315 and miR‐3588 [15, 16]. PM of all sizes is hypothesized to act as potential neurotoxins that may contribute to brain changes and/or cognitive decline.

Pneumoconiosis represents a group of chronic, fatal lung diseases caused by long‐term inhalation of inorganic dust particles and is one of the most common occupational lung diseases worldwide [17, 18]. Globally, incident cases of pneumoconiosis increased by approximately 66.0% from 1990 to 2017 [19]. This burden is likely underestimated, particularly in rapidly industrializing regions of the Global South, due to challenges in surveillance and diagnosis [20, 21]. Although a few studies have reported poorer cognitive performance and a higher prevalence of cognitive impairment among individuals with pneumoconiosis [22, 23], this population remains understudied in cognitive neuroscience. More importantly, the underlying mechanisms and neural correlates linking pneumoconiosis to cognition remain largely unknown, particularly objective evidence of associated brain alterations.

Given their prolonged and well‐documented exposure to high concentrations of respirable dust, individuals with pneumoconiosis represent a unique human model for studying the long‐term effects of PM on the brain. Using structural and functional MRI, researchers can non‐invasively detect morphological changes in the brain [24], measure spontaneous brain activity, and explore intrinsic neural networks [25]. This study, therefore, aimed to investigate brain structural and functional alterations in pneumoconiosis patients and assess their cognitive performance using a comprehensive neuropsychological battery. We hypothesized that patients would demonstrate significant brain alterations correlated with domain‐specific cognitive deficits. By examining this model population, this study seeks to enhance our understanding of the neural consequences of long‐term PM exposure and provide insights relevant to both public health policy on air pollution and the clinical management of pneumoconiosis.

Methods

Subjects

Twenty‐one male workers previously diagnosed with pneumoconiosis (PW) were recruited through public advertisements and underwent comprehensive assessments at the Memory Clinic of the Neurology Department, Fujian Medical University Union Hospital. Twenty‐one well‐matched normal controls (NCs) were selected from a community‐based cohort in Fuzhou, China, and were matched to the PW group as a whole based on age, sex, and years of education to the greatest extent possible.

All the subjects from the PW group had a clear occupational history in either the tunnel or the stone industry. The diagnosis of PW was made by two independent experienced respiratory physicians according to the established guidelines and published national diagnostic criteria (GBZ70‐2015) [26]. Subjects of the NC group had no objective cognitive impairment in cognitive tests, no history of neurological or psychiatric disease, and no self‐perceived cognitive decline concern. The details regarding the exclusion criteria are provided in the Supporting Information S1.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital, and written informed consent was taken from all individual participants. The study data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Neurocognitive Assessments

All participants received a comprehensive set of clinical evaluations, which included demographic characteristics, history‐taking, neurological examinations, and a suite of neuropsychological tests, by the same experienced neurologist. To clarify the condition of cognitive function and characteristics of different impaired cognitive domains in PW, the neuropsychological tests and their sub‐tests were further categorized into four cognitive domains, based on previous studies on cognitive processes [27]. Afterwards, the raw scores of each test were standardized into Z‐scores by using the SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, New York). The mean of the related tests' Z‐scores were regarded as the composite scores of each cognitive domain. The details regarding the neuropsychological assessments and division of four cognitive domains are provided in Supporting Information S1.

Image Data Acquisition

MRI images were acquired using a 3 T GE Discovery 750 scanner (Chicago, IL, USA) equipped with a 64‐channel head coil. The structural MR imaging protocol included sagittal, coronal, and axial pre‐contrast axial fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) T1‐weighted (T1W) images. T1‐weighted images were acquired using three‐dimension brain volume imaging (3D‐BRAVO) sequence: repetition time [TR] = 8.6 ms, echo time [TE] = 3.4 ms, flip angle [FA] = 12, field of view [FOV] = 256 × 256 mm, slice thickness = 1.1 mm, data matrix = 256 × 256, and slices = 180. Bold‐fMRI images were obtained using an echo‐planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: 200 volumes, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 40 ms, FA = 90°, FOV = 256 × 256 mm, slice gap = 1 mm, 3 mm thickness by 40 slices, matrix = 64 × 64, and voxel size = 4 × 4 × 3 mm3. All subjects were requested to lie quietly with their eyes closed and keep awake throughout the scan. To minimize the disturbance of head motion, the heads of the subjects were cozily positioned with cushions inside the coil.

Data Postprocessing

Voxel‐Based Morphometry (VBM) Analysis

The T1‐weighted images were processed using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12) [28] and the Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12) [29]. In short, all images were denoised, bias‐corrected, affine‐registered, and skull‐stripped. Then the images were segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using the tissue probability maps (TPM) and normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space and modulated. Then the modulated GM images were smoothed using an 8 × 8 × 8 mm full‐width‐at‐half‐maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel for VBM analysis.

fMRI Data Preprocessing

All fMRI data were preprocessed using SPM12 [29] and the Data Processing & Analysis for Resting‐State Brain Imaging (http://rfmri.org/dpabi, V6.0) [30] as described in previous studies [31]. For images stabilization and to allow the subjects to adapt to the environment, the first 10 time frames were discarded. The remaining volumes were corrected for timing differences and motion effects. Then the individual T1‐weighted images were co‐registered to the mean functional image and segmented into GM, WM, and CSF by using a unified segmentation algorithm. The motion‐corrected functional volumes were spatially normalized to MNI space and resampled to 3 mm isotropic voxels by using the normalization parameters estimated during the unified segmentation. After smoothing with a Gaussian smoothing kernel (FWHM = 6 × 6 × 6 mm), nuisance signals (including a linear trend of the time course, Friston 24‐head motion parameters, the mean global signals, as well as WM and CSF signals) were extracted and regressed out to reduce the effects of non‐neuronal signals. Subjects were discarded if head motion exceeded 3 mm translation in x, y, or z directions or 3° of maximum rotation about the three axes.

Calculation of Mean Fractional Amplitude of Low‐Frequency Fluctuation (mfALFF)

fALFF represents the ratio of the regional power spectrum of low frequency to that of the whole frequency range. Following the method of previous study [32, 33], the mfALFF analysis was calculated in the frequency range of 0.01 to 0.08 Hz by dividing the fALFF of each voxel by the global mean fALFF value.

Seed‐Based Functional Connectivity (FC) Analysis

Considering the roles of anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [34] and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) [35] involved in cognitive function, as well as the significantly altered cluster from mfALFF calculation, they were respectively selected as the region of interests (ROIs), and the time series were extracted. Then seed‐based FC analysis was performed, in brief, Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between the seed and every voxel in the brain. Fisher's z‐transform analysis was applied to these coefficients in order to enhance normality. The resulting FC maps were utilized for the FC analysis.

Statistical Analyses

The statistical analyses of neurocognitive assessments were performed with SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, New York). The two‐sample t‐tests and the chi‐squared test were adaptively performed to compare the data of demographic and neurocognitive tests. The statistical threshold was set to p < 0.05. The statistical tests were two‐sided.

The group difference between the PW and NC groups in gray matter volume (GMV) and mfALFF were performed using two‐sample t‐tests in SPM12, and for the GMV analysis, total brain volume was used as covariate. The statistical significance was set at voxel‐level p < 0.01, cluster size > 100 without correction for GMV; voxel‐level p < 0.01, cluster‐level p < 0.05, Gaussian Random Field (GRF) corrected for mfALFF, and voxel‐level p < 0.01, cluster‐level p < 0.05, GRF corrected for z‐value FC patterns.

Pearson correlation coefficients were utilized to investigate the associations between GMV, mfALFF, mean FC signal values and different cognitive domains (p < 0.05).

Results

Demographics and Cognitive Functions

There were no significant differences for age, education, depressive levels [assessed by 17‐item Hamilton Depression Rating scale (HAMD17) and Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D)], activities of daily life [assessed by Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study ‐ Activities of Daily Living scale (ADCS‐ADL)], and everyday cognition [assessed by the Measurement of Everyday Cognition (ECog)] between the PW and NC groups (all p > 0.05). The Chinese version of the Mini—Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Beijing version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) were utilized to understand the performances of global cognitive functions in subjects. Compared with the NC group, the PW group performed more poorly in both MMSE and MoCA (both p < 0.05) (Table 1).

GMV Differences

No significant differences in GMV were observed under voxel‐level thresholds of p < 0.001 or p < 0.01, cluster‐level p < 0.05, after applying Family‐wise error rate or GRF corrections. However, significant GMV differences in the PW group emerged under an uncorrected threshold of voxel‐level p < 0.01 with a minimum cluster size > 100. Compared with NC, the PW group showed decreased GMV in both cerebral hemispheres of middle temporal gyrus and superior frontal gyrus, as well as left supramarginal gyrus; meanwhile GMV increased in right middle occipital gyrus (Figure 2A, Supporting Information S1: Table 1).

MfALFF

The cluster in the left occipital cortex showed significantly increased mfALFF in the PW group (Figure 2B, Supporting Information S1: Table 1). The peak voxel was chosen as the center of ROI 1 constructed for further FC analysis (peak MNI coordinate: −48, −60, −12).

FC Analysis

ROI 1 showed decreased FC in the cluster located in the left superior temporal gyrus. When utilizing ACC as ROI 2, decreased FC were showed in three clusters, which were located in the left middle temporal gyrus, the right superior temporal gyrus and the left postcentral gyrus. While using PCC as ROI 3, the altered FC were identified in seven clusters: the right middle temporal gyrus, the right superior temporal gyrus and the right superior frontal gyrus showed decreased FC; while the left superior frontal gyrus, the left superior temporal gyrus, the right transverse temporal gyrus and the left medial frontal gyrus showed increased FC (see details in Figure 2C and Supporting Information S1: Table 1).

Associations Between GMV/mfALFF/FC and Cognitive Function

Pearson correlation coefficients were utilized to investigate the relationships between GMV, mfALFF, FC and different cognitive domains (Figure 3). The GMV of left middle temporal gyrus (cluster 1) was positively related to the registration memory (r = 0.416, p = 0.048, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.31, 0.71). The verbal cognitive ability was significantly positive correlated with the left middle temporal gyrus (cluster1; r = 0.442, p = 0.035, 95% CI: 0.24, 0.72) and the left supramarginal gyrus (cluster7; r = 0.473, p = 0.023, 95% CI: 0.38, 0.74) in GMV, as well as showing positive correlations with the left superior temporal gyrus (ROI1; r = 0.705, p < 0.000, 95% CI: 0.37, 0.88) and the left middle temporal gyrus (ROI2 cluster1; r = 0.662, p = 0.002, 95% CI: 0.40, 0.86) in FC.

Discussion

The present study investigated brain structural and functional alterations, domain‐specific cognitive impairments, and their potential associations in patients with pneumoconiosis. Our main findings indicate that, compared with the NC group, the PW group exhibited: (a) significant impairments in registration memory, verbal ability, and visuospatial ability; (b) regional GMV differences, though these did not survive stringent multiple comparison correction; (c) increased mfALFF in the left occipital cortex; (d) disrupted FC among specific brain regions; and (e) specific correlations between certain imaging measures (GMV, FC) and cognitive domain scores. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have focused on the domain‐specific cognitive performances as well as the alterations of brain structure and function in pneumoconiosis workers. Here, we also reveal the potential associations between the impaired cognitive domains and the intrinsic structures/activities of brain.

Compared with the NC group, the PW group showed poorer global cognitive performance, consistent with previous studies [23]. More granular analysis revealed that the most affected cognitive domains were registration memory, verbal ability, and visuospatial ability registration memory, verbal cognitive ability and visuospatial cognitive ability. These findings align with prior studies linking air pollution to visuospatial dysfunction in adults [36, 37]. It is noteworthy that visuospatial processing is also a domain commonly affected in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer's disease (AD) [38]. However, this parallel should be interpreted with caution, as the underlying pathophysiology may differ substantially. The PW group also demonstrated worse verbal ability, consistent with previous studies on air pollution and cognition [39, 40, 41]. While some studies suggest that verbal episodic memory can predict conversion to AD dementia [42], and there is an increased awareness of shared cognitive impairment patterns and increased risks between pneumoconiosis and MCI/AD, the interactions and any potential causality remain speculative and require further elucidation.

Brain atrophy detected by MRI is regarded as an important biomarker of neurodegeneration [43]. In the PW group, the decreased GMV were primarily in the prefrontal cortex and middle temporal gyrus. Significantly, these brain structural alterations in specific regions of PW are in line with the similar changes caused by air pollution, exhibiting the characteristics of AD‐like cortical atrophies [7]. Decreased GMV in the left supramarginal gyrus was also described in the PW group. The supramarginal gyrus is part of the somatosensory cortex, which is related to the perception of the body's position in space and memory function [44]. Consistent with a surface‐based morphometry study, high‐level exposure to PM was proved to be related with reduced thickness in the supramarginal gyrus [45]. It is important to emphasize, however, that these GMV differences reached significance only under uncorrected statistical thresholds and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Besides atrophies, we also observed increased GMV in the right middle occipital gyrus in pneumoconiosis patients. However, previous results about PM exposure and the occipital lobe changes were controversial: some studies demonstrated a positive association [45, 46], while others indicated a negative correlation [47]. Furthermore, the increased mfALFF was detected in the left occipital cortex of the PW group, which indicated an enhanced intrinsic neural activity in this region. Given the role of the occipital lobe in visual processing [48] and the observed visuospatial deficits, one speculative interpretation is that the increased mfALFF might reflect a potential compensatory mechanism. However, this remains highly hypothetical, and alternative explanations, such as neuroinflammatory responses, cannot be ruled out.

When using the cluster with significantly increased mfALFF as an ROI (ROI 1), decreased FC was observed with the left superior temporal gyrus, a region important for visual‐spatial awareness [49]. This finding is consistent with a study on early deafness, which found that FC of the superior temporal gyrus was positively associated with visuospatial ability and working memory [50]. This may offer a potential, though preliminary, neural explanation for the visuospatial deficits observed in our patients.

Exposure to air pollution and PM has been correlated with decreased functional connectivity [37] and distinct network changes [51] within the default mode network (DMN). Since ACC is the central to a broad array of cognitive, sensorimotor, affective and visceral functions involved in DMN [52, 53], whereas PCC is a major node within the DMN with high metabolic activity and dense structural connectivity to widespread brain regions [54], for this reason, we then respectively selected ACC and PCC as the ROIs for FC analysis. When using ACC as the ROI (ROI 2), the decreased FC was identified in the middle temporal gyrus; while when using PCC as ROI (ROI 3), a mixed pattern of connectivity was observed: the decreased FC in the right superior temporal gyrus and the right superior frontal gyrus, and the increased FC in the left superior temporal gyrus and the left superior frontal lobe. These altered regions are parts of the DMN [55], indicating that the connectivity of DMN was significantly changed in pneumoconiosis patients. Interestingly, when using the PCC as an ROI, we observed a pattern of decreased FC in the right hemisphere and increased FC in the left hemisphere. Some previous research has suggested that air pollution might be weakly associated with cortical thinning in right‐hemispheric DMN regions, potentially accelerating aging‐like processes in the brain [56]. While the phenomenon of progressive asymmetry‐loss is observed in both aging and AD [57], the pattern we observed is complex and its interpretation remains uncertain. We emphasize that these parallels are speculative and should not be construed as evidence that pneumoconiosis directly causes or mimics AD‐like neuropathology.

The present study also demonstrated correlations between neuroimaging measures and cognitive domain scores. The GMV of the middle temporal gyrus was positively related to the registration memory and verbal cognitive ability, which is consistent with the established role of this region in semantic processing [58, 59]. A study on stroke supported the same finding of the positive correlation between the FC of the left superior temporal gyrus (using the left occipital cortex as ROI) and the verbal cognitive ability [60]. When using ACC as ROI, the FC of the left middle temporal gyrus showed a significant positive correlation with the verbal cognitive ability, consistent with findings from a recent study [61].

Epidemiological studies and animal experiments have highlighted the association between long‐term exposure to PM exposure, air pollution, and cognitive impairment [62, 63, 64]. Although the type and concentration of particles may differ from those in general air pollution, the occupational exposure history of pneumoconiosis provides a unique and extreme case to observe and study the specific actions and mechanisms of long‐term PM exposure on cognition. In addition to brain imaging and cohort studies, more works are needed to further understand and clarify the mechanisms of pneumoconiosis and PM exposure on cognitive impairment and long‐term damage to health. On the other hand, it is equally crucial to enhance occupational safety awareness and promote the protective measures in the workplace, and to increase the investment and attention to the improvement of air pollution. Our findings underscore the value of a neuroscientific perspective as a complementary dimension to pulmonology and occupational medicine.

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations and methodological considerations should be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design and relatively small sample size may limit generalizability. Although power analysis indicated a Cohen's d of approximately 0.80 (two‐tailed α = 0.05), suggesting sufficient power to detect large effects, the study may have lacked sensitivity to identify smaller, yet potentially meaningful, differences. Notably, GMV alterations were only significant under uncorrected thresholds, and no differences were observed under more stringent corrections, warranting cautious interpretation. Additionally, the heterogeneity of the pneumoconiosis group, such as varying durations of PM exposure and the inability to assess physicochemical properties of PM (e.g., diameter, composition), further complicates interpretation. The absence of detailed quantitative data on occupational exposure (e.g., cumulative dust dose) and ambient or indoor air pollution also limits exploration of dose–response relationships. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal designs with repeated brain MRI scans, larger multi‐center cohorts, and comprehensive exposure assessments to disentangle occupational and environmental contributions. Integrating pulmonary function tests, detailed occupational histories, and radiological staging under the guidance of pulmonology or occupational medicine specialists would enhance clinical and etiological relevance. Moreover, well‐designed animal studies may help clarify the neurobiological impacts of specific PM characteristics.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our findings highlight the potential value of a neuroscientific perspective in comprehensively understanding the health impact of pneumoconiosis, complementing the established domains of pulmonology and occupational medicine. The observed cognitive deficits, particularly in memory and visuospatial function, may have meaningful implications for the quality of life and clinical management of individuals with pneumoconiosis or those exposed to similar environmental conditions. These findings underscore the importance of early cognitive monitoring and proactive occupational health protection.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that patients with pneumoconiosis exhibit poorer cognitive performance and significant brain alterations, which are linked to deficits in specific cognitive domains. These findings offer valuable insights into the potential neurological consequences of long‐term PM exposure and underscore the importance of public health strategies aimed at occupational safety and air quality improvement.
