Guided completion at scale: completion worksheets and embedded review videos for microbiology
Authors: Jordan A. Smink, Robert W. Lamb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLarge-enrollment microbiology courses often leave little time for the guided practice students need to reconstruct complex biological processes. This Tips and Tools article describes a scalable guided completion workflow developed in a 285-student General Microbiology course. Instructors convert process diagrams into partially completed worksheets, provide answer keys for immediate self-check, and embed short document-camera walkthroughs into unit review videos. Students first attempt the worksheet from memory, use course materials to complete remaining gaps, check their work, and then revisit the process through instructor-modeled reasoning. A DNA replication worksheet illustrates how the approach supports learning of sequence, directionality, enzyme function, and mechanistic consequence. The workflow uses familiar instructional tools but organizes them into a reusable bridge between lecture, independent study, and assessment. This article describes implementation only and does not report student outcome data.

INTRODUCTION

Large-enrollment microbiology courses often move students from instructor explanation directly to independent study. This transition is efficient for coverage, but it can compress guided practice, where students attempt a task, receive feedback, and see expert reasoning modeled. That missing middle step matters because novices benefit from worked examples, guided practice, and scaffolds that make expert thinking visible before independent performance (1–3).

Microbiology is especially suited to this guided practice support because many core topics are sequential, causal processes rather than isolated facts. Students learning DNA replication, biofilm formation, CRISPR gene editing, metabolism, host-pathogen interactions, adaptive immunity, or microbial digestion must track start and end states, directionality, intermediate steps, decision points, and consequences. Without structured practice, they may memorize vocabulary while missing pathway logic. For example, a student may know what replication enzymes are but misplace their functions, confuse leading and lagging strand synthesis, or fail to connect strand orientation to primer placement and Okazaki fragment formation.

With hundreds of students, it is difficult for instructors to check in with each learner or provide timely, individualized feedback, especially in courses without substantial teaching assistant support. Students may also be reluctant to ask questions in front of a large group, and some may not connect a poor grade with a specific gap in understanding. Guided completion creates a lower-stakes checkpoint: students attempt the process on their own, compare their reasoning with instructor feedback, and then see common misconceptions addressed as part of the review. The instructional challenge is to create a scalable bridge between lecture and independent performance. Students need to reconstruct a process from memory, check their understanding, and then watch an expert model the same task. This design draws on evidence that retrieval practice and feedback support learning more effectively than passive review alone (4, 5). PROCEDURE

Guided completion workflow

Guided completion combines three familiar instructional moves into one scalable routine: a partially completed worksheet, immediate self-check, and reusable instructor modeling. The novelty of the workflow is not the invention of guided practice itself, which has long been used in education, but the way these familiar elements are organized, reused, and scaled for large-enrollment undergraduate biology courses. In many college biology courses, guided practice is difficult to sustain because instructors must balance content coverage, limited class time, and large student numbers. This workflow adapts principles from worked examples, completion problems, and instructor modeling into a practical format that can be implemented without requiring individual feedback on every student submission. In lecture, the instructor identifies selected mechanisms as “draw/describe” targets, signaling that students should reconstruct and explain the process rather than simply recognize it. After introducing the topic, the instructor converts a pathway figure or process diagram into a completion worksheet by removing selected labels, steps, arrows, outcomes, or decision points while preserving enough structure for orientation. This format draws from completion problems and worked-example research, which suggests that partial solutions can reduce unnecessary cognitive load while requiring meaningful reasoning (6–8).

Students first attempt the worksheet from memory. They then use notes or course materials to fill the remaining gaps before checking their work against a completed answer key on the reverse side or in a companion file. This keeps the first attempt retrieval-based while giving students immediate feedback before misconceptions are reinforced.

At the end of each unit, the same worksheet reappears inside an exam-preparation review video. A brief document-camera segment is stitched into the relevant point of the larger review, where the instructor completes the worksheet while narrating the logic behind each step. Pause cues invite students to try portions before watching the solution. This segmented, learner-controlled use of video is consistent with multimedia learning principles, particularly the value of breaking complex explanations into manageable segments (9, 10). The same visual grammar is reused on assessments with different blanks or prompts, allowing students to practice transfer rather than memorize one completed image.

Example: DNA replication worksheet

The DNA replication worksheet, as included as Appendix 1A, illustrates how guided completion moves students from recognizing a finished diagram toward reconstructing a mechanism. The front side presents a replication fork schematic with key callouts removed. A constrained word bank separates “Orienting Pieces,” such as 5′/3′ ends, leading and lagging strands, primers, and Okazaki fragments, from “The Enzymes,” such as helicase, primase, DNA polymerase, ligase, and single-strand binding proteins. This structure reduces the burden of recalling every term from scratch while requiring students to work through strand directionality, enzyme placement, and continuous versus discontinuous synthesis.

The worksheet also includes “Stop and Think” prompts that ask students to connect labels to mechanism. Rather than only naming parts, students consider why the lagging strand requires fragments, how primer placement follows from strand orientation, and what each enzyme contributes. The reverse side provides the completed figure as an immediate answer key, followed by “Your Next Moves” prompts for spaced retrieval and “What If” questions about inhibiting specific replication components.

In the unit review video, the instructor completes the worksheet under a document camera while explaining decisions about enzyme placement, strand directionality, and primer location.

Practical tips, adaptations, and limitations

Several design choices make guided completion more useful and manageable. First, avoid over-blanking. Students need enough anchors to recognize the process and begin reasoning. Blanks should target high-value features such as sequence, directionality, enzyme function, causal transitions, or consequences rather than every label. Second, keep document-camera walkthroughs brief. The review video may cover an entire unit, but each guided completion segment should focus on one process and include pause cues before key answers.

Plan accessibility and logistics from the beginning. Provide captions, downloadable PDFs, and low-bandwidth alternatives when possible. Use consistent file names for blank worksheets, answer keys, and review videos so that students can locate materials and instructors can reuse them. The workflow can be adapted to biology topics such as microbiology, cell biology, and many other subdisciplines in which students track sequence or causality. Biofilm formation worksheets can ask students to reconstruct attachment, EPS production, maturation, and dispersion. CRISPR worksheets can separate guide-RNA targeting, Cas-mediated cleavage, and editing outcomes. The same approach can support metabolism, host-pathogen interactions, adaptive immunity, and microbial digestion.

This article describes implementation rather than student outcomes. Instructors who wish to evaluate the impact could map exam items to worksheet steps, review learning management system analytics, collect brief feedback, or analyze common errors on worksheet-aligned assessments. The faculty should consult their Institutional Review Board before disseminating student data. Limitations include upfront preparation time, image-permission constraints, possible over-scaffolding, and the need for clear navigation in longer review videos.

CONCLUSION

Guided completion is novel not because its parts are new but because they are organized into a scalable feedback loop for large-enrollment biology courses. Completion worksheets create a structured recall attempt. Answer keys provide immediate self-check. Stitched document-camera segments model expert reasoning where students are already reviewing. Together, these pieces restore the guided-practice step often lost between lecture and independent study.

In practice, the workflow was manageable to develop and disseminate. For routine course use, worksheets were created by selecting an appropriate course image, placing it into PowerPoint, and overlaying white-filled text boxes to remove selected labels, steps, or features. A small set of follow-up questions was then added to prompt explanation beyond labeling. Once an image was selected, a worksheet could generally be prepared, checked, and printed in approximately 15 min. For publication, an openly licensed image was selected to avoid permission concerns. In the course, worksheets were made available both as course materials and as printed copies outside the instructor’s office. A file box was kept stocked so that students could pick up one or more worksheets without needing to schedule a formal appointment.

Completion was optional rather than required, which allowed the worksheets to function as a low-stakes study tool. This distribution method also had an unexpected benefit: students who may not have known where the instructor’s office was, or who may have been hesitant to use office hours, often stopped by to pick up a worksheet and then asked a brief question. From the instructor perspective, this made student confusion more visible and created more focused conversations about specific steps, terms, or misconceptions.

Although this article does not report formal assessment data, informal student comments and department course reviews suggested that students found the worksheets helpful. Students reported that the activity often revealed gaps they had not recognized during passive review; in some cases, they believed they understood a topic until the worksheet required them to reconstruct it independently. The completed answer keys and review-video walkthroughs then helped students compare their reasoning with the instructor’s explanation, ask more specific questions, and direct their studying more effectively.

For process-heavy biology topics, this alignment helps students move beyond recognizing completed diagrams toward reconstructing sequence, directionality, mechanism, and consequence. The approach uses ordinary materials, including worksheets, answer keys, review videos, and pathway figures, but combines them in a reusable, accessible, and practical way for large-enrollment instruction.
