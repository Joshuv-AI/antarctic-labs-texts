Odyssey from the street: a successful aortic arch replacement after firearm injury
Authors: Michele D’Alonzo, Antonino Massimiliano Grande, Antonio Fiore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis case report depicts a rare clinical scenario where emergency cardiac surgery was required following a firearm wound resulting in traumatic aortic arch injury. A penetrating injury of the aortic arch is a life-threatening condition with a high overall mortality rate.Case presentationIn this paper we present a 53-year-old male who presented to the emergency department after being shot. The patient's management started with a comprehensive assessment including contrast-enhanced computer tomography (CT) scan, crucial in delineating the precise anatomical extension of the injury. In our specific case, the CT-scan showed aortic arch upper wall rupture between the brachiocephalic arterial trunk and the left common carotid caused by a .22 calibre bullet without exit orifice from the mediastinum. The patient was rushed to the operating room for ascending aorta and aortic arch replacement with brachiocephalic artery reimplantation. The exceptional nature of this case lies not only in its infrequency but also in the surgical complexities amidst an emergent situation.ConclusionsAddressing this complexity demanded a multidisciplinary approach integrating meticulous preoperative planning and intraoperative dexterity to achieve a favourable outcome in high-stakes scenario.Keywords: Aortic arch, Firearm, Gunshot, Debranching, Emergency surgery

Introduction

Firearm-related injuries have emerged as an increasing concern in Europe, particularly in France, where recent studies report an incidence of 10.4 cases per 100,000 inhabitants annually. This alarming statistic marks a significant six-fold increase over the last decade, shedding light on the pressing need for comprehensive strategies to address this escalating issue [1].

Within this concerning landscape, thoracic gunshot-related trauma stands out as a considerable issue, accounting for approximately 18.5% of cases and ranking second only after head injuries. Tragically, almost half of victims succumb to their injuries before reaching the hospital. Few casualties manage to reach medical facilities alive due to haemorrhagic shock and an even smaller fraction arrives promptly for surgical intervention. The prognosis for thoracic aortic injuries remains exceedingly grim, with a staggering overall mortality rate exceeding 90% [2].

In this case report we present a 53-year-old male who presented to the emergency department after been shot and underwent cardiac surgery for ascending aorta and aortic arch replacement.

Case history

A 53-year-old male presented to the emergency department after a gunshot wound in the left para-sternal area, inflicted by a 0.22 calibre (approximately 6 mm) bullet which remained within the mediastinal cavity without exiting. Initially the patient was stable in both respiratory and cardiac functions, retaining consciousness throughout. Given the clinical context, a CT-scan was immediately performed, revealing evident laceration of the aortic arch wall between the brachiocephalic trunk and the left common carotid artery, otherwise other delicate structures such as the mammary artery and the lungs were spared (Fig. 1A). Concurrently, the foreign object was detected within the mediastinum, proximate to the right anterolateral wall of the trachea, without resulting in its damage (Fig. 1B). Concomitant with these findings, there was an onset of severe hemopericardium and a notable left pleural effusion. Soon after the completion of the CT scan (near 1 h after the shot), the patient experienced a rapid decline in clinical status, manifesting in hemodynamic instability (BP 90/50 mmHg, PR 135/min, RR 35/min), necessitating immediate transfer to the operating theatre for emergent cardiothoracic surgery.

(A) At CT scan a large hematoma is evident due to the traumatic rupture of the brachiocephalic trunk. (B) 3-dimension CT-scan reconstruction showing the bullet relationships with the supra-aortic vessels

Under general anaesthesia, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was established along with cannulation of right subclavian artery, femoral artery and femoral vein. A median sternotomy was performed, facilitating the evacuation of a sizable retrosternal hematoma. Following this crucial step, manual compression to the brachiocephalic trunk was applied to better control the bleeding until cooling in extracorporeal circulation was achieved. The supra-aortic branches were isolated, revealing aortic arch and brachiocephalic trunk wall rupture, while the left common carotid artery appeared unaffected. The bullet has been removed from the mediastinum. (Fig. 2)

Fully preserved bullet after surgical extraction from the chest

After the initial aortic clamping, cardioplegic arrest was achieved by anterograde injection of cold crystalloid cardioplegia. Once hypothermia at 28 °C was reached, the brachiocephalic trunk was clamped as high as possible, ensuring cerebral protection through anterograde cerebral perfusion while splanchnic circulation arrest began. Then, the surgeon performed the replacement of the ascending aorta and arch using a 26-sized prosthetic graft, extending up to the origin of the left common carotid artery. The proximal and distal anastomoses of the vascular graft were performed with a continuous single-layer 4 − 0 Prolene suture, reinforced with an interposed felt strip, in a manner similar to that used for aortic dissection repair. After the proximal anastomosis, the standard technique of LV venting was employed using venting through the right superior pulmonary vein. Following aortic unclamping, sinus rhythm was spontaneously restored. Finally, re-implantation of the brachiocephalic trunk in its proximal segment was complete. The total duration of cardiac arrest was 45 min.

The patient was discharged after seven days of hospitalization. The post-operative recovery course was free of complications. The patient had a follow-up CT scan at 6 months, which showed an excellent result of the aortic arch reconstruction.

Discussion

Firearm-related traumatic aortic arch injuries persist as impressive and highly lethal challenges. Mitigating the mortality and morbidity associated with these injuries necessitates comprehensive strategies, from preventive measures to optimized surgical approaches, all underscored by the imperative need for multidisciplinary collaborations and rapid, decisive clinical interventions to improve patient outcomes in these critical scenarios [3]. The gold standard for diagnosing acute traumatic aortic injury (ATAI) is a contrast-enhanced CT scan, which provides sufficient information for patient management. In cases of uncertainty, further investigations should be decided collaboratively between the radiologist and the surgical team, although direct signs of ATAI do not require confirmation through catheter angiography [4]. Given the extreme urgency of the case, there was no opportunity to involve the vascular surgery team. However, it was considered that in such an emergency scenario, TEVAR would not have been the optimal choice regardless, due to the presence of massive hemopericardium, a bullet lodged within the mediastinum, and severe compromise of multiple aortic vascular structures.

A multitude of factors substantially contribute to the intricacy and severity of thoracic trauma and to the large range of clinical scenarios: these determinants encompass the type of firearm employed, the precise locations where the bullet enters and exits the thorax, and the spatial distance separating the shooter from the victim.

In certain cases, the anatomical location of the injury, such as the posterior wall of the aortic arch, presents remarkable challenges due to the intricate vascular structures in proximity. For instance, the proximity of the lesion behind the left subclavian artery complicates surgical accessibility, because of the hindering left carotid artery, emerging in front.

The conventional method entails a full median sternotomy, providing comprehensive access and visualization of the mediastinum [5]. A pivotal aspect of the surgical technique involves inducing circulatory arrest under hypothermic conditions, serving dual purposes. Primarily, this technique facilitates bloodless visualization of the supra-aortic vessels, optimizing the surgical strategy and facilitating aortic arch repair. Furthermore, it enables cerebral perfusion through the right subclavian artery during the critical phase of aortic arch repair, safeguarding neurological function [6]. One practical recommendation emerging from this experience is to always establish a double arterial line—typically through the femoral artery and the right subclavian artery—whenever feasible, to be prepared for the worst-case scenario of an extended rupture involving the supra-aortic vessels. If the subclavian route cannot be used, a deeper hypothermic arrest and retrograde cerebral perfusion would be required, with the well-known drawbacks of increased platelet dysfunction, prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time, and delayed rewarming. In conclusion, the primary goal should always be to save the patient’s life, even if this requires limiting the extent of the surgical procedure. As experience with aortic dissections has shown, extensive aortic repair does not significantly reduce the risk of either distal or proximal reintervention [7].

Key clinical message

Despite advancements in surgical techniques, traumatic aortic arch injuries remain fraught with challenges, characterized by high mortality rates. For this scenario, it is essential to have a well-defined organizational framework and standardized protocols, promoting close cooperation between the emergency physician, radiologist, cardiothoracic surgeon, and anaesthesiologist to ensure rapid and effective patient care.
