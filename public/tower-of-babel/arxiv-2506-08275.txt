The stochastic heat inclusion with fractional time driven by time-space Brownian and Lévy white noise
Olfa Draouil, Rahma Yasmina Moulay Hachemi & Bernt Øksendal

Abstract
We study a time-fractional stochastic heat inclusion driven by additive time-space Brownian and Lévy white noise. The fractional time derivative is interpreted as the Caputo derivative of order α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ) . \alpha\in(0,2). We show the following: a) If a solution exists, then it is a fixed point of a specific set-valued map. b) Conversely, any fixed point of this map is a solution of the heat inclusion. c) Finally, we show that there is at least one fixed point of this map, thereby proving that there is at least one solution of the time-fractional stochastic heat inclusion. A solution Y ⁡ ( t , x ) Y(t,x) is called mild if 𝔼 ⁡ [ Y 2 ​ ( t , x ) ] < ∞ \mathbb{E}[Y^{2}(t,x)]<\infty for all t , x t,x . We show that the solution is mild if α = 1 \alpha=1 & d = 1 , d=1, or α ≥ 1 \alpha\geq 1 & d ∈ { 1 , 2 } d\in\{1,2\} . On the other hand, if α < 1 \alpha<1 we show that the solution is not mild for any space dimension d d .

1
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to study fractional stochastic heat inclusions which can be used to model the transport of substances in porous and fractured media subject to noise and uncertainty. The applications show how our theoretical framework directly addresses real world environmental challenges, the combination of fractional derivatives, set valued maps and Lévy noise captures the phenomena. We illustrate this by two motivating examples:

1.1
Modelling groundwater contaminant transport by stochastic fractional heat inclusion

Contaminant transport in fractured aquifers.

A fractured aquifer is a groundwater system where water (with contaminant) migrate primarily throught cracks or fractures in rocks as well as through porous media like sand or gravel.

The model we use to describe this is the following fractional stochastic heat inclusion:

\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}Y(t,x)\in\lambda\Delta Y(t,x)+G(Y(t,x))+\sigma W(t,x)+\gamma V(t,x);\;(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.1)

with boundary conditions:

\displaystyle Y(0,x)

\displaystyle=\delta(x)\text{ (the Dirac measure at }x),

(1.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow+/-\text{ }\infty}Y(t,x)

\displaystyle=0.

(1.3)

•

represents memory and anomalous diffusion (
\alpha<1
subdiffusion, and when
\alpha>1
superdiffusion) at time t in position x, important in fractured systems where movement doesn’t follow normal diffusion, also we can explain trapping and release of memory effects when the pollutant are trapped in fractures for days and then released suddenly,

•

\Delta Y=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}Y}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}(t,x),

(1.4)

Mathematical details of the model

We now explain our model more precisely:

Let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})

Definition 1.2

(Definition of a solution to fractional stochastic heat inclusion).

We say that

Remark 1.3

(Explanation of the model)

In the classical case, i. e. when
\alpha=1
, this equation models the normal diffusion of heat in a random or noisy medium, the noise being represented by the time-space white noise
W(t,x)
.

- When
\alpha>1
the inclusion can be used to model
superdiffusion or enhanced diffusion
, where the particles spread faster than in regular diffusion. For example, this may occur in some biological systems.

- When
\alpha<1
the inclusion models
subdiffusion
, in which the times of travel of the particles are longer than in the standard case. Such a situation may occur in transport systems.

In all cases the drift term
G(Y(t,x))
represents a sink/source. It is set-valued because it models an uncertainty in the possible values it can have.

We consider the inclusion (
1.5
) in the space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of tempered distributions, and in Theorem
i
we show that an
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
-valued solution
Y(t,x)
(if it exists) must be a fixed point of a corresponding set-valued map and, conversely, any fixed point of this map is also a solution of the fractional heat inclusion. Then we show that there is at least one fixed point of this map. We conclude that the fractional heat inclusion has at least one solution.

In agreement with the terminology of Y.Hu
[
7
]
, we define a solution to be
mild
if
Y(t,x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
for all
t,x
.
It is well-known that in the classical heat
equation
case with
\alpha=1
, the solution is mild if and only if the space dimension
d=1
. See e.g.
[
7
]
. In Theorem
6.2
we show that if
\alpha\in(1,2)
the solution is mild if
d=1
or
d=2
,
and we show that if
\alpha<1
then the solution is not mild for any space dimension
d
.

2.1
The space of tempered distributions

We recall some of the basic properties of the Schwartz space
\mathcal{S}
of rapidly decreasing smooth functions and its dual, the space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of tempered distributions.

Let
n
be a given natural number. Let
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the
space of rapidly decreasing smooth real
functions
f

on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

equipped with the family of seminorms:

\|f\|_{k,\gamma}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big\{(1+|x|^{k})|\partial^{\gamma}f(x)|\big\}<\infty,

where

,
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{n})
is a multi-index with
\gamma_{j}=0,1,...

(j=1,...,n)
and

\partial^{\gamma}f(x):=\frac{\partial^{|\gamma|}}{\partial x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{n}^{\gamma_{n}}}f(x)

for
|\gamma|=\gamma_{1}+...+\gamma_{n}
.

Then

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
Fréchet space.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be its dual, called the space of
tempered distributions
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the family of all
Borel subsets of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
equipped with the
weak* topology. If
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and
f\in\mathcal{S}
we let

\Phi(f)\text{ or }\langle\Phi,f\rangle

(2.1)

denote the action of
\Phi
on

.

Example 2.1

•

Time-space white noise

In this subsection we summarize the construction of time-space white noise and its properties.

Let
n
be a fixed natural number. Later we will set
n=1+d
.
Define
\Omega={\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, equipped
with the weak
∗
topology. This space will be the base of our basic
probability space, which we explain in the following:

As events we will use the family
\mathcal{F}={\cal B}({\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
of Borel subsets of
{\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and our
probability measure
\mathbb{P}
is defined by the following result:

Theorem 3.1

(The Bochner–Minlos theorem)

There exists a unique probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
{\cal B}({\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))

with the following property:

\mathbb{E}[e^{i\langle\cdot,\phi\rangle}]:=\int\limits_{\cal S^{\prime}}e^{i\langle\omega,\phi\rangle}d\mathbb{P}(\omega)=e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\phi\|^{2}};\hskip 10.00002pti=\sqrt{-1}

for all
\phi\in{\cal S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, where

\|\phi\|^{2}=\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},\hskip 10.00002pt\langle\omega,\phi\rangle=\omega(\phi)
is the action of
\omega\in{\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
on

\phi\in{\cal S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}
denotes the expectation
with respect to
\mathbb{P}
.

We will call the triplet
({\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),{\cal B}({\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})),\mathbb{P})
the
white noise probability
space
, and
\mathbb{P}
is called the
white noise probability measure
.

The measure
\mathbb{P}
is also often called the (normalised)
Gaussian
measure
on
{\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. It is not difficult to prove that if
\phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
we choose
\phi_{k}\in{\cal S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that
\phi_{k}\to\phi
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
then

\langle\omega,\phi\rangle:=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}\langle\omega,\phi_{k}\rangle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{exists in}\hskip 10.00002ptL^{2}(\mathbb{P})

and is independent of the choice of
\{\phi_{k}\}
. In
particular, if we define

\widetilde{B}(x):=\widetilde{B}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n},\omega)=\langle\omega,\chi_{[0,x_{1}]\times\cdots\times[0,x_{n}]}\rangle;\hskip 10.00002ptx=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

where
[0,x_{i}]
is interpreted as
[x_{i},0]
if
x_{i}<0
,
then
\widetilde{B}(x,\omega)
has an
x
-continuous version
B(x,\omega)
, which
becomes an
n
-parameter Brownian sheet
, in the following sense:

By an
n
-parameter Brownian sheet
we mean a family

\{B(x,\cdot)\}_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}
of random variables on a probability space

(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
such that

•

B(0,\cdot)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{almost surely with respect to }\mathbb{P},

•

\{B(x,\omega)\}
is a continuous and Gaussian stochastic process

•

For all
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
,

y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n}
,

B(x,\cdot),\,B(y,\cdot)
have the covariance

\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\wedge y_{i}
. For general

the covariance is

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta_{x_{i}}(s)\theta_{y_{i}}(s)ds
, where

\theta_{x}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=\theta_{x_{1}}(t_{1})\cdots\theta_{x_{n}}(t_{n})
, with

\theta_{x_{j}}(s)=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{ if }0<s\leq x_{j}\\
-1\quad\text{ if }x_{j}<s\leq 0\\

It can be proved that the process

defined above has a modification

which satisfies all these properties.
This process

then becomes an

-parameter Brownian sheet
.

We remark that for

if we restrict ourselves to

.

With this definition of the Brownian sheet it is natural to define the

n

by

\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\phi(x)dB(x,\omega):=\langle\omega,\phi\rangle;\hskip 10.00002pt\omega\in{\cal S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

We see that by using the Bochner–Minlos theorem we have obtained an easy construction of

. Moreover, we get a representation of the space

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the Fréchet space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This is an advantage in many situations, for example in the construction of the Hida-Malliavin derivative, which can be regarded as a stochastic gradient on
\Omega
.
See e.g.
[
6
]
and the references therein.

In the following we put
n=1+d
and let

B(t,x)=B(t,x,\omega);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\omega\in\Omega

denote the (1-dimensional) time-space Brownian sheet with probability law

. Since this process is

Thus we define the time-space white noise

W(t,x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial^{d}B(t,x)}{\partial x_{1}...\partial x_{d}}.

(3.1)

In particular, for

and putting

we get the familiar identity

4.1

Definition 4.1

A

is a stochastic process

3.

Remark 4.2

Definition 4.4

(i)

Let

denote a box in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and let
U
be a Borel subset of
\mathbb{R}_{0}:=\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
.
Define
the jump measure

N(R,U)
to be the number of jumps of
L
within the rectangle
R
of jumps size in
U
. By stationarity the map
R\mapsto N(R,U)
only depends on the volume of
R
and we can write the random measure generated by
(R,U)\mapsto N(R,U)
in differential form as
N(dx,du)
.

(ii)

Put
R_{1}=[0,1]^{n}
. The measure
U\mapsto\nu(U):=\mathbb{E}[N(R_{1},U)]
is called the Lévy measure of
L
.

(iii)

The corresponding
compensated jump measure
is defined by

\widetilde{N}(dx,du)=N(dx,du)-\nu(du)dx,

where
dx=dx_{1}dx_{2}...dx_{n}
is the volume differential at
x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In the following we will for simplicity assume that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z^{2}\nu(dz)<\infty;\ \text{ where }\mathbb{R}_{0}=\mathbb{R}\setminus 0..

(4.1)

Then by the Lévy-Khinchine theorem the Lévy sheet
L
can be given the following representation:

\displaystyle L(x)=\alpha\Pi_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}+\sigma B(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z\widetilde{N}(x,dz);\ x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},

(4.2)

for some constants
\alpha,\sigma
, where
B(x)
is a Brownian sheet.

In particular, if we assume that
\alpha=\sigma=0
then we have the representation

\displaystyle L(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z\widetilde{N}(x,dz)=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\widetilde{N}(d\xi,dz);\ x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}.

(4.3)

4.2
The Lévy white noise probability space and the Lévy sheet

Similarly to the Brownian sheet case, it is possible to construct a Lévy sheet with a given Lévy measure
\nu

as a process on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. The details are the following:

Let
\nu
be a measure on
\mathbb{R}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z^{2}\nu(dz)<\infty.

(4.4)

By the Bochner-Minlos-Sazonov theorem (see
[
12
]
), there exists for a probability measure
P
on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
such that

\int_{\Omega}e^{i\langle\omega,\varphi\rangle}P(d\omega)=\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Psi(\varphi(x))dx\right);\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(4.5)

where
i=\sqrt{-1}
and
\langle\omega,\varphi\rangle
denotes the action of
\omega\in\Omega:=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
on
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

\Psi(w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(e^{i\,w\,z}-1-i\,w\,z\right)\nu(dz)

(4.6)

We let
E[\cdot]=E_{P}[\cdot]
and
Var[\cdot]=Var_{P}[\cdot]
denote expectation and variance, respectively, with respect to
P
.

The following result follows easily from the definition of
P
:

Lemma 4.5

Let
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then we have

E[\langle\cdot,g\rangle]=0

(4.7)

and

Var[\langle\cdot,g\rangle]:=E[\langle\cdot,g\rangle^{2}]=M\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g^{2}(x)dx,

(4.8)

where
M=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z^{2}\nu(dz)
.

In particular,

\displaystyle E[L^{2}(x)]=M\Pi_{j=1}^{n}x_{j};\ x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}.

(4.9)

Using this result we can for each
\omega\in\Omega
extend the definition of
\langle\omega,f\rangle
from
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as follows:

If
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, choose
f_{n}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
f_{n}\rightarrow f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then by the Lemma above we see that
\{\langle\omega,f_{n}\rangle\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(P)
and hence convergent in
L^{2}(P)
.
Moreover, the limit depends only on

f
and not the sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
We denote this limit by
\langle\omega,f\rangle;\ \omega\in\Omega,f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

In particular, if we define

\widetilde{L}(x):=\widetilde{L}(x,\omega):=\langle\omega,\chi_{\Pi_{j=1}^{n}[0,x_{j}]}\rangle;\hskip 10.00002ptx=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},

then we have the following result:

Theorem 4.6

The stochastic process
\widetilde{L}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
has a càdlàg version, denoted by
L
.
This process
L(x)\ ;x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
is a (pure jump) Lévy
sheet with Lévy measure
\nu
.

4.3
A chaos expansion

We now proceed to define the white noise of the Lévy sheet constructed in the previous section. We will follow the presentation in
[
6
]
Section 13.2 closely, but with the necessary modifications due to the fact that we are now dealing with an
n
-parameter Lévy sheet, not a classical 1-parameter Lévy process. For completeness we give the details:

From now on we assume that the Lévy measure
\nu
satisfies the
following condition:

For all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\lambda>0
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}\backslash(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\exp(\lambda\left|z\right|)\nu(dz)<\infty

(4.10)

One can prove that if this holds, then
\nu
has finite moments of all orders
n\geq 2
.
The condition clearly holds if
\nu
is supported on
[-M,M]
for some
M>0
.

From this condition one can prove that the polynomials are dense in
L^{2}(\rho)
,
where

\rho(dz)=z^{2}\nu(dz).

(4.11)

See
[
18
]
. Now, let
\left\{l_{m}\right\}_{m\geq 0}=\left\{1,l_{1},l_{2},...\right\}
be the orthogonolization of

\left\{1,z,z^{2},...\right\}
with respect to the inner product of
L^{2}(\rho)
.

Define

p_{j}(z):=\left\|l_{j-1}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{-1}zl_{j-1}(z);\text{ }j=1,2,...

(4.12)

and

m_{2}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z^{2}\nu(dz)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left\|l_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\left\|1\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}.

(4.13)

In particular,

p_{1}(z)=m_{2}^{-1}z\text{ or }z=m_{2}p_{1}(z).

(4.14)

Then
\left\{p_{j}(z)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
is an
orthonormal basis

for
L^{2}(\nu)
.

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of all natural numbers. Define the bijection
\kappa:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
by

\kappa(i,j)=j+(i+j-2)(i+j-1)/2;\hskip 10.00002pti,j=1,2,...

(4.15)

As in Section 2.2.1 in
[
9
]
we let
\left\{e_{j}(x)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
be a fixed ordering of all
n
-times tensor products of the Hermite functions.
This family forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Define

\delta_{\kappa(j,k)}(x,z)=e_{j}(x)p_{k}(z);\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},z\in\mathbb{R}_{0}.

(4.16)

In the following we let
\mathcal{J}
denote the family of all finite multi-indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...,\alpha_{m});\ m=1,2,...
of nonnegative integers
\alpha_{j}
.

If
\alpha\in\mathcal{J}
with
Index(\alpha)=j
and
\left|\alpha\right|=m
, we define
\delta^{\otimes\alpha}:(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{0})^{m}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\delta^{\otimes\alpha}(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})

(4.17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{1}^{\otimes\alpha_{1}}\otimes...\otimes\delta_{j}^{\otimes\alpha_{j}}(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{\alpha_{1}\text{ factors}}{\underbrace{\delta_{1}(x^{(1)},z_{1})\cdot...\cdot\delta_{1}(x^{(\alpha_{1})},z_{\alpha_{1}})}}\cdot...\cdot\underset{\alpha_{j}\text{ factors}}{\underbrace{\delta_{j}(x^{(m-\alpha_{j}+1)},z_{m-\alpha_{j}+1})\cdot...\cdot\delta_{j}(x^{(m)},z_{m})}}.

We define
\delta_{i}^{\otimes 0}=1.

Finally we let
\delta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}
denote the

symmetrized
tensor product of the
\delta_{k}

{}^{\prime}s:

\delta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})=\delta_{1}^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha_{1}}\otimes...\otimes\delta_{j}^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha_{j}}(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m}).

(4.18)

For
\alpha\in\mathcal{J}
define

K_{\alpha}:=I_{\left|\alpha\right|}\left(\delta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}\right),

(4.19)

where in general
I_{m}
is the symmetrized iterated integral with respect to
\widetilde{N}
, defined for symmetric functions
g:(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{0})^{m}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle I_{m}(g)=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{0})^{m}}g(x^{(1)},z_{1};x^{(2)},z_{2};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})\widetilde{N}^{\otimes m}(dx^{(1)}dz_{1};...,dx^{(m)}dz_{m}).

(4.20)

Example 4.7

For example, if
m=1
we get

\displaystyle I_{1}(g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}g(x,z)\widetilde{N}(dx,dz),

(4.21)

and if
m=2
we have

\displaystyle I_{2}(g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}g(x^{(1)},z_{1};x^{(2)},z_{2})\widetilde{N}(dx^{(2)},dz_{2})\Big)\widetilde{N}(dx^{(1)},dz_{1}).

(4.22)

Example 4.8

With
\varepsilon^{(k)}=(0,...,0,1,0,...)
with
1
on
k
th
place, we have, writing
\varepsilon^{(\kappa(i,j))}=\varepsilon^{(i,j)}
,

K_{\varepsilon^{(i,j)}}=I_{1}(\delta^{\otimes\varepsilon^{(i,k)}})=I_{1}\left(\delta_{\kappa(i,j)}\right)=I_{1}\left(e_{i}(x)p_{j}(z)\right).

(4.23)

As in the Brownian sheet case one can prove that
\{K_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}
are
orthogonal in
L^{2}(P)
and

\|K_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P)}=\alpha!.

Note that if
|\alpha|=m
,

\alpha!=\|K_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P)}=m!\|\delta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((\lambda\times\nu)^{m})},

where
\lambda
denotes Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By our construction of
\delta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}
we know that
any
f\in\widetilde{L}^{2}((\lambda\times\nu)^{m})
can be written

f(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})=\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=m}c_{\alpha}\delta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}(x^{(1)},z_{1},...,x^{(m)},z_{m}).

(4.24)

Hence

I_{m}(f_{m})=\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=m}c_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}

(4.25)

This gives the following chaos expansion:

Theorem 4.9

(Chaos expansion)

Any
F\in L^{2}(P)

has a unique expansion of the form

F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}c_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}.

(4.26)

with
c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover,

\left\|F\right\|_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}\alpha!c_{\alpha}^{2}.

(4.27)

Example 4.10

Let
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and define, for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
,

\displaystyle F=\int_{0}^{x}h(\xi)dL(\xi).

(4.28)

By (
4.3
) this can be written

\displaystyle F=I_{1}(h(\xi)\chi_{\Pi_{i=1}^{n}[0,x_{i}]}(\xi)\zeta).

(4.29)

Then
F
has the expansion, writing
\chi_{\Pi_{i=1}^{n}[0,x_{i}]}=\chi_{[0,x]}
for short,

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{1}(\sum_{i\geq 1}(h\chi_{[0,x]},e_{i})e_{i}(\xi)\zeta)=\sum_{i\geq 1}(h\chi_{[0,x]},e_{i})I_{1}(e_{i}(\xi)\zeta)

(4.30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{x}h(\xi)e_{i}(\xi)d\xi\right)K_{\varepsilon^{(i,1)}}m_{2}.

Recall that
z=m_{2}p_{1}(z);
see (
4.14
).
In particular,

L(x)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{x}e_{i}(\xi)d\xi\right)K_{\varepsilon^{(i,1)}}m_{2}.

(4.31)

Definition 4.11

(The Lévy-Hida spaces)

(i)

Stochastic test functions
(\mathcal{S})
.
Let
(\mathcal{S})
consist of all
\varphi=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(P)

such that

\left\|\varphi\right\|_{k}^{2}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}^{2}\alpha!(2\mathbb{N})^{k\alpha}<\infty\text{ for \emph{all}
}k\in\mathbb{N},

(4.32)

equipped with the projective topology, where

(2\mathbb{N})^{k\alpha}=\prod_{j\geq 1}(2j)^{k\alpha_{j}},

(4.33)

if
\alpha=(\alpha_{1,}\alpha_{2,},...)\in\mathcal{J}
.

(ii)

Stochastic distributions
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
.

Let
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
consist of all expansions
F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}b_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}
such that

\left\|F\right\|_{-q}^{2}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}b_{\alpha}^{2}\alpha!(2\mathbb{N})^{-q\alpha}<\infty\text{ for \emph{some} }q\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.34)

endowed with the inductive topology.
The space
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
is the
dual of
(\mathcal{S}).
If
F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}b_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\in(\mathcal{S})^{*}
and
\varphi=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\in(\mathcal{S}),
then the action of
F

on
\varphi
is

\left\langle F,\varphi\right\rangle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}b_{\alpha}\alpha!.

(4.35)

(iii)

Generalized expectation.

If
F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\in(\mathcal{S})^{*}
, we define
the generalized expectation
E[F]
of
F
by

E[F]=a_{0}.

Note that
E[K_{\alpha}]=0
for all
\alpha\neq 0
.
Therefore the generalized expectation coincides with the usual expectation if
F\in L^{2}(P)
.

4.4
Lévy white noise

Now we have the prerequisites to define the white noise of a Lévy sheet. In view of (
4.31
) the following definition is natural:

Definition 4.12

(i)

The
Lévy white noise

\overset{\bullet}{L}(x):=V(x):=\frac{\partial^{n}L(x)}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}...,\partial x_{n}}

is defined by the expansion

\displaystyle V(x):

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{2}\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}(x)K_{\varepsilon^{(i,1)}}=m_{2}\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}(x)I_{1}(e_{i}(x)p_{1}(z))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{2}\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}(x)I_{1}(e_{i}(x)z).

(ii)

The
white noise

\overset{\bullet}{\widetilde{N}}(x,z)
of the compensated random measure
\widetilde{N}(dx,dz)
is defined by the expansion

\overset{\bullet}{\widetilde{N}}(x,z)=\sum_{i,j\geq 1}e_{i}(x)p_{j}(z)K_{{}_{\varepsilon^{(i,j)}}}(\omega).

(4.36)

Remark 4.13

Note that for
\overset{\bullet}{L}(x)
we have

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}c_{\alpha}^{2}\alpha!(2\mathbb{N})^{-q\alpha}=m_{2}^{2}\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}^{2}(x)2^{-q}\kappa(i,1)^{-q}<\infty\text{ }

for
q\geq 2,
using that
\kappa(i,1)=1+(i-1)i/2\geq i,
and the following
well-known estimate for the Hermite functions:

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|e_{m}(x)\right|=O(m^{-\frac{1}{12}}).

(4.37)

Therefore
\overset{\bullet}{L}(x)\in(\mathcal{S})^{*}
for all
x.
Similarly
\overset{\bullet}{\widetilde{N}}(x,z)\in(\mathcal{S})^{*}

for all
x,z.

Remark 4.14

(i)

Note that by comparing the expansions

L(x)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\int_{0}^{x_{2}}...\int_{0}^{x_{n}}e_{i}(\xi)d\xi\right)K_{\varepsilon^{(i,1)}}m_{2}

and

\overset{\bullet}{L}(x)=m_{2}\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}(x)K_{\varepsilon^{(i,1)}},

we get formally

\overset{\bullet}{L}(x)=\frac{\partial^{n}L(x)}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}...,\partial x_{n}}\text{ (derivative in }(\mathcal{S})^{*}).

(4.38)

This can be proved rigorously.

(ii)

Choose a Borel set
\mho
such that
\overline{\mho}\subset\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.
Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}(x,\mho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{1}\left(\chi_{[0,x]}(\xi)\chi_{\mho}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\geq 1}(\chi_{[0,x]},e_{i})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(\chi_{\mho},p_{j})_{{}_{L^{2}(\nu)}}I_{1}\left(e_{i}(\xi),p_{j}(\zeta)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\geq 1}\int_{0}^{x}e_{i}(\xi)d\xi\cdot\int_{\mho}p_{j}(\zeta)\nu(d\zeta)K_{\varepsilon^{(i,j)}}(\omega).

This justifies the “relation”

\overset{\bullet}{\widetilde{N}}(x,z)=\frac{\widetilde{N}(dx,dz)}{dx\times\nu(dz)}\text{ (Radon-Nikodym derivative).}

(4.39)

(iii)

Also note that
\overset{\bullet}{L}
is related to
\overset{\bullet}{\widetilde{N}}
by

\overset{\bullet}{L}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\overset{\bullet}{\widetilde{N}}(x,\zeta)\nu(d\zeta).

(4.40)

To see this consider

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\overset{\bullet}{\widetilde{N}}(x,\zeta)\zeta\nu(d\zeta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\sum_{i,j\geq 1}e_{i}(x)p_{j}(\zeta)K_{{}_{\varepsilon^{(i,j)}}}(\omega)\zeta\nu(d\zeta)=\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}(x)I_{1}(e_{i}p_{j})\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}p_{j}(\zeta)\zeta\nu(d\zeta))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}(x)I_{1}\left(e_{i}(\xi)\zeta\right)=\overset{\bullet}{L}(t).

Remark 4.15

It is possible to combine the Brownian sheet
B(x)
and the Lévy sheet
L(x)
on
\Omega
in such a way that
B(x)
and
L(x)
are independent sheets. We will in the following assume that this has been done and we will use
\mathbb{P}
to denote the combined probability measure.

The triple
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{P})
is called the combined Brownian and (pure jump) Lévy white noise probability space.

5
The fractional stochastic heat inclusion

We are now ready to state and prove our main results.

First, recall the definition of Hausdorff distance
d(X,Y)
between two subsets
X
and
Y
of a complete metric space
\mathcal{Z}
with distance metric
d(\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle d(X,Y)=\max\Big\{\sup_{x\in X}d(x,Y),\sup_{y\in Y}d(y,X)\Big\}

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle d(x,Y)=\inf_{y\in Y}d(x,y)\ \text{ and }\ d(y,X)=\inf_{x\in X}d(y,x).

(5.2)

Definition 5.1

•

We say that an
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})-
valued process
Y(t,x,\omega):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\mapsto\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})
is adapted if
the stochastic process
\eta_{\phi}(t,x)=\langle Y(t,x),\phi\rangle
is adapted for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d}).

•

Let
\mathcal{Y}
denote the set of all adapted
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})-
valued processes
Y(t,x)=Y(t,x,\omega);(t,x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
.

The first part of the following result and its proof follow closely the presentation in Theorem 2 in
[
17
]
.

Theorem 5.2

Let
G:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})\mapsto\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d}))
be a set-valued function and let

\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}(Y)
denote the set of all selectors of
G(Y(t,x))
, i.e. the set of all distribution-valued functions
g:\mathbb{R}^{1+d}\mapsto\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})
such that
g(t,x)\in G(Y(t,x))\ \forall t,x
.

Assume that

1.

All elements of the set
\mathbb{G}(Y)
are bounded adapted processes with compact support on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, equipped with the
L^{\infty}-
norm with respect to
d\mathbb{P}\times dtdx
, denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.

2.

The map
Y\mapsto G(Y)
is continuous as a map from
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d})
into
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{1+d}))
, equipped with the Hausdorff metric.

Then the following holds:

(i)

If
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
is a solution (in the sense of distribution) of the fractional stochastic heat inclusion

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}Y(t,x)

\displaystyle\in\lambda\Delta Y(t,x)+G(Y(t,x))+\sigma W(t,x)+\gamma V(t,x);

\displaystyle(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(5.3)

then
Y
is a fixed point of the inclusion

\displaystyle Y(t,x)

\displaystyle\in I_{1}(t,x)+I_{2}(t,x,Y(t,x))+I_{3}(t,x),

(5.4)

where

\displaystyle I_{1}(t,x)=I

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ixy}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{\alpha}(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ixy}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda t^{\alpha}|y|^{2})^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+1)}dy

(5.5)

\displaystyle I_{2}(t,x,Y(t,x))=

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-d}\Big\{\int_{0}^{t}(t-r)^{\alpha-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i(x-\zeta)y}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}y^{2})^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha(k+1))}dy)g(r,\zeta)drd\zeta;g(t,x)\in G(Y(t,x))\Big\}

(5.6)

\displaystyle I_{3}(t,x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{0}^{t}(t-r)^{\alpha-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i(x-\zeta)y}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2})dy\right)\big(\sigma B(dr,d\zeta)+\gamma L(dr,d\zeta)\big)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{0}^{t}(t-r)^{\alpha-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i(x-\zeta)y}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda(t-r)^{\alpha}|y|^{2})^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\alpha))}dy\right)\big(\sigma B(dr,d\zeta)+\gamma L(dr,d\zeta)\big).

(5.7)

(ii)

Conversely, if
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
is a fixed point of (
5.4

7
Example: Nitrate transport in sandy porous aquifers

We end this paper with another example, illustrating how our theoretical result which combines fractional derivatives, set-valued maps and Lévy noise can be used to describe how nitrate pollution spreads through sandy groundwater systems.

Sandy aquifers are made of loose or compact sand that allows water and dissolved substances to move through tiny pores between the grains. These systems are common in many parts of the world and are especially vulnerable to contamination from agricultural activities, such as the overuse of fertilisers.

We focus on the transport of nitrate , a highly mobile and widely observed groundwater pollutant. Our model is based on the following fractional stochastic inclusion:

\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}Y(t,x)\in\lambda\Delta Y(t,x)+G(Y(t,x))+\sigma W(t,x)+\gamma V(t,x),\hskip 9.24994pt(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

with initial and boundary conditions:

Y(0,x)=\delta(x),\hskip 9.24994pt\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}Y(t,x)=0

Let us modelise term by term:

The fractional time derivative
\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}Y(t,x)
captures memory effects and anomalous diffusion.

When
\alpha<1
, it models slower spreading and trapping of nitrate.

When
\alpha>1
, it reflects faster-than-normal movement, such as during sudden water infiltration.

The diffusion term describes how nitrate naturally spreads out due to differences in concentration.

The nonlinear set-valued map
G(Y)=[k_{1}Y,k_{2}Y]
represents uncertainty in nitrate adsorption, depending on local soil properties. Sandy soils might absorb very little (low
k_{1}
), while areas with organic matter might absorb more (higher
k_{2}
).

The white noise term
W
accounts for random microscopic variations in permeability and flow conditions.

The jump noise
V
models sudden inputs of water for example, from heavy rain or irrigation that can make nitrate deeper or farther into the aquifer.

About the initial condition:

The first means that there an instantaneous release of nitrate at a single point, such as a leaking septic tank, fertilizer spill, or burst irrigation pipe. This helps us understand how pollution evolves from a local source.

In reality, this could correspond to an initial nitrate concentration of 50 to
200mg/L
, which is well above the World Health Organization recommended limit of
50mg/L
for safe drinking water.

This model is especially useful for studying how nitrate behaves in sandy aquifers, where its high mobility and low absorption make it a serious risk. The fractional-time derivative helps us account for the fact that nitrate does not always move at a steady rate; it can get stuck, then suddenly surge forward, depending on the water flow and soil structure.

Using Brownian and Lévy noise terms and set-valued uncertainty, we can also simulate the effects of unknown parameters, natural variability, and unexpected events, all of which happen in real aquifer systems.

Now, the question is how our models can help and predict the risk of a high concentration of nitrate.
Our solution
Y(t,x)
represents the concentration of nitrate at time t and position x, it captures how the nitrate spreads through fractures or porous media, how it accumulates in certain zones, how it is suddenly released due to geological events (modeled by Lévy jumps), it also represents how the system remembers past states because of the fractional derivative.
The high concentration of nitrate in groundwater poses serious risks, especially to human health and the environment. For example, we point out the
Blue baby syndrome
, which affects infants by reducing their blood’s ability to carry oxygen. In addition, there is growing evidence of a link between long-term exposure to nitrate and certain cancers, especially stomach cancer. The solution
Y(t,x)
tells us where and when the concentration of nitrates may exceed the safe limits. In addition, it spots potential danger zones early, places where nitrate concentration could rise too high, it simulates real-world events such as heavy rains or earthquakes that can suddenly release trapped contamination, it helps communities and decision makers prepare by showing when and where protective actions like filter installation might be needed, and it reveals which areas are most vulnerable and why.
