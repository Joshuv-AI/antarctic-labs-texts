Effectiveness of an HIV peer support precision matching platform: a randomized controlled trial protocol
Authors: Xin Xie, Shuyu Han, Yangfeng Wu, Tao Huang, Junyi Duan, Dongmei Li, Shuyu Chen, Bo Zhou, Jiangping Zhang, Lili Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeer support is a promising and significant way for people living with HIV (PLWH) to improve their health outcomes. Matching PLWH with HIV peer volunteers can effectively stimulate and mobilize the function of peer support in health management. We develop the online platform Aspark, which features a precision matching function for PLWH and volunteers. This protocol provides a comprehensive overview of the randomized controlled trial design based on Aspark.MethodA parallel randomized controlled trial using block randomization will be conducted at the Beijing YouAn Home of Loving Care. The intervention group (n = 70) will have access to all 12 features of Aspark, including precise volunteer matching, health management tools, drug interaction queries, and medication reminders. The control group (n = 70) will have access to two features of informational support on Aspark. The outcomes of both groups will be evaluated at baseline and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after platform registration. The primary outcome is perceived social support. The secondary outcomes include mental symptoms, quality of life, self-efficacy, medication adherence, and health behaviors. Linear mixed models or generalized linear mixed models will be applied to test the intervention effects.DiscussionThe Aspark trial is a randomized controlled trial designed to examine the influence of a mobile health (mHealth) application that integrates peer support and precision matching to strengthen social support for PLWH in China. This trial has the potential to significantly enhance social support for PLWH while also optimizing the delivery of peer support services and fully mobilizing the role of peer support in the overall health management of PLWH.Trial registrationClinical registration number: ChiCTR2300077454.Keywords: HIV/AIDS, Peer support, Quality of life, Randomized controlled trial, Clinical trial protocol

Introduction

As reported by UNAIDS, the global population of people living with HIV (PLWH) was as high as 39 million in 2022, with approximately 630 thousand fatalities resulting from AIDS-related illnesses [1]. Since antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV infection into a chronic condition [2], the need for comprehensive care and support for PLWH remains paramount. The "95-95-95" goal launched by the UNAIDS has further emphasized the importance of enhancing the quality of life of PLWH [3]. However, health-related quality of life includes but is not limited to domains related to physical, emotional, psychological, cognitive, and work- or role-related health, as well as disease-related symptoms and financial status over time [4]. Moreover, PLWH have various care needs [5]. Under these circumstances, the shortage of medical resources and the prevalence of internalized discrimination against PLWH make this a complex challenge [6, 7]. Consequently, PLWH need more medical and social support to meet their various needs and achieve a high quality of life [8].

Peer support emerges as a vital complement to medical resources, offering PLWH authentic empathy due to shared experiences and potentially aiding in reducing HIV-related discrimination and enhancing their quality of life [9]. Moreover, compared to other forms of social support, peer support is more cost effective at increasing self-testing among PLWH and decreasing HIV risk behaviors [10, 11]. Peer support has been found to be effective at enhancing many health-related outcomes among PLWH, including improved mental health, social support, and medication adherence [12, 13]. Therefore, many countries [14–16] have published their own national standards or practice guidelines for HIV peer support practices. Although peer support is widely recognized as a promising practice, its effectiveness and sustainability are significantly influenced by context. A systematic review of peer support interventions revealed mixed results regarding ART adherence among PLWH, with many studies revealing only short-term intervention effects [17]. Therefore, further innovations and attempts are necessary for HIV peer support practices to mobilize and stimulate its role in health management.

Our research team conducted a realist synthesis to explore the mechanisms of effectiveness and sustainability of HIV peer support [18]. The results indicated that the relationship between PLWH and peer volunteers is a crucial mechanism that impacts both the effectiveness and sustainability of peer support. Therefore, we designed an innovative peer support intervention that highlighted the need for precise matching between PLWH and peer volunteers. We hypothesize that the precise matching of PLWH and peer volunteers can promote a more positive relationship between them so that PLWH are more accepting of peer volunteers' support and that peer volunteers have fewer negative feelings during the peer support process, thus helping to improve the effectiveness and sustainability of HIV peer support. This innovative peer support intervention is visualized and available on the online platform Aspark. In the future, we aim to examine the impact of peer support on PLWH through this precise matching function. This article outlines the protocol of the clinical trial.

Methods

Study design

This is a parallel randomized controlled trial. The protocol is reported according to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendation for Intervention Trials (SPIRIT) [19]. We will report this clinical trial according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials of electronic and mobile health application and online telehealth (CONSORT-EHEALTH) statement of randomized controlled trials [20]. We have completed the registration (ChiCTR2300077454), and any major changes will be reported to the registry and updated accordingly.

Setting and participants

We will use a continuous enrollment approach to enroll PLWH from the Beijing YouAn Home of Loving Care. The inclusion criteria for participants are as follows: 1) are diagnosed with HIV infection, 2) are aged 18 or older, 3) are receiving ART, and 4) provide voluntary written consent to participate in the study. Participants are excluded if they 1) are diagnosed with severe comorbidities, such as opportunistic infections and HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND), 2) are currently receiving treatment from a psychologist or counselor, or 3) are taking part in other HIV-related research projects at the same time.

Sample size calculation

Based on the primary outcome of social support, utilizing a significance level (α) set at 0.05, a power (β) of 0.2, and an anticipated effect size of 0.5, the estimated sample size for each group was 64 [21]. We plan to recruit 70 participants for each group considering the potential sample loss rate.

Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding

Participants will be allocated to either the intervention group or the control group through 1:1 block randomization (block size = 4). The RANDBETWEEN function in WPS Excel is used to generate random numbers ranging from 1 to 100. Odd numbers are introduced in the control group, and even numbers are introduced in the intervention group.

The software engineers directly implant the encrypted randomization sequence into the user registration system. After signing the informed consent form, participants will scan a QR code that help them automatically allocate their groups. During this process, neither the researchers nor the participants will be aware of the group allocation in advance, thus ensuring allocation concealment.

Participants, intervention providers, and data collectors are blinded in this study. Participants will only be able to access the platform of their assigned group (control or intervention), which will help ensure participant blinding. Moreover, all the interventions will be delivered through an online platform, so there is no opportunity for intervention providers to distinguish participants in these groups. All the data will also be collected through the platform, thus ensuring blinding of the data collectors.

Intervention

According to our realist synthesis, peer volunteers can provide PLWH with five kinds of social support, including informational support, instrumental support, emotional support, affiliation support, and appraisal support [18]. Information support can help PLWH better comprehend medical information. Instrumental support consists of medical instrumental support and daily life instrumental support, such as scheduling appointments or food and housing security support. Emotional support requires peer volunteers to listen and use reflective techniques to help PLWH and to demonstrate caring, empathy, and comfort to help them alleviate their negative emotions. Affiliation support can help PLWH reduce isolation and connect to social networks. Appraisal support can help PLWH make decisions about their treatment, self-care, and daily life conflicts.

All the above five kinds of interventions are included in our study. In addition, we insert Aspark into the WeChat official account (WOA), which is one of the main functions of WeChat. It can transmit messages to users [22]. WeChat is China’s most popular social media platform, with more than 1.25 billion active users in 2021 [23]. As one of the main functions of WeChat, WOAs are also widely used by WeChat users. In 2019, more than 76% of WeChat users followed more than ten WOAs [24]. Thus, this method ensures participants' easy access to the platform and increases their engagement. In addition, during the course of the study, any adverse events can be reported to the ethics committee at any time by the investigators, who may also propose terminating or withdrawing from the study. All data are only accessible to the investigators, and only they are aware of this process.

The platform uses a matching algorithm based on demographic variables and interpersonal style data of both groups; this algorithm was designed under the guidance of symbolic interaction theory [25], social determinants of health (SODH) [26], and the interpersonal circumplex (IPC) model [27]. Once the matching is completed, the PLWH and volunteers can communicate through the platform (Fig. 4), providing informational and emotional support and appraisal support to the PLWH. As communication deepens, volunteers can also provide instrumental and affiliation support to PLWH.

Topic discussion

All users can browse and comment on the questions posted by others in the topic discussion and initiate private conversations. This ability can provide users with informational, emotional, and affiliation support (Fig. 5).

Informational support module

Users in the information sharing center can browse a wealth of health education information (Figs. 6 and 7), and the administrator posts five pieces of knowledge pushes per week. The content of these pushes follows an information support framework [28], which includes three levels: 1) the individual level, which focuses on health management for PLWH, including basic knowledge about HIV and laboratory tests; 2) the community level, which emphasizes social functions such as career development and social support; and 3) the society level, which covers news and policies related to research progress and medical insurance. Expert sharing involves direct reprinting from other HIV-related public accounts, while the information center releases original knowledge pushed by the research team.

Data collection

Participants will sign an informed consent form, which also includes a statement that the data from this study may be used for ancillary studies, and scan a QR code to join a group. Subsequently, they will complete the baseline questionnaire on-site. The platform will initiate a timer postregistration and send reminders to participants at 3-month intervals (3, 6, 9, and 12 months). If a participant misses a task deadline, the researcher will contact them directly. As an incentive, participants will receive 30 yuan for each follow-up session and each completed questionnaire. All the questionnaires will be collected via an online platform using electronic questionnaires that prevent oversight in necessary answers and mitigate the influence of human factors, thus ensuring the quality and integrity of the data. Our database is encrypted and is not accessible to everyone. Only the Principal Investigator (PI) and members of the project group have access to the final collected data. Measure

Demographic variables

The demographic variables in the questionnaire include age, sex, education level, ethnicity, and religious belief. The social variables include occupation, income, marital status, and residence. Disease-related variables include the time of confirmed HIV infection, the time of initiating antiviral treatment, the CD4 count, the number of comorbidities, the number of complications, and the antiviral drug treatment regimen.

Social support

The Social Support Assessment Scale for PLWH is developed in house and consists of 26 items. Our research team first conducted a realist synthesis and identified five key components of social support in the HIV field: informational support, instrumental support, emotional support, affiliation support, and appraisal support. Based on the above five aspects and interviews with medical workers, peer volunteers, and PLWH who were routinely followed up at Beijing YouAn Hospital, we gained an understanding of the social support of PLWH and formed a pool of questionnaire items. We used the Delphi expert consultation method to finalize the items and evaluate the content validity of the scale [29]. Finally, we tested the psychometric properties of the social support measurement instrument for PLWH using the COSMIN method [30]. A 5-point Likert scale was used for all the items. Higher scores indicate greater social support among PLWH.

Mental health symptoms

The mental health symptoms that we assess in PLWH included anxiety, depression, and the high-incidence psychological symptoms identified in our previous research, including feelings of pain, uncertainty about the future, frustration about the future, and a sense of worthlessness [31]. We apply the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2) to assess depression [32] and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-2) to assess anxiety [33].

Quality of life (QoL)

QoL is assessed using the SF-36 [34]. This assessment tool contains 36 items, with higher scores indicating a better quality of life. The SF-36 has eight subscales: physical functioning (PF), role-physical (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), role-emotional (RE), and mental health (MH). It has been reported that Cronbach's α ranges from 0.75 to 0.90 for a scale [35].

Medication adherence

We evaluate participants’ ART medication adherence by self-reports and the medication possession ratio (MPR), which are both widely used in chronic disease care. We will also ask the participants two questions that were designed and used by the research group in the past [36]: ① In the past month, did you miss taking ART medication or mistakenly taking ART medication? ② In the past month, did you delay taking or taking ART medication ahead of time by more than 2 hours? If a participant answers “no” to both questions, we will define him or her as having good adherence; otherwise, the participant will be defined as having modest adherence. In addition, we use the visual analog scale (VAS) and ask the participants to complete a linear scale to indicate percent adherence (0–100%) within the past month [37].

The MPR determines the proportion of days of medication supply within a certain time interval. MPR = time interval between two prescriptions or recommended time intervals for treatment × 100%. An MPR of zero means no adherence, while an MPR of one means perfect adherence. Consistent with the findings of previous studies by our research group, we define good adherence as an MPR of ≥95% and other conditions as poor adherence [36, 38].

Health behavior

A diet and life compliance questionnaire developed by Li, which consists of 5 items, will be used to assess participants' health behavior. Participants will be asked about their frequency of smoking, drinking, sexual behavior, condom use during sexual behavior, and physical exercise in the past week [39].

Self-efficacy

The chronic disease self-efficacy scale developed by Lorig et al. is used to assess participants’ self-efficacy and includes 6 items and uses a 10-point Likert scale. Among these items, four are designed to assess symptom management self-efficacy, while the remaining two assess general disease management self-efficacy [40]. Ritter and Lorig conducted two new studies and reviewed eight independent studies to investigate the psychometric properties of the scale. The minimum Cronbach's alpha was 0.88 across all the studies [41].

Statistical analysis methods

The data obtained from the questionnaires will be input into SPSS 26.0 and Stata 16.0 statistical analysis software. For descriptive analyses, the continuous variables will be summarized using the mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR). The categorical variables will be summarized using frequency (n) and percentage (%). All continuous variables are checked for normality. Both parametric and nonparametric statistical methods, including the independent sample t test, chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test and Wilcoxon rank-sum test, will be applied to compare the baseline characteristics between groups. The intent-to-treat (ITT) strategy will be used to analyze the intervention impact. Linear mixed models or generalized linear mixed models will be used to test the intervention effects.

Discussion

Our study focuses on a randomized controlled trial that aims to improve social support for PLWH through the use of an innovative mHealth application called Aspark and verify its validity. Aspark combines peer support and precision matching algorithms to provide personalized assistance to PLWH. We have incorporated a volunteer training system based on the five key components of social support to enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of peer support. However, our study's limitation is that the data are collected only in Beijing, and most participants are homosexual men, which limits its generalizability. Nonetheless, if the results of our research are positive, they can help optimize peer support strategies, enrich peer volunteers’ training methods and content, and provide a reference for the feasibility of mobile health in HIV care and peer support.
