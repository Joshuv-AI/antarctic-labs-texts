The Role of Symmetry in Generalized Hong-Ou-Mandel Interference and Quantum Metrology
Éloi Descamps, Arne Keller, Pérola Milman

Abstract
The Hong-Ou-Mandel interferometer is a foundational tool in quantum optics, with both fundamental and practical significance. Earlier works identified that input-state symmetry under exchange of the two spatial modes is fundamental in the understanding of the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect. We now show that this notion of symmetry is central to generalizing this effect. In particular, this point of view enables the construction of extensions beyond the standard two single-photon case to arbitrary input states, as well as to configurations with more than two spatial modes via a natural generalization of the beam splitter to a discrete Fourier transform interferometer. Beyond its conceptual significance, this framework offers direct insights into quantum metrology, showing how symmetry properties of input states allow the computation of explicit precision bounds. By focusing on symmetry, we provide a perspective that simplifies and unifies a range of known results, while paving the way for new developments in quantum interference and sensing.

At its core, the HOM effect concerns the mixing of two modes that are otherwise identical in all other degrees of freedom, which can nevertheless serve as path markers carrying which-way information; in the case considered here, these modes are spatial and are mixed via a BS.

We now briefly discuss the role and effects of imperfections. First, it is experimentally impossible to produce states that exactly satisfy the symmetry conditions required for our computation of the FI to hold. This limitation is a generalization of a problem already present in HOM metrology, and in
[
38
]
we presented a general approach to address it: the symmetry imperfections reduce the visibility of the HOM interferometer and by proposing a model for the visibility dependence, we observed that the precision (maximal attainable FI for this specfic measurement strategy) decreases quadraticaly with the visibility. This approach can be adapted to our present contribution.

We now discuss photon loss. Since the protocol relies on accurate photon-number statistics to evaluate
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}
, photon losses alter the outcome probabilities. Assuming a fixed number of photons, post-selection on events where all photons are detected, can be used. While it becomes inefficient as losses scale exponentially with photon number, under realistic experimental efficiencies (
\eta\gtrsim 0.9
), protocols involving up to ten photons are within reach of current photonic platforms
[
16
]
. In
[
1
]
, we have developed further tools to help analyze the effect of losses on the precision and its relation to state preparation.

Finally, while our analysis has been presented in the context of photonic systems, the underlying principles can be extended to any bosonic one. As superconducting microwave cavities in circuit QED
[
34
]
, quasi particle such as phonons
[
15
]
, or magnon excitations in solid state systems
[
47
]
lead to the observation of HOM-like effects, exploring our framework and protocol in these alternative platforms, could open new perspectives for quantum sensing and more broadly for quantum systems manipulation and control.

In conclusion, we have presented a general analysis that encapsulates much of the previous works within a unified picture inspired by a novel and fruitful point of view for studying the HOM interferometer using a symmetry-based approach. Secondly, it led to a generalization of the experimental setup to a more complex scenario, offering new and promising avenues for developing and testing metrological protocols with quantum optical systems. A natural next step is the design and implementation of a concrete experimental realization. We have presented a way to produce the states with the desired symmetry, and the required tailored photon states can, in principle, be produced with current technology. Such an experiment would not only demonstrate the metrological capabilities of the proposed interferometer but also showcase its broader versatility: beyond precision measurements, it could serve as a powerful diagnostic tool to probe and characterize the quantum state of light, in a manner reminiscent of how the HOM effect is routinely employed in quantum optics.

Appendix A
Explicit expression of multimode light states

In this supplemental material (SM), we detail the properties of the creation and annihilation operators and provide more explicit information on the types of states considered throughout the main text.

We set
\ket{\text{vac}}
to be the vacuum state and introduce the annihilation operators
a_{i}(\lambda)
(for
i=0,\dots,n-1
). We assume the usual bosonic commutation relations:

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{i}(\lambda_{1}),\hat{a}_{j}(\lambda_{2})]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{1}),\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{2})]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{i}(\lambda_{1}),\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{2})]

\displaystyle=\delta(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\delta_{i,j}.

(13)

As remarked in the main text, the parameter
\lambda
can encompass both discrete and continuous degrees of freedom. Accordingly, the Dirac delta functions should be understood as encompassing both discrete Kronecker deltas and continuous Dirac deltas. Similarly, integrals should be interpreted as incorporating both discrete sums and continuous integrals.

A state
\ket{\psi}
with
k
photons in the first spatial mode and
l
photons in the second is characterized by its wave function
F(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{l})
via

\ket{\psi}=\int\differential\lambda_{1}\cdots\differential\lambda_{k}\differential\mu_{1}\cdots\differential\mu_{l}\,F(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{l})\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{1})\cdots\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{k})\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}(\mu_{1})\cdots\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}(\mu_{l})\ket{\text{vac}},

(14)

where the function
F
can be assumed to be symmetric in its first
k
arguments and symmetric in its last
l
arguments. Explicitly, this means that for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
and
\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
(with
\mathcal{S}_{m}
denoting the symmetric group on
m
elements), we have

F(\lambda_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\lambda_{\sigma(k)},\mu_{\tau(1)},\dots,\mu_{\tau(l)})=F(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{l}).

(15)

However, note that no symmetry can be assumed between the
\lambda_{i}
and
\mu_{j}
variables.

The normalization condition for
F
is obtained by computing
\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
using the commutation relations and the fact that
\hat{a}_{i}(\lambda)\ket{\text{vac}}=0
(which also implies
\bra{\text{vac}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}(\lambda)=0
), yielding

\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi}=k!\,l!\int\differential\lambda_{1}\cdots\differential\lambda_{k}\differential\mu_{1}\cdots\differential\mu_{l}\,\absolutevalue{F(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k, \mu_1, \dots, \mu_l)}^{2}.

(16)

If
F
is normalized such that the integral of its modulus squared is
1
, then the state must be normalized by a factor of
1/\sqrt{k!\,l!}
to yield a properly normalized quantum state. This is reminiscent of the usual normalization factor when defining number states in the simple Fock space with one creation operator:
\ket{n}=\frac{(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{\text{vac}}
.

A pure state with an indefinite number of photons is defined as a coherent superposition of states
\ket{\psi_{k,l}}
of equation (
14
):

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k,l}\ket{\psi_{k,l}},

(17)

for arbitrary complex coefficients
\alpha_{k,l}
such that
\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{\alpha_{k,l}}^{2}=1
. The extension of all these expressions to the case of
n>2
spatial modes is straightforward, albeit resulting in long and cumbersome expressions with a similar structure.

Appendix B
Discussion about symmetry operator
\hat{S}

In this section, we discuss the role and interpretation of the symmetry operator
\hat{S}
, introduced in the main text. It is a linear optical operator whose action on the creation operators is given by

\displaystyle\hat{S}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\hat{S}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\hat{S}\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\hat{S}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda).

(18)

Assuming that the vacuum state is invariant under
\hat{S}
(
i.e.
,
\hat{S}\ket{\text{vac}}=\ket{\text{vac}}
), one can verify that these properties completely characterize the action of
\hat{S}
on all quantum states. As previously mentioned,
\hat{S}
has the simple physical interpretation of exchanging the two spatial modes. Applying the definition of
\hat{S}
twice yields
\hat{S}^{2}=\mathds{1}
, implying that
\hat{S}
is Hermitian. Furthermore, the property
\hat{S}^{2}=\mathds{1}
implies that
\hat{S}
is a linear symmetry. A basic result from linear algebra tells us that any state can be decomposed into a symmetric and an antisymmetric part

\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi_s}+\ket{\psi_a}=\frac{\ket{\psi}+\hat{S}\ket{\psi}}{2}+\frac{\ket{\psi}-\hat{S}\ket{\psi}}{2},

(19)

with
\hat{S}\ket{\psi_s}=\ket{\psi_s}
and
\hat{S}\ket{\psi_a}=-\ket{\psi_a}
. We can also define the action of
\hat{S}
on any Hermitian operator
\hat{H}
by
\hat{S}:\hat{H}\mapsto\hat{S}\hat{H}\hat{S}
. Similarly, we verify that
\hat{S}^{2}=\mathds{1}
when acting on Hermitian operators, confirming that it also acts as a linear symmetry. As such, a similar decomposition of
\hat{H}
into symmetric and antisymmetric parts exists:

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{s}+\hat{H}_{a}=\frac{\hat{H}+\hat{S}\hat{H}\hat{S}}{2}+\frac{\hat{H}-\hat{S}\hat{H}\hat{S}}{2},

(20)

with
\hat{S}\hat{H}_{s}\hat{S}=\hat{H}_{s}
and
\hat{S}\hat{H}_{a}\hat{S}=-\hat{H}_{a}
.

We now explain how one can obtain a quantitative measure of the symmetry of any state
\ket{\psi}
using the operator
\hat{S}
. Starting with the state
\ket{\psi}
, we decompose it as before:
\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi_s}+\ket{\psi_a}
. Since these components are not normalized, a meaningful measure of symmetry can be obtained by comparing their relative norms. We thus compute:

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\psi_s}{\psi_s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\bra{\psi}+\bra{\psi}\hat{S})(\ket{\psi}+\hat{S}\ket{\psi}),

(21a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi}+2\bra{\psi}\hat{S}\ket{\psi}+\bra{\psi}\hat{S}^{2}\ket{\psi}),

(21b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+\bra{\psi}\hat{S}\ket{\psi}),

(21c)

and similarly,
\innerproduct{\psi_a}{\psi_a}=\frac{1}{2}(1-\bra{\psi}\hat{S}\ket{\psi})
. If we now define the normalized states
\ket*{\tilde{\psi}_s}
and
\ket*{\tilde{\psi}_a}
, we can write:

\ket{\psi}=\alpha\ket*{\tilde{\psi}_s}+\beta\ket*{\tilde{\psi}_a},

(22)

with

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}=\frac{1+\bra{\psi}\hat{S}\ket{\psi}}{2},

\displaystyle\quad\beta^{2}=\frac{1-\bra{\psi}\hat{S}\ket{\psi}}{2}.

(23)

A natural measure of the symmetry is then given by

\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}=\bra{\psi}\hat{S}\ket{\psi}.

(24)

This formula can also be interpreted as the overlap between the original state
\ket{\psi}
and its swapped version
\hat{S}\ket{\psi}
. If
\ket{\psi}
is nearly symmetric, the expectation value approaches
1
; if it is nearly antisymmetric, it approaches
-1
. A simple application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields:

-1\leq\bra{\psi}\hat{S}\ket{\psi}\leq 1.

(25)

We note that the value
\bra{\psi}\hat{S}\ket{\psi}=0
corresponds to an anionic symmetry.

Appendix C
Matrices and linear optical operations

In this section of the SM, we review, for completeness, the properties of linear optical operations and their matrix representations. A linear optical operation, or mode transformation, that does not mix the internal degree of freedom
\lambda
is described via the action of unitary matrices. If we consider
M\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, any
n\times n
complex matrix, its action on orthogonal modes
\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}(\lambda),\dots,\hat{a}_{n-1}^{\dagger}(\lambda)
is given by

\displaystyle M:\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\mapsto\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{kj}\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{j}(\lambda)\mapsto\hat{b}_{j}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{kj}^{*}\hat{a}_{k}(\lambda).

(26)

Note the choice of index ordering in the matrix
M
. Choosing
M_{jk}
instead would be equally valid but would yield slightly less appealing formulas. If the
a
modes are orthogonal (
[\hat{a}_{j}(\lambda),\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}(\mu)]=\delta(\lambda-\mu)\delta_{jk}
), then it is a valid mode transformation if and only if the
b
modes are also orthogonal. Indeed,

\displaystyle[\hat{b}_{j}(\lambda),\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}(\mu)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m=0}^{n-1}M^{*}_{lj}M_{mk}[\hat{a}_{l}(\lambda),\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}(\mu)]

(27a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m=0}^{n-1}M^{*}_{lj}M_{mk}\delta(\lambda-\mu)\delta_{lm}

(27b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}M^{*}_{lj}M_{lk}\delta(\lambda-\mu)=(M^{\dagger}M)_{jk}\delta(\lambda-\mu),

(27c)

so the
b
modes are orthogonal if and only if
M^{\dagger}M=\mathds{1}
,
i.e.
, as stated above,
M
is a unitary matrix. From now on, we will denote by
\hat{M}
the operator that acts on the Hilbert space via the corresponding mode transformation. Since it is a proper mode transformation, it is automatically unitary (for example, it preserves the norm of states). It is characterized by the relations

\displaystyle\hat{M}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\hat{M}^{\dagger}=\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{kj}\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\hat{M}\ket{0}=\ket{0}.

(28)

Now, if
M
and
N
are two unitary matrices, we can compute the action of
\hat{M}\hat{N}
:

\hat{M}\hat{N}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\hat{N}^{\dagger}\hat{M}^{\dagger}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}N_{kj}\hat{M}\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\hat{M}^{\dagger}=\sum_{k,l=0}^{n-1}N_{kj}M_{lk}\hat{a}_{l}^{\dagger}(\lambda)=\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(MN)_{lj}\hat{a}_{l}^{\dagger}(\lambda).

(29)

Thus, the composition of
\hat{M}
and
\hat{N}
corresponds exactly to the transformation induced by the matrix product
MN
, or more compactly,
\hat{M}\hat{N}=\widehat{MN}
. In the language of group theory, this defines a group homomorphism from
U_{n}(\mathbb{C})
to the group of unitary operators on the
n
-mode Fock space.

For
n=2
, we have simple matrix representations for the symmetry operator
\hat{S}
and the beam splitter (BS)
\hat{U}
. Following our definitions and conventions, we get

\displaystyle S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},

(30)

which we recognize, respectively, as the Pauli
X
matrix and the Hadamard matrix.

Appendix D
Discussion on the BS implementation

In the Letter, we have considered a BS with a specific choice for its action, which, as discussed in the previous section of the SM, can be described by the Hadamard matrix

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},

(31)

resulting in an inherent asymmetry between the two spatial modes. This explains why our formulas involving the parity operator
\hat{\Pi}_{1}
exhibit an explicit asymmetry in the roles played by the spatial modes. In this section, we discuss alternative choices for the implementation of the BS and their consequences.

There are many different representations of a balanced BS, and the choice depends on the physical implementation. The most general parametrization of a balanced BS is given by the matrix

\frac{e^{i\tau}}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&e^{-i\phi}\\
e^{i\phi}&-e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix},

(32)

for
\theta,\phi,\tau\in[-\pi,\pi]
, where the condition that the BS is balanced is ensured by all matrix elements having equal modulus. For common parameter choices, we obtain frequently used BS matrices such as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\
i&1\end{pmatrix}.

(33)

We now address the following question: if we compute
\mathbb{P}[n_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{2}]
using a different BS implementation, which symmetry
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}
of the state are we effectively measuring? Let us assume that the operators
\hat{U}
act via the general matrix

\frac{e^{i\tau}}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&e^{-i\phi}\\
e^{i\phi}&-e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix}

(34)

as written above. Following the same reasoning as in the main text, the relevant symmetry operator is

\hat{\mathfrak{S}}=\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\Pi}_{1}\hat{U},

(35)

which corresponds to the matrix product

\left[\frac{e^{i\tau}}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&e^{-i\phi}\\
e^{i\phi}&-e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix}\right]^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\frac{e^{i\tau}}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&e^{-i\phi}\\
e^{i\phi}&-e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-i(\theta+\phi)}\\
e^{i(\theta+\phi)}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(36)

This implies that the action of
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{S}}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\hat{\mathfrak{S}}^{\dagger}=e^{i(\theta+\phi)}\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{S}}\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\hat{\mathfrak{S}}^{\dagger}=e^{-i(\theta+\phi)}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda).

(37)

From these expressions, we can verify that
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}
is Hermitian and unitary, satisfying
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}^{2}=\mathds{1}
. Thus, it defines a valid symmetry: by computing
\mathbb{P}[n_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{2}]
, we are probing the symmetry of the state
\ket{\psi}
with respect to this new symmetry operator
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}
. We now observe that if we redefine the phases of the creation operators as

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda)=\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda)=e^{i(\theta+\phi)}\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda),

(38)

then the action of
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}
on the new operators
\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}(\lambda)
becomes

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\mapsto\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\mapsto\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda),

(39)

which corresponds to the standard symmetry relations defined by
\hat{S}
. This demonstrates that the only difference lies in the phase conventions for each spatial mode.

Finally, we note that if
\ket{\psi}
is a state with the same number of photons in each mode (or a coherent superposition of such states), then the action of
\hat{\mathfrak{S}}
is equivalent to that of
\hat{S}
. Indeed, the phase factors
e^{i(\theta+\phi)}
and
e^{-i(\theta+\phi)}
appear in equal numbers and cancel out. This is particularly relevant for the standard HOM experiment, where two single photons are input into different arms. Thus, we recover the well-known result that the specific implementation of a balanced BS does not affect the coincidence probability in HOM interferometry. This explains why such discussions are often omitted in that context. However, in more general settings, the implementation of the BS can indeed play a significant role.

Appendix E
Diagonalisation via Fourier matrices

In this section of the SM, we provide a detailed verification of a simple and well-known formula for the diagonalization of a permutation matrix. The primary aim is to present an explicit derivation of the result. In the main text, we introduced the operator
\hat{P}
, characterized by the matrix

P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&1\\
1&&&0\end{pmatrix},

(40)

which consists of
n
ones and zeros elsewhere. The ones are placed along the first upper diagonal (
n-1
entries), with a single 1 in the lower-left corner. Interpreting
P
as a discrete translation operator with periodic boundary conditions, it is natural to seek its eigenvectors in the form of (discrete) plane waves. Define
e_{j}=(1,\omega^{j},\omega^{2j},\dots,\omega^{(n-1)j})^{T}
, where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}
is a primitive
n
th root of unity. A straightforward computation shows that

Pe_{j}=P\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\omega^{j}\\
\vdots\\
\omega^{(n-1)j}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{j}\\
\vdots\\
\omega^{(n-1)j}\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\omega^{j}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\vdots\\
\omega^{(n-2)j}\\
\omega^{(n-1)j}\end{pmatrix}=\omega^{j}e_{j}.

(41)

Thus, the vectors
(e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1})
form an eigenbasis of
P
. Normalizing by
1/\sqrt{n}
and defining the matrix of eigenvectors as
U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\begin{pmatrix}e_{0}&e_{1}&\cdots&e_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}
, we obtain the diagonalization

P=UDU^{-1},

(42)

where
D=\operatorname{diag}(1,\omega,\dots,\omega^{n-1})
. Observing that the entries of
U
are given by
U_{kl}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\omega^{kl}
(with indices ranging from
0
to
n-1
), we identify
U
as the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrix. It is well known (or can be readily verified) that such a matrix is unitary. We thus obtain

P=UDU^{\dagger}.

(43)

Taking the complex conjugate (not the Hermitian conjugate) and noting that
U^{*}=U^{\dagger}
and
D^{*}=D^{\dagger}
, we also find

P=U^{\dagger}D^{\dagger}U.

(44)

Finally, from the results established in the SM on linear optical operations, if we associate to these matrices the corresponding operators
\hat{U}
,
\hat{P}
, and
\hat{D}
, we obtain the operator identities

\displaystyle\hat{P}=\hat{U}\hat{D}\hat{U}^{\dagger},\qquad\hat{P}=\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{\dagger}\hat{U}.

(45)

Appendix F
Fourier interferometer photon number formula

In this section, we formally prove the formula expressing the probability of measuring a certain photon number distribution at the output of a DFT interferometer, in terms of the expectation value of powers of
\hat{P}
evaluated on the initial state

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bra{\psi}P^{l}\ket{\psi}.

(46)

To do so, we first show how, in the absence of the interferometer, expectation values of
\hat{D}
relate to the photon number distribution. The situation is as follows: we consider a general quantum state
\ket{\psi}
distributed over
n
modes, with an arbitrary photon number distribution in each mode and an arbitrary distribution over internal degrees of freedom. We assume that photon numbers can be measured in each mode, irrespective of the internal degree of freedom. To build intuition, we begin with simple pure states and gradually generalize.

Temporarily setting aside the dependence of the operator on
\lambda
, we observe that for integers
m_{0},\dots,m_{n-1}
,

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger})^{m_{0}}\cdots(\hat{a}_{n-1}^{\dagger})^{m_{n-1}}\hat{D}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(\hat{D}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{\dagger})^{m_{0}}\cdots(\hat{D}\hat{a}_{n-1}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{\dagger})^{m_{n-1}}

(47a)

\displaystyle=(\omega^{0}\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger})^{m_{0}}\cdots(\omega^{n-1}\hat{a}_{n-1}^{\dagger})^{m_{n-1}}

(47b)

\displaystyle=\omega^{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}(\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger})^{m_{0}}\cdots(\hat{a}_{n-1}^{\dagger})^{m_{n-1}}.

(47c)

This implies that if
\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{0}!\cdots m_{n-1}!}}(\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger})^{m_{0}}\cdots(\hat{a}_{n-1}^{\dagger})^{m_{n-1}}\ket{\text{vac}}
is a Fock state with a definite number of photons in each spatial mode, then

\displaystyle\hat{D}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{0}!\cdots m_{n-1}!}}\hat{D}(\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger})^{m_{0}}\cdots(\hat{a}_{n-1}^{\dagger})^{m_{n-1}}\hat{D}^{\dagger}\hat{D}\ket{\text{vac}}

(48a)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}}{\sqrt{m_{0}!\cdots m_{n-1}!}}(\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger})^{m_{0}}\cdots(\hat{a}_{n-1}^{\dagger})^{m_{n-1}}\ket{\text{vac}}

(48b)

\displaystyle=\omega^{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}\ket{\psi},

(48c)

so that
\expectationvalue*{\hat D}:=\bra{\psi}\hat{D}\ket{\psi}=\omega^{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}
.

The extension to states including internal degrees of freedom is similar, though more cumbersome to write explicitly. A general state
\ket{\psi}
with
m_{j}
photons in mode
j
and an arbitrary wave function is written as

\ket{\psi}=\int\differential\{\lambda_{jk}\}F(\{\lambda_{jk}\})\prod_{j,k}\hat{a}_{j}(\lambda_{jk})^{\dagger}\ket{\text{vac}},

(49)

where the integration is over all variables
\lambda_{jk}
with
j=0,\dots,n-1
and
k=1,\dots,m_{j}
. The function
F
depends on all these variables. Since the action of
\hat{D}
is independent of the
\lambda_{jk}
, only the number of creation operators in each spatial mode matters. Therefore, we again have
\hat{D}\ket{\psi}=\omega^{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}\ket{\psi}
, and consequently
\expectationvalue*{\hat D}=\omega^{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}
.

For such a state
\ket{\psi}
with
m_{k}
photons in each mode, the expectation value of powers of
\hat{D}
is readily computed. Since
\hat{D}^{l}\ket{\psi}=\omega^{l\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}\ket{\psi}
, it follows that
\expectationvalue*{\hat D^l}=\omega^{l\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}
. Summing over
l=0,\dots,n-1
and using the geometric series formula, we obtain

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue*{\hat D^l}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\left(\omega^{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}\right)^{l}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\text{if }\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(50)

The most general pure state over
n
time-frequency modes can be written as

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{m_{0},\dots,m_{n-1}=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{\vec{m}}\ket{\psi_{\vec{m}}},

(51)

where
\ket{\psi_{\vec{m}}}
is a state with
m_{j}
photons in mode
j
, of the form given in Eq. (
49
), and
\alpha_{\vec{m}}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\sum_{\vec{m}}|\alpha_{\vec{m}}|^{2}=1
. As states with different photon numbers are orthogonal,
\innerproduct{\psi_{\vec m}}{\psi_{\vec m'}}=\delta_{\vec{m},\vec{m}^{\prime}}
, and the action of
\hat{D}
preserves photon number, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat D^l}_{\ket{\psi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\vec{m},\vec{m}^{\prime}}\alpha_{\vec{m}}^{*}\alpha_{\vec{m}^{\prime}}\bra{\psi_{\vec m}}\hat{D}^{l}\ket{\psi_{\vec m'}}

(52a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\vec{m}}|\alpha_{\vec{m}}|^{2}\omega^{l\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}

(52b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{m}}|\alpha_{\vec{m}}|^{2}\delta_{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}}

(52c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{m}\\
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}\end{subarray}}|\alpha_{\vec{m}}|^{2}=\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}\right].

(52d)

We now insert the interferometer
\hat{U}
between the state and number-resolving photon detection. Based on the result above and using the identity
\hat{P}=\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{\dagger}\hat{U}
, the probability that the measured photon numbers satisfy
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat D^l}_{\hat{U}\ket{\psi}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bra{\psi}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{l}\hat{U}\ket{\psi}

(53a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bra{\psi}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\hat{D}^{\dagger})^{-l}\hat{U}\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bra{\psi}\hat{P}^{-l}\ket{\psi}

(53b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat P^l}_{\ket{\psi}},

(53c)

where we used
\hat{P}^{n}=\mathds{1}
and the change of index
l\to n-l
. Thus, the probability of observing a photon number distribution satisfying the modular constraint is determined by the symmetry of the incoming state under the cyclic permutation
\sigma
. Since
\hat{D}
diagonalizes
\hat{P}
, the eigenvalues of
\hat{P}
are exactly
1,\omega,\dots,\omega^{n-1}
. If
\ket{\psi}
is an eigenstate of
\hat{P}
with eigenvalue
\theta=\omega^{k}
, then

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\theta^{l}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\text{if }\theta=1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(54)

Therefore, eigenstates of
\hat{P}
determine the extremal values of the probability. Furthermore, it means that the operator

\hat{\Pi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\hat{P}^{l},

(55)

can be understood as the projector onto the subspace of eigenvectors
\ket{\psi}
of
\hat{P}
such that
\hat{P}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}
.

Appendix H
Other results for the linear combination
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}

In the main text and in the previous section of the SM, we argued, based on the equation

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{P^l}_{\ket{\psi}}=\expectationvalue{\hat\Pi}_{\ket{\psi}},

(60)

that the probability that the measured photon numbers satisfy
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}
equals the squared norm of the component of
\ket{\psi}
in the eigenspace of
\hat{P}
associated with the eigenvalue
+1
. In this section, we describe how other values of
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}
provide insight into the norm of the component of
\ket{\psi}
in the other eigenspaces of
\hat{P}
. First, observe that for
j\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, the operator

\hat{\Pi}_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(\omega^{-j}\hat{P})^{l}

(61)

is a projector (it is straightforward to verify that it is Hermitian) onto the eigenspace of
\hat{P}
associated with the eigenvalue
\omega^{j}
. Recall that the eigenvalues of
\hat{P}
are precisely
1,\omega,\dots,\omega^{n-1}
. If
\ket{\psi}
is an eigenstate of
\hat{P}
with eigenvalue
\omega^{\ell}
, we have

\hat{\Pi}_{j}\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(\omega^{-j}\hat{P})^{l}\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(\omega^{-j}\omega^{\ell})^{l}\ket{\psi}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\ket{\psi},&\text{if }j=\ell,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(62)

Now consider an initial state
\ket{\psi}
for which
\ket{\varphi}=\hat{U}\ket{\psi}
has exactly
m_{k}
photons in output mode
k
. Then,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat\Pi_j}_{\ket{\psi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\omega^{-jl}\bra{\psi}\hat{P}^{l}\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\omega^{-jl}\bra{\psi}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{U}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{U}\ket{\psi}

(63a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\omega^{-jl}\bra{\varphi}(\hat{D}^{\dagger})^{l}\ket{\varphi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\omega^{-jl}\left(\omega^{-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}\right)^{l}

(63b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\left(\omega^{-j-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}}\right)^{l}

(63c)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1,&\text{if }\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv-j\pmod{n},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv-j\pmod{n}\right]=\expectationvalue{\hat\Pi_j}_{\ket{\psi}},

(64)

demonstrating that values of
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}
modulo
n
probe the squared norm of the component of
\ket{\psi}
in the other eigenspaces of
\hat{P}
.

Appendix I
FI and small parameter expansion

In this short section, we show how to compute the Fisher information (FI) in the vicinity of
\kappa=0
under certain assumptions on the probabilities. We assume that the experiment has only two outcomes, with probabilities denoted by
p(\kappa)
and
q(\kappa)
, where
p(0)=1
. Furthermore, we assume that the probabilities can be expanded near
\kappa=0
as

p(\kappa)=1-\kappa^{2}A+o(\kappa^{2}),\quad q(\kappa)=\kappa^{2}A+o(\kappa^{2})

(65)

for some arbitrary non-negative constant
A
. In this case, for
\kappa\neq 0
, the FI is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\kappa)}\left(\frac{\differential p(\kappa)}{\differential\kappa}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{q(\kappa)}\left(\frac{\differential q(\kappa)}{\differential\kappa}\right)^{2}

(66a)

\displaystyle=\frac{(p^{\prime}(\kappa))^{2}}{p(\kappa)(1-p(\kappa))}

(66b)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\kappa A+o(\kappa^{2}))^{2}}{\kappa^{2}A+o(\kappa^{2}))(1-\kappa^{2}A+o(\kappa^{2}))}

(66c)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\kappa^{2}A^{2}+o(\kappa^{2})}{\kappa^{2}A+o(\kappa^{2})}

(66d)

\displaystyle=4A+o(1)

(66e)

so that

\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\mathcal{F}(\kappa)=4A.

(67)

The precision is thus directly proportional to the parameter
A
defined in the expansion above.

Appendix J
Explicit expressions of the FI

In this section, we derive the different expressions for the FI presented in the main text. To do so, we utilize the expansion performed in the previous section. We begin by considering a state arriving at the DFT interferometer of the form
e^{i\kappa\hat{H}}\ket{\psi}
and expand the expression of
\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\;[n]\,\middle|\,\kappa\right]
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\;[n]\,\middle|\,\kappa\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\bra{\psi}e^{-i\kappa\hat{H}}\hat{P}_{\sigma}^{l}e^{i\kappa\hat{H}}\ket{\psi}

(68a)

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}e^{-i\kappa\hat{H}}\hat{\Pi}e^{i\kappa\hat{H}}\ket{\psi}

(68b)

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{ \left(1 - i\kappa\hat{H} - \tfrac{\kappa^2}{2} \hat{H}^2 \right) \hat{\Pi} \left(1 + i\kappa\hat{H} - \tfrac{\kappa^2}{2} \hat{H}^2 \right) }+o(\kappa^{2})

(68c)

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{ \hat{\Pi} + i\kappa(\hat{\Pi} \hat{H} - \hat{H} \hat{\Pi}) - \tfrac{\kappa^2}{2} \left( \hat{H}^2 \hat{\Pi} + \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}^2 - 2\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H} \right)}+o(\kappa^{2})

(68d)

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi}}+i\kappa\left(\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}-\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi}}\right)-\tfrac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\left(\expectationvalue{\hat{H}^2 \hat{\Pi}}+\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi} \hat{H}^2}-2\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}\right)+o(\kappa^{2}).

(68e)

We want this equation to take the form
p(\kappa)
or
q(\kappa)
from the previous section, which requires that
\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi}}=1
or
0
. As the computations differ slightly, we treat each case separately.

\blacktriangleright
Let’s first assume that
\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi}}=1
. Since
\hat{\Pi}
is a projector, this implies that
\hat{\Pi}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}
. As such, we can simplify the previous expression using the fact that each occurrence of
\hat{\Pi}
to the left or right of an expectation value can be removed. We obtain

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\;[n]\,\middle|\,\kappa\right]=1-\kappa^{2}\left(\expectationvalue{\hat{H}^2}-\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}\right)+o(\kappa^{2}).

(69)

We observe that the term
\expectationvalue*{\hat{H}^2}-\expectationvalue*{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}
can be expressed as the variance of two different operators

\expectationvalue*{\hat{H}^2}-\expectationvalue*{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}=\Delta(i[\hat{H},\hat{\Pi}])=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Delta\left(n\hat{H}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}\right).

(70)

Let us first verify the left-hand identity. The commutator of two Hermitian operators is anti-Hermitian, hence, we use
i[\hat{H},\hat{\Pi}]
to obtain a Hermitian operator. Since
\hat{\Pi}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}
, we find:

\expectationvalue{i[\hat{H}, \hat{\Pi}]}=i\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi}}-i\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}=i\expectationvalue{\hat{H}}-i\expectationvalue{\hat{H}}=0.

(71)

Then,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{(i[\hat{H}, \hat{\Pi}])^2}

\displaystyle=-\expectationvalue{(\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} - \hat{\Pi} \hat{H})^2}

(72a)

\displaystyle=-\expectationvalue{ \hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H} \hat{\Pi} - \hat{H} \hat{\Pi}^2 \hat{H} - \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}^2 \hat{\Pi} + \hat{\Pi} \hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H} }

(72b)

\displaystyle=-\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}+\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}+\expectationvalue{\hat{H}^2}-\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}

(72c)

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\hat{H}^2}-\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}.

(72d)

To verify the second expression, we use the identity
\hat{P}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}
, which implies we can remove
\hat{P}
when it appears on the left or right of an expectation value

\expectationvalue{n\hat{H} - \sum_{l=0}^{n-1} \hat{P}^l \hat{H} \hat{P}^{-l}}=n\expectationvalue{\hat{H}}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat{H}}=0.

(73)

Then,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\left(n\hat{H} - \sum_{l=0}^{n-1} \hat{P}^l \hat{H} \hat{P}^{-l} \right)^2}

(74a)

\displaystyle=n^{2}\expectationvalue{\hat{H}^2}-2n\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{P}^{-l} \hat{H}}+\sum_{k,l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{P}^{l-k} \hat{H}}

(74b)

\displaystyle=n^{2}\expectationvalue{\hat{H}^2}-2n\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{P}^{-l} \hat{H}}+n\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{P}^{l} \hat{H}}

(74c)

(74d)

\blacktriangleright
Now assume
\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi}}=0

\hat{\Pi}

\hat{\Pi}\ket{\psi}=0
. Thus, any expectation value involving
\hat{\Pi}

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\;[n]\,\middle|\,\kappa\right]=\kappa^{2}\expectationvalue*{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}+o(\kappa^{2}).

(75)

As before, the quadratic term can be expressed as a variance

\expectationvalue*{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}=\Delta(i[\hat{H},\hat{\Pi}]).

(76)

Indeed,

\expectationvalue{i[\hat{H}, \hat{\Pi}]}=i\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi}}-i\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}=0,

(77)

and

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{(i[\hat{H}, \hat{\Pi}])^2}

\displaystyle=-\expectationvalue{(\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} - \hat{\Pi} \hat{H})^2}

(78a)

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}.

(78b)

To derive a second formula similar to the case
\hat{\Pi}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}
, we assume that
\ket{\psi}
is an eigenvector of
\hat{P}

\hat{P}\ket{\psi}=\theta\ket{\psi}
, with
\theta\neq 1

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Delta\left(\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\theta^{l}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}\right)=\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}.

(79)

Note that for
\theta\neq-1
(the only real non-trivial root), the operator
\sum_{l}\theta^{l}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}
is not necessarily Hermitian. Hence, only the case
\theta=-1
(possible when
n

\expectationvalue{\sum_{l=0}^{n-1} \theta^l \hat{P}^l \hat{H} \hat{P}^{-l}}=\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\theta^{l}\theta^{-l}\theta^{-l}\expectationvalue{\hat{H}}=\expectationvalue{\hat{H}}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\theta^{-l}=0,

(80)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\left(\sum_{l=0}^{n-1} \theta^l \hat{P}^l \hat{H} \hat{P}^{-l}\right)^2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=0}^{n-1}\theta^{k+l}\expectationvalue{ \hat{P}^k \hat{H} \hat{P}^{l-k} \hat{H} \hat{P}^{-l} }

(81a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=0}^{n-1}\theta^{0}\expectationvalue{ \hat{H} \hat{P}^{k-l} \hat{H} }

(81b)

\displaystyle=n\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{P}^l \hat{H}}=n^{2}\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}.

(81c)

Appendix K
Extension to mixed states

In this section, we explain how the various results presented in the paper can be extended to the case of pure states. More explicitly, we first describe how the symmetry of a mixed state should be measured, then we explain how Eq. (
1
) and Eq. (
10
) can be extended, showing how the photon-number distribution at the output of the interferometer is related to the symmetry of the mixed input states. Finally, we show that, given an initial mixed input state with the required symmetry property, our protocol provides an explicit measurement scheme for which we can evaluate the precision and assess optimality. We thus consider a mixed state
\hat{\rho}
with the pure-state decomposition
\hat{\rho}=\sum_{j}p_{j}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}
(with all
p_{j}\neq 0
). This decomposition can be thought of as a diagonalization of the density matrix (with
\innerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_k}=\delta_{j,k}
), but our results hold for any decomposition, not necessarily with orthogonal pure states.

First, the natural extension of the measure of symmetry
\bra{\psi}\hat{S}\ket{\psi}
(for
n=2
) or
\bra{\psi}\hat{P}\ket{\psi}
(for general
n
) is obtained by taking the mixed-state expectation values

\displaystyle\tr(\hat\rho\hat S)

or

\displaystyle\tr(\hat\rho\hat P).

(82)

As usual, for the expectation value of a mixed state, we have

\tr(\hat\rho\hat P)=\sum_{j}p_{j}\tr(\ketbra{\psi_j}\hat P)=\sum_{j}p_{j}\bra{\psi_j}\hat{P}\ket{\psi_j},

(83)

so that
\tr(\hat\rho\hat P)
can be interpreted as the average symmetry of the pure components of
\rho
weighted by the probabilities
p_{j}
. An important case is when
\tr(\hat\rho\hat P)
takes extremal values,
i.e.
,
\absolutevalue{\tr(\hat\rho\hat P)}=1
. In this case, let us write
\tr(\hat\rho\hat P)=e^{i\phi}
. It is then natural to expect that all pure-state components of
\hat{\rho}
satisfy
\bra{\psi_j}\hat{P}\ket{\psi_j}=e^{i\phi}
. This can be formally proven by a clever use of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

1=\absolutevalue{\sum_j p_j \bra{\psi_j}\hat P\ket{\psi_j}}^{2}\leq\sum_{j}p_{j}\sum_{j}p_{j}\absolutevalue{\bra{\psi_j}\hat P\ket{\psi_j}}^{2}=1,

(84)

where we have used
\sum_{j}p_{j}=1
and
\absolutevalue{\bra{\psi_j}\hat P\ket{\psi_j}}\leq 1
since the eigenvalues of
\hat{P}
have unit modulus. The condition for equality in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that the following vectors are proportional

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{p_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{p_{n}}\end{pmatrix}\propto\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{p_{1}}\bra{\psi_1}\hat{P}\ket{\psi_1}\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{p_{n}}\bra{\psi_n}\hat{P}\ket{\psi_n}\end{pmatrix}.

(85)

As all
p_{j}
are assumed to be nonzero, this implies that all
\bra{\psi_j}\hat{P}\ket{\psi_j}
are equal, necessarily to
e^{i\phi}
. An important consequence is that if
\tr(\hat\rho\hat S)=1
(resp.
-1
), then all pure states appearing in any decomposition of
\hat{\rho}
are automatically symmetric (resp. antisymmetric).

If the state
\rho
is injected into the DFT interferometer, the photon-number distribution in the output ports will simply be a weighted superposition of the distributions obtained for each pure component. As such, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\Tr(\hat P^l\hat\rho)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}p_{j}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\expectationvalue{\hat P^l}_{\ket{\psi_j}}=\sum_{j}p_{j}\mathbb{P}_{\ket{\psi_j}}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}\right]=\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\rho}}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}\right],

(86)

where the notation
\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\sigma}}[\cdots]
denotes the probability of a certain photon-number distribution when the state
\hat{\sigma}
is injected into the DFT interferometer. This formula is directly analogous to Eq. (
10
), linking the photon-number distribution to the symmetry properties of
\hat{\rho}
. In the case
n=2
, this equation reduces to

\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\rho}}[n_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{2}]=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tr(\hat\rho\hat S)\right),

(87)

which is the natural extension of Eq. (
1
) to mixed states.

Finally, we consider the metrological problem of estimating
\kappa
when the evolution
e^{i\hat{H}\kappa}
is performed before the DFT interferometer with an initial mixed state. Since Eq. (
86
) is linear in the quantum state, we can use the pure-state expansions of Eq. (
68
) to develop the probability to second order in
\kappa
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0\;[n]\,\middle|\,\kappa\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}p_{j}\Bigg[\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi}}_{\ket{\psi_j}}+i\kappa\left(\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}_{\ket{\psi_j}}-\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi}}_{\ket{\psi_j}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\tfrac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\left(\expectationvalue{\hat{H}^2 \hat{\Pi}}_{\ket{\psi_j}}+\expectationvalue{\hat{\Pi} \hat{H}^2}_{\ket{\psi_j}}-2\expectationvalue{\hat{H} \hat{\Pi} \hat{H}}_{\ket{\psi_j}}\right)+o(\kappa^{2})\Bigg].

(88)

Following the same reasoning as before, we require the constant term to be either
0
or
1
, which means that
\tr(\hat\rho\hat\Pi)=1
or
\tr(\hat\rho\hat\Pi)=0
. In both cases, using Eq. (
70
) and Eq. (
76
), we obtain

\mathcal{F}=\sum_{j}p_{j}\Delta_{\ket{\psi_j}}(i[\hat{H},\hat{\Pi}]).

(89)

Considering Eq. (
11
), we can provide a more physically concrete formula if we make further assumptions on the symmetry of
\hat{\rho}
. If all pure components of
\hat{\rho}
are symmetric, which is equivalent (as argued above) to
\tr(\hat\rho\hat P)=1
, we get

\mathcal{F}=\frac{4}{n^{2}}\sum_{j}p_{j}\Delta_{\ket{\psi_j}}\left(n\hat{H}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}\right).

(90)

Conversely, if all pure components of
\hat{\rho}
are antisymmetric, which is equivalent to
\tr(\hat\rho\hat P)=-1
, we have

\mathcal{F}=\frac{4}{n^{2}}\sum_{j}p_{j}\Delta_{\ket{\psi_j}}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{l}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}\right).

(91)

Knowing that the quantum Fisher information (QFI) is convex, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\geq\mathcal{Q}\left(\hat{H}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}\right)

or

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\geq\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{l}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}\right),

(92)

depending on whether
\tr(\hat\rho\hat P)
equals
1
or
-1
, where
\mathcal{Q}(\hat{H}^{\prime})
denotes the QFI in the situation where the operator
\hat{H}^{\prime}
is used for the evolution instead of
\hat{H}
. As we now argue, we have equality in both equations above. Define
\hat{H}^{\prime}
as
\hat{H}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}
or
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{l}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}
, depending on whether we are considering the first or the second case. We can consider the alternative metrological situation where
\hat{H}^{\prime}
is used for the evolution instead of
\hat{H}
. Applying Eq. (
92
) with
\hat{H}^{\prime}
instead of
\hat{H}
bounds the FI as
\mathcal{F}(\hat{H}^{\prime})\geq\mathcal{Q}(\hat{H}^{\prime})
. Since the QFI gives the optimal precision obtainable, we necessarily have the reverse inequality
\mathcal{F}(\hat{H}^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{Q}(\hat{H}^{\prime})
. With a straightforward computation, we can verify that in both cases,
\mathcal{F}(\hat{H}^{\prime})=\mathcal{F}(\hat{H})
. Consequently, the precision of the protocol when the original generator
\hat{H}
is used is

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{Q}\left(\hat{H}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}\right)

or

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{l}\hat{P}^{l}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{-l}\right),

(93)

depending on whether
\tr(\hat\rho\hat P)
equals
1
or
-1
. These formulas can be analyzed in the same spirit as those valid for pure states. In particular, if
\hat{H}
is antisymmetric under cyclic permutation (
\hat{P}\hat{H}\hat{P}^{\dagger}=-\hat{H}
), the measurement device is optimal with
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{Q}
.

Appendix L
Analysis of the photon loss

In this appendix, we present an approach to understanding the effect of photon losses. Throughout this section, we assume that photon loss occurs independently in each mode, with the same transmissivity
\eta
for all modes. This model encompasses photon absorption, scattering, and imperfect detection efficiency. In practice, we represent losses using a lossy BS model, which transforms the creation operators as

\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\to\sqrt{\eta}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)

(94)

where
\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)
denotes the creation operator of an environmental mode initially in the vacuum state.

\blacktriangleright
Position of the losses

Losses can occur at any stage of the experiment—before or after the evolution
\hat{V}(\kappa)
and the interferometer
\hat{U}
. If they occur immediately before the measurement process, they simply modify the photon-number statistics. However, losses that occur earlier can alter the quantum state itself, potentially affecting its subsequent evolution and interference. Nevertheless, as we now argue, losses can be commuted with linear optical operations, meaning that the same output statistics are obtained whether loss occurs before or after a linear optical transformation. Consider the mode transformation
\hat{M}
defined by

\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\mapsto\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{k,j}\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}.

(95)

Depending on the order of composition, the operator
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)
transforms as

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\mapsto\sqrt{\eta}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{k,j}\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}(\lambda)+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\text{(loss after }\hat{M}\text{),}

(96a)

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\mapsto\sqrt{\eta}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{k,j}\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}(\lambda)+\sqrt{1-\eta}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{k,j}\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\text{(loss before }\hat{M}\text{).}

(96b)

In these two expressions, the only difference lies in the environmental modes:
\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)
versus
\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{k,j}\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}(\lambda)
. However, since these modes are unobserved, the distinction is irrelevant: the particular mode in which photons are lost does not affect the measurable output statistics. Assuming that the environmental modes are initially in the vacuum state, we can compute the output probabilities for a general input state
\ket{\psi}
by expanding the transformed creation operators. Because of the equivalence between the environmental modes in the two scenarios, the output statistics are identical. Hence, losses commute with linear optical operations without affecting the measurement outcomes.

Since the interferometer
\hat{U}
is, by definition, a linear optical transformation, we can equivalently assume that loss occurs either before or after
\hat{U}
. Similarly, in many cases, such as phase estimation or delay measurement, the evolution operator
\hat{V}(\kappa)
is also linear. In such situations, all losses can be commuted to the beginning of the experiment, effectively acting as imperfect state preparation.
Therefore, we can consider losses as occurring at any point in the experimental setup without changing the output statistics.

\blacktriangleright
Linking ideal and lossy probabilities

Having established that losses can be considered at the detection stage, we now relate the ideal and lossy probability distributions. As the observable counts the number of photons detected in each spatial mode, we define

\displaystyle P_{j_{0},\dots,j_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[m_{0}=j_{0},\dots,m_{n-1}=j_{n-1}],

\displaystyle Q_{k_{0},\dots,k_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\eta}[m_{0}=k_{0},\dots,m_{n-1}=k_{n-1}],

(97)

where
P_{j_{0},\dots,j_{n-1}}
denotes the ideal probability of detecting
j_{l}
photons in mode
l
, and
Q_{k_{0},\dots,k_{n-1}}
the corresponding probability in the presence of losses. For a single mode (
n=1
), a simple combinatorial argument yields

Q_{k}=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\binom{j}{k}\eta^{k}(1-\eta)^{j-k}P_{j}.

(98)

This can be interpreted as follows: detecting
k
photons after loss could originate from any initial number
j\geq k
of photons, of which
k
were transmitted and
j-k
lost. The binomial coefficient counts the number of possible transmitted subsets, while the factors
\eta^{k}
and
(1-\eta)^{j-k}
represent the transmission and loss probabilities, respectively. Extending this reasoning to
n
modes, we obtain

Q_{k_{0},\dots,k_{n-1}}=\sum_{j_{0}=k_{0}}^{\infty}\cdots\!\!\!\sum_{j_{n-1}=k_{n-1}}^{\infty}\prod_{l=0}^{n-1}\binom{j_{l}}{k_{l}}\eta^{k_{l}}(1-\eta)^{j_{l}-k_{l}}P_{j_{0},\dots,j_{n-1}}.

(99)

In practice, one typically measures
Q_{k_{0},\dots,k_{n-1}}
and wishes to recover the ideal probabilities
P_{j_{0},\dots,j_{n-1}}
. For
n=1
, this inverse relation can be derived conveniently using generating functions. Define

\displaystyle F(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P_{k}x^{k},\qquad G(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}Q_{k}x^{k}.

(100)

From the relation above, we obtain

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}Q_{k}x^{k}

(101a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\binom{j}{k}(1-\eta)^{k}\eta^{j-k}P_{i}x^{k}

(101b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}(1-\eta)^{k}\eta^{j-k}P_{j}x^{k}

(101c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\big[\eta+x(1-\eta)\big]^{j}P_{i}

(101d)

\displaystyle=F\big(\eta+x(1-\eta)\big).

(101e)

Thus, the inverse map
x\mapsto\frac{x-\eta}{1-\eta}
gives

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=G\left(\frac{x-\eta}{1-\eta}\right)

(102a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{x-\eta}{1-\eta}\right)^{j}Q_{j}

(102b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-\eta)^{j}}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}(-\eta)^{j-k}x^{k}Q_{j}

(102c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\binom{j}{k}\frac{(-\eta)^{j-k}}{(1-\eta)^{j}}Q_{j}x^{k}.

(102d)

By identifying coefficients, we recover

P_{k}=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\binom{j}{k}\frac{(-\eta)^{j-k}}{(1-\eta)^{j}}Q_{j}.

(103)

Extending this to
n
modes yields

P_{j_{0},\dots,j_{n-1}}=\sum_{k_{0}=j_{0}}^{\infty}\cdots\!\!\!\sum_{k_{n-1}=j_{n-1}}^{\infty}\prod_{l=0}^{n-1}\binom{k_{l}}{j_{l}}\frac{(-\eta)^{k_{l}-j_{l}}}{(1-\eta)^{k_{l}}}Q_{k_{0},\dots,k_{n-1}}.

(104)

This relation allows one to reconstruct the ideal probabilities from the measured lossy ones, thereby enabling the computation of the ideal probability
\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}km_{k}\equiv 0;[n]]
required for parameter estimation. Furthermore, using the formal expression of the Fisher information, one can quantify the efficiency of estimation in the presence of losses.

\blacktriangleright
Symmetry of lossy single photon states

As argued in the main text, the symmetry of the input state plays a crucial role in the applicability of our methods and the success of the estimation procedure. Since we have shown that losses can be commuted to the state-preparation stage (when
\hat{V}(\kappa)
is an optical linear operation), losses are effectively equivalent to imperfect state preparation. This motivates an analysis of the relationship between loss and state symmetry. For single-photon states, an interesting structure emerges. Recall that such a state can be written as

\ket{\psi}=\int\differential\lambda_{0}\cdots\differential\lambda_{n-1}F(\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n-1})\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{0})\cdots\hat{a}_{n-1}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{n-1})\ket{\text{vac}}.

(105)

Under the loss model, this state transforms into

\ket{\psi_\eta}=\int\differential\lambda_{0}\cdots\differential\lambda_{n-1}F(\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n-1})\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\sqrt{\eta}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{j})+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{j})\right)\ket{\text{vac}}.

(106)

To manipulate this expression, let us introduce a convenient notation.
For a binary string
s\in\{a,b\}^{n}
, define
\ket{\psi_s}
as the state obtained from
\ket{\psi}
by replacing
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{j})
with
\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{j})
whenever the
j
th letter of
s
is
b
, and leaving it unchanged otherwise. For example, for
n=3
and
s=aba
,

\ket{\psi_{aba}}=\int\differential\lambda_{0}\differential\lambda_{1}\differential\lambda_{2}F(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\hat{a}_{0}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{0})\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{1})\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{2})\ket{\text{vac}}.

(107)

We also have
\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi_{a\cdots a}}
. Using this notation,

\ket{\psi_\eta}=\sum_{s\in\{a,b\}^{n}}\eta^{n_{a}(s)/2}(1-\eta)^{n_{b}(s)/2}\ket{\psi_s},

(108)

where
n_{a}(s)
and
n_{b}(s)
count the occurrences of
a
and
b
in
s
, respectively. After tracing out the environmental (
b
) modes, we obtain

\hat{\rho}_{\eta}=\sum_{s,t\in\{a,b\}^{n}}\eta^{\frac{n_{a}(s)+n_{a}(t)}{2}}(1-\eta)^{\frac{n_{b}(s)+n_{b}(t)}{2}}\tr_{b}(\outerproduct{\psi_s}{\psi_t}).

(109)

Since
\ket{\psi_s}
and
\ket{\psi_t}
differ in at least one mode whenever
s\neq t
, we have
\tr_{b}(\outerproduct{\psi_s}{\psi_t})=0
for
s\neq t
. Thus, only the diagonal terms contribute

\hat{\rho}_{\eta}=\sum_{s\in\{a,b\}^{n}}\eta^{n_{a}(s)}(1-\eta)^{n_{b}(s)}\tr_{b}(\outerproduct{\psi_s}{\psi_s}).

(110)

The average symmetry of the resulting state is therefore

\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{\eta}\hat{P})=\sum_{s\in\{a,b\}^{n}}\eta^{n_{a}(s)}(1-\eta)^{n_{b}(s)}\tr(\tr_b(\ketbra{\psi_s}{\psi_s}) \hat P).

(111)

Here,
\tr_{b}(\outerproduct{\psi_s}{\psi_s})
contains a photon in spatial mode
j
only if
s_{j}=a
. Since
\hat{P}
cyclically permutes the spatial modes, the trace above is nonzero only if
s
is invariant under cyclic permutation. The only such strings are
s=a\cdots a
and
s=b\cdots b
. Hence,

\tr(\hat\rho_\eta\hat P)=\eta^{n}\bra{\psi}\hat{P}\ket{\psi}+(1-\eta)^{n}.

(112)

For a mixed single-photon state
\hat{\rho}
, this generalizes to

\tr(\hat\rho_\eta\hat P)=\eta^{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{P})+(1-\eta)^{n}.

(113)
