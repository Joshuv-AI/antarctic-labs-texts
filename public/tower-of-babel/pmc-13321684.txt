Severe anemia as a risk factor in Japanese patients with ovarian cancer receiving olaparib: a retrospective study
Authors: Kenta Yamaoka, Manabu Kume, Ayako Matsumoto, Masaki Hirabatake, Nobutaka Hayashi, Tadashi Shimizu, Takuya Aoki, Nobuyuki Muroi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, and recurrence after initial platinum-based chemotherapy remains a major challenge. Olaparib, a poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitor, is an essential maintenance therapy, particularly for patients with homologous recombination deficiency, with proven efficacy. However, hematologic toxicity—especially anemia—is common and frequently leads to dose reductions or treatment interruptions. Japanese patients may be more susceptible to this toxicity because of smaller body size and potentially higher systemic drug exposure. Although dose reduction is recommended for patients with moderate renal impairment in Western guidelines, standardized criteria have not been established in Japan, and real-world evidence on predictors of severe anemia is limited. This study aimed to identify clinical factors associated with grade ≥ 3 anemia during olaparib therapy.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of patients with ovarian cancer who initiated olaparib at Kobe City Medical Center General Hospital between July 2018 and December 2022. Patients who began therapy at external institutions were excluded. Adverse events were assessed using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to evaluate predictors of grade ≥ 3 anemia, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.ResultsAmong the 75 patients included the median baseline creatinine clearance (Ccr) was 75.7 mL/min. Twenty-five patients (33%) had prior exposure to pegylated liposomal doxorubicin (PLD), and eight (11%) had a baseline Ccr < 50 mL/min. Grade ≥ 3 anemia developed in 33 patients (44%). Multivariate analysis identified prior PLD exposure (hazard ratio [HR] 4.37, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.30–8.29), baseline Ccr < 50 mL/min (HR 4.03, 95% CI 1.53–10.63), and baseline Grade 2 anemia as independent predictors. Treatment duration was significantly shorter in patients with prior PLD exposure.ConclusionPrior PLD therapy, impaired renal function, and pre-existing anemia substantially increased the risk of severe anemia during olaparib treatment. These findings highlight the need for risk-adaptive monitoring strategies and proactive management in patients with high risk for severe anemia. Further studies with larger cohorts of patients with renal impairment are recommended to support dose-adjustment strategies and optimize treatment safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16148-2.

Background

Ovarian cancer is one of the most common gynecologic malignancies in Japan, and the majority of cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage [1]. Although platinum-based combination chemotherapy remains the first-line treatment and shows high response rates [2], disease recurrence is common, occurring in > 50% of patients following initial remission [3]. Patients with mutations in the breast cancer susceptibility genes (BRCA1/2) exhibit increased sensitivity to platinum-based agents due to defects in DNA damage repair; therefore, the platinum-free interval is an important factor in determining treatment strategies after recurrence [4, 5]. To extend progression-free survival, the clinical benefit of bevacizumab maintenance therapy has been established [3, 6].

Recently, olaparib, a poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor, has emerged as a novel therapeutic option [7, 8]. Multiple clinical trials have confirmed the efficacy of olaparib, both as monotherapy and in combination with bevacizumab, for maintenance after platinum-based chemotherapy [9, 10].

However, the use of olaparib is frequently complicated by adverse events. Common toxicities include gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea and vomiting, hematologic events such as anemia, as well as renal dysfunction, fatigue, and malaise [9, 10]. Among these, anemia is particularly prevalent, with grade ≥ 3 anemia reported in approximately 44% of patients [10]. Anemia often leads to dose reductions or treatment interruptions; therefore, it poses a serious concern for maintaining therapeutic intensity during the planned 2-year treatment period [9].

In Japan, the approved dose of olaparib is 300 mg, administered orally twice daily, consistent with international standards [9, 10]. However, there are concerns that Japanese patients, who generally have a smaller body size than Western populations, may be at increased risk of adverse events owing to higher systemic drug exposure. Similarly, Japanese patients have exhibited higher rates of dose-limiting toxicities than Western populations during oral anticancer therapy, such as capecitabine and sorafenib [11–17]. In a phase I pharmacokinetic study, patients with moderate renal impairment (creatinine clearance [Ccr] 31–50 mL/min) showed a 26% increase in peak plasma concentration and a 44% increase in the area under the curve compared with patients with normal renal function [18]. This notable increase in systemic exposure resulted in a dose-reduction recommendation by regulatory bodies, such as the United States Food and Drug Administration, which advises a dose of 200 mg twice daily for this specific patient population owing to the potential risk of increased toxicity [18, 19]. By contrast, in Japan, no standardized dose-adjustment criteria exist, as the efficacy and safety of reduced dosing have not been adequately evaluated.

We previously conducted a pharmacovigilance analysis using the Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report database and found that reports of olaparib-associated anemia in Japanese patients occurred within the first month of treatment [17]. This pattern suggested that anemia may be reported earlier in clinical practice than in clinical trials. Few studies have systematically evaluated the risk factors for olaparib-induced anemia; however, real-world evidence in Japanese patients remains limited. In clinical practice, olaparib is often administered either as monotherapy or in combination with bevacizumab, yet the risk of anemia in combination therapy has not been fully examined [15, 16, 20, 21]. Although there have been studies predicting risk factors for anemia associated with olaparib in Japanese patients, these were limited to monotherapy and did not examine the effects of combination therapy with bevacizumab [21].

We investigated clinical risk factors associated with the development of grade ≥ 3 anemia in Japanese patients with ovarian cancer receiving olaparib. The present study aimed to identify clinical predictors of severe anemia during olaparib therapy in a real-world Japanese cohort, as severe anemia frequently leads to dose modification or treatment interruption in clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted at Kobe City Medical Center General Hospital in Japan. Patients with ovarian cancer who initiated olaparib therapy at our institution between July 2018 and December 2022 were eligible. Patients were excluded if they (1) had initiated olaparib therapy at another institution, (2) received olaparib for < 1 month, (3) were < 20 years, or (4) had grade ≥ 3 anemia at the time of olaparib initiation. The observation period was defined as up to 730 days (i.e., 2 years) or until March 31, 2024, whichever occurred first.

Data collection

The following data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records: age, sex, body weight, body surface area (BSA), BRCA1/2 mutation status, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS), olaparib dose and regimen, prior chemotherapy history (including pegylated liposomal doxorubicin [PLD] and gemcitabine [GEM]), and baseline laboratory data (red blood cell [RBC] count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, serum creatinine). Ccr was calculated using the Cockcroft–Gault formula. Anemia severity was graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was to identify clinical risk factors associated with the development of grade ≥ 3 anemia. The secondary outcome was the association between anemia-related risk factors and the duration of olaparib treatment.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. To identify risk factors for the development of Grade ≥ 3 anemia, multivariate analysis using a Cox proportional hazards regression model was performed. As this study was conducted as a preliminary investigation of risk factors for anemia, variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were selected for inclusion in the multivariate model. Ccr was used as a marker for renal function and calculated using the Cockcroft–Gault equation. Patients were stratified into two groups: Ccr ≥ 50 mL/min and Ccr < 50 mL/min, based on reported thresholds. In this study, variables included in the multivariate analysis, in addition to Ccr, were selected based on previously reported threshold values that have been suggested as potential risk factors [15, 16, 20, 21]. The association between identified risk factors and treatment duration was assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, with group comparisons conducted using the log-rank test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. All analyses were conducted using JMP® Pro 18 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of Kobe City Medical Center General Hospital (Approval no. zn230808, July 28, 2023). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study, and an opt-out approach was employed.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

Seventy-five patients met the inclusion criteria for this study. Patient characteristics, stratified by the development of grade ≥ 3 anemia, are summarized in Table 1. Among the 75 patients, 33 (44.0%) developed grade ≥ 3 anemia during the treatment period. The relationship between anemia grade and time to onset in this study is presented in Supplementary Table 1. Consistent with findings from clinical trials, a substantial proportion of patients developed grade 3 or higher anemia at 3 months after treatment initiation. The median age and body weight of all patients were 65 years (Interquartile range [IQR]: (55.0–71.0) and 50.4 kg (IQR: 44.9–56.1),, respectively. Eight patients (10.7%) had a baseline Ccr < 50 mL/min. Patients with a Ccr < 30 mL/min were not included in this study. Most patients (97.3%) initiated olaparib at a dose of 600 mg/day, regardless of renal function status, and dose reductions were rarely implemented at treatment initiation.

Incidence and risk factors for grade ≥ 3 anemia

To identify risk factors for grade ≥ 3 anemia, we first conducted univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. Four variables were significantly associated with the development of grade ≥ 3 anemia: prior administration of PLD (hazard ratio [HR] = 4.31, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.32–8.02, P < 0.001), baseline Ccr < 50 mL/min (HR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.27–7.31, P = 0.013), pre-existing Grade 2 anemia (HR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.44–4.97, P = 0.002), and age ≥ 65 years (HR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.17–3.83, P = 0.013). These four variables were subsequently included in the multivariate analysis. As summarized in Table 2, three independent risk factors were identified: prior PLD treatment (HR = 4.37, 95% CI: 2.30–8.29, P < 0.001), Ccr < 50 mL/min (HR = 4.03, 95% CI: 1.53–10.63, P = 0.005), and pre-existing Grade 2 anemia at the start of olaparib (HR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.24–4.54, P = 0.009). By contrast, age ≥ 65 years was not statistically significant in the multivariate model (HR = 1.34, 95% CI: 0.71–2.54, P = 0.364). Furthermore, among patients with a history of PLD administration, no significant difference was observed in cumulative PLD dose between those who developed grade ≥ 3 anemia (median 248 mg) and those who did not (median 230 mg) (P = 0.750).

Association between risk factors and treatment duration

To investigate the impact of severe anemia on treatment duration, a Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed comparing patients with and without grade ≥ 3 anemia. The log-rank test revealed no statistically significant difference in treatment duration between the two groups (P = 0.650) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

We investigated clinical risk factors associated with the development of grade ≥ 3 anemia in Japanese patients with ovarian cancer receiving olaparib. Our results showed that a history of PLD administration, baseline Ccr < 50 mL/min, and pre-existing Grade 2 anemia were independently and considerably associated with the development of grade ≥ 3 anemia. Conversely, although age ≥ 65 years was significantly associated with anemia in univariate analysis, no association was observed in the multivariate model, suggesting that its independent contribution was limited. These findings provide clinically relevant information that may support risk assessment prior to treatment initiation and closer hematologic monitoring during the early phase of therapy.

PLD is commonly administered for recurrent or refractory ovarian cancer. As liposomal formulations are known to persist in systemic circulation for extended periods, prior PLD therapy may have contributed to bone marrow exhaustion, thereby potentially increasing the risk of anemia during subsequent treatment [22–25]. GEM, similar to PLD, i is used in the treatment of recurrent or refractory ovarian cancer; however, no significant association was observed between GEM use and the development of Grade ≥ 3 anemia in this study. Given the limited number of patients who received GEM, the statistical power to detect a meaningful association may have been insufficient, and thus, these results should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, factors such as the relatively short half-life of GEM and its potentially lower impact on anemia may also have contributed to this finding [26, 27]. In contrast, although PLD is generally considered to have lower myelosuppressive effects than conventional doxorubicin, anemia has been consistently reported as a representative hematologic toxicity associated with PLD [28]. The liposomal formulation allows prolonged circulation, leading to sustained exposure of hematopoietic progenitors [24, 29]. Such prolonged exposure may compromise marrow reserve or induce subclinical damage, which in turn may synergize with the DNA repair inhibition induced by olaparib, thereby amplifying the risk of severe anemia. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether this association reflects a direct pharmacologic effect of PLD or confounding by treatment line and cumulative prior therapy, as PLD is commonly used in recurrent or refractory settings. To disentangle these possibilities, future investigations should stratify analyses by treatment line and objective indicators of drug exposure, including pharmacokinetic evaluation of olaparib. The consistency of our findings with previous studies highlights the importance of integrating treatment history into risk assessment when initiating olaparib, although causality cannot be definitively established in this retrospective single-center study. Prospective multicenter studies, ideally incorporating pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic data, are warranted to validate these observations and refine dose-adjustment strategies in high-risk populations. From a clinical perspective, these usefulness findings suggest that patients with prior PLD exposure may require careful risk assessment prior to treatment initiation and closer monitoring for anemia, particularly during the early phase of olaparib therapy.

The occurrence of anemia associated with olaparib treatment is considered concentration-dependent [30]. In this study, Ccr < 50 mL/min was significantly associated with an increased risk of grade ≥ 3 anemia. Given that moderate renal impairment increases plasma concentrations of olaparib [18], our findings suggest that enhanced drug exposure may contribute to hematologic toxicity. In this study, among patients with Ccr > 50 mL/min, one patient who developed Grade ≥ 3 anemia and one patient who did not develop anemia initiated treatment at a reduced dose of 400 mg. Further investigation is warranted to determine the optimal dosing strategy and its relationship with adverse events in patients with impaired renal function.

Patients who had Grade 2 anemia at the initiation of olaparib therapy were considerably more likely to develop grade ≥ 3 anemia during treatment. This trend is consistent with previous reports; hence, reproducibility has been confirmed in this study [21]. Patients with a history of anemia during previous treatments are generally considered to be at higher risk of developing anemia in subsequent treatments [31].

Here, age ≥ 65 years was substantially associated with the development of grade ≥ 3 anemia in univariate analysis; however, this association was not retained in the multivariate model. Older patients tended to have short treatment durations, suggesting that age-related physiological changes may influence treatment continuity. Previous studies have similarly reported a higher incidence of dose modifications and treatment discontinuations owing to adverse events among older patients, and our findings are consistent with these observations [32]. Conversely, the present results also suggest that other clinical factors, including weight loss and impaired renal function, may confound this relationship, indicating that chronological age may not represent a strong independent risk factor. Therefore, further studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to validate these findings and to determine whether individualized risk assessment—considering renal function and baseline hematologic parameters—is more appropriate than uniform dose adjustments based solely on age.

Previous studies have suggested that BRCA1/2 mutation status and high olaparib exposure, such as a daily dose of 12 mg/kg, may be associated with an increased risk of anemia [15, 16]. However, in this study, neither BRCA1/2 mutation status nor olaparib dosage showed a significant association with the incidence of anemia. This finding may be attributed to the lower proportion of BRCA 1/2-mutated cases in our cohort than in previous reports, as well as to the fact that most patients weighed less than 50 kg and received olaparib at the standard recommended dose.

This study has some limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which limited the number of patients with moderate renal impairment (Ccr < 50 mL/min). Consequently, the analysis of this subgroup may have been underpowered. Second, as PLD was administered according to institutional treatment guidelines—particularly as a standard first-line agent for recurrence—it was difficult to disentangle the effects of PLD from those of the recurrent disease on anemia development and treatment duration. In addition, this study has several limitations related to sample size. Based on the observed incidence of anemia (64% in patients receiving PLD vs. 34% in those not receiving it), the estimated effect size corresponds to a moderate-to-large difference between groups. However, according to conventional sample size calculations for comparing two independent proportions, approximately 120–140 patients in total would be required to achieve adequate statistical power (80% power at a two-sided α of 0.05).

In contrast, the present study included a relatively small number of patients, which may have resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect significant associations and increased uncertainty in the estimates. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution. Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these results. Third, although adequate sample size considerations are essential when developing multivariable models, this study was retrospective and exploratory in nature, and a formal sample size calculation was not performed. Instead, all eligible patients treated during the study period were included in the analysis. Importantly, the number of candidate variables in the multivariable model was limited to four, with 33 events of grade ≥ 3 anemia observed, resulting in approximately eight events per variable. This ratio falls within the acceptable range suggested by previous methodological studies, indicating that the risk of model overfitting is likely limited and that the stability of the model is reasonably maintained in the context of an exploratory analysis [33, 34]. A limitation of this study is that a comprehensive analysis of anemia during post-relapse maintenance therapy was not feasible due to the limited number of eligible patients. To address these issues, future studies should involve larger patient cohorts. To achieve this, we believe it is necessary to first validate findings through multicenter observational studies and claims database analyses. In addition, the measurement of olaparib plasma concentrations may help clarify the relationship between renal function, drug exposure, and hematologic toxicity, particularly in patients with impaired renal function.

Conclusion

A history of PLD administration, Ccr < 50 mL/min, and Grade 2 anemia at baseline were associated with a high risk of developing severe anemia, suggesting the need for careful risk assessment prior to treatment initiation and closer hematologic monitoring during the early phase of therapy. On the other hand, no significant association was observed between severe anemia and treatment duration, which may be attributable to early treatment discontinuation due to disease progression or other adverse events. This indicates the need for further investigation, including an increase in sample size and consideration of the relationship with blood concentrations.
