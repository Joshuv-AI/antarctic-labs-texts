Effects of digital literacy and nursing informatics competency as job resources on nurses’ burnout and work engagement: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jeehae Chung, Hyesil Jung, Sang Mi Park, Kyeongmin Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs information and communication technology continues to advance rapidly within the healthcare sector, digital literacy and nursing informatics competency have become fundamental skills required for nurses. Nurses with digital literacy and nursing informatics competency exhibit enhanced nursing performance, high productivity, and improved work efficiency. Research remains limited on how digital literacy and nursing informatics competency influence nursing outcomes, such as burnout and work engagement. Therefore, this cross-sectional exploratory study utilized the framework of the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model to investigate how digital literacy and nursing informatics competency function as job resources and influence nurses’ burnout and work engagement.MethodsIn June 2025, 130 nurses working in inpatient wards, intensive care units, and emergency unit of a tertiary hospital in South Korea were recruited using convenience sampling. Participants completed a questionnaire measuring their digital literacy, nursing informatics competency, positive organizational culture, work demands, burnout, and work engagement. In total, 130 valid questionnaires were analyzed using multivariable linear regression. Mediation analyses were performed to examine whether positive organizational culture mediates the relationship between digital literacy or nursing informatics competency and work engagement.ResultsPositive organizational culture fully mediated the relationship between digital literacy and work engagement (direct effect = 0.420, 95% CI [-0.142, 0.982]; indirect effect = 0.349, 95% CI [0.108, 0.623]). Nursing informatics competency was also found to indirectly affect work engagement through positive organizational culture (indirect effect = 0.072, 95% CI [0.006, 0.170]).ConclusionsThis study extends the JD-R framework, by indicating that digital literacy and nursing informatics competency may operate as a personal resource that facilitates greater work engagement indirectly through the organizational resource of positive nursing culture. It emphasizes the interdependence between personal and organizational factors in shaping nurses’ work engagement. Therefore, strengthening nursing organizational culture is essential. Within such a positive culture, educational programs designed to improve nurses’ digital literacy and nursing informatics competency may further promote their work engagement.

Background

Work burnout adversely affects job satisfaction and organizational commitment, leading to adverse outcomes, such as personnel turnover and absenteeism. Among healthcare professionals in particular, burnout has been associated with a perceived inability to perform duties at an optimal level, diminished motivation, and reduced job-related self-esteem [1]. However, burnout can be mitigated by high levels of work engagement, which reflects a state of satisfaction, commitment, and motivation [2]. Work engagement is defined as ‘a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind that is characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption’ [3]. Vigor represents a high level of energy and mental resilience in working, dedication reflects a sense of significance and enthusiasm, and absorption denotes complete concentration and deep involvement in one’s work. Collectively, these dimensions characterize work engagement, which is considered the opposite of burnout. In general, challenging work conditions are correlated with increased levels of burnout, whereas access to adequate resources fosters greater work engagement.

A representative theoretical framework that explains the relationship among job characteristics, burnout, and work engagement is the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, which has been extensively employed across various fields, including social work [4, 5] and healthcare work [6–8]. In this model, job characteristics are divided into two broad categories—job demands and job resources [9]. Job demands encompass the physical, psychological, social, or organizational elements of a job that necessitate continuous physical and/or psychological (including cognitive and emotional) effort. Such demands may include high work pressure, excessive role responsibilities, emotional strain, and unfavorable environmental conditions. Conversely, job resources comprise the physical, psychological, social, or organizational aspects of a job that alleviate job demands and the associated physiological and psychological strain. These resources may exist at various levels: organizational (e.g., adequate salary, opportunities for career advancement), interpersonal (e.g., support from colleagues, positive team dynamics), work structure (e.g., role clarity, participation in decision making), and task-related (e.g., job autonomy) [10]. Generally, job demands and job resources have contrasting effects on employee well-being: high job demands are associated with greater risk of burnout, while job resources facilitate work engagement and may buffer the effects of job demands on burnout [10]. In this framework, personal resources, which can be important determinants of individuals’ adaptation to work environments, also play a crucial role. Personal resources refer to individuals’ perceptions of their ability to control and successfully influence their environment, and they function in a manner similar to job resources by enhancing work engagement, and mitigating the negative effects of job demands [11].

The widespread use of information and communication technology (ICT) has transformed clinical practice, offering benefits such as improved data accessibility, enhanced efficiency, and evidence-based decision-making. In particular, the adoption of electronic health records (EHRs) has become a core component of modern healthcare delivery, facilitating more comprehensive and coordinated patient care. However, alongside these advantages, digitalization has also introduced new challenges for healthcare professionals. Previous studies have highlighted that the increasing use of digital systems can lead to cognitive overload, time pressure, and elevated clerical burden, which may contribute to clinician stress and burnout [12, 13]. These challenges, often intensified by extended work hours and frequent system interruptions, represent a form of challenging work condition in digitalized clinical environments. In such circumstances, digital literacy and nursing informatics competency have emerged as core competencies for nurses.

Digital literacy is described as the ability to use ICT to find, evaluate, create, and communicate information, requiring both cognitive and technical skills by the American Library Association. In healthcare settings, nurse’ digital literacy is the ability to use ICT effectively and responsibly for patient care. Nurses with digital literacy can ‘demonstrate the numeracy, literacy, digital and technological skills required to meet the needs of people in their care to ensure safe and effective nursing practice’ [14]. They are able to ‘effectively and responsibly use a range of digital technologies to access, input, share and apply information and data within teams and between agencies’ [14]. Thus, it can reduce routine and repetitive nursing tasks and facilitate prompt evidence-based decision making and delivery of accurate nursing interventions [15]. The use of ICT is therefore expected to lead to improvement in health outcomes, such as patient safety and greater efficiency in nursing care [16, 17]. Within this context, nursing informatics competency, defined as a satisfactory level of knowledge, skills, and abilities to successfully perform specific informatics tasks [18], is recognized as a critical competency for nurses by the American Nurses Association and American Association of Colleges of Nursing. Nursing informatics competency has been found to be significantly associated with patient safety outcomes [19], problem-solving ability [20], nursing performance, and productivity [21–23]. Accordingly, it is considered a key element in enhancing patient safety and improving the quality and efficiency of nursing care. Building on the JD-R model, digital literacy and nursing informatics competency can therefore be understood as personal/job resources that enable effective adaptation to digitalized work environments, enhance performance, and mitigate technology-related stress. Therefore, these competencies may play a crucial role in influencing burnout and work engagement among nurses. Nevertheless, research examining their influence on burnout and work engagement is still limited.

Accordingly, this study aimed to examine the effects of digital literacy and nursing informatics competency, conceptualized as job resources based on the JD-R model, on nurses’ burnout and work engagement.

Methods

Study design and settings

This cross-sectional exploratory study aimed to examine the influence of job resources-including digital literacy, nursing informatics competency, and positive organizational culture-and job demands (work demands) on nurses’ work engagement and burnout, based on the JD-R model. The study was conducted in a tertiary hospital with 818 beds in South Korea. Reporting of this study adheres to the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional research.

Study population and sample size

Participants were recruited from inpatient wards, intensive care units, and emergency unit in June 2025 using convenience sampling. The inclusion criteria were that the participants were registered nurses (RNs) who provided direct patient care, received a full explanation of the purpose and content of the study, and voluntarily agreed to participate. Nurses who did not provide direct patient care or who requested withdrawal due to psychological discomfort were excluded.

The sample size was determined to achieve sufficient statistical power. In this study, the parameters were set as follows: an effect size of 0.15 according to Cohen [24], a power of 0.80, a significance level of 0.05, and 10 predictors. Based on these parameters, a minimum sample size of 118 participants was required. Considering a 10% dropout rate, this study collected data from 130 participants.

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics

Sociodemographic parameters included age, sex, educational level, marital status, job position, working unit, length of career as a nurse and in the current workplace, and types of shifts (three-shift or day shift).

Job demands

Job demands were assessed by the work demands subscale of the Korean version of the Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire (COPSOQ II); these subscales comprise 11 items from quantitative demands, work pace, and emotional demands. The COPSOQ II was developed by Pejtersen et al. [25] to assess the psychosocial work environment at the workplace level. The Cronbach’s α coefficients of the scale were between 0.82 and 0.87. In 2013, June and Choi [26] translated it into Korean and tested its validity. Internal consistency reliability estimated by Cronbach’s α was between 0.71 and 0.80. All items are based on a 5-point Likert-type scale from 1 = “Never” to 5 = “Always.” The total score ranges from 11 to 55, with higher scores indicating higher work demands. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.88, and three subscales ranged from 0.77 to 0.89.

Job resources

The job resources consist of three variables; digital literacy, nursing informatics competency, and positive nursing organizational culture.

Digital literacy was assessed using six items in the ICT literacy domain of a tool developed to evaluate the effectiveness of using digital textbooks and smart education [27]. ICT literacy is defined as the ability to use technology as a tool to collect, analyze, evaluate, and communicate information. The items reflect three dimensions, information literacy, media literacy, and information ethics. A higher score indicates a higher level of ICT literacy, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.93. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.81.

Nursing informatics competency was evaluated using the nursing informatics competence scale for Korean clinical nurses (K-NICAS), developed by Jang and Kim [28]. It has 20 items comprising five subscales, including basic ICT use, nursing information usage and management, professional accountability and ethics, ICT use in nursing, and attitude toward nursing informatics utilizing a 4-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 = “Not proficient at all” to 4 = “Highly proficient.” The score range was from 20 to 80 and the higher the score, the higher the level of nursing informatics competency. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was reported as 0.91 during the initial development of the instrument and was found to be 0.90 in this study.

A positive nursing organizational culture was measured using an instrument developed by Kim and Kim [29] that is based on the 7 S model (shared value, strategy, structure, system, staff, skill, and style) and reflects the realities of nursing organizations in Korea. The instrument consists of 26 items, each measured on a 5-point Likert scale. The total score ranges from 26 to 130, with higher scores indicating a greater perception of a positive organizational culture. The overall reliability of the instrument was reported as Cronbach’s α = 0.95, with the subscales ranging from 0.83 to 0.95. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the instrument was 0.96, and the four subscales ranged from 0.84 to 0.95.

Burnout

Burnout was assessed using the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) developed by Kristensen et al. [30]. The Korean version of the scale was validated in 2022 [31]. The scale has 18 items rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale from 1 = “Never” to 5 = “Always.” The total score ranges from 18 to 90, with a higher score indicating a greater level of burnout. The CBI comprises three subscales that measure three aspects of burnout, including personal burnout (n = 6), work-related burnout (n = 6), and client-related burnout (n = 6). The Cronbach’s α coefficient for internal reliability at the time of development ranged from 0.85 to 0.87 [30]. For the Korean version of the CBI, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.95, and from 0.90 to 0.94 for its three subscales [31]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.95, and the three subscales ranged from 0.88 to 0.93.

Work engagement

The Korean version of the 9-item Utrecht work engagement scale (UWES-K), developed by Schaufeli et al. [32] and translated into Korean by Kim, Park, and Kwon [33], was used to assess participants’ work engagement. It has 9-items rated on a 7-point Likert-type scale from 0 = “Never” to 6 = “Always,” where a higher score indicates higher job engagement of the employees. This scale comprises three dimensions; vigor, dedication, and absorption. The score ranges from 0 to 54. At the time of development, the Cronbach’s alpha for the total 9-item UWES ranged from 0.85 to 0.92, and from 0.60 to 0.90 for its three subscales [32]. For the UWES-K, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient ranged from 0.89 to 0.91 for the subscales [33]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.92, and the three subscales ranged from 0.79 to 0.91.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0. Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed tests, and set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations (SD), and percentages, were calculated to summarize participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and key study variables. Group differences were examined using independent samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variances (ANOVAs). When significant differences were found in the ANOVA, Scheffé’s test was conducted as a post-hoc analysis.

Missing data were minimal, with five missing values across all items and participants. As these missing values were determined to be missing completely at random, they were imputed using the median value of each corresponding variable.

Pearson’s correlation analyses were used to examine bivariate relationships among digital literacy, nursing informatics competency, positive nursing organizational culture, work demands, work engagement, and burnout. To investigate the effects of digital literacy, nursing informatics competency, positive nursing organizational culture, and work demands on work engagement and burnout, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted, with sex, total working career, and working unit included as covariates.

We also explored the possibility that positive organizational culture might mediate the relationship between digital literacy or nursing informatics competency and work engagement. Initial univariable linear regression analyses showed that both digital literacy and nursing informatics competency were significantly associated with work engagement. However, when other variables were controlled for in the multivariable linear regression model, these associations were no longer statistically significant. Given these findings, we conducted mediation analyses using Hayes’ PROCESS macro v4.2 (Model 4) [34]. A bias-corrected bootstrapping method with 5,000 resamples and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was employed to estimate the indirect effects. Indirect effects were considered statistically significant if the 95% CI did not include zero [35].

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Of the 130 nurses who participated (Table 1), 122 (93.8%) were women and 85 (65.4%) were aged 30 years or younger. Their mean age was 29.2 (SD = 3.9) years. Most nurses had completed a 4-year university education (110, 84.6%) and were unmarried (110, 84.6%) at the time of the study. The participants had an average of 5.5 (SD = 4.1) years of total clinical experience and an average of 4.7 (SD = 3.6) years of experience in their current unit. Most participants worked in three-shift rotations and were evenly distributed across general wards, intensive care units, and emergency department. Levels of work engagement and burnout did not significantly differ across demographic or occupational categories, except for sex.

Correlation among digital literacy, nursing informatics competency, positive nursing organizational culture, work demands, work engagement, and burnout

Table 2 presents the correlations among the key study variables. Nurses with higher digital literacy tended to show greater nursing informatics competency (r = 0.619, p < 0.01) and to perceive a more positive nursing organizational culture (r = 0.548, p < 0.01). Both digital literacy and nursing informatics competency were positively associated with work engagement (r = 0.309, p < 0.01; r = 0.216, p < 0.05, respectively), and nurses who perceived a more positive organizational culture reported higher work engagement (r = 0.485, p < 0.01) and lower burnout (r = − 0.241, p < 0.01). In contrast, greater work demands were linked to increased burnout (r = 0.648, p < 0.01) and decreased work engagement (r = − 0.307, p < 0.01). Nurses who experienced higher work engagement tended to report lower levels of burnout (r = − 0.497, p < 0.01).

Regression analysis of factors affecting work engagement and burnout

A multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to identify the factors influencing participants’ work engagement and burnout (Table 3). The regression model for work engagement explained approximately 41% of the variance in work engagement, and the model was statistically significant (p < 0.001). The tolerance values (0.432–0.920) and variance inflation factor (VIF) values (1.086–2.316) observed in this study showed no evidence of multicollinearity among the independent variables. The Durbin–Watson statistic was 1.974, confirming the independence of residuals. After adjusting for all control variables, a more positive nursing organizational culture was associated with higher levels of work engagement among nurses (β = 0.307, p = 0.001).

Mediation analysis of digital literacy and nursing informatics competency

Mediation analyses were conducted to examine whether the relationship between digital literacy or nursing informatics competency and work engagement was mediated by positive nursing organizational culture (Table 4). For digital literacy, the direct effect on work engagement was not significant (effect = 0.420, 95% CI [-0.142, 0.982]). When positive nursing organizational culture was included as a mediating variable, a significant indirect effect emerged (effect = 0.349, 95% CI [0.108, 0.623]). Nurses with higher digital literacy tended to experience greater engagement through the perception of a more positive organizational culture. Discussion

Overview of the study findings

This study investigated the influence of digital literacy and nursing informatics competency, conceptualized as job resources within the JD-R model, on nurses’ work engagement and burnout. To provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying nurses’ occupational outcomes, positive nursing organizational culture was incorporated as a key job resource and work demands as a job demands within this framework. The findings revealed that a positive nursing organizational culture significantly increased work engagement, whereas work demands heightened burnout, when controlling for other variables. Moreover, digital literacy and nursing informatics competency exerted indirect effects on work engagement through organizational culture.

Relationships between job resources, job demands, and nurses’ outcomes

From a theoretical perspective, consistent with the JD-R model, our results confirmed the dual pathways between job resources and job demands. A positive nursing organizational culture was associated with higher engagement, aligning with previous studies. A recent integrative review highlighted these dual pathways of the JD-R model, identifying supervisor support, fair and authentic management, transformational leadership, interpersonal relations, autonomy, and professional resources as pivotal job resources influencing nurses’ motivation, whereas work overload, lack of formal rewards, and work–life interference were identified as key job demands [36]. Similarly, a systematic review by Aungsuroch et al. [37] emphasized that positive nursing organizational climate and supportive work environments serve as primary drivers of nurse work engagement. In contrast, work-related stressors, such as high workload, long shifts, emotional demands, and insufficient organizational support, significantly contribute to burnout and turnover intentions among nurses [38].

In our multivariable regression analyses, positive nursing organizational culture emerged as a significant predictor of work engagement, whereas digital literacy, nursing informatics competency, and work demands were not significantly associated with work engagement after controlling for other variables. These findings suggest that organizational culture exerts a stronger influence than individual competencies or job demands in shaping nurses’ work engagement. In addition, the nonsignificant effects of digital literacy and nursing informatics competency contrast with some prior studies. It appears that the impact of these competencies may vary according to contextual and organizational factors. Jung and Han [22] observed that nursing informatics competency remained a significant predictor of nursing performance even after accounting for emotional labor and work environment. This discrepancy may reflect differences in outcome variables and settings. While their study examined nurses’ performance in relatively stable convalescent hospitals, our study focused on work engagement among tertiary hospital nurses, who face heavier workloads and more complex digital systems. In such demanding environments, individual competencies alone may be insufficient to sustain work engagement without supportive organizational resources.

Work demands also did not emerge as a significant predictor of work engagement in this study, which contrasts with the findings of Ni et al. [39], which reported that higher job demands significantly reduced nurses’ work engagement. This difference may be attributed to how job demands were conceptualized. While they focused primarily on emotionally taxing demands that depleted psychological energy, our study assessed job demands more comprehensively. These findings suggest that the effect of job demands on work engagement depends on the balance between challenging and hindering elements and on the availability of organizational resources that buffer strain.

Additionally, positive nursing organizational culture did not show a significant association with burnout in this study. A recent systematic review on organizational culture and work-related stress among nurses [40] found that a positive organizational culture tends to reduce nurses’ stress and burnout levels. However, the review also noted that the evidence is not entirely consistent across studies. Such inconsistencies were attributed to differences in national healthcare systems, hospital type, and leadership styles. Likewise, in tertiary hospital settings characterized by high workload intensity and complex organizational structures, the protective effects of a positive culture may be attenuated.

The role of digital literacy and nursing informatics competency

While both digital literacy and nursing informatics competency were significantly associated with work engagement in our univariable linear regression analyses, these relationships became nonsignificant in multivariable regression models, after controlling for other variables. Correlation analyses also revealed that both digital literacy and nursing informatics competency were positively related to work engagement and positive nursing organizational culture, suggesting that higher digital competence tends to coexist with greater organizational support and engagement potential. Given these findings, we conducted a mediation analysis to determine whether the influence of these competencies on work engagement operated indirectly through positive nursing organizational culture. Digital literacy demonstrated a significant indirect effect on work engagement through positive nursing organizational culture, whereas its direct effect was not significant. Likewise, nursing informatics competency showed a smaller but significant indirect effect via positive nursing organizational culture. These findings suggest that both competencies function as personal resources that interact with organizational resources to enhance engagement. This nuanced interpretation avoids overstating the indirect relationship across the three constructs and instead emphasizes that digital literacy and nursing informatics competency foster engagement primarily when embedded within a positive organizational context. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the proposed mediation pathways in this study conceptually align with the JD-R framework, wherein personal resources, represented by digital literacy and nursing informatics competency, are hypothesized to influence work engagement indirectly through the job resource of a positive nursing organizational culture.

These findings are consistent with the JD-R framework, which theorizes that personal resources interact with job resources and influence work engagement [11]. Recent theoretical advancements in the JD-R model highlight a Person × Situation approach, which emphasizes the dynamic interaction between individual dispositions and daily work environments in shaping work engagement and burnout. Personal resources, such as self-efficacy, resilience, and collective efficacy, not only amplify the benefits of job resources, but also operate in a reciprocal cycle, whereby individuals with higher personal resources tend to acquire more job resources; this, in turn, further strengthens their personal capacities. This perspective positions personal resources as central, rather than peripheral, in the motivational pathway of the JD-R model [41, 42]. This theoretical integration provides the rationale for the mediation model and demonstrates how the motivational process of the JD-R model was operationalized in the present study. Our findings further extend this framework by suggesting that digital literacy and nursing informatics competency function as enabling personal resources whose work engagement related benefits are realized primarily within supportive organizational environments. Further research is to gain a deeper understanding of how personal and organizational resources interact in a reciprocal manner to promote favorable outcomes for nurses and healthcare organizations.

Implications for nursing management and research

The current study’s findings provide implications for nursing management and policy. In the rapidly evolving digital healthcare environment, nurses’ digital literacy and nursing informatics competency are evident job resources for enhancing work engagement; however, their effectiveness is contingent upon a positive nursing organizational culture. A positive nursing organizational culture refers to behavior patterns, norms, and expectations that exert a constructive influence on the thoughts and actions of nursing organization members [43]. This positive culture has been reported to be associated with lower levels of work-related stress [44] and to maximize members’ potential, enhance the efficiency of work and human resource management, and contribute to organizational performance and sustainable growth of the nursing organization [29, 45]. Therefore, efforts to improve nursing organizational culture—that is, the work environment of nurses—are essential, including encouraging open communication, promoting teamwork and collaboration, providing opportunities that support nurses’ personal development and growth, and ensuring appropriate staffing to reduce overwork [46]. Within such a positive nursing organizational culture, providing educational programs that enhance nurses’ digital literacy and nursing informatics competency may further increase their work engagement. By aligning individual competencies with organizational resources, healthcare institutions can enhance nurse engagement, reduce burnout, and ultimately improve both staff retention and patient care outcomes.

Study limitations

Despite its clear contributions, certain limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design prevents conclusions about temporal or causal relationships. Although the mediation models provide insights into potential pathways, longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to confirm directionality. Second, data were based on self-reported questionnaires, which may be affected by recall bias, common method bias, or social desirability. Third, the study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital, limiting generalizability to other institutions or healthcare systems where organizational culture and work demands may differ. Finally, although the sample size was adequate to detect mediation effects of moderate size [47], smaller effects may have gone undetected. Future research with larger and more diverse samples across multiple institutions is warranted to improve generalizability and statistical power.

Conclusion

This study investigated the influence of digital literacy and nursing informatics competency, conceptualized as job resources within the JD-R model, on nurses’ work engagement and burnout. The findings confirmed the dual pathways of the JD-R framework: positive nursing organizational culture was strongly associated with greater work engagement, whereas work demands contributed to increased burnout. This study emphasizes the interdependence between personal and organizational factors in shaping nurses’ work engagement. While digital literacy and nursing informatics competency provide valuable personal resources, their impact is maximized only within supportive organizational environments. By highlighting these dynamics, this study extends the application of the JD-R model to nursing contexts and emphasizes the importance of integrated strategies that simultaneously foster individual digital literacy or nursing informatics competency and cultivate positive organizational culture where such competencies can be effectively utilized.
