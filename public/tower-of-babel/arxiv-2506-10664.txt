Logarithmic Smoothing for Adaptive PAC-Bayesian Off-Policy Learning
Maxime HaddoucheInria Paris, DI-ENS,PSL, Paris, France maxime.haddouche@inria.fr, Otmane SakhiCriteo AI LabParis, France o.sakhi@criteo.com

Abstract
Off-policy learning serves as the primary framework for learning optimal policies from logged interactions collected under a static behavior policy. In this work, we investigate the more practical and flexible setting of adaptive off-policy learning , where policies are iteratively refined and re-deployed to collect higher-quality data. Building on the success of PAC-Bayesian learning with Logarithmic Smoothing ( LS ) in static settings, we extend this framework to the adaptive scenario using tools from online PAC-Bayesian theory. Furthermore, we demonstrate that a principled adjustment to the LS estimator naturally accommodates multiple rounds of deployment and yields faster convergence rates under mild conditions. Our method matches the performance of leading offline approaches in static settings, and significantly outperforms them when intermediate policy deployments are allowed. Empirical evaluations across diverse scenarios highlight both the advantages of adaptive data collection and the strength of the PAC-Bayesian formulation.

Introduction

Off-policy learning has become a ubiquitous task for practitioners in a wide range of applications such as recommender systems
(

)
, ad placement
(

These interactions, typically captured as tuples of contextual features, action played, and reward received, hold valuable insight into the underlying dynamics of the environment. Each tuple represents a single round of interaction, where the agent observes a context (including relevant features), takes an action according to its current, behavior policy, and receives a reward that depends on both the observed context and the taken action.

The off-policy learning literature focused on developing efficient approaches to learn from these logged interactions

;
swaminathan2015batch
;
london2019bayesian
;
sakhi2023pac
. At the core of these methods the development of tailored off-policy estimators with improved concentration properties

;
aouali23a
;

;
sakhi2024logarithmic
)
, combined with tight generalization bounds allowing efficient learning
(
swaminathan2015batch
;
sakhi2023pac
;
sakhi2024logarithmic
)
. The methodologies employed derive from the pessimism principle
jin2021pessimism
, which has been demonstrated to be optimal for learning in these scenarios. Particularly, analyzing this learning problem from the PAC-Bayesian learning theory lens
london2019bayesian
proved efficient, improving the seminal work of
(
swaminathan2015batch
)
. This direction was further explored by
sakhi2023pac
;
aouali23a
;
pac_bayes_unified
;
gabbianelli2023importance
;

, reducing assumptions and improving the pessimistic principle,
i.e.
controlling the worst-case scenario, with latest developments
(

)
resulting in state of the art pessimistic approaches.

The success of these pessimistic learning approaches fundamentally depend on the quality of the logged data
(
jin2021pessimism
;
gabbianelli2023importance
;

)
, thus the quality of the behavior policy and its ability to play meaningful actions. In particular, it was demonstrated that off-policy learning approaches converge to the optimal policy with enough samples, with faster convergence when the behavior policy explores well the
optimal actions

(
sakhi2024logarithmic
)
. To achieve this convergence, a naive strategy consists in collecting more data with the same logging system. However, this approach is limited as it does not consider the benefits of learning from previous interactions. Then, a better

strategy would use the already collected data to refine the design of the behavior policy, adapting better to the underlying dynamics of the problem, and collecting interaction data of higher quality. More precisely, the adaptive strategy performs a sequence of data collection experiments by determining at each round a new behavior policy using interaction data collected during previous experiments. The obtained policy is then deployed for the next round to collect better quality samples.

This adaptive (or sequential) off-policy learning approach recently sparked interest
(

;
scrm
)
. However, existing algorithms are not in line with common practices, either requiring strong assumptions such as uniform coverage

;

Intuitively, an adaptive PAC-Bayesian strategy would yield in better behavior policies at each deployment than

Framework

Adaptive Contextual bandits.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the context space, which is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mathcal{A}=[K]
be a finite action set. An agent’s actions are guided by a
stochastic
policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
within a policy space
\Pi
. Given a context
x\in\mathcal{X},\pi(\cdot\mid x)
is a probability distribution over the action set
\mathcal{A};\pi(a\mid x)
is the probability that the agent selects action
a
in context
x
. In a general adaptive framework, we consider that the agent interacts sequentially with a data stream: at time
k\geq 0
, the agent deploys a new behavior policy
\pi_{k}
and a new batch of contexts
\{x_{j}\mid j\in I_{k}\}
observed by the agent is revealed, where
|I_{k}|=n_{k}
and all
(I_{k})_{k\geq 0}
are disjoint. These contexts are drawn
i.i.d.
from a
fixed
, unknown distribution
\nu
. For each
j\in I_{k}
, the agent selects for context
x_{j}
an action
a_{j}\sim\pi_{k}\left(\cdot\mid x_{j}\right)
, where
\pi_{k}
is the current
behavior
policy of the agent at time
k
, and receives a stochastic cost
c_{j}\in[-1,0]
that depends on the observed context
x_{j}
and the taken action
a_{j}
. This cost
c_{j}
is sampled from an unknown cost distribution
p\left(\cdot|x_{j},a_{j}\right)
and we denote by
c(a,x)
its expectation under
p\left(\cdot|x_{j},a_{j}\right)
. After
|I_{k}|=n_{k}
interactions, the agent adaptively updates its behavior to
\pi_{k+1}

w.r.t.
previous behavior policies
(\pi_{j})_{j=0\cdots k}
and is allowed to use all available data
\{x_{i},a_{i},c_{i}\}_{i=1\cdots N_{k}}
, where
N_{k}:=\sum_{i=0}^{k}n_{i}
. To ease notations, we denote by
P(\pi):=\nu\times\pi\times p
the distribution of
(x,a,c)
tuples generated by policy
\pi
.
The performance of any policy
\pi
is assessed by its
risk
, which is defined as the expectation under the context distribution
\nu
, and the played actions
a
by
\pi
of the expected costs
c(x,a)
, as in:

R(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,a,c)\sim P(\pi)}[c]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu,a\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[c(x,a)]\,.

In this work, our goal is to come up with adaptive learning algorithms that guarantee the convergence to the optimal policy
\pi_{\star}
, defined as the policy
\pi
minimizing the
risk
as:

\pi_{\star}=\argmin_{\pi\in\Pi}R(\pi)\,.

PAC-Bayes learning.
To learn the optimal policy
\pi_{\star}
, we employ the pessimism principle
(
jin2021pessimism
)
, shown to be optimal for off-policy learning
jin2021pessimism
. Specifically, we aim at minimizing an empirical, tight upper bound on the risk
R(\pi)
over policies
\pi\in\Pi
. To do so, we leverage PAC-Bayes learning
(
alquier2024user
)
which controls the generalization guarantee of a
posterior
distribution
Q
over a predictor space
\Theta
(namely
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
). To make this consistent with controlling the risk of a policy
\pi
, we exploit a specific parametrization of policies
\pi\in\Pi
. For any
\theta\in\Theta
, we define the deterministic policy:
\forall x,d_{\theta}(x):=\mathds{1}\{a=f_{\theta}(x)\}
, where
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, a parametrized predictor. Then, each distribution
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
defines a policy
\pi_{Q}
by setting the probability of action
a
given context
x
as the probability that a random predictor
f_{\theta}\sim Q
maps
x
to action
a
, that is:

\displaystyle\forall(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\,,\quad\pi_{Q}(a|x)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q}\left[\mathds{1}\{a=f_{\theta}(x)\}\right]\,.

This expression is not restrictive as any policy can be represented in this form
(
sakhi2023pac
, Theorem 3.1)
. With this characterization, the space of policies considered
\Pi
can be
entirely described
by a predictor space
\Theta
and a set of distributions over it
\mathcal{C}(\Theta)\subset\mathcal{P}(\Theta)

sakhi2023pac
. With this duality, we can obtain with the PAC-Bayesian toolbox, by fixing a reference distribution
P
, and setting
\delta\in(0,1]
a bound of the following general form, holding with probability
1-\delta
, simultaneously for all
Q
:

\displaystyle\forall Q\in\mathcal{C}(\Theta)\,,R(\pi_{Q})\leq\hat{R}(\pi_{Q})+\mathcal{O}\left(\operatorname{KL}(Q||P)\right)\,,

upper bounding the true risk of
\pi_{Q}
by a a risk estimator
\hat{R}(\pi_{Q})
and
\operatorname{KL}(Q||P):=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log\left(\frac{dQ}{dP}\right)\right]
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
P
and
Q
. The tightness of the bound fundamentally depends on the concentration properties of the chosen estimator
\hat{R}

sakhi2024logarithmic
.

Adaptive algorithms.

We define the full (countable) dataset
S:=\cup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}S_{k}
, where
S_{k}:=\left\{(x_{j},a_{j},c_{j})\mid j\in I_{k}\right\}
is the set of logged data at time
k
. We also define
\mathcal{F}_{k}
a filtration adapted to
\left(\cup_{j=0}^{k}S_{j}\right)_{j\in 0\cdots k}
(
\mathcal{F}_{-1}
is the trivial
\sigma
-algebra). This framework generalizes the pure
static
case, on which is based
sakhi2024logarithmic
, as
S_{0}
summarizes the interactions of a single data batch with the starting policy
\pi_{0}
. We denote as
adaptive algorithm
, any algorithm
Alg
such that its output
\pi_{k+1}
at time
k
is
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable. In other words, if we denote all the collected interactions at time
k
as
S_{0:k}:=\cup_{\ell=0}^{k}S_{\ell}
, , we can express
\pi_{k+1}=\texttt{Alg}(S_{0:k})
. We assume that for each
k
,
(x_{j})_{j\in I_{k}}\sim\nu^{\otimes n_{k}}
is
i.i.d.
and that, conditionally to
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
,
S_{k}
is constituted of mutually independent random variables.

Regularized IPS.
Given the successive arrival of data batches
(S_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and for all
j\geq 0
,
\pi_{j+1}=\texttt{Alg}(S_{0:j})
is given by an adaptive algorithm, we can leverage the collected data at any time
k\geq 0
to define an Inverse Propensity Scoring (IPS) estimator

, for the risk of any policy
\pi
:
\hat{R}_{0:k}^{\textsc{IPS}}(\pi)=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\frac{\pi(a_{i}|x_{i})}{\pi_{j}(a_{i}|x_{i})}c_{i}\,,

which recovers the static case when
k=0
. Even with the potential dependencies between the behavior policies
\pi_{j}
, this estimator is unbiased under the common support assumption
mcbook
. However, as in the static case, this estimator has poor concentration properties
metelli2021subgaussian
and regularized variants, trading some bias for lower variance are preferred
bottou2013counterfactual
;
optimistic_shrinkage
;
aouali23a
;
sakhi2024logarithmic
. The majority of these regularized estimators can be defined by specific choices of a regularizer
h:[0,1]^{2}\times[-1,0]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{-}
in the following general expression:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{0:k}^{h}(\pi)=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{i\in I_{j}}h(\pi(a_{i}|x_{i}),\pi_{j}(a_{i}|x_{i}),c_{i})\,,

with
h
respecting
\forall(p,q,c)\in[0,1]^{2}\times[-1,0]\,,\quad pc/q\leq h(p,q,c)\leq 0
.
h(p,q,c)=pc/q
recovers the IPS estimator while other choices recover known, well behaved estimators such as clipped IPS
bottou2013counterfactual
, harmonic
metelli2021subgaussian
and Implicit-Exploration
gabbianelli2023importance
to name a few. When
k=0
, we denote
\hat{R}_{0:0}^{h}
as
\hat{R}_{0}^{h}
.

Warm-up on Pessimistic Logarithmic Smoothing

3.1
Batch learning algorithms with theoretical guarantees

Pessimistic Logarithmic Smoothing recently appeared as a fruitful theoretical approach to obtain both concrete learning algorithms and generalization guarantees guarantees for contextual bandits. Those coupled benefits naturally stems from the use of PAC-Bayesian theory
alquier2024user
which provide empirical generalization bounds whom the optimization yield original learning algorithms with theoretical guarantees. In
sakhi2024logarithmic
, pessimistic PAC-Bayes bounds have been developed for contextual bandits, providing a novel learning algorithm with optimality guarantee. We first recall the definition of Logarithmic smoothing as well as its associated regularized IPS estimator. For any
\lambda>0,k\geq 0
,

h_{\mathrm{LS}}^{\lambda\mathrm{-Lin}}(p,q,c):=-\frac{p}{\lambda}\log\left(1-\frac{\lambda c}{q}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{R}_{0:k}^{\lambda\mathrm{-LS}}:=\hat{R}_{0:k}^{h_{\mathrm{LS}}^{\lambda\mathrm{-Lin}}}.

Using these notions,
sakhi2024logarithmic
obtained results for static off-policy learning
i.e.
data are gathered in a single batch
S_{0}
of size
n_{0}
. We recall them below.

Proposition 3.1

(Propositions 10 and 11 of
sakhi2024logarithmic
)
.

Given a prior
P\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta),\delta\in(0,1]
and
\lambda>0
, the following holds with probability at least
1-\delta
over
S_{0}
:

\forall Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta),\quad R\left(\pi_{Q}\right)\leq\hat{R}_{0}^{\lambda-\mathrm{LS}}\left(\pi_{Q}\right)+\frac{\operatorname{KL}(Q\|P)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{\lambda n_{0}}.

Furthermore, for any
\mathcal{C}(\Theta)\subset\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, denoting by
Q_{n_{0}}\in\operatorname{argmin}_{Q\in\mathcal{C}(\Theta)}\hat{R}_{0}^{\lambda-\mathrm{LS}}\left(\pi_{Q}\right)+\frac{\operatorname{KL}(Q\|P)}{\lambda n_{0}}
and
Q^{\star}\in\operatorname{argmin}_{Q\in\mathcal{C}(\Theta)}R(\pi_{Q})
, we have:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq R(\pi_{Q_{n_{0}}})-R(\pi_{Q^{\star}})\leq\lambda\mathcal{S}(\pi_{Q^{\star}})+\frac{2\operatorname{KL}(Q^{\star}\|P)+\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{\lambda n_{0}},

where
\mathcal{S}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{(x,a,c)\sim P(\pi_{0})}\left[\frac{\pi(a\mid x)c^{2}}{\pi_{0}^{2}(a\mid x)}\right]
is a pseudo-variance term.

Those results provide two distinct hindsights:
(i)
, the learning procedure yielding
Q_{n_{0}}
(and exploited in practice in
sakhi2024logarithmic
) minimises a generalisation bound and
(ii)
, once
\mathcal{S}(\pi_{Q^{\star}})
finite, taking
\lambda=1/\sqrt{n_{0}}
gives a convergence rate of
1/\sqrt{n_{0}}
to the optimal value of the risk. However,
Proposition

3.1
and its associated learning algorithm suffers from theoretical and practical limitations.

1.

A batch procedure cannot handle a progressive arrival of data
(S_{k})_{k\geq 0}
. In particular,
Proposition

3.1
does not allow to re-train our policy
w.r.t
all past data when a new data batch arrives.

2.

The pseudo-variance term of

3.1
does not go to zero, even in the oracle case where

, whatever the optimal policy
\pi_{Q^{\star}}
is. This exhibits a fundamental limitation of this batch procedure as the slow convergence rate of

is inevitable.

We provide below alternatives for those two fundamental issues. To answer 1. we show in
Section

3.2
that leveraging online PAC-Bayes and supermartingale techniques

;
haddouche2023pac
it is possible to obtain an adaptive version of

3.1
, allowing both the arrival of new data on-the-fly and re-using former ones. Then, re-using online PAC-Bayes techniques, we answer 2. in
Section

by providing a novel adaptive learning algorithm with accelerated convergence rate.

Towards a first adaptive pessimistic learning algorithm

In this part, we assume, for pedagogical concerns, that all datasets

.

4
Adaptive Logarithmic Smoothing with accelerated convergence.

While
Theorem

3.2
provide a natural adaptive extension of
sakhi2024logarithmic
,
Algorithm

still suffers (

3.3
) from a slow convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{km})
due to the nature of the pseudo-variance term. Is it then possible to avoid such a term? Yes, by adjusting

introduced in

h^{\text{adj}}_{\lambda}(p,q,c):=-\frac{p}{\lambda}\log\left(1-\frac{\lambda c}{q(1+\lambda c)}\right)
and denote it as the

6
Conclusion

In this work, we extended the Logarithmic Smoothing learning principle to the adaptive setting. We derived two novel adaptive algorithms for off-policy learning and established convergence guarantees to their respective optima. One of the algorithms enjoys accelerated convergence under mild assumptions and demonstrated improved empirical performance. These theoretical and practical benefits highlight the potential of combining online PAC-Bayes techniques with adaptive contextual bandits. Importantly, online PAC-Bayes methods do not rely on the
i.i.d.
assumption, opening promising directions for future research. In particular, relaxing the assumptions of a fixed context distribution and
i.i.d.
contexts would allow us to address distribution shift—where the context or cost distribution evolves during training. This setting better reflects real-world applications, where user behavior and responses naturally change over time.

Appendix A
Limitations

This work develops theoretically grounded and practical learning approaches for the adaptive contextual bandit setting, where the decision-maker can dynamically adjust behavior policies to collect higher-quality data. Our theoretical analysis generalizes the static (batch) setting and highlights the advantages of multiple updates and deployments. While this represents a clear improvement over the batch setting, the proposed algorithm is still reliant on the initial behavior policy and may not converge to the optimal policy if the starting policy fails to explore optimal actions. Another limitation arises from the offline contextual bandit framework itself, which assumes i.i.d. observations. Although this assumption may be valid in simple scenarios, it becomes inadequate in real-world applications where user interests evolve over time. Extending our results to settings with distribution shifts, for example, could be achieved using similar proof techniques and would present an important avenue for future research.

Appendix B
Broader impact statement

We contribute to the development of principled and practical pessimistic algorithms for the adaptive off-policy contextual bandit setting. These algorithms improve the robustness of decision-making by reducing unnecessary exploration, accelerating convergence to optimal policies, and prioritizing safety while mitigating uncertainty-related risks. By incorporating pessimistic strategies, our approach introduces a conservative bias that can lead to better outcomes in high-stakes environments where the cost of mistakes is significant. While our framework and algorithms have broad potential applicability, their social impact will ultimately depend on the specific domains in which they are deployed.

Off-Policy Learning.

Off-Policy Learning in the contextual bandit setting, or counterfactual risk minimization
[
47
]
is a widely adopted framework for learning from past interactions. In most real world applications, large datasets summarizing past interactions are often available, allowing agents to improve their policies offline. In this setting, the goal is to find the optimal policy using previously collected interactions. Two primary approaches exist for learning from this data. The direct method
[
43

This approach is well-understood, and the quality of the learned policy depends entirely on our ability to accurately model the reward. The other approach directly learns a policy from the data by minimizing an importance weighting estimator

6

The research community has predominantly focused on this second approach, as it presents significant theoretical challenges and offers greater generality by not relying on any reward modeling. At the core of directly learning a policy is the development of tailored off-policy estimators with improved concentration properties
[

3

35

[

41
,
42

[

[

Adaptive Off-Policy Learning.

Adaptive policy learning builds upon off-policy learning by enabling the decision-maker to refine the initial behavior policy and gather higher quality interactions with each new deployment. This approach is prevalent in practice as decision systems are continuously updated; however, its theoretical understanding remains limited and underexplored. Despite its practical significance, few works have addressed this question.
[
49
]
and subsequently
[
5
]
investigated learning from adaptively collected data in a purely online setting, where policies are updated after each step. In contrast to these online methods, our work considers updates occurring after the arrival of batches of arbitrary sizes. This better models real-world scenarios and provides a framework that interpolates between the fully static and fully online regimes. Our proposed algorithms achieve the convergence rate of the batch setting and accelerate it once we can refine our behavior policy. Furthermore, the analysis in
[
49
,
5

[

employs a doubly robust estimator and provides regret guarantees but assumes uniform coverage to control variance. Under the same uniform coverage assumption,
[
5

introduces a novel maximal inequality and subsequently derives fast rate regret guarantees under a margin condition similar to ours. However, their results are limited to the online setting and also require uniform coverage, which can decay slowly over time. Recently,
[
48
]
extended
Counterfactual Risk Minimization
to the adaptive setting, allowing for intermediate policy deployments to facilitate the learning of better policies. Their algorithm achieves accelerated convergence rates under a Hölderian assumption but necessitates bounded importance weights, which, in the discrete action space case, is equivalent to the uniform coverage assumption. Additionally, their proposed algorithm only functions with exponentially increasing batch sizes, which is unrealistic, and only utilizes the last batch of interactions instead of the entire dataset. These limitations primarily stem from the theoretical tools employed, rendering the algorithm impractical. We analyze this problem from a PAC-Bayesian perspective and present the first algorithms that interpolate between full batch and full online regimes, matching the rates of both and operating under the coverage of the optimal actions alone.

E.3
Proof of Lemma
4.3

Proof.

We are looking for
\gamma_{k}>0
such that:

\displaystyle\frac{L(\pi_{\star},\pi_{k})}{R(\pi_{k})-R(\pi_{\star})}\leq\gamma_{k}.

Let us start rewriting the suboptimality
R(\pi_{k})-R(\pi_{\star})
. We have:

\displaystyle R(\pi_{k})-R(\pi_{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[c(a,x)\right]-c(a_{\star}(x),x)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[c(a,x)-c(a_{\star}(x),x)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{a,x}\right].

On the other hand, we have by definition of
L(\pi_{\star},\pi_{k})
:

\displaystyle L(\pi_{\star},\pi_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a)}\left[c^{2}\right]\right]+\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{k}(a^{\star}(x)|x)}-2\right)\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a_{\star}(x))}\left[c^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a)}\left[c^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a_{\star}(x))}\left[c^{2}\right]\right]+\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{k}(a^{\star}(x)|x)}-1\right)\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a_{\star}(x))}\left[c^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\left[\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a)}\left[c^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a_{\star}(x))}\left[c^{2}\right]\right]+\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{k}(a^{\star}(x)|x)}-1\right)\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a_{\star}(x))}\left[c^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\left[\sigma^{2}(a,x)-\sigma^{2}(a_{\star}(x),x)+c^{2}(a,x)-c^{2}(a_{\star}(x),x)\right]\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{k}(a^{\star}(x)|x)}-1\right)\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a_{\star}(x))}\left[c^{2}\right]\right].

As the costs are in
[0,1]
, we can bound the variance term by:

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\left[\sigma^{2}(a,x)-\sigma^{2}(a_{\star}(x),x)\right]\right]\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\right]=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right].

We then use the definition of
\Delta_{x,a}
to develop
c(a,x)^{2}
and obtain:

\displaystyle c(a,x)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\Delta_{a,x}+c(a_{\star}(x),x)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{a,x}^{2}+2c(a_{\star}(x),x)\Delta_{x,a}+c(a_{\star}(x),x)^{2}\leq\Delta_{a,x}^{2}+c(a_{\star}(x),x)^{2}.

Plugging these two results into our previous equation, we get:

\displaystyle L(\pi_{\star},\pi_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{x,a}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{k}(a^{\star}(x)|x)}-1\right)\mathbb{E}_{p(\cdot|x,a_{\star}(x))}\left[c^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{x,a}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{k}(a^{\star}(x)|x)}-1\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{x,a}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{C^{\star}_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a^{\star}(x)|x)\right].

We then proceed at upper bound the quantity:

\displaystyle\frac{L(\pi_{\star},\pi_{k})}{R(\pi_{k})-R(\pi_{\star})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{x,a}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{C^{\star}_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a^{\star}(x)|x)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{a,x}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{C_{k}^{\star}}\right)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{a,x}\right]},

because
\Delta_{x,a}^{2}\leq\Delta_{x,a}
as
\Delta_{x,a}\leq 1
.

Now, we proceed to define:

\bar{\Delta}_{k}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{a,x}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right]}.

\bar{\Delta}_{k}
represents the expected sub-optimality incurred when policy
\pi_{k}
selects a non-optimal action. A large
\bar{\Delta}_{k}
means that the environment penalizes us more for not choosing an optimal action, while
\bar{\Delta}_{k}\approx 0
means that the environment has multiple near-optimal actions. We can characterize
\gamma_{k}
by this quantity, or loosen our bound and have a more interpretable condition. We choose the second approach. Let us suppose that there exists a
\Delta_{u}>0
for a
u\in[0,1)
such that:

\displaystyle P_{x}\left(\min_{a\neq a^{\star}(x)}\Delta_{x,a}\geq\Delta_{u}\right)=1-u.

Let us focus on the numerator of
\bar{\Delta}_{k}
. We have:

\displaystyle P\left(\Delta_{x,a}\geq\Delta_{u}|a\neq a^{\star}(x)\right)\geq P_{x}\left(\min_{a\neq a^{\star}(x)}\Delta_{x,a}\geq\Delta_{u}\right)=1-u.

Indeed, the event
E_{1}=\{\Delta_{x,a}\geq\Delta_{u}|a\neq a^{\star}(x)\}
represents choosing a random action from the suboptimal set, and finding that
\Delta_{x,a}\geq\Delta_{u}
, while the event
E_{0}=\{\min_{a\neq a^{\star}(x)}\Delta_{x,a}\geq\Delta_{u}\}
means that all non-optimal actions have a sub-optimality bigger than
\Delta_{u}
. We have
E_{0}\subset E_{1}
, and thus:

\displaystyle P\left(\Delta_{x,a}\geq\Delta_{u}|a\neq a^{\star}(x)\right)\geq 1-u.

Additionally, we have by the definition of conditional expectations:

\displaystyle P\left(\Delta_{x,a}\geq\Delta_{u}|a\neq a^{\star}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\mathds{1}\left[\Delta_{a,x}>\Delta_{u}\right]\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\mathds{1}\left[\Delta_{a,x}>\Delta_{u}\right]\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right]}\geq 1-u.

Now, we write:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{a,x}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{a,x}\mathds{1}\left[\Delta_{a,x}>\Delta_{u}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Delta_{u}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\mathds{1}\left[\Delta_{a,x}>\Delta_{u}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Delta_{u}(1-u)\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right].

Finally, we have:

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}_{k}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{a\neq a_{\star}(x)}\pi_{k}(a|x)\Delta_{a,x}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[1-\pi_{k}(a_{\star}(x)|x)\right]}\geq\Delta_{u}(1-u).

and we finally obtain our result:

\displaystyle\frac{L(\pi_{\star},\pi_{k})}{R(\pi_{k})-R(\pi_{\star})}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{C_{k}^{\star}}\right)\frac{1}{\Delta_{u}(1-u)}=\gamma_{k}

This ends the proof.
∎

F.1
Detailed Setup

General Setup.

Our experimental framework follows the standard multiclass-to-bandit conversion widely used in prior work
[
15
,
47
]
. Each multiclass dataset consists of feature-label pairs, which we transform into contextual bandit problems by treating features as contexts and labels as actions. The reward
r
for taking action
a
for context
x
is modeled as Bernoulli with probability
p_{x}=\epsilon+\mathds{1}\left[a=a^{\star}(x)\right](1-2\epsilon)
, where
a^{\star}(x)
is the true label of features
x
, and
\epsilon
is a noise parameter, that we set to
\epsilon=0.2
in all our experiments. This setup ensures an average reward of
1-\epsilon
for the optimal action
a^{\star}(x)
and
\epsilon
for all others. In our experiments, we use parametrized Linear Gaussian Policies
[
41
,
3
]
, these policies interact with the contextual bandit environment to construct a logged bandit feedback dataset in the form
\{x_{i},a_{i},c_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
, where
c_{i}=-r_{i}
is the associated cost.

In this procedure, we need three splits:
D_{l}
(of size
n_{l}
) to train the logging policy
\pi_{0}
, another split
D_{c}
(of size
n_{c}
) to generate the logging feedback with
\pi_{0}
, and finally a test split
D_{test}
(of size
n_{test}
) to compute the true risk
R(\pi)
of any policy
\pi
. In our experiments, we split the training split
D_{train}
(of size
N
) of the four datasets considered into
D_{l}
(
n_{l}=0.05N
) and
D_{c}
(
n_{c}=0.95N
) and use their test split
D_{test}
. The detailed statistics of the different splits can be found in Table
2
. Recall that
K
is the number of actions and
p
the number of features.

All datasets are used without processing aside from
CIFAR100
that we embed using a pretrained Resnet, obtaining a low dimensional vector of
100
for each image.
