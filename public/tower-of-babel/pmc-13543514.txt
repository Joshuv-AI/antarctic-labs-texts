Integrative transcriptomic and experimental analysis identifies GREM1 as a pro-metastatic mediator in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Zhen-Qing Li, Hui-Rong Huang, Ke-Rong Zhai, Bin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe TGF-β signaling pathway can effectively activate epithelial-mesenchymal transition and glycolysis, thereby promoting tumor metastasis and drug resistance. Bioinformatics analyses have indicated that GREM1 may facilitate tumor progression and metastasis. However, the mechanism by which GREM1 promotes metastasis in lung adenocarcinoma remains largely unknown.MethodsWe applied pathway-focused integrative clustering to characterize molecular subgroups demonstrating differential activation of EMT, glycolysis and TGF-β pathways, subsequently developing different prognosis subtypes. Comprehensive functional investigations encompassing wound healing assays, migration, invasion, apoptosis evaluations and metastasis models were performed to elucidate GREM1’s role in cancer progression. Mechanistic explorations through WB, co-IP techniques and virtual knockout revealed critical downstream effectors and signaling networks modulated by GREM1 activity.ResultsIntegrative clustering identified two LUAD subtypes with distinct oncogenic pathways activity and different survival outcomes. GREM1 was prioritized as a CS2-associated core gene and was associated with poor prognosis. Functional assays showed that GREM1 enhanced LUAD cell migration, invasion, glucose uptake, and lung metastatic colonization, while suppressing apoptosis. Mechanistically, GREM1 interacts with BMP2 and induces epithelial–mesenchymal transition and glycolysis via the TGF-β signaling pathway, thereby promoting LUAD progression and metastasis.ConclusionsGREM1 emerges as both a metastasis driver and therapeutic target, offering opportunities for biomarker-guided precision oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08793-9.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, GREM1, EMT, TCGA, GEO

Introduction

Globally, pulmonary carcinoma persists as the primary contributor to cancer-associated deaths, constituting a major public health burden given high incidence and unfavorable clinical outcomes [1]. Although remarkable progress has been achieved in diagnostic techniques and therapeutic strategies, the underlying molecular pathways and causative factors responsible for the development of pulmonary malignancies remain incompletely elucidated. Chronic cigarette use is a well-recognized contributor to disease pathogenesis [2], with additional risk factors including atmospheric contaminants, hereditary predisposition, prolonged exposure to occupational hazards or environmental toxins, and persistent metabolic imbalance syndromes [3].

Histologically, pulmonary malignancies are divided into two primary groups: small cell carcinoma (SCLC) and non-small cell carcinoma (NSCLC), with the latter representing roughly 85% of diagnosed cases [4, 5]. The NSCLC category includes distinct pathological variants, most notably squamous cell carcinoma, pulmonary adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and undifferentiated large cell carcinoma. Conventional clinical indicators such as TNM staging and histological grading remain essential for guiding treatment strategies and estimating patient prognosis. However, even among patients with similar stages, the heterogeneity of clinical outcomes highlights the limitations of the current classification system. This underscores the urgent need to identify new molecular biomarkers for more accurate prognostic assessment. Molecular subtyping may provide a more refined approach for stratifying patients and developing personalized treatment strategies. In addition, elucidating the molecular characteristics behind differential prognosis can enhance our understanding of tumor progression and metastatic potential.

Molecular subtyping has been widely used to dissect the heterogeneity of NSCLC. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) research network has identified gene expression–based subtypes in both lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and LUAD [6, 7]. More recently, a multi-platform classification of NSCLC across 1,023 patients revealed nine distinct molecular categories [8]. Other classification schemes based on specific gene sets have also been proposed [9, 10]. Notably, early-stage NSCLC often exhibits unique molecular characteristics. However, prognostic information from these classifications—particularly when integrated with gene expression data—remains underutilized, limiting their predictive power. Inconsistencies in prognostic significance across studies may reflect small sample sizes, regional differences, and methodological variability.

Despite therapeutic advances, the five-year survival rate for LUAD remains only 28%. Disease stage remains the most powerful predictor of survival in NSCLC, with five-year relative survival rates declining sharply from 65% in localized disease to only 9% in the presence of distant metastases. Metastatic spread is closely linked to the acquisition of migratory and invasive properties by tumor cells, processes often mediated by epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [11, 12]. This cellular reprogramming involves suppression of epithelial characteristics (notably E-cadherin reduction) and adoption of mesenchymal markers like N-cadherin/vimentin, coupled with junctional complex disassembly. Experimental models consistently show EMT activation enhances malignant cell motility, tissue penetration, and metastatic colonization [13, 14]. TGF-β signaling emerges as a pivotal regulator of this process, coordinating the activation of master transcriptional regulators while silencing epithelial differentiation markers [15–20]. Conversely, inhibition of EMT through genetic or pharmacological means can markedly reduce metastatic potential [21, 22].

Gremlin-1 (GREM1) is a member of the differentially selected gene (DAN) family in neuroblastoma and is a secreted glycoprotein and also a classic antagonist of the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling pathway [23, 24]. Studies have shown that exosomes derived from cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) can induce macrophages to polarize towards an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype by carrying the GREM1 protein, thereby promoting the formation of the metastatic microenvironment of non-small cell lung cancer [25]. Additionally, circ_001621 can upregulate GREM1 expression by sponging miR-199a-3p, promoting the proliferation and invasion of NSCLC cells [26]. He-Chan Pian suppresses non-small cell lung cancer metastasis via miR-205-5p–mediated regulation of the GREM1/Rap1 axis [27]. BMPs belong to the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily and mainly regulates cell proliferation, apoptosis and differentiation through downstream SMAD proteins and p21 molecules, playing a key role in tumor development [28]. Among the BMP family members, GREM1 has a significantly higher affinity for BMP2 than for BMP4 and BMP7, and can preferentially bind to BMP2 to inhibit its signal transduction [29]. GREM1 plays a key role in regulating tumor cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis by specifically antagonizing BMP2-mediated signal transduction [30–33]. Overall, GREM1 plays an important role in the tumor metastasis process. However, how GREM1 participates in the malignant progression events of lung adenocarcinoma regulated by the BMP2/TGF-β still needs further exploration.

In this study, we first performed pathway-focused integrative clustering to identify LUAD subtypes characterized by differential EMT, glycolysis, and TGF-β pathway activity. We then prioritized GREM1 as a subtype-associated candidate gene and evaluated its prognostic association, functional role in malignant phenotypes, and potential interaction with BMP2/TGF-β signaling using clinical samples, in vitro and in vivo assays, rescue experiments, radiomics, and single-cell virtual perturbation analyses.

Materials and methods

Datasets and samples

The TCGA-LUAD dataset, including gene expression profiles and complete clinical annotations, was obtained from TCGA and used as the discovery cohort for molecular subtype construction, pathway characterization, survival analysis, and candidate gene prioritization. To evaluate the reproducibility of the identified subtypes, three independent LUAD datasets with available survival information were obtained from GEO, including GSE30219 [34], GSE72094 [35], and GSE31210 [36]. These GEO datasets were analyzed independently to assess subtype-specific pathway activity, subtype assignment consistency, and survival differences.

Gene set variation analysis

Hallmark gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database. After removing duplicated gene symbols, sample-level pathway activity scores were calculated using the GSVA package (v1.22.4) [37]. For subtype discovery, the hallmark epithelial–mesenchymal transition, glycolysis, and TGF-β signaling gene sets were used as the primary pathway features. GSVA scores for the full hallmark collection were also used for downstream pathway characterization of the identified subtypes.

Integrative clustering for subtype discovery

Gene sets for epithelia-mesenchymal transition (EMT), glycolysis and TGF-β signaling were obtained from the HALLMARK collection of the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). For each dataset, we extracted the expression values of the genes belonging to each pathway, generating three separate mRNA expression matrices (EMT, glycolysis and TGF-β). These three matrices were then used as distinct omic layers for multi-omics integrative clustering via the MOVICS framework [38], which incorporates 10 algorithms: iClusterBayes [39–41], moCluster [42], CIMLR [43], IntNMF [44], ConsensusClustering [45], COCA [46], NEMO, PINSPlus [47], SNF [48], and LRA. Cluster stability was assessed using the clustering prediction index (CPI) and Gap statistical analysis to determine the optimal number of subgroups [44]. All datasets were processed independently with identical parameters to ensure cross‑dataset reproducibility. This consensus‑based approach identified two molecular subtypes with distinct activation patterns of the EMT, glycolysis, and TG-β pathways.

Subtype validation using nearest template prediction and partitioning around medoids

To validate the reproducibility of the identified LUAD subtypes in independent cohorts, nearest template prediction (NTP) and partitioning around medoids (PAM) were applied to the GSE30219, GSE72094, and GSE31210 validation cohorts. Subtype-specific templates were constructed from the TCGA-LUAD discovery cohort using the top 100 upregulated genes for each subtype. For each validation cohort, the expression matrix was restricted to genes shared with the subtype templates and then z-score transformed. NTP was used to assign each sample to the closest subtype template based on subtype-specific expression patterns. In parallel, PAM clustering was performed on the same z-score-transformed expression matrix with k = 2. Concordance between NTP- and PAM-derived subtype labels was evaluated using Fisher’s exact test or chi-square test, as appropriate. Survival differences between assigned subtypes were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis and log-rank testing.

Differential expression analysis

Using the limma package [49], we detected transcriptional variations between molecular subtypes through moderated t-tests with Benjamini–Hochberg multiple testing correction (FDR-adjusted p < 0.01). Significant differentially expressed genes were defined as those meeting both statistical significance thresholds: FDR-controlled p-value < 0.01 and |log2FC| >1.

Signature gene construction

To identify genes whose expression was associated with the two molecular subtypes, pearson correlation analysis was then performed between gene expression and subtype assignment, with CS2 encoded as 1 and CS1 encoded as 0. We retained genes with statistically significant correlations (P < 0.05) to ensure model robustness and prevent the resulting signatures from becoming overly broad. Among the significant genes, those with a positive correlation coefficient (R > 0) were classified as Signature A, and those with a negative correlation coefficient (R < 0) as Signature B. The complete gene lists are provided in Table S1.Genes with Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.5 were defined as core CS2-associated genes and were used for downstream protein–protein interaction analysis.

Construction of PPI

The STRING database serves as a predictive tool for protein–protein interactions (PPI) and was utilized in this research to establish PPI networks for module-associated genes. These networks were subsequently integrated into Cytoscape software (Version 3.8.2) for further analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue microarrays containing 90 paired tumor-normal specimens (HLugA180Su12) from LUAD patients were procured from Shanghai Outdo Biotech (Shanghai, China). All participants provided written informed consent, and ethical approval was granted by Shanghai Outdo Biotech’s Ethics Committee in compliance with Helsinki Declaration standards. GREM1 protein expression was evaluated using Abcam’s anti-GREM1 antibody (ab22138) with the UltraSensitive™ IHC Kit (Maxim, KIT-9720), following standardized protocols. Categorical variables comparing GREM1 expression patterns were assessed using chi-square testing, while continuous data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges and analyzed through Mann-Whitney U tests. Statistical significance threshold was established at p-values below 0.05.

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to evaluate survival differences according to GREM1 expression level and molecular subtype. To assess the statistical significance of the differences between groups, the log-rank test was utilized. The “survival” package was used to conduct statistical analyses, while the “survminer” package was used for visualization.

Cell culture

The human LUAD cell lines A549, H-1975 and PC-9 (ATCC® CCL-185™) were grown in F-12 K and RPMI-1640 culture media (Cytiva; previously HyClone), respectively, both enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). All cultures were kept in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Gene silencing

GREM1-targeting siRNAs (si-GREM1) and non-targeting control sequences (si-NC) were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Following a reverse transfection protocol using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), cells were processed 48 h post-transfection for functional analyses. Transfection complexes were added to cells 24 h after plating, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Transwell assay

Transwell inserts were pre-treated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences) and maintained at 4 °C for half an hour to achieve homogeneous coating. Cell suspensions (3 × 10⁴ cells/well) prepared in serum-free medium were loaded into the upper compartments, with the lower chambers supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemical attractant. The assembly underwent 48-hour culture under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, humidified atmosphere). Post-incubation, membrane-penetrating cells were fixed with methanol and stained with 1% crystal violet solution (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology). Cellular migration/invasion capacity was assessed through microscopic enumeration (Olympus CKX53; 200× magnification) of violet-positive cells within standardized observation areas.

Cell apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was measured using the Annexin V-APC/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit (Multi Sciences, AP105). After collecting 1 × 10⁵ to 1 × 10⁶ cells, they underwent rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before being re-suspended in 500 µl of 1× Binding Buffer. Subsequently, 5 µl of Annexin V-APC and 10 µl of 7-AAD staining solutions were introduced into the respective tubes and thoroughly mixed. The prepared specimens were maintained in darkness at ambient temperature for 5 min prior to analysis. Ultimately, cellular fluorescence signals were quantified using a Beckman Coulter flow cytometer.

Wound healing assay

To investigate the complex mechanisms underlying cellular movement, carefully prepared cell cultures were maintained in 6-well plates until reaching 90% density. Three standardized linear scratches were precisely created in each well employing a P-200 micropipette tip. The culture medium was then replaced with serum-depleted F12K solution. Sequential imaging at predetermined time points documented the gradual progression of scratch healing, demonstrating characteristic motility patterns among different cellular populations.

Western blotting

The protein was separated, transferred, and then incubated with 5% milk for 2 h. Antibodies including GREM1 (Santa Cruz, sc-515877), TGF-β1 (Santa Cruz, sc-130348), smad23 (Santa Cruz, sc-398844), p-smad2 (ABclonal, AP1007), p-smad3 (ABclonal, AP0727), smad1 (ABclonal, A19113), smad5 (ABclonal, A22749), smad8 (Santa Cruz, sc-518051), BMP2(ABclonal, A27101), snail(ABclonal, A11794), E-cadherin (Proteintech, Cat No. 20874-1-AP), N-cadherin (proteintech, Cat No. 22018-1-AP), Vimentin (Santa Cruz, sc-373717), HK2 (Proteintech, Cat No. 22029-1-AP), LDHA (ABclonal, A21893), PKM2 (ABclonal, A0268), TPI1(ABclonal, A2579) and β-Actin (ABclonal, AC038) were incubated with the protein overnight at 4 °C. Following the initial incubation, the membranes were exposed to a secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-1; SA00001-2) conjugated with HRP for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the Enhanced Chemiluminescence kit (Biosharp, BL523) and subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.49, National Institutes of Health).

Co-immunoprecipitation, immunofluorescence, and molecular docking

Co-immunoprecipitation was performed to evaluate the interaction between GREM1 and BMP2. Total protein lysates were incubated with anti-GREM1 or anti-BMP2 antibody, with normal IgG used as a negative control and total lysate used as input control. Immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by Western blotting. Immunofluorescence staining was used to assess the cellular localization of GREM1 and BMP2. Molecular docking was performed to explore the potential binding interface between GREM1 and BMP2 and was interpreted as supportive structural modeling rather than direct experimental proof of binding specificity.

2-NBDG fluorescent staining for glucose uptake assessment

The fluorescent glucose analog 2-NBDG (2-[N-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazol-4-yl)amino]− 2-deoxyglucose) was employed to evaluate the effect of GREM1 expression alterations on glucose uptake capacity in lung adenocarcinoma cells. 2-NBDG is taken up by cells and accumulates intracellularly, with fluorescence intensity proportional to glucose uptake. Semi-quantitative analysis of metabolic activity was performed by observation under an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Establishment of a metastatic lung cancer model in BALB/c nude mice

Six-week-old BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Huachuang sinoPharmaTechCo., Ltd (Jiang Su). This study’s animal protocols received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Lanzhou University Second Hospital (Protocol: D2025-406), complying with NIH guidelines for laboratory animal care. Twenty-seven female mice underwent one-week acclimatization in SPF environments before being randomly assigned to three experimental groups (sh-GREM1-1, Control and oe-GREM1), each containing nine subjects. Each group of mice was injected with 0.2 mL of cell suspension via the tail vein, with an injection dose of 1 × 10⁶ cells per mouse. Following a 35-day experimental period, all animals underwent Micro-CT scanning prior to dissection. Post-euthanasia, pulmonary tissues were collected for macroscopic tumor evaluation and comparative analysis of neoplastic development rates across groups. The three experimental groups were analyzed via t-tests. Inhalation-based euthanasia protocols were implemented, where mice underwent anesthesia in transparent sealed chambers using isoflurane overdose administration. This approach guaranteed observable monitoring while preventing undue distress. Post-euthanasia, pulmonary specimens underwent fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin followed by hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining for histological evaluation.

Micro-CT radiomics and machine-learning analysis

Micro-CT images from the mouse lung metastasis model were used for exploratory radiomic analysis. For each mouse, the largest pulmonary metastatic nodule was selected as the analysis target. Three-dimensional regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated around the selected nodules using ITK-SNAP. ROI segmentation was performed by radiologists, who were blinded to experimental group assignment. A total of 27 ROIs were included for feature extraction, corresponding to one metastatic nodule per mouse, with 9 ROIs in each experimental group. This one-lesion-per-mouse strategy was used to avoid pseudo-replication from multiple nodules within the same animal.

Radiomic features were extracted using PyRadiomics including Firstorder, Shape, GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, GLDM and NGTDM. Before model construction, radiomic features were standardized across samples. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used for feature selection, and original_shape_LeastAxisLength was identified as the feature with the largest coefficient and was used for downstream model construction.

The selected radiomic features were used to train machine-learning classifiers to distinguish GREM1 knockdown, control, and GREM1 overexpression groups. Eight machine-learning algorithms were compared, including logistic regression, support vector machine, k-nearest neighbors, random forest, ExtraTrees, XGBoost, LightGBM, and multilayer perceptron. Given the limited sample size, model performance was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation. Classification performance was summarized using AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, precision and recall.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing and cell annotation

The lung cancer dataset GSE131907 [50] was obtained from GEO and used to characterize the cellular context of GREM1 and BMP2 expression in the LUAD microenvironment. Gene expression matrices were normalized, highly variable genes were identified, and principal component analysis was performed. Harmony was used to correct batch effects within the single-cell dataset. Cell clustering was performed using the first 30 principal components and Harmony-reduced dimensions at a resolution of 0.5. Major cell types were annotated according to canonical marker genes and prior biological knowledge, including myeloid cells, B lymphocytes, epithelial cells, T lymphocytes, NK cells, mast cells, endothelial cells and fibroblasts.

UNAGI model architecture

A deep autoencoder model, UNAGI (Universal Neural-network Autoencoder for Gene perturbation Inference) [51], was employed for virtual gene knockout analysis. The symmetric autoencoder architecture consisted of an input layer with 23,568 dimensions, encoder hidden layers with dimensions of 512, 256, and 128, and decoder hidden layers with dimensions of 128, 256, and 512. ReLU activation function was applied, with a dropout rate of 0.2 and batch normalization implemented in each hidden layer. GREM1 was selected as the target gene for virtual knockout. After setting GREM1 expression to zero, the trained UNAGI model was used to predict genome-wide expression changes. To minimize prediction noise, 50 independent predictions were performed for each cell and averaged. Log₂ fold changes were calculated as Log₂FC = Log₂(predicted expression after knockout / original expression + 10⁻⁸).

UNAGI model training strategy

Data were randomly partitioned into training (85%) and validation (15%) sets. Model training parameters were configured as follows: 35 training epochs, batch size of 512, learning rate of 0.001, Adam optimizer, and mean squared error loss function. Early stopping was applied, terminating training if validation loss failed to improve for 8 consecutive epochs, and the model weights with optimal validation performance were saved for subsequent predictions. Principal component analysis was performed on highly variable genes, retaining the first 30 principal components, and batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm. Cell clustering was conducted based on principal components and Harmony-reduced dimensions (resolution 0.5), with cell type annotation performed according to marker genes and prior knowledge. The scRank algorithm [52] was used to simulate BMP2 perturbation, constructing regulatory networks between drug targets and downstream genes based on reference databases and gene functional annotations. Drug response scores were calculated for each cell, categorizing cells into high, medium, and low sensitivity grades, with response scores summarized and ranked across different cell types. These virtual perturbation analyses were used to generate hypotheses regarding potential GREM1- and BMP2-associated transcriptional effects and cell-type contexts. They were not interpreted as direct experimental evidence of causality.

Pharmacological intervention

To investigate the involvement of TGF‑β signaling in GREM1‑mediated EMT and glycolytic phenotypes, GREM1‑silenced A549 cells were treated with 10 µM SRI‑011381 hydrochloride [53], a TGF‑β pathway activator at a concentration previously shown to activate TGF-β signaling in cell-based rescue experiments. The experimental groups included control cells, GREM1-silenced cells, and GREM1-silenced cells treated with SRI-011381. To examine whether the GREM1-associated phenotype involved GREM1-BMP signaling, GREM1-overexpressing A549 cells were treated with 150 nM ginisortamab (UCB6114) [54], a GREM1-neutralizing monoclonal antibody previously shown to block gremlin-1-mediated inhibition of BMP signaling in vitro. The experimental groups included control cells, GREM1-overexpressing cells, and GREM1-overexpressing cells treated with ginisortamab.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.0). Comparative analyses between two groups used independent Student’s t-tests for normally distributed data and Mann-Whitney U tests for nonparametric distributions. Multi-group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA or alternative nonparametric or mixed-effects approaches, as appropriate. Categorical variables were examined using two-tailed Fisher’s exact tests. Optimal CMLS score thresholds were established through the surv_cutpoint() algorithm from the survminer package, optimizing the log-rank test statistic. Variable associations were quantified using Pearson’s correlation analysis. Multiple hypothesis testing adjustments were implemented using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p-values < 0.05.

Results

Identification of two EMT, glycolysis and TGF-β–based LUAD subtypes

Previous studies have shown that the differences in the activities of EMT, glycolysis and the TGF-β pathway are related to the prognosis and biological heterogeneity of LUAD, and we sought to classify patients into distinct molecular subtypes based on these pathways. We applied integrative clustering based on EMT, glycolysis and TGF-β pathway gene sets in 519 tumor samples. CPI and Gap statistic (Fig. 1A) indicated that the optimal solution comprised two distinct molecular subtypes, each supported by equivalent stability scores (Figs. 1B-D). To further characterize these subtypes, we examined the top 100 upregulated genes in each group and performed Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 1F). Genes with correlation coefficients > 0 were defined as Signature A, and those with coefficients < 0 as Signature B. Functional enrichment analyses revealed that Signature A genes were predominantly associated with immune response processes (Fig. 1G-H), whereas Signature B genes were enriched in energy metabolism–related pathways.

Multi-cohort validation reveals pathway characteristics and survival differences across distinct subtypes

Having identified two EMT/glycolysis/TGF-β–based subtypes, we next sought to characterize their distinct molecular and genomic features to better understand the biological processes underlying each group. We next quantified pathway activity and survival differences across cohorts. In TCGA-LUAD, CS2 showed higher activation of EMT, glycolysis, TGF-β signaling, and other tumor-promoting programs, whereas CS1 showed lower activation of these pathways and more favorable survival (Figure S1A and E). Consistent patterns were observed in the GSE72094, GSE30219, and GSE31210 validation cohorts, where CS2 displayed stronger EMT/glycolysis/TGF-β pathway activity and poorer survival than CS1 (Figure S1B–H). Subtype assignments generated by NTP and PAM showed significant concordance (P < 0.005, Figure S1I–L), supporting the robustness of this classification.

Identification of GREM1 as a core gene of CS2 subtype and its clinical prognostic association

We next performed subtype-associated gene analysis to identify candidate mediators of the CS2 phenotype. Pearson correlation analysis identified 59 genes with correlation coefficients > 0.5 with the CS2 subtype, which were defined as core CS2-associated genes (Table S1). To further explore the potential association between these genes and the TGF-β pathway, glycolysis and EMT pathway, we used the STRING database to conduct protein-protein interaction network analysis on the 59 core genes and the molecules related to the above pathways, and screened significant interactions with the threshold of combined score > 0.9. The results showed that GREM1 was the most prominent, with an interaction score with BMP2 as high as 0.995 (Fig. 2A), suggesting that GREM1 is the core molecule that is most closely associated with BMP2. We then evaluated the potential interaction between GREM1 and BMP2 experimentally. Double immunofluorescence staining showed cytoplasmic co-localization of GREM1 and BMP2, with reciprocal changes in BMP2 intensity after GREM1 knockdown or overexpression (Fig. 2C). To assess binding specificity, we performed bidirectional co‑immunoprecipitation using anti‑GREM1 and anti‑BMP2 antibodies, with normal IgG as a negative control in each direction. Both co‑IP assays pulled down the reciprocal partner, whereas no specific bands were detected in the IgG control groups, supporting a specific interaction between GREM1 and BMP2 (Fig. 2D). Molecular docking analysis predicted favorable binding between GREM1 and BMP2, including five potential hydrogen bond interactions (Fig. 2B).

Given that the hallmark feature of the CS2 subtype is the synergistic enrichment of TGF-β, EMT, and glycolysis, and that the TGF-β pathway plays a pivotal upstream role, we subsequently investigated GREM1’s effects on downstream effectors of BMP2 and TGF-β. Western blot analysis showed that GREM1 knockdown significantly down-regulated the expression of TGF-β1, Smad2/3, p-Smad2 and p-Smad3, while up-regulating the expression of BMP2 and its downstream effector molecules Smad1, Smad5 and Smad8, and GREM1 overexpression showed the opposite trend, that is, enhancing TGF-β pathway signaling and inhibiting BMP2 signaling (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that GREM1 expression is associated with reciprocal changes in BMP2/Smad1/5/8 and TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling. This observation is consistent with a model in which GREM1 shifts the BMP2/TGF-β signaling balance toward TGF-β pathway activation in LUAD cells. To further verify the clinical significance of GREM1, we used immunohistochemical staining to detect the expression of GREM1 in lung adenocarcinoma tissue microarrays, and found that the expression intensity of GREM1 in LUAD tissues was significantly higher than that in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 2F and G). Survival analysis showed that the overall survival time of patients in the GREM1 high expression group was significantly shorter than that in the low expression group (Fig. 2H), suggesting that the expression level of GREM1 was closely related to the poor prognosis of lung adenocarcinoma patients. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that the expression level of GREM1 was an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma patients (Table S2).

GREM1 affects the malignant phenotype of lung adenocarcinoma cells

The TGF-β pathway serves as a classic upstream trigger for epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), driving tumor cells to undergo EMT and acquire an invasive phenotype. The synchronous enrichment of the EMT pathway in the CS2 subtype suggests that GREM1 may induce EMT by activating the TGF-β pathway, thereby promoting the malignant phenotype of the CS2 subtype. To assess the functional impact of GREM1, we established knockdown and overexpression models in A549, PC-9, and H-1975 cells. In wound healing assays, GREM1 knockdown reduced scratch closure, whereas GREM1 overexpression enhanced wound closure (Fig. 3A–D). Consistently, Transwell migration and Matrigel invasion assays showed that GREM1 knockdown decreased, whereas GREM1 overexpression increased, the number of migrated and invaded cells (Fig. 3E–L). Western blotting further showed that GREM1 knockdown decreased the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail while increasing E-cadherin, the opposite pattern was observed after GREM1 overexpression (Fig. 3M–N). These data indicate that GREM1 promotes LUAD cell migration and invasion in association with EMT activation.

GREM1 suppresses apoptosis and enhances glucose uptake in LUAD cells

Glycolysis can promote metastasis by remodeling the tumor microenvironment and serves as another critical driver of malignant tumor progression. Notably, in the CS2 subtype, the glycolytic pathway is co-enriched with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), suggesting that GREM1-mediated activation of TGF-β may not only drive EMT but also participate in metabolic reprogramming. Flow cytometry showed that GREM1 knockdown increased the proportions of early and late apoptotic A549 cells, whereas GREM1 overexpression reduced the total apoptotic fraction (Fig. 4A–C). These findings indicate that GREM1 suppresses apoptosis in LUAD cells. Glucose uptake was then evaluated using 2-NBDG staining in A549, PC-9, and H-1975 cells. GREM1 overexpression increased 2-NBDG fluorescence intensity, whereas two independent GREM1 knockdown sequences consistently reduced glucose uptake (Fig. 4D–F). Western blotting further showed that GREM1 overexpression increased the expression of glycolysis-related proteins PKM2, LDHA, HK2, and TPI1, while GREM1 knockdown decreased their expression (Fig. 4G). These results suggest that GREM1 promotes glycolytic activity in LUAD cells.

GREM1 promotes lung metastatic colonization in vivo

To extend the vitro observations, we further assessed the impact of GREM1 on metastatic dissemination in vivo. We employed micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) to monitor pulmonary tumor burden in the BALB/c nude mouse metastasis model. Tumor lesions appeared as gray, high-density nodules against the black background of aerated lung tissue. Due to resolution limitations, closely adjacent nodules occasionally appeared confluent rather than as discrete foci. At 35 days post–model establishment, the oe-GREM1 group exhibited the highest number of metastatic foci, whereas the sh-GREM1 group showed the fewest lesions (Fig. 5A–B). Micro-CT scans revealed that oe-GREM1 lungs contained diffusely distributed, high-density nodular shadows, consistent with typical metastatic cancer patterns (Fig. 5C–H). In contrast, sh-GREM1-1 lungs displayed minimal metastatic involvement. Histopathological analysis by H&E staining further validated these findings, showing significantly greater metastatic nodule counts in the oe-GREM1 group and markedly reduced counts in the sh-GREM1-1 group (Fig. 5I–L). These in vivo data further confirm the driving role of GREM1 in LUAD metastasis.

Exploratory radiomics links GREM1 expression to metastatic lesion morphology

To explore whether GREM1-associated metastatic phenotypes were accompanied by measurable imaging features, we performed an exploratory radiomics analysis using micro-CT images from the mouse lung metastasis model. For each mouse, the largest pulmonary metastatic nodule was manually segmented as a three-dimensional ROI, yielding 27 ROIs in total, with 9 ROIs per group. Seven categories of radiomic features were extracted using PyRadiomics, and LASSO regression identified original_shape_LeastAxisLength as a prominent feature contributing to group discrimination (Fig. 6A–C).

We then compared eight machine-learning classifiers for distinguishing GREM1 knockdown, control, and overexpression groups using five-fold cross-validation. KNN showed high discriminative performance with relatively stable performance compared with more complex ensemble models, whereas several high-capacity models showed signs of potential overfitting in this small dataset (Table S3). Therefore, KNN was selected as a representative classifier for this exploratory analysis (Fig. 6D–F). Overall, these findings suggest that GREM1-associated metastatic growth may be accompanied by measurable changes in lesion morphology.

Single-cell virtual perturbation suggests candidate pathway and cell-type contexts for the GREM1/BMP2 axis

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis identified eight major cell populations, including myeloid cells, B lymphocytes, epithelial cells, T lymphocytes, NK cells, mast cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts (Fig. 7A). Baseline expression analysis showed that GREM1 was enriched mainly in myeloid cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 7B). To explore potential transcriptional effects of GREM1 perturbation, we performed UNAGI-based virtual GREM1 knockout. Predicted expression changes were enriched in pathways related to cell proliferation, cell-cycle regulation, metabolic activity, and immune signaling, including MYC targets, E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, mTORC1 signaling, oxidative phosphorylation, TNF-α signaling, and interferon-γ response (Fig. 7C–E). These results suggest that GREM1 may participate in maintaining cell cycle progression, anabolic metabolism, and immunosuppressive microenvironment. The enrichment results of the above calculation predictions are consistent with the malignant phenotypic changes trend caused by GREM1 in lung adenocarcinoma cells. Together with the experimental findings, these computational results provide supplementary evidence for GREM1’s role in promoting the malignant progression of lung adenocarcinoma. As a classical BMP antagonist, GREM1 exhibits the highest affinity for BMP2, preferentially binding and blocking BMP2‑mediated downstream signaling. Given that BMP2 itself regulates cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation through SMAD‑dependent pathways and plays a significant role in tumor development, we speculate that the tumor‑promoting effect of GREM1 may be partly achieved through antagonizing BMP2 signaling. We further used scRank to simulate BMP2-related perturbation and observed cell-type-specific differences in predicted response scores, with relatively higher scores in myeloid cells, B cells, epithelial cells, and T cells (Fig. 7F–H). Notably, there was no strict positive correlation between BMP2 basal expression level and sensitivity score, suggesting that the cellular response to BMP2 inhibitors may relies on the activity status of its downstream signaling network.

SRI-011381 reverses the inhibitory effect of GREM1 knockdown on TGF-β signaling pathway-mediated EMT and glycolysis

To determine whether TGF-β signaling functions as a downstream effector of GREM1 in LUAD cells, we treated GREM1-knockdown A549 cells with the TGF-β pathway agonist SRI‑011381 and assessed the recovery of GREM1-knockdown associated phenotypes. The scratch assay showed that the cell migration ability was significantly weakened after GREM1 knockdown, while the migration ability basically recovered to the control level after adding SRI-011381 (Fig. 8A). In Transwell migration and invasion assays, the number of transmembrane cells in the knockdown group was significantly reduced, and recovered to the control level after agonist treatment (Fig. 8B-C). The results of the glucose uptake assay showed that the 2-NBDG fluorescence intensity was significantly reduced after GREM1 knockdown, and SRI-011381 treatment could effectively reverse this effect (Fig. 8D). At the molecular level, western blot analysis showed that SRI‑011381 treatment effectively reversed the GREM1‑knockdown‑induced reduction in TGF‑β signaling, EMT marker expression, and glycolytic protein levels, restoring them to near‑control values (Fig. 8E). Collectively, these rescue experiments indicate that TGF-β signaling serves as an important downstream mediator of GREM1-driven EMT and glycolytic reprogramming in LUAD cells.

(C) Effects of GREM1 knockdown and rescue (sh-GREM1 + SRI-011381) groups on 2-NBDG uptake levels in A549 cell line. (D) Western blot quantification of TGF-β signaling proteins, glycolysis and EMT markers in each group

Blockade of GREM1-BMP2 interaction reverses GREM1 overexpression-induced malignant phenotypes and signaling activation

To test whether the pro‑malignant effects of GREM1 depend on its direct binding to BMP2, we treated GREM1‑overexpressing A549 cells with Ginisortamab, a monoclonal antibody that specifically blocks GREM1‑BMP2 interaction, and evaluated whether blocking GREM1-BMP2 interaction could reverse the phenotypes induced by GREM1 overexpression. Wound healing assays demonstrated that GREM1 overexpression significantly enhanced cell migration capacity, which was substantially reversed upon Ginisortamab treatment to near-control levels (Fig. 9A). Transwell migration and Matrigel invasion assays revealed that the increased number of traversed cells in the oe-GREM1 group was effectively attenuated by Ginisortamab (Fig. 9B-C). Similarly, 2-NBDG glucose uptake assays showed that elevated fluorescence intensity in GREM1-overexpressing cells was restored to baseline levels following Ginisortamab treatment (Fig. 9D). Collectively, these results support a model in which GREM1 interacts with BMP2 and shifts the BMP2/TGF-β signaling balance, thereby contributing to EMT-associated phenotypes and glycolytic reprogramming in LUAD cells.

Discussion

While therapeutic advances have improved the management of lung cancer, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality, largely because delayed diagnosis, metastatic dissemination, and treatment resistance continue to limit clinical outcomes [55, 56]. A growing body of evidence highlights EMT, glycolytic reprogramming, and TGF-β signaling as key biological processes involved in tumor invasion, immune escape, therapeutic resistance, and metastasis [57–60]. TGF-β-induced EMT can shift epithelial tumor cells toward a mesenchymal and more plastic state, thereby enhancing motility, invasiveness, and metastatic potential. In this study, pathway-focused transcriptomic analysis identified two LUAD subtypes characterized by differential activation of EMT, glycolysis, and TGF-β signaling programs, with distinct survival outcomes. Further analysis identified GREM1 as the core candidate gene for the CS2 subtype, with its expression levels closely associated with malignant pathway activation and poor prognosis. GREM1 has previously been described as an antagonist of BMP signaling in several biological and cancer contexts, and its ability to modulate BMP-dependent differentiation, tumor plasticity, and malignant progression has been reported in other tumor types [30–33]. In our study, we systematically validated GREM1’s regulatory role in the malignant phenotype of LUAD cells through a series of functional experiments. Functional assays showed that GREM1 enhanced migration, invasion, glucose uptake, and metastatic colonization, while suppressing apoptosis. These findings support a pro-tumor role of GREM1 in LUAD and provide experimental evidence connecting GREM1 expression with EMT-associated phenotypes and metabolic reprogramming.

At the mechanistic level, our data support a model in which GREM1 interacts with BMP2 and modulates the balance between BMP2/Smad1/5/8 and TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling. GREM1 knockdown increased BMP2-associated signaling while reducing TGF-β/Smad2/3 activation, whereas GREM1 overexpression showed the opposite pattern. Rescue experiments using SRI-011381 and Ginisortamab further supported the involvement of TGF-β signaling and the GREM1-BMP2 interaction in regulating EMT and glycolytic activity.

To explore whether the expression level of GREM1 has potential clinical imaging application value, we conducted exploratory radiomics analysis using micro-CT images of the lung metastasis model. The identification of original_shape_LeastAxisLength suggests that GREM1-associated differences in metastatic growth may be accompanied by measurable morphological changes on micro-CT.

The occurrence and development of tumors not only depend on the genetic alterations of tumor cells themselves, but are also dynamically regulated by highly heterogeneous cell populations and their complex interaction networks in the microenvironment. To systematically analyze the regulatory characteristics of GREM1 in different cell subpopulations within the lung adenocarcinoma microenvironment, we further utilized single-cell transcriptome analysis to construct a virtual knockout model of GREM1. The single-cell analysis suggested that GREM1 expression was enriched mainly in stromal and immune compartments, particularly fibroblasts and myeloid cells, indicating that GREM1 may function as a microenvironment-derived paracrine mediator in LUAD. Furthermore, the transcriptional changes induced by GREM1 deficiency primarily affect pathways related to cell proliferation, cycle regulation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune signaling, further supporting its multidimensional regulatory role in the microenvironment. Although stroma cells are the primary producers of GREM1 in the clinical microenvironment, genetic manipulation of epithelial tumor cells is a standard and highly valid in vitro approach to dissecting cell-intrinsic downstream TGF-β/Smad and metabolic cascades. This strategy can directly evaluate the regulatory effect of GREM1 on the tumor cell’s own signaling network and malignant phenotype without the interference of stromal components. Moreover, the existence of the secretory GREM1 enables the epithelial tumor cell model to have clear biological relevance when studying the intrinsic effects of GREM1-mediated cell responses. Therefore, our in vitro functional experiments and the paracrine effects from the matrix source in vivo complement each other at different levels, jointly revealing the complex regulatory function of GREM1 in lung adenocarcinoma.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the transcriptomic subtype analysis was based on retrospective public cohorts, with TCGA-LUAD used for discovery and independent GEO datasets used for validation. Although this design reduces the risk of reusing the same cohort for discovery and validation, cohort-specific differences, platform heterogeneity, and incomplete clinical annotation may still influence subtype assignment and survival analyses. Second, our candidate prioritization strategy was pathway-informed, while this design was essential for testing our hypothesis, it may have introduced selection bias and may have missed relevant regulators of LUAD progression that operate through other pathways. Third, although the in vitro and in vivo experiments support a pro-metastatic role for GREM1, the tail-vein lung metastasis model does not fully recapitulate the complete metastatic cascade, and additional orthotopic or spontaneous metastasis models would strengthen the biological interpretation. Fourth, the radiomics analysis was based on 27 three-dimensional ROIs from the largest pulmonary metastatic nodule in each mouse and should therefore be considered exploratory. The association between original_shape_LeastAxisLength and GREM1-associated metastatic morphology requires validation in larger independent animal cohorts and clinically annotated imaging datasets before it can be considered a clinically applicable imaging biomarker. Fifth, single-cell analysis suggested that GREM1 is enriched mainly in stromal and immune compartments, whereas functional validation was performed by manipulating GREM1 in epithelial LUAD cell lines. Although this approach allows controlled evaluation of tumor-cell-intrinsic downstream signaling, it does not fully capture stromal–tumor paracrine interactions in the tumor microenvironment. Future studies using stromal–tumor co-culture systems, organoid models, conditioned medium experiments, or cell-type-specific perturbation will be needed to define the relative contribution of stromal-derived GREM1. Sixth, the UNAGI virtual knockout and scRank-based BMP2 perturbation analyses are computational predictions and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than direct experimental evidence of causal pathway regulation. Finally, larger clinical cohorts and additional mechanistic studies are required to validate the prognostic and therapeutic relevance of the GREM1-BMP2/TGF-β axis in LUAD.

Conclusions

This study identifies two LUAD molecular subtypes characterized by distinct EMT, glycolysis, and TGF-β pathway activity, and prioritizes GREM1 as a subtype-associated gene with prognostic relevance. Functional experiments support a role for GREM1 in promoting migration, invasion, glycolytic activity, and metastatic colonization while suppressing apoptosis. Mechanistically, our findings support a model in which GREM1 interacts with BMP2 and shifts the BMP2/TGF-β signaling balance toward EMT-associated and glycolytic phenotypes. Exploratory radiomics further suggests that GREM1-associated metastatic growth may be accompanied by measurable morphological changes. Together, these findings provide a context‑specific clinical and functional extension of the GREM1/BMP signaling framework in LUAD, and offer a basis for further validation of this axis as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.
