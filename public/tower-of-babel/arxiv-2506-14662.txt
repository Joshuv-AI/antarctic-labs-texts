PGLib-CO2 : A Power Grid Library for Computing and Optimizing Carbon Emissions
Young-ho Cho, Min-Seung Ko, and Hao Zhu

Abstract
A sustainable electricity infrastructure requires the explicit integration of carbon emissions into power system modeling and optimization paradigms. However, existing open-source datasets for power system R&D lack generator-level carbon emission profiling, limiting the ability to benchmark and compare various carbon-aware grid operational strategies. To address this gap, this work introduces PGLib-CO2 , an open-source extension to the widely adopted PGLib-OPF test case library. PGLib-CO2 enriches standard network cases with CO 2 and CO 2 -equivalent emission intensity factors by expanding the fuel-type categorization used by PGLib-OPF , attaining a realistic generator-level carbon profiling. It is also packaged for both Python’s pandapower and Julia’s PowerModels.jl , for a seamless, user-friendly integration of emission modeling into grid computation and optimization tasks. The dataset produced by PGLib-CO2 can support grid-based carbon accounting, emission metric evaluation, and integration into AC optimal power flow (OPF) and optimal load shifting (OLS) formulations. We demonstrate PGLib-CO2 ’s utility through case studies that quantify cost – emission trade-offs and optimize a carbon-aware objective function. By standardizing carbon-enhanced test cases, PGLib-CO2 provides an open-source, reproducible foundation for benchmarking carbon-aware computation, facilitating future research in sustainable power system operation.

I
Introduction

Electric power grids are among the largest sources of greenhouse gas emissions, serving as crucial infrastructure to drive rapidly growing societal and industrial energy demands
[
1
]
. Notably, the electricity sector produces approximately 40% of global carbon dioxide (CO
2
) emissions
[
2
]
. Due to environmental and societal reasons, it is widely considered to transition from purely cost and reliability-driven grid operations towards sustainability-aware paradigms that explicitly account for the carbon emission impacts of generating electricity
[
3
]
. For this reason,
carbon accounting
, which traces electricity back to generation-related emissions, has gained increasing attention (as a basis for grid decarbonization planning and market design).
[
4
,
5
]
. Hence, accurate carbon modeling for power systems lays the foundation for effective carbon evaluation, informed mitigation strategies, and the design of electricity markets and regulatory mechanisms.

Traditionally, power grid operations have focused mainly on cost minimization and reliability enhancements by employing optimization formulations such as economic dispatch or optimal power flow (OPF) under security constraints
[
6
,
7
]
and linearized nonlinear power flow constraints
[
8
,
9
]
.
Similarly, ongoing efforts to develop synthetic power grid test systems
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
or to unify power network data formats
[
13
]
primarily focus on traditional metrics such as generation cost functions and network operational limits.
Thanks to a recent effort of the IEEE PES Task Force, a comprehensive standardized set of AC transmission system test cases has been developed, as termed by
PGLib-OPF
, the power grid library for OPF. This library includes the IEEE Power Flow test systems and synthetic large-scale test systems
[
12
]
, among other well-known systems, each with complete information on network topology, generation, and load data needed to formulate traditional OPF problems. This not only enables benchmarking various AC-OPF algorithms but also facilitates the generation of large-scale OPF-learning datasets, as in
[
14
,
15
]
.
However, the
PGLib-OPF
test systems have not incorporated any carbon emission information for the generation resources, pointing out a critical gap for benchmarking carbon-aware computation and optimization solutions.

Driven by global sustainability concerns, there is a rapidly increasing R&D effort to incorporate the impact of carbon emissions directly into power system computation and optimization tasks
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.
Taking into account the emission level of CO
2
, or CO
2
-equivalent (CO
2
e) that also includes methane and nitrous oxide, grid operators can better understand and evaluate both economic efficiency and environmental impacts
[
19
,
20
]
. Moreover, recent research has expanded traditional OPF formulations to incorporate carbon emissions costs and constraints directly, enabling a cleaner generation portfolio
[
21
,
22
]
.
In parallel, spatial load shifting that reallocates flexible demand across the grid are being explored to reduce system‐wide emissions
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
. Despite these promising techniques, the lack of standardized test systems that directly include generator-side carbon information remains a critical barrier for benchmarking various R&D work.
As a result, almost all research papers have to either come up with custom carbon settings or retrofit existing ones, possibly using an ad-hoc assignment.
Although recent tools such as
ElectricityEmissions.jl
support system-wide and locational carbon emission metrics, they still do not provide generator-level carbon intensity assignments. Therefore, a unified carbon-enriched extension of power system test cases is still missing, yet essential for benchmarking the performance of R&D work in developing and validating carbon computing and optimization tasks.

Inspired by this need, we propose
PGLib-CO2
, which is built directly on the widely-adopted
PGLib-OPF
test systems and incorporates a standardized setting of CO
2
and CO
2
e intensity factors to be consistent with the fuel‐type assignments therein. We extend the fuel categorization in
PGLib-OPF
to enable more precise generator carbon profiling. Our carbon‐enhancing tool is packaged for both Python’s
pandapower
and Julia’s
PowerModels.jl
, attaining a seamless integration of emission data into grid computation and optimization tasks. It also allows users to configure their own intensity factor values or develop general use cases. We demonstrate the utility of
PGLib-CO2
through case studies that quantify cost–emission trade-offs and optimize a carbon-aware objective function. The
PGLib-CO2
dataset is available at Github PGLib-CO2 repository
1
1

1

https://github.com/jacobcho0103/PGLib-CO2
.
In a nutshell, our main contributions are three-fold:

•

Standardized Carbon-Enriched Dataset:

PGLib-CO2
provides open-source, user-friendly functions to incorporate carbon emission factors into
PGLib-OPF
test systems in a systematic manner.

•

Integration to Use Cases:
We demonstrate
PGLib-CO2
’s seamless integration with
pandapower
and
PowerModels.jl
through case studies on carbon-aware optimization, quantifying cost–emission trade-offs.

•

Reproducible, Open-source Research:
We deliver open-source codes and datasets for benchmarking carbon accounting and carbon-aware optimization, including developing new carbon emission metrics and optimization algorithms.

II
Overview of
PGLib-CO2

This section presents how
PGLib-CO2
performs generator-level carbon profiling to support carbon emissions modeling and calculations in power systems.
The key lies in associating the active power output of each generator with their carbon emission intensity factors.
As illustrated in Fig.
1
, for bus
i
connecting generation
g
to the grid, we define
e^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}
to denote the constant carbon emission intensity of generator
g
, expressed in tons per megawatt-hour (t/MWh). Letting
P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}
(in MW) represent the active power output of any generator
g
in the set
{\mathcal{G}}
, we can compute its own carbon emissions as

R^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}=e^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}\cdot P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g},~~\forall g\in{\mathcal{G}}.

(1)

If multiple generators are connected to bus
i
, their individual
R^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}
values are aggregated to compute the total emissions at that bus.
This generator-level emission modeling serves as a foundational step for downstream emission calculations and carbon-aware optimization tasks, as detailed later in Section
III
.

The intensity factor
e^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}
is the key characteristic for carbon modeling of each generator
g
and depends mainly on its fuel type
[
26
]
.
For example, clean energy sources such as wind, solar, and hydro have
e^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}=0
, while coal-fired units tend to have the highest
e^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}
values. Based on this principle,
PGLib-CO2
has expanded the fuel type information originally provided in
PGLib-OPF
.
The
PGLib-OPF
dataset includes only a few fuel types, such as distillate fuel oil (
PEL
), natural gas (
NG
), bituminous coal (
COW
), and nuclear (
NUC
)), which are a subset of fuel types needed for precise carbon profiling
[
27
]
.
To address this, our
PGLib-CO2
introduces a more detailed fuel classification that incorporates additional categories such as anthracite coal (
ANT
), combined-cycle gas turbines (
CCGT
), internal combustion engines (
ICE
), and the aforementioned clean energy sources.

Following the research on quantifying carbon emissions,
PGLib-CO2
has also provided two types of intensity factors for each fuel type: One for quantifying the CO
2
emissions and the other for CO
2
e emissions. The inclusion of CO
2
e values is crucial as the latter accounts for the impact of methane (CH
4
) and nitrous oxide (N
2
O) in addition to CO
2
, thereby offering a more complete assessment of global warming potentials
[
26
]
.

Table
I
presents the complete list of fuel types and their corresponding intensity factors that have been adopted by
PGLib-CO2
. These fuel types and factors follow from well-established research
[
28
]
. By providing both CO
2
and CO
2
e metrics, the dataset offers researchers the flexibility to select the factor that best aligns with their specific analytical needs and ensures consistency with existing literature.

II-A

Workflow for Using
PGLib-CO2

PGLib-CO2
has designed a streamlined workflow to enrich the standard
PGLib-OPF
dataset with the generator carbon characteristics.
A detailed example of this process is available in the Appendix.
First, it starts with loading the network data from
PGLib-OPF
test systems into one of the two environments, either Python using
pandapower

[
29
]
or Julia using
PowerModels.jl

[
30
]
. Second, one can use the
fuel_dict_generation
function to create a dictionary that maps generators to their fuel types and assigns CO
2
as the default type of intensity factors.
Note that this dictionary is fully editable, allowing users to access and change values according to their specific system, such as reassigning a fuel type or switching the intensity factor to CO
2
e.
Finally, this customized dictionary is passed to the
carbon_casefile
function to embed the fuel type and intensity factor as formal attributes within the generator data structure. This step is necessary because fuel type information exists only within inaccessible comments in the original
PGLib-OPF
database.
Through the
PGLib-CO2
format conversion, the resultant test cases are suitable for direct use in carbon-aware analysis or optimization in either Python or Julia.

III
Use Cases of
PGLib-CO2

This section presents the key use cases of
PGLib-CO2
that can extend classical power system computations and optimizations to account for carbon emissions. Through generator-level carbon profiling,
PGLib-CO2
enables a variety of emerging R&D efforts in carbon-aware grid operations.

III-A

Computing Carbon Emission Metrics

A fundamental use case enabled by
PGLib-CO2
is to compute and track carbon emissions produced by generation plants, by using both system-wide and location-based metrics. This carbon computation task is the basis for evaluating the environmental impact of electricity generation. This capability directly supports
carbon accounting
practices where emissions are attributed to end users to clarify responsibility in planning and operational decisions of energy demands
[
5
]
. It is actively pursued by datacenter operators
[
24
,
31
]
and other large consumers
[
32
]
.
In particular, the computation of location-based carbon emissions remains a vibrant area of research aimed at ensuring fair attribution of emission responsibility across network nodes
[
33
,
34
]
.

First and foremost, we define the
system-wide total emissions
obtained by summing over the emissions in (
1
) from all generators in
{\mathcal{G}}
, as

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}R^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}=\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}e^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}\cdot P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}.

(2)

The total emissions
R^{\mathrm{tot}}
can be used to compute several metrics, as listed here.

1.

Average carbon emission (ACE):
Based on the total load demand
\sum_{i=1}^{N}P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}
, the ACE metric is defined as

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{ACE}}:=\frac{R^{\mathrm{tot}}}{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}}.

(3)

ACE captures the average carbon footprint per unit of demand and is widely used to evaluate system-wide carbon efficiency
[
35
]
.

2.

Locational marginal carbon emission (LMCE):
While the ACE metric is useful for understanding the system-wide performance, it does not provide the sensitivity of carbon emissions to locational demand changes. The LMCE metric can offer this spatial variability needed for carbon computation
[
36
]
. At bus
i
, it is defined for an incremental change in load
P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}
by

e^{\mathrm{LMCE}}_{i}:=\frac{dR^{\mathrm{tot}}}{dP^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}}.

(4)

Thus, LMCE quantifies the sensitivity of total system emissions based on the generation redispatch rules for serving an incremental load change at a specific bus. It can be analytically computed using the market optimization model through Lagrangian sensitivity analysis
[
33
]
. We will adopt a numerical difference approach in the simulations by changing
P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}
by a small amount (e.g., 1MW) and directly resolving the dispatch to find the change of
R^{\mathrm{tot}}

[
37
]
.

Although LMCE has the spatial variability, it can represent only
marginal sensitivity
and is insufficient for analyzing larger load shifts or non-marginal generator redispatch
[
34
]
.
However, its bus-based granularity makes LMCE a valuable signal for real-time grid operations under the spatial flexibility in load demands.

3.

Locational average carbon emissions (LACE):
There are ongoing R&D efforts to design a new metric that can combine the strengths of ACE and LMCE to quantify location-specific average carbon impacts. One notable approach towards LACE is the network-based
carbon emission flow (CEF) model

[
18
,
38
]
.
The CEF model treats power flows as fluid flows, and disaggregates the carbon emissions at any node to all outflow directions using the proportional power sharing principle.
Although this principle is mathematically sound, it does not follow the Kirchhoff’s circuit laws or the electricity market responses, and thus the CEF model can incur unfair carbon allocations during the disaggregation of power flows. For example, a recent paper
[
39
]
has shown a paradox in CEF-based carbon allocation due to the presence of counter‐directional loop flows in meshed networks.

To address this issue, the latest attempt is to incorporate the market-dispatch information along an integration path to compute the overall carbon emissions for serving a given load demand
[
33
]
. This dispatch-based LACE metric can well match the market dispatch, but it is highly sensitive to the chosen integration path and may fail to fully capture the global dispatch information for regions not on the path.
Thus, definitions and computation methods for LACE are still an ongoing research direction. Thankfully,
PGLib-CO2
can conveniently integrate different approaches for this metric.

In a nutshell, all these carbon emission metrics offer complementary perspectives for quantifying the impact of load demands, each with distinct advantages and limitations. The
PGLib-CO2
dataset has been designed to generalize both the existing approaches and future needs in carbon computation.

III-B

Carbon-aware Optimal Power Flow (OPF)

The OPF problem, a fundamental power system optimization task, can be extended to incorporate carbon-related objectives and constraints to form a carbon-aware OPF
[
21
,
22
]
.
A basic approach is to add a carbon-associated cost to each generator based on a fixed carbon tax rate
[
3
]
.
Let
c^{\mathrm{E}}_{g}(\cdot)
and
c^{\mathrm{C}}_{g}(\cdot)
denote the economic and carbon cost functions for generator
g\in{\mathcal{G}}
. We can express the latter as

\displaystyle c^{\mathrm{C}}_{g}(P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g})=(\tau\cdot e^{\mathrm{G}}_{g})\times P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g},

(5)

with
\tau
denoting the carbon tax rate in dollars per ton of emissions ($/t).
Incorporating this cost into the conventional
carbon-neutral
OPF objective yields:

\displaystyle\min~

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}\left\{c^{\mathrm{E}}_{g}(P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g})+c^{\mathrm{C}}_{g}(P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g})\right\}

(6a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\underline{V}_{i}\leq V_{i}\leq\bar{V}_{i},~\underline{\theta}_{ij}\leq\theta_{ij}\leq\bar{\theta}_{ij},

(6b)

\displaystyle\underline{P}^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}\leq P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}\leq\bar{P}^{\mathrm{G}}_{g},~\underline{Q}^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}\leq Q^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}\leq\bar{Q}^{\mathrm{G}}_{g},

(6c)

\displaystyle\underline{P}_{ij}\leq P_{ij}\leq\bar{P}_{ij},~P^{2}_{ij}+Q^{2}_{ij}\leq(\bar{S}_{ij})^{2},

(6d)

\displaystyle P_{ij}=V_{i}V_{j}(G_{ij}\cos\theta_{ij}+B_{ij}\sin\theta_{ij}),

(6e)

\displaystyle Q_{ij}=V_{i}V_{j}(G_{ij}\sin\theta_{ij}-B_{ij}\cos\theta_{ij}),

(6f)

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}-P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}=\textstyle\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{L}}P_{ij},

(6g)

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}Q^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}-Q^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}=\textstyle\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{L}}Q_{ij},

(6h)

where the series admittance value for any branch
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{L}}
is given by
(G_{ij}+\mathord{\mathrm{j}}B_{ij})
.
The operational limits and power flow constraints in (
6b
)–(
6h
) follow from the
carbon-neutral
OPF problem.
This formulation in (
6
) demonstrates a direct application of
PGLib-CO2
to account for environmental impacts along with considerations of economic cost.
Of course, generalizing (
6
) to include location-specific or temporal carbon constraints (for multi-period OPF models), is also possible but could be challenging to solve; see e.g., the recent work
[
21
,
22
]
. For simplicity, we will solve (
6
) in the simulations to demonstrate the effects of using a carbon-sensitive objective only.

III-C

Carbon-aware Optimal Load Shifting (OLS)

Beyond generation adjustments, load-side flexibility offers further opportunities to reduce carbon emissions, which can be effectively modeled by
PGLib-CO2
. We highlight this possibility by providing an example of the carbon-aware OLS problem
[
24
]
.
We consider it as an extension of OPF in (
6
) by allowing for load flexibility to replace the fixed load demand
P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}
with an adjustable value
\hat{P}^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}
. This change essentially expands the feasible region and thus can further reduce the objective cost value in (
6a
).
With the spatial variability discussed in Section
III-A
, OLS works by reallocating the demand from high-emission to low-emission locations, thereby reducing total emissions without curtailing the overall energy consumption.
Adding the load flexibility into the carbon-aware OPF yields:

\displaystyle\min~

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}\left\{c^{\mathrm{E}}_{g}(P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g})+c^{\mathrm{C}}_{g}(P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g})\right\}

(7a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\eqref{OPF1}-\eqref{OPF5}~\text{and}~\eqref{OPF7},

(7b)

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}P^{\mathrm{G}}_{g}-\hat{P}^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}=\textstyle\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{L}}P_{ij},

(7c)

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}\leq\hat{P}^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}\leq(1+\epsilon)P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i},

(7d)

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{P}^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}.

(7e)

Note that the OLS problem includes a new decision variable
\hat{P}^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}
for the updated nodal balance in (
7c
).
The constraints in (
7d
) bound each
\hat{P}^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}
to be within a given threshold level
\epsilon
of its nominal value
P^{\mathrm{D}}_{i}
. More importantly, the equality in (
7e
) ensures that the total system demand remains constant, allowing emission reductions to be achieved through load reallocation rather than demand reduction.
Using this targeted shifting of demand, OLS can further improve the utilization of low-carbon generation resources over OPF, without compromising overall demand consumption or operational feasibility.
Like the OPF problem, setting the carbon cost
c^{\mathrm{C}}_{g}(\cdot)=0
in (
7a
) leads to
carbon-neutral
OLS that is related to optimal demand response problem
[
40
]
. Note that this OLS formulation in (
7
) requires the operator to precisely know the amount of load shifting capabilities given in (
7d
)-(
7e
), which could be restrictive in practical markets. More realistic OLS formulations have been developed in
[
23
,
25
]
, but for simplicity, we will use (
7
) in the numerical studies.

PGLib-CO2

IV
Numerical Results

We demonstrate the capabilities of
PGLib-CO2
through numerical evaluations of the carbon metrics and carbon-aware optimization using the IEEE 30-bus and 118-bus test systems. We have used the network data from
pglib_opf_case30_ieee
and
pglib_opf_case118_ieee
in
PGLib-OPF
. To better demonstrate the impact of carbon-aware solutions, we have slightly modified the generator fuel types given by
PGLib-OPF
, which tend to have very similar emission intensities and lead to small spatial variability. Specifically, we have reconfigured the systems by assigning each generator to one of three fuel types: high-emission anthracite coal (ANT), low-emission gas combined cycle (CCGT), or zero-emission renewables. This modification has effectively increased the variability of the system-wide emission profile, making the impact of carbon-aware methods more pronounced. We have used the corresponding CO
2
e factors for these three fuel types given in Table
I
, with the specific assignment listed in Table
II
. For the load profiles, we have used the nominal demands specified in the case files to evaluate both OPF and OLS performance. All optimization problems have been formulated in Julia using JuMP
[
41
]
with PowerModels.jl
[
30
]
and solved using the IPOPT 3.14.4 optimizer
[
42
]
on a laptop with an Intel
®
CPU @ 2.70 GHz and 32 GB of RAM.

IV-A

Carbon-aware OPF Simulation Results

We validate the effectiveness of carbon-aware OPF by comparing its operational cost and carbon emissions with carbon-neutral OPF.
The operational cost is referred to as the total generator economic cost
\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}c^{E}_{g}(P^{G}_{g})
in (
6a
), while the carbon emissions are evaluated by
R^{\mathrm{tot}}
.
To highlight the impact of using the carbon-sensitive objective in (
6a
), we use the results of carbon-neutral OPF as the baseline values to normalize the carbon-aware OPF outputs.
Table
III
lists the resulting normalized costs versus emissions for the two test systems. Note that for this table and the following table on OLS results, the
bold
font is used to indicate the best performance in each metric. We consider the carbon tax rates
\tau
of $10/t, $20/t, and $30/t.
For the 30-bus system, using a carbon tax of $10/t, the carbon-aware OPF reduces the emissions by 38.4% compared to the carbon-neutral OPF. This reduction is achieved by increasing the operational costs by 17.4%.
Further increases in the tax rate continue to reduce emissions at the expense of modestly higher operational costs.
This shows the trade-off when steering the dispatch toward cleaner units. Similar trade-offs are consistent across different systems. For the 118-bus system, a carbon tax of $30/t leads to a 37.3% emission reduction with a 15.9% increase in operational cost.
In short, we have shown that carbon-aware OPF can effectively reshape generation portfolios toward lower-emitting resources, providing notable environmental benefits with manageable economic impact.

IV-B

Carbon-aware OLS Simulation Results

We further validate carbon-aware OLS with a load flexibility of
\epsilon=0.3
.
Table
IV
lists the same two metrics by using the carbon-neutral OPF solution as the normalization baseline again. Because OLS is a special extension of OPF to include load flexibility, using the same baseline can better demonstrate the additional benefits of the extension.
It also ensures that any changes from the OPF results arise solely from the expansion of the feasible region enabled by load shifting.
For the 30-bus system, the carbon-neutral OLS results in a 0.4% decrease in operational cost relative to the carbon-neutral OPF baseline, with no significant change in emissions.
In contrast, the carbon-aware OLS with a $10/t carbon tax has reduced emissions by 38.7%, with an increase in operational costs by 17.1%.
More importantly, when compared to the carbon‐aware OPF counterparts in Table
III
, carbon-aware OLS has reduced both operational costs and carbon emissions. This comparison confirms that load-side flexibility can provide an additional optimization dimension, further enhancing the improvements achievable through generation-side adjustments alone.

The advantages of carbon-aware OLS become more pronounced in the larger 118-bus system, showing better carbon reduction potentials.
In this system, carbon-neutral OLS reduces operational costs by 1.5% while increasing the total emissions by 0.9% over the carbon-neutral OPF baseline. Optimizing solely the economic cost can shift demand to cheaper yet more carbon-intensive generators, potentially increasing system-wide emissions to serve the same total load demand.
Carbon-aware OLS reverses this trend: for every
\tau
, it delivers substantial emission reductions relative to the carbon-neutral OPF baseline. Furthermore, the former outperforms carbon-aware OPF in both cost reduction and carbon mitigation. For example, carbon-aware OLS with a carbon tax of $30/t achieves an additional 3.7% emissions reduction and a 0.9% lower operating cost compared to carbon-aware OPF.
These results underscore that coupling carbon pricing with demand-side flexibility can unlock deeper decarbonization potentials and avoid the perverse outcomes of cost-only, carbon-neutral OLS.

IV-C

LMCE Analysis Results

To examine the locational impacts, we compare the LMCE metric between carbon-neutral and carbon-aware dispatch strategies. Fig.
2
shows the LMCE profiles for the 30-bus system computed by the numerical difference approach.
Fig.
2
(a) and Fig.
2
(b) respectively show LMCE profiles under nominal and uniform loading, respectively.
The nominal loads are those specified in the case files, while the uniform loading assigns 11 MW to every load bus, representing the maximum load level at which the OPF remains feasible.
The observed locational differences are insightful for targeted load curtailment or shifting. Under nominal loading in Fig.
2
(a), carbon-aware strategies, both OPF and OLS, have shown to yield higher LMCEs than cost-only carbon-neutral ones. This difference implies that carbon-sensitive strategies tend to fully utilize lower-emission generators such that the marginal generators are likely to have higher emission intensities than carbon-neutral solutions.
Our studies on the 30-bus system under nominal loading have confirmed that carbon-aware strategies have fully utilized the capacity of CCGT and renewable generation, forcing any marginal increase in load to be served by the ANT units, the most carbon-intensive sources. In contrast, under carbon-neutral dispatch, CCGT units are not at their capacity limit due to a higher operational cost than other sources. This way, a marginal demand increase would be met by a mix of CCGT and ANT units, resulting in lower LMCE values than in the carbon-aware strategies.

Compared to carbon-aware OPF, carbon-aware OLS achieves slightly lower LMCE values at certain buses, particularly the last few load buses. This again confirms the additional benefits offered by using spatial load flexibility.
Basically, carbon‐aware OPF can effectively utilize the generation flexibility to seek a cleaner supply, while carbon‐aware OLS further reduces marginal emission intensities by incorporating load flexibility.
In the 30-bus system under nominal loading, carbon-aware OPF is limited by transmission congestion, which restricts access to low-emission generation. By introducing spatial load flexibility, carbon-aware OLS can help to relieve this congestion and reduce line losses. As a result, the additional generation output required to meet a marginal load increase is reduced from OPF, decreasing both the generation operational cost and carbon emissions.

The advantages of OLS become even more pronounced under uniform loading in Fig.
2
(b), demonstrating improved carbon relocation potentials.
Under uniform loading, OLS fully exploits CCGT and renewable capacities by shifting loads away from congested areas, thereby reducing reliance on high-emission marginal units. In contrast, carbon-aware OPF faces severe congestion, limiting the dispatch of low-emission resources despite available capacity. As a result, marginal load increases may be met by different local generators, producing a wide spread of LMCE values.
At certain locations, incremental load increases activate new generators, producing discontinuities in LMCE profiles. While this can reduce total emissions for some locations (e.g., load buses 7 and 16), it can also generate very high marginal emissions at certain buses (e.g., load buses 17-19).
Combined with Table
IV
, these results confirm that combining carbon pricing and load-side flexibility unlocks additional decarbonization potential beyond generation-side strategies alone.

V
Conclusions and Future Work

We developed the
PGLib-CO2
dataset, a novel extension of the widely used
PGLib-OPF
that incorporates carbon attributes of generator fuel types and intensity factors.

PGLib-CO2
enriches standard network case files with CO
2
and CO
2
-equivalent emission intensity factors assigned by the extended fuel categorization in
PGLib-OPF
.

PGLib-CO2
is packaged for both Python’s
pandapower
and Julia’s
PowerModels.jl
, attaining a seamless integration of emission data into grid computation and optimization tasks. Users can immediately implement the default intensity values or customize them with their own values, supporting flexible analyses across a variety of scenarios. We demonstrated its effectiveness through carbon‑aware optimal power flow (OPF) and optimal load shifting (OLS) case studies using the standard IEEE test systems, highlighting how
PGLib-CO2
reveals the cost–emissions trade‑offs and optimizes a carbon-aware objective function. By standardizing carbon-enhanced test cases and workflows,
PGLib-CO2
establishes an open-source, reproducible foundation for benchmarking carbon accounting and carbon-aware optimization.

Future expansions of
PGLib-CO2
include the addition of more complex test systems, comprehensive modeling of renewables and storage, and refined emission data calibrated by real-world conditions. Researchers may explore advanced sampling approaches or historical load/generation traces to create scenarios reflecting actual or extreme operational states. By offering a robust foundation for carbon-aware decision,
PGLib-CO2
paves the way for innovative work in addressing the carbon footprint of the electric energy infrastructure.
