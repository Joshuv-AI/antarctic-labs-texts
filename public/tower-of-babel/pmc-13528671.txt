Assessment: An Unresolved Intercurrent Event in a Randomized Trial of Ciprofol versus Propofol [Letter]
Authors: Shengchao Zhang, Tong Li, Yong Liu
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the randomized controlled trial by Zhang et al comparing ciprofol with propofol for emergence agitation (EA) and recovery quality in children undergoing dental treatment under general anesthesia.1 The investigators addressed an important question in pediatric anesthesia and reported that, although the primary outcome of EA incidence did not differ significantly between groups, ciprofol was associated with lower Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED) scores and several favorable recovery outcomes. We would like to raise one methodological issue that may be important for interpreting the primary endpoint: the temporal relationship between rescue sedation and the prespecified 5-minute PAED assessment.

The trial defined its primary outcome as EA, represented by a PAED score >10 at 5 minutes after awakening.1 However, the rescue sedation protocol permitted additional study medication when a child exhibited a PAED score >10 for at least 3 consecutive minutes or severe movement posing a risk of self-injury or surgical-site bleeding. Children in the propofol group received propofol 0.5 mg/kg, whereas those in the ciprofol group received ciprofol 0.1 mg/kg; one repeat dose was permitted after 3 minutes if agitation persisted.1 This sequence creates an important temporal question: could rescue sedation have been administered before the primary PAED assessment at 5 minutes?

If rescue medication was administered before that assessment, it would represent an intercurrent event with direct implications for interpretation of the primary outcome. The ICH E9(R1) estimand framework defines intercurrent events as events occurring after treatment initiation that affect either the interpretation or the existence of measurements related to the clinical question, and explicitly identifies additional or alternative medication as an example.2 Importantly, the guideline notes that the interpretation of an outcome obtained after additional medication differs from that of the same outcome obtained without such medication.2 This issue is particularly relevant in anesthesia trials, in which rescue medication is a common intercurrent event and its handling should be aligned with the clinical question the trial is intended to answer.3

In the present trial, consider a child whose PAED score exceeded 10 continuously during the first 3 minutes after awakening and who consequently received rescue sedation before minute 5. For such a patient, a PAED score obtained at the prespecified 5-minute time point may partly reflect the effect of rescue medication rather than the emergence profile attributable to the randomized anesthetic strategy before rescue. If the 5-minute score subsequently fell to ≤10, classifying that patient as not having experienced EA would answer a different clinical question from whether clinically relevant EA had occurred during early emergence. In other words, an outcome measured after rescue treatment may represent post-rescue emergence status, rather than untreated early emergence behavior.

This concern cannot be resolved from the published rescue-sedation results. The authors reported mean postoperative rescue-sedation doses of 0.25±1.76 mg in the ciprofol group and 2.33±8.38 mg in the propofol group (P=0.033).1 However, these numerical doses should not be interpreted as direct evidence that rescue sedation was required less frequently with ciprofol. The two groups received different rescue drugs at deliberately different protocol doses—ciprofol 0.1 mg/kg versus propofol 0.5 mg/kg.1 Therefore, a comparison of mean milligrams of two pharmacologically distinct agents does not by itself establish a difference in the frequency of rescue treatment. More informative data would be the number and proportion of children receiving rescue sedation, the indication triggering rescue, and, critically, the timing of each rescue dose relative to awakening and the 5-minute PAED assessment.

The direction and magnitude of any resulting effect on the primary comparison cannot be assumed without these data. If pre-assessment rescue sedation occurred more frequently in the propofol group, successful treatment of early agitation could have reduced subsequent PAED scores in that group and attenuated the observed between-group difference in EA incidence. Conversely, if all rescue medication was administered only after the 5-minute assessment, this concern would largely disappear. Clarification of the treatment timeline is therefore essential before inferring whether the reported 3.8% versus 7.5% EA incidence reflects the natural early emergence state under the randomized anesthetic regimens or a state that had already been therapeutically modified.

Several additional analyses could clarify this issue using the existing trial data. First, the authors could report, by randomized group, the number and percentage of children who received rescue sedation, the clinical criterion that triggered treatment, and the interval from awakening to the first rescue dose. Second, the PAED score immediately before rescue treatment, if recorded, would be highly informative. Third, for children receiving rescue medication before minute 5 specifically because PAED remained >10 for at least 3 consecutive minutes, a sensitivity analysis could incorporate the occurrence of such rescue into the outcome definition, consistent with a composite strategy for handling an intercurrent event.2 Alternatively, the maximum pre-rescue PAED score during early emergence could be analyzed. These analyses could be presented alongside the originally reported 5-minute outcome, which would correspond more closely to a treatment-policy perspective in which outcomes are assessed regardless of subsequent rescue medication.2 Such complementary analyses would distinguish the effect of the randomized anesthetic regimen on early EA from the overall emergence course after clinically indicated rescue treatment.

We emphasize that this issue does not necessarily weaken the possibility that ciprofol provides a smoother emergence profile. Indeed, depending on the timing and distribution of rescue medication, accounting explicitly for rescue treatment could leave the original comparison unchanged or could reveal a larger difference between the randomized strategies. Our concern is therefore not the direction of the reported treatment effect, but the precise clinical quantity being estimated.

In conclusion, the key question is whether the prespecified 5-minute PAED assessment occurred before or after therapeutic modification of early agitation. Because rescue medication can directly alter the outcome that subsequently defines EA, its timing and handling are integral to interpretation of the primary endpoint rather than merely ancillary treatment details. We believe that reporting rescue-sedation frequency and timing, together with an appropriate sensitivity analysis if rescue preceded the primary assessment, would substantially strengthen the interpretation of this clinically valuable randomized trial.
