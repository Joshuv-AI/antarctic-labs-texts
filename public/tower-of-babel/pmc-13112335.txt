Accelerating Quantum Mechanical/Molecular Mechanical (QM/MM) Simulations with Stochastic Iso-Kinetic Nosé–Hoover Multiple Time Step Integrator in CHARMM
Authors: Kwangho Nam, Yihan Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The high computational cost of ab initio quantum mechanical/molecular mechanical (ai-QM/MM) simulations has long been a major barrier to their widespread use in large-scale modeling of enzyme mechanisms. To overcome this limitation, we present an accelerated ai-QM/MM simulation method that combines a stochastic iso-kinetic Nosé–Hoover chain (SIN­(R)) thermostat with a multiple time step (MTS) integration scheme, implemented in CHARMM. This approach reduces the number of costly ai-QM/MM calculations by applying periodic corrections at the target ai-QM/MM level of theory, while the SIN­(R) thermostat minimizes resonance artifacts associated with large outer time steps. Initial tests on the SN2 reaction between CH3Cl and Cl– show that the SIN­(R)-based MTS method can stably use outer time steps up to 10 fs with less than 1.0 kcal/mol errors in barrier height. Application to the hydride transfer reaction catalyzed by dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) further demonstrates the method’s applicability to complex enzymatic systems, with stability with outer steps as large as 10 fs achieved by introducing augmented MM bonded terms in the QM region and systematically correcting them during outer step integration. Furthermore, we explored the applicability of this approach for accelerating semiempirical (se) QM/MM methods by simulating the phosphoryl transfer reaction catalyzed by adenylate kinase (AK). Compared to reference simulations without MTS or augmented bonded terms, stable dynamics and accuracy were obtained with an outer time step of 2 fs, which corresponds to a 4-fold speed-up relative to conventional 0.5 fs simulations. Together, these results demonstrate that the SIN­(R)-based MTS method can substantially accelerate QM/MM simulations, providing a practical tool for mechanistic studies of enzymatic catalysis.

Introduction

Elucidating molecular mechanisms of enzymes requires accurate characterization of their reaction free energy profiles. − Hybrid quantum mechanical/molecular mechanical (QM/MM) simulations remain the most effective approach for this purpose, with the reactive region described using electronic structure theory and the surrounding environment treated using classical molecular mechanics. − For the QM region, although ab initio and density functional theory (ai/DFT) methods are desired for their accuracy, their high computational cost limits their widespread use. Instead, most QM/MM free energy simulations have been carried out using semiempirical (se) QM methods, such as the AM1, PM3 and DFT-based tight-binding methods, at the expense of accuracy. Thus, the trade-off between efficiency and accuracy remains a central challenge: i.e., while se-QM/MM lacks sufficient accuracy for mechanistic studies, ai/DFT-QM/MM (hereafter referred to as ai-QM/MM) remains impractical for long-time scale sampling. ,

One practical strategy to overcome the computational bottleneck of ai-QM/MM simulations is the multiple time step (MTS) method. − In this approach, molecular dynamics (MD) trajectories are propagated using an inexpensive, low-level se-QM/MM Hamiltonian at a regular (inner) time step, while periodic corrections from a more accurate ai-QM/MM Hamiltonian are applied on-the-fly at a larger (outer) time step, thereby reducing the number of costly ai-QM/MM calculations without loss of accuracy. ,, Alternatively, high-level corrections can be introduced a posteriori, as in dual-QM/MM Hamiltonian , or indirect thermodynamic integration approaches. ,− Although computationally efficient, such postcorrection methods depend on the ability of the low-level Hamiltonian to reproduce correct reaction mechanisms, such as concerted vs stepwise mechanisms, and to sample configurations that sufficiently overlap with those of the high-level theory simulations. In contrast, the MTS method carries out the sampling directly at the target ai-QM/MM potential energy surface, which guarantees accuracy but requires more frequent evaluations of ai-QM/MM energy and gradients. Recently, machine learning (ML)-based QM/MM methods have emerged as a powerful alternative, enabling efficient simulations with near-DFT accuracy. − However, these approaches still rely on ai-QM/MM calculations to generate high-quality training data (i.e., configurations and labeling) and also to benchmark the trained ML potentials (MLPs). Therefore, it remains essential to develop accelerated ai-QM/MM methods that substantially reduce the computational cost of free energy simulations of enzymatic catalysis while maintaining accuracy.

In our earlier developments of MTS ai-QM/MM method, we used the reversible reference system propagator algorithm (r-RESPA) as the time propagator, in which high-level corrections are applied at an outer time step while low-level se-QM/MM dynamics are propagated with a particle mesh Ewald (PME) treatment of long-range QM-MM and MM-MM electrostatic interactions. , This approach successfully reproduced free energy profiles obtained without the MTS algorithm. However, when applied to complex reaction systems, it exhibited limitations known as resonance phenomena. − Specifically, when the outer time step is too large compared to the fastest bond vibrations, typically those involving hydrogen atoms (X–H bonds), energy builds up in those high frequency modes, leading to geometric distortions of the QM subsystem. In some cases, self-consistent field (SCF) convergence fails for outer time steps greater than ∼4 fs.

To address resonance issues in classical MD simulations, Tuckerman and coworkers developed the stochastic iso-kinetic Nosé–Hoover chain RESPA (SIN­(R)) method, − in which the stochastic process was introduced to thermostat variables to dissipate energies built up on high frequency modes. Applied to fixed charge and polarizable MM force fields, SIN­(R) allowed to extend outer time steps as large as ∼100 fs for slowly varying long-range interactions. , While such large outer time steps may not be directly achievable with the ai-QM/MM simulations, the SIN­(R) integrator offers the potential to extend outer time step beyond those feasible with r-RESPA-based QM/MM schemes. Recently, Pan et al. adopted this approach in AMBER, using a recalibrated PM3 model benchmarked against DFT energies and gradients, and achieved stable MTS ai-QM/MM simulations with outer time steps up to 10 fs. However, their applications were limited to systems with relatively small QM regions, and it thus remained uncertain whether similar performance gain could be achieved for more complex enzymatic systems with larger and flexible QM regions. In addition, Osadchey et al. reported very recently that severe resonance artifacts persist in SIN­(R)-based MTS simulations when outer time steps are larger than 3 fs for a proton transfer reaction involving fast oscillatory dynamics and large biasing force constants. In the same study, they also showed that resonance errors could be reduced by increasing the mass of hydrogen atoms or by using better chosen umbrella sampling force constants, both of which effectively dampened high-frequency X–H vibrations.

In this study, we present the implementation of the SIN­(R) integrator in CHARMM , within our MTS ai-QM/MM framework. In particular, we adopted the middle-SIN­(R) scheme, which has been shown to provide enhanced stability and accuracy. ,, This approach has also been employed in recent studies by Pan et al. and Osadchey et al. Unlike the approach of Pan et al., where long-range, periodic QM/MM electrostatic interactions were treated using a charge projection scheme, , our implementation employs the QM/MM-PME method developed by Nam with high-level corrections applied only to interactions within the real-space cutoff from the QM region. Using this implementation, we show that outer time steps up to 10 fs can be achieved with less than 1 kcal/mol error in the computed free energy barrier for a small model reaction. To improve stability for systems with large and flexible QM subsystems, we introduce augmented MM bond terms into the low-level QM Hamiltonian, which are subsequently corrected by ai-QM/MM calculations. This strategy stabilizes the QM region against large perturbations during outer loop updates and enables stable simulations with outer time steps up to 10 fs. Moreover, the same augmented bond approach is applied to accelerate se-QM/MM simulations within the SIN­(R)-based MTS scheme, which allows stable se-QM/MM simulations with outer time steps up to 2 fs without compromising the accuracy of computed free energy profiles. Together, these developments provide an accurate and stable methodology that enables free energy simulations at the accuracy of ai/DFT-QM/MM theory with a fraction of the computational cost, thereby expanding the applicability of MTS-based QM/MM simulation approaches to realistic enzymatic systems.

Theory

MTS Ai-QM/MM Method under Periodic Boundary Condition (PBC)

In the MTS ai-QM/MM method, the total energy of the system under PBC is defined as 1 Etotai−QM/MM=Ese−QM/MMPBC+[Eai−QM/MMRS−Ese−QM/MMRS]≡Ese−QM/MMPBC+ΔEcorRS where Ese−QM/MMPBC is the total energy evaluated at the low-level se-QM/MM theory, and Eai−QM/MMRS and Ese−QM/MMRS are the ai- and se-QM/MM energies, respectively, computed within the real-space (RS) cutoff. In this formulation, long-range QM/MM electrostatic interactions are treated at the low-level theory using the QM/MM-PME method, , while the difference term, ΔEcorRS (i.e., Eai−QM/MMRS−Ese−QM/MMRS ), provides the high-level correction within the cutoff region. If ΔEcorRS varies slowly with time, MD trajectories can be propagated on the Ese−QM/MMPBC surface using the regular (inner) time step, while the high-level corrections are applied less frequently at an outer time step using the MTS integrator. Moreover, when the dominant inaccuracies of the se-QM Hamiltonian are localized to a subset of the QM region, the ai-QM/MM correction can also be applied to that subset (i.e., a reduced QM region), providing a strategy that further reduces computational cost without significant loss of accuracy. Finally, the high-level correction term may be evaluated using full periodic boundary ai-QM/MM methods, which can provide a more rigorous treatment of the long-range contributions when required. −

The MTS propagation of the ai-QM/MM trajectory relies on decomposing the total atomic forces into corresponding contributions from the low-level theory and the high-level correction, i.e., 2 Ftotai−QM/MM=Fse−QM/MMPBC+ΔFcorRS

In this decomposition, if the low-level theory provides a reasonable description of the chemical system, the correction force ΔFcorRS remains small and can be evaluated at larger outer time steps. More specifically, in the r-RESPA-based MTS simulation, , atomic positions and momenta are propagated at the se-QM/MM level using the inner time step δt, while the correction forces from the ai-QM/MM level are applied in the outer loop at every N-th δt. This procedure can be expressed using the Liouville time propagator, L, for a phase-space point Γ­(t) = { r (t), p (t)}. For example, for the velocity-Verlet integrator, the MTS propagation is given by 3 Γ(t+Δt)=eiLΔtΓ(t)=eiLΔFcorΔt/2(eiLFseδt/2eiLrδteiLFseδt/2)NeiLΔFcorΔt/2Γ(t) where L r is the classical Liouville operator for position propagation, LFse and LΔFcor are the Liouville operators associated with the se-QM/MM and (high-level) correction forces, respectively, and Δt = Nδt. Then, the accuracy and stability of the MTS simulation depend on (1) the quality of the low-level Hamiltonian in describing reaction pathways (e.g., concerted vs stepwise mechanisms), energetics and geometries of key states such as transition states (TSs) and intermediates, (2) the magnitude and smoothness of the correction forces ΔFcorRS , which should be small and slowly varying to minimize resonance artifacts and (3) the choice of outer time step Δt, since too large values can lead to instabilities in high-frequency vibrational modes, especially bonds involving hydrogen atoms. When these conditions are met, the MTS approach can achieve substantial speed-ups, scaling approximately with N, while retaining the accuracy of conventional (non-MTS) ai-QM/MM dynamics and free energy calculations.

SIN­(R)-Based MTS Simulation

In the SIN­(R) method, ,, atomic coordinates and velocities are propagated together with extended-system thermostat variables to control the temperature of the system. Specifically, for each atomic degree of freedom, “L” pairs of two thermostat velocities are introduced. Each pair consists of an iso-kinetic thermostat that is directly coupled to the atomic velocity and constrained to satisfy the iso-kinetic condition, and a second thermostat variable that is coupled to the first and subject to stochastic dynamics. In this thermostat, L determines the number of such coupled thermostat pairs associated with each degree of freedom. Increasing L introduces additional variables that provide more pathways for energy exchange between the physical system and the stochastic thermostats, thereby enhancing dissipation of excess energy, particularly in high-frequency modes, and reducing resonance artifacts. However, due to the iso-kinetic constraint, the instantaneous kinetic temperature is typically lower than the target value at small L, but approaches the target as L increases. Nevertheless, the SIN­(R) thermostat maintains ergodic sampling and reproduces the canonical ensemble required for accurate free energy calculations.

In the present work, we employed the middle-SIN­(R) scheme, , in which the velocity-Verlet propagator in eq is modified to incorporate the SIN­(R) thermostat integration in the middle of each inner step integration, i.e., 4 eiLΔt=eiLΔFcorΔt/2(eiLFseδt/2eiLrδt/2eiLthermostatδteiLxδt/2eiLFseδt/2)NeiLΔFcorΔt/2

Here, eiLthermostatδt corresponds to the thermostat integration. In addition, the two momentum propagation steps, i.e., eiLFseδt/2 and eiLΔFcorΔt/2 , are modified to include coupling with the first thermostat velocities under the iso-kinetic constraint. As shown by Pan et al., this formulation allows significantly larger outer time steps than conventional r-RESPA-based simulations, while maintaining numerical stability and accuracy. The stabilizing advantage of the middle-SIN­(R) integrator has also been demonstrated in previous studies, , including applications within the se-QM simulation, where time steps of up to 2 fs were achieved for a relatively simple model reaction system. Motivated by these results, we adopted the middle-SIN­(R) scheme to accelerate our MTS ai-QM/MM simulation approach. We have implemented this middle-SIN­(R) scheme in CHARMM (version c49a2), interfaced with Q-Chem as the high-level QM engine, to enable SIN­(R)-based MTS ai-QM/MM simulations. Further algorithmic details of the middle-SIN­(R) scheme can be found in Abreu and Tuckerman.

Introduction of Augmented MM Bonded Terms to the Low-Level QM Hamiltonian

In conventional QM/MM methods, MM bonded terms, including bonds, angles, dihedral and improper angle terms, are removed for atoms in the QM region to avoid double counting, since these interactions are already explicitly described by the QM Hamiltonian. Within the MTS ai-QM/MM framework, inaccuracies of the low-level Hamiltonian can be systematically corrected by high-level ai-QM/MM terms. This offers a flexibility in choosing or modifying the low-level Hamiltonian to improve numerical stability during MD simulation. Building on this idea, we reintroduced MM bonded terms into the low-level Hamiltonian, while subtracting their contributions during the high-level correction step to recover the original, unmodified QM/MM Hamiltonian (Scheme A). These augmented bonded interactions are expected to stabilize the QM region by counteracting the buildup of energy along bonds during MTS integration, which can otherwise cause instabilities when large outer time steps are used.

Schematic Illustration of MTS QM/MM Simulation Strategies with Augmented MM Bonded Terms: (A) Ai-QM/MM Hamiltonian and (B) Se-QM/MM Hamiltonian

More specifically, the augmented MM bonded terms can be applied selectively to bonds involving hydrogen atoms, i.e., X–H bonds, in the QM region, excluding those that directly participated in the chemical reaction, and their associated bond length parameters can be calibrated to ensure consistency with high-level QM theory. To this end, eq is modified to, 5 Etotai−QM/MM=Ese−QM/MM′PBC+[Eai−QM/MMRS−Ese−QM/MM′RS]

In eq , the prime notation­(E′) indicates that the se-QM/MM energy is evaluated in the presence of the augmented MM bond terms, i.e., 6 Ese−QM/MM′X=Ese−QM/MMX+∑i=1NX−Hkb[(bi−bi,0h)2−(bi−bi,0l)2] where Ese−QM/MMX is the original (unmodified) se-QM/MM energy, with X referring to either PBC or RS. N X–H is the number of augmented MM X–H bonds; b i the instantaneous X–H bond distance; k b the force constant; and bi,0l and bi,0h the equilibrium bond lengths determined at the low-level se-QM and high-level ai-QM levels, respectively. Overall, these augmented MM bond terms restrain the X–H bond distances so that they follow more closely the high-level ai-QM/DFT equilibrium distances, thereby improving numerical stability in MTS ai-QM/MM simulations.

To achieve this, two QM geometry optimizations are performed for each reacting species in the gas phase: one at the se-QM level and the other at the target DFT level, for both the reactant and product states. The resulting equilibrium bond lengths are then compared, and corrective bond terms are introduced to the low-level se-QM/MM Hamiltonian to compensate for these differences, with the same terms subtracted in the high-level correction. In the present study, three different augmented MM bond strategies were tested. In the first approach, the equilibrium X–H bond distances (i.e., bi,0l and bi,0h ) were defined as the averages of the corresponding reactant and product state geometries, thereby making the restraint effect approximately halfway along the reaction coordinate. This strategy was designed to improve configurational overlap between the low-level and high-level theories, particularly, in X–H bond lengths, where the focus on X–H bonds was motivated by the fact that these represent the stiffest vibrational modes in biomolecular systems. Therefore, they are most susceptible to resonance artifacts and numerical instabilities when Δt exceeds a few femtoseconds. By selectively augmenting these terms, such instabilities can be effectively dampened, thereby improving the stability of MTS ai-QM/MM simulations. In the second approach, the equilibrium X–H bond distances were taken entirely from the reactant state geometries. In the third approach, the augmented MM bond terms were extended beyond X–H bonds to include all MM bonds within the QM region. These two approaches were adopted to assess the sensitivity of the simulation results to the choice of augmented MM bonded terms. In all cases, the force constants were determined from the corresponding CHARMM general force field parameters.

Augmented MM Bonded Terms for the MTS Se-QM/MM Simulations

Augmented MM bonded terms for the QM region can also be used to accelerate se-QM/MM simulations when combined with the MTS scheme (Scheme B). In conventional se-QM/MM methods, the total potential energy of the system is expressed as 7 Etotse−QM/MM=EQM+EQM/MM+EMM where EQM is the quantum mechanical energy of the QM region, E MM is the MM energy of the classical region and E QM/MM is the interaction energy between the QM and MM subsystems. The E MM energy is given by 8 EMM=Ebond+Eangle+Edihe+Eimpr+EvdW+Eelec+Eothers where E bond , E angle , E dihe and E impr denote bonded interactions, E vdW and E elec represent nonbonded Lennard-Jones and electrostatic interactions and Eothers include additional potential terms, such as CMAP and the umbrella potential.

In the present MTS se-QM/MM scheme, eq is partitioned into (i) fast components propagated in the inner loop and (ii) remaining terms propagated in the outer loop. Following standard force field-based MTS strategy, E bond and E angle terms define the fast degrees of freedom and are integrated using a short time step. Accordingly, the inner-loop energy is defined as 9 EinnerMTS=Ebond+Eangle+EbondQM′+EMorseQM+EangleQM where EbondQM′ and EangleQM are the bond and angle terms associated with atoms in the QM region. EMorseQM represents the Morse potentials used to model forming and cleaving bonds during umbrella sampling simulations (see Supporting Information). The prime in EbondQM′ indicates that the bonds that are described by the Morse potentials are excluded.

The corresponding outer-loop energy is then given by 10 EouterMTS=Etotse−QM/MM−EinnerMTS so that the energy terms evaluated during the outer-loop propagation include: E QM , E QM/MM and the MM terms E dihe + E impr + E vdW + E elec + E others . In addition, the inner-loop bonded terms (i.e., EbondQM′ , EMorseQM and EangleQM ) are subtracted to recover the original unmodified se-QM/MM energy in eq at each outer-step evaluation. This procedure, therefore, closely resembles conventional force-field-based MTS dynamics, with the key distinction that the QM region and its interactions with the MM environment are described by the se-QM/MM Hamiltonian. As demonstrated in the Results and Discussion section, this strategy enables stable se-QM/MM simulation with an outer time step of Δt = 2 fs and an inner time step of δt = 0.5 fs, corresponding to a 4-fold speed-up compared to standard single-time-step simulations. Importantly, this gain in efficiency does not compromise accuracy, as shown by the close agreement between computed reaction free energy profiles and those obtained from reference simulations without MTS acceleration.

Computational Details

Three systems were prepared to test the SIN­(R)-based MTS QM/MM methods: (1) the SN2 nucleophilic substitution reaction between CH3Cl and Cl– in water, (2) the hydride transfer reaction catalyzed by the enzyme dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) and (3) the phosphoryl transfer reaction catalyzed by adenylate kinase (AK). The first two systems were simulated by applying the MTS ai-QM/MM method and the last one with the AM1/d-PhoT se-QM/MM method.

CH3Cl and Cl– in Water

The first system was prepared to simulate the SN2 reaction between CH3Cl and Cl– in a 35.0 Å rhombic dodecahedron box of 990 TIP3P waters. For electrostatic interactions, the PME method was used with 40 × 40 × 40 Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) grid and 11.0 Å real space cutoff. The same cutoff distance was used for van der Waals interactions. The QM region consisted of the CH3Cl and Cl– species and was represented by the AM1 method as the low-level theory and either the HF/3-21G or PBE0/6-31+G­(d) method as the high-level theory. All bonds of (MM) TIP3P water molecules were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm. For umbrella sampling, the reaction coordinate (ξ) was defined as the distance difference from the forming and cleaving C–Cl bonds. Since the reaction was symmetric, the sampling was only performed between ξ = −2.5 Å and 0.0 Å with the harmonic force constant ranged between 60.0 kcal/mol/Å2 and 90.0 kcal/mol/Å2. For SIN­(R), we simulated with L values between L = 1 and 35, associated coupling parameter of 10–4 for both thermostat velocities and 100 ps–1 for friction kernel, following Abreu and Tuckerman. For the r-RESPA-based MTS simulation, the Nosé–Hoover thermostat was used at 300.0 K with the temperature coupling parameter of 1,000 kcal/s–2. Each simulation was run for 30,000 outer MD time steps with δt = 0.5 fs inner loop time integration, and N was varied between 1 and 20. Therefore, for example, for N = 1, the umbrella sampling simulation was run for 15 ps, and for N = 20, it corresponded to 300 ps sampling. We intended this to ensure the same number of high-level ai-QM/MM calculations. The potentials of mean force (PMF) were then computed using the weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM) and statistical uncertainties in the computed free energy barriers and reaction free energies were estimated using a block averaging approach.

DHFR System for the Hydride Transfer Reaction between N5-Protonated 7,8-Dihydrofolate (H3folate+) and Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate Hydride (NADPH)

The wild-type E. coli DHFR structure (PDB ID: 1RX2) was used to prepare the DHFR system (Figure ). The ligands present in the X-ray structure were modified to H3folate+ and NADPH, respectively, using the CHARMM-GUI QM/MM Interfacer. The protonation state of each protonatable residue was assigned according to its pK a in water or based on local hydrogen bonding network. The protein and ligands were then solvated in a 71.0 Å cubic box of TIP3P waters and neutralized by the addition of 150 mM K+ and Cl– ions. The CHARMM36m force field parameters were used to describe the protein, while the two ligands were modeled using CGenFF v2.6. The QM region was defined by breaking two covalent bonds away from the reaction site and introducing two hydrogen-link atoms to cap the dangling QM bonds, resulting in a total of 69 QM atoms, described by the SRP-AM1 model. For the high-level QM region, we further defined a reduced QM region comprising of 40 atoms (Figure S1), described by the PBE0/6-31+G­(d) level of theory. This reduced QM region was designed to minimize the number of atoms treated with computationally intensive DFT method while retaining the key atoms essential to the catalytic reaction. Using the SRP-AM1 se-QM/MM-PME method, the system was first energy minimized with positional restraints on the ligand and protein heavy atoms, followed by equilibration at 298.15 K for 10 ps. In all simulation, the PME electrostatic and van der Waals interactions were evaluated with 12.0 Å cutoff. Similar to the above system, SHAKE was applied to all bonds involving non-QM hydrogen atoms. In addition, the center of mass of the protein and ligands’ heavy atoms were harmonically restrained around the origin with a 1.0 kcal/mol/Å2 force constant, to prevent the QM region from drifted toward the edge of the simulation box.

The umbrella sampling free energy simulations were performed using both the SRP-AM1 se-QM/MM-PME method and the MTS ai-QM/MM method. The reaction coordinate (ξ) was defined as the distance difference between the cleaving and forming bonds, between ξ = −1.9 Å and 1.9 Å with 0.1 Å interval, thus a total of 39 windows, and the force constant was set to 120.0 kcal/mol/Å2. For se-QM/MM simulations, the sampling was performed for 100 ps for each window with 0.5 fs as the integration time step. For the SIN­(R) MTS ai-QM/MM simulations, we performed for 4,000 outer MD time steps for each window with N = 2 (i.e., Δt = 1 fs), 8 (Δt = 4 fs), 16 (Δt = 8 fs) or 20 (Δt = 10 fs), respectively. The inner time step was set to 0.5 fs and L = 3 while all other thermostat parameter values were the same as the CH3Cl and Cl– reaction system.

AK System for the Phosphoryl Transfer Reaction between Adenosine Tri- and Monophosphates (ATP and AMP)

The AK system in complex with ATP, Mg2+ and AMP (i.e., substrates) was prepared based on the wild-type E. coli AK structure (PDB: 1AKE) following the procedure of Ojeda-May et al., where one of the γ-phosphoryl oxygens was protonated. The system was solvated in a 72.0 Å cubic box of TIP3P waters and neutralized by the addition of Na+ and Cl– ions at 150 mM ionic concentration. After minimization and equilibration for 25 ps at 300.0 K, the QM/MM umbrella sampling simulations were performed, in which the substrates, Mg2+ ion and its coordinated waters were described by the AM1/d-PhoT se-QM model and the rest by the CHARMM27 − /TIP3P forces fields. For each window, the simulation was performed for 70 ps for the non-MTS simulation with δt = 0.5 fs; For the SIN­(R) MTS simulations, the length was 140 ps for Δt = 1 fs and 280 ps for Δt = 2 fs, i.e., 140,000 outer loop MD steps. The reaction coordinate (ξ) was defined as the distance difference between the cleaving and forming P–O bonds (Figure ), between ξ = −2.1 Å and 2.1 Å with 0.1 Å interval, thus a total of 43 windows. The simulations were performed both in the forward (i.e., ATP+AMP to 2 ADP) and reverse directions (i.e., 2 ADP to ATP+AMP) to achieve converged sampling of O–H orientation between the two directions. Electrostatic interactions were evaluated with the QM/MM-PME method and van der Waals interactions were evaluated with 12.0 Å cutoff. SHAKE was applied to all bonds of the MM TIP3P waters, and the center of mass of the protein heavy atoms was restrained around the origin with a 5.0 kcal/mol/Å2 force constant. All simulations were performed using the SIN­(R) thermostat with L = 3, and the other thermostat parameter values were the same as the CH3Cl and Cl– reaction system. For the simulations with the Nosé–Hoover thermostat, the temperature coupling parameter was 5,000 kcal/s–2.

Results and Discussion

MTS Ai-QM/MM Simulations of SN2 Reaction between CH3Cl and Cl– in Water

The accuracy of the MTS ai-QM/MM method was evaluated for the SN2 reaction between CH3Cl and Cl– in water using the umbrella sampling free energy (FE) simulations, where we used the AM1 method as the low-level QM theory and HF/3-21G or PBE0/6-31+G­(d) for the high-level corrections. The resulting free energy profiles are provided in Figure . The figure shows that both the r-RESPA- and SIN­(R)-based MTS ai-QM/MM methods reproduce FE profiles that are in close agreement with the reference single time step (STS) ai-QM/MM results. In contrast, substantial deviations are observed relative to the AM1 se-QM/MM profile, consistent with our previous work. These results demonstrate that the MTS scheme effectively corrects inaccuracies of the low-level se-QM/MM Hamiltonian.

For the SIN­(R)-based MTS approach, the outer time step Δt can be extended up to 10 fs with less than 1 kcal/mol deviation in the computed free energy barrier height (Figure and Table S1). In all cases, the PMF profiles exhibit robust convergence, with negligible variation in the computed barrier when evaluated from successive blocks of 10,000 outer MD steps, particularly for simulations with Δt > 5 fs. For example, the standard deviation σ of the barrier ranges from 0.26 kcal/mol for the non-MTS simulations to 0.05 kcal/mol for Δt = 5 fs and 0.03 kcal/mol for Δt = 10 fs. Increasing Δt, however, lowers the barrier slightly, but the overall profile remains consistent. Figure C compares the impacts of the numbers of thermostat pairs, i.e., L values, showing that the free energy barrier is essentially unaffected by L values ranging from 1 to 35, and the results are relatively insensitive between the different Δt values (Figure D). These results support that SIN­(R) produces the correct canonical distribution required for free energy calculations. This conclusion is further supported by Figure S2, which compares the average system temperature as a function of L. Although the system temperature is generally lower than the target temperature, it approaches the correct value as L increases, and the scaled temperature remains constant regardless of the L values used. In comparison, r-RESPA-based MTS ai-QM/MM simulations generate accurate and stable results up to Δt = 4 fs. Importantly, both r-RESPA- and SIN­(R)-based MTS simulations yield essentially identical free energy profiles, confirming the accuracy of the MTS QM/MM method independent of the MTS integrator used for high-level corrections. However, the maximum achievable outer time step is determined by the stability limit of the chosen integrator, with SIN­(R) supporting larger Δt values. Finally, due to the simplicity of the QM region (i.e., CH3Cl and Cl– ion), we did not observe resonance instabilities even with Δt = 10 fs.

MTS Ai-QM/MM Simulation of the Hydride Transfer Reaction Catalyzed by DHFR

To evaluate the applicability of the SIN­(R)-based MTS ai-QM/MM method to enzymatic systems, we simulated the hydride transfer reaction catalyzed by DHFR (Figure C). DHFR is a small monomeric enzyme that catalyzes the reduction of 7,8-dihydrofolate (H2folate) to 5,6,7,8-tetrahydrofolate (H4folate) using the cofactor NADPH. In this study, we focused on the hydride transfer from NADPH to H3folate+, producing NADP+ and H4folate as products. This reaction has been extensively characterized both experimentally ,, and theoretically, − making it a well-suited benchmark system.

The free energy profiles obtained from the umbrella sampling simulations are shown in Figure , and the results are summarized in Table S2. In our previous work, we compared the AM1 and SRP-AM1 se-QM/MM models, along with ML-assisted QM/MM approaches, where the SRP-AM1 model refers to the reparametrized AM1 model calibrated against B3LYP and G4MP2 reference data by Doron et al. In the present study, we used SRP-AM1 as low-level QM model and performed the high-level QM/MM corrections at the PBE0/6-31+G­(d) level of theory. Compared to the PBE0-QM/MM results, the SRP-AM1-QM/MM simulations severely underestimated both the reaction barrier (by ∼4.1 kcal/mol) and the reaction free energy (by ∼10.3 kcal/mol). In addition, the transition state is located ∼0.15 Å earlier along the reaction coordinate, which is consistent with the more exergonic reaction free energy calculated at the se-QM/MM level.

Comparison of PMF profiles for the hydride transfer reaction catalyzed by DHFR obtained using the SRP-AM1 se-QM/MM method and the MTS PBE0 ai-QM/MM approach with different outer time steps (Δt). All MTS PBE0 ai-QM/MM simulations were carried out using the SRP-AM1 model as the low-level method and PBE0/6-31+G­(d) as the high-level theory. In addition, augmented MM bonded terms were applied to X–H bonds, excluding the bonds directly involved in the hydride transfer.

For the MTS PBE0-QM/MM simulations, the computed free energy barriers are largely insensitive to the choice of the outer time step (Δt): e.g., 15.3 kcal/mol for Δt = 1 fs vs 16.0 kcal/mol for Δt = 10 fs. In contrast, the reaction free energy increases slightly but systematically with increasing Δt, from −5.6 kcal/mol at Δt = 1 fs to −2.6 kcal/mol at Δt = 10 fs, although it remains below −4.0 kcal/mol up to Δt = 8 fs. In the present study, all simulations used δt = 0.5 fs as the inner time step; the results for δt = 0.5 and 1.0 fs were very similar as shown in Figure S3. We note that all MTS PBE0-QM/MM simulations shown in Figure have been carried out with the augmented MM bond terms applied to X–H bonds in the QM region (Scheme A), except for the bonds associated with the transferring hydride atom. Because these terms may not be fully removed by the high-level correction at large outer time steps, the associated errors are expected to increase with increasing Δt. Consistent with this expectation, the shallow free energy basin near ξ = −1.3 Å observed in the Δt = 10 fs simulation results can be attributed to incomplete cancellation of the augmented bonded terms. Similarly, the reaction free energy shows a larger sensitivity to Δt than the barrier height, which remains affected by less than 1 kcal/mol. However, for reactions involving later transition states (TSs), larger deviations in barrier heights may be anticipated, reflecting the increased sensitivity of the reaction free energy. In Figure S4, we further compare the influence of different choices of augmented MM bonded terms on the computed free energy profiles. In contrast, in the absence of augmented MM bond terms, SIN­(R)-based MTS simulations could only be stably extended up to Δt = 4 fs, but the results matched closely with those at Δt = 1 fs.

To further evaluate the errors introduced by the augmented MM bonded terms, we performed se-QM/MM simulations in which these MM bond terms were included but not corrected. Under these conditions, we expected the resulting errors to be larger than those observed in the MTS ai-QM/MM simulations, where the augmented terms are removed through the high-level outer step corrections. Consistent with this expectation, the direct inclusion of the augmented MM bonded terms leads to a larger impact on the free energy profile, increasing the free energy barrier by 1.9 kcal/mol and the reaction free energy by 4.3 kcal/mol (Figure ). Notably, the direction of these deviations is consistent with that observed in the MTS ai-QM/MM simulations.

Overall, these results demonstrate that introducing augmented MM bonded terms allows extending the outer time step to 10 fs with less than 1.0 kcal/mol error in the computed free energy barrier, although deviations of up to ∼3.0 kcal/mol are observed for the reaction free energy. Similar stabilization could, in principle, be achieved using SHAKE constraints applied to hydrogen-involving bonds in the QM region. However, we found that SHAKE severely perturbed bond lengths and led to larger deviations in the computed free energy profiles than unconstrained simulations. We therefore do not recommend applying SHAKE constraints to the QM region within the MTS ai-QM/MM simulation scheme. Recently, Li and Voth proposed combining machine learning (ML)-based potentials with MTS integrator in ML/MM simulations to accelerate the path integral ab initio (ai) MD simulations. Within the QM/MM context, a closely related strategy was more recently suggested by Osadchey et al., highlighting the potential of integrating MTS ai-QM/MM methods with MLP and/or ΔMLP corrections to further extend the outer time step, which we are currently pursuing.

MTS Se-QM/MM Simulations of the Phosphoryl Transfer Reaction Catalyzed by Adenylate Kinase and SN2 Reaction between CH3Cl and Cl– in Water

Based on the success of applying the augmented MM bond terms in the MTS QM/MM simulations, we envisioned that the same strategy could be applied to accelerate the se-QM/MM simulations, with the inner loop time propagation carried out using only MM bond and angle terms, while the outer loop integration included all remaining energy contributions, including the se-QM/MM-PME energy (Scheme B). During the outer step se-QM/MM calculations, the MM bond and angle terms associated with the QM region are also removed, thereby recovering the original se-QM/MM energy function. This procedure is therefore analogous to conventional force-field-based MTS simulations, except that the QM region and its interactions with the MM environment are described by the se-QM/MM Hamiltonian. In addition, bonds directly involved in the chemical reactions, such as the forming and cleaving bonds, are modeled with Morse potentials (see Supporting Information) instead of harmonic potentials to achieve a stable MM-level description of the chemical process. Furthermore, while the overall MTS procedure is similar, this MTS method also differs from the ai-QM/MM case. In the MTS se-QM/MM method, the inner loop propagates the dynamics using only MM bond and angle energies, whereas in the MTS ai-QM/MM, the inner loop is propagated on the full se-QM/MM energy surface.

To test the augmented MM bond strategy for accelerating se-QM/MM simulations, we simulated the phosphoryl transfer reaction catalyzed by adenylate kinase (AK), with the resulting free energy profiles provided in Figure A and the results are summarized in Table S3. AK is a small enzyme ubiquitously found in all life forms and catalyzes the reversible phosphoryl transfer between adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and adenosine diphosphate (ADP), to maintain cellular energy homeostasis. Because of the importance of AK’s two ligands-binding subdomains in catalytic turnover, , this enzyme has been extensively studied both experimentally − and theoretically, ,− particularly to elucidate the coupling between subdomain dynamics and overall catalytic activity. In this study, we performed umbrella sampling simulations using the AM1/d-PhoT se-QM/MM Hamiltonian along with the SIN­(R)-based MTS scheme. To ensure adequate sampling of the phosphoryl transfer process, simulations were, in particular, carried out in both the forward and reverse directions, i.e., both from ATP+AMP to 2 ADP (forward reaction) and from 2 ADP to ATP+AMP (reverse reaction). In the forward direction, the proton on the γ-phosphoryl group was oriented toward the nucleophilic oxygen, to stabilize the incoming AMP nucleophile, while in the reverse direction it was rotated toward the leaving oxygen to stabilize the departing ADP. The rotation of this proton between the two orientations was rarely observed during each umbrella sampling simulation. Therefore, to ensure sufficient sampling of both orientations, simulations in both directions were required, as otherwise the calculated FE profiles would be biased. As shown in Figure A, the FE profiles thus determined are very close to each other for outer time steps up to Δt = 2 fs, demonstrating that the SIN­(R)-based MTS strategy can be successfully applied and provide a 4-fold acceleration of the se-QM/MM simulations compared to the reference, single-time-step simulations conducted with a δt = 0.5 fs integration time step.

In both systems, simulations were performed using MTS se-QM/MM methods: the AM1/d-PhoT se-QM model for (A) and the PM3 se-QM model for (B). Traditionally, achieving 2 fs time integration in QM/MM simulations has been challenging because the application of SHAKE constraints to X–H bonds in the QM region requires caution. In QM/MM simulations, equilibrium bond lengths in the QM region change dynamically with molecular geometry and reaction progress. Therefore, constraining these bonds to fixed MM force-field values can introduce bias and lead to inaccuracies in the computed free energy profiles. For this reason, such constraints are generally avoided, and QM/MM simulations have typically been carried out using 0.5 or 1.0 fs time step, with 0.5 fs often preferred for improved stability. In contrast, since the present MTS strategy corrects inaccuracies introduced by augmented MM bonded terms during the outer step se-QM/MM calculations, employing larger outer time steps is possible without loss of accuracy in the free energy results. This is demonstrated in Figure B and Table S4, where the application of augmented MM bonded terms to the reaction between CH3Cl and Cl– has allowed the extension of the outer time step to 3.0 fs, while 2.0 fs outer step integration appears more accurate. Together, these results demonstrate that the augmented MM bonded terms strategy, when combined with the SIN­(R)-based MTS scheme, enables long-time sampling with significantly larger integration time steps, and thereby provides a robust and practical approach for accelerating se-QM/MM simulations while maintaining accuracy in both reaction barrier heights and free energy changes.

Current Limitations and Future Directions

Although the present SIN­(R)-based MTS ai-QM/MM approach enables substantial speed up of ab initio-level free energy simulations, its applicability to complex enzyme systems involves inherent trade-offs associated with dual-level MTS integration. The first practical limitation concerns residual bias that may arise when certain high-frequency (i.e., fast varying) components of the low-level Hamiltonian are not fully corrected during the outer step integration. Unlike in classical force field-based MTS simulations, where fast and slowly varying interactions are partitioned based on their spatial and time-scale separations (e.g., bonded vs long-range nonbonded terms), , the present QM/MM MTS approach applies the high-level correction force, ΔF cor = F ai–QM/ MM – F se–QM/ MM , to compensate for the deficiencies of the low-level Hamiltonian in both fast and slowly varying components. Because this correction is applied in discrete steps at intervals of Δt, the cancellation of rapidly varying forces may be incomplete at large Δt. This can introduce systematic shifts in configurational sampling and, consequently, in the computed PMF values. Despite this potential limitation, the free energy barriers in the systems examined here remain reliable even at Δt = 10 fs. Meanwhile, reaction free energies show small but systematic deviations that increase with Δt. These deviations remain modest, nevertheless, and can be reduced by moderately decreasing Δt, thereby enabling us to control the balance between computational efficiency vs formal accuracy. For example, large outer time steps can be used when exploring reaction mechanisms, followed by refinement with smaller Δt for high accuracy free energy evaluation.

A second limitation concerns the augmented MM bonded strategy, which is introduced to improve stability of the MTS dynamics at large Δt. In this context, these bonded terms assume a fixed topology and remain unchanged during the catalytic process. However, if their topology changes during the reaction, for example due to changes in bond orders or hybridizations around heavy atoms, the augmented bonded terms may introduce geometric bias, particularly when large outer time steps are used. In such cases, smaller outer time steps or application of the high-level corrections at each MD step would reduce this bias. Alternatively, the augmented bonded terms could be restricted to bonds that are essential for maintaining stable MTS dynamics. Based on this consideration, in the present work, we applied the augmented terms primarily to X–H bonds, where topology changes are rare and geometric discrepancies between the low- and high-levels are modest. For more complex topology changes, however, alternative strategies could be used, such as diabatic (two-state) bonded descriptions, although these would require additional parameterizations.

Furthermore, although the SIN­(R) thermostat effectively suppresses resonance artifacts, the maximum stable outer time steps remain system dependent and may be limited by highly anharmonic proton motions or by the choice of umbrella sampling force constants. In addition, SIN­(R) is designed for equilibrium ensemble sampling and is therefore not directly suitable for computing dynamical properties that depend on momentum distributions, such as transmission coefficients, diffusion constants or real-time rate constants, without additional validation.

Looking forward, several directions could enhance the robustness and efficiency of the present method. First, improving the quality of the low-level Hamiltonians, for example through reaction-specific recalibration, , could reduce the magnitude of the correction term and thereby permit larger outer time steps. Second, adaptive outer time step schemes that respond dynamically to fluctuations in ΔF cor could help further control time step-dependent bias. Finally, integration of the SIN­(R)-based MTS strategy with machine learning-based correction potentials ,,, provides a particularly promising approach. By combining well-calibrated ML-based corrections with controlled outer step integration, near-DFT accuracy can be achieved at substantially reduced computational cost. In this context, the present work provides a stable and extensible framework for next-generation accelerated QM/MM free energy simulations of complex enzymatic systems.

Conclusion

In this work, we implemented and evaluated the multiple time step (MTS) ai-QM/MM method combined with the SIN­(R) thermostat in the CHARMM software package. The SIN­(R) thermostat was specifically designed to reduce resonance artifacts that limit the use of large outer time steps in MTS simulations. Compared to our previous RESPA-based MTS method, the SIN­(R) thermostat enabled stable time integration with outer time steps of up to 10 fs, achieving nearly an order-of-magnitude acceleration. This capability was first demonstrated for the SN2 reaction between CH3Cl and Cl– in water, where the relatively small QM region allowed extended outer time steps without resonance instabilities. Application to the hydride transfer reaction catalyzed by dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) presented greater challenges to employ large outer time steps due to the need to define a substantially larger QM region embedded in flexible protein environment. To address this, we introduced augmented MM bonded terms for X–H bonds in the QM region, which stabilized geometries against large perturbations from large outer step corrections. With this modification, simulations with outer steps of up to 10 fs were achieved with only minor deviations in the computed free energy barriers. We further extended this strategy to the se-QM/MM simulations by combining the augmented MM bonded terms with the SIN­(R)-based MTS scheme. Applied to the phosphoryl transfer reaction catalyzed by adenylate kinase (AK), this approach showed stable simulations with a 2 fs outer time step, while maintaining accuracy in computed free energy profiles. Overall, these results demonstrate that combining the SIN­(R) thermostat with the MTS QM/MM method provides a practical strategy for accelerating QM/MM simulations by reducing the frequency of costly QM/MM evaluations. While the achievable outer time steps and accuracy depend on the quality of the low-level Hamiltonian and the nature of the reaction system, the present approach provides a robust framework for extending QM/MM free energy simulations to larger systems and longer time scales. Looking to the future, integrating the SIN­(R)-based MTS scheme with improved low-level Hamiltonians or machine learning-based correction potentials could further improve the efficiency and applicability of accelerated QM/MM simulations.
