Viral spectrum differences between outpatients and inpatients with suspected pediatric acute respiratory infections: a prospective sentinel surveillance study in Pudong, Shanghai, China
Authors: Zou Chen, Xuechun Zhang, Li Zhang, Chuchu Ye, Yifeng Shen, Rongxin Wu, Bing Zhao, Lipeng Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute respiratory infections (ARIs) remain a leading cause of pediatric morbidity worldwide. However, comparative data on the viral spectra driving these infections across outpatient and inpatient settings within integrated unified health systems remain limited, especially in the post-COVID-19 era.MethodsWe conducted a prospective sentinel surveillance study in Pudong New Area, Shanghai (April 2023–March 2025), enrolling children under 15 years who presented with ARIs from both outpatient and inpatient settings. Nasopharyngeal swabs and clinical data were collected, and nine respiratory viruses were detected using a high-throughput microfluidic real-time polymerase chain reaction platform.ResultsSignificant differences were observed among the 3,533 enrolled children (2,378 outpatients and 1,155 inpatients). Inpatients included a higher proportion of infants (0–2 years; 9.84% vs. 15.06%, P < 0.001), whereas adolescents (12–14 years) were more common among outpatients (14.47% vs. 6.75%, P < 0.001). Background

Acute respiratory infections (ARIs) are a leading cause of childhood morbidity and hospitalization worldwide [1], accounting for 19.6% of pediatric hospitalizations in China and posing a substantial public health burden. The primary causative agents are respiratory viruses, including the influenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and rhinovirus [2, 3]. The post-COVID-19 era has further disrupted this landscape with significant alterations in viral seasonality and circulation, highlighting the critical demand for up-to-date epidemiological insights [4, 5].

The absence of integrated surveillance is a critical public health challenge. Current systems typically operate in silos, focusing exclusively on either severe inpatient cases or mild community outbreaks, thus failing to characterize the complete disease spectrum [6]. Consequently, a unified comparison of viral etiology across the outpatient–inpatient continuum is urgently needed, but has not yet been conducted. This knowledge gap directly impedes the optimization of resource allocation in tiered systems like China’s.

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, respiratory viruses in China, particularly in temperate regions like Shanghai, exhibited established seasonal patterns characterized by distinct winter epidemics of influenza and RSV and generally low summer activity. The post-pandemic era has altered these patterns, underscoring the critical need for up-to-date, integrated surveillance data. By leveraging the well-defined hierarchical medical structure of Shanghai’s Pudong New Area, we addressed this gap using a prospective sentinel surveillance platform (April 2023–March 2025). This study presents the first unified analysis of the viral spectra of pediatric ARIs in both outpatient and inpatient settings. Our objectives were to quantify the variations in viral pathogens in the outpatient and inpatient settings, and stratify the findings according to age.

Methods

Study population and case definition

This prospective sentinel surveillance study was conducted in the Pudong New Area, Shanghai, China, from April 2023 to March 2025. We enrolled children aged < 15 years who presented with an ARI-like illness at designated sentinel hospitals encompassing both outpatient clinics and inpatient departments. A suspected ARI case was defined as a child with symptom onset within 10 days, exhibiting at least one of the following: fever (temperature ≥ 37.3 °C), cough, sore throat, runny nose, expectoration, fatigue, headache, dyspnea, chest pain, or respiratory distress, all of which were clinically diagnosed as infectious in origin. Inpatients do not include those who contracted infections within the hospital; they only refer to patients admitted for treatment due to suspected respiratory infections. Cases were included in the study if they met clinical criteria as well as provided a qualified respiratory specimen for testing with the respiratory pathogen panel. Data were collected using a standardized questionnaire specifically designed for this prospective sentinel surveillance study (see Additional File 1). The questionnaire was completed by the legal guardians; items such as occupation refer to the guardian. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants.

Specimen source

Trained healthcare personnel collected nasopharyngeal swabs from all the participants using disposable sterile flocked swabs. For inpatients, sputum or other lower respiratory tract samples (e.g., bronchoalveolar lavage fluid) were collected when clinically indicated.

All specimens were immediately placed in 3 mL of universal viral transport medium and transported under cold chain conditions (2–8 °C) to the central laboratory of the Shanghai Pudong New Area Center for Disease Control and Prevention within 4 h of collection. Upon receipt, samples were aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until nucleic acid extraction. To minimize the risk of false-negative results due to declining viral loads, specimens were typically collected within 24 h of outpatient presentation or hospital admission.

Pathogen detection

Viral nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) were extracted from 200 µL of each specimen using an automated system (Brand: TianLong, Model: Gene Rotex 96) with TianLong Viral Nucleic Acid Extraction Kits, in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

We performed pathogen detection using the QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The assay utilized the TaqMan™ Microbial Array Specialty Card (Catalog number: 4342253, Applied Biosystems). This microfluidic card was configured in a standard 48-plex format, allocating 43 targets for specific respiratory pathogens and the remaining for internal controls. The reactions were performed using TaqMan™ Fast Advanced Master Mix (Lot: 3115712, Applied Biosystems). These include influenza virus (IFV), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human parainfluenza virus (HPIV), human metapneumovirus (HMPV), human coronavirus (HCoV), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), human rhinovirus (HRV), human adenovirus (HAdV), human bocavirus (HBoV). The present analysis exclusively focused on the nine common respiratory viruses listed.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical data were prospectively recorded via a standardized questionnaire and managed using the Pudong New Area Integrated Health Monitoring Platform. All analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 22.0) and R (version 4.2.0). For univariate comparisons between outpatients and inpatients, categorical variables (e.g., age groups, symptoms, and pathogen detection) were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. To account for potential confounding by age, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed with the clinical setting (inpatient vs. outpatient) as the dependent variable. Independent variables included clinical manifestations and viral pathogens that were significant in the univariate analysis, with age (standardized) included as a covariate. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort demographics and clinical presentation

Between April 2023 and March 2025, 2,378 outpatients and 1,155 inpatients aged < 15 years with ARIs were enrolled. The cohorts exhibited marked differences in age structure (P < 0.001). Infants (0–2 years) represented a larger proportion of inpatients (9.84% vs. 15.06%), whereas adolescents (12–14 years) were more prevalent among outpatients (14.47% vs. 6.75%). The median age was 6 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 4–8 years) for inpatients and 7 years (IQR: 5–10 years) for outpatients. The sex distribution was comparable between the two groups (P = 0.352) (Table 1).

Fever was the most common symptom in both settings, with no significant difference in prevalence (81.88% vs. 83.90%, P = 0.138). However, the symptom profiles diverged markedly. Signs of lower respiratory tract involvement, including cough (65.43% vs. 82.34%, P < 0.001) and expectoration (18.04% vs. 46.75%, P < 0.001), were significantly more frequent in inpatients. Conversely, upper respiratory symptoms, such as runny nose (37.30% vs. 20.26%, P < 0.001) and sore throat (38.35% vs. 10.39%, P < 0.001), were predominant in outpatients. Chest pain (P < 0.001) was also more common in inpatients, while outpatients reported higher rates of headache (P < 0.001) and fatigue (P < 0.001).

Viral detection

The overall viral detection rate was significantly higher in outpatients than in inpatients (34.36% [817 of 2,378] vs. 19.91% [230 of 1,155]; P < 0.001). Influenza A (10.18%), HAdV (4.84%), and HRV (4.29%) were the most frequently detected pathogens in outpatients. Among the inpatients, RSV (3.98%), HAdV (3.64%), and HRV (3.64%) were predominant. Notably, IFV (13.04% vs. 2.51%) and SARS-CoV-2 (3.32% vs. 0.78%) were detected significantly more often in outpatients (P < 0.001), whereas RSV (2.23% vs. 3.98%, P < 0.001) and HBoV (0.21% vs. 0.69%, P = 0.026) showed higher detection rate in inpatients. Influenza B also showed a higher outpatient detection rate (2.86% vs. 0.78%; P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Detection by sex

The overall viral detection rate was comparable between males (29.90%) and females (29.36%, P > 0.05). However, SARS-CoV-2 was detected significantly more often in males than in females (3.02% vs. 1.93%, P = 0.038). This difference was driven primarily by the outpatients, where SARS-CoV-2 positivity was higher in males (4.03% vs. 2.55%, P = 0.044). Among inpatients, no pathogens showed a statistically significant sex-based difference.

Detection by age

Age-specific detection patterns

The following analyses describe the age-specific detection rates of specific viruses at a given age group in outpatient and inpatient settings. The overall viral detection rate was the highest in children aged 0–2 years (35.54%) and lowest in those aged 6–11 years (27.09%). This pattern was consistent across both settings, with outpatient detection rates significantly higher across all age groups. Age-specific pathogen distributions are shown in Fig. 1. Notably, RSV detection was the highest in inpatient infants aged 0–2 years (7.47%). In outpatients, the highest detection rates for IFV and SARS-CoV-2 were observed in adolescents (12–14 years), whereas HPIV and HMPV were most common in children aged 3–5 years.

Age distribution of key pathogens

To further delineate the age profiles, we compared the median age of infection for key viruses between settings. Hospitalized children infected with RSV or HBoV were among the youngest (median age 4 years for both). In contrast, children positive for IFV and SARS-CoV-2 in outpatient settings were generally older (median ages 9 and 10 years, respectively). Age-adjusted multivariable analysis

To ensure a fair comparison between settings given these demographic differences, we performed a multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age (standardized). The model confirmed that, after age adjustment, lower respiratory symptoms remained strongly and independently associated with the inpatient setting, including expectoration (aOR = 3.84, 95% CI: 3.18–4.60, P < 0.001), chest pain (aOR = 3.96, 95% CI: 1.58–9.92, P = 0.003), and cough (aOR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.55–2.32, P < 0.001). Conversely, upper respiratory symptoms like sore throat (aOR = 0.21, P < 0.001) and runny nose (aOR = 0.39, P < 0.001) were negatively associated with hospitalization.

Regarding viral etiology, the age-adjusted multivariable model revealed a critical insight: no single virus emerged as an independent predictor of hospitalization in our age-adjusted multivariable model. While influenza virus (aOR = 0.17, P < 0.001) and SARS-CoV-2 (aOR = 0.21, P < 0.001) remained significantly associated with outpatient settings, the association between RSV and hospitalization observed in univariate analysis was fully attenuated (aOR = 1.19, P = 0.401). Similarly, HBoV and other pathogens showed no independent significance. This suggests that the high inpatient burden of these viruses is driven primarily by the vulnerability of the infected age groups, rather than intrinsic viral severity independent of host age (Table 4).

Temporal patterns of virus detection

At the population level, the circulation of respiratory viruses exhibited distinct seasonal dynamics throughout the study period (2023–2025) (Fig. 2 and Figure S1). IFV and RSV generally drive the major winter epidemics in the community. For example, overall influenza positivity surged to over 30% in January, while RSV transmission concentrated heavily around December. Conversely, SARS-CoV-2 demonstrated a distinct summer wave, peaking in July. Other pathogens displayed different ecological niches: HRV and HPIV circulated predominantly during the spring and summer, whereas HMPV activity was more prominent in winter and early spring.

These population-level temporal distributions directly drove the observed healthcare utilization patterns. Rather than possessing distinct seasonality, the inpatient and outpatient burdens simply reflected the severity profile of whichever virus was circulating in the community at that time. For instance, during the intense winter RSV epidemic, a disproportionately higher number of hospitalizations occurred, specifically reflecting the severe impact of this virus on vulnerable infants. Similarly, elevated HPIV circulation in the spring and summer months correlated with specific spikes in hospital admissions for this pathogen. In contrast, while the massive winter Influenza peaks and summer SARS-CoV-2 waves primarily drove massive surges in outpatients, they generated a comparatively steady, lower volume of hospitalizations. This reinforces the concept that hospitalization rates track directly with the dominant circulating virus in the community and its intrinsic severity.

Variations in viral subtype distribution

Subtype analysis revealed differences in the distribution of circulating strains between settings (outpatient vs. inpatient). (Tables 2 and 3). For RSV, subtype B was dominant in outpatients (64.15%), whereas subtype A was prevalent in inpatients (58.70%; P = 0.020). A parallel trend was observed for HCoV, with NL63 subtype dominating in outpatients (52.94%) and OC43 in inpatients (54.55%, P = 0.025). Regarding IFV, surveillance data indicated that influenza A(H1N1) pdm09 was the predominant circulating strain in Shanghai during the study period [7]. Correspondingly, H1 was the predominant subtype in both groups, with no significant differences in its distribution.

Co-detection

Among the 1,047 virus-positive cases, the vast majority (97.52%, n = 1,021) had single-virus infections, whereas 26 (2.48%) had co-detection (Supplementary Table S1). Correlation analysis revealed significant viral associations, with the strongest positive correlation observed between RSV-A and influenza A (r = 0.523, P < 0.005). A significant positive correlation was also observed between HCoV-229E and HPIV I (r = 0.464, P < 0.001). These correlations are visualized in the heatmap (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Principal findings and global context: two distinct epidemiological entities

Leveraging a prospective sentinel surveillance design within a unified hierarchical healthcare system, this study provides compelling evidence that pediatric ARIs in outpatient and inpatient settings constitute two distinct epidemiological and clinical entities [1, 2]. We identified fundamental divergences in three core dimensions: (1) patient demographics, with infants (0–2 years) showing a significantly higher proportion among inpatients (9.84% vs. 15.06%), underscoring their heightened vulnerability; (2) viral etiology, characterized by the outpatient dominance of IFV (13.04% vs. 2.51%) and SARS-CoV-2 (3.32% vs. 0.78%) versus the inpatient association of RSV (2.23% vs. 3.98%) and HBoV (0.21% vs. 0.69%); and (3) temporal dynamics, evidenced by setting-specific seasonal circulation patterns, such as the winter peak of outpatient influenza versus the spring–summer prevalence of inpatient RSV and HPIV. In summary, while outpatient detection patterns mirrored established community epidemic seasons, inpatient detection rates for key viruses (RSV, HPIV) were shifted or attenuated. This divergence is therefore more consistent with a clinical severity gradient shaping healthcare utilization, where the timing and likelihood of hospitalization for a given virus are influenced by the patient’s age and vulnerability, rather than by separate community transmission patterns. These findings underscore that the outpatient–inpatient distinction reflects profound differences in host-pathogen interactions and disease severity.

Etiological drivers and pathogen-specific severity

When interpreting these distinct epidemiological entities, it is crucial to consider the altered landscape of the post-pandemic era. Compared with historical data before the COVID-19 pandemic, our 2023–2025 surveillance revealed several notable shifts in viral circulation. Before 2020, respiratory viruses in temperate regions like Shanghai usually exhibited highly predictable seasonal peaks. However, our data showed atypical temporal dynamics and expanded susceptible age groups, such as the intense resurgences of RSV and IFV. These shifting patterns are potentially driven by the “immunity debt” caused by long-term non-pharmaceutical interventions [3], suggesting that the ecological balance of respiratory pathogens has been temporarily destabilized [4–5]. Within this context, the significantly higher overall viral detection rate in outpatients (34.36% vs. 19.91%) aligns with the concept of a high community burden of mild viral ARIs, potentially amplified in the post-pandemic era by an “immunity debt” and altered healthcare-seeking behaviors [3]. More importantly, divergent pathogen profiles offer insights into viral pathogenicity. The strong association of RSV and HboV with hospitalization is consistent with their global reputation as primary drivers of severe lower respiratory tract infections in young children [8, 9], a fact corroborated by our age-stratified analysis showing RSV detection peaking at 7.47% in hospitalized infants aged 0–2 years. However, it is important to note that as a DNA virus, HBoV can persist in the respiratory tract for prolonged periods following an initial infection. Therefore, PCR-based detection does not definitively establish an active, causal relationship with the current acute episode, and these specific detection patterns should be interpreted with caution.

Furthermore, our co-detection analysis identified significant pathogen associations, notably a strong positive correlation between RSV-A and influenza A (r = 0.523). The co-circulation dynamics of these two major pathogens are particularly noteworthy when considered through the lens of viral interference. Existing literature suggests that infection with Influenza A can induce robust non-specific innate immune responses (e.g., interferon production) that transiently protect the host against other respiratory viruses, including RSV [3]. This phenomenon of viral interference could partially explain the complex temporal dynamics, alternating peaks, and specific co-detection patterns we observed. The precise immunological underpinnings of these interactions warrant further longitudinal investigation.

Our study adds a novel layer to this understanding by revealing significant differences at the subtype level. The predominance of RSV-A among inpatients (58.70% vs. 33.96% in outpatients), along with the higher prevalence of coronavirus OC43 in inpatients (54.55% vs. 14.71% in outpatients), suggests these subtypes may be more frequently associated with hospitalization. A plausible biological context for this OC43 pattern exists. OC43, along with HKU1, is a betacoronavirus. This lineage is phylogenetically distinct from the alphacoronaviruses (e.g., 229E, NL63) and includes other coronaviruses known to cause severe respiratory disease, such as SARS-CoV-2 and MERS-CoV. Emerging evidence suggests that beta-coronaviruses, particularly OC43, may be linked to more severe lower respiratory tract infections, aligning with our observation [10]. This aligns with literature indicating that viral genetic factors may contribute to clinical outcomes [11, 12]. Such subtype-specific distribution might enhance future prognostic models [13].

Furthermore, our age-stratified analysis delineated distinct risk groups: RSV was predominantly associated with hospitalization in infants under two years of age, whereas the highest detection rates for HPIV, HMPV, and HRV in children aged 3–5 years, highlighting pathogen-specific age vulnerabilities [14].

These pathogen-specific age patterns, combined with the subtype distributions (e.g., RSV-A in inpatients), can sharpen clinical suspicion. For example, a hospitalized infant with bronchiolitis should strongly prompt consideration of RSV (particularly subtype A), while a preschooler with croup or wheezing might point toward HPIV or HMPV.

Implications for clinical practice and public health

Translating these findings into actionable strategies, we propose a tripartite conceptual framework for decision makers in clinical practice and public health.

Supporting Clinical Assessment and Triage Decisions.

Supporting Clinical Assessment and Triage Decisions.

The divergent symptom profiles between settings highlight the importance of the anatomical site of involvement in assessing disease severity. While fever was common in both groups (81.88% of outpatients vs. 83.90% of inpatients, P = 0.138), lower respiratory tract symptoms were significantly more frequent among inpatients (cough: 65.43% of outpatients vs. 82.34% of inpatients; expectoration: 18.04% vs. 46.75%), whereas upper respiratory symptoms predominated in outpatients (runny nose: 37.30% of outpatients vs. 20.26% of inpatients; sore throat: 38.35% vs. 10.39%). This pattern reinforces the established clinical principle that the presence of prominent lower respiratory symptoms, especially in young infants, warrants heightened vigilance and a lower referral threshold within a hierarchical care system. Our study quantifies this differential risk, providing tangible evidence to support existing triage protocols [15].

2. Informing Season-Specific Resource Preparedness.

2. Informing Season-Specific Resource Preparedness.

Informing Season-Specific Resource Preparedness.

The distinct seasonal patterns in virus detection rates between settings have practical implications for proactive resource management. For example, outpatient facilities could anticipate higher patient volumes for IFV during winter and for SARS-CoV-2 during summer, aligning resources for testing and management accordingly. While a summer peak was observed in our study period, SARS-CoV-2 epidemiology remains dynamic, and future seasonal patterns may vary. Conversely, the spring-summer predominance of RSV and HPIV among inpatients highlights predictable periods of increased demand for pediatric inpatient beds and respiratory support equipment. The consistently low and less seasonal detection of IFV and SARS-CoV-2 among inpatients suggests a relatively stable, year-round hospitalization burden for these viruses, necessitating steady (rather than seasonal) clinical readiness [16–18].

3. Guiding Targeted Preventive Interventions.

3. Guiding Targeted Preventive Interventions.

Guiding Targeted Preventive Interventions.

Our study delineates the relationships between pathogens and specific age groups and clinical settings, offering critical targets for precision prevention. Overall, children infected with RSV were markedly younger (median age 4.0 years) than those infected with pathogens like IFV (median age 9.0 years), a finding that precisely identifies the highest-risk cohort.

Based on this, our data provide direct evidence for two strategic approaches:

First, for passive immunization, the concentrated burden of severe RSV in infancy provides a direct rationale for prioritizing the deployment of long-acting monoclonal antibodies (e.g., nirsevimab) in this population [19].

Second, for active immunization, this age profile also supports the strategy of maternal vaccination to reduce the infant hospitalization burden at its source.

These novel tools create an opportunity to address the inpatient burden, while our data help clarify the target for their precise allocation within a tiered healthcare system.

For viruses predominantly detected in outpatients, adolescents (12–14 years) showed the highest outpatient detection rates for both IFV and SARS-CoV-2. While the clinical severity in this age group is often lower, this finding raises the hypothesis that efforts to reduce transmission in adolescents could be one strategy to mitigate community-wide spread, consistent with some public health approaches focused on transmission interruption.

Limitations and unexplored associations

This study had some limitations. First, its single-center design requires validation through multiregional studies. Second, the overall viral detection rate in our study was moderate. This may be partly explained by our highly sensitive case definition for surveillance purposes, which inevitably includes cases with non-viral origins (e.g., bacterial infections). Additionally, this moderate rate likely reflects the age distribution of our cohort; children under 5 years of age, who typically exhibit higher viral detection rates, constituted a relatively lower proportion of our study population. Third, our analysis was restricted to common respiratory viruses, future studies incorporating comprehensive bacterial and atypical pathogen panels are needed. Fourth, precise information regarding the exact timing of specimen collection relative to initial symptom onset was not uniformly available, delayed presentations could potentially lead to false-negative PCR results due to declining viral loads. Fifth, we observed a higher detection of SARS-CoV-2 in males, particularly outpatients (4.03% vs. 2.55%), the etiological underpinnings of which remain unexplored. Finally, while our multivariable models successfully adjusted for age, we did not incorporate other important potential confounders such as underlying medical conditions, immunosuppression, or specific illness severity scores at presentation. Therefore, our findings are best interpreted as descriptive associations, requiring further confirmation in studies with more comprehensive clinical datasets.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the viral etiology, clinical presentation, and seasonal patterns of pediatric ARIs differ fundamentally between outpatient and inpatient settings. We therefore contend that the optimization of hierarchical healthcare systems necessitates a paradigm shift from uniform policies to precision public health. The decision framework that we propose, which integrates symptomatic triage, seasonal forecasting, and pathogen-specific prevention, provides a concrete roadmap for leveraging rich surveillance data to build more resilient and effective healthcare responses for children.
