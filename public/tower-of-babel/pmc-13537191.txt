Recent medicinal chemistry efforts of targeting protein kinases for treating neurological conditions of Parkinson's and Alzheimer's diseases
Authors: Rajwinder Kaur, Damanpreet Kaur Lang, Ankita Sood, Thakur Gurjeet Singh, Balakumar Chandrasekaran, Mahmoud A. Al-Sha'er
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human genome encodes a wide variety of protein kinases that regulate multiple cellular functions. These enzymes play a crucial role in amplifying and propagating intracellular signals during signal transduction. Dysregulation of protein kinase signaling is associated with vascular diseases, inflammatory disorders, cancer, and various neurological conditions. Kinase-targeted therapies have already demonstrated clinical efficacy in oncology and inflammatory diseases, prompting growing interest in their potential application in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD). Several kinases, including PDK1, CK1, CK2, c-Abl, p38 MAPK, PKA, GSK-3β, PINK1, and ROCK, have been implicated in the pathogenesis of AD and PD, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets. However, the development of kinase inhibitors for central nervous system (CNS) disorders remains challenging due to limited blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration and cytochrome P450-mediated metabolism. This review summarizes protein kinase targets involved in AD and PD, discusses kinase inhibitors under preclinical and clinical investigation, and highlights emerging strategies to overcome pharmacokinetic and therapeutic limitations in the development of disease-modifying therapies.

Introduction

Protein kinases are phosphotransferases that catalyse the transfer of the γ-phosphate group of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to hydroxyl residues on protein substrates.1 The first-ever protein kinase to be discovered was protein kinase A, a cyclic adenosine 3′,5′-monophosphate (cAMP)-dependent enzyme.2 Protein kinases regulate the structure, function and activity of cellular proteins in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic systems.3 Kinase-mediated protein phosphorylation is essential for critical cellular processes, including cell survival, differentiation, proliferation and apoptosis. Approximately 20% of human proteins undergo phosphorylation mediated by kinases, resulting in altered cellular activity, subcellular localization, and protein–protein interactions. Dysregulation of protein kinase signalling is implicated in numerous pathological conditions, including cancer, neurodegenerative disease, metabolic diseases, autoimmune, and inflammatory conditions.4 Therapeutic inhibition of kinases is achieved through interference with ATP binding, suppression of kinase expression, or disruption of kinase–protein interactions.3 Several classes of kinase inhibitors, including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK), and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, are already in clinical use for non-CNS disorders.5 Kinase inhibitors approved for inflammatory and autoimmune disorders are summarized in Table 1.6 Accumulating evidence has established abnormal kinase signalling as a major contributor to the molecular pathogenesis of both AD and PD. Unregulated kinase activity is linked to several pathological processes including but not limited to abnormal phosphorylation of tau and α-synuclein, amyloid precursor protein processing, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired autophagy, neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuronal degeneration.7,8

However, these kinase inhibitors were not originally designed to penetrate the CNS efficiently. Effective CNS-directed kinase inhibitors must overcome efflux transporter activity, achieve sufficient BBB penetration, and maintain adequate systemic and brain exposure.4 Currently, the therapeutic landscape is evolving, and there is clinical evaluation going on for highly selective brain-penetrant kinase inhibitors, particularly those targeting leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β), c-Abl, etc.7,9 Although various drug development initiatives have failed to demonstrate clinical benefit, others have provided insight into target engagement, biomarker development, patient stratification, and the feasibility kinase inhibition in neurodegenerative disorders.10 Recent clinical studies evaluation agents such as masitinib and LRRK2 inhibitors have further refined our understanding of the opportunities and limitations of kinase-directed therapies.

Protein Kinase inhibitors investigated in CNS disorders

Developing on the conceptual framework explained in Fig. 1, our work focuses on protein kinase targets with established roles in the pathogenesis of AD and PD, for which various therapeutic inhibitors have been studied in preclinical and/or clinical studies. We selected these targets based on their involvement in AD and PD mechanisms, consisting of neuroinflammation, impaired autophagy, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuronal degeneration, α-synuclein aggregation, and amyloid-β and tau pathology. This review summarises the biological roles of the targets and their involvement in the studied diseases, and current therapeutic strategies targeting these kinases. It discusses the challenges as well as future directions for the development of CNS-directed therapies.

Kinase targets in neurodegenerative diseases (AD and PD)

Kinases are being studied as an important class of drug targets, and kinase-targeted therapies have great potential in treating various disorders, including AD and PD. Fig. 2 demonstrates various kinase targets for the development of potential novel therapies for common neurodegenerative diseases, including AD and PD.34

Disease-specific protein kinase targets implicated in AD and PD.

AD is the most prevalent reason for dementia and also accounts for around 80% of cases of neurodegenerative diseases.35 The most common pathologies behind AD include amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition, neurofibrillary tangles formation, as well as tau proteins.36 Some studies demonstrate that kinase inhibition, which is involved in amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing can decrease disease progression in mice. 3-Phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase 1 (PDK1) activity is highly elevated in neuronal cells affected by the deposition of amyloid-β. When it was inhibited in mouse models of AD using BX912, it was observed that amyloid plaques decreased, sAPPα levels increased, and there was an improvement in memory and cognition compared to controls.37 Increased phosphorylation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) has been observed in the brains of patients suffering from AD, and it was demonstrated in mouse models study that deleting the kinases including Protein kinase R (PKR)-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) or General control nonderepressible 2 kinase (GCN2), responsible for phosphorylating eIF2α, decreases the phosphorylation process and prevents amyloid-β-induced impairments.38,39

Casein kinases 1 and 2 (CK1 and CK2) play an essential role in the pathology of AD and PD, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis-associated at the molecular level. CK1 belongs to the serine/threonine kinase family, which is expressed in eukaryotes and is comprised of isoforms α, β, γ1–3, δ, and ε, further expressed in striatum and cortex. CK1δ is highly increased in AD and is involved majorly in tau phosphorylation, whereas CKα is associated with neurofibrillary lesions. Overexpression of CKε is accountable for increased Aβ production.40 Microtubule affinity-regulating kinases (MARKs), a member of calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase, regulate tau-microtubule binding and play an essential part in the neurons. MARKs facilitate the tau phosphorylation at the AD-specific Ser262 site on the KXGS motif, present in the MT-binding domain. MARKs and other tau kinases are involved in pre-phosphorylation.41

Another serine/threonine kinase, glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β), is predominantly engaged in tau hyperphosphorylation and is encoded by the genes GSK-3A and GSK-3B. GSK-3β is the most abundant in CNS, highly activated in AD patients being the main tau kinase in the pathology of the disease and contributing towards microtubule disassembling and neurofibrillary tangle formation. GSK-3β also advances amyloid deposition production affecting the function of presenilin 1, cleavage of APP mediated by β-secretase (BACE-1), and neuroinflammation process by producing cytokines in astrocytes and microglia.42 Downregulation and phosphorylation of β-catenin signalling, impairment of hippocampal neurogenesis, and neuronal degeneration by hyperactivation of N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptors are also triggered by GSK-3β.43

Death-Associated Protein Kinase 1 (DAPK1) plays a vital role in cell growth, tumorigenesis, inflammation, and neurodegeneration. DAPK1 regulates the levels of phosphorylated tau and Aβ, and it is highly expressed in the hippocampus of AD patients. DAPK1 allele-specific expressions are regulated by Single-Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with late-onset AD.44 In the brain of AD patients, p38 MAPK immunoreactivity was linked to neurofibrillary tangles and the paired helical filament (PHF) of tau. p38 MAP kinase (p38MAPK) is a constituent of a complex intracellular signalling pathway and has been widely associated with neuritic plaques. p38 signalling pathway is activated by Aβ and is involved in tau phosphorylation and inflammation due to IL-1, IL-6, and COX-2 involved in AD. Some studies revealed increased phosphorylated p38MAPK and higher levels of phosphorylated Janus kinase in neurons of AD and PD patients, indicative of disease severity. The activation of p38 is more evident in middle-stage AD, indicating it as an early event in AD pathology.45 Modulating the p38MAPK pathway activates MAPK-activated protein kinase 2 (MK2) in the CNS, which controls the activation of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and neurotoxic substances in the brain, resulting in neuroinflammation. TREM22 and CD33, two immunological receptors linked to AD, are regulated by MK2. As p38MAPK has several physiological substrates while MK2 is only involved in regulating downstream inflammatory cascades, blocking MK2 has a distinct advantage over directly inhibiting p38MAPK and has a lower risk of side effects. Targeting MK2 paves the way for providing efficient therapy in neuroinflammation related to AD.46 The cdc2-like kinases (CLKs) belong to cyclin-dependent kinases, mitogen-activated protein kinases, glycogen synthase kinases, CDK-like kinases, and isoforms CLK1, CLK2, CLK3, and CLK4. CLK1 is majorly involved in the pathophysiology of AD by carrying out phosphorylation of the serine residue in serine and arginine-rich proteins. CLK regulates the alternative splicing of the microtubule-associated protein tau. After being phosphorylated, serine and arginine-rich proteins move within the nucleoplasm and bind to the ribonucleoprotein involved in splicing.47

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a bradykinetic condition marked by the loss of pars compacta nigral cells and aggregated α-synuclein accumulation in the brain stem, spinal cord, and cortical areas.48 The pathogenesis of PD has been linked to several kinases, including leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2), protein kinase B (Akt), GSK-3β and GSK-3α, cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5), and c-Abelson kinase (c-Abl).49 CK1 and CK2 phosphorylate α-synuclein (present in Lewy bodies) at Ser129, and therefore prove their role in PD.50 Parkin gene phosphorylation via CK1 and CDK5 reduces its solubility, resulting in accumulation and inactivation.51 Abelson Murine Leukemia Viral Oncogene Homolog 1, c-Abl (ABL1) is found in PD patients in its active form and is responsible for phosphorylating genetic risk factors for PD, including α-synuclein at Y39 and the E3 ligase parkin.52 Deleting the ABL1 gene curtailed neuropathology and α-synuclein aggregation along with protective effects against MPTP or 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1, 2, 3, 6-tetrahydropyridine, responsible for aggravating PD symptoms by deletion of the ABL1 gene.53

LRRK2 and PTEN-induced kinase 1(PINK1) are two kinases mutated in PD and have the potential to act as PD drug targets. LRRK2 is expressed highly in several brain regions, immune cells, kidneys, and lungs, involving processes at the cellular level like microtubule binding, vesicular trafficking, autophagy, and mitophagy. Besides, autosomal dominant mutations in LRRK2 play an essential role in both familial and sporadic PD.54 Post-mortem of substantia nigra neurons of sporadic PD patients was suggestive of activated LRRK2 kinase. Phosphorylated tau and α-synuclein were found to be elevated in post-mortem studies of PD striata, indicating that they might be contributors to mutations in LRRK2.55 Homozygous missense mutations in the PINK1 gene are the reason for familial PD; these mutations are the second most cause of recessive PD, and PARK6 autosomal recessive PD is linked to PINK1. PINK1 mutations, unlike LRRK2, limit kinase activity and generate an atypical Parkinson's disease with an earlier onset and slower clinical progression. The temporal cortex, amygdala, substantia nigra, cerebellar Purkinje cells, and the dentate nucleus all have high levels of PINK1 Mrna.56 Polo-like kinases (PLKs) comprise subfamilies including PLK1, PLK4, and PLK2 further containing, PLK2, PLK3, and PLK5. PLK1-3 carries out phosphorylation of α-synuclein. While PLK2 and PLK3 phosphorylate it directly at Ser129 as observed in vitro studies, PLK4 and PLK5 lacked this action at the site.57 G Protein-coupled receptor kinases (GRKs) consist of isoforms GRK1, GRK7, GRK2, GRK3, GRK4, GRK5, and GRK6, which are responsible for phosphorylating α-synuclein. GRK2 is known to phosphorylate α and β synuclein isoforms, whereas GRK5 phosphorylates Ser-129 of α-synuclein at the plasma membrane and induces its translocation to the perikaryal area along with the formation of soluble oligomers mostly in sporadic PD patients.58

Single nucleotide polymorphisms in the locus of cyclin G-associated kinase (GAK) were associated with PD susceptibility. For instance, the higher expression of -synuclein in the brain of PD patients is linked to the GAK SNP rs1564282.59 A c-JunN-terminal kinase (JNK) is known to be active in PD models, including LPS, MPTP, and 6-hydroxydopamine.60 MPTP-induced neurodegeneration in rodents is protected via the deletion of p38 substrate MK2 by reducing neuroinflammation caused due to MPTP lesions.61 Inhibiting GSK3β acts as a protective factor against MPTP, 6-OHDA, and neurotoxicity induced by LPS. Phosphorylated GSK3β was found in neurons of substantia nigra and striatum of PD patients, and this phosphorylation is correlated with tau and α-synuclein pathology. Rho-associated kinase (ROCK) inhibition gives protection against cell death (induced by MPTP) of dopaminergic neurons because of its axon-stabilising properties.62

Kinase inhibitors in treating PD (preclinical and clinical perspective)

LRRK2 kinase inhibitors

Kramer et al. documented that staurosporine (Fig. 3A) is a widely used ATP-competitive kinase inhibitor with IC50 values ranging from 0.2 to 40 nM. Its inhibitory activity against LRRK2 was demonstrated using in vitro assays, including radioactive, TR-FRET, and AlphaScreen assays.63 The isoindolinone derivatives of staurosporine, including K-252a, K-252b, and Gö6976, also inhibited LRRK2. K-252a inhibited both WT and G2019S LRRK2 with reported IC50 values of approximately 25 nM (WT) and 2.8 nM (G2019S), whereas K-252b and Gö6976 inhibited WT LRRK2 with IC50 values of approximately 50 nM and 250 nM, respectively (Fig. 3A).63 The IC50 values reported by Kramer et al. are extracted by them from various other studies that have used different enzyme constructs, substrates, ATP concentrations, and assay platforms (radioactive, TR-FRET, and AlphaScreen), direct comparison between studies should be interpreted carefully.63

Kinase inhibitors in treating Parkinson's disease: LRRK2 kinase inhibitors [A], c-Abl inhibitors [B], and ROCK inhibitor [C].

Indazolyl-spiro hexane-carbonitrile derivatives, such as LRRK2 Inhibitors were synthesised, and LRRK2 Km ATP LanthaScreen Assay was performed to screen their potency. Compound 1 (Fig. 3A) had the highest potency with an IC50 value of 0.9 nM.64 LRRK2-IN-1 (Fig. 3A), a diaminopyrimidine scaffold and ATP competitive kinase inhibitor, is recognised as the first-generation potent and selective LRRK2 kinase inhibitor against LRRK2 wild-type and G2019S mutant and performs actions by suppressing LRRK2 activity by dephosphorylating Ser910/Ser935, removing 14-3-3 protein binding and LRRK2 build-up in aggregate fibrillar structures.65 It was observed that it could inhibit LRRK2 at a dose of 1–3 µM in HEK293 cells, and it possesses an IC50 value equivalent to 13 nM. It also exhibited an excellent pharmacokinetic profile with a half-life of 4.5 hours as studied in mice. Although highly selective for LRRK2, it exhibits limited blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration due to its high Caco-2 efflux ratio and molecular size. It also demonstrated cytotoxicity with IC50 value of 49.3 µM in HepG2 cells and showed genotoxicity in the GreenScreen HC assay.66 Koshibu K. et al. carried out a comparative study between LRRK2-IN-1 and compound 2 (Fig. 3A), patented by GSK. In vitro assay revealed that compound 2 had a higher clearance as compared to LRRK2-IN-1. In vivo pharmacokinetics study carried out in rats by administering a 10 mg kg−1 dose of each intervention indicated that compound 2 had good exposure in the brain with the increased brain-to-plasma ratio compared to LRRK2-IN-1.67 Compound 2 possessed IC50 values against LRRK2 wild-type and G2019S mutant equivalent to 7 and 36 nM. Overall, compound 2 had an excellent pharmacokinetic profile with no toxicity demonstrated and improved kinase selectivity compared with LRRK2-IN-1. Kinase profiling showed that LRRK2 was the only kinase inhibited above 80% by compound 2, whereas LRRK2-IN-1 inhibited seven additional off-target kinases, including ERK5 (MAPK7), Aurora Kinase A (AURKA), and doublecortin-like kinase 2 (DCLK2). These findings suggest that compound 2 is a promising and highly selective LRRK2 inhibitor that warrants further investigation.67

CZC-54252 and CZC-25146, sunitinib analogues, are potent LRRK2 inhibitors which have been identified using a TR-FRET kinase assay. CZC-54252 exhibited IC50 values of 1.28 and 1.85 nM against wild-type and G2019S LRRK2 respectively, whereas CZC-25146 had IC50 values of 4.76 and 6.87 nM against wild-type and G2019S LRRK2 respectively. Although both compounds exhibited poor Blood–Brain Barrier (BBB) penetration, with a brain-to-plasma ratio ∼4.1% for CZC-25146, the latter demonstrated favourable pharmacokinetic properties, including a clearance of 2.3 L h−1 kg−1, a volume of distribution of 5.4 L kg−1, a terminal half-life of 1.6 h following IV administration and oral bioavailability of 133%.68 TAE684 is a diaminopyrimidine ATP competitive kinase inhibitor (Fig. 3A) exhibited IC50 values of 7.8 and 6.1 nM against wild type and G2019S LRRK2, respectively, and effectively inhibited Ser910/Ser935 phosphorylation in vivo. However, reduced selectivity and limited brain activity restricted its further development.69

HG-10-102-01 (Fig. 3A), a diaminopyrimidine-based compound was identified to be the first selective LRRK2 inhibitor capable of crossing the BBB and blocking Ser910/Ser935 phosphorylation in the mouse brain following intraperitoneal treatment of dosages as low as 50 mg kg−1.70 It exhibited IC50 values of 20.3 nM and 3.2 nM against wild type and G2019S mutant of LRRK2. G1023 (also known as GNE-1023; Fig. 3A), is a next-generation aminopyrimidine LRRK2 inhibitor developed through optimization of HG-10-102-01 scaffold, it has shown improved metabolic stability, kinase selectivity, and CNS exposure while maintaining potent inhibition of LRRK2. It exhibited potent biochemical inhibition of LRRK2 with an IC50 value of approximately 2 nM and served as the basis for the subsequent development of brain-penetrant LRRK2 inhibitors.70–72 MLi-2 (Fig. 3A) developed by Merck, is a highly potent and selective LRRK2 inhibitor with IC50 values of 0.76 nM in vitro and 1.4 nM in vivo, oral bioavailability, and good BBB penetrability. Although lung changes were observed in animal studies, these effects were reversible and non-pathological.73,74 PF-06447475 (Fig. 3A) is a second-generation pyrrolopyrimidine LRRK2 inhibitor identified by Pfizer, possessing high potency, selectivity, and BBB penetration with IC50 values of 3 nM and 11 nM against wild-type and G2019S LRRK2, respectively. However, suboptimal pharmacokinetics limited its progression.73 GNE-7915 and GNE-0877 (also known as DNL201) (Fig. 3A) are potent and brain-penetrant LRRK2 inhibitors developed by Genentech. GNE-7915 exhibited high solubility and potency but caused reversible lung changes in non-human primates, while GNE-0877 showed improved potency with an IC50 of 3 nM.75

GSK2578215A (Fig. 3A) is a second-generation arylbenzamide LRRK2 inhibitor, demonstrated neuroprotective effects by inducing autophagy in dopaminergic cell models, with IC50 values of 10.9 nM and 8.9 nM against wild-type and G2019S LRRK2. BMPBB-32, a structurally related compound, showed improved brain penetration and selective inhibition of LRRK2.76 5′-Deoxyadenosylcobalamin (AdoCbl) (Fig. 3A): AdoCbl is an active form of vitamin B12 and acts as an LRRK2 inhibitor as identified through a high-throughput screen. It works by targeting the allosteric site of the LRRK2 kinase domain and disrupting LRRK2 homodimerisation and neuron loss rescue. However, very high concentrations were used in in vivo studies raising the issue of using it for pharmacological treatment.77

CDK5/p25 inhibitor

Truncated Peptide 5 (TP5) possesses neuroprotective and neurorestorative actions. TP5 inhibits the increased CDK5 activation, and it has a stronger binding affinity to CDK5. It also protects dopaminergic neurons.78

c-Abl inhibitors

c-Abl inhibitors are known to improve motor behaviour, inhibit the loss of dopamine neurons and CDK5 phosphorylation, regulate α-synuclein phosphorylation and suppress tyrosine phosphorylation of parkin. The drugs studied as c-Abl inhibitors include nilotinib, imatinib, and bafetinib (Fig. 3B). Nilotinib augmented the stability of parkin and its interaction with Beclin-1 along with the degradation of intracellular α-syn aggregates. It possessed the properties of crossing BBB and preventing the loss of dopamine transporters.79 Imatinib can cross the cell membrane as observed in cultures of primary brain microvessel endothelial cells and hindered MPTP-induced depletion of striatal dopamine. Bafetinib could enhance apical-to-basolateral transport across the monolayers of primary brain microvessel endothelial cells and hampered MPTP-induced decrease of dopamine, 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid, and homovanillic acid.80

ROCK inhibitors

ROCK inhibitor fasudil (Fig. 3C) reduces ROCK activity, attenuates l-DOPA-induced dyskinesia, and provides dopaminergic neuroprotection in PD models.81 It exhibits an IC50 value of approximately 0.74 µM against ROCK1 and ROCK2.82

Kinase inhibitors under clinical trials

LRRK2 inhibitors DNL201 and DNL151 developed by Denali Therapeutics demonstrated safety, tolerability, and target specificity in early clinical studies, achieving significant inhibition of LRRK2 activity. Advanced clinical development of DNL151 is ongoing in collaboration with Biogen.83 Abl tyrosine kinase inhibitor K0706 (SCC-138) has also shown potential in preclinical PD models by preventing dopaminergic neuron loss and improving behavioural outcomes.84

Kinase inhibitors in Alzheimer's disease

Dual-specificity tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinase-1A inhibitors

Patients suffering from AD have shown upregulation of Dual-specificity tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinase-1A (DYRK1A) in the brain's frontal area. It belongs to the group of CMGC kinase and is encoded on the 21q22.2 chromosome.85 A link was found between cell signalling pathways concerned with neurodegenerative disorders and their phosphorylation by DYRK1A. In the brain, DYRK1A can activate signalling pathways such as the PI3K/Akt (phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B) and ASK/JNK1 (Apoptosis signal-regulating kinase/c-Jun N-terminal kinase), which are linked to neuronal death in pathological circumstances.86

Harmine (Fig. 4A), a type of β-carboline alkaloid, suppresses DYRK1A at IC50 of 80 nM. It even blocks the phosphorylation of tau protein in neurofibrillary tangles when studied on the H4 neuroglioma cell model and in vitro at IC50 of 700 nM.87 Another DYRK1A inhibitor EGCg (Epigallocatechin-3-gallate) (Fig. 4A), which is present in green tea as polyphenolic catechin, has IC50 as 330 nM. It has shown substantial improvement of cognitive functions in the mouse model and reduction in Aβ aggregation.88 Leucettamine B (Fig. 4A), marine 2-Aminoimidazolone alkaloids, and its derivative leucettine L41 (Fig. 4A) inhibit DYRK1A at IC50 of 60 nM and 95 nM, respectively. Leucettine L41 has shown DYRK1A inhibition in HT22 cell lines, showing protection against neurodegenerative pathways and preventing apoptotic actions generated by APP (Amyloid Precursor Proteins) and glutamate.89 Although these naturally derived compounds exhibit kinase–inhibitory activity in vitro and have demonstrated neuroprotective effects in preclinical studies, but clinical translation as kinase-targeted therapy remains uncertain due to limited bioavailability and suboptimal brain exposure, which may prevent attainment of concentrations needed for sustained kinase inhibition in vivo.90,91

Kinase inhibitors in Alzheimer's disease: dual-specificity tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinase-1A inhibitors [A], thiadiazolidinones [B], extracellular signal-regulated kinase-2 inhibitors [C], cyclin-dependent kinase 5 inhibitors [D], and multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor [E].

SP600125 (Fig. 4A) is a C-Jun N-terminal kinase-3(JNK3) inhibitor, a member of the JNK kinases family. JNK3 was found to be aggressive in the brains of AD patients and phosphorylate tau protein in paired helical filaments. JNK3 is also responsible for colonising neurofibrillary tangles and Aβ deposits in numerous animal models.92 SP600125 inhibits okadaic acid-induced phosphorylation in cultured hippocampus neuronal models and reduces tau phosphorylation in Ser422 (Phospho-Tau) at IC50 of 7.2 µM.93

Multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor

Masitinib (Fig. 4B) is an orally active multitargeted/multikinase tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting KIT, LYN, FYN, and CSF1R, with therapeutic effects in AD. It modulates multiple signalling pathways involved in mast cell activation, neuroinflammation, neuronal survival, and microglial function, all of which have been linked in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders.94 It has been evaluated as an adjunct to standard treatment in patients with mild-to-moderate AD. Its efficacy has been established in a randomised Phase-3, placebo-controlled trial (AB09004 study), where masitinib (4.5 mg per kg per day) improved cognitive outcomes when compared to placebo with a considerable safety profile.24

Cyclin-dependent kinase 5 inhibitors

Cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) is a proline-directed Ser/Thr (Serine/Threonine) kinase and is activated when it binds tightly with neuron-specific proteins such as p35. When there is excessive cellular stress, the p35 is cleaved into p25, leading to the activation of CDK5. Increased levels of p35 and CDK5 are directly linked with AD. The cleavage of tau protein by CDK5/p25 complex is more aggressive than CDK5/p35.95 Bellidin, 4-aminothiazole derivative-1, and diazaphenanthrene (Fig. 4C) were reported as CDK5/p25 with inhibition properties with IC50 values of 0.2 µM, 17 µM, and 2.0 µM. 4-Aminothiazole derivative-2 (Fig. 4C) was also reported to inhibit GSK3-β and CDK2 at IC50 of 45 nM and 25 nM, respectively. Derivatives of roscovitine derivatives (1 and 2) (Fig. 4) inhibited CK1 and CDK5. Derivative-1 had IC50 130 nM against CDK5 and 600 nM for CK1. In the case of derivative-2, IC50 against CDK5 was 80 nM and 14 nM for CK1. Both the derivatives showed inhibition to produce Aβ40 in N2A cells.96

Thiadiazolidinones

In transgenic mouse models, thiadiazolidinones (Fig. 4D) showed a reduction in amyloid deposition, a reduction in memory impairment, and lowered raised levels of tau phosphorylation. These were labelled the first non-competitive inhibitors of ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and GSK-3 (Glycogen synthase kinase 3). Various thiadiazolidinone derivatives were studied for their mechanism of potent inhibition against GSK-3 through structure–activity relationship analysis. It was concluded that the presence of heteroaromatic rings in the compounds leads to modulation in the GSK-3 activity.97 The study also revealed the presence of aromatic substitutes on two nitrogen atoms of the thiadiazolidinone ring, which influenced the inhibitory potential. Cultured neuronal cells treated with thiadiazolidinones showed a reduction in tau phosphorylation, and thiadiazolidinones such as NP031112 (also called Tideglusib), NP00111 and NP03115 (Fig. 4D) were neuroprotective in various animal models.26,98

Extracellular signal-regulated kinase-2 inhibitors

Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase-2 phosphorylates all the 17 Ser/Thr–Pro (Serine/Threonine–Proline)components in tau protein. In the post-mortem brains of AD patients, raised levels of extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase-2 were found in the neurons along with an accumulation of neurofibrillary tangles and hyperphosphorylated tau proteins. SRN-003-556 (Fig. 4E) showed inhibition for extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase-2 at IC50 of 0.6 µM in the hippocampus of an adult rat.99 SRN-003-556 reduced tau hyperphosphorylation induced by okadaic acid at IC50 of 0.6 µM in AP422 and AT8 epitope sites. JNPL3 transgenic mice having mutant human P301L 4R0N tau, showed a delay in developing motor deficits.100

Challenges in targeting protein kinases in CNS disorders

Despite the promising therapeutic potential of protein kinase inhibitors in neurodegenerative diseases, several critical challenges limit their clinical translation. One of the primary obstacles is the restricted penetration of BBB, which significantly reduces drug delivery to the CNS. Many kinase inhibitors are substrates of efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein, further limiting their brain availability.7 Another major concern is the lack of selectivity, as the conserved nature of ATP-binding sites across kinases often leads to off-target interactions and associated toxicity. This can result in undesirable systemic side effects, especially during long-term treatment required for chronic neurodegenerative conditions. Pharmacokinetic limitations, including rapid metabolism mediated by cytochrome P450 enzymes, poor bioavailability, and suboptimal half-life, also hinder therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, the complexity of neurodegenerative diseases, involving multiple signaling pathways and overlapping pathological mechanisms, makes it difficult to achieve sufficient disease modification by targeting a single kinase.7,8 Emerging strategies such as the development of brain-penetrant kinase inhibitors, allosteric modulators, and targeted protein degradation approaches like proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) offer promising solutions. Furthermore, combination therapies targeting multiple pathways may enhance therapeutic outcomes in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease. Conclusions and future perspectives

In summary, the development of further evidence has reinforced the importance of protein kinase dysregulation in the pathogenesis of AD and PD. These protein kinases, which include GSK3β, CDK5, DYRK1A, p38 MAPK, LRRK2, c-Abl, and ROCK, mediate critical pathological events, which range from abnormal protein phosphorylation to amyloid-β aggregation, tau hyperphosphorylation, mitochondrial malfunction, neuroinflammation, and synaptic damage. In this regard, protein kinase inhibitors hold promise for emerging therapeutic strategies for neurodegenerative diseases. However, current clinical evidence supporting their disease-modifying efficacy in AD and PD remains limited. Most of the kinase inhibitors that have been studied for their efficacy in addressing AD and PD so far were initially used for cancer or inflammation and later repurposed for neurodegenerative diseases with many challenges associated with BBB penetration, long-term safety, and toxicity profiles. Although several of them, including LRRK2 and c-Abl kinase inhibitors, have been in clinical trials, their disease-modifying benefits remain elusive. Current trends in the study of protein kinase inhibitors in neurodegenerative disorders will involve the translation of the following studies: the design of brain-permeable mutation-selective protein kinase inhibitors that target LRRK2, GSK-3β, and CDK5 kinases and the design of non-ATP competitive inhibitors to reduce off-target-related toxicity. Kinase degrader approaches, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), and domain-specific kinase inhibitors represent emerging strategies developed to overcome the limitations of conventional ATP-competitive inhibitors in neurodegenerative disease therapy. The use of downstream inflammatory markers such as MK2 is another current trend that will improve the effectiveness of protein kinase inhibitors in the near future. Future therapeutic success will depend on the development of CNS-optimized and highly selective kinases modulators. Future studies on protein kinase inhibitors in the treatment of AD and PD will focus on improving their selectivity, CNS penetration, and long-term safety profiles.
