‘Certainly not in the heterosexual public eye’: Qualitative study of the contexts of syphilis transmission among heterosexually-identifying people in England
Authors: Megan Walsh, David Reid, Josh Forde, Lynsey Emmett, Medhat Basta, David Phillips, Malini Raychaudhuri, Freddy Green, Will Nutland, Alison R. Howarth, Mateo Prochazka, Gwenda Hughes, Kirsty Foster, Catherine H. Mercer, Hamish Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent increases in syphilis diagnoses among heterosexual individuals are a growing public health concern. The NEXUS study aimed to qualitatively assess the contexts surrounding syphilis transmission among heterosexually-identifying individuals in England.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with heterosexually-identifying individuals diagnosed with primary, secondary, or early latent syphilis in the previous year, and with healthcare professionals (HCPs) involved in syphilis management. Participants were recruited from sexual health services across three regions in England between December 2023 and September 2024. A qualitative descriptive and interpretive approach was used. Transcripts were analysed using framework analysis, incorporating deductive and inductive coding. Service user and HCP data were triangulated to compare perspectives and enhance credibility.ResultsNineteen service users (53% male, 74% White British) and seven HCPs were interviewed. Syphilis was reported to occur in the context of condomless vaginal or oral sex. Many heterosexual individuals perceived low STI risk due to few partners, presumed exclusivity, or isolated changes in behaviour. Syphilis knowledge was limited and the infection was often viewed as rare or historical. Symptom recognition was poor, particularly for non-genital symptoms, and some service users initially sought care in non-sexual health settings, contributing to delayed diagnosis. HCPs highlighted similar gaps in awareness among non-sexual health clinicians. Following diagnosis, awareness of STI risk increased and most service users described intentions to reduce risk, although understanding of infectiousness and follow-up remained variable.ConclusionsFindings highlight gaps in awareness, risk perception, and clinical recognition of syphilis among heterosexual populations and non-sexual health HCPs. Public health strategies should emphasise syphilis as relevant to all sexually active people, raise awareness, and support earlier diagnosis by non-sexual health HCPs.

Introduction

Syphilis is a bacterial infection caused by Treponema pallidum and is transmitted through sexual contact, as well as vertically during pregnancy. 1 Disease progression is considered in stages based on clinical presentation including primary, secondary, early latent (when diagnosed within the first two years of infection), late latent, and tertiary; the first three stages are the most infectious and represent early syphilis. 2 Although syphilis can be effectively treated with antibiotics, untreated infection can result in chronic and serious complications, including neurological, cardiovascular, and multi-system disease. Congenital syphilis, resulting from transmission during pregnancy, can lead to miscarriage, stillbirth, or neonatal death, 3 and is a key public health concern.

The number of infectious syphilis diagnoses in England has increased by 184% over the past decade, from 3,345 in 2013 to 9,513 in 2023. 4 Most diagnoses are among gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (GBMSM) (76% of diagnoses in 2023 4 ), but there have been larger proportional increases among heterosexual men and women in recent years. There were increases of 65% (499 to 825) in women who have sex with men (WSM) and 33% (851 to 1,133) in men who exclusively have sex with women (MSW) between 2021 (the year national COVID-19 lockdowns ended) and 2023, compared to a 28% increase (5,082 to 6,527) in GBMSM over the same period. 5 These trends are accompanied by a rise in congenital syphilis cases in England, increasing from one case in 2015 to eight cases in 2023, 3 reinforcing concerns about transmission within heterosexual populations and the potential for severe adverse outcomes.

The increase in syphilis diagnoses among heterosexual individuals, also observed across other nations of the UK and Europe,6–9 may be explained by several factors including changes in sexual practices. A greater understanding of the factors contributing to the rise in syphilis in heterosexuals could help identify and more effectively target interventions to reduce risk. The NEXUS study aimed to understand the risk factors, lifestyles, and contexts facilitating syphilis transmission among people in England who identify as heterosexual.

Methods

Design, setting, and context

NEXUS was a qualitative study that used semi-structured one-to-one interviews to explore experiences of heterosexual service users diagnosed with early syphilis and healthcare professionals (HCPs) involved in their care. An interpretivist framework approach guided the design to capture participants’ perspectives. 10

Participants were recruited between 8th November 2023 and 30th September 2024 from sexual health services (SHSs) in three NHS Trusts in England (South Tyneside and Sunderland (North East England), Cambridgeshire Community Services (East of England), and Croydon Health Services (London)). Sites were selected after using STI surveillance data to identify those diagnosing many heterosexual service users with syphilis across different regions of England. Interviews were also conducted with HCPs from participating SHSs and those involved in writing national syphilis management guidelines 11 to provide broader clinical context.

Patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE)

This study was informed by PPIE during protocol development. Draft materials were reviewed by clinicians, members of the British Association for Sexual Health and HIV, and sexual health community organisations (Terrence Higgins Trust and The Love Tank). Feedback informed eligibility criteria, provision of interpretation, and refinement of culturally appropriate interview topic guides, particularly given the stigma associated with syphilis.

Participants and recruitment

We used purposive sampling to recruit heterosexually-identifying service users and HCPs involved in their care. All recruitment was facilitated via participating SHSs. We invited service users who identified as heterosexual; were 18 years of age or older; had received a diagnosis of primary, secondary, or early latent syphilis in the previous 12 months at a participating SHS (the 12 months timeframe was applied from the date of first identifying eligible service users and did not include the time between identification and interview); could provide informed consent; and understood English sufficiently to arrange an interview. We offered an interpreter where needed. Eligibility was based on self-identified sexual orientation; participants were not excluded on the basis of gender identity, sexual behaviour, or sex work.

Eligible service users were identified through multiple routes: (1) by HCPs during confirmatory, treatment, or follow-up appointments; (2) through searches of SHS STI diagnosis surveillance data 12 conducted by MW, with depersonalised patient codes shared with clinic staff; and (3) by staff at SHSs through their electronic patient record systems.

HCPs explained the study to eligible service users in clinic or by telephone and recorded (in a service log) verbal permission to pass their contact details to the interviewer (DR). We provided study information sheets in English, Romanian, Lithuanian, Spanish, Portuguese, Bulgarian, and French; these languages were selected based on the countries of birth (where English is not the official language) of service users diagnosed with syphilis in the three study areas. Within one week, contact details were shared with the interviewer, who made up to three attempts to telephone each service user. Researchers documented all contact attempts and participation decisions, assigned recruited service users a unique study ID, and arranged interviews.

We invited HCPs (doctors, nurses, health advisers) who had recent experience of working with heterosexual individuals diagnosed with syphilis; understood English sufficiently to arrange an interview; and could provide informed consent. Principal investigators at each SHS nominated eligible staff. The study team also invited one HCP involved in national syphilis management guideline development. HCP participants reviewed an information sheet and provided consent.

For both groups, recruitment continued until we achieved sufficient information power 13 to address the study aim.

Ethical review

The London – Bromley Research Ethics Committee (Reference 23/LO/0699) reviewed and approved the study. Participants could withdraw at any time. All participants (both service users and HCPs) gave written or audio recorded consent, as preferred; we required written consent for service users who requested an interpreter. Data were stored on a secure, encrypted server accessible only to the researchers.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

Interviews were conducted by DR, a trained and experienced qualitative researcher. DR and MW, both working in sexual health research, led the analysis. Neither researcher had any relationship to participants. The interviewer demonstrated an ethnographic perspective informed by extensive experience in sexuality and sexual health research. The researchers sought to understand participants’ experiences and meaning within their medical, social, and cultural contexts. We engaged in reflexive discussion within the research team to consider how personal characteristics such as gender and age, professional experience, and researcher-participant interactions might influence data collection and interpretation.

Data collection

Interviews took place between December 2023 and September 2024 via Microsoft Teams, using audio or video according to participant preference. Service user interviews (84% audio, 16% video) lasted 60–90 min and HCP interviews (100% video) lasted 30–40 min; these were conducted concurrently. One service user interview involved an interpreter. Semi-structured topic guides (Supplemental material) explored syphilis testing and treatment experiences, sexual health communication needs, and lifestyle and social context for service users, and clinical management practices for HCPs. Guides were developed iteratively with input from stakeholders (see PPIE), ensuring relevance and acceptability. Before the interview, participants were briefed on the purpose, reminded they could withdraw at any time, and gave verbal consent for the recording to begin. At the end of the interview, participants were thanked and service users received a £40 gift card.

Data processing and analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim by Microsoft Teams; the interpreted interview was transcribed by a professional agency under a confidentiality agreement. DR and MW checked all transcripts for accuracy, anonymised them, and imported data into NVivo R1 (2020). De-identification procedures were applied to remove names, locations, and other identifiers.

A framework analysis approach14–16 provided a systematic structure for identifying patterns across cases while retaining attention to individual narratives. The process involved familiarisation, development of deductive and inductive coding indices, indexing, charting, and theme generation. DR and MW independently reviewed initial transcripts, compared interpretations, and agreed coding frameworks for service user and HCP data. Coding differences were resolved through discussion. Data were charted into matrices to support cross-case and within-case interpretation. Analysis was iterative and aligned with the interpretivist paradigm, 10 emphasising participants’ accounts and contextual meaning. MW and DR triangulated service user data and HCP data post-analysis by identifying comparable and contrasting themes between the two participant groups. Researcher and data triangulation and documentation of coding decisions and framework development enhanced credibility, trustworthiness, and confirmability.

Results

The time between syphilis diagnosis and interview varied between two weeks and 18 months (mean: 7 months), and seven service users had been diagnosed within the preceding three months. Service user interviews lasted between 52 and 98 min (mean: 74 min).

Contexts of syphilis acquisition and perceptions of sexual risk

Most service users felt confident identifying what they saw as the likely context of syphilis acquisition, drawing on their own partner timelines or assumptions about low partner numbers: ‘I hadn’t had sex like for a year before that’ (Service user 09). Around the time of diagnosis, approximately half reported one sexual partner, with the remainder reporting multiple partners. A quarter believed they acquired syphilis within a partnership initially perceived as exclusive, often following partner infidelity: ‘I’ve gone to get a new boyfriend who’s decided to cheat on [me] and just lumber [me] with that now’ (Service user 01). Some reported sexual encounters with casual one-off partners or with regular but non-exclusive partners, while few reported sex with sex workers (including one encounter outside the UK). One participant reported sex with a mixed-gender couple met via a swingers website.

Perceived STI risk was mitigated in some cases due to reliance on partner trust, exclusivity, or low partner numbers. HCPs noted that some heterosexual individuals diagnosed with syphilis would not have anticipated an STI diagnosis, and some service users reported little engagement with STI information: ‘I never really hear about them. So I just kind of like, forget they’re there’ (Service user 09). Although many had previously considered pregnancy and STI risks, pregnancy prevention was more salient, particularly among women using oral contraception who relied on partners for condom use: ‘It didn’t cross my mind, STIs. I thought everyone was clean’ (Service user 04).

Partners were commonly met through bars, clubs, dating apps, or social networks. Reported practices with these partners almost universally included vaginal intercourse and oral sex, while anal intercourse was uncommon. Condom use was rare, but more likely in contexts perceived as higher risk, such as new or paid-for sex partners; only two service users reported condom use for vaginal or anal sex, and none for oral sex: ‘I think it was only when it was penetration’ (Service user 02). Some described engaging in atypical practices, such as first experience of oral sex without a condom, or anal intercourse: ‘it was the only […] sexual partner who was performing oral sex without protection, without a condom’ (Service user 11). Some service users recognised potential transmission routes, including condomless oral sex or condom failure: ‘I thought you got less chance of catching it off oral but obviously I did’ (Service user 02).

Alcohol use influenced sexual decision-making for half of service users, with some acknowledging riskier behaviours when intoxicated: ‘I think if I was sober I would never do that’ (Service user 09). Following diagnosis, all reported increased awareness of STI risk, and most expressed intentions to change behaviour, including reducing partner numbers, improving condom use, moderating alcohol-related risk, and engaging in more regular STI testing: ‘I’ve learned my lesson’ (Service user 15).

Patient journey: symptoms to diagnosis to treatment

Most participants reported having, and wanting relief from, symptoms as reason for seeking testing and treatment (Table 2). Many service users suspected they had a STI before they had tested, but only five suspected they had syphilis; suspicions of syphilis mainly came from researching symptoms: ‘the sore it wasn’t going away, it was getting bigger and so I thought right, I’ll Google it, and syphilis came up straight away’ (Service user 04). The minority who did not suspect a STI reported seeking help from non-STI clinicians (including general practitioners (GPs), a dentist, an ophthalmologist, and an ear, nose, and throat (ENT) consultant) for non-genital symptoms, such as diffuse rash, mouth ulcers, eyesight problems, swollen lymph nodes, or neurological issues. This had sometimes led to delayed syphilis diagnosis: ‘‘don't worry, we will get to the bottom of it’, he said. ‘I'm ruling everything out that I can think of’ and eventually they came up with syphilis’ (Service user 14). HCPs noted gaps in the ability of some non-STI specialists to identify syphilis and described the importance of increasing awareness among HCPs to test people with different presentations due to syphilis ‘being the great mimicker’ (HCP 06). They suggested universal testing for syphilis to identify infections that would not otherwise be recognised: ‘We'd get a steady stream of diagnoses in those groups of people who have had unexplained presentations, sometimes for over a year and they've seen multiple, you know, specialists who've been unable to make a diagnosis. And eventually someone does a syphilis blood test’ (HCP 01).

Of the service users who tested due to partner notification, one was asymptomatic, and others attributed symptoms to allergies (rash), having dry sex (lacerations on penis), and a broken tooth (mouth sores): ‘I thought there is no point getting tested like I’m alright, I haven’t got anything’ (Service user 19).

Many service users described their syphilis diagnosis as shocking and embarrassing, often due to STI stigma: ‘people think […] you must be like sleeping around’ (Service user 09). Most did not share their diagnosis with anyone, fearing judgement: ‘it’s not something you want to go shouting from the rooftops’ (Service user 14). Those who did share said people they told were surprised but supportive.

At diagnosis, many focused solely on receiving treatment and struggled to absorb other information: ‘I don’t really care what it is, I just need the treatment’ (Service user 15). Penicillin injections were most common, followed by oral or intravenous antibiotics. Three service users received treatment before syphilis confirmation, one was unaware of their diagnosis until they returned for testing a year later: ‘I wasn’t aware for the whole year that that’s actually what I’d contracted’ (Service user 17). One HCP related other examples of this: ‘It might be that they had that explained to them a long time ago, and they’ve forgotten, […] they didn’t quite grasp it at the time, or it wasn’t explained properly.’ (HCP 06).

All service users were offered partner notification support, often simultaneously with treatment to prevent loss to follow-up: ‘once you let them out of the clinic, we don’t know whether we will be able to contact them’ (HCP 03). Some service users only informed recent or suspected acquisition partners. All service users were amenable to follow-up syphilis testing. HCPs reported varying success with follow-up: ‘I had many patients who did not come back for follow up. And this is a big problem.’ (HCP 05).

Knowledge and understanding of syphilis testing, treatment, and prevention

Before diagnosis, most service users reported having little to no knowledge of syphilis, with four having never heard of it: ‘I didn't really know what syphilis was’ (Service user 08). HCPs observed this low awareness: ‘You're using this word and term that no one's ever heard of. It's not really in the public, certainly not in the heterosexual public eye’ (HCP 04). Limited STI experience and understanding led some to initially equate syphilis with HIV, focusing on severity and treatment. Some contrasted syphilis with more familiar STIs like chlamydia or gonorrhoea, viewed as less severe and more easily treatable: 'I just knew it was a serious STI really […] because you know, everyone talks about like, chlamydia, gonorrhoea, they're all very treatable, very common, but like no one ever really speaks about syphilis or HIV’ (Service user 15). Although most service users had heard of syphilis, understanding was vague, often viewing it as archaic or rare. Most recognised it could be serious but lacked knowledge of symptoms: ‘I didn't really know anything about it […] I didn’t know any of the symptoms or anything’ (Service user 03). Only three service users knew it was bacterial or how it was treated.

Information sources varied. Many searched online after developing symptoms or while awaiting treatment, using Google, NHS pages, or social media forums: ‘I was […] on Reddit, I was just like scrolling for hours, researching about it’ (Service user 15). Verbal or written information from HCPs was trusted and valued, particularly regarding treatment and reassurance: ‘He reassured me that the medication would take it away and I trusted him, and it all worked out’ (Service user 04). A few had prior knowledge from sex education or media. Most expressed a desire for earlier general awareness, through mass media or community sources, to highlight risk and the existence or prevalence of syphilis: ‘Don’t just think ‘Oh, it'll never be me’’ (Service user 02). Others felt extra information would not have altered behaviour: ‘I don't think there's any information that […] could be given before that would, you know, change the way I acted’ (Service user 13), aligning with HCPs’ emphasis on regular testing. Some service users believed earlier awareness might have improved symptom recognition but not prevented acquisition: ‘it wouldn't have probably stopped us getting it, but it would have made us get a check quicker’ (Service user 12).

Some remained unsure about interpreting follow-up test results, including whether later results indicated past or active infection: ‘it means there’s still something there’ (Service user 14). Almost all understood syphilis is curable; half did not know that symptoms can take up to 12 weeks to appear. Understanding of infectiousness varied: some believed they could resume sex after two, four, or six weeks, while others waited for follow-up testing or were uncertain: ‘That's what I'm a little bit confused [about], even if I'm negative then […] I have to use the condoms’ (Service user 18). HCPs described using analogies and comparisons (e.g., chickenpox) to support comprehension of diagnosis, infectiousness, and future testing.

Discussion

We conducted a qualitative study of heterosexual service users and HCPs and found that syphilis transmission among people identifying as heterosexual in England was reported to occur in multiple contexts. Syphilis acquisition was unexpected due to low awareness and perceived risk and was reported in contexts of perceived exclusivity, few partners, or isolated changes in behaviour. Compared to other bacterial STIs, there was less knowledge and a greater degree of stigma associated with syphilis; limited knowledge from both heterosexual individuals and non-specialist HCPs contributed to delayed diagnoses.

Perceptions of risk were generally low, shaped by unawareness and beliefs that syphilis was rare or historical. These perceptions were reinforced by having few partners, presumed monogamy, or prioritising pregnancy prevention over STI prevention; similar patterns have been commonly reported among heterosexual populations.17,18 Such low perceived vulnerability is consistent with broader evidence: a nationally representative British survey found that heterosexual individuals often underestimate their risk of STIs, even when engaging in condomless sex with new or multiple partners. 19 Our qualitative data therefore reflect typical heterosexual risk perceptions but extend these findings by illustrating that syphilis appeared further outside awareness than other STIs, indicating a specific gap in knowledge and risk appraisal. This may have contributed to limited symptom recognition and delayed diagnoses when seeking care outside of SHSs. Condomless sex was widely reported, and trust in condom effectiveness for syphilis prevention varied.

There were few reported examples of same-sex encounters among service users or their partners, suggesting sex between men did not contribute to syphilis transmission in this sample. Substance use may influence risk; service users described alcohol-related riskier decision-making in the contexts surrounding syphilis acquisition, aligning with prior evidence linking substance use to partner numbers, condomless sex, and syphilis diagnosis.20,21

Our findings showed that a syphilis diagnosis was often unexpected by service users and non-sexual health HCPs alike. There was a lack of awareness of the potential for syphilis acquisition and symptoms; genital symptoms were suspected to be other STIs such as herpes and non-genital symptoms were attributed to other issues including allergies and oral health conditions. Some service users delayed healthcare seeking until partner notification; this mirrors prior observations of deferred help-seeking despite STI symptoms. 22

Syphilis testing was often not directly sought; many first sought information after symptoms developed and some initially attended non-sexual health services. Consistent with previous mixed-methods research, people often sought reassurance from others’ experiences and presented symptoms to GPs rather than attending SHSs. 23 In non-specialist settings, alternative investigations sometimes preceded syphilis testing, contributing to diagnosis delays. As highlighted by HCPs and England’s Syphilis Response Plan, awareness of syphilis epidemiology and presentation among non-sexual health HCPs should be strengthened. 24 There may be value in adopting an indicator condition approach, where syphilis testing is considered in the presence of key clinical presentations or associated diagnoses in non-specialist settings. Providing accurate, accessible information to both non-sexual health HCPs and the heterosexual population may facilitate timely syphilis testing and partner notification. 23 Social and mass media were viewed as potentially useful channels for syphilis health promotion among the heterosexual population, 25 including targeted promotion on online platforms used for meeting partners. Information on the ease of testing, and treatment may also help reduce stigma. 26 However, some service users felt pre-diagnosis messaging would not have altered behaviour, echoing evidence that risk perception is neither necessary nor sufficient for STI care-seeking. 19 Expanding opt-out screening (e.g., in emergency departments) in areas with high syphilis diagnosis rates in sexual health services may therefore identify undiagnosed infections.27–29

There were some outstanding knowledge needs post-diagnosis. One service user was unaware of their syphilis diagnosis and treatment until returning a year later; others were uncertain about infectiousness and prevention post-treatment. Improving understanding of diagnosis, follow-up testing, treatment efficacy, and future prevention is warranted; good practice could be shared among HCPs. Verbal explanations during face-to-face appointments and high-quality written information, tailored to health literacy levels, may support comprehension. 30

We found that a syphilis diagnosis often prompted intentions to change behaviour to reduce future STI risk (e.g., consistent condom use). This aligns with prior research showing post-diagnosis risk-reduction intentions and their enactment at 3-months follow-up. 31 Prioritising risk-reduction counselling for individuals who are less likely to change behaviour could help prevent reinfection, a key element of syphilis control.24,31 Delivering this sustainably requires attention to SHS capacity and maintaining skilled sexual health advisors (HCPs with expertise in partner notification for STIs).

This study has several strengths and limitations. It was designed and conducted by a multidisciplinary team with input from researchers, clinicians, and community-based organisations. It specifically focused on heterosexual individuals diagnosed with syphilis, a comparatively lower-prevalence population (than gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men) in England, providing insight into less commonly captured experiences. Heterosexual service users and expert HCPs were recruited across multiple SHSs, supporting breadth of insight. The demographic profile of service users broadly reflected national surveillance data on heterosexual individuals diagnosed with syphilis in England, where most are White British. 4 However, participants were recruited from selected geographical areas and may differ from those diagnosed elsewhere or those not accessing SHSs; transferability of findings to populations not engaging with SHSs may be more limited. Service users who agreed to participate may have been more engaged with their diagnosis or less affected by stigma. The mean interval of seven months since diagnosis may have introduced recall bias, though this reflects the practical challenges of recruiting people with recently diagnosed acute infections. 32 Researchers may have shaped interpretation; we mitigated this via team coding discussions and triangulation of service user and HCP narratives. We judged information power sufficient to address the study aim given sample specificity, cross-site recruitment, and the relevance and richness of participant data. 13 This study contributes evidence that syphilis is perceived as less of a priority than other STIs among heterosexual individuals.

In conclusion, the NEXUS study highlights important gaps in awareness, risk perception, and recognition of syphilis among heterosexually-identifying individuals and non-sexual health HCPs in England. These findings highlight the need to raise awareness of syphilis in heterosexual individuals and to reinforce messaging on how to reduce the risk of infection. Health promotion should emphasise syphilis as relevant to all sexually active people, and prioritise clear messaging on symptoms, testing, and treatment. Targeted education for non-sexual health HCPs, particularly in primary care and other non-sexual health services such as dermatology, neurology, and ophthalmology, may support earlier recognition and reduce diagnosis delays. Expanding accessible and opportunistic testing may improve case detection in populations less likely to attend SHSs. Alongside this, providing clear, accessible information to patients at and after diagnosis may support understanding, reduce stigma, and encourage engagement with follow-up and prevention. Together, these findings support a broader, system-wide response to syphilis that integrates public health messaging, clinical education, and testing strategies to improve detection and reduce harms due to untreated syphilis.
