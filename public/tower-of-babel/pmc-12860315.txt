Senile thoracolumbar tuberculosis with kyphosis and severe osteoporosis treated with polymethylmethacrylate-augmented screw fixation
Authors: B. Yogesh Kumar, Bharath Loganathan, D. Vijayakumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Elderly osteoporotic patients with kyphosis secondary to spinal tuberculosis (TB) are at high risk for implant failure. For these patients, polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA)-augmented pedicle screw fixation provides immediate stability but increases the risk of infection. Here, we evaluated the outcome for a 78-year-old osteoporotic female with TB-related kyphosis successfully managed with PMMA-augmented pedicle screw fusion.Case Description: A 78-year-old female presented with mid-back pain, progressive kyphotic deformity, and increasing paraparesis. The magnetic resonance imaging revealed infectious T12-L1 spondylitis with accompanying vertebral body destruction/kyphosis. She underwent a single-stage posterior PMMA-augmented screw fixation from T9-L4 supplemented with anterior reconstruction and cage/bone grafting. The histology/cultures were consistent with spinal TB for which she received anti-tuberculosis therapy, followed by once weekly teriparatide (60 µg for 6 weeks). Neurological recovery was complete within 3 months, and by 2 years, the imaging confirmed a stable fusion.Conclusion: A 78-year-old osteoporotic female with T12/L1 TB-related spondylitis successfully underwent selective PMMA-augmented pedicle screw fixation from T9-L4 supplemented with anti-tubercular therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Spinal tuberculosis (TB) is the most common form of osteoarticular TB, accounting for 50% of all bone and joint cases. Notably, many of these patients present with significant TB-related spinal deformity/instability warranting fusion.[1] Elderly patients with spinal TB are more likely to have attendant osteoporosis and are therefore at increased risk of implant failure with spinal instrumentation.[3] To enhance pedicle screw purchase and stability in these osteoporotic patients with TB-related deformity, polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) provides immediate stability.[4] Here, a 78-year-old female with T12-L1 spinal TB, osteoporosis, and deformity was successfully managed with multilevel PMMA pedicle screw fixation.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 78-year-old osteoporotic female presented with 2 months of pain and 1 month of increasing lower extremity paraparesis. On examination, she had dorsolumbar kyphosis, localized tenderness, paraspinal muscle spasm, 3/5 bilateral lower extremity weakness, and hyperactive deep tendon reflexes, including Babinski responses; nevertheless, she had intact sensation and sphincter control. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein levels were high. X-ray and computed tomography (CT) imaging showed T12/L1 vertebral body collapse with kyphosis of 64°, severe osteoporosis, and osteolytic vertebral body destruction with retropulsion [Figures 1a-c]. The magnetic resonance imaging confirmed T12/L1 vertebral body destruction with bony retropulsion causing cord compression plus a paravertebral abscess [Figure 1d]. The positron emission tomography-CT ruled out cancer and documented the absence of additional spinal lesions.

Surgery

The patient underwent a single-stage PMMA-augmented screw fixation from T9-L4 accompanied by anterior reconstruction; the kyphosis was correlated with a cage/bone grafting. PMMA-augmented screws were placed away from the infected lesion, while standard screws were used adjacent to the infected sites. The anterior column was reconstructed utilizing a titanium cage packed with autologous bone graft, followed by posterior column shortening/kyphosis correction to 22° [Figures 2a and b].

(a and b) Postoperative X-ray anteroposterior showing polymethylmethacrylate-augmented screw fixation away from the active disease.

Pathology and postoperative course

Histopathology confirmed the presence of TB in the lumbar vertebral bodies. The patient received anti-tubercular therapy for the next 12 months, followed by 6 weeks of treatment with teriparatide and pulse teriparatide therapy with a customized Taylor’s brace. She neurologically fully recovered within 3 postoperative months by which time the CT showed stability of the construct and progression toward fusion [Figures 3a-c]. At 2 postoperative years, the X-rays and CT studies documented a stable fusion without recurrence of TB.

(a-c) Computed tomography sagittal, axial &amp; 3D view showed an increase in cancellous and cortical bone growth around the cage with enhanced stability after 6 weeks of treatment with teriparatide.

DISCUSSION

TB as a medical disease

Spinal TB in elderly osteoporotic patients can lead to vertebral collapse, kyphotic deformity, and neurological deficits.[9,11] Early diagnosis and appropriate medical or surgical management are crucial to prevent deformity and improve outcomes. In this case, after 2 months of symptoms, the patient’s T12/L1TB-related vertebral collapse/deformity was successfully managed with a single-stage circumferential decompression and fusion from T9-L4 accompanied by anterior reconstruction and anti-tubercular therapy.

Rationale for using PMMA screws

Among available options – expandable, cortical trajectory, or HA-coated screws – PMMA-augmented pedicle screws provide excellent immediate fixation in osteoporotic bone.[3,7,10] Choma et al.[3] reported a five-fold increase in pull-out strength with 2 mL of bone cement. Paré et al.[7] and Amendola et al.[1] also found significant improvement in fixation strength using small-volume PMMA through fenestrated screws. Liu et al.[5] demonstrated optimal screw fixation in osteoporotic vertebrae using controlled cement volume.

Planning of PMMA screws to avoid spread of infection

Cement injection into diseased or infected vertebrae can increase infection risk.[12] Zou et al.[12] described TB infection following vertebroplasty and kyphoplasty. In the present case, PMMA screws were inserted before decompression and debridement, and PMMA was avoided near the infected focus, likely reducing the risk of reinfection.

Techniques to avoid cement leakage

Cement leakage is a known complication of PMMA augmentation, though usually asymptomatic.[1,5,7,10] Liu et al.[5] reported that an injection volume between 1.5 and 3.0 mL is safe and effective. In our case, 1.5 mL of cement was injected in the dough phase under continuous fluoroscopy, preventing leakage.

Effect of postoperative weekly teriparatide

Teriparatide (parathyroid hormone 1–34) activates osteoblasts, increases bone mineral density, and prevents vertebral fractures.[6,8] Weekly administration has been reported to enhance bone healing in severe osteoporosis.[8] In our patient, six once-weekly doses likely contributed to improved fusion and sagittal correction – from 64° preoperative kyphosis to 22° postoperative alignment. CONCLUSION

We successfully fused a 78-year-old osteoporotic female with T12-L1 TB and vertebral collapse/deformity utilizing PMMA to augment pedicle screw fixation from T9-L4 accompanied by anterior reconstruction and anti-tubercular therapy.
