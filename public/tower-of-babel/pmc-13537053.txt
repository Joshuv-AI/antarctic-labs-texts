Epidemiology of olfactory dysfunction in China: a nationwide population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Shuting Yu, Wenwen Diao, Xiaoli Zhu, Huijing He, Yaoda Hu, Guangliang Shan, Xingming Chen, Yingying Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlfactory dysfunction is associated with adverse health outcomes such as neurodegenerative diseases. However, few large-scale epidemiological studies in China have focused on olfactory function using psychophysical testing. This study seeks to investigate the prevalence of olfactory dysfunction among healthy adults through a large-scale, nationwide survey and to examine its associations with demographic and other factors.MethodsThe study was conducted across multiple locations in China between 2023 and 2024, involving local residents aged 20 and above. A total of 12,153 participants underwent the T&T olfactometer test and completed a questionnaire survey. We reported the epidemiological burden of olfactory dysfunction and identify its demographic, behavioural and clinical correlates.ResultsThe overall prevalence of olfactory dysfunction in the participants was 15.3%. Age (PR increased with age, ≥70 years PR = 54.70; 95% CI, 17.60–170.08; p < 0.01), rural residence (PR = 1.93; 95% CI, 1.76–2.12; p < 0.01), current smoking (PR = 1.20; 95% CI, 1.04–1.39; p < 0.01) and self-reported olfactory dysfunction (PR = 3.97; 95% CI, 3.61–4.37; p < 0.01) were independently associated with a higher prevalence of olfactory dysfunction, while being female (PR = 0.78; 95% CI, 0.71–0.85; p < 0.01), having more years of education (High school PR = 0.60; 95% CI, 0.54–0.66; College and above PR = 0.45; 95% CI, 0.38–0.52; p < 0.01), and higher income level (p < 0.01) were independently associated with lower prevalence.ConclusionLarge-scale olfactory screening with the T&T olfactometer is viable. The prevalence of olfactory dysfunction demonstrates a progressive increase with advancing age. Independent factors associated with olfactory dysfunction include male gender, residing in rural areas, having lower educational levels, low income, current smoking and self-reported olfactory dysfunction.Keywords: Healthy population, olfactory dysfunction, prevalence, factors, China

Introduction

Olfaction refers to the chemical perception of volatile odorants and is closely intertwined with daily human life. From an evolutionary perspective, its primary function was to serve as an early warning system for detecting imminent dangers such as fire, air pollution and spoiled food [1,2]. Additionally, olfaction plays a role in social communication. In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to olfactory dysfunction, which mainly includes hyposmia and anosmia, due to its relationship with neurodegenerative disorders. Olfactory dysfunction is common in the general population, particularly among the elderly, yet it often remains undiagnosed [3]. Objective assessments estimate the prevalence of olfactory dysfunction at 28.8% in the general population, whereas subjective self-reports suggest a prevalence of approximately 9.5% [3]. Due to variations in assessment methodologies, reported prevalence proportions across different studies have ranged from 1.4% to 62.4% [3].

To date, there is no standardised diagnostic protocol for olfactory dysfunction. Although olfactory event-related potentials and olfactory electroencephalograms can provide objective measures of olfactory function, their application as diagnostic tools remains limited. Common psychophysical tests include the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT), the Sniffin’ Sticks test and the T&T olfactometer [4–6]. UPSIT is a single-use, relatively expensive test that assesses only odour identification. The Sniffin’ Sticks test, widely used in Western countries, evaluates olfactory function comprehensively but is time-consuming. Furthermore, both UPSIT and Sniffin’ Sticks contain many odorants unfamiliar to the Chinese population and, given their disposable nature, are costly and unsuitable for large-scale application. The T&T olfactometer, developed by Japanese researchers, is a standardised tool for assessing olfactory function. Due to its standardisation and capacity for quantitative analysis, it has been widely adopted in both clinical and research settings to evaluate olfactory performance [7,8].

According to the literature review, the reported prevalence of olfactory dysfunction among the Chinese population ranges from 2.1% to 2.4% [9,10], with higher proportions observed among the elderly. However, previous studies have largely relied on questionnaire-based assessments of olfactory impairment, and large-scale epidemiological studies utilising psychophysical tests remain scarce. Although several studies in Western countries have used psychophysical tests to examine olfactory dysfunction, its prevalence in the general Asian population remains unclear [11–15]. The present study aims to investigate the prevalence and associated factors of olfactory dysfunction with T&T olfactometer among individuals in a large Chinese population from urban and rural areas.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study is part of the Medical and Health Technology Innovation Project of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, a population-based prospective cohort study initiated by the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College Hospital. This cross-sectional study was conducted between 2023 and 2024 across multiple locations in China, including Jilin Province, Jiangsu Province, Beijing and Tianjin, aiming to assess the current status of olfactory function among individuals. Residents who were aged 20 years or older and resided locally for at least 1 year were eligible to participate. Individuals with severe mental illness, neurodegenerative diseases, or disabilities were excluded. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Approval No. K1486). Prior to enrolment, written informed consent was procured from each participant.

Demographic and behavioural data

Demographic data, behavioural data and health related data were collected via self-administered questionnaires, as previously described [16]. Variables included sex, age, location, education level, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia nasal disease history, self-reported olfactory dysfunction and cigarette and alcohol consumption. Current smokers were classified as those smoking a minimum of one cigarette daily for at least six months, and current drinkers were defined as those who consumed alcohol at least twice per month, with a minimum of 50 g of alcohol or one bottle of beer each time. All investigators received standardised training. Face-to-face interviews with on-site consistency checks and immediate cross validation were performed, and double data entry with independent verification was applied to ensure data quality [17].

Testing methods

The T&T olfactometer was utilised according to the manufacturer’s manual (Beijing MaiChuang Hengye Technology Co., Ltd.). This test employs five distinct odorants: garlic-like odour (acetic acid, diallyl trisulphide, ethanol); pineapple-like odour (ethyl butyrate, isoamyl acetate, allyl hexanoate, propylene glycol); rose-like odour (phenylethanol, geranium oil, rose alcohol, propylene glycol); mint-like odour (linalool, (-)-menthone, menthol, ethanol) and ginger-like odour (ginger oil, citral, leaf alcohol, ethyl acetate, ethanol). Specifically, the T&T olfactometer employs eight graded concentration levels for each odorant, labelled as −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5, representing stepwise logarithmic increases in odorant concentration. These levels are standardised and pre-defined by the manufacturer, allowing for quantitative assessment of both olfactory detection and identification thresholds. Levels −2 and −1 correspond to extremely low concentrations, which are typically below or near the normal detection threshold. Level 0 approximates the mean detection threshold observed in healthy individuals. Levels 1 to 5 represent progressively higher suprathreshold concentrations, with level 5 being the highest concentration provided. Detection thresholds were tested first, followed by identification thresholds, with concentrations increasing sequentially. The lowest concentration at which an odour was detected was recorded as the detection threshold, and the lowest concentration at which an odour was correctly identified was recorded as the identification threshold. If the highest concentration level was not perceived or identified, a score of 6 was assigned. The mean identification threshold across the five odorants was used to categorise olfactory function as follows: ≤1.0: normal olfactory function; 1.1–2.5: mild dysfunction, 2.6–4.0: moderate dysfunction, 4.1–5.5: severe dysfunction and >5.5: anosmia. The cut-off values applied in this study are based on the standardised scoring system of the T&T olfactometer originally proposed by Takagi (1987) and subsequently adopted in the Japanese clinical practice guidelines for olfactory dysfunction [7,18]. These classification criteria have been extensively validated and widely applied in clinical and epidemiological studies across East Asian populations, as well as in international comparative studies [8,19]. The olfactory dysfunction definition refers to mean identification threshold > 1.1.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the participants were presented as frequencies and percentages. The prevalence of olfactory dysfunction was reported similarly. Associations between variables and the prevalence of olfactory dysfunction were analysed using crude and adjusted prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) through modified Poisson regression. For the association between age and olfactory dysfunction, we reported the crude (unadjusted) association. For sex, we adjusted for age, education, income and area. For area, we adjusted for age, sex, education and income. For education and income, we adjusted for age and sex. For cigarette and alcohol use, we adjusted for age, sex, education, income and area. For hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidaemia, we adjusted for age, sex, education, income and area. For cerebrovascular diseases, we adjusted for age, sex, education, income, area, hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidaemia. For self-reported olfactory dysfunction, we adjusted for age, sex, education, income, area and nasal diseases. Finally, for Nasal diseases, we adjusted for age and sex. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of participants

A total of 12,153 participants were included in this olfactory dysfunction prevalence survey. The number of participants from Jilin Province, Jiangsu Province, Beijing and Tianjin were 3,715, 3,717, 1,532 and 3,189, respectively. The areas covered by the survey within the four provinces are shown as illustrated in Figure 1. The median age of the participants was 57, with a mean age of 55.79. As shown in Table 1, 43.20% of respondents were ≥60 years. The female-to-male ratio was 1.70:1. Approximately 69.0% of participants resided in urban areas. In terms of educational attainment, 70.70% of participants had not received a university-level education. The self-reported prevalence proportions of hypertension, diabetes mellitus and hyperlipidaemia were 31.7%, 11.6% and 26.0%, respectively. A total of 15.9% of participants had never undergone lipid testing. Additionally, 4.6% of participants reported a history of cerebrovascular insufficiency, cerebral infarction, or cerebral haemorrhage. Currently, 36.70% of male participants were smokers, compared to only 1.8% of female participants. Alcohol consumption was reported by 53.6% of male participants and 8.5% of female participants. Self-reported olfactory dysfunction was present in 10.3% of participants, and 6.3% had a history of rhinitis or sinusitis.

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Prevalence and factors associated with olfactory dysfunction

Among the 12,153 participants, the overall prevalence of olfactory dysfunction detected by the T&T olfactometry test was 15.3%. Olfactory dysfunction was detected in 19.3% of male participants and 13.0% of female participants. In rural areas, 25.4% of participants had olfactory dysfunction, compared to 10.8% in urban areas. Among participants with an educational level below primary school, 31.4% exhibited olfactory dysfunction, whereas among those with a university education or above, the prevalence was 6.8%. In participants with a history of cerebral infarction or cerebral haemorrhage, the prevalence of olfactory dysfunction was 30.1%, compared to 14.6% in those without such a history. Among current smokers, 20.4% were diagnosed with olfactory dysfunction, whereas 13.4% of participants who never smoked exhibited olfactory dysfunction. Among current drinkers, the prevalence was 17.3%, compared to 13.9% among non-drinkers. Among participants who self-reported olfactory dysfunction, 58.7% were diagnosed with olfactory dysfunction, while 10.4% of those who did not report dysfunction were also found to have olfactory dysfunction. Among participants with a self-reported history of rhinitis or sinusitis, 14.7% had olfactory dysfunction compared to 15.4% among those without such history.

Modified Poisson regression was used to analyse factors associated with olfactory dysfunction, as shown in Table 2. Besides crude PR, the adjusted PRs were reported to reflect the independent associations after controlling for potential confounders, which differed slightly from the unadjusted estimates and provided more robust effect size estimates. The sets of covariates adjusted for each variable have been described in the Statistical analysis section. In univariable analysis, significant differences in the prevalence of olfactory dysfunction were observed across different age groups, with increasing age associated with a higher prevalence compared with participants aged 20–29. Those aged over 70 had the highest prevalence (PR = 54.70; 95% CI, 17.60–170.08; p < 0.01). Female participants had a significantly lower prevalence of olfactory dysfunction compared with males (PR = 0.68; 95% CI, 0.62–0.74; p < 0.01). Participants living in rural areas had a significantly higher prevalence of olfactory dysfunction compared with those in urban areas (PR = 2.35; 95% CI, 2.15–2.57; p < 0.01). Having an education level below primary school was associated with a significantly increased prevalence of olfactory dysfunction (p < 0.01) compared with other participants. Income level was associated with prevalence of olfactory dysfunction, with subjects whose annual personal income falls below ten times the national poverty threshold demonstrating a significantly higher prevalence of olfactory dysfunction (p < 0.01).

Factors associated with olfactory dysfunction in univariable and multivariable analyses

Individuals with self-reported hypertension had a higher prevalence of olfactory dysfunction compared with those without such history (PR = 1.74; 95% CI, 1.59–1.91; p < 0.01). Individuals with self-reported diabetes mellitus or hyperlipidaemia also had a higher prevalence of olfactory dysfunction compared with those without such history (PR = 1.49; 95% CI, 1.32–1.69; p < 0.01 and PR = 1.28; 95% CI, 1.15–1.42; p < 0.01). A history of cerebral infarction or haemorrhage was associated with a significantly increased prevalence of olfactory dysfunction (PR = 2.06; 95% CI, 1.75–2.43; p < 0.01) compared with those without such history. Compared with non-smokers and non-drinkers, smokers and drinkers showed significantly higher prevalences of olfactory dysfunction (both p < 0.01). Participants who self-reported possible olfactory dysfunction had a significantly higher likelihood of olfactory dysfunction (PR = 5.65; 95% CI, 5.14–6.20; p < 0.01). In contrast, a history of nasal diseases did not show significant associations with olfactory dysfunction in the univariable analysis.

In multivariable analysis (Table 2), male sex (PR = 1.28; 95% CI, 1.18–1.41; p < 0.01), rural residence (PR = 1.93; 95% CI, 1.76–2.12; p < 0.01) and current smoking (PR = 1.20; 95% CI, 1.04–1.39; p = 0.04) were also independently associated with higher prevalence of olfactory dysfunction. Significantly higher prevalence of olfactory dysfunction was also found in participants with an education level below primary school (p < 0.01) or annual personal income below ten times the national poverty threshold (p = 0.012). Self-reported olfactory dysfunction was still a strong predictor of examined olfactory dysfunction (PR = 3.97; 95% CI, 3.61–4.37; p < 0.01).

Discussion

Olfactory dysfunction, defined as a reduction or complete loss of the sense of smell, is frequently reported to be associated with adverse health outcomes [20–22], yet it remains underdiagnosed in the general population [11,23]. Older adults are at increased prevalence of olfactory impairment, which extends across various domains, including sensitivity and discrimination [24], as well as higher-order cognitive tasks such as contextual odour identification [25]. Estimates suggest that olfactory dysfunction affects approximately 40%–70% of the general ageing population [11,24], compared to a prevalence of around 5%–15% among younger individuals [23,26]. To date, there has been a lack of large-scale epidemiological studies on olfactory dysfunction in China using psychophysical methods. This study addresses this gap by investigating the prevalence and associated factors of olfactory dysfunction among Chinese adults in a large Chinese population from urban and rural areas.

Clinically observed causes of olfactory impairment are commonly categorised into four main groups: head trauma, inflammation (including rhinitis and sinusitis), prior infections of upper respiratory tract and neurodegenerative diseases (such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease) [12,27]. Less frequent causes include toxic chemical exposure, congenital anomalies, surgery, epilepsy, cerebrovascular events, tumours in the olfactory sulcus or temporal lobe and endocrine disorders [28]. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying olfactory dysfunction may involve progressive damage to the olfactory epithelium, injury to olfactory receptors, alterations in central olfactory pathways, changes in neurotransmitter activity, nasal congestion, reduced mucosal metabolic enzyme activity and neurodegenerative processes [29–31]. Olfactory dysfunction is closely linked to the overall state of the central nervous system and is associated with a variety of demographic [32,33], genetic [34], clinical [21,35] and behavioural factors [11]. While these variables may exhibit synergistic effects, the precise mechanisms underlying olfactory dysfunction development in non-neurodegenerative individuals remain unclear. This study examines the associated factors for olfactory dysfunction in the elderly population and employs multivariable analysis to minimise the confounding effects among individual variables.

In this study, it was found that among individuals, the prevalence of olfactory dysfunction increased progressively with age, and advancing age was independently associated with a higher prevalence of olfactory impairment. This finding is consistent with previous studies [11,13,24,29]. Compared with large-scale studies conducted in the United States, the prevalence proportions of olfactory dysfunction in the 60–70 age groups in this study were higher [11]. Beyond the known aetiologies of olfactory dysfunction, age-related olfactory decline independent of disease status may involve multiple mechanisms, including both peripheral and central mechanisms. It is hypothesised that cumulative damage to the olfactory mucosa from various diseases and environmental pollutant exposure leads to replacement of olfactory epithelium by respiratory epithelium and progressive atrophy of the olfactory bulb/tract with ageing [36,37]. Functional magnetic resonance imaging has demonstrated age-related volume loss in medial temporal lobe regions vital for olfactory processing [38], and reduced activation in specific brain regions during olfactory tasks has been observed in older adults [39].

This study also identified gender differences in olfactory dysfunction, a phenomenon reported in other research as well [11,15,40]. Such gender differences have been observed in all age groups, even prior to puberty, across various measures of olfactory function [4,41,42]. The underlying reasons for these differences remain unclear, although similar gender disparities have been noted in other sensory modalities, including vision, hearing and touch.

Moreover, this study showed that urban participants had a lower prevalence of olfactory dysfunction compared to rural participants. Regional disparities in disease prevalence may be attributed to differences in demographic composition, climate and socio-economic and cultural characteristics. Urban areas generally have higher income levels, more medical resources and greater access to healthcare services than rural regions [11,43]. In addition, lower educational attainment was associated with a higher prevalence of olfactory dysfunction, potentially because individuals with lower education levels may have lower income and reduced healthcare access. Higher education levels are often correlated with healthier lifestyles and better overall health status. Income level was also an independent factor associated with the prevalence of olfactory dysfunction. Low-income individuals are more susceptible to the effects of environmental pollution and environmental pollutants on olfactory dysfunction [44]. Besides, low-income populations frequently face nutritional imbalances, lacking essential nutrients that are critical for maintaining olfactory nerve function [45].

In this study, both current smoking and former smoking were associated with an increased prevalence of olfactory dysfunction in univariable analysis, and current smoking remained a significant factor in multivariable analysis. This finding is consistent with large-scale epidemiological investigations conducted in the United States [11] and several previous studies [46]. A meta-analysis has shown that current smokers had a significantly higher prevalence of olfactory dysfunction compared to never smokers (PR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.37–1.85) [46]. One potential mechanism is squamous metaplasia, whereby the airways’ normal pseudostratified columnar epithelium can be reversibly replaced by squamous epithelium under the stimulation of tobacco smoke [47,48]. Increased squamous metaplasia has been observed in the olfactory mucosa of smokers compared to non-smokers [49]. Variations noted among current and former smokers may reflect the reversibility of these metaplastic changes. Studies on respiratory mucosa have indicated that metaplastic changes are more pronounced in current smokers than in former smokers [50] and that the extent of squamous metaplasia can decrease in six months after smoking cessation [51]. Emerging evidence has shown that smoking shifts oral and nasal microbial composition toward a pro-inflammatory milieu, with enrichment of opportunistic pathogens, and that specific microbial signatures are associated with impaired olfactory performance.Tobacco smoke exposure has also been associated with an increase in apoptosis of olfactory neurons [52]. Additionally, evidence suggests that smoking may impair olfactory function by affecting central neural pathways dedicated to olfactory processing [53].

In this study, participants who self-reported olfactory dysfunction had a significantly higher prevalence of measured olfactory dysfunction compared to those who did not. However, self-report only detected 39.3% of individuals with objectively confirmed olfactory impairment. Among participants who believed they had normal olfaction, 10.4% were found to have olfactory dysfunction upon testing. Previous studies have similarly indicated that self-reporting systems tend to significantly underestimate the prevalence of olfactory impairment compared to psychophysical olfactory testing, especially in older adults [11]. In this study, a self-reported history of nasal diseases was not significantly associated with olfactory dysfunction in modified Poisson regression analysis, which may be attributed to the relatively low proportion of participants reporting nasal disease history.

This large-sample survey revealed the prevalence and associated factors of olfactory dysfunction among healthy individuals in China and provided further evidence that, even in the absence of neurodegenerative diseases, the prevalence of olfactory impairment increases with age. Given that olfaction is a critical component of flavour perception, decline in olfactory function can lead to diminished taste of food, and the age-related diminution of flavour sensing is a potential factor in compromised dietary choices and reduced nutritional status [54]. Food selection becomes increasingly important in maintaining energy balance and adequate nutrition among older adults [55]. Awareness of the role of olfactory dysfunction in dietary intake may help improve nutritional status in the elderly population [56]. Moreover, older adults with impaired olfaction are at greater risk for injuries caused by gas leaks, explosions and fires, emphasising the need to raise awareness of the importance of installing smoke detectors in homes for individuals with olfactory dysfunction.

This study found that among individuals aged 60 years and older, more than one-quarter were identified with olfactory dysfunction through testing. Given that olfactory impairment is closely associated with cognitive disorders [29] and often serves as an early warning sign of Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, olfactory testing holds significant value for the early diagnosis and monitoring of neurodegenerative diseases [29,57,58]. This study demonstrates that large-scale olfactory screening using the T&T olfactory test is feasible and suggests that such screening, particularly targeting populations at high risk for cognitive diseases, could play an important role in early prevention and intervention strategies.

In this study, olfactory dysfunction was defined primarily based on odour identification performance rather than detection threshold, in line with both the diagnostic criteria of the T&T olfactometer and clinical relevance. Odour identification reflects higher-order central olfactory processing and shows stronger associations with disease severity and clinical outcomes, whereas detection thresholds are more susceptible to physiological and environmental variability [59–62]. Therefore, odour identification was selected as the primary endpoint. Nevertheless, olfactory sensitivity impairment may provide complementary insights, and future studies specifically addressing detection threshold alterations are warranted.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although the T&T olfactory test is a widely used and standardised psychophysical method for olfactory assessment in East Asia, the test substances and criteria may differ from those used in Western countries, potentially limiting direct comparability with international studies. Second, as a cross-sectional survey, this study could only demonstrate associations between factors and olfactory dysfunction rather than establish causal relationships. Third, although the sample size was large and covered multiple regions, it may not fully represent the entire population in China, especially given regional differences in economic development and healthcare access. Fourth, the information regarding comorbidities and health-related behaviours was based on self-reported questionnaires, which could be subject to recall bias or misreporting. Finally, some potentially important factors influencing olfactory function, such as occupational exposure to chemicals or environmental pollution levels, were not included in the survey due to practical limitations. Consequently, large-scale, multi-regional studies and prospective natural history research are warranted to elucidate the epidemiological profile and risk factors of olfactory dysfunction in China.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study represents the largest population-based epidemiological investigation of olfactory dysfunction in China, providing comprehensive evidence on its prevalence and associated factors at the national level. We identified that advanced age, male sex, residing in rural areas, having lower educational levels, low income and current smoking were independently associated with olfactory impairment. Importantly, given the well-established association between olfactory decline and neurodegenerative disorders, our results further support the role of olfactory dysfunction as a promising early indicator of neurodegenerative risk in the general population, and T&T olfactometer is viable for large-scale olfactory screening.
