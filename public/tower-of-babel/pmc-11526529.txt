“You’re dealing with the bottom here…” understanding reasons for reduced utilisation: a qualitative study on colorectal cancer screening among vulnerable men at a drop-in centre in Denmark
Authors: Camilla Rahr Tatari, Pia Kirkegaard, Berit Andersen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) screening can reduce both CRC incidence and mortality, and faecal immunochemical testing (FIT)-based screening programmes are therefore now being implemented in many countries. However, social inequality in FIT-based screening participation is well documented, and initiatives to address this challenge are understudied. We explored the perceptions of CRC screening and the perceived barriers and facilitators towards FIT-based CRC screening among men visiting a drop-in centre for people with severe social problems in Denmark.MethodsThe study was a qualitative interview study. Participants were sixteen men visiting a drop-in centre in Denmark. A local staff member provided supplementary information and assisted with the recruitment process. The interviews were transcribed verbatim, followed by an inductive content analysis.ResultsThe men were often dealing with health and social problems, and they often had low self-esteem. At first, they stated that they did not think much about cancer and their own risk of being diagnosed with it. They argued that they had little time, energy, and resources to participating in, for example, CRC screening programmes, and barriers to participating were facts of life such as comorbidity and cognitive difficulties. Further, they were not sure how to participate, and some misunderstood the concept of screening. However, during the interviews, the main part of the participants became very keen to participate, and they suggested that in the future, they could receive regular information about cancer screening in face-to-face interactions with someone who cared and was interested in helping them.ConclusionMen in a vulnerable position visiting a drop-in centre were interested in CRC screening. If we intervene in a way that meets the needs among these vulnerable citizens, it may contribute to reducing social inequality in FIT-based CRC screening programmes.Keywords: Colorectal Neoplasms, Mass Screening, Early Detection of Cancer, Participation, Non-Participation, Healthcare Disparities, Vulnerable Populations, Qualitative Research, Denmark

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is both the second most common cancer and the second leading cause of death for European men [1]. Survival depends on the stage at which the cancer is diagnosed, highlighting the importance of early detection, which may, in some cases, be the result of participating in CRC screening [2, 3]. CRC screening programmes may further reduce the risk of having the disease since adenomas can be removed [4–7]. CRC screening is therefore increasingly being implemented in many Western countries [8]. CRC screening presents potential harms, including false positives, overdiagnosis, physical discomfort, and psychological impact. While the benefits of early detection and prevention of CRC are significant, it is crucial to assess these against the potential harms. Making an informed decision about participating in screening is recommended by most health authorities [9].

A recent review [10] showed that lower socioeconomic status is associated with an increased risk of CRC, later stage at diagnosis, and poorer survival [10, 11]. Moreover, individuals with a lower socioeconomic status participate less in CRC screening than those with a higher socioeconomic status. In faecal immunochemical test (FIT)-based programmes, adherence to follow-up colonoscopy after a positive screening result is notably lower among individuals from a lower socioeconomic background [12, 13]. Men have a higher risk of developing CRC as compared to women, and they are less likely to participate in the screening programme [14], but recently it has been shown that they derive greater benefit from participating in CRC screening compared to women, as CRC mortality is significantly reduced – even with a relatively short follow-up [15]. The implementation of CRC screening might therefore increase undesirable inequalities in CRC morbidity and mortality if left unaddressed.

Doubeni et al. [16] suggested that a health equity framework with different strategies to eliminate disparities in CRC screening might be effective. Strategies should focus on addressing structural barriers, barriers at all levels of influence, and integrating community insights into the strategies [16]. To gain community insights, it is essential to involve the target group. This may be the key to identifying relevant strategies for increasing CRC screening participation [17].

In this study we aimed to explore perceptions and experiences with cancer and the Danish FIT-based CRC screening programme and identify barriers and facilitators for participation among men visiting a drop-in centre in Denmark. This group was chosen to represent men from a very low socioeconomic status background who are known to be at high risk of CRC [18, 19] and have low engagement with screening services [13]. Furthermore, we investigated the potential for developing a tailored intervention to increase informed decision-making about participation in CRC screening and thereby reduce health disparities, morbidity and mortality related to colorectal cancer in this part of our population.

Methods

Setting and participants

This study was carried out at a drop-in centre in a Danish medium-sized municipality. The drop-in centre is directed at people over the age of 25 with severe social problems e.g. homelessness or inability to use their home, substance abuse problems, mental health problems, and loneliness. The drop-in centre is open every day except on Sundays, and it is frequented by about 60 users on weekdays and about 30 on Saturdays. About 60% of the users visit the drop-in centre regularly, and most of the users are men. The users are between 25 and 70 years old and the average age is mid-forties. Four out of five of the users are of Danish origin, one out of five comes from Greenland and only a few are from other countries. The drop-in centre is staffed by a minimum of two social workers at all times, and sometimes additional kitchen staff.

The Danish national CRC screening programme was introduced in 2014 inviting all citizens ages 50–74 years to take part every other year. The invitations are sent out with a letter, including a screening FIT kit [20, 21], with an instruction cartoon that depicts how to collect the sample and label it, and a preaddressed prepaid return envelope. A reminder is sent if the screening kit is not returned within 45 days. Denmark has a universal healthcare system to ensure that all residents have access free of charge to both preventive care and treatment. This covers participation in cancer screening programmes and any necessary follow-up, allowing individuals to seek medical assistance based on their healthcare needs rather than their financial status [22, 23].

Design and recruitment

The study was conducted as a qualitative interview study. Before the interviews were conducted, a meeting took place involving three staff members from the drop-in centre and first author (FA). The staff members contributed with first-hand descriptions and background insights about the potential participants. Additionally, they were briefed on the study’s purpose. A staff member agreed to assist in the recruitment process. She was briefed on the study’s inclusion criteria: men who frequented the drop-in centre, aged between 45 and 75 years, and who were able to speak and understand Danish. The participants were recruited directly from the drop-in centre by this staff member. She briefly introduced the study and its purpose to potential participants. Only a few were not interested in being interviewed; one declined and left quickly, while another said he would return after sorting something out but did not come back. Out of respect for the potential challenges faced by those who attended, further inquiry into the reasons for non-participation was not pursued. When someone expressed interest, the staff member introduced FA immediately. FA provided detailed information about the study for them to decide whether they wanted to participate in an interview. Upon the staff’s recommendation, participants received verbal information only, as written material was avoided due to potential cognitive challenges.

In this study, FA played a crucial role in the data construction. FA is an experienced researcher in the field of harder-to-reach groups and has conducted interviews with the target group before. The data collection phase was conducted under the supervision of last author to warrant the reliability and integrity of the constructed data.

Data collection

The interviews were conducted from July to October 2020 and were not limited by COVID-19.

Prior to conducting these interviews, FA engaged in informal conversations to familiarise herself with the men’s backgrounds and life circumstances. This initial interaction allowed FA to gain preliminary insights into their social and cultural contexts. The interviews with the participants included both single and group interviews, depending on the participants’ preference. Furthermore, participants were given the option to choose their interview setting: either the common room, where staff and other centre users may be present, or a designated meeting room, both located within the drop-in centre. This approach was chosen in consultation with the staff to secure a safe and comfortable environment for the participants. The participants signed a consent form and verbally completed a questionnaire with background information together with FA prior to the interview. Every interview started with a short briefing about the study. All the interviews were audio recorded with the participants’ consent. The interviews were conducted by FA using a semi-structured interview guide [24] (Table 1). During the interviews, FA used open-ended questions to explore participants’ personal experiences, beliefs, and attitudes towards colorectal cancer screening. By actively listening and probing, FA sought to uncover nuanced details about their circumstances, challenges, and motivations, capturing diverse viewpoints and understanding the complexities of their screening decision-making processes. The interview guide was discussed and developed by the authors with consultation from the three social workers from the drop-in centre. The interview guide was divided into three parts: (1) Understanding health behaviour and cancer awareness, (2) Insights into cancer screening knowledge and barriers, and (3) Exploring intervention strategies and facilitating factors. The phrasing of the interview questions was slightly modified or left out according to the participants’ cognitive attention. In the study, the term “barrier” appeared in the interview guide, albeit not posed as a direct interview question. It was used contextually to explore factors that might have hindered individuals from participating in colorectal cancer screening. The normative implications associated with this term typically suggested that addressing these obstacles could lead to increased participation rates. However, it is important to clarify that the study did not imply a deterministic relationship where eliminating barriers guaranteed universal participation in screening. Barriers were viewed as complex and multifaceted, influenced by various individual, social, and structural factors. It was recognised that while addressing certain barriers may have enhanced participation for some individuals, broader systemic issues and diverse perspectives also significantly influenced screening behaviours.

Opinions about other ideas (from the other interviews and literature)

In the second part, FA delivered a brief presentation about cancer and screening, including potential benefits and harms and the importance of informed decision making. Participants were introduced to the rationale behind the Danish CRC screening programme. Additionally, they were introduced to the screening invitation, including the FIT kit. Throughout the presentation, FA encouraged the participants to share their thoughts and comments on the subject and the material. The interviews ended with a debriefing where the participants had an extra opportunity give their views.

After each interview, FA kept a field diary to record her field notes, observations and thoughts, which enabled FA to pick up clues about the participants and their everyday life. This served to understand the social setting and to systematise the data collection as suggested by Julia Philippi and Jana Lauderdale [25].

Moreover, as interviews progressed, FA and the last author constantly assessed the data material to determine the adequacy of the sample size. FA also presented and discussed the initial findings with a highly experienced staff member who had worked across various drop-in centres and shelters. This iterative process secured the depth and richness of information obtained from each interview, contributing to the information power. Data quality was prioritised over quantity, focusing on the richness and depth of insights shared by the participants [26].

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim by FA and an assistant. An inductive content analysis of the interviews was performed in overall three phases in accordance with the methodology [26]: (1) Preparation, (2) Organising, and (3) Reporting the analysis process and the results. The purpose was to explore the research questions shown in Table 1 based on the participants’ statements and experiences. As part of the first phase, the interviews were carefully read by FA to obtain an overall impression of the material. Furthermore, the field notes from the interviews and observations from the drop-in centre were used to contextualise the findings [27]. The second phase included open coding, creating categories and abstraction. It began with an open coding of the transcriptions, followed by a grouping to subcategories and finally main categories. This abstraction process, with the grouping of categories in higher order, was an iterative process for all the authors discussing the process from open codes to the final main categories reported in the third phase [27]. The data analysis was carried out in Danish. The selected quotes presented in this paper were translated by FA.

Ethical considerations

Participants in this study turned out to be in a very vulnerable position. Here, vulnerability refers to adults who experience severe social problems, including homelessness, mental health problems, substance abuse issues, or/and loneliness. We were aware that our ethical responsibilities were not limited to directives or approval of an ethics committee [28]. In particular, some participants had cognitive difficulties, and the staff generally recommended providing oral and not written information, as usually recommended. To meet this challenge, the staff knowing the participants at the drop-in centre played an important role in ensuring that participants only consented if they really wanted to, and that they did not give their consent without understanding. Further, the participants were told, that they could always withdraw from the interview. During the interviews, FA encouraged the participants to take short or longer breaks when needed. At the end of the interviews, participants were given the opportunity to reflect on the interview. Further, they were told to contact the staff if they wanted to withdraw their consent or in case they needed to talk further, and the staff could then contact FA if needed [28].

Results

A total of 16 men participated in 13 interviews and their characteristics can be seen in Table 2. Of these, 11 were single interviews and 2 were group interviews. All names are pseudonyms. The length of the interviews varied from 12 min to 45 min with an average length of 28.5 min. Only one participant was younger than 50 years. The average age was 57.7 years and ranged from 48 to 66 years. All participants were on social support apart from one participant. Some of them were dealing with functional homelessness, i.e., they had a fixed address but felt unable to live there. Furthermore, all lived alone, and 10 out of 16 participants had grown-up children. Seven participants reported having more than one somatic disease at the same time.

In the analysis of the data, we established four categories describing aspects of importance among men in a vulnerable position to engage in colorectal cancer screening: (1) Addressing cancer concerns and varied perspectives on the screening concept, (2) Cognitive difficulties and the need for social support, (3) Experiences with the health system and comorbidity, and (4) In solitude with low self-esteem.

Addressing cancer concerns and varied perspectives on the screening concept

In general, the participants did not think much about cancer and their own risk for being diagnosed with it: “No, I don’t think about it, err, not at all, actually. What should I do that for?” [I11, Nicolai]. Only a few with family members who had died from cancer thought about it from time to time. Diseases were not normally something they often talked about among themselves; neither was health in general nor prevention and early detection of cancer. They believed that factors like diet, exercise, stress, genetics, and the environment and pollution might cause cancer. Most of them did not mention smoking or alcohol as factors: “Yes, they say smoking causes cancer, but I don’t believe that. It has nothing to do with it. It must be the way you live; if you’re not healthy enough, you might get cancer… Because your immune system becomes weak if you don’t eat salad and carrots and things like that. If the immune system isn’t strong enough, the disease might sneak up on you.” (I4, Thomas). Lifestyle and genetics were for the participants the main causes of cancer, and they did not consider themselves to be in any particular risk: “I know it sounds strange, but I survived many horrible things that would’ve killed the most… So I’ll probably make it anyhow, err, I know it sounds strange, but that’s how I feel” (I6, Paul).

However, they were more concerned about their own health as time passes and they grew old: “I have filled myself with everything bad that could kill me. But it [health] has become important now” (I1, Robert) and “My entire family has died from cancer, so now it’s about time for me to do something… I mean I don’t want to die when I’m 60. I’m also starting to call the doctor more often” (I4, Thomas).

Half of the participants reported having performed a faeces sample for participation in CRC screening or to help diagnose other conditions: “But I have been asked to hand in a sample to the hospital. That is, when I was hospitalized (I: a stool test? ) - yes. So I have participated in my own way.” (I2, John). Some of them mentioned confusion about getting the results back from the test. They wondered why they never got the test results back but thought it must be negative since the hospital did not contact them. As an extension to this, some did not see the purpose in participating the next time they were invited since they have already taken the test once: “I don’t give a damn – I haven’t heard back from them […]. If they want a new test they can look at the old one first” (I11, Nicolai). Other barriers were mentioned about the self-sampling kit; one mentioned he forgot to put his ID sticker on the test before mailing it back. Other mentioned that it was a long way to the nearest mailbox and they quickly forgot all about the test after taking it. However, several mentioned that it was a nice gesture that the postage was already paid for, like Michael and David put in the third interview: “It’s nice you don’t have to stick a stamp on the envelope. The state already paid for that.” “Yes, it must be very important then”. They also believed that cancer screening was a way of contributing to the statistics and the society, which several of them would like to. Finally, everyone mentions that their health was their own responsibility, like Daniel and Paul discuss in the sixth interview: “You have the responsibility yourself. No one else can take that for you.” “Yes, but we men are not good at going to the GP, I’ve heard [both laughs]”. They mention that they generally have to pull themselves together and the best way for them to succeed is by getting help.

Written information could be a challenge for many: “I come across lots of things that I don’t understand.” (I12, Eric). Instead, verbal communication from for instance radio, television and what they heard or talked to others about were preferred channels for knowledge: “I don’t read, at all. Most of my insights is from the radio” (I10, Ronald) and “… through TV, uh, yes, and also what I hear from my surroundings. And if the conversation comes to that, that’s where I get my information from.” (I7, Henrik).

The invitation to cancer screening gave rise to confusion. Some did not know why they were invited and had not given it much thought since. Some thought it was an investigation of how widespread cancer is in the population: “I would imagine it was to get an overview of how much cancer there is in a certain group? I’m right or what?” (I4, Thomas). Most did not associate cancer screening with the possibility of early treatment with a better prognosis for survival. Finally, some mentioned that screening can detect cancer early: “It [cancer screening] can probably detect cancer in the start-up phase?” (I5, Mark). Everyone except from one accepted the brief introduction to cancer screening during the interview. During this introduction many nodded in agreement: “Yes! I knew that. So, the earlier they discover it, the better. Yes, I knew that” (I8, Kenneth). The rationale behind cancer screening was familiar and made sense to them. Many knew that you can be sick without having symptoms and they compared screening with COVID-19: “… It’s like… err… In these corona times, you can be a carrier without knowing it yourself” (I7, Henrik).

Most of them found participating in cancer screening relevant after talking about it: “There is a reason why you are invited. I might be at risk myself so of course I would like to participate” (I1, Robert). They would like more awareness of the screening offer at the drop-in centre as had been the case with COVID-19 during the pandemic.

Cognitive difficulties and the need for social support

Many lived from day to day with varying surplus energy: “I don’t want to talk about it [cancer]. What should I do that for? I have enough to think about” (I8, Kenneth) and “… Given that I’m [mental disorder], it is hard to get my act together " (I5, Mark). This could challenge communication with the system in general. As a result, many used support at home or the staff at the drop-in centre for help to contact and communicate with the municipality and healthcare. They brought their letters to the drop-in centre or checked their digital post with the staff. When it came to their health some took a companion to their medical appointment. Several reported that this helped them to complete their course of treatment. A participant described how he would like to participate at a general level but he had different challenges such as remembering different things in his everyday life: “If I’m in a place in my life where I manage to remember things and have the energy for it, then I remember to ask my support worker about it. Especially if it doesn’t slip my mind… I couldn’t do without her and her support.” (I12, Eric).

In continuation of their need for social support, several described themselves or their peers as dyslexic with the need for help with official letters. In general, they preferred all information as simple as possible with pictures. “The pictures about how to take the test works for me. There is nothing to be mistaken about… At all. They show you exactly how to do it and they follow one after the other. That’s enough. All that about why you offer it [cancer screening] doesn’t matter.” (I3, David).

Several mentioned that they felt more motivated to participate in screening just by sitting and talking to a person about it: “And now I’m thinking it could be nice with the opportunity for participating right away. Now, we’re sitting and talking about it I’m also thinking “so now I have to get my act together” (I3, Michael). Furthermore, they mentioned good experiences with different courses at the drop-in centre about how to take care of themselves in their everyday life. They saw a great potential to present cancer screening to them in this context.

Experiences with the health system and comorbidity

Most were pleased with their own general practitioner and the doctors they had met at the hospital. They experienced them as competent and understanding of their situation. However, some also mentioned disagreements about medication or that it is necessary to have a companion with you for several reasons. Despite their experiences from COVID-19 and their understanding about the possibility of being sick without having symptoms, symptoms needed to be present before they visited the doctor. They did not make preventive medical appointments: “I have my own GP who I’m familiar with… err, but I need a reason - something that [swear word] hurts! Otherwise, I don’t go. So my only unfinished business I have at the moment is that test you talk about “(I13, Jonathan).

About half of the participants were seriously ill, most with a heart and/or lung condition. Medication, transportation, and ongoing medical appointments took a lot of their time and energy. Their conditions took up their surplus energy and motivation for other medical examinations: “As long as I have my poor heart to think about, I don’t wonder so much that I might be unlucky to get cancer as the only one in the family.” (I9, Brian). Furthermore, several also believed that the medical examinations they participated in at the hospital would also detect if they had cancer.

In solitude with low self-esteem

Loneliness was, in general, a major social problem for many. They did not have a tradition for visiting each other at the hospital. A longer course of a disease was associated with extra loneliness and served as barrier for participating in CRC screening. When you are sick, you are on your own, like one participant described his hospitalisation: " I could really use someone to talk to… I was left all on my own. When your parents have passed away and you don’t have children, then there is no one” (I1, Robert). Cancer was, for some, the least of their worries – the feeling of loneliness itself was for some a greater barrier for participating than getting cancer and potentially dying from it. Furthermore, many were dealing with low self-esteem and did not believe their life was worth saving: “You are dealing with the bottom here, don’t you think so? They die anyway […] " (I9, Brian).

Discussion

Interpretation of results

This qualitative study is the first to explore perceptions and experience with cancer and FIT-based CRC screening among men in a very vulnerable position using drop-in centres. The men in the study were often dealing with health and social problems, and they often had a low self-esteem. At first, they stated that they did not think much about cancer and their own risk for being diagnosed with it. They argued that they had little time, energy and resources for participating in, for example CRC screening programmes, and barriers for participating were their life conditions including comorbidity and cognitive difficulties. Further, they were not sure how to participate, and some misunderstood the concept of screening. However, during the interviews the main part of the participants became very keen to participate. They suggested that in the future, they could receive regular information in a face-to-face interaction with someone who cared and was interested in helping them. Possibly those already assisting them with other health-related concerns or facilitating contact with public authorities.

Comparison with previous research

It is well known from quantitative analyses including major register-based studies [12, 13, 15], that social inequality in cancer screening participation exists. However, mechanisms and potential to reduce non-participation among some of the most vulnerable groups in our society are understudied. Comparisons with other studies might be difficult due to different health care systems and different CRC screening tests. Worldwide, the faecal tests are the most widely used in screening, and FIT may over time replace the guaiac fecal occult blood test (gFOBT) in most countries [29]. However, the use of colonoscopy as the primary screening test is increasing. In the USA and Germany, this strategy has superseded all alternatives [29]. Different screening tests might have different advantages and disadvantages for vulnerable citizens.

A quantitative study from 2014 determined CRC rates based on medical records from homeless people in New York City including 443 men and women, aged 50–85 years [30]. The study found that only 28.2% were up-to-date for CRC screening. This was in line with the participants in our study’s low history of CRC screening participation. In contrast to our results, this study found that people with chronic diseases were more likely to be up-to-date with CRC screening. The participants with comorbidity in our study were less likely to be motivated to participate in screening. This difference might be explained by the screening test, since a colonoscopy, which is widely used in the US might be easier to access if you are in contact with the health care system due to other diseases. Further, obtaining a faecal sample mailed directly to your home could be difficult if you don’t have a home or if you have difficulties understanding the logistics of providing the sample. This is also underlined by the participants in our study describing forgetting to label the faecal sample mailed to the laboratory. The study from New York also concluded that older people were more likely to be up-to-date for CRC screening. This supports our results with an increased interest and concern about own health and participating in CRC screening later in life.

Another American qualitative study from 2015 explored perspectives and perceptions about cancer screening in a qualitative set-up among homeless people recruited from shelter-based clinics. The participants had primarily experience with colonoscopy for CRC screening [31]. The participants in our study are not necessarily homeless but are dealing with some of the same severe mental and social problems in an uncertain everyday life. The participants in both studies cared about their own health and welcomed support to facilitate participating in CRC screening [31]. The American study was also in line with the participants in our study’s suggestions and strategies to improve CRC screening: tailored cancer screening education, awareness material, and most importantly, individual support and companionship. However, the American study also highlights a strategy that was not mentioned in our study, namely the possibility of incentives, such as small gifts or bus passes for participating [31]. The participants in our study did not have any costs related to transport or other indirect costs, and they mentioned that it was their own responsibility to participate, but a simple gesture such as a small financial incentive may well influence their motivation in a positive direction. On the other hand, it can also be discussed whether it is ethically correct, as their screening behaviour should be based on an informed decision and not a financial incentive.

Doubeni et al. [16], who developed the health equity framework, highlights the importance of community integration including, the engagement of stakeholders/partners. Our study highlighted the participants’ interest in screening and their need for social support in order to take care of their health and manage everyday life. The drop-in centre could be seen as a community with an intervention potential. However, according to Doubeni, simply creating and implementing an intervention does not make it accessible to underserved communities. The community engagement is critical for participation in any intervention and the community needs to be included in the development process [16]. A future intervention could be inspired by Doubeni’s “The Principles of Community Engagement” and emphasize the following key aspect: (1) Have shared goals with the community, (2) Understand the community and its history of engagement, (3) Build trust and seek commitment from stakeholders, (4) Respect diverse perspectives within the community, (5) Identify and mobilize community assets, (6) Partner with the community, (7) Assure community ownership and control of actions, and (8) Long-term commitment [16]. Collaboration with both the target group and staff members could be essential in future interventions based on community engagement.

Our initial interviews with the staff at the drop-in centre, as well as the interviews with the drop-in centre users, indicated that the participants in our study might be some of the most vulnerable men in this age group in the Danish society. Other health problems and complex social problems may hinder their participation in CRC screening, and it must of course in each case be considered which problems to handle first. It should also be noted that the balance between benefits and harms in screening participation depends on factors such as, for example, comorbidity. However, the participants’ initial expression of not thinking much about cancer might be a coping strategy, especially for those who became interested in participating during the face-to-face talk. Some even ended up asking to receive information and help to participate from the staff at the drop-in centre already helping with other issues such as digital mail and contact to the health care system when relevant. A systematic review and meta-synthesis of cancer fears in the general population from 2017 [32] pointed out that participants in more than half of the included studies (N = 102) chose to ignore the existence of cancer. Furthermore, some participants ignored the threat of cancer due to more pressing worries such as other health problems and the demands of everyday life. The review investigated the general population, and this strategy was especially prominent for those from poorer backgrounds [32]. The staff member supporting our study also mentioned that most of the citizens visiting the drop-in centre were frightened at the prospect of severe and terminal diseases, such as cancer.

Our study adds to the knowledge base indicating that even though many citizens gain benefit from the organized cancer screening programmes, different kinds of tailored interventions are needed to reach the entire population and especially reduce equality in the outcome of cancer screening programmes. These results were promising for a future tailored intervention for some of the most vulnerable in the society, aiming towards more equality in cancer screening.

Methodological considerations

A major strength of this study was the recruitment strategy and the facilitation of a trusted familiar setting. Even though it was a time-consuming approach, it served its purpose of giving rarely heard men in a vulnerable position in the society the time to express their perceptions and experience with cancer and the CRC screening programme. Some participants faced challenges in reflecting on the subject due to cognitive difficulties. To address this, preparatory measures were taken, including informal discussions with staff about their experiences with the participants. These conversations served to understand nuances, gather insights, and establish guidelines for interaction. Additionally, the staff provided valuable feedback on the interview guide, offering suggestions to rephrase certain questions, enhancing the clarity and relevance of the interview process.

One limitation was the recruitment strategy, with one staff member pointing out potential participants. While this process was implemented for the safety of the researcher and to prevent potential harm to men in a vulnerable position, it could unintentionally introduce limitations in participant selection. The reliance on staff assistance might have accidentally excluded some potential participants or influenced the selection criteria, potentially affecting the representativeness of the sample. This precautionary measure was crucial to ensure safety, underscoring the paramount importance of participant well-being throughout the data collection process.

All interviews were conducted from the same drop-in centre. We therefore discussed the generalisability of our results with one of the staff who had many years of experience working in the field and at different drop-in centres. She confirmed our results and deepened our understanding of them. Thus, we believe that our results may be transferable to other drop-in centres with men in a vulnerable position with severe social problems in similar contexts.

Implications for practice

The results of this study showed interest in participating in cancer screening among men in a very vulnerable position, and they welcomed a tailored intervention to increase participation in CRC screening. Based on our results, a screening intervention focusing on health education in an existing setting, awareness material at the drop-in centres, and a social support strategy with guiding completion of the screening kit and correct returning of it to the laboratory might be beneficial. Such interventions should include the possibility of companionship at the hospital if the screening results call for further examinations.

Conclusion

Men in a vulnerable position with severe social problems were interested in participating in CRC screening. Despite different levels of experienced barriers, the findings emphasized the potential of tailored intervention focusing on social support, face-to-face interaction, and social support in attempts to secure more equality in screening participation.
