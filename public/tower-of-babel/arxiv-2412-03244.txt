Clustering logarithms up to six loops
K. Khelifa-Kerfa

Abstract
We compute the leading clustering (abelian non-global) logarithms, which arise in the distribution of non-global QCD observables when final-state partons are clustered using the k t k_{t} jet algorithm, up to six loops in perturbation theory. Our calculations are based on the recently introduced formula for the analytic structure of k t k_{t} clustering [ 1 ] . These logarithms exhibit a pattern of exponentiation and are subsequently resummed into an exponential form. We compare this resummed result with all-orders numerical calculations.



I
Introduction

The intricate dynamics of QCD are vividly manifested in the formation and evolution of jets at high-energy colliders, such as the Large Hadron Collider (LHC). Jets–collimated sprays of hadrons arising from the fragmentation of final-state partons–are indispensable tools for probing the structure of QCD across both perturbative and non-perturbative scales. Theoretical calculations of jet observables, such as jet shapes and substructure, demand a nuanced understanding of QCD radiation patterns, governed by the interplay of soft and collinear emissions.

Non-global observables constitute a class of QCD observables that depend on the energy flow within restricted regions of phase space. These observables pose formidable theoretical challenges. Unlike their global counterparts, their distributions are shaped by the intricate interplay of soft radiation both within and outside the measured region. This interplay gives rise to large logarithms, known as non-global logarithms (NGLs)
[
2
]
, which disrupt the perturbative series. NGLs encapsulate correlations between soft, wide-angle gluons emitted across disparate energy scales and angular regions, making their precise calculation critical for advancing the understanding of jet shapes and substructure. Over recent years, NGLs have been extensively studied (see, for instance,
[

).

Another subclass of non-global logarithms, referred to as clustering logarithms (CLs), arises intrinsically from the application of jet algorithms, which are fundamental to experimental jet reconstruction. These logarithms, associated with large contributions from the clustering of abelian, independent primary gluon emissions, are often termed abelian non-global logarithms. CLs were first identified in
[
13
]
in the context of the
k_{t}
jet algorithm
[
14

15

, where it was demonstrated that while clustering suppresses the impact of standard NGLs, as earlier established in
[
16
]
, it simultaneously generates a hierarchy of CLs at every perturbative order in the non-global observable’s distribution. Consequently, an accurate determination of CLs is indispensable for precision computations.
The all-orders resummation of leading CLs has, until recently, been achieved solely through the Monte Carlo program of
[
2
]

k_{t}

[

have numerically resummed leading CLs within the framework of Soft and Collinear Effective Theory (SCET) for the gap-between-jets observable, encompassing various sequential recombination jet algorithms, including
k_{t}

Fixed-order calculations of CLs at the leading logarithmic level were conducted in the original work
[
13
]
for the gap-between-jets (interjet-energy-flow) observable, employing the

[

[
23

[
18

All of these computations were carried out within the framework of eikonal (soft) theory, under the assumption of strong energy-ordering of the emitted gluons, which significantly simplified the calculations. However, the cited works adopted a brute-force approach to calculate CLs for these specific observables. Notably, no general methodology was available for systematically computing CLs for arbitrary observables at any loop order.

Recently, a generalized formula for the analytic structure of

[
1
]

N_{c}

[
25

In this paper, we extend the application of the formula from
[
1

to compute CLs at five- and six loop orders for a generic non-global observable. As shown in the main text, the clustering function (the component of the observable cross-section accounting for the effect of the
k_{t}
jet algorithm) factorizes at each loop order into two distinct terms: the first is a
reducible
component related to lower-order clustering functions, while the second is an
irreducible
term, which is genuinely new and not related to lower loop orders. This factorization pattern is subsequently reflected in the structure of the integrated cross-section.

To illustrate, we explicitly evaluate the integrals for the single-jet mass observable previously studied in
[
7
]

[
2
]
show good overall agreement. However, the five- and six loop contributions provide only a negligible improvement over the four loop result derived in
[
7
]
, owing to the vanishingly small size of the corresponding CLs coefficients.

2

CLs up to four loops: recap

II.1
Definitions

We consider the lepton process of
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation into two final-state jets produced back-to-back in the threshold limit:
e^{+}e^{-}\to j_{q}+j_{\bar{q}}+X
. Here, we assume that only the invariant mass squared of the quark-initiated jet,
j_{q}
, is measured, while the anti-quark-initiated jet,
j_{\bar{q}}
, is left unmeasured. Soft activity,
X
, outside the two jets may be restricted by an energy veto. Although the latter introduces large logarithms that can be computed straightforwardly (see, for instance,
[
4
,
5
,
18
]
), these are not addressed in this work. At the partonic level, the process can be schematically expressed as follows:

\displaystyle e^{+}+e^{-}\to q(p_{a})+\bar{q}(p_{b})+g_{1}(k_{1})+\cdots+g_{n}(k_{n}),

(1)

where
p_{a}
and
p_{b}
are the four momenta of the quark and anti-quark, respectively, and
k_{i}
is the four-momentum of
i^{\text{th}}
gluon. We work within the eikonal (soft) approximation, assume all partons to be massless (
p_{i}^{2}=k_{j}^{2}=0
) and impose a strong energy-ordering condition on the emitted soft gluons:
Q\gg\omega_{1}\gg\cdots\gg\omega_{n}
, where

represents the hard scale of the collision
1
1

1

Alternatively, this ordering can be expressed in terms of the transverse momenta of the emitted gluons, using the relation
k_{t}=\omega\sin\theta
.

[
26
]
).
The four-momenta described above can be parameterized as:

\displaystyle p_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{Q}{2}\left(1,1,0,0\right),\qquad p_{b}=\frac{Q}{2}\left(1,-1,0,0\right),

\displaystyle k_{i}

\displaystyle=k_{ti}\left(\cosh\eta_{i},\cos\phi_{i},\sin\phi_{i},\sinh\eta_{i}\right),

(2)

where
k_{ti}

\eta_{i}

are the transverse momentum, rapidity and azimuthal angles, respectively, of the
i^{\text{th}}
gluon with respect to the beam axis.

We define the normalized invariant jet mass squared,
\varrho

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{Q^{2}}\left(p_{a}+\sum_{k\in j_{q}}k_{i}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i\in j_{q}}\varrho_{i}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k_{t}^{2}}{Q^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\varrho_{i}

\displaystyle=8\frac{p_{a}\cdot k_{i}}{Q^{2}}=2x_{i}\left(\cosh\eta_{i}-\cos\phi_{i}\right),

(3)

where we have introduced the energy fraction

and neglected terms proportional to
k_{t}^{2}/Q^{2}
in the soft limit. The jets are defined using the sequential recombination
k_{t}
jet algorithm, as formulated in Ref.
[
27
]
. We aim to compute the integrated jet cross-section (or equivalently the jet mass fraction) in the
k_{t}
clustering,
\Sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)
, defined as:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\varrho}\Theta\left[\rho-\varrho(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\right]\mathrm{d}\varrho,

(4)

where
\sigma_{0}
is the Born cross-section, and
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\varrho}
is the differential cross-section of the process. Note that the mass (squared) of the measured quark jet,
\varrho(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})
, is restricted to be less than a value
\rho
, which we refer to as the jet veto. The presence of widely separated energy scales in this scattering process (such as
Q
and
\rho
) results in the emergence of large logarithms in the ratio of these scales, as we shall later demonstrate. The fixed-order perturbative expansion of the jet mass fraction, Eq. (
4
), is given by:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)=1+\Sigma_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)+\Sigma_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)+\cdots,

(5)

where the
m^{\text{th}}
contribution in the above expansion is given by
[
9
,
1
]
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{1}>\cdots>x_{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{d}\Phi_{i}\right)\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}\,\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{m}\mathcal{W}_{12\dots m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}},

(6)

where the sum is over all gluon configurations
X
,
\mathcal{W}_{12\dots m}^{X}
represents the squared eikonal amplitude for a particular configuration
X
, and
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{m}
denotes the measurement operator at the
m
th order in the perturbation expansion. In the strong-energy ordering approximation, it factorizes into a product of single-soft-gluon measurement operators
\hat{u}

[
10
,
9
,
1
]
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{m}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\hat{u}_{i}.

(7)

Detailed definitions and derivations of these terms are provided in our previous works (see, for instance, Refs.
[
9
,
1
]
). In particular, the measurement operator for the
i
th soft emission may be recast as follows
[
9
,
1
]

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{i}=\Theta_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{V}}+\Theta_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}\left[\Theta_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}+\Theta_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}\Theta\left(\rho-\varrho_{i}\right)\right]=1-\Theta_{i}^{\rho}\Theta_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}\Theta_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},

(8)

where
\Theta_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{V}}=1
if gluon
i
is virtual and zero otherwise,
\Theta_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}=1
if gluon
i
is real and zero otherwise,
\Theta_{i}^{\mathrm{in}(\mathrm{out})}=1
if gluon
i
is in (out of) the jet region and zero otherwise, and
\Theta_{i}^{\rho}=\Theta(\varrho_{i}-\rho)
.
Therefore, applying
\hat{u}_{i}
to the eikonal amplitude squared,
\mathcal{W}_{12\dots m}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}}
, results in a non-vanishing contribution only if gluon
i
is real, emitted inside the jet region, and satisfies the condition
\rho>\varrho_{i}
.
Notice that the last equality in Eq. (
8
) follows directly from the identities:

\Theta_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{V}}+\Theta_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}=1,\Theta_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}+\Theta_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}=1,
and
\Theta_{i}^{\rho}=1-\Theta(\rho-\varrho_{i})
.

The phase space factor for the emission of gluon
k_{i}
is expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Phi_{i}=\bar{\alpha}_{s}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{i}}{x_{i}}\mathrm{d}\eta_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{i}}{2\pi},

(9)

where
\bar{\alpha}_{s}=\alpha_{s}/\pi
. For the emission of
m
energy-ordered
primary
gluons, the eikonal amplitude squared,
\mathcal{W}_{12\dots m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}
, including the jet veto constraint, may be cast as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1\dots m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}=-\left(-1\right)^{n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{V}}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{C_{F}}\,w_{ab}^{i},

(10)

with
n_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{V}}
being the number of virtual gluons in configuration X and the one loop eikonal antenna function,
w_{ab}^{i}
, is given by:

\displaystyle w_{ab}^{i}=k_{ti}^{2}\frac{\left(p_{a}\cdot p_{b}\right)}{\left(p_{a}\cdot k_{i}\right)\left(p_{b}\cdot k_{i}\right)}.

(11)

The factor
\mathrm{C_{F}}
in Eq. (
10

\mathrm{C_{F}}=(N_{c}^{2}-1)/2N_{c}
, with
N_{c}
being the number of quarks.
The jet veto constraint
\Theta_{i}^{\rho}
is given by:
\Theta_{i}^{\rho}=\Theta(\varrho_{i}-\rho)
. The simplicity of the eikonal amplitudes for primary emissions, as presented in Eq. (
10
), facilitates the calculation of CLs to arbitrary loop orders in the perturbative series. However, the
k_{t}
clustering algorithm introduces non-trivial modifications to the final-state phase space, which complicates the computation.
The
k_{t}
jet algorithm, detailed extensively in Ref.
[
27
]
and in our earlier works
[
7
,
1
,
25
]
, may be summarized in the following steps:

1.

Begin with a list of final-state particles, denoted as
I
.

2.

For each pair
(ij)
in
I
, compute the distance measures:

\displaystyle d_{ij}

\displaystyle=\min\left(k_{ti}^{2},k_{tj}^{2}\right)\frac{\Delta R_{ij}^{2}}{R^{2}},\qquad d_{iB}=k_{ti}^{2},

(12)

where
\Delta R_{ij}^{2}=\eta_{ij}^{2}+\phi_{ij}^{2}
, with
\eta_{ij}=\eta_{i}-\eta_{j}
, and
\phi_{ij}=\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}
.
R
is the jet-radius parameter and
B
denotes the beam direction.

3.

If the smallest distance is
d_{ij}
, merge particles
(i,j)
into a single pseudo-jet by summing their four-momenta using the E-scheme. If
d_{iB}
is the smallest, declare particle
i
as a final jet and remove it from
I
.

4.

Repeat steps 2 and 3 until
I
is empty.

Notably, the
clustering condition
for a particle pair
(i,j)
is given by:

\displaystyle\Delta R_{ij}^{2}<R^{2}.

(13)

Thus, if two particles
i
and
j
are within a circle of radius
R
in the rapidity–azimuth space, they will be clustered together by the
k_{t}
algorithm; otherwise, they will not.

In the subsequent sections, we compute the summation over
X
in Eq. (
6
) and integrate over the relevant phase space to derive the jet mass fraction.

II.2
One and two loops

For the emission of a single soft gluon,
k_{1}
, all jet algorithms, including
k_{t}
, leave the phase space unaffected. Clustering effects only arise with the emission of at least two gluons. At this order, there are precisely two gluon configurations:
X=R
(real gluon) and
X=V
(virtual gluon). Using the measurement operator rules outlined in
[
1
]
and summarized above, we find:

\displaystyle\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}\hat{u}_{1}\,\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}=\hat{u}_{1}\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}+\hat{u}_{1}\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{V}}=-\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}\,\Theta_{1}^{\rho}\,\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},

(14)

where, at this loop order,
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{1}=\hat{u}_{1}=1-\Theta_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}\Theta_{1}^{\rho}
. The step function
\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}=\Theta\left(R^{2}-\Delta R_{1j}^{2}\right)=1
if gluon
k_{1}
ends up inside the measured jet,
j
, after applying the jet algorithm and zero otherwise. The one loop
clustering
function is then given by:

\displaystyle\Xi_{1}(k_{1})=\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}.

(15)

Substituting (
14
) back into the expression of the jet mass fraction (
6
) (for
m=1
) we find, at SL accuracy,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi_{1}\,\Xi_{1}\,\Theta_{1}^{\rho}\,\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}},

\displaystyle=-\bar{\alpha}_{s}\,\mathrm{C_{F}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}}{x_{1}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}\eta_{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{1}}{2\pi}\,w^{1}_{ab}\times

\displaystyle\times\Theta\left[2x_{1}\left(\cosh\eta_{1}-\cos\phi_{1}\right)-\rho\right]\Theta\left(R^{2}-\eta_{1}^{2}-\phi_{1}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle=-\bar{\alpha}_{s}\,\mathrm{C_{F}}\,\frac{L^{2}}{2},

(16)

where
L=\ln(R^{2}/\rho)
.
The exponentiation of
\Sigma_{1}
gives the well-known Sudakov form factor, which resums leading logarithms. Its expansion manifests at each order in the perturbative distribution of the jet mass fraction, corresponding to the case where
all
gluons are clustered into the measured jet. It is now well established that this form factor misses crucial contributions for non-global observables, starting at two loops. These missing contributions include mainly NGLs and CLs. In this paper, we focus exclusively on determining the missing CLs contributions for the single-jet mass observable up to six loops.

At two loops, the integrand in (
6
) (for
m=2
) reads
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{2}\mathcal{W}_{12}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}=\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}\left[-1+\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}\,\bar{\Omega}_{12}\right]\Theta_{1}^{\rho}\Theta_{2}^{\rho}\mathcal{W}_{12}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}}.

(17)

where
\bar{\Omega}_{ij}=1-\Omega_{ij}=\Theta\left(d_{ij}-d_{jB}\right)
. The above clustering function (the product of
\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}
and the square bracket) becomes:

\displaystyle\Xi_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(k_{1},k_{2})=-\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}\,\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}-\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}\,\Omega_{12}=-\prod_{i=1}^{2}\Xi_{i}-\overline{\Xi}_{12}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}},

(18)

where we have replaced
-1
in the square bracket in (
17
) by
-\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}-\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}
and simplified the resultant expression to arrive at (
18
).
The first term in the above equation is the product of the one loop clustering function (
15
), while the second term represents the two loop (irreducible) clustering function:

\displaystyle\overline{\Xi}_{12}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}=\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}\,\Omega_{12}.

(19)

Substituting back into the formula for the jet mass fraction, we find, at two loops:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)=\int_{x_{1}>x_{2}}\mathrm{d}\Phi_{1}\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}\left[\Xi_{1}\Xi_{2}+\overline{\Xi}_{12}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}\right]\Theta_{1}^{\rho}\Theta_{2}^{\rho}\mathcal{W}_{12}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}}.

(20)

In the first term above, one can relax the energy ordering,
x_{1}>x_{2}
, and multiply by
1/2!
, resulting in the integral factorization:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2!}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi_{1}\Xi_{1}\,\Theta_{1}^{\rho}\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}\,\int\mathrm{d}\Phi_{2}\Xi_{2}\,\Theta_{2}^{\rho}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}=\frac{1}{2!}\left(\Sigma_{1}\right)^{2}.

(21)

For the second term, the energy integrals factorize, yielding

, and the CLs contribution can be written as:

\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)=+\bar{\alpha}_{s}^{2}\frac{L^{2}}{2!}\,\mathrm{C_{F}^{2}}\,\mathcal{F}_{2}(R),

(22)

where the two loop CLs coefficient,
\mathcal{F}_{2}
, is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}(R)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}\int_{2}\overline{\Xi}_{12}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}\,w_{ab}^{1}w_{ab}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}\eta_{1}\mathrm{d}\eta_{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{1}}{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{2}}{2\pi}\,\Theta\left(\eta_{1}^{2}+\phi_{1}^{2}-R^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\Theta\left(R^{2}-\eta_{2}^{2}-\phi_{2}^{2}\right)\Theta\left(R^{2}-\eta_{12}^{2}-\phi_{12}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{2}{\cosh^{2}\eta_{1}-\cos^{2}\phi_{1}}\frac{2}{\cosh^{2}\eta_{2}-\cos^{2}\phi_{2}},

(23)

where we have introduced, for brevity, the shorthand notations:

\displaystyle\int_{i}\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}\eta_{i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{i}}{2\pi}.

(24)

The integral above can be evaluated numerically using, for instance, the multidimensional
Cuba
library
[

. The results are shown in Fig.

Three loops

At three loop order the integrand in (
6

m=3

[
1

\displaystyle\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{3}\mathcal{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{X}}_{123}

\displaystyle=\Theta_{3}^{\mathrm{in}}\Big[1-\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}\,\bar{\Omega}_{13}-\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{out}}\,\bar{\Omega}_{23}+

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt+\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}\left(\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{out}}+\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}\Omega_{12}\right)\bar{\Omega}_{13}\bar{\Omega}_{23}\Big]\times

\displaystyle\times\Theta_{1}^{\rho}\Theta_{2}^{\rho}\Theta_{3}^{\rho}\mathcal{W}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{R}}}_{123}.

Following the same procedure highlighted at two loops, specifically using the relations
\Theta_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}+\Theta_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}=1
and
\Omega_{ij}+\bar{\Omega}_{ij}=1

\displaystyle\Xi_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{3}\Xi_{i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ijk=1\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{3}\overline{\Xi}_{ij}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}\,\Theta_{k}^{\mathrm{in}}+\overline{\Xi}_{123}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}},

(26)

where the first two terms on the left-hand side of the above equation are reducible, i.e., they can be expressed in terms of the one and two loop clustering functions. The last term represents the new (irreducible) three loop clustering function, which is given by:

\displaystyle\overline{\Xi}_{123}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}

\displaystyle=\Theta_{1}^{\mathrm{out}}\Theta_{3}^{\mathrm{in}}\left[\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{out}}\,\Omega_{13}\Omega_{23}+\Theta_{2}^{\mathrm{in}}\Omega_{12}\left(-1+\bar{\Omega}_{13}\bar{\Omega}_{23}\right)\right].

(27)

The jet mass fraction (
6
) may then be shown to reduce to the form:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)=\frac{1}{3!}\left(\Sigma_{1}\right)^{3}+\Sigma_{1}\times\overline{\Sigma}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}+\overline{\Sigma}_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}},

(28)

where the CLs contribution at this order,
\overline{\Sigma}_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}
, is given by:

\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}_{3}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}(\rho)=-\bar{\alpha}_{s}^{3}\,\frac{L^{3}}{3!}\,\mathrm{C_{F}^{3}}\,\mathcal{F}_{3}(R),

(29)

with the three loop CLs coefficient:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{3}(R)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}\int_{2}\int_{3}\overline{\Xi}_{123}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{k_{t}}}\,w_{ab}^{1}w_{ab}^{2}w_{ab}^{3},

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\int_{1_{\mathrm{out}}}\int_{2_{\mathrm{out}}}\int_{3_{\mathrm{in}}}\Omega_{13}\Omega_{23}+

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\int_{1_{\mathrm{out}}}\int_{2_{\mathrm{in}}}\int_{3_{\mathrm{in}}}\Omega_{12}\left(-1+\bar{\Omega}_{13}\bar{\Omega}_{23}\right)\Bigg]w_{ab}^{1}w_{ab}^{2}w_{ab}^{3},

(30)

where we have additionally introduced the shorthand notation

V
All-orders treatment

V.1
Comparison to all-order numerical results

The expressions for both the clustering functions and the jet mass fractions at each order up to six loops strongly suggest a pattern of exponentiation for the CLs distribution. However, at each higher order, there is a new CLs term that is not captured by the expansion of the exponential from previous orders. We may represent the said exponential using the following formula:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(t)=\exp\left[\sum_{n\geq 2}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\mathcal{F}_{n}(R)\,(2\,\mathrm{C_{F}}\,t)^{n}\right],

(46)

such that, when expanded,
\mathcal{C}(t)
reproduces the fixed-order distributions derived above. The evolution parameter,
t

\alpha_{s}

\displaystyle t=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\frac{\rho}{R^{2}}}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}\,\alpha_{s}\left(xQ/2\right)=-\frac{1}{4\pi\beta_{0}}\ln\left[1-2\alpha_{s}\beta_{0}\,L\right],

(47)

where the second equality is the one loop expansion of
t
, with
\beta_{0}=\left(11\mathrm{C_{A}}-2n_{f}\right)/12\pi

22

29

36

41

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented, for the first time in the literature, the full structure of the leading clustering logarithms (CLs) up to six loops in perturbation theory for the
k_{t}
jet algorithm. The formalism developed herein applies to a broad class of non-global logarithms arising in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation as well as in lepton-hadron and hadron-hadron collisions. As an example, we have explicitly demonstrated the calculations for the invariant single-jet mass observable.

Based on eikonal theory and imposing strong energy-ordering, the calculations are significantly simplified while retaining their validity up to single logarithmic accuracy. The master formula for the analytic structure of
k_{t}
clustering, recently derived in Ref.
[
1
]
, forms the backbone of the present work. The symmetric pattern of
k_{t}
clustering enables the computation of higher-loop contributions in the perturbative expansion of generic non-global observables. While such calculations are feasible for primary abelian independent emissions, due to the simplicity of abelian eikonal amplitudes, they remain highly challenging for secondary non-abelian correlated emissions, given the inherent complexity of their corresponding eikonal amplitudes.

The computed CLs coefficients at two, three, four, five, and six loops share similar characteristics. Their dependence on the jet-radius parameter is weak (up to
R\approx 1.2
), and they approach a constant as
R\to 0
. This behavior has been previously associated with edge (or boundary) effects in the literature. Additionally, all coefficients are relatively small, with a suppression of
1/n!
at each
n
-loop order. This smallness is reflected in the all-orders resummed form factor, which contributes at most
\mathcal{O}(5\%)
of the full distribution (which includes both global logarithms and NGLs).

It was previously observed
[
22
,
7
]
and further confirmed in this work that CLs exhibit a pattern of exponentiation. To evaluate the reliability and precision of the analytical exponential form and its series expansion, as well as the impact of missing higher-order coefficients, comparisons to all-orders numerical results were performed. Overall, the agreement is excellent across a wide range of logarithmic and jet-radius values, achieving percent-level accuracy. This indicates that the analytical calculations are phenomenologically robust and that the impact of higher-order terms is negligible.

There are numerous directions for future work building upon the current study. These include computing CLs coefficients beyond six loops, deriving an evolution equation for CLs analogous to that for NGLs, employing advanced techniques to probe the all-orders structure of CLs, considering next-to-leading CLs distributions, and generalizing the calculations to other jet algorithms, among others.
