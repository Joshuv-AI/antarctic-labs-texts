Two telomere-to-telomere genome assemblies and comparative genomics reveal the conserved “toolkit” genes associated with sugar accumulation in the genus Rubus
Authors: Xiao-Peng Li, Xian-Yan Han, Shou-Cheng Liu, Qun-Ying Zhang, Ji-Yuan Guan, Meng-Zhuo Zheng, Ya-Jun Tang, Peng-Bo Xu, Jian-Xin Bian, Kui Li, Zheng-Dong Wang, Hou-Cheng Zhou, Guo-Hui Yang, Hong-Li Lian, Hang He, Jun-Hui Zhou
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Rubus, a genus of perennial berry shrubs in the Rosaceae, comprises more than 740 species distributed across diverse latitudes worldwide, providing rich germplasm resources for evolutionary studies (Jennings, 1988). Rubus species, valued as “third-generation fruits,” are particularly notable for their high contents of bioactive compounds (e.g., ellagic acid, superoxide dismutase, and raspberry ketone), which contribute to their antioxidant, nutritional, and medicinal properties (Moyer et al., 2002). In particular, the low sugar content of raspberries drives their popularity in Western countries, whereas East Asian consumers prefer sweeter cultivars. Consequently, delineating the genetic “toolkit” and molecular basis of sugar accumulation in Rubus is crucial for both foundational research and genome-editing-driven breeding. Rubus species also exhibit a short juvenile phase, self-compatibility, predominantly diploid genomes (2n = 2x = 14), and amenability to transformation, making them an attractive model for research on woody fruits.

The genome of black raspberry (Rubus occidentalis) was assembled and annotated in 2016 (VanBuren et al., 2016), followed by chromosome-level genomes of Rubus chingii Hu (Wang et al., 2021), Rubus corchorifolius (Yang et al., 2022), and Rubus idaeus (Davik et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023). However, the Rubus genus shows striking phenotypic and genomic diversity across its subgenera, driven by frequent hybridization, complex polyploidy (2n = 2x–12x), and extensive heterozygosity, rendering genomic information from one species largely non-transferable to another. Current genome assemblies remain fragmented, with gaps in centromeric and telomeric regions and incomplete annotations. High-quality, telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assemblies for representative subgenera are therefore needed.

Here, we assembled two highly continuous, completely gap-free reference genomes for the Rubus subgenus: Rubus hirsutus Thunb. ‘Penglei’ (XMM) and Rubus eustephanos Focke ex Diels ‘Dahongpao’ (DHP). These species are widely distributed in southern China and have similar phenotypic traits (Supplemental Figure 1), but their ripe fruits differ markedly in sugar accumulation (Supplemental Table 1), making them ideal candidates for investigation of sugar accumulation in Rubus.

The T2T genomes of XMM and DHP were assembled using Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) ultra-long reads, PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) reads, Illumina short reads, and High-throughput Chromosome Conformation Capture (Hi-C) data (Figure 1A; Supplemental Figures 2 and 3; Supplemental Table 2), yielding the most complete Rubus genomes to date (Supplemental Table 3) and featuring fully resolved telomeres enriched with the plant telomeric repeat motif (TTTAGGG) (Supplemental Figures 4 and 5; Supplemental Table 4). The XMM and DHP genomes (213.53 and 218.26 Mb, respectively) exhibited BUSCO completeness values of 99.18% and 99.23%, HiFi/ONT read mapping rates and coverages ≥99.10%, long terminal repeat assembly index values of 26.49 and 20.53, and quality values of 67.75 and 65.96 (Supplemental Tables 5 and 6). Chromosome-level collinearity was supported by comparing the chromosome sequences with those reported in raspberry assemblies (Supplemental Figure 6). We also identified two centromeric satellites (CEN18 and CEN17), which share 84% sequence similarity and occupy 19.94% and 23.90% of the centromeric regions in XMM and DHP, respectively, with centromeres supported by ≥3 of 5 evidence tracks (Supplemental Figures 7 and 8; Supplemental Table 7).

Transposable elements (TEs) totaling 99.48 Mb (46.59%) and 102.98 Mb (47.18%) were identified in the XMM and DHP genomes, respectively (Supplemental Table 8). Continuous read-depth and TE annotation signals across repeat-rich loci, together with predominant genome-wide haploid windows and low non-haploid fractions within TE regions, support the high assembly quality of both genomes, including repetitive regions (Supplemental Figure 9; Supplemental Table 9). By integrating de novo prediction, homology-based annotation, and transcriptome evidence (Supplemental Figure 10A), we identified 28 204 (XMM) and 28 569 (DHP) protein-coding genes (Supplemental Table 10). These gene annotations achieved ∼98.5% BUSCO completeness, surpassing those of previously published Rubus genomes (Supplemental Table 11). Gene models were supported by transcript and comparative evidence, with high orthogroup assignment across the Rosaceae; most predicted genes clustered into shared orthogroups with high sequence conservation (Supplemental Figure 10B–10D; Supplemental Tables 12–14).

Comparative analysis of the XMM and DHP genomes identified 380 syntenic blocks (≥79.00% one-to-one collinearity) with 939 717 SNPs, 180 328 insertions or deletions, 867 structural variants (349 translocations, 408 duplications, and 110 inversions), and a significant chromosome 6 (chr6) centromeric inversion (both genomes) confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure 1B and 1C; Supplemental Figure 11A; Supplemental Tables 15 and 16). Furthermore, a large inversion on chr7, containing 33 genes, was confirmed by aligning Hi-C reads from DHP to the XMM genome and visualizing the Hi-C heatmap (Supplemental Figure 11B). We also identified 2013 and 2146 presence/absence variation sequences in the XMM and DHP genomes, respectively, totaling 1.33 and 1.28 Mb. Over 87.50% of these presence/absence variation regions were shorter than 1 kb (Supplemental Figure 12A). A sugar transporter gene (MFS1), present in XMM but absent in DHP, exhibited higher expression across six tissues in XMM (Supplemental Figure 12B–12D). Transient expression assays showed that RhMFS1 localizes to the plasma membrane and positively regulates the accumulation of total soluble solids (Supplemental Figure 12E–12G).

Chromosome collinearity is conserved across Rubus, and phylogenetic mapping showed that the inversion occurs only in R. corchorifolius (Yang et al., 2022), R. idaeus ‘Joan J’ (Zhou et al., 2023), and R. occidentalis (VanBuren et al., 2018) (Supplemental Figure 13). Reported chr4 inversions (R. idaeus vs. R. occidentalis [Davik et al., 2022] and R. corchorifolius vs. R. chingii [Yang et al., 2022]) support recurrent chr4 rearrangement. Ancestral state reconstruction revealed two independent inversion events: one in R. corchorifolius after its divergence from R. chingii Hu (∼6.8 Ma) and an earlier one in the common ancestor of R. idaeus ‘Joan J’ and R. occidentalis (∼9.1–16.5 Ma) (Supplemental Figure 14).

Phylogenetic analysis of XMM, DHP, and ten published Rosaceae genomes from the Genome Database for Rosaceae (Jung et al., 2019) identified 33 275 ortholog groups, including 27 209 genes from XMM and 27 527 genes from DHP. A phylogenetic tree for these 12 Rosaceae species was constructed using 3969 conserved single-copy genes (Supplemental Figure 14). XMM and DHP formed a distinct clade, having diverged from the common ancestor of four Rubus species approximately 12.44 million years ago. The divergence between XMM and DHP occurred approximately 3.21 million years ago, with R. chingii Hu and R. corchorifolius identified as their closest relatives.

Following the divergence of XMM and DHP, 198 gene families expanded and 617 contracted, with expansion of families related to glycoprotein biosynthetic and metabolic processes and contraction of those related to (1→3)-β-D-glucan biosynthetic process (corrected P < 0.05). Five sugar-related orthogroups showed copy-number changes on the XMM branch (Supplemental Figures 15–18; Supplemental Tables 17 and 18).

We achieved highly accurate phasing for both XMM and DHP through ONT, PacBio HiFi, and Hi-C data for haplotype-resolved assembly. Each diploid assembly was ∼422 Mb, nearly twice the size of the haploid consensus genome, and phasing was supported by multiple lines of evidence (Supplemental Figure 19; Supplemental Tables 19–21). We identified 4.77 and 4.64 million SNPs between the two haplomes of XMM and DHP, respectively (Supplemental Figures 20 and 21).

Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry showed that XMM had a high sugar content, approximately 3.64 times greater than that of DHP. We performed transcriptome sequencing for eight other Rubus materials and classified the 10 materials into high-sugar and low-sugar groups on the basis of total fruit sugar content (Supplemental Figure 22; Supplemental Table 22). Gene expression analysis revealed 3531 differentially expressed genes significantly enriched in the molecular function “active transmembrane transporter activity” (Figure 1D; Supplemental Table 23). We also identified 40 candidate genes associated with differences in sugar accumulation between XMM and DHP (Figure 1E; Supplemental Tables 24 and 25).

Of the 40 candidate genes, RhSTP13 and RhSTP7 were prioritized for further analysis because they showed the greatest differences in expression and were annotated with the Gene Ontology (GO) terms “active transmembrane transporter activity” and “symporter activity.” RhSTP13 was upregulated in the high-sugar samples, whereas RhSTP7 was downregulated. A recent study also indicated that STP genes are crucial for tomato locule development at low temperatures because of their role in the modulation of sugar transport (Li et al., 2025). Subcellular localization revealed that both RhSTP7 and RhSTP13 are localized to the plasma membrane (Figure 1F). To verify their roles in sugar accumulation, we transiently overexpressed and silenced RhSTP13 and RhSTP7 by agroinfiltration in the fruits of R. argutus ‘Hillquist’ and R. idaeus. Overexpression (OE) of RhSTP13 increased total sugar content by 17.48%, whereas overexpression of RhSTP7 reduced it by 41.73% (Figure 1G–1I; Supplemental Table 26). Silencing of RhSTP13 caused a 74.69% reduction in total sugar content, and silencing of RhSTP7 resulted in a 30.22% increase (Supplemental Figure 23). 3D structure predictions and membrane-topology mapping showed no global conformational or topological differences and placed all substitutions in the same topology class (Supplemental Figure 24; Supplemental Table 27), consistent with expression-level regulation as a major contributor to the different sugar-accumulation phenotypes. RhSTP7, RhSTP13, and RhMFS1 may act as sugar transporters that mediate sugar efflux and influx, thereby contributing to sugar homeostasis in Rubus fruits (Figure 1J). The high-quality T2T genomes thus provide a robust foundation for genomic and functional studies in Rubus, particularly for dissecting the genetic basis of fruit flavor and other economic traits.

We have established the RubusDB database (http://rubusdb.cn), which documents 10 genomic and transcriptomic datasets and 34 phenotypic traits of Rubus species (Supplemental Figure 25). RubusDB also offers basic analytical tools, such as batch query and BLAST, to support gene discovery and genomic investigations across Rubus.
