The thermal backreaction of a scalar field in dS spacetime. II. Spectrum enhancement and holography
Antonis Kalogirou

Abstract




Abstract

We study a spacetime obtained from the semi-classical backreaction computed via the Thermo-field dynamics approach in the Poincare patch of de Sitter spacetime. The resulting bulk equation takes the Whittaker form and we examine two distinct applications. At leading order, the co-moving curvature perturbations are shown to match a constant-roll model in the frozen attractor regime, corresponding to a UV enhancement of the spectrum with
n_{S}\sim 2
. In the holographic context, we compute the CFT two-point function at the future boundary, and away from it we construct the flow-equation of the dual QFT that matches beta-function of the
Sp(N)
model in three dimensions.

Introduction

In early-universe cosmology, it is suggested that the universe underwent a brief epoch of rapid expansion, commonly referred to as cosmic inflation. The latter is successfully described by a spacetime metric in Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) coordinates, characterized by
a scale factor
a(t)
which relates physical distances to the comoving spatial coordinates
x^{i},\ i=1,...,d
. In the presence of a cosmological constant
\Lambda
driving the expansion, the assumptions of homogeneity and isotropy lead to the solution in cosmic time
t

\displaystyle a_{0}(t)=e^{H_{0}t}

(1.1)

where
\Lambda=\frac{d(d-1)}{2}H^{2}_{0}
. In these coordinates, the metric takes the form of the Poincaré (co-moving) patch of de Sitter (dS) spacetime which we will restrict to 4 (
d=3
) dimensions. Furthermore, dS spacetime is maximally symmetric, with its isometry group
SO(1,4)
constraining the structure of quantum correlation functions. This makes it a particularly useful toy model for studying quantum fields and semiclassical gravitational effects in curved spacetime. Therefore, one usually considers quantum fields at a fixed curved background which for negligible gravitational quantum effects, is considered as a good first order approximation.

Spacetimes with non-zero curvature in most cases lack the notion of a global timelike Killing vector. Consequently, there is no unique way of defining positive-frequency modes since they are observer-dependent and any initial choice can be written as a linear combination of mixed modes via a Bogolyubov transformation (BT). Similarly to the case of black holes,

\beta_{\rm dS}\equiv 1/T_{\rm dS}=2\pi/H_{0}
which we define as the inverse dS temperature parameter. In the context of dS space, the connection between the above and the particle production as perceived by two distinct observers was studied to great extent
[

The BD vaccum, which was originally considered by Chernikov and Tagirov
[
9

SO(1,4)

Even though, globally the BD state is pure, inside their causal patch, a static observer would measure a thermal mixed state caused by the tracing out of the inaccessible region

In the dS temperature case, it was shown that the introduced thermal corrections absorb the zero-temperature result so that the residue corresponds to the extracted thermal effects from the BD vacuum. The latter in the Poincare patch admit two possible interpretations: either they correspond to an explicit thermal bath, or describe an intrinsic thermodynamic behavior of the system, effectively characterized by
\beta_{\rm dS}

The thermal EMT was then computed via the point-splitting regulator and was plugged in the RHS of the Einstein equations. At first order in Plank mass
M_{\rm pl}=(8\pi G)^{-1/2}

In this paper, we take advantage of the new metric that retains its FLRW form, interpret it as a quasi–dS spacetime, and propose two distinct physical applications. The starting point is the scalar equation of motion (eom), which in conformal-time coordinates acquires a Whittaker form rather than the usual Bessel form. We then quantize the scalar field and discuss its normalization condition, which can be viewed as an extension of its analogue in de Sitter spacetime. By requiring the solution to be asymptotically equivalent to the Bunch–Davies vacuum towards the future boundary, we establish a non-trivial connection between the two bases and thereby set up the framework on which the remainder of the paper is built.

The first application focuses on an effective inflaton description sourcing the quasi–dS background and resulting in a scale-dependent curvature power spectrum. This framework is physically meaningful only in a specific regime that includes the case of a massless minimally coupled test field. In particular, we obtain a blue-tilted scalar spectrum characterized by a spectral index
n_{S}\sim 2
. This suggests that thermal backreaction could play a role in facilitating a transition between the slow-roll regime and a transient phase relevant for primordial black hole (PBH) formation
[
26

[
27

The second application focuses on the dS/CFT holographic proposal which, unlike its AdS counterpart, involves several subtleties. During our analysis, we remain within the complementary series so that the field does not oscillate outside the event horizon and the main non-unitary issues of the dual CFT’s can be avoided. Then, by assuming that the Hartle-Hawking wavefunctional is the CFT generating functional, as considered in
[
25

[
33

Away from the future boundary, which we interpret as a UV fixed point (FP) of a 3-dimensional QFT, time-evolution is treated as an inverse scale transformation. Following the Hamiltonian-Jacobi formalism and the Wilsonian picture in holography
[
17

We argue that tracing out UV degrees of freedom in the dual theory has a direct correspondence with the coarse-graining of bulk degrees of freedom, thereby motivating the use of the TFD framework in the bulk description. Our results further support the proposed duality between dS and
O(N)

[

2
Canonical quantization in quasi-dS

2.1
The solution to the bulk eom

Our starting point is the toy model of a light scalar field
\phi
propagating freely in a 4d quasi-dS spacetime which is described via a metric of FLRW form:

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)dx^{2}=a^{2}(\tau)\left(-d\tau^{2}+dx^{2}\right)

(2.1)

where we denote
\tau
as the conformal time. The corresponding action is

\displaystyle{\cal S}=\frac{1}{2}\int d\tau d^{3}x\sqrt{-g}\left\{g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi+\mu^{2}\phi^{2}\right\}

(2.2)

with
\mu^{2}=m^{2}+\xi R
. Here,
R=\frac{6\ a^{\prime\prime}(\tau)}{a^{3}(\tau)}
is the scalar curvature with the prime denoting differentiation with respect to
\tau
, while in the dS case the former is equal to
12H^{2}_{0}
with
H(\tau)=\frac{a^{\prime}(\tau)}{a^{2}(t)}
defined generally as the Hubble parameter. At late times (
Ht\rightarrow\infty
), we assume that the scale factor
a(t)
is written as a first order correction to the corresponding dS scale factor:

\displaystyle a(t)=a_{0}(t)\left[1-{\cal C}(m,\xi)\frac{H^{2}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}e^{-H_{0}t}+{\cal O}\left(\frac{H^{4}_{0}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)\right],

(2.3)

with
{\cal C}(m,\xi)
a real function of the mass
m
and coupling
\xi
to gravity of the scalar field. Then, the definition of
dt=ad\tau
lets us express conformal time

\displaystyle\tau=\int d\tau=\int\frac{dt}{a(\tau)}\simeq\int dt\ e^{-Ht}\left(1+{\cal C}(m,\xi)\frac{H^{2}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}e^{-H_{0}t}\right)=\tau_{0}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}{\cal C}(m,\xi)\frac{H^{2}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}e^{-H_{0}t}\right)

(2.4)

in terms of
t
and
\tau_{0}=-\frac{1}{H}e^{-H_{0}t}
the dS conformal time. Since the correction term is next to leading order and drops exponentially at late times, we can assume
\tau\simeq\tau_{0}
and the scale factor in Eq. (
2.3
) can be read as

\displaystyle a(t)=a_{0}(t)\left[1+{\cal C}(m,\xi)\frac{H^{3}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}|\tau|+{\cal O}\left(\frac{H^{4}_{0}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)\right]\ .

(2.5)

We note that this is a usual assumption made when one considers quasi-dS spacetimes. For instance, in slow-roll the corresponding scale factor ansatz is
a(\tau)=-(H\tau_{0})^{-1}
with
H
a function of time which is assumed to be nearly constant.

A scale factor of the form (
2.5
), was found in
[
21
]
where a semi-classical backreaction to the original dS spacetime was considered due to thermal-like fluctuations. It was suggested that the latter arise when a comoving observer integrates out the lost information due to horizon crossing so that near the end, the system can be regarded as a thermal equilibrium described by the inverse Gibbons-Hawking temperature
\beta_{\rm dS}=2\pi/H
. From the static’s observers perspective this corresponds to the Unruh effect. The resulting correction was found to be proportional to

\displaystyle{\cal C}(m,\xi)=-\frac{1}{144\pi^{3}}\nu\cot(\pi\nu)(1+12\eta^{-1}-6\xi),

(2.6)

with

\displaystyle\nu=\sqrt{\frac{9}{4}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{H^{2}_{0}}}

(2.7)

and
\eta=R/m^{2}

and explicitly note when we substitute Eq. (
2.6

Moving on, up to boundary terms the scalar field satisfies on-shell the e.o.m.:

\displaystyle\left(\Box-\mu^{2}\right)\phi=0

(2.8)

and due to the fact that the metric has an FRW from, the above can be massaged into

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}-\nabla^{2}\phi+2\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}\phi^{\prime}+a^{2}\mu^{2}\phi=0\ .

(2.9)

\displaystyle\phi(\tau,{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int d^{3}k\ \phi_{|\mathbf{k}|}(\tau)\ e^{i\mathbf{k}{\mathbf{x}}}

(2.10)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}_{|\mathbf{k}|}+2\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}\phi^{\prime}_{|\mathbf{k}|}+\left({|\mathbf{k}|}^{2}+a^{2}\mu^{2}\right)\phi_{|\mathbf{k}|}=0\ .

(2.11)

We start by making the field redefinition:

\displaystyle f_{|\mathbf{k}|}(\tau)=a(\tau)\ \phi_{|\mathbf{k}|}(\tau)

(2.12)

3
The enhanced curvature perturbation spectrum

Now, we are ready to examine what are the consequences to the inflationary history of the universe produced by the quasi-dS spacetime described by Eq. (
2.5
). Firstly, we consider the resulting time-dependent Hubble scale:

\displaystyle H=H_{0}\left[1-2{\cal C}(m,\xi)\frac{H^{3}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}|\tau|+{\cal O}\left(\frac{H^{4}_{0}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)\right]

(3.1)

from which it is clear that the linear dependence in time of the correction will produce a transient effect on the evolution of the inflationary period. This becomes even more evident if one calculates a gauge-invariant quantity like the Ricci scalar
R
which will obtain corrections to the usual dS result. We define the Hubble flow parameters

\displaystyle\epsilon_{H}\equiv-\frac{H^{\prime}}{aH^{2}}=-2{\cal C}(m,\xi)\frac{H^{3}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}|\tau|+{\cal O}\left(\frac{H^{4}_{0}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)

(3.2)

and

\displaystyle\eta_{H}\equiv\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{aH\epsilon}\sim-1\ .

(3.3)

As the inflationary period approaches its ending
\tau\rightarrow 0^{-}
,
\epsilon_{H}
becomes negligible and does not match the expected behavior i.e.
\epsilon_{H}\rightarrow 1
. Therefore, the thermal backreaction is not a suitable candidate to describe the transition between the inflationary and reheating periods of the universe’s history. However, as we will showcase below this kind of behavior can be understood as a certain case of the more-generic constant-roll models
[
27
,
26
,
28
]
which we briefly review in Section
B
.

We consider a single-field model described by the action

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{\rm ifl}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left\{g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\varphi\partial_{\nu}\varphi+V_{\rm eff}(\varphi)\right\}

(3.4)

where
\varphi
is the minimally coupled effective inflaton producing the considered quasi-dS spacetime. Note that we have restored cosmic time coordinates since they are preferable by most cosmological conventions. In these coordinates, the field eom and Friedman eq. are given by

\displaystyle\ddot{\varphi}+3H\dot{\varphi}+\frac{\partial V_{\rm eff}}{\partial\varphi}=0\ ,

(3.5a)

\displaystyle 3H^{2}=\frac{1}{2M^{2}_{\rm pl}}\left[(\dot{\varphi})^{2}+V_{\rm eff}\right]

(3.5b)

and

\displaystyle\dot{H}=-\frac{1}{2M^{2}_{\rm pl}}(\dot{\varphi})^{2}

(3.5c)

with the dot denoting the derivative with respect to cosmic time, as usual. The constant-roll proposal is centered around the assumption

\displaystyle\frac{\ddot{\varphi}}{\dot{\varphi}}=\alpha H

(3.6)

with
\alpha
a constant of order unity. The above condition produces a family of models characterized by
\alpha
which is constrained between
-3<\alpha<0
with the maxima corresponding to slow-roll and the minima to ultra-slow roll
[
22
,
7
]
. In this regime, the power-spectrum obtains a blue-tilt which is why these models are of particular physical interest. In particular, they naturally generate an enhancement of the curvature power spectrum at small scales, which may lead to the formation of PBH
[
26
]
. Such enhancements typically occur for modes exiting the horizon around 10–30 e-folds before the end of inflation. This gives enough time for the system to “heat-up” up to
\beta=\beta_{\rm dS}
, thereby indicating that information-loss could be the origin of the spectrum enhancement.

We substitute Eq. (
3.1
) into the LHS of Eq. (
3.5c
) and obtain

\displaystyle\dot{H}=2{\cal C}(m,\xi)\frac{H^{4}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}e^{-H_{0}t}\ .

(3.7)

Then, by integrating both sides of Eq. (
3.5c
) we find

\displaystyle\varphi=-4H_{0}\sqrt{-{\cal C}(m,\xi)}e^{-\frac{H_{0}t}{2}}+{\cal C}_{1}

(3.8)

with
{\cal C}_{1}
an arbitrary constant. Its specific value is not of importance, nevertheless we could for instance require that the inflaton vanishes at
t\rightarrow\infty
so that
{\cal C}_{1}=0
. Clearly, Eq. (
3.8
) makes physical sense only for
{\cal C}(m,\xi)<0
. Otherwise the expectation value of the inflaton and its derivatives become imaginary resulting into a complex stress-energy tensor and a potential loss of unitarity. This restricts the parameters
m,\xi
of the initial test-field
\phi
probing the dS spacetime. In particular when
{\cal C}(m,\xi)={\cal C}_{\beta}(m,\xi)
, this is satisfied by
1<\nu<\frac{3}{2}
which corresponds to a nearly massless minimally coupled test-field
\phi
.

The above relation has two implications. Firstly, the double-dot derivative of Eq. (
3.8
) satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{\ddot{\varphi}}{\dot{\varphi}}=-\frac{1}{2}H_{0}\sim\left(-\frac{1}{2}-{\cal C}(m,\xi)\frac{H_{0}^{2}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}e^{-H_{0}t}\right)H

(3.9)

where for the last step we made use of Eq. (
3.1
) in order to express
H_{0}
in terms of
H
. Then, we can directly compare Eq. (
3.6
) and (
3.9
) and find
\alpha\sim-\frac{1}{2}
along with a non-leading time-dependent part of order
H^{2}_{0}/M^{2}_{\rm pl}
. This places
\alpha
in the
-3<\alpha<0
regime which enables us at leading order, to read the thermal backreaction as a transient nearly constant-roll model that produces, for a few e-folds, a blue-tilted power-spectrum. In particular, we define the scalar perturbations
\delta\varphi(\tau,{\mathbf{x}}),\delta g(\tau,{\mathbf{x}})
so that

\displaystyle\bar{\varphi}(\tau,{\mathbf{x}})=\varphi(\tau)+\delta\varphi(\tau,{\mathbf{x}}),\qquad\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}(\tau,{\mathbf{x}})=g_{\mu\nu}(\tau)+\delta g_{\mu\nu}(\tau,{\mathbf{x}})

(3.10)

are their total counterparts. In the co-moving gauge
[
25
]
, the inflaton remains unperturbed
\delta\varphi=0
while

can be parametrized by

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-a^{2}d\tau^{2}+a^{2}(1-2{\cal R})dx^{2}\ .

(3.11)

with
{\cal R}
the curvature perturbation. We define the new Mukhanov-Sassaki variable

\displaystyle\frac{A^{\prime\prime}}{A}=\frac{3}{4\tau^{2}}-\frac{{\cal C}H_{0}^{3}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}|\tau|}+{\cal O}\left(\frac{H^{4}_{0}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)\ .

Substituting the above into the Mukhanov-Sassaki Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}v}{dz^{2}}+\left(-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\tilde{\kappa}}{z}-\frac{3}{4z^{2}}\right)v=0

with

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}=i\frac{{\cal C}(m\xi)H^{3}_{0}}{2M^{2}_{\rm pl}{|\mathbf{k}|}}\ .

Similarly to before, the general solution to the above equation is

\displaystyle v_{|\mathbf{k}|}={\cal A}_{|\mathbf{k}|}({|\mathbf{k}|}|\tau|)^{3/2}+{\cal B}_{|\mathbf{k}|}({|\mathbf{k}|}|\tau|)^{-1/2}

with

Moving on, we have argued that after the horizon exit of the CMB fluctuations, our universe entered a stage described by Eq. (
2.5
) which at leading order has a constant-roll behavior. This expands the idea proposed in
[

of a transient constant-roll phase over the course of

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{R}\equiv\frac{{|\mathbf{k}|}^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}|R_{|\mathbf{k}|}|^{2}\sim\frac{1}{4\pi^{3}}\left[-\frac{{|\mathbf{k}|}}{{\cal C}(m,\xi)H_{0}}+{\cal O}\left(\frac{H^{4}_{0}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)\right]

at leading order while the residual terms become negligible in the superhorizon limit. In the above relation we made use of Eq. (

Secondly assuming that

\displaystyle H(\varphi)=H_{0}\left[1+\frac{\varphi^{2}}{8M^{2}_{\rm pl}}+{\cal O}\left(\frac{H^{4}_{0}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)\right]\ .

The above result can be read as a first order approximation of Eq. (

\displaystyle H(\varphi)=H_{0}\cosh\left[\frac{\varphi}{2M_{\rm pl}}\right]

which was thoroughly examined in

for general

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(\varphi)=M_{\rm pl}^{2}H_{0}^{2}\left[6+\frac{5}{4}\frac{\varphi^{2}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}+{\cal O}\left(\frac{\varphi^{4}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)\right]

as a quadratic function of

In this section we showcased that a semiclassical backreaction speculated to be produced by information loss at the late-time limit, leads to an amplification to the curvature power-spectrum. Hence, we argue that the former is a sufficient candidate to describe how inflation exits its initial slow roll phase towards an intermediate era leading to PBH formation. Nevertheless, several questions need further investigation. In particular, the exact mechanism describing the transition from the intermediate phase to the final slow-roll phase is yet to be built.
For instance, a possible getaway would be to consider a time-dependent
H_{0}
due to a slow-roll background. This will lead to the flow parameter

\displaystyle\epsilon_{H}\equiv\epsilon_{0}-2{\cal C}(m,\xi)\frac{H^{3}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}|\tau|+{\cal O}\left(\frac{H^{4}_{0}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(\varphi)=M_{\rm pl}^{2}H_{0}^{2}\left[\text{const}+\left(\frac{5}{4}-\frac{\delta m}{H^{2}_{0}}\right)\frac{\varphi^{2}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}}+{\cal O}\left(\frac{\varphi^{4}}{M^{4}_{\rm pl}}\right)\right]\ .

4
The holographic effects

4.1
The future infinity boundary

The spacetime geometry considered in this paper is asymptotically dS at the late-time limit which means that at
\tau=0
it shares the same asymptotic boundary. Thus by assuming that the dS/CFT correspondence holds, in this section we will check how the conjectured dual theory is affected by the corrected scale factor Eq. (
2.5
).

Consider the action (
2.2
) defined in between the initial and final times
\tau_{i}
and
\tau_{f}
. Integrating by parts and assuming either a vanishing field or its derivative at spatial infinity we find

\displaystyle{\cal S}=\frac{1}{2}\int^{\tau=\tau_{f}}_{\tau=\tau_{i}}d\tau d^{3}{\mathbf{x}}\ a^{2}(\tau)\phi\left[\partial^{2}_{\tau}+2\frac{a^{\prime}(\tau)}{a(\tau)}\partial_{\tau}-\nabla^{2}+a^{2}(\tau)\mu^{2}\right]\phi-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}{\mathbf{x}}\ a^{2}(\tau)\phi\partial_{0}\phi\biggl|^{\tau=\tau_{f}}_{\tau=\tau_{i}}

(4.1)

with the first term vanishing on-shell due to the eom (
2.11

\tau_{f}=0

(\tau_{i}=-\infty)
. For future reference, when
\tau_{f}\neq 0
instead we will assume mixed boundary conditions which will let the field to fluctuate.

In the usual holographic dictionary
[
16
,
38
]
adapted to the dS case
[
25
]
, the main postulate is that the wavefunctional at the future-infinity
\tau_{f}=\epsilon
defined by

\displaystyle\Psi[\phi(\epsilon);\epsilon]\equiv\int{\cal D}\phi\exp\left\{-{\cal S}\right\}

(4.2)

can be interpreted as the generating functional

\displaystyle{\cal Z}_{\rm CFT}=\braket{e^{-\int d^{3}xJ(x){\cal O}}}_{\rm CFT}

(4.3)

of a CFT in Euclidean space where
J(x)
is the external source of bulk origin corresponding to a CFT operator
{\cal O}
. The bulk field
\phi
is decomposed into two independent parts, the leading and sub-leading from either which, we fix
J(x)
leading to the standard or alternative quantization respectively. Here we have introduced the negative regulator with magnitude
|\epsilon|<<1
. This will let us deal with the upcoming divergences via the holographic renormalization procedure reviewed in
[
33
]
.

Since the eom is singular at the late-time limit, we can take advantage of Frobenius’s method i.e. express the solution as a power-series of the form:

\displaystyle\phi_{|\mathbf{k}|}(\tau)=\sum^{\infty}_{j=0}a_{j}{|\mathbf{k}|}^{\Delta+j}|\tau|^{\Delta+j}

(4.4)

with the coefficient
a_{0}\neq 0
fixed by the initial conditions of the ODE and
\Delta
the leading order. Then, we substitute the above into Eq. (
2.11
), collect together similar powers of
|\tau|
and demand a trivial solution. This results into:

\displaystyle\Delta(\Delta-3)+\frac{\mu^{2}}{H^{2}}=0

(4.5)

\displaystyle a_{1}\left[(\Delta-2)(\Delta+1)+\frac{\mu^{2}}{H^{2}_{0}}\right]+a_{0}\frac{2{\cal C}(m,\xi)H^{3}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}{|\mathbf{k}|}}\left[\Delta+\frac{\mu^{2}}{H^{2}_{0}}\right]=0

(4.6)

and the recurrence relation

\displaystyle a_{j+2}\left[(\Delta+j+2)(\Delta+j-1)+\frac{\mu^{2}}{H^{2}_{0}}\right]+a_{j+1}\frac{2{\cal C}(m,\xi)H^{3}_{0}}{M^{2}_{\rm pl}{|\mathbf{k}|}}\left[(\Delta+j+1)+\frac{\mu^{2}}{H^{2}_{0}}\right]+a_{j}=0\ .

(4.7)

By solving the first equation, we find the two-independent solutions
\Delta_{\pm}=\frac{3}{2}\pm\nu
which serve as the classical scaling dimensions of the two independent parts of the bulk field. From the above it is clear that our case has a similar leading behavior as dS case and by fixing either
\Delta_{\pm}
results into a choice between the standard or alternative quantizations.

In the usual dS case where
{\cal C}(m,\xi)=0
, the second and third equations for
\Delta=\Delta_{\pm}
and
2\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
force
a_{1}=0
and
a_{2n+1}=0,n\in\mathbb{Z}
correspondingly. Otherwise, when
\nu
is a half integer the recurrence relation breaks down and one needs to introduce logarithmic terms in the ansatz Eq. (
4.4
) as the usual prescription suggests
[
12
]
. Then, the logarithmic counterterms introduce an explict scale-dependence to the on-shell action which results to the emergence of a RG flow. Notice that in Eq. (
2.6
),
\nu=\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}
leads to
{\cal C}_{\beta}(m,\xi)=0
so that dS symmetry is restored. This implies that the thermal deformation of the boundary theory and the above running do not mix. Contrastingly, an arbitrary non-zero
{\cal C}(m,\xi)
for
\nu=\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}
leads to a non-zero
k
-dependent
a_{1}
proportional to
a_{0}
and could potentially safeguard the recurrence relation. However, this case will not be examined here. Overall, note that in the light field regime where
\frac{1}{2}\leq\nu\leq\frac{3}{2}
, we obtain
\Delta_{-}\geq 0
and there is no IR divergence arising from the limit
{|\mathbf{k}|}\rightarrow 0
in Eq. (
4.4

) results into
{|\mathbf{k}|}^{-(j+2)}
at most for

The above analysis confirms that

remains the only free parameter of our case which we end up fixing by choosing initial conditions. In particular, we directly compare Eq. (

\phi_{|\mathbf{k}|}(\tau)=a^{-1}(\tau)f_{|\mathbf{k}|}({|\mathbf{k}|}|\tau|)
around
\tau\rightarrow 0^{-}
via Eq. (

5
Conclusions

Throughout this work, we consider a spacetime that is slightly deformed due to a semiclassical thermal backreaction of the initial Poincare patch of the de Sitter background. Accordingly, it constitutes a direct continuation of our previous study
[
21
]
. There, a thermal state characterized by the de Sitter temperature was considered, whose origin could be either an explicit thermal bath or the coarse-graining associated with horizon crossing of modes. The resulting spacetime retains the FLRW form, with a scale factor receiving a constant first-order correction that, in turn, brings the original bulk equation of motion into Whittaker form.

The goal of this paper was to explore the physical implications of the corresponding modes in two key applications of de Sitter spacetime, namely inflation and holography. Using canonical quantization, each mode is expressed as a linear combination of the two Whittaker functions, from which we derive the normalization condition that constrains their coefficients. The latter can be understood as a generalization of its de Sitter analogue, fixed by a nonzero parameter, and is required to match the Bunch–Davies condition at future infinity.

At leading order, the deviation produces a contribution to the de Sitter Hubble parameter that is linear in conformal time. This, in turn, yields a first flow parameter
\epsilon_{H}
at late times and a second flow parameter
\eta_{H}
of order minus one. We interpreted this via a single field model and showed that its effective potential is quadratic and matches at first order the potential of a certain constant-roll model in the attractor regime. Accordingly, the co-moving curvature perturbations obtain an extra contribution that freezes at the superhorizon limit and amplifies the power-spectrum at small scales. This type of UV enhancement is speculated to have occurred for a transient period of a few e-folds near the inflation end and resulted to the formation of primordial black holes. We briefly discussed that a distinction from the constant-roll model should happen at second order and will result to further fluctuations.

At the future boundary, we utilized the dS/CFT postulate together with the new normalization condition and demonstrated that, in the complementary series, the coefficient of the CFT propagator takes a more general form. Following the Holographic normalization procedure, we argued that the thermal backreaction does not introduce additional logarithmic terms and computed the counterterms of the on-shell action at the boundary. The latter is treated as a UV fixed point and the introduced scale-dependence alters the flow-equation of the Wilsonian RG flow. Near this fixed point, we expressed the beta-function of the generalized double-trace coupling as a series in terms of its deviation from the fixed point value and calculated the corresponding anomalous dimension. Our results further support the proposed duality between a bulk quasi dS scalar model and a 3d boundary
Sp(N)
theory.

\displaystyle M_{\kappa,\nu}(z)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}z}z^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}\ M\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu-\kappa,1+2\nu,z\right),

(A.2a)

\displaystyle W_{\kappa,\nu}(z)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}z}z^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}\ U\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu-\kappa,1+2\nu,z\right)

(A.2b)

which are themselves written in terms of the confluent hypergeometric fuctions. The former, contains Kummer’s function:

\displaystyle M(a,b,z)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\frac{(a)_{n}}{(b)_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\ ,

(A.3)

while the latter is defined via the Tricomi function

\displaystyle U(a,b,z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int^{\infty}_{0}dt\ e^{-zt}t^{a-1}(1+t)^{b-a-1}\ .

(A.4)

The
M_{\kappa,\nu}(z)
satisfies the analytic-continuation formula:

\displaystyle M_{\kappa,\nu}(ze^{\pm\pi i})=\pm ie^{\pm\nu\pi i}M_{-\kappa,\nu}(z)\ .

(A.5)

Some of the known Wronskians between the Whittaker functions are

\displaystyle W\left[M_{\kappa,\nu}(z)\ ,W_{-\kappa,\nu}(ze^{\pm\pi i})\right]=\frac{\Gamma(1+2\nu)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu+\kappa\right)}e^{\mp\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu\right)\pi i},

(A.6a)

\displaystyle\tilde{W}\left[W_{\kappa,\nu}(z)\ ,W_{-\kappa,\nu}(e^{\pm\pi i}z)\right]=e^{\mp\kappa\pi i}\ .

(A.6b)

Both Whittaker functions can be associated with the Bessel functions via the connecting formuilae:

\displaystyle M_{0,\nu}(z)=2^{2\nu}\Gamma(1+\nu)\sqrt{z}I_{\nu}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right),

(A.7a)

\displaystyle W_{0,\nu}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{z}{\pi}}K_{\nu}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)

(A.7b)

In the small
|z|\rightarrow 0
limit, the limiting forms of both functions are:

\displaystyle M_{\kappa,\nu}(z)=z^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}},\qquad 2\nu\neq-1,-2,...

(A.8a)

and

\displaystyle W_{\kappa,\nu}(z)=\frac{\Gamma(2\nu)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu-\kappa\right)}z^{\frac{1}{2}-\nu}+\frac{\Gamma(-2\nu)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-\nu-\kappa\right)}z^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}+{\cal O}\left(z^{\frac{3}{2}\pm\nu}\right)\ .

(A.8b)

Appendix B
Constant-roll inflation

Constant-roll inflation is a family of single-filed models which satisfy the extra condition

\displaystyle\frac{\ddot{\varphi}}{\dot{\varphi}}=\alpha H\ .

(B.1)

Then, for a monotonic
t(\phi)
we can write
\dot{H}=\dot{\phi}\frac{dH}{d\phi}
and substitute it into the second Friedman Eq. (
3.5c
). Differentiating the result with respect to
t
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}H}{d\varphi^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2M^{2}_{\rm pl}}\alpha H

(B.2)

which has the general solution

\displaystyle H(\varphi)={\cal C}_{1}\cosh\left(\sqrt{\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}\frac{\varphi}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)+{\cal C}_{2}\sinh\left(\sqrt{\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}\frac{\varphi}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)\ .

(B.3)

In
[
27
]
each distinct solution is examined with the most interesting case given for
{\cal C}_{2}=0
. Otherwise,
{\cal C}_{2}\neq
can lead to
\ddot{a}<0
i.e. a decelerating phase especially at late-times.
Substituting Eq. (
3.5c
) into Eq. (
3.5b
) along with Eq. (
B.3
), we solve for the potential and find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2M^{2}_{\rm pl}}V_{\rm eff}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=3H(\varphi)^{2}-\frac{1}{4\pi G}\left(\frac{dH(\varphi)}{d\varphi}\right)^{2}

(B.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\cal C}^{2}_{1}\left[3-\alpha+(3+\alpha)\cosh^{2}\left(\sqrt{2|\alpha|}\frac{\varphi}{M_{\rm pl}}\right)\right]\ .

Notice that for
\alpha=-3

The Mukhanov-Sassaki equation arising from the co-moving curvature perturbation
\cal R
is

\displaystyle v_{|\mathbf{k}|}^{\prime\prime}+\left({|\mathbf{k}|}^{2}-\frac{A^{\prime\prime}}{A}\right)v_{|\mathbf{k}|}=0\ .

(B.5)

with

\displaystyle\frac{A^{\prime\prime}}{A}\sim\frac{\nu^{2}-\frac{1}{4}}{\tau^{2}},\quad\nu=|\frac{3}{2}+\alpha|\ .

(B.6)

In the superhorizon limit
{|\mathbf{k}|}^{2}<<A^{\prime\prime}/A

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{|\mathbf{k}|}=A_{|\mathbf{k}|}+\int dt\frac{B_{|\mathbf{k}|}}{a^{3}\epsilon_{H}}

(B.7)

Appendix C
The Hamilton-Jacobi equation

Consider the UV wave functional:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\rm UV}[\phi_{c},\tau_{c};\phi_{0},0]\simeq\exp\left\{-\left[\int^{0}_{\tau_{c}}d\tau\ \sqrt{-g}{\cal L}+{\cal S}_{\rm ct}+{\cal S}_{\rm bd}\right]\right\}

(C.1)

with
{\cal L}
representing the on-shell Lagrangian obtained from the classical solution and
{\cal S}_{\rm bd}[\phi_{c},\tau_{c}]
a Gibbons-Hawking like term for the matter field that ensures a well-defined variational principle and encodes the data at the cut-off. Specifically, in order for the requirement
\delta{\cal S}/\delta\phi=0
to give the classical eom, we find the boundary condition:

\displaystyle\pi_{c}=\frac{\delta{\cal S}_{\rm bd}}{\delta\phi_{c}}

(C.2)

with
\pi_{c}
the canonical momentum of the bulk field at
\tau=\tau_{c}
.

Consequently, the Wilsonian effective action is found by performing the path integral (
4.27
) with respect to
\phi_{c}
:

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-{\cal S}_{\rm eff}\right\}=\int{\cal D}\phi_{c}\exp\left\{-\left[{\cal S}_{\rm bd}[\phi_{c},\tau_{c}]-\int d^{3}xJ(x){\cal O}\right]\right\}

(C.3)

and since the on-shell part of Eq. (
C.1
) is fixed by the classical solution, we can disregard its contribution to the above path integral.
Of course, the dual theory should be independent from the choice of
\tau_{c}
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau_{c}}Z=\frac{d}{d\tau_{c}}\Psi[\phi_{\epsilon},\epsilon]=\frac{d}{d\tau_{c}}\braket{e^{-S_{\rm eff}}}=0

(C.4)

where the brakets denote the path integral (
4.28
). This requirement is satisfied by the Schrodinger equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{c}}\Psi_{\rm IR}[\tau_{c},\phi_{\tau_{c}}]=-\hat{\cal H}(\hat{\phi}_{c},\hat{\pi}_{c})\Psi_{\rm IR}[\tau_{c},\phi_{\tau_{c}}]

(C.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{c}}\Psi_{\rm UV}[\phi_{c},\tau_{c};\phi_{0},0]=\hat{\cal H}(\hat{\phi}_{c},\hat{\pi}_{c})\Psi_{\rm UV}[\phi_{c},\tau_{c};\phi_{0},0]

(C.6)

which describe how each wavefunctional flows towards opposite directions. Here we promote the canonical momentum to the operator
\hat{\pi}_{c}=-\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi_{c}}
in the Schrodinger representation and

\displaystyle\hat{\cal H}(\hat{\phi}_{c},\hat{\pi}_{c})=-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\left\{\frac{1}{a^{2}}\hat{\pi}^{2}_{c}+a^{2}\delta^{ij}\partial_{i}\hat{\phi}_{c}\partial_{j}\hat{\phi}_{c}+a^{4}\mu^{2}\hat{\phi}^{2}_{c}\right\}

(C.7)

the Hamiltonian operator that corresponds to Eq. (
2.2
) for a flat slicing choice of the original metric. Here, the Hamiltonian operator acts as the generator of time-evolution as usual and governs the flow of each wavefunctional as we push
\tau_{c}
away from the future boundary. In the semiclassical limit, the Fourier transform of Eq. (
C.6
) leads to the Hamilton-Jacobi equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{c}}{\cal S}_{\rm bd}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}\mathbf{k}\ \left\{\frac{1}{a^{2}}\frac{\delta{\cal S}_{\rm bd}}{\delta\phi_{c,\mathbf{k}}}\frac{\delta{\cal S}_{\rm bd}}{\delta\phi_{c,-\mathbf{k}}}+a^{2}\left({|\mathbf{k}|}^{2}+a^{2}\mu^{2}\right)\phi_{c,\mathbf{k}}\phi_{c,-\mathbf{k}}\right\}

(C.8)

where we have made use of the Fourier transform of the boundary condition (
C.2
). Clearly, the above equation describes the flow of the Hamilton-Jacobi functional
{\cal S}_{\rm bd}
as governed by the bulk mechanics and is a direct product of the boundary condition (
C.2
).

The usual ansatz is to write
{\cal S}_{\rm bd}
as a quadratic expansion of the Fourier transform
\phi_{c,\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{\rm bd}={\cal C}(\tau_{c})+\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d}\mathbf{k}\sqrt{-\gamma}\left\{G(\tau_{c},\mathbf{k})\phi_{c,-\mathbf{k}}+\phi_{c,\mathbf{k}}F(\tau_{c},\mathbf{k})\phi_{c,-\mathbf{k}}\right\}

(C.9)

and plug it in Eq. (
C.8
). Grouping together the similar functional forms, we find the flow equations for the coefficients:

\displaystyle a^{-4}(\tau_{c})\partial_{\tau_{c}}\left(\sqrt{-\gamma(\tau_{c})}F(\tau_{c},\mathbf{k})\right)=F^{2}(\tau_{c},\mathbf{k})+a^{-2}(\tau_{c}){|\mathbf{k}|}^{2}+\mu^{2}

(C.10a)

\displaystyle a^{-4}(\tau_{c})\partial_{\tau_{c}}\left(\sqrt{-\gamma(\tau_{c})}G(\tau_{c},\mathbf{k})\right)=G(\tau_{c},\mathbf{k})F(\tau_{c},\mathbf{k})

(C.10b)

and

\displaystyle a^{-4}\partial_{\tau_{c}}\left(\sqrt{-\gamma(\tau_{c})}{\cal C}(\tau_{c})\right)=\int d^{3}\mathbf{k}\ G^{2}(\tau_{c},\mathbf{k})\ .

(C.10c)
