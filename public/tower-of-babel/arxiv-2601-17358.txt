Generalizations of the Squircle-Lemniscate Relation and Keplerian Dynamics
Zbigniew Fiedorowicz, Muthu Veerappan Ramalingam

Abstract
. This paper establishes a generalized relationship between the arc length of sinusoidal spirals r n = cos ⁡ ( n ​ θ ) r^{n}=\cos(n\theta) and the area of generalized Lamé curves defined by x 2 ​ n + y 2 ​ n = 1 x^{2n}+y^{2n}=1 . Building on our previous work connecting the lemniscate to the squircle, we prove an integral identity relating these two curves for any positive integer n n , which we further generalize to arbitrary positive real exponents and general superellipses. We further extend this correspondence to a geometric relationship between radial sectors of the Lamé curve and arc lengths of the spiral, providing a physical interpretation where keplerian motion on the Lamé curve corresponds to uniform motion on the spiral. Additionally, we derive an explicit central force law for keplerian motion along the Lamé curve. Finally, we introduce “policles”—a new class of curves generalizing the squircle—and demonstrate a direct geometric mapping between their sectors and the arc lengths of sinusoidal spirals.

When

In the context of this paper, sinusoidal spirals are of particular interest because their arc length formulas are given by
integrals which are simple generalizations of the elliptic integral
\int\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{4}}}

(4)

ds=\sqrt{r^{2}d\theta^{2}+dr^{2}}.

Usually the polar equation is given in the form
r=f(\theta)
, in which case the arc length integral takes the form

(5)

L=\int_{\Theta_{1}}^{\Theta_{2}}ds=\int_{\Theta_{1}}^{\Theta_{2}}\sqrt{r^{2}+\left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^{2}}\,d\theta.

However if the polar equation can be written in the form
\theta=g(r)
, then the arc length formula takes the form

(6)

L=\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}ds=\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\sqrt{1+r^{2}\left(\frac{d\theta}{dr}\right)^{2}}\,dr.

In the case of the sinusoidal spiral restricted to the interval
0\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2n}
, the polar equation
can be rewritten as
\theta=\frac{1}{n}\arccos(r^{n})
, and using equation (
6
) we obtain

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\sqrt{1+r^{2}\left(-\frac{r^{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}\right)^{2}}\,dr

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{r^{2n}}{1-r^{2n}}}\,dr

(7)

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}},

where
0\leq R_{1}\leq R_{2}\leq 1
.

For reference we also recall the formula for the area of radial sectors of polar curves:

(8)

A=\int_{\Theta_{1}}^{\Theta_{2}}\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\,d\theta.

3.
The Lamé-Sinusoidal Spiral Relation

We first establish the fundamental integral identity linking the geometry of the Lamé curve to that of the sinusoidal spiral.

Theorem 1
.

For any positive integer
n
, the following equality holds:

(9)

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}=2^{1/n}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt[2n]{1-x^{2n}}\,dx

Proof.

The integral
A=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt[2n]{1-x^{2n}}\,dx
represents the area of the portion of
the Lamé curve
x^{2n}+y^{2n}=1
contained within the first quadrant. An alternative way of
computing this area is to use the following parametrization of the Lamé curve:

x=\cos^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt),\ y=\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt),\quad 0\leq t\leq\frac{\pi}{2n}.

[Note that these are the same as the polar equations of the sinusoidal spiral restricted to the interval
\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2n}\right]
.] Thus we have

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\left(x\frac{dy}{dt}-y\frac{dx}{dt}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\left(\frac{\cos^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt)\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt)\cos(nt)}{\sin(nt)}+\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt)\cos^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt)\sin(nt)}{\cos(nt)}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\left(\frac{\cos^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt)\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt)\left(\cos^{2}(nt)+\sin^{2}(nt)\right)}{\cos(nt)\sin(nt)}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{1}{n}}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\left(\frac{2^{\frac{1}{n}}\cos^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt)\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nt)}{2\cos(nt)\sin(nt)}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{1}{n}}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(2nt)}{\sin(2nt)}\,dt

Substituting
u=2t
into the last integral, we obtain

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{1+\frac{1}{n}}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{n}}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)}{\sin(nu)}\,du

(10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{1+\frac{1}{n}}}\left[\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)}{\sin(nu)}\,du+\int_{\frac{\pi}{2n}}^{\frac{\pi}{n}}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)}{\sin(nu)}\,du\right]

Using the substitution
v=\frac{\pi}{n}-u
, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{\pi}{2n}}^{\frac{\pi}{n}}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)}{\sin(nu)}\,du

\displaystyle=-\int_{\frac{\pi}{2n}}^{0}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(\pi-nv)}{\sin(\pi-nv)}\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nv)}{\sin(nv)}\,dv

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)}{\sin(nu)}\,du

Thus (
10
) simplifies to

(11)

A=\frac{1}{2^{1+\frac{1}{n}}}\left[2\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)}{\sin(nu)}\,du\right]=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{1}{n}}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)}{\sin(nu)}\,du.

Making the substitution
r=\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)
, we obtain

\frac{dr}{du}=\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)\cos(nu)}{\sin(nu)}=\frac{\sin^{\frac{1}{n}}(nu)\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}{\sin(nu)},

which implies that

(12)

A=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{1}{n}}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}

Hence

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}=2^{\frac{1}{n}}A=2^{\frac{1}{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt[2n]{1-x^{2n}}\,dx.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Generalized Geometric Relations

In our previous work, we utilized a result of Siegel to establish a geometric correspondence between the lemniscate and the squircle,

[
3
, Theorem 10]
. We now provide a generalized version of this theorem which relates the sinusoidal spirals to the generalized Lamé curves.

Theorem 5
.

Let
n
be a positive real number,
0\leq T\leq 1
, and
R^{n}=\frac{2T^{n}}{1+T^{2n}}
. Then

(17)

\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}=2^{\frac{1}{n}}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\,dv}{\sqrt[n]{1+v^{2n}}}.

Proof.

We make the substitution
r^{n}=\frac{2v^{n}}{1+v^{2n}}
in the integral on the left hand side. Then we have

\left(\frac{r}{v}\right)^{n}=\frac{2}{1+v^{2n}}

Raising both sides of this equation to the
-\frac{n-1}{n}
power, we obtain

(18)

\left(\frac{v}{r}\right)^{n-1}=\frac{(1+v^{2n})^{1-\frac{1}{n}}}{2^{1-\frac{1}{n}}}

We also have

\displaystyle 1-r^{2n}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad 1-\left(\frac{2v^{n}}{1+v^{2n}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\frac{(1+v^{2n})^{2}-4v^{2n}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\frac{1+2v^{2n}+v^{4n}-4v^{2n}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\frac{1-2v^{2n}+v^{4n}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\frac{(1-v^{2n})^{2}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}

Hence we have

(19)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}=\frac{1+v^{2n}}{1-v^{2n}}

Differentiating the substitution we obtain

\displaystyle nr^{n-1}\,\frac{dr}{dv}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\frac{2nv^{n-1}(1+v^{2n})-2v^{n}\cdot 2nv^{2n-1}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\frac{2nv^{n-1}+2nv^{3n-1}-4nvv^{3n-1}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\frac{2nv^{n-1}-2nv^{3n-1}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad 2nv^{n-1}\frac{1-v^{2n}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}

Hence we have

(20)

dr=2\left(\frac{v}{r}\right)^{n-1}\frac{1-v^{2n}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}\,dv

Substituting (11), (12) and (13) into the integral on the left hand side of (10), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1+v^{2n}}{1-v^{2n}}\right)\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{v}{r}\right)^{n-1}\frac{1-v^{2n}}{(1+v^{2n})^{2}}\,dv

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{1+v^{2n}}\right)\left(\frac{(1+v^{2n})^{1-\frac{1}{n}}}{2^{1-\frac{1}{n}}}\right)\,dv

\displaystyle\quad=\quad 2^{\frac{1}{n}}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\,dv}{\sqrt[n]{1+v^{2n}}}.

∎

6.
General Force Law for Lamé Curves

It is well known that a particle moving in keplerian motion around the origin is subject to a central force directed
towards the origin. In this section we derive an explicit formula for the central force which compels a particle
to move in keplerian motion around the Lamé curve
x^{2n}+y^{2n}=1
.

Theorem 8
.

For
n>1
the central force law for a particle in keplerian motion around the Lamé curve
x^{2n}+y^{2n}=1
has the form

(25)

F(r)=-Cr^{4n-3}w^{2n-2},

where
w=\sin\theta\cos\theta=\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta)
and
C
is a constant depending on the physical properties
of the moving particle.

Proof.

The differential equation of an orbit under a central force
F(r)
directed towards the origin is given by Binet’s equation
[
16
]
,
[
4
, p. 87 (3.34)]
,
[
2
]
:

(26)

F(r)=-mh^{2}u^{2}\left(u+\frac{d^{2}u}{d\theta^{2}}\right)

where:

•

u=\frac{1}{r}
,

•

h
is the specific angular momentum (constant),

•

m
is the mass of the orbiting body.

We begin by rewriting the cartesian equation for the Lamé curve in polar coordinates:

x=r\cos(\theta)
and
y=r\sin(\theta)
:

\displaystyle x^{2n}+y^{2n}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad 1

\displaystyle[r\cos(\theta)]^{2n}+[r\sin(\theta)]^{2n}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad 1,

obtaining

\displaystyle r^{2n}(\cos^{2n}(\theta)+\sin^{2n}(\theta)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad 1

\displaystyle r^{2n}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\frac{1}{\cos^{2n}(\theta)+\sin^{2n}(\theta)}

\displaystyle u^{2n}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad\cos^{2n}(\theta)+\sin^{2n}(\theta)

To avoid notational clutter we abbreviate
c=\cos(\theta)
and
s=\sin(\theta)
. Thus

(27)

u^{n}=c^{2n}+s^{2n}:=A

Differentiating with respect to
\theta
:

\displaystyle 2nu^{2n-1}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2ns^{2n-1}c-2nc^{2n-1}s

\displaystyle u^{2n-1}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=sc(s^{2n-2}-c^{2n-2})

Let
w=sc
and
B=s^{2n-2}-c^{2n-2}
.
We have:

(28)

u^{2n-1}u^{\prime}=wB

Differentiating (
28
) with respect to
\theta
we obtain:

(29)

(2n-1)u^{2n-2}(u^{\prime})^{2}+u^{2n-1}u^{\prime\prime}=(wB)^{\prime}=w^{\prime}B+wB^{\prime}

We compute the derivatives of the components:

•

w^{\prime}=(sc)^{\prime}=c^{2}-s^{2}

•

B^{\prime}=(s^{2n-2}-c^{2n-2})^{\prime}=(2n-2)s^{2n-3}c-(2n-2)c^{2n-3}(-s)

•

B^{\prime}=(2n-2)sc(s^{2n-4}+c^{2n-4})=(2n-2)wC
, where
C=s^{2n-4}+c^{2n-4}
.

Substituting these back we get:

(30)

(2n-1)u^{2n-2}(u^{\prime})^{2}+u^{2n-1}u^{\prime\prime}=(c^{2}-s^{2})B+(2n-2)w^{2}C

It will be convenient in what follows to take

X=u^{4n-1}(u+u^{\prime\prime})=u^{2n}(u^{2n-1}u^{\prime\prime})+u^{4n}=A(u^{2n-1}u^{\prime\prime}).

From (
30
), we isolate
u^{2n-1}u^{\prime\prime}
:

u^{2n-1}u^{\prime\prime}=(c^{2}-s^{2})B+(2n-2)w^{2}C-(2n-1)u^{2n-2}(u^{\prime})^{2}

From (
28
), we have
(u^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{w^{2}B^{2}}{u^{4n-2}}
.
Thus:

(2n-1)u^{2n-2}(u^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{(2n-1)w^{2}B^{2}}{u^{2n}}

Substituting this into the expression for
X
:

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=A\left[(c^{2}-s^{2})B+(2n-2)w^{2}C-\frac{(2n-1)w^{2}B^{2}}{A}\right]+A^{2}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=A^{2}+A(c^{2}-s^{2})B+(2n-2)w^{2}AC-(2n-1)w^{2}B^{2}

We simplify the first two terms
A^{2}+A(c^{2}-s^{2})B
:

\displaystyle(c^{2}-s^{2})B

\displaystyle=(c^{2}-s^{2})(s^{2n-2}-c^{2n-2})

\displaystyle=s^{2n-2}c^{2}-c^{2n}-s^{2n}+s^{2}c^{2n-2}

\displaystyle=s^{2}c^{2}(s^{2n-4}+c^{2n-4})-(s^{2n}+c^{2n})

\displaystyle=w^{2}C-A

Therefore:

A(c^{2}-s^{2})B+A^{2}=A(w^{2}C-A)+A^{2}=Aw^{2}C-A^{2}+A^{2}=Aw^{2}C.

Substituting this simplified term back into
X
:

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=(Aw^{2}C)+(2n-2)Aw^{2}C-(2n-1)w^{2}B^{2}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=(2n-1)Aw^{2}C-(2n-1)w^{2}B^{2}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=(2n-1)w^{2}[AC-B^{2}]

Finally we evaluate
AC-B^{2}
:

\displaystyle AC

\displaystyle=(s^{2n}+c^{2n})(s^{2n-4}+c^{2n-4})=s^{4n-4}+c^{4n-4}+s^{2n}c^{2n-4}+c^{2n}s^{2n-4}

\displaystyle B^{2}

\displaystyle=(s^{2n-2}-c^{2n-2})^{2}=s^{4n-4}+c^{4n-4}-2s^{2n-2}c^{2n-2}

Subtracting:

\displaystyle AC-B^{2}

\displaystyle=s^{2n}c^{2n-4}+c^{2n}s^{2n-4}+2s^{2n-2}c^{2n-2}

\displaystyle=s^{2n-4}c^{2n-4}(s^{4}+c^{4}+2s^{2}c^{2})

\displaystyle=w^{2n-4}(s^{2}+c^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=w^{2n-4}(1)^{2}=w^{2n-4}

Substituting back into
X
we get

X=(2n-1)w^{2}[w^{2n-4}]=(2n-1)w^{2n-2}

Since
X=u^{4n-1}(u+u^{\prime\prime})
, we obtain the following:

(31)

u+u^{\prime\prime}=\frac{(2n-1)w^{2n-2}}{u^{4n-1}}

Substituting (
31
) into equation (
26
) we obtain

\displaystyle F(r)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad-mh^{2}u^{2}\left(\frac{(2n-1)w^{2n-2}}{u^{4n-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad-C\left(\frac{(2n-1)w^{2n-2}}{u^{4n-3}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\quad-Cr^{4n-3}w^{2n-2},

where
C=(2n-1)mh^{2}
.
∎

Remark 9
.

The case
n=1
is degenerate. A circular orbit is compatible with any attractive central force.

Remark 10
.

It might appear that equation (
25
) is a defective central force formula, since it involves both
polar coordinates
r
and
\theta
instead of
r
alone. However one should keep in mind that the polar coordinates

r
and
\theta
are not independent, since they are related to each other by the equation of the Lamé curve. In
fact by applying the half-angle formulas we can rewrite

(32)

u^{2n}=\cos^{2n}(\theta)+\sin^{2n}(\theta)=\left[\frac{1+\cos(2\theta)}{2}\right]^{n}+\left[\frac{1-\cos(2\theta)}{2}\right]^{n}

Moreover in equation (
32
), the odd powers of
\cos(2\theta)
cancel out and by another
application of the half-angle formula
\cos^{2}(2\theta)=\frac{1+\cos(4\theta)}{2}
,
u^{2n}
can be expressed as a
polynomial in
\cos(4\theta)
. Further using the trigonometric identity

\cos(4\theta)=1-2\sin^{2}(2\theta)=1-8w^{2},

we can rewrite
u^{2n}
as a polynomial in
w^{2}
. For
n=2,3
the relation between
u^{2n}
and
w^{2}
is linear and for

n=4,5
the relation is quadratic. Hence in those cases we can explicitly solve for
w^{2}
in terms of
u=\frac{1}{r}
, and
thus obtain central force formulas explicitly in terms of
r
alone. One obtains the following formulas:

\displaystyle n=2

\displaystyle\quad:\quad F(r)=Cr(1-r^{4})

\displaystyle n=3

\displaystyle\quad:\quad F(r)=-C\frac{(1-r^{6})^{2}}{r^{3}}

\displaystyle n=4

\displaystyle\quad:\quad F(r)=Cr\left(\sqrt{2r^{8}+2}-2r^{4}\right)^{3}

\displaystyle n=5

\displaystyle\quad:\quad F(r)=Cr^{2}\left(\sqrt{5r^{10}+2}-5r^{5}\right)^{3}

Sinusoidal Spirals and Policles

The reader might observe that Theorem 2 generalizes
[
3
, Theorem 10]
, which is a
reformulation of the main result
[
3
, Theorem 1]
, based on a calculation of Siegel
[
13
]
.
The reader might inquire whether there is a simple direct generalization of that main result relating
the squircle and lemniscate to one between Lamé curves and sinusoidal spirals. Regrettably, this is
not the case. However there is such a simple direct generalization if we replace the Lamé curves
x^{2n}+y^{2n}=1

by a different generalization of the squircle: curves given by polar equations

(33)

r^{4}=\frac{n\sin^{2}(n\theta)}{1-\cos^{2n}(n\theta)}

When
n=2
this curve is a squircle, a rounded square.
For general
n
these curves look like rounded regular
2n
-gons.
We call these curves
policles
(“polygon”
+
“circle”) by analogy with squircle (“square”
+
“circle”).
With this modification, we have the following simple direct generalization of
[
3
, Theorem 1]
,
illustrated by the following figure for the case
n=3
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
B
be a point in the polar sector
0\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2n}
of the sinusoidal spiral

r^{n}=\cos(n\theta)
and let
B^{\prime}

be its radial projection onto the policle
r^{4}=\frac{n\sin^{2}(n\theta)}{1-\cos^{2n}(n\theta)}
. Let
C
be the point on this polar sector of the sinusoidal spiral such that

\overline{OC}=\overline{OB}^{n}
. Then

(34)

l=2a\sqrt{n},

where
l
is the arc length of the sinusoidal spiral from
C
to
P=(1,0)
and
a
denotes the area of the policular sector
OPB^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let the polar coordinates of
B
be
(r,\theta)=(R_{1},\alpha)
and the polar coordinates of
C
be

(r,\theta)=(R_{2},\beta)
. Then by definition we have

(35)

R_{2}=\overline{OC}=\overline{OB}^{n}=R_{1}^{n}=\cos(n\alpha)

Calculating arc lengths and radial sector areas in polar coordinates, we obtain

(36)

l=\int_{R_{2}}^{1}\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}=\int_{\cos(n\alpha)}^{1}\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}

and

(37)

a=\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\,d\theta=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{\sqrt{n}\sin(n\theta)d\theta}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2n}(n\theta)}}

Making the substitution
r=\cos(n\theta)
in equation (
37
), we have

dr=-n\sin(n\theta)\,d\theta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sin(n\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{1}{n}dr

and we obtain

a=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\cos(n\alpha)}^{1}\frac{\sqrt{n}\frac{1}{n}dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\int_{\cos(n\alpha)}^{1}\frac{\,dr}{\sqrt{1-r^{2n}}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}l.

Thus
l=2a\sqrt{n}
.
∎

As in Section
5
, we can interpret this correspondence as relating keplerian motion around the policle to uniform motion
around the sinusoidal spiral. We leave the details as an exercise for the reader.
