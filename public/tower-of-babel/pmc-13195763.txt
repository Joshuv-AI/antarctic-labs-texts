Drug resistance profile and species diversity in tuberculosis mycobacteria isolated from patients with presumptive tuberculosis in Brazzaville, Republic of Congo

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Dominance of Mycobacterium tuberculosis sensu stricto over Mycobacterium africanum.•High prevalence of non-tuberculous mycobacteria in isolates.•Significant monoresistance to first-line anti-tuberculosis (TB) drugs observed.•Multidrug-resistant TB prevalence at 7%, indicating rising treatment failure risk.•Provides key data to guide TB control in Central Africa.Keywords: Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, Drug resistance, Republic of Congo, Non-tuberculous mycobacteria

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a global public health priority, with around 10.8 million cases, including 1.1 million related deaths in 2023 [1]. The emergence of multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) significantly complicates control efforts and treatments, mainly in resource-constrained settings such as the Republic of Congo. In 2023, approximately 400,000 people developed drug-resistant TB; however, only 44 % were diagnosed and enrolled on appropriate treatment regimens [1].

The Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) is composed of different species. Accurate identification of these species and determination of their drug susceptibility profiles are crucial to effectively manage TB [2]. This requires implementation of accurate, rapid, and easy-to-use tools to continually assess the drugs susceptibility profiles of MTB strains while appropriately identifying species within MTBC to properly adapt treatment and curb the spread of resistant strains [3]. In the Republic of Congo, data on the diversity of not only MTBC species but also non-TB mycobacteria (NTM) and their related resistance to anti-TB drugs remain limited, hampering efforts to control the disease.

With an incidence of 368 per 100,000 inhabitants and approximately 560 MDR/rifampicin (RIF) TB cases, the Republic of Congo is one of the high–TB burden countries. However, due to limited resources and logistical challenges, the implementation of phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (DST) is hindered, thus further complicating the situation.

Although RIF resistance is annually documented, there is a lack of knowledge regarding resistance to other critical anti-TB drugs, which may significantly impact the efficacy of the recently implemented Bédaquiline, Prétomanide , Linézolide , and Moxifloxacine (BpaLM) regimen. In addition, very little is known about the MTBC species or NTM circulating in Brazzaville. Therefore, this study aims to characterize the drug resistance profile and identify the MTBC and non-TB species isolated from patients with presumptive TB in Brazzaville. With the support of the Central African Clinical Research Network (CANTAM) [4], this study was conducted to provide epidemiologic data, guide therapeutic strategies, and strengthen the national capacity to prevent drug resistance development.

Methodology

Ethical approval

The protocol received approval from the institutional ethics committee of the Congolese Foundation for Medical Research (015/CIE/FCRM/May 30, 2018). Procedures were all conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national ethical guidelines. Before enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from adults, whereas assent, followed by guardian consent, was obtained for minors.

Study site

The study was conducted in the political capital city of the Republic of Congo (Brazzaville). Participants were recruited from Makélékélé reference hospital, which is the second-largest reference hospital located south of Brazzaville. The facility provides diagnostic, treatment, and hospitalization services.

Sample collection

The study design was based on a previously published investigation conducted among the same group of patients with presumptive TB [4]. From October 2018 to October 2019, sputum samples were collected from patients with presumptive TB who had never received treatment. Sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical data were recorded using a standard structured questionnaire. On the day of consultation, one blood and one sputum sample were collected from each participant (Figure 1) for HIV testing (blood) or for MTB testing by Xpert MTB/RIF (sputum), respectively. No matter the Xpert MTB result, a second sputum (early morning sputum) was collected by patient to ensure diagnostic confirmation.

Mycobacterial liquid culture using the MGIT 960 system

After decontamination, sputum samples of all participants were transported to the Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-III) Tuberculosis Laboratory at the Centre de Recherche Médicale de Lambaréné (CERMEL), Gabon for culture and line probe assay. For liquid culture, approximately 0.5 ml of each sample was inoculated into a MGIT 960 tube (Becton Dickinson, USA) supplemented with OADC (oleic acid, albumin, dextrose, and catalase) (Becton Dickinson) and PANTA (polymyxin B, amphotericin B, nalidixic acid, trimethoprim, and azlocillin) (Becton Dickinson) antimicrobial agents. Then the tubes were incubated in the BACTEC MGIT 960 system, where positive cultures, typically obtained around day 15, were confirmed for acid-fast bacilli via Ziehl–Neelsen staining and checked for sterility on blood agar. Cultures were considered negative only after 42 days of incubation without proof of growing [5].

Identification of MTBC and NTM

The BD MGIT TM TBc Identification Test (Becton, Dickinson, USA) was used to identify the MTBC and the NTM. It is a rapid immunochromatographic assay which functions by detecting the MPB64 antigen, specific to MTBC species members, via antigen-antibody interaction on a lateral flow strip. A control line ensures validity. Although the appearance of a second line confirms the presence of MTBC, the absence of the test line indicates the isolate is likely an NTM [6].

MTBC species identification

Species identification within the MTBC was performed using the GenoType MTBC version 1.x kit (Hain Lifescience GmbH, Nehren, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s specifications [7]. Briefly, DNA was extracted from positive culture samples using thermal lysis and sonication. The resulting DNA extract was used as a template for polymerase chain reaction amplification, followed by hybridization and detection on a TWINCUBATOR platform. Banding patterns were interpreted using the provider’s interpretation chart to distinguish MTBC species.

DST

DST was performed using the indirect proportion method on the BACTEC MGIT 960 system (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions [8]. The drug concentrations used were: 0.1 µg/ml for isoniazid (INH), 1 µg/ml for RIF, 5 µg/ml for ethambutol (EMB), 1 µg/ml for streptomycin (STR), and 100 µg/ml for pyrazinamide, respectively [9]. MGIT tubes were supplemented with 0.8 ml of SIRE supplement (MGIT 960 SIRE supplement; Becton Dickinson), then inoculated with 0.1 ml of the drug solution and 0.5 ml of the bacterial suspension from a positive culture. For the drug-free growth control tube, the bacterial suspension was diluted 1:100 in sterile saline, and 0.5 ml was inoculated (proportion method) [10].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software, version 24 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of continuous variables’ distribution. Non-normally distributed data were reported as median with interquartile range, whereas normally distributed data were reported as mean with SD. Qualitative data were presented as counts and proportions with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Bivariate analyses were conducted to assess relationships between variables. Associations between categorical variables were examined using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated culture (Mycobacteria growth indicator tube) positivity. Results were presented as crude odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs and the adjustment of ORs (AOR) were done including in all the retained variables. For all analyses, P <0.05 was considered significant.

Factors associated with MTB culture positivity

The association between potential exposure variables and M. tuberculosis culture positivity is presented in Table 1. A positive chest X-ray was strongly associated with culture positivity (AOR = 18.8; 95% CI: 1.68-20.9; P = 0.017). In contrast, MTB/HIV co-infection was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of culture positivity (AOR = 0.06; 95% CI: 0.01-0.28; P = 0.0001).

Among clinical symptoms, only weight loss showed a significant association with culture positivity (AOR = 4.58; 95% CI: 1.00-23.4; P = 0.031). Other symptoms, including chronic cough, acute cough, fever, anemia, neurologic signs, night sweats, asthenia, and anorexia, were not significantly associated with MTB detection. Smoking (AOR = 2.45; 95% CI: 1.02-5.87; P = 0.044) was significantly associated with MTB positivity. No significant associations were observed for sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, sex, marital status, education level, or recruitment season (Table 1).

Distribution of culture-positive isolates, MTBC distribution, and drug resistance profiles

Of the 48 culture-positive samples, 41 (85.4%) were identified as belonging to the MTBC, of which 33 (80.5%) were M. tuberculosis and eight (19.5%) were Mycobacterium africanum, whereas seven (14.6%) were NTM. All drug resistance cases were confined to M. tuberculosis isolates, with diverse patterns of mono-, multi-, and poly-resistance observed (Table 2).

Discussion

This study was designed to determine the phenotypic drug profile and the species diversity of MTBC and NTM isolates circulating in the south of Brazzaville, Republic of Congo.

The profile of patients with bacteriologically confirmed TB by culture in this study showed a predominance of young adults, with weight loss and chronic cough being the most common symptoms. These symptoms are consistent with the classical presentation of pulmonary TB [11], [12], [13], [14]. The extremely high proportion of chest X-ray positivity in this study, as revealed by lung abnormalities consistent with TB (as infiltrates, cavities, nodules, or pleural effusion), clearly reinforces its role as a key diagnostic indicator [15]. We also found a relatively low frequency of HIV co-infection, contrasting with trends often observed in similar settings. Lastly, a notable finding was the high proportion of patients reporting smoking, highlighting the important role of behavioral factors in TB occurrence and progression [16]. HIV co-infection markedly reduced the likelihood of culture positivity, which may reflect the lower bacterial load often observed in co-infected patients [17]. Among clinical variables, only weight loss emerged as a significant marker of culture positivity [18,19]. The association with smoking further emphasizes the role of behavioral risk factors in TB infection [20]. In contrast, no significant link was observed with sociodemographic characteristics, suggesting that behavioral and biological features could play a more critical role in determining bacteriological confirmation than demographic background. This is more likely because behavioral factors such as smoking, alcohol use, or bacteriological factors such as HIV co-infection or other comorbidities have been repeatedly associated with TB because they impair lung defense or strongly influence bacillary load [21].

Regarding analysis related to mycobacteria culture positivity, we found that 85.42% of isolates were MTBC and 14.58% NTM. This was consistent with the previously reported species distribution of TB mycobacteria in Brazzaville [22].

In this study, the prevalence of NTM was also slightly higher than in some previous studies, such as those from Gabon (10%) [23], Cameroon (8.3%) [24], and Nigeria (11%) [25]. This variability may be related to environmental conditions (soil, water), natural reservoirs, or some differences in laboratory techniques [26] and the number of patients included in the analysis. The presence of NTM, which does not respond to standard TB treatments, highlights the need to strengthen differential diagnosis to avoid inappropriate and costly treatments.

Within the MTBC, M. tuberculosis accounted for 80% of isolates, whereas M. africanum accounted for 20%. This finding is noteworthy because M. africanum is geographically mostly restricted to West African countries [27,28], such as Nigeria, Côte d’Ivoire, the Gambia, and Ghana [29,30], although its prevalence varies between setting. The presence of 20% in our study could suggests an endemic presence in the Congo Basin, possibly linked to historical and geographical factors [31]. Although its transmissibility and virulence are often lower than those of M. tuberculosis [32], M. africanum should be included in molecular surveillance strategies.

When looking at drug resistance patterns according to MTBC species, the analysis revealed that all drug resistance was confined to M. tuberculosis isolates, with diverse patterns of mono-, multi-, and poly-resistance observed. This result may be explained by M. africanum being often less prevalent and, thus, less exposed to drug pressure [33].

We found a pattern of frequent monoresistances, mainly to RIF, followed by EMB and INH. This unexpectedly high prevalence, particularly, in INH and RIF may reflect a broader factor, such as their use in preventive therapy or latent TB infection (which may be a monotherapy with INH or RIF or a 3-month combination) treatment. Moreover, the widespread use of RIF and INH preventive therapy in a high HIV/TB population may further explain the elevated baseline resistance. These resistant strains can then be transmitted, contributing to the high burden of monoresistance observed in active TB cases.

The MDR-TB (INH and RIF) prevalence was about 7.3% comparable to the 8.3% recently reported from Brazzaville [34]. Because RIF and INH are the cornerstones of first-line treatment, resistance to these drugs is particularly concerning. Of note, the country is not among the highest DR-TB burden countries. However, the number of participants could also explain these proportions. Other previous studies have also reported similar levels of monoresistance, ranging from 15% to 35% [35,36]. Several factors may account for this high prevalence, including treatment interruptions [37], self-medication, poor adherence, and difficulties in drug supply.

All resistant patterns, including 2.4% resistant to STR, INH, and EMB; 2.4% resistant to INH, RIF, and EMB; and 19.5% MDR in INH and EMB, reflect prolonged drug pressure and the progressive selection of resistant strains, as previously noted by other authors [38,39].

Limitations

The relatively small sample size (fewer than 100 patients) represents a key limitation of this study because it may affect the representativeness of the findings, reduce the statistical power to detect significant associations, and limit the generalizability of the results to the broader population. In addition, the study was conducted in a single referral hospital, which may introduce a selection bias because the included patients are more likely to present with more severe or advanced forms of the disease than in cases in the general community.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important preliminary and context-specific data in a setting where epidemiologic and microbiological information remains scarce. These findings should, therefore, be interpreted with caution but are valuable in guiding future research. In particular, they highlight the need for larger, multicenter, and prospective studies, which are planned, to better characterize the epidemiology of M. tuberculosis complex and non-tuberculous mycobacteria, as well as patterns of drug resistance in the Republic of Congo.

Conclusion

This study highlights a high prevalence of culture-confirmed TB, with a predominance of M. tuberculosis, alongside a notable presence of M. africanum and non-tuberculous mycobacteria. Young adults with weight loss and chronic cough were most common symptoms, whereas risk behaviors such as smoking increased patient vulnerability. The observed resistance profile, characterized by high rates of monoresistance and concerning levels of multidrug resistance, underscores the urgent need to strengthen diagnostic capacity, genomic surveillance, and integrated prevention strategies.
