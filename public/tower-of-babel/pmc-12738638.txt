Using Strain-Hardening Cement-based Composites (SHCC) reinforced with steel meshes for strengthening concrete bearing strength
Authors: Sabry Fayed, Rabeea W Bazuhair, Ehab A Mlybari, Md Habibur Rahman Sobuz, Sani Aliyu Abubakar, Mohamed Ghalla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to improve the bearing strength of concrete pedestals exposed to concentric and eccentric local compressive pressure, this study investigates the application of several external confinement techniques. Concrete blocks were tested under both concentric and eccentric local pressure after being cast and externally supplemented with SHCC jacketing. The main variable was the use of steel meshes (SMs) to reinforce SHCC. These SMs vary in number (1, 2, and 3) and height-to-block height ratios (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1). Furthermore, the effects of the following were investigated: the use of conventional stirrups reinforcement inside SHCC jacketing and the confinement sides of the block; the number of grooves on the substrate concrete-SHCC interface, the load eccentricity ratio (0, 0.25, and 0.39), and the steel fiber content (0, 0.5, and 1%). The bearing strength rose 17.3% when the control block was reinforced with 25 mm-SHCC jacketing. In comparison to the un-strengthened sample, the bearing strength increased by 50%, when three layers of steel meshes were used. When the block sample was reinforced from one side by the SHCC layer, the bearing strength did not increase.Keywords: Bearing strength, Concrete, Local pressure, Strengthening, SHCC, Steel mesh, Confinement, EccentricitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The concrete bearing strength is regarded as a crucial design value for the safe transfer of the forces to concrete supports, including column-foundation joints, girder bearings, corbels, bridge pedestals, hinges, post-tension participants, support anchors, and other types of super-structure supports. Significant concentrated loads may be applied to these structural supports, potentially compromising the skeletal system of the building. Unequal distribution of load may result from massive concentrated loads acting on the concrete’s surface1,2. Because of this, when designing these participants, the bearing capacity is typically assumed to be, depending on the ratio of the loaded to the unloaded area (A2/A1), between the compressive strength and double that value. But the bearing capacity is significantly underestimated by this method. On the other hand, not much research has been done on the local bearing capacity of concrete under pressure. Thus, the purpose of this study is to learn more about the bearing performance of the concrete, particularly when the concrete is externally confined.

Particularly in post-tensioned prestressing buildings, concrete local bearing capacity is an essential mechanical indication of concrete constructions3. Local stress propagates lateral across the thickness direction until the intense prestress is uniformly distributed across the section as a result of the concentrated prestress being transferred to the structure across the bearing plate. The anchorage zone (AZ) is the region where the aforementioned process occurs. In engineering design, it is usually important to make sure that the AZ local breakdown cannot appear before the failure of the entire structure. Because of the complicated and chaotic stress conditions in the AZ, the plane section assumption is not followed in the computation4.

The theory proposed by Au and Baird5 to explain bearing failure in concrete blocks proposed the formation of an inverted pyramid beneath the loading plate, with the assumption that the horizontal forces would originate from the downward penetration of the inverted pyramid before its collapse, causing tension and combined bending strain in the concrete block, failing when the maximum tensile stress at the block top exceeds the tensile strength of the concrete. In accordance with the findings of an experimental study on bearing capacity of concrete-reinforced segments with an A2/A1 ratio of four, Bonetti6 demonstrated that the first cracking load brought on by the bearing stresses in the block is only dependent on the A2/A1 ratio and the compressive and tensile properties of the concrete, and is unaffected by the level of stirrups reinforcing. Furthermore, as the confining steel ratio grows, the bearing strength improves, according to the data. The relationship between steel and concrete plate was studied by Zain et al.7, specifically looking at how confinement effects affected the concrete blocks’ ability to support weight. Twelve 200 × 200 × 200 mm concrete blocks were tested under compression load until they failed. According to experimental data, using a larger bearing plate produced a higher value of load bearing capacity than using a smaller bearing plate due to the larger contact area, which improved the confinement effect. It was discovered that when a steel plate is positioned on top of concrete blocks, the blocks collapsed to form an inverse pyramid. In addition, splitting cracks and vertical cracks that emerged at the contact area’s outside border are additional failures. Yahya8 investigated the bearing effectiveness of the concrete by analyzing the impact of varying circular loaded bearing plate heights that were placed on top of concrete cylinders of varying heights. Compared to 200 mm concrete cylinders, it was found that the circular concrete block with a low block height (50 mm) had a better load capability. It was also discovered that the results show that radial cracking starts from the edge of the contact region before the concrete bearing fails globally. Such crack lines are also extending along the upper surface and extending the length of the concrete blocks, indicating that concrete bearing damage is due to concrete shear failure and spalling.

According to the rules of the codes of practice, concrete structures are built for the service lives that they have chosen. Higher mechanical loading that is greater than the design value can cause failure by enhancing early degradation9,10. Apart from the consequences of extreme mechanical stress and harsh environmental circumstances, there may be times when the constructed structures need to be modified to meet the changing needs. For instance, additional lanes are given by modifying the current lane widths on existing road bridges as a relief solution for traffic congestion11. In these situations, the planned design load may not be met by the structure’s load, necessitating the strengthening of key structural elements. The necessity of retrofitting is further underscored by the possibility of design faults, construction errors, and modifications to the intended use of structures, particularly in buildings. In light of the previously described aspects, strengthening reinforced concrete structures is becoming more and more important in order to increase their capacity. Therefore, the goal of this study is to improve the bearing performance of concrete by conducting external confinement using concrete jacketing.

One of the most crucial materials for strengthening and repairing concrete structures in recent years is Strain-Hardening Cement-based Composites (SHCC). SHCC has a high capacity for energy absorption in shear, compression, and tension12–14. High-performance polymer micro-fibers, usually in volume fractions of 2%, are used to strengthen these synthetic materials. Because of the production of numerous cracks with a width of no more than 100 μm, SHCC may attain tensile strain up to 5% before failure localizes15,16. Because of their fresh-state characteristics, SHCC are also very well suited for use as exterior reinforcing layers via spraying or lamination17. Furthermore, SHCC show excellent mechanical and physical compatibility with concrete substrates, including the bond, because of their cementitious foundation18,19. Ultimately, even though SHCC is roughly two to three times more expensive than regular concrete (mostly due to the fiber cost), its price is still significantly less than that of the most widely available materials for repair20.

A number of upgrading techniques have been developed to reinforce and restore structural components that are inadequate; different types of jacketing are one such notion. These consist of steel wrapping and concrete21–24 or fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) layered in a U-shape to improve shear strength25–29 or completely wrapped throughout a rectangular component to strengthen plastic hinge regions. In general, to considerably improve the original concrete members, concrete or SHCC jacketing must provide an external reinforcement layer within the layer thickness, such as steel meshes or stirrups, to improve their performance. There have been no studies conducted on the use of SHCC jacketing to enhance the local bearing response of concrete.

Because SHCC is unable to swap out all of the steel bars needed to support the force in one direction. For various uses in construction, scientists have suggested using them in conjunction with reinforcement materials30,31. An important factor in developing a satisfactory performance of the composite structure is evaluating the binding behavior between the SHCC matrix and the reinforcement materials32. Steel meshes (SMs) and cement-based materials are currently widely used in the restoration of reinforced concrete structures33. In an experiment with retrofitted RC bridge columns, Kim34 used SM and permeable polymer mortar. He proposed a significant improvement in the column’s hysteretic behaviors as well as a satisfactory boost to the column’s carrying ability. The application of SMs in conjunction with SHCC improved current RC beams’ load bearing ability and longevity, delaying the onset of cracks in the concrete tension zone35. Adding SHCC layers to structural components enhanced them and increased their structural integrity, according to multiple research studies36–38.

Analysis has been done on the application of fiber reinforced polymer (FRP) to reinforce single cell box beams made of reinforced concrete40. The experimental work in a comprehensive examination for torsional strengthening of multicell box section RC girders using externally-bonded Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer (CFRP) strips is reported by Abeer A. For safe force transmission to the concrete supports, such as column-foundation connections, girder bearings, corbels, bridge pedestals, and other superstructure supports, the concrete bearing strength is a crucial characteristic. These structural supports may be subjected to large concentrated loads, which might cause the structure to fail. If the use of structures changes, the status of the structures deteriorates, or there is a design or execution fault, it could be essential to strengthen the structural parts. Strain hardening cementitious composites (SHCC) is one of the most important strengthening methods in use today due to its remarkable properties. The strength of these SHCC layers would be greatly increased if they were strengthened with steel meshes. Prior research has not examined the use of steel meshes to strengthen SHCC. This study examines the use of various external confinement strategies to increase the bearing strength of concrete pedestals subjected to concentric and eccentric local compressive pressure.

In this study, improving bearing strength of concrete using external confinement is investigated. To achieve that, many concrete blocks with size of 250 × 250 × 200 mm were cast then tested under concentric local pressure until failure. A steel plate with area of 80 × 80 mm was used to apply compression stress on the concrete block surface. External confinement was conducted using strain hardening cementitious composites (SHCC) jacketing around the concrete block. Variables are: (1) using conventional stirrups reinforcement inside SHCC jacketing, (2) using steel mesh (SM) inside SHCC jacketing, (3) effect of SM height, (4) effect of SM amount, (5) confinement sides of block (one, two, three and four sides), (6) impact of steel fiber content, (7) influence of grooves on the substrate concrete-SHCC interface and (8) effect of load eccentricity in case of blocks with/without strengthening.

Experimental investigation

Resources used

The same combination was used to make the concrete blocks that were being examined. Graded crushed basalt dolomite (1270 kg/m3) with an ideal size of 10 mm as a coarse aggregate, local sand (600 kg/m3) as a fine aggregate, water (175 kg/m3), and Portland cement (350 kg/m3) with a 42.5 degree were the ingredients of the concrete mixture. Three cubes with size of 150 × 150 × 150 mm were taken from the concrete mix to determine the properties of concrete such as compressive strength of the concrete. After 28 days, the cubic compressive characteristic was 32.5 MPa, 31.8 MPa and 31.1 MPa. The mean cubic compressive strength of the concrete was 32.1 MPa after 28 days.

External strengthening using strain hardening cementitious composites (SHCC) jacketing was performed around the concrete blocks. The same SHCC-combination was used to make jacketing. As shown in Table 1, sand, Portland cement, silica fume, quartz, water-reducer, polypropylene fiber (PF) and water were mixed to create SHCC. The lengths and diameter of PFs were 14 mm and 0.01 mm, correspondingly. Both SHCC and concrete blocks used the same sand and cement. Quartz was substituted with coarse aggregate. To make SHCC mix, a high cement quantity and a modest water to cement ration are needed. A small water/cement level will produce a mixture that is dry unless a water-reducer or silica fume is applied. In this project, the water to binder proportion was 0.21. In addition to being a component of cement, silica fume was also utilized to improve the properties of fresh cast and fully cured concrete. Three standard 150 × 150 × 150 mm cubes were taken from the SHCC mix to determine the compressive strength. After 28 days, the cubic compressive strength was 58.4 MPa, 58.8 MPa and 58.1 MPa. The mean cubic compressive strength of SHCC was 58.4 MPa after 28 days. Also, three cylinders (150 mm in diameter and 300 mm in height) were taken from the SHCC mix to determine the splitting strength. After 28 days, the split strength was 7.7 MPa, 7.6 MPa and 7.45 MPa. The average value of tension strength was 7.6 MPa after 28 days. Finally, with tension and compressive capacities of 7.6 MPa and 58 MPa, respectively, SHCC was used.

Concrete blocks and testing device

Up to their collapse, 22 concrete blocks were poured and tested under local compression pressures. Every concrete block was the same size, measuring 200 mm in height and 250 × 250 mm in cross-section. Three layers, each one equal to one-third of the block height, were cast for each block. To prevent segregation, the concrete was thoroughly compressed after each layer was cast. More than 28 days later, the SHCC strengthening was performed. After more one month, the bearing tests were carried out. After each pouring process, the curing with water was continued for seven days. No materials were used to bond new concrete (SHCC) with the old concrete (block) because the SHCC was good in the bond with the old concrete.

As seen in Fig. 3, a steel plate of 80 by 80 mm in area and 30 mm thick was positioned on the upper surface of the concrete block to apply local compression loading. The load was then imparted to the steel plate. In certain experiments, the plate was positioned in the middle of the concrete block’s top surface, whereas in other instances, it was positioned eccentrically. A load cell and displacement transducers were connected to a digital data recording system to capture bearing load and displacement values (slide of the loading plate). Using a hydraulic jack, a local compression load was applied to each sample test. A cross head displacement under the applied bearing load was estimated using a transducer with a 100 mm stroke and an accuracy of 0.01 mm. A 1.2 kN/s incremental concentric/eccentric load was applied.

Local compression loading (Bearing test).

Application of SHCC jacketing

Eighteen concrete blocks were tested under local compressive loading, three of them were with no SHCC strengthening and fifteen were strengthened, as illustrated in Fig. 4; Table 2. SHCC mix was the same in all samples. Wall thickness of SHCC jacketing was 25 mm in all samples. Height of SHCC jacketing was 200 mm in all samples which equal the concrete block height. Surface of block sides was well cleaned before casting SHCC jacketing. This investigation studied many parameters that applied on the tested samples. The studied variables are: (1) using conventional stirrups reinforcement inside SHCC jacketing (samples N1, N2,N3), (2) using steel mesh (SM) inside SHCC jacketing (sample SI4-1), (3) effect of SM height (samples SI4-0.25, SI4-0.5, SI4-0.75, SI4-1), (4) effect of SM amount (samples SI4-1, SII4-1, SIII4-1), (5) confinement sides of block (one SI1-1, two SI2-1, three SI3-1 and four sides SI4-1), (6) effect steel fiber reinforcement with two contents (samples SF0.5G0 and SF1G0), (7) conducting one and two vertical grooves on each block side (samples SF0.5G1 and SF0.5G2) and (8) effect of load eccentricity in case of blocks with/without strengthening (samples Ca, Cb, SI3-1a, SI3-1b).

Results and discussion

Failure modes

Figure 6 shows failures of all samples. In the first loading levels, the loading plate descends into the concrete surface without any cracking around the edges of the loading plate or on the sides of the block. In all bearing samples, the first crack appeared near the maximum load of the bearing. In almost samples, crack load-to-ultimate load equals 0.85–92.85 approximately. The un-strengthened specimen C directly and suddenly broke into pieces hence crack load-to-ultimate load equals 1 approximately. The strengthened specimen S also split in original block and strengthening layer as well as debonding between original block and strengthening layer occur. For samples N1, N2 and N3 that reinforced with lateral ties, the splitting failure appeared at both original block and strengthening layer although many cracks occurred in block N3 included three ties. For samples SI4-1, SII4-1 and SIII4-1 that provided with one, two and three of steel mesh, the splitting failure appeared at original block as well as partially cut occurred in the steel mesh. Same trend occurred in the three samples SI4-0.25, SI4-0.5 and SI4-0.75. It is evident in the previous samples that the failure pattern is often similar (splitting mode), as all of the samples were reinforced with a jacket on all sides. As the compressive load on the bearing plate increases, horizontal tensile forces are generated on the sides of the block. These forces cause cracks in the strengthening jacket, causing the mesh reinforcement to rupture, and the sample subsequently collapses. Samples that were strengthened on one, two and three sides (SI1-1, SI2-1 and SI3-1) behaved the same way as well as debonding between original block and strengthening layer occur in sample SI3-1. For samples with eccentric loading (Ca, Cb, SI3-1a and SI3-1b), cracking occurred on the displacement side more than on the other sides, which accelerated the collapse. It is clear that the strengthening jacket executed from three sides succeeded in reducing the splitting. In four specimens with grooves, the strengthening jacket was cut along its full height, followed by cracking of the original block. The grooves successfully prevented separation between the strengthening layer and the original concrete block.

Results of the bearing strength.

SHCC jacketing

Figure 7 shows ultimate bearing strength (fu) of two samples C and S. The fu was 40.6 MPa and 47.7 MPa in the samples C and S, respectively. When the control block (C) was strengthened with 25 mm-SHCC jacketing, the fu increased by 17.3% over that of sample C. Furthermore, during local compression, the restricted concrete block S showed many cracks with rather narrow crack widths. While plain concrete sample C had normal brittle failures, the reinforced sample S displayed typical plastic failures. By subjecting the core concrete to a triaxial compression state, the SHCC jacket produced a notable confinement effect on the concrete, as demonstrated by the test results and phenomena. This modified the concrete pedestal’s failure mode and increased the bearing strength (fu).

Effect of SHCC jacketing on ultimate bearing strength (fu) of the concrete supports.

Ties reinforcing

Figure 8 shows effect of SHCC jacketing reinforced with transverse ties on ultimate bearing strength (fu) of the concrete supports. Three samples N1, N2 and N3 were strengthened with 25 mm-SHCC jacketing but every one differed in internal reinforcement of SHCC. One, two and three ties made of 6 mm-diameter smoothed rod were used in samples N1, N2 and N3, respectively. The fu was 48.4, 65.6 and 71.9 MPa, respectively, in samples N1, N2 and N3 while fu of control sample C was 40.6 MPa. Compared to C, the fu of samples N1, N2 and N3 increased by 20%, 64% and 80%, respectively. It was seen that as the transverse ties increased, the fu improved more. The reason for this improvement is that the presence of reinforcement increases the jacketing’s strength. The greater the jacketing’s transverse strength, the greater the enclosure of the original block, which improves compressive strength and, consequently, the bearing strength in the local load area under bearing plate clearly improved. When ties reinforcement is present inside the SHCC layer, the transverse strain of original block and SHCC jacket is reduced, the concrete in the core is compressed and its behaviour improves. Also, the ties reinforcement bears part of the horizontal tension load in the SHCC layer, thus delaying the collapse of the SHCC layer and thus improving the bearing load of the original block.

Effect of SHCC jacketing reinforced with transverse ties on ultimate bearing strength (fu) of the concrete supports.

SM reinforcing

The impact of SHCC jacketing supplemented with various steel mesh (SM) layers on the concrete supports’ ultimate bearing strength (fu) is depicted in Fig. 9. Although 25 mm-SHCC jacketing was used to strengthen samples SI4-1, SII4-1, and SIII4-1, each sample’s SM number varied. For samples SI4-1, SII4-1, and SIII4, one, two, and three layers were utilized, respectively. The fu values of samples SI4-1, SII4-1, and SIII4 were 53.1, 56.3, and 60.2 MPa, respectively. The fu of samples SI4-1, SII4-1, and SIII4 improved by 32, 40 and 50%, respectively, compared sample C. It was observed that the fu improved more when the quantity of SMs grew. The strength of the jacketing is increased by the presence of SM reinforcement, which explains this improvement. The enclosure of the original block increases with the transverse strength of the jacketing, improving compressive strength and, as a result, the bearing strength in the local load region under the bearing plate. The transverse strain of the original block and SHCC jacket is decreased, the concrete in the core is compressed, and its behavior is improved when SM reinforcement is provided inside the SHCC layer. Additionally, SM reinforcement contributes to the horizontal tension stress in the SHCC layer, which delays the SHCC layer’s collapse and increases the original block’s bearing load.

Effect of SHCC jacketing reinforced with different layers of steel meshes on ultimate bearing strength (fu) of the concrete supports.

SM height

All samples (SI4-0.25, SI4-0.5, SI4-0.75 and SI4-1) were provided SHCC jacketing with different heights of SM within SHCC layer. The impact of SM height on the fu is depicted in Fig. 10. SM height-to-block height ratio was 0.25, 0.50, 0.75 and 1.0 in samples SI4-0.25, SI4-0.5, SI4-0.75 and SI4-1, respectively. The fu values of samples SI4-0.25, SI4-0.5, SI4-0.75 and SI4-1, respectively, were 49, 50, 52 and 53 MPa. The fu of samples SI4-0.25, SI4-0.5, SI4-0.75 and SI4-1, respectively, improved by 22, 25, 29 and 32.5%. It was observed that the fu slightly improved when the height of SM grew from 0.25% to 100% of the block height. When only a quarter-height SM was used in sample SI4-0.25, a 22% enhance ratio was achieved in the fu compared to control sample C. But with the increase in the height of the SM there was no noticeable increase in the fu of samples SI4-0.5, SI4-0.75 and SI4-1. Therefore, it is recommended to reinforce the SHCC layer that strengthened the original block with a steel mesh of incomplete height. SM reinforcement within SHCC layer is useful at the top of the block due to the fact that compressive stress is greatest at the top and diminishes as it descends.

Effect of SHCC jacketing reinforced with different heights of steel meshes on ultimate bearing strength (fu) of the concrete supports.

Confinement sides

The samples SI1-1, SI2-1, SI3-1 and SI4-1 were strengthened with SHCC jacketing having one, two, three and four sides around the concrete block as well as the control one sample C was without jacketing. Effect of number of SHCC jacketing on the fu of the tested blocks is shown in Fig. 11. The fu of samples SI1-1, SI2-1, SI3-1 and SI4-1, respectively, was 40.6, 50, 52.3 and 53.1 MPa while the control one C showed 40.6 MPa. It was found that the fu did not improve when the block sample SI1-1 was strengthened from one side. The reason for this is that the concrete will not be enclosed when the SHCC layer is poured from only one side. Or perhaps this strengthened side improves slightly, but weaknesses appear on the other sides (un-strengthened), causing rapid collapse, just like the reference sample C. But when the SHCC layer is poured from both sides (sample SI2-1), a noticeable improvement in bearing resistance (fu) begins to occur. The fu of sample SI2-1 was 23% higher than that of control C. When the SHCC layer is bonded on both sides, the improvement in enclosure begins to take effect due to the bearing of part of the horizontal forces, which causes an increase in resistance under local loading. But with the increase in the sides of the SHCC layer there was no noticeable increase in the fu of samples SI3-1 and SI4-1.

Effect of SHCC jacketing sides on ultimate bearing strength (fu) of the concrete supports.

SF volume

Figure 12 shows effect of SF content on the bearing capacity of the tested blocks. Although 25 mm-SHCC jacketing was used to strengthen samples S, SF0.5G0 and SF1G0, each sample’s SF rate varied. For samples S, SF0.5G0 and SF1G0, 0, 0.5, and 1% of steel fiber were performed inside SHCC to work as reinforcement, respectively. The fu values of samples S, SF0.5G0 and SF1G0 was 47.7, 58, and 61.3 MPa, respectively. It was observed that the fu improved more when the quantity of SF reinforcement inside SHCC layer grew. The fu of samples SF0.5G0 and SF1G0 was 21.5 and 28.5%, respectively, higher than that of sample S without SF. The strength of the jacketing is increased by the presence of SF reinforcement, which explains this improvement. This SF prevented cracks to pass across straight lines and force the cracks to go in winding paths. These randomly distributed filaments within the SHCC layer help create bridges in front of cracks, so the crack either causes the filament to break or wrap around it, lengthening the path. This delay collapse and improves the tensile strength of the SHCC layer, which in turn improves the compressive strength of the original block. This, in turn, improves local load resistance under the loading plate.

Effect of steel fiber content inside SHCC jacketing on ultimate bearing strength (fu) of the concrete supports.

Grooves number

Figure 13 explained influence of grooving at SHCC jacketing-old concrete interface on the fu of the concrete supports. Although 25 mm-SHCC jacketing was conducted to strengthen samples SF0.5G0, SF0.5G1 and SF0.5G2, each sample’s grooves number varied. For specimens SF0.5G0, SF0.5G1 and SF0.5G2, the number of grooves performed on one side was 0, 1 and 2. The fu of samples SF0.5G0, SF0.5G1 and SF0.5G2 was 58, 60, and 62.3 MPa, respectively. It was observed that the fu slightly improved when the number of grooves at contact interface between old concrete and SHCC layer increased. The fu of SF0.5G1 and SF0.5G2 was 3.4 and 6.8%, respectively, higher than that of sample SF0.5G0 without grooves. The effect was small because the primary function of the grooves was to increase the bond between the old and new concrete. However, the collapse in the SHCC layer was split, not separation at the contact surface. Therefore, implementing grooves was not useful in this case because the bond between SHCC and original block was sufficient. Also, implementing a SHCC shirt on all sides around the original block reduced the role of grooves because the SHCC layer surrounded the blocks well and bonded them together from all sides.

Effect of grooving at SHCC jacketing-old concrete interface on the fu of the concrete supports.

Eccentricity of loading

Unstrengthen samples

Figure 14 shows how loading eccentricity affected the fu of the control blocks without strengthening. The local compression pressure was applied at the center of sample C while this load was eccentric in two samples Ca and Cb. The eccentricity of the loading plate was 63 mm and 97 mm, respectively, in two samples Ca and Cb. In other words, eccentricity-to-plate width (e/B) ratio was 0.25 and 0.39, respectively, in two samples Ca and Cb. It was observed that as e/B increased, the fu clearly declined. The fu of samples C, Ca and Cb was 40.6, 31.3 and 25 MPa, respectively. The fu of eccentric samples Ca and Cb was, respectively, 23% and 38% less than that of concentric sample C. The reason for this is the change in the thickness of the concrete surrounding the loading plate. In the case of sample C, this thickness was greater than that of the eccentric samples Ca and Cb. Also, in sample C, this thickness was equal on all sides around the loading plate while it was unequal in other two samples. Every time the loading plate moves off-center, the concrete thickness decreases on one side of the concrete block, which reduces the concrete’s resistance to stress distribution on that side. Therefore, eccentricity creates a concentration of stresses on one side at the expense of the other, which accelerates collapse. The greater the displacement of the bearing plate, the less the thickness of the concrete, and the faster the collapse occurs.

Impact of loading eccentricity on the fu of the control blocks without strengthening.

Strengthen samples

Figure 15 shows impact of loading eccentricity on the fu of the strengthened blocks with three-edges SHCC jacketing. In two strengthened samples, SI3-1a and SI3-1b, the load was eccentric, but in sample SI3-1, the local compression pressure was applied at the center. Two samples, SI3-1a and SI3-1b, had loading plate eccentricities of 63 mm and 97 mm, respectively. In other words, the two samples SI3-1a and SI3-1b had eccentricity-to-plate width (e/B) ratios of 0.25 and 0.39, respectively. Three sides of the block were strengthened using the SHCC layer. Three sides were chosen according to the eccentricity’s orientation. The side farthest from the offset is the only one that rides without strengthening. It was found that the fu obviously decreased when e/B rose. Samples SI3-1, SI3-1a, and SI3-1b have respective fu values of 52, 44.5, and 28 MPa. Compared to the concentric sample SI3-1, the eccentric samples SI3-1a and SI3-1b had fu that was 15% and 46% lower, respectively. This is because the concrete around the loading plate has changed in thickness. While this thickness was uneven in the other two samples, it was uniform on all sides around the loading plate in sample SI3-1. The thickness of the concrete on one side of the block decreases each time the loading plate shifts off-center, lowering that side’s resistance to stress distribution.

Impact of loading eccentricity on the fu of the strengthened blocks with three-edges SHCC jacketing.

Three-edges jacketing

Figure 16 shows impact of three-edge SHCC jacketing on the fu of the strengthened blocks at different eccentricities of the loading. Three degrees of loading eccentricities (e/B) were examined; 0.0 in samples C and SI3-1, 0.25 in samples Ca and SI3-1a and 0.39 in samples Cb and SI3-1b. Three samples C, Ca and Cb were without strengthening to work as controls. On the other hand, three samples SI3-1, SI3-1a and SI3-1b, were provided with three-edge SHCC jacketing. The findings showed that the fu of strengthened blocks clearly improved compared to control samples at degrees of loading eccentricities 0, 0.25 and 0.39. At e/B equal 0, the fu of sample SI3-1 was 29% higher than her counterpart C without strengthening. At e/B equal 0.25, the fu of sample SI3-1a was 42% higher than her counterpart Ca without strengthening. At e/B equal 0.39, the fu of sample SI3-1b was 12.5% higher than her counterpart Cb without strengthening. The results showed that the strengthening shirt improved the bearing resistance of the concrete in the region where the bearing stresses are concentrated towards the loading eccentricities.

Comparison with previous work

The current improvements in overall behavior of concrete block due to SHCC jacketing reinforced with steel meshes were close to those of concrete columns reported by Emara et al.41. In order to assess the behavior of circular RC columns that were restricted either internally or externally using steel mesh, Emara et al.41 conducted an experimental test. The remaining short RC circular column specimens were restricted with steel mesh, while six were tested under compression. Numerous factors were examined, such as the presence of steel stirrups, the quantity of steel mesh wraps, the position of the steel mesh (internally or externally), and the steel mesh patterns. The findings demonstrated that use SM in conjunction with stirrups improves confinement compared to utilizing stirrups alone. The zones of concrete cover spalling were reduced when Expanded Metal Mesh (EMM) was used in either a single wrap or double wraps for partial interior confinement. A single wrap of EMM applied externally demonstrated significant confinement, in which the internal core remained intact but the cement mortar layer around the steel mesh failed. Double wraps of SM over the stirrups produced the highest capacity, with the maximum load exceeding the control specimen by around 16%. Nonetheless, the entire internal confinement example showed the highest ductility (55% increase over the control specimen). The RC circular column was confined with remarkable efficiency using SM as full internal confinement instead of the traditional stirrups. The column was able to regain 99.13% of its initial control column capacity without seeing a decrease in its initial ductility value. Generally speaking, improving the volumetric ratio of mesh reinforcement at the same confinement location boosts the reinforcement’s efficiency, which raises the column capacity and improves ductility. Particularly when employing two wraps of SM as partial internal confinement, where the energy absorption rose by 163% because of the small degradation in this arrangement, all confined column specimens absorbed more energy than the control sample. Additionally, the current improvements in overall behavior of concrete block due to SHCC jacketing reinforced with steel meshes were close to those of concrete structures reported in many previous studies42–54.

Conclusion

In this study, upgrading bearing strength of the concrete pedestals subjected to concentric and eccentric local compressive pressure using strain hardening cementitious composites (SHCC) jacketing around the concrete block was investigated. The concrete blocks were cast. A steel bearing plate with area of 80 × 80 mm was used to apply bearing stress on the concrete block surface. The main variable was using steel meshes (SMs) for reinforcing SHCC jacketing. These SMs having different layer number (1, 2 and 3) and different height-to-block height ratios (0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1). Also, conventional stirrups inside SHCC jacketing was used. Moreover, confinement sides of the block (one, two, three and four sides), impact of steel fiber content (0%, 0.5% and 1%), conducting grooves (one and two groove) and load eccentricity level (0%, 0.25% and 0.39%) were studied. Effects of all studied variables on the failure and ultimate bearing strength (fu) of the concrete blocks were discussed. The findings showed the following observation:

The un-strengthened concrete block directly and suddenly broke into pieces due to local compression load while concrete blocks strengthened with SHCC jacketing showed splitting failure in the original block and the SHCC layer.
