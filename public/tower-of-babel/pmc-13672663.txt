Creating Awareness of preScribing Cascades and Managing Adverse Drug Effects (CASC-ADE) Tool: Appraisal by Future End Users
Authors: Kjell van der Walle, Petra Denig, Toone Boerman, Fabiënne J H Magdelijns, Johannes T H Nielen, Fatma Karapinar-Çarkit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite increasing attention for prescribing cascades, healthcare providers (HCP) need tools for taking action. We developed a tool, comprising fact sheets that summarise background information and provide guidance for recognising, managing, and preventing specific prescribing cascades. The aim of this study was to explore end users’ appraisal of the initial version of this ‘Creating Awareness of prescribing Cascades and managing Adverse Drug Effects’ (CASC-ADE) tool.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using an online questionnaire including 15 items (13 scored on 7-point Likert scales, 2 on a 7-point bipolar scale, with a score of 4 being adequate), covering four domains: purpose, rigour, usability, and usefulness. The questionnaire, alongside at least one fact sheet, was sent to a convenience sample of HCPs, medical and pharmacy students. Suggestions for improvement could be made by respondents. Descriptive statistics were evaluated separately for HCPs and students.ResultsThe study included 17 HCPs (9 pharmacists, 8 clinicians) and 12 students (6 pharmacy, 6 medical). All items received positive median scores: Likert scale items between 5 and 7 for HCPs and 5 and 6 for students, and bipolar scale items 4 for HCPs and 4–4.5 for students. Highest scores were seen for purpose, usability, and usefulness for recognising prescribing cascades (median 6–7). Usefulness for managing and preventing prescribing cascades received slightly lower scores (median 5–6). Fifteen HCPs and nine students were positive about using the tool in practice but some mentioned this would depend on its practical implementation. A few respondents suggested including more specific guidance for taking action.ConclusionsThe initial CASC-ADE tool was positively appraised across the assessed domains by the HCPs and students. Further research is needed to assess whether its use will improve recognition, management and prevention of prescribing cascades.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40266-026-01334-5.

Key Points

The CASC-ADE tool translates evidence related to adverse drug reactions into fact sheets with advice for (future) healthcare providers regarding the management of prescribing cascades in clinical practice.Potential end users, including clinicians, pharmacists as well as medical and pharmacy students, evaluated the initial version of the CASC-ADE tool positively across four assessed domains.Suggestions for improvements were made that are useful for the further development and implementation of the CASC-ADE tool.

Introduction

When an adverse drug reaction (ADR) to a medication triggers the prescription of additional medication, this can be seen as a prescribing cascade [1]. Although there is no universal definition for this phenomenon, most often prescribing cascades arise from a misinterpretation of an ADR as an individual medical condition leading to the prescription of additional medication [2]. Prescribing cascades represent a multifactorial and heterogeneous problem. Depending on the clinical context, they can be classified as intentional or unintentional and as appropriate or inappropriate [2, 3]. Prescribing cascades can have far-reaching consequences for both patients and healthcare systems, highlighting the need for interventions that improve their detection, management and prevention [4–6]. They have been associated with a significantly increased risk of serious adverse events, including emergency department visits and hospital admissions, as well as reduced quality of life [4, 5]. Recent reviews and observational studies illustrate that there are many different prescribing cascades, related to a wide range of medications, that occur in clinical practice [7–11]. Studies investigating specific prescribing cascades have reported estimated incidences ranging between 1 and 10% of new medication users, indicating that prescribing cascades may represent a preventable form of potentially inappropriate prescribing [12–16]. Health care providers (HCP) and patients face challenges in the detection, management and prevention of prescribing cascades [17, 18]. Both HCPs and patients have mentioned gaps in knowledge regarding the purpose, chronology and effects of medications making it difficult to recognise ADRs, while HCPs also describe insufficient knowledge and skills to manage ADRs effectively [17–21]. These knowledge gaps are compounded by deficiencies in undergraduate and postgraduate education, as well as by inadequate procedures or tools to monitor and interpret medication-related symptoms in clinical practice [17–21]. Some view prescribing cascades as unavoidable or too challenging to address in case of polypharmacy [22].

Several solutions have been proposed to support HCPs in the recognition, management and prevention of prescribing cascades [6, 20, 23–25]. These usually include general recommendations or approaches to review medication and deprescribe inappropriate medication, and to start medication at low doses while monitoring ADRs [6, 23, 25–27]. Existing tools to reduce inappropriate prescribing, such as the STOPP criteria, include only very few prescribing cascades to be avoided [28]. Prescribing cascade awareness and management require novel, explicit tools that can be applied in routine practice [21, 29–31]. ThinkCascades is such a tool, listing 9 clinically important prescribing cascades that HCPs should consider when prescribing and reviewing medications [32]. More recently, Rochon et al. published a list with 65 potentially inappropriate prescribing cascades (PIPC) based on input from an international expert panel [33]. However, the STOPP criteria, ThinkCascades tool and PIPC list do not provide guidance on recommended actions. Moreover, these tools generally lack detailed ADR information that might support their identification, such as symptomatology (e.g. time to onset and associated symptoms) [6, 18]. Therefore, a tool encompassing these items is needed to aid current and future HCPs in reducing prescribing cascades [6, 20, 34]. The ‘Creating Awareness of preScribing Cascades & managing Adverse Drug Effects’ (CASC-ADE) project aims to develop a tool comprising evidence-based fact sheets providing information and guidance to HCPs relevant for recognising, managing and preventing prescribing cascades. The uptake of a new tool to guide practice depends on how the content is created (i.e. evidence synthesis) and communicated to end users (i.e. formulation of messages and format of the document) [35]. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to explore future end users’ appraisal of an initial version of the CASC-ADE tool. A secondary objective was to explore options for improvement of the fact sheets.

Methods

Study Design and Study Population

A cross-sectional survey study was conducted to assess end users’ appraisal of the CASC-ADE tool. Given the early-stage, exploratory nature of this evaluation, a convenience sample of future end users was recruited across different regions in the Netherlands through the authors’ network. Given the current practice regarding medication optimisation in the Netherlands, we aimed to include clinicians and pharmacists from both primary and secondary care, as well as medical and pharmacy students as future end users. To capture input from different expertise, we aimed for including at least three hospital pharmacists (involving at least one clinical pharmacologist), three community pharmacists, three general practitioners, three medical specialists often dealing with polypharmacy (e.g. cardiologist, internal medicine physician and geriatrician), six medical students and six pharmacy students. Students in the final 2 years of their Master’s program, as well as those who had graduated within the past 6 months, were eligible to participate. In the Netherlands, this implies that these students have clinical experience from clinical rotations and/or internships. Participants were invited by e-mail, with reminders sent to non-responders and to those who agreed to participate but did not complete the survey.

The study was conducted in concordance with the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guidelines [36].

This study is part of a broader CASC-ADE project. Ethical approval for the CASC-ADE project was granted by the Medical Ethics Review Committee of Maastricht University Medical Center (2024-0442).

CASC-ADE Tool

The CASC-ADE tool consists of a preamble and a series of fact sheets, each summarising evidence on a specific prescribing cascade, including a background document that scientifically substantiates the summary, and a supplementary table synthesizing the evidence from studies on the approaches for managing the ADR. The preamble outlines the purpose, relevance and development process of the tool. The fact sheets aim to synthesize the available evidence from literature on specific prescribing cascades into a concise, structured and practice-oriented information resource. They provide an overview of information relevant for the recognition and management of prescribing cascades that is often fragmented and difficult to locate. The CASC-ADE tool will include a growing number of fact sheets covering prescribing cascades that have been classified as potentially inappropriate and occurring in clinical practice [8, 10, 33]. For the purpose of this study, we selected two of the first developed fact sheets covering the prescribing cascades of cough suppressants for ACE inhibitor-induced cough and of diuretics for calcium channel blocker-induced peripheral oedema. The fact sheets include topics such as epidemiology and symptomatology of the ADR, and evidence-based advice for the management of the prescribing cascade (i.e. dose reduction, medication switch within and outside the pharmacological class and non-pharmacological approaches). As no standardized or evidence-based guidance exists regarding the format and content of fact sheets for the identification and management of prescribing cascades, key topics and structural components were identified from available literature on prescribing cascades [7, 8, 17, 18, 23, 26, 32, 37]. These informed the development of a preliminary format for the fact sheets, which was subsequently refined through iterative discussion within the research team until consensus was reached. The preamble and format of the fact sheets evaluated in this study are provided in Supplementary File A.

A stepwise, structured procedure including extensive literature review was followed to develop the fact sheets. The sources used to search for evidence included, handbooks, drug and clinical reference tools and bibliographic databases (e.g. Medline OvidSP and Embase OVID). Study screening and data extraction were performed independently by two researchers, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus between both researchers. The fact sheets were written by a hospital pharmacy resident using the predefined format (Supplementary File A) and subsequently reviewed by a team of four experts in medication use: a geriatrician, a hospital pharmacist-epidemiologist and board-certified geriatric pharmacist, a hospital pharmacist–epidemiologist and clinical pharmacologist and a pharmacist–researcher specialized in drug utilisation quality. Feedback was implemented and the fact sheets were adjusted until satisfactory according to the team.

Assessment of End Users’ Appraisal

To assess the CASC-ADE tool, separate questionnaires were developed for HCPs and students. Both included 15 similar statement-based items to cover four domains considered relevant for end user appraisal: clarity of its purpose, rigour of its development, its usability and its usefulness. The questionnaire was constructed using Qualtrics XM® (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA). The questionnaire was distributed alongside one fact sheet for students (diuretics for calcium channel blocker-induced peripheral oedema) and two fact sheets for HCPs, and a preamble explaining the concept of prescribing cascades and the CASC-ADE tool.

The questionnaire for HCPs started with two demographic questions about profession and working experience (Supplementary File B). The 15 items included for the primary appraisal by HCPs and students were based on the AGREE II (Appraisal of Guidelines, Research and Evaluation) instrument and the User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ) [38, 39]. The AGREE II has been developed to assess the process and reporting of guideline development, whereas the UEQ was developed as an end user questionnaire to assess user experiences with products. Items were selected to cover domains of these instruments that require end user appraisal. AGREE II domains on stakeholder involvement and editorial independence were omitted, as these aspects can be assessed objectively. The domain ‘purpose’ included one item based on AGREE II (Scope and purpose), ‘rigour’ included four items based on AGREE II (Rigour of development), ‘usability’ included one item based on AGREE II (Clarity of presentation) and five items based on UEQ (Efficiency, and Perspicuity). Finally, the domain ‘usefulness’ included four items based on UEQ (Dependability). Some items were modified to specify they were referring to the fact sheets, to particular parts included in the fact sheets or regarding its format but the items were not referring to one specific prescribing cascade. Participants were thus asked to evaluate an initial version of the CASC-ADE tool including the preamble with one or two fact sheets. Most items were scored on 7-point Likert Scales, consistent with those used in the AGREE II and UEQ. Scores ranging from 5 to 7 were categorised as positive, scores from 1 to 3 as negative, and a score of 4 was categorised as neutral. For two statements on amount of information, a bipolar scale was used ranging from 1 (too little) to 7 (too much), with a score of 4 defined as adequate. Additional items were included in the HCP questionnaire (three items) and student questionnaire (six items), respectively, which were not comparable and therefore not included in the main analyses but only presented in Supplementary Files B, C and D.

To gain more in-depth insight on the end user’s experiences and explore options for improving the fact sheets, the HCPs could provide explanations for their answers to the items in free text, and two open-ended questions were included. The open-ended questions asked for the HCPs’ general impression and suggestions for improvement of the fact sheets (Supplementary File B). To collect suggestions for improvement from the students, four open-ended questions were included asking for their thoughts about the usefulness of the fact sheet, information that was missing, lay-out and general impression of the fact sheet (Supplementary File C). This difference in approach regarding the collection of in-depth information was chosen based on input from a pharmacy student (T.B.) involved in the development of the student questionnaire. The HCP questionnaire additionally included an assessment of which prescribing cascades respondents gave the highest priority for the development of future fact sheets, the results of which are not presented given the purpose of the present study.

Data Analyses

A mixed-methods analysis, including quantitative and qualitative analyses, was performed on the survey results. For the primary objective, median scores and response distributions (positive, neutral and negative) were reported separately for HCPs and students. For the secondary objective, content analysis was used for the responses to open-ended questions focusing on options for potential improvement in relation to the domains of interest [40]. These domains aligned with the four domains used for developing the questionnaire. Regarding usability, the subdomains ‘format of the document’ and ‘formulation of messages and content’ were prespecified as potentially relevant [35]. The students’ answers to the open-ended questions were first analysed by two study team members (P.D. and T.B.) to develop a coding scheme guided by the prespecified domains but allowing to adapt or add subdomains. Next, this coding scheme was refined by applying it to HCPs’ answers to the open-ended questions. The answers to open-ended questions were scored by two team members individually (P.D. and K.v.d.W. for the HCPs, and P.D. and T.B. for the students), identifying and categorising sentences or parts of sentences within (sub)domains. The final coding scheme included the following domains: (1) purpose of the document, (2) rigour of the evidence synthesis, (3) format of the document, (4) content of the document, (5) usefulness of the document for practice and (6) requirements for implementation. Separate consensus meetings were held to discuss and resolve differences in coding for HCP and student data, respectively. In the next step, comments and suggestions within the domains were clustered per topic by a senior researcher (PD), who was not involved in the initial content development of the fact sheets. The results were critically evaluated by another team member involved in coding of the open-ended questions (K.v.d.W. for HCPs and T.B. for students) to diminish personal bias or error in interpreting the data. Finally, these findings were summarised and tabulated with excerpts and codes to identify the discipline of HCPs (e.g. community pharmacist, cardiologist and general practitioner) and students (i.e. medical and pharmacy students) who made the comment or suggestion. Microsoft Excel 2016® (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) was used for both the quantitative and qualitative analysis.

Results

Of the 26 HCPs invited, 23 consented to participate, of whom 17 ultimately completed the online questionnaire (65%), including 6 hospital pharmacists, 3 community pharmacists, 2 general practitioners, 3 cardiologists, 2 internal medicine physicians, and 1 psychiatrist. The majority of HCPs (11/17) had 10 or more years of working experience, while four had 5–10 years, and two had 1–5 years of experience.

Of the 15 medical students and 12 pharmacy students invited, 6 medical (40%) and 6 pharmacy (50%) students completed the online questionnaire. All respondents provided answers to the open-ended questions, and completion of the questionnaire took at least 10 min for the HCPs and 17 min for the students. The option to explain their statement-based item scores was used by a limited number of HCPs, i.e. 2–6 for most items, except the item ‘I would use these fact sheets in practice’ for which ten HCPs provided more in-depth information.

Quantitative Results

Figure 1 shows the proportions of negative, neutral, and positive scores alongside the corresponding median scores (see Supplementary File D for medians and numbers per item as well as the scores for the additional items that were only answered by HCPs or by students). The median scores were positive for all items, ranging between 5 and 7 for HCPs and 5 and 6 for students on the 7-point Likert scales, and 4 for HCPs and 4–4.5 for students on the 7-point bipolar scale (Fig. 1, Supplementary File D). There were no negative responses regarding the purpose of the tool. For three HCPs, it was not clear for whom the documents were intended (additional question, Supplementary File D). There were no negative responses for most items in the domains rigour and usability. However, three HCPs (18%) expressed that the advantages and disadvantages of the advice were not well described. Students more often gave neutral scores for items in the domain Rigour. In the domain usability, some negative scores were given to the amount of information, with three HCPs (18%) and four students (33%) judging the amount as too much (score 6 or 7) for the summary, and two HCPs (12%) and four students (33%) as too much for the background information. Regarding the domain usefulness, 15 HCPs (88%) and nine students (75%) expressed that they would use the document in (future) practice, whereas there was 1 HCP (6%) who did not expect to do so. All respondents expressed that the document clarified or would help them to recognise the specific prescribing cascade, and 15 HCPs (88%) and all students expressed that the document would also support them to manage the prescribing cascade. Five HCPs (29%) and four students (33%) responded neutral or negative regarding the usefulness of the documents to prevent the specific prescribing cascade.

Qualitative Results

Multiple topics were identified per domain, most of which included comments or suggestions made by HCPs as well as students (Supplementary File E). Most topics covered positive and negative comments or suggestions for improvement. In general, respondents described the documents as useful and of good quality, particularly with regard to the evidence synthesis. Usefulness by HCPs was particularly seen for creating more awareness, for education of physicians, pharmacists and pharmacy staff, and for taking action. The one-page summary was mentioned as being particularly helpful for practice. One HCP mentioned that the documents may not be useful to prevent prescribing cascades, since they focus on what to do when a prescribing cascade has already occurred (Supplementary File E).

Suggestions made for optimising the fact sheets are summarised per topic in Table 1. Several HCPs and students suggested starting each document specifying for whom and why it is relevant. It was mentioned that more information could be provided on the date and search for evidence, and on grading of the evidence (Table 1). Suggestions for improvement of the format were made mostly by pharmacists (eight out of nine pharmacists regarding this domain) and pharmacy students (five out of six students regarding this domain). For instance, several recommended starting the summary page with a schematic description of the prescribing cascade and advice on its management, followed by more explanation (Table 1). Furthermore, students suggested including more figures, schemes, headers and a table of content in the document. With regard to the content and formulation of the messages, two HCPs and four students mentioned that they would like to have a prioritisation of the advice or a step-by-step plan (Table 1). Three HCPs and four students wanted more details on how to make dose adjustments, discontinue medication or which switches can be made. Three HCPs mentioned that additional advice on how to conduct deprescribing or prevent prescribing cascades might be useful. No further suggestions were made to improve the usefulness of the tool but two HCPs suggested involving a professional organisation to approve and implement the tool (Table 1). Other suggestions were made to stimulate or support the use of the tool by linking the fact sheets to triggers or clinical rules in clinical decision support systems or to other information sources (Table 1).

Discussion

This is a first study evaluating a newly developed tool that summarises evidence-based information intended to support (future) HCPs in recognising, managing and preventing prescribing cascades. The CASC-ADE tool was generally perceived positively by its future end users, although some mixed opinions were observed regarding the amount of information presented. Most respondents indicated that they would use the tool in practice. In particular, respondents perceived its value for supporting the recognition and management of prescribing cascades, and to a lesser extent for supporting prevention. Furthermore, several suggestions for improvement were provided by the respondents, especially regarding more explicit guidance for taking action and implementation of the tool in practice.

Although no negative median scores were observed for any statement, there were four items with negative scores from a few respondents, mainly in the domains rigour and usability. Suggestions made to address these issues referred to layout and adding more clarification on the purpose and creation of the fact sheets. Lower scores may partially reflect different needs or expectations. Students were more critical regarding rigour, expecting more recent or high-quality publications. However, evidence related to ADRs is often limited by a high risk of bias and heterogeneous findings, and recent high-quality studies may be scarce [41]. Previous research has reported that HCPs with prior knowledge and experience with prescribing cascades may already have a higher ability to detect prescribing cascades [18]. Students, in contrast, typically have limited experience with prescribing cascades [42]. Consequently, they may prefer more explicit, evidence-based guidance on appropriate actions, which aligns with several students reporting limited familiarity with the concept and a need for clearer recommendations in the CASC-ADE tool. Given the exploratory nature of this study, it is too early to draw conclusions regarding the need for developing different versions of the CASC-ADE tool.

Although several respondents indicated that the fact sheets contained too much information, almost all expressed that relevant information was understandable and easy to find. Overall, the usability of the CASC-ADE tool was judged positively. Notably, the first page summary was particularly valued for its practical usability. These findings align with previous research indicating that tools and strategies aimed at addressing prescribing cascades should be both efficient and effective for clinical use [18, 37, 43]. This is especially important given that time constraints in healthcare complicate the identification and management of prescribing cascades [18, 20, 21]. Therefore, HCPs may not always have sufficient time or capacity to search for the information needed to take appropriate action and may therefore benefit from a tool that efficiently provides the relevant information.

Usefulness of the CACS-ADE tool was particularly seen by the respondents for recognizing and managing prescribing cascades, and to a lesser extent for preventing prescribing cascades. This appears to be related to the way in which they foresee implementation of the tool in clinical practice related to accessibility of the fact sheets. Respondents—particularly HCPs—emphasised that a combined approach integrating the CASC-ADE tool with (electronic) tools for detection of prescribing cascades, such as clinical decision support systems, would enhance its utility in clinical practice. These views are consistent with previous research highlighting the need for a multifaceted approach to optimally manage prescribing cascades [6, 17, 20]. In such a way, the fact sheets may support actions to be taken once a prescribing cascade has occurred. Additionally, within the conduct of clinical medication reviews, the tool may support HCPs to detect and manage potential prescribing cascades. It may be more difficult to see how the tool will prevent the problem that ADRs are mistakenly seen and treated as a new symptom. Nevertheless, a prescribing cascade can be prevented in case an ADR reported by a patient during a consult is recognised by the HCP and the CASC-ADE tool is subsequently used to explore alternatives (e.g. dose reduction or medication switch), rather than treating the ADR using additional medication.

Additionally, the use of the CASC-ADE tool may result in a learning effect, where increased knowledge of ADR identification and management improves detection and handling of ADRs, thereby preventing future prescribing cascades. This is particularly relevant, as previous research has highlighted insufficient knowledge of ADRs as a key contributing factor to prescribing cascades [18, 21]. Therefore, educational interventions have been identified as key strategies to address prescribing cascades [6, 17, 20]. This role is supported by the present study’s findings, as respondents reported additional usefulness of the CASC-ADE tool for raising awareness and for educational purposes. Education on prescribing cascades should, however, extend beyond HCPs. Patients may also benefit from education on the identification and reporting of ADRs [6, 17]. Accordingly, involving patients in the development of the CASC-ADE tool or creating a patient-focused version warrants consideration, as suggested by several respondents.

Respondents provided suggestions to improve the CASC-ADE tool. Several HCPs and particularly students expressed a desire for more explicit guidance, such as prioritising actions, providing a step-by-step-plan or offering detailed instructions on dose adjustments or medication switches. However, advice was deliberately kept non-prescriptive as prescribing cascades are multifactorial and the chosen actions should be patient dependent [6, 17]. The occurrence of prescribing cascades in clinical practice is often characterised by complexity and ambiguity, making it difficult to recommend the most appropriate solution, also because evidence on the most appropriate action often is absent in literature [6, 17, 18, 37]. Moreover, the appropriateness (i.e. whether the advantages of the two drugs combined outweigh the disadvantages) depends on the individual patient context [3, 17]. The advice provided in the CASC-ADE tool, on the other hand, were appreciated for being specific and unambiguous. In a study evaluating letters on prescribing cascades send to HCPs, Bloomstone et al. noted mixed views about including general or specific recommendations in these letters [37]. Some HCPs appreciated recommendations that allowed clinical discretion, whereas others suggested that more specific recommendations (e.g. dose reduction or identifying alternative medications) could improve their ability to manage prescribing cascades [37]. Their ultimate strategy was to include more specific recommendations, which was rated as informative and actionable by most HCPs in their follow-up evaluation [37]. This approach was already incorporated into the CASC-ADE tool, which provides evidence-based advice for taking action while preserving flexibility for patient-specific decision-making.

Several strategies have been proposed to optimise the recognition, management, and prevention of prescribing cascades [6, 20, 23–25]. Nevertheless, practical tools to support these processes remain limited. Existing tools, such as the ThinkCascades tool and PIPC-list, provide lists of clinically relevant and potentially inappropriate prescribing cascades that may facilitate their identification during medication reviews [32, 33]. However, these tools primarily focus on the combination of the offending drug and a second drug prescribed to treat a potential ADR and provide limited information on clinical presentation of the ADR or its management. Consequently, prescribing cascades identified when using these tools may already have resulted in patient exposure to avoidable adverse outcomes. The CASC-ADE tool provides detailed information on the associated ADRs, including symptomatology and risk factors, which may support HCPs in recognising and assessing suspected ADRs in clinical practice. Furthermore, the CASC-ADE tool offers evidence-based management recommendations that may help HCPs consider appropriate actions before initiating a second medication, thereby potentially supporting the prevention of prescribing cascades. Overall, the CASC-ADE tool may complement existing resources by extending their focus beyond the identification of prescribing cascades to support their recognition, management and prevention in both clinical practice and educational settings.

Strengths and Limitations

Combining quantitative and qualitative results, this exploratory study provides important insights from (future) end users on their evaluation of an initial version of the CASC-ADE tool. A range of potential end users was included, with a high response rate among HCPs and an intermediate response rate among students. All open-ended questions were coded by two people and meetings were held to reach consensus.

This study has some limitations, particularly regarding its generalisability and comprehensiveness of the appraisal. The sample size was rather small, and convenience sampling was used including respondents who were based in the Netherlands. Only clinicians, pharmacists and medical and pharmacy students were included, whereas optimal management of prescribing cascades involves other stakeholders, such as nurses, caregivers and patients. Perspectives on the management of prescribing cascades may differ across countries and healthcare systems. Nevertheless, the study sample was heterogeneous with respect to region, professional background and years of experience. Recruitment was conducted through the study team’s network, which can have the risk of respondents being more informed about prescribing cascades. Given that several respondents reported limited or no prior familiarity with the concept of prescribing cascades, this may not represent a major limitation. There is the risk of giving socially desirable answers but none of the respondents were connected to the authors in a dependent position. The students were recruited by the student in the research team (T.B.) and not connected to team members in terms of courses or grading. For the qualitative analysis, there was no formal decision regarding data saturation. Suggestions for improvement are not expected to cover the views of all potential end users but considered relevant as input for further evaluation and implementation of the tool. All researchers were related to or part of the team that developed the first version of the CASC-ADE tool. This can result in bias towards focusing on positive comments in the qualitative analysis. Given the objective to retrieve comments and suggestions for improvement, however, we particularly searched for ‘negative’ comments and constructive feedback indicating limitations, suggestions for modification, and other aspects of the CASC-ADE tool that respondents found unclear or difficult to apply. Finally, the use of a non-validated questionnaire with 15 statement-based items may have limited the depth of retrieving participants’ insights. This was partly mitigated by the inclusion of open-ended questions and opportunities to provide additional comments to the statements. Nevertheless, other study methods, such as interviews or focus groups, allow for probing of responses and retrieval of topics not covered in the questionnaire.

Implications for Future Research and Practice

Given the positive results, the concept of the CASC-ADE tool can be considered as potentially valuable for managing prescribing cascades, as perceived by clinicians, pharmacists, medical and pharmacy students. Suggestions made by respondents can be used to make adaptations to the layout and content of the fact sheets. Importantly, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting participants’ evaluations based on theoretical engagement with the CASC-ADE tool, rather than using it in actual practice. Further research is needed to evaluate users’ real-world experiences with the tool and to determine whether the perceived value transforms into measurable impact in practice. The present study forms an early-stage exploration within a larger project aimed at developing and evaluating the effectiveness of the CASC-ADE tool to support the identification, management and prevention of prescribing cascades. If found to be effective, subsequent steps for successful implementation in clinical practice should be taken. This includes evaluating whether different versions of the tool are warranted to meet the varying needs identified by respondents in this study, such as a more practice-oriented version for routine clinical use, a more detailed version for educational purposes or adapted versions for other end users. Additionally, professional organisations should be involved to support endorsement of the fact sheets and ensuring continuous grading and updating of evidence. In addition, strategies to facilitate easy and timely access to the fact sheets should be explored, as suggested by respondents in this study. Moreover, patient involvement in the development of patient-specific materials should be explored.

Finally, the findings of this study suggest that the educational potential of the CASC-ADE tool warrants further investigation, particularly given evidence that improved knowledge of ADRs is associated with enhanced detection of ADRs and that HCPs are open to further training and information to both identify and manage prescribing cascades [20, 44].

Conclusions

The initial version of the CASC-ADE tool was positively appraised by clinicians, pharmacists as well as medical and pharmacy students. It was perceived as potentially useful for the identification and management of prescribing cascades in clinical practice, and to a lesser extent for their prevention, with additional perceived educational value. Further steps can be made to improve the format of the fact sheets, evaluate the effectiveness of the tool in various real-world settings and explore options or best strategies to implement the tool in clinical practice.
