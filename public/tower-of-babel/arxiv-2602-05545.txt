Probing Anharmonic and Heterogeneous Carrier Dynamics Across Sublattice Melting in a Minimal Model Superionic Conductor
Sucharita Niyogi, Takenobu Nakamura, Genki Kobayashi, Yasunobu Ando, Takeshi Kawasaki

Abstract
Despite decades of research, the microscopic origin of sublattice melting and fast ion transport in superionic conductors remains elusive. Here, we introduce a chemically neutral minimal binary model consisting of a rigid host lattice stabilized by short-range steric repulsion and a soft carrier sublattice interacting via long-range Wigner-type forces. This contrast naturally produces distinct melting temperatures and an intermediate sublattice-melting phase in which carriers become fluidlike while the host remains crystalline. Molecular-dynamics simulations identify three dynamical regimes—crystalline, sublattice-melt, and fully molten—marked by sharp changes in diffusivity, structural correlations, and dynamic heterogeneity. Near sublattice melting, carrier motion is strongly anharmonic and spatially heterogeneous, beyond mean-field hopping descriptions. By tuning the density, we demonstrate that sublattice melting can be continuously controlled, establishing a direct link between lattice softness, anharmonicity, and collective ion transport. This work provides a unified microscopic foundation for designing mechanically robust, high-performance superionic conductors operable near ambient conditions. Keywords: Solid-state batteries, carrier transportation, sublattice melting, dynamic heterogeneity, anharmonicity.



I
Introduction

The rapid advancement of energy storage technologies has placed rechargeable batteries at the center of modern society, powering applications from portable electronics to electric vehicles and large-scale renewable energy integration. At the core of all such battery technologies is ionic transport: ionic conductors, whether liquid or solid, are essential for efficient charge transport and directly determine battery performance, longevity, and safety. While liquid electrolytes in conventional Li-ion batteries provide high ionic mobility and performance

Some recent analyses of layered and tunnel-type superionic conductors have shown that carrier transport is strongly influenced by many-body interactions among mobile ions, reshaping the diffusion landscape beyond simple single-particle hopping; here, Raman measurements and MD simulations further show that short-range cation-cation repulsion modifies activation barriers, underscoring the collective nature of fast-ion motion

Despite these advances, the microscopic origin of such selective disorder remains poorly understood, as most existing descriptions rely on mean-field or defect-based frameworks that neglect correlated particle motion and its feedback on the lattice vibrational landscape.

Selective sublattice disorder reshapes the vibrational potential energy landscape, introducing

High-pressure analyses

Despite these advances, most theoretical models neglect correlated dynamics within the mobile sublattice, leaving the interdependence of these effects largely unexplored.

Together, these studies suggest that sublattice melting and anharmonicity are not independent phenomena but mutually reinforcing aspects of the same underlying physics, in which selective ionic disorder feeds back into lattice softening, and vice versa. In particular, recent work on Li

This coexistence of mobile and immobile regions is a well-known hallmark of glass-forming systems and other dynamically heterogeneous materials.
More broadly, dynamic heterogeneity is a central organizing principle in the physics of glass-forming systems, where it has been extensively studied as the microscopic origin of transport anomalies, violation of the Stokes-Einstein relation, and cooperative motion

In this light, dynamic heterogeneity offers a natural conceptual bridge between sublattice melting and anharmonicity in superionic conductors: spatial variations in local anharmonicity amplify mobility contrasts, giving rise to cooperative, string-like dynamics beyond mean-field descriptions, as observed in glass-forming liquids where enhanced vibrational fluctuations precede collective string-like rearrangements

To elucidate this connection, we employ a minimal and tractable model to systematically probe how temperature and interactions control sublattice melting and collective carrier motion. This approach allows us to identify the conditions under which liquid-like regions emerge within an otherwise rigid lattice, quantify correlated ion dynamics beyond mean-field descriptions, and explore the
possibility of achieving superionic conduction near room temperature

II
Results

Our analysis focuses on three key mechanisms—selective sublattice melting, structural heterogeneity, and anharmonicity—to elucidate ion-transport dynamics in two-dimensional systems. In two dimensions, the true long-range translational order is prohibited at finite temperature by the Hohenberg-Mermin-Wagner theorem
43
;
47
;
49
;
48

Our conclusions do not rely on strict translational long-range order; rather, they concern the emergence of liquid-like transport within an ordered host through enhanced anharmonicity and spatially heterogeneous, string-like motion. Two-dimensional systems are therefore used primarily for clarity and visualization, while corresponding three-dimensional results are presented in the Supporting Information.

II.1
Microscopic Configurations and Sublattice Disorder

To investigate the microscopic dynamics of the two-dimensional host-carrier system, we employ model systems in which the host and carrier form stable crystals at low temperatures.
The choice of such a system reflects the long-range nature of the interaction potential, analogous to the Coulomb force (see “Materials and Methods” for details).
Under these conditions, low-density carriers naturally organize into ordered patterns at low temperatures, forming a well-defined crystalline state. As the temperature increases, the host lattice remains crystalline while the carrier sublattice progressively melts.
This configuration provides a mechanically stable reference state from which the subsequent thermal evolution and melting processes can be systematically explored, ensuring that the high-temperature dynamics originate from physically meaningful particle arrangements established at low temperatures.

Dynamical Heterogeneity in Solid Ionics

While our earlier analyses suggest that the system might exhibit significant dynamical heterogeneity, a direct visualization offers deeper insight. To this end, we trace the trajectories of representative carrier particles within the host lattice and map their motion across distinct time windows and temperature regimes.

This observation confirms that near the sublattice melting transition, the dynamics are governed by spatially correlated heterogeneous motion, characteristic of cooperative rearrangements in partially ordered systems.

To probe the connection between microscopic dynamics and transport, we examined the inverse relaxation times,

In the Arrhenius regime, relaxation is governed by an approximately fixed activation barrier
E_{a}
, such that the characteristic relaxation time or diffusivity follows an Arrhenius law. This implies that particles cross comparable energy barriers irrespective of the instantaneous configuration of their neighbors, a hallmark of weakly correlated dynamics typical of normal liquids or dilute systems.
However, near the sublattice melting transition, particle motion becomes increasingly cooperative. The system develops pronounced dynamical heterogeneity, where regions of mobile and immobile particles coexist, and structural relaxation requires collective rearrangements rather than isolated hopping events. As a result, the effective activation energy for relaxation,
E_{\mathrm{eff}}(T)
, becomes temperature-dependent and generally increases upon cooling because larger clusters of particles must move collectively. This leads to a super-Arrhenius temperature dependence,
\tau_{\alpha,\mathrm{C}}\propto\exp\!\left[E_{\mathrm{eff}}(T)/k_{\mathrm{B}}T\right]

E_{\mathrm{eff}}(T)
increases as
T

\tau_{\alpha,\mathrm{C}}
versus
1/T
therefore reflects the onset of collective dynamics. By contrast, purely local, single-particle hopping dynamics
31

The corresponding Stokes-Einstein ratios

This violation of the Stokes-Einstein relation is a hallmark of growing dynamical heterogeneity: structural relaxation slows markedly as correlated regions develop, whereas diffusion remains comparatively fast due to cooperative carrier motion. The resulting decoupling between diffusion and relaxation time scales becomes pronounced once heterogeneous dynamics are long-lived

\gamma_{{\rm L},{\rm C}}(t)
for the carriers increases smoothly without a sharp transition, indicating that their fluctuations are governed by intrinsic anharmonic vibrations rather than by a structural instability.

The Debye-Waller factor,
u_{\alpha}^{2}
(see Materials and Methods), further quantifies this contrast in vibrational behavior [Fig.
6
C
]. For the host particles,
u_{\rm H}^{2}
increases linearly with temperature,
u_{\rm H}^{2}\propto T
, indicating that their dynamics are dominated by harmonic cage vibrations, as expected for an elastically stable lattice
33
. For the carriers, by contrast,
u_{\rm C}^{2}
exhibits a nonlinear temperature dependence of the form
u_{\rm C}^{2}=aT+bT^{2}

33
, with the quadratic term
bT^{2}
becoming increasingly pronounced near the onset of sublattice melting. Within standard statistical-mechanical descriptions of lattice vibrations, such nonlinear temperature dependence arises from anharmonic terms in the effective potential, signaling local softening and strongly non-harmonic carrier fluctuations even while the host lattice remains elastically stable.

Together, these results demonstrate that anharmonicity increases continuously as the sublattice melting point is approached: the host retains harmonic, lattice-governed vibrations, whereas the carrier particles display pronounced nonlinear responses, reflecting the breakdown of harmonic confinement and the onset of dynamically heterogeneous motion.

Summary

We have investigated a minimal two-dimensional binary mixture model of a superionic conductor to disentangle three intertwined ingredients of fast ion transport—
sublattice melting
,
anharmonicity
, and
dynamic heterogeneity
—and to clarify how density governs their emergence.
The model captures selective sublattice melting within a chemically agnostic, coarse-grained framework.
Host (H) particles interact via short-range steric repulsion, forming a rigid lattice, whereas carrier (C) particles interact through much softer, long-range Wigner-type forces that promote collective delocalization.
This asymmetry in interaction range naturally produces distinct melting temperatures for the two sublattices, enabling a selective carrier-melting phase where the host remains ordered while the carrier sublattice becomes fluidlike (Fig.
1
).
Brownian dynamics was used for equilibration, followed by long microcanonical molecular-dynamics simulations to capture intrinsic dynamics (see Materials and Methods).

Snapshots and radial distribution functions reveal clear
selective disordering
: the carrier sublattice loses positional order while the host remains crystalline (Figs.
1
,
2
).
Diffusivity-temperature plots identify three regimes (Fig.
2

): (I) crystalline, (II) a broad
sublattice-melting
region with mobile carriers in an ordered host, and (III) full melting.
Carrier diffusivity evolves smoothly across regime II, whereas the host shows a sharp discontinuity, consistent with a first-order transition (Fig.
2
B,C
). Carrier trajectories (Fig.
3
) visualize these regimes.
At high
T
, motion is homogeneous and isotropic. Within regime II, carriers move cooperatively along host-lattice-aligned pathways forming transient honeycomb-like networks, while at lower
T
they exhibit heterogeneous motion, alternating between localized and collective migration. These patterns confirm that sublattice melting arises from correlated, anharmonic motion rather than uncorrelated hopping.

Time-correlation analyses quantify these dynamics (Fig.
4
).
The mean-squared displacement and self-intermediate scattering function reveal transient caging and stretched-exponential relaxation, marking cooperative slow dynamics. The four-point dynamic susceptibility
\chi_{4,\alpha}(t)
peaks near the freezing side of regime II, indicating growing spatial correlations, and collapses in the liquid phase, signifying a crossover from heterogeneous to homogeneous relaxation.
The Stokes-Einstein ratios for carriers
D_{\mathrm{C}}\tau_{\alpha,\mathrm{C}}
and
D_{\mathrm{C}}\tau_{\chi_{4}}
deviate strongly near the transition, confirming a breakdown of single-particle transport and the emergence of collective, anharmonic dynamics.

Spatially resolved mobility maps reveal pronounced dynamic heterogeneity near the sublattice-melting transition (Fig.
5
A
). Carriers segregate into coexisting mobile and immobile domains, with string-like cooperative displacements and correlated hopping events indicating collective, rather than single-particle, transport. Enhanced mobility preferentially appears near the boundaries of crystallized regions, where particles transiently occupy interstitial positions.
The corresponding static snapshots (Fig.
5

This spatial correlation parallels dynamic heterogeneity in glass-forming systems and implicates
anharmonic lattice distortions
as the
microscopic origin of heterogeneous carrier dynamics
near sublattice melting.

The
Lindemann index
and
Debye-Waller factor
link these dynamical changes to local vibrations (Fig.

For hosts,
u_{\rm H}^{2}\propto T
, consistent with harmonic motion; for carriers,
u_{\rm C}^{2}=aT+bT^{2}

Lower density broadens regime II and strengthens carrier anharmonicity, demonstrating that density controls both the range and cooperativity of the transition by reshaping the local energy landscape.

Together, these results provide a unified microscopic picture linking order-disorder dynamics, anharmonic vibrations, and dynamical heterogeneity. Unlike conventional mean-field or single-particle hopping descriptions, which assume independent ion motion in a fixed lattice potential, our model reveals that fast ionic transport emerges from collective, anharmonic rearrangements constrained by the crystalline host. This cooperative mechanism naturally couples local vibrational softening to large-scale sublattice disorder, bridging the gap between microscopic lattice dynamics and macroscopic transport. These insights suggest that
sublattice melting can be engineered—potentially even at ambient conditions—via density control
, offering design principles for robust, high-conductivity solid electrolytes. The roles of dimensionality and carrier size in shaping transport behavior are examined in detail in the Supporting Information.

Discussions

While the present model captures the essential physics of sublattice melting, several directions remain for future exploration.
To rigorously validate the microscopic mechanism proposed here, it is crucial to connect this coarse-grained framework with first-principles descriptions.

Ab initio
molecular dynamics and lattice-dynamical analyses could quantify anharmonic phonon modes, identify soft vibrational branches, and map potential-energy landscapes in representative ionic conductors such as AgI, PbF
2
, and Na
3
PS
4
.
Comparing the temperature dependence of the Debye-Waller factors and Lindemann indices obtained from first-principles simulations with those derived in this study would establish a direct correspondence between lattice-level anharmonicity and emergent sublattice disorder.
Furthermore, electronic-structure analyses could elucidate how bonding characteristics, ionic radii, and mass asymmetry govern the selective instability of carrier sublattices.

Experimentally, our findings offer clear guidelines for materials design.
In real systems, the effective density—or equivalently, the lattice volume or chemical pressure—can be tuned through compositional substitution, strain, or external pressure.
We predict that reducing the effective density broadens the sublattice-melting regime and enhances carrier anharmonicity, implying that low-density or expanded-lattice phases may exhibit sublattice melting near room temperature.
Advanced X-ray or neutron scattering measurements of the Debye-Waller factor could directly test the predicted crossover from harmonic to anharmonic behavior, while Raman and quasielastic neutron spectroscopy could probe phonon softening and correlated ionic motion for quantitative comparison with simulation.

In conclusion, this study provides a quantitative and conceptual framework that bridges phenomenological modeling with an atomistic understanding of sublattice melting.
Unlike conventional mean-field or single-ion hopping descriptions—which treat ionic transport as independent motion in a static potential—our results reveal that fast ion conduction arises from
collective, anharmonic, and density-dependent
dynamics constrained by the crystalline host.
This cooperative mechanism unifies order-disorder transitions, vibrational anharmonicity, and dynamical heterogeneity within a single physical picture, offering a microscopic foundation for interpreting nonlinear transport behaviors observed in superionic conductors.
Combining first-principles simulations with controlled experiments will be essential to verify the universality of these mechanisms and translate them into design principles for next-generation, room-temperature solid electrolytes.

Methods

III.1
Simulations

To reproduce the essential physics of sublattice melting within a minimal and chemically agnostic framework, we impose distinct interaction types on the two species. The host particles interact through short-range steric repulsion, forming a mechanically rigid crystalline framework, whereas the carrier particles experience a much softer, effectively long-range Wigner-type interaction that promotes collective delocalization within the host lattice. This asymmetry in interaction range and stiffness naturally yields widely separated melting temperatures for the two sublattices, enabling the emergence of a selective carrier-melting phase in which the carrier sublattice becomes fluidlike while the host lattice remains ordered.

To place our approach in context, prior studies of superionic conductors have shown that ion transport and sublattice-selective melting can emerge from relatively simple interaction schemes. In tunnel-type solids, a balance of Coulombic, polarization, and short-range repulsive forces governs the off-axis migration pathways and activation barriers of mobile ions, producing size-selective mobility
14
. Likewise, it has been demonstrated that the superionic behaviour of
\alpha
-AgI can be captured using only Coulombic and soft-core repulsive interactions, with an ordered anion sublattice coexisting alongside a highly delocalized cation sublattice
15
. These works highlight that coarse-grained molecular dynamics with simplified but physically motivated interactions can reproduce essential features of ion-conducting phases. Our model follows this spirit but adopts an even more generalized, chemically agnostic formulation, retaining the core interplay between host confinement and carrier delocalization while avoiding system-specific assumptions.

Radial distribution function

The pair distribution function (PDF) of particles of type
\alpha\in\{{\rm C,H}\}
(carrier or host) is defined
19
as

\displaystyle g_{\alpha\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{\rho_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}}\left\langle\sum_{j\neq k,\;j,k\in\alpha}\delta\!\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{j}+\mathbf{r}_{k}\right)\right\rangle,

(3)

where
\rho_{\alpha}=N_{\alpha}/(L_{x}L_{y})
is the number density of particle of species
\alpha
and
N_{\alpha}
is the number of particles of species
\alpha

The radial distribution function (RDF),
g_{\alpha\alpha}(r)
, was obtained by averaging the PDF over all angular directions.
This function quantifies the local structural correlations within each particle species and provides a measure of the characteristic interparticle spacing and short/long-range order.

III.3
Mean squared displacement

The mean squared displacement (MSD) of particles of type
\alpha\in\{{\rm C,H}\}
(carrier or host) was computed as

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}(t)^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{N_{\alpha}}\sum_{j\in\alpha}\left\langle\big[\mathbf{r}_{j}(t+t_{0})-\mathbf{r}_{j}(t_{0})\big]^{2}\right\rangle.

(4)

At long times, the MSD exhibits diffusive behavior, from which the self-diffusion coefficient
D_{\alpha}
is determined as

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}(t)^{2}\rangle\sim 4D_{\alpha}t.

(5)

This analysis was conducted separately for the carrier and host species, allowing a direct comparison of their respective mobilities and of the temperature dependence of their transport dynamics.

\rm{C}
(carrier) was calculated as

\displaystyle F_{s,\mathrm{C}}(q,t)=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{C}}}\sum_{j\in\mathrm{C}}\left\langle\exp\!\left[i\mathbf{q}\cdot\big(\mathbf{r}_{j}(t+t_{0})-\mathbf{r}_{j}(t_{0})\big)\right]\right\rangle,

(6)

where
\mathbf{q}
is the wave vector, whose magnitude was chosen to correspond to the characteristic length scale of the host particles, i.e., near the first peak of the static structure factor.

The structural relaxation time

\displaystyle F_{s,\mathrm{C}}(q,t)\sim A\exp\{-[t/\tau_{\alpha,\mathrm{C}}]^{\beta}\},

(7)

where
A
and
\beta

This analysis was performed independently for the carrier and host species, allowing a direct comparison of their microscopic relaxation behaviors and the temperature dependence of their dynamical responses.

III.5
Four-point dynamic susceptibility

To quantify dynamic heterogeneity consistent with our analysis of
F_{s,\alpha}(q,t)
, we evaluated the four-point dynamic susceptibility

;
31
;
47
for particles of type
\alpha\in\{{\rm C,H}\}
using the self-overlap order parameter.
The single-particle overlap is defined as

\displaystyle w^{(\alpha)}_{j}(t_{0}{+}t,t_{0})\equiv\Theta\!\Big(a-\big|\mathbf{r}_{j}(t_{0}{+}t)-\mathbf{r}_{j}(t_{0})\big|\Big),

(8)

where
\Theta(\cdot)

is a microscopic cutoff that sets the cage scale.
Unless otherwise noted, we set

The species-resolved (self) overlap is

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}(t)=\sum_{j\in\alpha}w^{(\alpha)}_{j}(t_{0}{+}t,t_{0}),

(9)

with averages taken over
t_{0}
(multiple time origins) and independent trajectories.
The four-point dynamic susceptibility is then obtained from the variance of
Q_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle\chi_{4,\alpha}(t)=\frac{V}{N_{\alpha}^{2}k_{\rm B}T}\Big(\langle Q_{\alpha}(t)^{2}\rangle-\langle Q_{\alpha}(t)\rangle^{2}\Big).

III.7
Debye-Waller factor

The Debye–Waller factor quantifies the amplitude of vibrational motion in the caged regime and is closely related to the plateau value of the Lindemann index.
For each species
\alpha\in\{{\rm C},{\rm H}\}
(carrier or host), we define

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}^{2}=\gamma_{L,\alpha}(t_{\rm p}),

(13)

where
\gamma_{L,\alpha}(t_{\rm p})
denotes the value taken at the characteristic turning time
t_{\rm p}
between the ballistic and diffusive regimes.
This time marks the cage-confinement timescale at which both the mean-squared displacement and
\gamma_{L,\alpha}(t)
exhibit a transient plateau.
For host particles (
\alpha={\rm H}
),
u_{\rm H}^{2}
corresponds directly to the plateau height of
\gamma_{L,{\rm H}}(t)
.
For carrier particles (
\alpha={\rm C}
), the plateau is less pronounced due to stronger anharmonicity; accordingly,
u_{\rm C}^{2}
is evaluated at the same
t_{\rm p}
to represent their effective vibrational amplitude.
This definition enables a consistent comparison of Debye–Waller factors between the two species and provides a microscopic proxy for the local stiffness of their respective environments.
If
u_{\alpha}^{2}\propto T
holds, the dynamics in that regime can be regarded as effectively harmonic.

III.8
Three-dimensional simulations

To assess the robustness of the observed phenomenology against dimensionality, we performed complementary three-dimensional molecular dynamics simulations using a model constructed according to the same physical principles as the two-dimensional system. In particular, the interaction hierarchy, mass ratios, and relative size asymmetry between host and carrier species were kept identical to those employed in two dimensions, ensuring that any qualitative differences arise solely from dimensionality.

The initial configuration was generated on a three-dimensional
face-centered-cubic
-like lattice composed of alternating host and carrier particles along all three spatial directions. The simulation box consisted of a periodic array of
4\times 4\times 4
unit cells, each containing an ordered arrangement of large host particles and smaller carrier particles, yielding a total particle number
N\simeq 512
with equal populations of the two species. Host particles were assigned an effective diameter
\sigma_{\mathrm{H}}=1.0
, while carrier particles were smaller, with
\sigma_{\mathrm{C}}=0.154
, promoting preferential occupation of interstitial regions within the host framework. Both species were assigned equal masses.

The lattice spacing was fixed at
a_{\mathrm{L\text{-}space}}=1.5
, corresponding to a volume packing fraction of
\varphi_{v}=0.6228
. The resulting cubic simulation box had a linear dimension
L=L_{x}=L_{y}=L_{z}=4\times a_{\mathrm{L\text{-}space}}=6.0
, with periodic boundary conditions applied in all three spatial directions. This choice places the system in a mechanically stable crystalline regime for the host sublattice while allowing sufficient free volume for carrier delocalization. Interparticle interactions followed the same design as in two dimensions: host-host and host-carrier interactions were purely repulsive and sufficiently stiff to maintain long-range crystalline order, whereas carrier-carrier interactions were deliberately softened to suppress direct mutual exclusion and promote collective, correlated motion.

The system was evolved using molecular dynamics with periodic boundary conditions in all three directions. After an initial equilibration stage, production runs were carried out in the microcanonical ensemble to probe intrinsic dynamical behavior without thermostat-induced artifacts.
Consistent with the two-dimensional results, the three-dimensional simulations exhibit selective melting of the carrier sublattice
(see Supplementary Information for more details).
These findings suggest that the collective and anharmonic mechanisms identified here persist in fully three-dimensional systems and are not restricted to two dimensions.
