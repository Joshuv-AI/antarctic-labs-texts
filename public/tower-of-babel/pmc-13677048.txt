Harnessing Methylselenol (CH3SeH): A Highly Effective Platform for Stimuli‐Responsive Delivery With Therapeutic Potential
Authors: Wenfang Liang, Liyue Wang, Xuan Wang, Xinran Li, Hao Xing, Lu Sun, Zhen Xi, Long Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMethylselenol (CH3SeH) is not only a key selenium (Se) metabolite but also a potent therapeutic molecule. Chemical tools for stimulus‐responsive delivery of CH3SeH should be crucial for exploring Se‐related biomedicine. Nevertheless, the classical 1,6‐self‐immolation is inefficient in releasing the poorly leaving selenol, and donation platforms for triggerable release of the CH3SeH remain lacking. Herein, we report the first general responsive platform (Triggered Donation for MethylSelenol, termed TD‐MSe) via fast intramolecular amino‐activated methylselenocarbamates (t 1/2 = 5 s at pH 7.4), enabling the efficient cyclization release of CH3SeH without electrophilic byproducts. We leverage the caged TD‐MSe motifs to successfully create triggered donors that are stable before activation but can liberate CH3SeH under different stimulus conditions in vitro and in live cells. Additionally, we demonstrate that the tumor‐associated oxidoreductase (hNQO1)‐triggered CH3SeH donor was activated in hNQO1‐positive HT‐29 cells but not in hNQO1‐negative cells. Moreover, we can use the CH3SeH prodrug to selectively kill metastatic cells of hNQO1‐overexpressing cancer types, providing a promising therapeutic strategy for difficult‐to‐treat diseases. We anticipate that the new‐release chemistry will be widely applied for developing advanced tools toward smart selenol delivery and therapy.

Introduction

Selenium (Se) is an essential trace element for chemoprevention of cancer, and various Se compounds have been assessed in clinical trials for multiple malignancies, including prostate, colon, and lung cancers [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. In the complex metabolism network, these Se compounds are metabolized into different redox‐active products that mainly belong to two pools (Figure 1A): a hydrogen selenide (H2Se) pool (e.g., from selenite) and a methylselenol (CH3SeH) pool (e.g., from methylseleninic acid (MSA), methylselenocysteine (MeSec)) [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Although H2Se pools exert certain antitumor activity, excess H2Se that surpasses the physiological threshold for selenoprotein biosynthesis may induce DNA single‐strand cleavage [14, 15, 16, 17], which markedly compresses the therapeutic safety window and raises metastatic risks of tumors. In contrast, CH3SeH is relatively nongenotoxic and acts independently of selenoprotein saturation levels [18, 19, 20]. It targets tumors via multiple mechanisms, including regulating the expression of NKG2D ligands [21], inducing caspase‐mediated apoptosis [22], modulating oxidative stress [23], and inhibiting the expression of the angiogenic factor VEGF to block angiogenesis [20, 24]. Furthermore, it can also synergistically enhance the effects of chemoradiotherapy and immunotherapy and reduce drug side effects, possessing numerous desirable antitumorigenic properties [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Accordingly, the development of CH3SeH precursors and donors should be of great significance for cancer prevention and treatment.

Natural MeSec can be solely metabolized to release CH3SeH [8, 12, 21], and this metabolic process is highly dependent on the expression level of endogenous β‐lyase, which should be enhanced to improve the release efficiency and therapeutic efficacy [31]. Selenomethionine (SeMet), a clinically prevalent Se dietary supplement, also serves as a CH3SeH precursor in the presence of γ‐lyase [7, 11], but the main metabolic pathway of SeMet is the conversion to H2Se [8, 12]. Although excess H2Se can also be metabolized into CH3SeH, SeMet at low micromolar concentrations cannot produce significant amounts of CH3SeH to exert anti‐tumor effects [10, 12, 32]. MSA needs to be converted by endogenous reduction systems to produce CH3SeH [6, 19, 33], and hydrolysis of methylselenocarbamates [34, 35, 36] and methylselenoesters [37, 38] can generate CH3SeH directly in physiological buffers. These donors and precursors have demonstrated impressive pharmacological activities due to their ability to liberate CH3SeH. Despite these successes, there are still problems such as uncontrollable release and low conversion efficiency, making it difficult to meet the needs of precise therapy. Moreover, selectively releasing CH3SeH in cancer cells without affecting normal cells is challenging. Developing stimulus‐responsive delivery platforms should be an effective strategy to address the dilemma [39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45], but this has not yet been reported for CH3SeH. An ideal stimulus‐responsive system can remain stable under physiological conditions and only convert to CH3SeH in response to a specific stimulus, enabling spatiotemporally controlled or “on‐demand” delivery in live biosystems. Motivated by these needs, herein we report a highly effective and general platform for the development of triggered CH3SeH donors.

To achieve this goal, we initially developed CH3SeH donors based on classical self‐immolative chemistry [45, 46, 47], but the release rate for CH3SeH was very slow (Figure 1B). On the other hand, distinct from the good stability of C = Se moieties in H2Se donors [44], these C‒Se scaffolds [34, 35, 36, 37, 38] lack sufficient water stability, restricting their applications for constructing triggered CH3SeH donors. Through extensive optimization, we identified a water‐stable methylselenocarbamate motif and then engineered an adjacent nucleophilic group to intramolecularly activate the motif, achieving rapid cyclization release of the CH3SeH (Figure 1C). Importantly, we created triggered donors that can be activated under specific stimuli and demonstrated their proof‐of‐concept applications for intracellular CH3SeH delivery.

Results and Discussion

1,6‐Self‐Immolation Is Very Slow for CH3SeH Donation

We first assessed the quinone methide self‐immolation [45, 46, 47] for developing CH3SeH donors and prepared compound 1 with an azide as the trigger (Figure 2A). We then examined the Staudinger‐mediated reduction of 1 to generate 1a, which may undergo a self‐immolative process to liberate CH3SeH and 1b. The reaction between 1 and tris‐(2‐carboxyethyl)‐phosphine (TCEP) in PBS (pH 7.4) at 25 °C was monitored by UV−vis experiments, permitting kinetic analysis of the Staudinger reduction. Time‐dependent spectra exhibited an isosbestic point at 247 nm within the initial 15 min (Figure S1), and the reaction kinetics was determined by monitoring the decrease at 257 nm. Pseudo‐first‐order rate constants (k obs) obtained at varying TCEP concentrations gave a linear relationship to yield a bimolecular rate constant k 2 = 1.43 M−1s−1 (Figure 2B). This rate should be due to the conversion from 1 to 1a, and HRMS analysis confirmed that 1a is the main product (Figure S2). We also observed the mass peaks of 1b and its hydrate, implying that the self‐immolation occurred very slowly. In time‐dependent 77Se NMR experiments (Figure S2), the results further support a fast Staudinger reaction followed by a very slow self‐decomposition of 1a. We further conducted time‐dependent 1H NMR experiments of 1 with TCEP and found that all resonances of 1 disappeared within the initial 0.5 h (Figure S3), and the ensuing changes from 0.5 to 266.5 h reflected the slow decomposition of 1a. Kinetic analysis of the decreasing resonances of 1a gave a self‐immolative rate of 2.63 × 10−6 s−1 (Figure 2B), supporting a very slow CH3SeH release (Figure 2A). The half‐life (t 1/2) of the self‐immolation is about 73 h, making such self‐immolative chemistry inefficient in the selenol donation.

Rational Design of a Water‐Stable Methylselenocarbamate Scaffold

To develop more efficient releasing tools, we envisioned that intramolecular nucleophilic attack toward a water‐stable methylselenocarbamate would lead to the formation of a cyclization product [48, 49, 50], providing access to rapid liberation of the poor leaving selenol. We first assessed the water‐stability of Se‐methyl N‐phenylcarbamoselenoate (2) (Figure 2C) and found that it decomposed quickly in PBS (pH 7.4) with t 1/2 of 9.4 min (Figure 2D and Figure S4). In addition, HRMS analysis revealed that 2 hydrolyzed to generate aniline. One explanation for this decomposition is that the selenocarbamate N–H would be deprotonated in PBS (pH 7.4) [51], resulting in isocyanate formation and CH3SeH release (Figure S4). This isocyanate would further hydrolyze to aniline. To investigate this hypothesis as well as to develop a water‐stable motif, we prepared Se‐methyl N‐phenyl‐N‐methylcarbamoselenoate (3) and assessed its stability in PBS (pH 7.4). HPLC analyses and absorbance spectra suggested that compound 3 was much more stable in buffers (Figure 2D and Figure S4E), implying that the N‐methylation should significantly inhibit the hydrolysis of 2. We also employed a selenol‐selective fluorescence probe, Sel‐green [52], to monitor possible CH3SeH release from the selenocarbamates (Figure 2C). Time‐dependent emissions confirmed the CH3SeH release from 2 but not from 3 (Figure 2E and Figure S5). Moreover, 3 showed remarkable stability even in the presence of physiologically relevant nucleophiles including thiols and amines (Figure S6). Therefore, the selenocarbamate motif in 3 should be suitable for further donor design.

Engineering of a Highly Efficient Donation Platform

We next installed a Boc‐protected amino group adjacent to the Se‐methyl N‐phenyl‐N‐methylselenocarbamate (4) and further prepared TD‐MSe1 with a protonated amine (Figure 2F). The deprotonation at pH 7.4 yielded a transient intermediate (TD‐MSe) that quickly underwent an intramolecular cyclization to form a non‐electrophilic byproduct 5 (Figure S7). In addition, the isolated yield of 5 from the spontaneous decomposition of TD‐MSe1 in PBS was quantitative, supporting a highly efficient cyclization process. Subsequently, methyl(phenyl)carbamic chloride was employed to trap the released CH3SeH from TD‐MSe1 in one pot, and the resultant product 3 was determined via HPLC analysis (Figure S8). The results demonstrated a CH3SeH release efficiency of up to 94.2 ± 2.7%, thereby validating TD‐MSe as a highly efficient platform for CH3SeH donation.

We further evaluated the cyclization kinetics using UV−vis experiments. The absorbance bands of TD‐MSe1 at pH 4.5 and 7.4 are significantly different (Figure 2G), and the result at pH 7.4 is consistent with that of compound 5 (Figure S9), suggesting a very fast conversion from TD‐MSe1 to 5 with k obs of 0.14 s– 1 (Figure 2G inset). In addition, we fitted the absorbance intensity curves to measure k obs for each pH value to determine whether the amine or its protonated form was the active nucleophile (Figure S10 and Table 1). If the amino group is the active nucleophile in donor activation, we would expect to observe a positively correlated relationship between log(k obs) and pH value (Figure S11) [53]. As expected, the plot of log(k obs) versus pH value was linear, with more acidic conditions resulting in slower conversion, which is consistent with the deprotonated amino group being the active nucleophile. HPLC analyses further confirmed the pH‐dependent rapid conversion from TD‐MSe1 to 5 (Figure S12). Altogether, the intramolecular amine can quickly attack the water‐stable selenocarbamate to result in rapid extrusion of CH3SeH under physiological pH.

We then employed an electrophilic reagent (iodoacetamide) to successfully trap the released CH3SeH from TD‐MSe1 in PBS buffers in one pot (Figure S13). In addition, the probe Sel‐green could be used to monitor the CH3SeH released from TD‐MSe1 by fluorescence spectra as well as by naked eye under a 365 nm UV lamp (Figure S14). The emissions at 515 nm increased with higher concentrations of TD‐MSe1 under physiological buffers (Figure 2H), supporting the CH3SeH‐releasing ability of the donor. HRMS analysis of a solution of TD‐MSe1 and Sel‐green identified the expected 2a, 5, and CH3SeH‐trapped products (Figure S14). Taken together, the TD‐MSe motif should be highly efficient for the CH3SeH release under physiological conditions.

Encouraged by the above results, we further investigated whether TD‐MSe1 could be used for intracellular CH3SeH delivery. Briefly, HeLa cells were pre‐treated with 10 µM probe Sel‐green for 20 min and then incubated with donor TD‐MSe1 (0–20 µM) for 30 min. As expected, negligible fluorescence was observed in the probe‐only treated cells, and a concentration‐dependent intracellular fluorescence increase was observed in the donor‐treated cells (Figure 2I). In addition, time‐dependent bioimaging results of the cells treated with TD‐MSe1 further supported the intracellular CH3SeH delivery (Figure S15). In contrast, using 3 instead of TD‐MSe1 could not trigger the intracellular fluorescence from Sel‐green. Therefore, the TD‐MSe platform should be useful for controlled CH3SeH donation in live cells.

Validation of the Caged TD‐MSe Platform for Light‐Triggered CH3SeH Release

To create stable CH3SeH donors under physiological conditions for triggerable delivery, the TD‐MSe platform was caged by an o‐nitrobenzyl moiety via a carbamate linkage to prepare TD‐MSe2 (Figure 3A). HPLC analysis revealed that TD‐MSe2 remained stable in PBS (pH 7.4) (Figure 3B and Figure S16), even in the presence of physiologically relevant concentrations of thiols (Figure S17), supporting the remarkable stability of the caged TD‐MSe motif before activation.

After irradiation activation, the donor TD‐MSe2 is expected to generate 5 and to release CH3SeH, and both time‐dependent HPLC and UV−vis experiments suggested light‐triggered decomposition of TD‐MSe2 in PBS buffers (Figure 3B and Figure S18). In addition, the expected byproducts 5 and 2‐nitrosobenzaldehyde from the light‐triggered TD‐MSe2 were identified by HRMS analysis. We also used excess 4‐bromomethylbenzyl alcohol to trap and confirm the CH3SeH released from light‐activated TD‐MSe2 in one pot (Figure S18). These results imply that TD‐MSe2 should be a light‐triggered CH3SeH donor.

To demonstrate the spatiotemporal control of the release, we further carried out the light on‐off experiments [54] with TD‐MSe2 under alternating “light” and “dark” conditions (Figure S19). The light‐triggered release of product 5 could be quantified by HPLC analysis (Figure S19). As shown in Figure 3C, under the “dark” condition, the photo‐uncaging process completely ceased, while the photolysis only proceeded under the “light on” condition. Therefore, the donation reaction is selectively controlled by light, and moreover, the release efficiency of 5 could reach over 80% after the photolysis (Figure 3C).

To provide mechanistic insights, we also used NMR spectroscopy to monitor the irradiation‐dependent activation of TD‐MSe2 in CD3CN solution. We could identify the characteristic peaks in irradiation‐dependent 1H NMR spectra for the generation of 5, 2‐nitrosobenzaldehyde and dimethyldiselenide (DMDSe) via comparison with the standard compounds (Figure 3D). Prolonging the irradiation time resulted in gradual disappearance of the TD‐MSe2 peaks as well as the appearance of the products’ peaks. Because selenols can be rapidly oxidized to diselenides under irradiation conditions [54], 13C{1H} and 77Se{1H} NMR spectra further demonstrated a clear irradiation‐dependent increase of DMDSe (Figures S20 and S21). Moreover, monobromobimane (mBB), a trapping reagent for selenol [55], was used to successfully trap the CH3SeH released from the light‐activated donor in the presence of 1,4‐dithiothreitol, and the characteristic Se isotopic patterns are consistent with the calculated mass distribution (Figure 3E). Building on these studies, TD‐MSe2 is highly efficient for light‐triggered CH3SeH/DMDSe release with a well‐defined releasing mechanism.

Inspired by Xian's preeminent work on Se‐based catalytic scavengers to eliminate ROS [56], we envisioned that the light‐triggered CH3SeH donor might alleviate oxidative stress. We then evaluated the antioxidant activity of TD‐MSe2 in live cells. A Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay suggested that TD‐MSe2 without or with 120 s UV (365 nm) irradiation following 2 h of incubation with HeLa cells exhibited negligible cytotoxicity (Figure 3F). In antioxidant studies, incubating HeLa cells with 400 µM H2O2 for 2 h significantly reduced the cell viability to near 22%. When HeLa cells were first treated with 30 or 100 µM TD‐MSe2 for 2 h and then irradiated for 120 s, prior to the incubation with H2O2, the cell viability increased to near 42 and 67 percent, respectively. In contrast, the negative control without irradiation did not provide cellular protection against the H2O2‐induced damage. We further found that longer photoactivation of the donor provided more obvious cellular protection against H2O2‐induced oxidative stress (Figure 3F). These results support that the CH3SeH/DMDSe released from TD‐MSe2 should be responsible for the cytoprotective abilities. It is noteworthy that HeLa cells contain millimolar endogenous GSH [57], which can reduce DMDSe to CH3SeH easily. In living cells, CH3SeH can react with ROS to generate CH3SeOH, and a catalytic cycle may be achieved by the reaction of GSH and CH3SeOH to regenerate CH3SeH [58], promoting efficient ROS elimination. To support the ROS‐scavenging activity of CH3SeH, we further used a commercially available ROS probe (2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescin diacetate) to detect the intracellular ROS levels, and as expected, cells treated with TD‐MSe2 with or without irradiation showed negligible or significant fluorescence, respectively (Figure S22). To the best of our knowledge, TD‐MSe2 represents the first light‐triggered CH3SeH donor, which should be capable of mimicking the GR activity to enhance ROS scavenging in live cells [56, 58].

Development of a Biomarker‐Triggered CH3SeH Donor for Cell‐Selective Therapeutic Delivery

To demonstrate the general applicability of the caged TD‐MSe platform, we chose a “trimethyl‐locked” quinone trigger that can be removed by a tumor‐associated oxidoreductase (hNQO1). hNQO1 is over‐expressed in some types of metastatic cells, including the human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line (HT‐29) [59, 60, 61]. We created the first example of hNQO1‐triggered CH3SeH donor TD‐MSe3 for cell‐selective delivery (Figures 4 and 5). UV−vis experiments suggested that TD‐MSe3 was stable in PBS (pH 7.4) over 70 h (Figure S23). Upon treatment with hNQO1 in the presence of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), the donor was activated to generate the expected products 5 and 11, which were identified by HRMS analysis (Figure S24). In addition, time‐dependent HPLC analysis suggested the fast enzyme‐triggered reaction of TD‐MSe3 to generate 11 followed by 5 (Figure 4B and Figure S24), supporting the uncaging of the trigger and the formation of 5 in tandem. Furthermore, the release efficiency of 11 from TD‐MSe3 after hNQO1 reduction exceeded 90% (Figure S25), implying a highly efficient enzyme‐activated process.

The released CH3SeH from hNQO1‐triggered TD‐MSe3 was successfully trapped by mBB in one pot (Figure S26). We also confirmed the CH3SeH release from the enzyme‐triggered TD‐MSe3 by measuring the time‐dependent fluorescence response of Sel‐green (Figure 4C and Figure S27). Such enzyme‐triggered CH3SeH release can be inhibited by the hNQO1 inhibitor dicoumarol [62] in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 4D and Figure S27). These results confirm the enzyme‐triggered TD‐MSe3 for controlled CH3SeH donation in physiological buffers.

We then investigated the intracellular CH3SeH release from TD‐MSe3 mediated by endogenous hNQO1 (Figure 5A). First of all, we evaluated the hNQO1 levels in three different cell lines using an hNQO1 fluorescent probe [61] and found that HT‐29 cells had high hNQO1 expression while HeLa and HUVEC cells had low hNQO1 expression (Figure S28). Accordingly, HT‐29 cells were selected for subsequent imaging experiments: upon co‐treatment with both probe Sel‐green and donor TD‐MSe3, the HT‐29 cells displayed pronounced intracellular fluorescence (Figure 5B), implying that endogenous hNQO1 effectively activates TD‐MSe3 to release CH3SeH intracellularly. When cells were pre‐incubated with dicoumarol, the intracellular fluorescence was substantially diminished, further confirming that the intracellular release of CH3SeH from TD‐MSe3 is hNQO1‐dependent (Figure S29). In contrast, HeLa cells, which possess low basal hNQO1 expression [61], exhibited negligible fluorescence compared with HT‐29 cells (Figure 5B). The results suggest that the intracellular activation of TD‐MSe3 is governed by endogenous hNQO1 levels and thus displays intrinsic cell selectivity.

To assess anticancer effects of the enzyme‐triggered CH3SeH donor, we examined the cytotoxicity of TD‐MSe3 in HT‐29 cells (Figure 5C). After 72 h of incubation, TD‐MSe3 exhibited a potent effect on cell viability (IC 50 = 23.4 µM), whereas control 3 showed no appreciable cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 150 µM. In contrast, TD‐MSe3 displayed substantially higher IC 50 values (> 200 µM) in hNQO1‐low cell lines of both HeLa and HUVEC cells (Figure 5D and Figure S30). Our preliminary data suggest that the CH3SeH release from the donor is crucial for the anticancer effect, and the therapeutic potential of TD‐MSe3 should have cellular selectivity toward hNQO1‐overexpressing cancer types, matching the above bioimaging results.

Subsequently, we further evaluated the long‐term anti‐proliferative effect of TD‐MSe3 using a colony formation assay (Figure 5E). Notably, TD‐MSe3 inhibited the HT‐29 cell proliferation in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 5F). At 2 µM, the number of tumor colonies was reduced by more than 35% relative to the untreated control, and the colony formation was almost completely abolished at 10 µM. These findings collectively demonstrate that TD‐MSe3 effectively suppresses the clonogenic survival of hNQO1‐overexpressing cancer cells in vitro, thereby underscoring its potential as a promising hNQO1‐activated antitumor candidate.

Conclusion

In summary, we have reported the first general responsive platform for harnessing CH3SeH in vitro and in live cells. We found that the classical self‐immolative chemistry is too slow to release CH3SeH (t 1/2 = 73 h) and then identified Se‐methyl N‐phenyl‐N‐methylcarbamoselenoate as a stable motif under physiological conditions. We further rationally designed an adjacent amino (nucleophilic) group for fast intramolecular activation of the water‐stable methylcarbamoselenoate, developing a highly efficient platform (TD‐MSe) for controlled release of CH3SeH. Moreover, the caged TD‐MSe motifs were successfully used to create the first examples of light‐ and hNQO1‐triggered CH3SeH donors, and these donors were successfully applied for intracellular CH3SeH delivery (Table S1). Importantly, the hNQO1‐triggered donor could be activated by an endogenous biomarker for killing metastatic cancer cells selectively. We anticipate that the new donation chemistry will significantly promote the development of advanced selenol donors, further advancing Se‐related biomedical research [63, 64, 65].

Experimental Section

The synthesis methods of all new compounds as well as compound characterizations are described in detail in the Supporting Information. We used HPLC analysis, HRMS/NMR spectra, and UV absorption/fluorescence experiments to evaluate the release performance of the synthesized donors. For cell imaging, HeLa cells were first incubated with the probe (10 µM) for 20 min, and then the probe‐treated cells were further incubated with the donor (0, 5, 20 µM) for 30 min. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV1000). To verify the intracellular CH3SeH release from the enzyme‐triggered donor, both HT‐29 and HeLa cells were treated with TD‐MSe3 (20 µM) and the CH3SeH‐responsive probe Sel‐green (10 µM) for 2 h, and fluorescence imaging was subsequently acquired using Axio‐Imager_LSM‐800. The cytotoxicity of the donors and control molecules was evaluated using the CCK‐8 assay. HeLa cells were used to evaluate the irradiation time‐dependent antioxidant activity of the light‐triggered donor TD‐MSe2. HT‐29, HeLa, and HUVEC cells were incubated with different concentrations of TD‐MSe3 for 72 h to evaluate the anticancer effects. For the colony formation assay, HT‐29 cells (3 × 103 cells per well) were incubated with culture medium containing the enzyme‐triggered donor at different concentrations (0, 2, 10, or 40 µM), and the medium was refreshed every three days. After 19 days of culture, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 15 min and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 5 min at room temperature. The plates were then imaged, and colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ software.
