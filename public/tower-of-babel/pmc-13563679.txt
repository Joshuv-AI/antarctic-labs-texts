Bidirectional Interactions Between Language and Music in a Dual Task Experiment Without Syntactic Violations
Authors: Aura A. L. Cruz Heredia, Micah Alan Johnson, David Cremins, Thomas McGee, Jade L. Elliott, Jeffrey N. Chiang, Martin M. Monti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate potentially shared syntactic mechanisms between language and music, we conducted a novel adaptation of the dual task paradigm without syntactic violations to assess the degree of bidirectional interaction between the two domains. Participants listened to music progressions while reading natural language sentences, and were then asked to provide, in randomized order, music closure judgments and language comprehension answers. We manipulated both language and music stimuli in terms of their syntactic structural complexity and syntactic working memory demands based on nonlocal dependencies between words or chords. We discovered a bidirectional yet unbalanced pattern of cross‐domain interaction whereby language exerted relatively more influences over music than vice versa, apparently resulting mostly from syntactic structural complexity instead of syntactic working memory demands, although we discuss the possibility that alternative heuristic strategies may have been employed during concurrent, resource‐demanding processes. We also found that cross‐domain interactions were often most prominent when syntactically critical words and keys temporally coincided across sequences. These results provide new evidence in support of theoretical models of shared resources between language and music.

Introduction

The structural parallels between language and music have been discussed for a long time (Fay, 1971; Lashley, 1951; Riemann, 1877). At the heart of both domains lies a combinatorial, generative process (i.e., syntax) for creating infinitely recursive sequences from discrete elements (e.g., sentences from letters, speech from phonemes, harmonic progressions from notes and chords) that unfold over time in hierarchical structures with superordinate and subordinate relations and with local and nonlocal dependencies (Chomsky, 1957; Fitch & Martins, Hauser, Chomsky, & Fitch, 2002; Lerdahl & Jackendoff, 1983; Patel, Rohrmeier, 2011).

Most discussions of shared versus independent resources in musicolinguistic processing focus on dependency processing across the two modalities. As detailed by Van de Cavey and Hartsuiker (2016), “dependency processing can be aligned with current structural processing theories in both tonal harmony and language” (p. 173; though see Patel, for a different approach). In language, prominent processing theories such as the dependency locality theory (DLT, e.g., Gibson, 1998, 2000) posit that incomplete structures need to be maintained active in memory and that incoming elements need to be integrated into this available structural “skeleton.” The longer the distance between two structurally dependent elements, such as heads and nonhead elements, the higher the processing load on the mechanisms and resources for structural integration, and the hierarchical structures in which these elements are embedded influence processing demands.

Similarly, music has been described as a mixture of short‐term, local relationships as well as a complex assortment of nested, nonlocal dependencies stretching over long ranges of musical passages (Lerdahl & Jackendoff, 1985), capable of creating tree‐like structures. Processing demands—analogous to those described for language above—can be established between structural harmonic “heads” and the incoming elements of a progression, which can be structured recursively (Rohrmeier, 2011). The dependency principle in Rohrmeier's account of tonal harmony states that “each element (chord) in a chord sequence is structurally connected to its preceding or succeeding chord or chord group in a dependency relationship… The chords in a harmony sequence form recursive dependency relationships until there is only one head for the whole sequence or phrase” (p. 38). Under Rohrmeier's account, dependent chords that are separated by other functional chords constitute long‐distance dependencies in music. Because there are no gaps posited in Rohrmeier's generative syntax of tonal harmony, there is not a strict analog to filler‐gap distance in music stimuli, even when the progression features a center‐embedding. However, there is an approximate analog to language in the sense that DLT more broadly posits that processing demands increase as a function of distance between elements that are to be integrated (Gibson, 2000).

Whether the hierarchical and syntactic similarities between language and music are substantial (i.e., based on shared neurocognitive resources or systems) or superficial (i.e., based on analogy only) is an important and unresolved question (Asano & Boeckx, 2015; Fedorenko & Varley, 2016; Peretz, Vuvan, Lagrois, & Armony, 2015) that reflects a broader debate surrounding the degree of interdependence between the structure‐based operations of language and those of other forms of human thought (Boeckx, 2010; Fitch & Martins, Gleitman & Papafragou, 2013; Lashley, 1951; Monti, 2017; Rosenbaum, Cohen, Jax, Weiss, & van der Wel, 2007). In the context of the relationship between language and music, the discussion is largely bound by two polar opposite positions.

On the one hand, there have been many neuropsychological observations of double dissociations between cases of amusia and aphasia (Ayotte, Peretz, & Brain, 2002; Luria, Tsvetkova, & Futer, 1965; Marin & Perry, 1999; Peretz et al., 1994; Piccirilli, Sciarma, & Luzzi, 2000). Additionally, behavioral (e.g., Bonnel, Faita, Peretz, & Besson, 2001; Perruchet & Poulin‐Charronnat, 2013) and neuroimaging evidence (e.g., Cheung, Meyer, Friederici, & Koelsch, 2018; Fedorenko, Behr, & Kanwisher, 2011) supporting modular independence has also been reported.

On the other hand, it has been proposed that language and music rely on common neurocognitive substrates, such as a “supramodal” mechanism for parsing structured hierarchies (i.e., supramodal hierarchical parser hypothesis, SHP; Fadiga, Craighero, & D'Ausilio, 2009; Tettamanti & Weniger, 2006), or a domain‐general mechanism for online structural integration of domain‐specific representations (i.e., shared syntactic integration resource hypothesis, SSIRH; Patel, 2012). These views account for behavioral data showing interaction effects during dual processing (Fedorenko, Patel, Casasanto, Winawer, & Gibson, 2009; Hoch, Benedicte, & Tillmann, 2011; Kunert, Willems, & Hagoort, 2016; Slevc, Rosenberg, & Patel, 2009), interactions between or colocalized electroencephalography signal components (Koelsch et al., 2005; Sammler et al., 2013) as well as spatial overlap of blood oxygen‐level dependent patterns in neuroimaging studies (Chiang et al., 2018; Koelsch et al., 2002). Some neuropsychological evidence on shared resources has also been reported (Patel, 2005; Patel, Iversen, Wassenaar, & Hagoort, 2008).

In a review of the available musicolinguistic literature, Fedorenko and Varley (2016) offered a reinterpretation of many studies that have been taken as evidence for shared domain‐specific resources. Namely, they argue that syntactic violation paradigms often used in these studies might recruit attentional demands that are related to the saliency of the violation, rather than to the specifics of the syntactic manipulations. For example, they proposed that observed event‐related potential responses that are sensitive to syntactic violations (e.g., P600) are better thought of as signatures of error detection, as they have been observed in response to unexpected events in the absence of structural violations (see Fedorenko & Varley, 2016, p. 139–142 for further discussion).

Notably, however, some studies employing such violation paradigms have used a variety of attention‐capturing or saliency controls (e.g., unexpected changes in timbre or volume) and have observed interactions between the modules in response to the syntactic violations but no interactions with the saliency controls (Fedorenko et al., 2009; Slevc et al., 2009). Furthermore, recent studies have manipulated music syntax using nondeviant chords in their progressions (i.e., less expected but in‐key subdominant chords, rather than in‐key tonic chords) and have found support for cross‐domain syntactic interactions (Hoch et al., 2011; Kunert et al., 2016). Overall, the currently available data paint a rather mixed picture with respect to the relationship between musical and linguistic processing, particularly along structural parameters.

For the present study, we conducted a violation‐free, bidirectional test of whether language and music interact and, if so, whether such interactions are preferentially due to syntactic structural complexity (SynSC) or shared syntactic working memory (SynWM) demands. Similar to Kunert et al. (2016), we employ a cross‐domain, dual task design in which participants listened to music progressions of different structural complexity, and provided closure judgments of how well the final chords resolved the preceding tonal progression, while reading natural language sentences of different structural complexity and providing comprehension judgments.

Although “the soldiers” in (3) acts as the direct object of the relative verb “saw,” the two appear several words apart and in noncanonical word order (object‐subject‐verb). In ORCs like (3), the fronting of the object of the embedded clause verb to the main subject position creates a long‐distance dependency between the fronted phrase (called a “filler”) and the “gap” it left behind. While SRCs like (1) and (2) also feature a filler‐gap dependency, the distance is noticeably shorter; indeed, this has been hypothesized as the source of processing differences documented in response to these kinds of sentences along a number of different methodologies and paradigms (e.g., Fedorenko et al., 2009). While the exact parsing and processing operations underlying these types of sentences are still subject to much theorizing, debate and development (Traxler, 2014; Traxler, Boudewyn, & Loudermilk, 2012), the literature on English sentences processing is consistent in labeling the difference between ORC and SRC2 sentences as resulting from a difference in working memory demands. Our definition of SynWM (ORC > SRC2; see Table 2), which compares the two center‐embedded sentences with different extracted relative clauses, follows this convention.

In order to address whether any pattern effects are more attributable to domain‐general resources like working memory or to domain‐specific ones responsible for actively computing hierarchical relations, our study employed another, less commonly studied, relative clause contrast. Namely, we compared subject‐extracted relative clauses that are center‐embedded (SRC2, see (2) above) to ones that are right‐branching (SRC1, see (1) above). In this case, the movement of the relative subject is the same across conditions—therefore, controlling for the working memory demands within the relative clause. However, the insertion of the center‐embedded clause in SRC2 (2) means that the parser encounters two nouns before encountering the first (role‐assigning) verb. According to some theories (Frazier, 1979; Frazier & Rayner, 1982), this operation incurs a processing cost associated with temporarily holding available two incomplete phrasal structures in SRC2 that does not occur in SRC1. In other words, syntactic complexity can be understood as the number of incomplete parses that one must hold active until all dependencies can be resolved. We define our syntactic complexity contrast (SRC2 > SRC1, see Table 2) accordingly, with SRC2 being more complex than SRC1.

It is important to note that this definition of syntactic complexity is not one that is intuitively, or even theoretically, disentangled from theories of SynWM. The relationship between the subject noun and predicate seems to be a long‐distance dependency that relies on working memory resources just as filler‐gap dependencies do. This conflict of intuition is unresolved in the literature. In fact, as detailed and debated in Gibson (1998), some early theories (e.g., Phrase Structure Grammars) posit that the matrix subject−predicate relationship is “cost‐free”—an exception to locality‐based accounts of processing difficulty—because the parser is always expecting a main predicate as soon as it encounters a main subject. Given the ongoing development of these theories, we must conclude that, while our contrasts may be reasonably able to disentangle effects of domain‐general and domain‐specific resources, they are not necessarily clear‐cut dissociations (e.g., see Makuuchi, Bahlmann, Anwander, & Friederici, 2009). We will return to this topic in the General Discussion.

Music syntactic complexity and working memory

Early empirical evidence suggested that highly hierarchical musical sequences are processed more efficiently (Deutsch, 1980) and that listeners’ expectations toward global harmonic contexts within musical progressions could be manipulated with less expected but licit chords (e.g., a fourth harmonic degree following a full cadence, V‐I‐IV, as opposed to simply the more expected V‐I authentic cadence) (Bigand & Pineau, 1997). In our study, we manipulated musical syntactic complexity with different structures either resolving in an authentic cadence relative to the initial tonal context (first‐key ending, i.e., Return) or the new context (second‐key ending, i.e., Stay). This music design was an adaptation of the original design by Kunert et al. (2016). We interpreted Return structures as more difficult than Stay structures to resolve for closure judgments because they required maintaining a representation of the initial tonal center while processing the new key, “without being reinforced by incoming chords” (Kunert et al., 2016; p. 5), followed by a reintegration of the first key. See Figure 1 for schematics and Table 2 for descriptions.

Pivot chords (i.e., chords that are harmonically related to two keys) were used to establish new key contexts1; these were always the IV chord of the first key. Similarly, return chords that were used to reintroduce the original tonic context were always the V chord of the current (second) key. A sequence considered to resolve in the second key would have no return chord (i.e., Stay). Therefore, our SynSC contrast (Return > Stay) compared more complex (Return), similar to center‐embedded sentences, to less complex (Stay) structures, similar to right‐branching sentences. On the other hand, our SynWM contrast (Pivot‐Return distance) was defined within the same structure (Return only), comparing different distances between pivot and return chords, analogous to the linear‐distance SynWM contrast in language. With this operationalization of SynWM based on the distance between structurally related elements, an explicit parallel can be drawn between the linguistic and musical stimuli. While the language SynWM manipulation depends on filler‐gap distance between elements, the musical SynWM manipulation depends on the distance between the two pivot chords, which, respectively, mark the closing and reintroduction of the initial tonal context. In order for the chord's functional tonal category to be recognized upon the return, it must be represented in relation to the initial tonal context. This would require a representation of the initial tonal context to be maintained throughout the progression (including the second tonal context) and integrated with chords following the harmonic return. Therefore, while the musical stimuli lack a filler‐gap dependency, the music SynWM manipulation seems analogous to the linguistic manipulation, since the former involves a relation between functional tonal categories whose distance is manipulated.

The point of embeddedness in music (i.e., pivot chord) occurred at either the second, third, or fourth chord. Return chords were also varied and could occur at the sixth, seventh, or eighth chord position. It is important to note that, for the SynSC contrast, although both structures contain key pivots, only Return structures contain key returns, so this creates a slight imbalance of working memory load between structures. However, in Return structures, the short and long distances are averaged together, so their differential effects should be minimized.

Research questions

The study was guided by two research questions. The first question was, do language and music interact bidirectionally in the absence of syntactic structural violations, and to what degree are such potential interactions due to SynSC, SynWM, or both? To address this question, in both domains, we manipulated SynSC and SynWM as described above (see Figure 1 and Table 1). We first assessed (Models 1 and 2) whether there were any general effects of processing both language and music domains simultaneously (dual task) versus separately (single tasks). We then assessed, within the dual task only (Models 3 and 4), whether any cross‐domain interaction occurred preferentially at the level of SynSC or SynWM, or both.

The second research question was, do music and language interact at the junction of theoretically critical syntactic moments within the progressions and sentences? As noted by Kunert et al. (2016), several previous studies have demonstrated “impaired syntactic integration abilities in language if a concurrent musical tone or chord is difficult to harmonically integrate” (p. 2), which is consistent with SSIRH predictions about cross‐domain interactions (Patel, 2012). Although we avoided harmonic deviance because of saliency effects, our language and music sequences did include moments where the syntactic structure changed, specifically at the beginning of embedded phrases (the relativizer that in language; the pivot chord in musical progressions) and the end of embedded phrases (the main verb in language; the return chord in musical progressions) (see Figure 1 and Table 1). We interpreted these transitions as syntactically critical points because the onsets and offsets of embedding are what create distance, or nonlocal dependency, between structurally dependent elements which need to be integrated (Gibson, 1998, 2000; Lerdahl & Jackendoff, 1985). Given that syntactic integration can be impaired at moments of harmonic deviance, we hypothesized that syntactic integration may also be impaired or influenced by syntactically critical points. We tested this hypothesis with Models 5 and 6, where we expected that music and language judgments would be maximally impacted in trials where the critical points coincide across domains compared to when they do not coincide.

Methods

Participants

Fifty participants (n = 50) aged 18–45 (M = 21.20, SD = 4.60) were recruited via UCLA's SONA system. All participants identified English as their native language. People with prior expertise or training in musical theory or linguistics were excluded so that all participants were relatively naïve to the task design and theoretical motivations. Extensive training and practice with the tasks, especially the musical task and closure judgments, was provided to each participant. Participants were compensated with course credit and given detailed informed consent. Planned sample size was determined with G*Power (Faul, Erdfelder, Lang, & Buchner, 2007), using the repeated measures, within factors option (which assumes a mix of between‐subjects and within‐subjects effects, so, therefore, it is overconservative, but there is no option for completely within‐subjects tests). We estimated power of 0.96 with sample size (n = 57) for a 3×3 repeated‐measures ANOVA (the highest factorial model in our analyses) with the following parameters: effect size = 0.30 (considered medium ANOVA effect, Cohen, 1988), alpha = 0.01 (to adjust for multiple comparisons), power = 0.95, correlation among repeated measures = 0.50 (default), and nonsphericity correction = 1 (default). Thus, our obtained sample size (n = 50), given our fully within‐subjects design, should have sufficed to observe relatively small effects with high power and high significance threshold, and it is also similar to that of a previous similar dual task experiment (Kunert et al., 2016, Experiment 1).

Language stimuli

Four types of sentences were used for the language task (see Figure 1 and Table 1). These included three types of structures with embedded relative clauses (RCs), two subject‐extracted (SRC1, SRC2) and one object‐extracted (ORC), as well as one sentence complement construction with no extractions (SE). All sentences were eight words long and used the same words in different order to control for low‐level lexical differences across items. In general, sentences always had one level of embedding (signaled by the relativizer that), two noun (N) phrases, and two verb (V) phrases. The point of embedding (i.e., relativizer “that”) varied across sentence structure and could occur in the third or fourth word position in the sentence.2 The main verb in each sentence was semantically reversible, such that no noun‐verb pair was more congruent than another. The second verb was transitive and accompanied by either an adverbial or nominal object. All subject nouns were semantically animate.

In sum, sentence structures were selected on the basis of two key criteria: (a) embeddedness structure: whether the sentence was right‐branching (i.e., less syntactically complex because there is no nonlocal dependency between matrix subject and verb) or center‐embedded (i.e., more syntactically complex because of the nonlocal dependency);(b) types of extraction: whether the sentence had no extraction, a subject‐extraction, or object‐extraction.

Music stimuli

The musical sequences used in this experiment were adapted and modified from those used in Kunert et al. (2016). See Figure 1 for schematic details. Each chord was created separately with a piano and recorded with a microphone. The audio file for each Intro chord was fixed at 1500 ms duration, and each subsequent chord was fixed at 750 ms duration. Sequences always had a fixed length of nine chords: one Intro tonal major chord (preceding the first word of a sentence) to establish the tonality of the first key, and eight chords to match the eight words of our sentences. For music, each sequence began with the tonic chord of a major key followed by several tonally related chords within that same key before pivoting keys. This new musical context was established by using the IV chord of the original key to pivot into a new key in which it acted as the I chord. For example, an F chord being the IV in the context of C major was used to pivot to the key of F major, where it is the I, and was followed by a Bb chord, which is the IV chord of F major but is not diatonic to C major. It is also important to note that while the same eight words were used in different order for the language stimuli, it was not possible to manipulate the order of the same nine chords for the music stimuli. This is because return structures require additional chords that are not contained in the initial key, so the set of nine chords in Return versus Stay structures cannot be identical.

Thus, musical structures mimicked sentence structures in both the embedding and distance manipulations: (a) embeddedness structure: whether the progression was right‐branching (resolving in the second key) or center‐embedded (resolving in the first key);(b) distance of resolution: for progressions ending in the first/initial key, how many chords were in between the pivot and return locations.

Tasks

Task type (language, single task)

Each sentence trial began with a fixation cross (1500 ms) followed by a sentence presented word‐by‐word (750 ms/word) on an otherwise blank screen. After each trial, participants saw one of two types of comprehension probes, which were randomized, regarding the first or second subject−verb relationship (randomly selected and counterbalanced across sentence types) and were required to answer yes or no to whether the probe was congruent with the sentence. Participants were given up to 6 s to respond to the probe using a standard computer keyboard. Language performance was interpreted as a percent accuracy on the comprehension probes.

Task type (music, single task)

At the start of each trial, participants were presented with an initial fixation cross accompanied by an initial (tonal major) chord (1500 ms) used to establish the opening key for subsequent chords in the musical sequence. Participants then listened to the sequences chord‐by‐chord (750 ms/chord) while only a fixation cross was displayed. At the end of each trial, participants were asked to give a closure judgment rating (i.e., how well the final chord/section of the sequence matched or closed the earlier sections, based on Kunert et al., 2016) on a numeric integer scale (1 = low closure; 4 = some closure; 7 = high closure). Participants were given up to 6 s to respond to each trial.

Task type (dual task)

The dual task condition merged the two single tasks so that, on each trial, a word‐by‐word sentence and a chord‐by‐chord progression were simultaneously presented, requiring the participant to attend to and evaluate both. After each trial, participants were required to give comprehension answers and closure judgments in randomized order.

Task blocks and trials

Single and Dual tasks were each separated into three blocks (24 trials each, 72 trials in total) with the block order and trial order randomized. For language stimuli, 18 sentences of each type (72 total) were included per task and, for music stimuli, 36 progressions of each type (72 total) were included per task. All progressions were equally counterbalanced and nested for pivot and return place as well (12 trials per each pivot/return location). Finally, trials within each block were counterbalanced by all structural parameters varied in the stimulus designs.

Presentation

The experiment was designed using PsychoPy2 (Peirce et al., 2019) and experimentation was conducted in a quiet room. Visual stimuli were presented at a refresh rate of 60 Hz and auditory stimuli were presented using over‐ear headphones.

Practice session

After participants completed the Informed Consent procedure, they were given detailed task instructions and a practice session (with the exact same design used above) consisting of 16 unique trials in each of the three task conditions. Participants were encouraged to ask questions about the task and repeat the practice trials, if necessary, to ensure complete understanding.

Analysis

Dependent variables (DV)

Linguistic processing was assessed with mean accuracy on the sentence comprehension probes. Music processing was assessed with mean closure judgments. Higher closure judgments either indicated better task performance, as interpreted by Kunert et al. (2016), or alternatively worse performance depending on the degree to which musical processing was impaired by concurrent language processing. Regardless of interpretation, any direction of change in closure judgments, in the presence of language, would be informative for hypothesized cross‐domain interactions.

Independent variables (IV)

The following independent variables (IVs) were used in our Models: Task Type (Dual, Language, Music), Sentence Structure (SE, SRC1, SRC2, ORC), Music Structure (Return, Stay), Pivot Place (P2, P3, P4), Return Place (R6, R7, R8), and Pivot‐Return Distance.

Specific planned contrasts

See Table 2 for summary. SynSC was specifically tested in language by comparing the more complex SRC2 structure to the less complex SRC1 structure and in music by comparing the more complex Return structure to the less complex Stay structure. SynWM was specifically tested in language by comparing the more SynWM‐dependent ORC structure to the less SynWM‐dependent SRC2 structure and in music with the Pivot‐Return Distance as a continuous variable where higher distances between key pivots and returns indicate higher music SynWM demands.

Linear mixed models

All modeling was conducted in R (R Core Team, 2013). The advantages of linear mixed models (LMM) over general linear models (GLM) for within‐subject designs include no assumption of sphericity, accurate modeling of crossed or nested factors, and mixing of fixed and random effects (Bates et al., 2014; Magezi, 2015; Nimon, 2012). For each model, we specified experimental conditions as fixed effects and subjects as a random effect on the intercept. We initially also systematically tested, with rigorous procedures (Barr, Levy, Scheepers, & Tily, 2013; Bates et al., 2014, 2015), the inclusion of more complex random effects structures, but none of these models converged, perhaps due to the complexity of model design relative to the number of trials and subjects. When language accuracy was the DV, tests of interaction and main effects were conducted with generalized LMMs (GLMMs) fitting a binomial logit function and likelihood ratio tests for significance tests, whereas estimation of condition means and post hoc tests were conducted with nonbinomial models and Satterthwaite approximation. All post hoc tests within each model were corrected for multiple comparisons with the Tukey method. When music closure was the DV, LMMs were fit with the mixed function and significance testing was conducted with the mixed and emmeans functions (using the Satterthwaite method for p‐value estimation and with Tukey correction). All LMMs were performed using the mixed function (nmle4 package, Bates et al., 2014) and all post hoc tests with the emmeans and emtrends functions. Finally, as an additional check for the consistency of our findings, all LMM results were externally compared with results from repeated‐measures ANOVA models (using the aov_ez function), which yielded highly similar results. All data, code, and model comparisons are provided in an online GitHub repository: https://github.com/Micasheous/JCS_LangMusic_Analysis.git

Results

Research question 1

To address the first research question, whether language and music interact bidirectionally (i.e., whether the dual task imposes a cost on performance for each domain, compared to the single task) due to SynSC or SynWM, we tested the following seven models.

Model 1: Task Type (Language, Dual) X Sentence Structure (SE, SRC1, SRC2, ORC)

Model 1 (DV = language accuracy) tested whether language processing was modulated by cross‐domain interactions between language and music structure. See Figure 2 and Table 3 for the results. There was a significant interaction effect (Table 3, #1). There were interaction effects such that language accuracy was lower in the Dual task compared to the single Language task for SE (Table 3, #4), SRC1 (Table 3, #5), and SRC2 sentences (Table 3, #6), but not for ORC sentences (Table 3, #7). The interaction effect (Dual – Language) was much larger in SRC2 (dropping to practically chance accuracy) compared to ORC (Table 3, #10), SE (Table 3, #12), and SRC1 (Table 3, #13). There were no other significant differences of interaction effect between SE, SRC1, and ORC (Table 3, #8, #9, #11).

For the language SynSC contrast, without music (Language task), the more syntactically complex SRC2 sentences appeared much more difficult to understand than the SRC1 sentences (Table 3, #14). The results of this analysis indicate that the presence of music may have had no substantial effect on SynWM demands of language, since ORC performance was spared, despite it having greater SynWM demands than SRC2 sentences under our operationalization of SynWM (although see also alternative possible explanations in the Discussion).

Model 2a: Task Type (Music, Dual) X Music Structure (Return, Stay)

Model 2a (DV = music closure) tested whether music processing was modulated by cross‐domain interactions between music structure (i.e., music SynSC: Return vs. Stay) and language structure. See Figure 3 and Table 4 for the results. There was a significant interaction (Table 4, #1). Closure judgments were higher in the Dual task compared to the single Music task for the more complex Return structure (Table 4, #4) but were not different for the less complex Stay structure (Table 4, #5). In other words, there was an interaction of language with the music SynSC contrast such that, in the Music task without language, Return structures were lower than Stay structures (Table 4, #6), and this difference decreased in the presence of language in the Dual task (Table 4, #7). These results indicated that the presence of language appeared to influence the processing of music syntactic complexity.

Model 2b: Task Type (Music, Dual) X Music SynWM (Pivot‐Return Distance)

Model 2b (DV = music closure) tested whether music processing was modulated by interactions between music SynWM and language (Dual vs. Music). See Table 5 for the results. There was a significant interaction (Table 5, #1). In the Music task, there was a reliable effect (i.e., confidence intervals of the beta slope do not include zero) of music SynWM (Table 5, #4), such that closure judgments decreased as the distance between key pivots and returns increased. Thus, the effect of language appeared to modulate the working memory demand of music syntax.

Model 3a: Sentence Structure (SE, SRC1, SRC2, ORC) X Music Structure (Return, Stay)

Within only the Dual task, Model 3a (DV = language accuracy) tested if language processing was modulated by cross‐domain interactions between Sentence Structure (SE, SRC1, SRC2, ORC) and Music Structure (Return vs. Stay), while controlling for music Pivot Place. See Figure 4 and Table 6 for the results. There was no significant interaction (Table 6, #1), and no significant main effect of Music Structure (Table 6, #3). There was a significant main effect of Sentence Structure (Table 6, #2), when averaging Return and Stay trials, which was due to SRC2 sentences being more difficult to understand than SE (Table 6, #4), SRC1 (Table 6, #5), and ORC sentences (Table 6, #6). These results indicate that language comprehension was not substantially affected by interactions between syntactic structures of music and language (i.e., no interactions between sentence and music structures). However, syntactic complexity manipulations of language did impact language comprehension, which was expected given that increasing complexity of syntax usually increases difficulty of comprehension.

Model 3b: Sentence Structure (SE, SRC1, SRC2, ORC) X Music Structure (Return, Stay)

Within only the Dual task, Model 3b (DV = music closure) tested if music processing was affected by cross‐domain interactions between Sentence Structure (SE, SRC1, SRC2, ORC) and Music Structure (Return vs. Stay), while controlling for music Pivot Place. See Figure 5 and Table 7 for the results. There was a significant interaction (Table 7, #1). Closure judgments were lower for the more complex Return compared to the less complex Stay structures during SE (Table 7, #4), SRC2 (Table 7, #6), and ORC sentences (Table 7, #7), but not during SRC1 sentences (Table 7, #5). This music SynSC effect (i.e., Return – Stay) was greater for SRC2 sentences compared to SRC1 (Table 7, #8) and SE sentences (Table 7, #9), but not ORC sentences (Table 7, #10). The music SynSC effect was also greater for ORC sentences compared to SRC1 (Table 7, #11) and SE sentences (Table 7, #12), with no difference between SE and SRC1 sentences (Table 7, #13). These results indicate that, when making musical closure judgments in the presence of language, language SynSC interacts with music SynSC, because both SRC2 and ORC, which are the most syntactically complex (i.e., center‐embedded) sentences, showed larger musical structure effects than both SE and SRC1, which are the least complex (i.e., right‐branching). Conversely, language SynWM does not seem to interact with music SynSC since the more SynWM‐dependent ORC sentences showed similar music structural effects as the less SynWM‐dependent SRC2 sentences.

Research question 2

To address the second research question, whether language and music interact bidirectionally at syntactically critical moments within the progressions and sentences, we tested the following four models.

Model 5a: Sentence Structure (SE, SRC2, ORC) X Pivot Place (P2, P3, P4)

Within only the Dual task and controlling for Music Structure (Return, Stay), Model 5a (DV = language comprehension) tested if language processing was modulated by interactions between the critical syntactic moments of music (the key pivots, i.e., Pivot Place: P2, P3, P4) and language (when the pivots coincided with the relativizer that at the start of embedding for all sentences except SRC1, which did not coincide with key pivots). See Figure 6 and Table 10 for results. There was a significant interaction (Table 10, #1). For SRC2, the critical interaction at pivot P3 produced lower accuracy than P2 (Table 10, #7) and P4 (Table 10, #8), but no significant difference between P2 and P4 moments (Table 10, #9). There were no other significant differences between pivot places for SE (Table 10, #4−6) and ORC (Table 10, #10−12). These results indicate that some language syntactic processing (for only SRC2 sentences) was affected by cross‐domain interaction at the syntactically critical moment.

Discussion

Summary of methods

Modeled on Kunert et al. (2016), our study employed a cross‐domain, dual‐task paradigm in which participants listened to musical progressions while reading natural language sentences. Our approach was novel in that we manipulated both domains with respect to their SynSC and SynWM in order to better understand which aspects, if any, of syntactic integration or processing may be shared across domains.

For language, SynSC was defined in terms of branching, with right‐branching sentences (SE, SRC1) being simpler than center‐embedded sentences (SRC2, ORC). In contrast, language SynWM was defined in terms of the type of extraction featured in a sentence, ranging from no extraction (SE) to short‐distance (SRC1, SRC2) and long‐distance (ORC) extractions. For music, SynSC was similarly defined in terms of branching, with right‐branching progressions (Stay) being simpler than center‐embedded progressions (Return). Music SynWM was defined in terms of the linear distance (three, four, or five chords) between key pivots (which signal entry into a new key) and returns (which signal re‐entry into the original key) in Return structures only.

The task required responses for both music closure and language comprehension in dual trials to assess any degree of bidirectional interaction between domains. We also compared performance on the dual task to performance on single tasks where participants either listened to musical progressions or read sentences. In line with previous dual task paradigms on this topic, we reasoned that any degree of cross‐domain interaction would be reflected in a significant change (±) in closure agreements (e.g., Kunert et al., 2016) or comprehension scores, signaling a split pool of shared resources for processing competing stimuli.

Finally, and crucially, none of our stimuli featured any syntactic, semantic, or musical anomalies, in order to avoid potential confounds of attentional demands related to the saliency of the anomaly or repair of the violation instead of the intended manipulations (c.f. Fedorenko & Varley, 2016).

General discussion

As noted in Fedorenko and Varley (2016), “a number of behavioral dual‐task studies have also argued for language/music overlap on the basis of superadditive processing difficulty when musical violations coincided with syntactic violations in language” (p. 140). The primary goal of this study was to evaluate whether effects of cross‐domain interaction between music and language could be observed, even in the absence of these anomalies or violations, that may draw on relevant domain‐general (e.g., attentional) and/or domain‐specific (e.g., syntactic) resources. Moreover, our study was designed to evaluate whether coinciding critical (but not anomalous) moments in language/music structures would lead to such “supperadditive processing difficulties” (Fedorenko et al., 2009). Our results show strong interaction effects consistent with the hypothesis that language and music rely on shared computational resources that indicate an unbalanced yet bidirectional relationship between language and music based mostly on SynSC. However, as we discuss below, it is also possible that subjects engaged heuristic strategies to compensate for the increased cognitive load of dual task processing.

Evidence of a bidirectional, yet unbalanced, relationship between music and language

Our first research question asked whether language and music interact bidirectionally due to modulations of SynSC or SynWM. Models 1, 2a, and 2b compared performance from the dual task to the single task to assess the general impact of dual‐processing and the direction of effects. Model 1 (DV = language) showed that the intrusion of music significantly affected language processing for all sentences except ORC (which showed no difference between task types), but the interaction effect was driven by a drastic reduction in accuracy for SRC2 sentences, resulting in an apparent modulation of the language SynSC contrast (SRC2 > SRC1) more than the SynWM contrast (ORC > SRC2). Models 2a and 2b (DV = music) similarly revealed that the concurrence of language during music listening significantly modulated closure judgments for all structures. Specifically, the intrusion of language interacted with both music SynSC (Model 2a, closure ratings were modulated during only the more complex Return structures) and music SynWM (Model 2b, closure ratings were modulated by the key pivot‐return distance).

To further understand the directions and degrees of cross‐domain interactions, we ran a number of models with planned contrasts that break down our results along the dimensions of SynSC and SynSWM, as defined above. These models used data from the dual task only.

Model 3a revealed that processing more complex music structures (Return > Stay) did not significantly affect language performance in general and did not interact with different language structures. Similarly, Model 4a revealed that pivot‐return distance did not have any consistent pattern of impact on language or differential effects between language SynSC or SynWM contrasts (although there were differential effects between SE and SRC1 sentences, which also differ in syntactic complexity). Taken together, these results provide partial support for a view in which the effect of music on language processing is perhaps limited to more domain‐general resources such as attention (Fedorenko & Varley, 2016). Alternatively, these results are also consistent with an interpretation in which domain‐specific (syntactic) resources are allocated differently between language and music during dual processing (Kljajević, 2010). For example, it is possible that when engaging in the dual task, resources are allocated to spare linguistic processing over musical processing. If so, one might suspect an effect of language on musical processing that goes above‐and‐beyond a domain‐general hypothesis.

With regard to the effect of language on music processing, the results of Model 3b and Model 4b paint a picture consistent with this latter interpretation. Closure judgments were significantly impacted when processing syntactically complex sentences with equal SynWM demand (i.e., comparing SRC2 to SRC1), but did not significantly vary as a function of linguistic stimuli with increased working memory demands when controlling for SynSC (i.e., comparing ORC to SRC2). Moreover, the impact of increased language syntactic complexity was more pronounced for the more complex Return structures, such that closure judgments were lower when Return structures coincided with SRC2 in comparison to SRC1. This result is consistent with models of shared structural processing resources whereby processing more syntactically complex structures in both domains incurs higher processing costs than increasing domain‐general demands (Fedorenko et al., 2009).

In summary, our results suggest that, although generally bidirectional, much of the cross‐domain interaction appeared to be unbalanced, with manipulations of language structures exerting more influence over music processing rather than vice versa. Assuming a model of shared resources, the pattern of effects observed hints at the possibility that, under certain circumstances, language processing may take priority over music processing such that resources are unevenly distributed to spare linguistic performance.

Evidence of superadditive effects of structural processing in language and music

Our second research question asked whether any cross‐domain interactions occurred specifically when syntactically critical moments in both language (start and end of embedding) and music (key pivots and returns) coincided temporally.

The results of Model 5a through Model 6b again suggested an unbalanced yet bidirectional pattern of interaction, such that music processing was affected more by language than vice versa. With regard to the start of embeddings, when measuring the effect of music on language, Model 5a showed the predicted effect (i.e., modulation of accuracy when critical moments overlapped) for only SRC2 sentences (but not SE and ORC sentences). On the other hand, for the effect of language on music, Model 5b revealed critical moment effects for all sentence types (SE, SRC2, ORC). With regard to the end of embeddings, for the effect of music on language (Model 6a), the predicted effect was again observed only in SRC2 sentences, whereas for the effect of language on music (Model 6b), all sentence types showed interactive effects, with the predicted pattern present in the two most syntactically complex types (SRC2 and ORC).

In sum, it appears that overlapping critical moments had a stronger effect on musical processing than linguistic processing. That we were able to observe such effects given our subtle experimental manipulations and coarse measures of language comprehension and musical closure judgments (of the entire sentence and chord progression, respectively) could indicate the potential for these focal interactions to at least partially account for language−music interaction effects previously reported in the literature.

Interpreting effects of music on center‐embedded, subject‐extracted relative clauses

Despite the pattern of results being largely consistent with the predictions of hypotheses like the SSIRH (Patel, 2012), the results for SRC2 sentences stood out as a notable exception. While decreased accuracies for SRC2 sentences over SRC1 sentences are consistent with the predictions for our SynSC contrast, the direction of the effect between SRC2 and ORC sentences was surprising given the almost commonplace understanding that, in English, object‐extracted relative clauses incur more processing costs than their subject‐extracted counterparts (e.g., Gibson, 2000). If SRC2 sentences are generally “easier” to process, why were they impacted so strongly by our musical manipulation? One possible explanation for these results is that SRC2 items differed from all other sentence structures in that both verbs link to the subject noun. Some example sentences from Figure 1 are shown below: SRC2: The soldiers (NP1) [that ___NP1 saw (VP1b) the man (NP2)] fell down (VP1a). ORC: The soldiers (NP1) [that the man (NP2) saw (VP2) ___NP1] fell down (VP1).

SRC2: The soldiers (NP1) [that ___NP1 saw (VP1b) the man (NP2)] fell down (VP1a).

SRC2: The soldiers (NP1) [that ___NP1 saw (VP1b) the man (NP2)] fell down (VP1a).

ORC: The soldiers (NP1) [that the man (NP2) saw (VP2) ___NP1] fell down (VP1).

ORC: The soldiers (NP1) [that the man (NP2) saw (VP2) ___NP1] fell down (VP1).

Given that all sentences except SRC2 had a 1‐to‐1 subject‐to‐noun mapping, it is possible that the pronounced musical interaction effect on SRC2 comprehension accuracy would be due to participants applying more heuristic interpretation strategies that are based on minimal processing efforts. In other words, rather than actually parsing the sentence and all of its structural dependencies, it is possible that SRC2 sentences lent themselves to low‐effort linear mappings in which the first noun and first verb encountered were correctly matched, and the second noun and second verb were incorrectly paired. This would lead to parsing of the local phrases “the soldiers saw” and “the man fell down.” The fact that subjects were asked to respond to prompts like “the soldiers fell down” could further reinforce such a local interpretation.

This interpretation is consistent with “Good Enough” (GE) parsing models (Ferreira et al., 2002; Ferreira & Patson, 2007) which posit that, under certain conditions, “language comprehension is similar to the use of fast and frugal heuristics for decision‐making” (Ferreira & Patson, 2007, p. 71) and can lead to incorrect local representations that are inconsistent with (correct) global ones. One possible prediction from the GE framework is that participants’ comprehension scores for SRC2 should have been impaired on the misleading comprehension probes (e.g., the man fell down), and we found exactly that (M = 66.83%, SE = 2.37%). Another possible interpretation is that the GE framework would predict inaccuracy for SRC2 regardless of the comprehension probe, based on findings of similar inaccuracy without any directed comprehension probes, indicating the phenomenon of constructing local sentences may be robust and not simply due to comprehension questions that prompt biased recall (Ferreira & Patson, 2007).

A GE processing framework could also explain spared comprehension accuracy for ORC sentences in the presence of music. The GE processing approach endorses a minimal effort principle for initial processing, in which “the comprehension system tries to construct interpretations over small numbers of adjacent words whenever possible” (Ferreira & Patson, 2007). While GE processing accounts have not directly addressed ORC sentences, under this framework, the comprehension system could locally parse “the man saw,” as this is the first adjacent sequence of words for which a plausible local analysis could be applied. This would result in the interpretation of “man” as the argument of “saw.”

Ferreira and Patson (2007) argue that reprocessing strategies are deployed when a local analysis cannot be integrated with a sentence's global structure. In the case of ORC sentences, however, the local interpretation of “man” as the argument of “saw” is consistent with the global structure of the sentence, leaving “fell” as the argument of the NP “the soldiers.” Tabor, Galantucci, and Richardson (2004) propose a related Local Coherence account of sentence processing, in which “partial parses which are syntactically compatible with only a proper subpart of the input are sometimes constructed.” While there are subtle differences between the Local Coherence and GE processing accounts, both approaches maintain that local interpretations are constructed over plausible, short sequences of adjacent words. While ORC sentences do not contain the canonical N‐V‐N constructions (corresponding to agent‐action‐patient) described in Townsend and Bever (2001), they do contain a syntactically plausible sequence of words that could be interpreted with a local processing strategy. Therefore, a minimal effort parsing strategy consistent with GE processing could be applied to ORC sentences, ultimately yielding correct global interpretations for this sentence type.

Disentangling working memory from structural complexity

It is important to note that a number of different theories have been proposed to explain both the underlying representations of these sentence structures and the cognitive operations needed to successfully build and make sense of them. For example, unlike Frazier and colleagues, Gibson (1990) posits that the key contributor to processing difficulties is the number of nouns encountered that have not yet been assigned a thematic role. Under this framework, SRC2 sentences are more difficult to process than SRC1 sentences because two nouns are encountered before their roles can be assigned, while SRC1 only has one noun ahead of the role‐assigning verb. Others even posit that the increased complexity is due to the number of predicted structures held online (see Cruz Heredia, Dickerson, & Lau, 2022, for a recent review) or the distance between nonlocal but related constituents (Gibson, 2000). Thus, though the competing theories seem to all align in predicting higher complexity for SRC2 than SRC1, we cannot rule out the possibility that these costs may in fact tap into some amount of working memory resources (e.g., Makuuchi et al., 2009). Yet, whether this inability to disentangle domain‐general working memory from domain‐specific structure building processes is true to language or simply an artifact of our experimental (task) design remains an open question. Recent studies, employing more naturalistic (“task‐free”) paradigms, indeed suggest that working memory demands are primarily an effect of tasks that are outside of the parsing and interpretation process itself (Shain, Blank, Fedorenko, Gibson, & Schuler, 2022). Such investigations emphasize the need for researchers to identify, at the offset, what functions are being assumed as part of the linguistic interpretation process, and we believe that future research should work to incorporate such findings into their methodology.

Methodology matters: Developing new methods for studying music and language

An ongoing challenge for uniting language and music, and for cognitive neuroscience in general, concerns identifying specific neural structures that cause specific cognitive functions (Price & Friston, 2005). Thus, a core tenet of this study was to evaluate the viability of a dual‐task design free of syntactic or semantic violations or anomalies of any other nature for neuroscientific research. The paradigm developed herein builds on a broad literature and presents a novel opportunity for investigating the neural regions and networks that underlie musicolinguistic processing, without relying on the recruitment of brain regions and networks involved—but not unique—to these cognitive systems. While the results presented in this paper are from a behavioral task with no temporally‐sensitive measures, the fact that we were able to observe such effects given our subtle experimental manipulations and coarse measures of language comprehension and musical closure judgments presents an exciting opportunity for more advanced investigations using methodology capable of mapping time‐courses and sources for these quintessentially human cognitive processes.

Relevantly, recent studies (e.g., Asano, Boeckx, & Fujita, 2022; Fedorenko & Shain, 2021) have argued for different ways of interpreting and understanding resource allocation under varying loads and modalities (e.g., dual processing), primarily on the basis of brain‐level data. For example, Asano et al. (2022) posit that neural reuse mechanisms, such as those detailed by Matchin (2018), are a fruitful framework through which to interpret the “three decades of comparative research on language and music…” (p. 260) that are foundational to our study. We hope that future studies employing more sensitive measures are able to incorporate our methodology in disambiguating between different frameworks posited to underlie music and language processing.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, we did not include nonsyntactic manipulations, such as arithmetic or semantic garden‐path sentences (Kunert et al., 2016; Poulin‐Charronnat, Bigand, Madurell, & Peereman, 2005), to measure for potential interactions unrelated to syntactic processing, which is the basis of alternative theoretical accounts of music−language interactions (Large & Jones, 1999; Perruchet & Poulin‐Charronnat, 2013). However, we chose instead to focus on structural manipulations in the absence of violations, with the rationale that resulting cross‐domain interactions between these carefully controlled syntactic manipulations would not likely be due to general attentional resources. If that were the case, then all manipulations should have generated robust interaction effects in both domains; instead, we observed a pattern of bidirectional interaction in the dual task that was both relatively unbalanced between language and music and appeared to be based more on syntactic complexity than SynWM demands. However, we acknowledge that general interaction effects comparing between dual and single tasks are consistent with attentional resources accounts, which is why the cross‐domain interactions within specifically the dual task were the primary focus of our study.

Second, although we experimentally manipulated structures based on SynSC and SynWM, we acknowledge that both processes mutually intertwine during syntactic integration, thereby precluding a pure dissociation between them. This could be considered analogous to the synergy between syntax and semantics in English and many other languages (i.e., changes to one subprocess usually invoke changes to the other subprocess), which seem usually dissociated from each other in natural language involving complex sentence processing, although they can be richly studied. Moreover, it seems likely that using structure‐based manipulations for both music and language tasks may have contributed to observed cross‐domain effects in syntactic structure versus SynWM. With this caveat in mind, we attempted to tease out and interpret differential effects between SynSC and SynWM based more on relative differences rather than absolute separations. The pattern of bidirectional interaction between domains that we observed seemed to emerge mostly from interactions based more on SynSC (in both language and music) and less on SynWM. However, we suspect that both SynSC and SynWM subprocesses are interacting during cross‐domain interaction in complex ways too intricate to be observed with the current experimental methodology.

Third, we found that dual music processing impaired language processing for SRC2 sentences substantially more than all other sentences. Although this is consistent with our hypothesized SynSC contrast (SRC2 > SRC1), it is inconsistent with our SynWM contrast (ORC > SRC2) predictions. This inconsistency can be interpreted as signifying that language SynWM may not have been the relevant processing cost that was impacted by the musical task. We speculate that local heuristic compensation strategies, consistent with the GE processing framework whereby participants matched nouns and verbs one‐to‐one, could be an alternative cause for low SRC2 scores (see Section 4.2.3). If participants relied on these heuristics for the comprehension task, then we cannot rule out the possibility that the language SynSC contrast finding is likewise impacted by this heuristic effect. Though previous research has employed similar materials under concurrent tasks without finding similarly low SRC2 comprehension scores, without having previously planned a way to disentangle these hypotheses, we consider our results tentatively inconclusive, pending further research that directly addresses the use of these minimal effort strategies in concurrent language‐music processing.

Fourth, the task design also created some other potential limitations. There was an inherent difference in the naturalness or difficulty of the task, given the expectation that people usually read language for comprehension, but they may not as often be listening for closure in music. Although, from a predictive processing framework, listening for closure may be a central aspect of music cognition: “When listening to music we constantly generate plausible hypotheses about what could happen next, while actively attending to music resolves the ensuing uncertainty” (Koelsch, Vuust, & Friston, 2019). The resolution of uncertainty in a tonal harmonic context seems very similar to what Kunert et al. (2016) mean by closure. Nevertheless, we attempted to reduce this difference by first providing each participant with extensive training and practice in making closure judgments, but it is still likely that some participants were naturally, or learned to be, more sensitive to musical closure than others, which likely also contributed to individual differences in task difficulty. Relatedly, participants’ performance on both language and music tasks fluctuated in both dual‐ and single‐task conditions. This complicated the interpretation of observed cross‐domain interactions regarding the degree to which language demands were drawing from music resources, or vice versa, which would have been more interpretable if one of the tasks was performed at ceiling in the single‐task condition. Finally, although we did not analyze reaction time data, the issue of additivity (i.e., manipulation of one factor influencing performance independently of another factor) may still be an important limitation of the logic of the dual task paradigm because nonadditivity does not necessarily imply evidence for interacting effects (Stafford & Gurney, 2011; Sternberg, 2013).

Fifth, while closure might not be a normal goal for typical music listening, it was requested as an overt goal for our subjects, who in an initial practice session were instructed and trained to focus on closure judgments. Individual differences in fluency of making closure judgments thus likely contributed to the results in ways that we could not assess in this study.

Conclusion

This study contributes new evidence to the ongoing debate about the relation between language syntax and music syntax, which nestles into the larger unresolved mystery surrounding the potential interdependence between language and other hierarchically structured forms of thought (Monti, 2017). Our dual‐task approach, without syntactic violations, shows novel evidence for bidirectional, yet unbalanced, interactions between language and music, such that performance on a music task was impacted more by a concurrent language task than vice versa, which might indicate prioritization of language over music if resources are shared. For these cross‐domain interactions, the effects appeared prominent for trials in which critical moments coincided in the two domains, suggesting the potential for these focal interactions to at least partially account for other previously reported musicolinguistic interactions. We also discovered that cross‐domain interactions seemed to have been driven by processing demands associated with more complex syntactic structures, even when controlling for SynWM demands. While more work is needed to confirm this result, it is possible that—under the dual‐task conditions—subjects engaged noncompositional (“Good Enough”) parsing strategies to manage the superadditive effects of complex structural processing in both domains. Taken together, these bidirectional interactions between language and music provide novel evidence consistent with models of shared syntactic resources for processing language and music and/or shared representations of musical and linguistic structures. The methods employed in this study present an exciting opportunity for future research nestled at the intersection of these two quintessentially human cognitive systems.
