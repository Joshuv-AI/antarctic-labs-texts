Alcohol exposure promotes cell communication network 1 cleavage in esophageal adenocarcinoma cells exclusively
Authors: Wula Aladan, Baoxin Chi, Zhiheng Chang, Mizhu Wang, Tong Dang, Xianmei Meng, Jianyuan Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlcohol consumption has been a risk factor for more than 200 diseases, including esophageal cancer. Cell communication network 1 (CCN1), a matricellular protein, is highly expressed in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) but is barely detectable in esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC). Alcohol consumption has been identified as a major contributor to ESCC development, but its role in EAC is uncertain. This study examines the impact of acute (30 min) or prolonged (12 h) alcohol exposure (5, 100, and 500 mM) on CCN1 expression and function in esophageal epithelial cells, including normal (HEEC), ESCC (KYSE150 and KYSE410), and EAC (OE19 and OE33), in association with the activity of MMP2, MMP9, and MMP14. It was found that alcohol exposure promoted CCN1 expression in both normal and tumor cells but induced CCN1 cleavage exclusively in EAC cells, generating an 18‐kDa fragment that promotes tumor growth. MMP9, which was active only in EAC cells, was found to mediate this cleavage. Forced activation of MMP9 in either normal or ESCC cells improved cell viability, whereas inhibition of MMP9 in EAC cells attenuated cell survival. Taken together, alcohol exposure promotes MMP9‐mediated CCN1 cleavage in EAC, converting CCN1 from a pro‐death to a pro‐survival factor for EAC. This makes alcohol consumption a risk factor not only for ESCC development but also for EAC progression. BACKGROUND

Alcohol is one of the most commonly abused substances worldwide, especially in Eastern Europe and Eastern Asia. 1 Although Eastern Europe has made remarkable progress in cutting down alcohol consumption per capita in recent years, the situation in Eastern Asia has gotten worse, particularly in China, India, and Vietnam, where the alcohol market has expanded by 104% from 1990 to 2017. 2 Alcohol consumption is causally related to >200 diseases, including several types of cancer, for example, head/neck cancer, esophageal cancer, liver cancer, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and others. 3 , 4 , 5 As shown in the year 2020, nearly 4.1% of new cancer cases were attributable to alcohol consumption, among which esophageal cancer was at the top, responsible for 25.59% of all cases. 6

The majority of cases of esophageal cancer are diagnosed as either esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) or esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), in an approximately 9:1 ratio. ESCC development has been directly linked to alcohol consumption, particularly when the drinker is also a tobacco smoker. 7 , 8 , 9 In China, offering alcoholic beverages and cigarettes is customarily viewed as two inseparable friendly gestures. “It is not a banquet without alcohol served,” as the locals say. Therefore, alcohol and cigarettes are commonly provided at most social gatherings as a custom. This observation might explain why >50% of the global cases of esophageal cancer are found in this country. Despite the predominance of ESCC, EAC diagnoses have been rising consistently over the past few decades, 10 , 11 becoming a growing concern globally.

Cell communication network 1 (CCN1/Cyr61) is a matricellular protein that primarily supports cell adhesion, proliferation, and migration, playing a critical role in multiple physiological and pathological processes, such as angiogenesis, wound healing, inflammation, and tumorigenesis. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Although some cancers (e.g., breast cancer and pancreatic cancer) take advantage of CCN1 to facilitate tumor growth and metastasis, others (e.g., endometrial cancer and non‐small‐cell lung carcinoma) are suppressed by CCN1. Esophageal cancer is an excellent example. CCN1 is highly expressed in ESCC but barely detectable in EAC. Consequently, CCN1 has been found to promote ESCC tumor growth but induces EAC cell death by apoptosis. 16 , 17

Metalloproteinases (MMPs) are known to facilitate cancer cell invasion and metastasis by digesting the extracellular matrix (ECM). 18 Alcohol exposure has been documented to increase MMP2 and MMP9 expression and activity in various types of cells, such as hepatocytes, where it also promotes CCN1 expression. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 As a component of the ECM, CCN1 contains multiple MMP digestion sites. It can be cleaved by MMP2 or MMP14 into three fragments: 29, 18, and 8 kDa, among which the 18‐kDa form has been found to stimulate cell proliferation more potently than the full‐length CCN1 (42 kDa) in retinal endothelial cells. 23

This study compared CCN1 expression and function in esophageal cancer cells to those of normal epithelial cells in the context of alcohol exposure.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture, transfection, and reagents

Human EAC cell lines OE19 (RRID: CVCL_1622) and OE33 (RRID: CVCL_0471) (MilliporeSigma) and ESCC cell lines KYSE150 (RRID: CVCL_1348) and KYSE410 (RRID: CVCL_1352) (BeNa Culture Collection) were cultured in the Roswell Park Momerial Institute medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% Fetal bovine serum (FBS) (MilliporeSigma). The human esophageal epithelial cell line HEEC (RRID: CVCL_3702) (BeNa Culture Collection) was maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS. An equivalent number of cells was plated in 6‐well plates and cultured to 80% confluency for subsequent experiments. The cell cycle was then synchronized in the basal medium for 6 h before treatments. Cells were treated with 5‐, 100‐, or 500‐mM alcohol for either 30 min or 12 h. To inhibit MMP2 or MMP9 activity, cells were preincubated with 100 μM MMP2 inhibitor I (MilliporeSigma, #444244) or 100 μM MMP9 inhibitor II (MilliporeSigma, #444293), respectively, for 1 h before treatment with ethanol. To activate MMP9, 4‐aminophenylmercuric acetate (APMA) (MilliporeSigma, #A9563) was used at 1 mM. To knock down MMP9 or CCN1, cells were transfected with a pRS vector carrying an shRNA specifically against MMP9 (OriGene Technologies, #TR311436) or CCN1 (OriGene, TR313575) using Lipofectamine LTX PLUS (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's protocol. The control cells were transfected with the same vector carrying a negative sequence (5′‐TACCAGAGCTAACTCAGATAGTACT‐3′). Assays were done 48 h after transfection. All the cell lines used in this study were authenticated periodically and have not been previously reported as misidentified or contaminated, including mycoplasma contamination. The cells were replaced every five passages with new stocks supplied by the manufacturers to ensure their authenticity.

Cell‐free CCN1 cleavage detection assay

Recombinant MMP9 (rMMP9) (R&D Systems, #911MP) was diluted to 100 μg/mL in assay buffer (50 mM Tris, 10 mM CaCl2, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Brij 35, pH 7.5). To activate MMP9, APMA was added to a final concentration of 1 mM. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 24 h and then further diluted to 0.4 μg/mL in the assay buffer. Recombinant full‐length CCN1 (OriGene, #TP750169) was diluted to 0.5 μg/mL in the assay buffer. The active MMP9 solution was combined with the CCN1 solution in a 1:1 ratio and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. The reaction products were boiled in the sample loading buffer for 5 min, separated by Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE), and stained with Coomassie blue R‐250 solution (MilliporeSigma).

Protein isolation and Western blotting

After treatment, total protein was extracted from cells using a modified radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 1% NP‐40. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 4°C, 10,000 × g for 10 min. Conventional Western blot analysis was performed. Briefly, equivalent amounts of protein samples (30–50 μg each) were resolved by SDS‐PAGE at 100 V and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane at 390 mA for 90 min. Subsequently, the membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with a primary antibody for 2 h at room temperature on a shaker. After three 10‐min washes with Tris Buffered Saline with Tween‐20 buffer, the membrane was incubated with an appropriate HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) on a shaker for 1 h. The signal was developed in enhanced chemillumunescence solution (Amersham Pharmacia), and images were captured and quantified using Gel Doc XR (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). β‐Actin was used as the loading control. The data were collected from multiple independent experiments and analyzed for statistical significance. The following primary antibodies were used: CCN1 (OriGene, #TA349858), Cyr61 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐271217), Cyr61 (LSBio, #LS‐C392953), MMP2 (OriGene, #TA378583), MMP9 (OriGene, #TA378586), MMP14 (OriGene, #TA332551), and β‐actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc69879). Recombinant human MMP2 (Boster, #PROT08253 and #PROT08253‐4), MMP9 (Novus, #911‐MP and 11602‐MP), and MMP14 (OriGene, #TP308917) were used in Western blotting to determine the sizes of the targets.

Zymography

Gelatinase activity was assessed using cell lysates and conditioned cell culture media. Briefly, protein samples in nonreducing sample buffer (0.4 M Tris, pH 6.8, 5% SDS, 20% glycerol, 0.03% bromophenol blue) were separated on 10% SDS‐PAGE gels containing 1 mg/mL gelatin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After electrophoresis, gels were washed in 2.5% Triton X‐100 with gentle shaking for 30 min and then incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the developing buffer (40 mM Tris‐HCl, 0.2 mM NaCl, 6.67 mM CaCl2, 0.1% Triton X‐100, pH 7.8). Next, the gel was incubated overnight in fresh developing buffer at 37°C. Next, the gel was stained for 2 h in a 0.5% Coomassie blue R‐250 solution containing 10% acetic acid and 40% methanol and then destained by washing with a 45% ethanol and 10% acetic acid solution three times (15 min per wash). The gel was kept in a 5% methanol and 0.75% acetic acid solution for storage and imaging. The active MMPs appeared as white bands at the appropriate molecular weight marker on a dark blue background.

Cell viability assay

Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK‐8; MilliporeSigma, #96992) assays were used to assess cell viability after the indicated treatments according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, an equivalent number of cells was distributed per well in a 96‐well plate at 100 μL/well. The plate was incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 24 h and then switched to the serum‐free medium for 6 h. After the indicated treatments, 10 μL of CCK‐8 reagent was added to each well of the cell culture. The plate was then placed back in the incubator for a further 4 h. Cell viability was measured using a plate reader at 450 nm.

Animal study

The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Baotou Medical College (BMC2021‐81960445DS) and performed following the ARRIVE Guidelines for the use of animals in experiments. A total of 10 male Sprague–Dawley rats with body weights ranging from 225 to 250 g were purchased from Saibainuo Biotech Company. The animals were allowed to acclimatize to the conditions at the facility (20–22°C, 60%–80% relative humidity, 12‐h light/dark cycle) for 7 days before the experiment commenced. Animals were fasted overnight, and the following day, 5 rats were randomly selected to drink 40% ethanol instead of water every other day. The ethanol concentration used was based on being comparable to that of regular liquor. The remaining 5 rats were given regular water as a control. The animal health was monitored daily by the facility staff. No animals were found dead during the experiment. All rats were euthanized 4 weeks later by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital at 200 mg/kg body weight, followed by exsanguination. The esophagus was removed for histological study. This method is consistent with the ARRIVE recommendations.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue specimens were fixed in 10% formalin at room temperature overnight and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, and antigen retrieval was performed in a microwave oven by pause heating. Slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for regular histological analysis or incubated with the primary antibody of interest for 2 h in a humidified chamber. After 3 × 10 min washes in phosphate‐buffered saline, signals were developed using an LSAB+ kit (Agilent Technologies) containing a universal secondary linker and HRP‐conjugated streptavidin with aminoethyl carbazole chromogen as the substrate. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Statistical analysis

All numerical data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation and were analyzed using single‐classification one‐way ANOVA. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

RESULTS

Alcohol consumption induces esophagitis and CCN1 overexpression

To determine how alcohol consumption affected esophageal CCN1 expression in vivo, drinking water for the experimental group of rats was replaced with 40% ethanol every other day for 4 weeks. This ethanol concentration was equivalent to that of common liquors on the market. The control group was given regular water. As shown in Figure 1, alcohol consumption resulted in severe inflammation in the esophagus, characterized by epithelial thickening and elongation of the lamina propria into the epithelium. Along with the development of esophagitis, epithelial CCN1 expression was highly upregulated (Figure S1).

Alcohol exposure upregulates CCN1 expression in both normal and cancerous esophageal cells but increases CCN1 cleavage only in EAC cells

To determine the mechanisms behind the CCN1 upregulation in alcohol‐induced esophagitis, we performed in vitro experiments using esophageal epithelial cells, including normal (HEEC), ESCC (KYSE150 and KYSE410), and EAC (OE19 and OE33). The following three concentrations of ethanol were used in our experiments: 5 mM, 100 mM, and 500 mM. The experimental conditions were selected based on the consideration of alcohol intoxication in humans. The definition of driving under the influence of alcohol varies from country to country. A driver can be arrested in the United States when their blood alcohol content (BAC) is >80 mg/100 mL (or 17.36 mM), whereas in China, this limit is 20 mg/100 mL (or 4.34 mM), and in India, it is 40 mg/100 mL (or 8.68 mM). However, it is considered potentially fatal when the BAC is >400 mg/100 mL (or 86.8 mM) regardless of geographical region. Some extreme cases are documented in the literature, among which individuals have survived with a BAC >1500 mg/100 mL (∼250 mM).

After a 6‐h synchronization in serum‐free media, cells were incubated in the ethanol‐containing medium for 30 min or 12 h. Total protein was extracted from the cells to examine CCN1 expression. As shown in Figure 2 and Tables 1, 2, 3, full‐length CCN1 (42 kDa) was upregulated in all three cell types, whereas an 18‐kDa isoform of CCN1 was found only in EAC cells and substantially increased in response to alcohol exposure.

Alcohol exposure promotes MMP9 activation in EAC cells only

CCN1 has been previously reported to be a substrate of MMP2 and MMP14, and both cleave CCN1, producing an 18‐kDa peptide. 23 We wondered whether the 18‐kDa fragment seen in EAC cells was the same one reported in the literature and how alcohol exposure affected the expression of these two MMPs. Because MMP9 shares many characteristics with MMP2, we included MMP9 in the study as well. Based on the amino acid composition, the molecular weights of MMP2, MMP14, and MMP9 are predicted to be 74 kDa (MMP2/pro), 66 kDa (MMP14/pro), and 78 kDa (MMP9/pro), respectively. However, due to post‐translational modifications, they often appear larger in SDS‐PAGE. These enzymes become active by removing an ∼10‐kDa fragment from the N terminus. As shown in Figure 3, MMP2 was found to be constitutively active in all three types of cells, particularly in ESCC, where active MMP2 was the only detectable form, regardless of alcohol exposure (Figure 3C,D). MMP9, on the other hand, was activated only in EAC cells, especially in the presence of alcohol (Figure 3E,F). It coincided with the increase in CCN1 expression and cleavage observed above (Figure 2E,F), making us wonder whether CCN1 in EAC cells might have been cleaved by MMP9, rather than MMP2, because MMP14 expression was undetectable in any cell lines used in this study. Zymography confirmed the exclusive activation of MMP9 in EAC cells after alcohol exposure (Figure 4A). Similar results were seen in vivo, where MMP9 was inactive in the esophageal epithelium regardless of alcohol uptake (Figure S1).

MMP9 mediates alcohol‐augmented CCN1 cleavage

The full‐length CCN1 consists of 381 amino acids across four domains: insulin‐like growth factor binding protein (IGFBP), von Willebrand factor Type C (VWC), thrombospondin Type 1 (TSP1), and C‐terminal cysteine‐knot region (CT). Based on a previous study, 23 MMP2 and MMP14 can both cleave CCN1 between the VWC and TSP1 domains, generating an 18‐kDa functional peptide containing the IGFBP and VWC domains (Figure 4B). To determine whether the CCN1 fragment seen in EAC cells was the one reported previously, we used two antibodies to detect CCN1: LS‐C392953 targeting amino acids 143–174 (VWC region) and sc‐271217 targeting amino acids 345–381 (CT region). As shown in Figure 4C, after cells were exposed to alcohol for 30 min, LS‐C392953 detected both forms of CCN1 (42 and 18 kDa), whereas sc‐271217 was only able to detect the full‐length CCN1 protein, indicating that the CCN1 cleavage site in EAC cells was very likely the same one recognized by either MMP2 or MMP14. 23

To clarify whether MMP2 or MMP9 was responsible for CCN1 cleavage in EAC cells, we incubated the cells with a specific MMP9 inhibitor (MMP9 inhibitor II) before alcohol exposure. As shown in Figure 4D, the 18‐kDa form of CCN1 completely disappeared in the presence of the inhibitor. However, when the same experiment was repeated using an MMP2 inhibitor, the 18‐kDa fragment remained (Figure 4E). The role of MMP9 in CCN1 cleavage was also demonstrated by knocking down MMP9 in OE19 cells (Figure 4F). To verify this result, we used a cell‐free system in which a full‐length recombinant CCN1 protein was incubated with rMMP9 at 37°C for 2 h in the presence or absence of APMA, a potent MMP9 activator. As shown in Figure 5A, in the presence of APMA, the CCN1 protein was cleaved into two bands, 18 and 24 kDa. Taken together, these results provide evidence that CCN1 in EAC cells was cleaved by MMP9 rather than MMP2, but likely at a site common to both, between the IGFBP and VWC domains.

Because MMP9 was found to be inactive in both normal (Figure 3A,B) and ESCC cells (Figure 3C,D), and CCN1 was not cleaved in these cells either (Figure 2A–D), we decided to test our hypothesis again using these cells. To do so, we incubated KYSE150 and HEEC cells with APMA before alcohol exposure. As shown in Figure 5B (HEEC) and Figure 5C (KYSE150), activation of MMP9 in either normal or ESCC cells resulted in CCN1 cleavage, confirming that activation of MMP9, rather than MMP2, was responsible for the generation of the 18‐kDa form of CCN1 in esophageal cells.

CCN1 cleavage benefits esophageal cell survival

The 18‐kDa fragment of CCN1 was previously reported to be a more potent stimulator for endothelial cell proliferation. 23 We wondered whether CCN1 cleavage in EAC cells also had this property. For this purpose, we treated cells with 100 mM alcohol for 30 min or 12 h in the presence or absence of the MMP9 inhibitor in 96‐well plates and assessed cell proliferation by CCK‐8 assays. As shown in Figure 6A, OE19 cell viability was increased in response to alcohol exposure but was attenuated by MMP9 inhibition (MMP9−), suggesting that MMP9 activity was critical for EAC cell survival in the presence of alcohol. To confirm the role of CCN1 cleavage in the improved EAC cell viability upon alcohol exposure, OE19 cells were transfected with shRNA against CCN1 and then exposed to alcohol. As shown in Figure 6B, CCN1 deficiency attenuated cell viability, suggesting that CCN1 availability was essential for EAC cell survival in the presence of alcohol. Because MMP9 was inactivated in both normal (Figure 3A,B) and ESCC cells (Figure 3C,D), we added APMA, the MMP9 activator, to the culture medium of KYSE150 and HEEC cells before alcohol treatment. As shown in Figure 6C,D, activation of MMP9 significantly improved the viability of both normal and ESCC cells (p < 0.05), indicating that the 18‐kDa CCN1 fragment facilitates esophageal cell growth.

DISCUSSION

CCN1 has been one of our primary research targets for years, with a particular focus on its role in the digestive system 16 . To date, we have found that CCN1 expression is generally low in the epithelial linings of the gastrointestinal tract under physiological circumstances but shoots up tremendously during injury (e.g., ulcers) or after exposure to hazardous insults (e.g., acid and alcohol) and stays high until the condition improves, after which it returns to low expression levels. During this process, CCN1 functions as a mediator, defending epithelial integrity and restoring tissue homeostasis by regulating cell proliferation/migration and interacting with other matrix molecules. EAC development is such a good example. The primary cause of EAC is gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), which initially induces esophagitis and then leads to esophageal metaplasia (Barrett’s esophagus) if the refluxate contains bile. Barrett’s esophagus is a precancerous condition of EAC. During the process of tumorigenesis, epithelial CCN1 in the esophagus rises to very high levels at the beginning (esophagitis) and declines gradually if the GERD episodes continue but still remains higher in the metaplastic esophageal epithelium than in normal tissue. Once EAC occurs, however, CCN1 essentially vanishes from the malignant esophageal mucosa. 16 , 17 In other words, CCN1 must go down for EAC to grow. Compared to EAC, ESCC does not downregulate CCN1 expression; instead, it upregulates CCN1 to support its tumor growth and metastasis.

Metastasis is a significant challenge of any cancer, and it requires the participation of MMPs to dissolve the ECM, allowing cancer cells to escape from the initial tumor and invade other organs. There are 23 MMPs in humans, which can process a variety of bioactive molecules, including cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, receptors, and ECM components. The functions of these MMPs, however, are highly redundant. For instance, MMP1, MMP8, and MMP13 all cleave collagens at one specific site. The two gelatinases, MMP2 and MMP9, also share several characteristics, both preferring to cleave between glycine and hydrophobic residues within a hydroxyproline bracket. In most organs, MMP2 is constitutively expressed, whereas MMP9 is inducible, and both degrade gelatin and remodel the ECM during wound healing, inflammation, and cancer invasion. They are abundantly expressed in the gastric epithelium, 24 , 25 liver fibrosis, 20 and lung tissue 26 after alcohol consumption.

Based on PROSPER analysis, the full‐length CCN1 protein contains at least 10 potential cleavage sites for MMPs and thus can be cleaved by multiple MMPs theoretically. 23 MMP2 27 and MMP14 28 have been reported to cleave CCN1 at the same site between the VWC and TSP1 domains, both producing an 18‐kDa fragment that promotes cell proliferation and migration. This cleavage product was also seen in our study, exclusively in EAC cells, but was not associated with either MMP2 or MMP14 activity. Instead, it was produced by MMP9 activation. Based on analysis using antibodies targeting the VWC and CT regions individually, this cleavage likely occurs between amino acids 207 and 219 (PVFGTEPRVLFNP), the hinge region between the VWC and TSP1 domains. In agreement with the previous study, 23 our study also showed that expression of this 18‐kDa fragment of CCN1 benefited cell growth, suggesting that alcohol exposure can convert CCN1 from an anti‐EAC agent into a pro‐EAC agent. Based on this notion, drinking alcohol not only increases the risk of ESCC development but also likely advances EAC progression.

Alcohol consumption is a leading risk factor for global diseases and death. According to the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study, there were 100.4 million cases of alcohol‐related diseases and 3 million alcohol‐related deaths in 2016, 1 , 29 and the incidence continues to grow year after year. According to a recent investigation, 30 >50% of males in China can be classified into the hazardous or harmful drinking categories, and this trend is predicted to grow by another 17% in the next few years. 2 This is reflected not only by the growing drinking population but also, more significantly, by the quantity of alcohol consumed, which grows 3 times faster than the rise in the drinking population. No doubt, increased alcohol uptake increases the overall risk of death. According to a theoretical analysis, if alcohol drinking can be completely stopped by 2030, the incidence of cancer in China alone would drop by 11.49% for males and 1.00% for females, among which esophageal cancer incidence would decrease by 25.78%. 30 That would be a theoretical reduction of 5,382,668 cases of cancer.

We are fully aware of the limitations of this study. First of all, it is primarily based on cell culture, and therefore, the findings may not reflect the true esophageal condition after alcohol consumption. There are no animal models currently available for alcohol‐induced esophageal cancer. The purpose of the animal component in this study was to learn how alcohol consumption changes esophageal expression of CCN1 in vivo, thereby establishing the basis for the rest of the in vitro work. To fill the gap from bench to bed, studies using clinical specimens are inevitable. Meanwhile, a viable animal model can also be useful for elucidating the molecular connections between alcohol consumption and esophageal cancer. Secondly, some mysteries that arose from this study remain troubling. For example, why is MMP9 active only in EAC cells, especially after alcohol exposure, but not in ESCC or normal esophageal epithelial cells? A digestive cut in CCN1 by MMP9 should supposedly produce two fragments; why is the 18‐kDa fragment the only product detectable in cells, whereas two fragments (18‐kDa and 24‐kDa) are visualized in a cell‐free setting? Investigation continues.

CONCLUSIONS

This study finds that alcohol exposure promotes MMP9‐mediated cleavage of CCN1 in EAC cells exclusively, thereby benefiting tumor cell survival. According to our previous studies, 16 , 17 overexpression of CCN1 in EAC cells leads to apoptosis. In contrast, this study shows that alcohol exposure can change the nature of CCN1, converting it from a pro‐death to a pro‐survival factor for EAC. This makes alcohol consumption a risk factor not only for ESCC development but also for EAC progression.
