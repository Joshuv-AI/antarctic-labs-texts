Hydrogeological deciphering of the poorly gauged karstic Mount Hermon Aquifer
Authors: Elad Ben-Zur, Haim Gvirtzman, Gideon Gal, Yaron Be’eri-Shlevin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Poorly gauged basins characterize most of the world’s aquifers and pose considerable challenges to hydrologists globally. A lack of data on water-table elevation, spring discharge and meteorological data limit our ability to assess and study historical, current, and possible future groundwater flows and spring discharges. These limitations, and others, severely hinder our ability to better understand and sustainably manage water resources, especially under a changing climate. We use the trans-boundary Hermon Jurassic Aquifer (HJA), which supplies substantial amounts of groundwater to Syria, Lebanon and Israel, as a case study to showcase an approach to address such challenges. To overcome the large data gap, we constructed a conceptual hydrogeological framework of the HJA along with numerical modeling, using only limited spring discharge data. Our groundwater flow and solute transport modeling reproduce with notable accuracy the transient hydrographs of the main springs over several decades and the main spatial sulphate composition distribution. Furthermore, our models define recharge areas and storage volumes for each spring, delineate groundwater flow-paths, constrain the spatial and temporal HJA dynamics, and explain the cause for varying spring chemistry. We, thus, show the potential of this non-conventional approach to study poorly gauged aquifers worldwide, especially karstic and phreatic regions, and how significant hydrological insights can be revealed, overcoming lacking hydrological information.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29454-9.

Introduction

Ungauged or poorly gauged aquifers are common worldwide1,2, especially in arid or remote regions and in developing countries3, where monitoring infrastructure is limited4 and especially where it is a trans-boundary aquifer5. Such aquifers pose crucial challenges when they are the primary water source for human populations and ecosystems2. The triple-key hydrological variable components traditionally considered mandatory for groundwater assessment and management are (a) recharge rates (precipitation minus evapotranspiration and runoff), (b) aquifer water levels, and (c) discharge (springs and wells) rates. Unfortunately, these variables are partially or completely missing in many cases. Facing such challenges, it is common in the hydrological community to combine climate reanalysis6, remote sensing7,8, hydrogeological modeling9,10 and field surveys11, and to depend on many assumptions and approximations about the aquifer boundaries12, porous media characteristics13, precipitation amounts and infiltration ratio14.

Numerical models of groundwater flow and solute transport provide reliable tools for predicting changes in water source availability under climate changes or various management scenarios15,16. Usually, the construction of these models involves the development of a conceptual model, which describes the principal behavior of the hydrological system under justified assumptions17. However, the construction and applications of these models require the collection of many hydraulic and chemical variables over the course of years based on extensive monitoring systems.

The trans-boundary Hermon Jurassic Aquifer (HJA) extends over areas in Syria, Lebanon and Israel in the Levant (Fig. 1a) and is a significant water source to these countries. It is, however, also an intriguing and challenging hydrological case study, as data for the recharge (meteorological stations) and water levels (observation wells) are actually non-existent and information about aquifer hydraulic properties is limited18. The region’s political conflicts make it even more difficult, with non-existent cooperation, as often takes place in other trans-boundary waterbodies worldwide19. Even critical data that has already been measured, especially direct springs discharge and chemistry, is not shared: thus, the study had to rely on indirect data for the parts outside of Israel. Here, we present a new approach, combining a conceptual hydrogeological framework of the HJA and hydrological modeling focused on the limited spring discharge data. In fact, we applied three numerical modeling steps to decipher the groundwater flow field and solute transport within the HJA. The direct local aim of this study was to construct, for the first time, the structure, water levels, flow paths, and main springs hydrographs of the HJA, considering that critical parts of the water balance data were missing. Following this, the study aimed to present the potential for the study of other ungauged aquifers worldwide with similar characteristics as the HJA.

Mount Hermon Jurassic Aquifer

Mount Hermon (also named Jabal al-Shaykh), located at the triangular border between Syria, Lebanon and Israel in the Levant, is an elongated narrow mountain range, 70 km long and 15 km wide, whose crest lies along a northeast-southwest trajectory, with a peak height of 2,814 m asl. The high Hermon anticline experiences uplifting since the Miocene by strengthened compression due to lateral movement along the active Dead Sea Transform; thus, it also contains longitudinal and transversal faults22. Due to intensive erosion, Jurassic carbonate formations are exposed throughout the mountain (Fig. 1b). The climate at Mount Hermon is Mediterranean type, with warm rainless summers, and chilly, rainy and often snowy winters. Intense current and future climate change has been observed23 and predicted24, exhibiting depletion of the water resources25. Rainwater and snow-melt water infiltrate downward through a thin poorly vegetated soil26, and a well-developed karst network27, which is typical for limestone formations28, feeding springs at the base of the mountain.

The total discharge of the HJA groundwater is estimated to be 693 million cubic meters per year (MCM/y) on average (Table 1). The largest portion (45%) discharges to the lowest drainage sites in Israel, namely at the Dan (232 MCM/y) and Banias and Kezinim (i.e., ‘Banias group’; 78 MCM/y) springs29. The second largest portion (29%) discharges into Syria, namely at the Awaj group30 (104 MCM/y) and Barada31 (98 MCM/y) springs. The smallest portion (26%) discharges to the Hasbani (38 MCM/y) and Wazzani (58 MCM/y) springs and a few smaller springs21,32 (85 MCM/y) in Lebanon. About one half of the spring-water that discharges in Lebanon, along with the entire volume of spring-water discharged in Israel, form the sources of the Jordan River which flows southwards to Lake Kinneret (Sea of Galilee), the largest freshwater lake in the Middle East and a key freshwater resource for Israel.

The springs discharge stem from the same aquifer and display a similar seasonal pattern with an increase in discharge rates during the rainy winter and spring seasons and a decrease during the dry summer and fall seasons. The differences, however, in their hydrological behavior are puzzling for three reasons: (a) the timing of their peak flow, (b) the differences in their hydrological memory, and (c) the differences in their sulphate concentrations. The Dan and Banias springs for example, for which long-term records exist, differ in the timing of their peak flows such that the peak discharge rate lags after the peak rainfall by about two months in the Banias Spring and by about three months in the Dan Spring33. Second, the Banias Spring has no hydrological memory, and thus even after abundant rainfall years, discharge rates decrease in the following summers to a minimum similar to the behavior during dry years. In contrast, the Dan Spring has a hydrological memory, and therefore after abundant-rainfall years, discharge rates may increase for a sequence of 5–7 years without significantly diminishing during the summers (Fig. 3a). Finally, sulphate concentrations in the Banias springs group are an order of magnitude higher than those in the other springs (Fig. 3b). These phenomena create a hydrological puzzle that has intrigued researchers for decades18,21,26,34,35.

Some researchers suggested a larger underground storage volume for the Dan Spring and smaller one for the Banias Spring18,34, but they neither drafted maps of the two storages nor analyzed them quantitatively. Others suggested heterogeneous karst networks for the two springs35. Recently, several hydrologists introduced numerical flow models for a few corners of the aquifer33,36, but there has been no application of a model for the entire system and thus, for example, conceptual constant head boundaries were assumed in extensive parts of the aquifer. Indeed, as expected, it is challenging to quantitatively evaluate and calibrate numerical models for such a poorly gauged basin.

Hydrogeological framework of the HJA

We hypothesized that the HJA can be viewed as a huge container-like reservoir that is sealed to the sides and bottom by impervious walls and floor (Fig. 4). Thus, infiltrating recharge water spills over the walls at their lowest points, i.e., creating the springs. We further hypothesized that groundwater level is almost horizontal in the northern and central parts of the aquifer. This is due to the well-developed karst system, characterizing the Jurassic carbonates27, as shown for other karstic aquifers39,40. The high hydraulic conductivity of the karstic carbonates leads to low hydraulic gradients, i.e., an almost flat water-table. Therefore, we estimated the groundwater levels in the northern and central parts of the aquifer based on the elevations of springs along the eastern, northern and western flanks of the structure.

The HJA is bordered in the south by the uplifted sealed base of the aquifer at the anticline crest21 (Late Triassic gypsum formation). This uplift also causes the thickness of the saturated zone to taper to zero in the middle of the aquifer (Supplementary material, Figure S1c,d,f). Because of the strong inclination of the layers southward, groundwater flow is possible only via two narrow flow paths along the eastern and western sides of the buried divide. Along these routes, the water-table levels drop over a relatively sharp gradient towards the low-elevated southern springs, Dan, Banias, Wazzani and Hasbani.

Considering the lack of in-situ precipitation records nor evaporation data to calculate recharge18, we calculated the groundwater balance using the outputs, rather than inputs. Thus, we argue that, on average, 693 MCM/y infiltrate into the aquifer. Furthermore, we ignore possible small leakages, for example toward the Damascus Basin and the Lebanon Valley or elsewhere41, as the groundwater in most confined parts is nearly stagnant26. We argue that it makes no difference if the total recharge and discharge are ~ 700 MCM/y, or if the total recharge is, say, ~ 730 MCM/y and there is a leak of an additional ~ 30 MCM/y at various points in the perimeter that are not monitored. In both cases, the same flow field is created in the phreatic region. Therefore, for practical reasons, for the numerical model runs, we assumed that the aquifer is sealed to flows in its confined parts and, thus, the relevant multi-year water balance is equivalent to the spring outputs, namely 693 MCM/y. We ran the numerical models in two modes, starting with a steady-state mode with the described water balance, followed by a transient mode. The latter considered temporal monthly recharge changes, based on trends from meteorological data collected south and outside the aquifer (Fig. 1a). For both modes of the models, the recharge spatial distribution was determined according to the aquifer surface elevation (Fig. 5; Methods).

Results

Steady-state groundwater flow modeling

Based on the conceptual hydrogeological model and associated assumptions, the calibration of the steady-state flow model achieved an excellent fit to all the observed springs’ discharges. The maximum absolute percentage error between the annual mean observed and simulate discharge was 1.3%, and a total 0.3% error (− 2.2 MCM/y) for the entire aquifer discharge (Table 1). Indeed, the results revealed an extensive groundwater reservoir, with almost flat water-table stands at ~ 1,250 m asl in its northern part, and a substantial southwestward head gradient on both sides of the uplifted crest (Figs. 6a, 7). It appears that the location of the uplifted crest dictates the flow-fields toward the various springs and their storage volumes. Flow in the west occurs through a wider and deeper corridor, while in the east through a narrower and shallower one. The crest itself is covered by a thin water layer with head gradients toward the entire perimeter. Moreover, we simulated groundwater flowlines (Fig. 6b), showing that the large northern storage, with a thickness of approximately 2000 m (Fig. 6c), mostly feeds the southwestern springs. Thus, the calculated storage volume (Table 2) of the Dan Spring is an order of magnitude greater than those of the Banias and Awaj springs groups. These results also explain the large memory behavior of the Dan compared with the Banias. Sulphate transport modeling

A plausible explanation for the difference in the sulphate concentration between the springs is obtained by sulphate transport modeling. The source of the sulphate in the HJA is the gypsum of the Triassic Mohila Formation (Fig. 2), which constitutes the impermeable aquifer base. This layer is buried at great depth throughout the area but rises above the groundwater level at the anticline crest (Fig. 7b). Rainwater seeping from the surface flows over the crest, dissolving the gypsum, creating a thin groundwater layer enriched with calcium and sulphate (Fig. 6c). It is likely that caves and tunnels are created by the dissolving gypsum, forming thin karst networks within the thin groundwater layer. The maximum gypsum concentration under saturation conditions is 1500 mg/liter, but due to dilution the concentrations are probably lower. The calculated groundwater flowlines emerging from the sulphate-rich water layer (Fig. 6c) continue either towards the Dan Spring (34% of flowlines), but with considerable dilution with groundwater coming from the northern reservoir, or towards the Banias (22%) and Awaj (44%) springs groups, where dilution is reduced (Fig. 3b). Transport simulations of the sulphate concentration at steady-state flow conditions with constant sulphate concentration boundary conditions (Fig. 6d) revealed higher concentrations at the HJA southeastern part, feeding the Banias and Awaj springs. Therefore, the simulated sulphate concentration of the Banias group was higher by 2.6 than the Dan. While the spatial distribution of the simulated sulphate concentration matched the general pattern of the sulphate transport throughout the HJA, it did not accurately reconstruct the observed values of springs sulphate (Banias/Dan concentration ratio equals ~ 6). This was most likely due to the approximated sulphate boundary conditions and the severe data scarcity.

Transient groundwater flow modeling

Here we turned to modelling in a transient mode, where precipitation was distributed only in winter months, as per the overserved conditions; thus, monthly stress periods with variable recharge amounts were applied (Methods). The simulated discharges displayed a similar fit to the observed discharges of the Dan, Banias and Awaj springs groups during both calibration and validation periods (Supplementary material, Table S3). The Dan and Banias groups, which have the most detailed discharge record and drain nearly half of the aquifer, exhibit satisfactory (NSE = 0.5) to a very good (NSE = 0.82) fit for the entire simulation period, respectively (Fig. 8 and Table 3). The differences in time-lag between precipitation peak and spring discharge peak between the springs, e.g., one month difference between the Dan and Banias springs, was another issue that required a deeper understanding of the system. The time-lag between the precipitation peak and the discharge peak depends on three components: (a) the time-lag between precipitation and soil infiltration, including snow melting, (b) the vadose zone flow period, and (c) the flow duration within the saturated aquifer. Discussion

Transient model performance

The performance of the transient numerical model in simulating the discharge of the Dan and Banias springs, two major outputs of the aquifer, was satisfactory to very good, consistent with results from other case studies of karst aquifers modeled numerically43–46. As anticipated, the Awaj group and Barada springs, located at a considerable distance from the rain gauges situated in the southern part of the aquifer, exhibited a lower degree of fit. Unlike the Dan and Banias groups, the Awaj group comprises a complex network of minor springs rather than the three discrete drains represented in the model. Furthermore, its total observed discharge was an extrapolation derived from the monthly measurements of the Beit Jinn Spring. Given this complexity, the model was not expected to reproduce the discharge values for the Awaj group precisely. Instead, it aimed to capture the overarching discharge pattern, which should align with the observed data, and this was achieved. The Barada spring presented a greater modeling challenge. Drain parameters were calibrated under steady-state conditions based on an average discharge of 98 MCM/year. However, the simulation was validated against a limited nine-year dataset with a lower average discharge of 52 MCM/year, resulting in an expected discrepancy in the continuous simulated output.

The transient numerical model captured the general discharge trends in the aquifer’s main drains, but with less success in simulating peak flows, particularly during extreme wet or dry years. This discrepancy likely stemmed from model simplifications, such as assigning constant conductance values to drains, whereas in reality, conductance may vary with water level due to changes in cross-sectional area. For instance, the simulated discharge of the Dan Spring during dry periods failed to reach observed minimum values. Another key limitation was the inadequate representation of porous media heterogeneity typical of karst aquifers. These systems feature dual flow regimes: rapid preferential flow through voids and fractures, and slower diffusive flow through the surrounding bedrock18. Flow rates depend on the degree of karstification and bedrock properties, which can vary significantly across the aquifer.

The hydrogeological meaning of the calibrated parameters

The calibrated horizontal hydraulic conductivity parameters fell into two distinct groups. Bottom layers (8–10) were assigned low values (< 1.5 m/d), while upper layers (1–7) exhibited higher conductivities (> 5 m/d) (Supplementary material, Figure S2). These conductivity values are consistent with those reported for other karst aquifers39,43. Layers 5–7, situated predominantly below the water table, showed the highest sensitivity (Supplementary material, Table S1), reflecting their role in facilitating major aquifer flow.

The aquifer formations J1–J4 share similar hydraulic properties. One plausible explanation for the division into two main groups is the differential karstification of the Jurassic carbonate layers, which tends to be more pronounced near the water table47. As the Hermon anticline uplifted, successive drops in the water table relative to the stratigraphy likely shifted karst development downward. Consequently, the deepest layers, persistently below the water table, underwent limited karstification.

Specific storage and specific yield values were consistent across layer groups, representing simulated homogeneous porous media voids. They ranged from (5–13) × 10⁻⁷ m−1 and (1.3–3.3) × 10−3, respectively. Although relatively low45, these values have been previously reported43. They suggest that despite the advanced karstification of the HJA, the proportion of voids relative to the massive carbonate matrix remains limited. However, uncertainty of the simulated results is high, as the model represents a single porous media type rather than the dual flow systems typical of karst aquifers. Specific storage sensitivity to the simulated spring discharge increased with depth, reflecting the confined behavior of deeper layers. Specific yield sensitivity was highest in middle layers (4–5), which function as key phreatic zones. Notably, layer 9 showed unexpected sensitivity, possibly due to its location on the mountain flank, where intense recharge coincides with proximity to the uplifted aquifer base.

It should be noted that the vertical division of the model domain into ten layers (Methods) was not ideal and was derived from the lack of direct stratigraphic data. Although the aquifer units, J1-J4, are considered to have similar hydraulic characteristics, one massive layer is not sufficient to get a stabilized numerical model, and ten layers were set to give the model numerical flexibility. As stated above, the hydrogeological vertical variability matches the influence of the karstification process, that often emerge from the paleo water-table placement48. Indeed, at the extremely thin parts of the aquifer, the seemingly arbitrary layering is not geologically realistic; it preserved, however, the vertical hydraulic behavior of the entire aquifer, and for simplicity this approach was chosen.

Validation of hypothesis

The flow modeling results, based on the conceptual hydrogeological framework of the HJA (Fig. 4) and our approach of using the discharge volumes rather than the inputs, successfully captured the spatial and transient discharge rates. More importantly, the model results provided an explanation for the three challenging differences between the springs (peak time-lags, hydrological memories and sulphate content). These achievements provide a system level validation49 of our hypothesized hydrogeological framework of the HJA. This study demonstrates the potential of applying our non-traditional approach, which is based on knowledge of the stratigraphy and structure of the aquifer-hosting rocks, to the study of poorly gauged aquifers worldwide, overcoming lacking direct hydrological information.

Methods

Numerical modeling experiments

To simulate the flow field numerically, we constructed a MODFLOW50 model with the NWT51 solver, which improves solutions for fluctuating groundwater water-tables, i.e., drying and rewetting unconfined aquifers. The solute transport model, MT3DMS52, was coupled with the flow simulations. For flowlines calculations, the MODPATH53 package was used. These models use the finite difference method to solve the equations of water and solute mass conservations and their fluxes at all cells of the modeling domain. The modeling domain elevation was delineated by subtracting the geological units’ averaged thickness (Fig. 2) from the topography data (Fig. 1a). The mesh of the model domain was divided vertically into ten layers (the carbonate units, J1-J4; Fig. 2), each layer containing 803 elements with an area of 1 km2 each (Fig. 5). The reasoning of this division is detailed in the Discussion section.

Boundary conditions were defined according to the conceptual hydrogeological model, such that the base and perimeter of the aquifer are sealed (Fig. 4). Percolating precipitation water (minus evaporation and runoff) fills the aquifer from the top, and exit through the simulated drains, representing the HJA main springs groups, using MODFLOW’s RCH (recharge) and DRN (drains) packages50, respectively (Fig. 5). Most of the simulated drains represented the actual location of a spring; however, due to data scarcity and consideration of the numerical calculation flexibility, the Banias drain represented the Banias and Kezinim springs, the Awaj springs were represented by three drains, and the Barada spring by two drains (Table 1). Initial conditions for groundwater levels were set according to the hypothesized high permeability of the well-developed karst system, characterizing the Jurassic carbonates, using spring elevations (Fig. 4 and Supplementary material, Figure S1). Fluctuations in groundwater levels caused certain layers in the model domain to change from fully saturated to partially saturated and vice versa. Using the confined/phreatic convertible technique, water mass balance was computed using the specific-yield/specific-storage values, respectively.

Numerical modeling of the HJA was conducted herein in three stages. First, running groundwater flow simulations under steady state conditions, namely, assuming precipitation falls throughout the year at a constant rate (Fig. 5), and springs emerge at a constant discharge. Calibration was performed by changing rock hydraulic conductivity for each layer (assuming lateral homogeneity) and the springs’ conductance parameters until a minimum absolute percentage error was obtained between spring’s simulated and measured annual discharge volumes (Table 1). At the end of the first stage, the spatial head distribution was quantified, including a map of the groundwater level, and the aquifer paths-lines were sketched (Figs. 6a–c, 7).

The second modeling stage included running of solute transport simulation, sulphate in our case, assuming groundwater flows under steady-state conditions as in the first stage. Initial longitudinal dispersion value was assumed 500 m, according to the domain’s cell dimension, and transverse to longitudinal dispersion ratio was set to 1, assuming isotropic conditions. Manual calibration was performed by changing boundary conditions and solute dispersion parameters, until a satisfied match was obtained between the simulated and measured sulphate concentrations in the main springs groups. Thereby, a mapping of the sulphate concentration for the entire aquifer was obtained (Fig. 6d).

The third modeling stage included simulations of groundwater flow under transient conditions, namely, when precipitation is distributed only in winter months, and the spring discharges vary seasonally, as do the spring hydrographs. The transient model was set to 44 years, representing the years 1977–2020, that were divided into monthly stress periods. The years 1977–1979 served as a warm-up period, 1980–2000 was used for calibration, and 2001–2020 were the validation years. Calibrated hydraulic conductivities and drain conductance values from the steady-state stage were used for the transient simulations. Calibration was performed by changing the rock storage parameters until an optimal match was obtained between the calculated and measured hydrographs (Fig. 8). At this stage, the monthly spring’s discharge was quantified and evaluated with three metrics54,55: Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency coefficient (NSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and percentage bias (PBIAS).

In the first and third flow stages, calibrations were performed automatically using statistical software (PEST57), defining the best-estimated parameters with their sensitivities (Supplementary material, Table S1 and Table S2). Thereby, satisfied fittings achieved between observed and simulated discharges. Unfortunately, the transport calibration had lower success, namely the measured sulphate concentrations in the Banias and Kezinim springs were approximately an order of magnitude greater than those in the Dan (Fig. 3), while the simulated difference was much lower (Fig. 6d). A better fit could not be achieved by either changing the concentration in the boundary cells or by changing the dispersion value. This limited fit most likely stems from the fast groundwater flow in karst conduits (as indicated by the sulphate concentrations at the Banias and Kezinim springs, which are adjacent to each other).

It is worth noting that the flow in the unsaturated zone was not simulated; thus, the recharge enters the model directly at the groundwater-table, neglecting the vadose zone flow time-lag. Instead, the vadose zone residence time is evaluated with the cross-correlation analysis56, by changing the recharge delay until best-fitting (normalized value, up to 1) was achieved between observed and computed spring hydrographs. In addition, the time-lag was calculated for the saturated zone flows, by analyzing the recharge and simulated discharge time-series. This analysis requires accurate precipitation and discharge data and was conducted only for the Dan and Banias springs groups (Fig. 9).

Recharge estimation

In the absence of precipitation gauges throughout the basin and limited precipitation data and acknowledging that satellite reanalysis data was not suitable (e.g., ERA558 and ERA5-Land59, Supplementary material, Figure S3), the spatial distribution of net rainfall infiltration was logically evaluated. For the transient model simulations, the monthly steady-state recharge of each cell (yearly divided by 12), was multiplied by a specific monthly factor for each stress period (Supplementary material, Figure S4). Then, each monthly value was divided by the multi-year monthly average of the synthetic time-series, to give the monthly factor. Thus, the factor of a dry or a rainy month was less or greater than 1, respectively (Fig. 8a).
