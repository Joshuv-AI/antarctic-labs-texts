Knowledge, Perception, and Intention to Promote Malaria Vaccination Among Students in Southwestern Nigeria: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: James Success Odubia, Majani Edward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionMalaria remains a major public health burden in Nigeria, and the introduction of malaria vaccines offers a promising strategy for reducing morbidity and mortality. This study assessed knowledge, perception, and intention to promote malaria vaccination among health technology students in Southwestern Nigeria.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted from November to December 2025 among 300 students using a structured questionnaire. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, reliability analysis, Spearman's correlation with approximate 95% confidence intervals, and HC3 robust multiple linear regression. Chi‐square analyses of socio‐demographic factors and intention were not interpreted because sparse cells violated test assumptions.ResultsMost respondents demonstrated moderate (50.3%) to good (40.7%) knowledge of the malaria vaccine. A positive overall perception score—reflecting both supportive attitudes toward vaccination and awareness of implementation‐related concerns—was observed in 63.7% of participants, while 81.3% showed high intention to educate and recommend vaccination. Median scores were 14.0/20.0 for knowledge, 34.0/40.0 for perception, and 39.0/40.0 for intention. Perception was strongly associated with intention (rho = 0.553, 95% CI 0.469–0.627, p < 0.001), whereas knowledge was not significantly associated with intention (rho = −0.095, p = 0.101). In robust regression, perception remained independently and strongly associated with intention (B = 0.455, 95% CI 0.371–0.539, p < 0.001).ConclusionWhile knowledge of malaria vaccination was moderate to good among health technology students, perception was more strongly related to intention to promote vaccination. Addressing misconceptions while strengthening accurate and context‐sensitive vaccine communication is essential for improving future vaccine promotion.

Introduction

Malaria remains one of the most persistent global public health challenges [1], disproportionately affecting low‐ and middle‐income countries despite decades of control efforts. According to the World Health Organization, there are approximately 249 million malaria cases and over 600,000 deaths globally in 2022, with sub‐Saharan Africa accounting for about 95% of cases and deaths [2]. Nigeria alone contributes nearly 27% of the global malaria burden [3], making it the single most affected country worldwide. While conventional strategies such as insecticide‐treated nets, indoor residual spraying, and antimalarial therapies have contributed to reductions in morbidity and mortality [4, 5, 6], progress has stagnated in recent years due to funding gaps, insecticide resistance, and health system constraints [7, 8, 9]. As such, the introduction of the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine represents a significant milestone in malaria prevention [10]; however, its moderate efficacy and complex implementation requirements have generated debates regarding its acceptance and long‐term impact.

Healthcare professionals play a pivotal role in shaping public acceptance of vaccines, acting as both educators and trusted sources of information [11]. Studies across various contexts, such as deKoning et al. [12] and Sackey et al. [13], have demonstrated that recommendations from healthcare providers significantly increase vaccine uptake, sometimes by as much as 20%–30%. However, it is worth noting that healthcare workers themselves may exhibit varying levels of hesitancy, particularly toward newly introduced vaccines. For instance, studies on COVID‐19 vaccination, such as McCabe et al. [14], Miles et al. [15], and Bakare et al. [11], revealed that even among trained professionals, concerns about safety, efficacy, and trust in health systems influenced their willingness to recommend vaccines. These findings challenge the assumption that professional training automatically translates into positive health promotion behavior.

Theoretical frameworks such as the Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) model and the Theory of Planned Behavior have traditionally been used to explain health‐related behaviors, positing that knowledge influences attitudes, which in turn shape intentions and actions [16]. While these models provide useful conceptual foundations, they have been increasingly critiqued for oversimplifying the complex pathways linking cognition to behavior. For instance, empirical studies such as Li et al. [17] in vaccine research have shown inconsistent relationships between knowledge and behavioral intention, indicating that increased knowledge may not necessarily lead to higher acceptance or advocacy. Moreover, affective factors such as trust, perceived risk, and social norms have been identified as more proximal determinants of intention than knowledge alone. These inconsistencies highlight the need to critically re‐examine the applicability of traditional models in the context of emerging health interventions like the malaria vaccine.

Despite the growing body of research on vaccine acceptance, significant gaps remain, particularly in the context of malaria vaccination and among future healthcare professionals. This is because the malaria vaccine is a new phenomenon in Nigeria [18]. Also, most existing studies focus on general populations or practising healthcare workers, with limited attention to students in health‐related fields who represent the next generation of frontline advocates. Furthermore, there is a paucity of empirical evidence examining how knowledge, perception, and socio‐demographic factors interact to influence intention to promote malaria vaccination in endemic settings like Nigeria. This study seeks to address these gaps by examining the predictors of malaria vaccine promotion intentions among future healthcare professionals, with a focus on knowledge, perception, and socio‐demographic factors.

Methodology

Study Design

A cross‐sectional research design was adopted for this study to systematically assess the knowledge, perception, and intention to promote malaria vaccination among future healthcare professionals at a single point in time.

Study Area

The study was conducted among future healthcare professionals in South Western Nigeria College of Health Technology, located in the Sagamu Local Government Area of Ogun State, South‐West Nigeria.

Study Population

The study population comprised all students of the South Western Nigeria College of Health Technology across the Community Health, Public Health, and Allied Health departments. Eligible participants were students who provided informed consent to participate in the study. Those who were sick or absent during the period of data collection were excluded from the study.

Sample Size Estimation

The sample size for this study was determined using Taro Yamane's formula to ensure statistical precision for a finite population. This formula was used because the population of respondents was known: Community Health = 1200 students, Public Health = 110 students, and Allied departments = 50 students, giving a total population of 1360 students. With a 5% margin of error, the minimum sample size was 309.

Data Collection

Data were collected electronically through Google Forms from November to December 2025. A total of 309 eligible students were approached. All 309 consented and submitted questionnaires. Nine questionnaires were excluded because they were incompletely filled, leaving 300 valid responses for analysis. Therefore, 300/309 (97.1%) represented the usable questionnaire completion proportion, not a population response rate.

The tool used for this study was an adapted semi‐structured, self‐administered questionnaire. The tool comprised a consent section for participation and four main sections: Section A addressed socio‐demographic characteristics; Section B assessed respondents' knowledge of malaria vaccine; Section C examined respondents' perception of malaria vaccine; and Section D examined intention to educate and recommend malaria vaccine. The complete questionnaire is provided as Supporting File 1.

The questionnaire items were author‐developed malaria‐vaccine‐specific items adapted from vaccine knowledge, perception, and vaccine confidence literature [19, 20]. The items were not taken from a single previously validated MVKS, MVPS, or MVEIS instrument; these labels were used in the questionnaire to identify the study‐specific sections. The adaptation process focused on malaria vaccine facts, perceived vaccine inclusion in routine immunization, safety and misinformation concerns, and future health‐promotion roles among students.

The questionnaire was pretested among 31 students at Caribbean School of Health Technology, a similar institution outside the study site. Pretest participants were excluded from the final analysis. A pilot reliability analysis of the pretest responses produced an overall Cronbach's alpha of 0.83.

Knowledge of malaria vaccine was measured using 10 true/false/do‐not‐know items. B1, B2, B5, B6, B8, and B10 were scored correct when answered true. B3, B4, B7, and B9 were negatively worded knowledge test items and were scored correct when answered false. Correct responses were scored 2, while incorrect and do‐not‐know responses were scored 0, giving a total obtainable score of 20. Bloom's cutoff was used to categorize knowledge as poor (< 60.0%), moderate (60.0%–79.0%), or good (80.0%–100.0%).

Perception of malaria vaccine among respondents was assessed using 10 items on a 5‐point scale scored as 0 = Strongly Disagree, 1 = Disagree, 2 = Undecided, 3 = Agree, and 4 = Strongly Agree. Seven items (C1, C2, C3, C5, C6, C8, and C10) captured supportive attitudes toward malaria vaccination and confidence in vaccine safety and delivery, while three items (C4, C7, and C9) captured awareness of practical implementation concerns—perceived risk of side effects, cultural or religious barriers, and susceptibility to misinformation. Agreement with C4, C7, and C9 reflects recognition of, rather than personal endorsement of, obstacles to vaccine acceptance, so these items do not carry the same evaluative direction as the supportive items. They were therefore retained rather than reverse‐scored as an explicit design decision: the composite is intended to capture overall engagement with the malaria vaccination issue—combining favorable attitudes with realistic awareness of barriers—rather than a unidimensional measure of vaccine favourability. Accordingly, we avoid describing a higher composite score simply as a “more positive perception” and instead interpret it, throughout the Results and Discussion, as an overall perception score reflecting both supportive attitudes and concern‐awareness. This composite approach is consistent with multidimensional vaccination‐attitude instruments such as the 5C scale [19], in which psychologically distinct antecedents (e.g., confidence and constraints) are assessed as separate but related components of an overall vaccination‐attitude profile. The total perception score ranged from 0 to 40 and was categorized descriptively as negative (< 60.0%), neutral (60.0%–79.0%), or positive (80.0%–100.0%), and item‐level results are reported separately to make the construct's dual content explicit.

Intention to educate and recommend malaria vaccine was measured using 10 items on the same 5‐point scale scored from 0 to 4, where higher scores indicated stronger intention. The total intention score ranged from 0 to 40 and was categorized as low (< 60.0%), moderate (60.0%–79.0%), or high (80.0%–100.0%). No intention item was reverse‐coded.

Data Management and Analysis

Data were exported from Google Forms into Microsoft Excel for preliminary coding, completeness checks, and cleaning. Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Continuous principal scores for knowledge, perception, and intention were reported using mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range, minimum, and maximum.

Statistical reporting was revised with reference to SAMPL guidance and the STROBE statement for observational studies [21, 22]. All statistical tests were two‐sided with an a priori significance level of 0.05. Internal consistency was recalculated using KR‐20/Cronbach's alpha, and corrected item‐total correlations with alpha‐if‐deleted values were produced from the item‐level dataset. Spearman's rank correlation was used to examine associations among knowledge, perception, and intention scores. Approximate 95% confidence intervals for Spearman's rho were calculated using Fisher's z transformation.

The planned Chi‐square analyses between socio‐demographic variables and intention category were inspected but not interpreted because the low‐intention group contained only one participant and several expected cell counts were below 5. Multiple linear regression predicting intention score was reanalyzed using HC3 heteroscedasticity‐robust standard errors because the intention score was ceiling‐skewed and residual diagnostics indicated non‐normality and heteroscedasticity. Categorical predictors were dummy‐coded with explicit reference groups: female vs. male, ND2 and HND vs. ND1, and non‐community program vs. Community Health. Marital status and religion were retained descriptively only because married respondents (n = 6) and traditional religion (n = 1) were too sparse for stable adjusted estimates. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors.

Ethical Approval

Ethics approval was obtained from the Research Committee of South Western Nigeria College of Health Technology, Sagamu, Ogun State, Nigeria, with approval code SWNCHT/S23/CH117; this Research Committee is the institutional body formally authorized to review and approve human‐subjects research at the College. The approval was valid from June 9, 2025, to June 8, 2026. The study was anonymous and non‐invasive. Participation was voluntary: students were free to decline or withdraw from the questionnaire at any point without academic penalty or any other consequence. Consent information was attached to the questionnaire, and participants had to actively consent (or assent, for minors) before answering; informed consent was obtained directly from all participants aged 18 years and above. Seventeen respondents were aged 16–17 years; because obtaining individual parental or legal‐guardian consent was not practicable within the school setting, school‐management consent (in loco parentis) was obtained together with each of these participants' own assent, and this substitution of school‐management consent for parental consent was explicitly reviewed and authorized in advance by the Research Committee as part of the approved study protocol.

Results

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

The majority were aged 20–24 years (61.0%), followed by those aged 16–19 years (35.3%), while only 3.7% were aged 25–36 years (mean age: 20.39 +/− 2.20 years). The sample was predominantly female (93.3%), and almost all respondents were single (98.0%). In terms of religion, 57.7% identified as Christians, 42.0% as Muslims, and 0.3% as adherents of traditional religion.

Regarding academic characteristics, ND2 students constituted the largest proportion (39.0%), followed by ND1 (31.0%) and HND students (30.0%). Most respondents were enrolled in the Community Health program (88.7%), with smaller proportions in Public Health (9.3%) and Allied Health Sciences (2.0%) (Table 2).

Knowledge of Malaria Vaccine

Nearly all correctly identified that the vaccine provides partial immunity (98.0%), is recommended mainly for infants and young children (93.3%), has undergone scientific trials to assess safety and effectiveness (97.3%), and requires government approval before inclusion in Nigeria's routine immunization schedule (98.3%). A majority also knew that multiple doses are required (79.0%) and that some African countries have introduced the vaccine (86.7%).

However, misconceptions were evident. For example, 21.7% incorrectly believed that once a child receives the malaria vaccine, other malaria prevention methods are no longer necessary, and 9.7% did not know. Also, 63.0% incorrectly believed that the malaria vaccine completely eliminates malaria infection in vaccinated individuals, 69.0% incorrectly believed that the malaria vaccine can increase the risk of malaria infection in children, and 44.7% incorrectly believed that the vaccine is meant to replace insecticide‐treated mosquito nets and other malaria control strategies.

Perception of Malaria Vaccine

Most agreed that malaria poses a serious health risk to children under 5 years (90.0%), and all respondents agreed or strongly agreed that they would support inclusion of the vaccine in Nigeria's national routine immunization schedule. Confidence in vaccines approved by Nigerian health authorities was also high (98.3%), and 98.7% believed the vaccine would reduce severe malaria cases.

At the same time, respondents recognized concerns that may affect implementation: 73.7% agreed or strongly agreed that the malaria vaccine may raise serious side‐effect concerns among caregivers, 65.3% agreed or strongly agreed that cultural or religious beliefs may discourage acceptance, and 83.3% agreed or strongly agreed that fear or misinformation could limit acceptance. Therefore, the perception score is interpreted as an overall perception score reflecting both supportive attitudes and awareness of implementation concerns, and a higher score is not read as a uniformly “more positive” or more favorable attitude toward the vaccine itself.

Intention to Educate and Recommend Malaria Vaccine

Respondents demonstrated a very high intention to promote malaria vaccination. Nearly all indicated willingness to educate caregivers (99.0%), inform parents about the correct age group for vaccination (98.0%), and encourage completion of all required vaccine doses (99.7%).

Similarly, strong proportions expressed readiness to advocate in their future professional roles (97.0%), correct misinformation (98.7%), collaborate with health authorities or campaigns (98.7%), and include malaria vaccine information in general health education (99.3%). Confidence in their ability to educate others accurately was also high (99.3%).

Categorization of Knowledge, Perception, and Intention

Using Bloom's cutoff points, half of the respondents (50.3%) had moderate knowledge, while 40.7% had good knowledge and 9.0% had poor knowledge. Most respondents (63.7%) had a positive overall perception score, while 35.7% had a neutral score and 0.7% had a negative score (categories reflect the composite perception construct described above, combining supportive attitudes and concern‐awareness). A large majority (81.3%) demonstrated high intention to educate and recommend malaria vaccine, while 18.3% had moderate intention and 0.3% had low intention (Table 3).

Association Between Knowledge, Perception, and Intention

Spearman's correlation analysis revealed a weak but statistically significant negative relationship between knowledge and perception (rho = −0.224, 95% CI −0.329 to −0.114, p < 0.001). This correlation is interpreted in light of the perception scale content, because the scale included both support items and concern‐awareness items. There was no significant relationship between knowledge and intention (rho = −0.095, 95% CI −0.206 to 0.019, p = 0.101). In contrast, a strong positive correlation was observed between perception and intention (rho = 0.553, 95% CI 0.469–0.627, p < 0.001), indicating that a higher overall perception score—reflecting both supportive attitudes and concern‐awareness—was associated with higher intention to promote malaria vaccination (Table 5).

Discussion

This study assessed knowledge, perception, and intention to promote malaria vaccination among health technology students in Southwestern Nigeria. The findings revealed generally moderate‐to‐good knowledge, positive overall perception, and strong intention to promote malaria vaccination, although important misconceptions and concerns persist.

The moderate‐to‐good knowledge observed in this study contrasts with findings from earlier studies in Nigeria, where awareness of malaria vaccines was reported to be relatively low. For instance, a study among health policy actors found that only 48.9% had prior knowledge of the malaria vaccine [23]. Similarly, a national survey among physicians reported that a considerable proportion still lacked adequate knowledge regarding malaria vaccines [24]. The higher knowledge levels observed in the present study may be attributed to the respondents' academic background in health‐related disciplines, which likely enhances access to accurate health information. However, the persistence of misconceptions, particularly regarding the belief that the vaccine completely eliminates malaria infection or replaces other preventive measures, highlights a critical gap in nuanced understanding. This aligns with evidence that malaria vaccines provide partial protection and must be used alongside existing interventions such as insecticide‐treated nets and chemoprevention [25].

The study also demonstrated an overall positive perception of malaria vaccines, with strong support for inclusion in routine immunization programs. This finding is consistent with a scoping review conducted in sub‐Saharan Africa, which reported that the majority of participants across multiple studies perceived malaria vaccines as necessary and beneficial for child health [25]. The high level of trust in health authorities observed in this study further reinforces the importance of institutional credibility in shaping vaccine acceptance. Additionally, the strong influence of health workers on vaccine acceptance aligns with evidence showing that healthcare providers are key drivers of vaccine uptake in African settings [26].

Despite the overall positive perception, notable concerns were identified, particularly regarding potential side effects, cultural beliefs, and misinformation. These concern‐awareness items were retained within the perception construct because they captured students' recognition of practical issues that may affect malaria vaccine implementation, rather than a separate study variable. This clarification explains why perception is not interpreted as a simple absence of concern. Similar concerns have been identified in Nigeria, where fear of adverse effects and vaccine hesitancy have influenced willingness to pay for and accept malaria vaccines [27]. A recent study among caregivers also reported that misinformation and safety concerns affected uptake despite high levels of acceptance [28].

The study further revealed a very high intention among respondents to educate and recommend malaria vaccination. This is in line with evidence indicating that healthcare workers and trainees are generally willing to advocate for vaccination programs when adequately informed [24]. The strong intention observed may also reflect the perceived severity of malaria and the respondents' anticipated professional roles in health promotion. Importantly, perception, not knowledge, showed the strongest independent association with intention in the robust regression model. This supports the view that knowledge alone may be insufficient unless accompanied by favorable perception and confidence in addressing concerns.

The previous Chi‐square interpretation of socio‐demographic factors was removed because the intention categories were too sparse for valid inference. In the robust regression model, HND level was associated with higher intention compared with ND1, while age showed a small negative association. This age pattern is broadly consistent with recent evidence on vaccination acceptance among school‐aged youth, which found that age was significantly associated with awareness, attitudes, trust in institutions, environmental influence, and personal experience related to vaccination, with the most favorable attitudes concentrated among young adults (21–35 years) and greater susceptibility to environmental and social influence among the youngest respondents [29]. Because our sample was similarly young (mean age 20.4 years, range 16–36 years) and largely within the age band associated with strong social and environmental influence on vaccine‐related views, the modest negative association between age and intention observed here may reflect a comparable underlying pattern of age‐related shifts in trust and susceptibility to social influence, rather than a substantive effect of age on capacity to promote vaccination. These findings are interpreted cautiously because the sample was dominated by female, single, Community Health students and because intention scores were highly skewed toward the upper end.

Overall, the findings highlight that while knowledge is important, perception plays a more critical role in influencing intention to promote malaria vaccination. Therefore, interventions aimed at improving vaccine uptake should not only focus on increasing knowledge but also on addressing perceptions, beliefs, misinformation, and communication confidence.

Notably, this study has strengths and limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. A key strength lies in the inclusion of health students across multiple disciplines, providing relevant insights into a population that represents the future healthcare workforce and is likely to play a role in malaria vaccine communication. However, the cross‐sectional design limits causal inference. The use of self‐reported data may introduce social desirability and response bias, particularly given the high levels of reported intention. The study was conducted within a single institution, which may limit generalizability. The knowledge section had low internal consistency, suggesting that it is interpreted as a factual checklist. In addition, the perception construct combined supportive attitudinal items with three concern‐awareness items (C4, C7, and C9) that captured recognition of implementation barriers rather than personal rejection of the vaccine; total perception is therefore interpreted as an overall perception score rather than a pure vaccine‐acceptance or vaccine‐favourability scale, and higher scores should not be read as uniformly more favorable attitudes. This scoring approach likely contributed to the weak negative correlation observed between knowledge and perception (rho = −0.224): more knowledgeable respondents may have been more likely to endorse the concern‐awareness items (e.g., recognizing genuine safety, cultural, or misinformation‐related barriers) even while holding equally or more supportive attitudes toward vaccination itself, which would depress the composite perception score without reflecting less favorable views of the vaccine. This construct‐related limitation should be considered when interpreting the knowledge–perception association, and future work should consider separately scoring or validating the concern‐awareness items as a distinct sub‐scale. Similarly, exclusions for incomplete questionnaires were tracked in aggregate (n = 9) rather than by department, so a department‐level breakdown of approached, completed, and excluded participants could not be reported. Future studies should consider multi‐center designs and longitudinal approaches to better understand changes in perception and behavior over time.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that health students possess moderate‐to‐good knowledge, positive overall perception, and strong intention to promote malaria vaccination. However, important misconceptions and concerns regarding vaccine safety and effectiveness persist. Perception showed the strongest independent association with intention, emphasizing the need for interventions that go beyond knowledge dissemination to address attitudes, beliefs, misinformation, and communication confidence. Strengthening risk communication, leveraging trusted health workers, and integrating vaccine education into training curricula are essential strategies to enhance vaccine advocacy and uptake. These findings underscore the potential role of future healthcare professionals in supporting malaria vaccine communication in Nigeria and similar endemic settings.
