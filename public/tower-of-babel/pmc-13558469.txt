Heartfulness Meditation-induced State and Trait Effects on Long-term, Short-term and Non-meditators Using Connectivity Analysis: An EEG Study on Prefrontal Cortex
Authors: Anurag Shrivastava, Bikesh Kumar Singh, Deepeshwar Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeartfulness meditation (HM) is a heart-based meditation practice that features a unique yogic transmission that involves transfer of subtle energy from a certified, experienced meditator to an aspirant.PurposeThis study was conducted on HM practice to assess the state (momentary changes during meditation result in temporary physiological and neurological shifts) and trait (long-term personality changes developed with experience) effects on the brain using electroencephalogram (EEG) signal analysis.MethodsThirty-four participants were recruited into three groups (long-term meditators [LTM], short-term meditators [STM] and non-meditators). EEG signals from the prefrontal cortex were recorded across four states: baseline, meditation, transmission and post-transmission. Functional connectivity and graph theory measures were calculated for each frequency band (delta, theta, alpha and beta). Within-group and between-group analyses were performed to quantify HM-induced state and trait effects. Significance level was tested through statistical analysis.ResultsThe results show improved coordination among brain networks, increased global and local efficiency, representing a positive state effect. While performing between-group analysis, significant trait changes were observed in the LTM group over frequency bands (delta, theta and alpha) of the EEG signal. These findings suggest that the altered EEG resulting from meditation reflects neurophysiological changes and functional integration of the brain network.ConclusionThe positive state and trait effects were observed for HM practice in the prefrontal cortex of the brain. Furthermore, the enhanced connectivity in EEG frequency bands may lead to significant neuroplastic changes. This finding provides insights into employing functional connectivity parameters as measures for quantifying meditation quality.

Introduction

Meditation practices are increasingly popular because they can improve psychological, emotional and physiological parameters, which significantly impact well-being, especially during the global COVID-19 pandemic. 1 A systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) performed on different meditation styles shows the efficacy of meditation practices in regulating anxiety, depression and pain.2–4 Meditation practices are characterised by analysing state (temporary neural shifts induced during meditation practice) and trait (long-lasting improvement in baseline related to personality) effects. The state effect can be assessed by comparing the meditation and resting state within the same group. In contrast, trait effects can be estimated by comparing the resting states of meditators and non-meditators (NM). 5 To analyse these effects, it is essential to record signals from the brain. Out of several available techniques, electroencephalogram (EEG) is gaining popularity due to its affordability, non-invasiveness and scalability. 6 Past studies have demonstrated that meditation can alter the EEG waves recorded from various brain regions. 7 Functional connectivity measures and graph theory measures are gaining attention among neuroscience researchers for analyzing the interconnections between different brain regions with the help of time series physiological signals like EEG.8, 9 These measures assist in diagnosing brain disorders, assessing cognitive performance and analysing different states of meditation.8, 10, 11

Although there are several forms of meditation practice known worldwide, the studies show that meditation practices strengthen the prefrontal cortex (PFC) of the brain, improving overall cognitive performance.3, 12 The meditation practice also induces neuroplastic changes in the prefrontal region of the brain. 13 An earlier study utilising EEG signals found that experienced meditators exhibit increased functional connectivity in the brain’s PFC compared to those who do not meditate. 14 Additionally, it has been observed that the PFC shows greater activation in seasoned meditators compared to those who have practiced meditation for only a short period. 15 One of the focus-attention meditations reported that trait meditation influences brain network function and operation. 16 Also, an EEG-based study on Bhramari pranayama meditation revealed better brain network integration in the alpha band during and after meditation, using functional connectivity and graph theory parameters. 17 Heartfulness meditation (HM), also known as heart-based meditation, is an adapted version of Raj yoga meditation that emphasises focusing on the heart instead of the breath. A distinctive aspect of HM is its yogic transmission. In the yogic transmission state, the subtle energy is transferred from the trainer (guru) to the meditator, which allows even beginners to experience the effects of meditation in a short period. Further, the HM practice involves coordination of both heart and brain, 18 which is helpful even in regulating cardiac parameters like blood pressure and heart rate. 19 These features differentiate HM from other meditation practices. The HM practice also proves to be useful in improving stress, state of depression and anger, sleep quality and regulating cardiac parameters.20–22 Previous studies demonstrate positive neurophysiological changes during meditation practice resulting from better functional integration.8, 9, 23 The research gaps identified in previous studies, as well as the contributions of the proposed study, are presented as follows: The unique feature of the HM practice is yogic transmission, which has not been explored much in previous studies. This study quantifies the yogic transmission in meditators and NM using EEG signal analysis.Both state and trait effects induced by meditation practices represent neuroplastic changes that have not been addressed in past studies carried out for HM practice. This study will assess both state and trait effects among meditators using functional connectivity and graph theory-based measures.

Methods

Participants

The study involved a total of 34 volunteers, consisting of 23 men and 11 women, aged between 20 and 45 years. Convenience sampling was used in our study for the recruitment of participants. This is the most convenient method that allows selection of participants based on their availability. 24 Participants were divided into three groups: the long-term meditators (LTM) group with more than 5 years of meditation experience, the short-term meditators (STM) group with less than 3 years of experience and the NM group, which had no prior meditation experience. Although there is no universal definition that exists for LTM and STM, earlier studies have considered meditators with regular meditation experience of greater than 5 years as LTM and meditators with experience of 6 months–3 years have been considered STM. In our study, we have grouped participants having at least 1 h of daily meditation experience for 5 years as LTM and from 0.5 to 3 years as STM.25–27 The average meditation experience of participants in our study is 98.71 ± 32.35 and 12.80 ± 6.48 months for LTM and STM groups, respectively. Each participant provided written informed consent after being briefed on the study’s design. Table 1 presents the characteristics of the participants whose EEG data were free from noise.

Electroencephalogram Acquisition

A high-density EEG recording was carried out using a 128-channel EGI Geodesic Transcranial Electrical system. The software used for data recording was EGI’s NetStation (version 4.5.8). The entire EEG signal was recorded once for each participant in four stages (baseline [5 min], meditation [10 min], transmission [10 min] and post-transmission [5 min]) at 250 Hz sampling. During the baseline state, participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed for 5 min during the recording of EEG signals. EEG recording continued during meditation for 10 min, during transmission for another 10 min and during post-meditation for 5 min. The NM group participants completed one session of HM practice to ensure procedural consistency. The process is summarised in Table 2.

Preprocessing and Band Extraction

EEG signals are measured through electrodes located away from their sources; they can be interfered with by nearby sources, leading to false correlations. To mitigate this, it is better to dissociate the EEG signal, which can be achieved by applying independent component analysis (ICA). It is a technique that involves linear decomposition of signals and proves efficient in artefact removal and overcoming volume conduction effects.25–27 Additionally, noise from ocular activity, muscles and head movement was removed through visual inspection and ICA. Further, frontal lobe channels were selected as shown in Figure 2. The EEG signals were then decomposed into four bands (delta [0.5–4 Hz], theta [4–8 Hz], alpha [8–13 Hz] and beta [13–30 Hz]) using bandpass filters.

Functional Connectivity Parameters

Functional connectivity measures the statistical relationship between various regions of the brain network through the physiological signals recorded in the time domain. The functional connectivity parameters (correlation coefficient [r], phase lag index [PLI] and phase locking value [PLV]) between 20 selected electrode pairs were calculated.

Correlation Coefficient: The correlation coefficient measures the connectivity between the signals from two nodes in a specific frequency range of the brain functional network. The expression for correlation coefficient is given by Equation (1). (1) Cora,b=Cov(a,b)VaraVar(b)

where cov(a, b) is the cross-spectral density between two signals from nodes a and b, and var(a) and var(b) are the auto-spectral densities for signals from nodes a and b, respectively. The correlation coefficient lies in the range 0–1, with a value of 0 indicating no correlation between the nodes, whereas 1 indicates perfect correlation.28, 29

Phase Lag Index: This parameter quantifies functional connectivity by measuring the consistency of phase lead/lag relationships between EEG signals, minimising volume conduction effects. EEG signals were segmented into frequency bands and instantaneous phases were extracted using the Hilbert transform.30, 31 Mathematically, PLI is defined as (2) PLI=|mean(sign(Δφ(t)))|,

where ∇φ(t) is the instantaneous phase difference at time t.

Phase Locking Value: It measures the mean of the phase difference between two signals over several epochs. Perfect synchronisation is reached when the value of PLV becomes one, which means the phase difference is zero; that is, the signals are phase-synchronised. The phase difference between the electrode pair (a,b) is given by Equation (3). (3) θ(a,b)=∅a−∅b

where øi and øj represent the respective phases of electrodes a and b. Then PLV is calculated by Equation (4), as follows: (4) PLV=1L∑-∞∞ejƟ(a,b)

The range of PLV is from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates the absence of synchronisation, whereas 1 represents perfect synchronisation between signals from a pair of electrodes.32, 33

Graph Theory Measures: Graph theory characterises the topological properties of the brain system by calculating global and local measures. These measures reflect the efficiency and specialisation of the brain system. In this study, shortest path (SP) and clustering coefficient (CC) are used as graph theory measures.

Characteristic Path Length/Shortest Path: In the brain’s functional networks, the SP assesses the level of functional integration among brain regions.25, 27 Typically, multiple paths exist between a pair of nodes, but only the shortest one is considered, as it represents the most efficient route for information transfer between the nodes. Consequently, this path length (PL) is referred to as the SP. A shorter PL indicates better functional integration. The calculation of the SP was based on Dijkstra’s algorithm.33, 34

Transitivity: Transitivity (T) is a graph-theoretical measure that captures the tendency of nodes to cluster by calculating the ratio of closed triplets (triangles) to all connected triplets in a network, expressed as T = 3 × number of triangles/number of connected triplets. This metric reflects the global clustering property and integration within the network, crucial for understanding complex connectivity patterns.30, 31, 35

Clustering Coefficient: CC measures local segregation. The measure of local segregation evaluates how effectively nodes are linked to create a cluster. When considering three nodes, j, k and l, if node j is linked to node l and node k is also linked to l, the CC indicates the likelihood of a connection between j and k, forming a triangle within a network. CC assesses the efficiency of information processing and transmission in a network.29, 33, 36, 37

Data Analysis for Quantifying State and Trait Effects

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics version 18.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. Statistical significance was established at a two-tailed p value < .05.

To investigate HM-induced state and trait effects, a mixed-design repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was conducted. In this model, group (LTM, STM and NM) was treated as the between-subject factor, while State (baseline, meditation, transmission and post-transmission) was treated as the within-subject factor.

The RM-ANOVA was used to evaluate: (a) the major group effect, representing trait-related differences attributable to meditation experience; (b) the state effect, representing acute state-dependent changes occurring across the meditation session; and (c) the Group × State interaction, indicating whether state-related changes differed among the three groups. Multivariate statistics (Pillai’s Trace, Wilks’ Lambda, Hotelling’s Trace and Roy’s Largest Root) were examined to assess the overall significance of within-subject and interaction effects.

The assumption of sphericity for repeated measures was assessed using Mauchly’s test. Whenever the assumption of sphericity was violated (p < .05), Greenhouse–Geisser corrected degrees of freedom were applied for inferential testing. Significant main effects or interactions were further explored using Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons to control for multiple testing. Effect sizes were quantified using partial eta squared (ηp2).

To further characterise state effects, within-group analyses were performed separately for the LTM, STM and NM groups by comparing meditation, transmission and post-transmission states against the baseline condition. To quantify trait effects, between-group comparisons were conducted using baseline measurements to evaluate differences between experienced meditators (LTM and STM) and NM, thereby isolating long-term effects associated with meditation practice independent of the acute meditation session.

Additionally, Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were performed within each group to examine associations among neurophysiological, behavioural and experiential variables. Correlation coefficients were interpreted according to conventional guidelines, and all reported p values were two-sided.

Results

A mixed-design RM-ANOVA was conducted to examine the effects of Group (LTM, STM and NM) and State (baseline, meditation, transmission and post-transmission) on functional connectivity and graph-theoretical measures. Multivariate analyses revealed a significant main effect of Group (Pillai’s Trace = 1.827, F(48,16) = 3.525, p = .004, ηp2 = 0.914), indicating differences among the three groups. A significant main effect of State was also observed (Pillai’s Trace = 1.357, F(72,207) = 2.373, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.452). Furthermore, a significant Group × State interaction was identified (Pillai’s Trace = 1.826, F(144,432) = 1.312, p = .020, ηp2 = 0.304).

Discussion

The current study focuses on assessing the state and trait analysis of HM through EEG signal analysis on the PFC of the brain. Functional connectivity and graph theory analyses were carried out among the LTM, STM and NM groups. There are very few research investigations that show the effect of HM practice on brain signals. The significant increase in the values of functional connectivity parameters (CC and PLV) with meditation experience shows a positive trait effect, while the increment during meditation and transmission states for each group (LTM and STM) reflects positive state effects. The connectivity plot also supports the findings. The PLI results show higher values across all bands for the LTM and STM groups compared to NM; significant differences are obtained only in the alpha band for the LTM group. The enhanced alpha synchronisation during meditation represents relaxed alertness and efficient network coordination. The observed increases in r, PLV and PLI values across delta, theta and alpha bands during meditation suggest heightened neural synchrony and functional connectivity.38, 39 This enhanced synchronisation indicates a more integrated brain state, potentially reflecting deeper states of mental absorption and focused attention. 38

Furthermore, the results for graph theory measures (CC and PL/SP) indicate increased local and global brain efficiency during HM across the delta, theta and alpha bands. These results align with some previous studies showing an increase in CC during focused attention (FA) meditation, which is a prominent feature to discriminate between meditative and non-meditative states in the frontal region, as well as other regions of the brain using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). 40 Another study on integrative body-mind training (IBMT) and relaxation training reveals an increase in the CC and global efficiency, thereby decreasing the average shortest PL. 41 The transmission state offers high functional connectivity as compared to the meditation state, which shows that yogic transmission serves as a core element that optimises the HM practice. The EEG frequency bands (delta, theta and alpha) are significantly affected by HM practice, which is supported by findings that the theta and alpha bands of the PFC are affected by mindfulness meditation and serve as a key signature to represent meditative states. 42 The overall results suggest the positive state effect of HM on the brain of the LTM group as compared to the STM and NM groups. The present study findings are aligned with the previous study that brain region activation due to meditation depends on the experience of meditation and is prominent in LTM as compared to STM. 1 Also, a positive and significant trait effect has been observed in delta, theta and alpha bands for the LTM group. This finding is supported by a study carried out on HM practice by Singh et al., which shows a trait effect resulting from enhanced alpha response. 43 The better functional connectivity and brain efficiency in the alpha, theta and delta bands in the frontal lobe can be used to categorise the meditative states in HM. The study has some limitations. First, the study has been carried out with a small sample size for all three groups, which limits the generalisation of the current study. The second limitation is the discomfort experienced by the meditator during the EEG setup, which was carried out in a closed room with minimal lighting. Furthermore, the effectiveness of HM can be verified by including a cardiovascular study incorporating an electrocardiogram (ECG) analysis along with the findings of the current study, which signifies the coordination between the heart and brain while performing meditation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this is a novel study on HM practice employing functional connectivity and graph theory-based measures for assessing the state and trait effects of HM on brain signals. The PFC of the brain observed positive and significant state effects in the LTM and STM groups; the significant positive trait effects in the LTM group signify changes in personality and brain function during the resting state that developed with meditation experience. Further, the result shows that the yogic transmission element enhances HM practice, resulting in a profound level of meditative awareness. Future work may incorporate cardiovascular parameters with an increased sample size to study mind-body function during meditation.
