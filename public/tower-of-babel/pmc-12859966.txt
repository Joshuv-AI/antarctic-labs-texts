Biomimetic cell membrane decorated calcium carbonate nanoparticles with asparaginase-metformin preloading for leukemia therapy
Authors: Guangtao Gao, Junyi Che, Bing Chen, Yuanjin Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemotherapy is the primary clinical treatment for leukemia, while its effectiveness is often limited due to undesired off-target effects and the reduced sensitivity of leukemia cells to chemotherapeutic agents. Therapies capable of targeting the bone marrow and addressing the acidic tumor microenvironment are anticipated to improve treatment efficacy. Here, we presented biomimetic cell membrane-decorated calcium carbonate nanoparticles co-loaded with asparaginase and metformin (AMNPs@CM) for bone marrow-targeted therapy of leukemia. The biomimetic cell membrane coating facilitated significant accumulation of AMNPs@CM in the bone marrow, where the nanoparticles released the payloads in response to the acidic tumor microenvironment. The released asparaginase and metformin could synergically induce mitochondrial dysfunction in leukemia cells, leading to inhibited cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis. Thus, our stratagem could effectively inhibit tumor burden and prolong survival in a C1498 leukemia-bearing mouse model. These results indicate the potential of the AMNPs@CM as a bone marrow-targeted delivery platform for combination therapy in the treatment of leukemia.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03962-2.Keywords: Biomimetic, Bone marrow homing, Leukemia, Drug delivery, Cell membrane

Introduction

Leukemia is a malignant proliferative disorder of the hematopoietic system, characterized by disrupted hematopoiesis and a poor prognosis, posing a serious health threat [1, 2]. Currently, chemotherapy is the primary clinical treatment for leukemia [3, 4]. Asparaginase (Asp) and metformin (Met) are two widely used drugs that have shown efficacy in leukemia treatment [5]. Asparaginase is an enzyme that induces apoptosis in cancer cells by catalyzing the degradation of asparagine, a vital substrate for their proliferation [6, 7]. Metformin limits tumor asparagine synthesis by inhibiting the electron transport chain [5]. Notably, these two drugs could exhibit synergistic effects, enhancing therapeutic efficacy and providing a promising strategy for leukemia patients. However, traditional systemic administration of asparaginase and metformin often fails to achieve effective concentrations in the bone marrow, requiring multiple administrations [8]. This can lead to severe side effects, including pancreatitis, hepatic steatosis, cirrhosis, and coagulation disorders [9, 10]. Additionally, the acidic tumor microenvironment further reduces the sensitivity of leukemia cells to chemotherapy, reducing its overall effectiveness [11]. Therefore, the development of drug delivery systems capable of targeting the bone marrow and correcting the acidic tumor microenvironment is expected to improve therapeutic outcomes [12].

Here, we propose novel biomimetic cell membrane-coated calcium carbonate nanoparticles co-loaded with asparaginase and metformin for bone marrow-targeted therapy of leukemia, as schemed in Fig. 1. Recently, as a new approach for modifying drug carriers, biomimetic cell membrane delivery systems have been increasingly used in tumor treatment [13, 14]. These strategies endow the systems with features of low immunogenicity, extended circulation time in the bloodstream, and strong targeting ability [15]. Especially, the leukemia cells can home to the bone marrow because of the expression of CXCR4 on their membrane [16]. Thus, we assumed that leukemia cell membranes decorated nanoparticles could inherit the bone marrow homing ability of the leukemia cells. On the other hand, calcium carbonate nanoparticles, a pH-responsive nanoparticle, can neutralize the acidic tumor microenvironment and release loaded drugs during their degradation [17, 18]. In addition, CaCO₃ nanoparticles can elevate intracellular Ca²⁺ levels in tumor cells, trigger mitochondrial damage, and ultimately lead to tumor cell death [19, 20]. Therefore, we conceive that integrating leukemia cell membranes with CaCO₃ NPs for the co-delivery of asparaginase and metformin offers significant promise for bone marrow-targeted therapy to improve leukemia treatment outcomes.

In this study, we constructed the desired biomimetic multifunctional nanosystem by integration of leukemia cell membranes and CaCO₃ nanoparticles, wherein metformin is loaded into CaCO₃ nanoparticles using a gas diffusion method and asparaginase is conjugated with the leukemia cell membranes by click reaction (Fig. 1). This biomimetic nanosystem exhibited efficient tumor cell uptake and the ability to evade macrophage recognition. Additionally, the biomimetic cell membrane decorated calcium carbonate nanoparticles with asparaginase-metformin preloading synergically induced mitochondrial dysfunction in leukemia cells, leading to inhibited cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis. Based on these features, we further evaluated their in vivo efficacy in a C1498 leukemia-bearing mouse model. The results showed that the biomimetic nanosystem could accumulate significantly in the bone marrow and released the loaded asparaginase-metformin in response to the acidic tumor microenvironment, resulting in inhibited tumor burden and extended survival. These finding highlighted the potential of our biomimetic nanosystem as a bone marrow-targeted delivery platform that response to the acidic tumor microenvironment for combination therapy in the treatment of leukemia.

Results and discussion

Metformin synergizes with asparaginase in suppressing leukemia in vitro

To explore the potential synergistic effects of metformin and asparaginase in leukemia treatment, we performed a series of in vitro assays using leukemia cell lines, including Jurkat, Nalm-6, and C1498 cells. Cells were treated with varying concentrations of metformin, asparaginase, or their combination for 72 h, followed by cell viability assessment, as depicted in Fig. 2A-D and Figure S1. Across all tested concentrations, the combination of asparaginase and metformin remarkably inhibited cell proliferation compare to either drug alone (Fig. 2E-F). To further examine whether metformin enhances the pro-apoptotic effect of asparaginase, apoptosis was evaluated by flow cytometry (Fig. 2G-H), which revealed a markedly increased apoptotic rate with the combined treatment. Additionally, cell cycle analysis demonstrated that the combined treatment exhibited a superior inhibitory effect on tumor cell proliferation than monotreatment (Fig. 2I-J). These results indicate that metformin synergizes with asparaginase to enhance anti-leukemic activity by promoting apoptosis and inhibiting cell proliferation.

Construction and characterization of AMNPs@CM

After confirming the synergetic effect of metformin and asparaginase, we proceeded to co-load them into nanoparticles. Metformin-loaded calcium carbonate (CaCO3) nanoparticles were initially synthesized using a gas diffusion method. These nanoparticles were then PEGylated with a phospholipid bilayer to enhance their dispersibility and stability in aqueous environment, yielding MetNPs. Leukemia cell membranes were obtained through repeated freeze-thaw cycles and conjugated with asparaginase via click chemistry, forming CMs/Asp. Subsequently, MetNPs and CMs/Asp were ultrasonically processed and extruded to construct the final hybrid nanoparticle system, AMNPs@CM, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. Figure S2 showed that the particle size of the CaCO3 nanoparticles can be controlled by adjusting the proportion of DMSO in the reaction system. The hydrodynamic diameter of AMNPs@CM nanoparticles was approximately 197 nm, as measured by dynamic light scattering (Fig. 3B). Meanwhile, TEM results showed that the size of the MetNPs significantly increases after loading Met and the dispersibility of AMNPs significantly improved after being PEGylated with a phospholipid bilayer (Figure S3-S4). Meanwhile, the UV-Vis analysis was used to determine the loading capacity of Met into CaCO3 nanoparticles (Figure S5). The drug loading efficiencies (DLE) of Met and Asp in final AMNPs@CM nanoparticles were 46.6 ± 1.0% and 5.7 ± 0.5% respectively. Additionally, the zeta potentials of MetNPs, CM/Asp, and AMNPs@CM were measured as 15.42, −14.50, and − 7.22 mV, respectively (Fig. 3C). These changes in hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potentials between AMNPs @CM and MetNPs initially indicate that the leukemia cell membrane has successfully been encapsulated on the surface of the MetNPs (Figure S6).

To evaluate the stability of AMNPs@CM nanoparticles, they were incubated in cell culture medium and their hydrodynamic size and polydispersity index (PDI) were monitored over 24 h. As shown in Figs. 3D-E, both parameters remained stable, indicating excellent nanoparticle stability. Furthermore, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed that calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) elements were evenly distributed in the nanoparticles. (Fig. 3F). The Ca signal originated from the CaCO3 core, while the P and sulfur (S) signal were attributed from the cell membrane. To investigated the pH-responsive behavior of AMNPs@CM in a tumor acidic environment, the nanoparticles were incubated at pH 5.8. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed structural disintegration of AMNPs@CM under this condition (Fig. 3G), indicating their sensitivity to tumor acidic microenvironment. Correspondingly, changes in hydrodynamic size and PDI further confirmed the pH-responsive release properties of AMNPs@CM nanoparticles (Fig. 3H), suggesting their potential for targeted drug delivery in the acidic tumor microenvironment. Finally, the in vitro release profiles of Met from AMNPs@CM were evaluated under pH 7.4 and pH 5.5 respectively. As showed in Figure S7, Met was rapidly released from AMNPs@CM in acidified PBS (pH = 5.5), achieving a high cumulative drug release of 60% within 8 h, which was about ten times to the rate under a neutral pH (7.4) at the same time. Collectively, these results demonstrate that AMNPs@CM nanoparticles possess acid-responsive properties and can release the loaded drugs in the acidic tumor microenvironment.

It is reported that the leukemia cells can home to the bone marrow because of the expression of CXCR4 on their cell membranes [21, 22]. Thus, we assumed that leukemia cell membranes decorated CaCO3 nanoparticles could inherit the bone marrow homing ability of the leukemia cells. Therefore, SDS-PAGE and western blot (WB) analyses were conducted to examine the retention of key membrane proteins after decoration onto AMNPs, including CXCR4, CD44, CD147, and CD47. As shown in Fig. 3I and Figure S8, these proteins were preserved on AMNPs@CM nanoparticles as intact cells, indicating that these leukemia cell membranes decorated CaCO3 nanoparticles could mimic the bone marrow homing ability of leukemia cells. Additionally, hemolysis testing using a direct contact method showed no significant hemolytic activity, with hemolysis percentages comparable to the negative control group (Fig. 3J). In vitro cytocompatibility was also assessed by incubating AMNPs@CM with Jurkat cells at various concentrations. No significant reduction in cell viability was observed, confirming excellent biocompatibility of AMNPs@CM nanoparticles (Fig. 3K-L).

Anti-leukemia effect of AMNPs@CM in vitro

To ensure the antitumor efficacy of AMNPs@CM nanoparticles, it was essential to evaluate their intracellular uptake behavior and tumor-targeting capacity. Therefore, the fluorescence probe DiD was incorporated into the nanoparticles to enable in vitro tracking. As shown in Fig. 4A, strong red fluorescence signals from DiDNPs were observed within RAW264.7 cells after incubation, whereas only weak fluorescence was detected in the DiDNPs@CM group. This observation was consistent with the results from flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 4B-C), which further confirmed a higher cellular uptake of DiDNPs in RAW264.7 compared to DiDNPs@CM. These findings suggest that the homologous leukemia cell membrane coating on DiDNPs confers immune evasion properties, thereby reducing nonspecific uptake by macrophages. To investigate whether DiDNPs@CM could specifically target tumor cells, the leukemia cells were incubated with DiDNPs@CM for 4 h to assess the effect of membrane coating on cellular uptake. As shown in the CLSM images, Jurkat cells endocytosed a greater number of DiDNPs@CM compared to DiDNPs (Fig. 4D), indicating that the membrane coating conferred enhanced recognition and binding ability to the source cells. Consistent with this observation, flow cytometry analysis further confirmed significantly higher uptake of DiDNPs@CM by leukemia cells (Fig. 4E-F). The results demonstrate that the DiDNPs@CM nanoparticles exhibited superior targeting specificity towards homotypic tumor cells, attributed to the biomimetic properties imparted by the homologous cell membrane coating.

After confirming the effective immune evasion and tumor cell-targeting capabilities of DiDNPs@CM nanoparticles, we further assessed their in vitro anti-tumor efficacies by evaluating cell proliferation, cytotoxicity, and apoptosis across different treatment groups. Cell proliferation was analyzed using an EdU Cell Proliferation assay, which revealed that AMNPs@CM treatment significantly inhibited the proliferation ability of leukemia cells compared with other groups, as evidenced by both qualitative imaging and quantitative analysis (Fig. 4G-H). To further investigate cytotoxicity and apoptosis, Calcein-AM/PI staining and Annexin V-FITC/PI assays were performed. As shown in Fig. 5A, Jurkat cells treated with AMNPs@CM displayed prominent red fluorescence, indicating substantial cell death. However, cells in the control and other treatment groups exhibited intact cell morphology and predominantly green fluorescence, suggesting high viability and minimal cytotoxic effects. Moreover, Annexin V-FITC/PI staining showed that nearly 50% of the leukemia cells underwent apoptosis following AMNPs@CM treatment (Fig. 5B). To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, key protein markers related to cell proliferation and apoptosis were examined by WB analysis. As exhibited in Fig. 5C-F, the expression levels of proteins related to the cell cycle (such as CDK4 and CDK6) significantly decreased in AMNPs@CM-treated leukemia cells, indicating cell cycle arrest. Meanwhile, the expression levels of apoptosis-related proteins such as Bcl-2 and Bcl-xl was significantly downregulated in the AMNPs@CM group. Just as shown in Figure S9, the red fluorescence from Rhod-2 AM and the green fluorescence from DCF in the AMNPs@CM treatment groups could be observed in leukemia cells. In addition, the mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using the JC-1 probe to detect the integrity of the mitochondria. As shown in Figure S9, intense red fluorescence from Rhod-2 AM and green fluorescence from DCFH-DA were observed in leukemia cells treated with AMNPs@CM, indicating a rise in intracellular calcium and ROS levels. Furthermore, mitochondrial damage was assessed by measuring alterations in the mitochondrial membrane potential using the JC-1 probe. The confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images in Fig. 5G revealed that leukemia cells treated with AMNPs@CM exhibited minimal red fluorescence and intense green fluorescence, confirming that the mitochondrial integrity was significantly compromised. These results indicate that AMNPs@CM exert potent anti-leukemic effects by inducing apoptosis through mitochondrial disruption and inhibiting cell proliferation via cell cycle arrest.

To further explore the antileukemic mechanisms of AMNPs@CM nanoparticles, transcriptomic sequencing analysis was conducted on leukemia cells treated with AMNPs@CM. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed significant transcriptional differences between the AMNPs@CM treated group and the control groups (Fig. 6A). 637 differentially expressed genes were identified in total, including 420 upregulated (log2 FC ≥ 1 and q < 0.05) and 217 downregulated genes (log2 FC ≤ − 1 and q < 0.05) (Fig. 6B). A heatmap visualization highlighted the differential expression patterns between the AMNPs@CM and control groups (Fig. 6C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 6D-E) indicated that differentially expressed genes were mainly related to biological processes like cell adhesion, calcium signaling pathway, and apoptosis. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis (Fig. 6F) further demonstrated significant enrichment in MAPK signaling pathway, Parkin-ubiquitin proteasomal system pathway and Unfolded protein response pathways. In addition, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that pathways related to apoptosis, calcium signaling pathway and autophagy were positively enriched in AMNPs@CM treated cells (Fig. 6G), suggesting these processes may underlie the therapeutic efficacy of AMNPs@CM.

Anti-leukemia effect and biosafety evaluation of AMNPs@CM in vivo

We next investigated the bone marrow-targeting ability and therapeutic efficacy of AMNPs@CM in vivo. To highlight the benefits of leukemia cell membrane coating, we compared the bone marrow homing efficiency of uncoated CaCO₃ nanoparticles (DiDNPs) and cell membrane coated CaCO₃ nanoparticles (DiDNPs@CM). Following intravenous injection, in vivo fluorescence imaging revealed that DiDNPs@CM exhibited significantly enhanced accumulation in the bone marrow compared to undecorated DiDNPs. Notably, the nanoparticles rapidly localized to the bone marrow as early as 9 h post-injection (Fig. 7A), exhibiting significantly stronger fluorescence signals in the bone marrow than DiDNPs. This observation further confirms their enhanced and active bone marrow targeting capability conferred by the cell membrane coating. The mice were then sacrificed and we collected the main organs including femur tissues for imaging ex vivo (Fig. 7B-C and Figure S10). The DiDNPs@CM group had the maximum fluorescence intensity, indicating bone marrow homing potential, which was in line with the findings from the imaging in vivo.

Subsequently, the anti-leukemia effect of AMNPs@CM was assessed in C1498 leukemia-bearing mouse models and the animal experiment setup was shown in Figure S11A. Bioluminescence imaging demonstrated a marked reduction in tumor burden in the AMNPs@CM-treated mice compared to controls (Fig. 7D). Meanwhile, the survival period of leukemic mice treated with AMNPs@CM was significantly prolonged compared with the other control groups (Figure S11B). Consistently, Wright-Giemsa staining of peripheral blood smears revealed substantial depletion of leukemic blasts in the AMNPs@CM group (Fig. 7E). These findings were further supported by flow cytometry, which confirmed a pronounced suppression of leukemia cells in the bone marrow (Fig. 7F andFigure S11C). Correspondingly, the weight of spleens was remarkably reduced in mice treated with AMNPs@CM group (Figure S11D-E). Overall, these results demonstrate the potent anti-leukemia activity and effective bone marrow targeting capability of AMNPs@CM in vivo. Finally, we evaluated the biological safety of AMNPs@CM in C57BL/6J mice (Figure S12A). We tested the peripheral blood hemogram of mice, and found that AMNPs@CM had no significant toxicity on all the parameters of peripheral blood routine and blood biochemistry (Figure S12B-K). Finally, the H&E staining results of organs also proved the biosafety of AMNPs@CM since no significant pathological damage was observed in the main visceral organs (Figure S12L). Taken together, these results show that the AMNPs@CM are safe in mice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, inspired by the phenomenon asparaginase-induced apoptosis, we developed a leukemia cell membrane-functionalized nanoparticle system (AMNPs@CM) to realize targeted and synergistic leukemia therapy. The cancer cell membrane coating endowed the AMNPs@CM with immune evasion, tumor-targeting, and bone marrow homing properties. Upon reaching the tumor site, the AMNPs@CM) exhibited pH-responsive release of the loaded asparaginase and metformin, enabling effective localized drug delivery. metformin further amplified asparaginase-induced apoptosis by enhancing asparagine deprivation effects. As a result, AMNPs@CM treatment led to mitochondrial dysfunction, cytoskeleton disruption and oxidative stress, ultimately inducing robust apoptotic responses in leukemia cells. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed that AMNPs@CM exhibited potent anti-leukemia efficacy with precise targeting and minimal systemic toxicity, consequently achieving cancer cell apoptosis and tumor inhibition. These findings suggest that AM@CM represents a promising and innovative strategy for targeted leukemia therapy, offering enhanced therapeutic efficacy and improved biosafety.
