Introduction

Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) is a surgery in which direct communication is made between the lacrimal sac and the nasal cavity through a bony ostium to bypass an obstruction in the nasolacrimal duct (NLD) and therefore facilitate tear drainage.[12] There are two main types of DCR: external and endonasal. The external DCR is the gold standard procedure and the most preferred to treat primary acquired nasolacrimal duct obstruction (PANDO), with a reported success rate that reaches up to 97%.[34] This surgical procedure has undergone some modifications throughout the years, one of which is whether to use silicone intubation or not.[4] In the 1970s, many studies advocated the use of silicone intubation to maintain the patency of the bony ostium created in the DCR. Later on, other studies reported increased surgery failure with silicone intubation due to granulomatous inflammation ignited by the presence of the silicone tube in the cavity.[5] This issue is still discussed in recent literature and is still debatable. Our study aims to find out the success rate of the anterior flap technique of DCR with and without silicone intubation in patients attending a tertiary hospital.

Materials and Methods

The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained.

In this retrospective cohort study, clinical records of all patients who underwent DCR surgery at a tertiary eye care hospital between 2017 and 2023 were reviewed. Pediatric patients who were <12 years of age at the time of the surgery, patients who had canalicular or common canalicular obstruction, patients who had repeat surgeries in previous failed DCR, and patients with secondary NLD obstruction (NLDO) were excluded. All consecutive cases of PANDO admitted for surgery and completed their 6-month follow-up till January 2023 were included. Out of five hundred and one records, one hundred and seventy-seven records fulfilled the inclusion criteria and were included in this study.

Patients included had follow-up at 1 week, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks postoperatively. All patients who missed their follow-up were excluded from the study.

From the data retrieved, anatomical success was determined by patency on syringing and/or negative fluorescein dye disappearance test, whereas functional success was determined by the symptomatic relief from epiphora according to Munk’s score (grades zero to one were taken as success).

The data obtained were analyzed with frequency and descriptive statistics. Statistical significance was determined via the Chi-square test. P < 0.05 is considered as statistically significant value.

Surgical steps

DCR surgery is performed under general anesthesia. A ten millimeter curvilinear incision is given in the skin at the anterior lacrimal crest, followed by suborbicularis dissection. The anterior lacrimal crest is reached, and the medial canthal tendon is identified. The periosteum anterior to the medial canthal tendon was incised and reflected to expose the lacrimal fossa. The lacrimal sac was carefully reflected laterally, exposing the lacrimal fossa. Bony rongeurs were used to create a large osteotomy of 15 mm × 15 mm, removing both lacrimal and maxillary bones to reach the nasal mucosa. A nasal pack was introduced at this stage. Nasal mucosal and lacrimal mucosal flaps were fashioned, and the posterior flaps were sacrificed. The nasal pack was removed, and the anterior flaps were sutured using 6-0 Vicryl suture with or without silicon intubation. Syringing was done to check the patency of the tract, followed by repeated nasal packing. The medial canthal tendon was repositioned. The incision was closed in two layers, and the wound was dressed with antibiotic ointment and paraffin gauze. Postoperatively, systemic antibiotics and systemic anti-inflammatory drugs were given for 5 days. Xylometazoline nasal drop was prescribed for 1 week, and antibiotic and corticosteroid eye drops were given topically for 1 month.

Results

One hundred and seventy-seven eyes were included in this study, of which thirty-two patients (18.1%) were male, and one hundred and forty-five patients were female (81.9%). The mean age was 47.75 (standard deviation: 14.88 years, range: 12–91 years).

DCR with silicone intubations was done in seventy-seven eyes (43.5%), while DCR without intubation was considered in one hundred eyes (56.5%).

Overall, the functional success of DCR was found to be 87.6% (n = 155), and the anatomical success was 89.8% (n = 159). The functional success of DCR with silicone intubation was 90.9% (n = 70), and without intubation was 85% (n = 85) (P = 0.261, confidence interval [CI]: 95%), whereas the anatomical success of the surgery with intubation was 90.9% (n = 70), and without intubation was 89% (n = 89) (P = 0.804, CI: 95%).

Discussion

Epiphora, acute, and chronic dacryocystitis are the main indications for performing DCR. It is common in females due to the smaller diameter of the bony NLD.[6] That explains the predominance of females (81.9%) in our results. DCR has multiple approaches, whether the best would be endoscopic, external, or laser-assisted transcanalicular DCR is debatable in the literature. In a systematic review and meta-analysis for secondary NLDO, the best modality of treatment of distal NLDO is external DCR, followed by endoscopic followed by transcanalicular DCR, although the difference between external and endoscopic DCR was statistically insignificant (P = 1.00).[7] Similar results were found in cases of primary NLDO,[8] and hence, it is debatable which procedure should be the mainstay of treatment. Vinciguerra et al. suggested that endoscopic DCR should be reserved for recurrent cases and primary cases with nasal pathologies, where external DCR is the choice when you choose to do the surgery under local anesthesia; otherwise, it is still debatable, and the floor is open for the surgeon to choose.[9] In another recently published systematic review and meta-analysis, endoscopic DCR was found to be less successful compared to external DCR (80.3% vs. 91.6%; odds ratio: 0.41; 95% CI: [0.27, 0.62]; P < 00001; I2 = 13%), but the difference was statistically insignificant. Endoscopic DCR was found to have less surgical time, less bleeding, less infection rate, but also less successful compared to external DCR.[10] External DCR may be performed in many ways, as mentioned in the literature. It can be performed by anastomosing both anterior and posterior flaps or anterior flaps only, it may be done with or without mitomycin C (MMC) or 5-fluorouracil (5 FU), or it may be performed with or without a silicon stent.[11] In our series, all the external DCRs were performed as a single flap technique with or without silicon intubation, and no MMC or 5 FU was used. There is no consensus about silicon stenting in primary DCR surgery. Some studies suggest that it increases the success rate,[12] while some suggest that there is no difference in the outcome.[71314] Regarding the duration of silicon stenting, a randomized clinical trial suggested that silicon intubation for short term (2–3 weeks) had a higher success rate than intubation for 3 months or without intubation; however, the results were statistically insignificant.[15] Another study suggests that 1 week of intubation was sufficient for long-term success.[16] Our series showed that DCR with intubation has a higher success rate than without intubation. However, it was also statistically insignificant (P = 0.80). Regarding the posterior flaps, whether to suture it or not makes no difference, as there is no statistically significant data that show any superiority of one procedure over the other.[17] In our results, the functional success of DCR was 87.6%, and the anatomical success was 89.8%. The functional success of DCR with silicone intubation was 90.9% and without intubation was 85%, while the anatomical success of the surgery with intubation was 90.9% and without intubation was 89%. The functional success was assessed by Munk’s grades, where zero-to-one was considered as a success and two-to-five is a failure. Although the functional success rate was found to be higher among patients with silicone intubation compared to those without silicone intubation (90.9% vs. 85%), the difference was statistically insignificant (P = 0.261). When the anatomical success rate is higher than the functional success rate, the difference is usually labeled as a functional failure. Lee et al. studied the risk factors of functional failure in external DCR, which was attributed to canalicular and common canalicular block; however, these causes were excluded from our study. In addition, they found that in primary NLDO, a small lacrimal sac was a risk of functional failure.[18] Our results matched the data of other studies present in the literature on the anatomical and functional success rate of external DCR. Anatomical failure of external DCR was reported to be caused by many factors, such as the closure of the ostium, narrow nasal cavity, the anterior position of the ethmoid sinus, canalicular obstruction, smaller lacrimal sac, thick maxilla, fibrous tissue growth, inappropriate size or location of bony ostium, polyposis, or an active systemic disease.[11] On the other hand, the only risk factor that was found to be related to functional failure was a small lacrimal sac.[15]

Conclusion

No statistically significant difference was found between the success rate of the anterior flap technique of DCR with or without silicone intubation. Overall, the functional success and the anatomical success of DCR were found to be 87.6% and 89.8%, respectively, which were similar to the other studies published in the literature.
