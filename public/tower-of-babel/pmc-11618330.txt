Dichotomous horizontal representation of acute deterioration risk on illnesses
Authors: Ryo Hishiya, Masami Ishihara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The possibility that risks interact with spatial information (such as the SNARC effect) has been explored but studies did not demonstrate a consistent left-to-right representation of risks. This is probably due to the perception of risk being different in each individual experience. The present study aimed to clarify the spatial characteristics of acute deterioration risks perceived from illnesses. Registered nurses and general students participated in the present experiment. They were instructed to judge, by pressing one of the left/right response buttons, whether the risk of a given target (illness name) presented on a computer screen was higher or lower compared to that of a standard stimulus. Reaction times to the target were measured. No spatial-risk association was observed in the comprehensive analysis, but further inspection revealed that approximately half of the participants have a left-to-right representation and the other half have a right-to-left representation. Moreover, participants’ responses appeared to be faster when the target was representationally farther away from the standard stimulus (i.e., distance effect). These findings therefore suggest a spatially aligned magnitude representation of acute deterioration risk (i.e., mental risk line) but this horizontal risk orientation is likely to be dichotomous depending on the individuals and/or given situations.

Introduction

In clinical spaces, nurses generally have a large number of opportunities to intervene with inpatients. If they underestimate or overlook a patients’ risk of acute deterioration, it could pose a critical danger for patients. Nurses′ subjective prediction of acute deterioration was found to be more accurate than the objective indicators1. However, the psychophysical study reported that nurses′ detectability of acute deterioration risk was decreased under the situation of time pressure2. This indirectly suggests that nurses may not always necessarily keep maintaining their judgement accuracy at a high level in clinical spaces. For this reason, it is a crucial issue for both patients and nurses that nurses acquire a balanced ability to perceive the risk “promptly” (i.e., demand for speed) and “precisely” (i.e., demand for accuracy)3. Therefore, empirical studies to clarify the functional mechanism of the nurses′ judgement concerning acute deterioration risk are essential and contribute to valuable insights into addressing this issue.

It has been reported that risks interact with spatial information encoded from left to right in human perceptual and behavioral information processing. In the previous study, participants were asked to compare the degree of the mortality risk of daily activities, such as cycling and base jumping, with the risk of reference activities, by pressing one of two horizontally aligned response buttons. The results showed that left-side responses were faster than right-side responses when participants perceived the activities as of low risk, whereas right-side responses were faster than left-side responses when they perceived the activities as of high risk4.

This finding is akin to the phenomenon that was originally observed in numerical information processing, called the SNARC (Spatial-Numerical Association of Response Codes) effect. In the left-to-right reading and writing culture, it has also been demonstrated that the left-side responses were faster than right-side responses when a smaller number was presented, while the reverse responses occurred when a larger number was presented5. This SNARC effect suggests the existence of left-to-right spatial properties of numbers in human internal representation, the so-called mental number line. Beyond numerical information, other stimuli have also shown the SNARC-like effect: musical pitch6,7, loudness8, luminance9, weight10, ordinal information such as week, month, and alphabet11,12, and temporal duration13–15. Even if the experimental stimuli are abstract concepts, participants’ behavioral performance is influenced by spatiality corresponding to their metaphorical representation (e.g., rationality-emotion16). Similar to these phenomena, the risk-related information can have spatial characteristics and show the SNARC-like effect, possibly suggesting the existence of a “mental risk line” in our internal representation.

The theory that comprehensively integrates these space-quantity associations, A Theory Of Magnitude (ATOM), proposes that a common coding system (i.e., inferior parietal cortex) plays a role in processing the numbers (or non-numerical magnitude information), space, and time17. Thus, the theory suggests that SNARC and the related effects can be generalized as the Spatial-Quantity Association of Response Codes (SQUARC) effects. At the same time, the results of the line bisection task, one of the neuropsychological tests, also support ATOM. In general, when healthy participants bisect a line, the midpoint tends to be slightly biased to the left from the true midpoint (i.e., pseudoneglect)18. While the patients with a right parietal lesion have difficulty of paying attention to the left-side space, the midpoint of the line bisection is consequently shown as a rightward bias19. Similar to the rightward bias for physical lines, when patients with left hemispace neglect are asked to answer a midpoint between two numbers, they tend to answer a number biased to the right from the true midpoint20,21. Moreover, when participants were asked to bisect a line consisting of the strings of the words meaning “2” or “9” in French (DEUX, NEUF), they positioned the midpoint of the bisection to “9” on the right side compared to the midpoint to “2”22. These phenomena suggest that magnitude information brings spatial biases. Therefore, these findings can be considered within a framework of ATOM in which all magnitude-related information (i.e., quantity) and spatial information do share the perceptomotor processes in a given environment.

These space-quantity associations were also observed in studies of newly enrolled first-graders23, preschool-aged children24,25, prelinguistic infants26,27, and newborns28,29, suggesting that they may be inherent functional characteristics. Although newborns are not naturally able to read and write, they tended to gaze at the left side when the number of presented dots was fewer than that of the reference stimulus, whereas they tended to gaze at the right side when the number of dots was more than the reference28. Furthermore, behavioral studies also suggested recently that chimpanzees30, gorillas, orangutans31, rhesus monkeys32, Clark’s nutcrackers33, chicks34–36, and even bees37 may have an association between space and quantity. For example, three-day-old chicks tended to select the left side when the number of target elements in a given set was smaller, and the right side when the number of elements was larger34,35. The observation that both humans and non-humans, even at birth, demonstrate a left-to-right spatial orientation of magnitude-related information indicates the existence of a default axis in the spatial-quantity association. To explain this phenomenon of specific spatial directionality, a hypothesis that considers the perspective of functional lateralization of processing emotion induced by stimuli has also been proposed38. Therefore, living beings, not just humans, are likely to have an innate ability to spatially integrate magnitude information (see also Rugani & de Hevia39), although, in some cases, the spatial direction (i.e., left-to-right or right-to-left) may vary depending on individuals31,40.

Conversely, in a right-to-left reading and writing culture, their mental number line was shown to be a reverse mapping of that observed in a left-to-right reading and writing culture5 (see also Rossetti et al.41). Additionally, in bilinguals, the strength of the associations between space and quantity was varied depending on the presented letters42. These findings therefore suggest that the knowledge and/or culture that we have habitually acquired may have influenced the expression of spatial representation concerning magnitude information. Moreover, not only long-term but also temporarily defined contexts can influence the spatial direction. It was indicated, for example, that the spatial mapping was reversed depending on the stimuli presented with a different numerical mapping (e.g., ruler or analog clock face)43,44. In the study of Bächtold et al.43, participants showed a left-to-right spatial-numerical association after receiving a context from 1 to 12 cm with a ruler, whereas they showed a right-to-left association after receiving a context from 1 to 12 o’clock with an analog clock face. Therefore, the spatial representation of humans is expected to be flexible depending on a given situation, allowing our behavior to adapt to the environment.

However, unlike the spatial-numerical associations, the degree of perceived risk is predicted to vary widely depending on the experience and/or values of the individuals. The previous study conducted by Macnamara et al.4 suggested that risks, abstract concepts without units, were possibly encoded from left to right as an internal representation. Their scientific achievement contributed to providing valuable evidence to consider the applicable scope of ATOM and to understanding the functional characteristics of human perceptual and behavioral information processing. In addition, risk estimations have been shown to be modulated by the ability to comprehend and utilize basic numerical information or concepts45–47. Furthermore, it has also been observed that the mapping bias of acute risk evaluations on everyday activities exhibited a resembling tendency to the bias observed in the number line task, where participants are required to map given numbers onto a line48. These findings indirectly suggest that risks share similar characteristics with quantitative information and may be interpreted as a part of a general magnitude system. Macnamara et al.4 also mentioned that further research is required to confirm the robustness of this phenomenon concerning risks since the effect size in their study was small. For example, the previous study reported that the facial expression, which is generally difficult to express as quantitative information as well as the risk, was also possibly assigned from the left to right direction depending on its magnitude49. However, a subsequent recent investigation revealed that the SNARC-like effect on facial expression was brought about rather by the mouth size accompanying changes of facial expression50. The more abstract or complicated the information of experimental stimuli presented, the more the dimensions related to magnitude supposedly increase. Therefore, a multi-faceted examination is necessary to clarify the characteristics of spatial representation concerning abstract concepts.

The present study aims to investigate the spatial representation of other risks, i.e., the perceived acute deterioration risk concerning illnesses, so as to clarify the functional characteristics of nurses’ clinical judgement to the abstract concept without units. In medical education studies, it is considered that clinicians have a constructed mental representation of illnesses, which is called “illness script”51. The illness script represents a knowledge structure on illnesses and consists of at least three components: Fault (a pathophysiological mechanism), Enabling Conditions (the characteristics of patients such as age, gender, occupation, and contextual factors), and Consequences (such as signs and symptoms)52. It has been revealed qualitatively that the illness scripts of nurses consist of components similar to those of clinicians53. At the same time, the richness of these illness scripts is related to the diagnostic accuracy of medical staff51. As a matter of course, the illness information is essential for judging patients’ conditions. Those illnesses were therefore adopted as experimental stimuli in the present study.

The fundamental hypothesis of our study is that if the space-risk associations (SRA) share the same mechanism with the space-number associations (SNA), then some common characteristics would be observed between SRA and SNA. Firstly, we expected to observe the SRA such that the left-side responses would be faster than the right-side responses when participants perceive a target stimulus as a low-risk illness, whereas the right-side responses would be faster than the left-side responses when they perceive a target stimulus as a high-risk illness. This expectation is based on a likely horizontal direction of the predicted mental risk line extended from left to right. Secondly, we also expected to observe a distance effect that the response would be faster when the risk of a given illness was much lower or higher relative to that of a reference illness. This expectation is based on the distance effect which is often shown in the numerical information processing54, where the reaction times to the farther numerical magnitude appeared to be faster compared to those to the closer magnitude. The previous study experimentally manipulated the degree of risk using the micromort which is an index of the mortality rate of each activity4. In the present study, since the acute deterioration risk concerning illnesses is one of the abstract concepts without units, we defined the risk as representational distance information that was obtained from the results of our pre-experiment measuring the subjective degree of perceived acute deterioration risk on various illnesses.

Additionally, in the present study, the participants were Japanese. It is important to note that, in the previous study, Japanese participants showed a left-to-right SNARC effect under the parity judgment task using horizontally aligned response buttons55. Thus, their spatial orientation of mental number lines can be considered similar to that of Western cultures (see more detail on Japanese reading and writing style in the Materials & method section).

Results

Twenty-four Japanese registered nurses and 24 general students participated in this experiment. Participants were instructed to judge whether the target stimuli (i.e., randomly presented strings of names concerning 20 illnesses) represented to be at lower or higher risk compared to the standard stimulus (i.e., “prostate cancer”). To judge the risk presented, participants responded by pressing either the left or right button of the computer mouse with the index (placed on the left button) or middle finger (placed on the right button) of their right hand according to their perception on the degree of the risk. The conditions of response mapping were experimentally manipulated: (A) the Compatible condition, in which participants were asked to press the left button for a lower risk and the right button for a higher risk, and (B) the Incompatible condition, in which this mapping was reversed.

Spatial-risk association of response codes (SRARC) effect

To examine the existence of the SRARC effect in the risk comparison task, firstly, a mixed design three-way ANOVA was conducted on the RTs with a factorial design of the group (i.e., nurses/general students) as a between-subjects factor, and the response side (i.e., left/right) and the risk (i.e., Extreme Low/Low/High/Extreme High) as within-subjects factors. In cases where the sphericity assumption (Mauchly’s test) was violated, corrections were applied using the Greenhouse-Geisser method56,57. The assumption of the present study was that a significant interaction between the response side and the risk was observed if participants had a common horizontal spatial characteristic of the risk.

The analysis indicated a significant main effect of the response side [F (1, 46) = 4.09, p < .05, η2p = 0.082], with participants responding faster on the right side (967.9 ms) than on the left side (987.0 ms). The main effect of the risk [F (3, 138) = 20.61, ε = 0.78, p < .001, η2p = 0.309] and the interaction between the group and the risk [F (3, 138) = 6.37, ε = 0.78, p < .01, η2p = 0.122] were also significant. The analysis of the simple main effects showed no significant differences between the two groups at any risk level. On the other hand, the analysis showed significant simple main effects of the risk levels for both nurses [F (3, 69) = 16.18, p < .001] and general students [F (3, 69) = 6.46, p < .001]. Bonferroni-corrected post hoc multiple comparisons revealed that nurses responded faster to Extreme High-risk than to Low-risk [p < .05, d = 0.562] or High-risk [p < .001, d = 0.989], and even faster to Extreme Low-risk and Low-risk than to High-risk [Extreme Low - High, p < .001, d = 0.802; Low - High, p < .05, d = 0.428]. The post hoc analysis revealed that general students responded faster to Low-risk and Extreme High-risk than to High-risk [Low - High, p < .001, d = 0.323; Extreme High - High, p < .05, d = 0.294]. No other significant main effects or interactions were observed, particularly the interaction between the response side and the risk was not significant [F (3, 138) = 0.24, ε = 0.51, p = .72, η2p = 0.005] (Fig. 1A/B).

Secondly, the three-way ANOVA was conducted on the arcsine-transformed correct response rate with the same designs of ANOVA performed on the RTs. The analysis indicated a significant main effect of the group [F (1, 46) = 42.70, p < .001, η2p = 0.481], with nurses (89.0%) responding more accurately than general students (75.1%). The main effect of the risk [F (3, 138) = 22.92, ε = 0.68, p < .001, η2p = 0.333] and the interaction between the group and the risk [F (3, 138) = 3.55, ε = 0.68, p < .05, η2p = 0.072] were also significant. The analysis of the simple main effects showed significant differences between the two groups for Extreme Low-risk [F (1, 46) = 7.21, p < .05], for High-risk [F (1, 46) = 18.99, p < .001], and for Extreme High-risk [F (1, 46) = 20.64, p < .001], with nurses responding more accurately compared to general students at all three risk levels. In addition, the analysis showed significant simple main effects between the risk levels for both nurses [F (3, 69) = 12.22, p < .001] and general students [F (3, 69) = 13.90, p < .001]. Bonferroni-corrected post hoc multiple comparisons analysis revealed that nurses responded more accurately to Extreme Low-risk than to Low-risk [p < .01, d = 0.968] or High-risk [p < .01, d = 1.335], and even more accurately to Extreme High-risk than to Low-risk [p < .01, d = 1.042] or High-risk [p < .001, d = 1.410]. The post hoc analysis revealed that general students responded more accurately to Extreme Low-risk, Low-risk, and Extreme High-risk than to High-risk [Extreme Low - High, p < .001, d = 1.638; Low - High, p < .01, d = 1.385; Extreme High - High, p < .001, d = 1.328]. However, no other significant main effects or interactions were observed (Fig. 1C/D).

The slope coefficient of the SRARC effect

To clarify both the strength and the directional preference of associations in each individual SRARC effect, a regression analysis was conducted for the results of individuals. The mean risk magnitudes of five illnesses at each risk level were used as independent variables, and the dRTs (the RTs of left-side response – the RTs of right-side response) were used as a dependent variable. The positive dRT indicates that the right-side responses tend to be faster than the left-side responses, and the negative dRT indicates that the left-side responses tend to be faster than the right- side responses. In the present study, the calculated slope coefficient indicates both the strength of spatial-risk associations (i.e., larger values indicate stronger associations and smaller values indicate weaker associations) and the directional preference of associations (i.e., positive values, left-to-right mapping; negative values, right-to-left mapping). A one-sample t-test was performed on the slope coefficient for each group. The analysis indicated that neither the slope coefficient of nurses nor that of general students differed significantly from zero [nurses: t (23) = 1.02, p = .32, d = 0.208; general students: t (23) = 0.20, p = .84, d = 0.042] (Fig. 2A/B). In addition, an independent samples t-test between nurses and general students showed no significant difference in the slope coefficient [t (46) = 0.89, p = .38, d = 0.257].

The scatter plot indicates the relationship between the RTs and the slope coefficient across all participants (Fig. 2C). The results indicated that approximately half of the participants (56.3%, 27 participants; 16 nurses and 11 general students) showed a slope with a positive value (i.e., left-to-right spatial orientation of the risk) and the other half (43.8%, 21 participants; 8 nurses and 13 general students) showed a slope with a negative value (i.e., right-to-left spatial orientation of the risk). Thus, the direction of the spatial representation on the risk was dichotomous among participants.

Additionally, the slope coefficient was separately calculated on the basis of the response of the first half and that of the second half in the task (Fig. 2D). Figure 2D indicates that the number of participants who showed the positive value of their slope increased in the second half (68.8%, 33 participants; 17 nurses and 16 general students) compared to the first half (52.1%, 25 participants; 14 nurses and 11 general students). We discuss this later in the Discussion section.

Subjective preference for the response mapping

Subjective preference for the conditions of the response mapping, i.e., conditions perceived as easier to perform by participants during the experiment, was elicited from each participant after completion of the risk comparison task (Table 1). The results of the Chi-square test indicated that the relationship between the group and the preferable mapping was not significant [χ2 (1) = 2.09, p = .15]. While more nurses tended to prefer the compatible mapping over the incompatible mapping and more general students tended to prefer the incompatible mapping over the compatible mapping, the statistical analysis showed no significance. The analysis also indicated that the relationship between the preferable mapping and the spatial orientation indexed by the slope coefficient was not significant [χ2 (1) = 1.27, p = .26]. Therefore, the preferable response mapping consciously evaluated by participants was not associated with the tendency of their manual responses.

Distance effect

To examine the existence of the distance effect, a correlation analysis was performed between the RTs to each illness and the representational distance from the standard stimulus, which was previously defined on the basis of the results of our pre-experiment (see the Materials & method section). The absolute value of the distance was used in the analysis. The results showed a significant negative correlation for nurses [r = − .69, p < .001] (Fig. 3A), but not for general students [r = − .12, p = .62] (Fig. 3B). Thus, the nurses responded faster to illnesses that were farther away in representational distance than to those that were closer.

Additional analysis

The orientation of participants’ spatial representation appeared to be influenced by the order of the conditions that participants completed. In this experiment, the order of the conditions was counterbalanced among participants: A-B-A-B or B-A-B-A. Here, to examine the effect of presentation order, a mixed design three-way ANOVA was performed on the dRTs with a factorial design of the starting condition (i.e., Compatible/Incompatible) and the group (i.e., nurse/general student) as between-subjects factors, and the risk (i.e., Extreme low/Low/High/Extreme high) as a within-subjects factor. The analysis showed that the main effect of the starting condition [F (1, 44) = 10.30, p < .01, η2p = 0.190] and the interaction between the starting condition and the risk [F (3, 132) = 29.38, ε = 0.56, p < .001, η2p = 0.400] were significant (Fig. 4). The analysis of the simple main effect showed significant differences between the risk levels in each starting condition [Compatible, F (3, 66) = 8.97, p < .001; Incompatible, F (3, 66) = 26.47, p < .001]. Bonferroni-corrected post hoc multiple comparisons analysis revealed that, for the compatible condition, dRT of Extreme Low-risk was significantly different from that of High-risk [p < .01, d = 1.177] and Extreme High-risk [p < .001, d = 1.135], and dRT of Low-risk was significantly different from that of Extreme High-risk [p < .05, d = 1.058]. Similarly, for the incompatible condition, dRTs of Extreme Low-risk and Low-risk were significantly different from those of High-risk and Extreme High-risk [Extreme Low - High, p < .001, d = 1.951; Extreme Low - Extreme High, p < .001, d = 2.110; Low - High, p < .01, d = 1.647; Low - Extreme High, p < .001, d = 1.806]. Therefore, the orientation of their representation concerning the risk was mediated by the response mapping of the first block and/or the order of the conditions. The results shown in Fig. 4 did not show a continuous point-to-point mapping but rather a categorical mapping of the risk.

Lastly, we performed the above analysis including trials that were defined as incorrect responses based on the pre-manipulated risk with four levels in the present study. To compare the results with those from the perspective of individual risk perception, the strength of the SRARC effect was recalculated based on the manipulated risk, which was binarily categorized as low or high risk. Another slope coefficient was also calculated based on participants’ actual reactions during the experiment (reaction-based risk) and on the magnitude of the risk representation that was evaluated numerically by participants after the experiment (subjective risk). For both the reaction-based risk and the subjective risk, the slope coefficient was calculated in a binary manner (i.e., only low or high) in each risk definition; there are no incorrect responses.

A mixed design two-way ANOVA was conducted on the slope coefficients with a factorial design of the group (i.e., nurses/general students) as a between-subjects factor, and the risk definition (i.e., manipulated risk/reaction-based risk/subjective risk) as a within-subjects factor. The results showed that neither the main effect of the group [F (1, 46) = 0.75, p = .39, η2p = 0.016], the main effect of the risk definition [F (2, 92) = 0.15, p = .86, η2p = 0.003], nor the interaction between the two factors were significant [F (2, 92) = 0.63, p = .54, η2p = 0.013]. Thus, these results imply no qualitative difference in the interpretation of the representational risk described above for each group, even when the effect of each risk definition is considered.

Discussions

The present study investigated the spatial representation concerning risks, especially the acute deterioration risk, from a behavioral approach. The risk degree of the presented illness names was experimentally manipulated on the basis of the results of our pre-experiment, which applied the magnitude estimation method with the aim of identifying magnitudes on nurses’ risk perception of illnesses. To assess the spatial characteristics of the risk, participants in the present study were asked to press one of two horizontally aligned response buttons with the index or middle finger of their right hand according to their perceived risk.

The assumption of the present study is whether risks were associated with spatial information similar to how numerical information is, then the SRA would share some common characteristics with the SNARC effect on human manual responses. As shown in our results, however, the responses to low (or high) risk had no advantage on the left (or right) side compared to the right (or left) side in the comprehensive analysis for all participants. When the results were inspected individually, approximately half of the participants showed a left-to-right spatial orientation of risk, whereas the other half showed a right-to-left orientation of risk. Therefore, our results do not support the hypothesis but rather show a dichotomous pattern of spatial-risk representation.

There are a number of possible interpretations for the results that differ from our hypothesis. Firstly, even if participants’ reading and writing directions are identical across participants, not all participants necessarily show an identical spatial orientation but some of them show the opposite to their reading/writing directions (e.g., see studies of the SNARC effect58 or the STEARC effect13). Thus, it is not surprising that the reverse mapping was observed with risk judgement. However, the proportion of the participants who showed reverse mapping was obviously higher than in other studies. Then, the phenomenon on reverse mapping is possibly not only due to individual differences but also to some implicit factors underlying participants’ daily performance. To check the reliability of higher proportions of reverse mapping, the test-retest method should be used in future studies.

Secondly, as one of the implicit factors, the context given to the participants might also affect the direction of their spatial representation. In spatial-numerical associations, the observed mapping of the spatial representations was reversed depending on the numerical mapping of the image that was presented before the experiment43,44. Furthermore, when participants were asked in a different way (e.g., whether the facial expression indicated happiness or not, or whether the facial expression indicated anger or not), the spatial mapping on the emotional values was reversed even when the same stimuli were presented49. These studies imply the possibility that our internal representations are flexibly varied depending on a given situation and/or context. This flexible association between space and numbers has been also observed in non-humans31,40. The spatial orientation of gorillas and orangutans, for example, was found to reverse depending on the instructions of the experiment31. Such a flexible variation of the representational space depending on the context requires the activation of either magnitude information or order information of the presented stimuli59. In the present study, the participants’ task was to make a direct comparison concerning the risk degree, leading the participants to activate the risk-related magnitude information in their internal representation. Therefore, it is possible that the dichotomous representation of the participants was due to the influence of a given context.

In particular, the dichotomous representation of our results appeared to be influenced by the starting condition and/or the order of the conditions of the experiment. In fact, the rule of response mapping was linked to the direction of the SNARC effect observed in previous studies. In the task with mixed magnitude comparison and orientation judgement of numbers, for example, the response mapping rule was reflected in the spatial direction of the SNARC effect in orientation judgement60. Additionally, frequent switching of response mapping influenced the strength of the SNARC effect by minimizing the hand-based SNARC effect61. To our knowledge, the response rule learned during the practice phase could reinforce the participants’ spatial direction identical to the rule62, but no studies have shown the spatial representation opposite to the response rule in the “first block”. Even though the counterbalanced assignment, which might have influenced the expression of spatial representation, was appropriately conducted in our experimental procedure, these subtle changes of the context might have influenced the association between abstract concepts and internal spatial representation.

A third explanation for the observed reverse mapping is the relationship between risk and time. In ATOM, it is assumed that the information of quantity, space, and time are processed in a common coding system17. The left (or right) side is supposed to be associated with a small (or large) magnitude5 and with a shorter (or longer) temporal duration13–15. Based on the assumption that the lower (or higher) risk is associated with the left (or right) side, the lower (or higher) risk is expected to be associated with the shorter (or longer) temporal duration. However, higher-risk situations generally require us to act quickly, and this is the case in the clinical space as well. This means that the lower (higher) risk could be associated with a longer (or shorter) temporal duration, resulting in a reverse mapping of spatial-risk association. Therefore, the relationship between risk and time in the real world is not always congruent with what is predicted in the representational space. Such an incongruence may be one of the factors why the direction of the SRA was different among individuals.

From the above discussions, risks seem to be outside of the framework of ATOM. However, there was a significant negative correlation between the representational distance and the RTs of nurses. This showed that the further away from a standard stimulus (i.e., prostate cancer) in their internal representations, the faster their comparison judgements were. Indeed, this result suggests the distance effect that has been observed in numerical information processing54 and even in social cognition, like social status63. The distance effect is one piece of evidence that our perceived information is spatially mapped onto our representation. The previous study also mentioned that judging risk showed a resemblance pattern to the judgement of numerical distances48. Integrating this evidence suggests that risks, like other magnitude-related information, may also interact with spatial information. At the same time, given the slope coefficient in the second half of the experiment of each participant, the number of participants who indicated a left-to-right spatial orientation of risk increased. The previous study focusing on time course changes of the SNARC effect reported gradually increasing the strength of the compatibility effect in a direct magnitude comparison task58, supposing that practice on the task may also influence the expression of the compatibility effect. Our experiment was designed to give participants five practice trials in each block. However, if participants had engaged in more practice trials, the association between risk and space would have been then further strengthened by the practice, resulting in their manual responses consistent with a left-to-right encoded spatial representation of acute deterioration risk, akin to the SNARC effect. Perhaps risks can be thought of as scalar information with no inherent spatial direction. Number and time are associated with space from an early age through experiences in daily activities64, whereas risks have fewer opportunities to be repeatedly associated with space. It is therefore speculated that repeated performance of a risk comparison task with spatially aligned responses might trigger a scalar-to-vector transformation of risk but the transformed “direction” would highly depend on the individuals and/or situations, resulting in a dichotomous spatial representation of risk. Although our study has demonstrated that the nature of acute deterioration risk contains dichotomous spatial characteristics which are different from that of spatially-aligned magnitude information (e.g., SNARC effect), the possibility that risk shares perceptomotor processes with spatial information cannot be entirely excluded. The influence of individual/situational factors that may evoke a directionality (i.e., left-to-right or right-to-left) in risk judgement should be further investigated.

The dichotomous phenomenon may also be interpreted from other perspectives. One major hypothesis for explaining the SNARC effect is based on the polarity correspondence principle, which posits that spatial stimulus-response compatibility effects arise from the negative/positive polarity of the stimuli65,66. According to this principle, linguistic aspects of the instruction used in the present experiment, such as “low” and “high”, may be associated with “good (i.e., right space dominant)” and “bad (i.e., left space dominant)” polarization, resulting in reverse mapping. Given the results of Fig. 4, the risk appeared to be categorically represented in space with no statistical difference between Extreme Low (Extreme High) and Low (High) risk, but the distance effect was observed as discussed above. The presence of a distance effect is a manifestation that the risk stimuli, abstract concepts without units, used in the present study were quantified. Such quantified risk information, potentially accompanied by a good/bad polarity, might be then categorically mapped onto a right/left space.

Another hypothesis suggests that emotional valence being processed may bring left-to-right spatial representations, due to differential hemispheric dominance38 (other alternative hypotheses have also been proposed for the functional characteristics of emotion processing67). In terms of risk, negative emotions often arise, regardless of whether the risk affects the self or others. Given this motivational hypothesis, the stronger the negative emotions elicited by a greater risk, the more likely individuals are to indicate a right-to-left spatial representation, reflecting a preference for leftward space. It is possible that participants who exhibited right-to-left spatial representations in the present experiment were influenced by such negative emotions, whereas those who exhibited left-to-right representations might focus on the magnitude of the risk itself, without the influence of emotion. The relationship between the risk and emotional value also appears to be incongruent in representational space, akin to the incongruent relationship between risk and time as discussed above (interpretation #3). However, further discussion on this hypothesis is challenging because the parameters showing emotional states were not measured in the present study. In future research on the spatial representation of risk, subjective and/or physiological parameters are required to assess the emotional states.

In the present study, two groups (i.e., nurses and general students) differing in expertise concerning the knowledge of illness took part in our experiment, and the results showed a significant difference between the two groups in the accuracy of the task. The group assignment as an experimental manipulation for expertise was thus successful. According to the previous study, the expression of the SNARC-like effect is modulated by expertise concerning the presented stimuli used in the experiment6. However, our results indicated no significant group related to three-way interaction in both the RTs and the correct response rate, suggesting no difference among the groups in terms of the relationship between the response side and risk level. No group difference was also found for the subjective preferred mapping. Interestingly enough, some general students mentioned after the experiment that a left click on the response button (i.e., computer mouse) made them activate a representation of a “go forward” signal of mouse control. Such a device-related representation, rather than the risk representation per se, may have influenced the subjective preferred mapping in the general students who had limited knowledge of illnesses and difficulty accessing risk representation. This may lead those students to favor the incompatible response mapping compared to nurses. Conversely, the nurses may have been able to access the risk representation based on their knowledge of illnesses, as indicated by their higher correct response rate, thus favoring compatible conditions based on the left-to-right mental risk line. In addition, although the one-sample t-test was not significant, Fig. 2A may suggest a possible trend for nurses exhibiting a left-to-right spatial representation of the risk which is not observed in general students (Fig. 2B).

As a limitation of the present study, firstly, there might be a variation of each individual concerning the degree of the risk perception because the risk is an abstract concept. We defined acute deterioration as the conditions where inpatients fall into death, cardiopulmonary arrest, or require more advanced medical care such as ICU admission. We explained this definition to our participants in advance. However, since the situations in which nurses have been involved in patients’ acute deterioration naturally differ among them, it is unclear to what extent they access similar representations of each other. Creating experimental stimuli representing more concrete patient conditions is one possible way to induce nurses to activate a similar risk representation. In addition to inter-individual differences, consistency within individuals should also have been examined. In the present study, the slope coefficients changed between the first half and second half of the experiment. This suggests that within-individual variability may be present in the risk comparison task. Nonetheless, a test-retest approach should be considered to assess the consistency of each participant’s responses. Secondly, although the present study focused on a horizontally aligned spatial representation, the representations of other axes (i.e., vertical or sagittal axis) will be of particular interest. Actually, previous studies investigated other representational directions (SNARC68–70; SMARC6,7; STEARC13,71). When nurses perceive some risks in a clinical situation, they need to go forward to help someone or go back to protect their own lives. If the nature of perceived risk would be spatially determined by our actual behavior, the sagittal direction, other than the horizontal axis, should be further considered to clarify general spatial representations of risk. Lastly, the task adopted in the present study demanded participants to directly compare the risk of target stimuli with that of a standard stimulus. In the future, we should also investigate whether acute deterioration risk is implicitly associated with the spatial processing in our behavior, for example, by developing an indirect discrimination which is similar to the parity judgement used in the SNARC paradigm.

Finally, we consider the potential application of the SRA in the nursing field. The previous study reported that the detection speed of a stimulus for the left side was faster when a smaller number was presented in advance, and vice versa for the right side72. In addition, the evaluation of the spatially placed product was influenced by the spatial information of the temporal characteristics of the product (i.e., past-future)73. If the risk interacts with spatial information, then a spatial assignment of risk-related information may influence nurses’ or nursing students’ attention to the risk and their risk evaluation. This assumption can contribute to designing a physical/environmental space, such as the assignment of patients’ rooms and a display of an electronic medical record. Furthermore, this study also would contribute to medical education. In general, since abstract concepts are perceived experientially and subjectively by individuals, it is not easy to share their representation among medical and paramedical staff. By quantifying the spatial magnitude of risk representation and confirming the validity of the quantified risk via behavioral parameters, we may be able to share the internal risk representation of staff with others. In the future, we should also pay attention to whether higher values of the slope coefficient, corresponding to a strong SRA, are observed in the experienced staff, whereas lower values, corresponding to a weak SRA, are observed in the inexperienced staff.

In conclusion, this is the first attempt to clarify the functional characteristics of the nurses’ judgement concerning acute deterioration risk from the perspective of spatial-risk association. The findings revealed no generalized left-to-right spatial-risk association was observed in the comprehensive analysis. However, on the individual inspection, a different directional spatial-risk association was observed, and participants’ responses were faster when the target stimulus was representationally farther away from the standard stimulus (i.e., distance effect). These findings suggest the existence of spatially aligned magnitude representations of acute deterioration risk (i.e., mental risk line) but this horizontal risk orientation is likely to be dichotomous depending on individuals and/or even on situations. Given the incongruences between risk and time or emotion in representational space, risks may have dissimilar spatial characteristics to what is generally considered magnitude information, and such quantified risks may interact with space from left to right or right to left. Further research is needed to clarify whether the Spatial-Risk Association of Response Codes (SRARC) effect can be integrated into the SQUARC effect, more specifically, whether risks can be thought of as quantities accompanied by a specific spatial direction or fundamentally as scalars with no inherent spatial direction in perceptomotor information processing. These investigations will provide valuable insights into the functional mechanism underlying dichotomous risk representation.

Materials and methods

Participants

To determine the number of participants, a priori power analysis was performed using G*power (ver. 3.1.9.7)74,75 to focus on the interaction of two within-participant factors (i.e., the response side and the level of risk). When the effect size and the power were assumed 0.25 and 0.80 respectively, the analysis showed that a total of 24 participants would be needed. Twenty-four Japanese registered nurses (21 females; mean age = 32.0 years, SD = 7.6 years; mean years of nursing experience = 8.6 years, SD = 7.2 years) and 24 general students (13 females; mean age = 20.6 years, SD = 2.1 years) were recruited using snowball sampling method and completed in this experiment. All participants were naïve to the purpose of the study. Except for one nurse and one general student, participants were right-handed. No participants completed the pre-experiment, which was conducted to identify magnitudes on nurses’ risk perception of illnesses and to select the experiment stimuli for the present study (see the Experimental Stimuli section). In other words, no participants knew which stimuli were assigned to the high-risk or low-risk levels.

It should be noted that the Japanese have two patterns of reading and writing direction. One of the patterns is left-to-right reading and a writing direction until the beginning of the next line, and lines of sentences continue progressing from top to bottom in this pattern. The other pattern is top-to-bottom reading and writing direction, and lines continue progressing from right to left. Despite the unique reading and writing culture, Japanese speakers exhibited a left-to-right spatial representation, akin to the Western people, in their manual responses to the parity judgement task where horizontal response mapping was applied, and also a bottom-to-top spatial representation was observed in the task using vertical response mapping55. Thus, the spatial representation of numerical information in Japanese speakers aligns with that of Western culture.

Experimental stimuli

The experimental stimuli used in the present study were selected on the basis of the results of our pre-experiment completed by 20 registered nurses. In the pre-experiment, the magnitude estimation method, one of the psychophysical methods, was used to quantify the magnitude of the mental representation regarding the perceived acute deterioration risk of 30 various illnesses. The geometric mean of the values for each illness evaluated by 20 nurses was calculated. We adapted the hierarchical cluster analysis to the results of the pre-experiment and categorized 30 illnesses into 5 clusters. A cluster with the closest representational distance from the standard stimulus was excluded, and five illnesses were selected from each of the remaining four clusters. Consequently, a total of 20 illnesses were used as experimental stimuli in the present study (Table 2). In addition, the standard stimulus was determined to be prostate cancer, which was also used as the standard stimulus in the pre-experiment. The mean number of characters written horizontally in Japanese in each risk level was 5.2 (Extreme Low), 6.5 (Low), 3.4 (High), and 4.2 (Extreme High), respectively. The names of each illness in the present study were presented in white letters on a black background in the center of the display. The size of the letters was standardized into 70 pixels regardless of the number of characters.

Procedures

Participants were seated in front of a computer display (NEC LAVIE, Pro Mobile) and were instructed to judge, by pressing one of two left/right response buttons (computer mouse: Lenovo, ThinkPad USB Laser Mouse), whether the perceived acute deterioration risk of a given target stimuli presented on a computer screen was higher or lower than that of the standard stimulus. On the display, firstly, the fixation cross was presented for 1200 ms, followed by the standard stimulus (i.e., the strings of “prostate cancer”) surrounded by a yellow line square of 1200 ms. Then, after the blank screen presentation for 1200 ms, the target stimulus was presented randomly among 20 illnesses until participants pressed the left or right button. Finally, the blank was presented again, and the next trial was automatically started (Fig. 5). Participants were asked to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. In addition, catch trials were adopted so that participants pay more attention to the risk comparison. In the catch trials, “prostate cancer” was presented instead of the target stimuli again. Participants were asked not to respond in the catch trials, and the string of “prostate cancer” was set to disappear after 3000 ms (no-go trials). A personal computer system (NEC LAVIE, Pro Mobile) with ‘Presentation’ software (Neurobehavioral Systems) was used to generate visual stimuli and to record participants’ responses. The viewing distance from the participant’s eyes to the center of the screen was approximately 60 cm. The mean luminance of the display was 5.4 cd/m2 and the mean illuminance in front of the display was 3.9 lx.

This experiment consisted of four trial blocks. The conditions of response mapping were experimentally manipulated: (A) the Compatible condition, in which participants were asked to press the left button for the lower risk and the right button for the higher risk, and (B) the Incompatible condition, in which the stimulus-response mapping of the compatible condition was reversed. The response mapping corresponding with the orientation of the predicted mental risk line was defined as the Compatible condition. In both conditions, all participants pressed the left button with the index finger and the right button with the middle finger of their right hand. The two conditions were conducted alternatively and the order of conditions was counterbalanced across participants (A-B-A-B or B-A-B-A). Each block consisted of 24 trials with a presentation of 20 illnesses and 4 catch trials. Therefore, a total of 96 trials were included in this experiment. In addition, five trials to practice were given to participants before running each trial block in each condition.

In the present study, the index and middle fingers of the right hand were adopted as the response effectors (i.e., unimanual response). Classically, the left and right index fingers have been used as the response effectors (i.e., bimanual response) in the SNARC experiment. However, the SNARC effect was also observed in the experiment where participants were instructed to unimanual respond with the right index or middle finger20,76. Moreover, it has been shown that the distance between response buttons also did not affect the strength of the SNARC effect77 and that the strength of the SNARC effect did not differ in the hand cross conditions5. These findings indicate that the SNARC effect is not influenced by anatomical effectors but by the relative response side. Therefore, if the SRARC effect shares a common mechanism with the SNARC effect, then it was expected that the SRARC effect would also occur even with an unimanual response.

At the end of the experiment, participants completed the questionnaire asking about their demographic information, their preference for the conditions of the response mapping, and risk perception for each illness. Specifically, the participants wrote down numerically the magnitude of the risk representation of each illness when the magnitude of the standard stimulus was defined as 25.0.

Statistical analysis

The trials that its RT was over + 3SD or under 120 ms were excluded from the analysis, referring to previous studies11,78. The proportional data (i.e., correct response rate) were analyzed after applying arcsine transform79. To examine the existence of the SRARC effect in the risk comparison task, firstly, a mixed design three-way ANOVA was conducted on both the RTs and the arcsine-transformed correct response rate with a factorial design of the group (i.e., nurses/general students) as a between-subjects factor, and the response side (i.e., left/right) and the risk (i.e., Extreme Low/Low/High/Extreme High) as within-subjects factors. If the risk corresponded with spatial information depending on its degree, an interaction between the response side and the risk would be observed.

Moreover, to clarify both the strength and the directional preference of associations in each individual SRARC effect, a regression analysis was conducted for individuals by regressing the dRT (the RT of left-side response – the RT of right-side response) on the mean risk magnitude of five illnesses at each risk level. The positive dRT indicates that the right-side responses tended to be faster than the left-side responses, and the negative dRT indicates that the left-side responses tended to be faster than the right-side responses. The slope coefficient of the regression analysis has been considered to be the index of the SNARC effect80. In the present study, positive slopes of the regression line indicate participants who showed the SRARC with “low risk - left” and “high risk - right” associations (i.e., left-to-right mapping), whereas negative slopes of the regression line indicate participants who showed the reversed SRARC with “high risk - left” and “low risk - right” associations (i.e., right-to-left mapping). One-sample t-test was performed on the slope coefficient to examine whether the slope coefficient differ from zero. In addition, to explore a relationship between the RTs and the strength of the SRARC effect, the scatter plot between the RTs and the slope coefficient was depicted.

For the distance effect, a correlation analysis was performed between the RTs to each illness and the representational distance from the standard stimulus to each illness on the basis of the results of the pre-experiment.

Additionally, to examine the influence of the condition order on participants’ manual responses, the mixed design three-way ANOVA was performed on the dRT with a factorial design of the assigned condition in the first trial block (i.e., Compatible/Incompatible) and the group as between-subjects factors, and the risk as a within-subjects factor.

Finally, although the degree of the risk was divided into 4 levels in advance, each participant did not definitely make a judgement based on the manipulated risk. The strength of the SRARC effect was then recalculated based on the manipulated risk, which was binarily categorized as low or high risk. Another slope coefficient was also calculated based on participants’ actual responses during the experiment (i.e., reaction-based risk) and on the magnitude of the risk representation that was evaluated numerically by participants after the experiment (i.e., subjective risk obtained from the questionnaire). For the reaction-based objective risk and the questionnaire-based subjective risk, the slope coefficient was also calculated as binary risk (i.e., only low or high). A mixed design two-way ANOVA was then conducted on the slope coefficients, with a factorial design of the group as a between-subjects factor, and the risk definition (i.e., manipulated risk/reaction-based risk/subjective risk) as a within-subjects factor.
