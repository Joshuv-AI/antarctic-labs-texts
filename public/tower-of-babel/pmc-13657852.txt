Incidence, characteristics, and exploratory analysis of factors associated with pelvic osteitis/osteoradionecrosis after radiotherapy for cervical cancer: a single-center retrospective cohort study with an unmatched case-control analysis
Authors: Sijia Deng, Jie Li, Zexin Wang, Renming Lv, En Long, Yuanyuan Luo, Zihan Chen, Tingting Dai, Rui Huang, Song Qiu, Ruisi Wang, Huijie Wei, Dan Zhou, Yuxi Dong, Shuqiang Zhang, Fengmei Wen, Ruyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPelvic radiotherapy is the standard treatment for locally advanced cervical cancer and has substantially improved patient survival. As survival has increased, late radiation-related complications have become increasingly important. Radiation osteitis (RO) and osteoradionecrosis (ORN) are uncommon but potentially disabling forms of radiation-induced pelvic bone injury. However, most previous studies have focused on pelvic insufficiency fractures (PIFs) rather than pelvic RO/ORN. Consequently, the incidence, clinical characteristics, imaging features, and risk factors of pelvic RO/ORN remain poorly defined, particularly in patients treated with contemporary intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT). This study aimed to investigate the incidence, clinical and imaging characteristics, and potential risk factors of pelvic RO/ORN in cervical cancer patients receiving pelvic radiotherapy.MethodsA retrospective cohort study with an unmatched case-control analysis was conducted among 308 cervical cancer patients treated with pelvic radiotherapy between January 2015 and December 2021. After follow-up and imaging review, 28 patients diagnosed with pelvic RO/ORN were identified. Fifty-six controls were selected by simple random sampling without replacement from the remaining cohort members in whom RO/ORN was not identified during available follow-up. Clinical characteristics, tumor features, and treatment-related variables were compared between groups. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were exploratory.ResultsPelvic RO/ORN was identified in 28 of 308 patients, corresponding to an incidence of 9.1%. The median time to diagnosis was 22.5 months [interquartile range (IQR), 15.5–33.0 months] after radiotherapy. The sacrum/sacroiliac region and ilium were the most frequently involved sites. Typical imaging findings included mixed osteolytic and sclerotic lesions, irregular cortical disruption, and inflammatory changes in adjacent soft tissues. Seventeen patients (60.7%) had pelvic or hip pain or movement-related symptoms, whereas 11 (39.3%) were identified on surveillance imaging without apparent symptoms. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed for the clinical and treatment variables examined (all P>0.05). Exploratory multivariable logistic regression did not identify clear independent associations in this dataset.ConclusionsPelvic RO/ORN is a clinically relevant late effect of pelvic radiotherapy for cervical cancer. In this dataset, no clear predictors were identified among the clinical and treatment variables examined; this negative finding should be interpreted cautiously because the number of events was limited and patient-specific pelvic bone dosimetry and bone-health measures were unavailable. Future risk assessment should move beyond conventional clinical parameters by integrating dosimetric, imaging, and biological markers to clarify risk factors and the value of earlier detection.Keywords: Cervical cancer, pelvic radiotherapy, osteoradionecrosis (ORN), osteitis, risk factors

Highlight box

Key findings

• Pelvic radiation osteitis/osteoradionecrosis (RO/ORN) was observed in 9.1% of cervical cancer patients after pelvic radiotherapy, with a median time to diagnosis of 22.5 months.

• RO/ORN showed clinically and radiologically heterogeneous presentations, including 11 imaging-detected cases without apparent symptoms. No statistically significant associations were identified among the clinical and treatment variables examined; these exploratory analyses were limited by 28 events and unavailable pelvic bone dosimetry.

What is known and what is new?

• Most previous studies of radiation-induced pelvic bone toxicity have focused on pelvic insufficiency fractures, whereas evidence regarding pelvic RO/ORN remains limited.

• This study represents one of the few systematic evaluations of the incidence, clinical manifestations, imaging characteristics, and exploratory risk factors of pelvic RO/ORN in a homogeneous cohort of cervical cancer patients treated with contemporary pelvic radiotherapy. The exploratory analysis did not identify clear predictors among the variables examined, and routinely collected clinical variables alone may be insufficient for risk prediction.

What are the implications, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should maintain awareness of RO/ORN in cervical cancer survivors presenting with persistent pelvic pain after radiotherapy.

• Future multicenter studies should integrate dose-volume histogram parameters, quantitative imaging biomarkers, and bone metabolic indicators to improve risk stratification and develop individualized surveillance and bone-protection strategies.

Introduction

Radiation-related pelvic bone injury is an increasingly recognized late effect of pelvic radiotherapy and encompasses pelvic insufficiency fractures (PIFs), radiation osteitis (RO), osteoradionecrosis (ORN), avascular necrosis, and joint dysfunction (1-3). Although skeletal complications are reported less often than gastrointestinal or genitourinary toxicities, they can cause persistent pain, impaired mobility, and diagnostic uncertainty when imaging findings resemble recurrent or metastatic disease (2-5).

Cervical cancer remains a major global malignancy among women, with approximately 600,000 new cases and 340,000 deaths reported in 2020 (6,7). Although human papillomavirus vaccination and screening have reduced incidence in some settings, locally advanced disease remains common (8,9). Pelvic radiotherapy, with or without concurrent chemotherapy, is a standard treatment for locally advanced and selected high-risk early-stage cervical cancer and improves local control and survival (10,11). As survivorship increases and advanced techniques reduce some acute toxicities, late treatment-related effects, including skeletal injury, have assumed greater clinical importance (1,12-14).

From a pathophysiological perspective, radiation-induced bone injury progresses from RO to ORN. Early changes include bone marrow edema, microvascular endarteritis, and disruption of trabecular architecture, while long-term damage may lead to osteocyte necrosis, cortical discontinuity, and irreversible effects such as secondary infections or fistula formation (15). Clinically, these changes are often described as “osteitis” or “osteoradionecrosis” and can progress to fractures and structural collapse, creating a pathological continuum from functional impairment to structural destruction (16). On imaging, RO/ORN typically presents with alternating sclerotic and osteolytic lesions, cortical destruction, sclerotic margins, and adjacent soft-tissue reactions (2). In patients with pelvic tumors, these findings may be difficult to distinguish from tumor recurrence or bone metastasis, complicating diagnosis and management (4,5).

Research on pelvic RO/ORN in cervical cancer is limited compared with studies on mandibular ORN in head and neck cancer. To date, research on pelvic radiotherapy-related bone toxicity has largely focused on PIFs, with RO/ORN often reported as part of broader “bone complications” (17,18). PIFs are stress fractures linked to reduced bone mass, typically presenting on imaging as fracture lines accompanied by bone marrow edema in the sacrum or pubic bone (19,20). In contrast, RO/ORN is characterized by focal osteolysis, cortical disruption, mixed sclerotic-lytic changes, and inflammatory reactions in adjacent soft tissues. Clinically, RO/ORN may be associated with persistent or progressive refractory pain, and in some cases, fistulas may develop (2,21). Although both conditions are part of the spectrum of radiation-induced bone toxicity, they differ substantially in terms of imaging appearance and clinical management.

Pelvic bone toxicity following pelvic radiotherapy for cervical cancer is not uncommon, and the literature on PIFs is relatively extensive. However, reports on destructive pelvic lesions dominated by RO/ORN are mainly limited to sporadic cases and small case series. The true incidence, clinical spectrum, and identifiable risk factors for RO/ORN, particularly in contemporary cohorts using intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), remain unclear. Systematic studies in Asian populations, focusing on a single tumor type and using osteitis and ORN as primary endpoints, are particularly rare.

To address this gap, we retrospectively enrolled cervical cancer patients treated with pelvic radiotherapy at the Affiliated Fuling Hospital of Chongqing University and conducted an unmatched case-control analysis nested within that source cohort. With pelvic RO/ORN as the primary endpoint, we aimed to describe its incidence, clinical characteristics, and imaging features and to explore associations with the available clinical and treatment variables. This study sought to provide baseline data for subsequent multicenter, large-sample studies and to inform the design of future research evaluating risk stratification and bone-protection strategies. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-2270/rc).

Methods

Study design and ethics

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study with an unmatched case-control analysis nested within the cohort. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Fuling Hospital of Chongqing University (No. 2024CDFSFLYYEC-029). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee because of the retrospective design and the use of de-identified data.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Clinical and imaging data were collected from cervical cancer patients who underwent pelvic radiotherapy at the Affiliated Fuling Hospital of Chongqing University between January 2015 and December 2021. A total of 308 patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and were included in the overall cohort.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) histopathologically or cytologically confirmed cervical cancer; (II) completion of definitive pelvic radiotherapy or postoperative adjuvant pelvic radiotherapy, with complete radiotherapy-related records; (III) absence of other primary malignant bone tumors or extensive bone metastases; and (IV) availability of at least 12 months of clinical and imaging follow-up.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) pre-existing pelvic infection, osteonecrosis, or a history of reconstruction after severe pelvic trauma before radiotherapy; (II) other diseases affecting pelvic structure (e.g., active suppurative osteomyelitis); and/or (III) insufficient clinical or imaging data preventing the evaluation of RO or ORN outcomes.

Treatment regimens

All patients received external-beam radiotherapy using a linear accelerator at the Affiliated Fuling Hospital of Chongqing University, and some also underwent intracavitary brachytherapy. External-beam radiotherapy included conventional two-dimensional four-field box irradiation and IMRT, with the specific technique determined by equipment availability and contemporaneous clinical practice standards. Target delineation was conducted according to current domestic and international guidelines. The clinical target volume (CTV) included the primary cervical lesion, parametrium, and pelvic lymphatic drainage regions, while the planning target volume was generated by expanding the CTV by 5–7 mm.

For definitive radiotherapy, the total external-beam dose ranged from 45 to 60 Gy, delivered in 25–28 fractions. For patients who underwent surgery (primarily radical hysterectomy with pelvic lymphadenectomy), postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy typically consisted of 45–50 Gy delivered in 25–28 fractions. Radiotherapy was administered once daily, 5 days per week, over 5–6 weeks. Some patients received intracavitary brachytherapy in addition to external-beam radiotherapy, at a dose of 5–6 Gy per fraction for 3–5 fractions. Concurrent chemotherapy typically involved platinum-based regimens with cisplatin, administered intravenously at 30–40 mg/m2 weekly for 4–6 weeks. The number of cycles was adjusted based on patient tolerance and blood counts.

Diagnostic criteria for pelvic osteitis/ORN

The diagnosis of RO/ORN was based on clinical context, imaging findings, exclusion of competing diagnoses, and biopsy when clinically necessary.

Control selection and data collection

After application of the eligibility criteria, 308 patients comprised the source cohort, and all 28 patients who developed RO/ORN were included as cases. From the 280 cohort members in whom RO/ORN was not identified through their last available follow-up, 56 controls were selected by simple random sampling without replacement using a random-number table. The prespecified 1:2 ratio was used to improve precision relative to a 1:1 sample while retaining feasible detailed chart and imaging review. Cases and controls arose from the same source cohort, met the same eligibility criteria, and had at least 12 months of clinical and imaging follow-up. No individual matching was performed, allowing age, stage, and other characteristics to be evaluated as candidate variables and avoiding exclusion of cases for which an exact match was unavailable. This strategy did not eliminate confounding; potential confounding was addressed using unconditional multivariable logistic regression, subject to the constraint imposed by 28 events. Because controls were sampled from end-of-follow-up non-cases rather than by incidence-density risk-set sampling, residual differences in observation time and time-window bias remained possible.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 and R version 4.4.3. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range (IQR)] based on normality, and were compared between groups using the independent-samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

In the nested case-control cohort sample, the occurrence of RO/ORN was treated as the dependent variable, and the aforementioned clinical and treatment-related variables were treated as independent variables. Univariable logistic regression was performed first. Given the small event count, the multivariable model was restricted to variables with univariable P<0.10 and selected clinically relevant covariates, and unconditional logistic regression was used because controls were not individually matched. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the risk-factor analysis, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed.

Results

Cohort overview and incidence of pelvic osteitis/ORN

Among the 308 patients included in the study, the median age was 50 years (IQR, 45–58 years), and 45.1% (139/308) were postmenopausal. The majority of patients were staged as FIGO IB–IIB (69.5%, 214/308), and squamous cell carcinoma accounted for 89.3% (275/308) of the cases. IMRT was administered to 89.6% (276/308) of the patients, and the external-beam total dose was predominantly 45–50 Gy.

During follow-up, 28 patients developed pelvic RO/ORN, corresponding to an observed cohort incidence of 9.1% (28/308). The median time to diagnosis was 22.5 months after radiotherapy (IQR, 15.5–33.0 months). Of the 28 cases, 25 had imaging findings classified as RO and three had more advanced clinical and imaging features classified as ORN, reflecting overlap along the spectrum of radiation-related bone injury.

The median follow-up for the overall cohort was 48.5 months (IQR, 36.2–72.4 months) after radiotherapy. The corresponding durations were 51.2 months (IQR, 38.5–74.1 months) for cases and 47.6 months (IQR, 35.8–71.5 months) for controls (P=0.45). By the administrative cutoff, cancer progression or recurrence had been documented in 72/308 patients (23.4%), and 49/308 patients (15.9%) had died, including 41 cancer-related deaths.

Clinical and imaging characteristics of pelvic osteitis/ORN

Clinically, 17 of the 28 patients (60.7%) were symptomatic: 13 (46.4%) experienced persistent dull pelvic or hip pain, and four (14.3%) primarily reported pain during movement accompanied by limping. The remaining 11 (39.3%) were identified through surveillance imaging without apparent symptoms. Of these patients, 12 required long-term oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or weak opioids, and three required hospitalization due to pain that significantly impaired daily activities.

Imaging findings demonstrated that the sacrum and sacroiliac region were the most frequently involved (21/28, 75.0%). The ilium was affected in 6 patients (21.4%), whereas acetabular involvement was uncommon (1/28, 3.6%). Lesions involving multiple anatomical sites were observed in several patients. Typical CT features included focal sclerosis with irregular osteolytic areas, cortical interruption or thinning, and a peripheral sclerotic rim in some lesions. MRI demonstrated decreased T1-weighted signal and increased T2-weighted/STIR signal, indicative of bone marrow edema, along with mild adjacent soft-tissue edema or enhancement in some cases (Figure 2).

In terms of management, patients with RO/ORN in this cohort were treated primarily with conservative measures, including oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, short-term weak opioids, weight-bearing restrictions, and activity modification. Additional measures included physical therapy (e.g., thermotherapy), calcium and vitamin D supplementation, and anti-osteoporosis therapy when clinically indicated. Symptomatic improvement was documented in the available records, although outcomes were not assessed using a standardized scale.

Exploratory logistic regression analysis of associated factors

In the 84-patient unmatched case-control sample, none of the examined variables was statistically significant in univariable logistic regression (Table 3). Several variables, including age ≥60 years, moderate-to-severe anemia, concurrent chemoradiotherapy, and additional brachytherapy/afterloading, showed a trend toward an OR >1; however, the 95% CIs were wide and crossed 1, and all P values were >0.05. Only a few variables had P values <0.20 (Table 3). These variables, along with clinically relevant covariates, were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model. In the multivariable analysis, the adjusted 95% CIs for all variables still crossed 1, and all P values remained >0.05 (Table 4). The external-beam total dose (>50 vs. ≤50 Gy) showed a trend toward a lower risk based on the point estimate (OR =0.127, 95% CI: 0.013–1.254; P=0.08), but the sparse exposure counts, wide CI, and potential confounding preclude interpretation as a protective association. Overall, the analysis did not identify clear independent associations among the variables examined.

Discussion

In this single-center cohort of 308 cervical cancer patients treated with pelvic radiotherapy, 28 patients (9.1%) met the study definition of pelvic RO/ORN, with a median onset of 22.5 months. This composite incidence should not be directly equated with the 0.44% frequency of symptomatic fracture-type pelvic ORN reported by Feltl et al. (24) or with earlier studies using fracture or severe osteolysis as endpoints (25). Our definition included imaging-detected RO as well as ORN, and 11 of 28 cases (39.3%) had no apparent symptoms at detection. Differences in endpoints, imaging intensity, follow-up, patient selection, and radiotherapy practice therefore offer more plausible explanations for the higher observed proportion than a true increase in severe pelvic ORN.

The clinical meaning of detecting asymptomatic or early imaging-defined RO differs from that of diagnosing symptomatic, structurally destructive ORN. In symptomatic or diagnostically uncertain patients, recognition can guide analgesia and weight-bearing advice, prompt evaluation for fracture or infection, and reduce misclassification as metastasis. For asymptomatic patients, an imaging diagnosis may provide a baseline for interpretation of subsequent studies; however, whether earlier detection alters progression, symptoms, or function is unknown. Imaging-detected RO can partially regress (2), and additional testing may expose patients to overdiagnosis, anxiety, biopsy, or unnecessary treatment. Accordingly, our findings support careful review of clinically obtained follow-up imaging and prompt evaluation of new pelvic symptoms.

Previous studies have associated pelvic radiation-related bone injury with osteoporosis, older age, menopause, low bone mineral density, higher skeletal dose, and concurrent treatment, although endpoints have often centered on PIFs or severe ORN (19,24,26-28). In contrast, no statistically significant associations were identified in our exploratory analysis. This result does not negate those factors or show that routinely collected variables lack predictive value; it indicates only that no clear associations were demonstrated among the variables measured in this dataset.

Several features limit interpretation of the negative association analysis. Only 28 events were available, limiting statistical power and the number of covariates that could be entered without severe overfitting. The study also relied on broad clinical and treatment categories. Uniform patient-specific pelvic bone DVH analysis was not feasible because original DICOM-RT structure-set and dose files were incomplete across the cohort, some patients had conventional two-dimensional plans, and pelvic bone substructures had not been routinely contoured. Recent studies have evaluated clinical and dose-volume correlates of PIFs after definitive chemoradiotherapy for cervical cancer, further supporting standardized pelvic-bone contouring and DVH extraction in future research; however, findings based on PIFs should not be directly extrapolated to RO/ORN (29). Restricting dosimetric extraction to the retrievable subset would have introduced selection and measurement bias. Baseline bone mineral density and bone-turnover markers were also not systematically available. Thus, the wide CIs are compatible with clinically relevant associations in either direction, and the regression results should be interpreted as exploratory signals requiring future validation rather than as definitive evidence of predictors.

RO/ORN and PIFs are related but nonidentical manifestations of radiation-related pelvic bone injury. PIFs are stress fractures associated with impaired bone strength and typically show a fracture line and characteristic marrow edema; RO/ORN more often shows focal osteolysis, cortical destruction, and mixed sclerotic-lytic change (2,3,19,20,30). The conditions may coexist or evolve along an overlapping continuum. Although a separate PIFs frequency would be clinically informative, PIF was not a prespecified endpoint, follow-up imaging was nonuniform, and the cohort was not systematically re-reviewed using predefined fracture criteria. We therefore did not report a numerical PIFs frequency that could be misleading because of incomplete ascertainment.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center retrospective design is vulnerable to selection, information, and ascertainment biases. Second, the small number of events limited precision and multivariable adjustment. Third, the unavailable whole-cohort pelvic DVH parameters, baseline bone mineral density, and bone metabolic markers prevented robust evaluation of dose-effect and metabolic relationships. Fourth, progression and death may truncate observation for late toxicity and were summarized descriptively rather than handled in a competing-risk analysis. Finally, the RO/ORN definition included asymptomatic imaging-detected osteitis, whose reproducibility and clinical consequences require prospective validation.

Future studies should use multicenter prospective cohorts with standardized imaging intervals and consensus diagnostic and severity criteria for RO, ORN, PIF, and avascular necrosis. Complete DICOM-RT radiotherapy datasets should support prespecified pelvic bone contouring and extraction of DVH metrics. Quantitative CT or dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and bone-turnover markers can characterize baseline skeletal health. Adequate event numbers, time-to-event or competing-risk methods, and internal and external validation would be required before a clinically useful risk-prediction model could be claimed.

Within these limitations, several cautious clinical implications can be considered in conjunction with prior evidence: Increase awareness: in patients with cervical cancer who have undergone pelvic radiotherapy, particularly those presenting with unexplained pelvic or hip pain months to years after treatment, RO/ORN should be actively considered, rather than limiting the differential diagnosis to degenerative disease or tumor recurrence or metastasis.

By focusing on pelvic RO/ORN as the endpoint in a cervical cancer population receiving pelvic radiotherapy, this study provides real-world data on the incidence and clinical/imaging characteristics of this condition. Although no statistically positive associations emerged from the exploratory risk-factor analyses, the findings underscore the clinical relevance and diagnostic challenges of this late toxicity and provide a foundation for larger prospective studies with standardized imaging, dosimetric assessment, and bone-health measures.

Conclusions

This study provides real-world data on the incidence, time to onset, and imaging characteristics of pelvic RO/ORN following pelvic radiotherapy for cervical cancer. The sacrum and ilium were the most commonly involved sites. Clinical manifestations range from asymptomatic osteitis found on imaging to persistent or progressive pelvic or hip pain and varying degrees of dysfunction. Imaging typically revealed mixed sclerotic-lytic changes and cortical destruction, which can be mistaken for osseous metastasis. The exploratory unmatched case-control analysis did not identify clear independent associations among the clinical and treatment variables examined; this should not be interpreted as evidence that those variables are generally nonpredictive. The limited event count, potential follow-up differences, and unavailable pelvic bone DVH and bone-health data constrain inference. Future large-sample, multicenter studies incorporating DVH parameters, bone mineral density, and bone metabolic markers are needed. Individualized bone health management should be integrated into long-term follow-up strategies for cervical cancer patients receiving pelvic radiotherapy.
