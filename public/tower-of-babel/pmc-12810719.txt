Knowledge-informed deep learning to mitigate bias in joint air pollutant prediction
Authors: Lianfa Li, Roxana Khalili, Frederick Lurmann, Nathan Pavlovic, Jun Wu, Yan Xu, Yisi Liu, Karl O’Sharkey, Beate Ritz, Luke Oman, Meredith Franklin, Theresa Bastain, Shohreh F Farzan, Carrie Breton, Rima Habre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of atmospheric air pollutants is critical for public health protection and environmental management. Traditional machine learning (ML) methods achieve high spatial resolution but lack physicochemical constraints, leading to systematic biases that compromise exposure estimates for epidemiological studies. Chemical transport models incorporate atmospheric physics but require expensive parameterization and often fail to capture local-scale variability crucial for health impact assessment. This gap between data-driven accuracy and physical realism presents a major obstacle to advancing air quality science. We address this challenge through a novel physics-informed deep learning framework that integrates advection–diffusion equations and fluid dynamics constraints directly into neural network architectures for multi-pollutant prediction. Our approach models air pollutant pairs across geographically distinct domains (NO2/NOx for California; PM2.5/PM10 for mainland China), providing a comprehensive framework for physics-constrained atmospheric modeling at high resolution. Through an efficient framework, our methodology demonstrates that incorporating proxy advection and diffusion fields as physical constraints fundamentally alters learning dynamics, reducing generalization error and eliminating systematic bias inherent in data-driven approaches while improving computational efficiency compared to graph networks. Site-based validation reveals unprecedented bias reduction: 21%–42% for nitrogen oxides and 16%–17% for particulate matter compared to the baseline deep learning methods. Our methodology uniquely generates physically interpretable parameters while providing explicit uncertainty quantification through ensemble techniques. The substantial bias reduction coupled with physically interpretable parameters has immediate implications for improving air pollutant exposure assessment and understanding in epidemiological research, potentially transforming health effect evaluations that rely on accurate spatial predictions.Keywords: Deep learning, Physics-informed modeling, Air pollution, Knowledge fusion, Bias mitigation, Joint prediction

Introduction

Nitrogen oxides (NOx) - comprising nitrogen dioxide (NO2), nitric oxide (NO) and related nitrogen compounds are released predominantly from human combustion activities including transportation and industrial sources, as well as generated via secondary atmospheric processes. Acute health impacts from NOx exposure encompass respiratory tract inflammation and various short-term pulmonary conditions (Hesterberg et al., 2009; Mills et al., 2015; Weinmayr et al., 2010). Chronic NOx exposure contributes to asthma development, heightened vulnerability to respiratory pathogens, and increased mortality risk (Faustini et al., 2014; Stieb et al., 2021). Consequently, NO2 serves as a regulated criteria pollutant under the U.S. Clean Air Act and international environmental legislation due to its established public health impacts. NO and NO2 represent chemically active atmospheric pollutants influenced by intricate physical atmospheric dynamics and chemical transformations spanning emission sources through transport processes to removal mechanisms (Fig. 1) (Edwards et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2020, 2013). Fresh vehicular NOx emissions from gasoline and diesel engines exhibit NO/NO2 ratios of 90/10% and 50/50%, respectively. Most NO undergoes conversion to NO2 through reactions with ozone and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) over timescales ranging from seconds to hours. NO2 demonstrates greater atmospheric persistence compared to NO, especially during winter months when cooler, drier tropospheric conditions decelerate photochemical processes, enabling pollutant accumulation or regional transport beneath or above inversion layers. NO2 can undergo reactions with atmospheric constituents to produce nitric acid, particulate nitrate (PM-NO3) and peroxyacyl nitrates (PANs), while solar radiation can reconvert NO2 to NO while generating ozone (O3) (Edwards et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2019). NO2 combines with moisture, oxygen and atmospheric chemicals to create acid precipitation that damages vulnerable ecosystems including aquatic and forest systems. During adverse meteorological scenarios, NO2 and its derivative pollutants constitute primary components of photochemical smog with detrimental health and environmental consequences (Goldstone, 2015).

NOx distributions exhibit substantial spatial heterogeneity at fine scales with steep concentration gradients near transportation corridors (Zou et al., 2006; Karner et al., 2010; Richmond-Bryant et al., 2017), creating challenges for local-scale prediction efforts. As a critical measure of air pollutant concentration prediction performance, estimation bias is defined as the systematic error between a model’s predicted mean values and observed values, describing how well the model captures the underlying data patterns without consistent over- or under-estimation (Kozdron, 2016). Multiple factors contribute to estimation bias (Fig. 1E): inadequate or obsolete emission inventories, inaccurate representation of transport and chemical processes, and incomplete information for modeling pollutant removal pathways etc. Beyond meteorological factors, traffic patterns and land-use characteristics serve as predictor variables in modeling frameworks to minimize biases from emission and removal processes (Li et al., 2017), though this approach provides an incomplete solution for capturing air pollution dynamics. Chemical transport models (CTMs) and dispersion models employ mathematical formulations of chemical and physical mechanisms to simulate pollutant transport and transformation, incorporating emissions from stationary, area, and mobile sources, buoyant plume behavior, advective-diffusive transport, atmospheric chemistry, removal processes and aerosol dynamics (Daly and Zannetti, 2007). These mechanistic CTMs typically operate at coarse spatial resolutions and cannot sufficiently address growing requirements for fine-scale predictions. Similarly, dispersion models provide fine spatial detail but omit detailed chemical reaction mechanisms. Pure statistical machine learning (ML) approaches, including random forest, gradient boosting and neural networks, depend entirely on data-driven statistical relationships between input variables (predictors) and NOx concentrations (Bellinger et al., 2017), or integrate with physical models (Kelp et al., 2020) to accelerate physicochemical computations. The reliance on statistical correlations cannot ensure that trained model outputs maintain physical principles governing air pollution fluid mechanics, particularly in regions lacking training observations, potentially resulting in substantial estimation bias (Karniadakis et al., 2021). Additionally, traditional model evaluation through random spatial–temporal sampling, widely applied in air quality studies, cannot provide authentic generalization assessment for novel spatial locations, potentially creating overconfident estimates of prediction performance (Tang et al., 2024). However, several recent studies such as Di et al. (2019) and Li et al. (2023) have employed more rigorous site-based or area-based cross-validation approaches in their evaluations.

This methodological gap has profound implications for air quality science and public health research. Systematic biases in air pollutant predictions directly translate to exposure misclassification in epidemiological studies, potentially leading to biased health effect estimates and flawed policy decisions. The lack of physical constraints in conventional ML models means they may produce physically unrealistic predictions (e.g., negative concentrations, violations of mass conservation) that undermine confidence in model outputs. Additionally, the ‘‘black box’’ nature of purely statistical models provides no insight into the underlying physical processes, limiting their utility for understanding pollution dynamics or developing targeted mitigation strategies.

The necessity for a new modeling paradigm becomes clear when considering the requirements of modern air quality applications: (1) high spatial resolution for exposure assessment, (2) computational efficiency for operational deployment, (3) physical consistency to ensure reliable predictions under diverse conditions, and (4) interpretability to support scientific understanding and policy development. Neither existing CTMs nor pure ML approaches can simultaneously satisfy these requirements, creating an urgent need for hybrid methodologies that combine the strengths of both paradigms while mitigating their individual weaknesses.

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) have emerged as a promising solution to bridge this gap by incorporating physical laws into deep learning models (Raissi et al., 2019). PINNs have demonstrated success in various fields such as fluid dynamics, heat transfer, and wave propagation by constraining neural networks to respect governing partial differential equations (Karniadakis et al., 2021). However, their application to atmospheric chemistry and air pollution modeling remains largely unexplored. Recent attempts using graph neural networks for atmospheric chemical mechanisms (Silva et al., 2021) and air pollutant estimation (Li et al., 2023) face significant limitations: extremely high computational complexity of graph network unsuitable for large-scale applications, failure to directly incorporate fundamental advection–diffusion processes, and inability to extract interpretable physical fields. These limitations highlight the need for more efficient PINN architectures specifically designed for atmospheric modeling applications.

To address these critical gaps, we develop a novel physics-informed deep learning framework that directly integrates fundamental atmospheric transport equations into neural network architectures. Our approach uniquely combines: (1) explicit enforcement of advection–diffusion physics through embedded partial differential equations (PDEs), (2) computational efficiency through MLP-based architectures rather than complex graph networks, (3) extraction of physically interpretable parameters (simulated velocity fields), and (4) theoretical analysis for bias reduction through constrained learning dynamics. This framework represents the first efficient integration of atmospheric fluid dynamics principles with deep learning for multi-pollutant prediction at high resolution, offering a transformative approach to air quality modeling that simultaneously achieves physical realism, computational efficiency, and interpretability.

In our method, embedding fluid physics in deep learning substantially has reduced estimation bias in predicting air pollutant concentrations with NO2 and NOx, as well as PM2.5 and PM10 as typical pair examples. Different from the complex graph neural network, our modeling framework is based on a 2-dimensional (2-D) terrain-following Eulerian system (Fig. 2 with two MLP-based full residual neural networks (Li et al., 2020a)) as modeling base framework, which has been proved in this study to be more efficient and interpretable than complex graph networks. The governing principles of atmospheric fluid dynamics included the conservation of mass and continuity principles for the atmosphere (Fleagle and Businger, 1981); the former assumed that the total mass of an air pollutant within a control volume is conserved in the balance given the inflows, outflows, emission sources and sinks during a time period, and the latter assumed that the atmospheric fluid is continuous across space (i.e., it contains no holes). For a grid cell or target location, the continuity equation simulated the temporal evolution of air pollutants, taking into account the pollutant sources, transport, sinks and meteorological, topological and geographical effects (Jacobson, 1999). Based on automatic differentiation learning (Baydin et al., 2018), the PINN method we developed can be trained efficiently.

In order to simulate the fluid fields, PDEs are included in the deep learning model to explicitly characterize 2-D advection and diffusion (Karniadakis et al., 2021) (Fig. 2C–F). Due to the lack of vertical measurements of NOx concentrations as is typical, we could not consider vertical advection, but we did include vertical reanalysis and meteorological field data such as air temperature and wind speed at different altitudes (2 m, 10 m, 50 m), total NO2 columns (OMI-NO2), and planetary boundary layer height (PBLH) to account for the influence of vertical processes on ground-level air pollutant concentrations. Thus, we primarily simulated horizontal advection and diffusion along orthogonal directions (latitude and longitude). In addition, given the significant impact of complex terrain on local dispersion of ground-level air pollutants (Whiteman, 2000), we also simulated the vertical dispersion of NOx within a small range of ground elevations (Fig. 1-F; Fig. 2-E). While the continuity equation was used to simulate fine-scale transport of NOx, we introduced another parameter to account for the balanced changes in emissions, chemical transformation and sink.

To demonstrate the broad applicability of our proposed method, we applied the modeling framework to evaluate statewide high-resolution predictions of long-term weekly reactive NO2 and NOx time series in California, USA, and national daily inert PM2.5 and PM10 time series in mainland China. As NOx includes NO2, PM10 also includes PM2.5, and so both concentration pairs follow similar hierarchical relationships (NO2 < NOx; PM2.5 < PM10) that can be encoded as constraints in the framework. However, except for this inclusion relationship, the physical and chemical relationships between NO2 and NOx are quite different from those between PM2.5 and PM10. Therefore, we used different predictors with different parameters for each pollutant pair. In addition, we also extracted the simulated advection and diffusion fields of NO2/NOx and PM2.5/PM10 and interpreted their spatiotemporal variability. Lastly, we mathematically derived an upper bound for the generalization error in our semi-supervised approach, and provided a theoretical justification for the improved independent test performance achieved by our PINN modeling method.

For the vertical direction of lz, it was naturally restricted to a limited range of elevation to account for changes in ground paired air pollutant concentrations, determined by the data samples. The air pollutant concentration variations over time (Fig. 2) consists of three components, advection, diffusion and the others. ρ denotes the variation of air pollutant concentration from emissions, transformation and deposition.

The modeling architecture consists of two sub full residual neural networks, that share the same input, total loss function and joint output of paired air pollutants (NO2 and NOx; PM2.5 and PM10) (Supplementary Fig. S1). One sub residual neural network (Fig. S1-A) was used to learn the parameters (each direction Reynolds velocity, each direction diffusion coefficient, and ρ) to be used in simulation of the continuity equations, and the other sub residual neural network (Fig. S1-B) was used to predict paired air pollutant concentrations. Full residual skip connections in two sub neural networks were used to improve learning efficiency in regression (Li et al., 2020a). The velocity, diffusion coefficient and ρ have been assumed to be affected considerably by meteorology, geography, terrain and vertical atmospheric variables (Ulfah et al., 2018; Andrews, 2010; Agency, 2022). In order to avoid complex parameterization in the advection and diffusion simulation, we constructed the specialized sub neural network to estimate these parameters with possible constraints. There may be differences in network topology and configuration between two sub neural networks. Through sensitivity analysis, we obtained an optimal network topology for the sub network of estimating parameters and the sub network of jointly estimating air pollutants (e.g., NOx or PM), respectively: the number of nodes in each encoding or coding layer was 1023 → 512 → 320 → 256 → 128 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 for sub parameter network and 512 → 320 → 256 → 128 → 64 → 32 → 16 for sub estimation network; the number of nodes in each decoding layer was symmetric with its corresponding encoding layer. In addition, the attention layers are added to weigh the influence of different parts of the input on the model’s predictions (Vaswani et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2016); the weight normalization (Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015) and non-linear activation functions such as swish and tanh (Goodfellow et al., 2016) layers were added to improve generalizability of the trained neural network.

Encoding fluid physics via PDE residuals

In our models we borrowed concepts from CTM but simplified the parameterizations for velocity, diffusion coefficients and others using automatic differentiation learning. The NOx concentration data alone do not provide sufficient information to generate a single solution for these unknown parameters but it is impossible to acquire such parameter data in practice and solving inverse problems with hidden physics is prohibitively difficult and expensive (Karniadakis et al., 2021). As shown in the model with hidden fluid mechanics (Raissi et al., 2020), we achieved a high quantitative agreement between observed and predicted proxy values of these parameters by relying solely on the information contained in the observed concentration data. Here, we constructed a physics-informed deep neural network to approximate the following mapping (Fig. 2) t,x,y,z,E,T,M,L,O→C,C′;θ,θ′ (t: time, x: longitude, y: latitude, z: elevation, E: emission or its proxy, T: transport, M: meteorology, L: land-use; O: others; CC′: air pollutant concentration, θθ′: parameters (velocity, diffusion coefficient and others) for paired air pollutants). Two full residual deep networks (Fig. 2B–C; Supplementary Fig. S1) were constructed for parameter estimation in support of PDE residuals, and joint predictions of air pollutants, respectively. The physical parameters have been reported to be affected by emission, meteorology, topography, geography and land-use (Jacobson, 1999). Full residual deep network has been used to improve efficiency in regression learning (Li et al., 2020a). The PDEs of paired air pollutants were encoded using two residuals, e1 and e2 (Fig. 2F). The PDE residuals enabled the model in training to maintain the continuity equation as much as possible. We assumed that all data (including training, testing and predicting samples) conformed to the mass conservation in support of the PDE residuals. Furthermore, the framework was based on residual multilayer perceptron (MLP), which acted as a general nonlinear approximator for any continuous function (Hornik, 1991) and could simulate the continuity of air pollutants well.

As a differentiable programming paradigm, Tensorflow uses automatic differentiation to optimize the parameters in training and also can be used to solve partial differentiation equation (PDE) problems (Abadi et al., 2016). During model training, minimizing the loss function makes the residuals of e1 and e2 close to 0, enforcing the trained model to conform to the continuity equation as closely as possible. Encoding thresholds and relationship

NO2 has complex atmospheric physicochemical relationships with NO and other air pollutants (Fig. 2I). To represent these relationships in a unified model, the joint outputs of paired air pollutants (NO2/NOx and PM2.5/PM10) were set up in Model B so that input and weight parameters of the constructed neural networks could be shared between both pairs to represent their interactions efficiently. Similar sharing has been used to improve multitask machine learning (Zhang et al., 2015; Bishop and Nasrabadi, 2006). In addition, the thresholds (maximums) for paired air pollutant concentrations were encoded via the residuals, e3 and e4, and their stoichiometric relationships (CNO2≤CNOx and CPM2.5≤CPM10) were encoded via the residual, e5. All data satisfied the thresholds and relationships, but learning using training samples minimized mean squared error (MSE) residuals between observed and predicted (e6 and e7, Fig. 2H) paired air pollutant concentrations.

For model training, the input data had three groups of samples: training, regular testing and site-based independent testing. The training samples used the measurements of paired air pollutant concentrations to train the model through the MSE residuals of e6 and e7, whereas the regular test and site-based independent test samples did not use air pollutant concentration measurements Y′ in training but used them for testing and validation. Since all the samples need to satisfy the continuity equation, threshold and relation constraints, the input data (X) for training and regular testing were used to train the model through the squared residuals of e1−e5. For ensemble predictions, we used all the samples (including training, regular testing and site-based testing) as unsupervised samples to train the models to make them more regulated, conforming to the continuity equation and physical constraints. The learning algorithm is briefly described in Supplementary Algorithm S1. The sensitivity analysis was conducted to find optimal solutions for the hyperparameters including mini-batch size (1666), number of epochs (160), learning rate (0.01), beta (0.09), and epsilon 1e−3. The clip norm was set as 1 to fix potential exploding gradients. We mainly used the combination of non-linear activation of swish and elu plus linear activation in the network architecture. Sensitivity analysis showed that nonlinear activations of pure swish, tanh, or sigma, or their mixtures may lead to vanishing gradients and slow convergence, while the appropriate combination of nonlinear and linear activations (e.g., swish + linear (last layer) activations for sub network for parameter estimation, and swish + elu activations for sub network for concentration estimation) achieved the best results.

Uncertainty estimation

We employed a nonparametric bootstrap approach to quantify prediction uncertainty, as it performs effectively for non-normal and non-linear data without distributional assumptions (Kumar and Srivistava, 2012). The methodology generates B bootstrap samples through sampling with replacement from the original training data, where each bootstrap sample maintains the same size as the original dataset.

For each bootstrap sample, we trained an independent model and obtained predictions across all data subsets (training, regular testing, site-based testing, and prediction samples), yielding B prediction sets. The final predictions represent the ensemble mean across all bootstrap samples, while uncertainty estimation combines model bias and sample noise through the 0.632+ bootstrap estimator (Hastie, 2009), which balances potentially optimistic training/regular testing errors with potentially pessimistic site-based testing errors.

Prediction intervals were constructed by stratifying predicted concentrations into eight levels and deriving level-specific uncertainty estimates. The standard deviations of predictions within each stratum were calculated, and 95% prediction intervals were obtained using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the error distributions. Detailed mathematical formulations, bootstrap sampling procedures, and uncertainty quantification methods are provided in Supplementary Section S1.

Generalization error

Generally, generalization error is defined as the error of the trained model on all the data (including the data at the unseen locations) within the spatiotemporal domain, and consists of estimation bias, variance and random noise (Bishop and Nasrabadi, 2006). We used the training and testing samples to derive the bias, and all the data (training and testing samples, as well as predictions) to derive the variance, and then derive an estimate of the generalization error (Supplementary Section S2).

Employing PDEs (e1 and e2) and additional physical constraints e3−e5 in the residuals allowed us to encode physics in deep learning. The mini-batch gradient descent optimizer Adam, enabled the trained model to penalize the residual formula for nearly ‘‘infinite’’ samples to conform to the laws of fluid physics (Raissi et al., 2020). In practice, instead of the regular grid system simulated by CTM models, our variable-spacing method has greater flexibility for simulation of the fine- and coarse-scale transport of pair air pollutants. In addition, according to the assumptions of PDE conditional stability and the quadrature rules, when using a large size of unsupervised and/or supervised samples to regularize the model parameters to conform to the physical laws, the generalization error could be smaller, approaching the training/testing error (Mishra and Molinaro, 2022).

In air quality assessment, a trained model is usually used to generate predictions at new, unobserved locations and/or times. Testing using methods such as leave-one-out-cross-validation by random sampling across space and time tends to result in inflated confidence in model performance and does not provide a true generalization of predictions at new spatial locations. Instead, we validated the trained model by site-based independent testing that used data from locations that were not used in model training. Further, using the joint physics-informed neural network (jPINN) as the base model, the meta-algorithm of non-parametric bootstrap aggregation (bagging) (Breiman, 1996) was used to generate ensemble predictions and uncertainty estimates (Kumar and Srivistava, 2012). We derived model and sampling bias from training and testing errors, and obtained model variance using bagging predictions (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

Simulation of proxy advection and diffusion

For simulation of advection and diffusion fields, our model can directly output the relevant variables when predicting air pollutant concentrations. However, in deep learning applications, these variables cannot be used directly as advection velocities or diffusion coefficients without transformation—specifically, shifting and scaling to convert them into real field values. These outputs just serve as proxy variables for advection and diffusion fields, effectively reflecting relative variations and source–target relationships.

In our field simulation plots, we first generated grid surfaces of predicted pollutant concentrations and their field-simulated values. Using these surfaces across latitude and longitude coordinates, we extracted vectors to create vector plots that indicate the relative direction and magnitude of advection and diffusion flows. As an analytical approach, we presented and analyzed these fields alongside wind speed data and predicted air pollutant concentration grids, as shown in Results.

Experimental design

Study regions and data

To comprehensively evaluate our physics-informed framework across diverse environmental conditions, we selected two representative air pollutant pairs from geographically distinct regions: reactive nitrogen species (NO2/NOx) in California, USA, and particulate matter (PM2.5/PM10) in mainland China. This dual-region approach provides contrasting testing environments with different emission patterns, meteorological conditions, topographical features, and monitoring network characteristics. California offers high-resolution ancillary data (e.g., traffic density) and well-established monitoring infrastructure optimal for reactive gas modeling, while mainland China presents complex national-scale environmental variability ideal for testing particulate matter prediction capabilities.

California NO2 and NOx case

California serves as our primary testbed for nitrogen oxide modeling, leveraging its comprehensive monitoring infrastructure and high-quality covariate datasets (Supplementary Figure S3). The state’s mature air quality monitoring network, combined with detailed traffic and meteorological data, provides optimal conditions for evaluating physics-informed modeling of reactive atmospheric species.

Our dataset encompasses 86,692 weekly averaged NO2 and NOx measurements spanning 17 years (2004–2020) from 1,342 strategically distributed sampling locations. The monitoring network integrates two complementary approaches: approximately 150 long-term Air Quality System (AQS) government stations ensuring temporal continuity (Li et al., 2017), and 1,192 locations from intensive saturation sampling campaigns designed to capture fine-scale spatial variability near emission sources (Fruin et al., 2014; Su et al., 2009). This monitoring strategy ensures comprehensive coverage of both temporal trends and spatial gradients critical for understanding nitrogen oxide transport processes.

Detailed information on data preprocessing, covariate selection, and training is provided in Supplementary Section S3 and Table S1.

Mainland China PM2.5 and PM10 case

To demonstrate broader applicability and cross-regional transferability, we extended our framework to national-scale prediction of PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations across mainland China. This region serves as an ideal contrasting testbed, characterized by complex topography, heterogeneous meteorological patterns, diverse emission sources, and pronounced urban–rural gradients that present significant challenges for accurate continental-scale air pollution estimation.

We focused on particulate matter due to China’s extensive PM monitoring network, while NOx monitoring infrastructure remains limited in this region. Importantly, PM2.5 and PM10 maintain an analogous hierarchical relationship to NO2 and NOx, where PM2.5 represents a size-based subset of PM10, enabling direct application of our joint modeling framework and similar physical constraints.

Our dataset comprises 2,055,509 valid daily measurements from 1,604 monitoring sites spanning 2015–2018, obtained from the China National Environmental Monitoring Center (CNEMC). The monitoring network exhibits typical national-scale spatial heterogeneity, with higher station densities in eastern China and major metropolitan areas, while western and rural regions have sparser coverage (Meng and Sharad, 2025).

Comprehensive details on data preprocessing, covariate selection, and training are provided in Supplementary Section S4 and our previous work (Li et al., 2023).

Evaluation

To evaluate model generalization, we trained 100 base models for each method using bootstrap sampling, where each base model within a method used the same bootstrap sample, but different methods used different bootstrap samples. The statistical distributions and violin plots of R2 and RMSE were compared across six representative methods: jPINN, jPINN without elevation PDE, separate PINN, XGBoost, random forest, and full residual neural network (FRNN). The latter three machine learning methods have been increasingly adopted for air quality prediction due to their demonstrated accuracy and computational efficiency, and were implemented as separate models for the prediction of paired air pollutant concentration.

Our evaluation employed a rigorous three-tier sampling strategy designed to assess both predictive accuracy and spatial generalizability. We allocated 81% of sampling locations for model development, sub-divided into training (78%) and regular testing (22%) data, with the remaining 19% reserved exclusively for site-based independent testing. Spatial–temporal stratification used administrative regions (counties in California, provinces in mainland China) and seasons as combined stratifying factors to ensure representative coverage across diverse environmental conditions.

This partitioning design addresses a critical limitation in air quality modeling: traditional random cross-validation often overestimates model performance by including spatially correlated samples in both training and testing sets. Our site-based independent testing using entirely separate monitoring locations provides authentic assessment of spatial transferability. The sample proportion ratios approximate the optimal bootstrap allocation (0.368) established in statistical learning theory (Berrar and Dubitzky, 2013).

We selected R-squared (R2) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) as complementary evaluation metrics based on their distinct analytical strengths. RMSE quantifies absolute prediction error magnitude and remains sensitive to systematic deviations, while R2 measures explained variance and indicates prediction–observation correlation strength. This metric combination serves dual analytical purposes: quantifying prediction accuracy across model architectures and detecting systematic bias through performance differential analysis.

The key diagnostic indicator is the performance gap between regular testing (spatially correlated with training data) and site-based testing (spatially independent). Large discrepancies signal systematic bias and poor generalizability—common problems in purely data-driven approaches. Conversely, consistent performance across evaluation protocols indicates robust spatial transferability. This evaluation framework aligns with established standards in environmental modeling research (Samad et al., 2023; Cerezuela-Escudero et al., 2023) while providing enhanced rigor for assessing model reliability. For the ablation analysis, we conducted a minimum of 5 iterations to determine the mean or range of performance differences.

Results

Validation of the PINN model for NO2 and NOx prediction

For all models, we incorporated high-reliable known proxy data to represent emissions and related factors, including traffic (NOx estimated by the CALINE4 dispersion model, traffic density, road intersections, and distance to major roadways), restaurant locations from OpenStreetMap, and satellite data (OMI-NO2) with high spatiotemporal resolution. Meteorological variables were obtained from gridMET (4 km spatial resolution for the contiguous US) and MERRA-2 CTM (coarser spatial resolution). Because jPINN does not internally account for chemical transformations, we introduced reanalysis data from MERRA2 GMI to capture background pollutant transformation at a coarser scale. We also included aircraft emissions, land-use data, and satellite-derived NDVI to reduce potential bias from emission and deposition processes.

We adopted several strategies to address missing values in our datasets. For satellite products (MAIAC AOD and OMI-NO2), we applied a deep residual neural network (Li et al., 2020b; Li, 2021; Li and Wu, 2021) to impute missing data. However, imputation was not required for ground-based air pollutant measurements. The jPINN operates in a semi-supervised manner, using only available measurements to calculate RMSE residuals while simultaneously leveraging all samples — regardless of measurement availability — to enforce the continuity equation and physical constraints. This semi-supervised meshfree design enables the model to effectively handle incomplete measurement data without introducing additional imputation bias.

Performance statistics of base models demonstrate that jPINNs achieved high generalization, as evidenced by high mean R2 (0.95–0.96) and low RMSE (1.57 ppb for NO2; 3.95 ppb for NOx) (Supplementary Table S2) from site-based independent tests, along with minimal variance in these statistics (Fig. 3AB and Supplementary Fig. S4). While XGBoost, random forest, and full residual neural network reported comparable training (R2: 0.92–0.98) and regular testing (R2: 0.88–0.92) metrics, they exhibited poor site-based independent test results (R2: 0.53–0.75; RMSE: 2.96–14.74 ppb for NO2 and 7.06–41.42 ppb for NOx). The substantial difference (0.21–0.42 in mean R2 and 2.24–3.94 ppb [NO2] / 6.36–10.40 ppb [NOx] in mean RMSE) between test statistics highlights that performance metrics from regular tests for purely data-driven methods are overly optimistic, significantly underestimating their true generalizability. In contrast, jPINNs achieved the lowest generalization error (RMSE: 1.57 ppb for NO2 and 3.95 ppb for NOx) with minimal discrepancy between training/regular testing and site-based testing, substantially reducing estimation bias. Comparisons with separate PINNs reveal that joint training of NO2 and NOx distributions in jPINN better captures their complex physicochemical interactions, resulting in more stable learning (smaller variance, Fig. 3) and significantly improved generalizability (by 9%–15% in R2 on average). The elevation PDE enhanced generalization performance, increasing R2 by approximately 2% while reducing RMSE by 0.10–0.28 ppb. Concentration threshold constraints provided an additional 1%–2% improvement in site-based test R2, and the stoichiometric relationship constraint ensuring NO2 < NOx contributed a further 2%–3% improvement in generalization performance.

Application to PM2.5 and PM10 concentration estimation

Unlike the relatively homogeneous gaseous NO2 and NOx pollutants, particulate matter constitutes a heterogeneous mixture of solid and liquid aerosols with diverse physicochemical properties, originating from a complex network of emission sources and undergoing sophisticated atmospheric processing. PM2.5 and PM10 comprise primary particles directly emitted from anthropogenic and natural sources, as well as secondary particles formed through atmospheric chemical transformations. This compositional complexity necessitates more sophisticated modeling approaches that account for differential transport mechanisms, variable deposition velocities, and multivariate source contributions across spatial scales. The spatiotemporal distribution of particulate matter is governed not only by local emission inventories but also by mesoscale and synoptic meteorological patterns that facilitate long-range transport, enabling transboundary pollution episodes where air masses carry particulate pollutants across geopolitical boundaries. This multiscale transport phenomenon creates significant challenges for conventional modeling frameworks that may inadequately represent the intricate interplay between local pollution dynamics and regional atmospheric circulation patterns. Given the considerable differences between the NO2-NOx pair and PM2.5-PM10 pair, we utilized different covariates while maintaining similar network structures with two full-residual deep networks and comparable loss functions. The formulations for advection and diffusion remained conceptually similar across both pollutant pairs, although NO2-NOx involves more complex photochemical reactions. Our covariates were obtained from highly reliable and validated data sources, including NASA’s MERRA-2 reanalysis, the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) reanalysis, and NASA’s MAIAC products etc. Although most covariates just have coarse spatial resolution, the quality and reliability of these datasets ensure robust model inputs. For satellite observations such as MAIAC AOD and OMI-NO2, we applied similar deep learning-based imputation methods to address missing values.

Consistent with our NO2 and NOx findings, the jPINNs demonstrated superior generalization capabilities in particulate matter modeling, achieving exceptional site-based independent test metrics (mean R2 of 0.87–0.88; RMSE of 12.91 μg/m3 for PM2.5 and 21.03 μg/m3 for PM10) with remarkably low variance (Supplementary Table S3, Fig. 6). While conventional machine learning approaches — XGBoost, random forest, and full residual neural networks — showed promising performance in training (R2 up to 0.98) and standard testing (R2 up to 0.90), they faltered significantly under site-based independent testing conditions (R2: 0.61–0.77; RMSE: 16.89–21.69 μg/m3 for PM2.5 and 28.45–35.47 μg/m3 for PM10). This marked disparity (0.11–0.26 in mean R2 and 3.98–8.78 μg/m3 [PM2.5]/7.42–14.44 μg/m3 [PM10] in mean RMSE) between standard and site-based test results reveals that conventional spatiotemporal (not site-based) cross-validation metrics substantially overestimate the true generalizability of purely data-driven methods. In contrast, jPINNs maintained consistent performance across all evaluation protocols, effectively minimizing estimation bias. Comparative analyses with individual PINNs revealed that joint training of PM2.5 and PM10 distributions enables jPINNs to capture the complex physicochemical interdependencies between different particulate matter fractions, resulting in more robust learning dynamics and enhanced generalizability (average R2 improvement of 2%–3%). This demonstrates that physics-informed neural architectures with multitarget optimization frameworks offer a more reliable approach for environmental monitoring applications where spatial transferability is essential. Concentration threshold constraints for PM2.5 and PM10 contributed approximately 1% improvement in site-based test R2, while the size-based relationship constraint ensuring PM2.5 < PM10 provided an additional 1%–2% improvement in generalization performance.

Unlike the reactive NO2 and NOx species, PM2.5 and PM10 are predominantly inert air pollutants whose spatiotemporal distributions are governed by complex multiscale transport phenomena operating at both local and regional scales. To capture these dynamics, we generated daily prediction surfaces of PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations with proxy advection and diffusion fields simulated by our jPINN framework. Mineral dust entrained from arid and semi-arid landscapes was subsequently transported to downwind regions across North China (Creamean et al., 2013). As evidenced in both the static visualization and temporal evolution plots (Supplementary Figs. Our simulated advection fields effectively captured the directional vectors and velocity magnitudes of PM10 migration patterns which, while broadly aligned with synoptic wind fields, exhibited important deviations attributable to complex terrain–atmosphere interactions and mesoscale meteorological phenomena. Notably, PM2.5 demonstrated markedly different transport characteristics compared to PM10, with fine particulate matter distributions showing greater sensitivity to local emission sources and reduced influence from long-range dust transport—an observation consistent with the predominance of coarse rather than fine particles in desert dust aerosols. This differential behavior highlights the importance of size-specific physical parameterizations in accurately modeling particulate matter transport phenomena.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a joint physics-informed deep learning framework that encodes advection–diffusion mechanisms to substantially reduce generalization error bias in paired air pollutant predictions. Conventional data-driven machine learning approaches, devoid of fluid dynamics principles and physical constraints to regulate data assimilation and model training, frequently encounter overfitting challenges. This limitation leads to artificially inflated performance metrics during the evaluation protocols by spatiotemporal CV due to implicit data leakage through spatiotemporal cross-sampling. To address this critical methodological gap, we implemented site-based independent testing — wherein all observations from evaluation sites remain entirely excluded from the training process — which effectively mitigates information leakage and provides a more stringent and valid assessment of model generalization capabilities for practical deployment scenarios. Our comprehensive evaluation strategy encompassed two representative pairs of atmospheric pollutants across distinctive geographical domains: reactive nitrogen species (NO2 and NOx) in California’s complex urban–rural interface and inert particulate matter fractions (PM2.5 and PM10) distributed across mainland China’s diverse topographical and meteorological landscapes.

For reactive NO2 and NOx in California, our approach achieved unprecedented accuracy (R2: 0.95–0.96; RMSE: 1.57–3.95 ppb) in site-based independent testing, substantially outperforming conventional deep learning methods in control tests (R2: 0.53–0.75). Due to significant differences in temporal resolution (daily vs. weekly), spatial coverage, and validation methodologies across existing studies, direct performance comparisons are not appropriate. Previous studies (Ross et al., 2006; Karner et al., 2010; Di et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023) in California regions have reported varying performance levels (R2: 0.76–0.91), though these employed different spatiotemporal scales and validation approaches. Our results demonstrate that the jPINN framework can effectively model physicochemical interactions between NO2 and NOx within our specific weekly prediction framework, resulting in more stable learning dynamics and enhanced generalizability.

These quantitative comparisons demonstrate our framework’s capacity to significantly reduce generalization bias. The performance of our approach stems from its ability to simulate advection and diffusion fields by effectively solving the inverse problem parameters inherent in atmospheric transport phenomena. NO2 exhibits a relatively extended atmospheric lifetime compared to NO, particularly during adverse winter meteorological conditions when temperature inversions constrain vertical mixing and trap pollutants within the boundary layer. Complex topographical features, such as California’s mountain ranges and coastal interfaces, further modulate pollutant transport mechanisms through orographic effects and land-sea breeze circulations. Conventional machine learning approaches, including XGBoost, random forest, and full residual neural networks, lack explicit representation of these physical mechanisms, potentially resulting in systematically biased predictions in regions where atmospheric dynamics dominate pollutant distributions.

Tree-based methods (e.g., random forest (Hu et al., 2017), XGBoost (Wong et al., 2021), and extremely randomized trees (Wei et al., 2022)) have demonstrated computational efficiency and strong validation performance in established products such as CHAP, GHAP (https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html), and TAP (http://tapdata.org.cn). However, these methods fundamentally rely on discretization processes that generate prediction surfaces with potential discontinuities. This algorithmic characteristic can introduce discontinuity bias (Li et al., 2020a) that violates fluid dynamics continuity principles. While such artifacts are negligible at coarser resolutions (e.g., 1 km typical for CHAP/TAP), they may become increasingly problematic at very high resolutions (e.g., 100 m), potentially creating unrealistic spatial variability as demonstrated in our analysis.

In contrast, our jPINN framework functions as a universal continuous function approximator while simultaneously encoding atmospheric physics constraints, enabling simulation of NO2/NOx temporal evolution in accordance with mass conservation principles. This approach successfully captures fine-scale (e.g., 100 m for NO2/NOx) transport phenomena while maintaining physical consistency. The high-resolution advection and diffusion fields generated by jPINN (Fig. 5; Supplementary Figs. S17–S20) enabled mechanistic interpretation of NO2 and NOx spatiotemporal distributions, revealing that these pollutants primarily originate from local traffic emissions rather than long-range transport—a finding consistent with their known atmospheric chemistry and emission characteristics.

Our results demonstrate the framework’s capability to predict high pollution concentrations, including extreme episodes exceeding 200 ppb for NOx and 150 μg∕m3 for PM2.5 (Figs. 5 and 7). The jPINN framework is particularly well-suited for extreme event prediction through three key mechanisms: (1) explicit simulation of atmospheric transport dynamics via advection–diffusion physics, which governs the rapid spatiotemporal evolution characteristic of extreme events—capabilities demonstrated in analogous atmospheric applications (Verma et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025); (2) continuous function approximation that naturally captures sharp concentration gradients without the discretization artifacts inherent in tree-based methods; and (3) integration of high-resolution meteorological covariates to resolve fine-scale atmospheric processes driving extreme episodes.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. The relatively limited representation of extreme pollution events in our training data may contribute to underestimation of the hourly highest concentrations. Future work should prioritize: (1) incorporating more extreme pollution events into training datasets to improve high-concentration prediction accuracy; (2) systematic evaluation using metrics tailored to rare events (e.g., precision–recall curves, extreme value statistics); (3) validation with high-temporal-resolution observations to assess hourly extreme event prediction performance; and (4) optimization of model architecture and loss function weighting specifically for extreme value prediction.

In our experiment with PM2.5 and PM10 across mainland China, our framework achieved good accuracy (R2: 0.87–0.88; RMSE: 12.91 μg/m3 for PM2.5 and 21.03 μg/m3 for PM10) in site-based independent testing. Performance was superior to conventional deep learning approaches in our control tests and several previous studies (Xiao et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2018) utilizing cross-validation (R2: 0.78–0.84), and comparable to recent high-performing studies (Wei et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2021) (R2: 0.88).

Beyond comparable predictive accuracy, our approach offers two critical advantages over pure machine learning methods: explicit incorporation of physical constraints and enhanced mechanistic interpretability. For instance, while Wei et al. (2019) achieved similar performance using space–time extremely randomized trees (STET) for PM2.5 prediction, tree-based methods can produce discontinuous predictions (Li et al., 2020a) that may violate continuity properties inherent in atmospheric transport. More importantly, our framework simultaneously extracts interpretable physical parameters governing advection and diffusion processes—a capability absent in conventional methods where high predictive performance typically comes at the expense of physical understanding.

Our method’s simultaneous generation of simulated proxy fields for advection and diffusion alongside paired air pollutant concentration surfaces represents a significant methodological advancement at the intersection of deep learning and atmospheric science. While these simulated fields primarily function as mathematical proxies for inverse problems within our PINN framework to mitigate bias and reduce generalization error, they transcend mere computational artifacts to become valuable diagnostic tools for atmospheric transport phenomena interpretation. The analysis of the catastrophic April 15, 2015 sandstorm event in northern China — the most severe since 2002 — revealed striking divergence in transport mechanisms between particle size fractions: PM10 was predominantly transported via long-range advection from Outer Mongolia, driven by synoptic-scale meteorological factors (Rossby waves), while PM2.5 exhibited fundamentally distinct spatiotemporal patterns governed primarily by local emission sources and secondary formation processes. This observed dichotomy between coarse and fine particulate matter transport mechanisms raises profound questions about the efficacy of uniform regulatory approaches to particulate pollution control across different size fractions and underscores the necessity of tailored intervention strategies that account for the divergent physical behavior of these pollutants.

While atmospheric transport processes theoretically should contribute more to model accuracy at finer temporal scales (daily vs. weekly) due to the shorter timescales of advection and diffusion relative to temporal averaging periods, our comparative results between weekly NO2/NOx models in California and daily PM2.5/PM10 models in mainland China reveal that covariate quality and pollutant characteristics may override temporal scale effects. The substantial difference in covariate availability between regions — approximately 91 high-resolution variables for California (including fine-scale traffic density, distance to cooking facilities, and airport kernel density) versus 56 predominantly coarse-resolution meteorological variables for China — likely enabled the California models to more effectively capture transport processes at fine spatial resolution (100 m), even when aggregated to weekly temporal resolution. Additionally, fundamental differences in pollutant behavior complicate direct comparisons: NO2/NOx represents reactive species undergoing rapid photochemical interconversion that enhances sensitivity to transport phenomena, while PM2.5/PM10 comprises diverse chemical components with varying formation mechanisms, sources, and atmospheric lifetimes that introduce complexity potentially obscuring advection–diffusion signals. These mechanistic differences are reflected in our quantitative results, where advection–diffusion improved mean R2 by 0.21–0.42 for NO2/NOx compared to 0.11–0.26 for PM2.5/PM10, demonstrating that high-resolution, spatially-resolved covariates are essential for capturing fine-scale transport processes in air quality modeling, particularly for reactive pollutants.

A key finding emerges when examining scenarios with limited labeled data—a common constraint in environmental monitoring networks: increasing unsupervised physics-informed samples Nint significantly reduces generalization error even when labeled data remains sparse. These unsupervised samples constrain the solution space by enforcing physical consistency throughout the computational domain, preventing extrapolation beyond physically plausible ranges and thereby mitigating overfitting—particularly in regions with sparse or absent measurements. Our sensitivity analyses across diverse pollutant pairs and geographical regions confirm this principle, demonstrating that the performance advantage of physics-informed methods over conventional machine learning approaches increases as observational data becomes scarcer. This finding validates that physical laws provide powerful inductive biases for modeling complex environmental systems, and proves particularly valuable for predicting extreme pollution events where training samples at high concentrations are inherently limited.

Our experiments further demonstrate that incorporating additional physics-based regularizers (threshold constraints, chemical equilibrium relationships, and conservation principles represented by residuals e3−e5) into the loss function introduces complementary regularization mechanisms that collectively minimize generalization error. For high-resolution concentration surface predictions, our methodology employs a particularly efficient mesh-free approach: grid points awaiting prediction function simultaneously as unsupervised samples in our semi-supervised learning framework, reinforcing physical constraints such as mass conservation and preventing implausible concentration gradients. This mathematical framework illuminates the fundamental advantage of embedding physical principles into deep learning architectures—they effectively constrain the hypothesis space to physically realizable solutions, producing models that generalize robustly across the spatiotemporal heterogeneities inherent in environmental monitoring networks.

Our jPINN framework demonstrates superior computational efficiency while maintaining comparable or better predictive performance than graph neural network approaches. Compared to our previous graph neural network implementation (Li et al., 2023), we achieved similar site-based independent test performance for PM2.5 (R2: 0.88 vs. 0.87) and PM10 (R2: 0.87 vs. 0.85) using substantially simpler dual full residual networks instead of computationally intensive graph convolutional operations. For NO2 and NOx prediction in California, our jPINN approach slightly outperformed separate graph neural networks (R2 = 0.95–0.96 vs. 0.93–0.94), demonstrating improved accuracy alongside reduced computational complexity. This efficiency advantage is quantified in our computational analysis (Supplementary Table S4): while our training requires approximately 3× longer than baseline machine learning models (∼1.5 h for NO2/NOx, ∼3.5 h for PM2.5/PM10 per run on Intel Xeon(R) CPU E5–2620 (2.10 GHz) and GPU Titan XP (12G)), our approach requires only 7%–9% of the training time needed for graph neural networks while achieving superior performance. Complete ensemble training (100 models) requires 6.25 days for NO2/NOx and 14.3 days for PM2.5/PM10, making the framework feasible for resource-constrained environments as computational resources continue to advance. Beyond computational advantages, our physics-informed approach provides critical scientific benefits by ensuring systematic consistency with fluid dynamics principles and maintaining physically meaningful predictions. Unlike black-box machine learning models that can produce discontinuous or physically impossible results, our method explicitly simulates advection and diffusion processes, enabling extraction of interpretable parameters that reveal underlying atmospheric transport mechanisms. This interpretability proves essential for downstream applications: scientists can understand the physical drivers of pollution patterns, while policymakers can develop targeted mitigation strategies based on mechanistic insights. The framework maintains competitive performance across different data resolutions—achieving superior accuracy with high-resolution covariates while preserving physical interpretability even with coarse-resolution PM2.5/PM10 data. Given the critical importance of physical consistency in environmental modeling and the growing need for reliable air quality predictions in health impact assessments and policy development, the modest computational overhead is well-justified by substantial gains in scientific rigor and practical utility.

While Transformers excel in sequential data processing, they are not well-suited for our spatiotemporal prediction application because our dataset consists of irregular spatiotemporal sampling points rather than structured time series data, making sequence-to-sequence modeling inappropriate. Unlike conventional time series forecasting that predicts future values from historical sequences, our objective is to estimate air pollutant concentrations at arbitrary spatiotemporal coordinates using sparse, irregularly distributed measurements. Our flexible jPINN framework specifically addresses these challenges through a semi-supervised approach that naturally handles temporal indices from irregular data samples and explicitly incorporates advection and diffusion physics to model the underlying temporal evolution of air pollutants. This physics-informed approach is fundamentally more appropriate for spatiotemporal interpolation tasks than transformer-based sequential modeling, which relies on temporal patterns rather than physical transport mechanisms. However, we leveraged attention mechanisms — a core technique from Transformers — within our network architecture to dynamically weight the contributions of influential factors and enhance model performance for air quality assessment.

Conclusions

This study presents a PINN framework that integrates atmospheric transport equations with data-driven learning to achieve high-performance air quality predictions while maintaining physical consistency and mechanistic interpretability. We demonstrated this approach across contrasting pollutant systems — reactive NO2/NOx in California and inert PM2.5/PM10 across mainland China — achieving predictive accuracy comparable to or exceeding state-of-the-art tree-based methods while offering distinct methodological advantages.

Our framework addresses three critical limitations of existing pure machine learning approaches in air quality modeling. First, by functioning as a continuous function approximator constrained by fluid dynamics principles, jPINN naturally preserves spatial continuity at fine scales, avoiding the discontinuity artifacts that can arise from discretization-based methods at high resolutions. Second, the framework simultaneously predicts pollutant concentrations and diagnoses physically interpretable transport parameters (advection velocity and diffusion coefficients), providing mechanistic insights into pollution dynamics that remain inaccessible to conventional black-box algorithms. Third, by enforcing physical constraints throughout the computational domain, including locations without measurements, jPINN demonstrates enhanced robustness in data-sparse regions where pure data-driven methods are prone to overfitting.

A key finding from our sensitivity analyses is that increasing unsupervised physics-informed samples substantially reduces generalization error even when labeled monitoring data remains sparse. This result demonstrates that physical laws provide powerful inductive biases for environmental modeling, with the performance advantage over conventional methods increasing as observational data becomes scarcer—a particularly valuable property given the inherent sparsity of environmental monitoring networks.

Compared to established products such as CHAP (Di et al., 2019) and TAP (Wei et al., 2022), which successfully employ tree-based methods to generate daily, kilometer-scale air quality surfaces, our jPINN framework serves as a complementary tool designed for applications requiring ultra-high spatial resolution (e.g., 100 m), physical interpretability, or robust predictions in data-limited regions. The consistent performance across diverse pollutant types and geographical contexts highlights the framework’s broad applicability for environmental modeling challenges where integrating physical principles with data-driven approaches offers advantages over purely empirical methods.

This work contributes to the growing recognition that hybrid physics-data approaches can address fundamental limitations of pure machine learning in scientific domains—a direction of increasing importance as environmental monitoring systems continue to generate higher-resolution yet spatially incomplete datasets.
