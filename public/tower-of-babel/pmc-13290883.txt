Effects of Guselkumab on Structural Damage Progression in Patients with Active Psoriatic Arthritis
Authors: Christopher T. Ritchlin, Laura C. Coates, Philip J. Mease, Désirée van der Heijde, Proton Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory disease with a heterogeneous presentation including peripheral joint arthritis, axial inflammation, enthesitis, dactylitis, and psoriatic skin and nail changes. A substantial proportion of patients develop structural joint damage that can be monitored using standard radiographs. In patients with PsA, structural damage progression has been associated with significant impairment of physical function, health-related quality of life, and work productivity. Tumor necrosis factor inhibitors were the first biologic therapies approved for patients with PsA and have demonstrated efficacy in reducing the rate of structural damage progression in these patients. More recently, biologics targeting the interleukin (IL)-23p19 subunit (guselkumab and risankizumab) and IL-17 (secukinumab, ixekizumab, and bimekizumab) have been approved to treat patients with active PsA and are the subject of this review, with a focus on guselkumab. In separate phase 3, randomized, controlled studies, participants with active PsA treated with guselkumab, secukinumab, ixekizumab, and bimekizumab exhibited less structural damage progression in comparison with placebo. Both guselkumab and risankizumab inhibit the IL-23p19 subunit; however, to date, only guselkumab has demonstrated statistically significant efficacy in inhibiting structural damage progression in this patient population.

Psoriatic Arthritis Disease Burden

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory disease of a heterogenous nature that primarily affects the skin and joints. The disease burden of PsA can be substantial, with symptoms spanning six domains recognized by the Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis (GRAPPA): peripheral joints, axial disease, enthesitis, dactylitis, skin, and nails [1]. The joint damage associated with PsA is progressive over time and may ultimately become irreversible [2], leading to poorer long-term outcomes, including reduced physical function and quality of life, and greater impairments in work productivity [3]. A delay in PsA diagnosis as short as 6 months is associated with a higher number of erosive joints and greater impairment of physical function, despite appropriate treatment [4]. Characteristics associated with increased risk of radiographic progression include the number of swollen joints, dactylitis, enthesitis, existing joint damage (numbers of joints with erosions or joint space narrowing [JSN]), and serum C-reactive protein (CRP) level [5–8]. Current treatment recommendations support the use of biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) for patients at increased risk of radiographic progression [1, 9, 10].

Tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) were the first class of bDMARD approved for patients with PsA in 2002 and remain the first bDMARD prescribed for many of these patients [1, 9, 10]. TNFi are efficacious in improving the signs and symptoms of PsA as well as inhibiting progression of structural joint damage [1, 9, 10]. Additionally, previous studies of TNFi in participants with active PsA and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) found that these agents limited radiographic progression vs placebo even among participants who did not achieve clinical response by other measures [11–13]. These results suggest a disconnect between disease activity and structural damage progression in that treatment may confer protection against radiographic damage even in the absence of achieving low levels of overall disease activity. While TNFi are effective for many patients with PsA, there remain substantial patient populations who experience either lack of efficacy or loss of efficacy with these therapies [14], or for whom TNFi use is contraindicated [15]. More recently, targeted synthetic DMARDs inhibiting Janus kinase and phosphodiesterase 4 [16–18] and bDMARDs that block T cell activation [19] and inhibit interleukin (IL)-12/23p40, IL-23p19, IL-17A, and IL-17A/F have been approved for patients with active PsA [20–25].

Here we review the effects of therapeutic antibodies inhibiting IL-23p19 and IL-17 on structural damage progression, with a focus on the IL-23p19-subunit inhibitor guselkumab.

Structural Joint Damage in PsA

Peripheral arthritis is the most common domain affected in patients with PsA and presents in either an oligoarticular or polyarticular pattern [26]. The hands and wrists are the most commonly affected joints, followed by feet, ankles, knees, and shoulders [27]. Patients often present with an oligoarticular pattern, characterized by ≤ 4 joints affected in an asymmetric manner, and many will progress to the more symmetrical polyarticular (≥ 5) pattern as the number of active joints increases [28]. Polyarticular PsA may appear similar to the distribution observed in patients with RA, with the exception that involvement of the distal interphalangeal joints is characteristic of PsA, but not RA [29]. The structural damage associated with PsA is typically slow [30]; however, up to half of all patients with PsA exhibit bone damage on radiography within 2 years of diagnosis [31].

The manifestations of structural damage in PsA are diverse, affecting both the axial and peripheral joints [32]. In contrast to RA, the radiographic changes that occur in PsA are generally a combination of destructive damage in the bone and cartilage (erosions and JSN) and proliferative new bone formation [27, 31–33]. The “pencil-in-cup” deformity, a result of severe erosions in the hands, is a notable radiographic feature that is unique to PsA [27]. Enthesitis in the peripheral joints can be observed as calcification and enthesophyte formation as well as erosions at the bone insertion sites for tendons and ligaments [34]. Pathologic bone formation in the axial skeleton can appear as syndesmophytes and ankyloses in patients with PsA [32].

Plain radiography has historically been the standard imaging technique for monitoring structural damage in patients with PsA and is considered the gold standard by global regulatory agencies for clinical trials. This imaging modality is relatively inexpensive and accessible for most patients and can be performed quickly [33]. However, plain radiography is limited to two-dimensional images [35], and the early stages of structural damage in PsA are often subtle and may be difficult to recognize on conventional radiographs [31, 35]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is highly sensitive and can detect soft tissue swelling and bone marrow edema as signs of inflammation; however, the high cost and low accessibility relative to plain radiography limit its utilization [35, 36]. Ultrasound is another option for assessing soft tissue (e.g., tendons and entheses) and has become increasingly popular in managing patients with PsA [37]. This imaging technique is particularly useful in detecting peripheral joint anomalies, such as synovitis and enthesitis, while generally being easily accessible for most patients [35]. However, ultrasonography is limited in that it cannot penetrate bone and, therefore, has a lower sensitivity for detecting erosions relative to MRI and also cannot be used to detect axial manifestations [33].

Assessments

Given the effects of structural joint damage on long-term patient outcomes, quantifying the extent and progression of structural joint damage is an important aspect of clinical trials evaluating treatments for patients with PsA [38]. There are several radiographic scoring systems for assessing peripheral joint damage in patients with PsA; all were initially developed for patients with RA and have been modified to incorporate PsA features [27]. The PsA-modified total Sharp score (mTSS) and, more commonly, the PsA-modified van der Heijde-Sharp (vdH-S) score are applied to assess structural damage progression in randomized controlled trials [27]. Both scoring systems consider erosions and JSN of the hands and feet, with more joints included in the vdH-S scoring system. The mTSS (range 0–470) includes hands (28 joints), wrists (erosion, 14 joints; JSN, 12 joints), and feet (erosions, 12 joints; JSN, 10 joints) [38]. Erosions are scored on a scale of 0 (no erosion) to 5 (destruction involving > 80% of the joint) with a maximum subscore of 270, and JSN is rated on a scale of 0 (none) to 4 (ankylosis) for a maximum subscore of 200. Pencil-in-cup changes, gross osteolysis, joint space widening, and other PsA-associated bone changes are also scored but are not included in the erosion and JSN subscores.

The PsA-modified vdH-S score (range 0–528) assesses erosions and JSN in 40 joints in the hands for maximum scores of 200 and 160, respectively, and in 12 joints in the feet, with maximum scores of 120 and 48, respectively (Fig. 1) [27]. Erosions are scored from 0 to 5: 0 = no erosions, 1 = discrete erosion, 2 = large erosion not passing the midline, 3 = large erosion passing the midline; combinations of these result in scores of 4 and 5 [27]. The maximum score per joint is 5 in the hands and 10 (5 for each side of the joint) in the foot. JSN is scored as follows: 0 = normal, 1 = narrowing up to 25%, 2 = narrowing up to 50%, 3 = narrowing of 50–99% or subluxation, and 4 = absence of joint space; ankylosis; or complete luxation.

Scoring systems utilized for evaluating radiographic progression in PsA have important limitations to consider when assessing this patient population. Both scores are restricted to the hands and feet and, therefore, do not include large peripheral (e.g., knee, ankle) or axial joints, and there is no consensus on what constitutes a “clinically significant” level of progression [39]. Nevertheless, these scoring methods have discriminated between placebo and several active treatments in patients with polyarticular PsA (see below).

IL-23 and Structural Damage

IL-23 is known as a “master regulator” in the pathogenesis of psoriasis and PsA by inducing T helper (Th) 17 cell differentiation and subsequent IL-17 expression [40]. Dysregulation of the IL-23/IL-17 axis in the skin is a primary driver of the keratinocyte proliferation characteristic of psoriatic lesions [41]. Support for the mechanism of psoriatic skin inflammation induced by IL-23 is the finding in murine models that IL-23 expands myeloid cell populations in the bone marrow (myelopoiesis) and IL-17A+ myeloid cells are increased in psoriatic plaques [42]. Additionally, elevated expression of IL-23 and IL-17 has been associated with pathologic changes in the joints. In the IL-23 murine minicircle model, severe bone loss and enhanced osteoclastogenesis were observed to be associated with an expansion of the myeloid precursor cells [43]. In a separate murine model of psoriasis, mice with elevated levels of IL-23 in the skin developed both psoriatic-like skin disease and experienced joint changes analogous to dactylitis and enthesitis in humans [44]. Both IL-17 and IL-23 are involved in bone homeostasis which can be markedly altered in patients with PsA [45]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF, IL-17, and IL-23, induce osteoclast formation, leading to the excess bone resorption that is evident as bone erosion in radiographs [45]. In contrast, the mechanism of bone formation in PsA is not well understood, with multiple pathways likely involved in stimulating osteoblasts [45, 46].

Guselkumab

Guselkumab is a fully human, dual-acting, monoclonal antibody that selectively inhibits the IL-23p19 subunit [24] and is also able to bind CD64+ myeloid cells [47]. DISCOVER-1 [48] and DISCOVER-2 [49] were phase 3, multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled trials of adults with active PsA. In both studies, participants had to have a PsA diagnosis for ≥ 6 months and meet the ClASsification criteria for Psoriatic ARthritis (CASPAR) [50], with active disease despite standard nonbiologic therapies. Eligible DISCOVER-1 participants had ≥ 3 swollen joints (0–66), ≥ 3 tender joints (0–68), and CRP level ≥ 0.3 mg/dL, while DISCOVER-2 participants were required to have ≥ 5 swollen joints (0–66), ≥ 5 tender joints (0–68), and CRP level ≥ 0.6 mg/dL. In DISCOVER-1, 31% of participants received one or two prior TNFi therapies, while all participants in DISCOVER-2 were biologic-naïve.

Participants in both studies were randomly assigned to receive guselkumab 100 mg every 4 weeks (Q4W), guselkumab 100 mg at weeks 0 and 4, then every 8 weeks (Q8W) thereafter, or placebo with crossover to guselkumab 100 mg Q4W at week 24. The final study drug administration in DISCOVER-1 was at week 48, with final efficacy and safety assessments at weeks 52 and 60, respectively [51]. In DISCOVER-2, the final study drug administration was at week 100, with final assessments at weeks 100 (efficacy) and 112 (safety) [52]. In DISCOVER-1, 381 participants were randomly assigned and received guselkumab Q4W (N = 128), guselkumab Q8W (N = 127), or placebo (N = 126) [48]; 739 participants were randomized and received study agent in DISCOVER-2 (Q4W N = 245, Q8W N = 248, placebo N = 246, respectively) [49].

In both studies, the primary endpoint was the proportion of patients achieving ≥ 20% improvement in American College of Rheumatology response criteria (ACR20) at week 24. Significantly greater proportions of guselkumab-treated participants achieved this response in both DISCOVER-1 (Q4W 59%, Q8W 52% vs placebo, 22%; p < 0.0001 for both) [48] and DISCOVER-2 (Q4W 64%, Q8W 64% vs placebo, 33%; p < 0.0001 for both) [49]. Guselkumab has demonstrated multidomain efficacy in these patient populations, with Q4W- and Q8W-treated patients achieving higher response rates at week 24 vs placebo for resolution of enthesitis and dactylitis, complete skin clearance, minimal disease activity, and low disease activity as assessed by composite indices. Guselkumab has further demonstrated robust efficacy across PsA domains through up to 2 years [51–53].

The effect of guselkumab on structural damage was evaluated only in DISCOVER-2. At baseline, the mean age was 45–46 years across treatment groups, 42–52% of participants were female; on average, participants had 12–13 swollen joints and 20–22 tender joints, and 64–72% of participants had enthesitis [49]. Radiographs of the hands and feet were obtained at weeks 0, 24, 52, and 100, were centrally read, and scored independently by two readers who were blinded to treatment group and timepoint [49, 52, 54]. Structural damage progression was assessed using the PsA-modified vdH-S score [27]. At week 24, least squares (LS) mean changes in vdH-S scores from baseline were statistically significantly lower in the Q4W group vs placebo (0.29 vs 0.95; p = 0.011). LS mean changes from baseline were numerically lower in the Q8W group relative to placebo but did not reach statistical significance (0.52 vs 0.95; p = 0.072) [49]. Low levels of structural damage progression were observed through 2 years in both guselkumab treatment groups [52, 54]. Mean changes in PsA-modified vdH-S score were numerically lower in the second year of treatment (weeks 52–100) relative to the first year [52].

Structural damage progression was also evaluated in the context of clinical responses, i.e., ACR20/50/70, Psoriatic Arthritis Disease Activity Score low disease activity (PASDAS LDA), Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA) LDA, minimal disease activity (MDA), and normalized Health Assessment Questionnaire-Disability Index (HAQ-DI; physical function) [55]. Participants who achieved these responses at 1 year had lower mean changes in vdH-S score at week 100 compared with those who were nonresponders at 1 year [55]. Additionally, among participants who were classified as radiographic nonprogressors (change in total vdH-S ≤ 0.5), response rates for achieving MDA as well as the MDA criteria of swollen joint count ≤ 1, patient pain ≤ 15, patient global assessment ≤ 20, and HAQ-DI ≤ 0.5 were numerically greater than those among participants who were radiographic progressors at weeks 24, 52, and 100 [55]. In a separate analysis, achievement of DAPSA LDA at week 8 was significantly associated with less structural damage progression through week 100 [56].

The cartilage breakdown that occurs with progressive inflammatory joint disease results in increased circulating levels of collagen fragments. Elevated levels of collagen degradation markers, indicating loss of cartilage in the joints, have been previously observed in patients with other inflammatory diseases, such as RA, ankylosing spondylitis, and systemic sclerosis [57–60]. In DISCOVER-2 participants, baseline levels of type I, III, IV, and VI collagen degradation (C1M, C3M, C4M, and C6M, respectively) were higher relative to healthy controls and were correlated with baseline 28-joint Disease Activity Score using CRP (DAS28-CRP) [61]. At week 24, levels of these biomarkers were lower among participants in the guselkumab groups in comparison with those in the placebo group [61]. Among participants in the Q4W and Q8W groups, reductions from baseline were observed for all four collagen degradation markers and reached statistical significance for C1M (weeks 24, 52, and 100) and C6M (week 100) [62].

APEX (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04882098) is an ongoing phase 3b, multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled study of guselkumab in biologic-naïve participants with active PsA who have demonstrated an inadequate response to standard therapies [63]. Eligible participants were enrolled and randomized 7:5:7 to receive guselkumab 100 mg at weeks 0, 4, and Q8W thereafter, guselkumab 100 mg Q4W, or placebo with crossover to guselkumab Q4W at week 24 [63]. The study includes a main study period through week 48 followed by a long-term extension through week 156 in contrast to the final efficacy timepoint of week 100 in DISCOVER-2 (Fig. 2). Risankizumab

Risankizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that inhibits the IL-23p19 subunit [25]. The efficacy of risankizumab in patients with PsA was evaluated in KEEPsAKE-1, a phase 3, multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled study [65]. The inclusion criteria for this study were generally similar to that in DISCOVER-2. Eligible participants were adults having a PsA diagnosis for ≥ 6 months and meeting CASPAR criteria with ≥ 5 swollen and ≥ 5 tender joints and evidence of psoriasis. In contrast with DISCOVER-2, participants in KEEPsAKE-1 had to have a CRP level ≥ 0.3 mg/dL (vs ≥ 0.6 mg/dL in DISCOVER-2) and ≥ 1 erosive joint as determined by centrally read radiographs of the hands and feet (vs no erosion requirement in DISCOVER-2). Participants in both studies had active disease despite prior therapy with standard nonbiologic therapies, and all were biologic-naïve.

A total of 964 participants were enrolled in KEEPsAKE-1, with 483 randomized to receive risankizumab 150 mg at weeks 0, 4, and 16 and 481 randomized to receive placebo. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of participants in this study were generally consistent with those of the DISCOVER-2 population. Across treatment groups in KEEPsAKE-1, the median age was 52 years; 48–51% were female; mean numbers of swollen and tender joints were approximately 12 and 21, respectively; and 60–61% of participants had enthesitis [65]. At week 24, significantly greater proportions of risankizumab-treated participants achieved an ACR20 response than placebo-treated participants (57.3% vs 33.5%; p < 0.001). Risankizumab demonstrated multidomain efficacy in this population, including achievement of resolution of enthesitis and dactylitis, almost clear skin, and minimal disease activity.

Structural damage progression was evaluated using centrally read plain radiographs of the hands and feet in both APEX and KEEPsAKE-1. While the APEX study utilized the PsA-modified vdH-S score (range 0–528), the KEEPsAKE-1 study employed the mTSS (range 0–470). In contrast with APEX, there was no significant difference observed in mean change from baseline in mTSS between the risankizumab group and the placebo group at week 24 in KEEPsAKE-1 (0.23 vs 0.32; p = 0.50) [65].

IL-17 Inhibitors and Structural Damage Progression

The phase 3, randomized, placebo-controlled FUTURE 5 study evaluated the efficacy and safety of secukinumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IL-17A, in participants with active PsA [66]. FUTURE 5 participants had to meet the CASPAR criteria and have active disease (≥ 3 swollen and ≥ 3 tender joints) despite prior treatment with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or up to two TNFi; there was no criterion for CRP level. In total, 996 participants were randomized to receive SC injections of placebo, secukinumab 300 mg or 150 mg at weeks 0, 1, 2, and 3, then Q4W, or secukinumab 150 mg Q4W. Structural damage was assessed by plain radiography (hands and feet) and scored using the PsA-modified vdH-S score (centrally read). At week 24, participants receiving secukinumab 300 mg with loading dose, 150 mg with loading dose, or 150 mg without loading dose had significantly smaller mean changes in total PsA-modified vdH-S score vs placebo (0.08 [p < 0.01], 0.17 [p < 0.05], and − 0.09 [p < 0.05] vs 0.50).

The efficacy and safety of ixekizumab, another IL-17A inhibitor, were evaluated in participants with active PsA in the phase 3, randomized, placebo-controlled study, SPIRIT-1 [67]. Eligible participants met the CASPAR criteria and had ≥ 3 swollen joints, ≥ 3 tender joints, and either ≥ 1 erosive joint (hands or feet; centrally read) or CRP ≥ 6 mg/L; all were biologic-naïve. Radiographic damage was assessed with the PsA-modified vdH-S score. Enrolled participants (N = 417) were randomly assigned to receive ixekizumab 80 mg every 2 weeks (Q2W), ixekizumab 80 mg Q4W, adalimumab 40 mg Q2W, or placebo. Participants receiving ixekizumab Q2W, ixekizumab Q4W, or adalimumab had significantly less radiographic progression at week 24 vs placebo (mean change in PsA-modified vdH-S: 0.08 [p ≤ 0.001]; 0.17 [p ≤ 0.01]; 0.10 [p ≤ 0.001] vs 0.49, respectively).

Bimekizumab is a bDMARD inhibiting IL-17A/F. The efficacy and safety of bimekizumab, a bDMARD inhibiting IL-17A/F, were evaluated in the phase 3, randomized, placebo-controlled study, BE OPTIMAL [68]. Participants in this study met the CASPAR criteria and had ≥ 3 swollen joints, ≥ 3 tender joints, and history of/current evidence of psoriasis despite standard nonbiologic therapies; all were biologic-naïve. Structural damage was evaluated using the PsA-modified vdH-S score; radiographs of the hands and feet were centrally read. In total, 852 participants were randomly assigned to receive bimekizumab 160 mg Q4W, placebo, or adalimumab 40 mg Q2W. At week 16, bimekizumab-treated participants had significantly less radiographic progression vs placebo (mean change 0.01 vs 0.31; p = 0.0012); participants in the adalimumab reference group had a mean change of − 0.03 (no statistical testing performed).

Future Outlook

Assessing efficacy in inhibiting structural damage progression in patients with PsA is often complex and limited by various clinical and practical considerations. The variable presentation of joint damage in patients with PsA and generally slow rates of progression may limit the ability to assess radiographic changes over the typical trial duration. In particular, placebo-controlled periods in trials for PsA are generally restricted to 6 months or shorter for ethical reasons. Although the DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2 studies demonstrated the efficacy of both guselkumab dosing regimens in treating the signs and symptoms of active PsA, the low levels of structural damage progression observed in the Q8W group (DISCOVER-2) did not reach statistical significance in comparison with placebo.

As noted in the descriptions above, previous studies assessing the efficacy of therapies inhibiting IL-23 and IL-17 on radiographic progression in PsA have utilized various combinations of criteria (including swollen and tender joint counts, number of erosive joints, and CRP level) to select populations with an elevated risk of structural joint damage. The APEX inclusion criteria were informed by analyses utilizing data from prior studies, including DISCOVER-1 and -2. Several factors were evaluated for utility in predicting structural damage: PsA subtype, PsA duration, serum CRP level, dactylitis count, presence of enthesitis, numbers of joints with erosions and JSN, and number of swollen joints [63]. Of these, the characteristics with the strongest predictive value for identifying patients at risk for future radiographic progression were serum CRP level and the number of erosive joints. Thus, to be eligible for APEX, participants had to have ≥ 3 swollen and ≥ 3 tender joints, a serum CRP level ≥ 0.3 mg/dL, and ≥ 2 erosive joints (hands/feet; centrally read) at baseline. In contrast, DISCOVER-2 participants were required to have ≥ 5 swollen joints, ≥ 5 tender joints, and a CRP level ≥ 0.6 mg/dL; however, there was no requirement for erosive joints to enroll (Fig. 2). APEX was adequately powered to evaluate changes in structural damage progression as the sample size was informed by changes in vdH-S scores observed in DISCOVER-2 [63]. Additionally, as part of the randomization strategy of APEX, participants were stratified into four stratification levels representing relative risk of radiographic progression and defined by combinations of the following factors: variability between radiograph readers (difference in number of erosive joints < 8; ≥ 8), corticosteroid use (yes/no), number of erosive joints (> 5 to < 16; ≥ 16), and CRP (< 5.7 mg/dL; ≥ 5.7 mg/dL) at baseline [64].

Stratification by predicted risk of progression was a unique approach that allowed for a more even distribution of participants across treatment groups [64] in comparison with DISCOVER-2. Of note, the overall disease activity of APEX participants at baseline was similar to that reported in the aforementioned prior studies evaluating the effect of bDMARDs on radiographic progression among participants with PsA [49, 64–68]. Although the key eligibility criteria varied across studies and the presence of erosive joints at baseline was not required for all studies, the reported disease activity as assessed by ACR response components was consistent across study populations (Table 1). Placebo-controlled studies have long been considered the gold standard for assessing treatment efficacy, and comparisons with placebo rather than an active control are required by regulatory agencies for many conditions. However, their use in evaluating PsA treatments is complicated by the ethical challenges of using a placebo when other efficacious treatments are available [69]. With anticipated advances in data collection methods, such as the use of more sensitive imaging and artificial intelligence, it is also expected that clinical trial design will ultimately change [70]. Future clinical trials may benefit from less reliance on placebo controls, shorter observation times, and use of advanced imaging techniques.

The ability to inhibit structural damage progression, along with achieving minimal levels of disease across domains, is an important factor in achieving remission and improving long-term outcomes in patients with PsA. Findings from studies such as APEX will broaden our understanding of structural damage in this patient population and the efficacy of guselkumab in patients with PsA and an increased risk of structural damage progression.
