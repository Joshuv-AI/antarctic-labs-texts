Enhancing the discharge capacity of contracted weirs using novel models
Authors: Erdinc Ikinciogullari, M Cihan Aydin, Ali Emre Ulu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study utilizes three-dimensional Computational Fluid Dynamics simulations to investigate the implementation of stepped and diagonal platforms as a means to enhance the discharge capacity of contracted sharp-crested weirs. Using numerical models validated against experimental data, various stepped and diagonal shifted configurations were compared to traditional rectangular slit weirs. The results demonstrate that these advanced geometries significantly outperform standard designs, increasing discharge efficiency by up to 30% across all nappe heads. While extended crest lengths drive this enhanced performance, increasing the number of steps introduces nappe interference at the corners, which can negatively impact the discharge coefficient. Furthermore, flow field analysis reveals that although these configurations improve discharge per unit channel width, asymmetrical vortices and streamline contractions may reduce efficiency per unit crest length. Ultimately, the study confirms that the proposed flow-area-based dimensionless parameter effectively characterizes hydraulic constraints, proving that stepped and diagonal designs offer a viable solution for enhancing weir capacity in hydraulic engineering.Keywords: Computational Fluid Dynamics, Contracted weir, Diagonal weir, Discharge coefficient, Stepped sidewall, OpenFOAMSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

The increase in extreme hydro-meteorological events due to global climate change necessitates the development of innovative approaches for water resources and flood risk management. Weirs are fundamental hydraulic structures widely utilized for flow control in open channels. The primary objective in weir design is to establish a precise hydraulic relationship between the upstream head and the discharge1. The weir height relative to the upstream head, the crest geometry, and the crest length are among the fundamental hydraulic parameters governing the discharge capacity. Hence, the discharge capacity denotes the flow passing over the crest for a given hydraulic head. Among these variables, the crest length has the most dominant influence on the discharge capacity. Consequently, the effective crest length can be maximized by designing the weir axis to fold in the planform. It has been reported that labyrinth side weirs exhibit a discharge coefficient up to five times greater than that of linear rectangular side weirs2,3. On the other hand, Aydin et al.4 reported that while the discharge capacities of stepped weirs in plan are up to twofold higher than those of linear-axis weirs, the discharge efficiency of stepped labyrinth weirs has increased by up to 50% compared to equivalent angled diagonal weirs. Ahmed and Altalib5 investigated the discharge coefficient of curved and curved labyrinth weirs, specifically analyzing the effects of cycle number, total curvature angle, and semicircular openings. The study found that reducing the cycle number increased the discharge coefficient in curved labyrinth weirs by up to 35.94%, while increasing the total curvature angle raised this rate to 32.4%. This superior hydraulic performance renders labyrinth weirs a viable alternative, particularly in projects where topographic constraints prevent the widening of the weir structure. Sharp-crested weirs are utilized in applications requiring high-precision discharge measurements. To maximize measurement accuracy, adequate aeration must be provided to ensure that the pressure beneath the nappe remains atmospheric6. Furthermore, the design of these weirs with full or partial contractions introduces significant flexibility into the hydraulic design7. The utilization of partially contracted weirs allows for lower crest heights, thereby minimizing the head drop and conserving the available delivery head8. Although their discharge coefficients vary depending on the weir geometry and hydraulic head, advanced calibration methods enable contracted weirs to provide a measurement accuracy comparable to that of standard fully contracted models7.

In the literature, studies on the hydraulic performance of contracted weirs have predominantly focused on the estimation of the discharge coefficient (Cd) and researchers proposed various empirical equations8–11. In these studies, the sidewalls are conventionally treated in a linear form12. experimentally investigated the hydraulic performance of rectangular sharp-crested weirs, proposing a more stable weir velocity (V) approach as an alternative to complex Cd formulations. Recent studies have shown that the discharge coefficient in weirs is strongly dependent not only on the water head but also on dimensionless parameters such as crest geometry, span ratio, constriction effect, and effective flow area. Al-Suhili et al.13 experimentally investigated contracted rectangular sharp-crest weirs with different weir width/channel width ratios and reported that the discharge coefficient is particularly affected by parameters such as the ratio of total head to narrowed span and constriction ratio. Similarly, Li et al.14 experimentally evaluated the discharge formulas in fully contracted rectangular sharp-crest weirs under high discharge and high water head conditions and showed that the constriction ratio is particularly important on the discharge coefficient at high heads. On the other hand, current studies on labyrinth and folded crest weirs reveal that changing the effective crest length or flow passage geometry under limited channel width can increase the discharge capacity; For example, in labyrinth weirs, the folded crest geometry can provide a higher discharge capacity compared to linear weirs of the same channel width15. Furthermore, it has been reported that anti-vortex elements can increase the discharge capacity in stepped labyrinth side weirs, and that in stepped labyrinth weirs, the stepped arrangement under a constant crest length affects hydraulic performance4,16. Hamidi et al.17 experimentally investigated the influence of the contraction ratio and flow discharge on energy dissipation of contracted thin weirs. In order to improve the hydraulic performance, altering the shape of the weir has been a subject of interest18–20. Using labyrinth configurations is a frequently employed alternative to increase discharge capacity without expanding the channel width21. The effectiveness of this approach is supported by numerous studies on various labyrinth models4,21–25. Furthermore, researchers have highlighted that the labyrinth form significantly improves discharge capacity compared to classical rectangular models2,26,27.

Conventional contracted sharp-crest weirs offer practical solutions for flow measurement and control under conditions of limited channel width. However, the contracted opening can limit discharge capacity and make hydraulic effects such as high velocities, flow separation, energy loss, and nappe ventilation more pronounced in the downstream region. Therefore, in cases where the available channel width cannot be increased or where structural space constraints exist, alternative weir geometries that can improve the effective flow passage area are needed. Although it is known that folded or angled geometries can improve hydraulic performance in conventional and side weirs, the applications of this approach on contracted weirs are quite limited. One of the rare studies in this context, Gupta et al.28, investigated the discharge characteristics of a sharp-crest contracted triangular planform weir and showed that this geometry could provide higher efficiency compared to conventional straight weirs. While this finding reveals the potential of giving angled forms to contracted sections, contracted weir designs with stepped geometries have not yet been sufficiently investigated. Accordingly, the main objective of the present study is to fill this gap by examining the hydraulic performance of newly developed stepped and diagonally contracted weirs. For this purpose, three-dimensional numerical simulations, validated with experimental data from the literature, were performed using the open-source OpenFOAM software, and the discharge coefficients of the proposed models were evaluated comparatively with those of a standard rectangular contracted weir.

Physical model

In this study, the experimental data of a partially contracted rectangular sharp-crested weir provided by Aydin et al.12 was utilized to validate the three-dimensional numerical model. The physical model in the reference study was positioned in a horizontal rectangular channel with a total width (B) of 32 cm. In the experimental study used in validation, flow rate measurements were performed by recording the change in water level in the measuring tank created by the water passing through the weir using an electronic pressure transducer. The water head on the weir was measured with a point gauge with an accuracy of 0.1 mm; however, it was noted that free surface fluctuations in the approach channel could affect the measurement accuracy. Therefore, in order to maintain a reliable measurement range in the experimental study, the average velocity in the approach channel was limited to V ≤ 0.55 m/s, and measurements above this value were excluded from the analysis; this limitation was taken into account when evaluating the validity range of the current numerical model validation. For the validation phase, a partially contracted weir configuration with a contraction ratio of b/B = 0.50 was selected. Accordingly, the weir opening width (b) was 16 cm, and the weir height (p) was maintained at 10 cm. To ensure flow separation, the flow-wetted edges of the weir plate were beveled at an angle of 60°, providing a sharp crest thickness of 1.5 mm (Fig. 1). To validate the CFD model, five different flow rates (Q) ranging from 0.00308 m³/s to 0.04044 m³/s were simulated. This selected discharge range corresponded to the dimensionless head ratios (h/P) between 0.48 and 2.51, which adequately represents the operational range of the proposed novel weir designs.

Proposed novel models

To mitigate the reduction in discharge capacity caused by the lateral constriction in contracted weirs, this study aims to enhance the hydraulic performance of classical contracted weirs using novel models (Fig. 2). The design of the geometric models was inspired by the models recently investigated by Aydin et al.4. In this context, the initial phase of the study investigated the effect of the number of steps (M1, M2, and M3) on the flow parameters for a constant weir opening width (b) of 16 cm and a contraction shift distance of 8 cm. Subsequently, the hydrodynamic impact of diagonal weir models (D1, D2, and D3) was examined for three contraction shift distances (4 cm, 8 cm, and 12 cm), keeping the weir opening width constant at 16 cm. Overall, the hydraulic performances of six newly developed weir designs were comprehensively evaluated under five flow rates selected from the experimental study12. Numerical model

The three-dimensional and two-phase flows over the contracted weirs were simulated using the open-source OpenFOAM software (v. 11). To accurately capture the complex free-surface dynamics and the interface between water and air, the transient solver incompressibleVoF, which is based on the Volume of Fluid (VOF) method, was employed. The fluid flow behavior was governed by the Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations along with the continuity equation, and the k-ω SST turbulence model (Shear Stress Transport) was utilized. This model was specifically chosen due to its superior performance in predicting flow separation and highly turbulent shear layers. The k-ω SST model was specifically preferred because it overcomes the limitations of the k-ε model by seamlessly blending the robust near-wall boundary layer resolution of the k-ω model with the free-stream independence of the k-ε formulation29. Furthermore, the inclusion of the shear stress transport limiter effectively prevents the overprediction of eddy viscosity29, making it highly superior for accurately predicting the complex flow separations and asymmetric vortex shedding introduced by the proposed stepped and diagonal weir configurations. The pressure-velocity coupling was achieved using the PIMPLE algorithm. Second-order upwind-biased schemes were applied to the velocity field for the spatial discretization of the convective terms. The GaussvanLeer scheme was specifically utilized for the volume fraction to maintain a sharp air-water interface. The transient terms were discretized using the first-order implicit Euler scheme. The Geometric-Algebraic Multi-Grid (GAMG) solver was utilized for the pressure equation (p_rgh). The simulation time step (Delta t) was dynamically controlled (adjustableRunTime) by restricting the maximum Courant number to 1.0. All numerical simulations were executed on the high-performance computing infrastructure of TRUBA (Turkish National Science e-Infrastructure).

Computational domain and boundary conditions

The computational domain was carefully constructed to encompass the physical dimensions of the experimental study12. To ensure fully developed approach flow conditions, the sharp crested weir was placed in a 2-m-long channel with a 1 m distance both upstream and downstream (Fig. 3). The inlet boundary condition for a constant flow rate was defined as variable height flow rate inlet velocity. To allow free fluid exit, the inlet/outlet condition was applied for the outlet boundary. The atmospheric surface was defined as the pressure inlet outlet velocity. Furthermore, the channel bed, side walls, and all weir plate surfaces were defined as no slip wall condition.

Mesh generation and grid independence study

The discretization of the computational domain was executed using hybrid meshing utilities. Initially, a structured hexahedral background grid was generated for the computational domain (2.0 × 0.45 × 0.32 m) using the blockMesh utility. Subsequently, the snappyHexMesh utility was employed to conform the mesh to the complex physical boundaries of the weir structure, and surface features were explicitly extracted (using surfaceFeatures code). Furthermore, refinement zones were implemented around the expected air-water interface and the downstream flow region to ensure highly resolved free surface (Fig. 4). To ensure grid independence, a Grid Convergence Index (GCI) analysis was performed in accordance with the procedure recommended by Celik et al.30. Three different meshes were generated to represent the computational domain: coarse (1,032,053 cells), medium (1,677,204 cells), and fine (3,018,971 cells) grids. The upstream hydraulic head (h) was selected as the critical target variable for this analysis (for Q = 0.01793 m³/s). The apparent order of accuracy was calculated to be 1.88, and the GCI value for the fine-to-medium grid pair (GCI21) was determined as 2.17%. As observed in Table 2, the error margins do not exhibit a strict monotonic convergence. In VOF-based free-surface modeling, refining the mesh reduces numerical diffusion, allowing the fine mesh to capture smaller-scale turbulent eddies and interfacial micro-fluctuations. This heightened resolution of transient dynamics can cause minor variations in the time-averaged results when compared to experimental measurements. Consequently, the medium mesh was selected as the optimum grid for all subsequent simulations. This decision was justified not only by its close agreement with the experimental data (2.7% error) but also because it represents an ideal balance between accuracy, numerical stability, and computational cost. Transitioning to the fine mesh nearly doubled the computational expense on the TRUBA infrastructure without providing macroscopic changes, whereas the medium mesh ensured a robust, sharp air-water interface with highly stable adjustable time steps. Validation of the numerical model

The numerical results were validated with the experimental data provided by Aydin et al.12. Due to the lack of directly comparable experimental data sets in the literature for the proposed diagonal and stepped narrowing weir geometries, the validation of these geometries could not be directly supported by experimental measurements. However, using the validated model, different geometry types were comparatively evaluated under the similar solution approach, boundary conditions, and mesh structure. This allows for the investigation of the relative hydraulic performance of the proposed geometries. The validation was conducted for the partially contracted rectangular weir for b/B = 0.50. Since the discharge was prescribed as the inlet boundary condition, the validation process focused on the average approach flow velocity (V) and the discharge coefficients (Cd). As illustrated in Fig. 6a, the numerical results for V corresponding to h exhibit a good agreement with the experimental results for the tested flow range. For these results, the maximum relative error was determined as 4.17% for V and 4.00% for h. Furthermore, the CFD model successfully captured the behavioral trend of the discharge coefficient curve (Fig. 6b). The minor deviations observed at the highest relative head conditions are fundamentally attributed to the three-dimensional flow separation dynamics at the sharp crest and high-velocity. Moreover, based on Eq. 1, Cd is inversely proportional to h1.5. Consequently, even a minimal numerical fluctuation in capturing the exact free-surface elevation is mathematically amplified by the power of 1.5 in the Cd. CFD results showed very good agreement with experimental results in terms of water head and average velocities (Fig. 6c), while the reliability of the results was demonstrated within a maximum error margin of ±%6 in terms of discharge coefficient (Fig. 7d). This validation confirms that the present numerical framework accurately resolves the hydrodynamics of sharp-crested weirs, thoroughly justifying its utilization for the subsequent investigation of the proposed novel geometric designs.

Results and discussion

Aydin et al.10 demonstrated that the discharge coefficient is a function of the Reynolds number (), the Weber number (), and the dimensionless parameters presented in Eq. (2).

Literature studies have investigated the effects of Reynolds (Re) and Weber (W) numbers on Cd in fully constricted slit weirs (b/B < 0.25) and partially constricted slit weirs. These studies show that for fully constricted slit weirs, the effect of viscosity and surface tension on Cd is significant, especially at low Re and W numbers, and this effect disappears as Re and W numbers increase (approx. W > 400). In partially constricted slit weirs, the effect of Re number is more complex, but Cd values remain close to each other in the range of 0.3 ≤ b/B ≤ 0.5 and show an insignificant change for approximately Re > 1.0 × 10+ 510,12,31. In addition, to maintain sharp-crested characteristics, the total head must exceed 15 times the crest thickness6, and to neglect viscous and surface tension effects, a minimum head of h = 0.02 m is practically required12. A general threshold of h > 0.03 m is also often adopted in the literature for free-surface weirs. Consequently, when Re and W exceed certain thresholds and the minimum nappe head is h = 0.02 m, the effects of Re and W on discharge efficiency can be neglected.

In the present study, since 1.4 × 10+ 5 ≤ Re ≤ 3.6 × 10+ 5 and 1812 ≤ W ≤ 1.2 × 10+ 4, and the minimum nappe head was maintained at h = 0.04 m, the influences of Re and W were considered negligible and subsequently removed from Eq. (2). Additionally, the Confined Flow Area Ratio (CFAR) was used in the study to evaluate the hydraulic performance of constricted weir geometries. When the constriction ratio was kept constant at b/B = 0.5, instead of the classical dimensionless parameters h/b and h/p, a dimensionless term representing the ratio of the restricted weir flow area to the total cross-sectional area of the channel flow was used. Thus, CFAR has been considered as a complementary dimensionless parameter that allows comparison of different geometric arrangements by relating the effective constricted area through which the flow can pass to the total water and weir cross-sectional area within the channel. Therefore, CFAR has been treated as a semi-empirical performance indicator that more holistically represents the constricted flow area under a constant constriction ratio.

The discharge performances of stepped and diagonally shifted contracted weirs in various configurations were investigated, and compared against a standard linear-crested rectangular contracted weir as a reference model. The flow conditions were strictly limited to free overflow conditions, preventing submerged flow downstream to ensure nappe aeration and avoid wall attachment. In addition, in the study, air-water interaction was represented using the VOF approach, and atmospheric pressure conditions were applied at the upper limit. However, a specific ventilation opening directly connected to the nappe area was not defined. Therefore, Cd results should be interpreted under VOF-based natural ventilation conditions.

Figure 7 presents a comparative evaluation of the discharge coefficients for various weir configurations, represented in Fig. 2; Table 1, as a function of the dimensionless constricted flow ratio (CFAR = bh/(Bh + Bp)). Overall, an evaluation of the relationship between Cd and the Constrained Flow Area Ratio (CFAR) indicates that for the shifted designs (M and D), Cd follows a downward trend as CFAR increases, eventually reaching a plateau or exhibiting only marginal increases for values of CFAR ≥ 0.3. In contrast, for rectangular slit weirs, the Cd coefficient begins to increase again after approximately CFAR ≥ 0.25. It should be noted that this variation is solely dependent on the nappe head (h). Figure 7a compares the discharge performance of stepped slit weirs with varying numbers of steps against that of a standard rectangular slit weir. These curves demonstrate that, across all nappe heads, the discharge efficiency of stepped slit weirs is approximately 5% to 30% higher than that of standard rectangular weirs. However, a comparative analysis of the stepped constrained weirs indicates that an increase in the number of steps exerts a negative influence on discharge efficiency. For the M1, M2, and M3 weir designs, which possess equal crest lengths, this variance is attributed to the increased effective crest length caused by nappe interference at the corners as the number of steps increases. Therefore, the efficiency loss between the stepped weirs is in the range of 2–6% for M2 and 3–9% for M3. The hydrodynamic implications of this phenomenon are examined in greater detail in the subsequent sections.

Figure 7b presents the performance of diagonally shifted slit weirs in comparison with classic rectangular weirs. As the shift distance increases, the effective crest length also increases, causing the flow to behave both linearly and as a lateral weir, thus improving the weir drainage performance per unit weir width. Unlike stepped weirs, the main reason for the increase in discharge performance in these weirs is the increasing effective crest length along with the shift distance. Compared to classic rectangular slit weirs, D1 provides an efficiency increase of up to 3%, D2 up to 12%, and D3 up to 25%, respectively. The increase in the Cd coefficient between D1 and D3 ranges from 15 to 22% depending on the varying headwater. For both weir types (M and D), although the discharge efficiency decreases along the unit crest, the discharge efficiency and performance increase along the unit width perpendicular to the flow. Figure 7c compares two types of slit weirs with the same shift distance to linear rectangular slit weirs (M1 and D2). These graphs indicate that the M1 stepped slit weir exhibits the highest performance. While the D2 weir provides a maximum performance increase of 12% compared to rectangular slit weirs at low headwater levels, the M1 weir provides a 30% increase. The rate of increase between the two weirs varies from 3 to 17% depending on the different water heads.

Figure 9 compares the three-dimensional free-surface flow patterns at peak discharge for each weir configuration. While the conventional rectangular slit weir produces a symmetric downstream flow, the stepped and diagonally shifted weirs generate asymmetric downstream flow structures. For traditional rectangular contracted slit weirs, discharge efficiency is primarily governed by the contraction behavior of the flow toward the downstream direction. In contrast, the hydraulic behavior of stepped weirs is more complex. In addition to flow contraction, vertical flow separations occurring at the step edges and the associated vortex structures play a significant role in determining discharge efficiency. Flow separation initiated at the upstream corners of each step induces relatively high levels of turbulence (see Fig. 10a). This elevated turbulence leads to flow deceleration and separation along the weir surface parallel to the main flow direction. Under the influence of higher shear stresses in the outer, faster-moving flow regions, upstream-oriented vortices are generated, as illustrated in Fig. 10b. These vortex zones effectively reduce the effective crest length of stepped weirs, thereby decreasing discharge efficiency. Moreover, the number of such vortices increases with the number of steps. This explains the adverse impact of increasing step number on discharge efficiency, even in cases where the total crest length remains constant.

In stepped and diagonally contracted weirs, the increase in discharge capacity is related to the change in the opening geometry, as well as the three-dimensional flow structures created by the shifted side walls. These side surfaces, positioned perpendicular or at an angle to the main flow, create secondary flows, local eddies, and horizontal momentum changes in the narrowing region, causing the flow to redirect along the opening. This situation can contribute to the regulation of flow separation and recombination zones, especially downstream, to more efficient use of the effective flow passage area, and thus to an increase in the discharge coefficient compared to a standard rectangular contracted weir. Figure 11 illustrates the contraction patterns of streamlines originating from the upstream and developing toward the downstream for each weir type at an identical flow rate. Due to the constraints imposed by the main channel walls and the confinement boundaries, vortices at the two corners of the channel emerge with the onset of contracted flow, thereby constricting the flow toward the channel center. While the contraction shapes and angles remain symmetrical in rectangular slit weirs, this symmetry is disrupted in shifted weirs because the weir flow is not parallel to the main channel flow. Specifically, the contraction angle is higher at the proximal (upstream) end, whereas it approximates that of a rectangular slit weir at the distal (downstream) end. Despite the increased contraction rates, the primary factor contributing to the enhanced discharge performance of M and D-type weirs is the extension of the crest length. On the other hand, it should be noted that while the discharge efficiency per unit channel width increases, the effective crest length may decrease due to base vortices and streamline contractions, potentially reducing the discharge efficiency per unit crest length.

To further substantiate the reported flow asymmetries and ensure they are not numerical artifacts or mesh-related errors, detailed vorticity contours were extracted from the numerical results (Fig. 12). Figure 12a demonstrates the Z-vorticity field for the standard rectangular weir. The flow separation and vortex shedding at the contraction edges exhibit perfect symmetry, confirming the numerical stability and accuracy of the utilized mesh structure. Conversely, as illustrated in Fig. 12b and c, the proposed shifted models exhibit distinctly asymmetric vorticity generation. It is important to note that these shifted weir geometries are not symmetrical with respect to the main flow axis due to their longitudinal offset. Therefore, the observed asymmetry is a physically meaningful hydrodynamic response, wherein the leading and trailing edges initiate flow separation at different longitudinal coordinates, rather than a convergence limitation.

Conclusion

In this study, the hydraulic performance and discharge characteristics of stepped and diagonal slit weirs, along with traditional rectangular slit weirs, were investigated through numerical models validated by experimental data under various flow conditions. The key findings indicate that stepped shifted slit weirs exhibit superior hydraulic performance compared to standard rectangular slit and diagonal shifted weirs, with discharge efficiency increasing by approximately 5% to 30% across all nappe heads. Comparative analysis of the stepped designs revealed that an increase in the number of steps negatively impacts the discharge coefficient. This reduction is attributed to nappe interference at the corners, which occurs as the configuration becomes more complex despite the increase in crest length. Under the investigated flow conditions (1.4 × 105 ≤ Re ≤ 3.6 × 105 and 1812 ≤ W ≤ 1.2 × 104, the effects of viscous and surface tension on the discharge coefficient were found to be negligible, provided the minimum nappe head remains h ≥ 0.04 m. For shifted designs (M and D), Cd values exhibited a decreasing trend with an increasing Constrained Flow Area Ratio (CFAR), reaching a stable plateau for CFAR > 0.3, whereas rectangular weirs showed a slight increase in Cd beyond a threshold of CFAR > 0.25. Furthermore, water surface profiles and velocity distributions confirmed that geometric modifications significantly alter the hydrodynamic behavior, particularly in the vicinity of the weir crest and steps. The enhanced discharge capacity of M and D-type weirs is primarily driven by the extended crest lengths, which effectively compensate for localized efficiency losses caused by flow contraction and asymmetrical vortex formations. Overall, the hydraulic performance of the proposed weir configurations is governed by the interaction between flow contraction, vortex dynamics, and crest geometry. While shifted and stepped weirs increase the discharge per unit channel width, the accompanying asymmetrical flow structures and step-induced vortices may reduce the effective crest length, potentially lowering the discharge efficiency per unit crest length. The proposed dimensionless parameter bh/[B(h + p)] effectively characterizes flow area constraints, and the results suggest that stepped configurations offer a viable method for enhancing weir discharge capacity in hydraulic engineering applications. The findings of this study demonstrate that stepped and diagonally contracted weir geometries can offer an alternative design approach to improve discharge capacity, particularly in applications where the existing channel width cannot be increased or where structural space constraints exist. However, the practical application of the proposed geometries should be evaluated not only in terms of hydraulic efficiency but also in terms of maintenance accessibility, sediment and debris accumulation, nappe aeration, and long-term structural strength. Future studies examining these geometries within the scope of experimental velocity field measurements, different flow and head conditions, sedimentary flows, and geometry optimization will strengthen the applicability of the findings to engineering applications.
