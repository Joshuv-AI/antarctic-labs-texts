Rare co-occurrence of parosteal lipoma and growing osteochondroma: A case report
Authors: Adila Iqbal, Khalid Javed, Aymen shahab, Mahpara nazir, Zulfiqar Ali, Kashif Siddique
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parosteal lipoma is a rare benign tumor originating from the periosteum and composed of mature adipose tissue closely attached to the bone cortex. Occasionally, it may induce reactive osseous or cartilaginous proliferation, forming a lesion resembling osteochondroma or a malignancy. We present a case of a 34-year-old male exhibiting a progressively enlarging mass on the right thigh, accompanied by intermittent pain that radiated down to the leg. Imaging studies indicated a significant lipomatous tumor featuring internal septations and cortical irregularities along the femoral shaft, which initially raised suspicion for sarcoma. However, a CT-guided biopsy revealed mature adipose tissue along with fibrocartilage and bone formation, thereby confirming the diagnosis of a parosteal lipoma with osteochondroma. This case emphasizes the diagnostic challenge involved in distinguishing benign parosteal lipomas with osseous components from malignant surface tumors. Accurate interpretation of imaging and correlation with histopathology are vital for proper management, since complete excision leads to an excellent prognosis with a low rate of recurrence.

Introduction

Lipomas are one of the most common benign soft tissue tumors, comprising adipose tissue. Lipomas are classified based on their location on the body as subcutaneous, intramuscular, intermuscular, intraosseous, or parosteal [1]. Parosteal lipomas are rare, benign tumors arising in proximity to the bony periosteum. These tumors usually grow slowly and exophytically from the periosteum of the bones [2]. They are usually located in extremities arising from long bones such as the femur, tibia, and fibula. Parosteal lipomas are mostly reported as a solitary lesion; however, a few cases have been reported in association/ with osteochondroma [3]. Cartilage and bone arising in a lipomatous lesion are a rare occurrence and are considered to result from the reactive changes. Parosteal lipoma with osteochondroma is a rare and unique neoplasm that often has a good prognosis and requires an extensive multidisciplinary collaboration for diagnosis and management [4]. Here, a case is presented of a parosteal lipoma associated with an osteochondroma in a young adult male.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old male presented with the complaint of swelling on the right thigh for the last 3 months associated with episodic pain radiating to the right leg. The patient had no previous history of any trauma, comorbidities, or addiction. He had a family history of carcinoma of the breast and bone in his mother and paternal grandfather, respectively. On examination, a firm, nontender swelling was present on the medial aspect of the right thigh, which was attached to the underlying tissue. Distal neurovascular status was unremarkable, and no lymphadenopathy was present.

On ultrasonography, a heterogeneous mass was identified in proximity to the right femur, along with the cortical irregularity. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lower limb was performed. MRI revealed an abnormal signal intensity osseous lesion arising from the mid-diaphysis of the right femur, in the parosteal region, which has a peripheral fatty component that is hyperintense on T1WI and T2WI and shows suppression on Short Tau Inversion Recovery (STIR) images. Given the benign histology, absence of significant neurovascular compression, and the patient's preference to avoid surgery, a conservative management plan with analgesia and serial clinical and imaging follow-up was instituted. The patient was counselled and reassured about the benign nature of the disease, and surgical excision will be performed if compression symptoms develop.

Discussion

The first case of parosteal lipoma was documented by Seerig in 1836, and the term parosteal lipoma was introduced in 1888 by Power [2]. The simultaneous occurrence of parosteal lipoma and osteochondroma is exceptionally rare and represents more than a simple anatomical juxtaposition. While parosteal lipoma itself accounts for less than 0.3% of all lipomas, its association with osteochondromatous differentiation has been reported only in isolated case reports. It is characterized by mature adipose tissue in close association with the bone cortex, often separated by a thin fibrous layer derived from the periosteum. It can manifest at any age, with the highest incidence occurring between 40 and 60 years, and it affects both genders equally [5]. In the present scenario, a male patient presented with a mass on right thigh in his fourth decade of life. Parosteal lipoma predominantly occurs in the thigh area, particularly around the femur, and in the upper limb around the proximal radius, along with the tibia, humerus, and scapula [6].

Parosteal lipoma is usually presented as an asymptomatic, gradually enlarging palpable mass. Neurovascular symptoms could present, such as pain, paresthesia, or restricted range of motion, resulting from the compression of surrounding structures. The extent of disruption of adjacent structures depends upon the size and position of the lesions [7]. In this case, the patient had a progressively increasing swelling, accompanied by intermittent radiating pain.

On plain radiographs, a parosteal lipoma appears as a well-defined mass, situated adjacent to the bone, and is marked by a reactive alteration in the neighboring bone. Such reactive alterations encompass osseous bowing, cortical erosion, and localized cortical hyperostosis [3]. On CT scan, a circumscribed, homogenous low-density mass with a bony component and adherence to underlying bone is identified on CT scan [8]. MRI is considered the primary choice for soft tissue tumor evaluation, particularly for preoperative planning purposes. On MRI, a juxtacortical mass exhibiting hypersignal in both T1 and T2 weighted sequences has been observed, with suppression noted in the fat saturation sequences [2]. In this case, MRI revealed a large lipomatous lesion with internal septations and focal bony outgrowth, initially raising the suspicion of sarcoma. However, the absence of aggressive features such as cortical destruction, periosteal reaction, or soft tissue invasion favors a benign etiology.

Histologically, the mature adipose tissue intermingled with fibrocartilaginous and osseous elements, which are characteristic of osteochondroma, was found, thus confirming the diagnosis of parosteal lipoma with secondary osteochondroma differentiation. Myung et al. [3] reported that in parosteal lipoma associated with osteochondroma, the histological examination revealed that the osseous region exhibited mature trabecular bone and fatty marrow, which were enveloped by a fibrocartilage cap, characteristics typical of osteochondroma. The fatty region consisted of mature adipose tissue that was firmly adhered to the periosteum, aligning with the features of parosteal lipoma. Parosteal lipoma may lead to secondary bone reactions as a result of ongoing periosteal irritation. These reactions can include cortical hyperostosis, bone excrescences, and osteochondromatous differentiation. The occurrence of both parosteal lipoma and osteochondroma, as illustrated in this case, constitutes a particularly rare benign condition [9]. The origin of the lipoma, along with the osseous and chondroid components present in the lipomatous mass, continues to be a topic of considerable discussion. The pathogenesis of this dual lesion is not completely understood. One suggested mechanism indicates that the persistent pressure applied by the lipomatous mass on the periosteum could encourage the development of cartilage via the metaplastic transformation of periosteal cells, leading to the enchondral ossification [10]. The radiological features of the lesion in this case showed the cortical continuity of the chondroid component, and histologically, the lesion demonstrated mature adipocytes adherent to periosteum, alongside a fibrocartilaginous cap comprising of uniform chondrocytes without atypical features. These features are suggestive of the origin from reactive periosteum rather than a de novo malignant lesion. Alternatively, the other possible association is transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, as monocytes that are stimulated by TGF-β secrete chemotactic and mitogenic cytokines, which attract endothelial and mesenchymal cells to enhance the synthesis of collagen and related constituents of the bone matrix. One cytogenetic analysis of soft tissue parosteal lipoma showed the presence of chromosomal translocation t (3; 12) (q28; q14), implying that bone and soft tissue lipomas are cytogenetically comparable and likely have a shared histopathogenesis [11,12].

A critical aspect of this case was the differentiation of the lesion from malignant tumors such as parosteal osteosarcoma and dedifferentiated liposarcoma. Unlike parosteal osteosarcoma, which typically exhibits dense ossification, cortical thickening, medullary invasion, and aggressive features, this lesion demonstrated a well-defined fatty component, preserved cortex, and no diffusion restriction [13]. The dedifferentiated liposarcoma was excluded due to the absence of thick septations, solid enhancing nodules, or restricted diffusion [14]. Sohier et al. [9] emphasized that the aggressive bone formation and atypical histologic features are critical indicators of malignancy; however, in this case, mature adipocytes and a benign fibrocartilaginous cap without atypia excluded sarcoma.

The preferred treatment is complete excision of both components, which offers a favorable prognosis, with no recurrences reported. Parosteal lipoma does not exhibit malignant potential; therefore, conservative follow-up may be considered in instances where there is no neurovascular involvement [15]. The patient in this case was managed conservatively with a regular follow-up.

This case emphasizes the necessity of correlating clinical, radiological, and histopathological findings to ensure an accurate diagnosis. Awareness of this rare condition is vital to prevent misdiagnosis as a soft tissue or surface bone sarcoma and to direct appropriate management.

Conclusion

Parosteal lipoma in co-occurrence with osteochondroma represents a notably rare benign lesion that may clinically and radiologically resemble malignant bone or soft tissue tumors. Accurate diagnosis relies on a careful correlation of imaging findings with histopathological evaluation. Recognizing this disease is critical to avoid misinterpretation and to prevent unnecessary, extensive surgical actions. A complete local excision of the lesion results in a good prognosis with a low risk of recurrence.
