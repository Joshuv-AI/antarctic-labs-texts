Prognostic Value of Epicardial Adipose Tissue in Heart Failure With Mid‐Range and Preserved Ejection Fraction: A Multicenter Study
Authors: Wenxian Wang, Yan Gao, Jian Wang, Congshan Ji, Hui Gu, Xianshun Yuan, Shifeng Yang, Ximing Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpicardial adipose tissue (EAT) accumulation is thought to play a role in the pathophysiology of heart failure (HF) with mid‐range ejection fraction and HF with preserved ejection fraction, but its effect on outcome is unknown.Methods and ResultsA total of 692 patients with HF with mid‐range ejection fraction or HF with preserved ejection fraction who underwent cardiovascular magnetic resonance at 2 medical centers in China between October 2016 and October 2022 were included in this study. EAT volume and extracellular volume were calculated using cardiovascular magnetic resonance. The main outcome was the composite of all‐cause mortality and first HF hospitalization. Of 692 participants, 41.3% were women. The mean age, body mass index, left ventricular ejection fraction, and EAT volume were 57.0 years, 27.2 kg/m2, 50.0%, and 67.1 mL/m2, respectively. During a median follow‐up of 34 months, 169 patients (24.4%) died or were hospitalized for HF. EAT volume exhibited a strong unadjusted association with the composite outcome (hazard ratio per 1 mL/m2 [HR], 1.57 [95% CI, 1.40–1.76], P<0.001). After fully adjusting, EAT remained associated with the outcome (HR, 1.62 [95% CI, 1.42–1.86], P<0.001). We constructed a baseline multivariable model including comorbidities, New York Heart Association functional class, extracellular volume, age, body mass index, left ventricular ejection fraction, and N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide. Addition of EAT volume to the baseline multivariable model significantly improved model performance (C statistic improvement: 0.711–0.760; P<0.001).ConclusionsEAT accumulation is associated with an adverse prognosis in patients with HF with mid‐range ejection fraction and those with HF with preserved ejection fraction. In addition, EAT provides incremental prognostic value beyond left ventricular ejection fraction and New York Heart Association class.

Heart failure (HF) with mid‐range ejection fraction (HFmrEF) or HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) accounts for >50% of hospitalized patients with HF and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) has gained much attention in recent years because of its special anatomical location between the myocardium and visceral pericardium, characterized by the paracrine or vasocrine secretion of several adipokines with proinflammatory and anti‐inflammatory properties. 5 , 6 , 7

Recently, the use of sacubitril/valsartan has been shown to modestly reduce hospitalization rates for HF in patients with HFpEF and is now the first drug approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for treating HFpEF. 8 , 9 , 10 Moreover, treatment with the sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor empagliflozin has been demonstrated to improve outcomes in patients with HFpEF, primarily through a reduction in HF hospitalizations. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Given these advancements in treatment options, it is critical to identify patients at higher risk for recurrent HF events and mortality. Therefore, we investigated whether EAT has a statistically significant prognostic association with poor prognosis beyond established clinical and imaging risk markers in patients with HFmrEF and HFpEF. In addition, considering the recent distinction between patients with a left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF) between 40% and 50% (HFmrEF) and those with LVEF >50% (HFpEF), we also examined these 2 populations separately.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Study Population

The patients were required to be older than 18 years, have a confirmed guidance‐based diagnosis 16 of HFmrEF or HFpEF, and have undergone cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) to be included in this multicenter retrospective study. HFmrEF or HFpEF was defined according to the HF signs or symptoms such as third heart sound, pulmonary rales, jugular venous distention, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II or III; increased levels of N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP; >125 pg/mL); and structural changes such as elevated left atrial volume index (>34 mL/m2). 16 All patients in this study were well characterized, including those with an NT‐proBNP level >220 pg/mL. LVEF had to be >40%. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who had myocardial infarction, percutaneous intervention, or coronary artery bypass grafting within the past 3 months; (2) patients with an internal cardiac defibrillator or pacemaker; (3) patients with complex congenital heart disease; (4) patients with known genetic or infiltrative cardiomyopathies; and (5) patients with any other contraindication to CMR imaging.

Institutional review board approval was obtained, and patient informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of this study.

CMR Protocol

CMR examination was performed using a standardized protocol on a 3.0‐T scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Healthcare) with an 18‐channel phased‐array body coil. 17 The CMR protocol included steady‐state free precession, 18 breath‐hold cines, and precontrast and postcontrast T1 mapping. The postcontrast T1 mapping was acquired 15 minutes after intravenous dose of gadopentetate dimeglumine (0.2 mmol/kg, Bei Lu Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd.) using the same slice location as the precontrast T1 mapping.

Myocardial T1 measurements were acquired in 3 short‐axis slices (basal, mid, and apical) using a MOLLI (modified Look‐Locker inversion recovery) sequence with breath‐holding and ECG gating. 19 T1 mapping before and after the contrast agent was performed with a 5(3s)3 and a 4(1s)3(1s)2 scheme, respectively.

The details of the scanning parameters are shown in Data S1.

Postprocessing and Imaging Analysis

A senior CMR physician, who was blinded to clinical information, contoured and analyzed all CMR images independently. The commercial postprocessing software CVI42 version 5.12.1 (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc.) was used to process the CMR images. T1 values and extracellular volume (ECV) for each segment were measured on the basal, mid‐chamber, and apical short‐axis slices according to the American Heart Association 17‐segment model (apex excluded) using the postprocessing software CVI, which yielded a total of 16 T1/ECV measurements corresponding to the 16 measured LV segments. The LV endocardium, epicardium, and blood pool were manually delineated to define the myocardium following a standardized protocol according to the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance postprocessing guidelines. 20 , 21 The papillary muscles, moderator bands, and epicardial border of each slice were carefully excluded. The hematocrit from the venous blood sample was obtained within 3 days before the CMR examination to calculate ECV, using the following equation: ECV=1−hematocrit×1/T1myocardium.post−1/T1myocardium.pre/1/Tlblood.post−1/T1blood.pre×100%.

The diameter, volume, and ejection fraction of the left ventricle were also measured after the definition of the epicardium and endocardium at the end of systole and diastole. The LV global longitudinal strain (GLS) was acquired using the feature‐tracking technique derived from the steady‐state free precession cine sequence.

Epicardial Adipose Tissue

The CMR short‐axis images were analyzed by a senior physician using dedicated postprocessing software (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging [CVi42] version 5.10.1). Cardiac adipose tissue was quantified on all short‐axis slices (base to apex) of the steady‐state free precession cine sequence in the end‐diastolic phase. EAT was defined as the adipose tissue located between the outer wall of the myocardium and the visceral layer of the pericardium. The analyst who measured the volume of cardiac fat was unaware of other patient characteristics. Interobserver variability was assessed by reanalyzing a random selection of 200 CMR images by a second observer who was blinded to other patient characteristics. The measurements for EAT volumes showed excellent intrarater reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.98) and good interrater reliability (ICC = 0.92) (Data S1).

Outcome

Electronic medical records were reviewed to assess outcomes and obtain medical history data. For cases with insufficient hospital records, patients or first‐degree relatives were directly contacted by telephone, and a scripted interview was conducted. Ascertainment of outcomes was performed until January 1, 2024, at which point the data set was locked. All standardized follow‐up procedures and adjudication of all events were independently performed by 2 cardiologists blinded to CMR results. The outcomes in this study were defined as a composite of all‐cause mortality and hospitalization for HF. Hospitalization for HF was defined as an unplanned overnight stay in a hospital (different dates for admission and discharge) as a result of progression of HF or directly related to HF. 22 Only the first event was counted when multiple events occurred in the same patient. All patients had the option to refuse follow‐up contact throughout the study duration.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS version 26.0 (IBM) and R version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) were used for statistical analysis. All tests were 2‐sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine the distribution of continuous variables. Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD or median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using either a 2‐sample Student t test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, depending on their distribution characteristics. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies with percentages and compared using χ2 test. The association between EAT volume and functional/structural parameters on magnetic resonance imaging was tested using linear regression analysis, with adjustments made for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and diabetes. Event‐free survival curves were displayed using the Kaplan–Meier curve according to the optimal cutoff threshold determined by the Youden index of survival receiver operating characteristic curve, and comparisons were made using log‐rank test. Associations with outcome were assessed using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models. The Cox proportional hazards assumption was tested using the cox.zph() function in R. This function checks the proportionality assumption by examining whether there is a trend in time for Schoenfeld residuals. Covariates that were univariably associated with the outcome were then adjusted using a multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression model. EAT volume was added to the model as a continuous variable. To verify whether EAT volume has a robust correlation with prognosis after eliminating the influence of confounding variables, we constructed the following multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression model for the outcome (composite of all‐cause mortality and HF hospitalizations). The model was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, HF severity (ie, NYHA functional class and NT‐proBNP), comorbidities (ie, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, and renal dysfunction) 1 and all univariably associated variables in a backward selection model. 2 To further evaluate its incremental prognostic value, we constructed a multivariable baseline clinical model by including variables that: (1) showed a robust association (P<0.05) with poor prognosis in univariate analysis; and (2) reflected clinical decision‐making according to current guidelines and practice. 16 The baseline multivariable model included Age, BMI, diabetes, NYHA class, LVEF, NT‐proBNP, ECV, atrial fibrillation, and renal dysfunction. EAT volume was then added to the multivariable model. We further calculated the change in model discrimination by the Harrell concordance index before and after adding EAT volume and generated the 3‐ and 5‐year calibration curves to confirm the prediction ability of the established models. Finally, we assessed the net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination index before and after adding EAT volume.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 767 patients met the initial inclusion and exclusion criteria. After further exclusion of 14 cases with poor image quality, 43 cases (5.6%) with incomplete clinical data leading to lost follow‐up, and 18 cases without strict consistency between pre‐T1 and post‐T1 mapping for ECV assessment, a cohort of 692 patients was formed for this analysis. Table 1 depicts the patient characteristics stratified according to the median EAT volume. The total of 692 participants had a mean age of 57.0 years (ranging from 37 to 88 years) and consisted of 41.3% of women, with a mean BMI of 27.2 kg/m2. Patients with high EAT volume (≥65 mL/m2) had a higher BMI, LV mass index, and ECV but lower LV GLS compared with those with low EAT volume. In addition, patients with high EAT volume were more likely to have diabetes and be diagnosed with severe HF. Typical examples of high and low EAT volume are shown in Figure 1. The characteristics of patients with HFpEF and HFmrEF are depicted in Table S1, where NT‐proBNP and NYHA class were higher in patients with HFmrEF compared with those with HFpEF (Table S1).

Associations of Clinical and CMR Parameters With Prognosis

During a median follow‐up of 34 months (IQR: 31,37), 109 patients (16%) were hospitalized for HF and 60 patients (9%) died. The majority of deaths (42 patients, 70%) were caused by cardiovascular causes. Univariate associations of clinical and CMR parameters with the outcome are presented in Table 2. BMI, LVEF, NYHA class, NT‐proBNP, diabetes, renal dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, and ECV showed significant associations with the outcome (P<0.050). In addition, EAT volume emerged as a strong univariate predictor for the outcome (hazard ratio per 1 mL/m2 [HR], 1.57 [95% CI, 1.40–1.76], P<0.001) (Table 2). No violation of the proportional hazards assumption was observed.

Model Discrimination and Incremental Prognostic Value

Next, a multivariable baseline clinical model was constructed based on the following criteria: (1) a robust association (P<0.05) with the outcome in univariate analysis; and (2) alignment with clinical decision‐making according to current guidelines and practice. 16 The baseline multivariable model included age, BMI, NYHA class, NT‐proBNP, diabetes, renal dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, CMR‐assessed LVEF, and ECV (model 3; Table 3). Adding EAT volume to the multivariable baseline model (model 4; Table 3) significantly improved the Harrell concordance index (0.711 [95% CI, 0.674–0.748]) to 0.760 [95% CI, 0.727–0.793]; P<0.001), as well as reclassification indices (integrated discrimination index: 4.0% [95% CI, 0.005–0.076]), P<0.001; net reclassification improvement: 8.4% [95% CI, 0.017–0.152], P<0.05) for outcome (Table 4; Figure 2). Moreover, the model calibration curves indicate that the model demonstrates enhanced predictive performance with the addition of EAT volume, whether in 3‐ or 5‐year follow‐up scenarios (Figure 3).

Prognostic Value of EAT in HFPEF and HFmrEF

Univariate associations of clinical and CMR parameters with major adverse cardiovascular events in HFpEF and HFmrEF are presented in Table S2. In patients with HFpEF, BMI, LVEF, NYHA class, NT‐proBNP, and ECV showed significant associations with the outcome (P<0.05). In addition, EAT volume was identified as a strong univariate predictor for the outcome (HR, 1.60 [95% CI, 1.35–1.88], P<0.001) (Table S2). In patients with HFmrEF, NT‐proBNP, diabetes, renal dysfunction, ECV, and LV GLS showed significant associations with the outcome (P<0.05). Furthermore, EAT volume also emerged as a strong univariate predictor for the outcome (HR, 1.76 [95% CI, 1.47–2.09], P<0.001) (Table S2).

After adjusting for factors that were significantly associated with major adverse cardiovascular events in univariate analysis, EAT remained closely associated with poor prognosis, whether in HFpEF (HR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.32–2.05], P<0.001) or HFmrEF (HR, 1.69 [95% CI, 1.40–2.03], P<0.001) (Table S3, Table S4).

Differences Between Obesity and EAT Regarding Prognosis

A significant yet weak association was observed between BMI and EAT (r=0.13, P<0.001). Patients were categorized into 4 groups based on the presence of obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) and EAT volume above or below 69 mL/m2. A total of 204 (29.4%) patients were classified as nonobese but had high EAT volume, whereas 95 (13.7%) patients were classified as obese but had low EAT volume.

In addition, Kaplan–Meier analysis of patients with obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) or those without obesity (BMI <30 kg/m2) stratified by the optimal cutoff value of EAT volume showed that the event‐free survival time was significantly lower in patients in the high EAT group, irrespective of obesity (P<0.001) (Figure 1C and 1D). However, in the low EAT group (below the optimal cutoff value), the event‐free survival time was high and unaffected by obesity (log‐rank P=0.074) (Figure 1B).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that: (1) the correlation between EAT volume and the composite outcome remained robust in patients with HFmrEF and HFpEF after adjusting for BMI, HF severity, and comorbidities; (2) in patients with HFmrEF and HFpEF, EAT volume by CMR provides incremental prognostic value for the composite outcome; (3) patients with high EAT had a significantly lower incidence of event‐free survival compared with patients with low EAT, regardless of obesity; and (4) patients with high EAT exhibited lower LV GLS and higher ECV. These data support the concept that the accumulation of EAT is important in the pathophysiology and risk stratification of HFmrEF and HFpEF. Therefore, assessing EAT may be considered in the evaluation of these patients with HF.

Several previous studies have examined the prognostic association of EAT volume in patients with HFpEF. For example, Pugliese et al assessed 188 patients with HFpEF and reported that the EAT volume was predictive of a composite end point consisting of HF hospitalization and cardiovascular death. 23 In addition, Woerden et al conducted a study on patients with HFpEF and HFmrEF to assess the prognostic value of EAT volume. 24 The study found that higher EAT volume was associated with a poorer prognosis.

The present study contributes to the existing literature in several significant ways. To the best of our knowledge, our cohort is the largest to date examining the role of EAT volume in improving prognosis among multicenter patients with HFpEF and HFmrEF. In addition, prior research has primarily concentrated on older patients. However, multiple large‐sample cohort studies have shown that visceral fat status is not constant among all age groups, and visceral fat content increases with age. 25 , 26 , 27 In the present study, the total of 692 participants had a mean age of 57.0 years, whereas in the study by Woerden et al, the mean age was 76.0 years. Given the possible age dependence of EAT, our cohort is well distinguished from previous studies in terms of age. Furthermore, while determining the association between EAT volume and poor prognosis, we also explored the incremental prognostic value of EAT volume relative to common clinical risk factors, which better reflects the value of EAT volume in clinical practice. As shown in our research results, incorporating EAT volume improved the predictive ability of guideline‐based comprehensive clinical model. Thus, EAT volume could serve as an indicator guiding treatment intervention decisions for patients with HFpEF and HFmrEF, as it is expected to improve prognosis. Furthermore, EAT volume can be rapidly derived from routine cine images with minimal postprocessing, which can play a significant role in clinical practice. In our research, the analysis of EAT volume for each case typically took only 2 to 3 minutes.

Our findings, along with those of Woerden et al, further substantiate the notion that EAT holds significant prognostic value in patients with HF. Similarly, previous studies have also demonstrated a significant association between elevated EAT and unfavorable outcomes in individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes or coronary heart disease. 28 , 29 , 30 The mechanistic link between EAT accumulation and impaired outcome in patients with HFmrEF and HFpEF is unknown, yet multiple mechanisms have been proposed. 31 On one hand, the accumulation of EAT may contribute to myocardial steatosis, potentially resulting from the infiltration of adipocytes into adjacent myocardium. 32 In chronic inflammatory diseases, especially those that lead to HFpEF, the epicardium becomes the site of deranged adipogenesis, leading to the secretion of proinflammatory adipokines. 5 Because it shares microcirculation with adjacent myocardium, it may damage adjacent myocardium in a local and paracrine way, resulting in microvascular dysfunction and fibrosis. 7 , 33 On the other hand, the accumulation of EAT could also have mechanical consequences, such as pseudopericardial constriction. In HFpEF, increased EAT thickness is associated with worse GLS of both the left atrium and left ventricle, higher right‐sided filling pressures, reduced peak oxygen consumption, and impaired peripheral extraction. 23 , 24 , 34 , 35 EAT can affect the risk and severity of HFpEF via several mechanisms. The accumulation of EAT has been associated not only with ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, and increased cardiac filling pressures but also with endothelial dysfunction and atrial fibrillation, all of which are highly prevalent in HFmrEF and HFpEF. 32 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40

Interestingly, in the present study, patients with a greater EAT volume was associated with worse LV GLS. Furthermore, a greater EAT volume was associated with higher cardiac ECV. These findings are consistent with previous research that has reported a close association between greater EAT and worse functional parameters, as well as markers of myocardial fibrosis in HFmrEF and HFpEF. 35 Based on these findings, the plausible explanation is that the chronic systemic inflammation that is seen in obesity, rheumatoid arthritis, and other disorders is accompanied by the significant accumulation of EAT. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Inflammation can drive adipogenesis, presumably acting as an adaptive mechanism that prevents the deposition of proinflammatory fatty acids in cells other than adipocytes. 45 In chronic inflammatory disorders, especially those leading to HFpEF, the epicardium becomes a site of deranged adipogenesis, leading to the secretion of proinflammatory adipokines. Some of those, such as interleukin (IL) 1β, IL‐6, tumor necrosis factor α, and monocyte chemoattractant protein 1, can contribute to the development of fibrosis and sclerosis in the myocardium, ultimately resulting in impaired cardiac function. 46 , 47 Yet, our cross‐sectional design precludes conclusions regarding causality, and our findings warrant validation in future large prospective studies.

BMI has limitations in providing comprehensive information on a patient's visceral fat status. A previous study found that visceral adiposity was associated with incident HF, especially HFpEF, after adjusting for BMI in a multiethnic cohort. 48 Likewise, in our study, the association between BMI and EAT volume was fairly weak, supporting that BMI is not a specific estimator of EAT volume. Furthermore, in our study cohort of 204 patients, one‐third were classified as nonobese, yet they had substantial EAT volumes. In addition, patients without obesity but with increased EAT volume had a worse prognosis compared with nonobese patients with low EAT volume. Among patients with low EAT volume, there was no significant difference in prognosis between obese and nonobese patients. All of this suggests that EAT volume provides prognostic value independent of BMI in patients with HFmrEF and HFpEF, despite the association between EAT and BMI.

The 2016 European Society of Cardiology guidelines have introduced a new classification of HF based on LVEF in combination with signs and symptoms of HF. The new guidelines classify patients with HF into 3 categories: HFpEF, defined by an LVEF ≥50%, HFrEF defined by an LVEF <40%, and HFmrEF defined by an LVEF of 40% to 49%. 49 However, in many instances, the classification of HFmrEF is simply the result of the variability in the measure of LVEF in patient cohorts with different degrees of impairment of myocardial function. LVEF is not a static measurement, and HFmrEF is certainly a heterogenous condition with variable evolutions rather than a stable phenotype. 50 Insights from previous studies have helped to illuminate the clinical characteristics of this group. Previous cohort and registry studies have found that demographic characteristics, symptom profiles, comorbidities, laboratory values, and short‐term outcomes of patients with LVEF between 40% and 50% were more similar to those of patients with HFpEF. 51 , 52 In addition, both OPTIMIZE‐HF (Organized Program to Initiate Lifesaving Treatment in Hospitalized Patients With Heart Failure) and a Canadian study involving hospitalized patients with HF indicated that patients with HFrEF had a slightly higher mortality rate, while patients with HFmrEF and HFpEF exhibited comparable mortality rates. 53 Similar to previous studies, the present study found that patients with HFmrEF showed similar sex, age, BMI, and comorbidities as those with HFPEF. Furthermore, patients with HFmrEF had higher BNP levels, NYHA class ratings, and ECV values, and worse LV GLS compared with patients with HFpEF.

EAT may have a different impact on HFpEF and HFmrEF. A study conducted by Kenchaiah et al showed that EAT accumulation was particularly associated with new‐onset HFpEF, and to a lesser extent with HFmrEF. 54 However, in another study conducted by Woerden et al, there were no significant differences observed in EAT between these patient populations. 55 Similarly, our study showed no significant difference in EAT volume between patients with HFmrEF and those with HFpEF (Table S1). Thus, it is necessary to establish whether there is indeed a pathophysiological difference in EAT accumulation between patients with HFpEF and those with HFmrEF. In addition, our study found that even after adjusting for factors significantly associated with major adverse cardiovascular events in the univariate analysis, EAT remained closely associated with poor prognosis, whether in HFpEF or HFmrEF. This indicates that EAT plays a significant role in the development of both HFpEF and HFmrEF, although there may be some pathophysiological differences.

LIMITATIONS

First, when measuring EAT volume, we could not entirely rule out the presence of pericardial effusion. Second, although this was a multicenter study, it was a small regional cohort with limited numbers of other ethnic groups. Third, although sleep‐disordered breathing is an important factor in obese‐phenotype HFpEF, our baseline clinical data did not include polysomnography. Fourth, given the retrospective nature of this study, potential biases are difficult to avoid, and our findings need to be confirmed in future large‐scale prospective studies.

CONCLUSIONS

EAT volume is a powerful predictor of adverse prognosis in patients with HFpEF and HFmrEF, incremental to common clinical and CMR risk factors.
