Geological conditions for shale gas accumulation and favorable zone evaluation in the Longmaxi formation of the Yanyuan Basin
Authors: Jinjie Yong, Shijia Chen, Shaoyong Hu, Zhicheng Liu, Mengtian Zheng, Tingshan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Yanyuan Basin, situated along the southern margin of the Songpan–Ganzi fold belt and outside the Sichuan Basin, is a Meso–Cenozoic fault–depression superimposed basin. The Lower Silurian Longmaxi Formation within this basin exhibits promising shale gas potential; however, its accumulation conditions remain poorly constrained due to complex structural settings. In this study, we integrate core observations, thin-section analysis, well-log interpretation, and laboratory data to comprehensively investigate the lithology, stratigraphic distribution, depositional environment, reservoir characteristics, and preservation conditions of the Longmaxi shale. On this basis, favorable and prospective areas for shale gas exploration are delineated. Results demonstrate that organic-rich shale is mainly developed in the lower part of the first member of the Longmaxi Formation, characterized by thick intervals of deep-water carbonaceous siliceous mudstone with high total organic carbon content and type I–II kerogen. The formation is generally deeply buried, with high overpressure coefficients and limited tectonic disturbance, which has facilitated the preservation of pore–fracture systems. Furthermore, the central and southern parts of the basin are distinguished by thick sealing strata, relatively stable structural frameworks, and weak deformation, providing optimal conditions for shale gas enrichment and defining these regions as priority targets for exploration.Keywords: Yanyuan basin, Longmaxi formation, Shale gas, Accumulation conditions, Favorable areaSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Shale gas has emerged as a strategic energy resource in the global “energy revolution,” playing a key role in reshaping the world’s energy landscape1–4. In recent years, China has achieved landmark breakthroughs in shale gas exploration and development5–7. Several national-level marine shale gas demonstration zones have been established in southern Sichuan, making China the first region outside North America to realize large-scale commercial shale gas production8–10. With continuous innovation in exploration technology and advancement of development, China’s shale gas exploration is extending from medium-depth reservoirs to shallower and deeper formations.

Sichuan Province in southwestern China possesses shale gas resources exceeding 40 trillion cubic meters, indicating promising prospects for exploration and development. Significant progress has been made in shale gas production from the Lower Silurian Longmaxi Formation in the Sichuan Basin. To further evaluate the resource potential of the Longmaxi Formation shale gas, public welfare shale gas basic geological surveys have been extended to structurally complex areas beyond the Sichuan Basin, including the Yanyuan region. However, due to the complex tectonic conditions and unclear hydrocarbon accumulation mechanisms in the Yanyuan Basin, it is of great importance to systematically assess the shale gas geological conditions and clarify the patterns of shale gas accumulation and enrichment in this area11.

In this study, we analyze the key geological factors controlling shale gas accumulation in the Longmaxi Formation of the Yanyuan Basin. Using data from outcrop sections, well core, well logs, and a suite of laboratory experiments, we evaluate the Longmaxi shale’s lithofacies, stratigraphic distribution, sedimentary setting, reservoir characteristics, and preservation conditions. Based on these results, we delineate favorable and prospective areas for shale gas and discuss the controls on gas enrichment. The findings provide insights into the shale gas accumulation conditions in the Yanyuan Basin and help inform exploration direction in this complex basin.

Geological setting

Yanyuan Basin, Sichuan Province, is a Meso–Cenozoic fault-depression superimposed basin, characterized by a tilted half-graben structure with the southern part downwarped and the northern part upwarped. The Yanyuan Basin is predominantly situated within the Sichuan–Yunnan–Guizhou “Rhomboidal Block” on the southwestern margin of the Yangtze Platform. This rhomboidal block is bounded by the Kangding–Yiliang–Shuicheng Fault to the east, adjacent to the Sichuan Basin; it connects with the Ganzī Indosinian Geosyncline to the north, separated by the Xiaojinhe Fault; it borders the Sanjiang Fold System to the west, with the Jinshajiang–Ailaoshan Fault as the boundary; and it adjoins the South China Caledonian Fold Belt to the south, demarcated by the Mile–Shizong–Shuicheng Fault.

The basin contains strata ranging from the Sinian to Quaternary, dominated by littoral to shallow marine sediments. Triassic strata are the most widespread, covering approximately 80% of the basin area (Fig. 1). During the Indosinian orogeny, the block experienced maximum subsidence, with Triassic marine clastic rocks, limestones, and coal-bearing deposits reaching thicknesses of up to 4,000 m. Bedding contacts between adjacent strata are mainly conformable or weakly unconformable, with gentle dips typically between 20° and 30°.

Samples and methods

78 shale samples were collected from multiple outcrop sections and one exploration well in the Yanyuan Basin, covering the range of facies and thickness variations of the Longmaxi Formation. Key outcrop sections sampled include Baizitian, Gelanghe (Huangcao Town), Goukou, Qinghe, Minsheng, Shuhe, Pingzi, and others across the basin, as well as core samples from the Chuanyan-2 well in the basin interior.

Petrographic and mineralogical analysis

Standard thin sections were prepared from representative shale samples(15 samples) for optical examination of lithology and texture. Mineral composition was quantified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) on powdered samples using a PANalytical diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation)12–14. The relative abundances of major minerals (quartz, feldspar, clay minerals, carbonates, etc.) were determined from XRD peak intensities. Clay mineral species were further identified using oriented clay mounts and glycolation, distinguishing illite, illite–smectite mixed layers, chlorite, and kaolinite.

Total organic carbon (TOC)

TOC content of 15 shale sample was measured using an elemental analyzer after removing inorganic carbon by acid treatment. A large number of samples (both outcrop and core) were analyzed to capture variability in organic matter abundance across the formation. In addition, some samples were characterized by Rock-Eval pyrolysis (if data available) or by evaluating oil and gas show data from field descriptions, to support the assessment of hydrocarbon generation potential15,16.

Thermal maturity

15 polished pellet samples were examined under both transmitted and reflected light microscopes to identify kerogen maceral types and measure vitrinite reflectance (Ro). Maceral analysis followed standard palynofacies and kerogen classification, focusing on recognizing marine algal/amorphous organic matter versus terrestrial vitrinite or inertinite. Vitrinite reflectance was measured on any vitrinite or solid bitumen particles present, using oil immersion objectives, to determine thermal maturity of the shale. Due to the high maturity of these rocks, true vitrinite is rare; reflectance measurements likely represent bituminite or vitrinite-like macerals17–19. Nevertheless, the Ro values provide an estimate of the thermal maturity stage of the organic matter.

Scanning electron microscopy

Selected 20 shale samples were examined with field-emission SEM to characterize the pore types and microstructures of the reservoir. Fresh fracture surfaces (and in some cases ion-milled sample surfaces) were gold-coated and observed under SEM at magnifications up to ~ 10,000×. Both secondary electron and back-scattered electron images were used to identify pores associated with organic matter, mineral grains, fractures, and fossils20,21. SEM imaging provided qualitative observations of pore morphology and connectivity at the nano- to micro-scale.

Low-temperature nitrogen adsorption

To quantitatively assess pore size distribution and specific surface area, low-pressure gas adsorption experiments were conducted on dried, powdered 10 shale samples. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using a surface area and porosimetry analyzer (e.g., Micromeritics ASAP series). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was used to calculate specific surface area, and the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model was applied to the adsorption branch to derive the pore volume and surface area distribution as a function of pore diameter22,23. This technique is most sensitive to mesopores (2–50 nm) and can infer the presence of micropores (< 2 nm) and macropores (> 50 nm) based on the shape of the isotherm and distribution curves.

Results

Stratigraphic and sedimentary characteristics

Lithologic characteristics

In the Yanyuan Basin, depositional intervals of the Longmaxi Formation corresponding to a rapid sea-level rise can be identified. These intervals, marked by the transgressive surface, are characterized by increased siliceous and carbonaceous content, and accordingly the Longmaxi Formation is divided into two Members (Fig. 2). In the first Member, the lower part consists of gray-black to gray-purple carbonaceous siliceous mudstone and siliceous rock, containing abundant belemnite fossils and pyrite (Fig. 3a, b). The upper part comprises gray-black to black carbonaceous siliceous mudstone and carbonaceous mudstone, with local lenticular limestone interbeds; from bottom to top, the siliceous component diminishes. In the second Member, the lower part is made up of gray-black siliceous carbonaceous mudstone and gray-black to dark gray carbonaceous mudstone, whereas the upper part consists of gray siltstone and gray-green mudstone. Again, the siliceous content decreases upward. The lower boundary conformably overlies the gray, medium- to thick-bedded calcareous muddy limestone of the Baota Formation (Fig. 3c, d).

X-ray diffraction analysis of shale samples from the Longmaxi Formation indicates that quartz and clay minerals are predominant. Quartz content ranges from 33% to 92% (mean 68.2%), and clay minerals from 4% to 61% (mean 18.5%) (Fig. 4). Feldspar content ranges from 0 to 20% (mean 7.5%). The average contents of calcite and dolomite are 2.4% and 1.9%, respectively (Fig. 5). Among the clay fraction, illite dominates, with minor illite–smectite mixed layers and chlorite. Kaolinite content is low and appears mainly in samples that have undergone intense weathering. For example, at the surface of the weathered profile in Gulanghe, Huangcao Town, kaolinite reaches 17%.

Stratigraphic characteristics

The thickness of the Longmaxi Formation increases from east to west (Figs. 6 and 7), with an overall thickness of about 80–100 m. For example, the formation thicknesses are 86.15 m at the Shuhe–Pingzi section (excluding intrusion thickness), 83.31 m at Fangniuping (Guosheng Township), 164.24 m at Minsheng, 35.23 m at Qinghe, 60.03 m at Well Chuanyandi 2, 94 m at the Haizidian section, 56.57 m at Wenquan Siluping, and 27.6 m at Ninglang Dacaozi. During the deposition of the Longmaxi Formation, an uneven early topography controlled sediment distribution: possible submarine uplift in the Dacaozi and Qinghe areas led to local thinning of the deposit (Fig. 7a).

Stratigraphic correlation diagram of the Longmaxi Formation in the Yanyuan Basin from Northwest-Southeast direction.

Stratigraphic Thickness (a) and thickness of organic-rich shale (b) in the Longmaxi Formation of the Yanyuan Basin (Maps generated using Coreldraw Graphics Suite 2018, https://www.coreldraw.com/cn/).

Organic-rich shale of the Longmaxi Formation is mainly distributed in the first Member and the lower part of the second Member. Dominant lithologies include carbonaceous siliceous mudstone, carbonaceous siliceous rock, carbonaceous mudstone, calcareous carbonaceous mudstone, and carbonaceous silty sandstone. TOC contents range from 1% to 5%. Notably, the carbonaceous mudstone and siliceous mudstone in the upper part of the first Member and the second Member have the highest TOC contents and constitute the main favorable target intervals.

By summing the cumulative thickness of organic-rich shale at each section, the areal thicknesses of organic-rich shale are found to be 56.85 m at the Haizidian section, 20.01 m at Qinghe, 58.54 m at Well Chuanyandi 2, 61.23 m at Minsheng, and only 2.98 m at Pingzi. Overall, the thickness of organic-rich mudstone increases from east to west; however, thinning occurs in the Dacaozi and Qinghe areas due to the effect of submarine uplifts. The organic-rich interval is thinnest in the east, near the shallow-water shelf zone and at Pingzi. Based on paleogeographic trends and thickness patterns of organic-rich shale profiles, it is predicted that west of the Baiwu-Yanyuan area the thickness of organic-rich shale exceeds 20 m, and in the Gesala–Yantang area, it ranges between 50 and 60 m, with thinning again at Dacaozi and Qinghe (Fig. 7b).

Sedimentary environment

The identification of graptolite fossils from the Baota Formation in well Chuanyandi 2 reveals the absence of Late Ordovician (Wufeng Age to Guanyinqiao Age) graptolite zones in the Yanyuan Basin, while the Long-1 to Long-9 members of the Longmaxi Formation exhibit a well-developed and complete succession of graptolite zones27. In the Hepingzi area, the presence of a clay-type weathering crust between the Longmaxi Formation and the underlying Baota Formation provides evidence for an exposure unconformity, indicating the stratigraphic absence of the Wufeng to Guanyinqiao formations in the Yanyuan Basin. A weathering crust developed at the unconformity between the Longmaxi Formation and the underlying Baota Formation, confirming that after the deposition of the Middle Ordovician Baota Formation, the basin was uplifted and exposed, and then during the Silurian Longmaxi period, it subsided again to allow marine transgression24–26. The Yanyuan Basin is located on the western margin of the Yangtze Block. After deposition of the Baota Formation, the basin underwent uplift and exposure, and subsequently subsided again in the Silurian. The paleogeographic setting was essentially “land to the east, sea to the west”: the eastern side was the Kangdian paleo-continent of the Yangtze Block (an uplifted region), while the western side was the passive continental margin sea of the western Yangtze platform27–29.

During the Longmaxi Period, the Yanyuan Basin was bounded by the Kangdian Paleoland to the east and the passive continental marginal sea on the western Yangtze Block to the west, representing an open marine shelf depositional environment. The sedimentary facies belt exhibited an east-to-west zonation from paleoland, through tidal flat and shallow-water shelf, to deep-water shelf environments. Due to the Jinhe–Qinghe thrust fault, the marginal facies near the eastern paleoland are not exposed, with only the Hepingzi area preserving a shallow-water shelf depositional environment. Further west toward the deep marine basin, two submarine uplifts—the Qinghe Submarine Uplift and the Dacaozi Submarine Uplift—developed in the shelf region as a result of differential subsidence of the sedimentary basement. In unexposed areas of the northern Yanyuan Basin, it is inferred that submarine uplifts similar to those in the Denghe and Dacaozi areas may exist. In summary, the region west of Hepingzi in the Longmaxi Formation of the Yanyuan Basin was consistently characterized by a deep-water shelf environment, where depositional microfacies included siliceous deep-water shelf, siliceous–argillaceous deep-water shelf, carbonaceous–argillaceous deep-water shelf, and silty deep-water shelf(Fig. 8). The two submarine uplifts at Dacaozi and Denghe played a controlling role in the distribution of organic-rich mudstones and shales through variations in lithology and thickness.

Source rock characteristics

Organic matter abundance

Single-layer samples from sections such as Haizidian, Goukou, and Minsheng, and Well Chuanyandi 2, exhibit maximum TOC values of 3–10%. In contrast, outcrop section samples have lower average TOC contents of 2.16%, 2.56%, 2.08%, and 2.15% (Fig. 9a). Among all samples, 49 (13%) have TOC > 3%; 57 samples (15%) have TOC between 2 and 3%, and 98 samples (26%) have TOC between 1 and 2%. The largest proportion of samples (46%) have TOC < 1% (Fig. 9b).

Organic matter abundance statistics of the Longmaxi Formation in the Yanyuan Basin. (a) Shale TOC from major sections; (b) Cumulative distribution of shale TOC.

Overall, the high-TOC zones of the Longmaxi Formation organic-rich shale in the study area are primarily concentrated in the southwestern part. The TOC content generally exhibits a decreasing trend from west to east. The Gesala–Huangcao–Yantang area shows the highest organic matter abundance, with TOC values commonly exceeding 2%. In contrast, regions such as Shuhe–Zemulong, which are adjacent to the Kangdian Paleoland, were less conducive to the accumulation of shallow-marine algae, bacteria, and plankton, resulting in relatively poor organic matter abundance.

Thermal maturity

The Longmaxi Formation source rocks in the study area are at a high level of thermal maturation, with no vitrinite observed in the maceral composition. Under transmitted light, the organic matter predominantly shows a cloud- and sponge-like amorphous structure with indistinct biological morphology; as maturity increases, the organic matter color gradually changes from pale yellow to brownish gray, and its transparency decreases30–34. Under reflected light, irregular granular outlines appear gray to light gray with pronounced relief; under fluorescence microscopy, a brownish-yellow to brown fluorescence of moderate intensity is observed. Maceral identification shows that the samples are dominated by typical marine source rock components, including flocculent sapropelic amorphous material, alginite, sapropel fragments, and organic laminae, with minor occurrences of some phytoclasts (filamentous remains), unstructured vitrinite, and inertinite-like matter (Fig. 10a, b).

Ro of the Longmaxi Formation in the study area ranges from 2.02% to 2.86%, averaging 2.36% (Table 1). Lower Ro values are found near the Dacaogou anticline (at Haizidian, Qinghe, and Minsheng) and near the Kangdian paleo-continent (at Shuhe and Pingzi). Overall, however, the formation is over-mature and lies in the dry gas generation window. Outside the study area, organic matter maturity is generally slightly lower; aside from the Jingu area (Ro = 1.87%, high-maturity stage), other sections are also in the over-mature to dry gas stage. Spatially, Ro values in the main target intervals of the Longmaxi Formation increase from east to west and from south to north, suggesting that the basin center exceeds Ro = 2.8%, consistent with an over-mature, dry gas stage.

Reservoir characteristics

The shale of the Longmaxi Formation in the Yanyuan Basin exhibits a multi-type composite development of pore space. Dissolution pores and clay interlaminar microfractures constitute the main storage space. The dissolution pores are primarily hosted in reactive minerals such as feldspar and carbonates (Fig. 11b, c), appearing as irregular cavities on or within mineral grains, some of which are partially filled with secondary minerals35–37. Dissolution pores are also widely developed in minerals such as gypsum and pyrite (Fig. 11d, e). In addition, organic pores, intercrystalline pores, and biogenic pores are variably developed. Organic pores mainly form during the hydrocarbon generation process; residual organic matter occurs as filling or detrital material within mineral matrices, forming a characteristic network of hydrocarbon-generation pores (Fig. 11a). Interparticle pores occur as the unfilled spaces between mineral grains or at organic-inorganic interfaces, including moldic pores formed after pyrite dissolution38,39 (Fig. 11g). Clay-interlaminar microfractures are dominated by illite (Fig. 11h), followed by illite-montmorillonite mixed layers (Fig. 11i); their platy or wedge shapes are closely related to the shale’s lamination and clay mineral characteristics.

Based on low-temperature nitrogen adsorption data and the BJH model, the pore size distribution curves dV/dD, dA/dD, dV/dlog(D), and dA/dlog(D) versus pore diameter D were used for characterization40–42. Based on the calculation results from the BET equation, the specific surface area of the Longmaxi Formation reservoirs in the study area exhibits considerable variation, ranging from 1.28 m²/g to 25.58 m²/g, with an average value of 7.90 m²/g. For the Longmaxi shale samples, the dV/dD and dA/dD curves continuously increase as D decreases and do not reach a peak down to the lower measurement limit of 1.7 nm. By comparing the effective ranges of the curves (segments where the values are greater than 0), it was found that the effective range for dV/dD begins at D = 100 nm, whereas for dA/dD it begins at D = 20 nm. This difference arises from the varying sensitivity of the two curves to macropores. Overall, the results indicate that the pore structure of these shales is dominated by mesopores and micropores, with macropores playing a subordinate role (Fig. 12a, b).

Gas content and preservation conditions

Burial depth and formation pressure

In the Yanyuan Basin, the Longmaxi Formation mainly outcrops along the Jinhe-Qinghe fault zone. Due to thrust faulting, depths near the Kangdian uplift (in areas such as Wenquan, Gesala, Zemulong, to northern Shuhe) are ≤ 4,500 m. Toward the basin center, burial depth increases rapidly: most areas exceed 6,000 m, reaching a maximum of over 9,000 m around the Mianya-Baiwu area (Fig. 13).

Burial depth of the lower boundary of the Longmaxi Formation in the Yanyuan Basin (Map generated using Coreldraw Graphics Suite 2018, https://www.coreldraw.com/cn/).

The formation pressure coefficients show that the central depression zone is an area of overpressure. Coefficients > 1.3 are mainly found southwest of Meiyu Town, northeast of Shuanghe, east of Wumu, and northeast of Mianya, where burial is relatively deep. In most of the basin, Longmaxi pressure coefficients are between 1.1 and 1.3, reflecting a transitional slope zone between depression and uplift. Pressure coefficients between 1.0 and 1.1 are mainly concentrated in uplift areas; notably, in Yanyuan County, Huangcao Town, and northeast of Gesala (toward the basin margin), values are generally below 1.1 (Fig. 14). Combined with burial depth analysis, the high formation pressure zones are primarily located in deeply buried depressions. Conversely, in the uplifted areas along the southern and western margins of the Yanyuan Basin, the formation pressure coefficients exhibit markedly lower values. Furthermore, in regions with similar burial depths, areas with relatively well-developed fault systems may have experienced the release of deep fluids and pressure, resulting in comparatively smaller formation pressure coefficients.

Burial and hydrocarbon generation history

The evolution of organic matter in the Lower Silurian Longmaxi Formation in the study area proceeded through the following stages (Fig. 15). During the Late Devonian (Frasnian) to early Middle Devonian, it entered the early-mature stage and began generating oil. From the Early Late Devonian (Famennian) to the late Early Permian, it remained in the mid-mature stage (within the oil window), with peak generation and expulsion occurring from the end of the Carboniferous (Westphalian) to the Early Permian (Asselian), yielding large-scale hydrocarbon production. In the early to mid-Late Permian, it entered the late-mature stage with strong expulsion; by the Late Permian, upon entering the over-mature stage, hydrocarbon generation and expulsion declined significantly. Thus, the hydrocarbon generation and expulsion peak of the Longmaxi source rocks was concentrated in the Westphalian–Early Indosinian (Late Carboniferous to Early Permian)43. Notably, Late Permian magmatic activity is temporally and spatially coupled with the source rock generation peak - when the magmatism occurred, the Longmaxi source rocks were at the peak of oil generation, transitioning to gas generation. This synchronicity meant that the magmatic thermal effects not only accelerated source rock maturation, but the associated fracture systems also became dual conduits for hydrocarbon migration or leakage. These conduits could facilitate trapping and accumulation, but also could lead to hydrocarbon loss. Therefore, the magmatic event during this period had a decisive influence on the initial accumulation of hydrocarbons. By the Early Indosinian, regional tectonics were characterized by broad uplift and a lack of intense orogeny, providing favorable conditions for shale gas preservation.

Discussion

Drawing on exploration experience from marine shale gas demonstration areas in the Sichuan Basin, and considering the shale gas accumulation conditions (including source rocks, reservoirs, and preservation) of the Yanyuan Basin, the overall resource conditions of the Longmaxi Formation in the study area are deemed favorable. Shale gas content generally decreases as structural conditions deteriorate, indicating that the tectonic setting plays a decisive role in gas-bearing quality. By analyzing factors such as cap rocks, structural deformation, and magmatic activity related to shale gas preservation, the study area can be divided into three categories (Fig. 16).

Distribution map of favorable and prospective areas for shale gas in the Longmaxi Formation of the Yanyuan Basin (Map generated using Coreldraw Graphics Suite 2018, https://www.coreldraw.com/cn/).

Favorable zone

Favorable Zones are characterized by superior caprock conditions, including good roof and floor strata and an intact regional seal. Structurally, there is no temporal or spatial coupling between tectonic deformation and the hydrocarbon generation/expulsion period of the target formation. These zones are located far from Grade-I fault zones and open faults, exhibit weak structural modification, and possess a favorable structural style. The target formation is at a moderate burial depth, situated far from uplifted and denuded areas, resulting in a short duration of post-generation gas dissipation. Furthermore, these areas are only weakly affected by magmatic activity.

The shale gas favorable zone is mainly distributed in the central part of the study area. It is characterized by broad, thick, and stable regional seals (top, bottom, and regional), and a stable depositional environment. These conditions match well with the spatial distribution, petrophysical, and mechanical properties of the Longmaxi shales27, providing strong sealing capacity conducive to gas preservation. Contemporaneous magmatic activity had minimal impact on the source rock’s generation-expulsion peak, so most source rocks in this zone remain well preserved. This zone is also distant from major first-order fault zones (Jinhe-Qinghe and Xiaojinhe faults), resulting in a relatively simple structure, low fault density, and weak structural reworking44–48. Folds in the zone are mainly broad and gentle closures, which favor shale gas enrichment and accumulation. The target intervals here are deeply buried, thus less affected by tectonic uplift and erosion, and exhibit strong self-sealing capacity.

Relatively favorable zone

Relatively Favorable Zones feature fairly good caprock conditions with a relatively intact regional seal. Some degree of temporal and spatial coupling exists between tectonic deformation and hydrocarbon generation/expulsion. They are located relatively far from Grade-I fault zones but closer to open faults, and have undergone relatively weak structural modification with a fairly good structural style. While the target formation is at a moderate depth, it is closer to uplifted and denuded areas, leading to a relatively short gas dissipation duration. These zones are moderately affected by magmatic activity.

The relatively favorable zone mainly surrounds the favorable zone. Its characteristics include generally well-developed and stable seals, weaker structural deformation, and a good match with the spatial and petrophysical characteristics of the Longmaxi shales. However, it is influenced by the Jinhe-Qinghe fault zone, Xiaojinhe fault zone, and the Songpan–Ganzi fold system, so the northwest, southeast, eastern, and western parts of the study area experience relatively intense structural reworking, with complex folding and faulting that can partially impair the preservation and accumulation of shale gas in the Longmaxi Formation49–52. Overall, in this zone, the seals are only slightly damaged and retain good sealing ability; the structure is relatively simple with modest reworking; the target layers are deeply buried and relatively unaffected by uplift and erosion; thus, the shale gas preservation conditions are moderately good.

Prospective zone

Prospective Zones are defined by poor caprock conditions, including an incomplete regional seal. A clear coupling is observed between tectonic deformation and hydrocarbon generation/expulsion in both time and space. These zones are in close proximity to Grade-I fault zones, have experienced intense structural modification, and possess an unfavorable structural style. The target formation is shallowly buried and close to uplifted and denuded areas, resulting in a long duration for gas dissipation. Additionally, they are strongly affected by magmatic activity.

The prospective (or exploration) zone is mainly located along the Jinhe–Qinghe and Xiaojinhe fault zones. Structural deformation is intense, with numerous thrust faults and fractures, resulting in poor fault sealing conditions and severe damage to both the local Longmaxi and the regional seals. In particular, near the Jinhe–Qinghe fault, the Longmaxi Formation has been uplifted, leading to shallower burial and weak self-sealing, which is unfavorable for gas preservation53–55. Additionally, in parts of the study area farther from the first-order faults (e.g., southwest Yanyuan), many Tongtian faults with high density are present, and the fractures associated with magmatic activity during this period become direct channels for shale gas escape. Consequently, these factors significantly hinder the initial accumulation and preservation of shale gas in the Longmaxi Formation in this zone. Overall, the shale gas preservation in the prospective zone is the poorest among the three zones.

Conclusions

The lower part of the first Member of the Longmaxi Formation in the Yanyuan Basin consists of gray-black to gray-purple carbonaceous siliceous mudstone and siliceous rock, rich in belemnite fossils and pyrite. The mineralogy is dominated by quartz and clay minerals (mainly illite, with minor illite/smectite mixed layers and chlorite). In map view, formation thickness increases from east to west (overall 80–100 m); Organic-rich shale is predominantly distributed in the first Member and the upper part of the second Member, and the thickness of organic-rich mudstone increases toward the west.

In the study area, the Longmaxi Formation has an organic matter abundance of 2%–10%; 13% of samples have TOC > 3%. Organic matter is dominated by flocculent sapropelic amorphous material. Thermal maturity is high (over-mature, dry gas stage), and Ro trends increase from east to west and south to north. Various pore types are developed, including organic pores, dissolution pores, intercrystalline pores, biological framework pores, intergranular pores, and clay interlayer microfractures. The pore structure is dominated by mesopores and micropores, with macropores subordinate.

Burial depth increases rapidly toward the basin center, with most areas exceeding 6000 m and reaching over 9000 m near Mianya–Baiwu. The central depression is an area of high formation pressure. Regional tectonics has been dominated by uplift without strong orogenic events, allowing most source rocks to be well preserved. The shale gas favorable and prospective zones are located in the central and southern parts of the basin.

The shale gas favorable zone is distributed in the basin center: it has well-developed, stable seals; is distant from major (first-order) faults; and has weak deformation and minor magmatic influence, resulting in the best preservation conditions. The relatively favorable zone lies around the favorable zone: it has good seal integrity but is slightly affected by fault zones and fold systems, with moderately good preservation.
