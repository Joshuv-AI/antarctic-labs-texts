The psycho–social dual-pathway perspective on healthy aging in digital age-friendly environments: longitudinal evidence from China
Authors: Bo Zhao, Xiaoyan Wang, Ren Chen, Eun Woo Nam, Fanlei Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAttitudes toward one’s own aging are closely related to health and well-being in later life. Meanwhile, digitalization during and after the COVID-19 pandemic has changed older adults’ access to services, social participation, and everyday health management. Digital inclusion may support healthy aging, but it may also create burdens when digital use is difficult, stressful, or poorly supported. Yet few studies have examined how aging attitudes and digital inclusion are jointly related to health over time. This study investigated the longitudinal relationships among aging attitudes, digital inclusion, and health among older adults in China.MethodsWe used two-wave panel data from the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS 2020 and 2023; N = 3,320). Generalized estimating equations were used to estimate adjusted longitudinal associations. Cross-lagged panel models examined temporal and potentially reciprocal relationships, and structural equation modeling tested whether digital inclusion mediated the association between aging attitudes and health. Models adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, chronic disease, and functional status.ResultsAging attitudes were highly stable over time. In the GEE and SEM analyses, more positive aging attitudes were associated with better health outcomes. However, the cross-lagged models showed a more complex pattern after baseline stability was considered. Digital inclusion also showed mixed associations with health: digital proficiency and digital ability were positively associated with health, whereas digital access alone showed no consistent benefit. Baseline digital inclusion was negatively associated with later health and aging attitudes in the cross-lagged models. Mediation analyses suggested that digital inclusion partially explained the relationship between aging attitudes and health, supporting the coexistence of psychological and digital-social pathways.ConclusionThe findings support a psycho-social perspective on healthy aging. Aging attitudes were generally linked to better health, while digital inclusion showed mixed associations, suggesting that digital engagement can be helpful when it is meaningful and supported, but burdensome when it exceeds older adults’ skills or needs. Policies and interventions should therefore go beyond expanding digital access and strengthen digital competence, age-friendly digital environments, and positive views of aging to support healthier and more inclusive aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07686-2.

Background

Population aging is no longer a distant demographic trend, but a reality that is reshaping public health systems and social life across the world. In China, this transition is particularly rapid. The number and proportion of older adults continue to rise, and more people are spending later life living alone [1]. These changes have brought growing concerns regarding older adults’ physical, psychological, and social health, making the identification of key determinants of health in later life a pressing public health priority [2]. Within this context, healthy aging is increasingly understood as a multidimensional process shaped not only by biological aging, but also by psychological dispositions and broader social environments [3]. This perspective is closely aligned with the concept of age-friendly environments (AFEs), which emphasizes that healthy aging is supported not only by individual capacities but also by enabling sufficient support, accessible services, inclusive technologies, and meaningful opportunities for participation [4]. As daily life becomes increasingly digital, digital inclusion has become an important part of this age-friendly context. It may affect how older adults maintain autonomy, stay socially connected, use health and community services, and manage their own health in later life [5].

Among psychological factors, attitudes toward aging have received increasing attention as an important influence on health and well-being in later life [6]. Aging attitudes refer to how individual think and feel about their own aging, as well as how they understand aging more broadly within society. According to stereotype embodiment theory, beliefs about aging are not simply external social ideas; over time, they may be internalized and become personally relevant as people grow older [7]. Once internalized, such beliefs can shape health through multiple pathways, including stress appraisal, emotional regulation, self-efficacy, and health-related behavior [8]. Related scholarship on views on aging further suggests that older adults’ subjective interpretations of aging are more than descriptive perceptions. They may influence how individuals adapt to age-related changes, cope with difficulties, and maintain psychological well-being [9]. Existing empirical studies consistently show that positive aging attitudes are associated with better mental health, greater life satisfaction, and higher overall well-being, whereas negative aging attitudes are often linked to depressive symptoms, poorer self-regulation, and stronger stress responses [10].

Recent research have further shown that aging attitudes should be understood as a multidimensional construct, including psychosocial loss, physical change, and psychological growth [11, 12]. These dimensions reflect different sides of the aging experiences. While psychosocial loss and physical change often capture concerns related to decline, limitation, or vulnerability, psychological growth points to the possibility of adaptation, maturity, and continued personal development in later life. Evidence from both longitudinal and cross-sectional studies indicates that negative perceptions of aging are associated with depressive symptoms and may also contribute to their onset and persistence over time [13, 14]. This indicates that aging attitudes are not only a reflection of older adults’ current psychological state, but may also shape future mental health trajectories. In this sense, aging attitudes provide a meaningful psychological perspective through which broader social and environmental conditions, including digital and community contexts, may be linked to health outcomes in older adulthood [15].

At the same time, rapid digitalization has changed the social conditions in which people grow older. Digital technologies now provide new ways for older adults to management health, access to services, maintain social ties, and participate in community life, especially since the COVID-19 pandemic made online platforms a more routine part of everyday life [16]. Yet older adults do not benefit from these changes equally [17]. While digital participation among older adults in China has increased markedly in recent years, clear gaps remain in access, skills, confidence, and the ability to use digital tools effectively [18, 19]. These gaps are often described as the digital divide, but they are not limited to whether a person owns a device or can connect to the internet. They also involve perceived competence, adaptability, and the extent to which digital services are actually usable and meaningful for older users [20]. From the perspective of the social determinants of health, such differences matter because they shape access to information, social resources, public services, and opportunities for participation across the life course [21]. More recently, the concept of digital determinants of health has extended this argument by showing that digital access, affordability, usability, literacy, and the wider technological environment may influence health both directly and indirectly [22]. Therefore, digital inclusion should not be understood as simple technology use. Rather, it reflects a socially patterned condition that may support or constrain health in later life, depending on how digital resources are distributed, designed, and used; it may also shape how older adults perceive their own aging in an increasingly digital society [23].

Existing research have not reached a uniform conclusion about how digital inclusion affects older adults’ health. Some evidence suggests that digital engagement can help older adults stay connected, obtain health information, and participate in social and community activities, which may support psychological and social well-being [24, 25]. However, digital technology is not always experienced as empowering. For older adults with limited digital skills, low confidence, or more negative views of aging, the use of digital tools may also bring frustration, loneliness, psychological distress, or additional cognitive burden [26, 27]. These contrasting findings suggest that digital inclusion may have both supportive and potentially adverse effects, depending on older adults’ personal characteristics and the contexts in which digital technologies are used [28]. This pattern can be better understood from a socio-technical systems perspective. Socio-Technical Systems Theory emphasizes that the effects of technology are shaped not by technical access alone, but by the interaction between peoples, technologies, tasks, and surrounding social arrangements [29]. In later life, this means that the health implications of digital inclusion depend not only on whether older adults are connected to digital resources, but also on whether there resources are usable, supportive, meaningful, and aligned with their capabilities and everyday needs [30].

Older adults’ health is therefore shaped by both psychological dispositions and the digital environments in which they live. Aging attitudes may influence how older adults perceive, engage with, and benefit from digital technologies. While, digital experiences may also affect how older adults understand their own aging, especially when technology either enhances autonomy and social connection or reinforces feelings of exclusion and inadequacy. Based on these considerations, the present study adopts a psycho-social dual-pathway framework. The psychological pathway refers to the ways in which aging attitudes may influence health through internalized age beliefs, emotional regulation, and coping-related processes. The social pathway refers to the role of digital inclusion as a socially and technologically structured condition that may shape participation, service access, and health-related engagement. These two pathways are not independent. Rather, psychological mechanisms may shape older adults’ engagement with digital technologies, while digital inclusion may in turn strengthen or weaken the health effects of aging attitudes [31, 32].

There are also theoretical grounds for considering digital inclusion as a mediating pathway between aging attitude and health. Older adults with more positive aging attitudes may be more likely to view digital technologies as learnable, useful, and compatible with later life. This may encourgae them to acquire digital skills, use online services, and participate in digital forms of social interaction [33]. In this way, aging attitudes may affect health not only directly, but also indirectly by shaping the extent and quality of digital engagement. This mediating process may be is especially relevant for active dimensions of digital inclusion, such as digital proficiency and digital ability, because these dimensions depend more heavily on motivation, confidence, learning capacity, and adaptation. By contrast, digital access may be more strongly influenced by household or infrastructural conditions [34]. At the same time, the association between aging attitudes and digital inclusion may also not be one-directional. Positive digital experiences may strengthen older adults’ sense of autonomy, competence and social connectedness, whereas negative perceptions may deepen frustration, exclusion, or perceived inadequacy [35, 36]. For this reason, the study examines digital inclusion not only as a possible pathway linking aging attitudes to health, but also as part of a dynamic relationships among aging attitudes, digital inclusion, and health over time.

Although aging attitudes and digital inclusion have both attracted increasing research attention, they have rarely been examined together in a longitudinal framework. Existing studies often focus on either psychological factors or digital participation separately, making it difficult to understand how these two domains interact in shaping health in later life [37]. Moreover, many studies rely on cross-sectional data or use relatively narrow measures of digital engagement, which limits the ability to clarify temporal ordering and underlying mechanisms. Another limitation is that these issues are not always connected to the broader age-friendly environment agenda or to the emerging discussion on digital determinants of health, despite the growing importance of digital systems in shaping later-life inclusion and exclusion. To response these gaps, the present study investigates the longitudinal relationships among aging attitudes, digital inclusion, and health outcomes among older adults in China. By integrating psychological and social perspectives and applying longitudinal analytical approaches, this study aims to clarify how older adults’ views of aging and their digital inclusion experiences jointly shape health trajectories in later life, and to provide a more context-sensitive understanding of healthy aging in the digital era.

Guided by the psycho-social dual-pathway framework, the present study is theory-informed but not strictly confirmatory. Although previous research provides a basis for expecting directional associations, the longitudinal relationships among aging attitudes, digital inclusion, and health may be more dynamic and potentially reciprocal. Methods

Study design and data source

This study used two-wave panel data from the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS) conducted in 2020 and 2023. CLASS is a nationally representative survey of community-dwelling adults aged 60 years and older across 28 provinces in China (excluding Hainan, Xinjiang, Tibet, Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan). A stratified, multistage probability sampling strategy with probability proportional to size (PPS) was adopted. The survey collected detailed information on sociodemographic characteristics, digital behavior, attitudes toward aging, physical and psychological health, functional status, and social adaptation.

The respondents who were successfully followed across both waves were included in this study. After excluding cases with incomplete information on key study variables, the final analytic sample consisted of 3,320 older adults. Consistent with the World Health Organization’s definition of health [38] as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, health outcomes were conceptualized as a multi-dimensional construct encompassing physical health, mental health, and social adaptation.

Digital inclusion

Digital inclusion was treated as a multidimensional construct reflecting not only access to digital technologies but also skills and effective engagement. Three dimensions were assessed:

Digital access: internet access and ownership of internet-capable devices, including mobile phones, tablets, or computers. Aging attitude

Aging attitudes were defined as older adults’ subjective experiences and evaluations of the aging process. Drawing on previous theoretical frameworks and the Attitudes to Ageing Questionnaire (AAQ), the seven-item scale has been confirmed in its reliability and validity among the older adult population in China, demonstrating good applicability [47]. In this study, the scale had a Cronbach’s α value of 0.660 and a KMO of 0.685, which was operationalized across three dimensions [48]: self-aging attitudes, social aging attitudes, and general aging attitudes.

Self-aging attitudes captured individuals’ perceptions of their own aging and aging-related losses. Social aging attitudes reflected perceived social exclusion or difficulty in forming social relationships due to age. Both dimensions were measured using negatively worded items that were reverse coded, with higher scores indicating more neutral or less negative attitudes.General aging attitudes assessed positive beliefs about aging, such as accumulated wisdom, problem-solving ability, and enjoyment in later life. Items were rated on a five-point scale, with higher scores indicating more positive general views of aging.

Covariates

All models controlled for age, sex, educational attainment, marital status, household size, household registration (rural vs. no-rural), chronic disease status, and employment status. Functional status was assessed using validated measures of basic activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), with lower scores indicating better functional independence.

Statistical analysis

Data management and descriptive analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) models were employed to examine the associations between digital inclusion, aging attitudes, and health outcomes while accounting for within-individual correlations over time. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to specify and test the pathways linking digital inclusion, aging attitudes, and overall health, including model fit evaluation and refinement. Cross-lagged panel models were further applied to assess longitudinal and reciprocal relationships among the key constructs. Additional analyses addressing endogeneity, heterogeneity, and robustness were conducted to ensure the stability of the findings. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided α level of 0.05.

GEE results of digital inclusion, aging attitude on health outcomes among older Chinese

Regarding RQ1, the results showed that more positive aging attitudes, particularly self-aging attitudes and social aging attitudes, were significantly associated with better health outcomes over time. The findings indicates that aging attitudes were as an important psychological factor related to later-life health. Regarding RQ2, digital inclusion showed a heterogeneous pattern. Digital proficiency and digital ability were positively associated with health outcomes, whereas digital access alone showed no significant association. These results indicate that different dimensions of digital inclusion were not equally related to health.

Relationships among aging attitudes, digital inclusion, and health

To further explore the temporal dynamics among the key constructs, cross-lagged panel models were estimated (Fig. 3). After accounting for the substantial autoregressive stability of aging attitudes, digital inclusion, and health, aging attitudes in 2020 were negatively associated with digital inclusion in 2023 (β = -0.07) and health in 2023 (β = -0.18).

At the same time, the results suggested reciprocal but non-symmetric relationships among the three constructs. Digital inclusion in 2020 was negatively associated with both health outcomes and aging attitude scores in 2023. Baseline health, in turn, negatively predicted digital inclusion but positively predicted aging attitude scores in 2023. These findings indicate that the longitudinal relationships among aging attitudes, digital inclusion, and health were dynamic rather than strictly unidirectional.

The measurement model also demonstrated strong factor loadings for all latent constructs at both time points, supporting the reliability of the observed indicators. Model fit indices suggested an acceptable overall fit to the data: CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.93, IFI = 0.95, GFI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.08.

Mediating role of digital inclusion in the association between aging attitudes and health

For RQ3, the SEM results presented in Fig. 4 showed that digital inclusion partly explained the association between aging attitudes and health. Aging attitudes were directly associated with health (β = 0.62) and were also positively associated with digital inclusion (β = 0.05). Digital inclusion was, in turn, positively related to health (β = 0.40). These pattern suggests that aging attitudes may be linked to health not only through direct psychological processes, but also through older adults’ engagement with digital resources.

Additional analyses

Several additional analyses were performed to support the endogeneity, heterogeneity and robustness of these findings. The results remained consistent after propensity score matching (Table 3), with both digital inclusion and aging attitudes showing significant associations with health outcomes. Stratified analyses (Fig. 5) suggested that the health-related role of digital inclusion varied more noticeably across subgroups, while the association between aging attitudes and health was comparatively stable.

Discussion

Principal results

This study examined how aging attitudes and digital inclusion are related to health among older adults in China. In the context of rapidly digitalizing after the COVID-19 pandemic, three main findings emerged. First, more positive attitudes toward aging were consistently associated with better subsequent health, although the cross-lagged results suggested a more complex pattern after baseline stability was taken into account. Second, the relationship between digital inclusion and health was not uniform. Digital proficiency and digital ability appeared to be more health-relevant than digital access alone, suggesting that simply being connected may not be sufficient. Third, digital inclusion partly explained the association between aging attitudes and health, indicating that older adults’ views of aging may shape health not only through psychological processes, but also through their engagement with digital resources. The cross-lagged results further showed that these relationships were not strictly one-directional; rather, they appeared to be dynamic, reciprocal, and uneven over time.

At the demographic level, some commonly examined characteristics, such as sex, age, lower educational attainment, and household registration, were not independently associated with health outcomes after functional status and psychosocial factors were considered. By contrast, marital disruption, larger household size, chronic disease, and continued employment were associated with poorer health outcomes. These findings are generally consistent with previous studies showing that marital relationships can paly a protective role, while chronic illness remians an important risk factor for poorer health in later life [49]. The association between continued employment and poorer health is also noteworthy. older adults who continued working may have faced greater exposure risks, economic pressure, or work-related stress, which may have outweighed the potential psychosocial benefits of employment during the COVID-19 period [50]. The positive associations of time and higher education with health suggest that socioeconomic resources, may continue to matter in later life, possibly by improving access to health information, supporting healthier behaviors, and strengthening older adults’ ability to cope with changing social conditions [51].

With respect to RQ1, aging attitudes were closely related to older adults’ health. More positive self-aging attitudes and social aging attitudes were associated with better health outcomes, while general aging attitudes showed no significant association. This difference suggests that health in later life is more strongly connected to how individuals perceive their own aging experiences and their position in social life, rather than to abstract beliefs about aging in general [52]. This finding is in line with theories that emphasize the importance of of self-referential aging beliefs. When older adults hold more positive views about their own aging, they may be better able to regulate emotions, cope with stress, maintain self-efficacy, and engage in health-related behaviors [6]. By contrast, general views about aging may be too distant from everyday personal experience to have a direct relationship with individual health outcomes [53].

Regarding RQ2, digital inclusion showed a more mixed pattern. Digital proficiency and digital ability were positively associated with health outcomes, whereas digital access alone showed no significant association. This distinction is important because it shows that simply having access to digital devices or the internet may not be enough to improve health. What appears to matter more is whether older adults can use digital tools confidently, effectively, and in ways that are relevant to their daily lives [54]. This result supports the view digital inclusion should be treated as a multidimensional construct, involving not only access but also skills, meaningful use, and active engagement. Without sufficient skills and confidence, digital access may not translate into real health benefits. In some cases, it may even create new burdens, such as information overload, digital frustration, or a stronger sense of exclusion [55].

This result also suggested that digital inclusion should not be interpreted as uniformly beneficial for older adults health. Its effect may depend on the conditions under which digital technologies are used and on whether older adults have enough skills, confidence, and support to use them effectively [56]. From a socio-technical perspectivce, the key issue is not simply whether older adults are connected to the internet or own digital devices, but whether the digital systems they encounter are usable, meaningful, and suited to their everyday needs and abilities [57]. This helps explain why digital access alone was not consistently associated with better health, while digital proficiency and digital ability showed more positive associations.

Technology may also shape older adults’ experiences in more subtle ways. As suggested by the theory of technological mediation, digital tools are not neutral channels for delivering services. They can influence how people understand their options, interact with others, and respond to everyday problems [58]. For some older adults, digital technologies may strengthen autonomy, support social participation, and improve access to health-related information and services. For others, especially those with limited skills or insufficient support, the same technologies may create frustration, information overload, anxiety, or a sense of inadequacy [59]. This helps explain why digital inclusion appeared to have both beneficial and burdensome aspects.

For RQ3, digital inclusion partly explained the link between aging attitudes and health. Older adults who hold more positive views of aging may be more open to learning digital technologies and more confident in adapting to new digital services [60]. Through this process, aging attitudes may influence health not only by shaping emotions, coping, and self-efficacy, but also by affecting how older adults engage with digital resources. At the same time, the longitudinal findings suggest that this process is not simple or one-directional.

The negative paths from baseline aging attitudes to later digital inclusion and health should be interpreted with caution. Because higher scores indicate more positive aging attitudes, these coefficients do not mean that positive aging attitudes are harmful. Rather, they indicate inverse prospective associations after prior levels and the strong autoregressive stability of aging attitudes, digital inclusion, and health were taken into account. One possible explanation is that older adults with more positive aging attitudes may already have had relatively favorable baseline health or adaptation resources, leaving less room for further improvement over time [61]. This may produce a ceiling effect or regression-to-the-mean pattern in the cross-lagged estimates. Another possibility is that the COVID-19 period weakened the extent to which positive psychological resources could be translated into later health gains, as older adults faced reduced social participation, service disruption, and greater reliance on digital systems [62].

Baseline health was negatively associated with later digital inclusion but positively associated with later aging attitudes. This may mean that some older adults turn to digital tools because declining health makes online information, services, or support more necessary. By contrast, better health may reinforce a more positive sense of aging [63]. Therefore, digital inclusion may carry different meanings across contexts: for some older adults, it reflects active participation and empowerment; for others, it may reflect a need to compensate for health limitations or reduced offline support.

Beyond the three main research questions, the cross-lagged analyses suggested that the relationships among aging attitudes, digital inclusion, and health may work in more than one direction. These cross-lagged coefficients capture relative changes after controlling for baseline levels, rather than simple overall correlations between the constructs. A particularly interesting finding was that digital inclusion at baseline was negatively associated with later health and aging attitudes. This is different from some pre-pandemic studies, which often found positive associations between internet use and health [64]. One possible reason is the special context of the COVID-19 period. During this time, many older adults used digital technologies not because they wanted to, but because daily services, health information, and social communication were increasingly moved online. For those with limited skills or support, this kind of digital engagement may have brought extra pressure. It may also have increased exposure to distressing information, misinformation, excessive screen time, or frustration with unfamiliar platforms [65]. In this sense, digital inclusion during the pandemic may have been both useful and burdensome. Its health effects likely depended on whether older adults had the skills, confidence, and support needed to use digital tools in a manageable way.

The results also show that aging attitudes may be linked to health through both psychological and social processes. On the one hand, more positive aging attitudes were directly associated with better health, which is consistent with theories of stress, coping, and self-regulation. On the other hand, digital inclusion partly explained this relationship. Older adults with more positive views of aging may be more willing to learn digital technologies, try online services, and use digital resources to support their daily life and health [35, 66]. This means that aging attitudes may matter not only because they shape emotions and coping, but also because they influence how older adults respond to a changing digital environment.

The additional analyses in subgroups add another layer to this interpretation. Digital inclusion appeared to be more beneficial for women, younger-old adults, rural residents, and those without chronic conditions. This may be because digital tools provide larger marginal benefits for groups who have fewer offline resources or more limited access to information and services [67]. In contrast, the relationship between aging attitudes and health was more stable across groups, although it was stronger among women and those without employment. These findings suggest that healthy aging interventions need to be more targeted. Improving digital access alone is unlikely to be enough. Programs should also strengthen digital skills, reduce digital frustration, and support more positive views of aging in ways that fit older adults’ different social and health contexts [68].

Overall, the findings support the psycho-social dual-pathway framework proposed in this study. Aging attitudes were linked to health both directly and indirectly through digital inclusion. Psychologically, more positive views of aging may help older adults manage stress, regulate emotions, maintain self-efficacy, and cope more effectively with later-life changes. Socially, digital inclusion may influence health by shaping access to information, services, social connections, and opportunities for participation. Positive aging attitudes may function as an important psychological resource, but their protective role may depend on external conditions, health needs, and opportunities for participation. Similarly, digital inclusion may support health when it reflects meaningful engagement, skills, and autonomy, but it may also reflect necessity-driven or compensatory use under constrained circumstances. Thus, healthy aging in the digital era cannot be explained by psychological attitudes or digital access alone. It requires attention to the interaction between older adults’ internal resources and the social, technological, and community environments that shape their daily lives.

Implications

The findings suggest that digital inclusion is not automatically beneficial for older adults. In the post-COVID context, digital technologies may improve access to information, services, and social support, but they may also create information overload, digital fatigue, frustration, or feelings of inadequacy [69]. This helps explain why digital inclusion showed both supportive and burdensome associations with health.

These results have two practical implications. First, healthy aging interventions should support more adaptive views of aging by strengthening older adults’ confidence, self-efficacy, and sense of social value. Positive aging education, peer support, and intergenerational activities may be useful components of community-based programs. Second, digital inclusion policies should move beyond internet access alone. More attention should be given to digital proficiency, user-friendly design, and ongoing support, so that digital services are meaningful, manageable, and responsive to older adults’ everyday needs.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although digital inclusion partly mediated the association between aging attitudes and health outcomes, the study did not fully capture the negative experiences of digital engagement. The measures mainly reflected access, proficiency, and digital activity, but not digital fatigue, frustration, exclusion, information overload, or perceived inadequacy. Therefore, the double-edged nature of digital inclusion may have been underestimated. Second, the 2020–2023 panel covered the pandemic and early post-pandemic period. Some associations may therefore reflect context-specific patterns of digital adaptation rather than stable long-term relationships. The heterogeneity analyses were also limited, as they focused mainly on sociodemographic factors and did not fully consider digital use contexts, specific health needs, or overlapping disadvantages such as socioeconomic vulnerability and functional limitations.

Future studies should use more detailed measures to distinguish empowering, routine, and necessity-driven digital engagement. They should also examine how family support, community infrastructure, service design, and age-friendly environments shape the health effects of digital inclusion. Longer follow-up studies are needed to determine whether the observed pathways persist beyond the post-COVID transition. This study also has strengths, including its nationally based longitudinal design, large sample, and use of complementary analytical approaches. These strengths support its contribution to understanding how aging attitudes and digital inclusion jointly shape healthy aging in a digital society.

Conclusion

This study examined how aging attitudes and digital inclusion are related to health among older adults in China. The findings suggest that more positive views of aging and meaningful digital inclusion are both relevant to healthy aging, but their roles are not simple or uniform. Aging attitudes were closely linked to health, while digital inclusion showed both supportive and potentially burdensome associations, depending on older adults’ digital proficiency, ability, and broader use contexts. Overall, the findings support a psycho-social view of healthy aging in the digital era. Interventions should not focus only on internet access. They should also strengthen positive aging attitudes, digital proficiency, and age-friendly environments that help older adults use digital resources with confidence and without unnecessary burden.
