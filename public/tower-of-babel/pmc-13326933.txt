A New Era in Treponema pallidum Cultivation: Current Advances and Future Directions
Authors: Kelly L. Hawley, Linda Grillová, Lorenzo Giacani, Steven J. Norris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although long-term in vitro cultivation of the syphilis agent, Treponema pallidum subspecies pallidum, was introduced less than a decade ago, it has revolutionized and accelerated research with this difficult pathogen. Moreover, it is allowing for the exploration of topics that could not easily be pursued when propagation in the rabbit model was the only possible method for the expansion of treponemes. This report, based on talks presented at the Shanghai International Syphilis Symposium 2025, provides an overview of how in vitro cultivation is currently being applied to syphilis research.

Progress of in vitro cultivation of T. pallidum presented at SISS 2025

In October 2025, global leaders in syphilis research, clinical practice, and public health policy gathered in Shanghai, China, for the invitation-only Shanghai International Syphilis Symposium (SISS). The summit aimed to catalyze urgent, collaborative action in an attempt to address critical gaps in diagnosis, treatment, and prevention against the alarming increase in the incidence of syphilis worldwide. On the third day of the symposium, a session focused on advances in the in vitro cultivation (IVC) of Treponema pallidum subsp. pallidum (T. pallidum), and is summarized in this commentary. While the primary focus of this article is a review on recent studies utilizing the T. pallidum IVC system, additional background information and references are also provided as part of Supplemental References, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B361.

Background and recent advances for IVC of T. pallidum

In 1905, Schaudinn and Hoffman identified a thin, pale, thread-like organism as the causative agent of syphilis and thereafter named the spirochete T. pallidum.1,2 IVC of T. pallidum immediately became a high-priority goal in syphilis research, with over 200 reports of success over the next 40 years (reviewed in3). These studies often utilized a combination of a serum-containing medium, tissue explants, and anaerobic conditions and indicated sustained survival and, in some cases, growth of the spirochete over periods of days, weeks, or even months. Unfortunately, these early experiments were either not reproducible or the result of contamination of the preparations with saprophytic skin spirochetes such as Treponema phagedenis. As a result, the study of T. pallidum has required propagation of the organism in experimentally infected laboratory animals, most commonly through intratesticular or intradermal inoculation of rabbits (1s,2s, See Supplemental Digital Content for all supplemental reference details, Supplemental References, https://links.lww.com/OLQ/B361). Historically, rabbits have been the preferred animal model because they recapitulate many features of primary human infection. Infection is typically established through intratesticular or intradermal inoculation, both of which enable robust and consistent propagation of the spirochetes and facilitate evaluation of T. pallidum infectivity and pathogenicity. Whereas intravenous inoculation is used to derive isogenic treponemal isolates from disseminated skin lesions. In the 1940’s, Dr. Robert Nelson introduced an extraction procedure and medium that supported the consistent retention of T. pallidum motility for 24 to 48 hours,3s providing the basis for the T. pallidum immobilization test, the first procedure for the detection of anti-T. pallidum antibodies. In parallel studies, Dr. Harry Eagle and colleagues4s defined the growth requirements of T. phagedenis biotype Reiter in the hopes that the determination of these conditions would aid in developing a similar growth system for T. pallidum. However, the physiologic differences between these organisms precluded the direct application of the resulting growth medium to T. pallidum culture. During the 1970s, the work of several groups revealed that the in vitro survival and metabolism of T. pallidum were enhanced by the presence of 1% to 5% O2 in the atmosphere and by co-culture with mammalian cells.5s–8s

A major breakthrough in the culture of T. pallidum occurred in 1981, when Fieldsteel, Cox, and Moeckli4 reported the consistent multiplication of T. pallidum in an IVC system for periods up to 2 to 3 weeks. The Fieldsteel et al.4 system consisted of a combination of a T. pallidum culture medium (TpCM-1) containing Eagle’s minimum essential medium (MEM) and 20% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, co-culture with Sf1Ep cottontail rabbit epithelial cells, and incubation at 34°C in an atmosphere with 1.5% O2 and 5% CO2. Under these conditions, T. pallidum multiplies with a characteristically long generation time of ~35 to 40 hours for a period of 7 to 10 days. Viability, as measured by motility or inoculation of rabbits, is maintained until the organisms enter the typical bacterial stationary and death phases. Survival can be extended to some extent by subculture or medium replacement, but proliferation does not occur under these conditions. This short-term multiplication was demonstrated in literally hundreds of experiments in the Fieldsteel, Cox, and Norris laboratories and served as the basis for many studies of the physiology and antimicrobial susceptibility of T. pallidum.6s,7s

In 2018, Edmondson et al.5 reported a modification of the Fieldsteel et al.4 system in which the Eagle’s MEM basal medium was replaced with a more complex medium, CMRL 1066. This change provided additional required nutrients and resulted in the consistent long-term multiplication of T. pallidum. In this system (Fig. 1A),6,7 co-incubation of T. pallidum with Sf1Ep cells in the modified medium (called TpCM-2) results in multiplication at the same rate as in the Fieldsteel et al.4 TpCM-1 medium (Fig. 1B); however, growth is maintained continuously in subsequent subcultures. Indeed, continuous cultures of T. pallidum Nichols have been maintained for up to 8 years under these conditions (Fig. 1C). In contrast, culture of T. pallidum in TpCM-2 under axenic conditions (in the absence of mammalian cells) results in little or no multiplication, indicating that the mammalian cells provide additional nutrients and/or activities (potentially including inactivation of reactive oxygen intermediates) not present in TpCM-2.5,8 All strains of T. pallidum subsp. pallidum (the causative agent of venereal syphilis) and T. pallidum subsp. endemicum (bejel or endemic syphilis) examined thus far have been cultured successfully in this system, although the rates of multiplication vary among different strains.5,8,9 To date, T. pallidum subsp. pertenue (causative agent of yaws in humans and naturally-occurring infections in nonhuman primates) has exhibited either very slow or no growth under these conditions (8; D. Šmajs, personal communication). Multiplication of strains of Treponema paraluiscuniculi (which causes sexually transmitted infections in rabbits and hares; also referred to as “Treponema paraluisleporidarum”) has not been established in the current IVC system.8 These results indicate that, despite an overall genomic nucleotide sequence identity of >99.2% among this closely related group, there are differences in the metabolic capabilities of the T. pallidum subspecies and T. paraluiscuniculi. It is reasonable to postulate that these differences are driven by a combination of factors, including regulatory variation and divergence at the transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic levels. Direct outcomes of IVC include the ability to clone T. pallidum strains in vitro,9s to isolate T. pallidum mutants and green fluorescent protein-expressing organisms,10,11,10s–12s and to prepare T. pallidum for ‘Omics’ studies of IVC T. pallidum13s–17s (Fig. 2).

Studies of metabolism and growth requirements

In the UTHealth Houston group, efforts have been focused primarily on a better understanding of the physiology and genetic aspects of T. pallidum, with emphasis on defining its growth requirements and developing improved or simplified IVC modalities. One of the major goals has been to further delineate the nutritional requirements of T. pallidum. Although it is known that the combination of TpCM-2 medium components and Sf1Ep cells fulfill the requirements for growth, it is not known what the specific requirements are. As an example, CMRL 1066 contains 23 compounds not present in the simpler Eagle’s MEM, so it is uncertain which of these components are needed to promote T. pallidum long-term multiplication. A three-pronged approach is being used to determine required components: (1) comparison of MEM and CMRL 1066 media components; (2) selective titration of CMRL 1066 components; and (3) metabolomic analysis of metabolites in the medium during culture. Of the 23 additional compounds present in CMRL 1066, only three (L-cysteine, nicotinic acid, and para-aminobenzoic acid) were found to be required by selective deletion. However, simple addition of these three compounds to Eagle’s MEM was not effective, indicating a more complex interplay is involved. Titration of L-cystine (which has a higher solubility than L-cysteine) revealed that addition of concentrations 15 times higher than in the standard CMRL 1066 medium actually promotes short-term multiplication of T. pallidum under axenic conditions; this limited growth under axenic conditions may provide a platform for further improvement of the medium, potentially leading to sustained axenic culture of T. pallidum. In collaborative studies with Dr. Ian Lewis’ group at the University of Calgary, comparison of metabolites in cultures of Sf1Ep cells alone, T. pallidum alone, Sf1Ep and T. pallidum cells together, and TpCM-2 medium alone led to the identification of several compounds, including α-keto acids, that appear to be secreted by Sf1Ep cells and consumed by T. pallidum. Preliminary experiments indicate that the addition of these compounds can enhance the proliferation of T. pallidum in the absence of mammalian cells. These studies are ongoing with the goal of identifying conditions that promote long-term axenic growth of T. pallidum.

Additional research being performed by the UTHealth Houston group is aimed at determining the current requirement for mammalian cells in the T. pallidum IVC system. Preliminary studies have shown that T. pallidum separated from the Sf1Ep cells by a semi-permeable membrane continues to grow, indicating that direct contact with mammalian cells is not required for the multiplication of T. pallidum. This result indicates that soluble, diffusible components produced by the mammalian cells are needed for the growth of the spirochete. As part of a collaboration with Drs. Indira Mysorekar and Eliza McColl at the Baylor College of Medicine, it was also found that three different human cytotrophoblast cell lines support the multiplication of T. pallidum at levels similar to that of Sf1Ep cell-containing control cultures (E. McColl et al., manuscript in preparation). These studies not only demonstrate that human cell lines can support T. pallidum growth, but also may facilitate the development of in vitro models of T. pallidum-placental interactions useful in understanding the pathobiology of congenital syphilis.

Testing of antibiotic susceptibility and resistance

Before the advent of the IVC system, several methods were used to assess T. pallidum susceptibility to antimicrobial agents. These approaches included evaluating spirochete motility after short in vitro exposure to antimicrobials,12,18s measuring incorporation of [35S]-methionine into bacterial proteins,13 and testing compounds in vivo in rabbits after experimental infection with T. pallidum.14,19s–21s In any case, all approaches were dependent on the availability of laboratory rabbits for T. pallidum propagation, as the above-mentioned in vitro assays also required freshly harvested, viable treponemes from the animal model. Until nearly a decade ago, in vivo testing in the rabbit model remained the approach of choice to investigate the causal association between point mutations in the T. pallidum 23S rRNA gene and azithromycin resistance.15

The evaluation of efficacy for several antibiotics became possible once steady propagation of T. pallidum was achieved,5 as evidenced by the rapid increase in research aimed at evaluating the spirochete’s susceptibility to a wide variety of antimicrobial classes.16–18,22s,23s Among other factors, the need to expand the spectrum of recommended drugs for treating syphilis is driven by recurrent shortages of penicillin G and by the potential benefits of alternative antibiotics that can be administered orally, achieve good central nervous system penetration, and do not require long-term adherence. Studies such as those led by Dr. Oriol Mitja (Fundació Lluita contra les Infeccions; Barcelona, Spain), one of the strongest promoters of drug repurposing for syphilis, for example, led linezolid to transition from preclinical to clinical evaluation for early syphilis treatment.17,22s The results of the second linezolid trial, reported at the SISS 2025 to be far more promising than the first trial,19 are eagerly awaited, as they could lead to the approval of a new agent for early syphilis.

Reliance on penicillin G and β-lactams for syphilis treatment is justified by their efficacy in clinical studies,20–22,24s-27s, their low minimal inhibitory concentration for T. pallidum23,24,28s, and by the lack of evidence that genetic resistance to this class of antibiotics never developed over 80 years of uninterrupted use25,29s. Investigators have hypothesized that point mutations in T. pallidum genes encoding penicillin-binding proteins could reduce the efficacy of these compounds or even confer resistance26,30s,31s. Recently, two laboratory-derived mutant T. pallidum strains carrying the A1873G mutation in the penicillin-binding protein-encoding tp0705 gene were reported to be less susceptible to ceftriaxone and penicillin G at low concentrations of these antibiotics,27 although with modest absolute effect sizes. At the SISS 2025, Dr. Giacani from the University of Washington reported testing the in vitro susceptibility to penicillin G and ceftriaxone of three T. pallidum clinical isolates (UW244B, UW249B, and UW330B), each carrying a distinct variant of the Tp0705 protein, including the variant studied by Pospíšilová et al.27 These three T. pallidum isolates, however, were all equally susceptible to penicillin G and ceftriaxone in our experiments.18

Doxycycline and tetracycline are effective for early and latent syphilis.28,32s,33s While there are no reliable reports of a tetracycline-resistant T. pallidum strain, concerns exist regarding the possible emergence of doxycycline resistance, as doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) is increasingly being adopted. Dr. Giacani also discussed that exposing in vitro T. pallidum intermittently to doxycycline for over 7 months and then continuously for 10 weeks did not alter the drug’s minimal inhibitory concentration for T. pallidum.23s

Interactions of TPA with human cells

The IVC system is not only valuable for propagating T. pallidum to generate material for downstream experiments, but it also provides an accessible model of early host-pathogen interactions at epithelial surfaces, where the bacterium first encounters the human host. To investigate T. pallidum interactions with human cell monolayers in real time, the team at the Wellcome Sanger Institute (WSI) identified suitable human cell lines to replace rabbit Sf1Ep cells and established the first real-time live imaging system for T. pallidum.29

To identify human alternatives to Sf1Ep cells, 6 well-characterized human epithelial or epithelial-like cell lines from diverse tissue origins (e.g., vulva, liver, placenta, and kidney) were selected. Hypoxia-tolerant cells with longer doubling times were prioritized to mirror key features of Sf1Ep cells. Among these, CAL-39 (vulva) and HepG2 (liver) supported T. pallidum survival, replication, characteristic growth dynamics, and long-term passaging at levels comparable to Sf1Ep. Growth was monitored daily over 10 consecutive days through detailed growth curves and further assessed across multiple weeks.

Importantly, the WSI team demonstrated that host cell division time is an important determinant of whether a cell line can sustain T. pallidum growth. This finding is consistent with previous observations that fast-growing human cell lines fail to support long-term treponemal survival.5s In addition, the UTHealth Houston group previously showed that higher passage numbers or increased Sf1Ep cell density reduce treponemal yield and motility, most likely due to nutrient competition, metabolite accumulation, and medium acidification.8 These observations open the possibility for other laboratories with access to slow-growing human cell lines to adopt this model, enabling more clinically relevant mechanistic studies and accelerating our understanding of T. pallidum-host interactions.

Using live imaging under physiologically relevant microaerophilic conditions, the team at WSI directly visualized T. pallidum interactions with host cells and described two distinct behavior mode during interaction with human and rabbit epithelia. First, T. pallidum exhibited highly dynamic, surface-associated motility characterized by close membrane contact and movement along the cell surface. This behavior resembles the “crawling” or “translocation via an undulating waveform” (Fig. 3) previously described for T. pallidum interactions with human platelets34s and for other pathogenic spirochetes, such as Leptospira and Borrelia.35s,36s Spirochetes displayed back-and-forth (reversal) movement using alternating poles and, in some cases, reversed direction using the same pole, likely due to transient self-entanglement. Second, a distinct subpopulation exhibited stable single-polar attachment to the host cell surface, with the opposite pole remaining free (Fig. 3).

The WSI team showed that during the first 72 hours of co-culture, T. pallidum could initially colonize all tested cell lines through both motility types, crawling-like and single-pole attachment, regardless of the cell line’s capacity to support long-term viability. This initial versatility aligns with clinical and historical evidence of systemic dissemination to diverse human tissues. However, in nonsupporting cell lines, crawling treponemes gradually disappeared, overall motility declined, and the remaining motile bacteria predominantly displayed single-polar attachment. In contrast, both motility types persisted throughout the full 10-day observation period in supporting cell lines.

The WSI team also observed that both T. pallidum subpopulations present in the IVC system, spirochetes attached to the mammalian monolayer and those freely floating in the medium, remained viable under standard culture conditions. Re-seeding experiments demonstrated that bacteria collected exclusively from the culture medium reattached to the surface of fresh monolayers and completed a full growth cycle. Notably, supporting cell lines showed significantly different proportions of adherent versus planktonic bacteria compared to nonsupporting cell lines. These distinct in vitro growth dynamics suggest that T. pallidum engages in specific molecular interactions with human cells that permit sustained growth. In contrast to nonsupporting systems, human monolayers capable of long-term culture provide a more biologically relevant model for studying T. pallidum–host interactions than short-term exposure systems.

Although cell-based systems cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of infection in vivo, replacing rabbit Sf1Ep cells with human cells increases clinical relevance and enables mechanistic studies of T. pallidum–human interactions central to syphilis pathogenesis, particularly through long-term culture approaches that may reveal previously unexplored stages of the bacterial growth cycle. In the future, it will be important to define the molecular determinants of this motility, evaluate the contribution of specific host factors to attachment dynamics and movement patterns, and verify these observations in more complex models such as organoids and explants.

New insights into functional immunity against T. pallidum

Lack of a long-term IVC system for T. pallidum has stunted the ability of researchers to explore the mechanistic properties of anti-treponemal antibodies. The rabbit model of experimental syphilis served to dissect immune responses against the invading syphilis spirochete, with seminal studies conducted by Dr. Sheila Lukehart and colleagues demonstrating the critical role of opsonic antibodies in macrophage-mediated clearance of T. pallidum.37s Consequently, opsonophagocytosis has served as the surrogate marker of protection to guide identification of surface-exposed antigens capable of mediating protective immunity. Building on evidence that extracellular loops (ECLs) of β-barrel forming outer membrane proteins (OMPs) represent a key “Achilles heel” of the spirochete, the UConn Health team leveraged their knowledge of the molecular architecture of T. pallidum’s outer membrane and its’ repertoire of OMPs (OMPeome)38s as a framework for vaccine candidate identification and characterization of the host’s humoral response.39s They used a Pyrococcus furiosus thioredoxin scaffold system to catalog the spirochetes immunodominant ECLs. Additionally, the scaffold has enabled native-like presentation of individual loops for the detection of both linear and discontinuous epitopes. Using this platform, the group identified several highly antigenic ECLs, including BamA ECL4 and multiple loops from TP0856 and TP0858, to which they generated high-quality antisera.

Historically, the evaluation of syphilitic sera and antisera has relied heavily on labor-intensive, manual assessment of opsonophagocytosis of the spirochete to determine the presence of “functional” antibodies, underscoring the urgent need for high-throughput approaches to systematically interrogate surface-directed antibody responses. The laboratories at UConn Health recently developed new assays, other than opsonophagocytosis, for assessment of functional activity of ECL-specific antibodies. A major technological advance resulting from IVC T. pallidum was the development of green fluorescent protein-expressing spirochetes10,11 enabling direct visualization and quantitative analysis of antibody effects on the bacteria.11 Although the spirochete’s morphology poses challenges for the use of flow-based studies, when the fluorescent spirochete was combined with propidium iodide stain, it became possible to assess membrane disruption at the single-organism level. This advancement has enabled direct evaluation of the bactericidal activity of anti-ECL antibodies against T. pallidum. The group demonstrated that select antibodies targeting BamA ECL4, as well as ECLs from multiple FadL OMPs can disrupt outer membrane integrity, independent of complement, and in some cases mediate direct killing.11,27

Recent genomic sequencing of globally circulating T. pallidum strains demonstrated that the majority of amino acid variants were found concentrated within the ECLs of T. pallidum OMPs,40s,41s similar to immune evasion strategies employed by other bacterial pathogens. Understanding OMP diversity across diverse geographies is an essential component of a syphilis vaccine design strategy since sequence variants can interfere with cross-strain antibody binding. Advances in IVC now allow for direct testing of heterologous sera against T. pallidum strains with defined OMPeomes. Data presented at SISS 2025 showed that heterologous immune rabbit serum exerts more limited effects on membrane integrity compared with strain-matched responses, highlighting the importance of antigenic matching and epitope variation. Integrating IVC along with structural epitope mapping, functional immunologic assays, and genomic epidemiology establishes a powerful experimental framework for advancing syphilis research. By enabling direct linkage of antigenic variation to functional antibody responses in live organisms, these approaches move the field closer to defining correlates of protection and support the rational design of a syphilis vaccine with global efficacy.

Future steps and concluding remarks

At the time of this writing, the T. pallidum IVC system has been successfully implemented by at least 11 research laboratories and applied across a wide range of experimental approaches (Fig. 2), underscoring its growing value for addressing fundamental questions in syphilis research. The breadth of applications already demonstrates its pivotal role in advancing the field, including enabling the identification of novel therapeutic options, assessing how single-point mutations influence antibiotic susceptibility, and supporting efforts to repurpose existing drugs or discover new antimicrobial compounds. Looking ahead, continued advances in IVC will be central to resolving key unanswered questions in T. pallidum biology. The ability to isolate and propagate clinical strains now allows direct experimental testing of hypotheses generated through genomic and proteomic analyses, such as defining how specific OMP alleles shape immune recognition and pathogen fitness. Coupled with expanded genomic sequencing to refine variant catalogs, IVC provides a powerful platform to identify epitopes responsible for functional antibody activity and to determine how genomic diversity drives bacterial proliferation and immune evasion.
