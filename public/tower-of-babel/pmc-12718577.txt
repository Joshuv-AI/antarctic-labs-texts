Figure rating scales used in sub-Saharan africa for body image assessment: a scoping review
Authors: Jamaiica S Hurston, Eliza A Huefner, Grace G Smith, Simon Pierre Bigirimana, Anne E Sumner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesBody image is integral to culture and influences health behaviour. In sub-Saharan African countries, body image preference is in flux. To facilitate research on attitudes about body size in Africans, our goal was to provide a central resource of the figure rating scales which have previously been used in African countries.DesignA scoping review was conducted.Data sourcesFive databases were accessed: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Global Health Database and African Index Medicus.Eligibility criteria(1) Study conducted in a sub-Saharan African country; (2) used a figure rating scale to assess body image; (3) enrolled adults; (4) published in English between 1983 and 12 April 2024.Data extraction and synthesis842 articles were flagged, and 63 articles identified as appropriate for inclusion.ResultsThese 63 studies were conducted in 14 African countries and used 23 different figure rating scales. These 23 scales were developed in: USA or Australia: 39% (9/23); Europe: 35% (8/23); Africa: 26% (6/23). A description of each scale is provided including: (a) number of images, (b) sex (ie, women only, men only or both women and men), (c) silhouette orientation (ie, frontal only or frontal and lateral views), (d) population used to develop the scale and (e) the African country which used each scale.ConclusionsUsing this review, investigators can optimise their choice of an existing figure rating scale or use the information as background to build a new one.Keywords: Epidemiology, Public Health, Scoping Review, Sociodemographic Factors

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

To address the weight-related epidemic, which is occurring in sub-Saharan Africa, it is essential to understand how attitudes towards body size are evolving in African countries. Figure rating scales are effective, easy to administer, inexpensive tools which provide key information on cultural attitudes towards body size. It appears that in the 21st century, attitudes towards body size preference in African populations are evolving. However, no resource exists which lists the figure rating scales which have been used in Africa.

With this review, we report that between 1983 and 2024, 23 different body image scales were used in 63 investigations conducted in 14 sub-Saharan African countries. We provide a comprehensive survey of these scales including: (a) number of images, (b) gender (ie, women only, men only or both women and men) and (c) silhouette orientation (ie, frontal or frontal and lateral views) (d) population used to develop the scale and (e) the specific African countries which have used each scale.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

With this comprehensive review, investigators can optimise their choice of an existing figure rating scale as well as have a foundation for the design of new scales which are sensitive to the specific body image concerns and cultural contexts of the African population they are serving. This review provides key information which could facilitate the design of programmes which champion in Africa the value of a healthy weight.

Worldwide, body size preference is influenced by culture and affects health behaviour.1,3 To design health programmes which promote a healthy weight, it is essential to understand sociocultural attitudes towards body size.1,3 However, in sub-Saharan African countries, there is a lack of data on body size preferences. This is important as countries across sub-Saharan Africa are experiencing an increase in non-communicable diseases, especially the ones triggered by rapid weight gain such as diabetes, heart disease and cancer.4

Across sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence of individuals with a body mass index which would be categorised as overweight or obese is rapidly increasing.4 This weight gain can be linked to increased consumption of soft drinks and fast foods, sedentary work and traffic congestion which leads to prolonged sitting on a daily basis in cars or buses.5 Paradoxically, there is also the challenge at the other end of the weight spectrum. With globalisation, young adult influencers and movie stars are glorifying underweight body sizes.6,14

In addition, traditional views on optimal body size and image must be considered. Even with heterogeneity across the continent, the prevailing consensus is that, historically, Africans have viewed larger body sizes in women as a sign of health and fertility and in men as wealth and muscularity.7 9 12 13 In contrast, smaller body sizes have been seen as a sign of poverty, malnutrition and ill health.7 9 12 13 Due to the complex interplay of so many internal and external factors, achieving an understanding of body size preferences in countries across sub-Saharan Africa is a challenge.

In 1983, Stunkard et al pioneered the use of figure rating scales as a method for assessing body image and body size preferences.1 15 Figure rating scales are currently defined as ‘psychometric instruments developed to measure individuals’ perception of physical appearance and subsequently, to determine the level of body dissatisfaction’.1 Figure rating scales consist of a series of drawings of progressively larger body sizes. These drawings are called silhouettes. Individuals are asked to view the silhouettes in the figure rating scale and choose the one which best represents their wish body size and the one which represents their current body size. By viewing the difference between wish and current body size, body size satisfaction can be determined. Since the publication of the first figure rating scale by Stunkard et al, scores of figure rating scales have been published.

To assess body size preferences in populations living in sub-Saharan Africa, a figure rating scale must be chosen. However, no resource exists which describes the figure rating scales which have been used in studies conducted in sub-Saharan African countries. This review provides a survey of the figure rating scales used in sub-Saharan Africa including: (a) number of images, (b) sex (ie, women only, men only or both women and men) and (c) silhouette orientation (ie, frontal view only or frontal and lateral views), (d) population used to develop the scale and (e) the specific African country which has used each scale.

Methods

Search strategy

Using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension of Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR), the NIH librarian accessed five databases to identify studies which were conducted in a sub-Saharan African country and made use of figure rating scales in their research focus. The databases were: PubMed (US National Library of Medicine), Web of Science (Clarivate Analytics), Embase (Elsevier), Global Health Database (CABI Digital Health) and African Index Medicus. Inclusion criteria were: (1) study conducted in a sub-Saharan African country; (2) used a figure rating scale to assess body image; (3) enrolled adults; (4) published in English between 1983 and 12 April 2024. Exclusion criteria were: reviews, dissertations, preprints and conference proceedings. Online supplemental appendix A is the search strategy used to identify the articles. Online supplemental appendix B is the PRISMA-ScR checklist.

Article identification and data extraction

Covidence (https://www.covidence.org/, Melbourne, Australia), an online search tool, was used to identify titles/abstracts and full texts. The tool automatically removed duplicates. Of the 842 articles identified by the search, 751 articles were deemed irrelevant (figure 1). This was determined by JSH and EAH working independently with titles and abstracts. The remaining 91 articles underwent full text review by JSH and EAH. 28 articles were excluded because they were conducted in children only (n=11), were not conducted in an African country (n=10) or did not use a figure rating scale (n=7). The figure rating scales in the remaining 63 articles were included in this review.

Due to the objective nature of the search, there were no disagreements between JSH and EAH for both the review of the 751 articles excluded because they were irrelevant or the 28 articles which did not meet the inclusion criteria. The study was either conducted in an African country, in adults, and used a figure rating scale, or not.

Patient and public involvement

As this was a literature review, there was no direct patient involvement. However, our inspiration for this scoping review was a direct result of our search to find figure rating scales appropriate for use in Africans living in the USA.16 Furthermore, African investigators, including our coauthor SPB, have been directly involved in all aspects of our research designed to understand body image preferences based on existing figure rating scales.

Scale development by global region and country of development

The scales were developed in three different regions of the world: Europe 35% (8/23)715 22,27 ; USA or Australia 39% (9/23)28,36 or sub-Saharan African country 26% (6/23)1920 37,40 (table 1, columns 1 and 2).

Development population

White population from both Europe and the USA was the design population in 57% (13/23) of the scales and Africans in 26% (6/23). The development population was a mixed cohort of white population and African Americans in 9% (2/23).29 30 In addition, one scale was developed for African Americans only (4% (1/23)) and one scale was developed for Mexican Americans only (4% (1/23)).28 34 For scales in which the population of scale development was not stated, the main population of the country where the research was done was listed35 36 39 40 (table 1, column 3).

For the six scales developed in Africa, 50% (3/6) were developed in Cameroon or Cameroon and Senegal.19 20 38 The remaining three were developed in three different countries, specifically Ghana, Nigeria and Kenya.37 39 40 The Stunkard Figure Rating Scale, which was developed in Denmark, was the commonly used scale. In fact, the Stunkard Figure Rating Scale, or a modified version of the Stunkard scale, was used in 49% (32/65) of the studies.28 9 12 13 40,55 The remaining 51% of the studies used scales of three different origins. Scales developed in African Americans were used in 17% of the studies (11/65).1314 18 21 30 56,61 Scales developed in Europe, USA or Australia but did not include Africans or African Americans were used in 17% (11/65) of the studies.711 17 62,69 Similarly, scales developed in African countries were also used in 17% (11/65) of the studies.1719 20 37,39 70

Number of silhouettes and distribution by sex

Women-only were the focus of 52% (12/23) of the scales (table 1, column 5). Men-only were the focus of 4% (1/23). Silhouettes for both women and men were provided in 44% (10/23) of scales.

By sex, the number of silhouettes per scale was 6–21 (table 1, column 6). The median number of silhouettes per scale was nine. Of note, in the original Stunkard Figure Rating Scale, there are nine silhouettes each for women and nine silhouettes for men.

For the scales developed in Africa, the median number of silhouettes was 8 (table 1, column 6).

Discussion

This review is the first survey of figure rating scales used in sub-Saharan African countries (table 2). With this information, investigators working on body image in African populations can: (a) identify the best currently available scale to answer their research question or (b) develop a new scale.

Stunkard Figure Rating Scale developed in Denmark

The Stunkard Figure Rating Scale brought global recognition to the value of the field of body image assessment.15 The high frequency with which the Stunkard Figure Rating Scale or a modified version has been used in body image assessments in African countries may be due to: (1) the wide availability of the scale; (2) the nondescript shape of the silhouettes, making them generally applicable to most populations and (3) the manageable number of silhouettes which range from nine in the original scale to seven or eight in the modified versions.22 24 Moreover, the scale lends itself to easy adaption. In a study of Malawi women, the investigators had the figures redrawn and hair, attire and skin colour changed to be culturally appropriate.23 81 82

Scales developed for African Americans

Figure rating scales which were developed for African Americans were used in 17% (11/65) of the studies. All three of these figure rating scales created a single scale with the idea that the scales would be well suited for use in either African Americans or Whites.28,30 These three scales, which were published between 2000 and 2022, have been used in 11 studies conducted in African countries.1719 20 37,39 70 These scales are: the Body Image Assessment for Obesity (BIA-O), the modified BIA-O and the Pulvers Figure Rating Scale.28,30 The first scale,29 BIA-O, was published in 2000 and was based on a cohort of 1209 men and women which was ~50% white and ~50% African American.29 There are 18 silhouettes for women and 18 silhouettes for men. The silhouettes were designed to represent a body size range from ‘very thin’ to ‘obese’. The methodology used to derive these silhouettes is only minimally described. However, the authors do state that the scale was based on silhouettes originally drawn to understand body image issues in women with eating disorders who did not have obesity. This original scale was known as BIA. For BIA-O, the authors state, without elaboration, that they expanded the original BIA scale to include men and women across the full BMI spectrum. After designing the BIA-O, the authors report that they validated the BIA-O with test-retest reliability measures in African Americans and white population.

Interestingly, in a departure from the other 22 scales, the BIA-O silhouettes are presented at a 45-degree angle rather than with full frontal images (table 1, column 7). The authors do not discuss why this was done. It may have been an attempt to incorporate body fat distribution into the discussion of overall body size. However, the BIA-O scale may be difficult to use. Most scales have 8–10 silhouettes to choose between. As the BIA-O presents 18 silhouettes, the participant may have difficulty making a final choice.

The second scale is a shortened version of the original BIA-O. Geczik et al, in their study of body image relative to breast health in Ghanaian women, compressed the 18 silhouettes of the BIA-O into a series of nine with identical features and orientation across the BMI spectrum as the original BIA-O.30

The third scale is the Pulvers Figuring Rating Scale.28 This scale has nine drawings of frontal drawings of women and nine drawings of frontal figures for men. These silhouettes were drawn by an artist with instructions and feedback from physicians, clinical psychologists and epidemiologists to: (1) cover the BMI spectrum from 16 kg/m2 to 40 kg/m2 and (2) provide hair and facial features which were ‘human-like”’ but not population-specific. Acceptance of the scale by African American study participants was judged to be high. In addition, inter-rater reliability, validity and correlation with the scale was high.

Scales developed in sub-Saharan African countries

Figure rating scale development in African populations is an emerging field. The six scales which we report as originating in African countries were published between 2007 and 2020.1920 37,40 Five out of the six scales demonstrate cultural sensitivity in choice of hairstyle, skin colour and clothing.

The most well-developed and validated scale is the Body Shape Scale (BOSHAS).20 It was published in 2020 and based on photographs of representative Cameroonian and Senegalese women and men. There are 10 silhouettes for women and 10 for men. The silhouettes take into account three factors: overall body size based on BMI, body fat distribution accounted for by waist-to-hip ratio and body shape. The BOSHAS was built based on experience with two earlier scales, also used in studies conducted in Africa and created by the same investigators.19 38 Those scales were: the Body Image Scale and the Body Size Scale published in 2011 and 2015, respectively. The Body Image Scale was based on photographs of Cameroonian women and men and had, by sex, six frontal silhouettes. The Body Size Scale was published 4 years later and based on photographs of both Cameroonians and Senegalese and included nine pairs of silhouettes by sex. The word ‘pair’ is used because in the Body Size Scale, each choice included both frontal and lateral silhouettes.

Published in 2007, the fourth figure rating scale we identified as developed in Africa is the: ‘Figural Stimuli of Ghanaian Women’.37 The 12 images of Ghanaian women were generated by computerised body morph assessment tools to specifically represent women who the authors describe as ranging in size from ‘very thin’ to ‘morbidly obese’. The silhouettes have hair and skin colour characteristic of Ghanaian women, and they are wearing tunics that extend from shoulders to the upper thigh.

The fifth and sixth scales are problematic. The fifth scale was developed in Kenyan men and applied to body size satisfaction in Ethiopian men relative to the fit of readymade garments.40 The sixth scale is from a study conducted in rural Nigeria by Akinpelu et al.39 However, this particular figure rating scale has more explicit depictions of anatomy than any other scale in this review and we believe it may not be acceptable for use in conservative societies.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this review is that it is the first to provide a comprehensive overview of the figure rating scales previously used in sub-Saharan Africa. To maximise the identification of investigations conducted in Africa, our literature search included both the Global Health Database and African Index Medicus, as well as PubMed, Web of Science and Embase (online supplemental appendix A). As a consequence, this survey identified figure rating scales previously used across all four African regions, West, Central, East and South (table 2). In addition, even though we only reviewed studies published in English, our survey included studies conducted in both French and English-speaking countries. Furthermore, the investigator using this review can easily determine the population group in which the scale was developed (table 1) and the African countries in which each specific scale has been used (table 2).

However, in the field of body image research, major limitations exist. First, the majority of figure rating scales have not undergone reliability testing or validity assessments.1 Of the 23 scales cited in this review, only five have been validated in African populations. These five scales are: the Stunkard Figure Rating Scale, BOSHAS, Body Size Scale, BOSHAS, Pulvers Figure Rating Scale and the Photographic Figure Rating Scale.19 20 22 27 58 Therefore, when future research is done with existing or new scales, we recommend validation measures be incorporated and reported.

Second, even though 30% of the sub-Saharan African population are Muslim, none of the 63 investigations of body image discussed whether women from conservative societies had discomfort in discussing their body size.83 As new scales are developed and protocols designed, participants should be queried about their degree of comfort in expressing their opinions about body size. If discomfort is uncovered, new research into how questions are framed and who in the community should make the inquiries could be undertaken.

A specific limitation of our study is that we did not review the medical literature published in French. It is possible that if we had, we may have identified additional figure rating scales used in Africa.

Conclusions

Figure rating scales are integral to achieving an understanding of body image preference.1 As there is no resource available to describe the figure rating scales previously used in Africa, this review helps to close that gap. Investigators can review the description of each of the 23 figure rating scales previously used in African countries to choose the scale which is the most appropriate for the population with which they are working. In addition, our review provides a foundation for the design of new figure rating scales which are sensitive to the specific body image concerns of African populations. To fill gaps, we recommend body image studies in the future be tested across different socioeconomic environments and include a validation component and an assessment of the level of comfort with the questions and quality and number of silhouettes. As the obesity epidemic surges across Africa,4 knowledge about body image preference can help in the development of programmes which are effective in promoting positive attitudes towards a healthy weight.
