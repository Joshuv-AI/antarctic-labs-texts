The danger of flipping an outside lipid to the inside
Authors: Todd R. Graham
Published: 2024

Cells fine-tune the composition and organization of the plasma membrane to ensure its functional integrity. A complexity of membrane biogenesis is that the lipid composition of the plasma membrane extracellular leaflet differs substantially from the cytosolic leaflet (1, 2). For most mammalian cell types that have been characterized, the extracellular leaflet is rich in sphingomyelin (SM), glycosphingolipids, and phosphatidylcholine (PtdCho), whereas phosphatidylserine (PtdSer) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PtdEth) are primarily found in the cytosolic leaflet. One mechanism for establishing this transverse membrane asymmetry is mediated by P4-adenosine triphosphatases (ATPases) or phospholipid flippases, which actively remove PtdSer and PtdEth from the extracellular leaflet by flipping them to the cytosolic leaflet. Thus, P4-ATPase substrates like PtdSer become “inside” lipids and nonsubstrate lipids like PtdCho primarily remain “outside” lipids. What would be the consequence of disrupting this delicate balance by bringing an outside lipid to the inside? In PNAS, Calienese and colleagues demonstrate that de novo mutations in the ATP11A flippase causing neurological disease do not alter its normal PtdSer and PtdEth transport activity; instead, the mutant flippase aberrantly gains the ability to flip PtdCho (3). Further, these ATP11A mutations lie in a position that, at first glance, seems to be on the wrong side of the membrane to influence substrate specificity (1–3). This study emphasizes the danger of bringing an outside lipid (PtdCho) inside and provides critical insights into P4-ATPase substrate recognition, transport mechanisms, and membrane homeostasis (3).

Much has been learned in recent years about P4-ATPase structure and function. The P4-ATPases are part of the P-type ATPase superfamily, which includes the well-characterized Na+/K+ ATPase and Ca++ ATPases. Like these cation pumps, the P4-ATPases have a membrane (M) domain with 10 transmembrane (TM1–TM10) segments and a cytosolic ATPase domain composed of nucleotide binding (N), phosphorylation (P), and actuator (A) subdomains (Fig. 1). Most P4-ATPases (α-subunits) are associated with a noncatalytic β-subunit in the CDC50 protein family. ATP11A forms a heterodimer with CDC50A, which has two TM segments, a glycosylated extracellular domain that caps the α-subunit M domain and cytosolic N and C-terminal tails associated with the ATPase domain. The catalytic cycle starts with ATP-binding to the N-domain (E1-ATP), transfer of the γ-phosphate to an invariant aspartic acid in the P domain (E1-P•ADP), release of ADP and transition to a transport substrate binding conformation (E1-P to E2-P•lipid), and subsequent dephosphorylation by the A domain (E2-P•lipid to E2 to E1) associated with lipid flip and release (4). Experimentally determined structures in multiple conformational states are available for several P4-ATPases, including the closely related ATP11C and other PtdSer/PtdEth flippases such as ATP8A1 and its yeast ortholog Drs2 (5–7). In addition, structures have been determined for naturally occurring PtdCho flippases (Dnf1 and Dnf2) from budding yeast (8, 9). Plasma membrane organization differs in yeast, with PtdCh, PtdSer, and PtdEth present in the inner leaflet and glycosphingolipid in the extracellular leaflet (2, 10). Many of these P4-ATPase structures are in an E2P state with bound substrate lipids, and mutagenesis studies have defined residues critical for substrate selection (6–8, 11, 12).

These studies have helped define the lipid substrate translocation pathway, which begins with substrate loading from the extracellular leaflet into a binding pocket called the entry site, formed by TM1, TM2, TM4, and TM6 residues (Fig. 1). The entry site begins a vertical groove along one side of the M domain, extending to an exit site near the cytosolic leaflet. However, this mostly polar groove is interrupted by a hydrophobic gate in the middle of the M domain. It is unclear how the lipid headgroup traverses past the hydrophobic gate as it flips and whether it docks in the exit site before release to the cytosolic membrane. Structures of yeast P4-ATPases Dnf1 and Dnf2 have PtdCho in both the entry site and the exit site bounded by TM2, TM4, a loop in the P-domain, and the N terminus of the β-subunit (8). Mutations in this exit site alter the substrate specificity, which supports the idea that the lipids observed there are substrates in transit to the cytosolic leaflet. However, there is a paucity of evidence beyond the yeast PtdCho flippases for substrate docking at the exit site or the functional relevance of this particular protein–lipid interaction (although see refs. 11 and 13). It seems that selecting the substrate lipid at the start of the journey at the entry gate would suffice. Why is there a second site of selection near the destination? How does the “wrong” lipid get past the first checkpoint to be screened again at the exit site? Are the two sites coupled so that mutations in the exit site alter lipid interaction with the entry site?

Part of the significance of the Calianese et al. PNAS paper is that the disease-causing ATP11A mutations found in the patients (S399L and E114G) are in the exit site and are gain-of-function mutations (3). S399L is in the P-domain loop that connects TM4 to a β strand containing the phosphorylated Asp residue, and E114G is in TM2 but is closely positioned to S399 (Fig. 1). The Nagata group had previously described an ATP11A mutation (Q84E) linked to severe neurological deterioration that caused the same gain-of-function transport of PtdCho (14). Q84 is an entry site residue known to play a crucial role in substrate recognition (6, 7, 12, 15). The observation that these three ATP11A mutants had very little influence on PtdSer or PtdEth transport indicated that the mutants were folded properly, could undergo the catalytic cycle with wild-type kinetics, and were correctly localized. Importantly, none of these ATP11A mutants transported SM, which has the same phosphocholine headgroup as PtdCho but with a sphingosine backbone (3). Thus, the ATP11A mutants retain specificity for glycerophospholipid and the entry and exit site mutations have very similar effects on PtdCho transport kinetics and neurological health of the patients.

How do the exit site mutations increase PtdCho transport when the entry site is still tuned for PtdSer transport? These substrate-defining exit site residues are very well conserved in other PtdSer flippases. In fact, introducing the same mutations in ATP11C or ATP8A2 allowed PtdCho transport without significantly perturbing PtdSer transport (3). The authors then used molecular dynamic simulations with the high-resolution structures of ATP11C to probe the molecular basis of the gain-of-function PtdCho transport. Even though none of the PtdSer flippase structures have substrate in the exit site, PtdSer could be modeled in the wild-type ATP11C exit site with a fairly small expansion relative to its dimensions when PtdSer is in the entry site. In other words, the movement of PtdSer from entry to exit does not require significant conformational changes, at least at the beginning and end of this process (the actual movement is not simulated). PtdCho can also be modeled in the entry and exit sites of WT ATP11A. However, a greater expansion of the exit site is needed to accommodate PtdCho. For ATP11C, the TM2 exit gate mutant (E109G) expanded the exit site when PtdCho was in the entry site, and no further expansion was needed to model this lipid in the exit site. In addition, the probability of electrostatic interactions between wild-type exit site side chains and the lipid headgroup was much higher for PtdSer relative to PtdCho. The exit site mutants increased these electrostatic interactions with PtdCho. Thus, it appears that subtle changes to the architecture of the exit site and improved binding to PtdCho are sufficient to permit its transport.

What are the cellular consequences of flipping an outside lipid to the inside? As expected, the extracellular leaflet exposes much less PtdCho, and cells respond by upregulating SM synthesis (Fig. 1) (3). Unlike PtdCho, which is primarily synthesized at the ER, where an undefined scramblase allows rapid equilibration of lipids between the two leaflets, SM is synthesized in the lumenal leaflet of the Golgi. Vesicular transport then moves the SM to the extracellular leaflet of the plasma membrane (16). In addition, SM synthase consumes PtdCho by transferring the phosphocholine headgroup to ceramide to produce SM and diacylglycerol. The upregulation of SM synthesis compensates for losing the outer leaflet PtdCho by replacing it with SM and perhaps protects cells by reducing PtdCho availability for ATP11A-dependent transport. The ATP11A mutants initially transport PtdCho to the cytosolic leaflet, but it is unclear whether the PtdCho stays in this membrane leaflet (question mark in Fig. 1). Phospholipids in the cytosolic leaflet can exchange rapidly by nonvesicular transport pathways to the endoplasmic reticulum and other organelles (17). It is much more challenging to measure the influence of aberrant PtdCho transport on the composition of the cytosolic leaflet and this remains an open question. These perturbations alter the properties of the plasma membrane, but how this perturbation leads to neuronal loss in the patients is unclear. Given the subtle nature of the effect of the mutations on ATP11A structure, it seems plausible that small molecule modulators could be identified that suppress PtdCho transport of the mutants and provide therapeutic value for the patients.
