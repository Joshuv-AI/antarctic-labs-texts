Convective Viscous Cahn-Hilliard/Allen-Cahn Equation with memory effects
P. O. Mchedlov-Petrosyan, L. N. Davydov

Abstract
The combination of the well-known Cahn-Hilliard and Allen-Cahn equations is used to describe surface processes, such as simultaneous adsorption/desorption and surface diffusion. In the present paper we have considered the convective-viscous Cahn-Hilliard/Allen-Cahn equation complemented by memory effects. Exact solutions are obtained and the combined action of the applied field, dissipation and memory are discussed.

I
Introduction

The present work is devoted to the study of the equation for the order parameter
\bar{w}
:

\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}-2\bar{\alpha}\bar{w}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial x^{\prime}}+\tau^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}+M_{2}\bar{\mu}\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left[M_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left(\bar{\mu}+\bar{\eta}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}\right)\right]-M_{2}\bar{\mu}.

(1.1)

This is a one-dimensional version of the Convective-Viscous Cahn-Hilliard/Allen-Cahn equation (CVCH/AC), modified by memory effects in diffusion. The combination of the well known Cahn-Hilliard (CH) and Allen-Cahn (AC) models was introduced in
1
to describe surface processes, such as simultaneous adsorption/desorption and surface diffusion.
Here
\bar{\mu}
is the chemical potential,
\bar{\alpha}
is proportional to the applied field,
\bar{\eta}
is the viscosity, and
\tau^{\prime}
is the characteristic memory time.
M_{1}
is the mobility and
M_{2}
kinetic coefficient. The chemical potential
\bar{\mu}
is a variational derivative of the double-well thermodynamic potential
F\left[\bar{w}\right]
, which contains
\left(\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial x^{\prime}}\right)^{2}
term and a fourth-power polynomial:

\bar{\mu}=-\bar{\varepsilon}\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{w}}{\partial x^{\prime 2}}+\bar{\rho}\left(\bar{w}-\bar{a}_{1}\right)\left(\bar{w}-\bar{a}_{2}\right)\left(\bar{w}-\bar{a}_{3}\right).

(1.2)

The roots
\bar{a}_{1}<\bar{a}_{2}<\bar{a}_{3}
of the polynomial correspond to the positions of two minima and a single intermediate maximum of the fourth-power polynomial in the thermodynamic potential;
\bar{\varepsilon}
is usually presumed to be of the order of the capillary length squared.

As it was pointed out by Thiele
2
, the combination of CH and AC equations could be considered as a rather general description of the time evolution of a dissipative system, combining conserved and non-conserved dynamics of the order parameter. Without memory effects (which will be discussed below) the time evolution is governed by

\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}=\nabla\left[M_{1}\left(\bar{w}\right)\frac{\delta F}{\delta\bar{w}}\right]-M_{2}\left(\bar{w}\right)\frac{\delta F}{\delta\bar{w}}.

(1.3)

Here
F\left[\bar{w}\right]
is the thermodynamic potential functional; in the present work
M_{1},\,M_{2}
are presumed to be constants.

Now, to understand the meaning of different terms in our modification (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) of this equation, we need to give some insight into the history of CH and AC equations.

Starting with the CH equation, we give only brief explanation of some basic assumptions and description of the model modifications; we also refer to the original papers
3
;
4
and excellent reviews
5
;
6
. The basic underlying idea of the Cahn-Hilliard model
3
;
4
is that for an inhomogeneous system, e.g. a system undergoing a phase transition, the thermodynamic potential (e.g. free energy) should depend not only on the order parameter, but on its gradient as well. For the thermodynamic potential, containing the square of the gradient, the local chemical potential
\bar{\mu}
, defined as a variational derivative of the thermodynamic potential functional, contains the Laplacian. In our one-dimensional version it is the second derivative, see (
1.2
). If the diffusional flux is proportional to the gradient of the chemical potential (Fickean diffusion), the diffusion equation for the order parameter is of the fourth order. The classic Cahn-Hilliard equation was introduced as early as in 1958
3
;
4
; the stationary solutions were considered, the linearized version was treated and corresponding instability of the homogeneous state identified. However, intensive study of the fully nonlinear form of this equation started essentially later
7
. Now an impressive amount of work is done on the nonlinear Cahn-Hilliard equation, as well as on its numerous modifications, see
5
;
6
. An important modification was done by Novick-Cohen
8
. Taking into account the dissipation effects which are neglected in the derivation of the classic Cahn-Hilliard equation, she introduced the
viscous
Cahn-Hilliard (VCH) equation, with the term
\bar{\eta}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}
added to the chemical potential
\bar{\mu}
. To account for the external field several authors considered the nonlinear
convective
Cahn-Hilliard equation (CCH)
9
;
10
;
11
. To study the joint effects of nonlinear convection and viscosity, Witelski
12
[12] introduced the
convective-viscous
-Cahn–Hilliard equation (CVCHE) with a general symmetric double-well potential
\Phi\left(\bar{w}\right)
:

\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}-\bar{\alpha}\bar{w}\frac{\partial w}{\partial x^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left[M_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left(\bar{\mu}+\bar{\eta}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}\right)\right],

(1.4)

\bar{\mu}=-\bar{\varepsilon}\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{w}}{\partial x^{\prime 2}}+\frac{d\Phi\left(\bar{w}\right)}{d\bar{w}}.

(1.5)

It is worth noting that all results, including the stability of solutions, were obtained without specifying a particular functional form of the potential. Thus, they are valid both for the polynomial and logarithmic
5
;
6
potential. Also, with an additional constraint imposed on the nonlinearity and viscosity, the approximate traveling-wave solutions were obtained. In
13
, for equation (
1.4
) with polynomial potential, see (
1.2
), several exact single- and two-wave solutions were obtained. Generally, the CH equation with convective and viscous terms appeared to be a useful tool in the study of further generalizations, such as external nonlinear sink/source terms and memory effects
14
;
15
.

Evidently, the Cahn-Hilliard equation describes the evolution of the locally conserved order parameter. For the evolution of the non-conserved order parameter Allen and Cahn
16
proposed the equation

\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}=-M_{2}\left[-\bar{\varepsilon}\Delta\bar{w}+f\left(\bar{w}\right)\right].

(1.6)

That is, the change of the order parameter is proportional to the deviation of the local chemical potential (
1.2
) of an inhomogeneous system from its equilibrium value.

The catalysis and deposition from liquid and gaseous phase involve, besides the overall mass transport, a manifold of processes at the surface itself, e.g. attachment/detachment of atoms, surface diffusion, chemical reactions, etc. Traditionally these surface processes were modeled by continuum-type reaction-diffusion models, see, e.g.,
17
. Such an approach usually neglects detailed interaction between the atoms. A different model was developed in
1
and studied in the series of papers
18
;
19
;
20
;
21
;
22
;
23
. On the microscopic level the basis of this approach is the modeling of surface processes by employing dynamic Ising type systems. Then the coarse-grained, mesoscopic models of Ising systems were derived
24
;
25
;
26
;
27
. As the next step, to illustrate the effects of multiple mechanisms, the simplification of the mesoscopic equation (which retains its fundamental structure) for the order parameter
\bar{w}
was introduced
1
;
18
:

\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left(M_{1}\frac{\partial\bar{\mu}}{\partial x^{\prime}}\right)-M_{2}\bar{\mu},

(1.7)

which is a composition of the CH and AC equations. Also a more general nature of the latter equation was then noticed
2
. It was found that the thin-film-type equations also fit the form (
1.7
), if due to some mechanism the mass is not conserved
2
;
28
;
29
.

If the conserved dynamics is described by the
convective-viscous
Cahn-Hilliard equation, the CH/AC equation, instead of (
1.7
), is

\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}-2\bar{\alpha}\bar{w}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial x^{\prime}}=-\frac{\partial J}{\partial x^{\prime}}-M_{2}\bar{\mu}.

(1.8)

Here
J
is the diffusional flux

J=-M_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left(\bar{\mu}+\bar{\eta}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}\right).

(1.9)

The latter equation is the ‘generalized Fick’s law’ (modified by the presence of the viscous term). The Ficks law was often criticized for the infinite velocity of the spread of the diffusing substance. The most popular alternative is the Maxwell-Cattaneo approach, see
30
for a comprehensive discussion; here we give only the one-dimensional formulae, in accordance with the spirit of the present paper. In the Maxwell-Cattaneo approach the mass-conservation equation (
1.8
) is not changed. However, instead of (
1.9
), the following relation is proposed:

\tau^{\prime}\frac{\partial J}{\partial t^{\prime}}+J=-M_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left(\bar{\mu}+\bar{\eta}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}\right).

(1.10)

Direct integration of the latter expression for the flux yields

J=-\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\left[\frac{M_{1}}{\tau^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\left(\bar{\mu}+\bar{\eta}\frac{\partial\bar{w}}{\partial t^{\prime}}\right)\right]\exp\left(\frac{t^{\prime\prime}-t^{\prime}}{\tau^{\prime}}\right)dt^{\prime\prime}.

(1.11)

So, this approach is also called ‘diffusion with memory effects’. Correspondingly,
\tau^{\prime}
is considered as the characteristic time of memory. On the other hand, eliminating
J
from (
1.8
) and (
1.10
) yields (
1.1
), i.e., the ‘hyperbolic modification’ of the convective-viscous CH/AC equation. The hyperbolic modification of the classical Cahn-Hilliard equation was proposed in
31
to model rapid spinodal decomposition in a binary alloy. However, from purely mathematical point of view – as a singular perturbation of the classic Cahn-Hilliard equation – it was considered even earlier
32
. These papers where followed by many others, both of physical and mathematical nature
33
;
34
;
35
;
36
;
37
. For the hyperbolic convective-viscous Cahn-Hilliard equation the exact traveling-wave solution was given in
13
.

As discussed above, the CH and AC ‘parts’ represent the different mechanisms involved in the process. Competition of these mechanisms may result in a quite complicated behavior, as found in the above cited papers
2
;
18
;
19
;
20
;
21
;
22
;
23
;

II

1.1

II
Traveling wave solution

Introducing the non-dimensional order parameter
w=\frac{\bar{w}}{w_{0}}
,
w_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}
, non-dimensional coordinate
x=\frac{x^{\prime}}{X}
and non-dimensional time
t=\frac{t^{\prime}}{T}
, we rewrite equations (
1.1
)-(
1.2

X=\sqrt{\bar{\varepsilon}}
;
T=\frac{\bar{\varepsilon}}{M_{1}}

\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}-2\alpha w\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}+\tau\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}+h\mu\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\left(\mu+\eta\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}\right)-h\mu.

(2.1)

\mu=-\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial x^{2}}+\left(w-a_{1}\right)\left(w-a_{2}\right)\left(w-a_{3}\right).

(2.2)

We also introduced the notations

\mu=\frac{\bar{\mu}}{w_{0}};\,\,\alpha=\bar{\alpha}\frac{w_{0}T}{X};\,\,T=\frac{X^{2}}{M_{1}};\,\,\eta=\frac{\bar{\eta}}{T};\,\,h=M_{2}T;\,\,\tau=\frac{\tau^{\prime}}{T};\,\,w_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\rho}}}.

(2.3)

Changing variables
x,\,t\,\Rightarrow z,\,t
, where
z=x-vt
, we get

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}-v\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}-2\alpha w\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}+\tau\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}-v\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}+h\mu\right)}

\displaystyle{-\tau v\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}-v\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}+h\mu\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}\left[\mu+\eta\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}-v\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}\right)\right]-h\mu}.

(2.4)

Looking for the traveling wave solutions we set
w=u\left(z\right)
; then (
II
) and (
2.2
) take the forms

-\frac{d}{dz}\left[vu+\alpha u^{2}-v^{2}\tau\frac{du}{dz}+v\tau h\mu\right]=\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}\left(\mu-\eta v\frac{du}{dz}\right)-h\mu,

(2.5)

\mu=-\frac{d^{2}u}{dz^{2}}+\left(u-a_{1}\right)\left(u-a_{2}\right)\left(u-a_{3}\right).

(2.6)

At
\pm\infty
all derivative terms equal to zero, so only solutions connecting the stationary states and turning to zero the polynomial in the right-hand side of (
2.6

a_{1}
at
+\infty

a_{3}
at

\frac{du}{dz}=\kappa\left(u-a_{1}\right)\left(u-a_{3}\right)=\kappa\left(u^{2}-up+q\right).

(2.7)

Here we introduced notations
p=\left(a_{1}+a_{3}\right);\,\,q=a_{1}a_{3}
for brevity;
\kappa
is presently an unknown constant,
\kappa>0
for

(‘anti-kink’), and
\kappa<0
for
\frac{du}{dz}>0
(‘kink’). Using (
2.7
) the expression for the chemical potential (
2.6
) could be transformed,

\mu=\left[\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(1-2\kappa^{2}\right)u+\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\kappa^{2}p-a_{2}\right)\right]\frac{du}{dz}.

(2.8)

Alternatively,

\mu=-\frac{d}{dz}\left[\frac{du}{dz}-\frac{1}{2\kappa}\left(u^{2}-2a_{2}u\right)\right].

(2.9)

Substituting (
2.9

2.5
) and integrating once we get

\displaystyle{\frac{d}{dz}\left[\mu-\left(\eta v+v\tau h\right)\frac{du}{dz}+\frac{v\tau h}{\kappa}\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}-a_{2}u\right)\right]+\left(h-v^{2}\tau\right)\frac{du}{dz}}

\displaystyle{+\left(\alpha-\frac{h}{2\kappa}\right)u^{2}+\left(\frac{ha_{2}}{\kappa}+v\right)u+C=0}.

(2.10)

Using expression (

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{d}{dz}\left\{\left[\left(1-2\kappa^{2}\right)u+\left(\kappa^{2}p-a_{2}-\eta\kappa v-v\kappa\tau h\right)\right]\frac{du}{dz}+\frac{v\tau h}{2}u^{2}-v\tau ha_{2}u\right\}}

\displaystyle{+\left(h-v^{2}\tau\right)\frac{du}{dz}+\left(\alpha-\frac{h}{2\kappa}\right)u^{2}+\left(\frac{ha_{2}}{\kappa}+v\right)u+C=0}.

(2.11)

Inspection of the powers of

(2.12)

\kappa\left(p-2a_{2}\right)=\eta v.

(2.13)

Then (

\left[\left(h\eta-1\right)v^{2}\tau+\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}\right]\kappa u^{2}+\left[\kappa p\tau v^{2}\left(1-h\eta\right)+\left(1-h\eta\right)v\right]u=0.

Here we used

(2.15)

\left(1-\eta h\right)\left(\kappa pv\tau+1\right)v=0;\,\,.

III
The parametric dependence of the solution

There are four constraints (
2.12
)-(
2.13
), (
2.15
)-(
2.16
) but only two unknowns
\kappa,\,v
. That is, a constant-velocity traveling wave could be generated by the joint action of two mechanisms only if the two additional constraints are imposed on the system parameters. From (
2.13
), it follows

v=\frac{\kappa}{\eta}\left(p-2a_{2}\right).

(3.1)

However, in writing the latter equation we implicitly presumed
\eta\neq 0
, and before proceeding we need to check, what happens if
\eta=0
. We get from (
2.13
), (
2.15
) and (
2.16
):

a_{1}+a_{3}=2a_{2};\,\,\,\,\frac{\alpha}{\kappa}=v^{2}\tau;\,\,\,\,\left(\kappa pv\tau+1\right)v=0.

(3.2)

So the solution is possible only if the additional constraint is imposed on the stationary states. If
\alpha=0,\,\,\tau\neq 0
, then
v=0
, i.e., only the static solution is possible. If
\tau=0
it should be necessary
\alpha=0
, and it follows again
v=0
. If
\alpha\neq 0,\,\,\tau\neq 0
, then
v=-\frac{1}{\kappa p\tau}
. The traveling wave is possible if in addition to
a_{1}+a_{3}=2a_{2}
, there is the constraint
\alpha\tau p^{2}=2\kappa
(
\left|\kappa\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, but we keep
\kappa
to point out that its sign should coincide with the sign of
\alpha
).

Now, if the viscosity
\eta
is non-zero, eliminating
v
from (
2.15
)-(
2.16
), we get two constraints imposed on the parameters of the system

2\alpha\eta^{2}=\kappa\tau\left(p-2a_{2}\right)^{2}\left(1-h\eta\right),

(3.3)

\left(1-\eta h\right)\left[p\tau\left(p-2a_{2}\right)+2\eta\right]\left(p-2a_{2}\right)=0.

(3.4)

The latter equation is satisfied if:

1)
p-2a_{2}=0
; then from (
3.3
) it should be
\alpha=0
, and from (
3.1
)
v=0
; i.e. only the static solution is possible;

2)
1-\eta h=0
; again from (
3.3
) it should be
\alpha=0
; however there is no limitation on
p-2a_{2}
, so the traveling wave is possible, and the velocity
v
is given by (
3.1
). In this case the memory time
\tau
drops out from the constraints, so there is no influence of memory. Naturally, this case corresponds to the problem without memory, which was considered in
38
(the notations in the present paper are different);

3)
p\tau\left(p-2a_{2}\right)=-2\eta
; or

\,\left(p-2a_{2}\right)=-\frac{2\eta}{p\tau}.

(3.5)

Substitution of (
3.5
) into the second constraint (
3.3
) yields

\,\alpha p^{2}\tau=2\kappa\left(1-h\eta\right).

(3.6)

If
\eta\neq 0
, the expression (
3.1
) for the velocity is valid. As discussed above, if
\eta=0
the equations (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) become

a_{1}+a_{3}=2a_{2};\,\,\,\,\,\,\alpha p^{2}\tau=2\kappa.

(3.7)

And the velocity is

v=-\frac{1}{\kappa p\tau}.

(3.8)

In the initial notations the dimensional velocity
\bar{v}
and the constraints are

For the case 2):

\bar{v}=\frac{\kappa\sqrt{\varepsilon\rho}}{\bar{\eta}}\left(\bar{a}_{1}+\bar{a}_{3}-2\bar{a}_{2}\right),

(3.9)

\bar{\eta}M_{2}=1;\,\,\,\alpha=0.

(3.10)

For the case 3):

if
\eta\neq 0
the velocity is still given by(
3.9
); if
\eta=0

\bar{v}=-\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\kappa\sqrt{\rho}\left(\bar{a}_{1}+\bar{a}_{3}\right)\tau^{\prime}}.

(3.11)

The constraints are generally

\left(\bar{a}_{1}+\bar{a}_{3}\right)\left(\bar{a}_{1}+\bar{a}_{3}-2\bar{a}_{2}\right)\rho=-\frac{2\bar{\eta}}{\tau^{\prime}},

(3.12)

\,\left(\bar{a}_{1}+\bar{a}_{3}\right)^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon}}=\frac{2\kappa}{\bar{\alpha}\tau^{\prime}}\,\left(1-\bar{\eta}M_{2}\right).

(3.13)

Summing up, for the conserved and non-conserved dynamics to be ‘cooperative’, the two additional constrains (
3.3
)-(
3.4
) should be satisfied. There are three essentially different cases when these constraints are satisfied. If a symmetry condition
a_{1}+a_{3}=2a_{2}
is imposed on the stationary states of the potential, and the convective term is absent, only the static solution is possible.

On the other hand, if there is a link (
3.10
) between two kinetic parameters, viscosity
\bar{\eta}
and kinetic coefficient
M_{2}
, and again
\alpha=0
, the traveling wave exists with velocity (
3.9
). Using (
3.10
), (
3.9
) could be rewritten as

\bar{v}=\kappa\sqrt{\bar{\varepsilon}\rho}M_{2}\left(\bar{a}_{1}+\bar{a}_{3}-2\bar{a}_{2}\right).

(3.14)

Remarkably, while the dependence of the velocity on the values of stationary states is exactly the same, as for the well known solution of the diffusion equation with cubic nonlinearity, i.e., on the Allen-Cahn part, the coefficient in (
3.14
) depends on
\bar{\varepsilon}
and
\rho
, i.e., on the Cahn-Hilliard part of (
1.1
). It evidently reminds the appearance of the surface tension in the expression for the velocity for the ‘motion by mean curvature’, though the ‘driving forces’ are essentially different. It is well known that for the classic Cahn-Hilliard equation only exact static solutions are possible. Nevertheless, the introduction of the convective and viscous terms, i.e., the appearance of the balance between the external field and dissipation, enables existence of exact one- and two-wave solutions
13
. However, for the convective-viscous CH-AC equation the situation is essentially different. In the absence of memory the very presence of external field makes the existence of an exact constant-velocity traveling wave solution impossible. On the other hand, the existence of such a solution is ensured by the balance between the dissipation and kinetics, see (
3.10
).

In the third case, the constraints are (
3.12
)-(
3.13
). Evidently, the very existence of this case is due to the memory. The both constraints exhibit the balance between the ‘Cahn-Hilliard-‘ and ‘Allen-Cahn-parameters’ with essential presence of the characteristic memory time
\tau^{\prime}
. However, for the non-zero viscosity
\eta
the expression for the velocity is the same. Only if
\eta=0
, the velocity becomes essentially different, see (
3.11
). In this case the dissipation is absent, and the balance between conserved and non-conserved dynamics is due to the memory.
