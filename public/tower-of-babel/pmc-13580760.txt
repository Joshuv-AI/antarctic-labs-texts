Docetaxel‐Induced Immune Activation Shows Antitumor Synergy With the Tumor‐Targeted CD40 Agonist KK2269
Authors: Yuta Tezuka, Kyoko Kawasaki, Yoshiki Sumitomo, Masato Saito, Akari Yao, Munetoshi Ando
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTumor‐targeted CD40 agonists have emerged as one of the novel strategies to activate intratumoral antigen‐presenting cells (APCs), subsequently inducing T‐cell‐specific antitumor responses. However, their clinical efficacy as monotherapy has been limited, highlighting the need for effective combination therapies. We developed KK2269, a bispecific antibody targeting epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) on tumor cells and CD40 on APCs, designed to selectively activate APCs in the presence of EpCAM‐positive tumors. Our studies demonstrated that KK2269 activates APCs only in the presence of EpCAM‐expressing cells. Using the immunologically cold B16F10‐mouse EpCAM subcutaneously transplanted model, the antitumor effects of KK2269 in combination with clinically approved drugs, including anti‐programmed cell death 1 (PD‐1) antibody, docetaxel, doxorubicin, oxaliplatin, and gemcitabine were evaluated. KK2269 showed significant antitumor activity in combination with an anti‐PD‐1 antibody, docetaxel, doxorubicin, or oxaliplatin, but not gemcitabine, with docetaxel showing the most significant antitumor effect(s). Intratumoral immune analysis showed that KK2269 was associated with increased expression of APC activation markers, whereas docetaxel was associated with increased expression of dendritic cell (DC)‐related genes. Moreover, expression of APC and T‐cell activation markers was higher with docetaxel+KK2269 than with either monotherapy. The antitumor and immune activation effects of docetaxel+KK2269 were also observed in the immunosuppressive intrahepatic transplantation model. This study provides a rationale for combining a tumor‐targeting CD40 agonist, KK2269, with docetaxel for the treatment of PD‐1 blockade resistant tumors, including intrahepatic tumors, and supports exploration of this combination strategy in the clinic.Keywords: antigen‐presenting cells, bispecific antibody, CD40, docetaxel, immunotherapy

Introduction

Following the approval of programmed cell death 1 (PD‐1) and its ligand (programmed cell death‐ligand 1 [PD‐L1]) blockade therapies for multiple cancers, the treatment landscape for cancer has changed substantially, making it a curable disease [1]. However, only a subset of patients responds [2], necessitating novel therapies against PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade‐resistant tumors.

CD40 is a member of the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor superfamily expressed on a variety of cells, including antigen‐presenting cells (APCs) such as dendritic cells (DCs), monocytes, and B cells, as well as platelets and endothelial cells [3, 4]. Engagement of CD40 by its ligand (CD40L) on activated CD4+ T cells induces receptor trimerization and signaling [5], leading to upregulation of costimulatory molecules including CD80 and CD86, major histocompatibility complex receptors, and proinflammatory cytokines including interleukin IL‐12. This process licenses DCs to prime antigen‐specific CD8+ T cells and generate cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [6]. In mouse tumor models, CD40 agonistic antibodies, which mimic CD40L to cross‐link with CD40, promote tumor‐specific CTL expansion, tumor rejection, and conversion of immunologically “cold” tumors into PD‐1/PD‐L1‐responsive “hot” tumors, supporting their development as cancer immunotherapy agents [7, 8, 9, 10].

However, first‐generation CD40 agonists, including recombinant CD40L and selicrelumab (CP‐870,893, F. Hoffmann‐La Roche Ltd.), a fully human CD40 agonist of immunoglobulin G (IgG) subclass 2, exhibited systemic toxicities, including elevated liver enzymes and cytokine release syndrome, thereby limiting their dosage and their antitumor potential in patients [11, 12]. In contrast, second‐generation tumor‐targeted CD40 agonists exhibited antitumor activity with reduced toxicity. Sotigalimab (Pyxis Oncology), an FcγR2b‐dependent CD40 agonist, showed limited efficacy as monotherapy but improved outcomes in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma when combined with gemcitabine plus nab‐paclitaxel and/or the anti‐PD‐1 antibody nivolumab [13]. Thus, we hypothesized that tumor‐targeted CD40 agonists as monotherapy may not be sufficient to induce meaningful antitumor effect(s) but may be beneficial in combination treatments. Therefore, therapeutic combination strategies are needed to maximize the antitumor effect(s) of CD40 agonists.

The aim of this study was to identify agents synergistic with a tumor‐targeted CD40 agonist. We utilized KK2269, a bispecific antibody targeting CD40 and epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) that activates APCs where EpCAM+ cells are present [14]. EpCAM is a well‐established tumor marker that is overexpressed in most epithelial cancers, such as colon, gastric, and lung cancers [15]. KK2269 is expected to target EpCAM in the tumor and stimulate intratumoral DC activation without off‐target toxicity. We show that docetaxel combined with KK2269 induces significant antitumor activity with delayed tumor regression in a PD‐1 blockade‐resistant mouse model, providing proof‐of‐concept for this combination strategy.

Results

KK2269 Binds to CD40 and EpCAM, and Activates DCs Dependent on the Presence of EpCAM + Cells

First, we utilized a tumor‐targeted CD40 agonist and evaluated its properties. We developed KK2269, a recombinant fully human IgG4‐based bispecific antibody directed against human CD40 and EpCAM antigens (Figure 1A), using the REGULGENT technology (Kyowa Kirin Co. Ltd.; for details regarding the development of KK2269, refer to Doc S1) [16].

To evaluate the binding activity of KK2269 by flow cytometry (FCM), we used Ramos or COLO 205 cells as human CD40 (hCD40)+/human EpCAM (hEpCAM)− or hCD40−/hEpCAM+ cells, respectively [17, 18]. KK2269 bound to Ramos and COLO 205 cells in a concentration‐dependent manner, whereas CD40 agonistic antibody 21.4.1 bound only to Ramos cells (Figure 1B). The binding kinetics of KK2269 against EpCAM and CD40 were evaluated using an SPR system (Table S1), indicating sub‐nanomolar binding activity for the both antigens. These data indicate that KK2269 binds to both hCD40 and hEpCAM.

To evaluate the CD40 agonistic activity of KK2269, human monocyte‐derived DCs (moDCs) were incubated with KK2269 in the presence or absence of COLO 205 cells. The positive control MEGA CD40L induced CD80 and CD86 expression independent of the presence or absence of COLO 205 cells. In contrast, KK2269 induced CD80 and CD86 expression only in the presence of COLO 205 cells in all tested donor‐derived moDCs, although KK2269 did not induce CD80 and CD86 expression in the absence of COLO 205 cells (Figure 1C, Figure S1). These results suggest that KK2269 has EpCAM‐dependent CD40 agonistic activity in human moDCs.

KK2269 Induces Intratumor Immune Effects Dependent on EpCAM Expression Without Induction of Liver Injury and Inflammatory Cytokine Expression in an Intrahepatically Transplanted Tumor Model

To evaluate the tumor‐specific properties of KK2269 in vivo, we used a syngeneic tumor mouse model expressing mouse EpCAM (mEpCAM) on tumor cells and hCD40 on host cells, as KK2269 binds to mEpCAM and hCD40, but not to mouse CD40 (mCD40; Table S1). To establish an mEpCAM+ tumor, we overexpressed mEpCAM in B16F10 cells, which are essentially mEpCAM− (Figure S2A, S2B). This model represents cold tumors [19], and mEpCAM expression did not exceed that of the human EpCAM+ cell line COLO 205, indicating physiologically relevant expression (Figure S2B).

To assess tumor‐specific agonistic activity, B16F10‐mEpCAM cells were inoculated intrahepatically, and the effects of KK2269 were compared between tumor and peritumor normal tissue. KK2269 was administered at 10 mg/kg, a dose with antitumor effects and intratumor immune changes in a B16F10‐mEpCAM subcutaneously transplanted model (Figure S2C, S2D), without body‐weight loss (data not shown). First, pathological analysis of normal liver tissue was performed. Following administration of the CD40 agonist 21.4.1 at 3 mg/kg, hepatic inflammatory cell infiltration, microgranulomas, and focal parenchymal necrosis were observed by Day 3 (Figure 2A, Table S2), together with blood AST/ALT elevations on Day 1 (Figure 2B). In contrast, KK2269 (10 mg/kg) showed no comparable liver pathology (Figure 2A, Table S2) or AST/ALT elevations at Days 1 (p = 0.6682 and 0.5454) or 3 (p = 0.6753 and 0.6147; Figure 2B). Consistent with the lack of liver toxicity, KK2269 did not induce an increase in circulating TNF at the efficacious dose (p = 0.9813), whereas 21.4.1 administration resulted in elevated systemic TNF levels in tumor‐free mice (Figure S3).

Next, the agonistic activity of KK2269 was examined. KK2269 upregulated Il12b expression in the intrahepatic tumor (p = 0.0117), as did 21.4.1, on Day 1. This increase persisted up to Day 3 with KK2269 (p = 0.0151) but not 21.4.1 (Figure 2C). mRNA expression of Cd80 and Cd86 showed similar trends but did not reach statistical significance.

Next, the effect of KK2269 or 21.4.1 administration in peritumor normal liver tissue was examined. Several inflammatory cytokines (IL‐12, TNF, IL‐6, and Interferon gamma [IFNγ]) has been reported to be induced by CD40 agonist administration [20]. mRNA expression in peritumor tissue was analyzed by qPCR using primers listed in Table S3. Il12b, Tnf, Il6, and Ifng expression increased more than 2‐fold on Day 1 in mice treated with 1 mg/kg 21.4.1, whereas no induction was detected after KK2269 administration on Days 1 and 3 (Figure 2D). We next analyzed mRNA expression of 47 genes involved in immune cell infiltration and activation, including the four inflammatory cytokines described above. Notably, on Day 1, 21.4.1 exhibited gene expression patterns distinct from those of the vehicle group (Figure S4A). In contrast, KK2269 clustered with the vehicle group on Day 1, and a similar pattern was observed on Day 3 (Figure S4B). These results showed that 21.4.1 induced the expression of inflammatory cytokines in the peritumor normal liver tissue, whereas KK2269 did not at the transcriptional level.

Collectively, unlike 21.4.1, KK2269 acts as a CD40 agonist only within EpCAM+ tumors and that its inflammatory effects are limited to EpCAM+ tumors with persistent effect.

KK2269 Exerts Antitumor Effect(s) in Combination With Approved Drugs

Clinical and nonclinical studies have suggested that CD40 agonists may provide better results when combined with other immunotherapies or chemotherapies [13]. Therefore, to identify synergistic agents with KK2269, we conducted antitumor effect studies in combination with clinical standard agents using an immunologically cold B16F10‐mEpCAM subcutaneously transplanted model. First, we tested an anti‐PD‐1 antibody, as it potentially enhances T cell‐dependent antitumor activities after CD40 agonist‐induced T‐cell priming. The median event‐free survival (EFS) was 10 days (95% confidence interval [CI]: 7–10), 10 days (95% CI: 10–14), 14 days (95% CI: 10–14), and 17 days (95% CI: 14–21) in the vehicle, KK2269, anti‐PD‐1 antibody, and anti‐PD‐1 antibody+KK2269 groups, respectively (Figure 3A). Anti‐PD‐1 antibody+KK2269 significantly prolonged survival compared with vehicle (p < 0.0001), KK2269 alone (p = 0.0079), or anti‐PD‐1 antibody alone (p = 0.0006). This suggests that KK2269 in combination with an anti‐PD‐1 antibody induces antitumor effects in a cold tumor model, where PD‐1 blockade alone has limited effects.

Similarly, we tested the antitumor effects of KK2269 in combination with conventional chemotherapies predicted to stimulate antitumor immunity including docetaxel, doxorubicin, and oxaliplatin, and predicted not to stimulate antitumor immunity including gemcitabine [21]. Compared with vehicle, median EFS was significantly prolonged in the docetaxel+KK2269 (not reached [NR] by Day 40 [95% CI: 7‐NR, p = 0.0001]; Figure 3B), doxorubicin+KK2269 (25 days [95% CI: 14–42 days, p < 0.0001]; Figure 3C), and oxaliplatin+KK2269 (19 days [10–24, p < 0.0001]) groups (Figure 3D). In addition, the median EFS of these combinations (docetaxel+KK2269, doxorubicin+KK2269, and oxaliplatin+KK2269) was significantly prolonged compared with that of individual monotherapies (Table S4), in which the hazard ratios are also presented. Interestingly, a delayed antitumor effect observed on Days 11 to 21, in which the tumor regressed significantly after a single expansion, was observed only in docetaxel+KK2269‐treated mice (Figure 3B).

In contrast, gemcitabine+KK2269 showed only a slight trend toward antitumor activity compared with each monotherapy (Figure 3E). Gemcitabine+KK2269 significantly prolonged survival compared with vehicle (p = 0.0060) and gemcitabine (p = 0.0100), but not with KK2269 (p = 0.3817; Table S4).

Thus, KK2269 showed synergistic antitumor activity with anti‐PD‐1 antibody, docetaxel, doxorubicin, and oxaliplatin, but not with gemcitabine. In particular, docetaxel induced a superior antitumor effect with KK2269, with strong tumor regression in a markedly delayed onset.

Docetaxel+KK2269 Is Associated With Intratumoral Immune Phenotype Changes

To explore the mechanism of synergistic antitumor activity after docetaxel+KK2269 administration, we performed immune analysis of tumor samples. Mice were treated with vehicle, KK2269, docetaxel, or docetaxel+KK2269. Tumor samples were collected on Day 4 for all groups and on Day 12 for the KK2269, docetaxel, and docetaxel+KK2269 groups. Untreated tumors collected on Day 0 were used as controls.

Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that Day 12 tumor samples from the docetaxel+KK2269 group exhibited apparent changes compared with those from other treatment groups (Figure 4A). Consistent with this observation, the cell‐type scores of most representative immune cells, including CD45+ cells (p < 0.0100) and CD8+ T cells (p < 0.0100), were all upregulated in the docetaxel+KK2269 group, suggesting that this combination is associated with changes in the immune landscape of the tumor microenvironment (Figure 4B). Given that KK2269 monotherapy induced DC activation markers (Figure S2D), we analyzed whether these changes were altered in the combination setting. In the KK2269 monotherapy group, genes in the CD40 downstream pathway (Il12b, Cd80, and Cd86) were upregulated on Day 4 (p < 0.0100; Figure 4C), although the expression tended to return to baseline level by Day 12 (p = 0.2789, p = 0.4111, p = 0.3012, respectively). In contrast, an increase of these markers was observed on both Days 4 and 12 in the docetaxel+KK2269 group, being markedly higher on Day 12 (p < 0.0100). These findings are consistent with the possibility that APC activation signals induced by KK2269 are sustained or augmented in the presence of docetaxel. In addition, the expression of genes associated with T‐cell activation and migration was higher in the docetaxel+KK2269 group on Day 12 than in the other treatment groups (p < 0.0100; Figure 4C, Figure S5), suggesting a potential association between combination treatment and enhanced T‐cell–related immune responses.

We next explored possible mechanisms by which docetaxel might contribute to the synergistic effects with KK2269. Since APC‐activating signals were enhanced by docetaxel+KK2269 compared with KK2269 alone, we hypothesized that docetaxel might affect the number of intratumoral DCs. Indeed, gene sets including DC markers such as Cd103 and Xcr1 were upregulated by docetaxel but not by KK2269 (Figure 4D). To further characterize the biological effects of docetaxel, we examined the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the docetaxel and vehicle groups on Day 4. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that most of the top pathways enriched in the docetaxel group, including the neuroinflammation signaling, pathogen‐induced cytokine storm signaling, toll‐like receptor (TLR) signaling, and the role of macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells in rheumatoid arthritis, were all related to TLR signaling involving myeloid cells (Figure 4E, Table S5). Together, these data suggest that docetaxel may influence the intratumoral myeloid environment, which could potentially contribute to modulation of KK2269‐induced immune responses.

Docetaxel+KK2269 Induces Antitumor Effect in the Immunosuppressive Intrahepatic Environment

Recent investigations have shown that a liver tumor–specific immunosuppressive immune milieu attenuates the efficacy of anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 therapy [22, 23]. To investigate whether KK2269 is effective against immunosuppressive intrahepatic tumors, the antitumor effect of docetaxel+KK2269 was evaluated using a mouse intrahepatic tumor model.

A comprehensive immune‐related gene expression analysis was performed to test whether mouse intrahepatic tumors possess immunosuppressive features. First, to test whether immune infiltration differs between intrahepatic and subcutaneous tumors, mRNA expression of the expanded immune gene signature was compared [24]. This signature has been shown to correlate with T‐cell infiltration into tumors and the clinical efficacy of anti‐PD‐1 in certain tumors. As the signature was developed for humans, murine homologs included in the Mouse PanCancer Immune Profiling Panel were analyzed. Expression of genes comprising the expanded immune gene signature was lower in intrahepatic tumors than in subcutaneous tumors (Figure 5A). Moreover, the expression of genes constituting the activated DC signature was lower in intrahepatic tumors than in subcutaneous tumors (Figure 5B) [25]. These results suggested that intrahepatic tumors exhibit reduced immune infiltration.

Next, the antitumor effect of docetaxel+KK2269 was evaluated. After 14 days of treatment, the tumor volume was smaller in the docetaxel group than in the vehicle group (Figure 5C). The mean tumor volume was also smaller in the docetaxel+KK2269 group than in the docetaxel group (Figure 5C). Notably, the mean tumor size in the docetaxel+KK2269 group did not increase from Day 14 to 21, in contrast to that in the docetaxel group, where the tumor size increased steadily (Figure 5C). Docetaxel+KK2269 significantly prolonged the survival of mice compared with the docetaxel (p = 0.0143; hazard ratio = 4.46 × 10−13; Figure 5D). Although docetaxel alone or docetaxel+KK2269 slightly reduced the body weight of treated mice, the addition of KK2269 did not exacerbate the weight loss caused by docetaxel (Figure S6).

In addition, we investigated whether docetaxel+KK2269 activates antitumor immunity against intrahepatic tumors. qPCR analysis of intrahepatic tumors after docetaxel and/or KK2269 administration was performed. Docetaxel+KK2269 treatment upregulated the expression of genes associated with T‐cell activation and migration, including Cd8a (p = 0.0505), Cxcl9 (p = 0.0298), Cxcl10 (p = 0.0134), Ifng (p = 0.0443), Gzmb (p = 0.0478), and Prf1 (p = 0.0260), indicating the infiltration and activation of CTLs in intrahepatic tumors (Figure 5E). In conclusion, these results suggest that docetaxel with KK2269 induces antitumor effect(s) on immunologically cold intrahepatic tumors.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated KK2269, a tumor‐targeted CD40 agonist, and found synergistic antitumor activity when combined with docetaxel in an immunologically cold tumor model. KK2269 is a bispecific antibody targeting human CD40 and EpCAM, with CD40 agonistic activity strictly controlled by EpCAM expression. KK2269 showed intratumoral DC activation not only in the subcutaneously transplanted EpCAM+ tumor mouse model but also in an intrahepatically transplanted mouse model. Remarkably, DC activation was not observed in peritumoral normal liver sites, and no signs of liver injury were detected. This is the first report showing that a tumor‐targeting CD40 agonist can activate APCs restricted to the EpCAM+ tumor site and does not affect the surrounding normal liver sites. These data highlight that KK2269, an EpCAM‐driven CD40 agonist, may overcome limitations of systemic CD40 agonists, whose dosing is constrained by toxicities. Additional studies addressing systemic toxicities, PK/PD relationships, receptor occupancy, and biodistribution will be critical to fully establish its safety profile.

Further, we utilized EpCAM overexpressing‐B16F10 tumor, which is known as a neoantigen low tumor representing a cold tumor model resistant to PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade [19]. This mice model exhibits EpCAM expression levels comparable to those observed in human cancer cells with EpCAM overexpression. A similar bispecific molecule targeting CD40 and EpCAM has been reported previously [26]. In that study, the antibody showed significant antitumor activity as monotherapy in EpCAM overexpressing‐MB49 tumor‐bearing mice, which was considered an inflamed tumor model. This might explain the tumor regression observed with the antibody as monotherapy. Considering the clinical results obtained with the previous CD40 agonists, monotherapy tended to have only a mild antitumor response, although the combination therapy seemed to significantly improve the response [13, 27]. Therefore, we aimed to identify clinically used agents that could synergize with KK2269, especially against cold tumors. While the combination of KK2269 with anti‐PD‐1 antibody, docetaxel, doxorubicin, or oxaliplatin significantly prolonged EFS compared with KK2269 monotherapy, the combination with gemcitabine was not significant. This emphasizes that the selection of a combination drug for tumor‐targeting CD40 agonists, including KK2269, is essential for successful cancer therapy. Notably, docetaxel plus KK2269 produced pronounced antitumor effects with delayed tumor regression approximately 2 weeks after treatment initiation, consistent with the timing of de novo acquired immunity.

To gain insight into the potential mechanisms underlying the observed activity of this combination treatment, we performed transcriptome analysis against the drug‐treated mice tumors. This analysis yielded two main observations: transcriptional changes associated with docetaxel monotherapy and distinct immune‐related signatures observed with docetaxel+KK2269. Importantly, the following interpretations are based solely on transcriptomic analyses and should be considered hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive.

First, docetaxel treatment was associated with increased expression of Cd103 and Xcr1, which are the representative markers expressed on Type 1 cDCs (cDC1s) [28]. cDC1s are the most potent APCs, crucial for tumor‐specific CD8+ T cell induction and recruitment [29]. Thus, the observed transcriptional upregulation of Cd103 and Xcr1 is consistent with the possibility that docetaxel alters the intratumoral cDC1‐related compartment, potentially through changes in recruitment or expansion.

In addition, docetaxel treatment was associated with enrichment of inflammatory pathways within the myeloid compartment. Previous studies have reported that docetaxel can promote monocyte differentiation toward an M1‐like phenotype and enhance antigen presentation [30], as well as synergize with adoptive T‐cell therapies by modulating suppressive myeloid populations [31]. In our analysis, several enriched pathways were related to TLR signaling, suggesting a potential link between docetaxel treatment and innate immune activation in myeloid cells. Nevertheless, TLR involvement in this context is inferred from pathway enrichment analysis and has not been directly tested; thus, the precise molecular mechanisms by which docetaxel affects myeloid cells in the tumor microenvironment remain to be elucidated.

The second observation was the distinct intratumoral immune transcriptional profile associated with docetaxel+KK2269 treatment. Compared with KK2269 monotherapy, the combination was associated with higher expression of genes related to APC activation and with transcriptional signatures linked to cytotoxic T‐cell activation. These findings suggest that the combination treatment is associated with broader immune modulation within the tumor microenvironment. We propose a working model in which docetaxel‐associated alterations in the myeloid compartment may create a tumor microenvironment more permissive to KK2269‐induced CD40 signaling and downstream immune activation. As these conclusions are inferred from transcriptomic data, further studies will be needed to obtain direct evidence at the cellular level.

In our study, gemcitabine+KK2269 did not produce significant antitumor effects. Previous studies have reported that gemcitabine exerts paradoxical effects on myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [21]. While preclinical studies show that it can deplete MDSCs and enhance antitumor immunity, other evidence indicates that it activates these cells, promoting immunosuppression [21]. Therefore, differences in myeloid activation between docetaxel and gemcitabine may have contributed to their distinct combinatorial effects with KK2269. Further studies examining the immunological changes of combination regimens, including gemcitabine+KK2269, and comparing them with that of docetaxel+KK2269 may provide insights into the immunological conditions required to maximize the antitumor efficacy of KK2269‐based therapies.

In addition, the potent antitumor effect of the docetaxel+KK2269 was recaptured in an immunosuppressive intrahepatic tumor model without compromising the immune status of the peritumoral normal liver. Liver metastasis is a major challenge in the treatment of some EpCAM+ cancers, such as colorectal and gastric cancers. Over 20% of gastric cancer patients eligible for systemic therapy have liver metastases [32]. In addition, it should be noted that previous studies have claimed that the presence of liver metastases attenuates the efficacy of anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 therapy, highlighting the importance of developing novel immunotherapies that overcome the immunosuppressive microenvironment in intrahepatic tumor [22, 33]. Although CD40 agonists are thought to be effective against liver metastases, a safer approach is warranted because of the potential risk of hepatotoxicity [11]. Therefore, docetaxel+KK2269 combination therapy may represent a potential treatment to overcome resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors in liver metastases of EpCAM+ cancers with a limited risk of hepatotoxicity.

In conclusion, this study provides a rationale for combining a tumor‐targeted CD40 agonist, KK2269, especially with docetaxel for the treatment of PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade‐resistant cold tumors, including those with liver metastases. While our conclusions are currently limited to engineered EpCAM‐expressing tumor models, these findings support further exploration of this combination strategy in more physiological settings and in clinical studies. A Phase 1 study of KK2269 in adult participants with solid tumors (ClinicalTrials.gov ID #NCT06266299) is ongoing.

Figure S1: KK2269 activates moDCs in the presence of EpCAM. moDCs were incubated with KK2269, MEGA CD40L, or anti‐DNP antibody as control in the presence or absence of EpCAM+ COLO 205 cells for 2 days. CD40 agonistic activity was evaluated by measuring CD80 and CD86 expression on human moDCs in each sample using flow cytometry. (A) The relative geometric mean of all measured samples were fitted into four‐parameter logistic curves using JMP. X‐axis is the concentration of KK2269 (μg/mL). Y‐axis is relative expression of CD80 (left) and CD86 (right). (B) Data from the remaining five donors related to Figure 1C.

Figure S2: KK2269 induces antitumor effects and intratumor immune effects in B16F10‐mEpCAM subcutaneously implanted mouse model. (A) Expression of mouse EpCAM on B16F10‐mEpCAM cells. Cells were stained with PE‐conjugated antimouse EpCAM antibody (blue) or control antibody (red), and fluorescence was evaluated by flow cytometry. Data are shown as histogram. (B) The expression of EpCAM on B16F10, B16F10‐mEpCAM, and COLO 205 were measured by flow cytometry. Calculated EpCAM molecules/cell of each sample and concentrations of anti‐human or mouse EpCAM‐PE antibody were plotted. (C) The antitumor effect of vehicle, KK2269 (10 mg/kg), and 21.4.1 (8.92 mg/kg) was evaluated in B16F10‐mEpCAM subcutaneously implanted hCD40 mice. Mice were treated on day 0 and tumor volumes were measured on day 10. Mice were promptly euthanized when the tumor volume exceeded 1500 mm3 or when a humane endpoint was reached. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean, n = 6–8. (D) Intratumor immune activation of vehicle and KK2269 (10 mg/kg) was evaluated in B16F10 mEpCAM subcutaneously implanted hCD40 mice. Mice were treated on day 0 and tumors were resected on Day 1. RNA was extracted and expression of Il12b, Cd80, and Cd86 was analyzed by qPCR. Ct values of each sample were normalized to Gapdh. The y‐axis indicates the ratio of each sample to the vehicle mean. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean, n = 5.

Figure S3: KK2269 does not induce circulating TNF. KK2269 (10 mg/kg), 21.4.1 (1 mg/kg), or anti‐DNP antibody (10 mg/kg) as a control was administered to hCD40 mice on Day 0, and circulating TNF levels were measured on Day 1. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean, n = 5.

Figure S4: KK2269 does not induce the expression of genes associated with immune cell infiltration or activation in normal liver tissue adjacent to tumors. Heatmap and unbiased hierarchical clustering of the expression levels of 47 genes associated with immune cell infiltration and activation in tumor‐adjacent normal liver tissue at Day 1 (A) and Day 3 (B) after administration of 21.4.1 (1 or 3 mg/kg), KK2269 (10 mg/kg), or vehicle. n = 5–6.

Figure S5: Docetaxel+KK2269 associates with intratumoral immune phenotype changes. Data related to Figure 4C. Specific gene expression of Cxcl9, Cxcl10, Gzmb, and Prf1 were analyzed and compared between each group. Data are presented as mean, n = 7–8.

Figure S6: Kinetics of body weight change by treatment with docetaxel and KK2269. Body weights of each mouse were measured on Days 0, 4, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13, and 14 after first injection of docetaxel or KK2269. Body weights at each time point were divided by the weight on Day 0 and multiplied by 100 to obtain the relative weight (%) of each mouse. Data are presented shown as mean ± standard error of the mean, n = 8.
