Long-Term Outcomes of Trauma Patients After Treatment With Prehospital Tranexamic Acid: A Subgroup Analysis From the PATCH-Trauma Trial
Authors: Biswadev Mitra, Sara MacKenzie, Elyssia M Bourke, Rumer Aldous, Abby Grant, Stephen Bernard, Dashiell Gantner, David J Read, Michael C Reade, Tony Trapani, Paul S Myles, Peter A Cameron, Russell L Gruen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aim of this study was to assess 24-month functional outcomes of patients randomly assigned to prehospital tranexamic acid (TXA) compared with those assigned to placebo.MethodsThe PATCH-Trauma trial assessed administration of TXA by prehospital clinicians and demonstrated greater survival at 28 days from injury among patients allocated to receive TXA, but no difference in favorable functional outcomes at 6 months.This was a subgroup analysis of patients enrolled in the PATCH-Trauma trial. The primary outcome measure was the 8-point Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOS-E) at 24 months after injury, dichotomized to favorable functional outcomes (GOS-E 5-8) and dead or unfavorable functional outcomes (GOS-E 1-4). We also assessed mortality at 24 hours, 28 days, 6 months, and 24 months after injury.ResultsThere were 584 patients eligible for inclusion, and 516 patients had data on 24-month outcomes available. Baseline characteristics were comparable. At 24 months, a favorable outcome was reported in 167 (64.0%; 95% CI: 57.8-69.8) patients in the TXA group and 149 (58.4%; 95% CI: 52.1-64.5) in the placebo group (P = .20). There was no difference in the change in GOS-E profile from 6 to 24 months (coeff −0.06; 95% CI: −0.30 to 0.19; P = .64). There was no difference in mortality at any timepoint.ConclusionPrehospital treatment and ongoing hospital infusion of TXA did not result in improved survival or statistically significant functional outcomes at 24 months compared to placebo. Consideration of longer-term outcomes after trauma is recommended for evidence to practice decisions.Keywords: wounds and injuries, blood transfusion, tranexamic acid, death, shock, hemorrhagic, emergency medicine

The Bottom Line

We followed 584 patients who were randomly allocated to receive prehospital tranexamic acid (TXA) or placebo as part of the PATCH-Trauma Trial. More patients in the TXA group reported a favorable outcome at 24 months after injury (64% vs 58%), but the difference did not reach statistical significance. Prehospital treatment and ongoing hospital infusion of TXA did not result in improved survival or statistically significant functional outcomes at 24 months compared to placebo.

Introduction

Background

Despite improvements in trauma systems, injury remains the leading cause of death among young adults, even in settings with advanced trauma systems.1 Although there have been some improvements in-hospital mortality, the overall burden of injuries on premature deaths remains relatively unchanged.2 The leading cause of preventable death after trauma is hemorrhage.3,4

Acute traumatic coagulopathy or trauma-induced coagulopathy due to pathological hyperfibrinolysis is commonly observed in severely injured patients with significant hemorrhage5 and has been associated with greater blood loss and adverse clinical outcomes.6 Antifibrinolytic medications are therefore a logical consideration during the management of patients with critical bleeding. Tranexamic acid (TXA) is the most commonly available antifibrinolytic agent, and early administration of TXA is one of the few resuscitative interventions that have been proven in a randomized controlled trial to reduce mortality after trauma.7

The PATCH-Trauma randomized controlled trial assessed administration of TXA by prehospital clinicians and demonstrated greater survival at 28 days from injury among patients allocated to receive TXA.8 However, there was no difference in favorable functional outcomes at 6 months as measured by the dichotomized Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOS-E 1-4 compared with GOS-E 5-8), and the results suggested that most additional survivors in the TXA group were still severely disabled (GOS-E 3) at 6 months after injury.

Importance

One unanswered question from the PATCH-Trauma trial was whether 6 months was long enough to assess recovery after major trauma. There is some evidence that more than 6 months of physical and psychological rehabilitation may be required for patients to achieve their best possible recovery.9

Goals of This Investigation

The aim of this study was to assess 24-month functional outcomes among a subgroup of patients enrolled in the PATCH-Trauma trial.

Methods

This was a secondary analysis of a subgroup of patients enrolled in the PATCH-Trauma trial, whose methods have previously been reported.10 In brief, adult patients (≥18 years of age) were enrolled at the scene of their incident by 15 emergency medical services in Australia, New Zealand, and Germany. Patients were eligible for inclusion in the PATCH-Trauma trial if they were assessed as being at high risk for trauma-induced coagulopathy and if the first dose of TXA or placebo could be administered within 3 hours after injury and before hospital admission. Prehospital assessment of coagulopathy risk was performed with the use of the Coagulopathy of Severe Trauma (COAST) score, with a score of ≥3 considered to be high risk and eligible for enrollment. COAST scores range from 0 to 7, with 1 point assigned for each of the following variables: entrapment in a vehicle, systolic blood pressure of <100 mm Hg, body temperature of less than 35 °C, suspected pneumothorax, and suspected intraabdominal or pelvic injury. Additional points were assigned if the systolic blood pressure was <90 mmHg or if the body temperature was <32 °C.11

In the PATCH-Trauma trial, randomization was stratified by state jurisdictions. The subgroup included in this study comprised those patients enrolled in the state of Victoria, Australia. This subgroup was analyzed by a joint research collaborative between The Alfred and Royal Melbourne Hospitals, which are the only 2 adult major trauma services in Victoria, Australia. Between them, they received more patients enrolled in PATCH-Trauma than any other state in Australia. Patients (or the person responsible) who were alive at 6 months were contacted by telephone, and the GOS-E was recorded using a structured interview.

Intervention

Prehospital clinicians administered one dose of TXA (1 g) or placebo intravenously as a bolus as soon as practicable at the scene or en route to the hospital. After hospital arrival, the second ampule in the trial pack, containing TXA (1 g) or placebo, as originally randomized, was infused over a period of 8 hours. In addition to TXA or placebo, patients received usual prehospital, in-hospital, and posthospital care.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a favorable functional outcome at 6 months, as assessed with the use of the GOS-E, administered as a standardized questionnaire by trained telephone interviewers.12 For this study, we extended the timeframe to assess GOS-E at 24 months using methodology consistent with the primary analysis. Favorable GOS-E was defined as scores of 5 to 8. We also assessed mortality at 24 hours, 28 days, 6 months, and 24 months.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics summarized using categoric variables were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test if the value in a cell was <5. Continuous variables with near-normal distribution were compared using Student’s t test, whereas skewed variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Outcomes of mortality and favorable GOS-E were presented using risk ratios and 95% CIs.

Furthermore, as longitudinal data of participants were likely to be correlated, we conducted an analysis of response profiles to test if there was a difference in the pattern of change in GOS-E from 6 months to 24 months between the TXA and placebo groups.13 This corresponded to an interaction. A mixed effects model was developed that included an effect of TXA (placebo as the reference group), an effect of time (with 6 months as the reference group), and an interaction between TXA and time. We did not include any random effects and assumed that the residuals within a patient were related and that the covariance matrix for the residuals within a patient was unstructured. We used restricted maximum likelihood for estimation and tested for parallelism between the change in GOS-E from 6 months to 24 months among the 2 groups. P values of <.05 were defined to be statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using Stata v 18.0, College Station, TX, USA. This secondary analysis was approved by The Alfred Hospital Human Research and Ethics Committee, with site-specific authorization from the Royal Melbourne Hospital Ethics Committee.

Results

The PATCH-Trauma trial enrolled 1310 patients with treatment allocation and consent for data valid for 1300 patients. Among these patients, 584 patients were enrolled in the state of Victoria, randomized within the strata of the state, and included in this study. Limitations

This study was limited to being a post hoc subgroup analysis of patients from the PATCH-Trauma trial. Although patients were randomized to TXA within this subgroup, the smaller sample size has reduced statistical power and as such is at risk of type 2 error. However, ongoing improvement in participants with severe disability at 6 months after injury was a possibility that could affect the interpretation of the PATCH-Trauma results. The population enrolled from Victoria was all transported by helicopter emergency medical services with long prehospital times, and, therefore, the results cannot be readily translated to patients experiencing shorter prehospital times. This subgroup also had greater injury severity, and more patients had traumatic brain injury.

Discussion

At 24 months after injury, there were no differences in the proportion of patients with favorable functional outcomes among groups randomly assigned to TXA or placebo. Improvements in functional outcomes from 6 to 24 months were observed for the overall cohort but were not significantly different between the 2 groups. Furthermore, in this subgroup of patients from the PATCH-Trauma trial, a difference in mortality between the TXA and placebo groups was not demonstrated at any timepoint up to 24 months after injury.

Following the CRASH-2 trial that demonstrated a significant mortality benefit following TXA administration for trauma, no other trial investigating TXA for trauma has demonstrated a difference for the primary outcome in a multitrauma population. In the STAAMP trial, patients who received TXA compared with placebo in the prehospital setting did not have a significantly lower rate of 30-day mortality (8.1% vs 9.9%).14 For the primary outcome of the PATCH-Trauma trial, prehospital administration of TXA followed by an infusion over 8 hours did not result in a greater number of patients surviving with a favorable functional outcome at 6 months than placebo (risk ratio, 1.00; 95% CI: 0.90-1.12).8

In this follow-up study, we demonstrate that while there was ongoing recovery of the study cohort, the recovery rate among patients assigned to the TXA group was not significantly different. Thus, in the setting of critical bleeding, the core principles of recognition and early hemorrhage control remain essential for resuscitation teams. Following this, the maintenance of perfusion through transfusion of blood or components is physiologically supported for the maintenance of tissue perfusion. We continue to recommend that, as an adjunct therapy, early TXA should be considered, given the low cost, low rates of adverse events, and signals of benefit for survival. This is based on the potential of benefit demonstrated in the CRASH-2 trial and secondary outcome measures of the PATCH-Trauma trial and the STAAMP trial.

Following major trauma, persisting disability among survivors over the long term has been highlighted.15, 16, 17 However, longitudinal studies in posttrauma patients assessing long-term outcomes are uncommon. Providing estimates of the nonfatal injury burden is therefore often overlooked when assessing outcomes after trauma, and few registries or trials have assessed this measure. Where assessed, adverse long-term outcomes appear to be amplified in low- and middle-income countries, reinforcing the need to consider long-term function before treatment decisions.18 No other randomized trial of TXA has reported long-term outcomes.

Given the trajectory of recovery, assuming a similar pattern was maintained over time, it is possible that early TXA could result in better functional recovery at a longer time point. It is possible that the observed numerical difference in long-term favorable outcome, if consistent with the full study protocol, may have reached statistical significance if long-term outcomes were available for the complete data set.

The best patient-centered outcome for trauma resuscitation continues to be debated.19,20 The single intervention of prehospital TXA is postulated to improve trauma-induced coagulopathy and, following on, early mortality. However, there are potential effects on longer-term outcomes that are mediated by effects, such as immunomodulation, unrelated to coagulopathy. The importance of survival alone vs the quality of survival may well be an individual choice that cannot be determined during trauma resuscitation. Faced with this uncertainty, it appears that clinicians prefer to prioritize survival alone, given the persistence and expansion of TXA into guidelines for critical bleeding. With varied individual preferences in any population, patient and public involvement through consumer engagement would be valuable in weighing up these possible outcomes.

Prehospital TXA with ongoing infusion over 8 hours did not result in improved functional outcomes at 24 months compared to placebo. It is possible that differences in functional outcomes might be observed at a longer time point.
