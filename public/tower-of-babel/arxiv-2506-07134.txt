Reliable Critics: Monotonic Improvement and Convergence Guarantees for Reinforcement Learning
Eshwar S. R, Gugan Thoppe, Aditya Gopalan, Gal Dalal

Abstract
Despite decades of research, it remains challenging to correctly use Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms with function approximation. A prime example is policy iteration, whose fundamental guarantee of monotonic improvement collapses even under linear function approximation. To address this issue, we introduce Reliable Policy Iteration (RPI). It replaces the common projection or Bellman-error minimization during policy evaluation with a Bellman-based constrained optimization. We prove that not only does RPI confer textbook monotonicity on its value estimates but these estimates also lower bound the true return. Also, their limit partially satisfies the unprojected Bellman equation, emphasizing RPI’s natural fit within RL. RPI is the first algorithm with such monotonicity and convergence guarantees under function approximation. For practical use, we provide a model-free variant of RPI that amounts to a novel critic. It can be readily integrated into primary model-free PI implementations such as DQN and DDPG. In classical control tasks, such RPI-enhanced variants consistently maintain their lower-bound guarantee while matching or surpassing the performance of all baseline methods.

Introduction

Function Approximation (FA) in Reinforcement Learning (RL) creates a fundamental challenge: while necessary for handling large state-action spaces, it also makes learning unreliable
[
1
]
. The risk is most evident in Policy Iteration (PI)
[
2
]
, a foundational RL algorithm that alternates between policy evaluation and a greedy policy update. In the tabular setting, the successive policies generated by PI improve monotonically and converge to the optimal one. However, with FA, the approximation errors during policy evaluation often mislead the greedy policy updates
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. In particular, FA methods tend to overestimate some state–action values and underestimate others, offering no consistent signal for reliable policy improvement
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.

Prior work primarily addresses this challenge by limiting the magnitude of policy updates. Conservative PI (CPI)
[
9
]
, for instance, bounds policy degradation by cautiously combining the existing policy with the one that is greedy with respect to the latest value estimate. Similarly, Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)
[
10
]
uses KL divergence to maintain proximity to previous policies, while Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
11
]
achieves a similar goal via clipping.

In this work, we instead target the root cause: policy evaluation. While tabular PI calculates exact value functions, standard FA variants typically settle for either a mean-squared projection
[
4
,
12
]
, the Bellman-residual minimizer
[
3
]
, the projected Bellman fixed-point
[
12
]
, or their bootstrapped extensions
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Despite their widespread adoption, these approaches fundamentally fail to preserve the theoretical guarantees that ensure PI’s stability and consistent improvement
[
1
]
.

We propose a new PI paradigm to address the above issues. Our key contributions include:

1.

Algorithm:
We introduce Reliable Policy Iteration (RPI) in Algorithm
1
. It uses the standard greedy policy update, but adopts a novel idea for policy evaluation: it finds a value estimate that is
farthest
from the previous estimate while satisfying a Bellman inequality constraint.

2.

Theoretical Guarantees:
In Section
3
, we show that RPI recovers the classical PI guarantees of per-step improvement and convergence in the FA setting—where these properties, so far, were out of reach. Specifically, we prove that RPI’s value estimates are non-decreasing and lower bound the true policy values, thereby safeguarding performance even when intermediate policies momentarily degrade. Moreover, RPI’s value estimates converge to a vector that partially satisfies the unprojected Bellman equation, demonstrating natural alignment with fundamental RL objectives. We also bound the performance gap between RPI’s terminal policy and the optimal policy. Separately, we establish RPI as a true generalization of classical PI and show that, with an
\ell_{1}
-type norm, its policy evaluation step admits a constrained projection interpretation.

3.

Practical Implementation:
In Section
5
, we develop a model-free realization of RPI’s policy-evaluation step. We embed the constraints as penalties in the objective and then invoke Jensen’s inequality to get a tractable function. This serves as a new critic loss (in the actor-critic paradigm) that seamlessly integrates into popular deep RL algorithms such as Deep Q-Network (DQN)
[
15
]
and Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)
[
16
]
.

4.

Empirical Results
: Our experiments in Section
6
show that RPI and its deep-RL variants consistently maintain their lower-bound property across diverse benchmarks—from discrete Inventory Control to classical continuous control tasks such as CartPole and Inverted Pendulum. Crucially,
RPI-enhanced DQN and DDPG outperform their vanilla versions and meet or surpass competitive baselines such as PPO
[
11
]
and TD3
[
8
]
in reaching performance thresholds.

Related Work

PI for finite, tabular Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) was introduced by Bellman
[
17
]
and formalized by Howard
[
2
]
. Since the Bellman operator is both a contraction and monotone, PI produces an increasing sequence of value estimates that converges to the optimum. There also are tabular extensions, such as modified
[
18
]
and asynchronous PI
[
19
]
, that retain these guarantees while allowing for incomplete policy evaluations and partial policy updates. Given this flexibility, a natural question is whether PI can be adapted to the FA setting while still preserving these guarantees.

The earliest use of dynamic programming with FA can be traced to Samuel’s checkers program
[
20
,
21
]
, which selected moves via multistage lookahead while evaluating positions with a value function expressed as a linear combination of handcrafted features. Since then, different attempts have been made to extend PI to the FA setting, which can be categorized and summarized as follows:

1.

Policy Iteration with Approximate Evaluation.
A large body of work is based on changing only the policy evaluation step, leaving the greedy policy update unchanged. During evaluation, these methods typically take one of three paths: (i) minimize mean-squared projection error, as in TD(1)
[
12
]
; (ii) solve for the projected Bellman fixed point, as in LSPI and TD(
\lambda
)
[
22
,
12
]
; or (iii) minimize the Bellman residual, as in Fitted Q-Iteration, DQN, and AMPI-Q
[
14
,
15
,
23
]
. However, to paraphrase
Bertsekas 2011
, “these methods assume that a more accurate value approximation will yield a better policy—a reasonable but by no means self-evident hypothesis.” These methods are often known to diverge, oscillate between worse policies, or exhibit unreliability in learning good policies, leading to weak performance bounds
[
1
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. An approach that can address these concerns under a greedy actor has remained elusive thus far.

2.

Policy Iteration with Cautious Actor.
A complementary research line has focused on making policy updates cautious either by (i) bounding the KL divergence between successive policies or (ii) forming a convex mixture between the current and the full greedy policies. This idea dates back to Conservative PI (CPI)
[
9
]
and underpins several influential algorithms, including TRPO
[
10
]
, PPO
[
11
]
, and Safe PI (SPI)
[
27
]
.
[
27
, Remark 3]
shows that such cautious updates can yield strong per-step improvement bounds.
However, these guarantees presuppose access to accurate value estimates for
every
state–action pair—an assumption that cannot be met in realistic FA settings. Also, deep RL approaches built on cautious policy updates—such as TRPO and PPO—are notoriously sample-inefficient since they rely exclusively on on-policy data.

3.

Soft Policy Iteration.
This family of methods, introduced in
[
28
]
, was designed to simultaneously remedy the unreliability of off-policy value-based methods such as DQN and DDPG and the high sample complexity of on-policy trust-region algorithms like TRPO and PPO. In its evaluation phase, Soft PI augments the standard discounted return with an entropy bonus, encouraging exploration and smoothing the value estimates. The subsequent improvement step then searches for a policy that stays close—in KL divergence—to the Boltzmann (soft-max) distribution induced by the current (soft) value estimates. Although this scheme has shown promise in practice, its formal per-iteration improvement guarantees still hold only in the tabular setting.

4.

Policy Iteration with Multi-step Lookahead.
In classical PI, the actor jumps to a policy that is greedy with respect to the current value function. Multistep-lookahead variants instead choose a policy that is greedy over an

h
-step roll-out. While this still guarantees monotonic improvement in tabular MDPs
[
29
]
, the picture changes once FA enters. In
[
30
]
, it is shown that, with least squares approximation during policy evaluation, the value estimates can diverge unless
h
exceeds a problem-dependent threshold. Even in tabular settings such lookahead ideas face inherent limitations: naive ideas for partial policy evaluations
may lead to divergence
[
31
]
, and conservative PI loses its one-step monotonicity as soon as
h>1

[
32
]
.
Collectively, these results point to fortifying the critic rather than tightening constraints on the actor.

5.

Value Iteration with Overestimation Bias Control.
Q-learning, DQN, and DDPG use the same value estimates both to choose and to evaluate policies
[
33
,
15
,
16
]
. This parameter sharing, combined with the max in the Bellman operator, introduces the classic over-estimation bias. Double Q-learning
[
34
]
was introduced to counter this bias by keeping two independent critics: one to picks the greedy action and the other to rate it. This idea has led to popular deep RL algorithms such as Double DQN
[
7
]
and TD3
[
8
]
, both of which markedly curb over-estimation in practice. Yet rigorous guarantees under FA remain an open question. This motivates our focus on formal ways to address the over-estimation bias.

Finally, there is classification-based PI
[
35
]
that treats the greedy policy step as a supervised learning problem, but forfeits per-iteration guarantees. There is also a linear programming
approach to frame dynamic programming with linear function approximation
[
36
]
. This approach may appear similar to our proposed RPI, but the resemblance is only skin-deep. Theirs is a single pass approach that yields a lower bound on the optimal Q-values. In contrast, our method arises from a new policy-evaluation step inside PI. It is iterative and supplies lower bounds on the value of the current policy.

In summary, in this work, we revisit the fundamental question of whether monotonicity and convergence guarantees can be salvaged, in a reasonable sense, with (arbitrary) value FA but aggressive (greedy) policy updates. Furthermore, we ask if this can be done to yield deep-RL methods that exceed or match the performance of existing deep-RL baselines.

Problem Formulation, Algorithm, and Main Result

We formalize our problem setting, then present RPI, and summarize its theoretical guarantees.

3.1
Setup and Problem Formulation

We have a stationary MDP
\mathcal{M}\equiv(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},r,\gamma).
Here,
\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{A}

and

Further,

from state

under action

Finally,

is the per-step reward function, and

Suppose

For a stationary policy

define its Q-value function

Q_{\mu}(s,a):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\middle|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a\right],
where
s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
and

Further, let

T_{\mu}Q(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime},a^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\mu(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

(1)

and

TQ(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

(2)

Our objective is to develop a PI variant for the FA setting that preserves the key monotonicity and convergence guarantees enjoyed by asynchronous PI. Henceforth, we use
\mathcal{F}=\{f:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}\}
to denote the given FA space for representing the Q-value functions. Clearly, each

Discussion about main results
: We highlight the following three key implications.

1.

Monotonic reliability under FA
. Theorem
3.1
proves that
(f_{k})
is coordinate-wise non-decreasing and lower bounds the true Q-values of the corresponding policies. Even if the policies themselves degrade, their performance cannot drop below
f_{k}.
This is the first guarantee of monotonic improvement for PI under general FA. In contrast, existing methods like CPI
[
9
]
, TRPO
[
10
]
, and AMPI-Q
[
23
]
require accurate
Q
-value estimates across all
SA
-many state-action pairs—an assumption that fails in the general FA setting.

2.

Convergence to Bellman-consistent points
. The same theorem shows that
f_{k}
converges to a limit
f_{\infty}
that
partially
satisfies both the optimality Bellman equation and the policy Bellman equation for
\mu_{\infty}
. While multiple such fixed points may exist (a fundamental FA limitation), RPI naturally aligns with the core RL goal of solving the unprojected Bellman equations. Moreover, unlike projection-based schemes that can oscillate between poor policies
[
1
]
, RPI guarantees that the limiting policy’s value is never below
f_{\infty}.

3.

Geometric interpretation in weighted-
\ell_{1}
. Theorem
3.3
reveals that under any weighted-
\ell_{1}
norm, RPI’s evaluation step is equivalent to projecting
Q_{\mu_{k}}
onto the carefully chosen constrained set
\{f\in\mathcal{F}:T_{\mu_{k}}f\geq f\geq f_{k}\}.
Unlike a full-space projection, this selective projection embeds the Bellman inequalities directly. While this exact projection interpretation does not generalize to arbitrary norms, the coordinate-wise monotonicity guarantees hold for any norm, making our constrainted optimization a faithful extension of tabular PI to the FA setting.

In summary, by embedding the Bellman inequalities directly into the evaluation program in (
3
), RPI fully restores the classical PI guarantees of monotonic improvement and convergence—properties unattainable by any previous FA-based PI variant.

\mu_{k}=\mu,
the monotonicity of
\|\cdot\|
would imply that the solution
f_{k+1}
of the optimization problem in (
3
) would have satisfied
f_{k+1}>f_{\infty},
a contradiction. This shows that
Tf_{\infty}\geq_{\textnormal{p}}f_{\infty},
as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Since
\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{R}^{SA},
we have that
Q_{\mu_{k}}\in\mathcal{F}.
On the other hand, Claim
4.1
implies that every element in
\{f\in\mathcal{F}:T_{\mu_{k}}f\geq f\geq f_{k}\}
satisfies
Q_{\mu_{k}}\geq f\geq f_{k}
or, equivalently,
Q_{\mu_{k}}-f_{k}\geq f-f_{k}\geq 0.
The strict monotonicity of
\|\cdot\|
then implies that
\|Q_{\mu_{k}}-f_{k}\|\geq\|f-f_{k}\|.
Hence,
f_{k+1}=Q_{\mu_{k}}.
Invoking the classical convergence proof for PI
[
4
]
now shows that
f_{\infty}=Q_{*}
and
\mu_{\infty}
is the optimal policy, as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

For any
f
satisfying the constraints in (
3
), we have from Claim
4.1
that
Q_{\mu_{k}}\geq f\geq f_{k}.
Hence, for any weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{SA}
with strictly positive values, we have

\|f-f_{k}\|_{w,1}=\sum_{s,a}w(s,a)[f(s,a)-f_{k}(s,a)]=-\|Q_{\mu_{k}}-f\|_{w,1}+\|f_{k}-Q_{\mu_{k}}\|_{w,1}.

Since the rightmost term in the last expression is independent of

the desired result follows.

5
Scalable Implementation of RPI’s Policy Evaluation Step

Our algorithm’s formulation in (
3
) is impractical for RL in large or continuous state–action spaces: it needs a separate constraint for every state–action pair and assumes full knowledge of the Bellman operator
T_{\mu_{k}}.
We now present a strategy to overcome these challenges.

5.1
Penalty-Barrier Reformulation

To rein in the large number of constraints, we first convert (
3
) into an unconstrained surrogate. Specifically, at each policy evaluation step, we draw a mini-batch of state-action pairs, embed the associated constraints within penalty-barrier terms, and then move them to the objective. For the
\ell_{1}
-formulation of (
3
), these steps yield the following unconstrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{C}}\hskip-1.49994pt\Big[\hskip-2.5pt-f(s,a)\hskip-1.49994pt+\hskip-1.49994pt\lambda_{1}\big[f(s,a)-(T_{\mu_{k}}f)(s,a)\big]_{+}\hskip-1.49994pt+\hskip-1.49994pt\lambda_{2}\big[f_{0}(s,a)-f(s,a)\big]_{+}\Big].

(10)

Here,
[x]_{+}=\max\{x,0\}=\textnormal{ReLU}(x).
Further,
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
are our algorithm’s hyperparameters,
\mathcal{C}
is a batch of sampled
(s,a)
pairs, and
f_{0}
is our initial value estimate.

We now discuss how we arrive at the above formulation, starting from (
3
). Since we consider the
\ell_{1}
-norm, the objective function is
\|f-f_{k}\|_{1}=\sum_{s,a}[f(s,a)-f_{k}(s,a)].
Also, the
(s,a)
-th constraint is of the form
(T_{\mu_{k}}f)(s,a)\geq f(s,a)\geq f_{k}(s,a).
In the model-free setting, note that our value estimates will be noisy. Hence, we drop
f_{k}
from the objective and relax the
(s,a)
-th constraint to
(T_{\mu_{k}}f)(s,a)\geq f(s,a)\geq f_{0}(s,a).
The change in the objective is insignificant since it will not affect the gradient. The change in the constraint implies we now aim for improvement only relative to the initial estimate
f_{0}
. Next, we move each of the relaxed constraints to the objective function and add them as the two penalty terms
\lambda_{1}\big[f(s,a)-(T_{\mu_{k}}f)(s,a)\big]_{+}
and
\lambda_{2}\big[f_{0}(s,a)-f(s,a)\big]_{+}.
Clearly, these ReLU-based terms incur a penalty exclusively when the corresponding constraint is violated. We now arrive at the formulation in (
10
).

5.2
Model-Free Reformulation

The reformulated objective in (
10
) depends on the Bellman operator
T_{\mu_{k}}
. By definition (see (
1
)),
T_{\mu_{k}}
takes an expectation over the next state. It, thus, needs knowledge of transition probabilities which is seldom available in practice. A typical workaround for expectation-based terms is to replace them with empirical averages. However, our
T_{\mu_{k}}
-based penalty term equals
\lambda_{1}[\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s,a)}[f(s,a)-(r(s,a)+\gamma f(s^{\prime},\mu_{k}(s^{\prime})))]]_{+},
i.e., it has an expectation inside the ReLU function. This nesting prevents a straightforward, sample-average approximation and poses a distinct computational challenge.

To overcome the above hurdle, we leverage Jensen’s inequality which states that
g(\mathbb{E}[X])\leq\mathbb{E}[g(X)]
for any convex function
g.
Since the ReLU function is convex, Jensen’s inequality shows that our
T_{\mu_{k}}
-based penalty term is bounded from above by
\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s,a)}[f(s,a)-(r(s,a)+\gamma f(s^{\prime},\mu_{k}(s^{\prime})))]]_{+}.
We now replace this expression with a sample average to arrive at our final model-free objective:

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{(s,a,r,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[

\displaystyle-f(s,a)\hskip-1.49994pt+\hskip-1.49994pt\lambda_{1}\Big[f(s,a)-r\hskip-1.49994pt-\hskip-1.49994pt\gamma f\big(s^{\prime},\mu_{k}(s^{\prime})\big)\Big]_{+}\hskip-1.49994pt+\hskip-2.5pt\lambda_{2}\big[f_{0}(s,a)-f(s,a)\big]_{+}\Bigg].\hskip-5.0pt

(11)

Here,
\mathcal{D}
is the experience dataset: every tuple
(s,a,r,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}
records that executing action
a
in state
s
yielded reward
r
and transitioned the system to next state
s^{\prime}
. The dataset
\mathcal{D}
may consist of experience tuples collected under several different behavior policies. Expressed this way, our objective function mirrors the empirical formulations commonly used in modern RL.

5.3
Integration with Deep Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

We now discuss how our critic loss from (
11
) can be plugged directly into popular Deep RL algorithms to address real-world tasks. DQN
[
15
]
, designed for finite-action environments, and DDPG
[
16
]
, suited for continuous-state, continuous-action environments, are natural choices for this integration. This is because both algorithms learn a Q-value function from data samples and derive their policies greedily from these Q-function estimates, aligning well with our algorithm’s approach.

In vanilla DQN and DDPG, the critic is trained by minimizing the Mean-Squared Bellman Error (MSBE)
\mathcal{L}^{\text{MSBE}}(f)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|}\left(y_{i}-f(s_{i},a_{i})\right)^{2}
, where
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
is a minibatch of sampled experiences, and
y_{i}=r_{i}+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}f_{\text{target}}(s^{\prime}_{i},a^{\prime})
in DQN and
y_{i}=r_{i}+\gamma f_{\text{target}}(s^{\prime}_{i},\mu_{\text{target}}(s^{\prime}_{i}))
in DDPG, with
f_{\text{target}}
and
\mu_{\text{target}}
being delayed copies of the critic optimizer and the current policy, respectively, maintained to provide stability during learning.

To obtain RPI-enhanced agents, we simply replace
\mathcal{L}^{\text{MSBE}}(f)
with our unified RPI objective

\mathcal{L}^{\text{RPI}}(f):=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|}\Bigg[-f(s_{i},a_{i})+\lambda_{1}\Big[f(s_{i},a_{i})-y_{i}\Big]_{+}+\lambda_{2}\Big[q_{\text{min}}-f(s_{i},a_{i})\Big]_{+}\Bigg].

(12)

The key difference between the above objective and one in (
11
) is that
y_{i}
now replaces
r+f(s^{\prime},\mu_{k}(s^{\prime}))
and
q_{\text{min}}
is a uniform lower bound on
f_{0}(s,a).
The resulting algorithms—RPI
DQN
and RPI
DDPG
—inherit all other training infrastructure from their baselines. These baselines are based on the Stable Baselines3 DQN codebase
[
37
]
and the TD3 authors’ DDPG code
[
8
]
, respectively.

7
Conclusions and Future work

We presented RPI—a new framework that restores the classical PI guarantees of monotonic value improvement and convergence under general FA settings. Whereas existing variants such as CPI, safe PI, and AMPI-Q offer theoretical assurances only in tabular MDPs, RPI’s analysis and guarantees hold directly with (arbitrary) approximated value functions. Extending RPI’s guarantees to the model-free regime is a primary direction for future work.
We also introduced a model-free critic loss that can be seamlessly dropped into deep-RL pipelines. Replacing the standard loss in DQN or DDPG with the RPI loss—without altering network architectures, buffer sizes, or other hyper-parameters—already delivers notable performance gains. A natural next step is to benchmark RPI-enhanced agents on more complex suites such as Atari and MuJoCo.

Appendix A
Evaluation Environments

In this section, we detail the three distinct environments used for evaluating our method. These environments were chosen to provide a diverse testbed across varying state and action space characteristics: (i) an inventory control problem (finite-state, finite-action), (ii) the CartPole environment (continuous-state, finite-action), and (iii) the Inverted Pendulum task (continuous-state, continuous-action). This selection comprehensively assesses our approach’s effectiveness and generalization across both discrete and continuous domains.

A.1
Inventory Control

In this work we consider a variant of the classical inventory control problem, modeled as a Markov Decision Process, where the objective is to maximize the expected long-term reward obtained from managing inventory in the presence of stochastic demand.

The inventory system has a fixed maximum capacity of
M
units. The state on day
t
, denoted by
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}=\{0,1,\dots,M\}
, represents the number of items currently in stock. The action space is
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1,\dots,M\}
, where an action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
denotes the number of items ordered at the beginning of day
t
. Each day yields a reward composed of three components as follows

•

Procurement cost
for the items ordered,

•

Holding cost
for leftover inventory, and

•

Revenue
from items sold.

Let
c
denote the unit procurement cost,
h
the holding cost per unit of unsold inventory, and
p
the unit selling price. Let
d_{t}
denote the demand on day
t
, sampled from a predefined demand distribution. Define the post-order inventory level as

\hat{s}_{t}=\min(s_{t}+a_{t},M),

which represents the total inventory available for sale on day
t
after ordering. The system evolves according to the transition rule

s_{t+1}=\max(\hat{s}_{t}-d_{t},0),

reflecting the remaining inventory after meeting demand. The immediate reward is given by

R(s_{t},a_{t},d_{t})=p\cdot\min(\hat{s}_{t},d_{t})-c\cdot a_{t}-h\cdot\max(\hat{s}_{t}-d_{t},0),

where the first term captures revenue from sales, the second term penalizes procurement, and the third penalizes excess inventory.

•

The

•

Figure
5
offers a detailed analysis of critic estimation accuracy in the CartPole environment, based on 25 learning curves, each from a unique random seed. For each seed, the solid lines represent the policy value, estimated via 100 Monte Carlo evaluations using the current policy. The dotted lines show the corresponding estimated value, computed by averaging the critic’s output for the initial state-action pair across these same trajectories.

In this CartPole-v1 environment, RPI
DQN
’s critic estimates consistently lower-bound the Monte Carlo policy estimates. PPO’s estimates are observed to be nearly accurate, though not strictly lower bounds. In stark contrast, standard DQN exhibits significant overestimation: as visually evident in Figure
5
, its dotted lines frequently appear far above the solid lines, often greatly exceeding the maximum possible environmental return (approximately 99.34 for a full 500-timestep episode with
\gamma=0.99
) even for suboptimal policies.

A similar trend of overestimation is observed in the Inverted Pendulum task (see Figure
6
), where we compare RPI
DDPG
against DDPG, PPO, and TD3. Here, standard DDPG critic estimates also suffer from substantial overestimation, with their dotted lines often surpassing the environment’s maximum possible value even for suboptimal policies. PPO’s critic estimates, however, are observed to tend to align with the true values; the dotted line comes close to the solid line in the figure. Conversely, both RPI
DDPG
and TD3 critic estimates are empirically observed to mostly lower-bound the Monte Carlo policy estimates.

In summary, original DQN and DDPG typically exhibit significant overestimation. By incorporating our new critic loss function, their RPI
DQN
and RPI
DDPG
variants now consistently yield a lower-bound on the policy values.
